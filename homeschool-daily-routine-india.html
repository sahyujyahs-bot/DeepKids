How to Plan a Homeschool Day in India (Ages 6–10)

By DeepKids  ·  November 2026  ·  9 min read

Most homeschooling advice for Indian parents is about curriculum — which board, which books, which syllabus. Almost none of it is about the thing that actually decides whether a family is still homeschooling in a year's time: the shape of the day.

Families rarely stop because the curriculum was wrong. They stop because every morning became a negotiation, because nobody knew what today was supposed to hold, and because by Thursday it felt like nothing had been achieved. That is a rhythm problem, and rhythm is learnable.

This is about children roughly 6 to 10 — Class 1 to Class 5. Younger children need something looser; older ones can carry more of their own structure. If you are still working out whether to homeschool at all, start with our honest guide to homeschooling in India.

How many hours a day is enough

For a 6 to 10 year old: two to three hours of focused work covers what a school day covers. Often less at the younger end.

This sounds impossible until you count what a classroom hour actually contains. Thirty children settling. Attendance. Repeating an instruction four times. Waiting for the slowest and parking the fastest. A lesson pitched at the middle of the room, which means roughly a third of the class is bored and a third is lost. Strip that out and what remains is startlingly compact.

The mistake new homeschooling parents make is recreating a six-hour school day at the dining table. It exhausts everyone, teaches no more than three good hours would, and is usually abandoned within a month — at which point the parent concludes homeschooling does not work, when what did not work was the timetable.

Rhythm beats a timetable

A timetable says 9:00 maths, 9:40 English, 10:20 break. It breaks the first time a child is slow to wake, a delivery arrives, or something genuinely interesting happens — and then the whole day reads as failed.

A rhythm says: mornings are for the things that need a fresh brain, afternoons are for making and reading, Wednesday we go out. It bends without breaking. The child still knows what is coming, which is the thing that actually reduces friction, but nothing collapses because a clock was missed.

Children at this age do not need to know the time. They need to know the order. "After breakfast we do maths" is a rhythm. "Maths at nine" is a hostage to fortune.

The shape of a day that works

Nearly every workable homeschool day for this age has the same four parts, in the same order, whatever the family calls them.

1. A warm-up (10–15 minutes)

Something small, done daily, that costs no willpower: mental maths in the kitchen, a poem, reading aloud, a page of handwriting. Its job is not content. Its job is the transition from morning to we are doing this now, without a confrontation.

2. The hard thing, while the brain is fresh (30–45 minutes)

Usually maths, or whatever the child finds most effortful. Do it first. Every family that moves maths to the afternoon "because mornings are difficult" discovers that afternoons are worse. Protect this slot and the rest of the day is downhill.

3. A language, and then something made (45–60 minutes)

English or Hindi — reading, writing, being read to. Then something with hands in it: an experiment, a drawing, a model, a long project that continues across weeks. This is where most of the real learning hides, and it is the first thing to be cut when a family is anxious about "falling behind". Cut it last, not first.

4. The world (open-ended)

Outside. A walk, a market, a garden, a repair, a visitor, a question followed to wherever it goes. Unscheduled and unmeasured, and the part a child will still be able to describe in ten years.

A worked example

A Tuesday for an eight-year-old. Not a prescription — a demonstration of the shape.

  • After breakfast: ten minutes of mental maths while the kitchen is cleared. Doubling, halving, times tables as a game, not a test.
  • Mid-morning: forty minutes of maths proper. One idea, worked slowly, with objects where it helps.
  • Break. Outside if at all possible. Not a screen.
  • Late morning: reading together, then the child writes something short and real — a letter, a list, an account of yesterday. Not a comprehension exercise.
  • Before lunch: science. Something that happens in front of them. Floating and sinking, a plant that has been watched for three weeks, why the fan cools you when it does not cool the room.
  • Afternoon: a project, free reading, a class you have outsourced, or nothing in particular.

Total focused time: a little over two hours. That is a full Class 3 day.

When it falls apart

It will. Three recurring failures, and what actually helps:

The child refuses. Usually the work is mispitched — too hard and they are protecting themselves, too easy and they are insulted — or it is the wrong hour. Shrink it. Five minutes of maths done willingly beats forty minutes of war, and tomorrow still exists.

The parent has a day. Work, a baby, illness, nothing left. Have a minimum day ready: read aloud, ten minutes of maths, go outside. Done. A declared minimum day is a day that worked. An abandoned day quietly becomes an abandoned week.

Three weeks disappear. Guests, a festival, travel, illness. This is normal and it is not catastrophic. Children do not forget everything in three weeks, and the compactness that makes a homeschool day short also makes lost ground quick to recover. What causes the damage is the guilt, not the gap.

Knowing whether it was enough

This is the hardest part of homeschooling and it has almost nothing to do with teaching.

In school, someone else carries the question. Reports arrive, terms end, a teacher says the child is doing fine. At home the parent carries it alone, usually at eleven at night, usually having forgotten the three genuinely good things that happened on Monday.

The only reliable fix is to write down what was done, on the day it was done. Not for anyone else — for the version of you in February who is certain nothing is happening. A term's worth of entries is a remarkably effective argument against that feeling, and it is usually an honest one.

It also answers the practical question of whether the child is covering the ground a school year would have covered — which is what matters if they may return to school, or sit NIOS later.
