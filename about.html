How this started

I fell in love with learning as an adult. Properly in love — the kind where one question about gravity keeps you up chasing ten more about orbits, atmospheres, and why anything falls at all. And inside that joy was a sting: why didn't school ever feel like this?

The more I dug, the clearer the gap in our education system became. So much of what we hand children is half-baked — simplified until it's wrong, memorised for a test, forgotten by summer. A kid can recite "gravity pulls things down" and still believe an astronaut floats because there's "no gravity in space." That recitation isn't knowledge. It's noise that sounds like knowledge.

Kids don't need more facts to memorise. They need to stand on one leg, drop the feather and the ball, and feel what a force is.

What we do differently

One game, one topic, real depth. Each DeepKids game takes a single subject — EscapeGravity takes gravity and its rival forces — and goes all the way in: many perspectives, real experiments, honest physics. Including the parts where scientists themselves still argue. No shallow trivia sprayed across a hundred topics.

Experience before explanation. Our games are built around physical challenges — balancing, tossing, blowing, jumping — because a force you've fought with your own body needs no mugging up. The idea arrives through the hands and stays in the head.

Respect for how deep kids are. A child's mind isn't fenced in by years of "that's just how it is." Hand it a genuine, first-principles question and it will turn the idea over in ways most adults have forgotten how to. We'd rather give a seven-year-old a real idea to wrestle with than a tidy lie. Kids rise to it. They always do.

Where we are

Our first game, EscapeGravity, is ready to order — a race to the International Space Station where gravity fights back, with 35+ physical challenges and 13 forces to master. It's been shaped by months of playtests with real families, refined every time a kid found a loophole or invented a better rule.

More worlds are coming: evolution, history, the sciences that deserve better than a paragraph in a textbook.
