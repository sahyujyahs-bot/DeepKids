NCERT, Cambridge or NIOS: Which Board for Homeschooling?

By DeepKids  ·  November 2026  ·  8 min read

This is the decision that stalls more Indian homeschooling families than any other. It is also the one most worth making quickly, because the comparison is genuinely interesting and will happily consume a year that the child needed you to spend teaching.

Here is the practical difference between NCERT, Cambridge Primary and NIOS for the primary years, what each actually commits you to, and the fourth option most comparisons leave out. For the wider picture — the legal position, cost and socialisation — see our guide to homeschooling in India.

What this choice does and does not decide

Before comparing: in the primary years, this decision matters less than it feels like it does.

No board certifies a child at Class 3. Nobody will ever ask which syllabus an eight-year-old followed. The content across all three is substantially the same — numbers, reading, writing, the body, plants, weather, the local area — because that is simply what children of this age learn everywhere.

What the choice does decide is the order and style in which that content arrives, how much it costs, and how easily a child could step into a school later. Those are real, but they are not irreversible. Most homeschooling families change track at least once, and children cope with it far better than parents expect.

NCERT (and CBSE)

NCERT is the national curriculum body whose textbooks underpin CBSE and most state boards. For a homeschooling family it is the default, and for good reasons.

What it gives you. Textbooks that are cheap and downloadable free as PDFs from the NCERT site. A sequence that matches what almost every school in India is doing, so a child who joins or rejoins a school lands roughly where their class is. An enormous supply of workbooks, guides and tuition that assume this syllabus. And if you are uncertain whether you will still be homeschooling in two years, it is the lowest-regret option by a distance.

What to watch. NCERT books are written for a teacher with thirty children and a term to get through. Read alone at a dining table they can feel thin — a topic is introduced, a few questions follow, the chapter ends. Homeschooling parents using NCERT almost always end up adding experiments, discussion and activity around the chapters. That is not a flaw so much as a job the book was never trying to do.

Choose it if school is a live possibility, you want the cheapest route, or you want the broadest support around you.

Cambridge Primary

Cambridge Primary is the international option, used by many IB and international schools in India and by families with an eye on moving abroad.

What it gives you. A genuinely different style: more inquiry, more "find out why", less memorise-and-recall. Materials are well made and well sequenced, with clear teacher guidance — which suits a homeschooling parent more than it may first appear, since you are the teacher. English is strong throughout.

What to watch. Cost, first — the books are several times the price of NCERT and there are no free PDFs. The cultural frame is international rather than Indian, so you will supplement for Indian history, geography and languages. And if your child later joins an Indian school on NCERT, the sequence will not line up neatly; it is a manageable gap but a real one.

Choose it if you want the inquiry-led style, English is a priority, international schooling is plausible, and the cost is comfortable.

NIOS

The National Institute of Open Schooling is the board built for learners outside conventional schools, which makes it the natural long-term home for many homeschooling families.

What it gives you. A recognised route to Class 10 and Class 12 certificates without ever enrolling in a school, which is the thing that quietly worries most parents. For the primary years its Open Basic Education programme covers the ground. Materials are inexpensive and designed for self-study rather than classroom delivery.

What to watch. NIOS is an examination and open-schooling body, not a publisher of beautiful primary textbooks. The primary-level material is functional rather than inspiring, and you will build the texture around it yourself. Many families use something else for the early years and come to NIOS at Class 10 — which is perfectly normal and loses nothing.

Choose it if you are confident about homeschooling through to the board years and want one consistent path, or you value the open-schooling route being settled early.

The fourth option: topic-led

All three above are textbook-led: the book sets the order, and the family follows it chapter by chapter.

The alternative is topic-led — the family follows an idea rather than a chapter. Floating and sinking leads to density, which leads to why ships made of steel do not sink, which leads to a bucket in the bathroom. A seed leads to what a plant eats, which leads to sunlight, which leads back to the food on the plate.

The case for it is that this is how curiosity actually moves, and a child who followed a question to its end remembers it years later in a way a completed chapter rarely matches. The case against is that it demands more of the parent, and without tracking it is genuinely possible to spend a year deep in volcanoes and never touch fractions.

In practice, a topic-led track works when it is mapped — when something is quietly checking that the ground a Class 3 would have covered is being covered, even though the route is not a textbook's route. That mapping is the whole difficulty, and it is why most families who love the idea end up back on a textbook.

How to actually pick

Four questions, and then stop reading comparisons:

  1. Is school a real possibility in the next two years? If yes, NCERT. The alignment is worth more than any other consideration.
  2. Is cost a constraint? If yes, NCERT or NIOS. Cambridge is a standing expense.
  3. Might you move abroad, or is English the priority? Cambridge.
  4. Are you confident you will homeschool all the way through? Then NIOS removes the certification worry early — though you can also arrive at it later with nothing lost.

Then give your choice a full term before reconsidering. A fortnight tells you nothing except that new things feel awkward. What matters far more than the board you pick is the shape of your days.
