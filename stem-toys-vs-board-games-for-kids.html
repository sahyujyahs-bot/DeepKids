STEM Toys vs Board Games for Kids: Which is Better for Learning?

By DeepKids  ·  June 2026  ·  7 min read

Every parent wants their child to love science. So when it's time to buy something educational, the question usually comes down to: a STEM kit (robotics, chemistry sets, coding toys) or an educational board game?

Both have their place. But they're not equal — and the right choice depends on what you actually want your child to get out of it. Let's break it down honestly.

What Are We Comparing?

STEM toys include things like robotics kits (Lego Mindstorms, mBot), chemistry sets, coding toys (Osmo, Cubetto), electronics kits, and science experiment boxes. They tend to be hands-on, solo or small-group experiences.

Educational board games include games specifically designed to teach science, maths, history, or critical thinking through play. Unlike STEM kits, they're inherently multiplayer and social.

Where STEM Kits Win

If your child has a specific passion — robotics, electronics, coding — a good STEM kit can go deep in a way a board game can't. A Lego Mindstorms set teaches actual programming logic. A circuit kit teaches real electronics. These are legitimate skills.

STEM kits also tend to have a satisfying "completion moment" — you built the robot, it works. That sense of achievement matters.

The problem: most kids try the kit once or twice and move on. The robot sits on a shelf. You've spent ₹5,000 on three sessions of play.

Where Board Games Win

A good educational board game is endlessly replayable. The science doesn't change, but the game always does — different dice rolls, different cards, different players. Kids don't feel like they're "repeating" the same lesson because the experience is always fresh.

Board games are also social by design. Science learned in competition or cooperation with other people sticks differently than science learned alone. Kids argue, teach each other, and remember what they experienced together.

And the best science board games don't just mention science — they make kids feel it. When a child has to balance on one leg while reciting a physics fact, that's an experience. Experiences build intuition. Intuition is what makes kids good at science class.

Our Verdict

For pure depth on a specific skill: STEM kit wins.

For value, replayability, family play, and broad science intuition: board game wins — and it's not close.

If you can only buy one thing, buy the board game. If you can buy both, start with the board game and add a STEM kit once you know which subject your child gravitates toward.
