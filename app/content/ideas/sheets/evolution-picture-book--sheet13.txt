R2: Red Algae (multicellular marine algae) | Inherited: No nervous cells at all – like their unicellular ancestors, they rely on cell-level chemical signaling (no neurons). Innovated: Multicellular coordination without nerves; cells communicate chemically to respond to light and stress, but no true nervous system is present. | Inherited: Gas exchange by simple diffusion across cell surfaces (no respiratory organs needed in water). Innovated: Thalli (algal bodies) are thin/leafy to maximize surface area for O₂/CO₂ diffusion in aquatic environments; no specialized respiratory structures since water delivers gases directly. | Inherited: Autotrophic nutrition via photosynthesis (chloroplasts from ancestral endosymbiosis) – no digestive organs; nutrients absorbed from water or produced internally. Innovated: Complex pigments (e.g. phycobilins) to capture low-light wavelengths and storage of sugars/starch internally; some can absorb dissolved organic matter, but there is no true digestive system (all feeding is at the cellular level). | Inherited: No circulatory system; internal transport occurs by cell-to-cell diffusion as in ancestral algae. Innovated: Simple cytoplasmic streaming and cell connections (e.g. pit plugs between cells) to distribute nutrients within the thallus; still no blood or vessels due to small size and aquatic habitat. | Inherited: Sexual reproduction with gametes (alternation of generations as in ancestral algae); still relies on water for fertilization. Innovated: Unique non-motile sperm (spermatia) and complex triphasic life cycle in some red algae – after fertilization, an enclosed carposporophyte develops on the mother plant, an innovation to increase zygote survival; spores (carpospores) are released, forming a separate generation. Also produce tough-walled spores that resist degradation, but still require aquatic dispersal. | Inherited: No muscle or motile organs; cells change shape or grow toward stimuli (very limited movement). Innovated: Flexible, cartilaginous cell walls and slippery mucilage allow bending with currents (helps prevent breakage); some can contract gelatinous holdfasts slightly to cling to rocks, but movement is entirely passive (via water flow). | Inherited: Rigid cellulose cell walls for structural support (as in ancestral plants). Innovated: In coralline red algae, deposition of calcium carbonate in cell walls (a rudimentary exoskeleton) for added support and herbivore deterrence – a new trait among algae. Most red algae have a gel-like matrix (agar/carrageenan) in their walls, giving them flexibility and shock absorption against waves. | Inherited: Basic chemical defenses against microbes/grazers (e.g. inherited ability to produce antimicrobial compounds as many algae do). Innovated: Thick cell walls and mucilage act as a barrier to pathogens and small herbivores; many red algae synthesize unique secondary metabolites (e.g. brominated compounds) that deter herbivores or fungal infections – a chemical immune strategy in lieu of cellular immunity. | Inherited: Cell-to-cell signaling molecules for growth and development (e.g. ancestral algae used simple hormones for reproduction). Innovated: Release of pheromone-like chemicals to coordinate gamete release in water; otherwise no specialized endocrine organs (each cell can respond to environmental cues like daylength or nutrients individually). | Inherited: No excretory organs; metabolic wastes (e.g. ammonia, oxygen from photosynthesis) diffuse out to the water directly. Innovated: Ability to deposit excess calcium or heavy metals in cell walls (effectively sequestering wastes); excess by-products can also be stored in inert form in cells. Red algae simply rely on water currents to carry away wastes, an unchanged strategy from single-celled ancestors. | Inherited: A simple epidermal cell layer with a polysaccharide cell wall (like ancestral algae). Innovated: Waxy and gelatinous coatings on the thallus that reduce desiccation during low tide exposure and protect against UV radiation; some (e.g. coralline algae) have a hard calcareous surface for protection. Overall, the “skin” is a mucilage-covered cell wall that deters pathogens and prevents drying, since true integument (multiple cell layers) is absent. | Inherited: Light-sensitive molecules (e.g. phycoerythrin pigment) allow orientation to light; some reproductive cells can sense chemicals. Innovated: Though lacking eyes or nerves, red algae can detect light intensity changes and adjust photosynthesis. A few have primitive photoreceptor patches on spores, but generally rely on inherited phototaxis mechanisms of their microscopic ancestors; no dedicated sensory organs evolved in this group.
R3: Fungi (aquatic/early fungi) | Inherited: No neurons or nervous system – like protist ancestors, communication is via chemical gradients and electric impulses along cells. Innovated: Mycelial network coordination – fungal hyphae can transmit signals (electrical and chemical) throughout the colony, functioning as a diffuse “network” that responds to nutrients or damage (e.g. a localized resource triggers whole-network growth toward it). There is still no true nervous system, but the fungus behaves as a coordinated organism despite lack of neurons. | Inherited: Gas exchange by diffusion across cell surfaces (ancestral fungi absorbed O₂ directly). Innovated: Thread-like hyphae permeate substrate and provide a large surface area for O₂ uptake and CO₂ release; in terrestrial forms, fungi often grow in humid microenvironments or within tissues to ensure gases stay dissolved. No dedicated respiratory organs evolved – even large fruiting bodies exchange gases via pores or surfaces. | Inherited: External digestion – like ancestral decomposer microbes, fungi secrete enzymes and absorb the breakdown products. Innovated: Powerful hydrolytic enzymes secreted into the environment to decompose complex polymers (cellulose, lignin) that earlier organisms couldn’t digest; this extracellular digestion allows fungi to “digest” food outside their bodies and then absorb nutrients. Hyphae then transport these nutrients inward. Fungi can decompose wood and other tough substrates, a novel ability in the ecosystem. | Inherited: No circulatory system; nutrient movement via diffusion from cell to cell. Innovated: Hyphal transport – cytoplasmic streaming within hyphae distributes nutrients throughout the mycelium (analogous to a simple circulatory function). Some fungi have septal pores between cells allowing nutrients and organelles to move freely. Thus, a fungus can translocate resources (e.g. send sugars from a sugar-rich area to growing tips elsewhere), an ability absent in single-celled ancestors. | Inherited: Spore reproduction (both sexual and asexual) as in ancestral fungi – producing numerous spores that disperse in water or air. Innovated: Complex life cycles with distinct mating types; sexual spores formed after hyphal fusion (plasmogamy/karyogamy) allowing genetic recombination. On land, fungi evolved stress-resistant spores with tough walls that survive desiccation. Some early fungi retained motile zoospores (with flagella) in water (an ancestral trait), but terrestrial fungi innovated non-motile spores dispersed by wind and fruiting bodies to launch them. | Inherited: No muscle tissue; movement occurs by growth (hyphae extending toward food). Innovated: Turgor-driven growth and movement – fungi penetrate substrates by generating internal pressure in hyphae, and some can rapidly eject spores (e.g. Pilobolus shoots spores by building up pressure). A few have trap structures (like constricting rings to snare nematodes) that snap shut via sudden turgor changes. These are “movements” achieved chemically, not by muscle, but represent new mechanisms to achieve tasks like prey capture or spore dispersal. | Inherited: Rigid cell walls (with chitin) providing shape (a trait shared with ancestral fungi). Innovated: Chitinous cell walls (a tough polysaccharide also found in arthropod exoskeletons) for strong structural support of hyphae; some fungi form large multicellular fruiting bodies (mushrooms) that stand erect – their cell walls and internal turgor form a rudimentary skeletal support. Fungi also produce hardened survival structures (sclerotia) that can be thought of as a protective “skeleton” to weather harsh conditions. | Inherited: Basic innate defenses – fungi produce antimicrobial metabolites (ancestral trait to outcompete bacteria). Innovated: Potent antibiotic production (e.g. Penicillium produces penicillin) to kill competitors – essentially chemical warfare as an immune strategy. Fungi can recognize self vs. non-self hyphae: if genetically different mycelia meet, they often reject fusion (an innate allorecognition system preventing parasitic takeovers). These innovations protect the fungus from microbes and other fungi, fulfilling immune-like roles without immune cells. | Inherited: Simple chemical regulation of growth and reproduction (e.g. ancestral fungi used pheromones for mating). Innovated: Mating pheromones that coordinate sexual reproduction (ensuring hyphae of different mating types grow toward each other); other hormones regulate processes like spore release and hyphal branching. While fungi have no discrete endocrine glands, specialized cells in the mycelium secrete signals (for example, to initiate fruiting body formation when conditions are right), acting as a primitive endocrine system. | Inherited: No excretory organs; waste metabolites diffuse out of cells. Innovated: External digestion blurs the line between digestion and excretion – fungi release enzymes and thereby “excrete” digestion byproducts into their environment, then reabsorb what they need. Toxic waste products can be retained in vacuoles or incorporated into inactive forms (e.g. oxalate crystals). Essentially, fungi innovate by dumping excess compounds into the environment (or into aging parts like fruiting body tissue that will be shed), relying on environmental dilution of wastes. | Inherited: A single-cell-thick outer layer (no skin or cuticle); ancestral fungi had cell walls for protection. Innovated: Chitin-rich cell wall that guards against environmental stress and predators; in terrestrial fungi, many hyphae aggregate and may form a protective outer layer on fruiting bodies (a pseudo-skin) to reduce water loss. Some fungi produce melanin in cell walls, shielding against UV radiation – an integumentary adaptation for life on land. | Inherited: Environmental sensing via receptors – ancestral fungi could sense chemicals and light (e.g. many spores germinate in response to nutrient cues). Innovated: Enhanced environmental sensitivity: fungi can detect subtle gradients of nutrients and grow towards them (chemotropism). Many fungi are light-sensitive; for instance, certain mushrooms only form when exposed to light, indicating evolved photoreceptors. They also sense gravity (negative gravitropism in mushroom stems to grow upward). Although fungi have no specialized sense organs, their mycelium acts as a diffuse sensory web, responding to touch, chemicals, light, and gravity to optimize growth.
R4: Green Algae (multicellular chlorophytes & charophytes) | Inherited: No nerve cells; each cell responds individually to stimuli (like light or chemicals) as did unicellular ancestors. Innovated: In motile forms (e.g. Volvox colonies), cells coordinate via chemical signals, allowing the whole colony to swim in a directed manner – an early step toward a nervous system. Some green algae show colony-wide coordination (e.g. flagellar beating in unison), but this is achieved chemically/electrically rather than by specialized neurons. | Inherited: Diffusion of gases across the entire cell or thallus surface (no respiratory organs, similar to ancestors). Innovated: Many developed flat, thin bodies or hollow tubes to maximize surface area for O₂/CO₂ exchange in water. No stomata in aquatic algae, but some charophyte algae living at land-water margins developed simple air‐exposed structures that could take up CO₂ from air when exposed. Overall, gas exchange remains via diffusion, but body shapes adjusted to facilitate it in new environments (e.g. surface-floating forms uptake CO₂ from air-water interface). | Inherited: Photosynthesis provides food; no ingestive digestion (as with ancestral algae). Innovated: Some green algae can also absorb dissolved organic nutrients or even exhibit mixotrophy (ingesting particles) in low light, an ability beyond strictly autotrophic red algae. Advanced forms (charophytes) protect their reproductive cells with sterile tissue, akin to primitive gametangia, providing nutrients to the developing zygote – a precursor to feeding an embryo, though true nutrient provisioning (endosperm) comes later in land plants. | Inherited: No circulatory system; each cell or filament obtains nutrients by diffusion from surroundings. Innovated: In larger multicellular green algae, rudimentary internal transport appears – for example, siphonous algae (like Caulerpa) have streaming cytoplasm that moves nutrients within giant cells. Some filamentous forms have specialized “transfer cells” to shuttle nutrients between adjacent cells. This is not a true circulatory system, but it’s an innovation toward internal distribution in big algae. | Inherited: Aquatic reproduction with flagellated gametes that swim (like ancestral protists). Innovated: Oogamy in some groups – i.e. evolution of a large non-motile egg and a small motile sperm (e.g. in charophytes), which is a step toward land-plant reproduction. Some green algae (e.g. Chara) developed protective jackets around gametes (primitive antheridia and oogonia), an inherited trait that green algae share with land plants. Also, many green algae can reproduce asexually by fragmenting or via motile spores, giving them flexible reproductive modes. | Inherited: No muscle tissue; motility comes from flagella on reproductive cells or motile unicells (a trait from unicellular ancestors). Innovated: Many green algae are motile in some stage – e.g. flagellar motility is refined: unicellular Chlamydomonas and colonial Volvox use eukaryotic flagella to swim. Colonial forms coordinate flagella for propulsion. A few large algae show minor contractile movements: for example, some filamentous algae can bend slowly toward light by differential growth. Nonetheless, contractile muscle did not evolve; movement remains limited to flagellar swimming or growth responses. | Inherited: Cellulose cell walls (like early plant ancestors) for structure. Innovated: Some calcify their tissues (e.g. Halimeda, a green alga, lays down calcium carbonate), giving a sturdier structure in wave-swept habitats. Charophyte algae (closest to land plants) developed plasmodesmata (tiny channels between cells) – an inherited trait passed to land plants – which structurally integrates multicellular algae and allows mechanical support via shared turgor pressure. While they have no true skeleton, turgid cell walls and sometimes calcium encrustation provide a simple support framework. | Inherited: Basic defense chemicals (e.g. antioxidants to handle UV or free radicals, like ancestral algae). Innovated: Green algae often form symbiotic relationships (e.g. with fungi in lichens), indicating biochemical compatibility and defense sharing. They produce deterrent molecules (some emit foul-tasting or toxic metabolites to avoid herbivory by fish/snails). Some filamentous green algae can differentiate a tough, dormant zygote with a resistant coat (sporopollenin in charophytes) – this protects the “embryo” from pathogens and harsh conditions, akin to an immune protective adaptation for that life stage. | Inherited: Simple chemical signaling for cell growth and reproduction (e.g. cues for gamete release tied to light cycles). Innovated: Introduction of plant hormones in rudimentary form: for instance, auxin-like substances are found in green algae and can influence cell elongation and division. Green algae also respond to pheromones during mating (e.g. Volvox releases sexual inducer proteins). These represent early endocrine-like communication, although dedicated organs are absent – every cell both senses and responds to such signals. | Inherited: No excretory organs; waste (like excess oxygen from photosynthesis, or ammonia from protein metabolism) diffuses into the water. Innovated: Some freshwater green algae can actively expel excess water (via contractile vacuoles in unicellular forms) to cope with osmotic stress, an ability carried from unicellular ancestors. Multicellular forms simply rely on diffusion and shedding parts (e.g. a dying portion of a thallus may sequester heavy metals or waste, then break off). Essentially, waste management remains minimal – an algae doesn’t concentrate toxins internally but releases or isolates them in inert cell wall material. | Inherited: A single-cell-layer “body surface” (no epidermis per se, just cell walls in contact with environment). Innovated: Some algae secrete a gelatinous sheath around colonies (e.g. Hydrodictyon nets or Nostoc balls in symbiosis) to prevent desiccation and shield from UV or predators – a primitive integument. In charophytes at water’s edge, a rudimentary cuticle-like polymer (sporopollenin) covers zygotes to prevent drying. Though true cuticle on vegetative parts is absent until land plants, these algae began evolving surface protections, foreshadowing the land plant integument. | Inherited: Light detection via eye-spots in motile cells – e.g. Chlorophyte algae like Chlamydomonas have a photoreceptive eyespot that guides swimming toward light, an ancestral sensory organelle. Innovated: Refinement of the eyespot and flagellar response for accurate phototaxis (green algae swim toward optimal light intensities for photosynthesis). Some green algae can sense gravity (negative geotaxis in certain planktonic forms) and many sense chemicals (moving toward nutrients). These sensory capabilities are still cell-autonomous (each cell reacts on its own), but in colonial forms, signals are coordinated so the entire colony moves as one toward light or nutrients.
R5: Sponges (Phylum Porifera) | Inherited: No neurons – sponges’ unicellular ancestors simply reacted to stimuli individually. Innovated: Intercellular communication that coordinates the whole sponge without nerves: sponge cells use chemical signals and calcium wave propagation to respond collectively (e.g. closing pores). They lack a true nervous system entirely​courses.lumenlearning.com, but can perform simple reflexes like contracting the body when irritated. This represents an early attempt at integrating responses among cells (myocytes around osculum contract together), even though no specialized neural tissue exists. | Inherited: Direct gas diffusion – every cell exchanges O₂/CO₂ with water, as did single-celled ancestors. Innovated: A constant water flow system: sponges pump water through their bodies (via choanocyte beating) which continuously brings in oxygen-rich water and expels CO₂ and wastes. This flow-through respiration means every cell is bathed in fresh water for gas exchange. There are no gills or respiratory organs; instead, the aquiferous canal system performs respiratory function by diffusion. | Inherited: Intracellular digestion – as in protozoan ancestors, sponge cells (choanocytes) engulf food particles and digest them inside vacuoles. Innovated: Filter feeding architecture: choanocytes create currents and trap bacteria/particles on their collars, ingesting them (phagocytosis)​courses.lumenlearning.com. Amoebocytes then distribute the nutrients. Sponges have no true digestive tract; the central spongocoel is not a stomach but a conduit for water. The innovation is a multicellular feeding strategy: filtering vast volumes of water to gather food, an ability unique to sponges at this stage. | Inherited: No circulatory system; ancestral condition of diffusion from cell to cell. Innovated: Internal water circulation doubles as nutrient transport: choanocytes capture food and amoebocytes wander, carrying nutrients to other cells. The constant flow of water acts like a primitive circulatory system, distributing oxygen and food throughout the sponge body​courses.lumenlearning.com. Sponges also have a gelatinous matrix (mesohyl) where amoebocytes move nutrients around – a proto-circulatory role without distinct vessels or blood. | Inherited: Asexual reproduction by fragmentation or budding (a trait common in simple organisms). Innovated: Both sexual and asexual reproduction: sponges produce sperm and eggs (often hermaphroditically) – sperm are released into water and captured by other sponges’ choanocytes which transport them to eggs (an internal fertilization strategy unique for such a simple organism). They also form gemmules (in freshwater sponges) – resilient cyst-like bundles of cells that can survive harsh conditions and regrow​courses.lumenlearning.com, an innovation allowing colonization of new areas after dormancy. | Inherited: No muscle fibers; ancestral cells could contract slightly with actin/myosin but no organized musculature. Innovated: Specialized contractile cells (myocytes) around openings and canals that can constrict the osculum or ostia, enabling the sponge to regulate water flow and “sneeze” out debris. The whole sponge body can also slowly contract by coordinating these cells. Though extremely slow and limited, this is the first appearance of multicellular body contractions without true muscle tissue. | Inherited: Structural support from spicules/minerals (some single-celled ancestors had biomineral structures). Innovated: A skeleton of spicules (silica or calcium carbonate spikes) and spongin fibers (collagen) that give the sponge its shape and rigidity. This internal meshwork is an innovation providing defense and support – spicules deter predators and support the canal system. Each sponge class has its own skeletal adaptation (glass sponges with elaborate silica networks, demosponges with spongin fibers), showing new evolutionary experiments in support structures. | Inherited: Basic self vs. non-self recognition at cellular level (even single cells can distinguish own vs. foreign). Innovated: Sponges exhibit primitive immune responses: if cells from a different sponge enter, they recognize and reject them (sponges will not fuse with non-kin – they “recognize” self tissue). This is an early immune system trait. Additionally, many sponges host symbiotic algae but can produce toxic chemicals to prevent over-colonization by microbes. These antimicrobial and antifouling compounds in their tissues are an adaptive defense, substituting for cellular immunity by chemically deterring pathogens. | Inherited: No distinct endocrine organs; ancestral sponges (and protozoans) used local chemical cues. Innovated: Sponge cells release chemical signals to coordinate activities – for example, pheromone-like substances can induce spawning (synchronized release of gametes) across many individuals. While lacking organs like glands, sponges rely on diffuse production of such signals. They also have growth factors within the mesohyl that regulate cell differentiation. This is a very rudimentary hormonal system operating within the sponge’s simple body plan. | Inherited: No excretory organs; metabolic wastes diffuse from cells into the water (the ancestral mode). Innovated: Using the water-current system to expel waste – ammonia and other wastes simply diffuse into the internal water stream and get flushed out the osculum​courses.lumenlearning.com. Essentially, the sponge’s constant filtering serves as a primitive excretory mechanism as well. Sponges have also specialized amoebocytes that can sequester and remove unwanted products (sometimes migrating to the sponge surface to discard wastes). | Inherited: A simple outer cell layer (pinacoderm) functions as a protective surface, similar to a proto-epithelium in single-celled ancestors that just had a membrane. Innovated: The pinacoderm acts like an integument: pinacocyte cells form a skin-like layer that protects inner cells and can regulate pore size. Sponges also embed gritty spicules in their surface and often secrete mucus, deterring predators and fouling organisms. Some even have symbiotic bacteria on their surface that produce protective chemicals. So, while only one cell thick, the sponge “skin” plus its biochemical defenses form an early integumentary system. | Inherited: Each cell is individually sensitive (ancestral protists responded to touch/chemicals). Innovated: A coordinated reaction to stimuli: e.g. if one part of a sponge is touched or encounters toxic water, the whole sponge can gradually close its openings. Sponges have mechano- and chemo-receptor cells scattered in the pinacoderm that detect changes (like increased sediment) and trigger a whole-body response (stop pumping water). There are no sense organs (no eyes, ears, etc.), but ciliated oscula can sense flow rates, and cells detect light vs. dark (many sponges contract in bright light). This represents the very start of integrated sensory response in multicellular animals without dedicated organs.
R6: Fungi on Land (early terrestrial fungi) | Inherited: No nerves, just mycelial network signaling as in aquatic fungi. Innovated: Greater mycelial network integration with plant partners – on land, fungi formed mycorrhizal networks connecting plant roots, effectively allowing signal exchange (nutrient and chemical cues) between plants via the fungus. The fungal mycelium can rapidly transmit signals (e.g. warning neighboring plants of pest attack through chemical cues), acting somewhat like a “nervous system” for plant communities. Still no neurons, but the scale of network signaling increased in soil ecosystems. | Inherited: Diffusion of gases across filament surfaces (as in aquatic fungi). Innovated: Adaptations to air exposure: terrestrial fungi often grow within moist soil or host tissues, ensuring their hyphae stay hydrated for gas exchange. Some have rhizomorphs (thick cords) that contain air channels to transport O₂ into low-oxygen environments (like waterlogged wood). Although fungi have no lungs or gills, the architecture of fruiting bodies (e.g. gills under a mushroom cap) maximizes surface area so that spores get oxygen and dry air for dispersal. The development of internal air spaces in large structures is a terrestrial innovation to facilitate respiration and spore release. | Inherited: External digestion and nutrient absorption as in aquatic fungi. Innovated: Ability to decompose tough terrestrial substrates: land fungi evolved enzymes like cellulases and ligninases to break down plant cell walls and wood – a major innovation enabling them to exploit abundant plant litter. They also formed symbiotic digestive partnerships (e.g. helping herbivores digest cellulose in guts, or forming lichens with algae to share nutrients). Additionally, mycorrhizal fungi feed by exchanging minerals for sugars with plants, effectively diversifying their “digestive” strategy from pure decay to mutualism. | Inherited: No circulatory system; cytoplasmic streaming in hyphae still moves nutrients. Innovated: Development of long-distance mycelial cords (rhizomorphs) that act like “fungal veins,” carrying water and nutrients over meters of soil or wood. These cords have differentiated core and outer layers and can rapidly translocate resources (e.g. moving water from moist areas to dry areas in a forest soil). Such transport efficiency is crucial on land where nutrients and water are patchily distributed. It’s not a closed circulatory system with a pump, but it allows a fungus to function as a integrated organism across large distances. | Inherited: Spore production (sexual and asexual) as primary reproduction, as in aquatic fungi. Innovated: Airborne spore dispersal – terrestrial fungi produce enormous quantities of dry spores that are released into the air (often from elevated structures like mushroom caps) to spread to new locations. They evolved ballistic spore discharge mechanisms (e.g. puffballs expelling spores in a cloud, or fungi that shoot spores using turgor pressure). Terrestrial fungi also often lost the ancestral motile gamete stage (no need for flagellated spores in soil). Additionally, many can reproduce asexually prolifically (molds spreading via conidia), ensuring colonization of ephemeral substrates. | Inherited: No muscles; growth and turgor changes continue to drive movement. Innovated: Terrestrial fungi show growth movements like tropisms – e.g. Pilobolus fungus can aim its spore shooters toward light (which often indicates open air for spores to travel), meaning it senses light direction and grows a stalk that rapidly launches spores toward that light. Also, some parasitic fungi can forcibly eject spores or even “zombie-ant” fungi direct their ant hosts to optimal positions – not a muscle system per se, but an evolved control over host behavior as a means of movement/dispersal. Overall, terrestrial fungi rely on strategic growth and turgor-powered motion (like mushrooms expanding overnight or spores being shot) as their form of movement. | Inherited: Chitinous cell walls for structure, as in aquatic fungi. Innovated: Multicellular fruiting bodies (mushrooms, brackets) with supportive tissues – e.g. densely packed hyphae form a stem and cap that can stand upright. Fungal cell walls on land often contain melanin or other polymers that add rigidity and protect from UV. Terrestrial fungi also produce sclerotia (hardened masses of hyphae) that act as tough survival structures in soil. In symbiosis, some fungi contribute to plant skeletal support by entwining with roots (forming mycorrhizae that help anchor plants). While fungi themselves remain mostly “soft,” the formation of large, self-supporting sporocarps is a significant skeletal innovation on land. | Inherited: Innate chemical defenses (antibiotics, etc.) as in earlier fungi. Innovated: Enhanced competitive and defensive chemistry: soil fungi secrete a wide array of antimicrobials to outcompete soil bacteria and other fungi. Many also evolved resistance to plant defense chemicals (allowing pathogenic fungi to infect plants). In terms of immunity, fungi on land encountered many new predators (like nematodes, insects) – some responded by evolving predatory structures (e.g. nematode-trapping loops) and producing toxins to deter fungivores. Additionally, mycorrhizal fungi can trigger plant immune responses systemically (helping plants resist disease), effectively extending a form of immune function to their plant partners. | Inherited: Diffuse production of signals (no glands). Innovated: Terrestrial fungi use complex pheromone systems to orchestrate mating (e.g. mating type factors ensure hyphae only fuse with a compatible type). They also respond to environmental hormones – for instance, ethylene from ripe fruit can stimulate fruiting in some fungi. The optic gland analogue in fungi doesn’t exist, but they do have something akin to hormonal control: e.g. in mushroom-forming fungi, certain cells (in the cap) release hormones that trigger spore maturation synchronously. Essentially, fungi expanded their chemical communication repertoire to include triggers from the environment (day-night cycles, plant hormones) to time their life cycle events on land. | Inherited: Diffusion and environmental dumping of waste. Innovated: Terrestrial fungi must often conserve water, so they can’t flush wastes freely. Many package waste products into fruiting bodies – for example, mushrooms often accumulate heavy metals or metabolic byproducts, removing them from the active mycelium. When the mushroom is shed or eaten by an animal, the waste is removed from the fungus. Also, in symbiotic fungi, some waste (like CO₂) is actually useful to plant partners and is transferred to them. Fungi essentially continue to rely on diffusion, but their lifecycle (producing disposable structures) and partnerships help manage waste in a terrestrial context. | Inherited: Single-celled covering (hyphal surface) as before. Innovated: Many terrestrial fungi developed hydrophobic cell wall coatings (proteins/hydrophobins) on their hyphae and spores to prevent desiccation. The surface of mushrooms is often water-repellent, protecting the spores until release. Some have a rind of thick-walled cells on structures like sclerotia or fruitbody surfaces, analogous to a skin. Fungi also frequently cover themselves in mucilage or slime (e.g. slime molds, though not true fungi, share this trait) to retain moisture. In short, fungi on land evolved a kind of integument: waterproof outer layers on their mycelium and spore structures to survive air exposure. | Inherited: Sensitivity to environment (chemotaxis, phototaxis) as in ancestors. Innovated: Land fungi became finely attuned to light, gravity, and humidity. For instance, mushroom fruiting bodies orient upward (negative gravitropism) so spores drop out under gravity – they have gravity-sensing mechanisms. Many fungi require specific light cues to form fruitbodies (ensuring spores release in optimal conditions), indicating developed photoreceptors. They also sense host-derived signals: pathogenic fungi can detect plant chemicals and grow toward them. Overall, terrestrial fungi have improved “senses” for environmental conditions – not via organs, but via specialized cells and receptors that detect light (blue-light receptors), volatile chemicals, moisture levels, and even surface touch (thigmotropism, e.g. rust fungi orienting growth by feeling stomata openings on leaves).
R7: Comb Jellies (Phylum Ctenophora) | Inherited: Primitive neural elements from a common ancestor with nerve nets (if we assume they diverged after nerves evolved, though it’s debated). Innovated: A diffuse nerve net throughout the body with an aboral (top) sensory organ integrating signals – ctenophores have no centralized brain, but a “subepidermal nerve net” that rings the mouth and concentrates near comb rows, pharynx, and tentacles​en.wikipedia.org. This nervous system, while still a net, is more structured than sponges’ absence of nerves. They independently evolved some neural circuitry (using unique neurotransmitters absent in other animals), possibly representing a parallel evolution of nervous systems. | Inherited: Gas diffusion through body surface (like cnidarian ancestors; no specialized system). Innovated: Ctenophores are gelatinous with a large surface area to volume ratio, so every cell can obtain oxygen directly from water. They lack gills; instead, their highly vascularized gastrovascular canals and thin body walls allow efficient diffusion. The beating of comb plate cilia also creates water currents that refresh the surface for gas exchange. No specific respiratory organ was needed due to their aquatic, mostly small or thin-bodied nature. | Inherited: A single opening gastrovascular cavity (from cnidarian-like ancestors) where digestion begins. Innovated: Through-gut (partial) – most comb jellies have a mouth and anal pores, meaning they can expel some wastes from the opposite end. Food is captured by sticky colloblast cells on tentacles, brought to the mouth, digested in a pharynx and stomach, and nutrients spread via a branched canal system. The presence of anal pores (two small openings) allows elimination of small waste, though larger egesta are still regurgitated​en.wikipedia.org. This is a step toward a one-way digestive tract, increasing feeding efficiency by not mixing waste with incoming food. | Inherited: No circulatory system; ancestral condition of gut cavity doubling as circulatory space. Innovated: A canal system branching through the jelly (from the central stomach to the comb rows and tentacles) that distributes digested nutrients and oxygenated fluid. The ctenophore mesoglea has muscle fibers that help stir the internal fluid. There is no heart or blood, but the gastrovascular canals functionally move nutrients around the body. The evolution of these canals is an innovation that supports their larger size compared to simpler nerve-net animals. | Inherited: Sexual reproduction with gametes spawned into water (like early metazoans). Innovated: Most ctenophores are simultaneous hermaphrodites, producing both eggs and sperm, often daily. They practice external fertilization: gametes are released through the mouth or specialized pores into the water column. Some can self-fertilize if mates are not nearby, ensuring reproduction even at low population density. The fertilized egg develops directly into a small ctenophore (cydippid larva) without a drastic metamorphosis, a relatively direct development compared to the metagenetic life cycles of jellyfish – an innovation that allows rapid population increase in good conditions. | Inherited: Epitheliomuscular cells for limited contraction (from cnidarian-like ancestors). Innovated: Ctenophores have muscle cells within their jelly (mesoglea) that operate the tentacles and mouthparts, but their main locomotion comes from ciliary comb rows, not muscle. The comb rows – bands of giant fused cilia – are unique: they beat in a coordinated fashion to propel the animal. This is the largest use of cilia for locomotion in the animal kingdom (they are the biggest animals to swim via cilia). So while they did evolve smooth and striated muscle for feeding (like swallowing prey) and maneuvering tentacles, the major innovation is this non-muscular locomotion system. | Inherited: Soft-bodied with hydrostatic support (like jelly ancestors). Innovated: A hydrostatic skeleton is formed by the fluid-filled gastrovascular canals and the mesoglea – by controlling the stiffness of their mesoglea and the volume of water in canals, comb jellies maintain body shape. They did not evolve hard parts; however, the statolith (a tiny grain of calcium carbonate in the aboral organ) rests on four tufts of cilia and acts as a weight for balance – this is a minuscule “hard” structure that helps orientation. Otherwise, the ctenophore’s form is maintained by elastic mesoglea fibers and muscle tension, an effective skeletal strategy for a pelagic gelatinous organism. | Inherited: Basic innate immunity at cellular level (as in all early metazoans). Innovated: Not well-studied, but ctenophores seem to resist infections in the open ocean by producing mucus that can trap invading microorganisms. Their exterior is constantly washed by water, and any foreign particles that stick can be shuttled to the mouth and expelled. Also, comb jellies have remarkable regenerative ability – they can regrow damaged tissues quickly, indicating robust wound-healing mechanisms. This rapid regeneration is a kind of defense, ensuring that injuries (from predators or environmental damage) do not easily become lethal or infected. | Inherited: No distinct endocrine organs; diffuse cellular regulation (like neurons releasing neuropeptides) as in jellyfish. Innovated: Ctenophores possibly use unique neurochemical messengers (since they lack some common neurotransmitters of other animals). They likely release hormones or hormone-like molecules to coordinate spawning (many ctenophores spawn daily at dawn or dusk, implying chemical timing signals). The aboral organ may also secrete factors that regulate ciliary beating rates. Though specifics are unclear, their life processes (growth, bioluminescence, reproduction) are controlled by chemical signals, which can be seen as a simple endocrine-like system spread through the nerve net and body cells. | Inherited: No specialized excretory organs; ammonia diffuses out of cells into the surrounding water. Innovated: The presence of anal pores allows them to eject some waste directly​en.wikipedia.org, reducing the need to expel everything via the mouth. Most metabolic waste (nitrogenous waste) still diffuses out through body surfaces. The anal pores in comb jellies are not true kidneys, but they represent a novel outlet for undigested particles and possibly excess water, which is a step beyond cnidarians. Essentially, ctenophores handle excretion by diffusion and occasional one-way expulsion through these small pores, an incremental improvement in “excretory” function. | Inherited: Diffuse sensitivity (ancestral nerve net detection of touch/chemicals). Innovated: A specialized apical sensory organ at the aboral end containing a statolith (gravity sensor) and ciliated balancers. This organ detects orientation – if the ctenophore tilts, the statolith presses on different cilia, which then signal to the comb rows to correct course, acting like an inner ear for balance. Comb jellies also have rudimentary light receptors in their epidermis (some species are attracted or deterred by light), though not image-forming eyes. Their tentacles are loaded with sensory cells that feel prey contact. Bioluminescence in many ctenophores may serve as a defensive sensory cue (flashing when touched to startle predators). In summary, while they lack eyes or ears, comb jellies innovated a gravity-sensing organ and distributed touch/chemo-receptors, giving them surprising awareness for an animal with no brain.
R8: Bilateria (First bilaterally symmetric animals) | Inherited: A simple nerve net or paired nerve cords from a diploblastic ancestor. Innovated: Cephalization – nerves concentrated at the front end forming a primitive brain (cerebral ganglion) to process sensory input from the anterior (where sense organs cluster). Bilaterians evolved longitudinal nerve cords (often ventral) connecting to this brain, coordinating the body. The nervous system became centralized and directional: signals travel along nerve cords to coordinate movement on both sides of the body. This is a major step up from a diffuse net, enabling faster and more complex responses. | Inherited: Gas exchange by diffusion through body surface (sufficient for small, flat animals). Innovated: A triploblastic body plan allowing larger size, which in turn drove evolution of specialized respiratory surfaces in some groups. Early bilateria were small worms that likely still breathed through skin. But the body flattening (as seen in flatworms) or development of a pseudocoelom (in roundworms) improved surface-area-to-volume ratio for diffusion. The innovation was flexibility: bilaterians in water could stay small and diffuse or grow larger and start evolving gills (seen later in bigger protostomes/deuterostomes). In sum, the bilaterian innovation was the potential for specialized respiratory organs (gills, etc.), though the very earliest remained diffusion-dependent. | Inherited: An incomplete gut cavity from ancestors (like a sac gut). Innovated: A through-complete digestive tract with mouth and anus, allowing one-way flow of food – many early bilaterians developed a tubular gut so they could continuously feed​en.wikipedia.org. This meant specialization of regions: e.g. a mouth for ingestion, enzymes secreted in a foregut, absorption in a hindgut. (Note: flatworms are an exception with no anus, possibly a secondary loss.) Overall, bilaterians established the complete digestive system as the norm, vastly improving digestive efficiency and enabling animals to process more food and energy. | Inherited: No discrete circulatory system; ancestral diffusion through a gastrovascular space. Innovated: Internal fluid transport – the evolution of a mesodermal layer allowed formation of a coelom or pseudocoel that could distribute nutrients and fluids. Many early bilaterians (like roundworms) use their fluid-filled body cavity as a simple circulatory medium (fluid moves as the animal moves). In others, a network of vessels and a contractile heart evolved (e.g. ancestral molluscs and annelids) to actively pump fluid. So the bilaterian lineage innovated the first circulatory systems (open or closed) to supply internal organs, with blood confined in vessels in some lineages for more efficient transport. | Inherited: External fertilization in water – releasing eggs and sperm to the environment (ancestral mode). Innovated: More complex reproductive systems with specialized gonads and often internal fertilization in some groups. Early bilaterians likely had paired gonads and could store gametes. Some (like flatworms) became hermaphroditic and evolved internal copulation (e.g. penis fencing in flatworms) – an innovation to ensure reproductive success without relying solely on chance meetings of gametes in water. Larval stages also diversified: bilaterians developed specific larvae (like trochophore in marine annelid/mollusk lineages, or direct development in some) to exploit new niches. These changes mark a shift to more controlled, efficient reproduction than the scatter-shot spawning of earlier animals. | Inherited: Longitudinal contractile fibers for body movement (from worm-like ancestors). Innovated: Mesodermal muscle layers – bilaterians developed well-organized muscle blocks, often with circular and longitudinal muscles working in coordination, enabling peristaltic locomotion (e.g. crawling or burrowing). Segmentation of muscles in many bilaterians (e.g. in early annelid-like forms) allowed different parts of the body to move independently. This gave bilaterians far more mobility – they could swim, crawl, burrow with precision. For example, a primitive bilaterian worm could undulate its body due to segmented muscle bands, achieving directed movement to chase food or escape predators, a big improvement over radially symmetric drifting or crawling. | Inherited: Hydrostatic support – maintaining shape by fluid pressure (ancestral cnidarians and flatworms use this). Innovated: Coelomic hydrostatic skeleton – the evolution of a fluid-filled cavity (coelom or pseudocoel) enclosed by muscles allowed efficient shape change and force transmission. By contracting longitudinal muscles against a fluid-filled chamber, an early worm could shorten and widen; contracting circular muscles lengthens the body. This dual muscle system and coelom enabled burrowing and wriggling with power. Additionally, some bilaterians began depositing hard materials: the first mineralized spicules or shells appear in bilaterian lineages (e.g. tiny calcareous scales in certain Precambrian bilaterians), foreshadowing exoskeletons. But the hallmark skeletal innovation was the modular body plan – repeated segments that could specialize or support appendages, giving structural modularity. | Inherited: Innate immune responses (phagocytic cells ingesting bacteria) from single-celled ancestors. Innovated: A dedicated immune cell population in the coelomic fluid or hemolymph – bilaterians evolved wandering amoebocytes/hemocytes that travel the body, engulf pathogens, and seal wounds (e.g. clotting in insects, coelomocytes in worms). They also evolved molecular immunity: producing lysozymes, antimicrobial peptides, and beginning the complement system (seen later in deuterostomes). In segmented bilaterians, if one segment is infected, others can respond by immune cells migrating there. So while still “innate immunity,” it became more systematized and effective in bilateria. | Inherited: Neurosecretory cells releasing simple hormones (like ancestors had for basic functions). Innovated: Endocrine organ precursors – bilaterians show the first distinct clusters of hormone-secreting cells. For instance, flatworms have cells in the brain that secrete hormones regulating growth and regeneration. As bilateral animals got more complex, they needed internal coordination: hormones like ecdysone (molting hormone) in protostomes or thyroid-like hormones in early deuterostomes evolved. Thus, bilaterians set the stage for true endocrine systems: they introduced signaling molecules that travel through the internal fluids to target distant organs, controlling metamorphosis, reproduction cycles, and metabolism in a more integrated way than before. | Inherited: Diffusion of waste out of cells into the environment. Innovated: Specialized excretory organs for osmoregulation and waste removal: protonephridia (flame cell systems) in the earliest bilaterians. Flatworms, for example, developed flame cells – ciliated cells that drive extracellular fluid through tubules, filtering out waste and excess water, excreting it through pores. This was a significant innovation to maintain internal chemical balance. In coelomate bilaterians, metanephridia (tubules with nephrostomes) evolved next. The appearance of these excretory structures meant bilaterians could regulate their internal environment and invade freshwater or variable salinity habitats that prior animals couldn’t tolerate as easily. | Inherited: Basic sensory cells (light-sensitive ocelli, touch-sensitive cilia) from earlier metazoans. Innovated: Concentration of sense organs in the head (due to cephalization): the first bilaterians evolved paired eyespots (pigment-cup eyes that can detect light direction), paired chemosensory organs (e.g. auricles in flatworms to “smell” dissolved chemicals), and mechanoreceptors. With bilateral symmetry, animals could compare inputs from left vs. right – enabling true orientation (e.g. sensing which side the light or smell is stronger on and turning accordingly). Many bilaterians also evolved statocysts (balance organs with grains inside) to sense up vs. down. These innovations – clustering multiple sensor types in a head – made bilaterians far more responsive to their environment, actively seeking food and mates and avoiding danger in a directed manner.
R9: Land Green Algae (Early land plant-like algae, e.g. Charophytes) | Inherited: No nerves, just cell-to-cell signaling as in aquatic algae. Innovated: Plasmodesmata between cells – charophyte green algae (ancestors of land plants) evolved microscopic channels connecting cells, allowing communication and coordination across the multicellular plant body. This meant one part of a filament could send chemical or electrical signals to another (e.g. to coordinate growth or reproduction). It’s not a nervous system, but it set the stage for whole-plant integration (all cells can respond to a localized stimulus via shared signals). | Inherited: Gas exchange by diffusion across plant surfaces (as in algae). Innovated: Waxy cuticle on exposed surfaces to prevent desiccation – but this barrier makes gas exchange harder, so early land plants developed stomata or fixed pores to allow CO₂ in​bio.libretexts.org. For example, some simple land “algae” (bryophyte-like) have pores on their surface, and slightly later, stomata with guard cells evolved (in moss sporophytes, etc.). Thus, these pioneers balanced water retention with the need for air, an innovation absent in their aquatic forebears. Internally, they had air spaces in tissues to facilitate gas diffusion from the pore on inward cells. | Inherited: Photosynthesis for food (no need for digestive system). Innovated: Rhizoids – filamentous root-like structures to anchor and absorb water/minerals from soil, an analog to roots in true plants. Early land algae likely formed symbiosis with fungi (mycorrhizae) to enhance nutrient uptake from barren rock/soil. They also retained the ancestral alternation-of-generations but innovated by keeping the zygote/embryo attached to the parent for nourishment (a key embryophytic trait). Essentially, nutrient acquisition extended beyond photosynthesis: these algae scraped minerals from substrata and began internally transporting them to nourish the growing embryo. | Inherited: No vascular transport; relied on diffusion and capillary action (as in algal mats). Innovated: Primitive conducting cells: early land plants (e.g. mosses) evolved hydroid cells for water transport and leptoid cells for sugar transport in their tiny “stems” – a precursor to xylem and phloem. Even without true wood, these channels moved fluids a few centimeters, supporting taller growth than a flat algal film. This is an evolutionary foothold for full vascular systems. The early land green algae were generally small, so diffusion still played a large role, but the trend toward internal conduction had begun. | Inherited: Water-dependent reproduction – flagellated sperm swimming to eggs (as in algae). Innovated: Retention of the egg and developing zygote on the parent plant (the defining feature of embryophytes). In these early land plants, the fertilization happened in a protected chamber (archegonium), and the multicellular embryo grew there, gaining nourishment from parent tissues. Also, they produced spores with tough sporopollenin walls that could survive air travel without drying out​bio.libretexts.org. This combination allowed reproduction on land: though sperm still needed a film of water (rain/dew) to swim to the egg, the resulting offspring had a much better start, and spores could disperse through the air to new moist spots. | Inherited: No muscle; movement via growth (still true). Innovated: Tropisms became pronounced – these plants could orient growth toward light (phototropism) or against gravity (gravitropism) thanks to hormones and differential growth. For instance, charophyte algae and early mosses would bend towards light, an early “motion” to optimize photosynthesis. They also developed rapid turgor movements in some cases: e.g. liverworts explosively disperse spores by sudden shrinkage of elaters (cells that twist when dry). This isn’t muscular movement, but it’s an active, controlled motion new to land colonizers, aiding spore dispersal. | Inherited: Cellulose cell walls for support (like algae). Innovated: Lignin-like compounds start appearing in some tissues​bio.libretexts.org – the earliest vascular plant fossils show lignified tracheids, but even early non-vasculars had some phenolic fortification of cell walls. These algae/plant hybrids stayed very small (cm-scale at most) because they lacked extensive support. They hugged moist ground, often forming low mats. But as an innovation, they produced a waxy cuticle and sometimes a rudimentary cortex that kept their shape on land. Cushiony mosses, for example, have supporting dead cell networks and turgid cells – an analog to a skeletal support that works only when wet. It was the subsequent evolution of true vascular tissue that fully solved the support issue. | Inherited: Baseline chemical defense (e.g. antimicrobial and anti-herbivory chemicals as algae had). Innovated: UV-protective pigments (flavonoids) to cope with intense sunlight on land – an “immune” response to UV stress that algae underwater didn’t face. These early land plants also had to contend with terrestrial pathogens and herbivores (initially few, but some arthropods started grazing). They evolved the first gene-for-gene pathogen interactions: secreting chemicals when attacked and having cellular responses to wall off infections. Also, the cuticle served as a first physical barrier to microbes. In short, embryophyte precursors ramped up their passive immunity: tougher spore/seed coats, antibiotic metabolites, and the ability to deactivate parts of tissue to contain an infection (localized cell death as a defense). | Inherited: Simple hormonal signals from algae (e.g. auxin-like molecules affecting growth). Innovated: A suite of plant hormones regulating growth and stress: early land plants used auxin to guide shoots upward and roots (or rhizoids) downward, and abscisic acid (ABA) likely evolved to deal with drought by inducing spore/seed dormancy and closing stomata in later groups​bio.libretexts.org. They also use pheromones to attract symbiotic fungi. While they have no endocrine “glands,” each plant part can produce hormones that travel through the plant’s thin tissues to target cells – establishing an internal communication network essential for coordinating development (like telling a bud to become a reproductive structure at the right time). | Inherited: No special organs; waste O₂, CO₂, etc., diffuse out as in algae. Innovated: Controlled water loss which is closely tied to excretion in plants: by developing stomata, they could regulate transpiration – effectively controlling the loss of excess water and oxygen. Nitrogenous wastes were mostly reused or stored in vacuoles as innocuous compounds (since plants are adept at recycling). Early land plants probably began storing heavy metals or toxins in cells (since dropping leaves wasn’t applicable yet for the very first land plants). Additionally, as a quasi-excretory function, they released secondary metabolites into the soil that could inhibit competing plants or microbes – effectively using excretion as a competitive tool. | Inherited: Thin “skin” of cells directly exposed to environment (like algal thallus). Innovated: Cuticle – a waxy layer over epidermal cells to seal moisture in and block UV​bio.libretexts.org. This was vital to prevent drying on land. However, cuticles could be patchy on early plants (perhaps on upper surfaces). Early land plants also kept a simple epidermis but some developed sporopollenin coating on spores (an integument for reproductive propagules). Over time, multicellular protective structures evolved: e.g. in mosses, a few layers of cells protect the sporophyte. These developments mark the beginning of a true integumentary system – a protective outer coating (waxy skin, multi-layered embryo sac) shielding the organism from terrestrial threats. | Inherited: Light sensors (phytochromes, etc.) to orient to light, as in algae. Innovated: Multiple sensory responses: early land plants could detect gravity via specialized starch-filled cells (statocytes) in root analogs, ensuring roots grew downward and shoots upward. They refined photoreceptors to distinguish day length (photoperiod) – crucial for timing reproduction in seasonal climates. They also evolved touch responses: for example, tendrils of some primitive vascular plants (later) coil when touching a support (thigmotropism), and even these earliest land plants likely had mechanosensitive cells to detect when they were in a damp crevice versus exposed. Additionally, they respond to drought by sensing internal water status (through hormone ABA). Though they lack a nervous system, land plants developed an intricate sensory network at the cellular level, allowing them to react appropriately to light, gravity, moisture, and physical contact.
R10: Cnidarians (Jellyfish, Corals, Anemones) | Inherited: Diffuse nerve cells from a common eumetazoan ancestor (none in sponges, but by cnidarians nerves existed). Innovated: A proper nerve net – cnidarians evolved true neurons that form a network across the body, coordinating muscle-like cells. There is no brain, but local nerve rings (e.g. around a jellyfish’s bell margin) act as pacemakers. This nerve net allows behaviors like tentacle contraction and swimming. It’s an innovation over ancestral animals with no nerves: cnidarians can sense and react rapidly (for instance, a touch causes a reflex tentacle retraction). | Inherited: Direct diffusion of gases in water (no respiratory organ). Innovated: Use of the gastrovascular cavity for gas exchange: because their body walls are thin and the gut cavity circulates water, oxygen can reach internal cells. Many cnidarians (jellyfish) are mostly water and have a large surface area relative to volume, so diffusion suffices. Some large anemones have internal septa that increase surface area inside the gut for both digestion and gas exchange. Still, no gills – reliance on body surface and gut cavity water flow remains the strategy. | Inherited: Sac-like digestion from a single opening (as in ancestral gastrula-like organisms). Innovated: Extracellular digestion in the gastrovascular cavity – cnidarians secrete enzymes into their central cavity to break down prey, allowing them to consume items larger than a single cell could digest. Nutrient broth is then absorbed by gastrodermal cells. The gastrovascular cavity also distributes food to all parts of the body. However, it has one opening (mouth = anus). In corals and anemones, the gut may have partitions and filaments loaded with digestive cells to increase efficiency. This is a step beyond purely intracellular digestion, letting cnidarians subdue and digest multicellular prey. | Inherited: No separate circulatory system; the gut cavity and water serve for transport. Innovated: Gastrovascular circulation – after digestion, the same cavity moves nutrients around the body; ciliary currents in the canals of jellyfish or the septa of anemones circulate fluid. In medusae (jellyfish), radial and ring canals branch through the bell, moving nutrients and oxygen to distant tissues. This functions as a primitive circulatory system (though it’s also the digestive cavity). Additionally, diffusion between the two cell layers (epidermis and gastrodermis) is quick because mesoglea is non-cellular. In sum, cnidarians achieved internal distribution of sustenance without dedicated blood vessels by using their gut as a transport network. | Inherited: External fertilization (gametes shed into water) like simple ancestors. Innovated: Alternation of sexual and asexual generations – many cnidarians alternate between a sessile polyp stage that reproduces asexually (budding off new polyps or medusae) and a motile medusa stage that reproduces sexually (spawning eggs and sperm). This metagenesis maximizes reproductive output. Polyps like corals and Hydra can also bud off exact clones, forming colonies. Some cnidarians (e.g. hydra) lost the medusa and do sexual reproduction as polyps with temporary gonads. The innovation is having both inherited methods (broadcast spawning) and new asexual proliferation, plus the protection of embryos in some cases (fertilized eggs often develop into free-swimming larvae inside the parent briefly, or in jellyfish they form a planula that settles). | Inherited: Contractile fibers (epitheliomuscular cells) in body wall, from ancestral muscle precursors. Innovated: True muscle-like cells derived from ectoderm and endoderm: cnidarians have circular and longitudinal fiber layers in polyps that allow them to shorten and elongate. Jellyfish evolved sheet-like muscle around the bell margin – a ring muscle that on contraction ejects water, propelling the medusa (jet propulsion). This gave cnidarians mobility: medusae can actively swim, and anemones can bend or even crawl slowly. Though not mesodermal muscle (cnidarians are diploblastic), functionally these are muscle cells enabling predation (entangling prey with tentacles) and reactive movement (retracting into a ball when threatened for anemones). | Inherited: Hydrostatic skeleton – like ancestral worms, they maintain shape by fluid in the gut cavity and contractile cells. Innovated: Some cnidarians developed exoskeletons: corals and many hydrozoans secrete calcium carbonate or chitinous skeletons outside their polyps. This provides structure and protection (reef-building corals create massive limestone frameworks that support colonies). Non-skeletonizing cnidarians rely on mesoglea (a jellylike substance) which acts as an elastic endoskeleton – for example, jellyfish mesoglea springs the bell back to shape after a contraction. So either through stony exoskeletons (corals) or gelatinous endoskeleton (mesoglea), cnidarians found ways to support their bodies beyond simple hydrostatic pressure. | Inherited: Innate defense – generalized cells that can engulf particles (some amoebocytes in mesoglea). Innovated: Cnidocytes (stinging cells) – a unique offensive/defensive cell containing a nematocyst harpoon that can inject toxin into prey or predators. This is a major innovation: these cells (especially abundant on tentacles) protect cnidarians from predators and help capture food by stunning prey. In terms of immunity, cnidarians can distinguish self from not-self to some extent (coral colonies of different genotypes will fight or reject each other on contact). They also have symbiotic algae (in corals) and have to manage immune tolerance to these symbionts – an immune modulation trait. | Inherited: Diffuse neural control of physiology (neurons that release peptides affecting cells). Innovated: Cnidarians produce neuropeptides and hormones that regulate digestion, growth, and metamorphosis. For example, spawning in many jellyfish is coordinated by light-sensitive nerve cells that trigger gamete release – a neuroendocrine response. Also, in Hydra, neurons secrete a head activator peptide that influences bud formation and a foot activator for basal disk formation (effectively hormonal patterning signals). No distinct glands exist, but the nerve net doubles as an endocrine system, with certain neurons acting like gland cells. This integration of neural and hormonal control in cnidarians is an evolutionary stepping stone to the separate endocrine organs of higher animals. | Inherited: Diffusion of wastes/ammonia from each cell to water. Innovated: Use of the gastrovascular cavity for expulsion of indigestibles and metabolic waste – essentially, anything not needed is flushed out the mouth when the animal contracts its body. While there are still no separate excretory organs (ammonia simply diffuses out through tentacles and surfaces), some specialization occurs: for instance, sea anemones can eject their internal water to remove waste or respond to stress (a form of “vomiting” waste and restarting with fresh seawater internally). Thus, excretion remains primitive: no nephridia or kidney-like structure evolved, but the combination of diffusion and active purging via the single opening suffices. | Inherited: One-cell-layer epidermis from earlier metazoans. Innovated: A two-layer body with an outer epidermis and inner gastrodermis, separated by mesoglea. The epidermis functions as a true integument: cnidarians have contractile epitheliomuscular cells for movement, sensory cells, and mucus-secreting cells in this layer. Many secrete mucus or slimy coatings over their surface to protect from abrasion and parasites. Some (jellyfish) have epidermal light-sensing organs (ocelli) embedded. Corals and anemones incorporate protective substances (like fluorescent proteins that act as sunblock for symbiotic algae). In essence, cnidarians established a dedicated body covering that not only protects (often with stinging cells embedded) but also aids in interaction with the environment (through gland cells and sensory cells). | Inherited: Primitive light- and touch-sensitivity from nerve net ancestors (e.g. scattered sensory cells). Innovated: Dedicated sensory structures: many medusae have rhopalia – specialized structures containing ocelli (simple eyes that detect light intensity) and statocysts (balance organs with a mineral concretion to sense orientation). For example, box jellies even have image-forming eyes with lenses – an astounding development for an invertebrate with no brain, allowing them to navigate around mangrove roots. Polyps like Hydra have rudimentary photoreceptors and will contract in response to sudden shadows. Chemoreceptors on tentacles allow cnidarians to “smell” prey in water. These advancements mean cnidarians can actively respond to their environment: swim toward light or prey, keep balance in water columns, and recoil from harmful stimuli. Their sensory evolution, while not centralized, laid the groundwork for more complex sense organs in bilaterians.
R11: Earliest Protostome (ancestral protostome animals) | Inherited: Bilaterian nervous system with an anterior ganglion and nerve cords. Innovated: Ventral nerve cord with segmental ganglia – protostomes established a nerve cord running along the belly (ventral side) as the main highway of signals, in contrast to the later dorsal cords of chordates. This often consisted of two fused cords with ganglia in each body segment controlling local muscles. The brain (anterior ganglia) became more complex, especially with the development of sensory mouthparts. This configuration (brain + paired ventral cords) is an innovation that would persist in arthropods, annelids, mollusks, etc., enabling refined motor control and integration of sensory input for forward-moving organisms. | Inherited: Diffusion and integumentary respiration (small ancestors absorbed O₂ through skin). Innovated: Gills in aquatic protostomes (the groundwork): as protostomes increased in size and metabolic activity, many evolved branchial structures. The earliest protostome might have been worm-like without true gills, but protostome lineages soon produced gill-like organs – from feathery external gills in marine worms to book gills in arthropods. Additionally, a dorsal heart pumping blood in an open system (in arthropod ancestors) helped carry oxygen in some groups​quora.com. In smaller or flat early protostomes, no special respiratory organ was needed, but the innovative potential for developing ones (like tracheae in terrestrial forms or internal gills in mollusks) was a protostome hallmark as they diversified. | Inherited: Complete gut with mouth and anus (from the bilaterian ancestor). Innovated: Mouthparts and specialization – protostomes evolved diverse feeding appendages and structures. The earliest protostome likely had a simple mouth opening with rings of cilia or primitive jaws to ingest food. Over time, this lineage innovated toothed tongues (radula in mollusks), muscular pharynges (in flatworms and nematodes), and jaws (as seen in early arthropods). The digestive tract also gained regions: a protostome foregut (ectodermal) often lined with cuticle for grinding, a midgut for digestion/absorption, and a hindgut for egestion. These regional specializations and feeding tools allowed protostomes to exploit a range of diets from detritus to active predation. | Inherited: No closed blood vessels; nutrients moved in a coelomic cavity or mesenteries. Innovated: Dorsal tubular heart and hemocoel in many – the protostome design (especially in arthropods) features a dorsal heart that pumps hemolymph forward, then it percolates back through the body cavity​quora.com. Ancestral protostomes likely had an open circulatory system if any – a pulsatile vessel or peristaltic waves moving fluid in the body cavity. In wormy protostomes (annelids), a closed circulatory system with vessels and multiple “hearts” evolved, showing the plasticity of this trait. The key innovation is active circulation of internal fluid: whether open or closed, protostomes did not rely solely on diffusion – they developed pumping organs (hearts) to distribute nutrients and gases, a big jump in physiological capability. | Inherited: Sexual reproduction with external fertilization (and some hermaphroditism) from bilaterian ancestor. Innovated: Internal fertilization and complex life cycles in many protostomes. For example, early protostomes in marine settings developed a trochophore larva (in mollusks/annelids) – a free-swimming larval stage with ciliary bands for feeding, which is a new addition to the life cycle aiding dispersal. Furthermore, as protostomes invaded land (e.g. insects, snails), they evolved internal fertilization to protect gametes from drying. The earliest protostome may have been a simple marine worm releasing gametes into water, but the lineage soon displayed remarkable flexibility: egg cases in some, live birth in others (e.g. some cockroaches), colony-level brooding in others. This is the clade where reproductive strategies exploded in variety. | Inherited: Well-developed longitudinal and circular muscles (from bilaterian ancestor). Innovated: Jointed appendage muscles and segmentation – protostomes, particularly arthropod ancestors, evolved limb muscles that attach to an exoskeleton, enabling complex leg movements. The ancestral protostome might have been segmental (like a primitive annelid) with repeated muscle blocks; this allowed the evolution of localized control (each segment’s ganglion controlling its muscles). In later protostomes, segments fused or specialized with their muscles (like the strong flight muscles in insects or the foot muscle in mollusks). Thus, protostomes innovated not in having muscles (they already did) but in how they used them: e.g. rapid body movements – squid jet propulsion via mantle muscles or insect flight – all trace back to the protostome propensity for evolving new muscle systems on their basic body plan. | Inherited: Hydrostatic skeleton (coelom or pseudocoel) from bilaterian ancestor. Innovated: Exoskeletons in Ecdysozoa (the molting protostomes) – the protostome line gave rise to chitinous exoskeletons (in early arthropods) that provided support and attachment for muscles. The earliest protostome might not have had an exoskeleton, but the capacity was there and realized early in Cambrian arthropods (e.g. Trilobites with jointed exoskeleton). Another innovation in others was rigid internal elements: some worms developed tiny bristles or spicules. Additionally, many protostomes evolved segmentation of the body cavity by septa (in annelids), which compartmentalized the hydrostatic skeleton for finer control. So, whether via an external cuticle or internal septa, protostomes greatly expanded skeletal adaptations, enabling larger size and terrestrial life (with support against gravity). | Inherited: Innate immune cells and enzymes in body fluids. Innovated: Chitinous exoskeleton or cuticle as a first-line defense in arthropods (preventing pathogen entry and physical injury). Protostomes also developed more specialized immune responses: for example, in insects, wounding triggers melanin deposition to encapsulate pathogens (phenoloxidase system), and production of antimicrobial peptides that specifically target bacteria or fungi. These are more advanced innate immune mechanisms than seen in earlier animals. The circulatory fluid of protostomes (hemolymph) contains hemocytes that can coagulate and form clots at wound sites, an innovation protecting against blood loss and infection. Overall, while still lacking adaptive immunity, protostomes honed their innate immunity and physical barriers significantly. | Inherited: Neurosecretory control of basic functions (e.g. reproductive timing by neurons) from bilaterians. Innovated: True endocrine glands in some – e.g. in insects, distinct organs like the prothoracic gland secrete ecdysone to drive molting, and corpora allata secrete juvenile hormone for metamorphosis control. These endocrine organs are a protostome hallmark, especially in insects: they coordinate complex life stages (larva -> pupa -> adult) via hormones. Also, mollusks have hormone-secreting cells that regulate growth and gamete development. The advent of such hormonal coordination allowed protostomes to achieve metamorphosis, diapause (dormancy in adverse conditions), and other life-history strategies, tightly integrating their physiology in ways simpler animals could not. | Inherited: Protonephridia (flame cells) in basal forms for osmoregulation and waste removal. Innovated: Diverse excretory systems: metanephridia in segmented protostomes (annelids) where each segment filters coelomic fluid and excretes waste, and Malpighian tubules in terrestrial insects for efficient nitrogenous waste removal with minimal water loss. The protostome lineage explored multiple solutions to excretion. The earliest protostome likely had simple tubules expelling to the outside. As habitats expanded, protostomes innovated: e.g. insects convert waste to uric acid crystals (solid urine) to save water, an advanced excretory adaptation. Overall, protostomes established regulated excretion, enabling them to thrive in freshwater (preventing salt loss) and on land (preventing water loss). | Inherited: One-cell-layer epidermis (possibly ciliated in larvae or flatworms) as a body covering. Innovated: Protective cuticle or shell – most protostomes developed an integument optimized for their environment. Roundworms (nematodes) have a flexible but tough collagen cuticle they molt; arthropods have a hardened, jointed chitinous cuticle (exoskeleton) that is also part of their integument, often with a waxy layer to prevent desiccation in terrestrial species. Mollusks grew a calcium carbonate shell over a mantle tissue – an external integumentary structure for defense. Additionally, many protostomes have specialized epidermal structures: setae (bristles) in annelids that help in movement and sensing, or trichomes (hairs) in caterpillars for defense. All these represent a big leap in integumentary complexity: from a simple skin to a multi-functional protective coat. | Inherited: Basic photo- and chemo-receptors from bilaterian ancestor. Innovated: Advanced eyes and sensor organs – protostomes were the first to evolve image-forming eyes (the compound eyes of early arthropods like trilobites​en.wikipedia.org). Compound eyes gave a mosaic vision useful for detecting motion, crucial for survival. In mollusks, the protostome line eventually produced camera-type eyes (as in cephalopods), independently rivaling vertebrate eyes. Protostomes also excelled in chemosensation: early aquatic forms had paired antennae or palps with dense chemoreceptors to “smell” water for food or mates. Mechanoreceptors like sensory bristles and equilibrium sensors (statocysts) became common. The earliest protostome likely had simple ocelli and statocysts, but the pathway to highly specialized sensors (antennae, eyes, ears in some insects that detect sound via tympanic membranes) was set. Protostomes turned the anterior end into a sophisticated sensor array, driving active foraging and complex behavior.
R12: Earliest Deuterostome (ancestral deuterostome animals) | Inherited: Bilaterian nervous system with anterior ganglia. Innovated: Likely a dorsal nerve cord (in the lineage toward chordates) and a generally more diffuse nervous system in others. The common deuterostome ancestor (perhaps a worm-like creature) had a nerve net and strands; one hypothesis is a dorsal neural tube beginning to form​sunyorange.edu (as seen in hemichordates’ collar cord and chordates’ hollow nerve cord). While hemichordates still have a nerve net and some dorsal, some ventral cords, the trend in deuterostomes was to concentrate the nervous tissue dorsally. This inversion relative to protostomes is notable. Additionally, deuterostomes (hemichordates, etc.) showed less cephalization – the earliest deuterostome might not have had a clear head brain, focusing instead on a network that controlled filter-feeding and crawling. The innovation here is subtle: reorganization of nerve placement and laying the groundwork for the chordate central nervous system. | Inherited: Diffusion and skin respiration (ancestral tiny worms breathed through surface). Innovated: Pharyngeal gill slits – a hallmark of deuterostomes was the evolution of openings in the pharynx that could serve dual roles in feeding and respiration​sunyorange.edu. The earliest deuterostome likely was a filter-feeder that passed water out through gill slits, extracting oxygen in the process. Hemichordates (acorn worms) have many pharyngeal slits and use them to breathe (much like primitive gills). This innovation meant water flowing through the mouth could deliver O₂ to blood vessels in the slits before exiting – essentially the first gill system. In chordates, these slits would become true gills (in fish) or other structures. Thus, deuterostomes were primed for effective respiration even at larger sizes thanks to this pharyngeal slit design. | Inherited: Complete gut from mouth to anus (the bilaterian inheritance). Innovated: Suspension-feeding pharynx – the ancestral deuterostome likely had a pharynx with gill slits and possibly a mucus-generating endostyle to trap food (as seen in modern acorn worms and lancelets). This allowed continuous one-way feeding: water in mouth, out gill slits, filtering food in the process. The stomach and intestine would process this caught food. Notably, the mouth in deuterostomes is a secondary opening (the blastopore becomes the anus), but functionally the innovation is the separation of feeding and respiratory flow by those slits. So while digestive enzymes and absorption remained similar to other bilateria, the front end was specialized into a filter-feeding apparatus that increased feeding efficiency and could be co-opted into new feeding modes (e.g. the evolution of jaws in chordates later on that repurposed gill arch supports). | Inherited: No dedicated circulatory system or an open system with contractile vessels (ancestral state). Innovated: Hemal system with a directional flow – early deuterostomes like hemichordates developed a rudimentary circulatory system: a dorsal heart-kidney (glomerulus) and a network of vessels. Typically, blood in deuterostomes flows forward along a ventral vessel and back along a dorsal vessel (opposite of protostomes)​sunyorange.edu. The earliest deuterostome likely had a simple pulsatile sinus or heart-like vesicle pumping blood through pharyngeal slits (for gas exchange) and distributing nutrients from the gut. In chordate lineage, this would become a muscular heart ventrally and a closed circuit. In echinoderms, it became an open hemal system plus the water vascular system. So deuterostomes innovated by organizing circulatory flow pattern (dorsal and ventral vessels) and linking it to their respiratory structures, setting the stage for efficient closed circulation in later chordates. | Inherited: External spawning in water. Innovated: Eggs with substantial yolk and possibly protective coats – deuterostome ancestors might have had larger, yolk-rich eggs compared to protostomes, as suggested by patterns in extant deuterostomes (e.g. tunicate eggs, some echinoderm eggs). The earliest deuterostome likely released eggs and sperm into the ocean (like hemichordates and echinoderms do), but an innovation was the development of bilaterally symmetric larvae (the dipleurula concept) that characterize deuterostomes. For instance, hemichordates have a tornaria larva (which resembles echinoderm larvae), with ciliary bands for swimming and feeding – a different larval strategy than the trochophore of protostomes. This larva suggests the ancestor had a distinct larval stage adapted to planktonic life. Additionally, in-line with deuterostome traits, separate sexes were common, and some brood protection evolved in certain lines (e.g. some acorn worms protect eggs in tubes). But internal fertilization was not a general deuterostome feature until much later (in amniotes). | Inherited: Longitudinal muscles and ciliary movement for locomotion (from tiny worm ancestor). Innovated: Many early deuterostomes were sessile or semi-sessile (e.g. hypothesized ancestors of echinoderms and hemichordates might have been attached filter feeders), but the lineage that led to chordates emphasized muscular locomotion. The notochord’s advent in chordates provided an anchor for segmental muscles to produce powerful lateral swimming strokes. However, in the common ancestor, innovation might have been limited to modified musculature for burrowing (as in acorn worms with a muscular proboscis) or swimming larvae. One key development could be the partitioning of the body coelom into sections (proto-somites) allowing localized muscle control – hemichordates show tripartite body (proboscis, collar, trunk) each with its coelomic cavities and musculature. This is a hint of segmentation and regionalization that the chordate line amplified (into myomeres along a notochord). | Inherited: Hydrostatic skeleton (fluid-filled coelom chambers for support). Innovated: Stiffened supportive structures: the early deuterostome likely evolved the stomochord in hemichordates (a rod-like structure in the proboscis) or a dorsal notochord precursor in chordates​en.wikipedia.org​courses.lumenlearning.com. The stomochord was once thought homologous to the notochord, though now disputed, but it does serve to stiffen the proboscis for burrowing. The true notochord, appearing in chordate lineage, was an innovation: a flexible rod that allows axial support for swimming. Echinoderms took a different route: they evolved internal calcite plates (endoskeleton) and eventually pentaradial symmetry with a water vascular system to act as a hydrostatic skeleton for tube feet. So the deuterostome heritage is dual: chordates inherited a dorsal supporting rod (notochord)​courses.lumenlearning.com, while others developed unique endoskeletons. The key early innovation was the idea of an internal supportive element that muscles could work against, breaking away from total reliance on coelomic pressure. | Inherited: Innate immunity with coelomic cells ingesting invaders. Innovated: Deuterostomes eventually produced the first hints of adaptive immunity (though not in the earliest forms) – for example, elements of the recombinase system are present in chordates. The immediate ancestor likely had a slightly more complex innate system than protostomes: complement proteins (primitive forms) and cytokine-like molecules for cell signaling in immune responses. Modern hemichordates possess phagocytic cells and can produce iodine-rich antimicrobial substances (their blood has an iodoform smell, possibly for defense). Also, being larger, deuterostomes evolved lymphatic-like channels in echinoderms (axial organ) and specialized immune organs in chordates (thymus, spleen, etc. later). So while the very first deuterostome didn’t have those latter features, the groundwork for a more sophisticated immune response was laid, with certain deuterostome-specific immune genes evolving (e.g. those for allograft rejection seen in colonial tunicates). | Inherited: Diffuse neuroendocrine system (neurons releasing hormones) from bilaterian ancestor. Innovated: Endostyle in the floor of the pharynx – present in early chordates (and possibly an analog in hemichordates with their stomochord region). The endostyle concentrated iodine and produced thyroxine-like compounds, making it the forerunner of the thyroid gland【28†L230
