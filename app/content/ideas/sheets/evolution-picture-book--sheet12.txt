R2: RNA (Pre-cellular replicators) |  | No neurons or true nervous system. These early self-replicating RNA molecules had at best primitive chemical sensing – e.g. certain RNA structures (riboswitches/aptamers) could change conformation in response to binding a ligand, acting as a rudimentary signal-response mechanism. Overall, environmental chemistry directly drove behavior (replication proceeded when substrates were present, and stopped when not), without any organized neural network. | No circulatory system or blood – not even cells existed. Transport of molecules was entirely by diffusion in the surrounding medium. If RNA replicators were within a protocell vesicle, the compartment was tiny enough that random molecular motion sufficed to distribute nutrients and remove waste. There were no dedicated vessels or fluids for internal transport; the primordial soup acted as the transport medium for reactants and products. | No specialized respiratory apparatus. Energy for RNA replication came from chemical reactions in the environment (e.g. spontaneous polymerization using activated nucleotides), not from breathing oxygen. These replicators lived in an anaerobic world and likely utilized high-energy molecules or surfaces to drive assembly. They did not exchange gases; any gas production or consumption was negligible. Metabolism was rudimentary – essentially the chemical potential of the environment fueled replication, rather than internal respiration. | No digestive system. RNA world entities did not “eat” in a traditional sense – they absorbed simple molecules (nucleotides and other building blocks) directly from their surroundings. There was no ingestion or enzymatic breakdown of nutrients because substrates were already small molecules. If within protocells, nutrients crossed the lipid boundary passively or via simple chemical processes. In summary, nutrition was externally supplied by the environment; replicators simply assembled available molecules into new RNA strands. | No dedicated excretory organs. Any waste products of RNA-catalyzed reactions (e.g. pyrophosphate from nucleotide polymerization) simply diffused away into the environment. Osmoregulation as a controlled process did not exist – if replicators were in membranous compartments, those protocells were leaky or semi-permeable, allowing ion balance passively. Thus, waste removal and salt balance were handled by passive diffusion through a protocell’s boundary or in the open environment, without active regulation. | No immune system. RNA replicators were vulnerable to chemical degradation and “parasitic” sequences. There were no dedicated defenses; harmful reactions or mutant parasitic RNAs could disrupt replication. The only protection was grouping in protocells, which could compartmentalize helpful RNA and keep out some parasites​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. Otherwise, stability depended on environmental conditions – for instance, mineral surfaces or cooperative networks might have provided some shelter, but there were no enzymes or immune reactions to guard these early molecules. | No true skeletal structure. These replicators had no physical support beyond their own molecular structure. Individual RNA strands are flexible; they did not produce any supportive extracellular matrix. If enclosed in a lipid vesicle (protocell), the vesicle’s membrane provided minimal structural integrity, maintaining a spherical shape​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. There were no cell walls or cytoskeletons yet – the only “support” came from the chemical stability of RNA itself and any surrounding surface or membrane that housed it. | No muscular system or motility organelles. Naked RNA molecules cannot move on their own except by Brownian motion or convection in fluids. They lacked flagella, cilia, or contractile fibers. If contained in early vesicles, those protocells also had no motile machinery. Movement was therefore passive, driven by environmental currents or diffusion. There was no active contractile apparatus at this stage – motion and mixing were left to nature’s randomness. | No hormones or endocrine organs. Regulation was purely chemical and local. The RNA world operated via direct feedback: e.g. if nucleotide supply dropped, replication slowed – a simple cause-and-effect, not a signaling pathway. There were no internally secreted signaling molecules controlling distant parts of a body (since there was no body). Each RNA or protocell responded autonomously to its immediate chemical environment. In essence, chemistry was the regulation – complex regulatory networks via molecules would only evolve later with proteins and DNA. | Reproduction was the defining feature here. These RNA replicators self-replicated by templated polymerization: an RNA strand could act as a template for assembling a complementary strand, and in some hypotheses, ribozymes (RNA enzymes) catalyzed this process​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. There was no cell division—replication happened at the molecular level, creating copies of RNA. This is analogous to a reproductive system where the molecule itself is the germline. However, fidelity was low and mutations frequent, limiting complexity. No sexual reproduction existed; it was asexual copying of information. | No true integument (skin), but possibly a lipid boundary. In later-stage RNA world, fatty acid membranes (protocells) may have encapsulated RNA, serving as a primitive protective envelope​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. This membrane would keep reactants concentrated and provide an inside vs. outside – akin to the most basic skin. It was leaky and had no specialized structures (no scales, mucus, etc.), but it did offer some protection from the external environment, e.g. shielding RNA from dilution or certain reactive chemicals. Without cells, free RNA had no covering at all, leaving it fully exposed. | No lymphatic system. There were no tissues or interstitial fluids to drain. Any fluid was the environment itself. Thus, no network of vessels or immune transport existed. Molecules floated in the primordial ocean, not in an internal fluid compartment. The concept of a lymphatic system would not be relevant until complex multicellular organisms with circulatory systems evolved.
R3: Prokaryotic Cells (First Cells) |  | No neurons, but primitive signal transduction existed. Early prokaryotes could sense and respond to environmental stimuli via molecular receptors. For example, chemotaxis pathways evolved in bacteria – membrane proteins and two-component systems that detect chemical gradients and cause the cell to move toward food or away from toxins (a basic sensory-motor response)​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. They also likely sensed light or temperature via simple photoreceptors or heat-shock responses. While there was no centralized nervous system, these single-celled organisms had rudimentary “sense-and-respond” networks (phosphorylation cascades, etc.) functioning as a proto-nervous system. | No circulatory system; diffusion reigned. These first cells were extremely small (microns in size), so passive diffusion was enough to move molecules within the cytoplasm quickly. There were no blood vessels or heart – each cell internally transported nutrients and gases by Brownian motion. For resources from the environment, the cell’s entire surface area acted as an exchange interface. Some prokaryotes evolved interior compartments later, but LUCA (the Last Universal Common Ancestor) was likely a simple single compartment. Thus, no dedicated circulatory apparatus was needed given the short distances inside a single cell. | Anaerobic respiration or fermentation. Early prokaryotes lived in an oxygen-free atmosphere​asm.org, so they employed anaerobic metabolic pathways for energy. They likely used processes like fermentation or anaerobic respiration with alternative electron acceptors (e.g. sulfur, nitrate) rather than O₂. The cell membrane housed proton pumps and electron transport chains that generated ATP chemiosmotically – LUCA is thought to have had a chemiosmotic mechanism and ATP synthase. Gas exchange was simple diffusion of CO₂ out and whatever electron acceptor gases (H₂, etc.) in. No lungs or gills – each cell surface directly exchanged gases with the environment. | Absorptive nutrition. Prokaryotic cells lacked mouths or digestive tracts; instead, they absorbed small molecules across the cell membrane. Early cells likely fed on organic molecules present in their environment (primordial oceans rich in organics) or performed chemosynthesis. Some secreted enzymes into the environment to break down complex food into smaller absorbable units. Essentially, the cell membrane acted as both a “taste” sensor and entry point – nutrients were transported in via membrane proteins or porins if small enough. In many cases, metabolism was autotrophic (e.g. using H₂ and CO₂) or simple heterotrophy; there was no compartmentalized digestion, only cellular metabolism of absorbed substrates. | Waste removal by membrane transport and diffusion. Without kidneys or specialized excretory organs, prokaryotes relied on the cell membrane to expel waste. Metabolic byproducts (e.g. acids, CO₂) diffused out or were pumped out by transport proteins. Early cells likely had rudimentary osmoregulatory mechanisms: for example, ion pumps or channels to maintain internal salt balance in changing environments. This was critical to prevent bursting or dehydration – the cell wall and membrane helped resist osmotic pressure. If the cell accumulated too much water, mechanosensitive channels could release solutes to relieve pressure. In short, active regulation was limited to pumping ions; most waste/fluid balance was handled by simple diffusion through the membrane. | Innate molecular defenses. Prokaryotes had no immune cells, but they evolved enzyme systems for defense. For instance, even early bacteria likely possessed restriction enzymes that cut foreign DNA (protecting against phages). Eventually, many prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) developed the CRISPR-Cas system, an adaptive immunity that stores snippets of viral DNA to recognize and defend against future attacks​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. The cell envelope itself was a defense: the cell wall acted as a physical barrier to harmful agents. Some bacteria also produce protective endospores to survive hostile conditions (a form of defense against heat, UV, etc.). Thus, while there was no immune organ, each cell carried its own defensive toolkit (enzymatic and structural) to survive predators (like viruses) and environmental stress. | Rigid cell wall as an exoskeleton. The first prokaryotes had cell walls outside their plasma membrane, providing structural support, shape, and protection​en.wikipedia.org​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. In bacteria, this is typically peptidoglycan, forming a tough lattice that prevents the cell from bursting under osmotic pressure. This cell wall essentially functioned like an external skeleton, giving the cell a fixed shape (rod, sphere, etc.) and mechanical strength​en.wikipedia.org. Additionally, an internal scaffold of proteins (the cytoskeleton) existed even in prokaryotes – e.g. FtsZ protein forms a contractile ring during cell division, and MreB helps maintain rod shape. So, while no bones, the wall + cytoskeletal proteins provided support, enabling the cell to maintain integrity and not collapse or deform easily. | No true muscles, but motility organelles emerged. Many prokaryotic cells became motile using flagella – helical protein filaments anchored in the cell membrane that rotate like propellers. This gave them the ability to swim through liquids, functioning as a simple “motor” system. Others had pili or slime extrusion for gliding. These are not muscles (no muscle fibers), but they serve a similar role: providing force and movement. A rotating flagellum driven by ion flow let bacteria chase nutrients or escape harmful conditions. Some early bacteria may have been non-motile, but flagella likely evolved fairly early. Thus, the analogue of a muscular system in prokaryotes is the set of motility structures (flagella, pili) and the cytoskeletal elements that enable cell division and shape change. They permit directed movement despite the absence of true muscle tissue. | Chemical regulation only. Prokaryotes do not have hormones or glands, but they do regulate internal processes via genetic and biochemical feedback loops. For example, two-component systems (histidine kinase and response regulator pairs) allow cells to adjust gene expression in response to environmental signals – a primitive internal signaling method​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. Bacteria also engage in quorum sensing: they release small signal molecules and sense their concentration to gauge population density, coordinating behaviors like biofilm formation or virulence as a group​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. This is analogous to an endocrine function in that a chemical produced by cells alters the behavior of those cells when it accumulates. Internally, metabolic pathways are regulated by repressor and activator proteins (e.g. the lac repressor controlling sugar metabolism), which is a form of intracellular hormonal regulation. Thus, while no endocrine organs, prokaryotes exhibit chemical communication both within one cell and among populations. | Asexual reproduction by binary fission. Prokaryotic cells replicate by growing and splitting in two. The DNA genome (which by now was likely a circular DNA molecule) is replicated, and the cell divides, distributing one copy to each daughter cell. This binary fission is simple and rapid – under ideal conditions some bacteria divide every 20 minutes. There is no formation of gametes or fertilization (no sexual cycle), though prokaryotes do have parasexual processes: horizontal gene transfer (via conjugation, transformation, transduction) that shuffles genes between cells. Those processes served as a primitive means of recombination but not true reproduction. The advent of DNA-based genomes (replacing the RNA world) improved the fidelity of inheritance – DNA’s stability meant offspring cells reliably carried complex information. In fact, because DNA is more stable and can be repaired using a template strand​ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, prokaryotes with DNA could maintain larger genomes, allowing more complex traits to be passed on. | Encased in a membrane & wall – a primitive skin. Prokaryotes are bounded by a lipid bilayer membrane that separates them from the environment​en.wikipedia.org. This plasma membrane is selectively permeable, controlling what enters and leaves – much like an integumentary system controlling water and heat loss in higher organisms. Outside the membrane, most have a cell wall (peptidoglycan in bacteria) providing an extra protective coat​en.wikipedia.org. Some also have an outer membrane (Gram-negative bacteria) or an S-layer (protein lattice in many archaea) as an additional shield. Many bacteria produce a capsule of polysaccharide slime on the very outside, which protects against desiccation and immune attack – analogous to mucus or skin secretions. Together, these layers form the integument of a prokaryote: they keep the cell’s insides in and the outside out, protect from UV and predators, and maintain chemical conditions internally. | Absent. As single-celled entities, prokaryotes have no need for a lymphatic system. There are no tissues with interstitial fluid to drain. The surrounding water handles the role of moving nutrients and waste around. If a prokaryote lived in a colony or biofilm, the space between cells might contain some polysaccharide matrix and fluid, but there is no organized vessel network. Immune functions (like toxin clearance) occur within each cell rather than in a body fluid. Thus, no lymph vessels or nodes exist at this stage of life – those arise much later in multicellular animals alongside circulatory systems.
R4: Asgard Archaea (Proto-eukaryotic Archaea) |  | No neurons, but expanded signaling complexity. Asgard archaea (a lineage of archaea thought to be closest to eukaryotes) likely had more sophisticated internal signaling than typical prokaryotes. They possess numerous eukaryote-like genes for regulatory proteins. They could respond to environmental cues (nutrients, other organisms) using two-component systems and perhaps small GTP-binding proteins internally. Some Asgard archaea live in symbiosis with bacteria, so they may respond to chemical signals from a partner – a rudimentary cell-cell communication. While still no nervous tissue, the signal transduction pathways were getting more complex, foreshadowing the more integrated responses of eukaryotic cells. | No true circulatory system. Each archaeal cell independently takes up and distributes materials by diffusion. As Asgard archaea are prokaryotic in size (though some form extensive filaments or networks), diffusion and localized cytoplasmic streaming suffice for internal transport. There is evidence some might form contact with partner bacteria (perhaps exchanging metabolites directly in a symbiotic relationship), but this is not a circulatory “system” – just cell-to-cell transfer. In essence, every cell still fends for itself in terms of moving molecules around, and any exchange is either via the external environment or direct cell contact, not through internal vessels. | Primarily anaerobic metabolism. Modern Asgard archaea have been found in oxygen-poor sediments, often living by metabolizing organic compounds in cooperation with other microbes. We infer that the ancestral Asgard archaean was likely anaerobic, perhaps a methanogen or fermenter, relying on chemical energy sources like hydrogen, sulfur, or simple organics. It did not use oxygen for respiration (this was before mitochondria), so ATP was generated via anaerobic respiration or fermentation at the cell membrane. Gas exchange was again by diffusion – for example, CO₂ or methane produced diffused out, substrates diffused in. Notably, by this stage, cells had the full complement of enzymes for core metabolism (glycolysis, etc.) encoded in DNA, enabling diverse energy pathways. There was still no specialized respiratory organ, just the cell membrane carrying out electron transport if applicable. | Absorption and potential symbiosis. Asgard archaea likely absorbed dissolved nutrients like amino acids or simple sugars from their environment (many are thought to live in syntrophy – partnership – with bacteria that provide them metabolites). There was no mouth or gut; nutrients crossed the membrane via transporters. However, a key innovation was their potential to engulf or at least physically associate with other cells: they formed protrusions that could enclose other microbes​mappingignorance.org, possibly an early form of phagocytosis. Even if they didn’t fully ingest prey, these membrane arms may have trapped bacterial partners close by for nutrient exchange. Thus, digestion was still external (no internal digestive compartment yet), but we see a hint of the future ingestive ability that would later be crucial for eukaryotes. | Active homeostasis in extreme settings. Archaea, including Asgard, often thrive in harsh environments, so they have robust systems to expel waste and balance ions. They have membrane pumps (for H⁺, Na⁺, etc.) to maintain pH and salt equilibrium. Any metabolic waste (e.g. sulfur compounds if they were sulfur-reducers, or various organic acids) would be excreted through membrane proteins or simple diffusion. Osmoregulation is critical: these cells likely had advanced osmoregulatory proteins (like archaeal chloride channels or ATP-driven ion pumps) to survive variable salinity. They lacked kidneys, but on a single-cell level they actively managed their internal environment. No specialized excretory organs exist, just the cell’s membrane and cytoplasm performing all waste management. | CRISPR-based and innate defenses. Asgard archaea, like other archaea, harbor CRISPR-Cas immune systems, providing adaptive immunity against viruses​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. This means they could capture snippets of viral DNA in their genome and use them to target and destroy viral genetic material – essentially a molecular memory of infections. They also have tough cell envelopes that act as physical defense. Internally, the presence of eukaryote-like quality control systems (chaperones, proteasome, etc.) hints that they could degrade misfolded proteins or possibly viral proteins, an intracellular defense akin to immunity. They did not have an immune “organ,” but every cell has these immune functions. In cooperating with symbiotic bacteria, they also had to avoid harmful immune reactions against their partners, so they likely evolved tolerance for certain foreign molecules. Overall, defense was at the molecular level (nucleases, enzymes, reactive molecule detoxification) within each cell. | Proto-cytoskeleton and unique envelope – stepping toward eukaryotes. A major innovation in Asgard archaea was an extensive actin-based cytoskeleton inside the cell​mappingignorance.org. Unlike typical small, rigid prokaryotes, Asgards have been observed with long cell protrusions and more dynamic cell shapes. Cryo-electron microscopy shows abundant actin filaments in their cell body and extensions​mappingignorance.org. This internal framework provided support and shape control, analogous to an internal skeleton. Their cell envelope is also unusual – they have a membrane possibly with a protein-rich coat (S-layer or other) described as “unique”​mappingignorance.org, which might give additional structural stability. They still possess a cell wall of sorts (many archaea have pseudopeptidoglycan or glycoprotein walls), so that acts as a tough exoskeletal layer. In summary, Asgard archaea had both an external wall for strength and an internal cytoskeleton for shape and flexibility, marking a significant increase in cellular structural complexity. | Dynamic membrane and rudimentary motility. Asgard archaea did not have muscles, but their enhanced cytoskeleton allowed active shape changes and potential motility. For example, with actin filaments and maybe motor proteins, they could extend and retract membrane protrusions (kind of like very simple pseudopods). This could help them anchor or perhaps slowly creep along surfaces. Some archaea have a rotary flagellum-like structure (archaellum), so Asgards might also swim using a rotating filament, although the real breakthrough was likely in crawling movement. These protrusions and cytoskeletal movements are analogous to a muscular system in that they generate force and movement from within. They likely lacked the coordinated beating of eukaryotic cilia or the speed of bacterial flagella, but they were no longer static blobs – they had controllable movement of parts of the cell. This set the stage for true phagocytic mobility and amoeboid motion in their eukaryotic descendants. | Complex internal regulation emerges. Asgard archaea contain many genes homologous to eukaryotic regulatory proteins (like small GTPases, ESCRT proteins for cell division, ubiquitin system components). This suggests they had begun to evolve more intricate internal regulatory networks – not hormones per se, but multiple interacting pathways controlling cell growth, division, and morphology. For example, ESCRT proteins in archaea help in cell division (like an early regulated cytokinesis mechanism). They may have also had protein kinases and phosphatases that modulate cellular processes in response to signals. If they lived in symbiosis, they possibly exchanged metabolites that signaled each other to adjust metabolism (analogous to an endocrine interaction between two different species). Still, there were no endocrine glands or long-range hormones within a single organism – regulation was cell-autonomous or within a microcolony. But the groundwork for eukaryotic intracellular regulation (which would later include calcium signaling, kinases, etc.) was being laid in these archaeal cells via their expanded protein toolkit. | Asexual, with the potential for symbiotic fusion. Asgard archaea reproduced by binary fission like other prokaryotes – duplicating their DNA and splitting one cell into two. There is no evidence of a sexual cycle in archaea (they do not form gametes or undergo meiosis). However, interestingly, the Asgard lineage set the stage for the ultimate sexual merger: one of them would fuse with a bacterium (the proto-mitochondrion) in an endosymbiotic event. While that was a rare evolutionary incident, it required the archaeal host to have a flexible cell structure to engulf another cell. In everyday terms, Asgard archaea likely exchanged genes through horizontal transfer (viruses or plasmids swapping DNA) but did not have true sex. Their DNA was stored in a single chromosome (with replication machinery similar to bacteria’s) and they had to coordinate segregating this during division. The reliability of DNA replication in these cells ensured faithful reproduction, and any genetic innovation spread by either fission or gene transfer rather than by mating. | Tough archaeal membrane and S-layer. Asgard archaea’s integument was their cell membrane and associated wall/coating. Archaeal membranes are made of ether-linked lipids, which are more stable in extreme conditions – a chemical innovation that acts like a durable “skin,” resisting heat and chemical attack. Many archaea, likely including Asgards, have an S-layer, a crystalline protein shell on the outside of the cell, which provides protection and shape. This could be considered akin to a scaly armor in primitive form. Microscopy indicates Asgard cells have a “unique cell envelope”​mappingignorance.org – possibly meaning an S-layer or other unusual outer structure that sets them apart. In essence, their integumentary system was a multi-layered envelope: a membrane to keep the interior in, plus extracellular protein/polysaccharide structures to shield against the environment. This protected them from predators, provided structural integrity, and controlled interactions with their symbiotic partner. They did not yet have anything like fur, skin glands, or cuticles, but their combination of membrane + wall + S-layer was a highly effective protective suit for a single cell. | None internally; environmental water serves the role. Even though Asgard archaea might form clusters or consortia with bacteria, they have no internal body cavity or fluid to manage, thus no lymphatic system. There are no lymph vessels or circulating immune cells. The surrounding sea or sediment water carries away waste and brings nutrients, performing the basic functions that a lymphatic or circulatory system might in a larger organism. Within a cluster of microbial symbionts, diffusion through the shared extracellular medium distributes compounds, but that is a far cry from a dedicated lymphatic circulation. Thus, no true lymphatic structures exist – immunity and fluid balance are handled at the single-cell level and via the external environment.
R5: Bacteria (Anaerobic) |  | Basic sensory capabilities. Anaerobic bacteria, like early fermenters or anoxygenic photosynthesizers, had no nervous tissue, but they did have sensory proteins. They could detect chemical gradients (food vs. toxin) and move accordingly via chemotaxis, as mentioned earlier. Some could also sense light (e.g. purple sulfur bacteria orienting to light for photosynthesis) or magnetic fields (magnetotactic bacteria aligning with Earth’s magnetic field to find optimal oxygen levels). These sensing mechanisms are mediated by molecular receptors and signaling proteins, functioning as a decentralized “nervous” system. Essentially, each bacterium is a self-contained sensor-effector unit – it monitors its environment and triggers movement or metabolic changes in response. This is a primitive analog of a nervous system: stimulus in, response out, all within one cell. | No circulatory system (single cell). Every anaerobic bacterium directly exchanges materials with its surroundings. There is no blood or circulatory fluid – nutrients and gases diffuse through the cytoplasm rapidly due to the small size. Some anaerobes formed multicellular filaments (e.g. Cyanobacteria, though those became oxygenic later), and in such filaments, cells can pass nutrients to neighbors through junctions; but in typical solitary anaerobes, each cell’s interior is uniformly mixed by diffusion. Because these bacteria often lived in liquid or porous sediments, the external medium flowed around them, distributing resources – that external flow effectively replaced any need for internal circulation. | Breathing without oxygen. Anaerobic bacteria thrived in oxygen-free habitats using diverse metabolic strategies. Many were fermenters – breaking down organic matter into products like ethanol, lactic acid, CO₂, and hydrogen to gain ATP. Others used anaerobic respiration: for instance, using sulfate (producing H₂S) or carbon dioxide (producing methane, in partnership with archaea) as terminal electron acceptors instead of O₂. They did not inhale/exhale, but gas exchange still occurred at the cellular level: CO₂ and other gases produced diffused out, and required gases (H₂, CO₂, etc.) diffused in. Some anaerobes were also photosynthetic (but not producing oxygen) – e.g. green and purple sulfur bacteria use light to fix CO₂ using H₂S or other donors, yielding sulfur instead of O₂. Overall, the “respiratory system” was the suite of enzymes in their membranes and cytoplasm that extracted energy from chemicals; all gases passed through the cell envelope by diffusion. The Great Oxidation lay in the future – at this stage, metabolism in living organisms was anaerobic​asm.org and life harnessed energy from earth’s chemistry without O₂. | External digestion and absorption. Anaerobic bacteria had to get nutrients from their environment. Many were decomposers: secreting enzymes into their surroundings to break complex organic molecules (like polysaccharides, proteins) into simpler sugars and amino acids, which they could then absorb. For example, a soil anaerobe might release cellulase to digest plant fiber and absorb the resulting glucose. Others were parasitic or mutualistic, absorbing nutrients directly from host organisms or partner bacteria. In all cases, there was no internal digestive tract – the “digestion” happened either outside the cell (via secreted enzymes) or was unnecessary if molecules were already small (like sugars from fermentation byproducts of other microbes). Once broken down, nutrients crossed the cell membrane through specific transporters. Some anaerobic bacteria could also synthesize everything from basic substrates (autotrophs), in which case their “digestive system” was their metabolic pathways converting inorganic molecules into biomass. In summary, nutrient acquisition was through membrane absorption, often aided by pre-processing of food externally. | Membrane-based excretion and equilibrium. Anaerobes excreted wastes like acids, alcohols, CO₂, and sometimes methane (if syntrophic with methanogens) through the cell membrane. If a bacterium fermented sugars to lactic acid, that lactic acid was expelled into the environment – essentially the cell “urinates” by dumping waste into its surroundings. To avoid self-harm from waste accumulation, many bacteria rely on diffusion and convection in their environment to carry wastes away. Internally, they maintain pH by pumping out protons if things get too acidic. Osmoregulation is handled by adjusting internal osmolytes; for example, bacteria synthesize or uptake compatible solutes (like glycine betaine) to balance osmotic pressure, or use ion pumps to expel salt if in a brackish environment. They often have mechanosensitive channels that act as emergency pressure-release valves, opening to let solutes out if the cell swells too much. Thus, while anaerobic bacteria lack kidneys or contractile vacuoles, they manage waste and water balance through a combination of membrane transporters and passive diffusion. | Molecular and structural defenses. Anaerobic bacteria faced threats such as bacteriophages (viruses) and predatory protists (once those evolved). They defended themselves using their cell wall as armor and producing various defensive molecules. For instance, some soil anaerobes produce antibiotics – chemicals that kill other microbes, thereby reducing competition (e.g., Clostridium species make bacteriocins). Against viruses, they share the same kind of defenses mentioned before: restriction-modification systems (cutting up foreign DNA) and sometimes CRISPR-Cas immunity to “remember” viral invaders. Additionally, anaerobes in oxygen-free niches had to sometimes survive brief exposure to air; for this, they evolved enzymes like superoxide dismutase and catalase, which are present in any aerotolerant or facultative anaerobe to neutralize toxic oxygen radicals​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (strict obligate anaerobes lack these and are killed by oxygen). So in terms of immunity, these bacteria had a suite of innate defenses (enzymes, toxins, physical barriers) but no specialized immune cells. Each cell autonomously mounts its defense when challenged. | Cell wall scaffold. Anaerobic bacteria possessed the same structural strategy as other bacteria: a rigid peptidoglycan cell wall that served as an external skeleton​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. This wall maintained their shape (rod, cocci, spiral, etc.) and protected them from osmotic lysis in their often hypotonic habitats (like freshwater swamps). In some anaerobes, especially Gram-positive ones, this wall was very thick and layered with teichoic acids, providing considerable strength. Gram-negative anaerobes had a thinner peptidoglycan layer but augmented it with an outer membrane. Either way, the cell wall gave mechanical support and anchorage for surface appendages (e.g., flagella are attached through the wall). Inside, bacteria have rudimentary cytoskeletal elements (proteins like MreB, FtsZ) organizing cell division and shape. So, while no internal bones, the wall + cytoskeleton system functions as a skeletal framework. It’s “exoskeletal” in that it’s outside the plasma membrane, but it fulfills similar roles: support, protection, and shape maintenance. | Flagella and beyond. Many anaerobic bacteria were motile. They often used flagella, rotating corkscrew filaments powered by a proton gradient, to swim in liquids – for example, toward regions rich in nutrients or away from waste zones. Some had a single flagellum (monotrichous), others a tuft or many (peritrichous), enabling them to propel and steer. Those without flagella might glide by secreting slime and using surface proteins to push (e.g., certain cyanobacteria and myxobacteria glide). Also, some filamentous anaerobes could move in coordinated ways; for instance, Oscillatoria (a cyanobacterium) got its name from the oscillating motion of its filaments, though the mechanism is more akin to growth and turgor changes than muscle. None of these involve muscle fibers, but they achieve movement: flagellar rotation, pili contraction (as in twitching motility), or shape changes allow bacteria to relocate. This fulfills the function of a muscular system on a single-cell level. For anaerobes specifically, motility could help them find optimal redox conditions (microzones with just the right amount of oxygen or none at all). Many anaerobic bacteria in sediments are non-motile and simply rely on growth and diffusion, but the capability for movement existed in others, serving as a selective advantage and being essentially the “muscle” of the bacterium. | Quorum sensing and gene regulation. Anaerobic bacteria regulate their behavior largely through gene expression control in response to environmental signals, similar to other prokaryotes. They secrete and detect signaling molecules in a process called quorum sensing, enabling the population to coordinate activities when a threshold cell density is reached​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. For example, some anaerobes only produce certain enzymes or toxins when they sense they are in a crowd (ensuring the effort will be effective). This is analogous to an endocrine system in that a chemical signal (autoinducer) is released and acts on other cells (including itself) to alter physiology. Internally, these bacteria respond to changes (like a drop in glucose or accumulation of a metabolic product) by adjusting enzyme levels – often through allosteric regulation or transcriptional regulators that act somewhat like hormonal feedback loops. Some anaerobic pathways are inhibited by their own end-products; for instance, if acid builds up, it might trigger a stress response turning on acid-resistance genes. All of this is done by diffusible molecules and protein regulators, not distinct endocrine organs. Thus, chemical communication (both intra- and inter-cellular) stands in for hormones – simple and direct, but effective for a single cell or colony. | Clonal reproduction with occasional gene mixing. Anaerobic bacteria reproduced by binary fission, producing genetically identical daughter cells. Under favorable conditions (e.g. a nutrient-rich anoxic habitat), they multiply rapidly and form colonies. There is no male/female or mating in the conventional sense. However, they could exchange genetic material through mechanisms like conjugation (a plasmid or DNA segment passing through a pilus from one cell to another) or transformation (uptake of free DNA). These processes allowed them to acquire new traits (like antibiotic resistance or new metabolic pathways) – a kind of parasexual reproduction that increases genetic diversity. Some anaerobic bacteria, especially Gram-positive spore-formers (like Clostridium), form endospores when conditions are harsh; while not reproduction (one cell turns into one spore), it’s a life cycle stage that ensures lineage survival and subsequent “germination” into reproducing cells. The invention of DNA genomes (replacing RNA) was fully in use – because of DNA’s superior stability, larger genomes could be maintained, enabling more complex metabolic capabilities​ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. This means anaerobic bacteria could carry genes for various enzymes and regulatory systems, supporting versatility and setting the stage for future evolutionary leaps. Still, no true sexual reproduction (involving fusion of gametes) had evolved in these organisms. | Multi-layered cell envelope. Anaerobic bacteria wore a suit of armor in the form of their cell envelope, which serves the role of skin. The plasma membrane is the inner skin, containing the cytoplasm. Beyond that, the peptidoglycan cell wall provides a tough, porous mesh that protects and gives shape. Many anaerobes (particularly pathogenic ones) also secrete a capsule, a thick gelatinous layer of polysaccharides outside the cell wall. This capsule is analogous to a slimy skin coating: it helps retain moisture (important in fluctuating wet/dry conditions like soil surfaces), protects against phagocytic predators, and can mediate attachment to surfaces. For Gram-negative anaerobes, an outer membrane lies outside the peptidoglycan, containing lipopolysaccharides – this extra membrane is part of their integument, providing chemical protection and a permeability barrier. Together, these layers function like an integumentary system: controlling interaction with the environment, protecting against mechanical and chemical damage, and even camouflaging the bacterium from a host’s immune system in pathogenic cases. Although rudimentary compared to multicellular skin, the bacterial envelope with its layers (membrane, wall, capsule) was a highly effective protective interface with the world. | Nonexistent. Anaerobic bacteria, being single-celled, have no lymphatic system. There is no body fluid to circulate or filter. If these bacteria lived in communities, any extracellular fluid is just the environment (water or substrate they inhabit). They lack any structure analogous to lymph vessels or nodes. Waste removal and immune defense occur at the cellular level (each cell dumps waste out, each cell defends itself). In biofilms (communities encased in slime), diffusion through the slime and movement of bulk fluid (when the biofilm is in water) handle distribution of nutrients and removal of wastes for the community, but there is still no dedicated lymph-like network. The lymphatic system concept simply doesn’t apply until organisms are composed of many cells with internal compartments.
R6: DNA (Emergence of DNA-based life) |  | No new nerves, but information storage boosted responses. The transition from an RNA/protein-based world to a DNA-based genetic system did not immediately create a nervous system, but it enabled greater complexity in regulatory networks. DNA’s stability allowed organisms to evolve more genes, including those for advanced receptors and signaling proteins. Over time, this supported more refined sensory apparatus (e.g. more kinds of chemotaxis, phototaxis proteins) because the genome could hold more information. So while organisms at the moment of DNA’s emergence still had only simple prokaryotic sensory systems, the foundation was laid for enhanced signal transduction. In essence, DNA allowed the “blueprints” for proto-nervous functions (like receptor proteins, ion channels, etc.) to be safely stored and passed on, setting the stage for future nervous system development in complex life. | No circulatory system (unicellular context). The invention of DNA does not equate to the invention of multicellularity or organs. Early DNA-based organisms remained single-celled for a long time. Therefore, internal circulation was unchanged – diffusion on the microscale was sufficient. However, with DNA genomes, cells could grow slightly larger or develop internal compartments (like cyanobacteria developed thylakoids) which in turn necessitated some intracellular transport (e.g. vesicles or protein motors) to move molecules around efficiently. One could say that DNA-based complexity eventually led to cells with endomembranes that traffic molecules (like the internal shipping department of a cell). But at the point of DNA’s emergence, no true circulatory innovation had occurred. So there were still no blood vessels – just perhaps more efficient intracellular distribution as cell architecture grew in complexity. | Higher metabolic complexity supported. The shift to DNA did not directly alter gas exchange (still by diffusion) but it allowed cells to encode far more enzymes and regulatory proteins. This means organisms could combine and regulate metabolic pathways more intricately. For instance, with more genes, cells could regulate anaerobic respiration in response to different electron acceptors, or evolve tolerance to small amounts of oxygen. In fact, DNA’s error-correction and larger coding capacity were prerequisites for the evolution of oxygenic photosynthesis and aerobic respiration down the line. Once oxygen appeared (via cyanobacteria), DNA-bearing life could adapt to use it. So while a DNA-based cell still just diffused O₂/CO₂ across its membrane, it had the genetic tools to eventually develop things like dedicated respiratory proteins (cytochromes, etc.) and manage reactive oxygen. In summary, breathing remained the same physically (diffusion), but genetically DNA allowed much more sophisticated control of respiratory metabolism. | Encoded enzymes expand dietary breadth. The DNA revolution meant organisms could carry a lot more hereditary information – enabling them to produce diverse enzymes for nutrient acquisition. For example, genes for various hydrolases (to break down cellulose, starch, lipids) could all be maintained in the same genome, allowing a single species to digest multiple food sources. Early on, a DNA-based microbe might have a battery of enzymes to utilize different substrates – e.g. if glucose is absent, switch to lactose (lac operon is an example of regulated digestion made possible by DNA-encoded regulators and enzymes). Additionally, some genes could code for membrane transporters for numerous nutrients. In effect, DNA-based life could evolve metabolic versatility: the “digestive system” at the cellular level became richer, since large genomes could encode complete pathways to synthesize essential molecules (vitamins, amino acids) or break down complex chemicals. This innovation was entirely at the microscopic scale – no gut, but a genetically flexible metabolism that functions like a Swiss army knife of digestion. | Better homeostasis genes. With DNA, cells could evolve and retain complex transport systems for waste management. For instance, multiple efflux pumps (proteins that eject toxic compounds) and sophisticated regulators for osmotic stress (like DNA-binding proteins that sense high salt and trigger production of osmoprotectants) became possible. DNA’s stability meant even multi-step stress response systems (requiring many genes working together) could be maintained in the genome. So while physically the cell still used its membrane to handle excretion, it now had genetic programs to respond to changing conditions: e.g. turning on a suite of genes to pump out excess acid or synthesize neutralizing agents if internal pH drops. Early DNA-based cells likely had improved repair enzymes for DNA itself, which indirectly improved survivability under stress (e.g. DNA repair proteins fixing UV damage meant cells could better handle sunlight). In short, the excretory/osmoregulatory functions remained membrane-centric, but DNA allowed these functions to be finely tuned and more resilient through complex gene networks. | Genomic stability fosters new defenses. The DNA genome brought higher fidelity replication, which in turn allowed organisms to devote resources to elaborate defense mechanisms without losing them to mutation. For example, the CRISPR-Cas immune system is recorded in DNA – only with stable DNA could cells archive viral sequences reliably and pass that memory to progeny. DNA also allowed the evolution of the SOS response – a global DNA-damage response in bacteria where a single protein (LexA) represses a battery of DNA repair and protection genes and is inactivated when DNA damage is sensed, thereby inducing a suite of protective functions. This kind of coordinated response is only possible with a DNA regulatory network. Moreover, DNA-based life likely saw the emergence of toxin-antitoxin systems, molecular immune-like systems where genes encode both a toxin and its neutralizer, which can help cells cope with stress or post-segregational killing (a kind of internal immunity). All these innovations are “immune” in the broad sense of protecting the organism, and they rely on DNA’s coding capacity and regulatory potential. Thus, while a DNA cell still defended itself with enzymes and barriers like before, the breadth and reliability of immune strategies increased, guarded by the stable storage DNA provided. | Cytoskeleton and membrane innovation coded by DNA. The existence of DNA didn’t create new structural elements overnight, but it permitted the accumulation of genes for structural proteins. For example, tubulin and actin homologues in prokaryotes (which became the eukaryotic cytoskeleton) could evolve and refine because DNA genomes could expand. Over time, cells could afford a larger set of structural proteins: beyond just a simple FtsZ ring and MreB, perhaps new filament systems or motor proteins started to appear (flagellar proteins are numerous – those genes can all co-exist thanks to large DNA genome). The “skeletal” systems of cells, like the peptidoglycan cell wall synthesis machinery, also became more complex and tightly regulated by DNA-encoded enzymes (multiple penicillin-binding proteins, autolysins, etc. for cell wall turnover). So, while the basic support (cell wall, primitive cytoskeleton) remained, DNA allowed enhancements: cells could grow bigger or change shape because they could produce more varieties of structural molecules and control them via gene regulation. In essence, the blueprint for more elaborate skeletal features was now safely stored and could gradually elaborate – eventually enabling, for instance, the development of eukaryotic cytoskeletal elements and even biomineralization in some lineages. But at this stage, it’s still a single-celled “skeleton” scenario, just with more genes to fortify and regulate it. | Prerequisite for true motility complexity. Motility structures like the bacterial flagellum are made of dozens of proteins – the evolution of such complex nanomachines likely depended on the stable, large-genome environment that DNA provided. Thus, DNA-based life could evolve intricate motors and assembly systems for movement. Once evolved, those genes (e.g. all the proteins of the flagellar basal body, hook, filament, export apparatus) could be retained together. So DNA indirectly spurred “muscular” innovation at the micro scale: more sophisticated flagella, contractile pili (like Type IV pili for twitching motility), or secretion systems that have motility roles. Also, DNA genomes allowed regulation of motility – bacteria could turn motility genes on or off in response to signals, something orchestrated by global regulators (e.g. sigma factors, cyclic AMP, etc.). None of this is muscle tissue, but it is the genetic groundwork that one day leads to muscle. For now, organisms with DNA continued using flagella and pili, but those systems were optimized and diversified thanks to DNA. For instance, some bacteria evolved differentiated states (swarm cells vs. sessile cells) where they produce flagella only when needed, a level of control enabled by complex gene networks on DNA. In summary, DNA didn’t add a new motility mechanism immediately, but it ensured that the machinery of motility could become more elaborate and efficiently inherited, eventually opening pathways to entirely new motile structures in eukaryotes (cilia, etc.). | Genome-based regulation and cell differentiation. The presence of DNA and its transcriptional regulation systems is essentially the birth of the modern regulatory regime in cells. Bacteria with DNA genomes have operons, promoters, and regulatory proteins that can turn sets of genes on or off in response to the environment – a primitive endocrine-like coordination. For instance, sporulation in some DNA-based bacteria (like Bacillus) is controlled by a cascade of sigma factors (transcription regulators) that act in sequence – resembling a hormonal cascade in that one molecular signal triggers another, and so on, ultimately leading to a developmental change (spore formation). DNA allowed the evolution of global regulators – proteins like CRP (cAMP receptor protein) in E. coli that respond to internal metabolites (cAMP) and coordinate multiple operons (sugar metabolism genes) at once. This is analogous to having an internal messenger (cAMP, in this case) that globally alters the cell’s state – similar to an endocrine hormone affecting multiple tissues, except here it’s one cell’s multiple pathways. Also, with DNA came plasmids and mobile elements that could carry regulatory circuits between cells, enriching regulatory diversity. So while still no hormones are secreted into a bloodstream (no bloodstream exists), intracellular signaling and gene regulation reached a new level of sophistication. Cells could mount stress responses (heat shock response turning on chaperone proteins), stationary phase responses, etc., all programmed in DNA. This is effectively an internal “management system” for the cell’s physiology – the evolutionary predecessor to hormone signaling pathways in multicellular life. | High-fidelity genetic reproduction. The emergence of DNA as the genetic material was a monumental reproductive innovation. DNA’s double-stranded structure allowed proofreading and error correction during replication, drastically reducing mutation rates. Consequently, organisms could maintain larger genomes – a prerequisite for evolving greater complexity​ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. Reproductively, cells now passed on DNA to offspring, and because DNA is more stable chemically than RNA, information was preserved across generations with fewer losses. This means traits and adaptations could accumulate over time (whereas in an RNA genome world, too high an error rate would erase large genomes). Also, DNA-based cells evolved dedicated apparatus for replication and segregation (like the origin of replication, DNA polymerase complexes, etc.), making cell division more reliable. In sexual terms, DNA set the stage for sex: it enabled processes like recombination. Early on, bacteria with DNA could exchange pieces of DNA (as noted, via conjugation or transformation), and the universality of DNA meant that even unrelated organisms could share genes – massively accelerating evolution through lateral gene transfer. Much later, the existence of a stable DNA genome made meiosis (sexual reproduction) feasible, since crossing over and chromosomal segregation require stable, complementary strands. In sum, DNA itself doesn’t equate to sexual reproduction – life remained mostly asexual in this phase – but it laid the groundwork for genetic recombination. The immediate effect was clonal reproduction with high fidelity and the capacity for a much larger set of traits to be inherited. This opened the door for the complex life cycles and sexual processes that would evolve in eukaryotes. | More durable “skin” components and novel surface molecules. Transition to DNA-based life didn’t drastically change the physical makeup of cell membranes or walls at first (those were set by chemistry and ancestry), but it did allow the evolution of new membrane proteins and structural polymers. For instance, cells could evolve glycoproteins, S-layer proteins with different properties, or enzymes to modify their lipid A or capsule polysaccharides, all encoded in DNA. This led to a richer integumentary diversity: some bacteria gained the ability to form extracellular slime layers, sheaths, or even mineral coatings (e.g., some precipitate iron or sulfur on their surfaces). DNA encoding also meant repair systems for the cell envelope could be more complex (e.g., multiple enzymes patching peptidoglycan, etc.), making the “skin” more robust. In effect, cells now had a stable blueprint to refine their protective layers: genes for UV-protective pigments (like carotenoids in the membrane) could accumulate, genes for efflux pumps to remove harmful substances added chemical protection, and so on. While in a single cell we still call this ensemble the cell envelope, it serves analogous functions to an integument: containing the organism and shielding it from the outside. DNA-based coding meant if a mutation produced a better capsule or a novel surface protein that helped evade predators, it could be retained and spread. Over evolutionary time, this enabled the development of complex cell coverings (e.g., the multiple-layered envelopes of Gram-negative bacteria, or the waxy coat of mycobacteria). But in the immediate sense, organisms at the dawn of DNA looked similar externally to their RNA-world predecessors – just with the potential to evolve much tougher and specialized integuments thanks to the stable genome. | None. The innovation of DNA had no direct impact on multicellularity or the need for a lymphatic system. All organisms were still unicellular (or simple colonies) at this point. There was no internal fluid separate from the environment that required circulation or filtering. Thus, no lymphatic vessels or nodes existed. Any intercellular communication was through direct contact or diffusion in the environment, not via an internal fluid network. The management of internal vs. external remained at the single-cell level. Only when organisms become multicellular and develop circulatory systems (to move nutrients) does the concept of a lymphatic system (to return leaked fluid and provide immune surveillance) become relevant. DNA did enable multicellularity later by allowing larger genomes (hence instructions for cell adhesion, communication, differentiation), but until those multicellular bodies evolved, there was simply no lymphatic function to speak of.
R7: Cyanobacteria (Oxygenic Photosynthesizers) |  | Primitive sensory and response systems. Cyanobacteria, though still bacteria, exhibit more complex behaviors suited to their photosynthetic lifestyle. They have photoreceptors to sense light quantity and quality – for example, Synechocystis has a phototaxis system with a UV/blue light sensor that directs movement. Indeed, photosynthetic cyanobacteria show phototaxis, moving towards light using a chemotaxis-like signaling pathway and type IV pili for crawling​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. This is a rudimentary vision system: a pigmented “eyespot” area in some filamentous cyanobacteria can help them distinguish light direction. Additionally, cyanobacteria possess circadian clocks at the molecular level (Kai proteins) to track day-night cycles and adjust metabolism – an internal timing signal akin to a very primitive nervous regulation. They also sense nutrients (like nitrogen or phosphate) and communicate internally to respond (e.g. initiating heterocyst differentiation when nitrogen is low). In summary, while they have no neurons, cyanobacteria coordinate environmental information and cellular response via integrated signaling networks, effectively a step up in sensory complexity relative to earlier microbes. | No blood, but some internal exchange in filaments. Individual cyanobacterial cells rely on diffusion, as before, for moving molecules around internally. However, many cyanobacteria form multicellular filaments or colonies, and within these, there is a degree of internal transport. For instance, some filamentous forms (like Anabaena) differentiate heterocysts (nitrogen-fixing cells) that share nitrogen compounds with neighboring photosynthetic cells, which in turn share sugars – this exchange happens through small pores between cells, somewhat like a crude circulatory exchange of nutrients within the filament. It’s not a dedicated circulatory system with a pumping mechanism, but cell-to-cell transfer through junctions does occur, distributing vital nutrients along the chain. In general, though, cyanobacteria have no specialized vessels or fluids; each cell of a colony still largely absorbs what it needs from the environment. In large layered colonies (biofilms or stromatolites), the diffusion of nutrients and signals between layers could be seen as a precursor to internal transport, but it remains passive. | Oxygenic photosynthesis and aerobic respiration. Cyanobacteria revolutionized respiration by producing oxygen. They perform oxygenic photosynthesis, using sunlight to split water, releasing O₂ as a byproduct​asm.org. During daylight, they take in CO₂ and light, and their internal thylakoid membranes carry out photosynthesis, generating sugar and oxygen. At night, they can switch to aerobic respiration (they consume the stored sugars and oxygen to produce energy). Thus, they have both “lungs” and “breath” in a manner of speaking: in light they act like plants (producing O₂), in the dark like animals (consuming O₂). All gas exchange is done across the cell membrane – there are no organs – but because many cyanobacteria are small or filamentous, every cell has access to water to diffuse CO₂ in and O₂ out. The accumulation of oxygen by cyanobacteria in Earth’s oceans and atmosphere (~2.4 billion years ago) profoundly changed the planet​asm.org, eventually enabling aerobic respiration in other organisms. Internally, cyanobacteria have specialized micro-compartments (carboxysomes) to concentrate CO₂ for efficient photosynthesis, which is an innovation to improve gas usage. In terms of a respiratory system, they lack anything like gills; diffusion suffices, but they are noteworthy as the first to supply atmospheric oxygen, linking the “respiratory” needs of the entire biosphere to their activity. | Self-feeding (autotrophy) with some versatility. Cyanobacteria are predominantly autotrophic, meaning their “digestive system” is photosynthesis. Their cells contain chlorophyll a and accessory pigments (phycobilins) that capture sunlight; they use CO₂ from the water and build sugars. Thus, they produce their own food internally (glucose) which then feeds into their metabolic pathways. They don’t ingest particles or organisms (no phagocytosis in bacteria), but they do absorb inorganic nutrients: nitrates, phosphates, iron, etc., from the environment – these are their “vitamins and minerals.” Some cyanobacteria can also fix atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) into ammonia using specialized cells called heterocysts (thick-walled cells that create an anaerobic interior for the nitrogenase enzyme). In a sense, heterocysts act as a digestive specialization – providing the “fertilizer” (ammonia) that the rest of the filament can use to build proteins. In certain conditions, some cyanobacteria can switch to mixotrophy – for example, they might uptake small organic acids or sugars if available, supplementing photosynthesis. But primarily, their capability to perform oxygenic photosynthesis was their major nutritional innovation – using light and water to generate biomass, essentially the first “plants” on Earth in terms of energy and carbon intake​asm.org. There is no gut, just a highly efficient cellular factory converting light into food. | Excreting oxygen and maintaining balance. The most notable “waste” cyanobacteria produce is oxygen, which they freely release into the water. What is waste to them became vital to aerobic life. They also excrete other metabolites; for example, excess fixed nitrogen (if too much) might be released as ammonium, or they may secrete mucus (polysaccharides) which carries away some waste by binding metals or toxins. Osmoregulation in freshwater species is crucial – many cyanobacteria live in freshwater ponds, so to avoid bursting from water influx, they rely on their cell wall and possibly contractile vacuoles in some (though classical contractile vacuoles are more common in eukaryotic algae; cyanobacteria mostly use other means). Primarily, ion pumps help maintain osmotic balance, and the cell wall prevents lysis by counteracting turgor pressure. Some cyanobacteria form akinetes (dormant spores) that accumulate polyphosphate or cyanophycin granules; these could be seen as storing excess nutrients (phosphate, nitrogen) and thus managing waste/nutrient excess by sequestration. Additionally, many cyanobacteria control their buoyancy with gas vesicles – protein-shelled bubbles inside the cell; while not for excretion, these allow them to move vertically for optimal light/nutrient conditions (by adjusting gas content they float or sink). If conditions become too oxygen-rich (which can be damaging), cyanobacteria can modulate photosynthesis or use mechanisms like Mehler reaction (pseudo-cyclic electron flow) to dissipate excess O₂. Overall, waste removal (like CO₂ release at night from respiration, O₂ release in day) is passive, but these cells evolved structures (thylakoids, cell walls, vesicles) to manage the byproducts and conditions of their metabolic activity, maintaining internal stability while dramatically changing their external environment. | Fortified against predators and oxidative stress. Cyanobacteria often form gelatinous sheaths or mucilage layers around their cells or colonies – a bit like a slimy skin that can inhibit protozoan predators from easily ingesting them and also protect against UV radiation. They also produce various bioactive compounds; some produce toxins (like microcystin in certain species) that deter grazing by zooplankton – an early form of chemical defense. Importantly, because they generate oxygen (which can form toxic reactive oxygen species), cyanobacteria have a strong complement of antioxidant enzymes: superoxide dismutase and catalase are present to neutralize superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, and peroxidases to deal with peroxides. These enzymes are essentially a cellular immune system against oxidative damage – without them, the oxygen they produce would harm or kill them. Cyanobacteria also can absorb high UV through pigments (their sunscreen-like pigments, e.g. scytonemin, protect them from intense sunlight in shallow waters). In terms of pathogen defense, cyanophages (viruses infecting cyanobacteria) do attack them, and cyanobacteria use CRISPR-Cas systems as well for adaptive immunity against phages. So they share the molecular immune strategies of other bacteria, but additionally have unique defenses suited to a photosynthetic lifestyle: UV-protective and antioxidant measures. As multicellular communities (some form thick mats), if one layer is attacked or dies, others can survive – a communal resilience. But there is no immune organ or circulating immune cells; each cyanobacterial cell must fend for itself using chemical and enzymatic defenses, aided by the shelter of colonies or filaments. | Cell wall and mucilaginous sheath. Like other bacteria, cyanobacteria have a peptidoglycan cell wall that gives them shape and strength. In fact, the peptidoglycan layer in cyanobacteria is intermediate in thickness between typical Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (they are Gram-negative in architecture, with an outer membrane). This wall is critical to withstand the osmotic pressures of their often freshwater habitats. Many cyanobacteria secrete a thick gelatinous sheath around the filaments or colonies – this extracellular polysaccharide matrix holds cells together in colonies (like in stromatolites) and provides structural integrity to the community, akin to a jelly-like skeleton. In some colonial forms, this sheath can be quite substantial, giving the colony a defined form (e.g., spherical colonies of Nostoc have a firm mucous ball structure). Some cyanobacteria even form rock-like structures (stromatolites) in concert with minerals – layer upon layer of cells and calcium carbonate deposition that creates a sort of reef; while the mineral part is not produced by the cells intentionally, their presence causes precipitation, effectively encasing them in a stony support. Internally, cyanobacteria don’t have the microtubules or actin cytoskeleton of eukaryotes, but they do have internal membranes (thylakoids) that help organize the cell’s interior. So the skeletal role in cyanobacteria is played by the cell wall (giving each cell rigidity) and the colonial sheath or biofilm matrix (giving the group form and anchoring them). These are primitive exoskeletal features, ensuring the cyanobacteria maintain their shape, whether it’s filaments waving in water or slimy mats carpeting a rock. | Gliding motility and buoyancy – movement without muscles. Cyanobacteria lack flagella (most are non-flagellated), but many can move. Filamentous cyanobacteria often show gliding motility: they can creep along surfaces, possibly by secreting slime and using tiny motors in their cell wall to ratchet along that slime. Some oscillate or bend towards light (hence names like Oscillatoria). Unicellular cyanobacteria like Synechocystis can move on moist surfaces using type IV pili, pulling themselves along (similar to how some bacteria twitch). Additionally, planktonic cyanobacteria like Microcystis regulate their depth using gas vesicles – by making or collapsing these internal gas bubbles, they rise or sink to find the right light levels, which is a form of movement in the water column. These motions are slow and lack the force of flagellar swimming, but they suffice to reposition the cells for optimal light and nutrients. Some filamentous forms (e.g., Anabaena) can also adjust positioning within colonies to some degree. In terms of analogies, their gliding is like a snail’s movement (very slow and slime-assisted) on a microscopic level. There are no muscle fibers or cilia, but surface proteins and polysaccharide extrusion generate movement. When conditions are adverse, cyanobacteria can also form akinetes (resting spores) that simply wait out bad times – not movement, but a strategy to disperse through time rather than space. Overall, cyanobacteria demonstrate that you can have directed movement (especially toward light, as noted under nervous/sensory) without any muscles, using just cellular structures and physics (pili pulling, or buoyant lifting). This allowed them to seek light (phototaxis) and form extensive blooms at the water surface. | Internal chemical coordination and community signaling. While cyanobacteria are unicellular/colonial, they show signs of internal regulation that parallels hormonal control. For example, in Anabaena filaments, when nitrogen is scarce, a fraction of cells differentiate into heterocysts. This patterning is controlled by a gradient of a small peptide (PatS) that diffuses from heterocysts to neighbor cells, inhibiting them from also becoming heterocysts – effectively a morphogen-like signal ensuring proper spacing of these specialized cells. That’s analogous to hormonal or developmental signaling in higher organisms, albeit on a simpler scale. Cyanobacteria also produce and secrete quorum-sensing molecules to coordinate biofilm formation and toxin production in blooms. Internally, each cyanobacterial cell uses signaling networks (two-component systems, second messengers like cyclic AMP or cyclic di-GMP) to adjust to changes – for instance, switching between day and night metabolism is tightly regulated by their circadian clock proteins and transcriptional regulators (a bit like an internal hormonal cycle keyed to light). They don’t have glands or bloodstream, but in a filamentous cyanobacterium, the movement of regulators or small signals from cell to cell acts like an endocrine system for the filament, ensuring cells specialize or act in unison when needed. Also, during stress, they may release allelochemicals that signal other organisms or their own kind. In sum, cyanobacteria introduced cellular differentiation and chemical communication in a community context, which are hallmarks of endocrine control (just in a very nascent form). | Alternation of growth and dormancy – no true sex. Cyanobacteria generally reproduce asexually by cell division. A filament grows by division of cells along its length; a colony expands as cells divide and secrete more matrix. Some cyanobacteria exhibit rudimentary lifecycle changes: they can form akinetes (dormant thick-walled spores) when conditions are unfavorable, and these akinetes later germinate into new filaments when conditions improve, which is a survival/reproductive strategy. But there is no sexual reproduction (no fusion of gametes) in cyanobacteria – they do not undergo meiosis. Genetic recombination can occur via horizontal gene transfer (viruses or direct uptake of DNA), which can introduce genetic variation similarly to sex. The real innovation in some cyanobacteria is cell differentiation as part of reproduction: heterocysts for nitrogen fixation (not exactly reproduction, but division of labor), akinetes for dormancy, and hormogonia (small motile filaments that break off from the main filament to disperse). Hormogonia formation is a dispersal strategy – a piece of a filament (just a few cells) differentiates into a motile form and glides away to colonize a new spot, then grows into a new filament. This is analogous to reproductive propagation of a colony. It’s not sexual, but it’s a step towards multicellular life cycles where different cell types and stages are involved. The presence of DNA allowed complex gene regulation for these stages. In summary, cyanobacteria’s reproduction remained asexual (binary fission, fragmentation), but with specialized stages (spores, motile fragments) that improved survival and spreading. They set the stage for later oxygen-dependent organisms to evolve true sex by oxygenating the planet and contributing to the DNA toolbox; however, within cyanobacteria themselves, reproduction was about making more identical cells and sometimes packaging or spacing them out for maximal success. | Thick cell walls, sheaths, and UV shields – a proto-skin. The integument of cyanobacteria is essentially their multi-layer cell envelope: a plasma membrane inside, a peptidoglycan cell wall for structure, and often an outer membrane (typical of Gram-negatives) plus an external polysaccharide sheath. Many also have protective pigments in their outer layers. For example, some species secrete sunscreen-like compounds (such as scytonemin, a pigment that sits in the sheath and absorbs UV light). This helps them survive intense sunlight in shallow waters or deserts, functioning like a skin pigment in higher organisms. The gelatinous sheath not only gives structural cohesion but also prevents desiccation by holding water, much like how terrestrial plant integuments prevent water loss (though cyanobacteria are mostly aquatic or in moist soils). In colonial forms, the outermost layer of the community can sacrifice itself, shielding inner cells from UV or grazing – analogous to a skin layer protecting inner tissues. Some cyanobacteria in mats form a stratified structure: a top layer of heavily pigmented cells absorbs harmful radiation and too much light, allowing cells beneath to photosynthesize optimally – this is a cooperative integumentary function at the colony level. In essence, cyanobacteria developed a robust outermost layer (cell wall + sheath + pigments) that acts as a skin: it isolates the cell from external fluctuations, prevents mechanical and radiation damage, and mediates interaction with the environment (e.g., by selectively allowing nutrients to diffuse in through the gelatinous matrix). They do not have an epidermis or cuticle in the plant sense, but these bacterial “skin” features were the precursors to those in multicellular photoautotrophs. | Absent – external water serves this role. Cyanobacteria, even when in multicellular filaments or colonies, do not have an internal fluid transport system separate from the environment. There is no lymphatic system; the surrounding water and the water-filled mucilage around cells handle distribution of substances. In a cyanobacterial mat, water flows between cells and layers, removing waste and bringing nutrients; if we stretch the analogy, the ocean or pond is the lymphatic fluid that bathes their community. They do have small pores between some cells in filaments, but these are for nutrient exchange, not for circulating an immune fluid. Also, cyanobacteria don’t have specialized immune cells to move around – each cell deals with immune challenges on its own. So, no lymphatic vessels or nodes exist. If a cyanobacterium is part of a lichen or microbial community, sometimes there are common channels in the biofilm, but that’s more like shared plumbing than a true lymphatic system. The evolution of a lymphatic system required complex multicellular organisms with circulatory systems, which is far beyond cyanobacteria.
R8: Aerobic Bacteria (O₂-utilizing) |  | Sensory systems tuned to oxygen and more. Aerobic bacteria typically possess all the sensory capabilities of anaerobes (chemotaxis, etc.) and then some specialized ones. For instance, many have an aerotaxis system – they can sense oxygen concentration via sensors (like the Aer receptor in E. coli) and swim toward an optimal O₂ level. This is crucial: too little oxygen and they starve for energy; too much could be harmful (if they lack defenses). So they navigate gradients of oxygen, which is a new environmental cue earlier life didn’t handle. Additionally, aerobic bacteria often live in dynamic environments (soil, host organisms), so they have complex two-component systems sensing everything from nutrients to signals from hosts. Pathogenic aerobic bacteria can sense host signals (like adrenaline, temperature) and turn on virulence genes – a primitive integration of sensory input to alter behavior akin to a neural-like response (though molecular). Some soil aerobes like Myxobacteria even coordinate in “wolf-pack” hunting of other microbes via chemical signaling, indicating group sensory-response behavior. In summary, no nerves, but networks of sensory proteins (histidine kinases, chemoreceptors) that monitor oxygen, redox state, quorum signals, etc., give these bacteria a responsive edge. They essentially have expanded “input channels” (e.g., dedicated O₂ sensors, oxidative stress sensors) feeding into regulatory circuits that orchestrate responses (motility, enzyme production), functioning as a diffuse neural-like system wholly at the cellular level. | Still none, but multicellularity experiments begin. Individual aerobic bacteria don’t need circulatory systems – they’re single cells. But interestingly, some aerobic bacteria started forming complex multicellular structures. For example, Myxobacteria (aerobic soil bacteria) aggregate by the thousands to form fruiting bodies when starving. Inside these fruiting bodies, cells differentiate into spores at the top. While there’s no circulatory system in a myxobacterial fruiting body, there is a primitive interior/exterior – cells on the interior get nutrients from those on the outside that may lyse. Similarly, Streptomyces bacteria grow as filaments (hyphae) in the soil, forming a network (mycelium) with branches that share cytoplasm; they even have septal pores allowing nutrient flow along the filament. This begins to resemble a circulatory function: nutrients can move within the colony through filamentous connections. Also, biofilms formed by aerobic bacteria (like plaque on teeth or slime on rocks) often have water channels that run through the biofilm, distributing nutrients to interior cells – an analog to blood vessels in a very loose sense. These are not genetically determined vessels, but emergent structures. So, while no true circulatory system evolved in bacteria, the cooperative structures of some aerobic bacteria foreshadow the need for internal distribution in larger assemblages. The vast majority of aerobic bacteria, however, remain unicellular or simple filaments and rely on diffusion from the environment (which is greatly enhanced by the presence of oxygen circulating in air or water). | Oxygen breathing and high-energy yield. Aerobic bacteria use oxygen as the terminal electron acceptor in respiration, allowing them to extract far more energy (ATP) from nutrients than anaerobes. They developed complete electron transport chains in their inner membrane with cytochromes that hand off electrons to O₂, forming water. Gas exchange in bacteria is still by diffusion through the cell envelope – O₂ diffuses in, CO₂ diffuses out – but aerobes often have adaptations to maximize this. For example, some have internal membranes (like Nitrosomonas bacteria have invaginated membranes to increase surface area for nitrification, an oxygen-requiring process). Others, like Azotobacter, increase their respiration rate to consume O₂ rapidly and create a low-O₂ environment internally so they can fix nitrogen (a balancing act – they intentionally respirate quickly as a form of oxygen control). The presence of oxygen allowed bacteria to live in new habitats (surface of waters, on land) and to evolve into bigger cells since energy was abundant. Some giant bacteria (like Thiomargarita, though anaerobic in parts, and Epulopiscium, an aerobic giant in fish guts) can exist, but they still rely on diffusion of O₂; they survive by having low metabolic needs or internal structures to circulate fluids. In general, aerobic bacteria “breathe” by diffusion, but they often position themselves in oxygen-rich environments (near air-water interfaces, in our lungs, etc.). The efficiency of aerobic metabolism also meant less waste per ATP – aerobes fully oxidize organic carbon to CO₂ and H₂O, which probably simplified waste management. Overall, introducing oxygen as their “fuel combuster” was the key respiratory innovation, not a new organ but a new gas and set of enzymes. Over time, some aerobes became obligate (cannot survive without O₂), whereas others remained facultative (could switch between aerobic respiration and fermentation). This flexibility is controlled by sensors (like an oxygen-sensing repressor that switches metabolism genes on/off). So, in terms of respiratory systems: aerobes didn’t develop lungs, they developed cytochrome oxidases and efficient respiratory pathways, and behavior to seek out oxygen. | Versatile and often extracellular digestion. Many aerobic bacteria became decomposers in ecosystems – armed with enzymes to digest complex organic matter that anaerobes left behind. With the oxidative power of oxygen, they could break down tough molecules like lignin or recalcitrant polymers. Aerobic soil bacteria (e.g., Streptomyces) secrete a cocktail of enzymes into the environment (proteases, cellulases, chitinases) to digest dead organisms, then absorb the resulting monomers. Some aerobic bacteria are predators on other bacteria – they release enzymes or even drills (like Bdellovibrio bores into Gram-negative bacteria and devours them from inside-out). Pathogenic aerobes often secrete toxins and enzymes to break host tissues (for nutrition). So we see an expansion in “digestive” strategies: outside-the-cell digestion is common, followed by absorption of nutrients. Additionally, oxygen allows complete oxidation of nutrients internally (via Krebs cycle), so aerobes can thrive on smaller concentrations of food – they “burn” it fully. Certain aerobes also developed storage inclusions (like PHB granules or glycogen) to bank nutrients when in excess, for use when scarce – a metabolic strategy akin to an organism storing fat. Overall, though they remain single-celled, aerobic bacteria exhibit a more aggressive and varied approach to obtaining nutrients: they can scavenge or actively decompose a wide range of organic substrates. There is no “stomach,” but in a colony of bacteria on say a leaf, the collective secretion of digestive enzymes and subsequent absorption by the community functions like an external gut. Moreover, aerobes in symbiosis (like gut bacteria in animals) contribute to digestion by breaking down compounds the host cannot, effectively acting as part of a digestive system for larger organisms. But from the bacteria’s perspective, they are simply feeding by absorption after enzymatic breakdown, as has been the mode since prokaryotes began – just now with O₂, the menu of what can be digested and the energy yield from it is much larger. | Active expulsion and detoxification. Aerobic metabolism generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) as byproducts (superoxide, peroxide). Aerobic bacteria thus required robust detox systems – producing enzymes like superoxide dismutase, catalase, and peroxidases to neutralize ROS​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. These enzymes are like an excretory system for toxic byproducts of metabolism, converting them into harmless substances (oxygen and water). In terms of waste, aerobes breathe out CO₂ which diffuses away; nitrogen waste often is excreted as ammonium or other soluble forms. Because they often fully oxidize their food, the end products (CO₂, H₂O) are readily lost by diffusion, meaning less problematic waste accumulation than fermenters (which accumulate acids or alcohol). However, living in oxygen means dealing with potential toxins like heavy metals in oxidative states; many aerobes have efflux pumps that actively pump out toxic metal ions or antibiotics (the latter being either waste of other microbes or signals – aerobic soil bacteria frequently encounter antibiotics in microbial warfare and evolved pumps to expel them). They also have membrane transporters to export excess metabolites – for instance, if a metabolic intermediate builds up, some bacteria can secrete it. Another adaptation in some aerobic bacteria is the microcompartment or organelle-like structures to isolate certain metabolic processes that produce dangerous intermediates (e.g., Nitrosomonas have carboxysomes for CO₂ fixation and annamox bacteria have an “annamoxosome” to handle toxic hydrazine). While these are more metabolic, they aid in containing and neutralizing waste internally. Osmoregulation remains vital; many aerobic bacteria inhabit fluctuating environments (like soil drying/wetting), so they accumulate osmoprotectant molecules or expel ions to maintain balance. Some can form cysts when desiccated, reducing metabolic waste production to near zero until water returns. Summarily, aerobic bacteria excel at waste management via enzymatic neutralization and active export. They pump out what they don’t need and break down harmful compounds internally before they can do damage. The fundamental excretory organ is still the cell membrane and the suite of proteins embedded in it that transport wastes out. If conditions overwhelm these systems (e.g., too much waste or salt), the bacteria will either move (if motile) to a better locale, form a resistant state, or die. But as a group, aerobic bacteria are quite hardy in waste management, contributing to them being successful in diverse, sometimes polluted environments (indeed, many are used in bioremediation to degrade pollutants). | Enhanced innate and competitive defenses. Aerobic bacteria often live in competitive, oxygen-rich niches where both microbes and host organisms (if pathogenic) exert pressures. As a result, they have evolved potent defense/offense mechanisms. Many soil-dwelling aerobic bacteria in the genus Streptomyces and others produce antibiotics – chemical weapons that inhibit competitors. These antibiotics also double as a defense: by killing or suppressing nearby microbes (including fungi and other bacteria), they reduce competition for nutrients. The producing bacterium is immune to its own antibiotic thanks to specific resistance genes (a form of self-immunity). Pathogenic aerobes, on the other hand, evolved ways to evade host immune systems: e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis has a waxy cell envelope that resists destruction by macrophages (analogous to armor against the host’s immune “attacks”). Many aerobic bacteria have biofilm formation ability – by living in a biofilm, they embed themselves in a protective matrix that antibiotics or immune cells have trouble penetrating. This communal defense can be likened to an “immune system” of the colony, where the matrix and the outer layer of cells sacrificially protect inner cells. At the molecular level, all the earlier-mentioned systems (restriction enzymes, CRISPR) are found in many aerobes, providing defense against phages. Actually, with oxygen present, the biosphere saw a proliferation of phages (viruses) and plasmids; aerobes often carry multiple toxin-antitoxin modules on their plasmids that ensure plasmid maintenance and can induce dormancy under stress – sort of a programmed self-defense against severe conditions (rather than external threats). Also, stress responses in aerobes (heat shock, oxidative stress response) are well-developed, allowing them to survive hostile conditions like spikes in temperature or oxygen radical exposure, which is a resilience mechanism analogous to immune stress responses in higher organisms. In summary, aerobic bacteria’s “immune system” is a combination of chemical warfare (antibiotics, siderophores to sequester iron away from others), physical barriers (biofilms, capsules), and genetic systems (restriction-modification, CRISPR, DNA repair). They still lack anything like immune cells or adaptive immunity beyond CRISPR, but as single cells they are arguably better defended than their anaerobic predecessors because they had to be, in the cutthroat oxygenated world. | Cell walls, capsules, and multicellular structures. Aerobic bacteria keep the proven design of a peptidoglycan cell wall for shape and support. Some, like Gram-positive aerobes (e.g., Bacillus), have very thick walls that allow them to live in drier, more variable environments, including forming endospores with additional protective layers. Many aerobic bacteria also secrete extracellular polysaccharides to form a capsule or biofilm matrix; when these bacteria aggregate, the matrix can serve as a shared exoskeleton, holding colonies together on a surface (like dental plaque has a scaffolding of polymers that bacteria adhere to). Additionally, certain aerobic bacteria develop multicellular fruiting bodies or hyphae – these come with structural differentiation. Streptomyces (an aerobic actinobacterium) forms a mycelium in soil – a network of filaments that behave much like fungal mycelium. This network provides support and penetration into organic material; it even produces aerial hyphae that stand upright and produce spores, demonstrating significant structural specialization (the aerial hyphae are supported by turgor and a rigid cell wall, acting like bristles). Myxobacteria form stalked fruiting bodies where many cells pile up into a column – the bottom of the column (stalk) comprises dead or metabolically sacrificed cells that form a rigid support for the spores at the top. Those stalk cells often undergo changes like forming a thick cell wall, making the structure stiff. These instances show incipient “skeletal” functions in bacterial groups: some cells or secreted materials form a supporting framework for others. Still, in a single bacterium, the cell wall remains the main skeletal element maintaining shape. For motile aerobes, the flagellar structure (basal body anchored in the wall) and the chemoreceptor arrays (which cluster at cell poles, often attached to the inner membrane and perhaps peptidoglycan) might be considered internal scaffolds that organize cell function. Flagella especially require a firm anchor – which the cell wall provides – showing the synergy between structure and function. In essence, the skeletal system in aerobic bacteria ranges from the simple rigid cell wall of solitary cells to the communal frameworks of biofilms and fruiting bodies, enabling them to conquer a variety of niches (from free-swimming in water to crusty growths on rocks to fuzzy colonies in soil). | Swift and directed movement. Many aerobic bacteria are highly motile – think of Pseudomonas or Bacillus species with several flagella allowing them to swim rapidly toward nutrients or away from harm. With abundant ATP from aerobic respiration, these bacteria can power strong flagellar motors; some are among the fastest bacteria in terms of body lengths per second. Swarming motility is a phenomenon in some aerobic bacteria (like Bacillus subtilis, Proteus mirabilis) where they differentiate into elongated, hyper-flagellated swarm cells and move across surfaces in coordinated packs – a bit like multicellular migration. This is an advanced “muscular” behavior: groups of bacteria pushing collectively, aided by flagella and slime, to travel and colonize new territory. Another example: Myxobacteria (aerobic) glide on surfaces and do so cooperatively, even displaying rippling patterns as they move in waves – this coordination is driven by cell-cell contact and signals, akin to how muscle cells coordinate contraction (though in myxobacteria it’s each cell moving itself, but with alignment to neighbors). Aerobic conditions also allowed larger bacteria with unusual motility: e.g., Spirochetes have internal flagella (axial filaments) that let them corkscrew through viscous media; pathogens like Treponema and Borrelia use this to move through host tissues. In essence, the “muscular” repertoire of bacteria expanded – flagella got refined, pili-based twitching became common for crawling on surfaces (especially in biofilm-forming aerobes), and collective movement emerged. These are all protein-based motility systems functioning at microscopic scale, but they parallel muscular functions by generating force and directional movement. No muscle fibers or actin-myosin complexes yet – those come with eukaryotes – but flagellar rotation and pilus retraction in large groups can achieve feats analogous to muscular movement (for instance, bacterial swarms can overcome surface tension and spread across agar, a kind of strength in numbers). Summarily, aerobic bacteria are exemplars of efficient, adaptable locomotion in the microbial world, achieving impressive navigational feats (chemotaxis in 3D gradients, swarming across solid surfaces) with their evolved motility organelles. | Chemical signaling for multicellular coordination. In oxygen-rich environments, bacteria often found themselves in dense communities and had to coordinate behaviors – this led to elaborate quorum sensing systems. Many aerobic bacteria produce quorum sensing molecules (like AHLs in Gram-negatives, peptides in Gram-positives) that accumulate and, upon reaching a threshold concentration, trigger a suite of changes: biofilm formation, virulence factor production, bioluminescence (in Vibrio bacteria), sporulation, etc. This is strikingly analogous to hormonal regulation in that a secreted molecule alters developmental state or behavior of the whole population once it hits a critical level​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. For example, Pseudomonas aeruginosa uses quorum sensing to regulate biofilm maturation and production of enzymes – one can liken the quorum signal to a hormone that says “we have a crowd now, behave like a community.” In Bacillus subtilis, different subpopulations communicate during biofilm formation and sporulation; they even have a cannibalism toxin system where, under stress, some cells produce a toxin that kills some siblings – releasing nutrients to delay sporulation in the survivors. This toxin production is regulated by signaling peptides and corresponds to a coordinated developmental decision (again akin to hormonal regulation of tissue fate, albeit in a bacterial context). Internally, aerobic bacteria have complex networks of transcription factors (e.g., Fur for iron metabolism, SoxRS for oxidative stress, etc.) that act like an endocrine web inside the cell – sensing small molecules (like iron, or superoxide) and globally adjusting gene expression. Notably, Caulobacter crescentus (an aerobic aquatic bacterium) undergoes a cell cycle with different cell types (a motile “swarmer” and a sessile “stalked” cell) each division; this differentiation is tightly regulated by internal signaling pathways (second messengers like cyclic di-GMP) which are reminiscent of how hormones regulate life-cycle transitions in higher organisms (though all within one cell’s lifetime here). So, while still far from having organs or hormones traveling through a bloodstream, aerobic bacteria make extensive use of chemical signals for both intercellular communication and intracellular regulation – effectively serving the role of hormones to coordinate behavior, development, and group dynamics. These signaling systems were a stepping stone to the more complex endocrine systems seen in multicellular life. | No sexual reproduction, but gene exchange persists; some life-cycle complexity. Aerobic bacteria remain asexual in reproduction – a single cell divides into two. However, the presence of oxygen often correlates with environments where gene exchange is rampant (soil, surfaces, hosts). Conjugation (bacterial “mating”) was notably characterized in aerobic bacteria (E. coli experiments, etc.), where plasmids carrying genes (like the F plasmid) transfer between cells via a pilus. This is not sexual reproduction in the eukaryotic sense (no formation of a zygote; it’s one-way DNA donation), but it creates partial genetic mixing. Additionally, transduction (virus-mediated gene transfer) and natural transformation (uptake of free DNA) are common among aerobes – for instance, Acinetobacter can take up DNA from its environment (which could be considered akin to a form of gene uptake “eating genes” from dead cells). All these lead to genetic recombination and variability. On the multicellular front, certain aerobic bacteria exhibit life-cycle stages: we discussed how Myxobacteria form fruiting bodies – that’s a reproductive cycle culminating in spores (which are dormant until conditions improve). Bacillus and Clostridium (facultative or aerobic) form endospores – again a developmental process: one cell divides asymmetrically into a mother cell and a spore, the mother cell sacrifices itself to encase the spore. This is a form of cell differentiation and propagation of the species (spores surviving harsh times to germinate later). While not sexual, it’s a complex reproduction-related process requiring tight regulation and coordination (like an embryo formation in simplistic terms). So, aerobic bacteria have begun to diversify their reproductive strategies: simple binary fission for regular growth, but spores for survival, and exchange of genetic material horizontally for innovation. True sexual cycles with meiosis and gametes still do not exist in bacteria. The ability to use oxygen didn’t directly trigger sex; however, the oxidative environment likely increased DNA damage (via ROS) and perhaps promoted mechanisms like recombinational repair (some bacteria take up DNA to repair their own, etc.), which is tangentially related to the origin of sexual recombination in the distant sense. For now, reproduction is mainly about making clones (fast and efficient) and sometimes specialized resting stages – with remarkable sophistication in timing and structure for some species, but no males or females or fertilization events. | Rugged cell envelopes and community “skins.” Aerobic bacteria often confront more variable environments (presence of oxidants, desiccation, immune systems of hosts), so their integuments are often especially reinforced. Many have capsules – thick, gummy polysaccharide layers – that not only help in evading host immunity but also in preventing drying out and buffering external changes. For example, Bacillus anthracis (which is aerobic) has a proteinaceous capsule that protects it from phagocytosis. Soil bacteria like Azotobacter secrete copious slime to encyst themselves in a protective covering when needed. Some aquatic aerobes have surface layers (S-layers) of protein that act like chain mail. Pathogens may have lipopolysaccharide in their outer membrane that confers resistance to bile salts and complements – this can be seen as a chemical shield. In biofilms, the outer layer of the biofilm (produced by aerobic bacteria in many cases) acts as a shared integument – it can absorb damage (like antibiotics or shear forces) and keep the community intact. The production of pigments by many aerobic bacteria not only serves immune evasion (some pigments scavenge ROS from host defenses) but also protects against UV light – these pigments often deposit in the cell envelope, giving coloration (e.g., Serratia marcescens produces a red pigment prodigiosin, Micrococcus luteus a yellow carotenoid). These are analogous to skin pigmentation protecting against UV. In some cases, aerobic bacteria orchestrate multicellular protective structures: Myxobacteria fruiting bodies have an outer layer of cells that die to form a sturdy casing for the spores inside – essentially a differentiated “integument” of dead cells covering the survivors. So, from the perspective of systems, we see an integumentary trend: multiple layers (membrane, wall, capsule, biofilm matrix) to defend and contain the bacteria. This is still microscopic – not a true skin with multiple cell types – but in function, it serves similarly. For instance, an encapsulated bacterium in a host is somewhat analogous to an organism with thick skin and mucus coat resisting external assault. In sum, aerobic bacteria retained the fundamental integument of prokaryotes (membrane + wall) but augmented it with additional coatings (capsules, S-layers), pigments, and communal matrices, making them exceptionally well-protected and giving a hint of specialized “outer tissues” (though not true tissues) that later multicellular life would refine. | None internally. Even when aerobic bacteria form complex colonies or fruiting bodies, they lack a lymphatic system. There is no internal fluid circulation separate from external flow. If in a host, they may end up in the host’s lymphatic system (as pathogens moving through lymph), but within themselves, no such thing exists. They also do not produce mobile cells that patrol for infection (each bacterium is its own immune defender). The concept of a lymphatic system – a network to return interstitial fluid and participate in immunity – only arises with multiple-cell layered organisms with a circulatory system. Aerobic bacterial communities rely on diffusion through their biofilm matrix to distribute nutrients and remove waste; if an immune threat (like a phage) attacks part of the colony, those cells might die but neighbors can survive – there’s no dedicated immune fluid to relocate “immune factors” like in higher animals. So, as before, no lymphatic vessels, no lymph nodes. The external water or host fluids remain the medium that carries things around for bacteria. Even in pseudo-multicellular arrangements like Streptomyces filaments, movement of fluid is either through the environment or simple streaming in the hyphae – not an independent lymph system. In conclusion, despite aerobes reaching new heights of single-cell complexity and forming primitive multicellular assemblies, a true lymphatic system is still absent; that awaited the advent of large multicellular organisms with closed circulatory systems (where leaking of plasma created a need for lymphatics).
R9: Eukaryote with Mitochondria (First Eukaryotic Cell) |  | Internalized signaling and beginning of excitability. The first eukaryote (an archaeal host merged with a bacterium turned mitochondrion) had no nerves, but it did develop far more elaborate internal communication. With a nucleus separating transcription from translation, regulation became more complex (mRNAs must be transported out, etc.). Early eukaryotes evolved calcium signaling: they sequester Ca²⁺ in organelles and release it as a second messenger, coordinating activities like muscle contraction (in later eukaryotes) or secretion. Even a single protozoan uses Ca²⁺ signals for processes like changing flagellar beat or triggering exocytosis of trichocysts (in ciliates). We also see the first membrane potentials: all cells have ionic gradients, but many protists can generate action potentials across their membrane (for instance, Paramecium fires a Ca²⁺-based action potential to reverse its cilia beating when it hits an obstacle). This is analogous to a very primitive nervous impulse. Moreover, within the eukaryotic cell, vesicle trafficking is guided by molecular signals (Rab GTPases, etc.), ensuring that internal communication directs proteins to correct organelles. So, while there is still no multi-cell nervous system, the single eukaryotic cell carries out rapid and complex signaling internally (and can respond to environmental stimuli in a more integrated way). If it was motile, it likely had a sophisticated chemotaxis and phototaxis ability orchestrated by networks of signaling proteins and ion channels. Thus, the “nervous system” at this stage is the intricate web of signaling pathways inside one cell – a leap beyond prokaryotes in scale – and the ability to generate electric responses to stimuli, foreshadowing true neurons. | Intracellular transport and compartmentalization (proto-circulation). The ancestral unicellular eukaryote was much larger than typical bacteria and filled with organelles. Diffusion alone is not enough for efficient internal transport in such a big cell, so it evolved a cytoskeleton with motor proteins (like kinesin and dynein) that ferry vesicles and organelles along microtubule “tracks”. This means, for example, nutrients taken in at the cell membrane could be packaged into vesicles and actively carried to lysosomes for digestion, or waste vesicles carried to the membrane for exocytosis. This is analogous to an internal circulation system: vesicular traffic moves substances around in a directed way, similar to how a bloodstream moves cargo in a body (albeit without a pumping heart – the motors provide the motive force). The cytoskeleton also enables cytoplasmic streaming (especially seen in large cells like algae or fungi): an active flow of cytoplasm that distributes metabolites. So within this single cell, there was a micro-transport network ensuring all parts of the cell got what they needed. Of course, there is still no circulatory system in the multicellular sense – there’s just one cell – but this cell likely could not survive by diffusion alone given its complexity, and thus internal transport processes were key. When it comes to sharing resources between cells, since we’re only at one cell, that’s moot; but this eukaryote is the precursor to multicellular lineages where circulatory systems will evolve. As a cell with mitochondria, it also had to distribute those mitochondria around – which it does via cytoskeletal movement, ensuring energy supply is circulated. In essence, the eukaryotic cell evolved an active internal distribution network – a critical stepping stone to the eventual invention of circulatory systems in multicellular organisms. | Efficient aerobic powerhouse. With mitochondria integrated, this eukaryote enjoyed vastly higher ATP production. Mitochondria carry out oxidative phosphorylation internally, so the cell no longer relies on its surface area for respiration as much – each mitochondrion is like a microscopic “lung” consuming O₂ and making energy. Gas exchange for the cell as a whole still occurs by diffusion across its outer membrane (and possibly across internalized membranes if it’s large), but because mitochondria are so efficient, the cell could afford to grow larger and have a higher metabolism without running out of energy. O₂ uptake likely increased – these cells would be avid consumers of oxygen given the mitochondria. Many such eukaryotes lived in O₂-rich environments (like shallow waters post-Great Oxidation). Internally, they needed to get oxygen to each mitochondrion; in very large single cells, cytoplasmic streaming helps move dissolved gases, but also mitochondria can move toward areas of higher O₂ if needed. The key respiratory innovation was that respiration became compartmentalized in mitochondria, freeing the rest of the cell to specialize. Because mitochondria generate ROS (reactive oxygen species), the cell also evolved better antioxidant defenses (some of which might be inherited from the mitochondrial symbiont). So catalase, SOD, etc., are present in the cell cytoplasm and mitochondria to protect against oxidative damage. In summary, the “respiratory system” is now an organelle: mitochondria serving as internal respiration units, analogous to tiny distributed lungs, dramatically boosting energy extraction​ucl.ac.uk. Gas exchange per se is still via diffusion through the cell’s surface, which suffices for single cells. (Interestingly, some large protozoans have cytoplasmic hemoglobin-like molecules to bind oxygen – a foreshadowing of blood pigments – to facilitate intracellular oxygen transport; for instance, some ciliates and algae have molecules to store O₂ for dark periods.) But broadly, acquiring mitochondria was like getting a high-performance furnace with internal pipelines – respiration became an internal affair within organelles, enabling cellular complexity and higher ATP budgets. | Phagocytosis – the first “mouth.” One of the hallmark capabilities of early mitochondriate eukaryotes was phagocytosis – the ability to engulf solid particles or whole other cells by extending the cell membrane around them and internalizing them into a vesicle (phagosome). This was a profound leap in “digestive” strategy. It means our single eukaryotic cell could actively hunt and eat bacteria or detritus, rather than only absorbing pre-digested molecules. Once ingested into a phagosome, the cell could fuse it with a lysosome (an organelle containing digestive enzymes) to form a phagolysosome where the prey is broken down – essentially an intracellular stomach. This required many innovations: a flexible cytoskeleton (actin) to deform the membrane, receptors to recognize food particles, and an array of digestive enzymes (proteases, nucleases, lipases) packaged in lysosomes. Because of phagocytosis, the eukaryote could access nutrients unreachable to smaller cells, fueling its larger size and supporting the costly mitochondria-containing structure. Some early eukaryotes might have still also harbored the ability to absorb dissolved nutrients across the membrane (like their ancestors), but phagocytosis gave them an edge in nutrient-poor environments by consuming biomass directly. Additionally, this eukaryote likely had a complete endomembrane system (ER and Golgi), which produces enzymes and processes food vacuoles. The Golgi apparatus can be likened to a digestive gland producing lysosomal enzymes, and the lysosomes to small “intestines” in the cell. All digestion occurs within membrane-bound compartments, preventing uncontrolled degradation of the cell itself. Once nutrients are broken down, they are transported out of the lysosome into the cytosol where mitochondria can use them. Waste material (indigestible bits) can be expelled by exocytosis (the phagolysosome fuses back to the membrane and dumps residual debris out) – akin to defecation on a cellular level. So, in summation, the first mitochondriate eukaryote had a true digestive system at the cellular scale: a mouth (phagocytic membrane), a stomach (phagolysosome), digestive secretions (lysosomal enzymes), and elimination (exocytosis). This predatory ability is what likely allowed the ancestral proto-eukaryote to capture the bacterial endosymbiont in the first place (leading to mitochondria), and thereafter, it remained a key feature of many protists. (Some descendants later became purely autotrophic or osmotrophic and lost phagocytosis, but phagocytosis was likely present in LECA – the Last Eukaryotic Common Ancestor.) | Organelle-based waste management. In the first eukaryotic cells, excretion became more structured. Contractile vacuoles likely evolved in freshwater protozoa to pump out excess water (to counteract osmotic influx). These vacuoles fill with water and then merge with the membrane to expel it, functioning like a simple kidney. The presence of a cytoskeleton and motor proteins allowed the cell to position the contractile vacuole and control its filling/expulsion cycle. Meanwhile, metabolic waste (CO₂, ammonia) can diffuse out or be transported out by membrane proteins, as in bacteria. However, eukaryotes often convert ammonia (which is toxic) to less toxic forms or sequester it in vacuoles until release. Some protists have microbodies (peroxisomes) dedicated to certain wasteful or harmful byproducts – e.g., peroxisomes break down fatty acids and toxins, generating hydrogen peroxide which they then decompose with catalase. This compartmentalization keeps dangerous reactions away from the rest of the cell. Osmoregulation in general was improved: beyond contractile vacuoles, eukaryotes maintain ion pumps on their plasma membrane (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPases in later eukaryotes) to regulate salt balance. The nuclear membrane also segregates transcription and translation, which indirectly helps manage macromolecular waste (e.g., improperly made mRNAs can be degraded in the nucleus, etc.). If this first eukaryote was a heterotroph, after digesting prey in lysosomes, it would expel indigestible remnants by exocytosis – a clear excretory process. The cell might also have stored certain waste in residual bodies if immediate expulsion wasn’t possible. All of this is analogous to having rudimentary excretory organs: contractile vacuole as a kidney/bladder for water, lysosome as a digestive waste processor, and peroxisome as a liver for detoxification. Notably, the separation of metabolic processes into organelles reduced interference and allowed specialized handling of byproducts. For example, mitochondria release CO₂ internally which diffuses out – if the cell is large, it may even have cytoplasmic circulatory flow to get that CO₂ to the surface. Summarily, early eukaryotes had a much more controlled internal environment than prokaryotes. They could actively pump and package wastes for removal, and thus maintain homeostasis in varying conditions. This protist-level excretory system was critical for managing a complex, high-turnover metabolism. | Innate immunity goes cellular – predation as defense. The advent of phagocytosis not only served nutrition but also is essentially an immune mechanism: eukaryotes could engulf and destroy would-be pathogens. Early single-celled eukaryotes rarely got “infected” by others (though there were viruses and possibly some intracellular bacterial parasites), but their phagocytic capability set the stage for the evolution of dedicated immune cells in multicellular life. Meanwhile, within one cell, having mitochondria and many organelles required quality control: eukaryotes evolved autophagy, a process where they sequester damaged organelles or protein aggregates in a double-membrane autophagosome and then fuse it with lysosomes to degrade the contents. This is like an immune system for the cell’s interior – removing “self” debris and potentially invasive particles. In terms of molecular immunity, eukaryotes lost CRISPR (that’s prokaryote-specific), but they gained the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway: small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and associated proteins that can target and slice foreign RNA (like viral RNA). RNAi likely evolved as a defense against viruses and transposons – it’s present in many protists. So our first mitochondriate eukaryote probably had some form of RNAi, given how conserved it is, thus providing an adaptive-like immune mechanism (not based on memory of specific sequences except those encountered and processed during infection) that silences invader genes. Another cellular immune aspect is the presence of peroxisomes and oxidative enzymes: eukaryotes can produce reactive oxygen or nitrogen species in controlled ways to kill engulfed prey or microbes (this is exactly what later macrophages do – a direct extension of single-cell protist predation). Also, eukaryotic cells have stress responses to infection – e.g. interferon response in multicellular context has roots in single-cell responses to dsRNA. Summing up: the unicellular eukaryote’s immune system was cellular digestion and molecular silencing. It could physically eat intruders and chemically dismantle foreign nucleic acids. DNA repair and checkpoint mechanisms also improved, preventing the cell from propagating if DNA was damaged (which is like an immune response to internal errors). While it had no separate immune cells or lymphatic support, every early eukaryotic cell was its own immune system, armed with an array of defensive tools far beyond those of prokaryotes. | Endoskeleton and dynamic shape. The first eukaryote had a fully developed cytoskeleton with microtubules, microfilaments (actin), and likely intermediate filaments (though those might have developed a bit later, they are present in most eukaryotes). This internal network provided not just transport (as above) but also mechanical support. Microtubules formed organizing centers (the centrosome with centrioles) to orchestrate cell division (mitotic spindle) – ensuring chromosomes were segregated properly. That mitotic spindle is an internal scaffold that temporarily becomes the “skeleton” during cell division, physically pulling chromosomes apart. Actin filaments underlie the plasma membrane in the cell cortex, allowing the cell to maintain or change shape, and to form structures like filopodia or lamellipodia for movement or engulfment. These filaments, along with myosin motors, can contract – a primitive muscle-like function used in processes like cytokinesis (contractile ring pinching the cell in two). The eukaryotic cell often lacks a rigid cell wall (many early ones, especially the ones that could phagocytose, would have had just a flexible membrane without a wall – more like animal cells). Without a wall, the cytoskeleton was solely responsible for structural integrity and shape – it’s an endo-skeleton in a literal sense. Some early eukaryotes (especially if we consider the ancestor of plants/fungi) might later evolve cell walls, but the initial one likely did not (as phagocytosis and cell motility were crucial). Instead, it may have secreted an extracellular matrix or glycocalyx for protection, but not a solid wall. This gave it plasticity: it could be amoeboid or have various shapes. Microtubules also formed structures like flagella and cilia – the eukaryotic flagellum is a bundle of microtubules (axoneme) powered by dynein motors, encased in the cell membrane. LECA (last common ancestor of eukaryotes) is thought to have had one or two flagella (and associated basal bodies/centrioles)​ucl.ac.uk​ucl.ac.uk. The flagellum requires mechanical support of the microtubules and exact architecture (9+2 arrangement), which is a skeletal structure at the cellular level allowing movement. Summarily, the eukaryote’s skeletal system was multi-faceted: an internal framework (microtubules/actin) that maintained cell shape, enabled division, and built motile appendages, and possibly a lack of external wall which meant the cell itself could be thought of as “soft-bodied” with an inside skeleton. This is analogous to how animals have internal skeletons and no rigid outer shells – these cells were more like tiny animals in that sense, versus the “exoskeleton” cell wall of bacteria. With this internal skeleton, the cell could achieve feats of strength and shape change that were impossible for prokaryotes – for example, extend pseudopods to crawl, or engulf large prey, or simply maintain a large cellular size without collapsing. It’s the precursor to the true skeletal systems in multicellular organisms, which in many cases simply scale up the principles of tension (actin) and compression (microtubules/bones) that even this single cell used. | True motility organelles (flagella, cilia, pseudopods). The mitochondriate eukaryote is the first cell with eukaryotic flagella (undulipodia) – which are fundamentally different from bacterial flagella. These are long, whip-like extensions of the cell containing an array of microtubules. By whipping back and forth, they can push or pull the cell through fluid. If our ancestral eukaryote had a flagellum, it could swim actively – an enormous advantage for finding food or mates (when sex later evolved). Cilia are essentially shorter flagella, but in unicellular eukaryotes with many of them (like a hypothetical ciliated ancestor or later ciliates), they act like hundreds of tiny oars for very precise and versatile movement, including the ability to rotate, translate, and even generate feeding currents. In addition, these cells could move via amoeboid motion: using actin and myosin to flow their cytoplasm and extend parts of the cell (pseudopodia) to crawl on surfaces or capture food. Amoeboid movement is actually quite fast on surfaces and allows navigation in complex environments (like slithering between grains of sediment). Thus, this cell effectively had muscle-like proteins (actin-myosin) working at the cell cortex to deform the cell for movement. It’s no coincidence that the proteins used (tubulin, dynein, actin, myosin) are the evolutionary precursors of muscle fibers and motors in multicellular creatures. The coordination of flagellar beating involves calcium and other signals, similarly to how muscle contraction is regulated – albeit at a single-cell level. Moreover, these cells could have chemo- and phototaxis that directs their movement: for instance, a photosynthetic flagellate (if it later acquired chloroplasts) will use an eyespot to steer toward light (as mentioned with eyespots) – that coupling of sensor to motor is akin to a neuromuscular reflex (though done with simple biochemical pathways). In sum, by having flagella and actin-powered shape change, the eukaryote possessed a dedicated locomotor toolkit – a true muscular analog. It could swim faster and navigate more complexly than any bacterium (though bacteria are faster in body lengths/sec, the eukaryote’s absolute speed and directional control in many cases surpass what tiny bacteria can do, especially in viscous conditions where flagellar size helps). This was critical for predation (chasing prey) and dispersal. So, the muscular system at this stage is intracellular and mechanical: microtubule motors for flagellar bending, actin-myosin for contraction, both energy-intensive but supported by abundant ATP from mitochondria. This synergy of energy supply and motility machinery allowed these cells to be the first true predators and explorers at the microscopic scale. | Hormone-like cell signaling and cell cycle control. Inside this first eukaryotic cell, regulation took on new levels. It had a nucleus with a cell cycle (phases G1, S, G2, M) controlled by cyclin and cyclin-dependent kinases – chemicals that rise and fall in concentration to trigger DNA replication or mitosis. These cyclins are analogous to hormones in that they are produced and degraded in a timed fashion and influence the cell’s “developmental” progression (here, the cycle). Additionally, if the cell engaged in any form of sexual reproduction (let’s assume not yet – sex is considered to have evolved later, but some evidence suggests LECA might have had meiosis​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), then it would have signaling pathways to induce mating or meiosis. Many protists release pheromone-like signals to find partners or to trigger mating reactions. Even in asexual protists, environmental sensing triggers (like starvation leading to cyst formation) involve signaling cascades that could be seen as endocrine-like: e.g. accumulation of a stress molecule inside triggers expression of cyst-specific genes. For example, Dictyostelium (a social amoeba) uses cAMP as a signal to aggregate – cells release cAMP pulses that attract others; that’s a clear chemical messenger coordinating behavior among cells (though Dictyostelium is multicellular in that stage, it shows how single cells use messengers to act in concert). For our single-cell eukaryote, the analog would be perhaps quorum sensing among eukaryotes or cues to encyst or divide. Eukaryotes also invented steroid and lipid signaling internally: they produce molecules like diacylglycerol, IP3, calcium, cAMP as second messengers to amplify signals from receptors (e.g., detecting a nutrient might lead to a signaling cascade that causes the cell to grow faster – much like a hormone telling a tissue to grow). Also, protein kinases in eukaryotes diversified greatly – allowing integration of many pathways (nutrient sensing, energy status sensing by AMPK, etc.). All these signaling molecules and kinases serve similar roles to hormones and hormone receptors: coordinating the physiology of the cell in response to internal and external cues. If this cell were in an environment with others of its kind, they might coordinate via sex pheromones or aggregation signals (as some extant protists do). So, broadly speaking, the eukaryote had a primitive endocrine system at the intracellular level and potential beginnings of intercellular signaling. It’s the prototype that in multicellular forms will become actual hormones traveling between cells. For now, chemicals did not travel far – perhaps from one part of the cell to another (nucleus to cytoplasm) or in the environment to a neighboring cell. But this was enough to yield regulated responses akin to endocrine control: entry into dormancy, mating type switching, growth and division cycles, etc., all managed by chemical regulators. | Asexual reproduction with mitosis, plus introduction of sex. The first eukaryotes reproduced predominantly by mitotic cell division – a much more complex process than binary fission. Mitosis ensured each daughter cell got an identical set of chromosomes from the separated nucleus. This process involved many “reproductive system” innovations: a mitotic spindle of microtubules, kinetochores on chromosomes, nuclear envelope breakdown and reformation, etc. It’s as if the cell built a whole apparatus (spindle = equivalent of a uterus?) to orchestrate proper division. The result was two daughter cells, akin to binary fission’s outcome but through a distinct mechanism. Now, crucially, eukaryotes also introduced sexual reproduction (meiosis and syngamy). While it’s debated exactly when sex arose, evidence suggests that LECA already had the genes for meiosis​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, meaning the ability to undergo a reduction division and form gamete-like cells that could fuse. If so, that implies our mitochondriate ancestor either had or soon evolved a sexual cycle. This would involve halving its chromosome number (meiosis), likely forming four haploid cells from one diploid, then those haploids could fuse in pairs to restore diploidy. The innovations here are staggering: pairing of homologous chromosomes, recombination (crossing over) between them (fostering genetic diversity), and cell fusion (where two cells’ membranes and nuclei merge). These events created true gametes (even if not named so then) and fertilization. Initially, gametes might have been isogamous (same size), just two cells of compatible type fusing. Sex allowed the mixing of genetic material between different lineages, speeding up evolution and clearing deleterious mutations via recombination. In terms of a “reproductive system,” this single-celled organism now had two modes: clonal reproduction via mitosis (analogous to vegetative propagation) and sexual reproduction via meiosis-fertilization. The sexual process required cellular differentiation (gametes vs. somatic phase) and timing (likely tied to stress or cell density). Regulatory pathways (possibly involving the aforementioned endocrine-like signals) would trigger meiosis or mating. This is the primordial version of having gonads and gametes – except all within one cell’s capabilities. We can think of the nucleus undergoing a switch to a meiotic program (like an ovary/testis generating gametes) and the cell then acting as a gamete that finds another to fuse with. Once multicellularity evolves, these processes become segregated into specialized cells, but at this stage, the one cell did it all. So, the early eukaryote’s reproductive system was the most advanced on Earth to date: it included both mitosis for growth and meiosis/fusion for sex, ensuring both the continuity of its lineage and the variability for adaptation​pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. This dual capability is one of eukaryotes’ defining features. It’s worth noting some protists may have temporarily lost sex and just do mitosis, but the capacity was there from very early on. | Flexible cell covering, extracellular matrix. Unlike its prokaryotic precursors, the first eukaryotic cell likely did not have a rigid cell wall (assuming it was phagocytic and motile). Instead, it was bounded by a plasma membrane supported by the cortical cytoskeleton. This gave it animal-cell-like properties: it was pliable and could change shape. As a result, it also became more vulnerable, but to compensate, eukaryotes developed other protective measures. One is the glycocalyx – a layer of carbohydrate-rich molecules on the cell surface (glycoproteins, glycolipids) which can protect the cell and help in recognition. Another is secreting an extracellular matrix (ECM) or capsule-like material. Many protists secrete mucilage or have a pellicle (in euglenoids, a pellicle of interlocking protein strips under the membrane provides both flexibility and support). Some early eukaryotes might have had scales or lorica (tiny mineral or organic scales on their surface) as additional armor; for instance, certain extant algae encase themselves in tiny silica scales (this likely came later, but shows the trend that eukaryotes often elaborate external coverings). If our ancestral eukaryote lived in water, UV radiation could damage it, so it may have retained pigmentation or developed melanin-like compounds to absorb harmful rays (some unicellular eukaryotes synthesize melanin in their cell wall or cyst walls). Over time, different lineages did different things: ancestors of plants/fungi reinstated a cell wall (cellulose or chitin) to protect and support, whereas the lineage leading to animals kept the soft membrane and instead relies on an ECM of collagen and other fibers for structural support when multicellular. But at the single-cell stage, likely a soft outer membrane with dynamic actin support was the main integument, allowing phagocytosis. This was partly offset by the presence of internal organelles handling threats (like lysosomes digesting invaders) and by behaviors (moving out of harm’s way). Some early eukaryotes might have harbored symbionts or pigments for protection, too. When we consider immune aspects: if an invader like a bacterium got past the membrane, the lysosome would handle it – which is an “integumentary” defense synergy with the immune function. The cell’s surface also had receptors and could shed and renew them, somewhat analogous to how skin sheds cells to remove attached pathogens. To summarize, the eukaryote’s integument was multi-component and dynamic: a fluid plasma membrane (with cholesterol now, which adds stability and flexibility) protected by a fuzz of carbohydrates (glycocalyx), sometimes supported by proteinaceous pellicles or scales, and capable of rapid repair (thanks to membrane flow and vesicle fusion). This is a very flexible protective strategy – it can seal wounds in the membrane quickly by exocytosis (handy if the membrane is punctured). As eukaryotes grew larger or formed colonies, some secreted slimy or hard coverings (e.g., algal colonies embedded in gelatin, or forams building calcium carbonate shells) – the first step toward specialized integument (like shells or skin in multicells). In the first single cell, though, the integument was essentially the cell membrane plus any immediately associated structures, which is the foundation from which all complex integuments (shells, skin, cuticle) in multicellular life would evolve. | None (single cell). Still, as a lone cell, there was no concept of an internal body fluid separate from the environment – so no lymphatic system. All fluid was either cytosol (inside the cell) or outside water. If this eukaryote lived in a colony (some protists can form temporary colonies), the space between cells was just the environment or some shared gelatinous matrix; there were no dedicated vessels to move fluid. Lymphatic systems proper begin to appear in animals that have circulatory systems – their absence continues in all single-celled and most colonial microbes. However, one could stretch an analogy: the contractile vacuole in freshwater protists removes excess water (like lymphatics remove excess tissue fluid). But it’s an intracellular organelle, not a body-wide vessel network. So effectively, no lymphatic system existed in a single-celled eukaryote. Each cell managed its own internal fluid and dumped excess directly out. Immune functions (like engulfing invaders) were handled by the cell itself, not by mobile immune cells in a lymph. When multicellularity eventually arose in eukaryotes, circulatory systems evolved in some, and only then did lymphatics come into being to complement blood circulation. Our protozoan ancestor is far from that stage – it relies on direct exchange with its environment for all fluid balance and on internal mechanisms for immune defense. Hence, it does not require or possess any lymphatic vessels or nodes.
R10: Eukaryote with Mitochondria & Chloroplast (First Photosynthetic Eukaryote) | They had primitive vision. ⏎  | Light sensing organelles – primitive vision. Once a eukaryotic cell acquired chloroplasts (from engulfing a cyanobacterium), it became imperative for it to seek light for photosynthesis. Many photosynthetic protists (like algae) developed a dedicated eyespot (stigma) as part of a light-sensing system. For example, a motile green alga has an eyespot – a pigmented organelle in or near the chloroplast – that shades a photoreceptor, so as the cell rotates, it can detect the direction of light and then the flagella steering is adjusted accordingly​britannica.com. This coupling of a photoreceptor signal to flagellar motor response is essentially a prototype of vision and neural response: the photoreceptor (often a rhodopsin-like molecule) triggers an electrical or chemical signal that influences the flagellar beating. Thus, the cell can swim toward optimal light (positive phototaxis) or away if it’s too intense (negative phototaxis). This is much more advanced than earlier light responses because it’s directional (requiring integration of signals as the cell rotates) and because it controls a locomotory organelle. We see here a sensory “organ” (eyespot) that is not just molecular but morphological. Aside from light, chloroplast-bearing eukaryotes also sense nutrients (they still need minerals like nitrate, phosphate) and may show chemotaxis for those when needed (though if fully autotrophic and motile, light is the main factor). Multicellular coordination isn’t relevant yet (still one cell), but within itself, this alga must coordinate chloroplast function with the rest of the cell’s needs – for instance, some have blue-light receptors that adjust the position of chloroplasts inside the cell to avoid photodamage or optimize light capture. All these signals – from eyespot to flagella, from chloroplast status to nucleus (e.g., chloroplast-to-nucleus retrograde signaling controlling gene expression) – needed integration. The cell likely had calcium signaling and other second messengers linking these sensory inputs to motor outputs. In effect, with both mitochondria and chloroplasts, the cell’s internal communication grew even more complex: it now regulated two types of energy organelles and responded to two environments (light vs. dark, nutrients in both). One could argue it’s developing separate “sensory modalities” (light for phototaxis, chemicals for chemotaxis) and integrating them – a task akin to a rudimentary nervous processing, albeit all inside one cell. Summarily, the nervous analog in this first alga was an integration of photoreception and other cues to modulate movement and internal metabolism. This was as close to a neuron as a single cell could get, using membrane potentials and signaling pathways to make “decisions” like moving toward light only when also low on internal sugar (thus needing photosynthesis). | Cell-to-cell transport in simple multicellular forms. The first photosynthetic eukaryote might have been unicellular (like a single-celled alga), but soon these cells gave rise to colonial or simple multicellular algae. For instance, some green algae form small colonies where cells adhere in a matrix (e.g., Volvox forms spherical colonies with somatic and reproductive cells). In such colonial organisms, circulation of nutrients starts to be an issue – Volvox solves it by having cytoplasmic bridges between cells so that some nutrient sharing can occur, and the rolling motion of the colony helps move fluid around the cells on the inside. If we consider the step where a single alga became a multicellular seaweed (e.g., red or green algae later), those developed some crude internal transport: cell-to-cell connections (plasmodesmata in algae/plants) and differentiation between cells that gather nutrients (photosynthetic surface cells) and those that distribute or store them. Red algae, for example, have pit plugs connecting cells – while primarily structural, they allow some exchange. However, focusing on the first cell with chloroplast, it’s likely solitary or simple filamentous. Within the one cell, the same cytoskeletal transport exists as before (moving proteins, messenger RNAs, and metabolites around). The chloroplast itself often has to move inside the cell to optimize light capture or avoid excess light – this is achieved by the cytoskeleton towing chloroplasts within the cell, which is like an internal distribution of organelles to where they’re needed (kind of like moving “organs” within a body for best function, albeit on a small scale). Some large unicellular algae (like Acetabularia, a single-celled giant) rely on extensive cytoplasmic streaming to move nutrients from the chloroplast-rich cap to other parts – a clear circulatory function inside one cell. But our first chloroplast-bearing eukaryote was probably not that large. If it had flagella, the beating of flagella also creates water currents around the cell that help bring in CO₂ and nutrients and remove O₂ and waste – an external circulation analog. To sum up, no dedicated circulatory system yet, but in colonial forms, nascent intercellular transport appears (shared cytoplasm), and inside the cell, streaming and directed vesicle/organelle traffic continue to serve the role of distributing materials. Once algae became larger and multicellular, they indeed evolved more specialized transport (e.g., sieve elements in giant kelps for nutrient translocation, though that’s analogous to phloem rather than heart/blood). But at the stage of a single photosynthetic protist or simple colony, diffusion and cytoskeletal movement were enough to meet transport needs due to relatively small size and aquatic environment. | Dual respiration and photosynthesis. This cell was capable of both releasing O₂ (from photosynthesis in chloroplasts) and consuming O₂ (in mitochondria). By day, it captured light and produced organic molecules and oxygen; by night, it respired those organics to stay alive. We have here the need for coordination between chloroplasts and mitochondria. Typically, such cells produce excess oxygen in light – much of which diffuses out (helping oxygenate the environment further). They also fix CO₂ into sugars. At night, they take in O₂ and release CO₂ via mitochondria. Gas exchange for the cell as a whole remained through the cell membrane by diffusion. However, an interesting twist is that oxygen produced in the chloroplasts can be directly used by mitochondria – reducing the need to take it from outside during daylight, and CO₂ produced by mitochondria can be re-fixed by chloroplasts when light is available. Some modern algae even time their processes so that during the day, mitochondria might run minimally (since the cell can get ATP from photosynthesis or at least has plenty of immediate energy), whereas at night, mitochondria ramp up using stored photosynthate. The integrated metabolism meant the cell had to avoid harmful conflicts: e.g., avoiding excessive oxygen radicals when both organelles are active (which they mitigate with antioxidants). Possibly, the chloroplast contributed its carbon-concentrating mechanisms to help gather CO₂ (many algae have Rubisco in pyrenoids within the chloroplast and use dissolved inorganic carbon pumps – effectively an internal “lung” improving CO₂ uptake for photosynthesis). So within one cell, the chloroplast membrane and associated pumps might concentrate CO₂ near Rubisco, while the mitochondria ensure O₂ is not building up too high (consuming some of it). In strong light, if sugar production exceeds what the cell needs, the cell can convert sugars to starch and store it (often within the chloroplast). At night, it will break that down for respiration – analogous to how animal livers store glycogen for later use. And any metabolic “exhaust” (like NH₃ from protein metabolism) can actually be re-used by chloroplasts to make new amino acids if there is enough energy. Also noteworthy: with chloroplasts, the cell could endure periods of scarcity of external food by making its own, which is a huge advantage and changed how it interacted with its environment (less dependent on finding prey, more on finding light). Summarily, respiration and photosynthesis were balanced in this cell. It was self-sustaining in daylight and only needed external minerals. Over evolutionary time, this balance would lead to diurnal cycles and maybe even division tied to day-night. But physically, still no lungs or gills – gas exchange by diffusion sufficed at the cellular level. Only when photosynthetic organisms became multicellular and large (like terrestrial plants) did they need specialized structures (stomata, etc.) for gas diffusion deep inside tissues. Our single cell didn’t have that constraint – as long as it could mix its cytoplasm or move in the water, all its parts got gas. | Autotroph with supplemental feeding. The cell’s chloroplast allowed it to make organic carbon from CO₂ and light, so its primary “diet” became sunlight and inorganic nutrients (much like a plant). Its digestive system shifted: the chloroplast itself is like an internal “gut” that converts raw environmental inputs (sunlight, CO₂, water) into usable energy (sugars). It even has its own small genome and enzymes, but the host nucleus controls much of it. The cell likely retained the ability to feed heterotrophically as well – many algae can also uptake dissolved nutrients or even phagocytose if they are not strictly wall-encased. For instance, some algae are mixotrophic: in low light they might eat bacteria for energy. So this first photosynthetic eukaryote may have been capable of phagocytosis too (the ancestor had it, and unless it lost it immediately, it could still snack on other microbes to get nutrients like nitrogen or to get energy in dark conditions). Over time, some lineages specializing in autotrophy lost phagocytosis and developed cell walls (like plants did), but some kept both modes. Having chloroplasts, the cell had to acquire more inorganic nutrients: notably, nitrogen and phosphorus for building biomass. It would uptake nitrate or ammonia and phosphate via membrane transporters – essentially “digesting” inorganic matter. The assimilation of nitrate to amino groups requires energy and enzymes (in plants this happens in roots and leaves; in a unicell, it happens in the cytosol or chloroplast). So, the cell’s “digestion” included enzymatic pathways to incorporate these nutrients into organic molecules. Possibly, symbiosis can be considered: if the chloroplast endosymbiont came from a cyanobacterium, initially there might have been a period where the host and symbiont exchanged metabolites – a sort of internal digestion where the host provided some nutrients and the symbiont provided sugars until it became fully integrated as an organelle. This is analogous to having an endosymbiotic gut microbe aiding digestion, a theme that repeats in animals. Also, any inability to directly digest cellulose (for example, if it ate a plant fiber) might be circumvented by storing such vacuoles until finding light and making own energy. In short, the chloroplast made the cell predominantly self-feeding (autotrophic), reducing the emphasis on external digestion of organic matter. Instead, its “digestive organs” were chloroplasts capturing carbon and lysosomes still present for processing whatever it ingests or recycling internal components. One could say photosynthesis shifted the “digestive paradigm” from consuming others to producing food internally. This is akin to how herbivores rely on plants (here the cell is the plant). The cell also had to avoid digesting its chloroplast (since originally it was a captured bacterium). Mechanisms evolved to suppress the normal phagosome-lysosome fusion for that symbiont – effectively a special status for the chloroplast inside. That’s like evolving a protected organ that won’t be broken down – an adjustment in the cell’s internal digestive rules to accommodate a beneficial part. Thus, the cell’s overall nutrient acquisition strategy became twofold: photosynthesis for carbon/energy, absorption or phagocytosis for everything else. This dual approach often persists (e.g., many algae can both photosynthesize and ingest dissolved organics). | Contractile vacuole and metabolic waste handling. A photosynthetic eukaryote in freshwater would still need a contractile vacuole to expel water (if it’s marine, less so). It also now has to deal with storing starch and lipids from photosynthesis – not wastes, but storage products. Excess oxygen produced in bright light could be a problem (can cause photorespiration or ROS) – many algae mitigate this by the photorespiratory pathway (glycolate pathway in peroxisomes) which helps recycle some of the byproducts of oxygenic photosynthesis. They essentially evolved a peroxisome-based mini-excretory system to handle the toxic metabolite glycolate that results when Rubisco grabs oxygen instead of CO₂. So, peroxisomes work with mitochondria and chloroplasts in a photorespiratory cycle to detoxify and recover carbon – an internal waste management for a photosynthetic cell. In general, waste products of metabolism (CO₂, O₂, ammonia) diffuse out or are reused. If the cell fixes nitrogen (some algae still had that ability if they kept some cyanobacterial capabilities, though typically primary endosymbiosis led to loss of that in the chloroplast – except some like glaucophytes? But likely not), ammonia would be a waste of nitrogen fixation. More commonly, ammonia is a waste of protein breakdown, which the cell can likely excrete or refix into amino acids if energy is abundant (especially with photosynthesis, there is plenty of ATP to drive ammonium assimilation via glutamine synthetase). Thus the cell probably excretes very little – it tends to recycle internal wastes into new building blocks given the energy available. On the osmotic side, accumulating sugars can cause water influx; often algae convert excess sugars to osmolytes that they can tolerate or store as starch to avoid osmotic issues. For salt balance, if in freshwater, contractile vacuole pumps out water constantly; in saltwater, they actively pump out sodium if too much enters or synthesize compatible solutes to balance osmotic pressure. Because they have both chloroplasts and mitochondria, they have to coordinate waste between them: oxygen and CO₂ shuttle, as mentioned. Many protists, photosynthetic included, will encyst (form a dormant cyst) in harsh conditions. Encystment is like excretion on a large scale – they shed everything they don’t need, round up, secrete a protective wall, and reduce metabolism, often purging excess water and halting waste output until conditions improve. It’s a survival excretion of activity. When forming a cyst, a cell might dump certain vacuoles or undergo autophagy to reduce volume. Summarily, the excretory needs in a photosynthetic eukaryote remain similar to other protists

R19: Earliest Protostome (ancestral protostome lineage) |  | Present: Complete digestive tract (mouth and anus) present. As a protostome, the mouth formed first in development. R21: Ambulacraria (Echinoderm & Hemichordate ancestor) |  | Present: Complete gut with mouth and anus. Both hemichordates (e.g. acorn worms) and echinoderms have through-guts. (No highly centralized CNS – that path was taken by chordates instead.) | Present: Muscles present for movement. R22: Chordate (early chordates, e.g. urochordate/cephalochordate ancestor) |  | Present: Complete digestive system with regional specialization. Chordates inherited the one-way gut and elaborated it. The early chordate (imagine something like a lancelet/amphioxus) had a mouth, pharynx with pharyngeal slits to filter-feed, an esophagus, intestine, and anus. The pharynx was a large filtering basket (in cephalochordates and tunicates) – it both fed the organism and did gas exchange. The post-pharyngeal gut was simpler but present. | Present: Closed circulatory system with a ventral heart-like pump and dorsal and ventral blood vessels – a chordate innovation. For example, amphioxus has a closed system (contractile vessels instead of a true heart) and vertebrate chordates developed a multichambered heart. (New: completely closed blood vessels for more efficient oxygen transport, unlike the open systems of ancestors). The notochord provided something firm for muscles to pull against, enabling powerful swimming while still being bendable. In early chordates like lancelets, the notochord extends tip-to-tip, supporting the entire body. Thus, chordates introduced a distinct organ for metabolic regulation (iodine metabolism) – New: the origins of a thyroid endocrine organ. Additionally, early chordates had other hormone-secreting cells (for example, neural hormonal control of gamete release, etc.). The stage was set for a full endocrine system (pituitary, etc.) in vertebrates. | Present: Sexual reproduction with gonads. The chordate ancestor (e.g. amphioxus) has many paired gonads along its body that release gametes into the atrium and out through an opening for external fertilization. Tunicates have reproductive organs (often hermaphroditic) that release gametes into water or even retain eggs for internal fertilization in some cases. In any case, chordates had distinct ovaries and testes. New: Some chordates (the lineage leading to vertebrates) evolved internal fertilization and protective embryonic structures (e.g. amniotic egg much later), but the early chordate stuck to external spawning and a free-swimming larval stage. | Present: Immune system present, primarily innate. Early chordates had phagocytic cells and immune surveillance. Notably, the chordate lineage is where adaptive immunity eventually appears – jawed vertebrates later evolved lymphocytes and antibodies. However, basal chordates (amphioxus, tunicates) do not have adaptive immunity; tunicates do have a primitive self/non-self recognition system and amphioxus has many innate immune genes. So the chordate ancestor’s immune system was similar to invertebrates’ (innate), with the potential that in the vertebrate branch, new innovations (adaptive immune cells) would evolve. (Jawless fish even have a unique adaptive-like system with VLRs, but that’s later.) | Present: A multi-layered integument starts to appear in chordates. The epidermis of an amphioxus is a single-cell layer, but vertebrates will develop a stratified epidermis. Early chordates were mostly aquatic, so their integument was not keratinized; instead, it was covered in mucus (for protection and to streamline water flow). Tunicates have a special integument: a tunic made of a cellulose-like polysaccharide – an unusual trait in chordates (essentially an exoskeletal coat around the body of urochordates). In cephalochordates and early vertebrates, the integument is just skin. So, while skin is present, New: chordates are the only animals to use a cellulose-like biopolymer (tunicates) in an integument, and vertebrates later develop scales, feathers, etc., from the integument. | Present: Highly developed sensory systems. Even the simplest chordates show advanced sensory capabilities. For example, lancelets have a small pigmented eye spot (ocellus) at the front of the nerve cord to detect light, as well as chemosensory organs (anterior nerve organ to sample water) and touch receptors along the body. Tunicate larvae have a clear sensory head: an eyespot that senses light and an otolith (gravity sensor) for orientation, allowing the tadpole larva to navigate before settling. (New: Chordates set the stage for complex sensory organs – image-forming eyes, inner ears, lateral lines – which appear in vertebrates). In the proto-chordate, we see the precursors: a dorsal eye spot linked to the brain, a balancing organ, and various mechanoreceptors. These innovations mark a significant increase in sensory complexity compared to earlier animals​quizlet.com.
R23: Mollusks (early protostome branch) |  | Present: Complete digestive system with specialized organs. Mollusks typically have a mouth with a radula (a toothed, rasping tongue) to scrape or cut food (New innovation: the radula, unique to mollusks, for mechanical breakdown of food)​allinonehighschool.com. The digestive tract is regionally specialized – e.g. esophagus, stomach with digestive glands, intestine – reflecting a more complex processing of food. | Present: Open circulatory system in most (except cephalopods). A heart (often 3-chambered) pumps hemolymph through arteries into open sinuses bathing organs​allinonehighschool.com. Blood returns to the heart via veins or ostia. This system is adequate for slower-moving mollusks (snails, clams). New: a true heart is present (first well-developed heart among these lineages)​en.wikipedia.org. Cephalopod mollusks later evolved a closed circulatory system for high activity, but the earliest mollusk had the simpler open circuit. Ex: a snail has a heart and open hemocoel. | Present: Gills (ctenidia) for aquatic mollusks. Located in the mantle cavity, these filamentous or plate-like gills facilitate gas exchange between water and blood​bio.libretexts.org. New: dedicated respiratory organs (gills) appear, which is a step beyond diffusion. Terrestrial mollusks (land snails) later modified the mantle cavity into a lung (a vascularized air space). Thus, mollusks innovated diverse respiratory strategies: aquatic gills and a primitive lung in land species. | Present: Nervous system consisting of paired ganglia and nerve cords. Mollusks typically have a set of ganglia: cerebral (brain) ganglia in the head, plus pleural, pedal, and visceral ganglia connected by nerve cords. The early mollusk (e.g. a primitive snail) had a ring of neural tissue around the esophagus and two pairs of nerve cords running to the foot and visceral mass​en.wikipedia.org. Cephalization varies – it is moderate in snails, extreme in cephalopods (which evolved a large brain and camera eyes). New: mollusks show significant advancement in neural organization – in cephalopods, the most advanced mollusks, we see the largest brains of any invertebrates (an evolutionary later development). | Present: Muscular system well-developed, especially the muscular foot used for locomotion​allinonehighschool.com. Mollusks evolved a large ventral foot (e.g. for crawling in snails, digging in clams, or as arms/tentacles in cephalopods – modified foot) as a new locomotor structure. The mantle also has muscle to control the mantle cavity and siphons. Overall, mollusks possess smooth and striated muscles for movement, feeding (radula control), and in some, jet propulsion (cephalopod mantle muscles). | Present: Exoskeleton (shell) in most mollusks, secreted by the mantle. Mollusks typically have a hard calcareous shell made of calcium carbonate​en.wikipedia.org. This shell (single, coiled in snails; two hinged valves in bivalves; internal or missing in some) protects the animal (New innovation: first major calcium carbonate external shell in soft-bodied animals for protection). Even shell-less mollusks (like slugs, octopus) have either lost it or retain internal remnants. The shell also serves as skeletal support for muscle attachment. | Present: Excretory system with nephridia (often called “kidneys” in mollusks). These metanephridia filter waste from the blood/coelomic fluid and release urine into the mantle cavity​en.wikipedia.org. Mollusks typically have one or two kidneys. This was an advancement over flame cells, allowing handling of larger volumes and reabsorption. In aquatic mollusks, waste is flushed out with water flow; terrestrial snails excrete uric acid to conserve water. | Present: Endocrine system (diffuse). Mollusks produce hormones that regulate reproduction, growth, and metabolism, though they don’t have discrete endocrine glands like vertebrates. E.g., gastropods have neuroendocrine cells in their brain that control egg laying; bivalves secrete hormones for gonad development. A notable endocrine innovation in mollusks is the hormonal control of metamorphosis in those with larvae, and in cephalopods, hormonal triggers for lifecycle events (like reproduction and death). So while mollusks lack a centralized endocrine organ, they have an internal chemical regulation system. | Present: Sexual reproduction (almost exclusively; only a few do parthenogenesis). Most mollusks have separate sexes (dioecious), though many snails are hermaphroditic. Gonads are well-defined – e.g., snails have an ovotestis (in hermaphrodites) or separate ovary/testis. Fertilization can be external (broadcast spawning in many bivalves) or internal (snails, cephalopods). Mollusks often have a trochophore larva (and a veliger larva in many gastropods and bivalves), indicating their protostome heritage. New: the trochophore larval stage in their life cycle ties them to annelids, and mollusks further evolved a veliger larva with the beginnings of a foot and shell. Terrestrial mollusks (e.g. land snails) innovated by laying eggs on land with protective gelatinous coatings (an adaptation for terrestrial life). | Present: Innate immune system. Mollusks possess immune cells called hemocytes circulating in hemolymph that phagocytose invaders and secrete antimicrobial substances. For example, snail hemocytes will encapsulate parasites, and bivalves have immune responses to pathogens in their open circulatory fluid. No adaptive immunity, but they do have molecular mechanisms for pathogen recognition (e.g. pattern recognition receptors). Some mollusks also have wound-healing processes aided by immune cells. | Present: Mantle and epidermis constitute the integument. The mantle is a specialized fold of skin that not only secretes the shell but also forms the mantle cavity protecting gills and organs​en.wikipedia.org. Mollusk skin is usually glandular – many produce mucus (e.g. snail slime) to prevent drying and ease movement. The integument can also have chemical defenses (some marine snails incorporate toxins in their skin or shell). In cephalopods, the skin contains pigment cells (chromatophores) for camouflage – an integumentary innovation in that class. Overall, a protective outer epithelium plus the shell (when present) form the defense against the environment. | Present: Highly developed sensory organs in many mollusks. New innovations: Image-forming eyes evolved independently in this group – especially in cephalopods, which have camera-type eyes comparable to vertebrates. Even some snails have eyes (though simpler) on tentacles. All mollusks have statocysts (balance organs) to orient themselves. They also have chemosensory organs like the osphradium in aquatic mollusks – a water sampling organ in the mantle cavity to test for silt or prey in water. Tactile receptors cover their body (e.g. tentacles of snails feel the environment). In summary, mollusks introduced a variety of senses: vision (from simple ocelli to complex eyes), chemical sense, balance, and touch – making them far more sensorily adept than earlier worm-like ancestors.
R24: Annelids (segmented worms, second protostome branch) |  | Present: Complete digestive tract, straight and tubular, often with differentiated regions. Annelids (e.g. earthworms, polychaete worms) have a mouth, pharynx (sometimes with jaws in predatory polychaetes), esophagus, crop and gizzard (in earthworms for grinding soil), intestine, and anus. This allows continual feeding and processing. The novelty here is not the presence of the gut (that existed already) but the specialization: New: development of a muscular gizzard for mechanical breakdown (in some annelids) and long intestine with a typhlosole (internal fold) in earthworms to increase absorption surface. | Present: Closed circulatory system in most annelids​en.wikipedia.org. Blood is confined to vessels – typically a dorsal vessel (pumps blood forward) and a ventral vessel (carries blood rearward), connected by segmental pairs of vessels. Many annelids have multiple “hearts” (contractile aortic arches) that pump blood between dorsal and ventral vessels (earthworms have ~5 pairs of pseudo-hearts)​britannica.com. (New: a fully closed blood vascular system evolved here, independently of chordates, enabling efficient oxygen transport in these active worms). Some small annelids or those in low-oxygen environments lack closed systems, but the common annelid ancestor likely had it. | Partial: Respiratory system varies – many annelids have no specialized respiratory organs, respiring through their skin (which must stay moist for gas exchange). This works for earthworms. However, in large or aquatic annelids (polychaetes), gills or branchiae often develop on segments (e.g. fan worms have feathery gills, polychaetes have parapodia with branched gills). The ancestral annelid was marine and likely had some form of external gill on its segments. So we mark as Partial: present in some. New in annelids: Segmental external gills (in polychaetes) to enhance gas exchange – an innovation for active burrowing or swimming worms that needed more oxygen than diffusion alone could supply. | Present: Central nervous system with a brain (cerebral ganglia) and a ventral segmented nerve cord. Annelids show clear segmentation in their nervous system: a pair of fused ganglia (nerve cell cluster) in each body segment connected by the double ventral nerve cord, and an anterior brain in the head region. This design allows each segment to have some autonomous control (for locomotion) under the coordination of the brain. (New: segmentation of the nervous system – each segment’s ganglion helps coordinate localized muscle movement, a novel architecture for efficient control of a segmented body). | Present: Well-developed muscles divided by segment. Each annelid segment contains circular muscles (encircling the segment) and longitudinal muscles (running lengthwise). By alternating contraction of these muscles and using the coelomic fluid as a hydraulic skeleton, annelids achieve peristaltic crawling. They can also anchor some segments via setae (bristles) and move others – giving them excellent burrowing ability. The segmentation allows localized muscle contractions without disturbing the whole body, which is a functional innovation. | Partial: Hydrostatic skeleton via segmented coelomic compartments. Annelids have fluid-filled coelom in each segment, separated by septa (internal walls). Each segment’s fluid acts as a hydrostatic skeleton that the muscles work against. There is no hard skeleton, but the segmentation of the hydrostatic skeleton is new, allowing finer control of body shape (one segment can stiffen while adjacent ones contract). No bone or shell exists, but some annelids have minor hard parts: e.g. some marine annelids have chitinous jaws or support rods for gills, and tubeworms secrete tubes, but these are specific cases. | Present: Excretory organs in each segment. Annelids typically have a pair of metanephridia in most body segments​en.wikipedia.org. These tubular excretory units collect coelomic fluid from one segment, filter out wastes, and expel urine through pores in the next segment. This segmentation of excretory function is an innovation – essentially a repeating kidney in each segment for efficient waste removal and osmoregulation. (Larval annelids start with protonephridia, but adults use metanephridia.) Earthworms, for example, have one pair of nephridia per segment that effectively remove metabolic waste. | Present: Endocrine system (mostly neuroendocrine). Annelids produce hormones that regulate processes like reproduction and regeneration. For instance, in polychaete worms, seasonal breeding and the transformation into a reproductive form (epitoke) are controlled by neurohormones. Earthworms have hormones that regulate their cocoon production and metabolism. While annelids don’t have discrete endocrine glands (no thyroid, etc.), they do have groups of neurosecretory cells in the brain and segmental ganglia that release hormones into the body fluid. So an endocrine system exists, but it’s distributed. (An example: the hormone erythrophoredin in earthworms stimulates red blood cell production.) | Present: Sexual reproduction, often with interesting variations. Many marine annelids (polychaetes) have separate sexes and spawn gametes into the water; some even undergo epitoky – transforming into a swimming reproductive form – new behavior linked to endocrine control. Fertilization is external and larvae are trochophores (shared with mollusks). Earthworms and leeches (clitellates) are hermaphroditic – they have both ovaries and testes. They perform mutual cross-fertilization and produce cocoons via a special structure called the clitellum which secretes a cocoon for eggs and sperm. New innovations: the clitellum in oligochaetes/leeches for protecting fertilized eggs on land, and the aforementioned epitoky in polychaetes for synchronized breeding. Both are unique reproductive strategies in annelids. | Present: Innate immune system. Annelids have coelomocytes (immune cells) in their coelomic fluid that ingest bacteria and produce antimicrobial agents. Earthworms, for example, secrete lysenin (a protein) to defend against parasites, and have an immune memory-like phenomenon in their coelomocyte responses. They lack an adaptive immune system with antibodies, but can recognize repeated infections with a heightened response. Also, the mucus of earthworms and leeches has antibacterial properties. Thus, annelids possess a competent innate immune defense, on par with other invertebrates. | Present: Moist cuticle over epidermis as integument. Annelids have a thin flexible cuticle (made of collagen) secreted by the epidermis, which protects them and prevents dehydration (on land, earthworms must stay in moist soil to keep cuticle wet for respiration). The skin has glands – e.g. earthworms secrete mucus to keep skin moist and ease movement. Many annelids also have chitin bristles called setae projecting from their integument (four pairs of setae per segment in earthworms) – these aid in traction and defense (they make the worm harder to pull from its burrow). Some polychaete annelids have additional integumentary structures: e.g. parchment tube worms secrete papery tubes (protective housing), others have iridescent cuticles with bristles for defense. Overall, the annelid integument is a versatile protective layer for a soft-bodied organism. | Present: Annelids often have well-developed sensory structures, especially in the head (for predatory or mobile species). Polychaete worms typically have a distinct head with eyes (from simple eyespots to fairly complex lensed eyes in some), antennae, palps, and nuchal organs (chemosensory pits)​en.wikipedia.org. These allow them to detect light, movement, and chemicals – crucial for hunting or avoiding predators. Earthworms, which burrow in soil, have no eyes but do have light-sensitive cells in their skin (they detect light to avoid coming out in daylight) and are very sensitive to vibrations (ground vibrations signal danger). They also have chemo-receptors in their anterior to sense food in soil. Leeches have multiple pairs of eyes along their body to sense hosts. In summary, annelids made advances in sensory organization: New: the development of nuchal organs (a unique chemosensory structure in polychaetes) and segmental sensors that together give these worms a good perception of their environment.
R25: Arthropods (early ones like Trilobites) |  | Present: Complete digestive system with highly specialized feeding appendages. Early arthropods (e.g. trilobites) had a mouth on the ventral side and paired appendages that likely helped direct food to the mouth. They had a foregut, midgut with digestive glands (hepatopancreas in modern terms), and hindgut. Fossil trilobites show evidence of a crop and branching digestive caeca​en.wikipedia.org. Arthropods as a group innovated diverse mouthparts: New: jointed jaws – e.g. mandibles in later arthropods, and specialized diets. Trilobites themselves had mouthparts formed by hard edges of appendages (and a calcified lip called a hypostome). The through-gut with regional specialization (storage, grinding, enzyme secretion, absorption) was fully present. | Present: Open circulatory system with a dorsal tubular heart. Arthropods pump hemolymph through an open cavity (hemocoel). The heart is a longitudinal vessel with ostia (valved openings) that suck blood in and pump it forward. In a trilobite or primitive arthropod, the heart lay along the back and pushed blood toward the head; blood then percolated through sinuses and returned dorsally. This system is less efficient than closed circulation but sufficient given arthropods’ other adaptations for oxygen transport (like tracheae or gills). Most arthropods retain this open system. (Cephalopods are the only invertebrates that went closed – arthropods did not, aside from some small diffusion-based exceptions.) So, no new circulatory innovation compared to mollusks/annelids’ closed system, but New: the combination of a heart and extensive sinuses supporting a high metabolism with the help of respiratory innovations (like tracheal tubes in insects) is an arthropod hallmark. | Present: Specialized respiratory systems. Aquatic arthropods (including trilobites) had gills – trilobites possessed filamentous gill structures on their appendages for gas exchange​en.wikipedia.org. These were likely the first clearly evident gills in the fossil record. Terrestrial arthropods later evolved tracheae – an extensive network of air-filled tubes delivering oxygen directly to tissues (insects, centipedes) – or book lungs (in arachnids). Thus, arthropods introduced new respiratory adaptations: New: book gills in horseshoe crabs (similar to what trilobites had), book lungs in spiders, and especially tracheal systems in insects which allowed arthropods to conquer land by delivering air internally. These are unique innovations not seen in earlier groups. | Present: Highly centralized nervous system with a brain and ventral nerve cord. Arthropods inherited the annelid-like nervous system but took it further. They have a fused brain (typically 3-part: protocerebrum, deutocerebrum, tritocerebrum) in the head and a double ventral nerve cord with ganglia in each segment. In trilobites, for example, there would have been a cerebral ganglion in the head and a chain of ganglia along the body. Arthropods show great cephalization – in insects and crustaceans, the brain controls complex behaviors, and in some (like bees) even learning. New: arthropods also developed complex compound eyes and other advanced sensory inputs that required more sophisticated neural processing. So their nervous system is an upgrade in complexity and processing power relative to earlier worms. They can perform fast, coordinated movements and behaviors (e.g. escape reflexes, hunting tactics) thanks to this advanced CNS. | Present: Powerful, segmented muscles attached to exoskeleton for movement. Arthropods replaced the hydrostatic skeleton with a jointed exoskeleton and thus evolved striated muscles that attach internally to the exoskeleton (via infoldings called apodemes​en.wikipedia.org). Trilobites, for instance, had muscles to move each leg segment and to enroll (roll up the body). Arthropod muscles contract much faster than those of annelids, enabling quick motions. New: Flight muscles in winged insects (much later) are an arthropod innovation, giving insects the first powered flight on Earth. Even early on, arthropods achieved movements like rapid running and precise manipulation with jointed limbs, far surpassing the locomotor abilities of soft-bodied ancestors. | Present: Exoskeletal system (hard external skeleton) made of chitin and proteins, often mineralized with calcium carbonate​en.wikipedia.org. Arthropods like trilobites encased their body in a jointed shell – providing protection and points for muscle attachment. This was a revolutionary innovation: New: a jointed exoskeleton that can be molted (ecdysis). The exoskeleton in trilobites had articulated segments allowing flexibility while maintaining armor. In later arthropods, this evolved into various forms (e.g. insect cuticle with wax layer against desiccation, crustacean carapaces). The exoskeleton also created specialized body regions (head, thorax, abdomen in many) by segment fusion. The cost of this system is the need to molt to grow, but its benefit is tremendous defense and support. Trilobite fossils show even the eyes had hard lenses of calcite​en.wikipedia.org. No earlier animal had such a complex exoskeleton – truly a defining arthropod feature. | Present: Excretory systems adapted to life in various environments. Arthropods evolved different excretory organs: aquatic ones (like crustaceans, including trilobites) have gland-based organs (antennal or maxillary glands, analogous to kidneys) that filter hemolymph and excrete urine. Terrestrial arthropods (insects, arachnids) use Malpighian tubules – thin tubes that empty waste into the gut for excretion with feces, an adaptation to conserve water. Trilobites likely had primitive glands at the base of legs (similar to green glands in modern crustaceans) to eliminate waste. So an excretory system was present and New adaptations: Malpighian tubules in insects allowed efficient nitrogen waste removal with minimal water loss – a key innovation for terrestrial life. | Present: Endocrine system well-developed. Arthropods have an array of hormones controlling molting, metamorphosis, reproduction, and metabolism. For instance, ecdysone hormone triggers molting of the exoskeleton – a unique need because of their growth by molting. Juvenile hormone in insects dictates whether a molt produces another juvenile stage or an adult – enabling complete metamorphosis. These are new endocrine innovations in arthropods. Also, neurohormones from the brain control these processes. So arthropods possess complex endocrine glands like the prothoracic gland (in insects) for ecdysone, corpora allata for juvenile hormone, etc. Such hormonal regulation of development and metamorphosis is a significant arthropod trait. | Present: Sexual reproduction (with rare exceptions like parthenogenesis in some insects). Arthropods typically have separate sexes and internal fertilization is common, especially in terrestrial groups. Early aquatic arthropods (trilobites) probably released eggs and sperm into the water (there’s evidence some trilobites may have carried eggs in specialized brood structures​en.wikipedia.org). Arthropods show immense variety: many insects perform elaborate mating behaviors and lay shelled eggs on land (a necessity for terrestrial life), while crustaceans often carry eggs until hatching. New innovations: Arthropods were the first to evolve internal fertilization in many lineages (e.g. insects, arachnids) to protect gametes from drying out, and the first to lay cleidoic (waterproof) eggs on land (insects). Additionally, metamorphosis – seen in many insects (holometabolism) – is an innovation where larval and adult stages are distinct, allowing exploitation of different niches. This extreme change is under hormonal control and is unique to arthropods among invertebrates. | Present: Innate immune system advanced. Arthropods have both cellular and humoral innate immunity. They have hemocytes that do phagocytosis and encapsulation of parasites (similar to other invertebrates). Remarkably, arthropods also produce antimicrobial peptides (e.g. defensins) upon infection – for instance, insects upon detecting bacteria can synthesize potent antibiotics to clear infection. Horseshoe crabs (an ancient chelicerate arthropod) have amoebocytes in their blood that coagulate in presence of bacterial endotoxins – so efficiently that modern medicine uses their blood for endotoxin tests. While still no adaptive immune cells, the genetic toolkit for innate immunity in arthropods is quite sophisticated (Toll pathways, etc., which even informed vertebrate innate immunity). Thus, arthropods can survive infections in varied environments with a robust innate immune response. | Present: Exoskeleton as integument – the arthropod cuticle is the integument, providing both support and protection from the environment (physical and chemical). It’s multilayered (waxy outer layer to prevent water loss in land arthropods, rigid layers for defense). Insects and spiders minimized dehydration with their cuticle – crucial for terrestrial success. New: The arthropod cuticle is not just armor; it forms specialized structures: e.g. waterproofing wax, coloration for camouflage or warning, sensory bristles that pierce through it, and in some, silk-spinning structures (spiders) or wing membranes (insects) – all integumentary derivatives. Arthropods also perform ecdysis – periodically shedding the old integument to grow – which is a vulnerability but necessary for a hard integument user. So the integumentary system in arthropods is dynamic and versatile, enabling life from deep oceans to dry deserts. | Present: Highly acute sensory organs – one of arthropods’ greatest strengths. New innovations: Compound eyes with multiple ommatidia (facets) giving a broad field of vision – present even in the earliest trilobites (which had large compound eyes with calcite lenses)​en.wikipedia.org. Compound eyes provided motion detection and mosaic vision with high frame rates, a huge leap in predator/prey awareness. Arthropods also developed antennae – segmented sensory appendages rich in chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors – essentially “smelling” and “feeling” the environment. Trilobites had antennae; modern insects’ antennae can detect pheromones miles away. They also have mechanosensory hairs and tympanal organs for detecting sound vibrations (e.g. cricket ears on legs). The arthropod head typically bundles major senses: eyes, antennae, sometimes palps for taste. Many arthropods (like bees or ants) can communicate via pheromones and detect them with exquisite sensitivity. Some insects evolved complex ocelli (simple eyes) in addition to compound eyes for light-level detection, and certain spiders (though not compound-eyed) evolved multiple lens eyes with high resolution. In summary, arthropods came to dominate environments in part due to their sensory/perceptive prowess – spotting predators or prey, finding mates by scent, hearing sounds – an array of senses unmatched in earlier animal groups.
