R50: Tides | 🌕 1. Tides happen because gravity isn’t the same everywhere ⏎  ⏎ Earth and Moon pull on each other gravitationally. ⏎ But here’s the key: ⏎  ⏎ The side of Earth facing the Moon feels a stronger pull than ⏎  ⏎ the center of the Earth, which feels stronger than ⏎  ⏎ the far side. ⏎  ⏎ That gradient — not the absolute pull — stretches the planet. ⏎  ⏎ 👉 This is why tides are caused by differential gravity, not just “the Moon pulling water.” ⏎  ⏎ 🌊 2. The Earth bulges on both sides ⏎  ⏎ This always surprises people. If the Moon pulls water toward it, why is there a high tide on the opposite side too? ⏎  ⏎ Near side: water gets pulled harder toward the Moon → bulge. ⏎  ⏎ Far side: the Moon pulls the Earth itself slightly more than the water far away → Earth moves closer to the Moon than the far-side water → bulge in the opposite direction. ⏎  ⏎ Result: ⏎  ⏎ Two bulges of water on opposite sides of the Earth. ⏎  ⏎ As the Earth rotates through these bulges, coastal regions experience two high tides and two low tides every ~24 hours. ⏎  ⏎ 5 ⏎ ☀️ 3. The Sun plays a role too ⏎  ⏎ The Sun is way bigger than the Moon, but it’s also much farther away, so its tidal effect is weaker than the Moon’s but still significant. ⏎  ⏎ When Sun + Moon line up (New Moon, Full Moon): ⏎ → tidal forces add up → Spring Tides (biggest highs and lows). ⏎  ⏎ When Sun and Moon are at right angles (First & Third Quarter): ⏎ → tidal forces partially cancel → Neap Tides (smallest highs and lows). ⏎  ⏎ 📅 Tidal patterns follow both the lunar day (~24h 50min) and the moon phases. ⏎  ⏎ 🪨 4. Land tides are real too ⏎  ⏎ Tides aren’t just about water. ⏎ The solid Earth also flexes by about 30 cm every day due to the same forces. ⏎ Water just makes it obvious because it flows more easily. ⏎  ⏎ 🌌 5. Tides explain a lot more than beaches ⏎  ⏎ Tidal locking: ⏎ The Moon always shows the same face to Earth because over millions of years, Earth’s tidal pull slowed the Moon’s rotation until it matched its orbit. That’s how we get one “near side” forever. ⏎  ⏎ Orbital evolution: ⏎ Earth’s rotation is gradually slowing down, and the Moon is slowly drifting away (about 3.8 cm/year) due to tidal energy transfer. ⏎  ⏎ Tidal heating: ⏎ Moons like Io (around Jupiter) experience huge tidal forces. The stretching and squeezing creates internal friction → heat → volcanic activity. ⏎  ⏎ Tidal disruption: ⏎ Get too close to a massive body, and tidal forces can tear things apart (e.g., Roche limit). ⏎  ⏎ 📈 6. The math behind tides (clean version) ⏎  ⏎ Tidal acceleration is roughly: ⏎  ⏎ 𝑎 ⏎ tidal ⏎ ≈ ⏎ 2 ⏎ 𝐺 ⏎ 𝑀 ⏎ 𝑅 ⏎ 𝑟 ⏎ 3 ⏎ a ⏎ tidal ⏎ 	​ ⏎  ⏎ ≈2GM ⏎ r ⏎ 3 ⏎ R ⏎ 	​ ⏎  ⏎  ⏎ where: ⏎  ⏎ 𝑀 ⏎ M = mass of the moon (or other body) ⏎  ⏎ 𝑅 ⏎ R = radius of the Earth ⏎  ⏎ 𝑟 ⏎ r = distance to the Moon. ⏎  ⏎ Notice the  ⏎ 𝑟 ⏎ 3 ⏎ r ⏎ 3 ⏎  in the denominator → tidal effects drop off very fast with distance. ⏎ That’s why distant stars don’t give us beach tides. ⏎  ⏎ ⚡ 7. Why tides vary from place to place ⏎  ⏎ Shape of coastlines ⏎  ⏎ Ocean depth ⏎  ⏎ Latitude and Coriolis effect ⏎  ⏎ Local resonance ⏎  ⏎ That’s why the Bay of Fundy has a record 16 m tidal range, while other coasts barely notice it.
