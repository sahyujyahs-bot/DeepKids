## Concept activities: Climate Zones & Classification
- **about:** How Earth’s regions differ in temperature, rainfall, vegetation.
- **principles:** Köppen climate types, latitude/altitude effects, ocean currents.
- **philosophy:** Diversity creates balance; many paths to sustain life.
- **experiments:** Compare soil and leaf samples from different garden micro-climates (sunny vs shady).
- **home:** Keep a week-long log of indoor/outdoor temperatures and humidity.
- **outdoors:** Map a park noting warm vs cool, wet vs dry spots.
- **dance:** Group dance: each kid embodies a different climate (desert stillness, rainforest sway).
- **drama:** Drama: council of climates debating how to share the planet.
- **art:** Paint a world map showing different climate belts in chosen colours.
- **music:** Compose short tunes capturing the “feel” of tundra, desert, rainforest.
- **yoga:** Slow, grounding “mountain pose” (tadasana) to sense stability across climates.
- **physical_games:** “Climate tag”: zones of the playground marked as desert/rainforest—rules change with zone.
- **games:** Trivia quiz: match animal/plant cards to the right climate.
- **reading:** “One Well” by Rochelle Strauss (global water & climate).
- **writing:** Write a story about a traveler experiencing all five climate zones in one year.
- **more:** Create a mini terrarium and a dry sand bottle—compare over weeks.
- **other:** Collect weather data from different world cities and graph patterns.

## Concept activities: Climate feedbacks (ice-albedo, greenhouse effect, carbon cycle)
- **about-the-concept:** Loops where climate change accelerates or slows itself.
- **principles-within-the-concept:** Ice-albedo positive feedback; greenhouse gas concentration; carbon sinks and sources.
- **philosophy-underlying-the-concepts:** Actions have consequences; small changes can amplify; interconnectedness of all living systems.
- **experiments:** Compare melt rate of ice on black cloth vs white cloth to show albedo; bottle two mini-greenhouses—one sealed with CO₂ from vinegar & baking soda, one normal—and measure temperature difference; use yeast in a bottle with balloon to visualise carbon cycle gas release.
- **activities-around-the-house:** Keep a daily CO₂ log from a cheap indoor sensor; compost kitchen scraps and measure temperature rise; monitor houseplant growth as a carbon sink.
- **activities-outside-the-house-in-nature:** Walk in a forest and measure cooler temps vs nearby asphalt; observe snow or frost reflecting sunlight; collect autumn leaves and track their decay.
- **dance:** Dance showing the feedback loop: ice retreat leads to more heat, leading to faster retreat; group movement of “carbon molecules” cycling through earth, air, plants.
- **drama:** Drama of a council of Earth systems debating how to keep balance; skit of greenhouse gases discussing their power; role-play carbon atoms moving through atmosphere and biosphere.
- **painting-drawing:** Paint a before/after polar landscape showing loss of ice; create a carbon-cycle infographic mural; draw a greenhouse with layers of trapping gases.
- **music:** Compose music that starts with soft reflective tones and builds to a heat crescendo; create a carbon-cycle rap; percussion piece rising in tempo to mimic accelerating feedback.
- **yoga-breathing:** Practice cooling breath and warming breath in sequence to feel feedback; tree-pose meditation reflecting stability vs change; pranayama while visualising carbon moving through you.
- **physical_games:** “Feedback chase”: tag game where each tagger adds another player to show positive feedback; “carbon cycle relay”: players carry “carbon tokens” through plant–animal–air stations; hot–cold freeze dance representing warming and cooling phases.
- **games-of-any-kind:** Cooperative board game where players must keep global temperature stable; card game matching feedback type to effect; trivia quiz on carbon sinks and sources.
- **reading:** Our Changing Climate by National Geographic Kids; articles on ice-albedo feedback and carbon cycle; NASA Earth Observatory pieces.
- **writing-observations-stories-poems-answers:** Diary entry from an ice sheet watching itself shrink; poem about a carbon atom’s long journey; essay: “How my choices feed back into Earth’s climate.”
- **more:** Build a terrarium to observe plant carbon uptake; use satellite images to track polar ice over months; create a school exhibit showing positive and negative feedbacks.
- **other:** Interview an environmental scientist; watch a documentary on Arctic ice loss; visit a botanical garden to discuss carbon storage.

## Concept activities: Greenhouse gases & global warming
- **about-the-concept:** Certain gases trap heat, causing Earth’s temperature to rise.
- **principles-within-the-concept:** Greenhouse effect; anthropogenic CO₂ and methane sources; energy imbalance.
- **philosophy-underlying-the-concepts:** Actions ripple globally; stewardship is shared responsibility; small changes matter.
- **experiments:** Two-bottle greenhouse experiment: one bottle filled with CO₂ from vinegar & baking soda warms faster under a lamp; compare clear vs. covered jar temperature rise; build a simple infrared thermometer circuit to track heat.
- **activities-around-the-house:** Audit your household for CO₂ sources—lights, appliances—and chart reductions; grow indoor plants to act as carbon sinks; measure temperature differences between shaded and sunlit windows.
- **activities-outside-the-house-in-nature:** Take a walk in a city park and compare temperatures of asphalt vs. grass; visit a landfill to observe methane capture if tours are offered; monitor local air-quality index.
- **dance:** Dance moving from cool blue to fiery red to show rising global temperatures; group improvisation where dancers act as greenhouse gases thickening around the Earth.
- **drama:** Drama of a council of greenhouse gases debating their effects; role-play a UN climate summit; skit of future children sending messages back to today.
- **painting-drawing:** Paint a “before and after” Earth showing pre-industrial and modern atmosphere; create a poster of major greenhouse gases and their sources; draw a heat map of your city.
- **music:** Compose a rap about greenhouse gases; soundscape mixing industrial noises with bird songs fading; create a music piece whose volume rises to mimic global warming.
- **yoga-breathing:** Practice “fire breath” (kapalabhati) to feel internal heat; cooling breath to counter it; yoga flow reflecting balance between warming and cooling.
- **physical_games:** “Carbon capture” tag: runners collect “carbon tokens” before taggers; relay race where each lap represents a decade of warming; hot-potato game symbolising rising temperatures.
- **games-of-any-kind:** Cooperative board game where players reduce emissions to keep global temperature below 2 °C; trivia quiz on greenhouse gases; card game matching sources to gases.
- **reading:** Our House Is on Fire (Greta Thunberg’s family story adapted for kids); kid-friendly guides to greenhouse effect and climate change; NASA Climate Kids website.
- **writing-observations-stories-poems-answers:** Letter to future grandchildren about what you did for the planet; poem on a single CO₂ molecule’s journey; essay “How my choices change Earth’s temperature.”
- **more:** Plant a tree and record its growth as a living carbon sink; set up a school-wide “no-plastic week”; track local temperature records over decades from public data.
- **other:** Join a community clean-up; visit a renewable-energy site; watch a documentary such as Before the Flood and discuss actions.

## Concept activities: Human impact (anthropogenic forcing, aerosols)
- **about-the-concept:** Human activities—burning fuel, industry, land use—alter climate by adding greenhouse gases and particles.
- **principles-within-the-concept:** Radiative forcing; aerosol scattering; feedback loops between human behaviour and climate.
- **philosophy-underlying-the-concepts:** Our choices ripple through Earth systems; responsibility and interconnection.
- **experiments:** Build two identical mini-greenhouses—one with added smoke or aerosol from a safe incense stick—to compare temperature; test visibility by shining a laser through clear vs. smoky jar; measure indoor CO₂ with a cheap sensor before and after people gather.
- **activities-around-the-house:** Conduct a “household emissions audit” (electricity use, travel, cooking); create a weekly low-carbon menu; make a DIY air filter and check collected particles.
- **activities-outside-the-house-in-nature:** Visit a busy street and a park to compare air quality using simple particulate counters; photograph haze at sunrise and midday; join a tree-planting drive.
- **dance:** Group dance shifting from chaotic industrial motions to slow regenerative ones; duet where one dancer scatters like aerosols while another clears the air.
- **drama:** Skit of “Earth on trial” where children play lawyers for and against human actions; role-play of future citizens pleading with present humans.
- **painting-drawing:** Draw a before/after cityscape—clear sky vs. smog; create infographics of global emissions; paint the invisible “cloud” of human influence.
- **music:** Compose a piece starting with factory clatter and resolving into natural sounds; create a percussion rhythm using recycled metal to symbolise industry.
- **yoga-breathing:** Alternate slow cooling breaths and fast heating breaths to embody climate swings; “breath audit”: notice indoor air quality while breathing mindfully.
- **physical_games:** “Pollution tag”: players with grey scarves represent aerosols that spread by tagging others; relay race carrying “carbon tokens” to illustrate emissions.
- **games-of-any-kind:** Cooperative board game where players must cut emissions before time runs out; trivia quiz on climate impacts; matching cards of human activities to their forcing effects.
- **reading:** This Changes Everything (adapted summaries for kids/teens); NASA Climate Kids articles on aerosols; IPCC children’s guides.
- **writing-observations-stories-poems-answers:** Diary of an air molecule passing through factories and forests; poem about human fingerprints on the sky; essay on personal responsibility for climate.
- **more:** Build a solar cooker and measure reduction in cooking emissions; host a “car-free day” challenge; create a local air-quality map from citizen data.
- **other:** Interview elders about how local air and seasons have changed; visit a renewable-energy site; watch a short documentary on aerosols.

## Concept activities: Climate models & predictions
- **about-the-concept:** Scientists use computer models to simulate Earth’s climate and forecast future changes.
- **principles-within-the-concept:** Numerical simulation; feedbacks and parameter sensitivity; scenario analysis.
- **philosophy-underlying-the-concepts:** The future is not fixed; knowledge allows foresight and stewardship.
- **experiments:** Run a simple online climate model (e.g., NASA Climate Kids simulator); compare outputs for different CO₂ levels; build a tabletop “mini-model” with heat lamps and water trays to mimic warming.
- **activities-around-the-house:** Keep a notebook of daily weather, then compare with professional forecasts; use spreadsheet to graph temperature trends at home; simulate greenhouse effect with two jars and lamps.
- **activities-outside-the-house-in-nature:** Visit a weather station or university meteorology lab; attend a public lecture on climate science; observe how local forecasts match reality over weeks.
- **dance:** Dance where each movement represents a climate variable—temperature, sea level, ice cover—changing with music tempo; group improvisation showing positive and negative feedbacks.
- **drama:** Drama of a team of future scientists explaining model predictions to world leaders; courtroom play debating different emissions scenarios.
- **painting-drawing:** Paint “possible futures”: contrasting canvases for high vs. low emissions paths; create a climate data mural with graphs and icons.
- **music:** Compose music where instruments enter one by one like variables in a model; use electronic loops to build complexity over time.
- **yoga-breathing:** Practice visualisation meditation: imagine Earth under different future scenarios; breath work to embody rising and falling climate patterns.
- **physical_games:** “Scenario tag”: each team represents a scenario (high/low emissions) and must reach a goal before “time” runs out; data-hunt relay using real climate datasets.
- **games-of-any-kind:** Board game where players adjust variables (CO₂, forests, oceans) and see resulting temperature changes; puzzle assembling a climate feedback diagram; trivia game on famous climate predictions.
- **reading:** How to Change Everything by Naomi Klein (youth edition); NOAA resources on climate modelling; NASA Earth Science outreach material.
- **writing-observations-stories-poems-answers:** Write a letter from a future child describing life under a chosen climate scenario; poem about “tomorrow’s weather” fifty years from now; short story where a climate model speaks as a character.
- **more:** Code a simple spreadsheet climate model; analyse open climate datasets; create a school exhibit comparing forecasts to observed trends.
- **other:** Interview a meteorologist; watch a documentary on supercomputers in climate science; visit a planetarium climate show.

## SCI Cards: scientists for this topic (founder's card game; strongest underdog stories first)
- **Rachel Carson** (1907-05-27–1964-04-14, American; Heretic, World Changer, Foundational, Underdog): Wrote Silent Spring (1962) — proving that pesticides (especially DDT) were killing wildlife, poisoning ecosystems, and accumulating in human tissues. Her book launched the modern environmental movement, led to the creation of the EPA, and ultimately resulted in the ban of DDT. She demonstrated that human chemical activity has planetary consequences. *Whisper:* Poisons don't stay where you spray them. DDT sprayed on crops enters insects, enters birds, enters fish, enters you. Everything is connected, and a marine biologist proved it while the chemical industry tried to destroy her. *Story:* When Carson published Silent Spring, the chemical industry spent millions attacking her. She was called hysterical, unscientific, a communist, and a spinster. Monsanto published a parody mocking her. She was dying of breast cancer while defending her work. She testified before Congress, calmly presenting the evidence. DDT was banned. The EPA was created. The modern environmental movement was born. She didn't live to see most of it — she died two years after publication. Virtually every environmental protection law worldwide traces its intellectual ancestry to Silent Spring.
- **Eunice Newton Foote** (17 Jul 1819–30 Sep 1888, American; IP Theft Victim, Ahead of Time, Invisible Giant, World Changer, Foundational): First person to demonstrate the greenhouse effect (1856) — three years before John Tyndall, who gets all the credit. She filled glass cylinders with different gases, placed them in sunlight, and showed that CO₂ traps far more heat than regular air. Her paper was read at the AAAS meeting by a male colleague because women couldn't present. Tyndall's more detailed 1859 experiments are credited as the 'discovery' — he never cited her. *Whisper:* A glass cylinder full of CO₂ gets hotter in sunlight than one full of regular air. This simple experiment — done by a woman in 1856 — is the foundation of everything we know about climate change. Credit went to a man who did it three years later. *Story:* At the 1856 AAAS meeting, Foote's paper was read aloud by Joseph Henry (secretary of the Smithsonian) because women couldn't present their own work. She showed that CO₂-filled containers heated dramatically more than air-filled ones in sunlight, and explicitly wrote: 'An atmosphere of that gas would give to our earth a high temperature.' This is the greenhouse effect, stated clearly, in 1856. Three years later, John Tyndall performed more elaborate experiments and is universally credited as the discoverer. He never referenced Foote. - **Svante Arrhenius** (19 Feb 1859–1927-10-02, Swedish; Ahead of Time, Foundational, World Changer, Wrong but Important): First to calculate that doubling CO₂ in the atmosphere would raise global temperatures by 4-6°C (1896) — remarkably close to modern estimates. Also developed the theory of electrolytic dissociation (ions in solution) and the Arrhenius equation for chemical reaction rates. His PhD thesis on ions was given the lowest possible passing grade — then won him the Nobel Prize. *Whisper:* A transparent gas (CO₂) can trap heat and warm an entire planet. The greenhouse effect is not a modern discovery — it was calculated by hand, on paper, in 1896. *Story:* His PhD thesis on ionic dissociation received the lowest possible passing grade — his examiners thought it was barely worth a degree. That same work won him the Nobel Prize 20 years later. In 1896, he hand-calculated (over thousands of hours) how CO₂ levels affect global temperature. He predicted that burning fossil fuels would warm the planet — but thought it would take 3,000 years and be beneficial. He got the direction right and the timescale disastrously wrong.
