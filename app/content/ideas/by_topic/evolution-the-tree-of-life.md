### Thought Predates Neurons
Form has always followed function in biological evolution.
The mind is no exception.
The most common misconception that even science prescribes,
Is the anthropocentric idea that intelligence, thought and awareness is unique to humans or
“higher beings”, while ignoring all the proof that biology and even evolution presents.

How does a thought manifest today in our brain? - Ions flow through our neurons to activate
different gates, different proteins, different genes - all to build a subjective response based on
the sensory inputs.

Now let's go back to the life of the simple prokaryotic cell. The great grandmother of all.
One of the things she could do was moving towards food.
How does she know where is food? How is the decision to move and choosing a direction
made?
She had sensors made up of proteins. These reacted to different chemicals, temperatures &
pressures.
If an environment has an abundance of food molecules, the relevant protein sensors on the
outer membrane of the cells get activated.
This triggered an avalanche of ion flow, that activated relevant proteins, and genes, that initiated
and executed the end action of moving towards the direction of the activated protein sensor.

The same ion flow, the same chemical influx that happens in our body, happens in these single
celled beings too.
Over time, patterns form - same triggers, same direction of ion flux, same genes getting
activated - contributing to experiential memory of the being.
Internal communication, conditional pathways, subjective gene and protein activation - the
fundamental mechanisms of thought emerged.
As we will see in the later chapters - Evolution built on it.
Neurons - tunnels through which these ions could flow, were built.
Now the communication was faster. More efficient.
Here too , patterns formed over time - the most used neural pathways were stronger than the
others - what we call memory - the most followed neural patterns.
Beings were getting bigger, surface areas increased, so did the number and types of sensors
that relayed information about the outside world. Conditions and possible pathways were also
increasing.
So, life made another move towards efficiency.
Built a central organ where all the sensory inputs can be collected, processed and a pathway
chosen - the brain, which first came 700 million years ago in the comb jellies and the early
bilaterians and built on over the centuries.

The way we experience thoughts & decisions, in our minds, like its being read out loud by some
sitting inside us? - that is how we experience thought.
Do the other beings also experience thought in similar ways?
Anthropocentric views says no, even though the biological and chemical mechanics are the
same.
There have been scientists including our own Jagadish Chandra Bose who have argued that
emotions, thoughts exist in all other beings including plants , just like it does in ours. But these

These are all questions we have to ask ourselves, before pandering & buying into the most

### The First Fungi
Quickly following the red algae, the Fungi evolved from eukaryotes with only mitochondria.

The parent eukaryote with mitochondria had innovated food & primitive internal digestion. But its
first child, the fungi, did not opt for that route. Instead, they chose the more historic route of
external digestion by secreting enzymes & breakdown molecules outside the body & absorbing
nutrients.
But why? Because their aspirations were greater than their parents. Internal digestion means a
defined body shape that can house the gut. And digestion would have to happen per meal.

Instead, they developed a whole new way to live & grow - A fungi will start extending on one
side like a tip, a tail, only adding some cell wall. And slowly the tip grows and the fungo mother
will divide her nucleus and give one to the hyphae. Now the hyphae has its own DNA, but
remains forever attached to the mother. This continues. New hyphaes get added like chains,
each having its own nucleus, but all hyphaes are still attached to the mother. They could cover
hundreds of acres this way.

They also highways and transporter proteins to carry the nutrient absorbed in the hypahes to
other older interior cells of fungi.
For internal communication between the cells, they used the same well established chemical
pathways, ion fluctuations and electrical impulses. The same fundamentals that happen within
neurons - tunnels that animals built for internal communication.

When the resources get scarce, the chemical signals activate the genes for reproduction & the
hyphaes pause focussing on growth and focus on reproduction , making spores - tiny cells
packed with their dna & eject into the world, for them to find homes elsewhere.

They, like the aerobic bacteria, and eukaryotes with mitochondria, needed Oxygen to produce
energy and continued consuming them from around them releasing CO2

Fungi - the scavengers with decentralized intelligence , covering acres, responding subjectively
to stimuli across distances. Not only did they cover the soils under the ocean, they also broke
down the quickly gathering bio waste & made more food available for other organisms that
came before & as you will see in future chapters, made life possible on land too.

### Green Algae
The green algae came next and proliferate the oceans & eventually the land.
They were different from their sibling red algae in two ways - first one, these green algae had
two types of chlorophyll pigments, one more than their sibling.
The green algae did not have the protein that the red algae their colour. Instead these guys
derived their green colour from the second chlorophyll pigment they developed. But they didn’t
need this protein either. The red algae needed the protein to use the blue green sunlight deep
under the ocean. But with the second chlorophyll the green algae could efficiently use sunlight

Parasites
Around 512 million years ago, in the crowded Cambrian seas, some arthropods began evolving
parasitic lifestyles. This moment marks a radical innovation—not in structure, but in strategy.
Cambrian explosion had crowded seas with many large, nutrient-rich filter feeders (e.g.,
brachiopods, early arthropods). Competition for food was fierce. Some organisms found it easier
to tap into others’ success than compete directly.

Parasites flipped the script: they latched onto or invaded other organisms to siphon
resources, rather than find or fight for them.
They evolved hooks and adhesive tubes to attach themselves to the host, modified their mouth
part to pierce and suck from the host. Simplified body parts and gave up complex sensory
organs. They had high rates of reproduction, laying larvae on hosts, since the survival rates
were low when born. But of their innovations towards a lazy lifestyle, the most cunning one, was
to start generating proteins just like their hosts, inorder to go undetected by the host’s immune
system.

This wasn’t the first time that a species developed parasitic properties too. It had happened
many times before, in prokaryotes, protists, fungi, and would happen again, even in plants.

Parasitism is a strategy that has evolved around 200 times across all species, whenever
resource was scarce, competition high & had stable host species.

So why are we especially talking about the arthropod parasites? Because these guys are still
vampiring on us and have been hugely successful. You might know them too - lice, fleas, ticks,
mosquitoes, some flies . While most other parasitic species live within host organisms, these
guys live outside the host. They do not only have parasitic property , but carry the genes too ,
infecting or rather, converting, other species too. Some of these parasites even have developed
anesthetics so hosts won’t even know when they bite & suck their nutrients. In sheer diversity
and ecological range, arthropods out-parasite nearly all other animal groups.
Well, though so much for a lazy lifestyle, they still innovated much.

## SCI Cards: scientists for this topic (founder's card game; strongest underdog stories first)
- **Mary Anning** (21 May 1799–9 Mar 1847, English; Underdog, Self-Taught, Invisible Giant, Foundational): Discovered the first complete Ichthyosaur skeleton (age 12), first complete Plesiosaur, first British Pterodactylus. Her fossil finds forced scientists to accept extinction as real and challenged the biblical timeline of Earth. *Whisper:* The Earth is old beyond imagination, and it is littered with the bones of creatures that no longer exist. Extinction is not failure — it is the normal fate of species. *Story:* Poor, working-class, and female in early 1800s England — three strikes against being taken seriously in science. She sold fossils from Lyme Regis cliffs to support her family. Male scientists bought her finds, published about them under their own names, and didn't credit her. She couldn't join the Geological Society (women were banned). She was struck by lightning as an infant and survived.
- **Beatrice Tinsley** (1941-01-27–1981-03-23, New Zealand-American; Ahead of Time, World Changer, Foundational, Underdog): Revolutionized how we understand galaxy evolution — proved that galaxies change dramatically over their lifetimes, which means using distant galaxies to measure the universe requires accounting for this evolution. Her work directly influenced the discovery that the universe's expansion is accelerating (1998 Nobel Prize work). Changed cosmology from static snapshots to dynamic evolution. *Whisper:* Galaxies are not fixed jewels — they grow, change, age, and die. Looking at a distant galaxy is looking at a younger version of itself. You cannot measure the universe without understanding that everything in it is evolving. *Story:* Tinsley's PhD thesis overturned decades of cosmological assumptions. Astronomers had been treating galaxies as unchanging 'standard candles' — Tinsley proved they evolve dramatically, meaning distance measurements based on galaxy brightness were wrong. Her work was initially dismissed (her PhD advisor barely supported her). She left a stifling marriage and New Zealand, got a position at Yale, and produced foundational work at extraordinary speed — she knew she was dying. She published 100+ papers before melanoma killed her at 40. The 1998 discovery of dark energy (accelerating expansion) relied on corrections to galaxy evolution that Tinsley had pioneered.
- **Jane Goodall** (1934-04-03–Living, English; World Changer, Heretic, Foundational): Proved that chimpanzees make and use tools — overturning the definition of humans as the only tool-using species. Discovered that chimps hunt, eat meat, have complex social hierarchies, wage wars, show empathy, and mourn their dead. Her 65+ years of continuous research at Gombe is the longest-running animal study in history. *Whisper:* The line between humans and animals is thinner than we thought. Chimpanzees make tools, grieve, wage war, and show compassion. We are not as special as we believed — and that's not diminishing, it's connecting. *Story:* Sent to Tanzania in 1960 by Louis Leakey with no degree and no formal training — just a notebook and binoculars. Within months she observed chimps making and using tools (stripping leaves from twigs to fish for termites). When she told Leakey, he said: 'Now we must redefine tool, redefine man, or accept chimpanzees as human.' The scientific establishment attacked her for naming her subjects instead of numbering them and for attributing emotions to animals. She was right, they were wrong. Over 65 years, her Gombe research revealed a mirror — showing us that much of what we call 'human' exists in our closest relatives.
- **Lynn Margulis** (1938-03-05–2011-11-22, American; Heretic, World Changer, Foundational, Controversial, Ahead of Time): Endosymbiotic Theory — proposed that mitochondria (cell powerhouses) and chloroplasts (photosynthesis units) were once free-living bacteria that were swallowed by larger cells and became permanent partners. This means every cell in your body is a collaboration between different life forms. Her paper was rejected 15 times before publication. *Whisper:* You are not one organism — you are a collaboration. Every cell in your body contains the descendants of ancient bacteria that merged with your ancestors billions of years ago. Life evolves through cooperation, not just competition. *Story:* Her 1967 paper was rejected by 15 journals before the Journal of Theoretical Biology published it. The idea that organelles were once independent bacteria was considered absurd. She was mocked, dismissed, and called a radical. DNA evidence eventually proved her right — mitochondria DO have their own DNA, separate from the cell's nucleus, confirming their bacterial origin. She challenged the Neo-Darwinian focus on competition, arguing that symbiosis (cooperation between species) is a primary driver of evolution.
- **Charles Darwin** (12 Feb 1809–19 Apr 1882, English; World Changer, Heretic, Foundational, Controversial): Theory of Evolution by Natural Selection — species evolve over time through variation, inheritance, and differential survival. Published 'On the Origin of Species' (1859). Also: theory of coral reef formation, extensive work on barnacles and earthworms. *Whisper:* You are not separate from nature — you are nature. Every living thing on Earth is your relative. Complexity doesn't require a designer; it emerges from simple rules repeated over deep time. *Story:* Studied to be a clergyman before joining the Beagle voyage at 22. Spent 20 years sitting on his theory, terrified of the backlash. Only published when Alfred Russel Wallace independently arrived at the same idea. Was mocked, attacked by the Church, and caricatured as an ape. Suffered chronic illness for decades (possibly Chagas disease from a bug bite in South America).
- **J. B. S. Haldane** (5 Nov 1892–1964-12-01, British-Indian (became Indian citizen); World Changer, Foundational, Controversial, Heretic): Co-founded population genetics — the mathematical framework for understanding how evolution works at the gene level. - **Frances Arnold** (1956-07-25–Living, American; World Changer, Applied, Foundational): Invented directed evolution of enzymes — using artificial selection (like dog breeding, but for proteins) to create enzymes that don't exist in nature. These engineered enzymes are now used to make biofuels, pharmaceuticals, and laundry detergents. She proved you don't need to understand a protein's design to improve it — just evolve it. *Whisper:* You don't need to understand how evolution works to use it. Just randomly mutate, test, and select — the same process that created all life on Earth can create custom enzymes in a laboratory. *Story:* Arnold's insight was simple and profound: if nature evolved enzymes over billions of years, why not speed up the process in a lab? She randomly mutated enzyme genes, tested the results, kept the best performers, and repeated. Within a few generations, she had enzymes that performed reactions nature never attempted. The chemical industry initially dismissed her: 'Why would you evolve something when you could design it rationally?' Because design requires understanding — and enzyme behavior is too complex to predict. Evolution doesn't need to understand anything; it just needs selection pressure. She was proved spectacularly right.
- **Sunetra Gupta** (1965-03-15–Living, Indian-British (Calcutta); Controversial, Foundational, Ahead of Time): Developed mathematical models showing how the structure of pathogen populations (antigenic diversity) determines epidemic patterns. Her work on influenza evolution explains why we need a new flu vaccine every year. Became publicly controversial during COVID-19 as a co-author of the Great Barrington Declaration. *Whisper:* Viruses don't just spread — they evolve strategically, changing their surface proteins to escape immunity. Understanding their evolutionary game is the key to predicting epidemics. *Story:* Gupta's mathematical models of infectious disease showed that the diversity of strains in a pathogen population — not just the total number of infections — determines epidemic dynamics. Her influenza work explained why flu keeps coming back: the virus diversifies its surface proteins to evade existing immunity. During COVID-19, she co-authored the Great Barrington Declaration advocating focused protection of the vulnerable rather than broad lockdowns — making her one of the most controversial scientists during the pandemic. She is also an award-winning novelist, writing in both English and Bengali.
