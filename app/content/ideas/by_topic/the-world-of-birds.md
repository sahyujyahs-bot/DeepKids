## Evolution picture book: introduction
"The History of Evolution of Life" Coloring Book

This coloring book is a carefully researched journey into the fascinating story of life on Earth, based on the best scientific knowledge we have today.
However, there are a few important points to keep in mind as we explore:

1. Evolution is an ongoing discovery.
Evolution is not an exact science; it is a well-supported framework based on evidence we’ve uncovered so far. Our understanding may change as new discoveries are made, and some current beliefs could be revised in the future.

2. Some mysteries of evolution remain unsolved. Even solved mysteries are not set in stone.
No step in evolution is fully understood, and theycontinue to remain subjects of debate, even among scientists. For instance, it's widely believed that life began with RNA molecules, precursors to DNA, but this is not universally agreed upon, and definitive proof is still elusive. Did living cells ccome first or the undead virus come first? debated. We believe Sponges were the first ever animal. But there is also a group that debates comb jellies came first. We don't know definitive answers to any question in evolution yet. About every step of evolvution of life that we have pieced together so far are still heavily debated in the scientific community. So consume the information with an open mind, and attempt to delve further into the questions by reading different perspectives.

3. Evolution is not a straight line.
Contrary to popular belief, evolution is not a simple progression from unicellular organisms to mammals and humans. It is a branching, ever-changing tree where every species, including the earliest unicellular prokaryotes, continues to evolve in its own unique way. We are finding new evidences everyday, that refute earlier understanding of the different aspects of evolution.

With these points in mind, let’s dive into the incredible history of life and explore the wonders of evolution together!

- **Margaret Hamilton** (1936-08-17–Living, American; World Changer, Foundational, Invisible Giant, Underdog): Wrote the onboard flight software for the Apollo 11 Moon landing. Her code's error-handling saved the mission during landing — when the computer overloaded 3 minutes before touchdown, her priority-interrupt system kept the critical landing program running. Coined the term 'software engineering.' Her code was hand-written and stacked taller than she was. *Whisper:* The Moon landing was saved by software, not hardware. A woman's code, written to handle errors that 'could never happen,' is the reason Armstrong's boot touched the lunar surface. *Story:* Hamilton led the team at MIT that wrote ALL the onboard software for the Apollo missions. During Apollo 11's landing, the computer threw error alarms (1202, 1203) — it was overloaded. Mission Control nearly aborted. But Hamilton's software had been designed to prioritize: it dumped non-essential tasks and kept the landing program running. Armstrong landed with 25 seconds of fuel left. Without her error-priority architecture, Apollo 11 would have been aborted on the brink of the Moon. The famous photo of her standing next to the printed code — a stack as tall as she is — shows the scale of what she built.
- **Valentina Tereshkova** (1937-03-06–Living, Russian; World Changer, Underdog, Foundational): First woman in space (Vostok 6, 16 June 1963) — 20 years before the first American woman (Sally Ride, 1983). Orbited Earth 48 times in 70 hours. Her flight proved that women could withstand the physical demands of spaceflight — a fact that was questioned and denied by male-dominated space agencies for decades after. *Whisper:* A textile factory worker became the first human woman to orbit the Earth — two decades before America sent a woman to space. She proved that space doesn't care about your gender. *Story:* A factory worker and amateur parachutist from Yaroslavl, Tereshkova was selected from over 400 applicants for the Soviet space program. On June 16, 1963, she launched aboard Vostok 6 and spent nearly three days in space — more time than all American astronauts combined at that point. Her callsign was Chaika (Seagull). Despite nausea and discomfort, she completed 48 orbits and landed safely. NASA wouldn't send a woman to space for another 20 years. The Soviets, despite their propaganda use of her flight, also didn't send another woman for 19 years. She later became a politician and engineer, and at age 76 volunteered for a one-way trip to Mars.
