## SCI Cards: scientists for this topic (founder's card game; strongest underdog stories first)
- **Jagadish Chandra Bose** (30 Nov 1858–1937-11-23, Indian (Bengali); IP Theft Victim, Ahead of Time, Invisible Giant, Underdog): Pioneered radio wave research (demonstrated wireless signaling before Marconi). Invented the crescograph (measuring plant growth). Showed that plants respond to stimuli — electrical signals in plants resemble those in animal tissue. First to use semiconductor junctions to detect radio waves. *Whisper:* Life does not begin where we draw the line. Plants feel, respond, and communicate — the boundary between 'alive' and 'just matter' is a human invention. *Story:* Refused to patent his radio inventions, believing knowledge should be free — while Marconi patented similar work and got the Nobel Prize. Bose demonstrated radio transmission in 1895, two years before Marconi's famous demonstration, but in Calcutta, far from European recognition. He then turned to biology and showed plants have electrical responses to injury — an idea ridiculed then, now confirmed by modern plant neurobiology.
- **Rita Levi-Montalcini** (1909-04-22–2012-12-30, Italian-American; Underdog, World Changer, Foundational, Heretic): Discovered Nerve Growth Factor (NGF) — the first growth factor ever identified, proving that cells produce chemical signals that guide how nerves grow and connect. This overturned the belief that nerve connections are predetermined and fixed. Her discovery founded the entire field of growth factor biology. *Whisper:* Nerves don't just grow randomly — they follow chemical breadcrumb trails. A molecule called NGF guides nerve cells to their destinations, and a woman discovered it in a bedroom laboratory during a fascist regime. *Story:* When Mussolini's 1938 racial laws barred Jews from universities, Levi-Montalcini built a laboratory in her bedroom. She dissected chicken embryos with watchmaker's tools and sewing needles, studying nerve growth by candlelight during Allied bombing raids. After the war, she moved to the US and discovered NGF — a molecule that tells nerve cells where to grow. The discovery took years to be accepted (she was told it was an artifact). She lived to 103, working until the end, and was made a Senator for Life by Italy. She said: 'I can say my mental capacity is greater now than when I was 20.'
- **A. P. J. Abdul Kalam** (1931-10-15–2015-07-27, Indian (Rameswaram, Tamil Nadu); Underdog, World Changer, Applied, Self-Taught): Led India's development of the SLV-III satellite launch vehicle and the Agni and Prithvi ballistic missiles. Architect of India's integrated guided missile development program. Later served as President of India (2002-2007). His work transformed India from a technology-dependent nation to a missile-capable space power. *Whisper:* Technology is not just for the privileged — a newspaper boy from a temple town can build rockets. Dreams are not about where you start; they're about where you point. *Story:* Born to a boat owner in Rameswaram, Kalam sold newspapers as a boy to support his education. He studied aerospace engineering, joined DRDO, then ISRO. He put India's first satellite (Rohini) into orbit. He then led the missile program — Agni, Prithvi, Akash, Trishul, Nag. After Pokhran-II nuclear tests, he became a national hero. Elected President in 2002, he remained approachable — famously asking children to dream. He died doing what he loved: giving a lecture to students at IIM Shillong. His last words were reportedly to the students present.
- **Radia Perlman** (1951-01-01–Living, American; World Changer, Foundational, Invisible Giant): Invented the Spanning Tree Protocol (STP) — the algorithm that makes large-scale network communication possible by preventing infinite loops in network data. Without STP, the internet as we know it could not function. Also contributed to network security protocols (TRILL). Called 'the Mother of the Internet.' *Whisper:* Networks naturally create loops — data going in circles forever, crashing everything. One algorithm, written in a day, solved this problem and made the internet possible. *Story:* Perlman was asked to solve the bridging loop problem at Digital Equipment Corporation. She solved it in a day by inventing the Spanning Tree Protocol — an algorithm that lets network switches automatically find loop-free paths. She also wrote a poem explaining it: 'I think that I shall never see / a graph more lovely than a tree...' STP became an industry standard. Every large network on Earth uses it. She dislikes the title 'Mother of the Internet,' noting that the internet is the work of thousands, but her STP is genuinely one of the foundational protocols that make it all work.
- **Tholkappiyar** (c. 300 BCE (estimated)–Unknown, Indian (Tamil Nadu); Foundational, Ahead of Time, Invisible Giant, World Changer): Authored Tolkāppiyam — one of the oldest extant works on linguistics and grammar in any language. Systematically classified Tamil phonetics, morphology, syntax, and poetics. Developed a complete theory of semantics and pragmatics. Classified human emotions into primary and secondary categories — prefiguring modern psychology's emotion classification by millennia. *Whisper:* Language has structure. It follows rules as precise as mathematics — and the first person to systematically prove this did it not for Greek or Latin, but for Tamil, over 2,000 years ago. *Story:* In the Sangam age, when Tamil literature was flourishing in southern India, Tholkappiyar wrote the first known systematic grammar of any Dravidian language. But Tolkāppiyam is far more than a grammar — it classifies human emotional states (meypāṭu), describes the relationship between land, climate, and human behavior (tiṇai), and develops a complete theory of meaning in communication. This is proto-linguistics, proto-psychology, and proto-ecology in a single text. Western linguistics credits Pāṇini (Sanskrit) as the first grammarian; Tholkappiyar's work in a completely different language tradition is equally systematic and arguably broader in scope.
- **Yamuna Krishnan** (1974.0–Living, Indian (works in USA); Ahead of Time, Foundational, Applied): Pioneered the use of DNA as a building material for nanoscale devices — creating DNA-based sensors that can measure pH, chloride, and other chemical signals INSIDE living cells in real time. Her 'I-switches' are DNA machines that change shape in response to their chemical environment. *Whisper:* DNA is not just a storage medium for genetic information — it's a building material. You can fold it into machines, sensors, and devices smaller than anything humans can manufacture. *Story:* Krishnan realized that DNA's ability to fold into precise shapes could be exploited to build nanoscale devices. She created 'I-switches' — tiny DNA machines that change shape when the pH around them changes, lighting up to report what's happening inside a living cell. Before her work, measuring chemical conditions inside individual cells was nearly impossible. Now her DNA sensors can map the pH of individual compartments inside a single cell. She moved from NCBS Bangalore to the University of Chicago, where she continues building increasingly sophisticated DNA devices.
