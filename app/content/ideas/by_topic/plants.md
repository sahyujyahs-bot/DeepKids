## Concept activities: Photosynthesis & vision (biophysics of light)
- **about-the-concept:** Plants capture light energy to make food; eyes convert light to electrical signals for vision.
- **principles-within-the-concept:** Chlorophyll absorbs photons; light-dependent & dark reactions; photoreceptor cells in retina.
- **philosophy-underlying-the-concepts:** Light is life’s bridge—turning energy into food and perception; the same photons feed plants and let us see them.
- **experiments:** Place leaves in sunlight, then test starch with iodine to show photosynthesis; build a pinhole camera to mimic eye optics; use prism to separate colours and compare plant growth under different hues.
- **activities-around-the-house:** Grow a potted plant in varying light and record growth; make a simple camera obscura to explore image formation; observe how eyes adapt when lights go off.
- **activities-outside-the-house-in-nature:** Take a nature walk noting plant colours at different times of day; visit a botanical garden; watch nocturnal animals’ eye shine.
- **dance:** Dance where one group “sunbeams” feeds another group “plants”; group movement shifting from dark to light.
- **drama:** Skit of a photon travelling from Sun to plant to human eye; drama of plants and humans thanking light; mime of eye capturing an image.
- **painting-drawing:** Paint a double portrait: plant leaf cross-section and human eye; draw a photon’s journey; create a chlorophyll-green colour study.
- **music:** Compose music that starts as a single bright note and blooms into complex harmonies like photosynthesis; create a melody that fades like night vision.
- **yoga-breathing:** Sun-gazing at dawn with eyes closed for gentle light meditation; slow breathing imagining light entering every cell; alternate nostril breathing visualising green growth.
- **physical_games:** “Photon relay”: players pass a “light baton” from Sun to plant to human; hide-and-seek in varying light levels to feel vision adaptation.
- **games-of-any-kind:** Board game where players gather photons to “feed” plants and “see” objects; quiz on light’s role in food and sight; card-matching parts of eye and chloroplast.
- **reading:** Planting the Sun (kids’ photosynthesis book); simple texts on how eyes work; biology picture books on light and vision.
- **writing-observations-stories-poems-answers:** Poem of a photon feeding a leaf and an eye; story of a Sunbeam’s journey; journal of colours noticed in a single day.
- **more:** Build a solar dehydrator to show sunlight energy; photograph plants in infrared filter to reveal hidden patterns; make chlorophyll prints on paper.
- **other:** Visit a greenhouse or science museum; attend a planetarium show about the Sun; watch a documentary on plant–light interactions.

### The First Fungi
Quickly following the red algae, the Fungi evolved from eukaryotes with only mitochondria.

The parent eukaryote with mitochondria had innovated food & primitive internal digestion. But its
first child, the fungi, did not opt for that route. Instead, they chose the more historic route of
external digestion by secreting enzymes & breakdown molecules outside the body & absorbing
nutrients.
But why? Because their aspirations were greater than their parents. Internal digestion means a
defined body shape that can house the gut. And digestion would have to happen per meal.

Instead, they developed a whole new way to live & grow - A fungi will start extending on one
side like a tip, a tail, only adding some cell wall. And slowly the tip grows and the fungo mother
will divide her nucleus and give one to the hyphae. Now the hyphae has its own DNA, but
remains forever attached to the mother. This continues. New hyphaes get added like chains,
each having its own nucleus, but all hyphaes are still attached to the mother. They could cover
hundreds of acres this way.

They also highways and transporter proteins to carry the nutrient absorbed in the hypahes to
other older interior cells of fungi.
For internal communication between the cells, they used the same well established chemical
pathways, ion fluctuations and electrical impulses. The same fundamentals that happen within
neurons - tunnels that animals built for internal communication.

When the resources get scarce, the chemical signals activate the genes for reproduction & the
hyphaes pause focussing on growth and focus on reproduction , making spores - tiny cells
packed with their dna & eject into the world, for them to find homes elsewhere.

They, like the aerobic bacteria, and eukaryotes with mitochondria, needed Oxygen to produce

Fungi - the scavengers with decentralized intelligence , covering acres, responding subjectively

This coloring book is a carefully researched journey into the fascinating story of life on Earth, based on the best scientific knowledge we have today.
However, there are a few important points to keep in mind as we explore:

1. Evolution is an ongoing discovery.
Evolution is not an exact science; it is a well-supported framework based on evidence we’ve uncovered so far. Our understanding may change as new discoveries are made, and some current beliefs could be revised in the future.

2. Some mysteries of evolution remain unsolved. Even solved mysteries are not set in stone.
No step in evolution is fully understood, and theycontinue to remain subjects of debate, even among scientists. For instance, it's widely believed that life began with RNA molecules, precursors to DNA, but this is not universally agreed upon, and definitive proof is still elusive. 3. Evolution is not a straight line.
Contrary to popular belief, evolution is not a simple progression from unicellular organisms to mammals and humans. It is a branching, ever-changing tree where every species, including the earliest unicellular prokaryotes, continues to evolve in its own unique way. We are finding new evidences everyday, that refute earlier understanding of the different aspects of evolution.
