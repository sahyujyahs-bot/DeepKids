# Puzzles: find the odd one, find the way, say why
`math.logic.puzzles.L1`

**Summary.** Four shapes sit in a row. Which one does not belong? You pick one. Someone else picks another. Can you both be right? You will test this with four cards, walk an ant through mazes, find what changed in a row and build a maze of your own. You will learn to say the reason for your answer, to go back to the last fork when a path stops, and to look in order. You will also find out which puzzles have one answer and which have many.

**Intro.** *Which one does not belong?* There are two good answers. The square does not belong, because the other three are circles. The blue circle does not belong, because the other three are red. Which one did you pick? Both are right, as long as you can say why. In this unit you will find out when a puzzle has one answer, when it has many, and why the reason matters as much as the answer.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can find the odd one out and say my reason.
1. I can find a second rule that makes a different one the odd one.
2. I can find the way through a maze and show its forks and dead ends.
3. I can go back to the last fork when I am stuck, or start from the end.
4. I can find what changed or where a piece goes by looking in order.
5. I can make a puzzle for someone and check their answer by its reason.

## Terms
- **Puzzle**: A question you cannot answer straight away. You have to look, try and think. *e.g.* Which lid fits this dabba? You find out by trying.
- **Rule**: The one thing you choose to look at when you compare things, such as colour, shape or size. *e.g.* With the rule 'colour', a blue circle is odd among red shapes.
- **Reason**: What you say after 'because'. It tells others why your answer is right, so that they can check it. *e.g.* The tumbler is the odd one, because the other three are food.
- **Odd one out**: The one thing in a group that does not fit a rule the others fit. *e.g.* Crow, parrot, pigeon, cat. The cat is the odd one out, because the others are birds.
- **Same and different**: Two things are the same in one way if they match in that way. If they do not match, they are different in that way. *e.g.* A red circle and a red square are the same in colour and different in shape.
- **Maze**: A puzzle made of paths and walls. You must find a way from the start to the end. *e.g.* The ant must walk on the white squares to reach the banana.
- **Path**: The part of a maze you can walk on. *e.g.* In our mazes the white squares are the path and the black squares are walls.
- **Fork**: A place in a maze where the path splits, and you must choose which way to go. *e.g.* At the fork the ant can go down or go right.
- **Dead end**: A place where the path stops. You can only turn round. *e.g.* The ant walked down and met a wall on three sides. It was a dead end.
- **Labyrinth**: A winding path with no forks. There is only one way to go. Many people also use the word for any maze. *e.g.* A path that winds round and round to the middle, with nothing to choose.

## Explanations
### What a puzzle is · *defined*
**Definition:** A puzzle is a question you cannot answer straight away. You have to look, try and think.

A puzzle has a goal and it has rules. In a maze, the goal is to reach the end. The rule is that you stay on the path. In odd one out, the goal is to find the one that does not fit. Solving a puzzle is not like remembering a fact. You try something, see what happens and try again.
- e.g. Which lid fits this dabba?
- e.g. Which way does the ant go to reach the banana?
- e.g. What changed in the row while your eyes were shut?
### Odd one out: find what three share · *defined*
**Definition:** The odd one out is the one thing in a group that does not fit a rule the others fit.

Here is a worked example. The things are an idli, a dosa, a vada and a tumbler. Step 1: look for something that three of them share. The idli, the dosa and the vada are all food. Step 2: check the fourth thing. A tumbler is not food. Step 3: say the answer with its reason. 'The tumbler is the odd one, because the other three are food.' The reason is part of the answer.
- e.g. Crow, parrot, pigeon, cat: the cat, because the others are birds.
- e.g. Bus, auto, cycle, boat: the boat, because the others have wheels.
- e.g. Three red circles and a red triangle: the triangle, because the others are circles.
### One set, more than one odd one · *defined*
**Definition:** The same things can have different odd ones. It depends on the rule you choose.

Look at the row: a red circle, a red square, a blue circle and a red circle. Choose the rule 'shape'. Three are circles, so the square is the odd one. Now choose the rule 'colour'. Three are red, so the blue circle is the odd one. Both answers are right, because both reasons are true. An answer with a false reason is wrong. 'The square is odd because it is blue' is wrong, because the square is red.
- e.g. Banana, mango, lemon, carrot: the carrot grows under the ground. The lemon is the only sour one.
- e.g. Crow, bus, mango, Moon: each one can be the odd one, with the right rule.
### A maze: forks and dead ends · *defined*
**Definition:** A maze is a puzzle made of paths and walls. Some paths lead on. Some stop at a wall.

In the maze here, the ant walks on the white squares. The ant moves up, down, left or right, one square at a time. The ant does not jump over black squares. A fork is a place where the path splits and you must choose. A dead end is a place where the path stops. This maze has one fork and one dead end. Find them with your finger.
- e.g. A fork: at one square the ant can go down or go right.
- e.g. A dead end: a white square with black squares on three sides.
### Stuck? Go back to the last fork · *defined*
**Definition:** When a path stops, go back to the last place where you had a choice. Then take a way you have not tried.

Here is a worked example. Step 1: the ant walks until the fork. Step 2: the ant tries one way, down. Step 3: the path stops. It is a dead end, marked with a cross here. Step 4: the ant goes back to the fork, not all the way to the start. Step 5: the ant takes the other way. The dead end was not a waste, because now the ant knows one way that does not work.
- e.g. Two ways at a fork, and one is a dead end: the other must be the way.
- e.g. Looking for a lost chappal: each room you have checked is one room less to search.
### Start from the end · *defined*
**Definition:** You can solve a maze backwards. Start at the end and walk to the start.

Here is a worked example. The ant can go in at door A, door C or door E. Only one door leads to the banana. You could try each door in turn. Or you can start at the banana and walk backwards. From the banana there is only one path, so you cannot go wrong. It brings you out at door A. In other mazes, going backwards meets the same forks as going forwards, so try both ends.
- e.g. Three doors and one banana: start at the banana and see which door you come out of.
- e.g. Looking for a lost pencil: think back from the place where you last used it.
### Picture puzzles: look in order · *defined*
**Definition:** In a picture puzzle you find what changed, what is missing or where a piece fits. The trick is to look in order, not all at once.

Here is a worked example. Two rows of shapes look the same, but one shape has changed. Step 1: put a finger on place 1 in both rows and compare. Step 2: move to place 2, then place 3, and so on. Step 3: stop where they do not match. Here it is place 4. The blue square has turned green. Looking at everything at once is quick, but it misses small changes.
- e.g. A cut-up picture: find the four corner pieces first, then the edges.
- e.g. A missing thing on a plate: say each thing in order and listen for the gap.
### A labyrinth: one path and no choices · *defined*
**Definition:** A labyrinth is a winding path with no forks. A maze has forks. In everyday talk, many people use both words for the same thing.

In a labyrinth you cannot get lost. There is only one way, and it winds on to the end. People have drawn labyrinths for a very long time. Coins from the island of Crete, more than 2,000 years old, show one. In India a winding path like this is often called a chakravyuha. That is the name of a battle formation in the Mahabharata. A stone carving at the Hoysaleswara temple in Halebidu, Karnataka, shows the young warrior Abhimanyu entering one. The temple is about 900 years old.
- e.g. The path here: 16 steps, and never a choice.
- e.g. A queue that winds back and forth between ropes at a railway station.
### The odd ones out in the night sky · *defined*
**Definition:** Long ago, people found the planets by noticing that a few lights in the sky did not behave like the rest.

On a clear, dark night you can see thousands of stars. Night after night, the stars keep the same places next to each other. But people long ago noticed five lights that did not. Week by week, these five moved among the stars. They were the odd ones out. The Greeks called them planetes, which means wanderers, and our word planet comes from that. The five are Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. They are not stars but worlds that go round the Sun, as our Earth does.
- e.g. A doctor looks for the one sign in your body that is not as usual.
- e.g. A mechanic listens for the one sound in an engine that does not fit.
- e.g. A farmer spots the one plant in a row whose leaves have turned yellow.
### Other living beings solve puzzles too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings find their way through mazes and tell what is the same from what is different.

A slime mould is a living being who looks like a patch of yellow jelly. Nobody has found a brain in a slime mould. In 2000, three scientists put a slime mould into a small maze, with food at both ends. At first the slime mould spread into every path, and then pulled back from the dead ends. In the end their body was one thin tube along the shortest way between the two meals. Honeybees can learn the rules 'same' and 'different'. In 2001, scientists trained bees to pick the colour that matched the one at the door of a small maze. The bees then kept to the rule with patterns they had never seen.
- e.g. A slime mould: every path first, then only the short way.
- e.g. A honeybee: blue at the door, so choose blue inside.
- e.g. Ants: a new line round a twig laid across their way.
- **One view:** The slime mould and the bees are solving a puzzle. They try, they rule out what does not work, and they keep what does. That is the same thinking you used in the maze.
- **Some scientists:** They say the slime mould is only growing towards food and shrinking where there is none. They would not call that solving a puzzle.

## Mostly agreed
- The same four things can have more than one odd one out. It depends on the rule you choose. *How we know:* In the Odd One Out Test, four cards gave four different odd ones: one by its dot, one by its shape, one by its colour and one by its size.
- If you mark the paths you have tried and always go back to the last fork, you can get through a maze. *How we know:* The French mathematician Édouard Lucas printed a marking rule like this in 1882. It came from an engineer named Trémaux. It works for every maze with clear paths, though not always by the shortest way.
- A slime mould can find the short way through a small maze. *How we know:* In 2000, Toshiyuki Nakagaki, Hiroyasu Yamada and Ágota Tóth put a slime mould in a maze with food at both ends. The slime mould left the dead ends and kept a tube along the shortest path.
- Honeybees can learn the rules 'same' and 'different'. *How we know:* In 2001, Martin Giurfa and four other scientists trained bees to choose the colour that matched a sample. The bees then did the same with stripe patterns they had never seen.

## How we found out
**Charles Pierre Trémaux, a telegraph engineer, and Édouard Lucas, who printed his rule, 1882.** People have built mazes of hedges and walls for hundreds of years. Walkers passed on one old tip: keep one hand on the wall and keep walking. In many mazes the tip works. But it fails when the place you want stands inside its own block of walls, not joined to the outer wall. Then the walker goes round and round and never arrives. In the 1800s a French telegraph engineer named Trémaux found a rule that does not fail. Here it is in simple words. Make a mark on every path as you enter it and as you leave it. At a dead end, turn back. At a fork, take a path with no mark if there is one. Never go down a path that already has two marks. The mathematician Édouard Lucas printed the rule in a book of maths games in Paris in 1882. A walker who follows it gets through any maze with clear paths, though not always by the shortest way.  
*What people believed before:* Keep one hand on the wall and you will always reach the goal. This is wrong for a maze where the goal stands inside a separate block of walls. The walker circles the outside and never touches the walls round the goal.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Every puzzle has exactly one right answer.** (Many worksheets and exam books; *debated*)  
  For: In a maze, a path reaches the end or it does not. In a cut-up picture, each piece has one place. Nobody can choose a different answer.  
  Against: In odd one out, the same four things can give four answers, each with a true reason. Books pick the answer most people see first. That is a habit people agreed on, not a law.
- **Keep one hand on the wall and you will always get through a maze.** (A very old tip, passed from walker to walker; *rejected*)  
  For: In a maze where every wall is joined to the outer wall, the tip works. Your hand leads you past every dead end and out again.  
  Against: If the place you want stands inside its own block of walls, your hand never touches those walls. You walk round and round the outside. Marking the paths you have tried works where the hand tip fails.
- **Puzzles are only play. They are not real maths.** (Many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Puzzles are fun, and nobody needs to add or take away to walk through a maze.  
  Against: In 1736 the mathematician Leonhard Euler solved a town puzzle about crossing seven bridges. His answer began a new part of maths about paths and joins. Looking for a rule, trying, and giving a reason is what mathematicians do all day.

## Roots
We hunted for odd ones, walked through mazes and found changes. Now let us think about who decides the answer to a puzzle.

**Deep truth.** In the Odd One Out Test, four cards lay on the floor. With one rule the square was the odd one. With another rule the blue circle was. You found a rule for every card. So no card is odd by itself: a card is odd only under a rule, and a person chooses the rule.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think every puzzle has one right answer, kept at the back of a book. But in odd one out, the cards do not hold the answer. The answer comes with the rule, and you have to say the rule.  
*Evidence:* In the Odd One Out Test, the same four cards gave four different odd ones, one for each rule: the dot, the shape, the colour and the size.

- *who am i:* You are someone who can say 'because'. When you give your reason, other people can check your thinking.
- *what is life:* A slime mould finds the short way through a maze. A honeybee learns to pick the one that is the same. Living beings of many kinds work things out.
- *what is this world:* The world does not come sorted. The night sky did not say which lights were odd. People looked, chose what to notice and found the planets.

Four cards on the floor are just four cards. Then you choose one thing to look at, such as colour. Now the cards fall into groups: three alike and one odd. Choose another thing, and they fall a different way. The cards did not change. All the different groupings come from one set of cards and one looker who chooses.

- **Udana 6.4 (Tittha Sutta):** In this old Buddhist story, a king has an elephant shown to men who were born blind. One touches the ear and says an elephant is like a winnowing basket. Another touches the foot and says an elephant is like a post. Each is sure that the others are wrong, and they begin to fight. (Each man had a true reason, taken from the part he touched. In odd one out, each picker has a true reason too, taken from the one thing they chose to look at. The story adds a warning: your true reason does not make the other person wrong.)
- **Thirukkural 423:** Thiruvalluvar says that whatever you hear, and whoever says it, wisdom is to find what is true in it. (A puzzle answer is right because its reason is true. It does not matter whether a child or a grown-up gave it, or whether the book agrees.)
- Maths (settled): Honeybees can learn the rules 'same' and 'different' and use them on things they have never seen before (Giurfa and others, 2001). So telling what fits from what does not fit is not only a human skill.
- Maths (open): Is the odd one out there in the things, or only in the mind of the one who looks? Thinkers still argue about this.
- Other view, Many mathematicians and puzzle makers: They say a good puzzle has one answer, and nobody chooses it. In a maze the walls decide. A path reaches the end or it does not, whatever anyone thinks. Even in odd one out, once the rule is said, the answer is fixed. Nobody can choose which card is blue.
- Practice, Three breaths at a dead end (3 min): Think of a time you were stuck in a puzzle. Now sit still and put your hands in your lap. Breathe in slowly and breathe out slowly, three times. Do not try to solve anything. Now look at one thing in the room, such as a tumbler. Find three things about it that you never noticed before. Next time you are stuck, do the same. Stop, take three breaths and look again.
- Ask: You and Amma pick different odd ones, and both give true reasons. Who is right?
- Ask: Is a card odd when nobody is looking at it?
- Ask: In a maze the walls stay where they are. What is left for you to choose?
- Ask: Can a puzzle have no answer at all?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Can you make a set of four things with no odd one out at all? (hint: What if two are red and two are blue? What if all four are exactly alike?)
- *deep:* Two people pick different odd ones, and both give true reasons. Is one answer better? (hint: Is a reason about colour better than a reason about where the card is lying?)
- *deep:* Is a wrong turn in a maze a mistake? (hint: What do you know after the wrong turn that you did not know before?)
- *big:* Why do people make puzzles for each other, when the maker already knows the answer? (hint: What does the maker enjoy? What does the solver enjoy?)
- *big:* Does an ant know they are solving a puzzle? (hint: How could you find out? What would you watch for?)
- *big:* Is anything in the world odd in every way? (hint: Think of the strangest thing you know. What does it still share with other things?)

## Story: A Pinch at Every Door
*Kayal, Muthu, Valli akka and Paatti are made up. Chettinad, in Tamil Nadu, is a real region. Its old mansions really do have long rows of rooms round open courtyards. Rice flour is what many families use to draw the kolam at the door.*

Paatti's house in Kanadukathan had more doors than Kayal could count.

It was an old Chettinad house, built by Paatti's grandfather. There was a front hall with tall wooden pillars. Behind it was a courtyard open to the sky. Round the courtyard ran a long verandah, and off the verandah opened room after room after room. Behind that was a second courtyard, with still more rooms. Most of them were kept shut. All the doors were the same dark wood, with the same brass rings.

Kayal was seven. Her cousin Muthu was five, and he was very good at hiding.

That morning the three cousins played hide and seek. Valli akka, who was eleven, found Kayal behind the grain bin in no time. Nobody found Muthu.

Then Paatti called them for lunch. Kayal came. Valli akka came. Muthu did not come.

"Muthu! The game is over! There is payasam!"

No answer. The house was very quiet.

"He has fallen asleep somewhere," said Paatti. "He does that. Go and find him, kannu, before his rice goes cold."

Kayal ran down the verandah. She pushed open a door. Sacks of rice. She pushed open the next one. Rolled-up mats. She ran across the courtyard and tried a door on the other side. Brass pots, stacked to the ceiling. She ran to the second courtyard, opened three doors there, and ran back.

She pushed open a door. Sacks of rice.

"I have been in here before," said Kayal.

She tried another. Brass pots, stacked to the ceiling.

"And here!"

She stood in the middle of the courtyard and turned round slowly. Every door looked like every other door. Which ones had she opened? Six? Ten? She did not know any more. She could run all afternoon like this, opening the same doors and missing the one that mattered.

Valli akka came out with a banana leaf in her hand. "Not found yet? I will look upstairs."

"Wait," said Kayal. She was looking at the front step. Beside it stood the little steel bowl of rice flour that Paatti used for the kolam every morning.

Kayal picked up the bowl.

She went to the first door on the verandah and opened it. Sacks of rice. No Muthu. She took a pinch of flour and dropped it on the doorstep, a small white heap on the dark stone.

"What are you doing?" asked Valli akka.

"Telling myself: not this one," said Kayal.

Next door. Mats. No Muthu. A pinch.

Next door. Old trunks. No Muthu. A pinch.

She did not run now. She went door by door, all the way round the first courtyard. Behind her, the white pinches made a dotted line. When she looked back, she could see at once which doors were finished.

"Eleven doors, and eleven times he was not there," said Valli akka. "That is a lot of wrong doors."

"They are not wrong," said Kayal. "Each one tells me where he is not. Look how few are left."

In the second courtyard there were nine doors. A pinch. A pinch. A pinch. Six were left. Then four. Then two.

Kayal stopped in front of the last two doors. Her heart was beating fast. If he was not behind the first, he had to be behind the second. There was nowhere else in the whole house.

She opened the first. Mattresses, piled high for the wedding guests who would come next month. On the top mattress, curled up like a kitten, with his thumb near his mouth, was Muthu. He was fast asleep.

"Found," whispered Kayal. She did not need the last door at all.

At lunch Muthu ate two helpings of payasam and was very pleased with himself. "Nobody found me for a hundred hours," he said.

Paatti came in from the verandah with her eyebrows up. "Who has been putting half a kolam at every door in my house?"

"That was me," said Kayal. "Every pinch is a place where Muthu was not."

Paatti looked at the long dotted line of white, all the way round both courtyards. Then she looked at Kayal.

"Leave them till evening," she said. "I want your grandfather to see how you looked for him."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kayal fails while she opens doors in any order, because she cannot tell the tried doors from the untried ones. She succeeds when she marks every door she has ruled out. Each 'wrong' door then becomes useful: it makes the search smaller. At the end she knows the answer before she opens the last door. This is how a maze is solved: mark the dead ends, and never try the same way twice.

- Ask: Why did Kayal keep opening the same doors at first?
- Ask: How did the pinches of rice flour help her?
- Ask: Valli akka said the doors were wrong doors. Kayal said they were not. Who do you agree with?
- Ask: When two doors were left, Kayal opened one and found Muthu. What if he had not been there?

## Games
### g1 · Odd One Out, Say Why (10 min)
- **rules:** You need the 24 cards, a dice and a small heap of beans for scoring.; Mix the cards. Lay 4 cards face up in a row. Call the places 1, 2, 3 and 4.; On your turn, roll the dice. If you roll 1, 2, 3 or 4, you must make the card in that place the odd one.; Say your reason like this: 'It is the only one that…'. The reason must be true for that card and for no other card in the row.; If you roll 5, choose any card in the row. If you roll 6, swap all four cards for new ones and roll again.; The other players check the reason. A true reason wins one bean. If you find no reason by a slow count of ten, you win nothing.; A reason used once for a row may not be used again for that row. When every player has had a turn, lay 4 new cards.; The first player with 5 beans wins.; Playing alone: lay 4 cards and find a reason for each one. Can you get 4 out of 4, three rows running?
- **Printable cards: 24 thing cards to cut out:** 🐦 crow; 🦜 parrot; 🐟 fish; 🐱 cat; 🐄 cow; 🐜 ant; 🛺 auto; 🚌 bus; 🚲 cycle; ⛵ boat; 🍌 banana; 🥭 mango; 🥥 coconut; ⚪ idli; 🫓 dosa; 🟡 laddu; 🥄 spoon; 🥛 tumbler; 🍽️ plate; 🍲 pot; ☀️ Sun; 🌙 Moon; ⭐ star; ⚽ ball
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 24 cards, or copy the words on slips of paper. Your child can draw each thing. 2. Mix the cards and lay 4 in a row. 3. Put the dice and a heap of beans nearby. 4. Read the rules aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Four cards lie in a row. The dice will tell you which card to make the odd one. You must give a true reason.  
  *Child mode:* Lay 4 cards in a row. The dice tells you which card must be the odd one.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one practice turn each. 2. Roll, point to the card and say the reason aloud. 3. Check together: is the reason true for this card and for no other?  
  *Parent says:* I rolled 3. The mango must be the odd one. It is the only one you can eat. Is that true of any other card?  
  *Child mode:* Roll the dice. Point to that card. Say: it is the only one that…
  - Note: The most common slip is a reason that is true but also true of another card. Ask: 'Is that true of any other card in the row?'
  - Check: The row is crow, bus, mango, Moon. You roll 2. Which reason makes the bus the odd one? → **It is the only one with wheels**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game. 2. Check every reason together. 3. When everyone has had a turn, lay 4 new cards.  
  *Parent says:* Your turn. Roll, and tell us why that card is the odd one. We will check your reason.  
  *Child mode:* Play the game. Give a true reason to win a bean. Check the other players' reasons.
  - Ask: Which card in this row was the hardest to make the odd one? Why? (good: The idli, because the dosa and the laddu are food too.; I had to look for something only that card has.)
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When a player has 5 beans, the game ends. 2. Ask which reason was the cleverest one of the game.  
  *Parent says:* Count your beans. Which reason did you like best today? Why?  
  *Child mode:* The first player with 5 beans wins. Which reason did you like best?
### g2 · Dice Switch (10 min)
- **rules:** Draw and colour the 12 shape cards on slips of paper. You also need a dice and beans for scoring.; One player is the maker. The maker lays 6 cards in a row and keeps the other 6 in a heap.; The finder looks at the row for a slow count of ten, then shuts their eyes.; The maker rolls the dice behind a hand. The number is the place to change. The maker swaps the card in that place for a card from the heap.; The finder opens their eyes and says which place changed.; The right place wins one bean. Saying what the old card was wins one more bean.; Swap jobs after every round. Play 6 rounds. The player with more beans wins.; A harder game: the maker rolls twice and changes two places. If both rolls are the same, only one place changes.
- **Printable cards: 12 shape cards to draw and cut out:** red circle; blue circle; yellow circle; red square; blue square; yellow square; red triangle; blue triangle; yellow triangle; red rectangle; blue rectangle; yellow rectangle
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the 12 shape cards on slips of paper and colour them. 2. Lay 6 cards in a row. Put the other 6 in a heap. 3. Count the places together from the left: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. 4. Read the rules aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a row of six cards. Let us count the places from the left. Soon one card will change. You must find which one.  
  *Child mode:* Lay 6 shape cards in a row. Count the places from the left. One card will change.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play a practice round with your child as the finder. 2. Tell your child to say the row aloud in order before shutting their eyes. 3. Roll the dice behind your hand and swap that card. 4. Your child finds the change.  
  *Parent says:* Say the row aloud from left to right. Now shut your eyes. Open them. Which place has changed? What was there before?  
  *Child mode:* Say the row aloud in order. Shut your eyes. Open them. Which place changed?
  - Note: Saying the row aloud in order gives your child something to check against. Children who only glance at the row tend to guess.
  - Check: Look at the two rows. Which place changed? → **Place 4**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game. Swap jobs after every round. 2. Lay a new row every two rounds. 3. Now and then, ask the finder how they found the change.  
  *Parent says:* Your turn to be the maker. Lay your row. I will shut my eyes. Roll the dice where I cannot see.  
  *Child mode:* Take turns to be the maker. Roll the dice in secret. Change the card in that place.
  - Ask: How did you find the change? (good: I said the row in my head and one card did not fit.; I checked each place from the left.; I remembered there was only one rectangle.)
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 6 rounds, count the beans. 2. Ask which change was the hardest to find, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Count your beans. Which change was the hardest to find? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your beans. Which change was the hardest to find?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Odd One Out Test: can every card be the odd one? (25 min)
- **question:** How many of four cards can be the odd one out, each with a true reason?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess: will everyone at home pick the same card? Then guess how many of the four cards can be the odd one: one, two or all four. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Everyone looks at the same four cards. Each person picks without hearing the others. A pick counts only if it comes with a reason, and the reason must be true for that card and for no other card.
- **record:** A table with one line for each card or thing, and the rule that makes it the odd one. Under the table, a grown-up writes what the child found, in the child's own words.
- **results table:** Set | The card or thing | It is the only one that… | Who found the reason
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tear a sheet of paper into four cards, each about as big as your palm. 2. Draw one shape on each card, as the table shows. 3. Lay the four cards in a row on the floor. 4. Say nothing about them yet.  
  *Parent says:* Here are four cards. Look at each one quietly for a moment.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up draws four cards. Lay them in a row. Look at each one.
  - Note: Make the big shapes fill the card. Make the small circle about as big as a coin. Colour the shapes in fully, so that red and blue are easy to tell apart.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child which card is the odd one out, and why. 2. Write the card and the reason in the table. 3. Ask whether everyone at home will pick the same card, and why. 4. Write that guess down too.  
  *Parent says:* Which card is the odd one out? Why? Will everyone at home pick the same card as you? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which card is the odd one out? Say why. Will everyone pick the same card? Guess, and say why.
  - Ask: Why is that card the odd one? (good: It is the only square.; It is the only blue one.; It is the only small one.; It is the only one with a dot.)
  - Note: Any of the four cards is a good answer if the reason is true. Do not say so yet. Just write down what your child says.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask one or two other people to pick the odd card, one at a time. 2. Each person must give a reason. 3. Pick a card yourself too, the one you really would pick. 4. Write every pick and reason in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now let us ask the others. Each person must say which card, and why. Listen to each reason. Is it true?  
  *Child mode:* Ask other people to pick the odd card. Each one must say why. Is each reason true?
  - Note: If only two of you are at home, your own pick is enough. You can also phone a grandparent and describe the cards. If everybody picks the same card, that is a real result too. Go on to the next step.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask how many of the four cards can be the odd one: one, two or all four. Ask why. 2. Write the guess. 3. Take the cards one at a time. For each card, your child hunts for a rule that makes it the odd one. 4. Write down each rule that is true for that card only.  
  *Parent says:* How many of these cards can be the odd one, each with a true reason? Guess first, and tell me why. Now let us test it. Start with card A. What is true about A and about no other card?  
  *Child mode:* How many cards can be the odd one? Guess first. Then find a reason for each card. The reason must be true for that card only.
  - Ask: What is true about this card and about no other card? (good: A is the only one with a dot.; B is the only square. B is the only one with corners.; C is the only blue one.; D is the only small one.)
  - Note: Give your child time. Card A is usually the last one found. If your child is stuck on A, hold A next to each other card in turn. Ask what A has that the other card lacks.
  - Check: A big red square, a big blue circle and a small red circle lie in a row. Which rule makes the small circle the odd one? → **size**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear the cards. Put out a steel spoon, a steel tumbler, a banana and a pencil. 2. Ask again how many of these can be the odd one. 3. Your child finds a reason for each thing. 4. Write the reasons in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now four real things. Can each of these be the odd one too? Find a reason for each.  
  *Child mode:* Now use a spoon, a tumbler, a banana and a pencil. Find a reason for each one to be odd.
  - Ask: What makes this thing the odd one? (good: The banana is the only one you can eat.; The pencil is the only one made of wood. It is the only one that writes.; The tumbler is the only one that holds water.; The spoon is the only one you eat with.)
  - Note: Any fruit works in place of the banana. Check each reason together. Is it true for this thing, and false for the other three?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put out three spoons that are exactly alike, and one tumbler. 2. Ask which is the odd one. 3. Then ask your child to find a reason for one of the spoons to be the odd one. 4. Talk about why this is so hard.  
  *Parent says:* Three spoons and one tumbler. Which is the odd one? Now try to make one of the spoons the odd one. Can you?  
  *Child mode:* Three spoons and one tumbler. Which is odd? Now try to make a spoon the odd one. Can you?
  - Ask: Why is it so hard to make a spoon the odd one? (good: The spoons are all the same.; Anything I say about one spoon is true of the other spoons too.)
  - Note: Some children say 'this spoon is the one on the left'. That is clever. Mix the spoons up and ask whether the same spoon is still the odd one. A good rule is about the thing itself, not about where it lies.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what your child found out. 2. Ask what they saw that makes them think so. 3. Write their words under the table. 4. Only then say it in one sentence: the rule decides which one is odd.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find out? What did you see that makes you think so? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found out. Say what you saw. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: How many of the four cards could be the odd one? (good: All four.; Every card, if I chose the right rule.; It depends on what you look at.)
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this: 'I thought only one card was odd. But I found a reason for every card. It depends on the rule. With three spoons exactly alike, only the tumbler could be odd.'

## Activities
### a1 · Ant and Banana: finding the way through a maze (15 min)
- **problem:** A path in a maze stops at a wall. What is the best thing to do next?
- **record:** Keep the paper maze. Draw a small cross on every dead end you met, and draw your way from the ant to the banana with a crayon.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the small maze on the screen. 2. Say the rules: walk on white squares only, one square at a time, up, down, left or right. 3. Your child traces the way to the banana with a finger. 4. Ask where the path split, and where it stopped.  
  *Parent says:* Help the ant reach the banana. Walk only on the white squares. No jumping over black squares, and no slanting steps.  
  *Child mode:* Help the ant reach the banana. Walk on white squares only. Go up, down, left or right.
  - Ask: Where did the path split into two? (good: Where I could go down or go right.; At the second square of the second row.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Copy the big maze on paper, 6 squares by 6 squares, and shade the black squares. 2. Your child guesses which way to go at the first fork, and says why. 3. Your child moves a rajma along the path. 4. At each dead end, your child leaves a chana there and goes back to the last fork.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the big maze. Which way will you try at the first fork? Why? When the path stops, leave a chana there. Then go back to the last fork and try another way.  
  *Child mode:* Move your rajma along the white squares. At a dead end, leave a chana. Go back to the last fork. Try another way.
  - Ask: You left a chana at a dead end. What does that chana tell you? (good: Not this way.; I have tried here already.; The way must be one of the others.)
  - Note: Make each square about two fingers wide. Copying takes about three minutes, and you can do it while your child traces the small maze. This maze has two forks and two dead ends. Your child may meet both, one or none.
  - Check: The ant's path stops at a wall. Where should the ant go back to? → **the last fork**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the maze with three doors. The ant may go in at door A, C or E. 2. Your child guesses which door leads to the banana, and says why. 3. Your child tests the guess with a finger. 4. Then your child starts at the banana and traces backwards to a door.  
  *Parent says:* The ant can go in at A, C or E. Which door do you think is right? Why? Test it. Now start at the banana and walk backwards. Where do you come out?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the right door: A, C or E. Test it. Then start at the banana. Walk backwards. Which door do you reach?
  - Ask: Which was quicker: trying the doors, or starting from the banana? Why? (good: From the banana. There was only one path.; From the doors I had to try more than one.)
  - Note: From the banana there is only one path, with no fork, so your child cannot go wrong. Door C is the right one. Starting from the end is quick here, but it does not help in every maze.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the last maze. 2. Your child solves it in any way they like. 3. Ask what your child found out about dead ends and about starting from the end. 4. Listen first. Then say it in one sentence.  
  *Parent says:* One more maze. Solve it any way you like. Then tell me: what do you do at a dead end? When does it help to start at the end?  
  *Child mode:* Solve the last maze your own way. Then say what you do at a dead end.
  - Ask: What do you do when the path stops? (good: Go back to the last fork.; Try a way I have not tried.; Remember that way is no good.)
  - Note: After your child has answered, you can say it: a dead end tells you one way that does not work, so you go back to the last fork and try another.
  - Check: In the last maze, which door leads the ant to the banana? → **Door E**
### a2 · The Cut-Up Picture: where does each piece go? (15 min)
- **problem:** A picture is in pieces. How do you work out where each piece goes without trying every place?
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the cutting.
- **record:** Keep the pieces in an envelope. On the envelope, write how many pieces there are and which pieces you placed first.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose a card with a clear picture. 2. Your child draws a thick dark line all round the edge of the picture. 3. Ask your child to name five things in the picture. 4. On the back, draw one line across and one line down, to make 4 equal parts.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a thick border round the picture. Now look hard. Tell me five things you can see in it.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a thick line round the edge of the picture. Name five things you see in it.
  - Note: The border matters. Later it shows which pieces belong on the outside of the picture.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut along the two lines to make 4 pieces. 2. Mix the pieces and turn some of them round. 3. Your child puts the picture together. 4. Ask how your child knew where each piece went.  
  *Parent says:* Here are four pieces, all mixed up. Put the picture together. How do you know where each piece goes?  
  *Child mode:* Put the 4 pieces together. How do you know where each piece goes?
  - Ask: How did you know where this piece goes? (good: The picture carries on from this piece to that one.; The border is on two sides, so it is a corner.; This piece shows the top of the tree, so I put the piece at the top.)
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask how many pieces there will be if each piece is cut in two. Ask whether it will be harder, and why. 2. Cut each piece in two, from top to bottom. 3. Mix the pieces. 4. Your child first finds the pieces with border on two sides, then puts the picture together.  
  *Parent says:* I will cut each piece in two. How many pieces will there be? Will the puzzle be harder? Why? Find the pieces with border on two sides first.  
  *Child mode:* Each piece is cut in two. How many pieces now? Find the pieces with border on two sides first.
  - Ask: Why is it a good idea to place the corner pieces first? (good: There are only four corners, so each corner piece has few places to go.; The border shows me which way round it goes.)
  - Note: There are now 8 pieces in 2 rows of 4. Four of them are corner pieces, with border on two sides. The other four have border on one side only.
  - Check: A piece of a cut-up picture has border along two sides that meet. Where does it go? → **in a corner**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child shuts their eyes. 2. Take one piece away and mix the rest. 3. Your child puts the picture together and says what is on the missing piece. 4. Show the piece. Then keep all the pieces in the envelope.  
  *Parent says:* Shut your eyes. I am taking one piece away. Put the picture together. What is on the piece I am holding?  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. One piece is taken away. Put the picture together. What is on the missing piece?
  - Ask: How did you know what was on the missing piece? (good: The cow's head is not there, so the missing piece must show the head.; I remembered the picture from before.; The pieces round the hole show what goes in it.)
### a3 · Maths in life: the Lid Puzzle (10 min)
- **problem:** Five boxes, five lids, all mixed up. How can you match them with the fewest tries?
- **record:** Write two numbers: the tries you needed with the lids mixed up, and the tries you needed with everything in order of size.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the lids off the five boxes. 2. Mix the lids in a heap. Stand the boxes in a row, in any order. 3. Ask how many tries your child will need to put every lid on its box. Ask why. 4. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Five boxes and five lids, all mixed up. How many tries will you need to match them all? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Five boxes, five lids. Guess how many tries you will need. Say why.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child picks a lid and tries it on a box. 2. For every try, fit or no fit, your child drops one bean in the bowl. 3. Carry on until every box has its lid. 4. Count the beans together.  
  *Parent says:* Try a lid on a box. That is one try, so drop one bean in the bowl. Carry on until every box has its lid.  
  *Child mode:* Try a lid on a box. Drop one bean for each try. Go on until every box has a lid.
  - Ask: When a lid did not fit, what did you learn from that? (good: It was too small, so I tried a smaller box.; Not that box. One of the others.)
  - Note: Without a plan, five boxes can take up to 15 tries. Most children take between 7 and 12.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the lids off again. Empty the bowl. 2. Your child stands the boxes in a row from smallest to biggest. 3. Your child lays the lids in a row from smallest to biggest. 4. Your child matches them again, with one bean for each try.  
  *Parent says:* This time, put the boxes in order from smallest to biggest. Do the same with the lids. Now match them. How many tries?  
  *Child mode:* Put the boxes in order of size. Put the lids in order of size. Match them again. Count your tries.
  - Ask: Why did you need fewer tries this time? (good: The smallest lid goes on the smallest box.; They were in the same order, so each lid fitted the first time.)
  - Note: With both rows in order, the tries come down to 5, one for each box. Ask your child to say why before you say anything.
  - Check: A lid is too small for the middle box. Which boxes could it still fit? → **only smaller boxes**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child shuts their eyes. 2. Slip the extra lid in among the others and mix them. 3. Ask your child to find the lid that has no box. 4. Ask how they knew.  
  *Parent says:* Shut your eyes. I have added one lid that has no box. Find the odd lid. How do you know?  
  *Child mode:* One lid has no box. Find the odd lid. Say how you know.
  - Ask: How did you find the odd lid? (good: I matched all the others, and it was left over.; It was a different shape from every box.)
### a4 · Everyone does maths: Ants and the Twig (15 min)
- **problem:** A line of ants meets something new lying across their way. How do the ants find a way round?
- **safety:** Watch without touching the ants, because some ants bite. Lay the twig down gently, away from any ant. Take the twig away when you finish.
- **record:** Draw two pictures: the ants' line before the twig, and the ants' way about ten minutes after the twig was put down.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a line of ants. 2. Sit a little way off and watch for two minutes. 3. Ask the three questions in the table. 4. Your child draws the ants' line on paper.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the ants without touching. Do they walk in one line? Do they go both ways? What do they do when two of them meet?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the ants. Do not touch. Do they walk in one line? Draw the line.
  - Note: Ants are easy to find near a kitchen shelf, a dustbin, a potted plant or the foot of a wall. If you cannot find any, watch the slime mould video in this unit, which shows another living being in a maze.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what the ants will do if a twig lies across their line. Ask why. 2. Lay the twig gently across the line, not on any ant. 3. Watch for five to ten minutes. 4. Your child says aloud what the ants do: stop, turn back, go left, go right or climb over.  
  *Parent says:* What will the ants do when the twig is in their way? Why do you think so? Now watch. Tell me what you see.  
  *Child mode:* Guess what the ants will do. Say why. Lay the twig across their line. Watch, and say what you see.
  - Ask: Did all the ants do the same thing at first? (good: No. Some went left and some went right.; Some turned back.; Some climbed over.)
  - Note: At first the ants usually stop and spread out in several directions. After some minutes a new line forms. If the ants simply climb over the twig, that is a way through too. Try a wider thing, such as a ruler lying flat.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws the ants' new way. 2. Ask how the ants found it. Listen to your child's ideas. 3. Then read the parent note aloud. 4. Take the twig away.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the way the ants go now. How do you think they found it? Did one ant tell the others?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the ants' new way. How do you think they found it?
  - Ask: How do you think the ants found the new way? (good: Some ants tried different ways.; The others followed the ones who got through.; They can smell where other ants walked.)
  - Note: Read this after your child has given their ideas. Ants leave a smell on the ground as they walk, and other ants follow the smell. The ants who happen to find a short way round get across sooner, so that way gets more smell, and more ants follow. Scientists watched Argentine ants do this in 1989.
  - Check: A twig blocks a line of ants. What do the ants usually do first? → **They spread out and try different ways**

## Projects
### p1 · Puzzle Post: five days, five puzzles (25 min, 5 days)
- **problem:** A puzzle is good only if someone else can solve it and say why. Can you make five that work?
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting on day 4.
- **record:** Keep the five puzzles in their envelopes. On each envelope, write who solved it and one word about how.
- **schedule:** day 1: Draw an odd one out puzzle: four things in a row. Whisper your own rule to a grown-up. Post it. (5 min); day 2: Draw a maze on squared paper, 5 squares by 5 squares, with one dead end. Post it. (5 min); day 3: Draw a row of 5 shapes. Draw it again below with one change. Post it. (5 min); day 4: A grown-up cuts an old card into 6 pieces. Post the pieces. (5 min); day 5: Collect the answers. For each puzzle, ask the solver for their reason and check it. (5 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws four things in a row for an odd one out puzzle. 2. Your child whispers their rule to you. Write it on the back of your hand or on a hidden slip. 3. Your child puts the puzzle in an envelope and names who gets it. 4. Leave it where that person will find it.  
  *Parent says:* Draw four things. One must be the odd one. Whisper your rule to me. Then post your puzzle to someone at home.  
  *Child mode:* Draw four things in a row. One is the odd one. Whisper your rule. Post the puzzle to someone.
- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Day 2: your child draws a maze on squared paper, 5 squares by 5 squares, with one dead end. 2. Day 3: your child draws a row of 5 shapes, then the same row below with one change. 3. Day 4: you cut an old card into 6 pieces. 4. Each day, your child posts the puzzle to someone.  
  *Parent says:* A new puzzle today. Before you post it, solve it yourself once. Does it work?  
  *Child mode:* Each day, make one new puzzle. Solve it yourself once. Then post it.
  - Ask: How do you know your puzzle can be solved? (good: I tried it myself.; I walked my finger through the maze.; I counted, and only one shape is different.)
  - Note: Ask your child to solve each puzzle once before posting it. A maze with no way through, or a row with no change, is a common first slip.
  - Wait: Do one puzzle on each of the next three days.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child visits each solver and asks for the answer. 2. Your child asks each solver: how do you know? 3. For the odd one out puzzle, your child says whether the solver's rule is the same as their own. 4. Ask whether a different rule can still be right.  
  *Parent says:* Ask each person for their answer and their reason. Is the reason true? Did anyone find a rule you had not thought of?  
  *Child mode:* Ask each person for their answer. Ask: how do you know? Is the reason true?
  - Ask: Someone picked a different odd one from yours. Is their answer wrong? (good: Not if their reason is true.; They used a different rule.; Mine was shape and theirs was colour.)

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: build a maze with only one way through (20 min)
- **problem:** Design a maze, 5 squares by 5 squares, with exactly one way from the ant to the banana and at least two dead ends.
- **your turn:** Now design your own maze. Draw the true way first. Add two or more dead ends. Shade the walls. Then check that there is only one way through, and give the maze to someone to solve.
- **constraints:** The maze is 5 squares by 5 squares. The ant starts in the top left corner and the banana is in the bottom right corner.; There is exactly one way from the ant to the banana. Steps go up, down, left or right.; There are at least two dead ends.
- **general:** Draw the true way first, in light pencil. Then add short side paths that stop.; Put a dead end at the very first fork.; Shade every square that is not on a path. Those squares are the walls.
- **creative:** A dead end that runs towards the banana and stops one wall away from it.; A true way that first walks away from the banana.; One dead end with a fork of its own inside it.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a grid of 5 by 5 squares, each about two fingers wide. 2. Read the problem and the three limits aloud. 3. Your child draws the ant and the banana in their corners. 4. Your child draws a winding way from the ant to the banana in light pencil.  
  *Parent says:* A maze maker draws the true way first. Draw a winding way from the ant to the banana. Do not go straight.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the ant and the banana. Draw a winding way between them in light pencil.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child adds two side paths that branch off the true way and then stop. 2. Your child shades every square that is not on a path. 3. Count the dead ends together.  
  *Parent says:* Now add side paths that go nowhere. Where will a solver be most tempted to go wrong? Then shade all the wall squares.  
  *Child mode:* Add two side paths that stop. Shade every square that is not a path. Count your dead ends.
  - Ask: Where did you put your dead ends, and why there? (good: Near the start, so the solver must choose early.; Pointing towards the banana, so it looks like the right way.)
  - Note: Two side paths must not touch each other or touch the true way a second time. If they do, they make a second way through.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child walks a rajma through the maze and hunts for a second way to the banana. 2. If there are two ways, your child shades one more square to block one of them. 3. Check again.  
  *Parent says:* Is there only one way through? Hunt for a second way. If you find one, block it by shading one square.  
  *Child mode:* Hunt for a second way to the banana. If you find one, shade a square to block it.
  - Check: Your maze has two different ways to the banana. How do you leave only one? → **Shade one square on one of the ways**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child gives the maze to someone at home. 2. Your child counts slowly while the solver works. 3. Your child asks the solver which dead end fooled them. 4. Ask what your child would change to make the maze harder.  
  *Parent says:* Give your maze to someone. Count slowly while they solve it. Which dead end fooled them?  
  *Child mode:* Give your maze to someone. Count while they solve it. Which dead end fooled them?
  - Ask: What would you change to make your maze harder? (good: Make a dead end longer.; Put a dead end that points at the banana.; Make the true way turn away from the banana first.)

## Worksheets
### Look and Say Why (core) (core, 13 questions)
1. (mcq) Which shape is the odd one out?  
   Options: a circle / the triangle / none of them  
   **Answer:** the triangle. *Why:* The triangle. The other three are circles. All four are red, so colour does not pick one out.
2. (mcq) Which colour is the odd one here?  
   Options: blue / yellow / red  
   **Answer:** yellow. *Why:* Yellow. Three squares are blue and one is yellow. All four are squares, so shape does not pick one out.
3. (fill) Which place holds the odd one? Write 1, 2, 3 or 4.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* Place 1. It holds a triangle, and the other three places hold circles. All four are green.
4. (truefalse) Three spoons and one tumbler lie in a row. The tumbler is the odd one out.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Three things are spoons and one is not.
5. (sort) Sort each thing: does it have wheels or not?  
   **Answer:** auto → Has wheels; boat → No wheels; bus → Has wheels; crow → No wheels; cycle → Has wheels; banana → No wheels. *Why:* The auto, the bus and the cycle roll on wheels. The boat, the crow and the banana have none. 'Has wheels' is a rule: it puts things in two groups.
6. (fill) How many dead ends does this maze have?  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* One. From the fork, the path going down stops after two squares. Every other white square leads on to the banana.
7. (mcq) Which door leads the ant to the banana?  
   Options: Door A / Door C / Door E  
   **Answer:** Door C. *Why:* Door C. Start at the banana and walk backwards: up, left, left, then up to door C. Doors A and E each stop at a wall.
8. (fill) Count the steps on the ant's way to the banana. One step is one square.  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* 6 steps: right, down, right, down, down, right. There is only one way, with no fork.
9. (mcq) One shape changed from the top row to the bottom row. Which place?  
   Options: Place 2 / Place 3 / Place 4  
   **Answer:** Place 3. *Why:* Place 3. The triangle was yellow and is now red. Check place by place from the left, and stop where the rows do not match.
10. (match) Match each thing to the group it belongs with.  
   **Answer:** spoon → plate, tumbler, pot; crow → parrot, pigeon, mynah; mango → banana, guava, papaya; bus → auto, car, lorry. *Why:* A spoon is a kitchen thing. A crow is a bird. A mango is a fruit. A bus carries people on the road. Each group shares one rule.
11. (draw) Draw three things that are alike and one that is odd. Circle the odd one.  
   **Answer:** Any four things where three share something and the fourth does not, with the fourth circled.. *Why:* A strong drawing lets a reader say the rule at once, for example three fruits and a shoe.
12. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. In a maze, what do you do at a dead end?  
   **Answer:** I go back to the last fork and try a way I have not tried.. *Why:* A strong answer says 'the last fork', not 'the start'. The squares before the last fork were fine.
13. (draw) Draw a row of 4 shapes. Draw it again below with one change. Ask someone to find the change.  
   **Answer:** Two rows of 4 shapes that match in three places and differ in one.. *Why:* A strong puzzle has exactly one change. Check it yourself, place by place, before you show it.
### Two Rules, Two Ways (stretch) (stretch, 13 questions)
1. (mcq) The rule is colour. Which one is the odd one now?  
   Options: the square / the blue circle / a red circle  
   **Answer:** the blue circle. *Why:* The blue circle. Three shapes are red and one is blue. Picking the square means using the rule 'shape'.
2. (mcq) This time the rule is shape. Which one is odd?  
   Options: the square / the blue circle / a red circle  
   **Answer:** the square. *Why:* The square. Three shapes are circles and one is a square. Picking the blue circle means using the rule 'colour'.
3. (truefalse) One set of four things can have two different odd ones.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A different rule can pick out a different one. In the Odd One Out Test, each of the four cards was the odd one under some rule.
4. (fill) The ant can go two ways. How many steps is the shorter way?  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* 5 steps: down, down, down, right, right. The other way goes along the top and round. It takes 9 steps.
5. (mcq) Which door should the ant take to reach the banana?  
   Options: Door A / Door C / Door E  
   **Answer:** Door A. *Why:* Door A. Start at the banana and walk backwards: left, left, left, then up to door A. Door C looks close, but it stops one wall above the banana.
6. (order) You are stuck in a maze. Put the three steps in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Notice that the path has stopped', 'Go back to the last fork', 'Take a way you have not tried']. *Why:* First you notice the dead end. Then you go back to the last fork. Then you take a way you have not tried.
7. (sort) Is it a fork or a dead end?  
   **Answer:** The path splits into two ways → Fork; The path stops at a wall → Dead end; You must choose left or right → Fork; You can only turn round → Dead end; Three ways to pick from → Fork; Walls on three sides → Dead end. *Why:* At a fork you have a choice. At a dead end you have none, and you turn round.
8. (fill) How many shapes changed from the top row to the bottom row?  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* Two. At place 2 the blue circle became green. At place 5 the blue square became a blue triangle. The other three places match.
9. (perspective) Banana, mango, lemon, carrot. Which is the odd one out?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other child has right.. *Why:* Both reasons are true, so both answers are good. Each child chose a different rule: where the food grows, or how it tastes.
10. (draw) Draw four things so that each one can be the odd one. Tell a grown-up your four rules.  
   **Answer:** Four things, each with something that only it has. For example: a big red circle with a dot, a big red square, a big blue circle and a small red circle.. *Why:* A strong set has one rule for each thing. Check each rule: is it true for that thing and for no other?
11. (write) Three tumblers are exactly alike. The fourth thing is a plate. Can a tumbler be the odd one? Say why.  
   **Answer:** No. Anything true of one tumbler is true of the other two, so no rule picks out just one.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the three tumblers are the same in every way. Only the plate has something the others lack.
12. (mcq) A cut-up picture has a border. One piece has border on one side only. Where does it go?  
   Options: along an edge, not in a corner / in a corner / in the middle  
   **Answer:** along an edge, not in a corner. *Why:* Along an edge. A corner piece has border on two sides. A middle piece has none.
13. (match) Match each set to the rule that three of them share.  
   **Answer:** crow, parrot, pigeon, cat → three are birds; idli, dosa, vada, spoon → three are food; bus, auto, cycle, boat → three have wheels; Sun, Moon, star, lamp → three are in the sky. *Why:* Find what three of the four share. The fourth is the odd one: the cat, the spoon, the boat and the lamp.
### Puzzle Maker (challenge) (challenge, 13 questions)
1. (fill) How many of these four shapes can be the odd one, each with a true rule?  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* All 4. One is the only triangle. One is the only square. One is the only green shape. One is the only yellow shape.
2. (mcq) Which rule makes the green circle the odd one?  
   Options: It is the only circle / It is the only green one / It is the only one with corners  
   **Answer:** It is the only green one. *Why:* It is the only green one. It is not the only circle, because the yellow shape is a circle too. A circle has no corners.
3. (truefalse) This maze has two different ways to the banana.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. One way goes straight down and then right, in 5 steps. The other goes along the top, down the right side and back, in 9 steps.
4. (fill) Find every dead end in this big maze. How many are there?  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* Two. One is in the top right corner. The other is on the left side, near the bottom.
5. (mcq) The ant is stuck at a dead end. Where is the best place to go back to?  
   Options: the start / the last fork / the banana  
   **Answer:** the last fork. *Why:* The last fork. It is the nearest place that still has a way the ant has not tried.
6. (order) Order these cut-up pictures from easiest to hardest to put together.  
   **Answer:** ['4 pieces', '8 pieces', '16 pieces']. *Why:* With more pieces, each piece has more places where it could go. So there is more to try and to rule out.
7. (sort) Does the puzzle have one answer only, or more than one good answer?  
   **Answer:** Which door leads to the banana? → One answer; Which is odd: crow, bus, mango, Moon? → More than one; How many steps is the shortest way? → One answer; Which lid fits this box? → One answer; Which of four friends is the odd one? → More than one; Which place changed in the row? → One answer. *Why:* In a maze, a lid puzzle or a find-the-change puzzle, the answer is fixed and you find it. In an open odd one out, the answer depends on the rule you choose.
8. (fill) A cut-up picture has 4 pieces. You cut each piece in two. How many pieces now?  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* Each of the 4 pieces becomes 2 pieces. 2 and 2 and 2 and 2 make 8.
9. (perspective) Keep one hand on the wall of a maze. Will you always get through?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other child has right.. *Why:* The second child is right about 'always'. The first child is right for mazes where every wall is joined to the outer wall. There the hand tip does work.
10. (draw) Draw a maze 5 squares by 5 squares, with one way through and two dead ends.  
   **Answer:** A grid of 5 by 5 squares with a start, an end, one way between them and two side paths that stop.. *Why:* A strong maze has been tested by its maker: walk a finger through it and check that there is no second way.
11. (write) Why is a wrong turn in a maze still useful?  
   **Answer:** It shows me one way that does not work. Now fewer ways are left to try.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a dead end rules a way out. Each way you rule out brings you closer to the one that works.
12. (mcq) Two shapes swapped places from the top row to the bottom row. Which two places?  
   Options: 1 and 2 / 2 and 4 / 3 and 5  
   **Answer:** 2 and 4. *Why:* Places 2 and 4. The blue triangle and the green circle have changed places. Places 1, 3 and 5 match.
13. (match) Match each puzzle to a good first move.  
   **Answer:** a maze with three doors → start at the end and walk backwards; odd one out → find what three of them share; find the change → check place by place from the left; a cut-up picture → find the corner pieces. *Why:* Each kind of puzzle has a way in. A good first move makes the puzzle smaller before you start trying things.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which one does not belong with the others?  
   Options: a square / the circle / they all belong  
   **Answer:** the circle. *Why:* The circle. The other three are squares. All four are green, so colour does not pick one out.
2. (mcq) Idli, dosa, vada, tumbler. Which is the odd one, and why?  
   Options: the tumbler: it is not food / the idli: it is food / the dosa: it is flat  
   **Answer:** the tumbler: it is not food. *Why:* The tumbler, because the other three are food. 'The idli: it is food' fails, because the dosa and the vada are food too.
3. (mcq) Someone picks the red circle as the odd one. Which rule are they using?  
   Options: colour / shape / number of corners  
   **Answer:** colour. *Why:* Colour. The red circle is the only shape that is not blue. By shape, the triangle would be the odd one.
4. (truefalse) With the same four things, a new rule can make a different one the odd one.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Red circle, red square, blue circle: by shape the square is odd, and by colour the blue circle is odd.
5. (mcq) The ant may go in at A, C or E. Which door leads to the banana?  
   Options: Door A / Door C / Door E  
   **Answer:** Door E. *Why:* Door E. Start at the banana and walk backwards: right, then up to door E. Door C goes a long way down and then stops at a wall.
6. (fill) Count the dead ends in this maze.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Three. One is in the top row, one square past the first fork. One is in the top right corner. One is in the bottom left corner.
7. (mcq) You reach a dead end in a maze. What is the best thing to do?  
   Options: Go back to the last fork / Start the whole maze again / Stop and give up  
   **Answer:** Go back to the last fork. *Why:* Go back to the last fork and take a way you have not tried. Starting again makes you walk over squares that were fine.
8. (mcq) A maze has three doors and one end. What is a quick way to find the right door?  
   Options: Start at the end and walk backwards / Always pick the middle door / Pick the door nearest the end  
   **Answer:** Start at the end and walk backwards. *Why:* Start at the end and walk backwards. If that path has no fork, it leads you straight to the right door. The nearest door is often a trap.
9. (mcq) Which place is different in the bottom row?  
   Options: Place 1 / Place 3 / Place 5  
   **Answer:** Place 5. *Why:* Place 5. The red triangle has become a blue triangle. Places 1 to 4 match.
10. (mcq) You are putting a cut-up picture together. Which pieces are easiest to place first?  
   Options: the corner pieces / the middle pieces / any piece at all  
   **Answer:** the corner pieces. *Why:* The corner pieces. There are only four corners, and the border on two sides shows which corner each one fits.
11. (truefalse) An odd one out answer is good only if its reason is true.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. 'The square is odd because it is blue' is a wrong answer if the square is red. The reason is part of the answer.
12. (mcq) A friend picks a different odd one from you and gives a true reason. What does that mean?  
   Options: Both answers can be right / Your friend must be wrong / You must be wrong  
   **Answer:** Both answers can be right. *Why:* Both answers can be right. Your friend used a different rule. Each answer is right for its own rule.

## Applications
- A doctor looks for the one sign in your body that is not as usual, and asks why.
- A mechanic listens to an engine for the one sound that does not fit.
- A farmer sorting mangoes picks out the few that are not like the rest, before they spoil the others.
- An electrician hunting for a fault checks one switch at a time and rules out the ones that work.
- A delivery rider in an old town goes back to the last turning when a lane is closed, and tries the next lane.
- A tailor fits cut pieces of cloth together, like a cut-up picture, so that the design matches at the seams.
- People who write computer programs tell a computer how to find the way across a map, one fork at a time.
- Astronomers still find new things in the sky by spotting the one light that does not behave like the rest.

## Spotted around us
- Washed socks being put into pairs. The one sock left without a partner is the odd one out.
- A bunch of keys and one lock. Each key that does not turn is one key less to try.
- A line of ants going round a fallen leaf or a spilt drop of water.
- The rails at a railway station ticket counter or a temple queue, which wind back and forth. They make a path with no forks.
- The stone carving of the chakravyuha at the Hoysaleswara temple in Halebidu, Karnataka.
- The lanes of the old part of a town, where some lanes end at a wall or a gate and you have to walk back.
- A tray of tomatoes at a vegetable shop, with one green one among the red.
- A steady reddish light among the stars on some evenings. That is the planet Mars. Over some weeks Mars changes place among the stars.

## Open questions
- **How does a slime mould find the short way, with no brain that anyone has found?** Scientists know that the tubes in a slime mould's body grow wider where more liquid flows and shrink where little flows. They are still working out how this adds up to such good choices.
- **Is every puzzle that is quick to check also quick to solve?** Checking a finished number puzzle is quick. Solving a big one can take very long. Nobody has proved whether a quick way to solve must exist whenever a quick way to check exists. A prize of one million dollars waits for the proof.
- **Do bees mean by 'same' what we mean by 'same'?** Bees pass the tests that scientists set. But nobody can look out through a bee's eyes, so scientists still argue about what the bees are doing.
- **Why do people enjoy puzzles, even with no prize?** Children all over the world play with puzzles for no reward. Scientists have several ideas about why working things out feels good, and no agreed answer.

## Connected maths
- In one maze, the long way to the banana is 9 steps. The short way is 5 steps. How many steps does the short way save? **4 steps.**. Take the short way from the long way. 9 take away 5 leaves 4. So the short way saves 4 steps. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- A cut-up picture has 4 pieces. A grown-up cuts each piece in two. How many pieces are there now? **8 pieces.**. Each of the 4 pieces becomes 2. That is double 4. 4 and 4 make 8. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.number-facts.L1`)
- In Dice Switch the row has 6 cards. You have checked places 1 to 4, and they have not changed. In how many places can the change still be? **2 places: place 5 or place 6.**. There are 6 places. You have ruled out 4. 6 take away 4 leaves 2. The change is in place 5 or place 6. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- In one family's Odd One Out Test, 3 people picked the square and 2 people picked the blue circle. How many more people picked the square? **1 more.**. Match each square pick with a blue circle pick. Two pairs are made, and one square pick is left over. So 1 more person picked the square. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- There are 5 boxes and 5 lids. You have matched 3 boxes. You pick up the 4th box. How many lids are left to try on it? **2 lids.**. 5 lids take away the 3 already used leaves 2. If the first of them does not fit, the other one must. So one try is enough to know. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L1`)
