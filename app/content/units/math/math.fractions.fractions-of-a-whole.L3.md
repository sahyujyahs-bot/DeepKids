# Unit fractions: one equal part of a whole, or of a group
`math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`

**Summary.** Which is bigger, one third of a dosa or one eighth of the same dosa? Many people pick one eighth, because 8 is bigger than 3. In this unit you cut paper strips and share out groundnuts to find the real answer. You learn to name and write fractions such as 1/3 and 1/8, and to find a fraction of a group, such as one quarter of 12. You also find out that a fraction has no size at all until someone says what the whole is.

**Intro.** *Which is more: half of a laddu, or a quarter of a laddu? Think first. Then open the box below.* Someone offers you a choice. You can have one half of a laddu, or one quarter of a laddu. Which do you take? Now here is the catch. You have not seen the two laddus yet. One of them might be as small as a marble. The other might be as big as a coconut. Can you still choose? A fraction is a way to talk about equal parts of something. One half means the thing was cut into 2 equal parts, and you have one of them. One quarter means it was cut into 4 equal parts, and you have one. But a half of what? A quarter of what? In this unit you will make your own fraction strips from paper and share out groundnuts. You will find a pattern that surprises most people the first time. Then you will use it in the kitchen, in music and in a game.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a fraction is: one or more equal parts of a whole.
1. I can name and write unit fractions: one half, one third, one quarter, one fifth, one sixth, one eighth and one tenth.
2. I can explain why more equal parts make each part smaller, so 1/8 of a thing is smaller than 1/3 of it.
3. I can find a unit fraction of a group by sharing it equally: one quarter of 12 is 3.
4. I can explain why a fraction has no size until I know what the whole is.
5. I can check that parts are equal in amount, even when they have different shapes.
6. I can point to what people chose about fractions: the whole, what counts as equal, and how to write them.

## Terms
- **Whole**: All of the thing or the group you are sharing. It is what we agree to call 'one'. *e.g.* One dosa can be the whole. A bag of 12 groundnuts can also be the whole.
- **Equal parts**: Parts that are the same amount as each other. They do not have to be the same shape. *e.g.* A square sheet folded corner to corner gives 2 equal triangles. Folded edge to edge, it gives 2 equal rectangles.
- **Fraction**: A number that tells how much of a whole you have, counted in equal parts. *e.g.* If a chapati is cut into 4 equal parts and you eat 1 part, you ate the fraction one quarter.
- **Unit fraction**: One single equal part of a whole. When we write it, the top number is 1. *e.g.* 1/2, 1/3, 1/4 and 1/8 are unit fractions. 3/4 is not, because it is three parts.
- **Half**: One of 2 equal parts of a whole. We write it 1/2. *e.g.* Break a coconut into 2 equal parts. Each part is one half.
- **Third**: One of 3 equal parts of a whole. We write it 1/3. *e.g.* Three friends share one banana equally. Each gets one third.
- **Quarter**: One of 4 equal parts of a whole. It is also called a fourth. We write it 1/4. *e.g.* A quarter of an hour is 15 minutes, because 4 lots of 15 minutes make an hour.
- **Fifth, sixth, eighth, tenth**: One of 5, 6, 8 or 10 equal parts of a whole. We write them 1/5, 1/6, 1/8 and 1/10. The name comes from the number of equal parts. *e.g.* A tray holds 6 eggs. One egg is one sixth of the tray.
- **Top number (numerator)**: The number written above the line in a fraction. It counts how many parts you have. Its proper name is the numerator. *e.g.* In 1/4 the top number is 1. You have one part.
- **Bottom number (denominator)**: The number written below the line in a fraction. It tells how many equal parts make the whole. Its proper name is the denominator. *e.g.* In 1/4 the bottom number is 4. Four equal parts make the whole.
- **Fraction of a group**: A fraction can be taken of a group of things. Share the group into equal smaller groups. One of those groups is the unit fraction. *e.g.* Share 12 groundnuts into 4 equal groups. Each group has 3. So one quarter of 12 is 3.
- **Bar model**: A drawing of a long box that stands for the whole. You split the box into equal parts to see a fraction. *e.g.* Draw a bar for 12. Split it into 4 equal boxes. Write 3 in each box.

## Explanations
### What a fraction is · *defined*
**Definition:** A fraction tells how much of a whole you have, when the whole is split into equal parts.

To make a fraction you need three things. First, you need a whole: one dosa, one strip of paper, or one bag of 12 groundnuts. Second, you split the whole into parts that are equal. Third, you count how many of those parts you have. If the parts are not equal, there is no fraction to name. A dosa torn into one big piece and one small piece has two pieces, but neither piece is a half.
- e.g. A strip of paper folded end to end has 2 equal parts. Each part is one half of the strip.
- e.g. A bar of chikki with 6 equal squares: one square is one sixth of the bar.
- e.g. A dosa torn into a big piece and a small piece: 2 pieces, but no halves.
### How to say and write a unit fraction · *defined*
**Definition:** A unit fraction is one equal part of a whole. It is written as 1, a short line, and the number of equal parts in the whole.

Count the equal parts in the whole. That number gives the fraction its name. Two parts give halves, 3 give thirds, 4 give quarters, 5 give fifths, 6 give sixths, 8 give eighths and 10 give tenths. On paper, write 1, draw a short line under it, and write the number of parts under the line. On a phone or in a book it is often written on one line, like this: 1/4. Here is a worked example. A strip is cut into 6 equal parts and you hold one. Count the parts: 6. So the name is one sixth. Write 1 on top, because you hold 1 part. Write 6 underneath, because 6 equal parts make the whole. You hold 1/6 of the strip.
- e.g. One of 3 equal parts: one third, written 1/3.
- e.g. One of 8 equal parts: one eighth, written 1/8.
- e.g. One of 10 equal parts: one tenth, written 1/10.
### More equal parts means smaller parts · *defined*
**Definition:** When the same whole is cut into more equal parts, each part is smaller. So a bigger bottom number means a smaller unit fraction.

Take two strips of paper, each 24 cm long. Cut the first into 3 equal parts. Each part is 8 cm long. Cut the second into 8 equal parts. Each part is only 3 cm long. The strips were the same length, so the paper had to be shared among more parts, and each part got less. This is why 1/8 of a thing is smaller than 1/3 of the same thing, even though 8 is a bigger number than 3. The bottom number is not a size. It tells how many parts the whole was cut into. The same thing happens with people. A dosa shared by 8 people gives each person less than the same dosa shared by 3.
- e.g. From the same strip: 1/2 is longer than 1/3, 1/3 is longer than 1/4, and 1/4 is longer than 1/8.
- e.g. One laddu shared by 2 children gives bigger pieces than one laddu shared by 10 children.
- e.g. This rule only works when the whole is the same. See the card 'The whole decides the size'.
### A fraction of a group · *defined*
**Definition:** To find a unit fraction of a group, share the group into that many equal smaller groups. The number in one small group is the answer.

A whole does not have to be one thing. It can be a group, such as 12 groundnuts. Here is a worked example: find one quarter of 12. The bottom number is 4, so share the 12 into 4 equal groups. Put one groundnut in each group, then another in each, until they are all used. Each group has 3. So one quarter of 12 is 3. You can draw this as a bar model. Draw a long box for the 12. Split it into 4 equal boxes. The 12 is shared equally, so each box holds 3. Sharing into equal groups is division. So one quarter of 12 is the same sum as 12 divided by 4.
- e.g. One half of 12 is 6, because 12 shared into 2 equal groups gives 6 in each.
- e.g. One third of 12 is 4, because 12 shared into 3 equal groups gives 4 in each.
- e.g. One fifth of 20 rupees is 4 rupees, because 20 shared into 5 equal groups gives 4 in each.
### The whole decides the size · *defined*
**Definition:** A fraction tells how many equal parts the whole was cut into. How much you really get depends on how big the whole is.

One quarter of 24 groundnuts is 6 groundnuts. One quarter of 12 groundnuts is 3 groundnuts. Both are one quarter, and they are different amounts. The fraction stayed the same and the whole changed. So you cannot say that one half is always more than one quarter. One half of 8 laddus is 4 laddus. One quarter of 40 laddus is 10 laddus. Here the quarter is more. You can only compare two fractions by their bottom numbers when both are fractions of the same whole. This is why, when someone says 'half price' or 'a quarter of the class', a careful person asks: half of what? A quarter of how many?
- e.g. A quarter of a kilogram of tomatoes is 250 grams. A quarter of 100 grams of pepper is only 25 grams.
- e.g. Half of a small dosa can be less food than a quarter of a very big dosa.
- e.g. One tenth of 100 rupees is 10 rupees. One half of 10 rupees is 5 rupees.
### Equal means the same amount, not the same shape · *defined*
**Definition:** The parts of a fraction must be equal in amount. They do not have to look the same, and they do not have to be the same height.

Fold one square of paper into 4 long strips. Fold another square of the same size into 4 small squares. Fold a third along both corner-to-corner lines, to make 4 triangles. A strip, a small square and a triangle look different. But each is one quarter of the same square, so each is the same amount of paper. You can check. Cut the triangle into two pieces, and the pieces fit exactly on top of the small square. The opposite mistake also happens: parts can look equal and not be. Water in a kadai is a good example. A kadai is narrow at the bottom and wide at the top. So the bottom half of its height holds much less than half of the water.
- e.g. Four triangles made by folding a square corner to corner, both ways, are four quarters.
- e.g. Three lines drawn side by side across a circle do not make quarters. The two middle pieces are bigger than the two end pieces.
- e.g. In a straight-sided glass, a quarter of the height is a quarter of the water. In a kadai it is not.
### More than one part: a first look · *defined*
**Definition:** Fractions can count more than one equal part. Three quarters, written 3/4, means 3 parts when the whole is cut into 4 equal parts.

Unit fractions are the building blocks of all other fractions. If one quarter of a dosa is one part, then two quarters are two of those parts and three quarters are three of them. The top number counts the parts. The bottom number still says how many equal parts make the whole. Here is a worked example with a group. Find three quarters of 12. First find one quarter: 12 shared into 4 equal groups gives 3. Three quarters is three of those groups: 3 and 3 and 3, which is 9. You will do much more with fractions like these in the next level. This card is only a first look.
- e.g. Two thirds of a strip, written 2/3, is 2 parts when the strip is cut into 3 equal parts.
- e.g. Four quarters, written 4/4, is all 4 parts. That is the whole.
- e.g. Three quarters of 12 groundnuts is 9 groundnuts.
### People wrote fractions in different ways · *defined*
**Definition:** The way we write a fraction, with one number above a line and one below, is a choice people made. Other people made other choices that worked too.

About 3,500 years ago in Egypt, scribes wrote almost every fraction as a unit fraction. They had a special sign for two thirds. For three quarters they wrote one half and one quarter. In India, mathematicians wrote one number above the other, with any number on top. Brahmagupta did this in the year 628. Arab mathematicians later added the line between the two numbers. Old Tamil accounts had their own names for small parts: arai for one half, kaal for one quarter, araikkaal for one eighth, maa for one twentieth and kaani for one eightieth. Another, mundiri, was 1 part in 320. Until 1957, the Indian rupee was split into 16 annas, so 'four annas' meant a quarter of a rupee. Today a rupee is split into 100 paise. None of these ways is wrong. Each one is a different way to cut up and name the same whole.
- e.g. Egyptian scribes wrote three quarters as 1/2 and 1/4 together.
- e.g. In a Tamil Nadu market you still hear 'kaal kilo' for a quarter of a kilogram and 'arai kilo' for half.
- e.g. A rupee was once 16 annas and is now 100 paise. The rupee is the same whole, cut differently.
### Fractions in the sky, the Earth and the day · *defined*
**Definition:** People use fractions to describe the world around them. Each time, someone first chose what to call the whole.

The Earth turns round once, and we call that one day. Long ago, people in Egypt counted 12 hours of daylight and 12 hours of night. Our 24 hours come from that count. Timekeepers in India cut the same day into 60 parts. In Tamil each part is called a nazhigai, and it lasts 24 minutes. The day is the same. The cutting was a choice. The Moon gives another example. When the Moon looks like a half circle, astronomers call it a quarter Moon. The quarter is not a quarter of the Moon's face. The Moon has gone one quarter of the way round the Earth. So the same Moon is a half or a quarter, depending on which whole you mean. About one fifth of the air you breathe is oxygen. A little less than three quarters of the Earth's surface is covered by water.
- e.g. One hour is one twenty-fourth of a day. One nazhigai is one sixtieth of a day.
- e.g. A first quarter Moon looks half lit. The Moon is one quarter of the way round the Earth.
- e.g. If you sleep 8 hours, you sleep for one third of the day.
### Other animals and parts of a whole · *idea*
Humans are not the only animals who can tell one part of a whole from another. In 1981, two scientists in the United States, Guy Woodruff and David Premack, tested a chimpanzee called Sarah. She was shown something like a jar half full of liquid. She then had to pick what matched it, such as half of a wooden disc or three quarters of a disc. Sarah picked the matching fraction far more often than chance would allow. Four younger chimpanzees in the same study did not manage it. In 2016, Rosa Rugani and her team in Italy trained chicks who were four days old. The chicks learned to choose pictures that showed a proportion of 4 to 1, and not pictures that showed 2 to 1. When the pictures were changed to new amounts, the chicks still chose the 4 to 1 proportion. So these animals can respond to how much of a whole there is. What it is like for them, and whether they think of a half the way we do, nobody knows.
- e.g. Sarah the chimpanzee matched a half-full jar with half a disc, not with three quarters of a disc.
- e.g. Four-day-old chicks chose the same proportion even when the amounts in the pictures changed.
- e.g. A lady's finger cut across usually shows 5 nearly equal chambers. A star fruit usually has 5 ridges.
### Can you halve something for ever? · *debated*
Fold a strip in half, then in half again, and again. The parts go 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16. With numbers, this never has to stop. Any number can be halved, and the answer can be halved again. With real things it is different, and people give different answers.
- e.g. A sheet of newspaper can be folded in half only about 6 or 7 times before it is too thick to fold.
- e.g. Half of 1 is 1/2, half of that is 1/4, then 1/8, then 1/16, and the list has no end.
- **Mathematics:** In numbers, yes. However small a fraction is, half of it is another fraction. There is no smallest fraction. Hui Shi, a thinker in China about 2,300 years ago, said the same about a stick: take half each day and it is never used up.
- **Chemistry and physics:** With real stuff, no. Keep cutting a grain of jaggery and you reach single sugar molecules. Cut a molecule and the pieces are no longer sugar. So a real thing can be halved many times, but not for ever and stay the same stuff.
- **Physicists who study space itself:** Nobody knows whether space can be halved for ever. Some physicists think there may be a smallest possible length. No experiment has settled it.

## Mostly agreed
- A fraction needs a whole and equal parts. *How we know:* If the parts are unequal, two people can each hold 'one of 4 pieces' and have different amounts. Then the name tells you nothing. You saw this with the circle cut by three side-by-side lines.
- From the same whole, more equal parts means smaller parts. *How we know:* Your 24 cm strips show it. Two parts are 12 cm each, 3 parts are 8 cm each, 4 parts are 6 cm, 6 parts are 4 cm and 8 parts are 3 cm. The length of one part times the number of parts is always 24.
- A unit fraction of a group is found by equal sharing, which is division. *How we know:* Share 24 groundnuts into 4 equal groups and each group has 6. Whoever does the sharing, and in whatever order, the answer is 6.
- The same fraction of different wholes gives different amounts. *How we know:* One quarter of 24 groundnuts is 6. One quarter of 12 groundnuts is 3.
- Once people agree on the whole and on what equal means, everyone gets the same answer. *How we know:* Egyptian scribes, Indian mathematicians and children today write fractions differently. All of them find that two quarters of a thing make one half of it.

## How we found out
**The scribe Ahmes, Brahmagupta, Mahavira, Arab mathematicians such as al-Hassar, and Simon Stevin, About 1550 BCE to 1585 CE.** About 3,500 years ago in Egypt, a scribe called Ahmes copied out a book of sums. Today it is called the Rhind papyrus. In it, nearly every fraction is a unit fraction, with 1 on top. The scribes had a special sign for two thirds, but they wrote three quarters as one half and one quarter. In Greece, about 2,300 years ago, Euclid wrote that a number is a collection of ones. On that view, the one cannot be broken, so a fraction is not a number at all. Mathematicians in India worked differently. By the year 628, Brahmagupta wrote fractions as one number above another, with any number on top, and added and multiplied them like other numbers. About 850, Mahavira, a Jain mathematician who probably worked at the Rashtrakuta court in the Deccan, gave rules for turning a fraction into a sum of unit fractions. Arab mathematicians later added the line between the two numbers. In 1585, Simon Stevin, from Flanders, argued that every quantity is a number, fractions included. That is how fractions are treated today.  
*What people believed before:* Greek mathematicians held that the one cannot be divided, so a fraction is not a number. People gave this up because fractions can be added, compared and put in order just like whole numbers. It was a change in what people agreed to call a number.

## Challenging perspectives
- **One eighth is bigger than one third, because 8 is bigger than 3.** (Many children, and many adults too, the first time they meet fractions; *rejected*)  
  For: With whole numbers, 8 really is more than 3. It feels natural to read the bottom number as a size.  
  Against: The bottom number counts how many parts the whole was cut into. Cut a 24 cm strip into 8 parts and each is 3 cm. Cut it into 3 parts and each is 8 cm. More parts means smaller parts.
- **A fraction is not really a number. Only whole numbers are numbers.** (Greek mathematicians such as Euclid, about 2,300 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: Euclid said a number is a collection of ones. If the one is the smallest thing you count with, it cannot be broken.  
  Against: Fractions can be added, compared, put in order and placed on a number line, just like whole numbers. Mathematicians in India worked with them as numbers. In 1585 Simon Stevin argued that every quantity is a number, and that is the view used today. It was a change in what people agreed to call a number.
- **Fractions were there before people. People only discovered them.** (Many mathematicians and philosophers, often called Platonists after the Greek thinker Plato; *debated*)  
  For: A chimpanzee and a chick can respond to a half or to a proportion without any human words. Two quarters make a half whoever does the sum, in any country and any century.  
  Against: The whole, the meaning of 'equal' and the way of writing all have to be chosen before any fraction exists. Egyptians, Tamil accountants and modern schools chose differently. Mathematics is a language people made, and its sentences are true inside the rules people chose.
- **Sharing equally is always the fair way to share.** (A common way of thinking; *debated*)  
  For: Equal parts are simple, anyone can check them, and nobody can say they were cheated.  
  Against: Mathematics can tell you whether parts are equal. It cannot tell you whether equal is fair. A baby and a farmer who has worked all day may not need the same share of rice. 'Fair' is something people decide. 'Equal' is something you can measure.
- **There is one right way to cut a thing into quarters.** (A common first idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Most pictures in books show a circle cut like a cross, or a bar cut into 4 boxes.  
  Against: A square can be cut into 4 strips, 4 small squares, 4 triangles and many other shapes. Each is a quarter as long as the 4 parts are the same amount.

## Roots
We have been cutting strips and sharing groundnuts into equal parts. Now we ask what a fraction tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Take 24 groundnuts and 12 groundnuts. One quarter of 24 is 6. One quarter of 12 is 3. It is the same 'one quarter', but the amounts differ. A fraction does not say how much. It says how much of what.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think one quarter is a fixed amount, like 4 groundnuts. It is not. It always means one quarter of something, and a person chooses that something.  
*Evidence:* In the Strip Test, one quarter of 24 was 6 and one quarter of 12 was 3. In the card game Bigger Share, one eighth of 40 beat one half of 4.

- *who am i:* You are one whole person. You are also one part of your family, and one of more than eight billion people. Each is true for a different whole.
- *what is life:* One living cell divides into two, and each half grows into a whole cell. In living beings, a part can become a whole.
- *what is this world:* The Earth turns once, and nothing marks that turn into parts. People cut it into 24 hours, or into 60 nazhigai.

A fraction starts with one whole thing. Cut it into parts and put the parts back together: you get the same whole. Any part can be picked up, called 'one', and cut again. Which thing we call 'one' is our choice. What follows from that choice is the same for everyone.

- **Isha Upanishad, opening verse (also Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.1.1):** That is whole, this is whole. From the whole the whole comes out. Take the whole from the whole, and the whole remains. (The verse is about the whole of everything, not about dosas. The small parallel in this unit is that a part taken from a whole can itself be treated as a whole. In the story, the last quarter of the jaggery became the new whole and was cut into fifths.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 33 (paraphrased):** Take a stick one foot long. Each day, take away half of what is left. Even after ten thousand generations it will not be used up. (This is the halving you did by folding: one half, one quarter, one eighth. With numbers there is always another half to take. With a real stick, the pieces at last become too small to cut.)
- Maths (settled): Numbers can be halved without end, but real stuff cannot. Cut a grain of sugar small enough and you reach single molecules. Cut a molecule and the pieces are no longer sugar.
- Maths (open): Some physicists think space itself may have a smallest possible length, so that it could not be halved for ever. No experiment has shown this.
- Other view, Platonists: many mathematicians and philosophers, named after the Greek thinker Plato: They say that numbers and fractions exist by themselves, whether or not anyone thinks about them. People choose the names and the signs, but one quarter of a thing would be one quarter of it even if no person had ever lived. On this view, people discover mathematics. They do not make it.
- Practice, One breath, cut in parts (2 min): Take one slow, full breath in. Now breathe it out in 2 equal puffs. Take another full breath. This time breathe it out in 4 equal puffs. Once more, in 8 small puffs. Which puffs were the smallest? The breath was the same whole each time.
- Ask: A quarter of 24 groundnuts and a quarter of 12 groundnuts are both 'one quarter'. Are they the same thing or different things?
- Ask: When you cut a dosa into four, is there more dosa, less dosa, or the same? What did change?
- Ask: Who decides what the whole is?
- Ask: You are a whole person and also a part of a family. Can something be a whole and a part at once?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Why is one eighth of a dosa smaller than one third of it, when 8 is bigger than 3? (hint: Think about how many people are sharing the dosa each time.)
- *deep:* Can a half ever be smaller than a quarter? (hint: A half of what? A quarter of what? Try a small laddu and a huge one.)
- *deep:* If you keep cutting a piece of paper in half, will you ever have nothing left? (hint: Each cut leaves half of what was there. Is half of something ever nothing?)
- *deep:* Are two halves of a dosa still one dosa? What changed and what did not? (hint: Count the pieces. Then think about how much dosa there is.)
- *big:* Ten guavas are big and small. Is a fair half 5 guavas each, or the same weight each? (hint: Both are 'equal', measured in two ways. Who decides which way to measure?)
- *big:* Who decided that a day has 24 hours? Could it have been 10 or 60? (hint: In Egypt people counted 12 hours for the day and 12 for the night. In India people also used 60 parts called nazhigai.)
- *big:* You are one whole person and also one small part of your family. Which is the real 'one'? (hint: The same thing can be a whole or a part. It depends on what you choose to look at.)
- *big:* A chick can pick out a proportion. Does the chick know fractions? (hint: What does it mean to know something if you have no word for it?)
- *big:* Is sharing equally always sharing fairly? (hint: Think of sharing food between a baby and a grown-up who is very hungry.)

## Story: Half of What Is Left
*Kuttanad is a real place in Kerala where the fields lie below the level of the sea and people travel by canal. Devu, Appu, Ammachi and the shop boat are made up.*

In Kuttanad, in Kerala, the fields lie lower than the sea, and the houses stand in a row along the canals. Devu's house had a canal where other houses have a road. The shop came by boat. Every Tuesday and every Friday a long black boat came down the canal with rice, soap, matches and jaggery under a blue plastic sheet, and the woman who rowed it rang a cycle bell tied to the front.

That June the rain came early and did not stop. The canal rose over its bank and over the path beside it. It rose up the first step of the house and stopped at the second. The shop boat did not come on Friday. It did not come on Tuesday. 'When the water goes down,' the neighbours called across to each other, and nobody could say when that would be.

There was rice in the bin, and Ammachi, Devu's grandmother, caught fish from the back step. But there was only one block of jaggery in the tin. It was dark brown and no bigger than her little brother Appu's fist. Jaggery was for the evening coffee. Without it the coffee was black and bitter, and Appu, who was five, made a face like a squeezed lime.

Ammachi put the block on the wooden board. 'We do not know how many evenings the boat will stay away,' she said. 'So this is the rule. Each evening we use half of what is left. Only half.' She pressed the big knife down through the middle. One half went into the pot. The other half went back into the tin.

That evening the coffee was so sweet it was nearly payasam. Devu lay awake afterwards, thinking about the rule. Half of what is left. Tomorrow, half of the half. Then half of that. 'Appu,' she whispered, 'it will never finish. There is always a half left to cut.' Appu was pleased. He fell asleep believing in jaggery for ever.

On the second evening Ammachi cut the half in half. Devu looked at the piece that went into the pot. 'That is one quarter of the block we began with,' she said. The coffee was sweet enough. But the piece that went back into the tin was the same size as the piece in the pot, and Devu did not like how small it looked.

In the morning she took a piece of charcoal from the stove and drew a long box on the dry top step. That was the whole block. She drew a line down the middle and rubbed out one side. First evening, one half. She cut the side that was left in two. Second evening, one quarter. Then she kept going. The third evening would get one eighth. The fourth would get one sixteenth, a piece like a tamarind seed. The fifth would get a crumb.

'Ammachi is right,' she told Appu. 'It never finishes.' She looked at her drawing again. 'And after tomorrow it is never enough.' The first evening had eaten half of everything. The fifth evening would get a piece too small to taste. The rule looked after the block. It did not look after the evenings.

She took the drawing to Ammachi, who looked at it for a long time. 'Then tell me how many evenings,' Ammachi said. 'If I knew it was four, I would cut four equal pieces. If I knew it was ten, I would cut ten. To make equal parts, child, you must first know how many parts. I do not know. So I halve.'

Devu went back to the step and sat with her chin on her knees. How many evenings? Then she saw the jetty post. On the day the path went under, she had tied a red thread round the post at the top of the water, to see what the water would do. The thread was dry now. She measured down from it with her hand. The water had dropped one hand in two days. She pushed a stick down beside the path. The path was still two hands under. One hand in two days. Two hands. 'Four days,' she said out loud. 'Four more evenings, if the water keeps going the way it is going.'

Ammachi came and measured with her own hand, which was bigger, and got her own answer, which was nearly the same. She looked at the sky. 'The water has not read your thread,' she said. But she fetched the tin.

She put the last piece on the board, the quarter of the old block. It was the whole of what they had now. Devu held her breath. Ammachi did not cut it into four. She cut it into five. 'Four for your four evenings,' she said. 'And one for the evening the water makes a fool of you.' Five equal pieces lay in a row, each a little smaller than a fourth would have been. Devu worked it out on the step. Each piece was one fifth of what was left. It was one twentieth of the block they had started with.

For four evenings the coffee was the same: not very sweet, but sweet, and the same for each evening. Appu stopped making the lime face on the second day. On the fourth morning the path was still under a finger of water. Devu said nothing. Ammachi said nothing either, but she tapped the tin.

On the fifth evening the last piece went into the pot. They were drinking the coffee on the step when they heard it, far down the canal, coming closer: a cycle bell.

**For the parent:** For the parent: there are two honest ways to share out something when you cannot get more. Halving what is left never runs out, but each share is half the one before: one half, one quarter, one eighth. Equal parts give every evening the same amount, but you can only cut equal parts when you know how many parts you need. Devu finds the number by measuring the water, and Ammachi cuts five parts, not four, to be safe. Five parts are each a little smaller than four parts would be. The 'whole' also changes in the story. The quarter that is left becomes the new whole, and one fifth of it is one twentieth of the first block.

- Ask: Ammachi's rule was 'half of what is left'. What was good about that rule, and what went wrong with it?
- Ask: Why could Ammachi not cut equal pieces until Devu had measured the water?
- Ask: Ammachi cut five pieces and not four. Each piece was smaller. Was that a good choice? What would you have done?

### g1 · Bigger Share (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 32 cards. Shuffle them. Deal them out equally, so each player has a pile with the fraction side up. Do not look at the backs.; Each player reads the top card of their own pile out loud, for example 'one quarter of 20'.; Each player says how many their card is worth. Use seeds or draw a bar if you need to. Then turn the card over to check.; The player whose card is worth the most takes all the cards from that round and puts them in a winnings pile.; If two players tie for the most, each of them keeps their own card.; A player who said the wrong number before checking cannot win that round. Their card goes to the best of the other players.; Play until the piles are used up. The player with the most cards in their winnings pile wins.; Playing alone: lay out two cards. Say which is worth more before you check. Score 1 point each time you are right. There are 16 pairs, so try to beat your last score out of 16.

  *Parent does:* Play one practice round together with the cards '1/2 of 10' and '1/5 of 20'. Share out seeds for each card. It does not count.

  *Child mode:* Practise first. One card says one half of 10. The other says one fifth of 20. Which is worth more? Share out seeds to check.
  - Ask: How did you find one fifth of 20? (good: I shared 20 into 5 equal groups and counted one group.; 20 divided by 5 is 4.)
  - Check: Which is worth more: one half of 10 or one fifth of 20? → **One half of 10**

  *Child mode:* Read your top card out loud. Say how many it is worth. Turn it over and check. The bigger number takes the cards.
  - Note: After a few rounds many children stop using seeds for halves and quarters. Let that happen by itself. If a card is too hard, draw a bar, split it into equal boxes and fill in the boxes together.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay out the cards '1/2 of 4' and '1/8 of 40' side by side. Talk about which won more often in the game: cards with halves, or cards with big groups.  
  *Parent says:* Did the half cards always win? Look at these two. Which is worth more? What decides it?  
  *Child mode:* Look at two cards. One says one half of 4. The other says one eighth of 40. Which is worth more? Why?
  - Ask: Why can one eighth beat one half? (good: Because it is one eighth of a much bigger group.; The whole is different, so you cannot just compare the fractions.)
  - Check: Which is worth more: one half of 4 or one eighth of 40? → **One eighth of 40**
### g2 · Small Number, Big Share (15 min)
- **rules:** Put 12 seeds in a row in the middle. These 12 seeds are the whole. They stay there for the whole game.; On your turn, roll one dice. Find your roll on the board.; Share the 12 seeds into that many equal groups. Count the seeds in one group. That is your score for this turn.; Write your score on the score sheet and add it to your total. Push the seeds back into a row for the next player.; If the seeds cannot be shared equally with none left over, you score nothing on that turn.; The first player to reach 20 or more wins. If you play alone, count how many rolls you need to reach 20. Then try to beat that.; Second game: use 24 seeds as the whole, and play to 40.
- **Printable board: 6 roll squares:** Roll 1: share the 12 into 12 equal groups. Score one twelfth of 12.; Roll 2: share the 12 into 2 equal groups. Score one half of 12.; Roll 3: share the 12 into 3 equal groups. Score one third of 12.; Roll 4: share the 12 into 4 equal groups. Score one quarter of 12.; Roll 5: try to share the 12 into 5 equal groups. If you cannot, score nothing.; Roll 6: share the 12 into 6 equal groups. Score one sixth of 12.
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player's name; Roll; Fraction of 12; Seeds scored; Total so far
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 12 seeds or beans in a row. 2. Put the board and one dice beside them. 3. Give each player a column on the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* These 12 seeds are our whole. The dice tells us how many equal groups to make. Your score is one group.  
  *Child mode:* Put 12 seeds in a row. They are the whole. Get one dice and the score sheet.
  - Ask: Before we play: which roll do you hope for, a big number or a small one? Why? (good: Any guess with a reason. Do not correct it yet.)
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns. Roll, share the 12 seeds into that many equal groups, count one group, and write the score. Play until someone reaches 20.  
  *Parent says:* Roll the dice. Share the 12 into that many equal groups. How many are in one group? What fraction of the 12 is that?  
  *Child mode:* Roll the dice. Share the 12 seeds into that many equal groups. Count one group. Write your score.
  - Note: The scores are 1, 6, 4, 3, 0 and 2 for rolls of 1 to 6. On a roll of 5, let your child try. They will get 5 groups of 2 with 2 seeds left over. Twelve cannot be shared into 5 equal groups of whole seeds. Do not tell them the scores. Let them find each one with the seeds.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the score sheet together. Ask which roll gave the most seeds and which gave the fewest. Then ask about the roll of 1.  
  *Parent says:* Which roll was the best to get? Is that the roll you hoped for at the start? Why does a roll of 6 give so few?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your scores. Which roll gave the most seeds? Which gave the fewest? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why does a 2 score more than a 6? (good: With 2 groups, each group gets more seeds.; Six groups have to share the same 12 seeds, so each group is small.)
  - Check: You share 12 seeds into equal groups and score one group. Which roll scores the most? → **A roll of 2**
  - Think: A roll of 1 made 12 groups and scored 1. What if a roll of 1 meant 'share into 1 group'? How much would you score then?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Strip Test: more parts, bigger or smaller? (35 min)
- **problem:** A fraction is one or more equal parts of a whole. You will cut five strips that are all 24 cm long into different numbers of equal parts, and measure one part of each. Then you will do the same with 24 groundnuts.
- **question:** When the same whole is cut into more equal parts, what happens to the size of each part?
- **predict:** Before you cut anything, guess. Which will be longer: one third of a strip, or one eighth of the same strip? Say why you think so, and write your guess down.
- **fair test:** Every strip must start at exactly 24 cm, so that every strip is the same whole. The only thing you change is the number of equal parts. For the groundnuts, always start with all 24.
- **record:** Fill in the table, one row for 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 parts. Then write one sentence that begins: 'When there are more equal parts, each part is…'
- **results table:** Number of equal parts | Length of one part of the strip (cm) | Groundnuts in one group (out of 24) | Name of one part
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason before any cutting. Write the guess at the top of the results sheet. Do not say whether it is right.  
  *Parent says:* We will cut one strip into 3 equal parts and one into 8 equal parts. Which part will be longer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. One strip is cut into 3 equal parts. Another is cut into 8. Which part is longer? Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will be longer? (good: Any reason is fine at this stage.; Many children say 'an eighth, because 8 is more than 3'. Write it down and let the test answer it.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut 6 strips, each exactly 24 cm long. Keep one whole. 2. Mark the others with a ruler. For 2 parts, mark every 12 cm. For 3 parts, every 8 cm. For 4 parts, every 6 cm. For 6 parts, every 4 cm. For 8 parts, every 3 cm. 3. Cut along the marks.  
  *Parent says:* Each strip is the same whole. We cut this one into 2 equal parts, this one into 3, then 4, 6 and 8. Keep each strip's pieces together.  
  *Child mode:* Cut 6 strips, each 24 cm long. Keep one whole. For 2 parts, mark at 12 cm. For 3 parts, mark every 8 cm. For 4 parts, every 6 cm. For 6 parts, every 4 cm. For 8 parts, every 3 cm. Cut on the marks. Keep each strip's pieces in its own heap.
  - Note: Help with the measuring, since small errors spoil the result. A quick check: stack the pieces from one strip. They should match. Younger hands may need help cutting the 3 cm pieces.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the whole strip at the top. 2. Under it, lay one piece from each cut strip, in order from longest to shortest. 3. Measure each piece and fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Put one piece from each strip under the whole strip, longest first. Measure each one. What do you notice as the number of parts goes up?  
  *Child mode:* Lay one piece from each strip under the whole strip. Put the longest first. Measure each piece. Write the lengths in your table. What do you notice?
  - Ask: What happens to the pieces as the number of parts goes up? (good: They get shorter.; More parts, smaller parts.)
  - Ask: Was your guess right? (good: An honest look back at the guess, whichever way it went.)
  - Note: The lengths should be 12, 8, 6, 4 and 3 cm for 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 parts. Do not explain yet. Let your child say what they see first.
  - Check: From strips of the same length, which piece is longer? → **One third of a strip**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count out 24 groundnuts. 2. Share them into 2 equal groups and count one group. Write it in the table. 3. Do the same for 3, 4, 6 and 8 groups, starting with all 24 each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now the whole is 24 groundnuts. Share them into 2 equal groups. How many in one group? Now try 3 groups, then 4, 6 and 8.  
  *Child mode:* Count out 24 groundnuts. Share them into 2 equal groups. Count one group and write it down. Now do 3 groups. Then 4, 6 and 8.
  - Ask: Look at the two columns of numbers in your table. What do you notice? (good: They are the same numbers.; The centimetres and the groundnuts match, because both wholes are 24.)
  - Note: The groups should hold 12, 8, 6, 4 and 3 groundnuts. These match the strip lengths, because the strip was 24 cm and there are 24 groundnuts. Wait for your child to spot this.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 12 of the groundnuts away. 2. Ask what one quarter of the 12 that are left will be. 3. Share them into 4 equal groups to check.  
  *Parent says:* One quarter of 24 was 6. Now there are only 12 groundnuts. What do you think one quarter of these will be? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Put 12 groundnuts away. Now the whole is 12. Guess one quarter of 12. Then share them into 4 equal groups to check.
  - Ask: One quarter was 6 before, and now it is 3. Why did it change? (good: The whole changed from 24 to 12.; A quarter of a smaller group is a smaller number.)
  - Check: One quarter of 24 groundnuts is 6. What is one quarter of 12 groundnuts? → **3**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the fraction on each paper piece: 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6 or 1/8. Fill in the last column of the table. Keep all the pieces in an envelope.  
  *Parent says:* Each piece has a name. This strip was cut into 6 equal parts, so each piece is one sixth. Write 1 above a line and 6 below it.  
  *Child mode:* Write its fraction on every piece. One of 6 equal parts is one sixth. Write 1, a line, and 6 under it. Keep the pieces in an envelope.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think now, what I saw, and why I think it happens.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now about more parts? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happens?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What do I think now? What did I see? Why does it happen?
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: more equal parts means smaller parts. What I saw: one third of the strip was 8 cm and one eighth was 3 cm. Why: the strip is the same, so with more parts each part gets less of it.
  - Think: If you cut the 24 cm strip into 24 equal parts, how long would one part be? What about 48 parts?
### e2 · A Quarter of the Water (25 min)
- **problem:** Equal parts must be equal in amount. With water, we often judge the amount by how high it stands. You will test whether that works in a pan with a round bottom and in a glass with straight sides.
- **question:** Is one quarter of the water always one quarter of the height?
- **predict:** You will pour 4 equal cups of water into a kadai, one after another. Before you start, guess. After the first cup, will the water be a quarter as deep as at the end, less than a quarter, or more? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Use the same cup every time and fill it to the brim every time, so each cup is the same amount. Keep the pan flat on the table. Always measure in the middle, where the water is deepest.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then write: in which one was the first cup a quarter of the final depth?
- **results table:** Pan or glass | Depth after 1 cup (cm) | Depth after 2 cups (cm) | Depth after 3 cups (cm) | Depth after 4 cups (cm)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the empty kadai and the cup. Explain that 4 cups will go in. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Four equal cups will go in. So one cup is a quarter of the water. After one cup, how deep will it be, compared with the end? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Four equal cups of water will go in the kadai. One cup is a quarter of the water. Will it reach a quarter of the way up? Guess, and say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Any reason. Many people say 'a quarter of the way up, because it is a quarter of the water'.)
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the cup to the brim and pour it into the kadai. 2. Hold the stick upright in the middle and mark the wet line with a pen. 3. Pour a second, third and fourth cup, marking the stick each time. 4. Measure the four marks with a ruler.  
  *Parent says:* Pour one full cup. Dip the stick straight down in the middle. Mark where the wet part ends. Now the next cup.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the cup to the top. Pour it in the kadai. Dip the stick in the middle and mark the wet line. Do this 4 times. Measure the marks.
  - Note: In a round-bottomed kadai the first cup often reaches about half of the final depth, and each new cup adds less height than the one before. If your pan has a flat bottom and sloping sides, the difference is small, only a few millimetres. A rounder bottom gives a clearer result.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dry the stick or turn it round. 2. Use the small measure and the straight glass. Pour in 4 equal measures, marking the depth after each. 3. Measure the marks.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same test in a glass with straight sides. Four equal measures. Mark the stick after each one. What do you see this time?  
  *Child mode:* Now use the straight glass and the small measure. Pour in 4 equal measures. Mark the stick each time. Measure the marks.
  - Ask: How are the marks different from the marks for the kadai? (good: They are evenly spaced.; Each measure lifts the water by the same amount.)
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the two sets of marks side by side. Ask your child to explain the difference. Listen first, then look at the shapes of the kadai and the glass together.  
  *Parent says:* Each cup was the same amount of water. So why did the first cup stand so tall in the kadai? Look at its shape.  
  *Child mode:* Look at both sets of marks. Each cup was the same amount. Why did the first cup stand taller in the kadai? Look at its shape.
  - Ask: Why does the same amount of water stand taller at the bottom of the kadai? (good: The kadai is narrow at the bottom, so the water has less room to spread.; Higher up it is wide, so the same water makes a thin layer.)
  - Check: A kadai is narrow at the bottom and wide at the top. It is filled to half its height. How much of the water is in it? → **Less than half of a full kadai**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why does it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What do I think now? What did I see? Why does it happen?
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a quarter of the water is not always a quarter of the height. What I saw: in the kadai the first cup came half-way up, and in the glass it came a quarter of the way up. Why: the kadai is narrow at the bottom.
  - Think: How could you mark a kadai so that the marks show true quarters of the water?

## Activities
### a1 · Fraction Hunt (15 min)
- **problem:** Many things at home come in equal parts: a strip of tablets, an egg tray, the panes of a window, the hours on a clock. For each one you will say what the whole is, how many equal parts it has, and what fraction one part is.
- **record:** Make a list of 8 things. For each, write the whole, the number of equal parts, and the fraction for one part.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the hunt and make three columns on the paper: the whole, the number of equal parts, and what one part is.  
  *Parent says:* Let's hunt for things that are already in equal parts. For each one, tell me the whole, and how many equal parts it has.  
  *Child mode:* Hunt for things in equal parts. Find 8. For each, say the whole. Then count its equal parts.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Walk round the home together. Look at windows, tiles, an egg tray, a strip of tablets, a clock, a calendar, a ludo board and an idli plate.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a strip of 10 tablets. What is the whole? What fraction is one tablet? Are all the parts equal?  
  *Child mode:* Look at windows, tiles, trays, clocks and boards. Count the equal parts. One part of 10 equal parts is one tenth.
  - Ask: The segments of an orange are not all the same size. Is one segment a fraction of the orange? (good: Not one we can name exactly, because the parts are not equal.; It is a part, but not one tenth or one eighth.)
  - Note: Good finds: one pane of a 6-pane window is one sixth. One tablet of a strip of 10 is one tenth. One day is one seventh of a week. One hour mark on a clock is one twelfth of the way round. Talk about things with unequal parts too, such as the segments of an orange. They are parts, but not a fraction you can name.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the list back together. Ask which thing had the most equal parts and which had the fewest.  
  *Parent says:* Which of your finds had the most equal parts? Is one part of that a big fraction or a small one?  
  *Child mode:* Read your list. Which thing has the most equal parts? Is one part of it big or small?
  - Check: A week has 7 days. What fraction of a week is one day? → **One seventh**
### a2 · Neer Mor by Quarters (20 min)
- **problem:** Cooks use fractions all the time: half a cup, a quarter of a spoon. You will mark a straight glass in quarters with a folded paper strip. Then you will mix a drink that is one quarter curd and three quarters water.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the ginger.
- **record:** Write your family's recipe as fractions of a glass: how much curd and how much water. Say which mix you liked best.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut a paper strip as tall as the glass. 2. Fold it in half, then in half again. Open it. 3. Tape it up the side of the glass. Draw a line on each fold.  
  *Parent says:* Fold the strip in half, and in half again. How many equal parts is it in now? Each part is one quarter of the glass.  
  *Child mode:* Cut a strip as tall as the glass. Fold it in half. Fold it in half again. Open it and tape it on the glass.
  - Ask: You folded in half twice. How many equal parts are there? (good: Four.; Half of a half is a quarter.)
  - Note: The glass must have straight sides. In a glass that is wider at the top, a quarter of the height is less than a quarter of the drink.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spoon curd into the glass up to the first line. Tip it into the jug. 2. Fill the glass with water up to the third line. Tip it into the jug. 3. Add a pinch of salt and whisk.  
  *Parent says:* Fill curd to the first line. That is one quarter of a glass. Now water to the third line. That is three quarters. Whisk them together.  
  *Child mode:* Fill curd up to the first line. Tip it in the jug. Fill water up to the third line. Tip it in. Add a pinch of salt and whisk.
  - Ask: One quarter of curd and three quarters of water. How much drink is that altogether? (good: Four quarters, which is one whole glass.)
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour out a little to taste. 2. Make a second mix: curd to the second line, and water to the second line. 3. Taste both. Add crushed curry leaves or ginger to the one you like.  
  *Parent says:* Now try half curd and half water. Which one do you like more? Which is thicker? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Taste your neer mor. Now make another: half a glass of curd, half a glass of water. Taste both. Which do you like?
  - Ask: Why is the second drink thicker? (good: It has a bigger fraction of curd.; Half is more than a quarter of the same glass.)
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Decide on the family recipe together. Write it down as fractions of a glass.  
  *Parent says:* Which recipe shall we keep? Write it as fractions. How much curd, and how much water?  
  *Child mode:* Choose your best recipe. Write it down as fractions of a glass. How much curd? How much water?
  - Check: A glass is one quarter curd. The rest is water. What fraction of the glass is water? → **Three quarters**
### a3 · Clap Adi Tala in Halves and Quarters (15 min)
- **problem:** Musicians and dancers count time in equal parts. Adi tala, the most common tala in Carnatic music, has 8 beats in three sections: 4 beats, then 2 beats, then 2 beats. You will find what fraction of the cycle each section is.
- **record:** Draw a strip of 8 boxes. Colour the first 4 boxes one colour, the next 2 another, and the last 2 a third. Write the fraction under each section.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit down. Keep the tala slowly together, counting out loud from 1 to 8. Beat 1 is a clap on the thigh. Beats 2, 3 and 4 are finger taps. Beats 5 and 7 are claps. Beats 6 and 8 are the hand turned palm up.  
  *Parent says:* Clap, little finger, ring finger, middle finger. Clap, turn. Clap, turn. That is 8 beats. Now again, without stopping.  
  *Child mode:* Clap on your thigh. Tap little finger, ring finger, middle finger. Clap, turn your hand over. Clap, turn. Count 1 to 8 as you go.
  - Note: If you know the tala, lead. If not, learn it together slowly. It takes a few rounds. An even speed matters more than a fast one.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a strip of 8 equal boxes. 2. Colour the boxes for the first section, beats 1 to 4. 3. Colour beats 5 and 6 in a second colour, and beats 7 and 8 in a third.  
  *Parent says:* The whole cycle is 8 beats. The first section is 4 beats. What fraction of the cycle is that? What about each short section?  
  *Child mode:* Draw 8 equal boxes in a row. Colour boxes 1 to 4. Use a new colour for 5 and 6. Use another for 7 and 8.
  - Ask: The first section fills 4 of the 8 boxes. What fraction of the cycle is it? (good: One half, because 8 beats in 2 equal parts gives 4 each.)
  - Ask: Each short section is 2 beats. What fraction is that? (good: One quarter, because 8 beats in 4 equal parts gives 2 each.)
  - Check: Adi tala has 8 beats. One beat is what fraction of the cycle? → **One eighth**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep the tala for 4 cycles. Your child says 'half' on beat 1, and 'quarter' on beats 5 and 7.  
  *Parent says:* Now say 'half' when the long section starts, and 'quarter' when each short section starts. Keep the beat even.  
  *Child mode:* Keep the tala going. Say 'half' on beat 1. Say 'quarter' on beat 5. Say 'quarter' on beat 7.
  - Think: The beats are equal parts of time, not of paper. How do you know two beats are equal when you cannot lay them side by side?
### a4 · My Day in 24 Boxes (20 min)
- **problem:** The Earth turns round once in a day. People cut that one day into 24 hours. You will use the hours as equal parts and find fractions of your own day.
- **record:** Under your strip, write a fraction of the day for at least three things, such as 'Sleep: 8 hours, one third of my day'.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a strip 24 cm long and 2 cm tall. Mark a line at every centimetre, to make 24 boxes. Explain that each box is one hour.  
  *Parent says:* This strip is one whole day. It has 24 equal boxes. Each box is one hour. What fraction of a day is one hour?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a strip 24 cm long. Mark every centimetre. Now you have 24 boxes. Each box is one hour of the day.
  - Ask: What fraction of the day is one hour? (good: One twenty-fourth, because 24 equal parts make the whole day.)
- **s2 (8 min)**

### a5 · One Quarter, Three Shapes (15 min)

  - Ask: How would you cut a circle into real quarters? (good: Fold it in half, then fold the half in half the other way, so the folds cross in the middle.)

### c1 · Three Dosas, Four People (25 min)

  - Ask: One plate has 3 quarters. Another has 1 half and 1 quarter. Are they the same amount? (good: Yes. A half covers two quarters exactly, so both plates have three quarters.)
  - Check: Three dosas are shared equally among 4 people. How much does each person get? → **Three quarters of a dosa**

### c2 · I Cut, You Choose (20 min)
- **problem:** It is easy to cut a paper strip into halves, because you can fold it. A banana, a mango or a lump of dough has no fold line. Find a way for two people to share one so that neither can say the other got more.
- **safety:** Use a blunt butter knife, not a sharp one.
- **your turn:** Find your own rule for 3 people to share one lump of dough so that nobody complains. Try it with real people. Did it work?
- **constraints:** There is no ruler and no weighing scale.; Both people must agree it was fair before anyone eats.; Only one cut is allowed.
- **general:** One person cuts and the other chooses first. The cutter then tries hard to make the two parts equal, because the cutter gets the part that is left.; Check afterwards by laying the parts side by side, or by holding one in each hand.
- **creative:** Roll the dough into an even snake first. Then fold a thread to find the middle of its length.; With 3 people it is harder. One way: the first person cuts off what they think is one third. Anyone who thinks it is too big may trim it. The last person to touch a piece keeps it.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the banana or dough on a plate between two people. Ask how to share it so both are happy. Listen to every idea.  
  *Parent says:* We have one banana and two people. There is no ruler. How can we share it so neither of us can complain?  
  *Child mode:* One banana. Two people. No ruler. How can you share it so nobody complains? Say your ideas.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try the rule. Your child cuts, and you choose first. Then swap with a second banana or a fresh lump of dough, so you cut and your child chooses.  
  *Parent says:* You cut, and I choose first. Think about where to cut. Then we swap.  
  *Child mode:* You cut. The other person chooses first. Cut carefully. Then swap over and try again.
  - Ask: When you were the cutter, why did you try to make the parts equal? (good: Because the other person would take the bigger part and I would get the smaller one.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the two parts side by side. Ask whether they are equal, and equal in what way: length, thickness or weight.  
  *Parent says:* Are these two halves? How can we tell? Is the longer one always the bigger one?  
  *Child mode:* Lay the two parts side by side. Are they equal? Is the longer part always the bigger part?
  - Ask: A banana is thin at the ends and fat in the middle. Is half the length always half the banana? (good: Not always. A short fat part can be as much as a long thin part.)
  - Check: In 'I cut, you choose', why does the cutter try to cut two equal parts? → **The cutter gets the part that is left**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask how the rule could work for 3 people. Try an idea with dough if there is a third person at home.  
  *Parent says:* This rule works for two. What could three people do? Try your idea with the dough.  
  *Child mode:* The rule works for 2 people. What could 3 people do? Think of a rule. Try it with dough.
  - Think: Two people can both feel they got the bigger half. How can that be?

### Equal Parts and Unit Fractions (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A dosa is cut into 4 pieces. When is one piece exactly one quarter?  
   Options: Only when the 4 pieces are equal / Always, because there are 4 pieces / Only when the dosa is round / Never  
   **Answer:** Only when the 4 pieces are equal. *Why:* A quarter is one of 4 equal parts. If the pieces are unequal, there are 4 pieces but no quarters. Picking 'always' usually means counting pieces without checking that they are equal.
2. (fill) A strip is cut into 6 equal parts. One part is one ____ of the strip.  
   **Answer:** ['sixth', '6th']. *Why:* The name comes from the number of equal parts. Six equal parts give sixths, so one part is one sixth, written 1/6.
3. (match) Match each fraction name to the way it is written.  
   **Answer:** one half → 1/2; one third → 1/3; one quarter → 1/4; one eighth → 1/8; one tenth → 1/10. *Why:* The top number is 1, because it is one part. The bottom number is the number of equal parts in the whole: 2 for halves, 3 for thirds, 4 for quarters, 8 for eighths and 10 for tenths.
4. (mcq) In the fraction 1/5, what does the 5 tell you?  
   Options: The whole is cut into 5 equal parts / You have 5 parts / The part is 5 cm long / There are 5 wholes  
   **Answer:** The whole is cut into 5 equal parts. *Why:* The bottom number tells how many equal parts make the whole. The top number, 1, tells how many parts you have. Picking 'you have 5 parts' mixes up the top and bottom numbers.
5. (order) These are parts of the same strip. Put them in order, from the SHORTEST piece to the LONGEST.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* More equal parts means shorter parts. The strip cut into 8 has the shortest pieces, then 6, then 4, then 3. The strip cut into 2 has the longest pieces.
6. (truefalse) One eighth of a chapati is bigger than one third of the same chapati.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A chapati cut into 8 equal parts has smaller parts than the same chapati cut into 3. Thinking it is true usually means reading the 8 as a size.
7. (fill) One half of 12 groundnuts is ____ groundnuts.  
   **Answer:** ['6']. *Why:* Share 12 into 2 equal groups. Each group has 6. So one half of 12 is 6.
8. (fill) One third of 12 groundnuts is ____ groundnuts.  
   **Answer:** ['4']. *Why:* Share 12 into 3 equal groups. Each group has 4. So one third of 12 is 4.
9. (fill) One quarter of 20 marbles is ____ marbles.  
   **Answer:** ['5']. *Why:* Share 20 into 4 equal groups. Each group has 5. So one quarter of 20 is 5.
10. (mcq) How do you find one sixth of 18 bananas?  
   Options: Share 18 into 6 equal groups and count one group / Take 6 away from 18 / Share 18 into 3 equal groups / Add 6 to 18  
   **Answer:** Share 18 into 6 equal groups and count one group. *Why:* The bottom number, 6, tells you how many equal groups to make. 18 shared into 6 equal groups gives 3 in each, so one sixth of 18 is 3. Picking 'take 6 away' treats the 6 as an amount to remove.
11. (sort) Sort these. Are the parts EQUAL or NOT EQUAL?  
   **Answer:** a square folded corner to corner → Equal parts; a circle cut by 3 lines side by side → Not equal parts; a strip folded end to end → Equal parts; a banana cut into a long piece and a short piece → Not equal parts; a rectangle folded in half twice → Equal parts; a dosa torn without looking → Not equal parts. *Why:* Folding a shape so that one side lies exactly on the other makes equal parts. Lines side by side across a circle leave fat middle pieces and thin end pieces. Tearing and guessing do not give equal parts.
12. (draw) Draw a rectangle. Split it into 3 equal parts. Shade one part and write its fraction.  
   **Answer:** A rectangle with 2 lines that split it into 3 parts of the same size. One part is shaded. The label is 1/3 or 'one third'.. *Why:* Three equal parts are thirds. One shaded part is one third. A strong drawing has parts that really look equal.
13. (write) A friend says 1/8 is bigger than 1/3, because 8 is bigger than 3. What would you show her?  
   **Answer:** I would take two strips of the same length. I would cut one into 3 equal parts and one into 8 equal parts, and lay one piece of each side by side. The eighth is much shorter. The 8 means the strip was shared into more parts, so each part is smaller.. *Why:* A strong answer shows it with the same whole cut two ways, and says what the bottom number counts.
14. (mcq) A tray holds 6 eggs. What fraction of the tray is one egg?  
   Options: 1/6 / 6/1 / 1/2 / 1/5  
   **Answer:** 1/6. *Why:* The tray is the whole and it has 6 equal places. One egg is one of 6 equal parts, so it is 1/6. Picking 1/5 usually means counting only the other 5 eggs.
15. (draw) Draw 12 small circles for 12 laddus. Put a ring round one quarter of them.  
   **Answer:** 12 circles with a ring round 3 of them. A strong drawing first splits the 12 into 4 equal groups of 3.. *Why:* One quarter of 12 is found by sharing 12 into 4 equal groups. Each group has 3.
16. (truefalse) Two quarters of the same square sheet must have the same shape.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A quarter can be a strip, a small square or a triangle. Quarters must be the same amount, not the same shape.
17. (write) You are offered half of one dosa or a quarter of another dosa. Why can you not tell which is more?  
   **Answer:** Because I do not know how big each dosa is. Half of a small dosa can be less than a quarter of a very big dosa. A fraction only tells me how many equal parts there are, not how big the whole is.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the two wholes may be different sizes, so the fractions alone cannot be compared.
### Fractions in the Market, the Clock and the Kitchen (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (fill) A kilogram is 1000 grams. One quarter of a kilogram is ____ grams.  
   **Answer:** ['250']. *Why:* Share 1000 into 4 equal parts. Half of 1000 is 500, and half of 500 is 250. In a Tamil Nadu market this is 'kaal kilo'.
2. (fill) A day has 24 hours. One third of a day is ____ hours.  
   **Answer:** ['8']. *Why:* Share 24 into 3 equal groups. Each group has 8. So one third of a day is 8 hours.
3. (mcq) A rupee is 100 paise. Which coin was one quarter of a rupee?  
   Options: The 25 paise coin / The 50 paise coin / The 20 paise coin / The 10 paise coin  
   **Answer:** The 25 paise coin. *Why:* 100 shared into 4 equal parts gives 25. So 25 paise is one quarter of a rupee. The 50 paise coin is one half of a rupee.
4. (mcq) Which is more: one half of 6 or one quarter of 20?  
   Options: One half of 6 / One quarter of 20 / They are the same  
   **Answer:** One quarter of 20. *Why:* One half of 6 is 3. One quarter of 20 is 5. The quarter is more here, because it is a quarter of a much bigger whole. Picking 'one half of 6' means comparing the fractions and forgetting the wholes.
5. (truefalse) One half is always more than one quarter, whatever the two wholes are.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. One half of 8 is 4, but one quarter of 40 is 10. A half is more than a quarter only when both are fractions of the same whole.
6. (order) Put these in order, from the SMALLEST amount to the BIGGEST.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Work each one out. One third of 9 is 3. One half of 8 is 4. One quarter of 20 is 5. One tenth of 60 is 6. The tenth is the biggest amount, because its whole is so big.
7. (match) A bag has 30 tamarind seeds. Match each fraction of the bag to its number of seeds.  
   **Answer:** 1/2 of 30 → 15; 1/3 of 30 → 10; 1/5 of 30 → 6; 1/6 of 30 → 5; 1/10 of 30 → 3. *Why:* Each time, share 30 into as many equal groups as the bottom number. More groups give fewer seeds in each group.
8. (mcq) Adi tala has 8 beats. Its first section has 4 beats. What fraction of the cycle is that?  
   Options: One half / One quarter / One eighth / Four  
   **Answer:** One half. *Why:* 8 beats split into 2 equal parts gives 4 beats in each part. So 4 beats is one half of the cycle. Picking 'one quarter' usually comes from seeing the number 4.
9. (fill) A class of 24 children makes 4 equal teams. Each team is one ____ of the class.  
   **Answer:** ['quarter', 'fourth']. *Why:* The class is the whole. It is split into 4 equal parts, so each part is one quarter. Each team has 6 children.
10. (mcq) One third of a string of beads is 5 beads. How many beads are on the whole string?  
   Options: 15 / 8 / 5 / 2  
   **Answer:** 15. *Why:* If one third is 5 beads, the whole string is 3 equal groups of 5. That is 15 beads. Picking 8 means adding 3 and 5. Here you go from the part back to the whole.
11. (write) You have 12 murukkus. Explain how to find one third of them.  
   **Answer:** I share the 12 murukkus into 3 equal groups, giving one to each group in turn until they are used up. Each group has 4. One group is one third, so one third of 12 is 4.. *Why:* A strong answer says to make 3 equal groups, because the bottom number is 3, and then counts one group.
12. (draw) Draw a bar for 20 rupees. Split it into 5 equal boxes. How many rupees go in each box?  
   **Answer:** A long box labelled 20, split into 5 equal boxes with 4 written in each. So one fifth of 20 rupees is 4 rupees.. *Why:* The bar is the whole. Five equal boxes share the 20 rupees, so each holds 4. One box is one fifth.
13. (perspective) Two children share a bag of 10 guavas. Some guavas are big and some are small.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both children have shared equally, but by different measures. One counted guavas and the other compared weight. It picks one and gives a reason, such as 'by weight, because we eat the fruit, not the number'. It also says the other way is equal too, in its own measure. The two must agree what to measure before they share.. *Why:* A fraction needs equal parts, and 'equal' needs a measure: number, weight, length or something else. People choose the measure. Once it is chosen, the sharing can be checked.
14. (mcq) Four equal cups of water go into a round-bottomed kadai. After 1 cup, the water is half-way up. Why?  
   Options: The kadai is narrow at the bottom / The first cup had more water / Water shrinks as you add more / The kadai was not flat  
   **Answer:** The kadai is narrow at the bottom. *Why:* Each cup is the same amount. At the narrow bottom the water has little room to spread, so it stands tall. Higher up, the kadai is wide, and the same amount makes a thin layer.
15. (write) Two children each eat one quarter of their own chikki bar. One of them eats more chikki. How can that be?  
   **Answer:** Their chikki bars are different sizes. One quarter of a big bar is more chikki than one quarter of a small bar. The fraction is the same but the wholes are different.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the two wholes are different, so the same fraction gives different amounts.
16. (truefalse) 12 beads can be shared into 5 equal groups with no beads left over.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Five groups of 2 use 10 beads and leave 2 over. 12 can be shared equally into 2, 3, 4, 6 or 12 groups, but not into 5.
### Beyond One Part (challenge) (challenge, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A strip is cut into 4 equal parts, and 3 of them are shaded. What fraction is shaded?  
   Options: 3/4 / 1/3 / 1/4 / 4/3  
   **Answer:** 3/4. *Why:* The bottom number is the number of equal parts in the whole, which is 4. The top number is how many parts are shaded, which is 3. So it is 3/4, three quarters. Picking 4/3 puts the numbers the wrong way up.
2. (fill) One quarter of 12 beads is 3 beads. So three quarters of 12 beads is ____ beads.  
   **Answer:** ['9']. *Why:* Three quarters is 3 lots of one quarter. One quarter is 3 beads, so three quarters is 3 and 3 and 3, which is 9.
3. (fill) Fold a sheet of paper in half 3 times. Open it. It has ____ equal parts.  
   **Answer:** ['8']. *Why:* The first fold makes 2 parts. The second fold doubles them to 4. The third fold doubles them to 8. Each part is one eighth of the sheet.
4. (mcq) What does each fold in half do to the parts of a sheet of paper?  
   Options: Twice as many parts, each half as big / Twice as many parts, each twice as big / One more part, each a bit smaller / The same number of parts  
   **Answer:** Twice as many parts, each half as big. *Why:* Every part is folded in two, so the number of parts doubles and each part is half the size it was. The parts go 2, 4, 8, 16. Picking 'one more part' usually means adding when the parts are doubling.
5. (fill) Half of one half is one ____.  
   **Answer:** ['quarter', 'fourth']. *Why:* Cut a whole into 2 halves. Cut each half into 2 equal parts. Now there are 4 equal parts, so each is one quarter.
6. (mcq) Two rotis are shared equally among 4 people. How much roti does each person get?  
   Options: One half of a roti / One quarter of a roti / Two rotis / One eighth of a roti  
   **Answer:** One half of a roti. *Why:* Cut each roti into 2 halves. That makes 4 halves, one for each person. Picking 'one quarter' usually means sharing only one of the rotis.
7. (truefalse) One half of a dosa and one quarter of the same dosa together make three quarters of it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. One half covers 2 quarters exactly. Add one more quarter and you have 3 quarters. Egyptian scribes wrote three quarters this way.
8. (order) Work each one out. Then put them in order, from the LEAST to the MOST.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Work each one out. One half of 16 is 8. One third of 27 is 9. One tenth of 100 is 10. One quarter of 44 is 11. The smallest fraction here does not give the smallest amount.
9. (mcq) One fifth of a number is 6. What is the number?  
   Options: 30 / 11 / 1 / 65  
   **Answer:** 30. *Why:* If one fifth is 6, the whole is 5 equal groups of 6. That is 30. Picking 11 means adding 5 and 6.
10. (write) Can you cut a strip of paper in half for ever? Answer for real paper, and then for numbers.  
   **Answer:** With real paper, no. After about 10 cuts the piece is too small to hold or cut, and in the end you would reach the tiny particles the paper is made of. With numbers, yes. Half of 1/8 is 1/16, half of that is 1/32, and there is always another half.. *Why:* A strong answer separates real things, which have a smallest piece you can cut, from numbers, which can always be halved again.
11. (draw) Draw a square. Shade one quarter of it in a shape that is not a square and not a straight strip.  
   **Answer:** One good answer is a triangle made by drawing both corner-to-corner lines and shading one of the 4 triangles. Other shapes work too, as long as the shaded part is exactly one of 4 equal amounts.. *Why:* A quarter must be one of 4 equal amounts. It does not have to be any special shape.
12. (mcq) A first quarter Moon looks half lit. What is the 'quarter' a quarter of?  
   Options: The Moon's trip round the Earth / The Moon's bright face / The night / The sky  
   **Answer:** The Moon's trip round the Earth. *Why:* Astronomers mean that the Moon has gone one quarter of the way round the Earth since the new Moon. The face we see is half lit. So the same Moon is a half or a quarter, depending on which whole you choose.
13. (match) Match the number of equal parts to the name of one part.  
   **Answer:** 5 equal parts → one fifth; 12 equal parts → one twelfth; 20 equal parts → one twentieth; 100 equal parts → one hundredth. *Why:* The name of a unit fraction comes from the number of equal parts in the whole. This works for any number of parts, however many.
14. (perspective) Two people argue about where fractions come from.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses evidence. For the first: we chose the whole each time, 24 groundnuts or 12, and we chose how to write 1/4. For the second: once the whole was chosen, one quarter of 24 was 6 whoever counted. It then says what the other side has right. Some children say both: the names and the wholes are chosen, and what follows is not.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement among mathematicians and philosophers, and nobody has settled it. What matters is using evidence and answering the other side fairly.
15. (draw) Draw a strip in 4 equal parts. Shade one half red and one quarter blue. How much is shaded?  
   **Answer:** A strip of 4 equal boxes with 2 boxes red and 1 box blue. Three of the 4 boxes are shaded, so three quarters of the strip is shaded.. *Why:* One half of the strip is 2 of the 4 boxes. One quarter is 1 box. Together they are 3 boxes out of 4, which is three quarters.
16. (sort) Sort these numbers of beads. Can they be shared into 3 equal groups with none left over?  
   **Answer:** 9 → Yes; 10 → No; 12 → Yes; 14 → No; 21 → Yes; 25 → No. *Why:* 9, 12 and 21 are in the 3 times table, so they share into 3 equal groups of 3, 4 and 7. With 10, 14 and 25 some beads are left over, so one third of them is not a whole number of beads.
17. (write) 12 sweets share equally among 2, 3, 4 or 6 people. 10 share only among 2 or 5. Which is better?  
   **Answer:** 12 is better if I do not know how many people will share, because it can be shared equally among 2, 3, 4 or 6 people. 10 only works for 2 or 5 people. This may be why eggs and bangles are sold by the dozen, and why a day was cut into 24 hours.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that a number with more ways to share equally is more useful for sharing. A child who picks 10 with a good reason, such as a family of 5, is also right.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these shows one third?  
   Options: A roti cut into 3 equal pieces, 1 taken / A roti cut into 3 unequal pieces, 1 taken / 3 whole rotis  
   **Answer:** A roti cut into 3 equal pieces, 1 taken. *Why:* One third is one of 3 equal parts of a whole. Unequal pieces are not thirds. Three whole rotis are three wholes, not a fraction.
2. (truefalse) If a whole is split into parts that are not equal, the parts cannot be named as halves, thirds or quarters.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The names of fractions only work for equal parts. Two unequal pieces are 'a big piece and a small piece', not two halves.
3. (mcq) How is one sixth written?  
   Options: 1/6 / 6/1 / 6 / 1/60  
   **Answer:** 1/6. *Why:* One part goes on top. The 6 equal parts of the whole go underneath. Picking 6/1 puts the numbers the wrong way up.
4. (mcq) In the fraction 1/8, what does the 8 tell you?  
   Options: How many equal parts make the whole / How many parts you have / How big each part is  
   **Answer:** How many equal parts make the whole. *Why:* The bottom number counts the equal parts in the whole. The top number counts the parts you have. The bottom number is not a size. A bigger bottom number means smaller parts.
5. (mcq) Three idlis of the same size are cut up. Which piece is biggest?  
   Options: 1/2 of an idli / 1/5 of an idli / 1/10 of an idli  
   **Answer:** 1/2 of an idli. *Why:* The idlis are the same size. The one cut into only 2 equal parts has the biggest parts. Picking 1/10 usually means reading the 10 as a size.
6. (truefalse) Cutting the same cake into more equal pieces makes each piece smaller.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The cake does not grow, so more pieces must share the same amount, and each gets less.
7. (fill) One quarter of 16 tamarind seeds is ____ seeds.  
   **Answer:** ['4']. *Why:* Share 16 into 4 equal groups. Each group has 4. So one quarter of 16 is 4.
8. (mcq) What is one fifth of 15 rupees?  
   Options: 3 rupees / 5 rupees / 10 rupees / 75 rupees  
   **Answer:** 3 rupees. *Why:* Share 15 into 5 equal groups. Each group has 3. Picking 5 usually means using the bottom number as the answer. Picking 10 means taking 5 away. Picking 75 means multiplying.
9. (mcq) What do you do to find one third of 21 shells?  
   Options: Share 21 into 3 equal groups, count one / Take 3 shells away from 21 / Share 21 into 7 equal groups, count one  
   **Answer:** Share 21 into 3 equal groups, count one. *Why:* The bottom number is 3, so make 3 equal groups. Each has 7 shells. One group is one third. Taking 3 away is subtraction, which answers a different question.
10. (mcq) Which is more: one half of 8 laddus or one quarter of 40 laddus?  
   Options: One half of 8 laddus / One quarter of 40 laddus / They are the same  
   **Answer:** One quarter of 40 laddus. *Why:* One half of 8 is 4 laddus. One quarter of 40 is 10 laddus. The wholes are different, so the bigger fraction does not give the bigger amount.
11. (mcq) Someone offers you 'one quarter'. What must you ask before you know how much you will get?  
   Options: A quarter of what? / Is a quarter more than a half? / How do you write a quarter?  
   **Answer:** A quarter of what?. *Why:* A quarter is one of 4 equal parts of some whole. Until you know the whole, you do not know the amount. A quarter of a kilogram and a quarter of a spoonful are very different.
12. (mcq) One paper square is folded into 4 triangles. Another like it is folded into 4 small squares. Which part has more paper?  
   Options: One triangle / One small square / They have the same amount  
   **Answer:** They have the same amount. *Why:* Each is one quarter of the same size of square, so they are the same amount of paper. Equal parts need the same amount, not the same shape.
13. (truefalse) A bowl is narrow at the bottom and wide at the top. Water half-way up it is half of a full bowl.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The narrow bottom half holds less than the wide top half. Height shows the amount only when the sides are straight.
14. (mcq) Which of these did people choose, so that it could have been different?  
   Options: Writing one quarter as 1/4 / Four quarters of a thing make the whole thing / 1/8 of a strip is shorter than 1/3 of it  
   **Answer:** Writing one quarter as 1/4. *Why:* The way of writing is a choice. Egyptian scribes and Indian mathematicians wrote fractions differently. The other two follow from what a fraction means, whoever does the sum and however they write it.
15. (mcq) Why does a day have 24 hours?  
   Options: People long ago chose to cut the day into 24 parts / The Earth turns 24 times in a day / The Sun has 24 sides  
   **Answer:** People long ago chose to cut the day into 24 parts. *Why:* The Earth turns once in a day. Cutting that one turn into 24 equal parts was a choice. In India people also cut the day into 60 parts called nazhigai.

## Applications
- Markets: 'kaal kilo' is a quarter of a kilogram and 'arai kilo' is half. Shopkeepers weigh these fractions all day.
- Cooking: half a cup of rice, a quarter of a spoon of turmeric, three quarters of a glass of water.
- Telling the time: half past, a quarter past and a quarter to are fractions of an hour.
- Music and dance: a tala is a cycle of beats, and its sections are fractions of the cycle.
- Money: a 50 paise coin was half a rupee. 'Half price' means one half of the price.
- Tailoring and patchwork: a tailor folds cloth in half and in quarters to find the middle and to cut equal panels.
- Sharing food: one dosa for 2, a packet of 12 biscuits for 4, a watermelon for 8.
- Medicine: a doctor may tell a grown-up to give half a tablet. The tablet has a line so it breaks into equal parts.
- Sport: a football match has two halves. Many basketball and kabaddi matches are split into halves or quarters of equal time.
- Astronomy: the first quarter and last quarter Moon are named for fractions of the Moon's trip round the Earth.
- Buses and trains: a child's 'half ticket' costs about half of the full fare.
- Farming and land: old Tamil land records used small fractions with their own names, such as mundiri, 1 part in 320.

## Spotted around us
- At the vegetable shop: listen for 'kaal kilo' and 'arai kilo', a quarter and a half of a kilogram.
- On a strip of tablets or a bar of chocolate: equal parts made in a factory, ready to be counted as fractions.
- On a clock face: the minute hand at 3 has gone a quarter of the way round, and at 6 it has gone half-way.
- In the evening sky: a Moon that looks like a half circle. Astronomers call it a quarter Moon.
- At a music or dance class: hands keeping Adi tala, 8 beats in sections of 4, 2 and 2.
- In the kitchen: cut a lady's finger across. The plant has usually made 5 nearly equal chambers. A star fruit usually has 5 ridges.
- In a hen's yard: chicks only a few days old can learn to pick out a proportion, such as 4 to 1. Scientists in Italy tested this in 2016.
- In a window, a tiled floor or a kolam grid: one whole made of many equal parts.

## Open questions
- **Can space itself be cut in half for ever?** Numbers can always be halved. Real stuff cannot, because it is made of particles. For space itself, some physicists think there may be a smallest length. No experiment has shown whether there is.
- **When a chimpanzee or a chick picks out a half or a proportion, what is it like for them?** Experiments show what these animals choose. They do not show what the animals experience, or whether a half means to them what it means to us.
- **Were fractions invented by people, or discovered?** Mathematicians and philosophers have argued about this for more than 2,000 years. Both sides have good reasons, and no test has been found that would settle it.
- **Why did Egyptian scribes write nearly every fraction as a sum of unit fractions?** The scribes left tables of answers but no explanation of why they worked this way. Historians have several ideas and no record that decides between them.

## Connected maths
- Tomatoes cost 40 rupees for one kilogram. How much does a quarter of a kilogram cost? **10 rupees.**. A quarter is one of 4 equal parts. Share the 40 rupees into 4 equal parts. 40 divided by 4 is 10. So a quarter of a kilogram costs 10 rupees. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L3`)
- You shared 24 groundnuts into 8 equal groups and found 3 in each group. Write this as a fraction sentence and as a division sentence. **One eighth of 24 is 3. 24 divided by 8 is 3.**. Sharing 24 into 8 equal groups is division: 24 divided by 8 is 3. One of those groups is one eighth of the 24. So the two sentences say the same thing in two ways. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- An hour has 60 minutes. How many minutes are in a quarter of an hour, and in three quarters of an hour? **15 minutes, and 45 minutes.**. Share 60 minutes into 4 equal parts. 60 divided by 4 is 15. So a quarter of an hour is 15 minutes. Three quarters is 3 lots of 15 minutes, which is 45 minutes. (queues `math.time.telling-time.L3`)
- A glass holds 200 millilitres. Your neer mor is one quarter curd and the rest water. How many millilitres of curd and of water do you need? **50 millilitres of curd and 150 millilitres of water.**. Share 200 into 4 equal parts. 200 divided by 4 is 50. One part is curd, so the curd is 50 millilitres. The other 3 parts are water. 3 lots of 50 is 150 millilitres. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L3`)
- You start with 32 groundnuts. Each day you eat half of what is left. How many are left after each day? On which day is only 1 left? **16, 8, 4, 2, 1. One groundnut is left after day 5.**. Half of 32 is 16. Half of 16 is 8. Half of 8 is 4. Half of 4 is 2. Half of 2 is 1. That is 5 halvings. Each number is half of the one before it. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- A ribbon is 1 metre long, which is 100 centimetres. It is cut into 5 equal pieces. How long is each piece, and what fraction of the ribbon is it? **Each piece is 20 centimetres long. It is one fifth of the ribbon.**. Share 100 centimetres into 5 equal parts. 100 divided by 5 is 20. One of 5 equal parts is one fifth. You can check: 5 pieces of 20 centimetres make 100 centimetres. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
