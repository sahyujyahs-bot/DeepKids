# Coins and notes: read the number, count the rupees
`math.money.money-in-life.L1`

**Summary.** Seven coins are in one hand and two coins are in the other. Which hand has more money? You cannot tell by counting the coins. You will run a chana shop at home to find out why, and you will see one coin beat eight. You will learn to read coins and notes, count the rupees in a small heap, make the same amount in different ways, and check whether you have enough to buy a thing. You will also find out what makes a small round piece of metal worth ten rupees.

**Intro.** *Which hand holds more money, A or B?* Hand B holds more. Hand A has seven coins, but each one is ₹1, so that is ₹7. Hand B has only two coins, but each one is ₹5, so that is ₹10. Counting coins tells you how many coins there are. It does not tell you how much money there is. For that, you read the number on each coin and count the rupees. In this unit you will test this yourself with real coins and a plate of chana.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the coins ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20, and the notes from ₹10 to ₹500, by reading the number on them.
1. I can say that the number on a coin tells how much it is worth, not its size or how many coins there are.
2. I can count the rupees in a small heap of coins, up to ₹20.
3. I can make the same amount of money in different ways.
4. I can tell whether I have enough money to buy a thing, by comparing my money with the price.
5. I can say that a coin or a note is worth something because people agree that it is.

## Terms
- **Money**: What people give to get things and to pay for work. Coins and notes are money. The number in a bank account is money too. *e.g.* Appa gives the shopkeeper ₹10. The shopkeeper gives him a packet of biscuits.
- **Coin**: A small flat piece of metal that is money. A number on the coin tells how many rupees it is worth. *e.g.* India has coins of ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20.
- **Note**: A piece of printed paper that is money. A number on the note tells how many rupees it is worth. *e.g.* A ₹10 note and a ₹10 coin buy exactly the same things.
- **Rupee**: The name of India's money. We count money in rupees, the way we count length in steps or hand spans. The sign for rupees is ₹. *e.g.* ₹5 is read as 'five rupees'. The sign ₹ was drawn by D. Udaya Kumar, a designer from Tamil Nadu, and chosen in 2010.
- **Value**: How much a coin or a note is worth. You find the value by reading the number on it. *e.g.* The value of a ₹5 coin is five rupees. One ₹5 coin has the same value as five ₹1 coins.
- **Price**: How many rupees you must give to buy a thing. *e.g.* The price of the pencil is ₹6. On a packet, the price is printed after the letters MRP.
- **Amount**: How much money there is in all, when you count the rupees. *e.g.* A ₹5 coin and a ₹2 coin together make an amount of ₹7.
- **Swap**: To give one thing and get another thing back. You can swap coins for other coins of the same value. *e.g.* You give five ₹1 coins and take one ₹5 coin. You still have five rupees.
- **Enough**: You have enough when your money is the same as the price, or more than the price. *e.g.* The chikki costs ₹8. You have ₹10. Ten is more than eight, so you have enough.
- **Buy and sell**: To buy is to give money and take a thing. To sell is to give a thing and take money. Every time someone buys, someone else sells. *e.g.* You buy a banana for ₹5. The fruit seller sells a banana for ₹5. It is one swap, seen from two sides.

## Explanations
### The number on the coin tells its value · *defined*
**Definition:** The value of a coin is the number of rupees written on it. To find how much money you have, count the rupees, not the coins.

Every coin has a number on it. A ₹1 coin is worth one rupee. A ₹5 coin is worth five rupees, so one ₹5 coin buys as much as five ₹1 coins. This is why two coins can be more money than seven coins. Here is a worked example. Hand A holds seven ₹1 coins. Count the rupees: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. That is ₹7. Hand B holds two ₹5 coins. Count the rupees: 5, 10. That is ₹10. Ten is more than seven, so hand B holds more money. The size of a coin does not tell you its value either. A ₹10 coin and a ₹20 coin are the same width. Only the number tells them apart. Now you try: which is more, three ₹2 coins or four ₹1 coins?
- e.g. One ₹10 coin is worth more than eight ₹1 coins. Ten is more than eight.
- e.g. Three ₹2 coins are ₹6. Four ₹1 coins are ₹4. The three coins are more money.
- e.g. A ₹10 note and a ₹10 coin look nothing alike. Both have the number 10, so both are worth ten rupees.
### The coins of India · *defined*
**Definition:** India has five coins in use: ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20.

Look for the number first. It is on one side of the coin, next to the sign ₹. The other side shows the lions of the Ashoka pillar, which is the sign of India. The newest coins also differ in colour and shape, as the table shows. Coins made in other years can look different. An old ₹5 coin may be thick and silver. So do not trust the colour or the size. Always read the number. There is no ₹3 coin and no ₹7 coin. To pay ₹3 you use a ₹2 coin and a ₹1 coin together.
- e.g. The ₹20 coin has 12 straight sides. All the other coins are round.
- e.g. The ₹10 coin has two colours: a golden ring around a silver middle.
- e.g. The newest ₹1 coin is the smallest. It is 20 millimetres across.
### The notes of India · *defined*
**Definition:** India has six notes in common use: ₹10, ₹20, ₹50, ₹100, ₹200 and ₹500.

A note is money printed on paper. The number is printed in more than one place on each side, so you can always find it. The front of every note shows Mahatma Gandhi. The back shows a famous place in India, and each note has its own colour. On the back there is also a box where the value is written in 15 Indian languages. A note is worth much more than the paper it is made of. A ₹500 note and a ₹10 note are made of the same kind of paper, but one buys fifty times as much. Older notes with other colours are still used, so read the number.
- e.g. The ₹50 note is blue. Its back shows the stone chariot at Hampi in Karnataka.
- e.g. The ₹10 note is brown. Its back shows the Sun Temple at Konark in Odisha.
- e.g. One ₹20 note is worth the same as two ₹10 coins.
### Counting a heap of coins · *defined*
**Definition:** To count mixed coins, start with the coin of the biggest value and count on for each of the other coins.

Here is a worked example. You have a ₹1 coin, a ₹5 coin and two ₹2 coins. First put them in order, biggest value first: ₹5, ₹2, ₹2, ₹1. Now touch each coin and count on. Touch the ₹5 and say 5. Touch a ₹2 and say 7. Touch the next ₹2 and say 9. Touch the ₹1 and say 10. The last number you say is the amount: ₹10. Starting with the biggest coin means you count on by small steps at the end, which is easier. If you forget where you are, push each coin away as you count it. Now you try: ₹10, ₹5, ₹2.
- e.g. ₹10, ₹5, ₹2: say 10, 15, 17. The amount is ₹17.
- e.g. ₹2, ₹2, ₹2: say 2, 4, 6. This is counting in twos.
- e.g. ₹5, ₹5, ₹1: say 5, 10, 11. The amount is ₹11.
### The same amount, many ways · *defined*
**Definition:** One amount of money can be made with different coins. All the ways have the same value.

Take ₹10. You can pay it with one ₹10 coin. You can pay it with two ₹5 coins, because 5 and 5 make 10. You can pay it with five ₹2 coins, or with ten ₹1 coins, or with a mix such as ₹5, ₹2, ₹2 and ₹1. The shopkeeper gets ten rupees every time. So you can swap coins without losing or gaining money: five ₹1 coins for one ₹5 coin, or two ₹5 coins for one ₹10 coin. Shopkeepers do this all day. With ₹1, ₹2, ₹5 and ₹10 coins there are 11 different ways to make ₹10.
- e.g. ₹6 can be 5 + 1, or 2 + 2 + 2, or 2 + 2 + 1 + 1.
- e.g. A ₹20 note, two ₹10 coins and four ₹5 coins are all twenty rupees.
- e.g. If you have no ₹5 coin, you can still pay ₹5 with ₹2, ₹2 and ₹1.
### Do I have enough? · *defined*
**Definition:** You have enough money when your amount is the same as the price or more than the price.

First count your money. Then read the price. Then compare the two numbers. Here is a worked example. You have a ₹5 coin and a ₹2 coin. That is ₹7. A chikki costs ₹8. Seven comes before eight when you count, so seven is less than eight. You do not have enough. You need ₹1 more. A balloon costs ₹2. Two is less than seven, so you have enough for the balloon, and money will be left over. You can spend up to the amount you have, and no more. If you want two things, add the two prices first, then compare.
- e.g. You have ₹10. The biscuits cost ₹10. The numbers are the same, so you have just enough.
- e.g. You have ₹15. The ball costs ₹20. Fifteen is less than twenty, so you cannot buy the ball yet.
- e.g. You have ₹20. A balloon for ₹2 and a chikki for ₹8 cost ₹10 together. You can buy both.
### Why can a coin buy things? · *debated*
**Definition:** A coin or a note can buy things because people agree to take it.

A ₹10 coin is a small piece of metal. You cannot eat it or wear it. The shopkeeper takes it because she knows the milkman will take it from her, and the milkman knows the bus conductor will take it from him. Everyone trusts that the next person will take it. A note even says so: read the line 'I promise to pay the bearer' on any note. When the agreement changes, the money changes. On 8 November 2016, the government of India said that the ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes of that time would stop being money at midnight. The paper was the same the next morning, but shops no longer took it. People changed the old notes for new ones at banks.
- e.g. A coin from another country is real money there, but a shop here will not take it.
- e.g. When a grown-up pays with a phone, no coin moves. Only numbers change in two bank accounts, and both people trust the numbers.
- e.g. Long ago, people in Odisha and Bengal used small cowrie shells as money, because everyone there agreed to take them.
- **People who say money is an agreement:** Money is worth something because people trust each other to take it. Shells, paper and numbers on a phone have all worked as money.
- **People who say the government makes money:** A note is worth something because the government prints it, takes it for taxes and says shops must take it.
- **People who say money must be worth something by itself:** The best money is made of silver or gold, which people want anyway. Old rupee coins were made of silver.
### What a price counts, and what it leaves out · *idea*
**Definition:** A price pays the people who worked to bring a thing to you. It does not pay the sun, the rain or the soil.

You give ₹5 and take a banana. Where does the ₹5 go? Some of it goes to the fruit seller. Some goes to the lorry driver who brought the bananas to town, and some to the farmer who grew them. But the banana plant needed more than people. They needed sunlight every day, rain or water from a well, and soil full of tiny living beings. Nobody pays the sun. Nobody pays the rain. So a price tells you how much work people did and how much they ask for it. It does not tell you everything a thing is worth. Air is free, and you could not live five minutes without it.
- e.g. A glass of water at home costs almost nothing. On a very hot day it can be worth more to you than a toy.
- e.g. A mango tree gives fruit to birds, squirrels and ants without asking for money.
- e.g. A drawing you make for Paati has no price, and she may keep it for years.
### Other living beings trade too · *idea*
**Definition:** To trade is to give one thing and get another thing back. Many living beings trade, and some give more to those who give them more.

At the Uluwatu temple in Bali, Indonesia, long-tailed macaques take things from visitors: glasses, phones, slippers, hair clips. A monkey keeps the thing until a person hands over food. Then they give it back. Scientists led by Jean-Baptiste Leca watched them for many months and wrote about it in 2021. The older monkeys took the things people want back most, such as glasses and phones. They often waited for more food, or better food, before they gave such a thing back. Young monkeys were not so careful. They learn it as they grow, by trying and by watching others. Under the ground there is another trade. A plant gives sugar to a fungus living on their roots, and the fungus gives the plant a food from the soil called phosphorus. In 2011 Toby Kiers and her team found that the plant gives more sugar to a fungus who gives more phosphorus, and the fungus does the same in return. This is not money. No coin is passed. But it is giving more for more, and that is the idea under every price.
- e.g. A macaque at Uluwatu may wait for more food before giving back a phone, and give back a hair clip for less.
- e.g. A bee takes sweet nectar from a flower and carries the flower's pollen to the next flower.
- e.g. A small cleaner fish eats the pests off a big fish. The big fish gets cleaned, and the cleaner gets a meal.
- **The scientists who studied the Uluwatu macaques:** They call it a token economy. The monkeys use the stolen things like tokens that can be traded for food, and they know which tokens are worth more.
- **Scientists who are more careful with words:** They say words like 'trade' and 'market' come from human life. The monkeys, plants and fungi may be doing something of their own that only looks like our trade from outside.

## Mostly agreed
- The number on a coin tells how much it is worth. The size does not. *How we know:* Measure a ₹10 coin and a ₹20 coin. Both are 27 millimetres wide. One buys twice as much as the other, and only the number tells you which.
- A few coins can be more money than many coins. *How we know:* You tested it in the Two Plates Test. One ₹10 coin bought 10 chana, and eight ₹1 coins bought only 8.
- The same amount of money can be made in many ways, and every way buys the same. *How we know:* Try it in the chana shop. Two ₹5 coins buy 10 chana. So do five ₹2 coins, and so do ten ₹1 coins.
- A note or a coin is money only while people agree to take it. *How we know:* On 8 November 2016 the old ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes stopped being money at midnight. The paper did not change. The agreement did.
- People are not the only living beings who trade. *How we know:* Scientists watched macaques at the Uluwatu temple in Bali give stolen things back in exchange for food (Leca and others, 2021). Plants and root fungi trade sugar for phosphorus (Kiers and others, 2011).

## How we found out
**Caroline Humphrey, who checked a story told by Adam Smith and many others, 1776 and 1985.** For a long time, people who wrote about money told one story. Long ago, they said, nobody had money, and everyone swapped things: a pot for a fish, a goat for a sack of rice. Swapping was hard, so people invented coins. A Scottish thinker, Adam Smith, wrote this in a famous book in 1776. But nobody had checked. In 1985 Caroline Humphrey, who studies how people live at the University of Cambridge, read the reports from villages all over the world. She wrote that no one had ever described a people who lived by swapping alone. In small villages, people mostly gave things to their neighbours and remembered who owed what. So keeping count of who owes whom may be older than coins. The first coins we know of in India are small pieces of silver with marks stamped on them, made about 2,500 years ago. The word rupee comes from the rupiya, a silver coin made by the ruler Sher Shah Suri in the 1540s.  
*What people believed before:* Before money, everyone lived by swapping things. In 1985 Caroline Humphrey showed that nobody had ever found a people who lived that way.

## Challenging perspectives
- **More coins means more money.** (Many young children, and sometimes a grown-up in a hurry; *rejected*)  
  For: With coins that are all the same kind, it is true. Seven ₹1 coins are more than five ₹1 coins.  
  Against: With mixed coins it fails. Two ₹5 coins are ₹10, and seven ₹1 coins are only ₹7. You must read each coin and count the rupees.
- **Real money must be made of something costly, like silver or gold.** (Many people in history, and a few thinkers about money today; *minority*)  
  For: Silver and gold stay wanted even when people stop trusting a government. Old rupee coins were made of silver, and people trusted them for hundreds of years.  
  Against: Steel coins, paper notes and numbers on a phone all work as money today, as long as people agree to take them. Cowrie shells worked too.
- **Before there was money, everyone got what they needed by swapping things.** (Adam Smith in 1776, and many school books since; *debated*)  
  For: Swapping is hard, as the Swap Game shows. Each person must want what the other has. So it makes sense that people would invent money to fix this.  
  Against: In 1985 Caroline Humphrey looked for a people who lived by swapping alone and found that nobody had ever described one. In small villages people mostly share, and remember who owes whom.

## Roots
We read coins, counted rupees and ran a chana shop. Now let us think about what a coin really is.

**Deep truth.** In the Two Plates Test, one ₹10 coin bought more chana than eight ₹1 coins. The ₹10 coin is not ten times as big or as heavy. It is worth ten because everyone agrees that it is. So the worth of a coin is not inside the coin. It is a promise that people keep with each other.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a coin is worth ten by itself, the way a mango is sweet by itself. But a coin is worth ten only while people agree to take it for ten.  
*Evidence:* In the Two Plates Test, one coin beat eight coins. And in 2016 the old ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes stopped being money in one night, though the paper had not changed.

- *who am i:* You are one of the people who make money work. Each time you take a coin and trust it, you keep the promise going.
- *what is life:* Every living being gives and takes. A bee takes nectar and carries pollen for the flower. People made coins to keep count of their giving and taking.
- *what is this world:* A ₹5 banana needed the sun, the rain, the soil, a banana plant and a farmer. The ₹5 pays only the people. The sun and the rain ask for nothing.

Money starts from one rupee. Two rupees is one and one more. A ₹10 coin is ten ones made into a single coin. So one coin can hold many, and many coins can be swapped for one. The one and the many are the same amount.

- **Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book 5, chapter 5:** Aristotle, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,300 years ago, says that money exists by agreement and not by nature. He says people have the power to change it, or to make it useless. (This is what the Two Plates Test and the old ₹500 notes both show. The worth is in what people agree, not in the metal or the paper.)
- **Tao Te Ching 33:** The Tao Te Ching, an old Chinese book, says: the one who knows they have enough is rich. (In this unit you asked 'Do I have enough?' and answered it by counting. Enough is not the biggest heap. It is the amount that meets the need.)
- **Thirukkural 247:** Thiruvalluvar, the Tamil poet, says: this world is not for those who have no wealth, and the other world is not for those who have no kindness. (Money buys what a shop sells, and a family needs it. The Kural puts kindness beside it as the other thing a person needs, and no coin can buy that.)
- Maths (settled): On 8 November 2016, the government of India announced that the ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes of that time would stop being money at midnight. The paper stayed the same. Only the agreement changed.
- Maths (debated): What makes money worth something? Some say trust between people. Some say the government's rule. Some say it must stand for something useful, like gold. People who study money still argue about it.
- Other view, Many people through history, and a few thinkers about money today: They say good money must be worth something by itself. Old rupee coins were made of silver, and the silver could be melted and sold. A paper note is only a promise, and a promise can be broken. Silver and gold stay worth something even when people stop trusting each other.

- Ask: If everyone forgot what coins were for, would a ₹10 coin still be worth ten?
- Ask: A coin is worth ten because we all agree. What else is true only because we all agree?
- Ask: Is there anything you would not swap for any amount of money? Why?
- Ask: The rain asks for nothing. Is there a way to give something back to the rain?

## Story: Nine Coins and One
*Malar, Anbu and the top seller are made up. The santhai, a weekly market, is real in towns and villages across Tamil Nadu, and Melur is a town near Madurai. The prices are made up for the story.*

On Sunday the santhai came to the tank road outside Melur. It came every Sunday: heaps of brinjal and drumstick, coils of rope, clay pots, glass bangles, and one old woman who sold wooden tops.

Malar was six. Her cousin Anbu was eight. Paati had given each of them money for the market, tied in the corner of a cloth.

Anbu untied his cloth before they had even left the house. Coins poured into his hand, silver and jingling. "One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine," he counted. "Nine coins!"

Malar untied hers. Two coins lay in the cloth. One was thick, with a yellow ring around a silver middle. The other was small and plain.

"Two," said Anbu. "Only two! I have nine. I am rich and you are poor." He shook his fist so that the coins rang. All the way down the lane he rang them. Malar's two coins made no sound at all. She held them so tightly that the big one left a round mark on her palm.

At the market they went straight to the top seller. Her tops stood in rows on a blue sheet, painted with red and green rings. She wound a string around one and flicked her wrist. The top landed on the hard ground and hummed.

"How much, Paati?" asked Anbu.

"Ten rupees for one top."

Anbu grinned at Malar. "I will take one. Maybe I will take two." He poured his nine coins onto the sheet.

The top seller did not count them the way Anbu had. She pushed them across the sheet with one finger, one at a time. "One rupee. Two rupees. Three." Each coin had a small 1 on it. "Seven. Eight. Nine." Her finger stopped. There were no more coins to push.

"Nine rupees, kanna. A top is ten. You need one more rupee."

Anbu stared at the sheet. Nine coins had felt like so many.

"And you, little one?"

Malar opened her hand. She put the thick coin with the yellow ring on the sheet. Just that one. The top seller picked it up, looked at it, and dropped it into her tin. Then she held out a top with green rings.

"One coin?" said Anbu. "She gave you one coin!"

"Look at it," said the top seller. She took the coin out of the tin again and turned it to the light. There was a number on it. It was not a 1. It was a 1 with a 0 beside it. "This one coin is ten rupees. It is ten of yours, made into one."

Anbu looked at his nine coins, and then at the single coin. He did not ring his fist any more. He began to pick his coins up, slowly, one by one.

Malar looked at the small plain coin still in her hand. It had a 2 on it. Two rupees. Anbu needed one rupee, and she had two. If only she could break the coin in half! But a coin does not break like a murukku.

She thought about it while a goat walked past and a bus honked on the road. Two is one and one. And Anbu's coins were all ones.

"Anbu," she said. "Give me one of your coins."

"Why? Then I will have only eight!"

"Give me one. And I will give you this." She held out the 2.

Anbu frowned, but he gave her one coin. She gave him the 2. He laid his coins on the sheet again, and this time he pushed them himself, the way the top seller had. "One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight." Eight coins with a 1 on them. Then he pushed the last coin, the one with the 2. "Nine..." He stopped. He looked at the 2 again. "Nine, ten," he said. "Ten!"

"Ten rupees," said the top seller. She swept the coins into her tin and gave him a top with red rings. "Nine coins from you. One coin from her. The same ten rupees, and the same top."

They wound their strings at the side of the tank road. Anbu's cloth was empty now, and it made no sound. Malar had one small coin left, tied in the corner of hers.

"You gave me a rupee," said Anbu. "You swapped a two for a one."

"I know," said Malar. "Now pull."

They pulled. The two tops hummed side by side in the dust, and for a long time neither one fell.

**For the parent:** For the parent: counting coins is not counting money. Anbu counts nine coins and feels rich. The top seller counts nine rupees, by reading each coin as she pushes it. Malar's swap works because a ₹2 coin is two ones. When she swaps it for a ₹1 coin, she gives away exactly one rupee, without breaking anything.

- Ask: Anbu had nine coins and Malar had two. Who had more money? How do you know?
- Ask: The top seller pushed the coins one at a time and said 'one rupee, two rupees'. Why did she not just say 'nine coins'?
- Ask: Malar could not break her ₹2 coin in half. What did she do instead? Why did it work?
- Ask: At the end, how much money does Malar have left? How much does Anbu have?

  *Child mode:* Put the coins in three heaps in the middle. That is the bank.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one practice turn each. 2. Show a swap. 3. With six ₹1 coins, push five to the bank and take a ₹5 coin. 4. Say the amount: 5 and 1 is ₹6.  
  *Parent says:* I have six ₹1 coins. Five of them go to the bank, and I take a ₹5 coin. Now I have ₹6.  
  *Child mode:* Roll and take ₹1 coins. Got five? Swap them for one ₹5 coin.
  - Note: The amount does not change in a swap. Ask 'how much before?' and 'how much after?' the first few times.
  - Check: You have four ₹1 coins and you roll 3. After you swap, what do you hold? → **One ₹5 coin and two ₹1 coins**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game. 2. After each turn, the player says their amount aloud. 3. Now and then, ask how many more rupees a player needs for the next swap.  
  *Parent says:* How much do you have now? How many more rupees until your next swap?  
  *Child mode:* Play the game. After each turn, say how many rupees you have.
  - Ask: You have a ₹10 coin, a ₹5 coin and three ₹1 coins. How much is that? (good: 18.; 10, 15, 16, 17, 18.)
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When someone holds two ₹10 coins, the game ends. 2. Each player counts their rupees aloud. 3. Ask who was closest to ₹20.  
  *Parent says:* Who has two ₹10 coins? Everyone else, count your rupees. Who came closest?  
  *Child mode:* Two ₹10 coins wins. Count your rupees. How close did you get?
### g2 · Pay the Price (10 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the price cards and put them in a stack, price side up. Put the coins in a pile that everyone can reach.; On your turn, read the price on the top card. Pay that exact amount with coins from the pile.; The other players check by counting the rupees aloud. Then turn the card over to see one way to pay.; If your coins make the exact price, you keep the card. Your way does not have to match the back of the card.; If the amount is wrong, the card goes to the bottom of the stack.; Put the coins back in the pile after every turn.; When the stack is gone, the player with the most cards wins.; Playing alone: pay every card. Then go through the stack again and pay each price a different way.

- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the cards, or write them on slips of paper with the answer on the back. 2. Stack them price side up. 3. Put the coins in a pile. 4. Read the rules aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Each card is a thing in a shop, with its price. You pay the exact price with coins from this pile.  
  *Child mode:* Stack the cards with the price side up. Put the coins in a pile.
  - Note: The prices are made up for the game, so that they fit the coins. Real prices are often higher.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one practice card together. 2. Turn up 'Chikki ₹8'. 3. Show how to start with the biggest coin that fits. 4. Count on aloud: 5, 7, 8.

  *Child mode:* The chikki costs ₹8. Start with a ₹5 coin. What else do you need?
  - Check: A banana costs ₹7. Which coins pay it exactly? → **₹5 and ₹2**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game. 2. Each payment is counted aloud by another player. 3. Now and then, ask whether there is another way to pay the same price.  
  *Parent says:* Count her coins aloud to check. Is there another way to pay the same price?  
  *Child mode:* Play the game. Count the other player's coins aloud to check.
  - Ask: Can you pay ₹6 without using a ₹5 coin? (good: 2 + 2 + 2.; Yes, three ₹2 coins.; 2 + 2 + 1 + 1.)
- **s4 (1 min)**

  *Parent says:* Count your cards. Which price was the hardest to pay? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your cards. Which price was the hardest to pay?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Two Plates Test: do more coins mean more money? (25 min)
- **question:** If one plate has more coins than the other, does it always buy more?
- **predict:** Before each round, guess: which plate will buy more chana, A or B, or will they buy the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The price never changes: one rupee buys one chana. We read the number on every coin. We line up the chana from each plate in a row, so that we can count them and compare.
- **safety:** Keep coins and chana away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them. Wash hands after handling coins.
- **record:** A grown-up draws the table. For each round, write the coins on each plate, the guess, and the chana each plate bought. At the end, the child says what they found, and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** Round | Coins on plate A | Coins on plate B | My guess | Chana for A | Chana for B
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tip the coins onto the mat. 2. Ask your child what they notice. 3. Ask them to find the number on each coin. 4. Sort the coins into kinds together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at these coins. What do you notice? Can you find a number on each one?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the coins. Find the number on each one. Put the same kinds together.
  - Ask: What is different about these coins? What is the same? (good: They have different numbers.; Some are bigger.; Some are yellow and some are silver.; They all have lions on one side.)
  - Note: Let your child look and talk before you name anything. If they do not spot the number, turn one coin over and wait.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say the rule of the chana shop. 2. Your child pays you a ₹1 coin, and you give 1 chana. 3. Your child pays a ₹2 coin, and you give 2 chana. 4. Take the chana back.  
  *Parent says:* This is a chana shop. One rupee buys one chana. This coin says 2. So how many chana will it buy?  
  *Child mode:* One rupee buys one chana. Pay a ₹1 coin. Then pay a ₹2 coin. What do you get?
  - Note: This is the one thing you tell your child: the number on the coin is how many rupees it is. Everything after this they find out themselves.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put seven ₹1 coins on plate A. 2. Put two ₹5 coins on plate B. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write the guess. 4. Your child buys chana for each coin and lines them up below the plate. 5. Count both rows and write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Plate A has seven coins. Plate B has two coins. Which plate will buy more chana? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Plate A has seven coins. Plate B has two. Guess which buys more. Then buy the chana and count.
  - Ask: Why did you guess that? (good: A has more coins, so more chana.; B has fives, and five is a lot.; I do not know yet.)
  - Note: Many six-year-olds choose plate A, because seven coins look like more. Accept any guess with a reason. Say nothing about who is right. Let the two rows of chana answer: 7 and 10.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear the plates and the chana. 2. Put five ₹1 coins on plate A and three ₹2 coins on plate B. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write the guess. 4. Your child buys the chana and counts both rows.  
  *Parent says:* Now plate A has five coins and plate B has three. Which will buy more this time? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Plate A has five coins. Plate B has three. Guess, then buy the chana and count.
  - Note: Plate A buys 5 chana and plate B buys 6. The gap is small, so the rows must be counted, not just looked at.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear the plates. 2. Put eight ₹1 coins on plate A and one ₹10 coin on plate B. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write the guess. 4. Your child buys the chana and counts both rows.  
  *Parent says:* Plate A has eight coins. Plate B has just one coin. Which will buy more? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Eight coins on plate A. One coin on plate B. Guess, then buy the chana and count.
  - Ask: How can one coin buy more than eight coins? (good: It says 10 on it.; It is worth ten rupees.; It is like ten of the small ones.)
  - Note: Plate A buys 8 chana and plate B buys 10. By now many children start reading the coins before they guess. That is the discovery, so let them say it in their own words.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear the plates. 2. Put a ₹5, a ₹2 and a ₹1 coin on plate A. 3. Put four ₹2 coins on plate B. 4. Ask for a guess and a reason. 5. Your child buys the chana and counts both rows.  
  *Parent says:* Three coins on plate A and four coins on plate B. Which buys more, or is it the same? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Plate A has ₹5, ₹2 and ₹1. Plate B has four ₹2 coins. Guess, then buy and count.
  - Note: Both plates buy 8 chana. Different coins, and a different number of coins, can be the same amount of money.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child what they think, what they saw, and why. 3. Write their words under the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all four rounds. Does the plate with more coins always buy more? What did you see?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Did more coins always buy more chana? What did you find?
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why is it so? (good: More coins is not always more money.; Two coins bought 10 and seven coins bought 7.; You have to read the number on the coin.; A big number coin is like many small ones.)
  - Check: You want to know how much money is in a purse. What do you do? → **Read each coin and count the rupees**

## Activities
### a1 · Coin Rubbings: get to know each coin (10 min)
- **problem:** Coins look alike at a quick glance. What exactly is on each one?
- **safety:** Keep coins and chana away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them. Wash hands after handling coins.
- **record:** Keep the rubbings. Write the value under each one. If you cut them out, they become paper coins for the games.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay one coin on the table, number side up. 2. Hold the paper over it. 3. Your child rubs the side of the pencil over the coin. 4. Turn the coin over and rub the other side next to it. 5. Do the same for each coin.  
  *Parent says:* Rub gently over the coin. Watch the picture come up. What can you see?  
  *Child mode:* Put a coin under the paper. Rub over it with the side of your pencil. Do both sides.
  - Note: Hold the paper still, or the picture smudges. A crayon with the wrapper peeled off works well for small hands.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the rubbings together. 2. Ask your child to point to the number on each one. 3. Look for the sign ₹, the lions and the year. 4. Write the value under each rubbing.  
  *Parent says:* Where is the number on this one? What else can you find on the coin?  
  *Child mode:* Point to the number on each rubbing. Can you find the ₹ sign and the lions?
  - Ask: Which part of the coin tells you how much it is worth? (good: The number.; The 5.; The number next to the ₹ sign.)
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts out the number-side rubbings. 2. Your child lays them in a row, from the least value to the most. 3. Ask which real coin is biggest, and whether that coin is worth the most.  
  *Parent says:* Put them in order, from the coin worth the least to the coin worth the most. How do you know the order?  
  *Child mode:* Put your rubbings in a row. Start with ₹1. End with the coin worth the most.
  - Check: Two coins are the same size. How do you know which is worth more? → **Read the number on each**
### a2 · Ten Rupees, Many Ways (15 min)
- **problem:** A packet of biscuits costs ₹10. How many different ways can you pay for it?
- **safety:** Keep coins and chana away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them. Wash hands after handling coins.
- **record:** Write each way as a sum on its own line, such as 5 + 5 or 5 + 2 + 2 + 1. Count how many ways you found.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sort the coins into kinds. 2. Show the biscuit packet, or draw one, with a ₹10 tag. 3. Ask your child to pay ₹10. 4. Count the rupees together to check, and write the way down.  
  *Parent says:* These biscuits cost ₹10. Pay me ten rupees with any coins you like.  
  *Child mode:* The biscuits cost ₹10. Pay with any coins you like. Count the rupees to check.
  - Note: If you do not have enough real coins, cut circles from card and write ₹1, ₹2, ₹5 or ₹10 on each one.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give the coins back. 2. Ask for a different way to pay ₹10. 3. Check each way by counting on from the biggest coin. 4. Write each way as a sum. 5. Keep going for five minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Now pay me ₹10 again, but with different coins. How many ways can you find?  
  *Child mode:* Pay ₹10 again with different coins. Write each way. How many ways can you find?
  - Ask: How do you know this way is really ₹10? (good: I counted 5, 7, 9, 10.; 5 and 5 is 10.; It buys 10 chana.)
  - Note: There are 11 ways with ₹1, ₹2, ₹5 and ₹10 coins. Finding 4 or 5 is a good result at this age. If a way is doubted, buy chana with it: it must buy exactly 10.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the list of ways. 2. Ask which way uses the fewest coins and which uses the most. 3. Ask whether the shopkeeper gets more money from the way with the most coins.  
  *Parent says:* Which way used the fewest coins? Which used the most? Does the shopkeeper get more money that way?  
  *Child mode:* Find the way with the fewest coins. Find the way with the most. Are both still ₹10?
  - Check: You pay ₹10 with ten ₹1 coins. Your friend pays with one ₹10 coin. Who paid more? → **We paid the same**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Practise swapping. 2. Your child gives you five ₹1 coins and you give one ₹5 coin. 3. Ask whether they have more money, less money or the same. 4. Swap two ₹5 coins for one ₹10 coin.  
  *Parent says:* Give me five ₹1 coins and I will give you this ₹5 coin. Do you have more money now, less, or the same?  
  *Child mode:* Swap five ₹1 coins for one ₹5 coin. Do you have more money, less, or the same?
  - Note: Some children feel they have lost money, because five coins became one. Let them buy chana with the ₹5 coin to see that it still buys five.
### a3 · Maths in Life: pay for it yourself (20 min)
- **problem:** You have ₹20. What can you buy, and how will you pay?

- **record:** Back home, draw the thing you bought. Write its price and draw the coins you paid with.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take three packets from the kitchen shelf. 2. Help your child find the letters MRP and the number after them. 3. Read each price aloud together. 4. Ask which one costs the most.

  *Child mode:* Put your coins in a row. Start with the biggest value. Count the rupees.
  - Note: With ₹10, ₹5, ₹2, ₹2 and ₹1, the count goes 10, 15, 17, 19, 20.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Walk to a nearby shop, vegetable cart or fruit seller. 2. Your child chooses one small thing and asks its price. 3. Your child decides whether ₹20 is enough. 4. If it is, they count out the coins and pay. 5. If money comes back, they keep it in the purse.

  - Ask: The price is more than ₹20. What can you do? (good: Choose something that costs less.; Ask for a smaller one.; Come back with more money.)
  - Note: Good things to buy for ₹20 or less: one or two bananas, a lemon, a bunch of curry leaves, a pencil, a small packet of biscuits. Tell the shopkeeper your child is learning to pay. Most are glad to wait.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At home, ask your child to tell someone what they bought. 2. They say the price and which coins they used. 3. Count any money left in the purse. 4. Draw the thing and the coins.  
  *Parent says:* Tell Paati what you bought. How much did it cost? Which coins did you give?  
  *Child mode:* Tell someone what you bought and what it cost. Draw the coins you paid with.
  - Check: You have ₹20. A pencil costs ₹6. Do you have enough? → **Yes, 20 is more than 6**

### a5 · The Swap Game: shopping without money (10 min)
- **problem:** Kavya has a mango and wants a pencil. Farhan has a pencil and wants a balloon. Meena has a balloon and wants a mango. Can everyone get what they want?
- **record:** Draw the three people in a ring. Draw arrows to show who gave what to whom.

  - Check: Farhan did not want a mango. Why did he take a ₹5 coin for his pencil? → **He can buy what he wants with it**

### p1 · Our Home Shop: three days of buying and selling (30 min, 3 days)
- **problem:** You have ₹20. How much can you spend, and on what?
- **safety:** Keep coins and chana away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them. Wash hands after handling coins.
- **record:** On day 2, write what you bought and what it cost. On day 3, write a bill for one customer: the things, the prices and the total.
- **schedule:** day 1: Choose six things, write a price tag for each, and lay out the shop. (10 min); day 2: You are the customer with ₹20. Buy one thing, then two things together. (10 min); day 3: You are the shopkeeper. Check each payment by counting the rupees. (10 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose six small things together. 2. Decide a price for each: ₹2, ₹4, ₹6, ₹8, ₹10 and ₹15. 3. Your child writes each price on a slip with the ₹ sign. 4. Lay out the shop on a stool or a mat.  
  *Parent says:* This is our shop. Which thing should cost the most? Which should cost the least? Write the price on each tag.  
  *Child mode:* Choose six things for your shop. Write a price tag for each one.
  - Note: Let your child choose which thing gets which price, and ask why. There is no wrong answer: the seller decides the price.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow for day 2, when you are the customer.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child ₹20 in coins: one ₹10, one ₹5, two ₹2 and one ₹1. 2. They buy one thing and pay the exact price. 3. Give the coins back. 4. Now ask them to choose two things they can buy together with ₹20. 5. They add the two prices and pay.  
  *Parent says:* You have ₹20. First buy one thing. Then find two things you can buy together. How do you know you have enough?  
  *Child mode:* You have ₹20. Buy one thing. Then find two things you can buy together.
  - Ask: Can you buy the ₹8 thing and the ₹15 thing together? Why? (good: No, that is more than 20.; 8 and 15 is 23.; I do not have enough.)
  - Note: To add two prices, your child can lay out the coins for both and count them all, starting with the biggest. ₹8 and ₹10 make ₹18, so that pair fits. ₹8 and ₹15 make ₹23, so it does not.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow for day 3, when you are the shopkeeper.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap places. Your child is the shopkeeper. 2. Buy three things, one at a time. 3. Pay the right amount twice. 4. Once, pay ₹1 too little and see whether the shopkeeper notices. 5. Your child counts every payment aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are the shopkeeper. I want to buy this. Here is my money. Is it right?  
  *Child mode:* Now you are the shopkeeper. Count the money for each thing. Is it right?
  - Note: If your child misses the short payment, do not point it out at once. Ask them to count it once more, pushing each coin.
  - Check: The comb costs ₹8. The customer gives ₹5 and ₹2. What do you say? → **You need to give ₹1 more**

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: pay with the fewest coins (20 min)
- **problem:** A purse full of coins is heavy. For any price up to ₹20, find the way to pay that uses the fewest coins. Then find the price that needs the most coins even so.
- **your turn:** Invent three coins for a new country. Write a number on each blank circle. One of them must be 1, or some prices cannot be paid. Now pay every price from 1 to 10 with the fewest coins. Is your set better than 1, 2 and 5, or worse?
- **constraints:** You may use only ₹1, ₹2, ₹5 and ₹10 coins.; You must pay the exact price, not more.; For each price, you must be sure that no way uses fewer coins.
- **general:** Start with the biggest coin that is not more than the price. Then do the same for what is left.; Lay out any way to pay, then swap small coins for bigger ones wherever you can.; Write the prices 1 to 20 in a list and fill in the fewest coins for each.
- **creative:** Look for a pattern: which prices need only one coin? Which need two?; Ask what would change if there were a ₹3 coin. Which prices would get easier?; Make a country with only three kinds of coin and test every price from 1 to 10.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to pay ₹8 in any way. 2. Count the coins used. 3. Ask for a way with fewer coins. 4. Keep going until no one can find fewer.  
  *Parent says:* Pay me ₹8. How many coins did you use? Can you pay ₹8 with fewer coins?  
  *Child mode:* Pay ₹8. Count your coins. Now pay ₹8 with fewer coins.
  - Note: The fewest is three coins: ₹5, ₹2 and ₹1. Two coins cannot make 8: try every pair and see.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the trick: take the biggest coin that is not more than the price. 2. Then do the same for what is left. 3. Try it for ₹13, ₹17 and ₹19. 4. Write the number of coins for each.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a trick. Take the biggest coin that fits. Then look at what is left and do it again. Try ₹13.  
  *Child mode:* Take the biggest coin that fits. Then do it again for what is left. Try ₹13.
  - Note: ₹13 is 10 + 2 + 1, three coins. ₹17 is 10 + 5 + 2, three coins. ₹19 is 10 + 5 + 2 + 2, four coins.
  - Check: What is the fewest number of coins that pays ₹9? → **3 coins**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the prices 1 to 20 down the page. 2. Your child finds the fewest coins for each and writes the number. 3. Ask which prices need only one coin. 4. Ask which price needs the most coins.  
  *Parent says:* Now do every price from 1 to 20. Which prices need just one coin? Which price needs the most?  
  *Child mode:* Write 1 to 20. Find the fewest coins for each. Which price needs the most coins?
  - Note: One coin: 1, 2, 5, 10. Two coins: 3, 4, 6, 7, 11, 12, 15, 20. Three coins: 8, 9, 13, 14, 16, 17. Four coins: 18 and 19. If you count a ₹20 coin, then 20 needs one.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child three blank circles. 2. They write a number on each to make their own coins. 3. They pay each price from 1 to 10 with the fewest of their coins. 4. Compare with the real 1, 2 and 5.  
  *Parent says:* Now make your own three coins for a new country. Which numbers will you choose? Test every price up to 10.  
  *Child mode:* Make three coins of your own. Write a number on each. Pay every price from 1 to 10.
  - Ask: Why did the people who make our coins choose 1, 2, 5 and 10? (good: You can make every price.; You do not need many coins.; They are easy to add.)
  - Note: With 1, 2 and 5, no price up to 10 needs more than three coins. With 1, 5 and 10, the price ₹4 needs four coins and ₹9 needs five. With 1, 3 and 4, every price up to 10 also needs three coins or fewer. There is more than one good answer, which is why this was a choice.

## Worksheets
### Read the Coins (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (fill) Count the rupees. There are ₹____ here.  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* Each coin is ₹1. Touch each coin and count: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. That is ₹6.
2. (mcq) Which coin is worth the most?  
   Options: ₹2 / ₹10 / ₹5  
   **Answer:** ₹10. *Why:* Read the numbers. Ten is the biggest number, so the ₹10 coin is worth the most.
3. (fill) One ₹5 coin is worth the same as ____ coins of ₹1.  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* The number on the coin says 5, so it is worth five rupees. Five ₹1 coins are also five rupees.
4. (truefalse) A coin with more sides or a bigger size is always worth more.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A ₹10 coin and a ₹20 coin are the same width. Only the number on the coin tells its value.
5. (fill) Count on from the ₹5 coin. How many rupees in all? ₹____  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* Start at 5. Count on for each ₹1 coin: 6, 7, 8. That is ₹8.
6. (order) Put these coins in order, from the least value to the most.  
   **Answer:** ['₹1', '₹2', '₹5', '₹10']. *Why:* Read the numbers: 1 comes first, then 2, then 5, then 10. A number you reach later when counting is worth more.
7. (mcq) A lemon costs ₹4. You have ₹5. Can you buy it?  
   Options: Yes, 5 is more than 4 / No, 5 is less than 4 / No, I need 4 coins  
   **Answer:** Yes, 5 is more than 4. *Why:* Five is more than four, so you have enough. Picking 'I need 4 coins' means counting coins. What matters is the rupees.
8. (fill) Add these coins. They make ₹____.  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* 2 and 2 make 4. One more makes 5. Three coins, five rupees.
9. (match) Match each one to the same amount in other coins.  
   **Answer:** one ₹10 note → two ₹5 coins; one ₹20 note → two ₹10 coins; one ₹5 coin → five ₹1 coins; one ₹2 coin → two ₹1 coins. *Why:* Count the rupees on each side. Two fives are 10. Two tens are 20. Five ones are 5. Two ones are 2.
10. (draw) Draw the coins you would use to pay ₹7. Write the number on each coin.  
   **Answer:** One way is a ₹5 coin and a ₹2 coin. Any coins that add up to 7 are right.. *Why:* 5 and 2 make 7. So do 2 + 2 + 2 + 1, and seven ₹1 coins. Check a drawing by counting its rupees.
11. (write) You have two coins. Your friend has five coins. Who has more money? Say what you must know first.  
   **Answer:** We cannot tell yet. We must read the number on each coin and count the rupees.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the number of coins is not enough. Two ₹5 coins are more than five ₹1 coins.
12. (draw) Draw a ₹10 coin and a ₹10 note. Circle the part that tells you the value.  
   **Answer:** A round coin and a paper note, each with the number 10 circled.. *Why:* The coin and the note look different, but both carry the number 10. The number is what gives the value.
### Same Amount, Many Ways (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (fill) Two ₹5 coins make ₹____.  
   **Answer:** ['10', 'ten']. *Why:* 5 and 5 make 10. Two ₹5 coins can be swapped for one ₹10 coin.
2. (fill) Count on, biggest coin first. How much is this? ₹____  
   **Answer:** ['18', 'eighteen']. *Why:* Say 10, then 15, then 17, then 18. The last number is the amount.
3. (mcq) Which of these is NOT a way to make ₹10?  
   Options: 5 + 5 / 5 + 2 + 2 + 1 / 2 + 2 + 2 + 2  
   **Answer:** 2 + 2 + 2 + 2. *Why:* Four twos are 8, not 10. You need five ₹2 coins to make ₹10. The other two ways both come to 10.
4. (truefalse) Three ₹2 coins are more money than five ₹1 coins.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Three twos are ₹6. Five ones are ₹5. Six is more than five, though there are fewer coins.
5. (fill) A pencil costs ₹6. You pay with a ₹5 coin and one more coin. The other coin is ₹____.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* Count on from 5 to 6. That is one step, so you need a ₹1 coin.
6. (sort) You have ₹10. Sort each thing: can you buy it or not?  
   **Answer:** Balloon ₹2 → Can buy; Notebook ₹15 → Cannot buy; Biscuits ₹10 → Can buy; Chikki ₹8 → Can buy; Ball ₹20 → Cannot buy; Murukku ₹12 → Cannot buy. *Why:* You can buy a thing if its price is 10 or less. 2, 8 and 10 are not more than 10. 12, 15 and 20 are more than 10.
7. (mcq) You have these three coins. How much money is it?  
   Options: ₹3 / ₹9 / ₹7  
   **Answer:** ₹9. *Why:* 2 and 2 make 4, and 5 more makes 9. Picking ₹3 means counting the coins and not the rupees.
8. (fill) Two ₹5 coins and two ₹2 coins make ₹____.  
   **Answer:** ['14', 'fourteen']. *Why:* Count on: 5, 10, 12, 14. That is ₹14.
9. (order) Put these heaps in order, from the least money to the most.  
   **Answer:** ['one ₹2 coin', 'six ₹1 coins', 'four ₹2 coins', 'two ₹5 coins']. *Why:* Count the rupees in each heap: 2, then 6, then 8, then 10. The heap with the most coins is not the one with the most money.
10. (draw) Draw two different ways to pay ₹8.  
   **Answer:** For example, 5 + 2 + 1 and 2 + 2 + 2 + 2. Any two different sets of coins that add up to 8 are right.. *Why:* One amount can be made in many ways. Check each drawing by counting on from the biggest coin.
11. (write) Meena pays ₹10 with one coin. Arjun pays ₹10 with ten coins. Did they pay the same? Say why.  
   **Answer:** Yes. One ₹10 coin and ten ₹1 coins are both ten rupees.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the value is the same, and that only the number of coins is different.
12. (perspective) You are going to the shop with ₹10. Which is better to carry?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what is good about the other way. Both are ₹10.. *Why:* Both carry the same amount. One coin is easy to carry. Small coins are easy to pay exact prices with. That is why we have both.
### Money Puzzles (challenge) (challenge, 12 questions)
1. (fill) The fewest coins that pay ₹7 exactly is ____ coins.  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* A ₹5 coin and a ₹2 coin make 7. No single coin is worth 7, so two is the fewest.
2. (fill) This goes past Class 1. A banana costs ₹7. You pay ₹10. You get back ₹____.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Count up from 7 to 10: 8, 9, 10. That is three steps, so ₹3 comes back. The money that comes back is called change.
3. (mcq) You have one ₹10 note and one ₹5 coin. Which can you buy?  
   Options: A ball for ₹20 / A notebook for ₹15 / Both of them  
   **Answer:** A notebook for ₹15. *Why:* 10 and 5 make 15. That is just enough for the notebook. It is less than 20, so the ball costs too much.
4. (truefalse) A ₹20 note buys more than a ₹20 coin.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both say 20, so both are worth twenty rupees. Paper or metal makes no difference.
5. (fill) ____ coins of ₹2 make ₹10.  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* Count in twos: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. You said five numbers, so five coins.
6. (match) Match the coins to the amount they make.  
   **Answer:** ₹10 + ₹5 + ₹2 → ₹17; ₹5 + ₹5 + ₹2 → ₹12; ₹10 + ₹2 + ₹2 → ₹14; ₹5 + ₹2 + ₹1 → ₹8. *Why:* Count on from the biggest coin each time. 10, 15, 17. Then 5, 10, 12. Then 10, 12, 14. Then 5, 7, 8.
7. (mcq) Which heap is the most money?  
   Options: nine ₹1 coins / three ₹5 coins / six ₹2 coins  
   **Answer:** three ₹5 coins. *Why:* Nine ones are ₹9. Three fives are ₹15. Six twos are ₹12. The heap with the fewest coins is the most money.
8. (fill) You have ₹20. You buy a notebook for ₹15. You have ₹____ left.  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* Take 15 away from 20. Count up from 15: 16, 17, 18, 19, 20. That is 5.
9. (order) Pay each price with the fewest coins. Put the prices in order, from fewest coins to most.  
   **Answer:** ['₹10', '₹7', '₹13', '₹18']. *Why:* ₹10 needs one coin. ₹7 needs two: 5 + 2. ₹13 needs three: 10 + 2 + 1. ₹18 needs four: 10 + 5 + 2 + 1.
10. (write) You have only ₹5 coins. Can you pay exactly ₹12? Say why.  
   **Answer:** No. With ₹5 coins you can make 5, 10, 15. You jump over 12.. *Why:* A strong answer counts in fives and shows that 12 is not one of the numbers. You would need ₹2 or ₹1 coins as well.
11. (draw) Draw the fewest coins that pay ₹19. Write the number on each.  
   **Answer:** Four coins: ₹10, ₹5, ₹2 and ₹2.. *Why:* Take the biggest coin that fits each time: 10, then 5 makes 15, then 2 makes 17, then 2 makes 19.
12. (perspective) What makes a ₹10 coin worth ten rupees?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other person has right. Old silver coins were worth their metal. Today's steel coins are not.. *Why:* Today the metal in a ₹10 coin is not what gives it its value. People agree on the value. But long ago, silver coins were worth about as much as their silver.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a real coin of India?  
   Options: ₹3 / ₹5 / ₹7  
   **Answer:** ₹5. *Why:* India's coins are ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10 and ₹20. There is no ₹3 or ₹7 coin. We make 3 and 7 with other coins.
2. (mcq) You find a note on the table. How do you know how much it is worth?  
   Options: By its size / By the number on it / By how new it looks  
   **Answer:** By the number on it. *Why:* The number printed on the note is its value. Size and newness do not tell you.
3. (truefalse) More coins always means more money.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Look at the picture: seven ₹1 coins are ₹7, and two ₹5 coins are ₹10. You must count the rupees, not the coins.
4. (mcq) Which is more money: one ₹10 coin, or eight ₹1 coins?  
   Options: The one ₹10 coin / The eight ₹1 coins / They are the same  
   **Answer:** The one ₹10 coin. *Why:* The one coin is ₹10 and the eight coins are ₹8. Ten is more than eight. Picking the eight coins means counting coins and not rupees.
5. (fill) How many rupees are in the picture? ₹____  
   **Answer:** ['10', 'ten']. *Why:* Start with the biggest coin and count on: 5, 7, 9, 10. That is ₹10.
6. (fill) Add the three coins. The amount is ₹____.  
   **Answer:** ['17', 'seventeen']. *Why:* 10 and 5 make 15. Two more makes 17.
7. (mcq) You count a ₹5 coin, then a ₹2 coin, then a ₹2 coin. What do you say?  
   Options: 1, 2, 3 / 5, 7, 9 / 5, 6, 7  
   **Answer:** 5, 7, 9. *Why:* You count on by the value of each coin: 5, then 2 more is 7, then 2 more is 9. Saying 1, 2, 3 counts the coins.
8. (mcq) You have no ₹5 coin. Which coins also make ₹5?  
   Options: 2 + 2 + 1 / 2 + 2 + 2 / 1 + 1 + 1  
   **Answer:** 2 + 2 + 1. *Why:* 2 and 2 make 4, and 1 more makes 5. Three twos make 6, and three ones make only 3.
9. (truefalse) Two ₹5 coins are the same amount as one ₹10 coin.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. 5 and 5 make 10. You can swap two ₹5 coins for one ₹10 coin and have the same money.
10. (mcq) A chikki costs ₹8. You have a ₹5 coin and a ₹2 coin. Can you buy it?  
   Options: Yes, I have enough / No, I need ₹1 more / No, I need ₹8 more  
   **Answer:** No, I need ₹1 more. *Why:* 5 and 2 make 7. Seven is one less than eight, so you need ₹1 more.
11. (sort) You have ₹15. Sort each thing: can you buy it or not?  
   **Answer:** Pencil ₹6 → Can buy; Ball ₹20 → Cannot buy; Notebook ₹15 → Can buy; Kite ₹18 → Cannot buy. *Why:* You can buy a thing when its price is 15 or less. 6 is less than 15, and 15 is the same. 18 and 20 are more than 15.
12. (mcq) Why can a ₹10 note buy a packet of biscuits?  
   Options: The paper is costly / People agree it is worth ₹10 / It is brown  
   **Answer:** People agree it is worth ₹10. *Why:* The paper itself is worth very little. The note works because the shopkeeper, and everyone else, agrees to take it for ten rupees.

## Applications
- A shopkeeper counts your coins by their value, biggest first, to check the amount in a few seconds.
- A bus conductor swaps your ₹10 coin for a ticket and gives smaller coins back when the ticket costs less.
- A vegetable seller in a weekly market keeps ₹1, ₹2 and ₹5 coins ready so that she can make any small amount.
- A family writes a list before going to the market and adds up the prices to see whether the money is enough.
- People who cannot see tell coins apart by touch. The 12 sides of the ₹20 coin are easy for fingers to find.
- A hundi or a piggy bank at home fills with small coins, and they are counted and swapped for notes.
- A mint, such as the one in Hyderabad, stamps the number onto plain metal discs to make coins.
- When a grown-up pays by phone, the bank adds and takes away the same amounts you count with coins.

## Spotted around us
- The price printed on a packet. Look for the letters MRP and the ₹ sign on a biscuit packet or a soap.
- The coin box of a tea stall or a flower seller. Coins are often kept sorted by value so they can be counted fast.
- A bus ticket. The price is printed on it, and the conductor carries a bag of coins to pay back the extra.
- The back of a ₹50 note. It shows the stone chariot at Hampi in Karnataka.
- The box of 15 languages on the back of any note. Find the line your family can read.
- A weekly market, a santhai. Sellers call out prices: 'Ten rupees a heap!' Listen for the numbers.
- A sign with a square pattern at a shop counter, for paying by phone. No coin moves, but the same rupees are counted.
- A bee on a flower. She takes nectar and carries pollen to the next flower. This is a trade with no coins.

## Open questions
- **How did money first begin?** It began before people wrote things down, so there is no record. Many scholars now think that keeping count of who owes whom came before coins, but nobody can be sure.
- **Do the macaques at Uluwatu think of a phone as 'worth more' the way we think of a ₹10 coin?** Scientists can see what the monkeys do. They cannot yet see what the monkeys think while they do it.
- **Will coins and notes disappear one day?** More and more people pay by phone. But many people still use only coins and notes, and nobody knows what people will choose in fifty years.
- **What makes money worth something: trust, the government's rule, or what it is made of?** People who study money have argued about this for more than two thousand years, and they still do.
