# Heavier and lighter: lift it, then balance it
`math.weight-capacity.weight.L1`

**Summary.** Which is heavier: one lemon or ten groundnuts? A small potato or a big empty bottle? You will guess with your hands. Then you will check with a balance made from a clothes hanger. You will find out what 'heavier', 'lighter' and 'as heavy as' mean. You will also find two boxes that fool your hands, though the balance is not fooled.

**Intro.** *One lemon. Five groundnuts. Look at the balance. Which side is heavier?* The lemon is heavier. Its side of the balance has gone down. There are more groundnuts, but more things does not always mean heavier. A balance does not count. It only shows which side is pulled down harder. In this unit you will make a balance from a clothes hanger. You will guess with your hands first, and then let the balance check.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can find which of two things is heavier by lifting one in each hand.
1. I can read a balance: the side that goes down holds the heavier thing.
2. I can say when two things are as heavy as each other: the balance is level.
3. I can say why a bigger thing, or more things, is not always heavier.
4. I can say that a thing weighs the same when only its shape changes.
5. I can say why a balance is a fairer judge of weight than my hands.

## Terms
- **Weight**: How hard the Earth pulls a thing down. You feel it when you lift the thing. *e.g.* A full water bottle has more weight than an empty one. It pulls your hand down more.
- **Heavy and light**: A heavy thing is hard to lift. A light thing is easy to lift. *e.g.* A full bucket of water is heavy. An empty bucket is light.
- **Heavier**: Has more weight than the other thing. *e.g.* A coconut is heavier than a lemon.
- **Lighter**: Has less weight than the other thing. *e.g.* A pencil is lighter than a steel spoon.
- **As heavy as**: Has the same weight as the other thing. Neither is heavier. *e.g.* Two spoons from the same set are as heavy as each other.
- **Balance**: A tool with two sides that shows which of two things is heavier. *e.g.* Your hanger with a bag on each end is a balance.
- **Pan**: The part of a balance where you put a thing. A balance has two pans. On your hanger balance, the two bags are the pans. *e.g.* The lemon goes in one pan and the groundnuts go in the other.
- **Level**: Flat and straight, with neither side lower than the other. *e.g.* With a spoon in each bag, the hanger stays level.
- **Tip**: To lean so that one side goes down and the other side goes up. *e.g.* Put a potato in one bag. The balance tips to that side.

## Explanations
### Heavier and lighter · *defined*
**Definition:** The heavier thing has more weight. The lighter thing has less weight.

The Earth pulls every thing down. That pull is the thing's weight. You feel it in your hand when you lift. Here is a worked example. Hold a steel spoon in one hand and a pencil in the other. The spoon pulls your hand down more. So the spoon is heavier than the pencil. We can also say that the pencil is lighter than the spoon.
- e.g. A coconut is heavier than a lemon. The lemon is lighter than the coconut.
- e.g. A full tumbler is heavier than an empty tumbler.
- e.g. A feather is lighter than a pencil.
### How to read a balance · *defined*
**Definition:** The side of a balance that goes down holds the heavier thing. The side that goes up holds the lighter thing.

A balance has two pans. The Earth pulls on both, and the pan that is pulled harder goes down. Here is a worked example. Put a potato in the left bag and a pencil in the right bag. The left side goes down, so the potato is heavier. The right side goes up, so the pencil is lighter. Before you use a balance, check that it is level when empty.
- e.g. Lemon on the left, groundnut on the right: the left side goes down.
- e.g. Pencil on the left, spoon on the right: the right side goes down.
- e.g. A vegetable seller checks that her empty balance is level first.
### As heavy as: a level balance · *defined*
**Definition:** When a balance stays level, the two things are as heavy as each other.

Sometimes neither side goes down. Then neither thing is heavier. Here is a worked example. Put one spoon in each bag, both from the same set. The hanger stays level. So one spoon is as heavy as the other. Two things do not have to look alike to be level. A small potato can be as heavy as a big onion.
- e.g. Two spoons from the same set: level.
- e.g. Two full matchboxes: level.
- e.g. A potato and an onion can be level, if they happen to weigh the same.
### Bigger is not always heavier · *defined*
**Definition:** Size is how much room a thing takes. Weight is how hard the Earth pulls it. They are two different things.

A big thing can be light, and a small thing can be heavy. Here is a worked example. A big empty bottle and a small potato go in the two bags. The potato side goes down. The bottle is bigger, but the potato is heavier. Why? The bottle is thin plastic with air inside, and the potato is solid all the way through. So you cannot tell weight by looking. You have to lift, or use a balance.
- e.g. A big bag of pori (puffed rice) is lighter than a small block of jaggery.
- e.g. A balloon is bigger than a coin, and lighter.
- e.g. A big empty dabba is lighter than a small full one.
### More things is not always heavier · *defined*
**Definition:** A balance does not count. Ten light things can weigh less than one heavy thing.

Here is a worked example. Put 1 lemon in the left bag and 10 groundnuts in the right bag. Ten is more than one, but the lemon side goes down. Each groundnut is very light, so even ten of them weigh less than one lemon. Counting tells you how many. It does not tell you how heavy. Counting helps only when all the things are alike. Five spoons from one set are heavier than three.
- e.g. 1 lemon is heavier than 10 groundnuts.
- e.g. 1 coconut is heavier than 20 curry leaves.
- e.g. 5 coins that are alike are heavier than 3 of the same coins.
### A new shape, the same weight · *defined*
**Definition:** If you change the shape of a thing and nothing is added or taken away, its weight stays the same.

Here is a worked example. Two balls of dough are level on the balance. You press one ball flat, like a chapati. It looks bigger now, but the balance is still level. No dough was added and none was taken away. So the weight did not change. Roll the dough into a long snake shape. Or break the dough into four small balls. If all the dough goes back in the bag, the balance is still level.
- e.g. A ball of dough and the same dough pressed flat weigh the same.
- e.g. A sheet of paper and the same sheet crumpled into a ball weigh the same.
- e.g. A banana and the same banana cut into pieces weigh the same.
### Your hands can be fooled · *defined*
**Definition:** When a small thing and a big thing weigh the same, the small one feels heavier to most people.

Your hands judge well when one thing is much heavier. When two weights are close, they are unsure. And they can be fooled. Here is a worked example. A small box and a big box are level on the balance, so they weigh the same. Lift them, and to most people the small box feels heavier. Augustin Charpentier, a doctor in France, tested this in 1891. Scientists think your eyes expect the big box to be heavy, but they still argue about the full reason.
- e.g. A small box and a big box of the same weight: the small one feels heavier.
- e.g. After you carry a heavy bag, a school bag feels light for a moment.
- e.g. A potato and an onion of nearly the same weight: your hands cannot be sure.
### Squirrels and crows judge weight too · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists have watched squirrels and crows choose things by how heavy they are.

A fox squirrel in North America picks up a nut in her mouth. Often she gives her head a quick flick. In 2009 two scientists, Stephanie Preston and Lucia Jacobs, studied this. They think the flick helps her feel how heavy the nut is. Crows can judge weight without touching. In 2019 Sarah Jelbert and her team hung light and heavy things on strings in front of a fan. The crows only watched them swing. Then they picked the right kind, light or heavy, about 3 times out of 4.
- e.g. A squirrel flicks her head with a nut in her mouth, then decides to eat it or bury it.
- e.g. A crow watches two things swing in the wind and picks the heavy one.
- e.g. A person choosing a coconut lifts and shakes each one.
### On the Moon, a level balance stays level · *defined*
**Definition:** Weight is the pull of the Earth. On the Moon the pull is weaker, so every thing weighs less there.

The Earth pulls every thing down, and that pull is weight. The Moon pulls too, but only about one sixth as hard. So on the Moon you could lift a full bucket easily. Now think about a balance. Here, a potato is level with an onion. On the Moon the potato weighs less, and the onion weighs less by just as much. So the balance stays level. A balance only compares the two sides, so it gives the same answer anywhere.
- e.g. On the Moon, a full school bag would feel as light as a nearly empty one feels here.
- e.g. A heavier thing is still heavier on the Moon. A coconut still beats a lemon.
- e.g. A level balance would stay level on Mars too, where the pull is also weaker than on the Earth.

## Mostly agreed
- On a balance with two equal arms, the side that goes down holds the heavier thing. *How we know:* You tested it. Every time, the bag that went down held the thing that also felt heavier in your hand, when the two were easy to tell apart.
- A thing weighs the same when only its shape changes. *How we know:* In Squash the Dough, the hanger stayed level when you pressed one ball flat. Nothing was added and nothing was taken away.
- You cannot tell which thing is heavier just by looking at its size. *How we know:* A small potato pulled the bag down against a big empty bottle. One lemon went down against ten groundnuts.
- When a small thing and a big thing weigh the same, the small one feels heavier to most people. *How we know:* Augustin Charpentier tested this in France in 1891. Many scientists have tested it since, with grown-ups and with children, and found the same.
- Other animals judge weight too. Crows can even do it by watching. *How we know:* In 2019 Sarah Jelbert and her team showed 12 crows light and heavy things swinging in the wind from a fan. The crows then picked the right kind about 3 times out of 4.

## How we found out
**Traders of the Indus cities, Ernst Weber and Augustin Charpentier, More than 4,000 years ago, 1834 and 1891.** For a very long time, people judged weight by lifting. A handful of grain was traded for a handful of salt. But hands do not agree with each other, and people quarrelled. So people made balances. More than 4,000 years ago, traders in the Indus cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro used balances with small cubes of stone as weights. The cubes found at one city match those found at another, far away. Still, many people believed that a careful hand feels weight just as it is. In 1834 a German scientist, Ernst Weber, tested this. He asked people to lift two weights and say which was heavier. When the weights were close, people could not tell. Then in 1891 a French doctor, Augustin Charpentier, gave people two balls of the same weight, one small and one large. Almost everyone said the small ball was heavier. The hand does not simply read weight. It can be fooled by size.  
*What people believed before:* A careful hand feels the weight of a thing just as it is. Weber's test in 1834 showed that hands cannot tell close weights apart. Charpentier's test in 1891 showed that a small ball feels heavier than a large ball of the same weight.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The bigger thing is always the heavier thing.** (Many children, before they test it; *rejected*)  
  For: It is often true. A big potato is heavier than a small potato. A big bucket of water is heavier than a small one.  
  Against: It is true only when both things are made of the same stuff. A big empty bottle is lighter than a small potato. A big bag of pori is lighter than a small block of jaggery.
- **If you squash a ball of dough flat, it gets lighter.** (Many young children, before they test it; *rejected*)  
  For: The flat piece looks thin. Thin things often feel light, like a sheet of paper.  
  Against: Nothing was taken away, so the weight cannot change. On the balance, the flat piece is still level with the ball.
- **How heavy a thing is has nothing to do with us. Weight is in the thing, and our feelings do not matter.** (Many scientists who study weight and forces; *debated*)  
  For: A balance gives the same answer for everyone. The two boxes were level whoever looked at them. The weight never changed when the feeling did.  
  Against: The word 'heavy' always needs someone who lifts. A bucket that is heavy for you is light for a grown-up. And the same weight felt different in a small box and a big box.

## Roots
We lifted things and tested them on a balance. Now we ask what 'heavy' really is, and where the feeling of heavy comes from.

**Deep truth.** In the Two Boxes Surprise, the small box and the big box hung level on the hanger. In your hands, the small box felt heavier. The boxes had not changed. So how heavy a thing feels comes from the thing and from you together. A balance compares only the two things.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think our hands simply read how heavy a thing is, the way eyes read a number on a page. But the feeling of heavy is made partly by us: by our eyes, our arms and what we expect. That is why people everywhere made balances.  
*Evidence:* In the Two Boxes Surprise, two boxes that were level on the hanger felt different in your hands. In the Hand and Hanger Test, your hands could not be sure about the potato and the onion, and the hanger could.

- *who am i:* When you lift a box, your eyes, your arms and your guesses all work together. What you feel is partly the box and partly you.
- *what is life:* A squirrel flicks her head to feel a nut. A crow watches things swing in the wind. Many living beings judge weight, each in their own way.
- *what is this world:* The Earth pulls on every thing, all the time: the lemon, the coconut, the hanger and you. Weight is that pull.

There is one Earth, and it pulls on every thing. That one pull shows up as many weights: small for a groundnut, large for a coconut. When a balance is level, the Earth is pulling the two sides by the same amount, even if they look nothing alike. And when the bags are empty, the hanger is level too. Nothing on one side balances nothing on the other.

- **Udana 6.4:** The Buddha tells of a king who asked people born blind to touch an elephant. One touched the ear, one the leg, one the tail. Each said the elephant was like the part they had touched, and they quarrelled. (Your eyes said the big box was heavier. Your hands said the small box was heavier. The hanger said they were the same. Each one met the boxes in a different way.)
- **Plato, Theaetetus 152b:** Socrates asks: when the same wind blows, does it not feel cold to one of us and not to the other? He is explaining the idea of a thinker named Protagoras, that the wind is cold for the one who feels cold. (A bucket of water is heavy for you and light for a grown-up. The small box felt heavy to your hand. The feeling depends on who is lifting.)
- **Quran 55:7-9:** These verses say that the sky was raised and the balance was set. They ask people to weigh fairly and not to give less than the balance shows. (People long ago saw that a seller's hand could not be trusted to judge weight, even an honest hand. So they agreed to trust the balance.)
- Maths (settled): When a small thing and a big thing weigh the same, the small one feels heavier to most people. Augustin Charpentier showed this in 1891. It has been tested many times since, with children as well as grown-ups.
- Maths (debated): Why does it happen? Many scientists think the eyes expect the big thing to be heavier, and the surprise changes the feeling. Others have found that this cannot be the whole reason. Nobody is sure yet.
- Other view, Many scientists who study weight and forces: They say the weight of a box is a plain fact about the box and the Earth. It is the same whoever lifts it, and even when nobody lifts it. The small box only seemed heavier. That was a mistake made by the body, and the balance put it right.
- Practice, Heavy arms (3 min): Stand up. Hold both arms straight out to the sides. Close your eyes. I will count slowly to thirty. Feel your arms as I count. Now let them down slowly. Did your arms get heavier while I counted? Did they really change? Where did the feeling of heavy come from?
- Ask: The small box felt heavier, and the balance said they were the same. Which one was right? Could both be right in some way?
- Ask: Is a bucket of water heavy if nobody ever tries to lift it?
- Ask: A squirrel and you both lift the same groundnut. Does it feel the same to both of you?
- Ask: Why do you think people everywhere chose to trust a balance more than a hand?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The potato is heavier than the pencil. Is the pencil lighter than the potato? Are these one fact or two? (hint: Look at the balance. Can one side go down without the other side going up?)
- *deep:* You press a ball of dough flat. It looks bigger. Why does it not weigh more? (hint: Was anything added? Was anything taken away?)
- *deep:* How could you find the heavier of two things without a balance and without lifting them? (hint: A crow watched things swing in the wind. What else could you watch? What about floating, or a see-saw?)
- *big:* Is a bucket of water heavy? Who is asking? (hint: Think of yourself, a grown-up, an ant and an elephant.)
- *big:* The small box felt heavier, but the balance said the same. Which one do you believe? Why? (hint: Would the balance change its answer for another person? Would your hands?)
- *big:* If nothing pulled things down, would anything be heavy? (hint: Astronauts in a space station float, and so do their spoons. Scientists say the Earth still pulls on them, and they are falling around the Earth all the time.)

## Story: The Big Bag and the Small Bag
*Kavya, Arul, Paati and the shopkeeper are made up. Pori is puffed rice. It is very light, and a large bag of it weighs little. Jaggery is sold in solid blocks and is heavy for its size. Carrying shopping home from a weekly market is everyday life in many towns in Tamil Nadu.*

Kavya was six. Her cousin Arul was nine, and he never let her forget it.

On Friday evening Paati sent them both to the market at the end of the street in Thanjavur. "One bag of pori and one block of jaggery," she said. "Tomorrow I am making pori urundai. Come straight home."

The shopkeeper was a tall woman with a pencil behind her ear. She scooped pori into a paper bag. She scooped and scooped. The bag grew as big as a pillow. Then she took a block of jaggery, dark and shiny, and wrapped it in newspaper. She put it in a small cloth bag, no bigger than Kavya's lunch box.

"Hold these while I count the change," she said, and she put one bag in each of Kavya's hands.

Kavya stood very still. Her left hand held the big bag. Her right hand held the small bag. Her right hand began to sink. She looked at it in surprise. She lifted both hands up and down, twice. Then she smiled a small smile and said nothing.

Arul came back from looking at the kites in the next shop. He saw the two bags on the counter. One was huge. One was tiny.

"I am older," he said quickly, "so I will choose. I will take the small bag. You can take the big one." He picked up the small bag before she could answer. His arm dropped a little. He pulled it up again and pretended that nothing had happened.

"Are you sure?" asked Kavya.

"Very sure. A big girl like you can carry a big bag."

"All right," said Kavya. She hugged the pori bag with both arms. It crackled. It was like hugging a cloud.

They walked. Past the flower seller, Arul moved the small bag to his other hand. Past the tea stall, he moved it back. Past the temple tank, he was holding it with both hands, and his fingers were red where the cloth handle cut into them.

Kavya skipped. She tossed the pori bag up a little and caught it.

"Stop that," said Arul. "It is a heavy bag. You will drop it."

"It is not heavy," said Kavya.

"It must be. Look at the size of it! It is four times as big as mine."

"Big is not heavy," said Kavya. "I held both of them in the shop. One in each hand. Yours went down."

Arul stopped walking. "You knew? And you let me take it?"

"You chose," said Kavya. "You chose with your eyes. I chose with my hands."

Arul put the jaggery down on the low wall of the tank and shook his fingers. He looked at the small bag. He looked at the big bag. Then, because he was nine and wanted to be sure, he held out his hands. "Give it here."

Kavya put the pori in his left hand. He picked up the jaggery in his right. His right hand sank at once. He lifted the two bags up and down, just as she had done. His face went pink.

"Pori is mostly air," said Kavya kindly. "Each little piece is puffed up. Jaggery is solid all the way through."

"I know that," said Arul. He did not know that until just then.

They stood by the wall for a moment. A crow watched them from the tank steps.

"We could swap," said Arul, in a small voice.

Kavya thought about it. The jaggery really was heavy. It was heavy for Arul, and he was nine. "No," she said. "We could share. Break the jaggery in two. You take one piece and I take one piece. Then we take turns with the pori, because it is nothing."

The block would not break in their hands. So Arul knocked it on the edge of the wall, inside its newspaper, and it cracked into two pieces. One piece was bigger. He held one piece in each hand and moved his hands up and down. "This one is heavier," he said, and he kept it. He gave her the lighter one.

They walked home. Arul carried the pori on his head for a while, like a crown, just to show how light it was.

Paati opened the newspaper and looked at the broken jaggery. "It was one block when I bought it in my head," she said.

"It is the same jaggery, Paati," said Kavya. "Two pieces weigh the same as one block. Nothing fell out."

Paati looked at Arul's red fingers, and at Kavya's face, and she laughed. "Next week," she said, "lift before you choose."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Arul picks his bag by looking, and takes size for weight. Kavya had already compared the two bags the only sure way she had, by holding one in each hand and feeling which hand sank. Pori is mostly air, so a big bag of it is light. Jaggery is solid, so a small block is heavy. At the end the children use the same hand test to share the load, and Kavya notices that breaking the block does not change how much it weighs.

- Ask: Arul chose the small bag. What did he think when he looked at the two bags?
- Ask: How did Kavya know which bag was heavier before they left the shop?
- Ask: Why is a big bag of pori lighter than a small block of jaggery?
- Ask: The jaggery broke into two pieces. Do the two pieces together weigh more, less or the same as the block? Why?

## Games
### g1 · Heavier Takes It (10 min)
- **rules:** Mix the 12 cards. Deal 6 to each player, face down in a pile.; Both players turn over their top card at the same time.; The player with the heavier thing says the sentence, for example 'A coconut is heavier than a lemon.' That player takes both cards and puts them under their own pile.; The other player says it the other way: 'A lemon is lighter than a coconut.'; If you do not agree which is heavier, look at the weight ladder. The thing higher up the ladder is heavier.; Stop after 10 turns, or when one player has no cards. The player with more cards wins.; Playing alone: mix the cards and lay all 12 in a line, lightest to heaviest. Then check your line against the ladder.
- **Printable cards: 12 cards to cut out: one thing on each card:** an ant; a small feather; a groundnut; a pencil; a lemon; a mango; a coconut; a watermelon; a goat; a cow; an elephant; a blue whale
- **Printable board: The weight ladder: the 12 things in order, lightest at the bottom, for checking:** 1. an ant; 2. a small feather; 3. a groundnut; 4. a pencil; 5. a lemon; 6. a mango; 7. a coconut; 8. a watermelon; 9. a goat; 10. a cow; 11. an elephant; 12. a blue whale
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 12 cards, or write the 12 things on slips of paper. 2. Lay them face up. 3. Ask your child to find the lightest thing and the heaviest thing. 4. Keep the weight ladder face down for now.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all twelve. Which one is the lightest of all? Which is the heaviest of all? How do you know?  
  *Child mode:* Look at all the cards. Find the lightest thing. Find the heaviest thing.
  - Note: The ant is the lightest and the blue whale is the heaviest. A blue whale is the heaviest animal known, far heavier than an elephant.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Deal 6 cards each, face down. 2. Play one practice turn. 3. Show how to say both sentences: one with 'heavier' and one with 'lighter'.  
  *Parent says:* I have a mango. You have a goat. A goat is heavier than a mango. You take both. Now I say: a mango is lighter than a goat.  
  *Child mode:* Turn over one card each. Who has the heavier thing? Say the sentence.
  - Check: You turn over a watermelon. Your friend turns over a lemon. Who takes both cards? → **You do, because a watermelon is heavier**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play 10 turns. 2. Each turn, the winner says the 'heavier' sentence and the other player says the 'lighter' sentence. 3. Use the ladder only when you do not agree.  
  *Parent says:* You have a pencil and I have a groundnut. Which is heavier? Could we check that one at home, with the hanger?  
  *Child mode:* Play ten turns. Say 'heavier' or 'lighter' every time.
  - Ask: You hold the lemon card. Which cards can beat it? Which cards can it beat? (good: Mango, coconut and all the bigger ones beat it.; It beats the pencil, groundnut, feather and ant.)
  - Note: Small pairs such as the pencil and the groundnut can be tested for real on the hanger. That is worth stopping the game for.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Each player counts their cards. 2. Together, lay all 12 cards in a line from lightest to heaviest. 3. Turn over the ladder and check.  
  *Parent says:* Count your cards. Now let us put all twelve in a line, lightest first.  
  *Child mode:* Count your cards. Then put all 12 in a line, lightest first.
### g2 · Hands or Hanger? (10 min)
- **rules:** Put 8 small things on a tray. Each must fit in a bag of the hanger balance.; On your turn, the other player picks any two things from the tray and gives them to you.; Hold one in each hand. Say which is heavier, or say 'the same'.; Put them in the two bags. The hanger is the judge.; If the hanger agrees with you, you get one tally mark.; The picker wants to choose two things that are hard to tell apart.; The first player with 5 tally marks wins.; Playing alone: pick two things with your eyes closed. Guess, then test. How many right out of 8 tries?
- **Printable score sheet: A score sheet: one row for each player, with space for tally marks:** Player; Right guesses
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Together, choose 8 small things and put them on a tray. 2. Check that the empty hanger is level. 3. Draw the score sheet. 4. Read the rules aloud.  
  *Parent says:* The hanger is the judge in this game. Your hands make the guess. Let us see how often they agree.  
  *Child mode:* Put 8 small things on a tray. Check that the hanger is level.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick two things that are easy to tell apart. Give them to your child. 2. They guess and test. 3. Then your child picks two for you. Say your guess aloud, with a reason.  
  *Parent says:* I think the onion is heavier than the eraser, because it pulls my hand down more. Let us ask the judge.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the two things. Say which is heavier. Then ask the hanger.
  - Check: You said the onion is heavier. On the hanger, the onion's bag goes up. Were you right? → **No**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play in turns. 2. The picker tries to find two things that feel nearly the same. 3. After each test, both players say the result as a sentence.  
  *Parent says:* You picked a hard pair for me. I am not sure. I will say the key. What does the judge say?  
  *Child mode:* Take turns. Pick two things that are hard to tell apart.
  - Ask: Which pairs fool the hands most often? (good: The ones that are nearly the same weight.; A small heavy thing and a big light thing.)
  - Note: Say your own wrong guesses cheerfully. It shows that grown-up hands are unsure too when two weights are close.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the tally marks. 2. Ask which pair was the hardest of the game, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which pair was hardest for our hands? Why was it hard?  
  *Child mode:* Count the tally marks. Which pair was the hardest?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Hand and Hanger Test: which one is heavier? (25 min)
- **question:** Which thing in each pair is heavier? Can your hands always tell? What does the hanger do when one thing is heavier?
- **predict:** Hold one thing in each hand. Which one is heavier, or are they the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The two bags are the same kind, so the bags themselves weigh the same. We check that the empty hanger is level before we start. We let the hanger stop swinging before we look. We guess first and test after, every time.
- **safety:** Keep groundnuts, coins and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** A grown-up draws the table. For each pair, write or draw what went in each bag, what the hands said and what the hanger said. At the end the child says what they found, and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** Left bag | Right bag | My hands say | The hanger says
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hang the hanger on a door handle or a hook, so that it swings freely. 2. Hang one bag on each end. If the bags slide, tape the handles in place. 3. Look at the empty balance. If one side is lower, stick a small piece of tape on the higher bag until it is level.  
  *Parent says:* This is a balance. Both bags are empty. Is it level? What do you think will happen if I put a potato in this bag?  
  *Child mode:* Hang a bag on each end of the hanger. Are both bags at the same height?
  - Ask: Why must the empty balance be level before we start? (good: So it is fair.; Or one side is already heavier.; Then we cannot trust it.)
  - Note: You can make the balance beforehand to save time. Let your child put one thing in a bag and watch it tip, just to see how it works.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the table with four columns. 2. Your child holds the spoon in one hand and the pencil in the other, and moves both hands up and down. 3. Ask which is heavier, and why. Write the guess. 4. Your child puts one in each bag. Wait for the hanger to stop. 5. Write which side went down.  
  *Parent says:* One in each hand. Move your hands up and down. Which one is heavier? Why do you think so? Now let the hanger check.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the spoon in one hand and the pencil in the other. Which is heavier? Then test.
  - Ask: The spoon side went down. What does that tell you? (good: The spoon is heavier.; The heavy one goes down.; The pencil is lighter, so it went up.)
  - Note: The spoon side goes down. Do not explain yet. Just ask what your child saw.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the potato and the empty bottle on the table. 2. Before your child touches them, ask which one looks heavier. 3. Then your child lifts one in each hand and guesses again. Write the guess. 4. Test on the hanger. Write what happened.  
  *Parent says:* Look first, without touching. Which one looks heavier? Now lift them. What do your hands say? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A small potato and a big bottle. Which is heavier? Guess, then test.
  - Ask: The bottle is much bigger. Why did the potato side go down? (good: The bottle is empty.; It has only air inside.; The potato is solid.; Big is not always heavy.)
  - Note: Many six-year-olds say the bottle by looking, and change their mind when they lift. Both answers are worth hearing. The potato side goes down.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds one spoon in each hand. Ask which is heavier. 2. Write the guess. 3. Test on the hanger. 4. Ask what the hanger is showing.  
  *Parent says:* Two spoons from the same set. Which one is heavier? What will the hanger do? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Hold one spoon in each hand. Which is heavier? Guess, then test.
  - Note: The hanger stays level, or very nearly. Give your child the words: one spoon is as heavy as the other.
  - Check: The hanger stays level with a spoon in each bag. What does that mean? → **The spoons are as heavy as each other**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds the potato in one hand and the onion in the other. 2. Ask which is heavier. Let them take their time. Write the guess, even if it is 'not sure'. 3. Test on the hanger. Write what happened.  
  *Parent says:* This pair is a hard one. Which is heavier? Are you sure? Let us see what the hanger says.  
  *Child mode:* A potato and an onion. Which is heavier? Are you sure? Test it.
  - Ask: Was this pair harder for your hands than the spoon and the pencil? Why? (good: They feel nearly the same.; I could not tell.; The hanger could tell.)
  - Note: Nobody can know this result beforehand. It depends on your potato and your onion. If the hands were unsure and the hanger tipped clearly, say so together: the hanger could tell when the hands could not.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 1 lemon on the table. Count out 10 groundnuts beside it. 2. Ask which side will go down, and why. Write the guess. 3. Your child puts the lemon in one bag and all 10 groundnuts in the other. 4. Write what happened.  
  *Parent says:* One lemon here. Ten groundnuts there. Which bag will go down? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* 1 lemon in one bag. 10 groundnuts in the other. Which bag goes down?
  - Note: The lemon side goes down. Some children expect the groundnuts to win because ten is more than one. Let the hanger answer.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why. 3. Write their words under the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all five pairs. Which bag went down each time? Were your hands always right? Was the bigger thing always heavier?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Which bag went down each time? Say why.
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it is so? (good: The heavier thing goes down.; The bottle was bigger but lighter.; Ten groundnuts were lighter than one lemon.; My hands were not sure about the potato and onion, but the hanger was.)
  - Check: A lemon and a pencil are on the hanger. The lemon's bag goes down. Which is heavier? → **The lemon**

## Activities
### a1 · The Two Boxes Surprise (12 min)
- **problem:** A small box and a big box. Your hands say one thing. What does the hanger say?
- **safety:** Keep groundnuts, coins and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Draw the two boxes. Under them write two lines: 'My hands said: ______.' and 'The hanger said: ______.'
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do this step without your child. Fill the small box to the top with rice, dal or coins. Close it. 2. Hang it in one bag. Put the empty big box in the other bag. 3. Add rice to the big box, a spoon at a time, until the hanger is level. 4. Close both lids with tape.  
  *Parent says:* I have made two boxes for you. Do not open them. First, just look. Which one do you think is heavier?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up gets two closed boxes ready. Look at them. Which looks heavier?
  - Note: If the full small box is still lighter than the empty big box, put coins or a steel spoon in the small box, or use a lighter big box. Keep each box light enough for one small hand.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put both boxes on the table. 2. Your child lifts one in each hand, then swaps hands. 3. Ask which is heavier. Write the answer.  
  *Parent says:* Lift one in each hand. Now swap them. Which box is heavier? How sure are you?  
  *Child mode:* Lift one box in each hand. Swap hands. Which box is heavier?
  - Note: Most people, children and grown-ups, say the small box is heavier. A few feel no difference. Write down whatever your child says, and do not correct it.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what the hanger will do. 2. Your child hangs one box in each bag. 3. Wait for the hanger to stop. 4. Ask what it shows.  
  *Parent says:* What will the hanger do? Put them in. What does it say? Is that what your hands said?  
  *Child mode:* Put one box in each bag. What does the hanger do?
  - Ask: The hanger is level. What does that tell us about the two boxes? (good: They weigh the same.; One is as heavy as the other.; My hands were wrong.)
  - Note: The hanger is level. Give your child a moment. This is a real surprise, and it surprises grown-ups too. Try it yourself.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lifts the two boxes again, knowing they weigh the same. 2. Ask whether the small one still feels heavier. 3. Ask which they would trust to share sweets fairly: hands or the hanger.  
  *Parent says:* You know now that they weigh the same. Lift them again. Does the small one still feel heavier? Whom do you trust, your hands or the hanger?  
  *Child mode:* Lift the boxes again. Does one still feel heavier? Which do you trust?
  - Ask: The boxes did not change. Why might the small one feel heavier? (good: My eyes thought the big one would be heavy.; I got ready for a heavy box.; I do not know.)
  - Note: For most people the small box still feels heavier, even after they know. Scientists think the eyes expect the big box to be heavy, and the surprise changes the feeling. They still argue about the full reason, so 'I do not know' is an honest answer.
  - Check: A small box and a big box are level on a balance. Which is heavier? → **Neither. They weigh the same**
### a2 · Maths in the kitchen: Squash the Dough (10 min)
- **problem:** Two dough balls weigh the same. You press one flat. Is it heavier now, lighter, or the same?
- **safety:** Wash hands before and after touching dough.
- **record:** Draw the ball, the flat piece, the snake and the four small balls. Beside each one write what the hanger did.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Split the dough into two balls. 2. Put each ball on a piece of paper and hang one in each bag. 3. If one side is down, pinch a little dough from that ball and add it to the other. 4. Go on until the hanger is level.  
  *Parent says:* Is it level? Which ball is heavier? What can you do to make them the same?  
  *Child mode:* Make two dough balls. Hang them. Pinch from the heavy one until it is level.
  - Note: This is what a cook does to make chapatis of the same size. It takes two or three pinches.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take one ball out. Your child presses it flat on a plate, like a small chapati. 2. Hold up the flat piece and the ball. Ask: is the flat one heavier now, lighter, or the same? Ask why. 3. Put the flat piece back, with all its dough. Look.  
  *Parent says:* You pressed it flat. Is it heavier now, lighter, or the same as the ball? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Press one ball flat. Is it heavier, lighter or the same? Guess, then test.
  - Ask: It is still level. Why did the weight not change? (good: It is the same dough.; Nothing was added.; Nothing was taken away.)
  - Note: Many young children expect the flat piece to be lighter because it is thin, or heavier because it is wide. Take any guess that has a reason. The hanger stays level.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rolls the flat piece into a long snake. Guess, then test. 2. Then break the snake into 4 small balls. Guess, then put all 4 in the bag. 3. Last, take 1 small ball away and test again.  
  *Parent says:* Now a snake. Heavier, lighter or the same? And four small balls? What if I take one small ball away?  
  *Child mode:* Roll it into a snake. Test. Break it into 4 balls. Test again.
  - Note: The snake is level. The four balls together are level. With one small ball taken away, that side goes up. Only adding or taking away changes the weight.
  - Check: You roll a ball of dough into a long snake. Nothing is added or taken away. What happens to its weight? → **It stays the same**
### a3 · Maths in the market: Two Bags to Carry (12 min)
- **problem:** You have to carry the vegetables home in two bags. How do you pack them so that both hands carry the same weight?
- **safety:** If the hanger bends, the bags are too heavy for it. Take some vegetables out, or check with your hands only.
- **record:** Draw the two bags and what is in each. Write: 'The bags were level when I moved the ______.'
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the vegetables. 2. Ask who carries the shopping at home, and how they hold the bags. 3. Your child packs all the vegetables into the two bags, any way they like.  
  *Parent says:* We must carry all of this home in two bags, one in each hand. Pack them so that both hands carry the same weight.  
  *Child mode:* Pack all the vegetables into two bags. Try to make both bags weigh the same.
  - Note: Do not help with the packing. Many children share by number: four things here and five there. That is a good start, and the next step will test it.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lifts one bag in each hand and says which is heavier. 2. Hang the two bags on the hanger to check. 3. Ask which bag is heavier, and what to do about it.  
  *Parent says:* Lift them. Which hand is pulled down more? Now let the hanger check. What could you do to make them level?  
  *Child mode:* Lift one bag in each hand. Which is heavier? Check on the hanger.
  - Ask: Both bags have four things. Why is one bag still heavier? (good: A potato is heavier than a tomato.; The things are not alike.; Counting does not tell the weight.)
  - Note: Carrying two bags of the same weight is easier on the body than one heavy bag. Your child can feel this: let them carry everything in one hand for a moment.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child moves one vegetable from the heavy bag to the light bag. 2. Check on the hanger. 3. Go on moving or swapping until the hanger is level, or as close as it gets. 4. Count the things in each bag.  
  *Parent says:* Move one thing across. Did it get better, or did it tip the other way? What will you try next?  
  *Child mode:* Move one thing from the heavy bag to the light bag. Check. Do it again.
  - Ask: It tipped the other way. What does that tell you about the thing you moved? (good: It was too heavy.; I should move a smaller thing.; I can swap a big one for a small one.)
  - Note: It may never be exactly level, because vegetables do not come in exact sizes. Swapping a big thing for a small one is a clever move. The two bags often end up with different numbers of things.
  - Check: The left bag is heavier than the right bag. How can you make them level? → **Move something from the left bag to the right**
### a4 · Everyone does maths: Pick Like a Squirrel (8 min)
- **problem:** Two matchboxes look exactly alike. One is heavier. Can you find it without opening it?
- **safety:** Keep groundnuts, coins and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Write a tick for each round where your hands and the hanger agreed. Write a cross where they did not.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child about the squirrel: she holds a nut in her mouth and gives her head a quick flick. Scientists think the flick helps her feel its weight. 2. Out of sight, put 3 coins in one matchbox and none in the other. Wrap the coins in paper so that they do not rattle. 3. Your child holds one box in each hand and flicks the wrists gently. They pick the heavier box. 4. Check on the hanger.  
  *Parent says:* A squirrel cannot open a nut to look inside before she buries it. She flicks her head to feel how heavy it is. You try, with your wrists. Which box is heavier?  
  *Child mode:* Hold a matchbox in each hand. Flick your wrists gently. Which one is heavier?
  - Note: The squirrel study was done with fox squirrels in North America. You can watch the squirrels near your home and see whether they do the same.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Out of sight, put 2 coins in one box and 1 coin in the other. 2. Your child picks the heavier box and says how sure they are. Check on the hanger. 3. Now put 3 coins in one box and 2 in the other. Do the same.  
  *Parent says:* This time it is harder. Which box is heavier? Are you sure, or only a little sure?  
  *Child mode:* Try again. This time the two boxes are closer. Which is heavier?
  - Ask: Which round was hardest for your hands? Why? (good: The last one.; The boxes were nearly the same.; There was only one coin more.)
  - Note: The closer the two weights, the less sure the hands are. The hanger still tips. One coin is enough to move it.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the ticks and crosses. 2. Ask when the hands were right, and when they were unsure.  
  *Parent says:* How many times did your hands agree with the hanger? When was it easy? When was it hard?  
  *Child mode:* Count your ticks. When were your hands right? When were they not sure?
  - Check: Two boxes are nearly the same weight. Which is the surest way to find the heavier one? → **Put them on a balance**

## Projects
### p1 · Is the Bigger One Heavier? Five days, five pairs (25 min, 5 days)
- **problem:** How often is the bigger thing the heavier thing? Always, sometimes or never?
- **safety:** Keep groundnuts, coins and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Draw two boxes on a sheet: 'The bigger one was heavier' and 'The smaller one was heavier'. Each day, draw the pair and put one tally mark in the right box.
- **schedule:** day 1: Pick a big thing and a small thing. Guess, test and make a tally mark. (5 min); day 2: A new pair. Try to find a pair where the small thing wins. (5 min); day 3: A new pair from the kitchen. (5 min); day 4: A new pair, chosen by someone else in the family. (5 min); day 5: One last pair. Then count the tally marks and say what you found. (5 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check that the empty hanger is level. 2. Your child picks one big thing and one small thing that fit in the bags. 3. They guess which is heavier, and say why. 4. Test. 5. Put a tally mark in the right box.  
  *Parent says:* Pick one big thing and one small thing. Which will be heavier? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Pick a big thing and a small thing. Guess which is heavier. Test.
  - Note: Good pairs: a sponge and a spoon, a slipper and a key, an empty dabba and an onion, a packet of pori and a lemon.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to hunt for a pair where they think the small thing will be heavier. 2. Guess, test and tally.  
  *Parent says:* Today, can you find a small thing that will beat a big thing? What makes you think it will?  
  *Child mode:* Find a small thing that might beat a big thing. Test it.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose today's pair from the kitchen. 2. Guess, test and tally. 3. Ask what the heavier thing is made of.  
  *Parent says:* Today's pair is from the kitchen. Which is heavier? What is each one made of?  
  *Child mode:* Pick a big thing and a small thing from the kitchen. Guess and test.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Someone else in the family chooses the pair today. 2. Your child guesses, tests and tallies.  
  *Parent says:* Paati has chosen today's pair. Lift them first. Which is heavier?  
  *Child mode:* Someone else picks the pair today. Guess with your hands. Then test.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Test one last pair. 2. Count the tally marks in each box. 3. Ask the big question again: is the bigger thing always heavier, sometimes or never?  
  *Parent says:* Count the marks in each box. So, is the bigger thing always heavier? What were the small winners made of?  
  *Child mode:* Count the tally marks in each box. Is the bigger one always heavier?
  - Ask: When the small thing won, what was it made of? What was the big thing made of? (good: The small one was steel or solid.; The big one was soft, or empty, or full of air.)
  - Check: Is the bigger thing always the heavier thing? → **Sometimes**
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: the Odd Packet (15 min)
- **problem:** Three packets look exactly alike. One is heavier than the other two. You may use the balance only once. Which packet is the heavy one?
- **your turn:** Now make three packets for a grown-up, with one heavy one. Watch how many times they use the balance. Then try it with four packets. Is one time on the balance still enough? Find out.
- **constraints:** You may not open the packets.; You may put packets in the bags only one time.; You may not use your hands to judge. The packets are too close for hands.
- **general:** Put one packet in each bag and hope that one of them is the heavy one.; Weigh two packets, then swap one for the third and weigh again. This works, but it uses the balance twice.
- **creative:** Put one packet in each bag and leave the third on the table. If one side goes down, that packet is the heavy one. If the hanger is level, both of those are ordinary. So the heavy one must be the packet on the table. One look is enough either way.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Out of sight, fold 2 coins into each of two papers, and 3 coins into the third. Fold all three the same way. 2. Mix them up. Mark them A, B and C. 3. Read the problem aloud.  
  *Parent says:* One of these three is a little heavier. You may use the balance only one time. How will you find the heavy one?  
  *Child mode:* Three packets. One is heavier. You may use the balance only once.
  - Note: Keep track of the heavy packet yourself with a tiny pencil dot that only you know about.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a plan before anything goes on the balance. 2. Let your child try it. Count how many times they use the balance. 3. If they used it twice, mix the packets and ask for a plan that needs only one time.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me your plan first. What will you put in each bag? What will you do if it is level?  
  *Child mode:* Make a plan. Which packets go in the bags? Try your plan.
  - Ask: You put A and B in the bags and it stayed level. What do you know about A and B? (good: They weigh the same.; Neither is the heavy one.; So it must be C.)
  - Note: Most children weigh two, then weigh again. The key step is to see that a level balance is also an answer. Ask the question above and wait.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix the packets again. 2. Put one in each bag and leave one on the table. 3. Before looking, ask: what are the two things that can happen? What does each one mean? 4. Look, and name the heavy packet. Open it to check.  
  *Parent says:* Two things can happen. It can tip, or it can stay level. What does each one tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Put one packet in each bag. If it tips, which is heavy? If it is level, which is heavy?
  - Check: One of packets A, B and C is heavier. A and B are level on the balance. Which is the heavy one? → **C**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child makes three packets for you, out of your sight. 2. Solve it aloud with one use of the balance. 3. Then make four packets, one heavy. Ask whether one time is still enough.  
  *Parent says:* Now you hide the heavy one for me. After that, let us try four packets. Is one time on the balance enough?  
  *Child mode:* Make three packets for a grown-up. Then try four packets.
  - Note: With four packets, one time is not always enough. Put one packet in each bag. If the hanger is level, the heavy one is one of the two on the table, and you need the balance again.

## Worksheets
### Heavier or Lighter? (core) (core, 14 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at the balance. Which is heavier?  
   Options: The coconut / The lemon / They are the same  
   **Answer:** The coconut. *Why:* The coconut side has gone down. The side that goes down holds the heavier thing.
2. (mcq) Look at the balance. Which is lighter?  
   Options: The spoon / The pencil / They are the same  
   **Answer:** The pencil. *Why:* The pencil side has gone up. The side that goes up holds the lighter thing.
3. (mcq) Look at the balance. What does it show?  
   Options: The left spoon is heavier / The right spoon is heavier / They are as heavy as each other  
   **Answer:** They are as heavy as each other. *Why:* The balance is level. Neither side is heavier, so the two spoons weigh the same.
4. (truefalse) True or false: the side of a balance that goes down holds the heavier thing.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Earth pulls harder on the heavier thing, so its side goes down.
5. (mcq) Which is heavier?  
   Options: A full water bottle / An empty water bottle of the same kind  
   **Answer:** A full water bottle. *Why:* The full bottle is heavier. It is the same bottle with water added, and adding makes a thing heavier.
6. (sort) Sort each thing: easy to lift with one finger, or needs two hands?  
   **Answer:** a pencil → One finger; a full bucket → Two hands; a leaf → One finger; a watermelon → Two hands; a key → One finger; a gas cylinder → Two hands. *Why:* A pencil, a leaf and a key are light. A full bucket, a watermelon and a gas cylinder are heavy. A grown-up should lift the cylinder, not you.
7. (mcq) Which bag goes down?  
   Options: The bag with 1 lemon / The bag with 5 groundnuts / Neither  
   **Answer:** The bag with 1 lemon. *Why:* One lemon is heavier than five groundnuts. A balance does not count. Picking the groundnuts means thinking that more things must be heavier.
8. (truefalse) True or false: the bigger thing is always the heavier thing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A big empty bottle is lighter than a small potato. You have to lift or use a balance to know.
9. (match) Match each word to what the balance does.  
   **Answer:** heavier → its side goes down; lighter → its side goes up; as heavy as → the balance stays level. *Why:* The heavier side goes down, the lighter side goes up, and two things of the same weight keep the balance level.
10. (order) Put these in order, lightest first.  
   **Answer:** ['a feather', 'a lemon', 'a coconut']. *Why:* A feather is the lightest. A lemon is heavier than a feather. A coconut is heavier than a lemon.
11. (fill) All the coins are alike. How many coins are in the pan that goes down? ____  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* When all the things are alike, more of them is heavier. Five coins are heavier than three.
12. (draw) Draw two things that would keep a balance level. Draw the balance too.  
   **Answer:** A level balance with two things of the same weight, such as two spoons that are alike.. *Why:* A strong drawing has the bar flat. Two things of the same kind and size are the safest choice.
13. (draw) Draw a balance with a heavy thing on one side and a light thing on the other. Show which side is down.  
   **Answer:** A tipped balance with the heavier thing on the low side.. *Why:* A strong drawing has the bar leaning and the heavier thing on the side that is down.
14. (write) Say it aloud, and a grown-up writes. Why did the small potato beat the big bottle?  
   **Answer:** The bottle was empty, with only air inside. The potato is solid. Bigger is not always heavier.. *Why:* A strong answer says that size and weight are different things, or that the bottle was empty.
### Think Before You Lift (stretch) (stretch, 14 questions)
1. (mcq) The two things swap bags. Which side goes down now?  
   Options: The left side / The right side / Neither. They are the same  
   **Answer:** The right side. *Why:* The mango is heavier wherever it sits. After the swap the mango is on the right, so the right side goes down.
2. (mcq) Two dough balls are level. You press the left one flat and put it back. What happens?  
   Options: The left side goes down / The left side goes up / It stays level  
   **Answer:** It stays level. *Why:* It stays level. Pressing changes the shape, not the weight. Nothing was added or taken away.
3. (truefalse) True or false: 10 groundnuts are always heavier than 1 lemon, because 10 is more than 1.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each groundnut is very light. One lemon is heavier than ten groundnuts. Counting tells you how many, not how heavy.
4. (mcq) Two boxes are level on the balance. The small one feels heavier in your hand. Which is true?  
   Options: The small box is heavier / They weigh the same / The big box is heavier  
   **Answer:** They weigh the same. *Why:* They weigh the same, because the balance is level. Hands can be fooled when sizes are different. A balance does not look at size.
5. (order) A mango is heavier than a lemon. A coconut is heavier than a mango. Put them in order, lightest first.  
   **Answer:** ['lemon', 'mango', 'coconut']. *Why:* The lemon is lighter than the mango, and the mango is lighter than the coconut. So the lemon is the lightest and the coconut is the heaviest.
6. (mcq) You take one small ball of dough out of the left bag. What happens?  
   Options: The left side goes up / The left side goes down / It stays level  
   **Answer:** The left side goes up. *Why:* Something was taken away from the left side, so it is lighter now. The lighter side goes up.
7. (fill) All the potatoes are alike. How many more potatoes must go in the right bag to make it level? ____  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* The left bag has 5 and the right bag has 3. Count on from 3 to 5: 4, 5. That is 2 more.
8. (sort) Sort each pair: is the bigger thing heavier, or is the smaller thing heavier?  
   **Answer:** a big potato and a small potato → Bigger is heavier; a balloon and a coin → Smaller is heavier; a bucket of water and a cup of water → Bigger is heavier; a big bag of pori and a small block of jaggery → Smaller is heavier; a sponge and a steel spoon → Smaller is heavier. *Why:* When both things are made of the same stuff, the bigger one is heavier. A balloon, a bag of pori and a sponge are big but full of air, so they are light.
9. (mcq) Which is the surest way to find the heavier of two things that are nearly the same?  
   Options: Look at them carefully / Lift one in each hand / Put them on a balance  
   **Answer:** Put them on a balance. *Why:* A balance tips even when the weights are close. Hands are unsure with close weights. Looking shows size, not weight.
10. (truefalse) True or false: a sheet of paper weighs less after you crumple it into a ball.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It is the same paper in a new shape. Nothing was torn off, so the weight is the same.
11. (match) Match each change to what happens to the weight.  
   **Answer:** pour water into a bottle → gets heavier; eat half of a banana → gets lighter; roll dough into a snake → stays the same. *Why:* Adding makes a thing heavier. Taking away makes it lighter. Changing only the shape leaves the weight the same.
12. (draw) Draw a ball of dough and three other shapes you can make from it. Do they all weigh the same?  
   **Answer:** A ball, and shapes such as a flat disc, a snake and a ring, with the answer yes.. *Why:* All the shapes weigh the same, because each is made from all of the same dough.
13. (write) Name one big thing that is light and one small thing that is heavy. Say why.  
   **Answer:** For example: a balloon is big and light because it is full of air. A stone is small and heavy because it is solid.. *Why:* Any true pair is right. A strong answer says what each thing is made of or filled with.
14. (perspective) Two children lift a small box and a big box that are level on the balance.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says Divya is right about the weight, because a level balance means the same weight. Nila is right that the small box really does feel heavier.. *Why:* The two boxes weigh the same. The feeling is real too, but it comes from the lifter as well as from the box.
### Balance Puzzles (challenge) (challenge, 13 questions)
1. (mcq) One of packets A, B and C is heavier. A and B are level. Which is the heavy one?  
   Options: A / B / C  
   **Answer:** C. *Why:* A and B weigh the same, so neither is the odd one. The heavy one must be C, which was not on the balance.
2. (mcq) One of packets A, B and C is heavier. Look at the balance. Which is the heavy one?  
   Options: A / B / C  
   **Answer:** A. *Why:* The side with A has gone down, so A is the heavy one. You do not need to weigh C.
3. (fill) One mango is level with 2 lemons. All the lemons are alike. How many lemons are level with 2 mangoes? ____  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* Each mango needs 2 lemons. Two mangoes need 2 and 2, which is 4 lemons.
4. (fill) A dough ball is level with 6 coins. Cut it into 2 equal pieces. One piece is level with ____ coins.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* The two pieces together still weigh the same as 6 coins. Two equal pieces share that: 3 and 3.
5. (truefalse) True or false: a potato and an onion are level on Earth. On the Moon, the same two would still be level.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. On the Moon both weigh less, by just as much. A balance only compares the two sides, so it stays level.
6. (mcq) A lemon is heavier than a pencil. A pencil is heavier than a feather. Which is heavier?  
   Options: The lemon / The feather / We cannot know without a balance  
   **Answer:** The lemon. *Why:* The lemon is heavier than the pencil, and the pencil is heavier than the feather. So the lemon is heavier than the feather too.
7. (mcq) The left bag has 2 big potatoes. The right bag has 3 small potatoes. Which side goes down?  
   Options: The left side / The right side / We cannot tell without testing  
   **Answer:** We cannot tell without testing. *Why:* We cannot tell. The potatoes are not alike, so counting does not help. Three small ones may be heavier or lighter than two big ones. Only the balance can say.
8. (order) The brick beat the book. The book beat the spoon. The spoon beat the key. Order them, heaviest first.  
   **Answer:** ['brick', 'book', 'spoon', 'key']. *Why:* Each thing beat the next one, so the order from heaviest is brick, book, spoon, key.
9. (fill) All the coins are alike. Move ____ coin from the left pan to the right pan to make it level.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* The left pan has 5 and the right has 3. Move 1 across: the left becomes 4 and the right becomes 4.
10. (match) Match each living being to how they may judge weight.  
   **Answer:** a fox squirrel → flicks her head with a nut in her mouth; a crow in a test → watches things swing in the wind; a person at a shop → lifts one thing in each hand. *Why:* Scientists think a squirrel's head flick helps her feel a nut's weight. In a test in 2019, crows judged weight by watching things swing. People lift, or use a balance.
11. (write) There are four packets and one is heavier. Explain how you would find it with a balance.  
   **Answer:** Put one packet in each bag. If one side goes down, that is the heavy one. If it is level, weigh the other two packets against each other.. *Why:* A strong answer uses a level balance as a clue: the two packets on it are both ordinary. Other plans can work too, such as two packets in each bag first.
12. (draw) Draw a balance puzzle for a friend: two things on a balance, and a question about which is heavier.  
   **Answer:** A balance, tipped or level, with one thing on each side and a question such as 'Which is lighter?'. *Why:* The puzzle works if the picture shows clearly which side is down, or that it is level.
13. (perspective) Two thinkers talk about a bucket of water.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. For example: the bucket's weight on a balance is the same for everyone. Or: the word heavy changes with who is lifting.. *Why:* Both have a point. The weight a balance shows does not depend on who looks. But 'heavy' and 'light' are words we use by comparing a thing with ourselves.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which thing is heavier: the onion or the key?  
   Options: The onion / The key / They are the same  
   **Answer:** The onion. *Why:* The onion side has gone down. The side that goes down holds the heavier thing.
2. (mcq) Which thing is lighter: the lemon or the mango?  
   Options: The mango / The lemon / They are the same  
   **Answer:** The lemon. *Why:* The lemon side has gone up. The side that goes up holds the lighter thing.
3. (mcq) You hold a brick in one hand and a pencil in the other. Which hand is pulled down more?  
   Options: The hand with the pencil / The hand with the brick / Both the same  
   **Answer:** The hand with the brick. *Why:* The brick is heavier, so it pulls your hand down more. That pull is the weight you feel.
4. (mcq) Look at the balance. What does it tell you about the two tumblers?  
   Options: The left one is heavier / The right one is heavier / They are as heavy as each other  
   **Answer:** They are as heavy as each other. *Why:* The balance is level, so neither is heavier. One tumbler is as heavy as the other.
5. (truefalse) True or false: two things must look the same to be level on a balance.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A small potato can be as heavy as a big onion. A level balance only says the weights are the same.
6. (mcq) A big empty bottle and a small potato go on a balance. Which side goes down?  
   Options: The bottle side, because it is bigger / The potato side, because it is heavier / Neither  
   **Answer:** The potato side, because it is heavier. *Why:* The potato side goes down. The bottle is bigger but has only air inside. Picking the bottle means taking size for weight.
7. (mcq) One coconut against six curry leaves. Which bag goes down?  
   Options: The bag with 1 coconut / The bag with 6 curry leaves / Neither  
   **Answer:** The bag with 1 coconut. *Why:* One coconut is far heavier than six curry leaves. A balance does not count the things. It compares their weight.
8. (mcq) You press a ball of dough flat. Nothing is added or taken away. Its weight is now:  
   Options: more / less / the same  
   **Answer:** the same. *Why:* The same. It is the same dough in a new shape. Picking 'less' usually means the flat piece looked thin.
9. (truefalse) True or false: a banana weighs the same after you cut it into four pieces, if you keep all four.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Cutting changes the shape and the number of pieces. Nothing was taken away, so the weight is the same.
10. (mcq) Two boxes are level on a balance. The small one feels heavier in your hands. What is true?  
   Options: The small box is heavier / The balance is wrong / They weigh the same, and hands can be fooled  
   **Answer:** They weigh the same, and hands can be fooled. *Why:* They weigh the same. For most people a small thing feels heavier than a big thing of the same weight. The balance does not look at size.
11. (mcq) A potato and an onion feel nearly the same in your hands. What is the best way to find the heavier one?  
   Options: Guess / Use a balance / Pick the bigger one  
   **Answer:** Use a balance. *Why:* A balance tips even when two weights are close. Hands are unsure with close weights, and size does not tell you weight.
12. (mcq) The two things swap sides. What happens?  
   Options: The left side still goes down / The right side goes down / It becomes level  
   **Answer:** The right side goes down. *Why:* The coconut is heavier wherever it sits. After the swap it is on the right, so the right side goes down.

## Applications
- A person carrying shopping home packs two bags of about the same weight, one for each hand.
- A cook makes dough balls of the same weight, so that every chapati or poori comes out the same size.
- A coconut seller lifts and shakes each coconut. A heavier one usually has more water inside.
- In wrestling and boxing, players are matched with others of about the same weight, so that the match is fair.
- A boatman asks people to sit on both sides of a small boat, so that it does not lean.
- A post office weighs every parcel, because a heavier parcel costs more to send.
- At the airport every suitcase is weighed, because a plane can carry only so much.
- A doctor weighs a baby at each visit, to check that the baby is growing well.

## Spotted around us
- A vegetable seller's balance or electronic scale. With a balance, watch which pan is down before she adds the last tomato.
- A see-saw in a park. The heavier child's side goes down, just like a balance.
- Someone carrying two bags from the market, one in each hand. Ask them whether the bags weigh about the same.
- A squirrel on a wall or a tree, holding something to eat. Watch closely. Does she turn it, or flick her head, before she eats it or runs off with it?
- A crow carrying a twig or a piece of food. Watch which things a crow picks up and which things the crow leaves.
- A coconut shop. Watch how people choose: they lift a coconut, shake it and listen.
- A big bag of pori or popcorn at a shop. Ask to hold it. It is much lighter than it looks.
- The weighing machine at a clinic or a railway station. It shows your weight as a number. You will learn to read it in a later unit.

## Open questions
- **Why does a small thing feel heavier than a big thing of the same weight?** Scientists have known about this since 1891. Many think the eyes expect the big thing to be heavy. But tests have shown that this cannot be the whole reason, and they still argue about it.
- **What does 'heavy' feel like to a squirrel or a crow?** Tests show what these animals choose. They cannot show what the choosing feels like from inside. Nobody knows how to find out.
- **Why does the Earth pull things down at all?** Scientists can say exactly how strong the pull is. Albert Einstein described it as a bending of space and time. But how this pull fits with the rest of physics is still an open question.
- **Is 'heavy' in the thing, in the one who lifts it, or in both?** A balance gives one answer for everyone. But the feeling of heavy changes with the lifter and with what the lifter expects. Thinkers and scientists do not agree on which of these the word 'heavy' should mean.

## Connected maths
- A mango is in the left bag and a lemon is in the right bag. The left side is down. Meena swaps the two things. Which side goes down now? **The right side.**. The left side was down, so the mango is heavier. The mango is heavier wherever it sits. After the swap the mango is on the right. So the right side goes down. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L2`)

Rolling does not change the weight, so the snake is still level with 6 coins. The 2 equal pieces share that weight. 6 is 3 and 3. So one piece is level with 3 coins. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L1`)
- On Earth, a potato is level with an onion. On the Moon every thing weighs less. An astronaut takes the balance, the potato and the onion to the Moon. What does the balance do there? **It stays level.**. On the Moon the potato weighs less. The onion weighs less too, by just as much. Both sides get lighter together, so neither side goes down.
