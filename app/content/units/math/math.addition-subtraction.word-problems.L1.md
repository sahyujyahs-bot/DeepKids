# Story sums
`math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L1`

**Summary.** 5 idlis and 3 more idlis. 5 girls and 3 boys. 5 red bangles and 3 green bangles. Are these three sums, or one? You will act out small stories with beans, draw them and write a sum for each. You will find out how to tell whether a story is adding or taking away, and why one word such as 'more' can fool you. Then you will turn it round. You will take a sum and make up your own story for it.

**Intro.** *3 crows sit on the wire. 2 more are flying in. How many crows will there be?* There will be 5 crows. Start at 3 and count on two: 4, 5. That was a story sum. A story sum is a small story with numbers in it and a question at the end. You do not need to remember a rule to work it out. You can act the story with beans, or draw it, and then write its sum: 3 + 2 = 5. In this unit you will do that with many stories, and then make up your own.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can act out a story sum with beans, with my fingers or with a drawing.
1. I can tell whether a story is adding or taking away, from what happens in it.
2. I can write the sum for a story.
3. I can say the answer with its name, such as 8 idlis, and check that it fits the story.
4. I can make up a story for a sum, such as 4 + 2 = 6.
5. I can say that many stories can have the same sum, and one picture can have two sums.
6. I can say why one word, such as 'more', does not tell me whether to add.
7. I can find a number in a story that the sum does not need.

## Terms
- **Story sum**: A small story with numbers in it and a question at the end. It is also called a word problem. *e.g.* '4 dosas are on the plate. 2 more come. How many dosas now?' is a story sum.
- **Question**: The part of a story sum that asks what you must find. It often starts with 'How many'. *e.g.* 'How many crows are left?' is the question.
- **Act it out**: To do what the story says with beans, with your fingers or with the real things. *e.g.* For '6 crows, 2 fly away', put out 6 beans and slide 2 away.
- **Altogether**: All of them, counted as one group. 'In all' means the same. *e.g.* 3 girls and 4 boys are 7 children altogether.
- **Left**: What is still there after some have gone. *e.g.* 7 balloons. 3 pop. 4 balloons are left.
- **Sum**: A short way to write what happened, with numbers and the signs +, − and =. *e.g.* '5 idlis, and 3 more come' is written 5 + 3 = 8.
- **Total**: The answer you get when you add. It is how many there are in all. *e.g.* In 5 + 3 = 8, the total is 8.
- **The answer's name**: The word that says what the answer is counting. *e.g.* The answer is not just 8. It is 8 idlis.
- **Check**: To look at your answer again and ask: does this fit the story? *e.g.* Some crows flew away, so fewer must be left. An answer bigger than the start cannot be right.
- **Extra number**: A number in a story that the sum does not need. *e.g.* 'Meena is 6 years old. She has 4 mangoes and gets 2 more.' The 6 is an extra number.

## Explanations
### A story sum is a small story with a question · *defined*
**Definition:** A story sum is a short story with numbers in it. It ends with a question that asks 'how many?'

Here is one. '4 dosas are on the plate. Amma puts 2 more on it. How many dosas are on the plate now?' The story tells you two numbers, 4 and 2. It tells you what happened: more dosas came. Then it asks a question. The answer is not written in the story. You work it out. 4 and 2 make 6, so there are 6 dosas.
- e.g. '3 kites are flying. 1 more goes up. How many kites are flying?'
- e.g. '7 mangoes hang on a branch. 2 fall. How many are still on the branch?'
- e.g. '2 cats and 3 crows are in the yard. How many animals are there?'
### Four steps: tell it, show it, draw it, write it · *defined*
**Definition:** To work out a story sum, tell the story again, show it with beans, draw it, and then write its sum.

Here is a worked example. '6 crows sit on a wire. 2 fly away. How many are left?' Step 1: tell it again in your own words. 'There were 6 crows and 2 went.' Step 2: show it. Put out 6 beans and slide 2 away. Step 3: draw it. Draw 6 circles and cross out 2. Step 4: write the sum, 6 − 2 = 4. Then say the answer with its name: 4 crows.
- e.g. '5 idlis, and 3 more come': 5 beans, push in 3 more, write 5 + 3 = 8, say '8 idlis'.
- e.g. You can use your fingers in place of beans when the numbers are 10 or less.
- e.g. When you know a story well, you can skip the beans and draw it, or see it in your head.
### When more come, you add · *defined*
**Definition:** If the story starts with some, and then more of them come, you add.

Here is a worked example. '5 idlis are on the plate. Paati puts 3 more on it. How many idlis now?' Something joined the group, so there are more than before. Put out 5 beans and push in 3 more. Count on from 5: 6, 7, 8. The sum is 5 + 3 = 8. There are 8 idlis. Now you try: 4 children are in the auto and 2 more get in.
- e.g. 2 goats are in the yard. 1 more walks in. 2 + 1 = 3 goats.
- e.g. 9 people are on the bus. 3 more get on. 9 + 3 = 12 people.
- e.g. You have 6 marbles. You win 4 more. 6 + 4 = 10 marbles.
### When two groups are counted together, you add · *defined*
**Definition:** If the story has two groups and asks how many there are altogether, you add. Nothing has to move.

Here is a worked example. 'Meena has 5 red bangles and 3 green bangles. How many bangles does she have?' Nobody came and nobody went. The bangles are all on her arm already. But the question asks about both groups together. So you add: 5 + 3 = 8. She has 8 bangles. Now you try: 3 girls and 4 boys are at the bus stop. How many children?
- e.g. 2 cats and 3 crows are 2 + 3 = 5 animals.
- e.g. 6 big spoons and 2 small spoons are 6 + 2 = 8 spoons.
- e.g. 4 idlis and 2 vadas are 4 + 2 = 6 things to eat.
### When some go away, you take away · *defined*
**Definition:** If the story starts with some, and then some of them go, you take away.

Here is a worked example. '8 crows sit on a wire. 3 fly away. How many are left?' Something left the group, so there are fewer than before. Put out 8 beans and slide 3 away. Count what is left: 5. Or count back three from 8: 7, 6, 5. The sum is 8 − 3 = 5. There are 5 crows left. Now you try: 7 balloons, and 2 pop.
- e.g. 8 bananas are in the bunch. 3 are eaten. 8 − 3 = 5 bananas.
- e.g. 10 laddus are in the box. 4 are given away. 10 − 4 = 6 laddus.
- e.g. 12 eggs are in the tray. 2 break. 12 − 2 = 10 eggs.
### Many stories, one sum · *defined*
**Definition:** Stories about very different things can have the same sum.

In the Six Stories Test, the idlis, the children and the bangles all gave 5 + 3 = 8. The sum does not say idlis or bangles. It does not say who brought them, or what colour they were. It keeps only two things: how many, and whether they were put together or taken away. That is why one short sum can fit so many stories. It also works the other way. Give anyone the sum 5 + 3 = 8, and they can tell a new story for it.
- e.g. 5 + 3 = 8 fits 5 autos and 3 more autos.
- e.g. 5 + 3 = 8 fits 5 stars you saw first and 3 you saw later.
- e.g. 8 − 3 = 5 fits crows flying off, bananas eaten and balloons popping.
### One word can fool you · *defined*
**Definition:** One word, such as 'more' or 'altogether', does not tell you whether to add or take away. What happens in the story tells you.

Read this story. '8 children are playing. 3 more children go home. How many are still playing?' The story has the word 'more'. But nobody came. Children went home, so fewer are playing. The sum is 8 − 3 = 5. Here is another. 'There were 9 laddus altogether. 4 were eaten.' It has the word 'altogether', but laddus went away. So do not hunt for one word. Tell the story again and act it.
- e.g. '5 more crows fly away' is taking away, though it says 'more'.
- e.g. 'How many are left over if 2 more join?' is adding, though it says 'left'.
- e.g. If you are not sure, put out beans and do what the story says.
### Say the answer with its name, and check it · *defined*
**Definition:** An answer has a number and a name. After you find it, check that it fits the story.

If someone asks how many idlis there are, '8' is only half an answer. '8 idlis' is the whole answer. The name comes from the question. '2 cats and 3 crows. How many animals?' The answer is 5 animals, not 5 cats. Then check. If more came, your answer must be bigger than the start. If some went away, it must be smaller. '7 mangoes, 2 fall, 9 are left' cannot be right. Nothing came, so there cannot be more than 7.
- e.g. 'How many children?' The answer is '7 children'.
- e.g. 6 dosas, 2 eaten. An answer of 8 dosas fails the check, because eating cannot make more.
- e.g. 4 kites, 3 more go up. An answer of 1 kite fails the check, because more kites came.
### A number the story does not need · *defined*
**Definition:** Some stories have an extra number. It is true, but the question does not ask about it.

Read this story. 'Meena is 6 years old. She has 4 mangoes. Her aunt gives her 2 more. How many mangoes does Meena have?' There are three numbers: 6, 4 and 2. The question is about mangoes. The 6 is about years, not mangoes. So leave it out. The sum is 4 + 2 = 6 mangoes. Real life is full of extra numbers. Before you write a sum, read the question again and ask which numbers it needs.
- e.g. 'At 5 o'clock, 3 crows came and 2 more came.' The 5 is not needed to count crows.
- e.g. 'Bus number 21 has 9 people. 1 gets off.' The 21 is not needed.
- e.g. 'Anu, who is 7, has 2 dolls and 2 balls. How many toys?' The 7 is not needed.
### Story sums at home and in the sky · *defined*
**Definition:** Whenever things join or leave, you can tell it as a story sum.

Story sums are not only in books. Six people sit down to eat and two guests come. The sky has story sums too. In January 1610 a man named Galileo looked at the planet Jupiter through a telescope. He saw 3 tiny dots of light beside it. Six nights later he saw 4. They were moons going round Jupiter. As they go round, one is sometimes hidden behind the planet. '4 big moons. I can see 3. How many are hiding?'
- e.g. There are 7 days in a week. 2 are over. How many are still to come?
- e.g. 10 fingers. You fold down 4. How many are still up?
- e.g. A bus has 9 people. At the stop 3 get off and 5 get on.
### A robin keeps track of worms · *debated*
People are not the only animals who notice when a count does not fit. In New Zealand, three scientists named Simon Hunt, Jason Low and K. C. Burns worked with 10 wild robins. A robin watched 2 mealworms being dropped into a small hole. Mealworms are the young of a beetle, and robins eat them. Then the hole was covered. One mealworm was hidden behind a secret door. The robin opened the cover and found only 1. The robin went on searching more than four times as long as on days when 1 went in and 1 was found.
- e.g. 2 mealworms go in and 1 is found. The robin keeps searching.
- e.g. 1 mealworm goes in and 1 is found. The robin eats and leaves.
- e.g. 8 go in and 4 are found. The robins did not search much longer. With big numbers they may lose track.
- **Scientists who say the robin keeps count:** The robin could not see into the hole after it was covered. To know that one was missing, the robin had to remember how many went in. That is the robin's own story sum: 2 went in, 1 came out, so 1 is still there.
- **Scientists who are more careful:** The robins did this only with small numbers. They may be remembering each mealworm one by one, or how much food there was, and not a number as we use it. So it is safer to say that robins keep track of their food in their own way.
### Stretch: the missing number is at the start · *defined*
**Definition:** In some stories the number you must find is not at the end. It is at the start, or in the middle.

This card goes further than Class 1. 'Some idlis were on the plate. Amma added 3. Now there are 8. How many were there at first?' The story says 'added'. But if you add 3 and 8 you get 11, and that cannot be right. There are only 8 now. So play the story backwards. Take the 3 away again: 8 − 3 = 5. There were 5 at first. Check by playing it forwards: 5 + 3 = 8.
- e.g. Some crows sat on a wire. 2 flew away. 4 are left. Play it backwards: 4 + 2 = 6 crows at first.
- e.g. You have 6 beans and you want 10. Count on from 6 to 10: you need 4 more.
- e.g. Lakshmi has 8 marbles and Farhan has 5. Lakshmi has 3 more, because 8 − 5 = 3.

## Mostly agreed
- The same sum can fit many stories that look nothing like each other. *How we know:* In the Six Stories Test, the idli story, the bus stop story and the bangle story all gave 5 + 3 = 8. You can test it again with any sum. Ask three people for a story for 4 + 2 = 6, and you will get three stories.
- One word, such as 'more', cannot tell you whether to add or take away. *How we know:* In the Six Stories Test, '3 more children go home' had the word 'more', and it was taking away. The beans showed it: 8 beans, 3 slid away, 5 left.
- Children can work out story sums by acting them out, even before they are taught to write sums. *How we know:* In 1993 Thomas Carpenter and four other researchers in the USA gave nine story sums to 70 children in kindergarten, the class before Class 1. The children used counters to act the stories. 32 of the 70 used a way that works on all nine stories.
- Children who sold things in a market did sums better in a real sale than on paper. *How we know:* In 1985 Terezinha Nunes Carraher, David Carraher and Analúcia Schliemann worked with five young sellers in the city of Recife in Brazil. The children worked out prices and change well while selling. They did less well when the same kind of sums were given on paper.
- A wild robin searches for longer when a mealworm they saw go into a hole is missing. *How we know:* In 2008 Simon Hunt, Jason Low and K. C. Burns tested 10 wild robins in New Zealand. When 2 mealworms went in and only 1 could be found, the robins searched more than four times as long as when 1 went in and 1 was found.

## How we found out
**Ahmes, Bhaskara, Terezinha Nunes Carraher, David Carraher, Analúcia Schliemann, Thomas Carpenter and many others, More than 3,500 years ago, 1150, 1985 and 1993.** Story sums are very old. A scribe in Egypt named Ahmes copied out a roll of them more than 3,500 years ago. Many are about sharing loaves of bread among workers. In India, in the year 1150, Bhaskara wrote a maths book called Lilavati. Its problems are small stories in verse, about bees, peacocks, snakes and lotus flowers. For a long time, many schools held that children must first learn to write sums, and only then meet stories. In 1985 Terezinha Nunes Carraher, David Carraher and Analúcia Schliemann worked with five young sellers in a market in Recife, Brazil. The children did sums well in real sales and less well on paper. In 1993 Thomas Carpenter and his team gave nine story sums to 70 kindergarten children in the USA. The children acted the stories with counters, and 32 of them used a way that works on all nine. So the story can come first.  
*What people believed before:* Children must learn to write sums before they can do story sums. The children in Recife and the kindergarten children showed that this is not so. Small children can work out a story by acting it, before they can write its sum.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Find the clue word. 'More' means add. 'Left' means take away.** (Many school books and tuition classes; *rejected*)  
  For: It gives the right sum for many easy stories. '3 more come' is adding, and 'how many are left' is usually taking away.  
  Against: It fails as soon as the story changes a little. '3 more children go home' is taking away. 'How many more do you need?' is taking away too. A child who acts the story gets these right. A child who hunts for one word gets them wrong.
- **Learn the sums first. Stories are only for practice afterwards.** (Many schools, for a long time; *debated*)  
  For: Knowing number facts quickly, such as 5 + 3 = 8, does make story sums faster.  
  Against: Small children can work out story sums with counters before they can write a sum. Thomas Carpenter's team saw this with 70 kindergarten children in 1993. And the young sellers in Recife did better with real sales than with bare sums on paper.
- **Every story has exactly one right sum.** (Many children, and many answer keys; *debated*)  
  For: The answer to the question is one number. 5 idlis and 3 more are 8 idlis, whoever counts them.  
  Against: The same story can be written 5 + 3 = 8 or 3 + 5 = 8. And one picture, such as 4 tumblers standing and 2 fallen over, can be told as 4 + 2 = 6 or as 6 − 2 = 4. The right sum depends on the question you ask.
- **Only humans can follow a story with numbers in it.** (Many people; *debated*)  
  For: Only humans tell stories in words, write sums and work with big numbers.  
  Against: Wild robins in New Zealand searched longer when a mealworm they had seen go into a hole was missing. Nobody trained them. Scientists still argue about how the robins do it.

## Roots
We acted stories with beans and wrote a sum for each. Now let us think about what a sum keeps from a story, and what it leaves out.

**Deep truth.** In the Six Stories Test, idlis, children and bangles all gave one sum: 5 + 3 = 8. A sum keeps only how many, and whether they joined or left. It drops the taste, the colour and the names. So a sum is a story with nearly everything left out. People chose what to keep.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think the sum is the real maths and the story is only a wrapping. But the story comes first. The sum is a short note about it, and people chose what goes in the note.  
*Evidence:* In the Six Stories Test, seven stories made only two heaps. And in the story Eight What?, the total 8 was right, but it could not tell the potter which lamps to give.

- *who am i:* You tell story sums all day without knowing it. 'I had 4 biscuits and I ate 1' is one. You are a storyteller who also counts.
- *what is life:* Other beings follow such stories too. A wild robin who saw 2 mealworms go into a hole, and found 1, kept on searching.
- *what is this world:* Things join and leave around you all the time: crows, buses, bananas. The world does not come with sums written on it. People write the sums.

5 + 3 = 8 is one short sum. It fits idlis, crows, bangles and stars. So one sum holds many stories. It can do that only because it leaves so much out. Go the other way, and one plate of idlis and vadas can be told as many sums. Which sum you write depends on the question you ask.

- **Thirukkural 423:** Thiruvalluvar says that whatever you hear, and whoever says it, wisdom is to find what is really true in it. (In a story sum, the word 'more' can be said about children going home. A wise reader does not grab the word. She finds what really happened.)
- **Udana 6.4:** The Buddha tells of blind men who each touch one part of an elephant. One says an elephant is like a pot. Another says it is like a broom. Each is sure, and each has only a part. (A sum touches one part of a story: how many. '8' is true, as the pot was true. But it is not the whole elephant.)
- **Tao Te Ching 1:** The Tao Te Ching opens by saying that a name that can be said is not the lasting name. (A sum is a kind of name for what happened. It is short and useful. It is not the thing that happened.)
- Maths (settled): Five young sellers in a market in Recife, Brazil, worked out prices and change well in real sales. They did less well when the same kind of sums were given on paper. For them the sums were easier inside the story. Terezinha Nunes Carraher, David Carraher and Analúcia Schliemann reported this in 1985.
- Maths (debated): Wild robins in New Zealand searched for longer when a mealworm they had seen go into a hole was missing. Scientists still argue about whether the robins keep a count as we do.
- Other view, Many mathematicians: They say that 5 + 3 = 8 was true before there were any idlis, and before there were any people. For them the sum is the lasting thing. Each story is only one example of it, and the stories will be forgotten while the sum stays true.
- Practice, One story, slowly (3 min): Sit with your child and put 5 beans in your open hand. Both of you breathe in slowly. As you breathe out, your child takes 1 bean. Do this three times without talking. Then ask: what is the sum for what we just did? And what does the sum not say about it?
- Ask: 5 idlis and 3 more. 5 crows and 3 more. Is it the same story, or only the same sum?
- Ask: When you say only '8', what have you left out?
- Ask: Which came first, the story or the sum?
- Ask: Is there anything about your day that no sum can hold?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* 5 idlis and 3 idlis. 5 crows and 3 crows. Why is the sum the same when the stories are so different? (hint: What does the sum keep? What does it leave out?)
- *deep:* Someone tells you only '8'. What can you not know about the story? (hint: Eight what? Did they come or go?)

## Story: Eight What?
*Thenmozhi, Kathir, Aachi and Valliammai the potter are made up. Karthigai Deepam is a real festival of lamps in Tamil Nadu. On that evening a great flame is lit at six o'clock on top of the hill at Tiruvannamalai, and families light clay lamps at home.*

It was the evening of Karthigai Deepam, the festival of lamps. In a village at the foot of the big hill of Tiruvannamalai, every house was getting ready.

Thenmozhi was seven. Her grandmother, Aachi, sat on the thinnai, the raised porch at the front of the house, rolling cotton wicks between her palms. Thenmozhi's little brother Kathir, who was four, was helping by sitting on the cotton.

"Thenu," said Aachi, "run to Valliammai the potter. We need five lamps for the steps and three for the niches in the wall. Take Kathir with you, before I roll him into a wick."

Thenmozhi ran down the lane with Kathir's hand in hers. "Five and three, five and three," she said as she ran, so that she would not forget.

Then she had an idea. Why carry two numbers when she could carry one? She held five in her head and counted on. Six, seven, eight.

"Eight," she said. "Kathir, we need eight lamps. Say it."

"Eight," said Kathir.

Valliammai sat in her yard among rows and rows of clay lamps, red from the fire. On one side were big lamps, as wide as Thenmozhi's hand, with deep bowls. On the other side were small ones, no bigger than half a lemon.

"Eight lamps, please, Valli akka," said Thenmozhi.

"Eight which?" said the potter. "Big or small?"

Thenmozhi opened her mouth and shut it again.

"Eight is eight," said Kathir, to be helpful.

"Eight big ones cost more than your Aachi sent," said Valliammai. "And eight small ones will blow out on your steps before the hill is lit. What did she say, exactly?"

Thenmozhi looked at the eight in her head. It was a good, round eight. But there was nothing inside it. No steps. No wall. She had added the story away.

She shut her eyes and walked back up the lane in her mind. There was Aachi on the thinnai. There were the cotton wicks. Five for the steps.

She saw the steps of her own house. One, two, three, four, five. She had jumped down them a thousand times. The wind came straight up the lane and onto those steps. They needed big lamps, with deep bowls.

And three for the niches. She saw the three small hollows in the front wall, each just big enough for her fist. They needed small lamps.

"Five big and three small," she said, and opened her eyes. "Eight was right. But eight was not enough."

Valliammai laughed. "That, I can sell you."

Thenmozhi carried the five big lamps home in a basket. Kathir begged to carry the small ones, so she let him hold them in the fold of his shirt.

Near the tea shop a cycle bell rang right behind them. Kathir jumped. Crack. One small lamp lay on the road in two pieces.

Kathir's lip began to shake. "We still have lots," he said. "Count them, Akka. Lots."

Thenmozhi counted the basket and the shirt. Five and two. "Seven lamps," she said. Then she stopped. Seven what? She was not going to do that twice in one evening.

So she told herself the whole story, slowly. Three small lamps. One broke. Two small lamps were left. And there were three niches in the wall.

"Two niches will have a lamp," she said. "One niche will be dark. We do not need one more lamp, Kathir. We need one more small lamp."

They walked back. Valliammai looked at Kathir's face and did not ask for a coin. She put a small lamp into his hand and closed his fingers round it. "This one likes to walk slowly," she said.

When they reached home the sky had turned the colour of a ripe papaya. Aachi poured oil and laid the wicks. Thenmozhi set the five big lamps on the five steps. Kathir, lifted up in Aachi's arms, put the three small lamps in the three niches.

Then, far above them, a flame rose on top of the hill, and the whole lane turned to look.

"So how many lamps are burning now, Thenu?" asked Aachi.

Thenmozhi looked at the steps, and the wall, and the hill. "Five big, three small," she said, "and one very, very big."

"Nine!" shouted Kathir.

"Nine what?" said Thenmozhi. And Aachi laughed so much that she had to sit down on the top step, next to the fifth lamp.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Thenmozhi's 8 was the right total, and it was still not enough to buy the lamps. A sum keeps how many and leaves out the rest of the story. So an answer needs its name, and when a number is not enough, you go back to the story.

- Ask: Thenmozhi said, 'Eight was right. But eight was not enough.' What was missing?
- Ask: How did Thenmozhi find out which lamps were big and which were small?
- Ask: After one lamp broke, Thenmozhi had 7 lamps. Why did she say they needed one more small lamp?
- Ask: When has someone told you a number and you had to ask, 'of what?'

## Games
### g1 · Story Dice (10 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the thing cards, or write the 12 words on slips of paper. Put them face down in a heap. Keep 12 beans ready for checking and a small heap of spare beans for points.; The teller rolls the dice twice. These are the two numbers for the story.; The teller flips the coin. Heads means more come, so it is an adding story. Tails means some go away, so it is a taking-away story that starts with the bigger number.; The teller turns over a thing card and tells a story with the two numbers. The story must end with a question.; The other player says the answer with its name, such as '9 kites'. Check it with beans.; A story that fits wins the teller one bean. A right answer with its name wins the other player one bean. Swap jobs after every story.; The first player with 5 beans wins.; Playing alone: tell each story to a toy, answer it yourself and check with beans. Try to get 5 right in a row.
- **Printable cards: Thing cards: what the story is about:** idlis; crows; bangles; autos; mangoes; kites; goats; laddus; marbles; balloons; coconuts; fish
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the thing cards face down in a heap. 2. Put out the dice, the coin and the beans. 3. Read the rules aloud.  
  *Parent says:* This game is Story Dice. The dice gives the numbers. The coin says if things come or go. The card says what the story is about.  
  *Child mode:* Put the cards face down. Keep the dice, the coin and the beans ready.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one practice round yourself. Say that you rolled 6 and 3, flipped tails and turned 'kites'. 2. Tell the story: '6 kites are flying. 3 come down. How many are still flying?' 3. Your child answers and checks with beans.  
  *Parent says:* I rolled 6 and 3. The coin says tails. My card says kites. 6 kites are flying. 3 come down. How many are still flying?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the story. Say the answer with its name. Check with beans.
  - Note: With tails, always start with the bigger number, so that there is enough to take away. If both rolls are the same, the answer is zero, and that is fine.
  - Check: 6 kites are flying. 3 come down. How many are still flying? → **3 kites**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game, swapping jobs after every story. 2. Each answer must have its name. 3. Check with beans whenever the two of you do not agree.  
  *Parent says:* Your turn to roll and tell. Remember to end your story with a question.

### g2 · Find My Sum (10 min)

## Experiments
### e1 · The Six Stories Test: six stories, how many sums? (25 min)
- **question:** Six different stories: do they need six different sums? And can one word in a story tell you whether to add or take away?
- **predict:** Before you act the stories, guess how many different sums the six stories will need: six, or fewer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We act every story with the same beans, and we do just what the story says. We write the sum only after the beans have shown it. We guess before each story, and we write the guess down even when it turns out wrong.
- **safety:** Keep beans and seeds away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** A grown-up draws the table and writes. For each story, write the guess first and then the sum. At the end the child says what they found, and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** Story | My guess: add or take away | The sum
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the table on paper. 2. Read story A aloud: '5 idlis are on the plate. Paati puts 3 more on it. How many idlis now?' 3. Your child acts it with beans and says the answer. 4. Write the sum on slip A.  
  *Parent says:* Listen. 5 idlis are on the plate. Paati puts 3 more on it. How many idlis now? Show me with the beans.  
  *Child mode:* Put 5 beans on the plate. They are idlis. Push in 3 more. How many idlis now?
  - Ask: Tell me the story again in your own words. What happened to the idlis? (good: More idlis came.; Paati put 3 more, so now there are 8.)
  - Note: The sum is 5 + 3 = 8. If your child says only '8', ask '8 what?' Write the sum yourself if your child does not write yet.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the other five slips. 2. Read each story once, without acting it. 3. Ask for the guess and the reason. 4. Write the guess under the table.  
  *Parent says:* Here are five more stories, about crows, children, bananas, bangles and balloons. Six stories in all. How many different sums will we need? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the six stories. How many different sums will they need? Guess, and say why.
  - Ask: Why did you guess that number? (good: Six, because the stories are all different.; Fewer, because some stories have the same numbers.; I am not sure. Let us try.)
  - Note: Most children say six. Accept any guess that comes with a reason, and do not correct it. The full stories are: B. 8 crows sit on a wire. 3 fly away. How many are left? C. 5 girls and 3 boys wait at the bus stop. How many children? D. 8 bananas are in a bunch. 3 are eaten. How many are left? E. Meena has 5 red bangles and 3 green bangles. How many bangles? F. 8 balloons hang on the wall. 3 pop. How many are left?
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read story B aloud. 2. Before the beans, your child says 'add' or 'take away'. Write the guess. 3. Your child acts it with beans, and you write the sum on the slip. 4. Do the same for stories C, D, E and F.  
  *Parent says:* Now the crow story. First tell me: will you add or take away? Then show me with the beans.  
  *Child mode:* Act each story with beans. First say: add or take away? Then write the sum.
  - Ask: In the bangle story nothing came and nothing went. So why did you add? (good: Because it asks how many bangles altogether.; I counted the red ones and the green ones together.)
  - Note: B, D and F are 8 − 3 = 5. C and E are 5 + 3 = 8. Stories C and E are the hard ones, because nothing moves. Let your child push the two groups of beans together to see it.
  - Check: 8 bananas are in a bunch. 3 are eaten. Which sum fits? → **8 − 3**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to put slips with the same sum together. 2. Count the heaps. 3. Look back at the guess from step 2. 4. Ask why such different stories share a sum.  
  *Parent says:* Put the stories with the same sum together. How many heaps are there? What did you guess before?  
  *Child mode:* Put the slips with the same sum together. How many heaps do you get?
  - Ask: Idlis and bangles are so different. Why is their sum the same? (good: Both are 5 and 3 put together.; The sum only has the numbers. It does not say idlis.)
  - Note: There are two heaps: three stories with 5 + 3 = 8 and three with 8 − 3 = 5. Let your child say why in their own words. Do not explain yet.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write story G on the last slip. 2. Read it aloud: '8 children are playing. 3 more children go home. How many are still playing?' 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. 4. Your child acts it with beans and puts the slip on a heap.  
  *Parent says:* One more story. 8 children are playing. 3 more children go home. How many are still playing? Add or take away? Why?  
  *Child mode:* 8 children are playing. 3 more children go home. Add or take away? Guess, then act it.
  - Ask: The story says 'more'. So why are there fewer children at the end? (good: Because they went home.; 'More' was about children going, not coming.)
  - Note: Many children say 'add' because they hear 'more'. Let the beans answer: 8 beans, 3 slide away, 5 are left. The slip goes on the 8 − 3 = 5 heap.
  - Check: 9 crows sit on a wire. 2 more crows fly away. How many are left? → **7 crows**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table and the two heaps together. 2. Ask what your child thinks now, what they saw, and why. 3. Write their words under the table. 4. Ask for one new story for the 5 + 3 = 8 heap.  
  *Parent says:* Look at our heaps. What did you find out? What did you see that makes you think so? Now tell me a new story for this heap.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your two heaps. What did you find out? Tell one new story for a heap.
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it is so? (good: Different stories can have the same sum.; Seven stories made only two heaps.; The sum keeps the numbers and what happened. It leaves out the idlis and the crows.; The word 'more' did not mean add. I had to act the story.)
  - Check: Which story has the same sum as '4 kites fly, and 2 more go up'? → **4 fish swim, and 2 more come**

### c1 · Stretch: find the start of the story (15 min)
- **problem:** Some beans are hidden under a plate. 3 more beans are slid under. Now there are 8 beans. Invent a way to find out how many were hidden at first.

## Worksheets
### Story Sums (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (fill) 3 crows sit on a wire. 2 more come. How many crows now? ____  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* 5 crows. More crows came, so you add. Count on two from 3: 4, 5.
2. (mcq) 6 idlis are on a plate. 2 are eaten. Which sum fits?  
   Options: 6 + 2 / 6 − 2 / 2 − 6  
   **Answer:** 6 − 2. *Why:* 6 − 2. Idlis were eaten, so they went away. That is taking away. 4 idlis are left.
3. (fill) 4 red bangles and 4 green bangles. How many bangles? ____  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* 8 bangles. The question asks about both groups together, so you add: 4 + 4 = 8.
4. (sort) Sort each story: do you add or take away?  
   **Answer:** 2 more goats walk in → Add; 3 balloons pop → Take away; 4 girls and 2 boys, how many children → Add; 5 crows fly off → Take away; 1 laddu is eaten → Take away; 3 more guests come → Add. *Why:* When some join, or two groups are counted together, you add. When some go away, you take away.
5. (truefalse) '5 girls and 3 boys' and '5 idlis and 3 more idlis' have the same sum.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both are 5 and 3 put together, so both are 5 + 3 = 8. The sum does not say what the things are.
6. (mcq) 7 balloons hang on the wall. 3 pop. How many are left?  
   Options: 10 balloons / 4 balloons / 3 balloons  
   **Answer:** 4 balloons. *Why:* 4 balloons. Popping takes away: 7 − 3 = 4. Picking 10 means the 3 were added. Picking 3 is the number that popped.
7. (match) Match each story to its sum.  
   **Answer:** 2 kites, and 3 more go up → 2 + 3; 5 kites, and 3 come down → 5 − 3; 5 kites, and 2 more go up → 5 + 2; 3 kites, and 2 come down → 3 − 2. *Why:* The first number is how many there were at the start. If more go up, the sign is +. If some come down, the sign is −.
8. (fill) 5 kites are up. 2 more go up. Fill in the total. ____  
   **Answer:** ['7', 'seven']. *Why:* 7 kites. Count on two from 5: 6, 7.
9. (draw) Draw this story: 4 fish swim. 3 more come. Write the sum under your drawing.  
   **Answer:** 7 fish in all, shown as a group of 4 and a group of 3, and the sum 4 + 3 = 7.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the two groups. The sum says they were put together.
10. (draw) Draw this story: 9 laddus. 4 are eaten. Cross out and write the sum.  
   **Answer:** 9 laddus with 4 crossed out, and the sum 9 − 4 = 5.. *Why:* Crossing out shows that 4 went away. 5 laddus are not crossed out.
11. (write) Say a story for 3 + 1 = 4. A grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** Any story where 3 of something are joined by 1 more, such as '3 goats were in the yard. 1 more came. How many goats?'. *Why:* A strong story has 3 at the start, 1 more that joins, and a question whose answer is 4.
12. (truefalse) 8 children play. 3 more children go home. To find how many still play, you add.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The children went home, so fewer are playing. You take away: 8 − 3 = 5. The word 'more' is about children going, not coming.
### Stories up to 20 (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (fill) 9 children are in the bus. 4 more get in. How many children now? ____  
   **Answer:** ['13', 'thirteen']. *Why:* 13 children. More got in, so you add. Count on four from 9: 10, 11, 12, 13.
2. (fill) 15 murukkus are in the tin. 5 are eaten. How many are left? ____  
   **Answer:** ['10', 'ten']. *Why:* 10 murukkus. 15 is ten and five. Take the five away and ten are left.
3. (mcq) 12 eggs are in a tray. Amma uses 3. Which sum fits?  
   Options: 12 + 3 / 12 − 3 / 3 − 12  
   **Answer:** 12 − 3. *Why:* 12 − 3. The eggs Amma used went away from the tray. 9 eggs are left.
4. (mcq) Meena is 6 years old. She has 4 mangoes and gets 2 more. How many mangoes?  
   Options: 12 mangoes / 6 mangoes / 10 mangoes  
   **Answer:** 6 mangoes. *Why:* 6 mangoes. The sum is 4 + 2 = 6. Her age is an extra number. It is about years, not mangoes. Picking 12 means all three numbers were added.
5. (truefalse) The word 'more' in a story always means you must add.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. '3 more children go home' is taking away. What happens in the story tells you, not one word.
6. (order) Put these stories in order, from the smallest answer to the biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['10 kites, and 2 come down', '10 kites, and none come or go', '10 kites, and 2 more go up']. *Why:* 10 − 2 = 8. 10 with no change is 10. 10 + 2 = 12. Taking away gives fewer than the start, and adding gives more.
7. (match) Match each auto story to its sum.  
   **Answer:** 11 autos, and 4 more come → 11 + 4; 14 autos, and 4 drive off → 14 − 4; 10 autos, and 7 more come → 10 + 7; 17 autos, and 10 drive off → 17 − 10. *Why:* Look at what happens. If more come, the sign is +. If some drive off, the sign is −. The number at the start comes first.
8. (fill) 11 autos wait. 3 more come. Start on 11 and hop forward 3. How many autos? ____  
   **Answer:** ['14', 'fourteen']. *Why:* 14 autos. From 11, hop 12, 13, 14.
9. (mcq) Which story fits the sum 9 − 4?  
   Options: 9 crows, and 4 more come / 9 crows, and 4 fly away / 4 crows, and 9 fly away  
   **Answer:** 9 crows, and 4 fly away. *Why:* 9 crows, and 4 fly away. The sum starts with 9, and the sign − means that 4 went away.
10. (draw) Make up a story for 10 − 3. Draw it. Write the answer with its name.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of 10 things with 3 gone or crossed out, and an answer such as '7 balloons'.. *Why:* A strong answer has 10 at the start, 3 that go away, and a name after the 7.
11. (write) There were 9 laddus altogether. 4 were eaten. Do you add or take away? Say why.  
   **Answer:** Take away, because the laddus were eaten. 9 − 4 = 5 laddus are left.. *Why:* A strong answer looks at what happened to the laddus. The word 'altogether' is in the story, but nothing was put together.
12. (perspective) A plate has 4 idlis and 2 vadas.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both sums fit the plate. Anu answers 'How many things to eat?' Kavya answers 'How many are idlis?'. *Why:* One plate can be told as two stories. Which sum is right depends on the question you ask.
### Think It Through (challenge) (challenge, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) Some crows sat on a wire. 2 more came. Now there are 7. How many were there at first?  
   Options: 9 crows / 5 crows / 2 crows  
   **Answer:** 5 crows. *Why:* 5 crows. Play the story backwards. Take away the 2 that came: 7 − 2 = 5. Picking 9 means 2 and 7 were added, but there are only 7 crows now.
2. (fill) You have 6 beans. You want 10. How many more do you need? ____  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* 4 more beans. Count on from 6 until you reach 10: 7, 8, 9, 10. That is four counts.
3. (truefalse) One sum can fit many different stories.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. 5 + 3 = 8 fits idlis, children, bangles and anything else where 5 and 3 are put together.
4. (mcq) Lakshmi has 8 marbles. Farhan has 5. How many more does Lakshmi have?  
   Options: 13 marbles / 3 marbles / 5 marbles  
   **Answer:** 3 marbles. *Why:* 3 marbles. Match the marbles one to one. 5 of Lakshmi's have a partner and 3 do not. 8 − 5 = 3. The word 'more' is in the question, but you take away.
5. (sort) Kavya is 7. She has 5 red kites. 2 tear. How many are whole? Sort what the sum needs.  
   **Answer:** Kavya is 7 → Not needed; 5 kites → Needed; The kites are red → Not needed; 2 tear → Needed. *Why:* The question is about how many kites are whole. It needs the 5 kites and the 2 that tear: 5 − 2 = 3. Her age and the colour are true, but the sum does not need them.
6. (order) Put the steps for a story sum in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Tell the story again', 'Show it with beans', 'Write the sum', 'Say the answer with its name']. *Why:* First make sure you know the story. Then act it, so that you see what happens. Then write the sum, and last say the answer with its name.
7. (fill) 5 children are in the auto. 2 get out. Then 4 get in. How many now? ____  
   **Answer:** ['7', 'seven']. *Why:* 7 children. First 5 − 2 = 3. Then 3 + 4 = 7. Act it with beans, one step at a time.
8. (truefalse) 9 idlis were on a plate. Some were eaten. 11 idlis can be left.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Eating takes idlis away, so fewer than 9 must be left. An answer bigger than the start fails the check.
9. (mcq) A robin sees 2 worms go into a hole. The robin finds 1. Which sum tells how many are missing?  
   Options: 2 + 1 / 2 − 1 / 1 − 2  
   **Answer:** 2 − 1. *Why:* 2 − 1. Two went in and one was found, so one is still missing. 2 − 1 = 1.
10. (draw) Draw one picture that can be told as an adding story and as a taking-away story.  
   **Answer:** A picture with two groups, such as 4 tumblers standing and 2 fallen. It can be 4 + 2 = 6, or 6 − 2 = 4.. *Why:* A strong picture has two groups you can count together, or one whole group with a part that has changed.
11. (write) Tell two different stories for 8 − 3 = 5. A grown-up writes them.  
   **Answer:** Any two stories where 8 of something lose 3, such as 8 crows with 3 flying off, and 8 bananas with 3 eaten.. *Why:* A strong answer has two stories about different things, each starting with 8 and losing 3.
12. (perspective) Two children talk about the story '5 idlis, and 3 more come' and its sum, 5 + 3 = 8.  
   **Answer:** There is no single right answer. A strong answer gives a reason, such as: the sum works for every story, or: without the story you do not know 8 of what.. *Why:* The sum is true for every story with 5 and 3 put together. But the sum alone does not say what was counted or why. People who think hard about maths still argue about which comes first.

## Applications
- A cook asks how many people will eat and adds up the idlis each one wants. That is the kitchen job in this unit.
- A bus conductor keeps a story going all day. Some people get on and some get off, and she knows how many seats are free.
- A shopkeeper tells herself a small story for every sale: the price, the money given and the change to give back.
- A tailor counts the buttons a shirt needs and the buttons in the box, to see how many more to buy.
- A nurse counts the tablets in a strip and the tablets taken, to know how many days are left.
- A farmer counts the coconuts picked and the coconuts sold, to know how many are still in the shed.
- A kabaddi scorer keeps track of the players who are out and the players who come back in.
- A scientist who watches birds counts the eggs in a nest on each visit, to find out how many hatched.

## Spotted around us
- The chappals at your door. Count them when the family is home. Count again when someone goes out. Tell the story.
- An egg tray. The empty hollows tell you how many eggs have gone, even if nobody told you the story.
- A bus stop. Watch one bus. Some people get off and some get on. Is the bus fuller or emptier when it leaves?
- The clothes line. Count the clips in the morning and after the clothes come in.
- Crows on a wire or a wall. They come and go all day. Each landing and each take-off is a small story sum.
- A strip of tablets. Count the full bubbles and the empty ones. Ask a grown-up to tell you its story. Never touch the tablets yourself.
- A banana bunch on the table. It tells a taking-away story every day until someone buys more.
- Jupiter in the night sky. With good binoculars held very still, you may see some of its four big moons as tiny dots in a line. Count them on two nights.

## Open questions
- **How does a robin know that a mealworm is missing?** The tests show that the robin searches for longer. They do not show whether the robin remembers a number, or each mealworm, or how much food went in. Scientists do not know yet.
- **Why are sums easier for many children inside a story than on their own?** The young sellers in Recife did better in real sales than on paper. Researchers have several ideas about why, such as that a story lets you picture what the numbers mean. They do not fully agree.
- **Is a sum such as 5 + 3 = 8 something people made, or something people found?** People who think hard about maths have argued about this for more than two thousand years. Some say numbers were there before any people. Some say people made them, as they made words. Nobody has settled it.
- **Do other animals tell each other stories with numbers in them?** Many animals keep track of small numbers of things. Whether any of them pass on to another animal something like 'two went in and one came out' is not known.
