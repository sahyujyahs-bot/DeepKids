# Equals: the same on both sides
`math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L1`

**Summary.** Put 3 coins and 2 coins in one pan of a balance. Put 4 coins and 1 coin in the other pan. Will one side go down, or will the balance stay level? You will make a balance from a clothes hanger and test it. You will find out what the sign = really means, and how to fix a balance that tips. You will also find out that two things can be equal in one way and not equal in another way.

**Intro.** *Left pan: 3 coins and 2 coins. Right pan: 4 coins and 1 coin. Will the balance tip?* No. The balance stays level. 3 and 2 make 5. 4 and 1 also make 5. The two sides look different, but they are the same amount. We write it like this: 3 + 2 = 4 + 1. The sign = is called the equals sign. It means 'is the same as'. In this unit you will test this yourself, with a balance made from a clothes hanger.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can tell whether two sides are equal, by counting or with a balance.
1. I can say what the sign = means: both sides are the same amount.
2. I can show that different sums can be equal, like 3 + 2 and 4 + 1.
3. I can make a tilted balance level by adding to the light side or taking from the heavy side.
4. I can say whether a number sentence is true or false.
5. I can say that equal means the same in one way, such as how many or how heavy.

## Terms
- **Balance**: A tool with two pans that shows which of two things is heavier. *e.g.* A vegetable seller puts a weight in one pan and tomatoes in the other pan.
- **Level**: Flat and straight, with neither side higher than the other. *e.g.* When both pans hold 5 coins, the bar of the balance is level.
- **Tip**: To lean so that one side goes down and the other side goes up. *e.g.* Put 5 coins in one pan and 3 in the other. The balance tips to the side with 5.
- **Heavier and lighter**: The heavier thing pulls down more. The lighter thing pulls down less. *e.g.* A coin is heavier than a groundnut. On a balance, the pan with the coin goes down.
- **Equal**: The same amount. Not more and not less. *e.g.* 3 + 2 and 4 + 1 are equal. Both are 5.
- **Equals sign (=)**: The sign = means 'is the same as'. What is on its left is the same amount as what is on its right. *e.g.* 5 = 5. Also 3 + 2 = 5. Also 3 + 2 = 4 + 1.
- **Number sentence**: A line of maths written with numbers and signs, the way a sentence is written with words. *e.g.* 2 + 2 = 4 is a number sentence. You can read it aloud: two and two is the same as four.
- **True and false**: A number sentence is true when both sides really are the same amount. It is false when they are not. *e.g.* 3 + 1 = 4 is true. 3 + 1 = 5 is false.
- **Not equal (≠)**: The sign ≠ means 'is not the same as'. It is an equals sign with a line through it. *e.g.* 5 ≠ 3. Five is not the same as three.

## Explanations
### A balance compares two sides · *defined*
**Definition:** A balance has two pans. The pan with the heavier load goes down. If both loads weigh the same, the balance stays level.

A balance does not count and does not read numbers. It only shows which side is heavier. Here is a worked example. Put 5 coins in the left pan and 3 coins in the right pan. All the coins are alike. The left pan goes down, because 5 coins weigh more than 3 coins. Now put 5 coins in each pan. Neither side is heavier, so the bar stays level. When all the things are alike, a level balance tells you that both pans hold the same number. Before you trust a balance, check it when it is empty. An empty balance must be level too.
- e.g. 5 coins on the left and 3 on the right: the left pan goes down.
- e.g. 4 coins on each side: the balance is level.
- e.g. A vegetable seller checks that her empty balance hangs level before she weighs anything.
### The sign = means 'is the same as' · *defined*
**Definition:** The equals sign says that what is on its left is the same amount as what is on its right.

Think of the sign = as the middle of a level balance. Whatever is written on the left is in the left pan. Whatever is written on the right is in the right pan. The sign says the two pans hold the same amount. Here is a worked example. In the Level Test, 3 coins and 2 coins went in the left pan. 4 coins and 1 coin went in the right pan. The balance stayed level. So we write 3 + 2 = 4 + 1. We read it as: three and two is the same as four and one. The sign does not mean 'now write the answer'. There is no answer in 3 + 2 = 4 + 1. There are only two sides that are the same. The answer can also come first: 5 = 3 + 2 is just as true as 3 + 2 = 5.
- e.g. 5 = 5. Both sides are five.
- e.g. 5 = 3 + 2. The single number is on the left this time, and the sentence is still true.
- e.g. 3 + 2 = 4 + 1. Neither side is 'the answer'. Both sides are five.
### Many sums can make the same number · *defined*
**Definition:** Two sums are equal when they come to the same number, even if they are made of different numbers.

Take 6 coins. You can split them into 5 and 1. You can split them into 4 and 2, or into 3 and 3. Every time there are still 6 coins, because none was added and none was taken away. So 5 + 1, 4 + 2 and 3 + 3 are all equal. Here is a worked example. Is 4 + 2 the same as 5 + 1? Work out each side by itself. 4 + 2 is 6. 5 + 1 is 6. Both sides are 6, so 4 + 2 = 5 + 1. There is a pattern too. If one pile gets 1 bigger and the other pile gets 1 smaller, the total stays the same. Zero also works: 6 + 0 = 6, because adding nothing changes nothing.
- e.g. 6 = 5 + 1 = 4 + 2 = 3 + 3.
- e.g. 2 + 3 = 3 + 2. Swapping the two piles does not change how many there are.
- e.g. 7 + 0 = 7. Adding zero leaves the balance as it was.
### True and false number sentences · *defined*
**Definition:** A number sentence with = is true when both sides are the same amount. It is false when one side is more.

A number sentence can be wrong, just as a spoken sentence can be wrong. To check one, work out the left side. Then work out the right side. Then compare. Here is a worked example. Is 2 + 2 = 5 true? The left side is 2 + 2, which is 4. The right side is 5. 4 is not the same as 5. So 2 + 2 = 5 is false. On a balance, the right pan would go down. For a false sentence we can use another sign, ≠, which means 'is not the same as'. So 2 + 2 ≠ 5 is true.
- e.g. 3 + 1 = 4 is true. Both sides are 4.
- e.g. 4 = 4 is true. It has no sum in it at all, and it is still a good number sentence.
- e.g. 1 + 4 = 6 is false. The left side is 5 and the right side is 6.
### How to make a tilted balance level · *defined*
**Definition:** To make a tilted balance level, add to the light side or take away from the heavy side, until both sides are the same.

Here is a worked example. The left pan has 5 coins and the right pan has 3 coins. The left pan is down. First find the gap: 5 is 2 more than 3. Way one: add 2 coins to the right pan. Now it is 5 and 5. Way two: take 2 coins from the left pan. Now it is 3 and 3. Way three: move 1 coin from the left pan to the right pan. The left pan loses 1 and the right pan gains 1, so now it is 4 and 4. Moving 1 coin closes a gap of 2. All three ways end with a level balance, but the piles are different each time.
- e.g. 6 and 4: add 2 to the light side, and it is 6 and 6.
- e.g. 6 and 4: take 2 from the heavy side, and it is 4 and 4.
- e.g. 6 and 4: move 1 across, and it is 5 and 5.
### Do the same to both sides, and it stays level · *defined*
**Definition:** If a balance is level and you add the same amount to both pans, or take the same amount from both pans, it stays level.

Here is a worked example. Each pan has 4 coins, so the balance is level. Add 1 coin to each pan. Now each pan has 5. It is still level. Take 2 coins from each pan. Now each pan has 3. It is still level. But add a coin to only one pan, and that pan goes down. In number sentences it works the same way. 4 = 4 is true. Add 1 to both sides and you get 5 = 5, which is also true. Older children use this rule to find a hidden number. You can try it in the Mystery Packet challenge.
- e.g. Level at 4 and 4. One more on each side: 5 and 5, still level.
- e.g. Level at 6 and 6. Two fewer on each side: 4 and 4, still level.
- e.g. Level at 4 and 4. One more on the left only: 5 and 4, and the left pan goes down.
### Equal in what way? · *defined*
**Definition:** Two things are equal in a certain way: in number, in weight, in length or in price. They can be equal in one way and not equal in another.

Three coins and three groundnuts are equal in number. Both are three. But put them on a balance and the coins go down. They are not equal in weight. So before you say 'equal', ask: equal in what way? Here is another example. A ₹10 note and ten ₹1 coins do not look the same, and they do not weigh the same. But they buy the same things. They are equal in what they are worth. In a number sentence, the sign = always means equal in amount. 3 + 2 = 4 + 1 says the two sides are the same number. It does not say they look the same.
- e.g. 3 coins and 3 groundnuts: equal in number, not equal in weight.
- e.g. A ₹10 note and ten ₹1 coins: equal in what they buy, not equal in weight.
- e.g. Two children can be equal in age and not equal in height.
### Honeybees can learn 'the same' · *defined*
**Definition:** In tests, honeybees learned to choose the thing that was the same as one they had just seen.

In 2001 a scientist named Martin Giurfa and his team tested honeybees. Each bee flew into a tunnel shaped like the letter Y. At the door she saw one colour, for example blue. Then the tunnel split into two arms. One arm was marked blue and the other yellow. Sugar water was waiting only in the arm with the same colour as the door. The bees learned this. Then the scientists changed the colours to striped patterns, which the bees had never been trained on. The bees still went to the arm that matched the door. So they had learned more than 'go to blue'. They had learned 'go to the same'. Other bees learned the opposite rule, 'go to the different one'.
- e.g. A bee sees blue at the door and chooses the blue arm.
- e.g. The same bee sees stripes that go up and down at the door. She chooses the arm with stripes that go up and down, though she was trained only with colours.
- e.g. On the new patterns the bees chose the matching arm far more often than luck alone would give. They were not right every time.
### Equal days and equal nights · *defined*
**Definition:** An equinox is one of two days each year when day and night are almost the same length everywhere on the Earth.

Most of the year, day and night are not equal. In June, days in India are longer than nights. In December, nights are longer. But twice a year, around 20 March and around 22 or 23 September, they come almost level. Each lasts about 12 hours. This happens all over the Earth on the same day. The day is called an equinox. The word comes from two Latin words that mean 'equal night'. To be exact, the day is a few minutes longer than the night even then. That is partly because the air bends the Sun's light, so we still see the Sun when it is just below the edge of the sky.
- e.g. Around 20 March, sunrise and sunset in Chennai are about 12 hours apart.
- e.g. On the same day, they are about 12 hours apart in Delhi, in London and in Sydney too.
- e.g. In June, a day in Delhi is almost 14 hours long, and the night is only about 10 hours.

## Mostly agreed
- The sign = does not mean 'write the answer here'. It means that both sides are the same amount. *How we know:* Number sentences such as 5 = 5, 5 = 3 + 2 and 3 + 2 = 4 + 1 are all true, and none of them has an answer after the sign. You can check each one on a balance.
- A balance with two equal arms stays level only when both pans hold the same weight. *How we know:* You tested it. With coins that were all alike, the hanger balance was level only when both cups held the same number of coins.
- If you add the same amount to both sides of a level balance, it stays level. *How we know:* Start with 4 coins in each cup. Add 1 to each cup. Anyone can try it, and the balance stays level every time.
- Many children think the sign = means 'the answer comes next'. *How we know:* In 1999, children in Classes 1 to 6 in the United States were given the sum 8 + 4 = __ + 5. In no class did even one child in ten write 7. Most wrote 12 or 17.
- Honeybees can learn to pick the thing that is the same as one they have just seen. *How we know:* In 2001 Martin Giurfa and his team trained bees to match colours in a Y-shaped tunnel. The bees then matched striped patterns they had never been trained on.

## How we found out
**Robert Recorde, and later Karen Falkner, Linda Levi and Thomas Carpenter, 1557 and 1999.** For a very long time, people who wrote maths had no sign for 'equals'. They wrote it out in words every time. In India, Brahmagupta wrote about equations in the year 628. His word for an equation was samikarana, which means 'making equal'. In 1557 Robert Recorde, a doctor from Wales, grew tired of writing 'is equal to' again and again. In his book The Whetstone of Witte he drew two lines of the same length instead. The sign caught on slowly. In time it was printed in every school book, mostly in sums like 3 + 2 = __. Many grown-ups believed that children understood the sign, because the children could do those sums. In 1999 a teacher, Karen Falkner, and two researchers, Linda Levi and Thomas Carpenter, tested this in the United States. They gave children in Classes 1 to 6 the sum 8 + 4 = __ + 5. In no class did even one child in ten write 7. Most wrote 12 or 17. The children had learned that = means 'write the answer here'.  
*What people believed before:* A child who can do sums like 3 + 2 = __ understands the sign =. The test in 1999 showed this was wrong. Almost all the children wrote 12 or 17 for 8 + 4 = __ + 5, because to them the sign meant 'the answer comes next'.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The sign = means 'now write the answer'.** (Many children, and the way many sums are printed; *rejected*)  
  For: Most sums in school books look like 3 + 2 = __, so the answer always comes after the sign. On a calculator, pressing the = key gives the answer.  
  Against: The sign only says that the two sides are the same amount. 5 = 3 + 2 and 3 + 2 = 4 + 1 are true, and neither has an answer after the sign. Children who know only the first meaning get stuck on sums like 8 + 4 = __ + 5.
- **If two piles have the same number of things, they will always balance.** (Many children, before they test it; *rejected*)  
  For: With coins that are all alike, it is true. Five coins always balance five coins.  
  Against: A balance compares weight, not number. Three coins do not balance three groundnuts. The same number balances only when every single thing weighs the same.
- **3 + 2 and 4 + 1 are not two things that people chose to call equal. They are one number, five, with two names.** (Gottlob Frege, a German mathematician, and many mathematicians today; *debated*)  
  For: Everyone who adds carefully gets five on both sides, in every country and every century. Nobody voted on it. In 1892 Frege wrote that the morning star and the evening star are two names for one planet, Venus. An equals sign between two names works in the same way.  
  Against: Two piles of coins are never the same in every way. To call them equal, someone first has to choose what to compare: how many, how heavy or how much they buy. That choice is made by people.

## Roots
We tested piles on a balance and found out which ones are equal. Now we ask what the word 'equal' really means.

**Deep truth.** In the Level Test, 3 and 2 coins balanced 4 and 1 coins, though the two sides looked different. Then 3 coins and 3 groundnuts did not balance, though both were three. So two things are never just 'equal'. They are equal in one way, such as how many or how heavy. Before we say equal, we choose which way to check.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think equal means exactly the same. But no two things are the same in every way. We pick one way to compare them, and then we forget that we picked.  
*Evidence:* In the Level Test, 3 + 2 and 4 + 1 looked different and still balanced. In Same Number, Not the Same Weight, 3 coins and 3 groundnuts were the same in number and did not balance.

- *who am i:* You and your friend are not the same height, and you do not like the same food. But each of you counts as one child. In that way you are equal.
- *what is life:* A honeybee can learn to pick the picture that is the same as one she has just seen. Noticing 'same' and 'different' is something many living beings do.
- *what is this world:* Twice a year, day and night are almost the same length all over the Earth. People noticed this long ago and named that day the equinox, which means 'equal night'.

Five can be shown in many ways: 5, 4 + 1, 3 + 2. The piles look different, but there is one amount under all of them. The sign = is how we write that down: many ways of showing, one amount. Zero has a place here too. Add nothing to one side of a level balance, and it stays level. So 5 + 0 = 5.

- **Thirukkural 118:** Thiruvalluvar, the Tamil poet, says that a balance is first made level, and then it weighs fairly. A wise person is like that balance and does not lean to one side. (You did the same before the Level Test. You checked that the empty balance was level. Only then could you trust what it showed.)
- **Tao Te Ching 77:** The Tao Te Ching says the way of nature is like the bending of a bow. What is high is brought down, and what is low is lifted up. It takes from what has too much and gives to what has too little. (This is how you fixed a balance that tipped. You took from the heavy side, or you gave to the light side.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 5.18:** The Bhagavad Gita says that the wise look with an equal eye on a learned person, a cow, an elephant, a dog and a person whom others look down on. (These five are not the same in size or shape. The verse says they are the same in one deep way. Like a balance, it names the way in which they are equal.)
- Maths (settled): The sign = was chosen by a person. Robert Recorde, a doctor from Wales, first printed it in a book in 1557. He chose two lines of the same length because, he wrote, no two things can be more equal.
- Maths (open): Is 3 + 2 = 4 + 1 a fact that was always there, or is it true because of the way people chose to count and compare? Mathematicians and thinkers still argue about this.
- Other view, Gottlob Frege, a German mathematician who wrote about this in 1892, and many mathematicians today: They say that 3 + 2 and 4 + 1 are not two things that we choose to call equal. They are two names for one number, five. In the same way, the morning star and the evening star are two names for one planet, Venus. Nobody chose that. It would be true even if nobody had ever made a balance.
- Practice, Be the balance (3 min): Stand up with your feet together. Hold your arms out to the sides, like the bar of a balance. Close your eyes. Feel whether you lean a little to one side. Now lift one foot just off the floor. Feel your body tip, and feel it bring itself level again. Put the foot down. Breathe in and out three times. What did your body do to stay level?
- Ask: Are 3 + 2 and 4 + 1 one thing or two things?
- Ask: Is there anything that is the same as you in every way?
- Ask: A ₹10 note and ten ₹1 coins are equal. Who decided that?
- Ask: If nobody ever compared two things, would anything be equal?

## Story: The Half-Kilo That Rolled Away
*Yazhini, Paati and Ilango are made up. Hand-held balances with iron weights are real. They are called tharasu in Tamil, and vegetable sellers all over Tamil Nadu used them for a very long time. Most shops now use electronic scales.*

Yazhini was seven. Every Sunday she helped her Paati at the vegetable stall in the Madurai market. Paati sold tomatoes, brinjals and ladies' fingers from three flat baskets. Yazhini had the best job. She held the tharasu.

The tharasu was a balance. It had an iron bar with a pan hanging from each end on three thin chains. Paati put an iron weight in one pan and vegetables in the other. Then she added a tomato, or took one away, until the bar was straight. "The bar does not lie," Paati always said. "When it is straight, nobody is cheated. Not the customer, and not me."

Paati had two weights. The big one was one kilo. The small one was half a kilo. They were black and smooth from forty years of hands.

That Sunday a handcart full of bananas came down the lane too fast. Its corner caught the leg of the stall. The baskets jumped. Tomatoes rolled everywhere, and the small weight rolled with them, across the stones and into the open drain. Plop.

Paati looked into the black water for a long time. "Gone," she said. She wiped her hands on her sari. "I must go and get change for a hundred. Mind the stall, kanna. Sell by the kilo only, till I am back."

Paati had hardly turned the corner when Ilango arrived. Ilango was ten and lived on Yazhini's street. He held out a cloth bag and some coins. "Half a kilo of tomatoes," he said. "Exactly half. Amma counted the money."

"I cannot," said Yazhini. "The half-kilo weight is in the drain."

"Then just guess," said Ilango. "Put in a few. Who will know?"

"The bar will know," said Yazhini. "Paati never guesses."

Ilango thought. "Weigh one kilo, then. Make two heaps on the sack, and give me one."

"Which heap is half?"

"The one that looks like half."

"Looking is guessing too," said Yazhini.

Ilango sighed and sat down on an upturned crate. He was going to be late, and Amma was waiting for the tomatoes.

Yazhini held up the empty tharasu. The two pans hung side by side, and the bar was straight. Nothing on this side, nothing on that side. The same on both sides. She looked at it for a while. Then she smiled.

She put the one-kilo weight in the left pan. She filled the right pan with tomatoes, and added and took away until the bar was straight. "That is one kilo," she said.

"I know that," said Ilango. "I want half."

"Wait." She lifted the iron weight out and set it on the sack. Now the left pan was empty and the right pan was full. The bar tipped hard to the right.

She took one tomato from the full pan and put it in the empty pan. Then another. Then another. The right pan rose a little each time, and the left pan sank. After the sixth tomato the bar tipped the other way. She moved a small one back. It was still not straight. She swapped a big tomato for a small one, and the bar came level and stayed there.

"There," said Yazhini. "All of these together are one kilo. And this pan weighs the same as that pan. So each pan is half a kilo." She tipped the left pan into his bag.

Ilango looked into the bag, and then at the other pan. He frowned. "Mine has five tomatoes. That one has six. How is that the same?"

"The tharasu does not count," said Yazhini. "It only asks which side is heavier. You have five big ones. That pan has six small ones. The bar is straight, so they weigh the same."

Ilango lifted the bag in one hand and the six tomatoes in the other, and moved his hands up and down. He could not feel any difference. He paid, and he ran.

When Paati came back, Yazhini told her everything. Paati listened without a word. Then she laughed until she had to sit down. "Forty years I have carried that half-kilo weight," she said. "And all that time the tharasu could find half by itself."

Just then an old woman stopped at the stall. "A quarter kilo of brinjals, ma," she said.

Paati opened her mouth to say that she had no weight so small. Yazhini was already reaching for the one-kilo weight. "Half of the half," she said. "I will need to do it twice."

**For the parent:** For the parent: a balance knows nothing about numbers or kilos. It answers one question only: are the two sides the same, or not? Yazhini uses that one answer to make a weight she does not have. If two piles balance each other, and together they are one kilo, each must be half a kilo. The piles do not look the same and do not have the same number of tomatoes. They are equal in weight, which is the only way the balance checks.

- Ask: Yazhini had no half-kilo weight. How did she still weigh out half a kilo?
- Ask: One pan had five tomatoes and the other had six. How could the bar still be straight?
- Ask: Ilango said to give him the heap that looked like half. Why did Yazhini say no?
- Ask: How will Yazhini weigh a quarter kilo of brinjals? Tell it step by step.

### g1 · Level! (10 min)
- **rules:** Mix the 16 cards. Lay them face down in 4 rows of 4.; On your turn, turn over two cards and read each one aloud.; If the two cards are the same amount, call 'Level!' and say the number sentence, for example '3 + 2 is the same as 4 + 1'. Keep both cards and take one more turn.; If they are not the same amount, say which one is more. Turn both cards face down again, in the same places.; If someone thinks a pair is wrong, build both cards with coins or chana and count.; When all the cards are taken, the player with the most cards wins.; Playing alone: count how many turns you need to clear all 16 cards. Play again and try to need fewer.

### g2 · Fix the Balance (10 min)
- **rules:** Put a bowl of about 30 chana in the middle. Each player also takes 10 chana as their own heap.; On your turn, roll the dice two times. The first roll is the left pan. The second roll is the right pan. Put that many chana from the bowl in each circle.; Say which pan is lighter.   *Child mode:* Draw two big circles. Count out 10 chana for your own heap.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one practice turn each. 2. If the rolls are 5 and 2, put 5 chana on the left and 2 on the right. 3. Show how to count on from 2 to 5 while adding: 3, 4, 5.  
  *Parent says:* I rolled 5 and 2. The right pan is lighter. I add one at a time: three, four, five. I added 3.  
  *Child mode:* Roll two times. Fill both pans. Which pan needs more? How many more?
  - Check: The left pan has 6 chana. The right pan has 4. How many must you add to the right pan? → **2**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game. 2. After each turn, the player says the number sentence. 3. Now and then, ask how else the mat could be made level.  
  *Parent says:* You added 3 to make it level. What is another way to make these two pans the same?  
  *Child mode:* Play the game. Say the number sentence after every turn.
  - Ask: 5 and 2. What is another way to make the pans level? (good: Take 3 away from the 5.; Take from the heavy side.)
  - Note: With 10 chana each, a game takes about five or six turns for each player. A player who rolls the same number twice adds nothing and rolls again.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When a heap is used up, the game ends. 2. Ask which roll needed the most chana, and which needed none.  
  *Parent says:* Which roll cost you the most chana? Which roll cost nothing? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Who used up their heap first? Which roll needed the most chana?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Level Test: which piles balance? (25 min)
- **question:** Which piles of coins make the balance stay level? Do two piles have to look the same to balance?
- **predict:** Before each try, guess: will the left side go down, will the right side go down, or will it stay level? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the coins are the same kind, so each one weighs the same. The two cups are the same. We check that the empty balance is level before we start. We let the hanger stop swinging before we look.
- **safety:** Keep coins, groundnuts and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** A grown-up draws the table. For each try, write what went in each cup, the guess and what happened. At the end the child says what they found, and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** Left cup | Right cup | My guess | What happened
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make two small holes near the rim of each cup, on opposite sides. 2. Tie a string through the holes to make a handle. 3. Hang one cup from each end of the hanger. Tape the strings so that they cannot slide. 4. Hang the hanger on a door handle or a hook, so that it swings freely. 5. If the empty balance is not level, stick a small piece of tape on the higher cup. Add tape until it is level.  
  *Parent says:* This is a balance. Both cups are empty. Is it level? What do you think will happen if I put one coin in this cup?  
  *Child mode:* Hang a cup on each end of the hanger. Are both cups at the same height?
  - Ask: Why must the empty balance be level before we start? (good: So it is fair.; Or one side is already heavier.; Then we cannot trust it.)
  - Note: You can make the balance beforehand to save time. The hanger must swing freely. If it sticks, tap it gently and let it settle.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw two big circles on the paper. Write Left and Right. This is the mat. 2. Put 3 coins in each circle. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Your child drops each pile into its cup. Write what happened. 4. Do the same with 5 coins on the left and 3 on the right.  
  *Parent says:* Three coins here and three coins there. What will the balance do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put 3 coins in each cup. Guess first. Then try 5 and 3.
  - Note: 3 and 3 stays level. With 5 and 3, the side with 5 goes down. Let your child say which side is heavier and which is lighter.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On the mat, put 3 coins and 2 coins in the left circle, as two small groups. 2. Put 4 coins and 1 coin in the right circle. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess. 4. Your child drops each side into its cup. Wait for the hanger to stop. 5. Write what happened.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the two sides. They do not look the same. Will the left side go down, the right side go down, or will it stay level? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Left: 3 and 2. Right: 4 and 1. Guess what the balance will do. Then test.
  - Ask: Why did you guess that? (good: The right side has a 4, and 4 is the biggest.; Both sides are 5.; I counted them all.)
  - Note: Many six-year-olds expect the side with the 4 to go down, because 4 is the biggest number they see. Take any guess that comes with a reason. Do not explain yet. Let the balance answer.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty the cups. 2. Try 2 and 2 on the left against 3 on the right. Guess, test and write. 3. Try 4 and 2 against 3 and 3. 4. Try 1 and 4 against 6.  
  *Parent says:* Here are three more. Guess each one before you test it. Tell me your reason each time.  
  *Child mode:* Try three more pairs of piles. Guess every time before you test.
  - Note: 2 and 2 against 3: the left goes down. 4 and 2 against 3 and 3: level. 1 and 4 against 6: the right goes down. Watch whether your child begins to add each side before guessing.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave 1 and 4 in the left cup and 6 in the right cup. The right side is down. 2. Ask how to make it level. Let your child try. 3. Then put 5 on the left and 3 on the right. 4. Ask for two different ways to make it level. 5. Last, ask your child to move just 1 coin from the heavy cup to the light cup.  
  *Parent says:* The balance is tipping. What could you do to make it level? Is there another way?  
  *Child mode:* The balance tips. Make it level. Can you find two different ways?
  - Ask: 5 and 3. You moved one coin across and it became level. Why was one coin enough? (good: One side lost one and the other got one.; Now it is 4 and 4.)
  - Note: For 5 against 6, add 1 to the left or take 1 from the right. For 5 against 3, add 2 to the light side, or take 2 from the heavy side, or move 1 across. The last way surprises most children.
  - Check: The left cup has 5 coins. The right cup has 3 coins. Which of these makes the balance level? → **Add 2 coins to the right cup**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child all 12 coins. 2. Ask them to make two piles that look different but will balance. 3. They set the piles on the mat, say why they will balance, and test.  
  *Parent says:* Now you choose. Make two sides that look different but will stay level. Tell me why they will balance before you test.  
  *Child mode:* Make your own two sides. They must look different and still stay level.
  - Note: Any two sides with the same total will do, such as 5 and 1 against 3 and 3. If the balance tips, ask your child to count each side again.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why. 3. Write their words under the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all our tries. When did the balance stay level? Did the two sides have to look the same?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. When did the balance stay level? Say why.
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it is so? (good: It is level when both sides have the same number of coins.; 3 and 2 was level with 4 and 1.; Because both are 5.; The piles can look different.)
  - Check: All the coins are alike. Left cup: 3 and 2. Right cup: 4 and 1. What does the balance do? → **It stays level**

## Activities
### a1 · Same Number, Not the Same Weight (10 min)
- **problem:** Three coins and three groundnuts. Both are three. Are they equal?
- **safety:** Keep coins, groundnuts and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Write two lines: '3 coins and 3 groundnuts: the same in ______, not the same in ______.' and '1 coin weighs about the same as ___ groundnuts.'
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay 3 coins in a row. Lay 3 groundnuts in a row under them. 2. Ask whether they are equal. 3. Ask what the balance will do, and why. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Three coins and three groundnuts. Are they equal? What will the balance do if we put them in the cups?  
  *Child mode:* Count the coins. Count the groundnuts. Will they balance? Guess.
  - Note: Some children say 'level, because 3 is the same as 3'. That is a good reason. Let the balance answer.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child puts the coins in one cup and the groundnuts in the other. 2. Wait for the hanger to stop. 3. Ask what happened.  
  *Parent says:* What happened? Both cups have three. Why is it not level?  
  *Child mode:* Put the coins in one cup. Put the groundnuts in the other. What happens?
  - Ask: Both cups have three things. Why did the coin side go down? (good: A coin is heavier than a groundnut.; The balance does not count.; It only feels the weight.)
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty the cups. Put 1 coin in the left cup. 2. Your child drops groundnuts into the right cup, one at a time, counting aloud. 3. Stop when the balance is level, or as close as it gets. 4. Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* How many groundnuts do you think will balance one coin? Guess. Then drop them in one at a time.  
  *Child mode:* Put 1 coin in one cup. Drop groundnuts in the other. Stop when it is level.
  - Note: Expect somewhere between 4 and 10, depending on the coin and the groundnuts. It may not come exactly level. Then write 'more than 6, less than 7', or whatever you find.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the two lines of the record together. 2. Ask in what way the first piles were the same, and in what way they were not.  
  *Parent says:* Three coins and three groundnuts were the same in one way and not in another. Which way was which?  
  *Child mode:* The coins and groundnuts were the same in one way. Which way? Not in which way?
  - Check: 3 coins are in one cup and 3 groundnuts are in the other. What are they equal in? → **In number**
### a2 · Ways to Balance 6 (12 min)
- **problem:** How many different pairs of piles balance 6?
- **safety:** Keep coins, groundnuts and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Write one line for each way you find, like this: 6 = 5 + 1.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 6 coins in the left cup. They stay there for the whole activity. 2. Give your child the other 6 coins. 3. Ask them to make two piles on the mat that will balance the 6. 4. Test, and write the number sentence.  
  *Parent says:* The left cup has 6. Make two piles for the right cup that will balance it. How do you know they will?  
  *Child mode:* The left cup has 6 coins. Make two piles that balance it. Test them.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a different pair of piles. 2. Test it and write it. 3. Go on until your child can find no more. 4. Ask whether 4 and 2 is the same way as 2 and 4.  
  *Parent says:* Is there another way? And another? How will you know when you have found them all?  
  *Child mode:* Find another two piles that balance 6. Then another. Write each one.
  - Ask: How do you know you have found them all? (good: I started with 5 and 1 and went down.; The first pile can be 1, 2, 3, 4 or 5.; After 3 and 3 they repeat.)
  - Note: The pairs are 5 and 1, 4 and 2, 3 and 3, 2 and 4, 1 and 5. Some children also offer 6 and 0, which is right. Putting the pairs in order is how to be sure that none is missing.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the list together, in order. 2. Ask what happens to the second pile each time the first pile gets 1 smaller. 3. Ask your child to try the same with 7 coins in the left cup.  
  *Parent says:* Look down the list. When the first pile goes down by one, what does the second pile do? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your list. One pile gets smaller. What does the other pile do?
  - Ask: Why does the second pile grow by one each time? (good: The coin moved from one pile to the other.; There are still 6 altogether.)
  - Check: Which two piles balance 6 coins? → **4 and 2**
### a3 · Maths in the market: Weigh Like a Shopkeeper (15 min)
- **problem:** A customer asks for chana that weighs the same as 6 coins. Can you weigh it out exactly?
- **safety:** Keep coins, groundnuts and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Draw your shop's balance with the weights on one side and the chana on the other. Write: 'The chana weighs the same as ___ coins.'
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask whether your child has seen a shopkeeper weigh vegetables or sweets. 2. Say how a balance is used: the weight goes on one side, the vegetables on the other. 3. Set up the shop. Your child is the shopkeeper. You are the customer.  
  *Parent says:* At the market, the seller puts a weight on one side. What does she put on the other side? How does she know when to stop?  
  *Child mode:* You are the shopkeeper. The coins are your weights. The chana is for sale.
  - Ask: How does the seller know when she has put in enough? (good: When the bar is straight.; When both sides are level.; When it stops tipping.)
  - Note: Many shops now use an electronic scale that shows a number. A balance does the same job by comparing: the vegetables against a weight that everyone trusts.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for 'chana that weighs 6 coins'. 2. Your child puts 6 coins in one cup and pours chana into the other. 3. They add or take away chana until the balance is level. 4. Tip the chana into a katori for the customer.  
  *Parent says:* I would like chana that weighs the same as six coins, please. Not more, not less.  
  *Child mode:* Put 6 coins in one cup. Add chana to the other until it is level.
  - Note: Children usually pour too much and then take some out. That is exactly what a shopkeeper does. Near the end, one chana at a time is enough to tip it.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now ask for chana that weighs 4 coins. 2. Before weighing, ask whether it will be more or less chana than last time. 3. Your child weighs it out.  
  *Parent says:* Now I want chana that weighs four coins. Will that be more chana than before, or less? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Now weigh chana for 4 coins. Will it be more or less than before?
  - Check: The weights are in the left cup and the chana is in the right cup. The left side is down. What must the shopkeeper do? → **Add more chana**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take all the coins away. 2. Put a handful of chana in a bowl. Say that two people must share it fairly, and nobody may count. 3. Let your child think and try. 4. If they are stuck, ask what the balance does when both cups hold the same.  
  *Parent says:* Two people want to share this chana fairly. We have no weights now, and no counting. How can the balance help?  
  *Child mode:* Share the chana between two people. No counting. Use only the balance.
  - Ask: How do you know the two shares are fair? (good: The balance is level.; Both cups weigh the same.; Each one is half.)
  - Note: The way is to put chana in both cups and move some across until the balance is level. Then each cup holds half. This is the trick in the story The Half-Kilo That Rolled Away.
### a4 · The Equals Card: true or false? (10 min)
- **problem:** Which card goes in the middle: 'is the same as' or 'is not the same as'?
- **record:** Copy three true number sentences and one false one from the game. Put a tick or a cross beside each.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write = on one card and ≠ on another. 2. Say what each sign means. 3. Write these on ten more cards: 3 + 1, 4, 2 + 2, 5, 4 + 1, 3 + 2, 6, 3 + 3, 2 + 1, 5 + 1.  
  *Parent says:* This sign means 'is the same as'. This one, with the line through it, means 'is not the same as'.  
  *Child mode:* Make two cards. One says =. One says ≠. Say what each one means.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the card 3 + 1 on the left and the card 4 on the right. 2. Your child puts = or ≠ between them and reads the sentence aloud. 3. Do the same for these pairs: 5 and 4 + 1. 2 + 2 and 5. 3 + 2 and 4 + 1. 6 and 2 + 1. 3 + 3 and 5 + 1. 4. If your child is unsure, they build each side with coins.  
  *Parent says:* Which card goes in the middle? Read the whole sentence to me, using the words 'is the same as' or 'is not the same as'.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the two sides. Put = or ≠ in the middle. Read it aloud.
  - Note: The answers are =, =, ≠, =, ≠, =. Listen for 'is the same as'. If your child says 'makes' or 'the answer is', ask them to read it again with the new words.
  - Check: Which sign goes in the gap? 3 + 2 __ 4 + 1 → **=**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child uses the cards to make a number sentence that is false, with the = card in it. 2. You find what is wrong. 3. Your child fixes it in two ways: by changing a number card, and by changing the sign card.  
  *Parent says:* Make me a sentence that is false. I will try to catch it. Then show me two ways to make it true.  
  *Child mode:* Make a false number sentence with the = card. Then fix it two ways.
  - Ask: 2 + 2 = 5 is false. How can you make it true? (good: Change the 5 to 4.; Change = to ≠.; Change 2 + 2 to 3 + 2.)
### a5 · Maths in the kitchen: Two Equal Tumblers (8 min)
- **problem:** Two people want the same amount of buttermilk. How do you pour two equal tumblers?
- **record:** Draw the two tumblers before and after, with a line for the water in each.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Out of your child's sight, fill one tumbler nearly full and the other less than half. 2. Your child closes their eyes and holds out both hands. 3. Put one tumbler in each hand. 4. Ask which hand is the heavy pan.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes. Your arms are a balance and your hands are the pans. Which pan is going down?  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Hold one tumbler in each hand. Which one is heavier?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child opens their eyes and puts the tumblers on the tray. 2. Ask how to make them equal without adding any new water. 3. Your child pours from one to the other, a little at a time. 4. They check by looking at the water lines.  
  *Parent says:* No more water from the jug. How can you make these two the same?  
  *Child mode:* Make the two tumblers the same. Use only the water in them.
  - Note: This is the same as moving coins from the heavy cup to the light cup. One tumbler loses what the other gains, so a small pour changes the gap quickly.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child closes their eyes and holds the tumblers again. 2. Ask whether the hands feel level now. 3. Ask who at home pours equal shares, and how they check.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes again. Is your balance level now? How can you tell?  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes and hold them again. Do your hands feel level?
  - Check: One tumbler has more water than the other. No new water is allowed. How do you make them equal? → **Pour from the fuller one into the other**

### p1 · What Weighs the Same? Five days, five things (25 min, 5 days)
- **problem:** How many coins weigh the same as a pencil, an eraser or a spoon?
- **safety:** Keep coins, groundnuts and other small things away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Each day, draw the thing on a strip of paper and write how many coins balanced it, like this: eraser = 3 coins. If it falls between two numbers, write both: more than 3, less than 4.
- **schedule:** day 1: Weigh an eraser against coins. Guess first. Write 'eraser = ___ coins'. (5 min); day 2: Weigh a pencil. Is it heavier or lighter than the eraser? (5 min); day 3: Weigh a teaspoon. (5 min); day 4: Weigh two more small things of your choice. (5 min); day 5: Put all the strips in order, lightest first. Find two things that should balance each other, and test them. (5 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check that the empty balance is level. 2. Your child holds the eraser in one hand and coins in the other, and guesses how many coins weigh the same. 3. Put the eraser in one cup. Your child adds coins to the other cup, one at a time. 4. Stop when it is level, or when it tips the other way. 5. Write the result on a strip.  
  *Parent says:* How many coins do you think weigh the same as this eraser? Hold them and guess. Then test.  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many coins balance the eraser. Then add coins, one at a time.
  - Note: Things rarely balance a whole number of coins exactly. If 3 coins are too few and 4 are too many, write 'more than 3, less than 4'. That is an honest answer.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Before weighing, your child holds the pencil and yesterday's eraser, one in each hand. 2. Ask which is heavier. 3. Weigh the pencil against coins and write it on a strip. 4. Compare the two strips.  
  *Parent says:* Which is heavier, the pencil or the eraser? Guess with your hands. Then let the coins tell us.  
  *Child mode:* Is the pencil heavier or lighter than the eraser? Guess. Then weigh the pencil.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Weigh a teaspoon against coins. Guess first. 2. Write the result on a strip. 3. Ask which of the three things is heaviest so far, and how your child knows.  
  *Parent says:* Now the teaspoon. More coins than the eraser, or fewer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Weigh the teaspoon. Guess first. Is it the heaviest so far?
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child chooses two more small things that fit in the cup. 2. They guess, weigh and write a strip for each. 3. If a thing needs more coins than you have, write 'more than 12 coins'.  
  *Parent says:* You choose today. Pick two small things. Guess each one before you weigh it.  
  *Child mode:* Choose two small things. Guess, then weigh each one.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out all the strips. 2. Your child puts them in order, from the fewest coins to the most. 3. Find two things with the same or nearly the same number of coins. 4. Ask what will happen if those two go in the two cups, with no coins. Test it.  
  *Parent says:* These two things each weighed about the same number of coins. If I put one in each cup, what will happen? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Put your strips in order, lightest first. Which two things might balance each other?
  - Ask: The eraser balanced 3 coins and the matchbox balanced 3 coins. Why should they balance each other? (good: They both weigh the same as 3 coins.; They are the same as the same thing.)
  - Check: A lemon balances 9 coins. A spoon balances 6 coins. Which is heavier? → **The lemon**
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: the Mystery Packet (20 min)
- **problem:** A paper packet hides some coins. The packet and 2 loose coins are in the left cup. 6 coins are in the right cup. The balance is level. How many coins are in the packet?
- **your turn:** Now make a mystery packet for a grown-up. Choose how many coins to hide and how many loose coins to put beside it. Make the balance level. Write the number sentence with a box. Can they find your number?
- **constraints:** You may not open the packet, shake it or feel it.; You may add coins to either cup, or take loose coins out.; You must be able to say why your answer is right.
- **general:** Guess a number. Take the packet out and put that many coins in its place. See whether the balance is still level.; Count on from 2 until you reach 6, and keep track of how many you counted.; Take coins out of the right cup, one at a time, until the packet side goes down.
- **creative:** Take the 2 loose coins out of the left cup, and take 2 coins out of the right cup. The balance stays level, because you did the same to both sides. Now the packet is alone, and the coins facing it tell you what is inside.; Write it as a number sentence with a box for the packet: □ + 2 = 6. Then ask what number in the box makes it true.
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Out of sight, fold 4 coins into a piece of paper. 2. Fold the second paper the same way, empty. 3. Put the packet and 2 loose coins in the left cup. 4. Put the empty paper and 6 coins in the right cup. Check that it is level. 5. Read the problem aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Some coins are hiding in this packet. The balance is level. How could we find out how many are inside, without opening it?  
  *Child mode:* A packet hides some coins. The balance is level. How many are inside?
  - Note: The empty folded paper on the other side makes the test fair, because paper has a little weight too. Tell your child why it is there.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for ideas. 2. Let your child try any idea that keeps to the rules. 3. If they are stuck, read one of the usual ways aloud.  
  *Parent says:* What could you try? Tell me your idea, and then test it.  
  *Child mode:* Think of a way to find the number. Try it.
  - Ask: You think there are 3 inside. How can the balance check your guess? (good: Put 3 coins where the packet was.; If it stays level, I am right.)
  - Note: Guessing and checking is a good method. Most children begin there. Do not show the take-away trick yet.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set the puzzle up again. 2. Ask what will happen if you take 1 coin from each cup. Test it. 3. Take 1 more from each cup. 4. Ask what the balance now shows.  
  *Parent says:* If I take one coin from this side and one from that side, will it stay level? Why? What if I do it again?  
  *Child mode:* Take 1 coin from each cup. Is it still level? Do it again.
  - Ask: Now the packet is alone on its side. What does the other side tell you? (good: There are 4 coins, so the packet has 4.; The packet is the same as 4 coins.)
  - Check: A packet and 3 coins balance 8 coins. You take 3 coins from each side. What is left? → **The packet balances 5 coins**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the packet and count, to check. 2. Now your child makes a packet for you, out of your sight. 3. They set up a level balance with the packet and some loose coins. 4. You solve it aloud, the slow way, so that they can catch your mistakes.  
  *Parent says:* Open it. Were we right? Now you hide some coins for me. Make the balance level, and I will find your number.  
  *Child mode:* Open the packet and count. Now hide some coins for a grown-up to find.
  - Note: With 12 coins, the packet and the loose coins together cannot be more than 6. Help your child see why: the other cup needs just as many.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the first puzzle as □ + 2 = 6. 2. Say that the box stands for the packet. 3. Ask your child to write the number sentence for the packet they made.  
  *Parent says:* The box is the packet. Box and two is the same as six. Which number in the box makes that true?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a box for the packet. Write your puzzle as a number sentence.

## Worksheets
### Level or Not? (core) (core, 13 questions)
1. (mcq) All the coins are alike. What will the balance do?  
   Options: The left side goes down / It stays level / The right side goes down  
   **Answer:** It stays level. *Why:* Both pans hold 4 coins. Neither side is heavier, so the balance stays level.
2. (mcq) All the coins are alike. Which side of the balance goes down?  
   Options: The left side / The right side / Neither side  
   **Answer:** The left side. *Why:* The left pan has 5 coins and the right pan has 3. Five coins are heavier than three, so the left side goes down.
3. (fill) Work out the sum. 2 + 1 = ____  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Two and one more is three. Both sides of the sign are now the same amount.
4. (mcq) What does the sign = mean?  
   Options: Write the answer next / Is the same as / Add them up  
   **Answer:** Is the same as. *Why:* The sign = means that the two sides are the same amount. Picking 'write the answer next' is common, because most sums are printed that way. But 5 = 5 is true and has no sum in it.
5. (truefalse) True or false: 4 = 4  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both sides are four. A number sentence does not need a plus sign to be true.
6. (mcq) All the coins are alike. The left pan has 2 and 2. The right pan has 3. Which side goes down?  
   Options: The left side / The right side / Neither side  
   **Answer:** The left side. *Why:* 2 and 2 make 4. Four coins are heavier than three, so the left side goes down.
7. (fill) Add ____ coin to the right pan to make the balance level.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* The left pan has 4 and the right pan has 3. One more coin on the right makes 4 and 4.
8. (truefalse) True or false: 2 + 2 = 5  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. 2 + 2 is 4, and 4 is not the same as 5. On a balance, the side with 5 would go down.
9. (match) Match each sum to the number that balances it.  
   **Answer:** 2 + 1 → 3; 2 + 2 → 4; 4 + 1 → 5; 3 + 3 → 6. *Why:* Work out each sum. 2 + 1 is 3, 2 + 2 is 4, 4 + 1 is 5 and 3 + 3 is 6.
10. (sort) Sort each number sentence: true or false?  
   **Answer:** 1 + 1 = 2 → True; 3 = 3 → True; 2 + 3 = 4 → False; 4 + 1 = 5 → True; 2 = 1 + 1 → True; 3 + 1 = 5 → False. *Why:* Work out each side and compare. 2 + 3 is 5, not 4. 3 + 1 is 4, not 5. In all the others, both sides are the same amount.
11. (draw) Draw a balance with 3 dots in one pan. Draw dots in the other pan so that it is level.  
   **Answer:** A level balance with 3 dots in each pan.. *Why:* A strong drawing has the bar flat and 3 dots on each side. The same number on both sides keeps it level.
12. (draw) Draw two piles that look different but would balance 5 coins. Write the number sentence.  
   **Answer:** Two piles that make 5, such as 4 and 1 or 3 and 2, with a sentence such as 5 = 4 + 1.. *Why:* Any two piles that come to 5 are right. The piles look different from a single pile of 5, but the amount is the same.
13. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. In the Level Test, why did 3 and 2 balance 4 and 1?  
   **Answer:** Because both sides had 5 coins, so both sides weighed the same.. *Why:* A strong answer says that both sides come to 5. The piles looked different, but the amounts were equal.
### Make It Level (stretch) (stretch, 13 questions)
1. (fill) Make the balance level. 3 + 2 = 4 + ____  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* The left side is 5. The right side has 4, so it needs 1 more to be 5.
2. (fill) Make it true. 2 + 4 = 3 + ____  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* The left side is 6. 3 and 3 more make 6. Writing 6 in the gap is a common slip. It comes from reading = as 'write the answer'.
3. (mcq) Which sum balances 4 + 3?  
   Options: 5 + 1 / 5 + 2 / 4 + 4  
   **Answer:** 5 + 2. *Why:* 4 + 3 is 7. 5 + 2 is also 7. 5 + 1 is only 6, and 4 + 4 is 8.
4. (truefalse) True or false: 6 = 6 + 0  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Adding zero adds nothing, so the right side is still 6.
5. (fill) How many coins must you add to the right pan to make it level? ____  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* The left pan has 7 and the right pan has 4. Count on from 4 to 7: 5, 6, 7. That is 3 more.
6. (mcq) You move 1 coin from the left pan to the right pan. What happens?  
   Options: It still tips left / It is level / It tips right  
   **Answer:** It is level. *Why:* The left pan goes from 6 to 5. The right pan goes from 4 to 5. Now it is 5 and 5, so the balance is level.
7. (fill) This balance is level. You add 2 to the left. Add ____ to the right to keep it level.  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* Do the same to both sides. 5 + 2 on the left needs 5 + 2 on the right.
8. (match) Match the sums that balance each other.  
   **Answer:** 4 + 4 → 6 + 2; 5 + 2 → 3 + 4; 2 + 2 → 3 + 1; 5 + 4 → 6 + 3. *Why:* Work out each sum. 4 + 4 and 6 + 2 are 8. 5 + 2 and 3 + 4 are 7. 2 + 2 and 3 + 1 are 4. 5 + 4 and 6 + 3 are 9.
9. (order) Put these piles in order, lightest first.  
   **Answer:** ['1 + 1', '2 + 1', '2 + 3', '3 + 3', '4 + 4']. *Why:* Work out each pile: 2, 3, 5, 6 and 8. The smallest amount is the lightest.
10. (mcq) 3 coins are in the left pan and 3 groundnuts are in the right pan. What happens?  
   Options: It is level, because 3 = 3 / The coin side goes down / The groundnut side goes down  
   **Answer:** The coin side goes down. *Why:* The coin side goes down. The two sides are the same in number, but a coin is heavier than a groundnut. A balance compares weight.
11. (write) Write three different sums that balance 7.  
   **Answer:** Any three of: 6 + 1, 5 + 2, 4 + 3, 3 + 4, 2 + 5, 1 + 6, 7 + 0.. *Why:* Each sum must come to 7. A good way to find them all is to start at 6 + 1 and move one across each time.
12. (draw) Draw a balance that tips, with 6 dots and 4 dots. Then show one way to make it level.  
   **Answer:** A tilted balance with 6 and 4, and then 2 dots added to the 4, or 2 crossed out from the 6, or 1 moved across.. *Why:* All three ways work. Adding 2 gives 6 and 6. Taking 2 gives 4 and 4. Moving 1 gives 5 and 5.
13. (perspective) Two children look at the sum 8 = 5 + 3.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other child has right. For example: Sita is right that both sides are 8. Malar is right that most sums in books are printed the other way round.. *Why:* The sentence is true. The sign = only says that both sides are the same amount, so it does not matter which side the single number is on.
### Balance Puzzles (challenge) (challenge, 13 questions)
1. (fill) A packet and 2 coins balance 7 coins. How many coins are in the packet? ____  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* Take 2 coins from each side. The balance stays level, and the packet is left facing 5 coins.
2. (fill) Make it true. 8 + 4 = ____ + 5  
   **Answer:** ['7', 'seven']. *Why:* The left side is 12. 7 and 5 make 12. Writing 12 in the gap is the most common slip. It comes from reading = as 'write the answer'.
3. (fill) Make it true. 9 = ____ + 4  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* 5 and 4 make 9. The single number can be on the left of the sign.
4. (truefalse) True or false: if you take 2 from both sides of a level balance, it stays level.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both sides lose the same amount, so they are still the same as each other.
5. (fill) Move ____ coins from the left pan to the right pan to make it level.  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* 9 and 5 make 14 coins altogether. A level balance needs 7 on each side. Moving 2 across gives 7 and 7.
6. (mcq) The left pan has 6 coins and the right has 3. Can you make it level only by moving coins across?  
   Options: Yes, move 1 coin / Yes, move 3 coins / No, it cannot be done  
   **Answer:** No, it cannot be done. *Why:* There are 9 coins altogether. Two equal piles would need 4 and a half coins each. Moving 1 gives 5 and 4. Moving 2 gives 4 and 5. It never comes level.
7. (fill) Make it true. 7 − 2 = 4 + ____  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* 7 take away 2 is 5. 4 and 1 more make 5.
8. (order) Put these in order, smallest amount first.  
   **Answer:** ['3 + 3', '2 + 5', '4 + 4', '10 − 1', '5 + 5']. *Why:* Work out each one: 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10.
9. (match) Match each packet puzzle to the number hidden in the box.  
   **Answer:** □ + 1 = 4 → 3; □ + 3 = 8 → 5; 6 = □ + 4 → 2; 2 + □ = 9 → 7. *Why:* Try the number in the box and check both sides. 3 + 1 = 4. 5 + 3 = 8. 6 = 2 + 4. 2 + 7 = 9.
10. (mcq) A ₹10 note and ten ₹1 coins. In which way are they equal?  
   Options: They weigh the same / They buy the same / They look the same  
   **Answer:** They buy the same. *Why:* They buy the same things, so they are equal in what they are worth. Ten coins are much heavier than one note, and they do not look alike.
11. (write) You move 1 coin from the heavy pan to the light pan. The gap closes by 2. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The heavy pan lost 1 and the light pan gained 1. Each change closes the gap by 1, so together they close it by 2.. *Why:* A strong answer says that two things happened at once: one side went down by 1 and the other went up by 1.
12. (draw) Draw a mystery packet puzzle for a friend to solve. Write its number sentence with a box.  
   **Answer:** A level balance with a packet and some coins on one side and coins on the other, and a sentence such as □ + 3 = 8.. *Why:* The puzzle works if the balance is level and the number in the box makes both sides the same.
13. (perspective) Two thinkers talk about 3 + 2 = 4 + 1.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason from the unit. For example: everyone who adds carefully gets 5 on both sides, or 3 coins and 3 groundnuts were equal only in number.. *Why:* This is a real argument with no agreed answer. Mathematicians and thinkers have held both views for a very long time.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) All the coins are alike. What does this balance do?  
   Options: The left side goes down / It stays level / The right side goes down  
   **Answer:** It stays level. *Why:* The left pan has 3 and 2, which is 5. The right pan has 4 and 1, which is 5. Both sides are the same, so it stays level.
2. (mcq) All the coins are alike. Which side of this balance goes down?  
   Options: The left side / The right side / Neither side  
   **Answer:** The right side. *Why:* The left pan has 2 and 2, which is 4. The right pan has 3 and 2, which is 5. Five coins are heavier, so the right side goes down.
3. (mcq) How do we read the sign = aloud?  
   Options: Is the same as / And now the answer is / Add these together  
   **Answer:** Is the same as. *Why:* We read it as 'is the same as'. It says that the two sides are the same amount. It does not tell you to do anything.
4. (truefalse) True or false: 5 = 5 is a true number sentence.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both sides are five. The sign = only needs the two sides to be the same amount.
5. (fill) Make both sides the same. 3 + 2 = 4 + ____  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* The left side is 5. 4 and 1 more make 5. Writing 5 in the gap means reading = as 'write the answer'.
6. (mcq) Which sum is the same amount as 2 + 4?  
   Options: 3 + 3 / 2 + 5 / 4 + 4  
   **Answer:** 3 + 3. *Why:* 2 + 4 is 6, and 3 + 3 is 6. 2 + 5 is 7, and 4 + 4 is 8.
7. (fill) Add ____ coins to the right pan to make this balance level.  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* The left pan has 6 and the right pan has 4. Count on from 4 to 6: 5, 6. That is 2 more.
8. (mcq) The left pan has 7 coins and the right pan has 5. Which of these makes it level?  
   Options: Add 2 to the left pan / Take 2 from the left pan / Take 2 from the right pan  
   **Answer:** Take 2 from the left pan. *Why:* Taking 2 from the heavy side leaves 5 and 5. Adding to the left, or taking from the right, makes the gap bigger.
9. (mcq) A level balance has 4 coins on each side. You add 1 coin to each side. What happens?  
   Options: It stays level / The left side goes down / The right side goes down  
   **Answer:** It stays level. *Why:* Both sides get the same extra amount. 5 and 5 are still the same, so it stays level.
10. (truefalse) True or false: 3 + 1 = 5  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. 3 + 1 is 4, and 4 is not the same as 5.
11. (mcq) Which number sentence is true?  
   Options: 2 + 3 = 6 / 6 = 4 + 2 / 4 + 1 = 4  
   **Answer:** 6 = 4 + 2. *Why:* 4 + 2 is 6, so 6 = 4 + 2 is true. 2 + 3 is 5, not 6. 4 + 1 is 5, not 4.
12. (mcq) 3 coins are on one side and 3 groundnuts are on the other. The balance tips. Why?  
   Options: 3 is not the same as 3 / A coin is heavier than a groundnut / The balance is broken  
   **Answer:** A coin is heavier than a groundnut. *Why:* A balance compares weight. The two sides are the same in number, but one coin is heavier than one groundnut.
13. (truefalse) True or false: two piles with the same number of things always weigh the same.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Three coins and three groundnuts are the same in number but not in weight. Equal in one way does not mean equal in every way.

## Applications
- A vegetable seller with a hand balance adds or takes away tomatoes until the pan of tomatoes is level with the weight.
- A cook who halves a recipe keeps the two sides equal: half the rice needs half the water.
- A shopkeeper giving change makes two sides equal. What you paid is the same as the price and the change together.
- In kabaddi, each team has 7 players on the court at the start, so that the two sides are equal in number.
- A tailor cutting a blouse folds the cloth in two, so that the left side and the right side come out the same.
- A person carrying two shopping bags walks more easily when both bags weigh about the same.
- An engineer building a bridge works out the push on each side of every part, and makes sure the two are equal, so that nothing moves.
- Older children solve equations by doing the same thing to both sides, just as you did with the Mystery Packet.

## Spotted around us

- The thulabharam at the Guruvayur temple in Kerala. A person sits in one pan of a giant balance. Bananas, jaggery or coconuts are heaped in the other pan until the two sides are level.

- A kabaddi court. Count the players: 7 on each side at the start.
- A butterfly resting with open wings. The left wing and the right wing match each other in size and pattern.
- A honeybee on a flower. In tests, bees like her have learned to pick the picture that is the same as one they just saw.
- The = key on a calculator or a phone. On a calculator it gives the answer. In a number sentence it means something more: the two sides are the same.
- The newspaper or a weather app around 20 March and 22 September. Look at the sunrise and sunset times. They are about 12 hours apart.

## Open questions
- **Is 'equal' something people found in the world, or something people chose?** Some thinkers say equal amounts are equal whether or not anyone looks. Others say that someone must first choose what to compare. No test has settled it.
- **When a bee picks 'the same', does she think of sameness the way we do?** The tests show what bees choose. They cannot show what choosing is like for the bee. Scientists do not agree on how to find out.
- **Do other animals mind when shares are not equal?** In 2003 two scientists, Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal, reported that capuchin monkeys refused a slice of cucumber when a neighbour got a grape for the same task. Later studies disagreed about what the monkeys were reacting to. The question is still argued.
- **Why do so many children read the sign = as 'write the answer'?** Most researchers think it comes from seeing only sums printed as 3 + 2 = __. Nobody is sure that this is the whole reason, or what the best way to prevent it is.

## Connected maths
- Meena has 3 murukkus on her plate and 2 in her tiffin box. Devi has 4 on her plate and 1 in her box. Who has more murukkus? **Neither. They have the same: 5 each.**. Work out each side by itself. Meena has 3 and 2, which is 5. Devi has 4 and 1, which is 5. So 3 + 2 = 4 + 1. Devi's plate looks fuller, but the amounts are equal. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.number-facts.L1`)
- A shopkeeper puts weights worth 8 coins in the left pan. The chana in the right pan weighs the same as 5 coins. How many coins' worth of chana must she add? **3 coins' worth.**. The chana side is lighter. Count on from 5 to 8: 6, 7, 8. That is 3 more. Then both sides weigh the same as 8 coins, and the balance is level. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L1`)
- Two sisters share 10 groundnuts. Anu has 7 and Banu has 3. Anu gives some to Banu so that both have the same. How many does she give? **2 groundnuts.**. There are 10 altogether, so a fair share is 5 each. Anu must go from 7 down to 5, so she gives 2. Banu goes from 3 up to 5. The gap was 4, but Anu gives only 2, because each groundnut she gives closes the gap by 2. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L1`)
- A balance is level with 6 coins on each side. Your little sister takes 2 coins out of the left pan. Find three different ways to make it level again. **Put 2 back on the left. Or take 2 from the right. Or move 1 from the right to the left.**. Now it is 4 and 6. The left side is lighter by 2. Adding 2 to the left gives 6 and 6. Taking 2 from the right gives 4 and 4. Moving 1 across gives 5 and 5. All three are level, with a different number of coins each time. (queues `math.logic.puzzles.L1`)
- A paper packet and 3 loose coins balance 9 coins. How many coins are in the packet? **6 coins.**. Take 3 coins from each side. The balance stays level, because you did the same to both sides. The packet is now alone, and 6 coins are left on the other side. So the packet holds 6. Check: 6 + 3 = 9. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L2`)
