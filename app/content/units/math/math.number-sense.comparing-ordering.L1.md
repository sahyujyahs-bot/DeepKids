# More, fewer, the same: comparing numbers
`math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`

**Summary.** Five spoons in a long row, and seven beans in a short row. Which has more? Your eyes may say the spoons. You will test this with spoons, beans and onions, and find a way to compare that never fools you. You will learn the words more, fewer and the same, which of two numbers up to 20 is bigger, and how to put numbers in order. You will also find out that 7 is a big number next to 5 and a small number next to 9.

**Intro.** *Meena and Kavya each have some laddus. Who has more? Can you tell without counting?* Meena has more. You can tell without counting. Each of Kavya's laddus sits under one of Meena's, like partners. Two of Meena's laddus have no partner. So Meena has more, and Kavya has fewer. Now count to check: Meena has 9 and Kavya has 7. In this unit you will compare things by partnering them, by counting them, and by finding their numbers on a number line.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can tell which group has more, which has fewer, or if they are the same, by partnering the things one to one.
1. I can say why looking alone can fool me: a long row or a heap of big things can have fewer.
2. I can say which of two numbers up to 20 is bigger and which is smaller.
3. I can put numbers up to 20 in order, and name a number between two numbers.
4. I can find how many more one group has than another.
5. I can say that a number is bigger or smaller only next to another number, and that 'more' depends on what we compare.

## Terms
- **Compare**: To look at two groups or two numbers and find out which has more, which has fewer, or if they are the same. *e.g.* You compare the idlis on your plate with the idlis on your sister's plate.
- **More**: A group has more when it still has things left after every thing in the other group has a partner. *e.g.* 6 tumblers and 4 plates: there are more tumblers than plates.
- **Fewer**: A group has fewer when it runs out first. We say fewer for things we can count. *e.g.* 6 tumblers and 4 plates: there are fewer plates than tumblers.
- **The same**: Two groups are the same in number when every thing has a partner and nothing is left over. Another word for this is equal. *e.g.* 5 people and 5 banana leaves: the same number. Everyone gets a leaf.
- **Partner**: To put one thing from one group next to one thing from the other group. One and one, again and again. *e.g.* You put one bean on each spoon. Each bean is the partner of one spoon.
- **Left over**: The things that have no partner after you have partnered all you can. *e.g.* 7 beans and 5 spoons: 2 beans are left over.
- **Bigger number**: The number that stands for more. When you count, you say it later. Another word is greater. *e.g.* 12 is bigger than 8. You say 8 first and reach 12 later.
- **Smaller number**: The number that stands for fewer. When you count, you say it earlier. We say 8 is less than 12. *e.g.* 8 is smaller than 12.
- **Order**: To put numbers in a line by size, from the smallest to the biggest, or from the biggest to the smallest. *e.g.* 9, 4 and 15 in order from the smallest: 4, 9, 15.
- **Between**: A number is between two numbers when it is bigger than one of them and smaller than the other. *e.g.* 10 is between 8 and 12.
- **Number line**: A line with the numbers written in counting order, with the same gap between them. Numbers further along the line are bigger. *e.g.* On a number line, 15 is further along than 11. So 15 is bigger.

## Explanations
### More, fewer, the same: partner them up · *defined*
**Definition:** To compare two groups, give each thing in one group a partner from the other group. The group with things left over has more. The other group has fewer. If nothing is left over, the two groups are the same in number.

Here is a worked example. There are 6 tumblers and 4 plates. Put one tumbler on each plate. All 4 plates now have a tumbler. 2 tumblers are left over. So there are more tumblers than plates, and fewer plates than tumblers. You did not need to count at all. Partnering works even with a heap too big for you to count. A note on words: we say fewer for things we can count, such as fewer plates. We say less for things we pour or measure, such as less sambar, and for numbers, such as 4 is less than 6.
- e.g. 7 children and 7 chairs. Every child sits and no chair is empty. The same number.
- e.g. 5 dosas and 3 people. Give one dosa to each person. 2 dosas are left over, so there are more dosas than people.
- e.g. 8 buttonholes on a shirt and 7 buttons. One hole has no button. There are fewer buttons than holes.
### Looking can fool you · *defined*
**Definition:** A group can look like more because its things are big, or spread far apart, or piled high. How much room a group takes up does not tell you how many things are in it.

Here is a worked example. Put 5 big onions in a long row. Under them put 8 small chana, close together. The onion row is much longer, and the onions are much bigger. Many people point to the onions and say more. Now partner them. Put one chana in front of each onion. Every onion has a chana, and 3 chana are left over. So there are more chana. The onions take up more room, but there are fewer of them. When you want to know which group has more things, do not trust the length of the row or the size of the heap. Partner the things, or count them.
- e.g. A row of 4 buses is far longer than a row of 10 cycles. There are still fewer buses.
- e.g. 10 one-rupee coins make a taller stack than 2 five-rupee coins. Here the taller stack really does have more coins. Looking is sometimes right. You cannot know until you check.
- e.g. Spread 6 beans wide and push 9 beans into a heap. The heap looks small, but the heap has more.
### Bigger and smaller numbers · *defined*
**Definition:** Of two numbers, the bigger number is the one you say later when you count. On a number line it is the one further along. The smaller number is the one you say earlier.

Here is a worked example. Which is bigger, 8 or 12? Count from 1: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. You have reached 8, and you have not yet said 12. You must go on: 9, 10, 11, 12. You say 12 later, so 12 is bigger. Why does this work? Every time you count one more thing, you say the next number. So a group of 12 things has all the partners that a group of 8 needs, and 4 more. Now find both numbers on the number line. 12 is further along than 8. We say '12 is more than 8' and '8 is less than 12'. Both sentences say the same fact.
- e.g. 15 is bigger than 11. On the number line 15 is further along.
- e.g. 0 is smaller than every other number on the line. Zero things is fewer than one thing.
- e.g. 9 and 9 are the same. Neither is bigger.
### How many more? Count the ones left over · *defined*
**Definition:** How many more is the number of things left over after partnering. It is also the number of hops on the number line from the smaller number to the bigger one.

Here is a worked example. Meena has 9 marbles and Kavya has 6. Partner them: one of Meena's with one of Kavya's. After 6 partners, Kavya has no marbles left. Meena has 3 left over. So Meena has 3 more than Kavya. We can also say Kavya has 3 fewer than Meena. On the number line, start at 6 and hop to 9: 7, 8, 9. That is 3 hops. In the picture, the 3 dashed circles show how many more Kavya would need to have the same as Meena.
- e.g. 7 beans and 5 spoons: 2 beans are left over. There are 2 more beans than spoons.
- e.g. One more than 14 is 15. One less than 14 is 13.
- e.g. 12 and 12: nothing is left over. Zero more.
### Putting numbers in order · *defined*
**Definition:** To order numbers is to put them in a line by size. From the smallest: start with the number you say first when you count. From the biggest: start with the number you say last.

Here is a worked example. Put 8, 3 and 5 in order from the smallest. Count from 1 and listen for the three numbers. You say 3 first, then 5, then 8. So the order is 3, 5, 8. In beans, the rows make a staircase: each row is longer than the row above it. From the biggest, the order turns round: 8, 5, 3. A number between two others is bigger than one and smaller than the other. 5 is between 3 and 8. So are 4, 6 and 7.
- e.g. 12, 7, 15 from the smallest: 7, 12, 15.
- e.g. The numbers between 10 and 13 are 11 and 12.
- e.g. House numbers on one side of a street, the pages of a book and the dates on a calendar are all in order.
### Ten and some more: comparing numbers from 11 to 20 · *defined*
**Definition:** Every number from 11 to 19 is one ten and some more ones. To compare two of them, compare the extra ones. A number with a full ten is always bigger than a number below ten.

Here is a worked example. Which is bigger, 14 or 17? 14 is one ten and 4 more. 17 is one ten and 7 more. Both have one full ten, so the tens are the same. Now compare the extra ones: 7 is more than 4. So 17 is bigger than 14. Another example: which is bigger, 9 or 12? 12 has a full ten and 2 more. 9 does not even fill one ten. So 12 is bigger, though the 9 looks like a big numeral and 12 ends in a small 2. And 20 is two full tens, so 20 is bigger than every number from 11 to 19.
- e.g. 16 and 13: both have a ten. 6 is more than 3, so 16 is bigger.
- e.g. 11 and 8: 11 has a full ten. 8 does not. 11 is bigger.
- e.g. 19 and 20: 19 is one ten and 9. One more makes the second ten full. 20 is bigger by 1.
### More of what? First choose what you compare · *defined*
**Definition:** The word more is not complete until you say more of what. More pieces is not the same as more paper. More coins is not the same as more money.

Here is a worked example. Take two sheets of paper of the same size. Tear sheet A into 2 pieces and sheet B into 8 pieces. Who has more? If you compare the number of pieces, B has more: 8 is more than 2. If you compare the amount of paper, they are the same, because the sheets were the same size. Both answers are right. They answer different questions. Before people compare, they choose the question. In a shop the choice matters. Ten one-rupee coins are more coins than one twenty-rupee note. But the note is more money.
- e.g. 2 big dosas or 5 mini dosas: the mini dosas are more in number. The big dosas may be more to eat.
- e.g. A cricket team with more wickets left has not won. The team with more runs wins. The rules of the game say what to compare.
- e.g. A class with more children is not a class with more teachers.
### Looking fools us in the sky too: the Sun and the Moon · *defined*
**Definition:** In the sky the Sun and the Moon look about the same size. They are not. The Sun is about 400 times as wide as the Moon. It looks small because it is about 400 times as far away.

In the Partner Test, a long row of spoons looked like more than a short row of beans. Your eyes compared the room each row took up, not the number. The sky plays a trick of the same kind. Hold a one-rupee coin close to your eye and it can hide a whole building far away. The coin is not bigger than the building. It is nearer. The Moon is near the Earth, and the Sun is very far. So the small Moon and the huge Sun look about the same size from here. Never look straight at the Sun, because it harms your eyes. People found the real sizes by measuring, not by looking. To compare truly, we need a careful way. For groups of things, that way is partnering and counting.
- e.g. A crow on your window sill looks bigger than an aeroplane high in the sky. The aeroplane is far bigger.
- e.g. From the top of a hill, buses on the road below look like toys.
- e.g. During a total eclipse of the Sun, the Moon covers the Sun almost exactly. That can happen only because the two look the same size from the Earth.
### Other living beings compare too · *debated*
Humans are not the only animals who compare numbers. In 1994 three scientists, Karen McComb, Craig Packer and Anne Pusey, played recorded roars to lionesses in the Serengeti in Tanzania. Sometimes the loudspeaker played one strange lioness roaring. Sometimes it played three roaring together. The lionesses who lived there walked towards one stranger more often than towards three. When they did go towards three, they went more slowly and carefully. In 2019 scientists in Thailand gave six Asian elephants two closed buckets of sunflower seeds. The elephants could smell the seeds but could not see them. Mostly they chose the bucket with more seeds. They did best when the two amounts were very different. In 2008 scientists in Italy found that a female mosquitofish, given a choice, swims to join the bigger group of fish. She could tell 3 fish from 4. She could not tell 4 fish from 5.
- e.g. A lioness hears three strangers roaring and stays back. Meeting a bigger group in a fight is dangerous.
- e.g. An elephant sniffs two closed buckets and walks to the one that holds more seeds.
- e.g. A small fish joins the bigger shoal. In a bigger group each fish is safer from a hunter.
- **Scientists who say the animals compare numbers:** The lionesses heard roars and the elephants smelled seeds. Neither could see a heap to judge its size. They still chose well. So they were comparing how many, or how much, in their own way.
- **Scientists who are more careful:** More fish also means more moving shapes, and more seeds means a stronger smell. The animals may compare amounts without anything like our exact numbers. Their choices get worse when the two amounts are close, and ours do not when we count. Nobody yet knows how the elephants do it by smell.
### Stretch: comparing two groups that never meet · *defined*
**Definition:** If A has more than B, and B has more than C, then A has more than C. You know this without comparing A and C at all.

Here is a worked example. Cup A has more beans than cup B. You found that by partnering. Cup B has more beans than cup C. You found that by partnering too. Now, does A have more than C? You have never put A and C side by side. But think of the partners. Every bean in C has a partner in B, and B has some left over. Every bean in B has a partner in A, and A has some left over. So every bean in C has a partner in A, with even more left over. A has more than C. With numbers it is the same. 9 is more than 6, and 6 is more than 4. So 9 is more than 4. This is why three numbers can be put in one line. Next year you will write it with a sign: 9 > 6 means 9 is more than 6.
- e.g. Akka is taller than you. You are taller than the baby. So Akka is taller than the baby, without measuring.
- e.g. 15 is more than 12. 12 is more than 7. So 15 is more than 7.
- e.g. This does not work for every kind of comparing. In stone, paper, scissors, stone beats scissors and scissors beat paper, but stone does not beat paper.

## Mostly agreed
- If you partner two groups one to one and one group has things left over, that group has more. *How we know:* Anyone can test it. Partner beans with spoons, then count both. The group with things left over always has the bigger count.
- Spreading a row out makes the row longer. It does not put more things in the row. *How we know:* In the Partner Test, 5 spoons far apart made a longer row than 7 beans close together. After partnering, 2 beans were left over.
- A number that you say later when you count is bigger than a number you say earlier. *How we know:* Each new number in the count stands for one more thing. Build 8 beans and 12 beans and partner them. The 12 always has 4 left over.
- Some other animals can tell which of two groups has more. *How we know:* Lionesses went towards one roaring stranger more often than towards three (McComb, Packer and Pusey, 1994). Asian elephants chose the bucket with more seeds by smell (Plotnik and others, 2019).

## How we found out
**Jean Piaget and Alina Szeminska. Later Jacques Mehler, Thomas Bever, James McGarrigle and Margaret Donaldson, 1941, 1967 and 1974.** About eighty years ago in Geneva, Switzerland, two scientists, Jean Piaget and Alina Szeminska, tested what young children think about more and fewer. They laid out two rows of counters, partnered one to one. The children agreed that the rows were the same. Then the scientists spread one row out. Many children under six now said the longer row had more. In a book in 1941, the two wrote that young children do not yet understand that a number stays the same when the things are moved. For years most teachers believed this. Then other scientists looked again. In 1967 Jacques Mehler and Thomas Bever tested children of two and three with two rows. Many of the youngest picked the row with more, even when it was the shorter row. In the 1970s James McGarrigle and Margaret Donaldson let a 'naughty teddy' mess up one row. Many more children now said the rows were still the same. So young children understood more than the first test showed. The way a question is asked can change the answer.  
*What people believed before:* Children under six cannot understand that a row keeps its number when it is spread out. Later tests, with a row chosen by the child and with a 'naughty teddy', showed that many young children do understand it when the question makes sense to them.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The longer row has more things in it.** (Many young children, and grown-ups in a hurry; *rejected*)  
  For: Often it is true. If the things are the same size and the gaps are the same, a longer row does have more.  
  Against: Change the gaps or the size of the things, and the rule fails. 5 spoons far apart make a longer row than 7 beans close together. Partnering shows the truth every time.
- **Children under about six cannot understand that a row keeps its number when it is spread out.** (Jean Piaget and Alina Szeminska, in a book from 1941; *debated*)  
  For: They spread out one of two equal rows in front of children. Many children under six then said that the longer row had more.  
  Against: In 1967 Jacques Mehler and Thomas Bever found that many children of two and three picked the row with more, even when it was the shorter row. In the 1970s James McGarrigle and Margaret Donaldson let a 'naughty teddy' mess up a row. Many more children then answered correctly. So part of the trouble was how the grown-up asked.
- **Seven is a big number.** (Everyday talk; *debated*)  
  For: In daily life we compare with what is usual. Seven dosas for one child's breakfast really is a lot.  
  Against: Seven grains of rice for breakfast is very little. Seven is bigger than 5 and smaller than 9. The word big always hides a second number that we are comparing with.

## Roots
We compared spoons and beans, cards and cricket scores. Now let us think about what 'bigger' and 'more' really mean.

**Deep truth.** In the card game Bigger or Smaller, your 7 was the bigger card next to a 5. Then the same 7 was the smaller card next to a 9. The 7 did not change. So 7 is not a big number or a small number by itself. It is bigger or smaller only next to another number.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'a big number' as if big were inside the number, like the colour inside a crayon. But big and small always need two things, side by side.  
*Evidence:* In the game Bigger or Smaller, the same card was the bigger one in one round and the smaller one in another. On the number line, 7 is further along than 5 and not as far along as 9.

- *who am i:* You are the big one next to a baby, and the small one next to Paatti. You did not change. Only the one beside you changed.
- *what is life:* A lioness hears that three strangers are more than one. An elephant smells which bucket holds more seeds. Many living beings compare.
- *what is this world:* The Sun looks as small as the Moon. But the Sun is far bigger, and very far away. To know what is bigger, we must compare with care.

Think of one bean alone on a plate. Is it more? Is it fewer? You cannot say. Put a second bean on the plate, and now there is one and one more. Add many beans, and you can make rows and put them in order. So more, fewer, bigger and smaller all begin when one becomes two.

- **Tao Te Ching 2:** The Tao Te Ching says that long and short shape each other, and high and low rest on each other. (A row of beans is long only next to a shorter row. A number is big only next to a smaller number.)
- **Thirukkural 104:** Thiruvalluvar, the Tamil poet, says: a help as small as a millet seed is counted as big as a palmyra palm by those who know its worth. (The same thing can be small by one measure and big by another. So before we compare, we choose what to compare. The two sheets of paper showed this: more pieces, the same paper.)
- Maths (settled): Babies who are six months old cannot count or talk. Even so, they can tell a picture of 8 dots from a picture of 16 dots. They cannot yet tell 8 dots from 12 (Xu and Spelke, 2000). So a rough sense of more comes before number words.
- Maths (open): Is 'more' out there in the world, or does it need someone to compare? Mathematicians and thinkers still argue about this.
- Other view, Plato, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,400 years ago, and many mathematicians today: They say that 7 is more than 5 whether or not anyone compares them. It was true before there were people, and it is true everywhere. Yes, you choose whether to compare pieces or paper. But once you have chosen, the answer is fixed. You find it. You do not make it.
- Practice, Short breath, long breath (3 min): Sit still. Take one small, short breath. Now take a long, slow breath. That second breath was long. Now take the longest breath you can. Next to this one, was the second breath still long? Nothing about that breath changed. Sit quietly for three more breaths and just notice each one.
- Ask: Are you big or small? Next to whom?
- Ask: If there were only one thing in the whole world, could it be big?
- Ask: One girl has 3 close friends. Another has 10 toys. Who has more? Can these even be compared?
- Ask: Did people make up 'more', or did they find it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Can you find out who has more without knowing any numbers at all? How? (hint: Think of what you did with the spoons and the beans.)
- *deep:* Is 10 a big number or a small number? (hint: 10 laddus for you. 10 grains of rice for you. 10 people in a city.)
- *deep:* Is there a number that is bigger than every other number? Is there one that is smaller than every other? (hint: Name a number. Can you say one more? Now try going down, one fewer each time.)
- *big:* A mango tree has fewer fruits than a neem tree has leaves. Does that make the neem tree better? (hint: More of what? Who decides what should be compared?)
- *big:* The Sun looks as small as the Moon. What else might look small only because it is far away? (hint: Look at the stars tonight. Each one is a sun.)
- *big:* A lioness chooses not to fight three strangers. Is she doing maths? (hint: She does not use number words. Does comparing need words?)

## Story: The Window Seat
*Yazhini, Nila and Paatti are made up. The beach is real: trains from Rameswaram do cross the sea on a long bridge to reach the mainland.*

On the last morning of the holiday, Paatti took the two sisters to the beach near Rameswaram. The train home to Madurai left that evening.

"There is one window seat," said Paatti. "And there are two of you."

Yazhini was nine. Nila was six. They looked at Paatti, and then at each other.

"So this is how we will do it." Paatti sat down on the dry sand and opened her umbrella against the sun. "Whoever brings me more shells before the water reaches that boat gets the window."

Yazhini ran. She knew this beach. Beyond the fishing boats the sea left big shells: fat white clams as wide as her palm, and once a spotted cowrie the size of a lemon. She had the cloth bag from the vegetable market, and she meant to fill it.

Nila did not run. She had only the small steel tumbler from the water bottle. She squatted where the last wave had turned back, and looked at the wet sand. It was full of shells too. They were tiny, pink and yellow and striped, each no bigger than her smallest fingernail. She picked them up one at a time and dropped them into the tumbler. Tik. Tik. Tik.

When the water reached the boat, Paatti called them back.

Yazhini came with the bag held in both arms. It bulged. It clacked when she walked. She set it down in front of Paatti like a sack of rice. Nila came behind her with the tumbler in one hand. It was not even full.

Yazhini looked at the tumbler and laughed. "I get the window."

"Mine are more," Nila said.

"Yours fit in a tumbler. Mine need a bag. Look how big my heap is." Yazhini tipped the bag out, and the shells slid into a hill on the sand. It was a fine hill. Nila tipped her tumbler out beside it. Her shells made a small flat patch, like spilled dal.

Nila's eyes filled. But she kept looking at her patch. There were a lot of shells in it. She was sure there were.

"Count, then," said Yazhini. She was kind about it, because she could see who was going to win. She counted her own shells first, touching each one. "Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen. Sixteen! Now you."

Nila began. "One, two, three." She got to twenty. After twenty she was not sure. She said twenty-eight, then twenty-six, and then she stopped. The little shells all looked alike, and she had lost her place.

"See?" said Yazhini. "You don't even know how many you have."

Paatti said nothing. She tilted her umbrella and watched.

Nila wiped her nose with the back of her hand. She did not know how many she had. That was true. But Paatti had not said the window was for the girl who knew her number. She had said it was for the girl with more.

Nila picked up one of Yazhini's big clams and put it down on the flat sand. Next to it she put one of her own tiny pink shells.

"What are you doing?" said Yazhini.

"One of yours. One of mine." Nila put down another big clam below the first, and another tiny shell beside it. "One of yours. One of mine."

Yazhini frowned. Then, because she was a fair girl, she knelt down and helped. Two lines grew along the beach, side by side. Big, small. Big, small. The line of big shells looked grand. The line of small shells looked like a trail of crumbs.

Then Yazhini reached for her hill, and there was nothing there. Her line stopped.

Nila's patch was still on the sand. She went on alone. She put one more tiny shell under the last one. Then another. Then another. Her trail of crumbs walked on past the end of Yazhini's line, and it kept going.

Nobody spoke. A crab ran sideways between the two lines and went down a hole.

"That is not fair," Yazhini said at last. "Mine are bigger. Look at this one. All of yours together would fit inside it. I have more shell."

"You have bigger," said Nila. "I have more."

They both turned to Paatti.

Paatti folded her umbrella. "What did I ask for?" she said. "Did I say the heaviest bag? Did I say the biggest shells?"

Yazhini looked at the two lines for a long moment. "You said more shells," she said. Then she added, "You should have said which kind of more."

"Perhaps I should have," said Paatti, and she laughed. "Next time I will ask for the heaviest bag, and you will win. Today I asked for more shells."

That evening Nila sat by the window with the tumbler in her lap. Yazhini sat next to her and held the spotted cowrie against her ear. When the train rolled out onto the long bridge and there was sea on both sides, Nila pulled her sister close, and they both pressed their faces to the bars.

Nila still did not know how many shells she had. She only knew that it was more than sixteen.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a bigger heap is not the same as more things, and you can find out who has more without being able to count that far. Nila partners one of her shells with one of her sister's until one line runs out. The story also shows that 'more' needs a question. Yazhini has more shell, and Nila has more shells. Do not explain this to your child. Ask the questions below and let them find it.

- Ask: Nila could not count all her shells. How could she still be sure she had more?
- Ask: One heap was a big hill and one was a small flat patch. Why did the small patch have more shells?
- Ask: Yazhini said, 'I have more shell.' Was she wrong? More of what?
- Ask: Find two groups of things at home where the bigger heap has fewer things in it.

## Games
### g1 · Bigger or Smaller? The coin decides (12 min)
- **rules:** Use the 20 number cards. To make them by hand, write 1 to 20 on slips of paper and draw that many dots on the back, in rows of five.; Two players: mix the cards and deal 10 each, face down.; Each round, toss a coin first. Heads: the bigger number wins. Tails: the smaller number wins.; Both players turn over their top card. Each says their own number aloud.; The winner says the comparing sentence, such as '12 is more than 7' or '7 is less than 12', and takes both cards.; Not sure? Turn both cards over and partner the dots. The card with dots left over is the bigger number.; After 10 rounds, lay your won cards in a row next to the other player's row, one to one. The player with cards left over wins the game.; Three or four players: deal 5 cards each. On heads the biggest card takes them all. On tails the smallest card takes them all.; Playing alone: turn over two cards and toss the coin. Say the sentence, then check with the dots. Try to get through all 10 pairs with no mistake.

### g2 · Secret Number: more or less? (10 min)
- **rules:** Lay the number strip from 0 to 20 between the players. Put a heap of about 20 beans beside it.; One player is the keeper. The keeper thinks of a secret number on the strip, and writes it on a slip of paper that stays hidden.; The other player is the guesser.   *Child mode:* Lay the number strip from 0 to 20. One player hides a number. The other guesses.

  - Note: 'More than 12' rules out 12 itself and every number before it. Children often cover only the 12. Ask about 9 and 5 as well.
  - Check: The keeper says, 'My number is more than 12.' Which number can it be? → **15**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play two rounds each. 2. Count the guesses for each round on your fingers. 3. After a round, ask which first guess works best.  
  *Parent says:* That took you five guesses. Which number is a clever first guess? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Play two rounds each. Count your guesses. Try to need fewer each time.
  - Ask: Why is 10 a better first guess than 1? (good: It cuts the strip in half.; With 1, I only cover one or two numbers.; The answer 'more' or 'less' covers ten numbers at once.)
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say each player's number of guesses. 2. Ask who wins. 3. Point out that in this game the smaller number wins.  
  *Parent says:* You needed 4 guesses and I needed 6. Who wins? Is the winner's number bigger or smaller?  
  *Child mode:* Who needed fewer guesses? That player wins.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Partner Test: can your eyes tell which has more? (25 min)
- **question:** Can we tell which of two groups has more just by looking? And is there a way to compare that is always right?
- **predict:** In each round, look without counting and guess: which group has more, or are they the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** In every round we guess first, by looking only. Then we check the same way each time: one thing from the first group gets one partner from the second group. Last, we count both groups. Nobody adds or takes away a thing between the guess and the check.
- **safety:** Keep beans, chana and buttons away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** A grown-up draws the table with four rounds. For each round, write the guess, which group had some left over, and the two counts. At the end the child says what they found, and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** Round | My guess by looking | Which group had some left over | The two counts
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the table with four rounds. 2. Lay 5 spoons in a long row, a hand-width apart. 3. Under them, lay 7 rajma close together. 4. Do not say the numbers. 5. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Do not count yet. Just look. Which row has more things, the spoons or the beans? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the two rows. Do not count. Which row has more things? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why did you guess that? (good: The spoon row is longer.; The spoons are bigger.; The beans look like more to me.)
  - Note: Many six-year-olds choose the spoons, because that row is longer. Accept any guess that comes with a reason. Do not say who is right. The next step will show it.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to put one bean on each spoon. 2. Ask which group has some left over. 3. Now count both groups together. 4. Write it all in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Give every spoon one bean. Are any left over? So which has more, the spoons or the beans?  
  *Child mode:* Put one bean on each spoon. Which group has some left over? That group has more. Now count both.
  - Ask: Your eyes said one thing. The partners said another. Which do you trust more? Why? (good: The partners, because every spoon got a bean.; The partners. The beans had two extra.; The long row tricked me.)
  - Note: Two beans are left over, so the beans are more: 7 and 5. If your child guessed right in step 1, ask how sure they felt. The next rounds are harder for the eyes.
  - Check: Every spoon has one bean. Two beans are left over. Which has more? → **The beans**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay 4 onions or potatoes in a row. 2. Beside them, put 9 chana in a small heap. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it. 4. Your child gives each onion one chana. 5. Count both groups and fill the table.  
  *Parent says:* Here are big onions and small chana. Which group has more things? Why do you think so? Now check.  
  *Child mode:* Big onions and small chana. Guess which group has more things. Then give each onion one chana.
  - Ask: The onions take up more room. Why are there still fewer of them? (good: Each onion is big.; The chana are small, so many fit in a small place.; Room is not the same as how many.)
  - Note: 4 onions and 9 chana leave 5 chana with no partner. If your child says the onions are more, ask: more what? There is more onion to eat, but there are fewer onions.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push 8 rajma into a tight heap. 2. Spread 8 chana in a wide ring around the heap. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it. 4. Your child gives each rajma one chana, then counts both.  
  *Parent says:* A small heap and a wide ring. Which has more, or are they the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A small heap and a wide ring. Guess which has more. Then give each rajma one chana.
  - Ask: Nothing was left over. What does that tell you? (good: They are the same.; Both are 8.; Every one has a partner.)
  - Note: This round has no winner: 8 and 8. Children often expect one group to have more every time. Let the partnering surprise them.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay 11 rajma in a row. 2. Under it, lay 12 chana in a row of the same length. 3. Ask for a guess. Then partner and count. 4. Draw a number line from 0 to 20. 5. For each round, your child points to the two counts on the line.  
  *Parent says:* These rows are the same length. Can your eyes tell which has more? Check. Then find both numbers on the line.  
  *Child mode:* Two rows, the same length. Guess, then partner them. Then find both counts on the number line.
  - Ask: Look at the two numbers from each round on the line. Where is the group that had more? (good: Further along.; It is always the later number.; Nearer to 20.)
  - Note: Wait for this discovery: the group with more always has the number further along the line. Round 1 was 5 and 7. Round 2 was 4 and 9. Round 4 was 11 and 12. Let your child say it in their own words.
  - Check: You partner 11 rajma with 12 chana. How many chana are left over? → **1**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 9 chana in one katori and 6 rajma in another. 2. Tell your child the two numbers. Cover the katoris. 3. Ask which has more, and how many will be left over. Ask why. 4. Your child partners them to check.  
  *Parent says:* This katori has 9 and this one has 6. Which has more? How many will have no partner? Why?  
  *Child mode:* One katori has 9. One has 6. Guess how many will be left over. Then partner them and see.
  - Ask: How could you find the 3 on the number line? (good: Hop from 6 to 9.; 7, 8, 9. Three hops.)
  - Note: 3 chana are left over. Any guess with a reason is fine. Afterwards, show the hops from 6 to 9 on the number line: 7, 8, 9.
  - Check: One cup has 10 beans and one has 7. How many more are in the first cup? → **3**
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child what they think, what they saw, and why. 3. Write their words under the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your guesses and the counts. When did your eyes get it wrong? What never got it wrong?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. When were your eyes wrong? What way of checking was always right?
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it is so? (good: Looking can be wrong. Partnering is always right.; The long row had fewer.; Big things take more room, but that is not more things.; The one with more is further along the number line.)
  - Note: A full answer has a claim, what was seen, and a reason. For example: 'Looking can fool me. The spoon row was longer, but the beans had two left over. Long is not the same as many.'
  - Check: 6 big lemons sit in a long row. 10 small beans sit in a short row. Which group has more things? → **The beans**

### a2 · The Bean Staircase: numbers in order (12 min)
- **problem:** What do the numbers look like when they stand in order? Build them and see.
- **record:** Draw your staircase from 1 to 7. Write the number beside each step.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to make a row of 1 bean. 2. Under it, a row of 2. Then 3, 4 and 5. 3. Start every row at the same left edge. 4. Ask what they notice about the shape.

  *Child mode:* Make rows of 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 beans. Start each row at the same edge. What do you see?
  - Ask: How much longer is each row than the row above it? (good: One bean.; One more each time.)
  - Note: The rows make steps. Each row has exactly one bean with no partner in the row above. That one extra bean is what 'one more' means.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child adds rows of 6 and 7. 2. Your child closes their eyes. 3. Swap two rows. 4. Your child finds the two rows and puts them back.

  *Parent says:* Pick five cards. Put them in order, smallest first. Which number would you say first when you count?  
  *Child mode:* Pick five number cards. Lay them in order, smallest first. Check with the number line.
  - Note: If two cards are in the wrong order, do not move them. Ask your child to find both on the number line and see which is further along.
  - Check: Which list is in order, smallest first? → **7, 12, 15**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say two numbers with a gap, such as 8 and 12. 2. Your child names a number between them. 3. Take turns. Let your child set numbers for you.  
  *Parent says:* I say 8 and 12. Tell me a number between them. Now you give me two numbers.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up says two numbers. Say a number between them. Then swap.
  - Ask: Is there a number between 6 and 7 on our number line? (good: No. They are next to each other.; Not on this line.)
  - Note: 9, 10 and 11 are all between 8 and 12. For 6 and 7, there is no whole number between. Later your child will meet halves, which do sit between.

### a4 · Maths in sport: mini hand cricket (15 min)
- **problem:** Who scored more runs? And how many more does the other player need to win?
- **record:** After each match, write the two scores. Circle the bigger one. Write how many more.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit facing each other. One player bats and one bowls. 2. Both hide a hand, say 'one, two, three' and show 1, 2 or 3 fingers. 3. Different numbers: the batter takes as many beans as her own fingers. 4. The same number: the batter is out. 5. The batter is also out after 6 balls.  
  *Parent says:* We each show one, two or three fingers. If they are different, you take your number in beans. If they match, you are out.  
  *Child mode:* Show 1, 2 or 3 fingers together. Different? Take your number in beans. The same? You are out.
  - Note: Real hand cricket uses the numbers 1 to 6, and players add up the runs in their heads. Here the numbers stop at 3 and beans keep the score. So the most a batter can score is 18.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the first match. Your child bats first, then you. 2. Each player's beans go in her own katori. 3. At the end, tip both katoris out. 4. Your child partners the beans, one from each row.  
  *Parent says:* The match is over. Do not count yet. Put one of my beans next to one of yours. Who has some left over?  
  *Child mode:* Play the match. Then partner your beans with the other player's beans. Who has some left over?
  - Ask: Who won? By how many runs? (good: I did, by 3. Three of my beans have no partner.; You did. You have 2 more.)
  - Note: The beans left over are the winning margin. Cricket reports say it the same way: 'won by 3 runs'. If nothing is left over, the match is a tie.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count each row of beans and write the two scores. 2. Your child finds both on the number line. 3. Play a second match. This time you bat first. 4. Before your child bats, ask what score will win.  
  *Parent says:* I scored 7. What is the smallest score that beats me? How many do you need?  
  *Child mode:* The grown-up bats first. Count her runs. What score do you need to win?
  - Ask: I have 7. You have 5 so far. How many more do you need to pass me? (good: 3 more. 6, 7, 8.; Two more to be the same, three more to win.)
  - Note: To win, the second batter needs one more than the first batter's score. In real cricket this number is called the target.
  - Check: The first batter scored 9 runs. What is the smallest score that beats her? → **10**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the four scores from the two matches on slips of paper. 2. Your child puts them in order, biggest first. 3. Ask which was the best innings and which was the lowest.  
  *Parent says:* Here are our four scores. Put them in order, biggest first. Whose was the best innings?  
  *Child mode:* Put the four scores in order, biggest first. Which was the best score?
  - Ask: Where else do people compare two numbers to see who won? (good: Kabaddi points.; Goals in football.; Marks in carrom.; Votes.)

### p1 · The More or Fewer Diary: five days of comparing at home (25 min, 5 days)

  *Child mode:* Each day, compare two new groups. Guess first. Then check and draw the dots.
  - Ask: How many more? How do you know? (good: 3 more. Three dots have no partner.; From 6 to 9 is three hops.)
  - Note: Doors and windows cannot be moved, so your child makes one pencil mark for each and partners the marks. For the names, write one letter in each box of squared paper, one name under the other.

- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 5, your child chooses two groups to compare, and does a page alone. 2. Then write the bigger number from each of the five pages on a slip. 3. Your child lays the five slips in order, smallest first.  
  *Parent says:* Today you choose what to compare. Then let us put the week's five numbers in order.  
  *Child mode:* Choose two groups yourself. Compare them. Then put the week's five numbers in order.
  - Ask: Which day had the biggest number? Which had the smallest? (good: Any answer that matches the slips.)

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: Four Cups in a line, without counting (20 min)
- **problem:** Four cups hold different numbers of beans. Put the cups in a line from the fewest beans to the most. You may not count and you may not say any number.
- **your turn:** Now make your own plan and try it with four new secret numbers. Count your partnerings. Can you do it in 5 or fewer? Then try with five cups.
- **constraints:** No counting and no number words.; You may tip out only two cups at a time, and partner their beans in two rows.; Put the beans back in their own cups before you take the next two cups.; Try to use as few partnerings as you can.
- **general:** Compare every cup with every other cup. That takes 6 partnerings. Then work out the order from your notes.; Find the cup with the most first: compare two cups, then compare the winner with the next cup. Set the top cup aside and start again with the rest.
- **creative:** Use what you already know. If red has more than blue, and blue has more than green, then red has more than green. You do not need to check that pair.; Put two cups in order first. Then fit each new cup into the line, starting from the middle of the line.; Draw as you go: an arrow from the cup with fewer to the cup with more.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Out of sight, put 5, 8, 11 and 14 beans in four marked cups. 2. Mix up the cups. 3. Explain the rules. 4. Your child picks any two cups and partners their beans. 5. Note which cup had more. Put the beans back.  
  *Parent says:* Line these cups up from fewest to most. No counting. You may partner two cups at a time.  
  *Child mode:* Pick two cups. Tip them out in two rows. Partner the beans. Which cup had more?
  - Note: Keep the two rows well apart so that every bean goes back to the right cup. If beans get mixed, count them back yourself, quietly.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child compares a second pair that shares one cup with the first pair. 2. Before a third check, ask what they already know. 3. Read the usual ways and the creative ways aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Red had more than blue. Blue had more than green. Do you need to check red and green? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Red has more than blue. Blue has more than green. What do you know about red and green?
  - Ask: How can you be sure without checking? (good: Red beat blue and blue beat green, so red has even more than green.; Green's beans all have partners in blue, and blue's all have partners in red.)
  - Check: Red has more than blue. Blue has more than green. Which cup has the fewest beans? → **Green**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child makes a plan and finishes the line of four cups. 2. Make one tally mark for each partnering. 3. Do not help unless asked.  
  *Parent says:* Finish the line. I will make a mark each time you partner two cups. Let us see how few you need.  
  *Child mode:* Finish the line from fewest to most. Use as few partnerings as you can.
  - Note: Four cups can always be ordered with 5 partnerings. Sometimes 3 or 4 are enough. 6 means every pair was checked. Any working way is good. Fewer checks is a bonus.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now your child counts the beans in each cup, along the line. 2. Check that the numbers go up. 3. Ask your child to explain their plan to someone else at home.  
  *Parent says:* Now you may count. Do the numbers go up along your line? Tell me how your plan worked.  
  *Child mode:* Now count each cup along your line. Do the numbers go up? Tell someone your plan.
  - Ask: Which check did you not need to do? Why not? (good: Red and green, because I knew from blue.; The biggest with the smallest.)

## Worksheets
### More, Fewer, the Same (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) Which row has more dots?  
   Options: Row A / Row B / They are the same  
   **Answer:** Row A. *Why:* Row A has more. Each dot in row B sits under a dot in row A, and row A has 2 dots with no partner.
2. (fill) Give each vadai one idli. ____ idlis have no partner.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* 4 idlis get a vadai as a partner. 3 idlis are left over. So there are 3 more idlis than vadais.
3. (truefalse) A longer row always has more things in it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A row can be long because its things are big or far apart. 5 spoons far apart make a longer row than 7 beans close together.
4. (mcq) Which number is bigger: 9 or 6?  
   Options: 9 / 6 / They are the same  
   **Answer:** 9. *Why:* 9 is bigger. When you count you say 6 first and reach 9 later. On the number line 9 is further along.
5. (fill) One more than 7 is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* The next number after 7 is 8. One more thing means the next number in the count.
6. (order) Put these numbers in order, smallest first.  
   **Answer:** ['2', '4', '5', '8']. *Why:* Counting up, you say 2 first, then 4, then 5, then 8. A number you say later is bigger.
7. (match) Match each pair of groups to the words that fit.  
   **Answer:** 6 tumblers and 6 plates → the same; 3 spoons and 5 plates → fewer spoons; 9 beans and 4 cups → more beans. *Why:* 6 and 6 leave nothing over, so they are the same. 3 spoons run out before 5 plates, so there are fewer spoons. 9 beans leave 5 over after 4 cups, so there are more beans.
8. (mcq) There are 5 children and 5 chairs. Every child sits. What is true?  
   Options: More children / More chairs / The same number  
   **Answer:** The same number. *Why:* The same number. Every child has a chair and no chair is empty. Nothing is left over on either side.
9. (sort) Sort each number: smaller than 10, or bigger than 10?  
   **Answer:** 7 → Smaller than 10; 13 → Bigger than 10; 4 → Smaller than 10; 18 → Bigger than 10; 11 → Bigger than 10; 9 → Smaller than 10. *Why:* 7, 4 and 9 come before 10 when you count, so they are smaller. 13, 18 and 11 come after 10, so they are bigger.
10. (draw) Draw 5 plates. Draw more than 5 spoons. Join each plate to one spoon.  
   **Answer:** 5 plates, 6 or more spoons, and one line from each plate to its own spoon.. *Why:* A strong drawing has at least one spoon with no line. That spoon with no partner is what 'more spoons' means.
11. (draw) Draw two rows of dots with the same number. Make one row long and one row short.  
   **Answer:** Two rows with the same number of dots, one spread out and one close together.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the long row and the short row can hold the same number. Count both rows to check.
12. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. In the Partner Test, why did the long row of spoons have fewer?  
   **Answer:** The spoons were far apart, so the row was long. There were only 5 spoons, and 7 beans.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the length of a row depends on the gaps and the size of the things, not only on how many there are.
### Bigger, Smaller, In Order (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) Which is bigger: 14 or 17?  
   Options: 14 / 17 / They are the same  
   **Answer:** 17. *Why:* 17 is bigger. Both have one full ten. 17 has 7 more and 14 has 4 more. 7 is more than 4.
2. (fill) Write a number between 11 and 14: ____  
   **Answer:** ['12', '13', 'twelve', 'thirteen']. *Why:* 12 and 13 are both right. Each is bigger than 11 and smaller than 14.
3. (fill) Which number is one less than 20? ____  
   **Answer:** ['19', 'nineteen']. *Why:* One less means one step back on the number line. One step back from 20 is 19.
4. (order) Put these in order, biggest first.  
   **Answer:** ['19', '16', '13', '8']. *Why:* 19 is the furthest along the number line, then 16, then 13. 8 has no full ten, so it is the smallest.
5. (mcq) How many more dots does Arjun have than Lakshmi?  
   Options: 4 / 7 / 11  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 4 more. 7 of Arjun's dots have a partner in Lakshmi's row. 4 have none. Picking 11 means counting all of Arjun's dots.
6. (truefalse) 12 is smaller than 9, because 2 is smaller than 9.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. 12 is one full ten and 2 more. 9 does not fill a ten. So 12 is bigger than 9.
7. (truefalse) If 8 is more than 5, then 5 is less than 8.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The two sentences say the same fact from the two sides. If 8 has some left over, 5 is the one that ran out.
8. (sort) Look at each pair. Is the first number more, or less?  
   **Answer:** 15 and 12 → First is more; 6 and 16 → First is less; 20 and 19 → First is more; 11 and 17 → First is less; 10 and 9 → First is more. *Why:* 15 is more than 12. 6 is less than 16. 20 is more than 19. 11 is less than 17. 10 is more than 9. Find each pair on a number line to check.
9. (mcq) Farhan has 12 marbles. Kavya has 9. How many more has Farhan?  
   Options: 3 / 9 / 21  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 3 more. From 9, hop to 12: 10, 11, 12. That is 3 hops. Picking 21 means adding the two numbers together.
10. (draw) Draw a staircase of dots for 3, 4, 5 and 6. Write each number beside its row.  
   **Answer:** Four rows of 3, 4, 5 and 6 dots, starting at the same edge.. *Why:* A strong drawing has each row exactly one dot longer than the row above. That is what one more looks like.
11. (write) Say it aloud. A grown-up writes. How do you know that 17 is bigger than 14?  
   **Answer:** Both have one ten. 17 has 7 more ones and 14 has only 4 more.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason: the tens are the same and 7 is more than 4. Or: you say 14 first and reach 17 later.
12. (perspective) Amma puts 2 big dosas on one plate and 5 mini dosas on another. Which plate has more?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says the other girl is also right about her kind of more.. *Why:* Both are right. Nila compares the number of dosas. Yazhini compares how much there is to eat. First choose what you are comparing.
### Think It Through (challenge) (challenge, 13 questions)
1. (fill) I am between 15 and 17. Which number am I? ____  
   **Answer:** ['16', 'sixteen']. *Why:* Only 16 is bigger than 15 and smaller than 17.
2. (fill) I am more than 9 and less than 12. I am not 10. I am ____.  
   **Answer:** ['11', 'eleven']. *Why:* The numbers more than 9 and less than 12 are 10 and 11. It is not 10, so it is 11.
3. (mcq) Red cup has more than blue. Blue has more than green. Which cup has the most?  
   Options: Red / Blue / Green  
   **Answer:** Red. *Why:* Red. Red has more than blue, and blue has more than green. So red has more than both.
4. (mcq) Which row has fewer dots?  
   Options: Row A / Row B / They are the same  
   **Answer:** Row A. *Why:* Row A has fewer. Row A has 12 and row B has 13. One dot in row B has no partner.
5. (mcq) Six 1-rupee coins or two 10-rupee coins. Which is more money?  
   Options: Six 1-rupee coins / Two 10-rupee coins / They are the same  
   **Answer:** Two 10-rupee coins. *Why:* Two 10-rupee coins make 20 rupees. Six 1-rupee coins make 6 rupees. 20 is more than 6. Picking the six coins means comparing the number of coins, not the money.
6. (truefalse) A heap of 4 big potatoes has more things than a heap of 9 small peanuts.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. 9 is more than 4. The potato heap is bigger, but it holds fewer things.
7. (truefalse) There is a number that is bigger than every other number.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Name any number, and one more than it is bigger. So no number can be the biggest.
8. (order) Put these five numbers in order, smallest first.  
   **Answer:** ['0', '9', '11', '15', '20']. *Why:* 0 stands for none, so it is the smallest. 9 has no full ten. Then 11 and 15, which have one ten. 20 is two tens.
9. (match) Match each pair of numbers to how many more the first one is.  
   **Answer:** 10 and 7 → 3 more; 15 and 14 → 1 more; 12 and 12 → 0 more; 20 and 15 → 5 more. *Why:* Hop from the smaller number to the bigger one and count the hops. 7 to 10 is 3. 14 to 15 is 1. 12 to 12 is no hop. 15 to 20 is 5.
10. (fill) Meenakshi scored 16 runs. Arjun scored 13. Meenakshi won by ____ runs.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* From 13, hop to 16: 14, 15, 16. That is 3 hops. So Meenakshi has 3 more runs.
11. (write) Say it aloud. A grown-up writes. Is 10 a big number or a small number? Give one example for each.  
   **Answer:** It depends what you compare it with. 10 laddus for one child is a lot. 10 grains of rice is very little.. *Why:* A strong answer says that 10 is big next to a smaller number and small next to a bigger number. No number is big by itself.
12. (draw) Draw 3 big circles in a long row and 6 small circles in a short row. Circle the row with more.  
   **Answer:** The row of 6 small circles is circled.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the short row holding more circles. 6 is more than 3, whatever the size of the circles.
13. (perspective) A long row has 6 lemons. A short row has 8 beans. Which row has more?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with the girl, because partnering leaves 2 beans over. It adds that a longer row has more when the things and the gaps are the same size.. *Why:* 8 is more than 6. Length depends on the size of the things and the gaps. The boy's rule works only when both rows use the same things with the same gaps.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) One banana for each child. Are there enough bananas?  
   Options: Yes, with some left over / Yes, exactly enough / No, 2 children get none  
   **Answer:** No, 2 children get none. *Why:* No. 6 bananas find 6 children, and 2 children are left with no banana. There are fewer bananas than children.
2. (mcq) Every cup has one spoon. No spoon is left over. What is true?  
   Options: More cups / More spoons / The same number  
   **Answer:** The same number. *Why:* The same number. Every cup has a partner and every spoon has a partner. Nothing is left over.
3. (truefalse) You spread 6 beans far apart. Now they are more than 8 beans in a heap.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Spreading beans out does not add any. 6 is still fewer than 8. Give each of the 6 a partner from the heap, and 2 are left over in the heap.
4. (mcq) 5 big potatoes sit in a long row. 9 chana sit in a short row. Which group has fewer things?  
   Options: The potatoes / The chana / They are the same  
   **Answer:** The potatoes. *Why:* The potatoes. 5 is fewer than 9. The potatoes take up more room, but room is not the same as how many.
5. (mcq) Which number is bigger: 13 or 8?  
   Options: 13 / 8 / They are the same  
   **Answer:** 13. *Why:* 13 is bigger. You say 8 first and reach 13 later. On the number line 13 is further along.
6. (fill) One less than 16 is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['15', 'fifteen']. *Why:* One less means one step back. One step back from 16 is 15.
7. (mcq) Which is smaller: 11 or 9?  
   Options: 11 / 9 / They are the same  
   **Answer:** 9. *Why:* 9 is smaller. 11 is a full ten and one more. 9 does not fill a ten. Picking 11 means looking at the numerals 1 and 1, not at the ten.
8. (order) Order these numbers from the smallest to the biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['6', '10', '14', '19']. *Why:* Counting up, you say 6 first, then 10, then 14, then 19.
9. (fill) Write the number between 18 and 20: ____  
   **Answer:** ['19', 'nineteen']. *Why:* 19 is bigger than 18 and smaller than 20. It is the only number between them on the number line.
10. (mcq) How many more dots does Kavya have than Farhan?  
   Options: 4 / 6 / 16  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 4 more. 6 of Kavya's dots have a partner in Farhan's row. 4 have none. Picking 16 means adding the two rows.
11. (mcq) You have 7 runs. The other player has 9. How many more do you need to have the same?  
   Options: 2 / 9 / 16  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 2 more. From 7, hop to 9: 8, 9. That is 2 hops. Picking 16 means adding the scores.
12. (truefalse) 7 is bigger than 5, and 7 is also smaller than 9.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. 7 is the bigger number next to 5 and the smaller number next to 9. A number is big or small only next to another number.
13. (mcq) One sheet is torn into 2 pieces. A sheet of the same size is torn into 8. What is true?  
   Options: 8 pieces is more paper / Same paper, more pieces / 2 pieces is more paper  
   **Answer:** Same paper, more pieces. *Why:* The sheets were the same size, so the paper is the same. Only the number of pieces differs. More pieces is not more paper.

## Applications
- A cricket scorer compares the two teams' runs. The team with more runs wins, and the report says by how many.
- A shopkeeper in the market checks whether you gave more or less money than the price, to know if change is due.
- A cook laying banana leaves for a feast partners leaves with guests, to see if there are enough.
- A bus conductor compares the number of people with the number of seats, to know if anyone must stand.
- A teacher puts roll numbers in order, so that every child's name is quick to find.
- A doctor compares your temperature with the usual number, to see if you have a fever.
- A tailor checks that a shirt has the same number of buttons as buttonholes.
- People counting votes compare the totals. The candidate with more votes wins.

## Spotted around us
- Chappals and feet at a doorway. If one chappal has no partner, somebody is hopping.
- A cricket scoreboard. Two numbers sit side by side so that everyone can see who has more runs.
- Price boards at a vegetable market. People look for the smaller number for the same vegetable.
- House numbers along a street, and berth numbers in a train coach. They go in order, so you know which way to walk.
- An idli plate with some hollows empty. You can see at once that there is less batter than hollows.
- A shoal of small fish in a pond or an aquarium. A fish who is alone will usually swim to the bigger group.
- The Moon and the Sun in the sky. They look the same size, though the Sun is about 400 times as wide. Never look straight at the Sun.
- The steps of a staircase, or the dates on a calendar. Each one is one more than the one before.

## Open questions
- **How can an elephant smell which bucket holds more seeds?** The scientists who did the test in 2019 said that they do not yet know how the elephants do it.
- **Do other animals have anything like our exact numbers, or only a sense of more and less?** Animals choose well when two amounts are very different, and less well when they are close. Scientists do not agree on what this tells us about the animals' minds.
- **How does a six-month-old baby tell 8 dots from 16, before learning any number words?** Tests show that babies can do it. How the brain does it is still being worked out, and scientists do not agree.
- **Is 'more' out there in the world, or does it need someone to compare?** Mathematicians and thinkers have argued for more than two thousand years about whether such facts are found or made. No test has settled it.

## Connected maths
- In hand cricket, Kavya made 9 runs and Arjun made 6. Who won? By how many runs? **Kavya won by 3 runs.**. 9 is more than 6, so Kavya won. Partner the run-beans: 6 of Kavya's beans find a partner, and 3 are left over. On the number line, 6 to 9 is 3 hops: 7, 8, 9. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- 8 people sit down for lunch. There are 5 banana leaves. Is there a leaf for everyone? How many more are needed? **No. 3 more leaves are needed.**. Give one leaf to each person. 5 people get a leaf, and 3 people have none. So there are fewer leaves than people, and 3 more leaves are needed. 5 and 3 more makes 8. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L1`)
- Three girls play kabaddi. Meenakshi scores 14 points, Lakshmi scores 9 and Farida scores 17. Put the scores in order, smallest first. Who scored the most? **9, 14, 17. Farida scored the most.**. 9 has no full ten, so it is the smallest. 14 and 17 both have one ten. 4 extra ones is fewer than 7 extra ones, so 14 comes before 17. Farida's 17 is the biggest. (queues `math.number-sense.place-value.L1`)
- Amma puts 6 big vadais far apart on one plate. She puts 8 small vadais close together on another plate. The first plate looks fuller. Which plate has more vadais? How can you check without counting? **The second plate has more vadais. Check by partnering.**. Looking at how full a plate is can fool you, because big things far apart take up more room. Put one small vadai next to each big vadai. Every big vadai gets a partner, and 2 small vadais are left over. So the second plate has 2 more. (queues `math.logic.puzzles.L1`)
- Yazhini has 16 shells. Nila has 19 shells. Who has more? Use the ten to decide. **Nila has more. 19 is bigger than 16.**. 16 is one ten and 6 more. 19 is one ten and 9 more. The tens are the same, so compare the extra ones. 9 is more than 6. So 19 is bigger than 16, by 3. (queues `math.number-sense.place-value.L1`)
