# Solid shapes: what rolls and what slides
`math.shapes-3d.solid-shapes.L1`

**Summary.** Put a ball and a matchbox on a sloping book. The ball runs away. The matchbox sits still. Why? And what about a round tin, which has flat ends and a curved side? You will test balls, boxes, tins and cones on a slope you build yourself. You will find out which part of a thing makes it roll, which part makes it slide, and which shapes you can stack into a tower.

**Intro.** *You put these four on a slope. Which ones will roll away?* The ball rolls away. So does the tin lying on its side. The cone rolls in a curve. The box does not roll at all: it stays, or it slides if the slope is steep. The ball, the tin and the cone each have a curved surface. The box has only flat faces. In this unit you will test this on your own slope, and find one thing that can roll and slide.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can tell a solid shape from a flat shape.
1. I can find the flat faces and the curved surface of a thing by touching it.
2. I can say which things will roll and which will slide, and give the reason.
3. I can sort things into balls, boxes, tins and cones.
4. I can say which shapes stack, and why.
5. I can show that one thing, such as a tin, can roll one way up and slide another way up.

## Terms
- **Solid shape**: A shape that is not flat. You can hold it in your hand, and it takes up room. *e.g.* A circle drawn on paper is flat. A ball is solid.
- **Flat face**: A flat part on the outside of a solid shape. Your whole palm can rest on it. *e.g.* A matchbox has 6 flat faces. Put your palm on the biggest one.
- **Curved surface**: A part on the outside of a solid shape that bends round. Your palm cannot lie flat on it. *e.g.* The side of a round tin is a curved surface. So is all of a ball.
- **Roll**: To move by turning over and over. *e.g.* A ball rolls down a slope. So does a pencil on a tilted desk.
- **Slide**: To move along without turning over. The same face stays on the ground. *e.g.* A matchbox slides down a steep slope. You slide down a slide in the park.
- **Slope**: A surface with one end higher than the other. *e.g.* Put books under one end of a tray. The tray is now a slope.
- **Stack**: To put things one on top of another so that they stay. *e.g.* You can stack matchboxes. You cannot stack lemons.
- **Ball shape**: A solid shape that is curved all over. It has no flat face. *e.g.* A cricket ball, a marble and a laddu are ball shapes.
- **Box shape**: A solid shape with 6 flat faces and no curved surface. *e.g.* A matchbox, a dice, a brick and a book are box shapes.
- **Tin shape**: A solid shape with 2 flat round faces and 1 curved surface between them. A tin is also called a can. *e.g.* A round dabba, a new candle and a rolling pin are tin shapes.
- **Cone**: A solid shape with 1 flat round face, 1 curved surface and a point at the other end. *e.g.* An ice-cream cone, a party cap and a funnel are cones.

## Explanations
### A solid shape is a shape you can hold · *defined*
**Definition:** A solid shape is not flat. It has a top, a bottom and sides, and it takes up room.

Draw round a coin on paper. You get a circle. It is flat, and you cannot pick it up. Now pick up a ball. You can close your hand round it, so it is solid. A flat shape lives on paper, or on one face of a thing. A solid shape is the whole thing. Now you try with a rectangle on paper and a matchbox.
- e.g. A circle on paper is flat. A ball is solid.
- e.g. A rectangle on paper is flat. A matchbox is solid.
- e.g. A picture of a laddu is flat. The laddu on your plate is solid.
### Flat faces and curved surfaces · *defined*
**Definition:** A flat face is a flat part of the outside. A curved surface is a part that bends round.

Hold a round tin. Put your palm on the lid. It lies flat, so the lid is a flat face. The bottom is a flat face too, which makes 2. Now wrap your hand round the side. Your palm has to bend, so the side is a curved surface. Now count the flat faces of a matchbox: top, bottom, front, back and two ends.
- e.g. A matchbox: 6 flat faces, no curved surface.
- e.g. A round tin: 2 flat faces, 1 curved surface.
- e.g. A cone: 1 flat face, 1 curved surface.
- e.g. A ball: no flat face. It is curved all over.
### A curved surface rolls. A flat face slides. · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing rolls when its curved surface is on the ground. A thing slides when a flat face is on the ground.

Put a ball on a gentle slope. Only a tiny spot of the ball touches the slope. The ball tips over on to the next spot, and the next. That is rolling. Now put a matchbox on the same slope. A whole flat face touches the slope and grips it, so the box stays. Make the slope steep. Now the box moves down with the same face on the slope all the way, and that is sliding.
- e.g. A marble rolls off a tilted plate at once.
- e.g. A book stays on a tilted desk until the desk is steep.
- e.g. A dice does not roll down a slope. It slides.
### One tin, two ways · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing with a curved surface and a flat face can roll or slide. It depends on which part is on the ground.

Lay a round tin on its side. Its curved surface is on the ground, so it rolls. Now stand the same tin on its end. A flat face is on the ground, so it stays, or slides if the slope is steep. Nothing about the tin changed. Only the part that touches the ground changed. A cone does the same: it stays on its flat face and rolls on its side. So always ask which part is touching the ground.
- e.g. A coin on its edge rolls. A coin lying flat slides.
- e.g. A bangle on its edge rolls. A bangle lying down stays.
- e.g. A glue stick lying down rolls off the desk. Standing up, it stays.
### Four families: balls, boxes, tins and cones · *defined*
**Definition:** Many things around us are close to one of four solid shapes: a ball, a box, a tin or a cone.

To find the family, feel the thing. Curved all over, with no flat face: a ball shape. Six flat faces and nothing curved: a box shape. Two flat round faces and a curved side: a tin shape. One flat round face, a curved side and a point: a cone. Size does not matter, so a marble and a football are both ball shapes. Many things, such as a spoon or a banana, fit no family. In later classes you will learn the maths names: sphere, cuboid, cylinder and cone.
- e.g. Ball shapes: a marble, a football, a laddu, a pea.
- e.g. Box shapes: a matchbox, a dice, a brick, a soap box.
- e.g. Tin shapes: a round dabba, a candle, a rolling pin, a drum.
- e.g. Cones: an ice-cream cone, a party cap, a funnel.
### Flat faces stack · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing can go in the middle of a tower only if it has a flat face below and a flat face on top.

Put one matchbox on another. Flat face sits on flat face, and it stays. Now put a lemon on a lemon. They touch at one tiny spot, and the top lemon rolls off. A tin standing up has a flat face below and a flat face on top, so tins stack well. A cone has one flat face and a point. It can sit on top of a tower, but nothing can sit on its point.
- e.g. Shops stack soap boxes and tins on shelves.
- e.g. Idli plates stack because each one is flat underneath.
- e.g. Oranges are piled in a heap, not stacked in a straight tower.
### Why a cone rolls in a curve · *defined*
**Definition:** A tin is the same width at both ends, so it rolls straight. A cone is wide at one end and has a point at the other, so it rolls in a curve.

Lay a cone on the floor and push it gently. Watch the two ends. In one full turn, the wide end travels a long way round. The point hardly moves. So the cone swings round its point. Both ends of a tin are the same width, so both travel the same distance, and the tin goes straight. A tumbler that is wider at the top is in between, and it rolls in a big curve.
- e.g. A party cap pushed on the floor goes round its point.
- e.g. A rolling pin rolls straight.
- e.g. A paper cup rolls in a wide curve.
### Who chose the names? · *defined*
**Definition:** A shape's name is a word people chose. For the tin shape, many people chose a word that means rolling.

The maths name for a tin shape is cylinder. It comes from an old Greek word, kylindros, which means roller. In Tamil the word urulai is used for this shape and for a wheel. It comes from urul, which means to roll. In Hindi the shape is called belan, which is also the rolling pin in the kitchen. So people far apart noticed the same thing about this shape: it rolls. But a tin also stands and stacks, and the names leave that out.
- e.g. English: cylinder, from the Greek word for roller.
- e.g. Tamil: urulai, from urul, to roll.
- e.g. Hindi: belan, the same word as a rolling pin.
### Is a coin flat or solid? · *defined*
**Definition:** Everything you can hold is a solid shape, even a very thin thing.

Look at a coin from the side. It has a little thickness. It has 2 flat round faces and a thin curved surface between them, so it is a very short tin. That is why it rolls on its edge. A sheet of paper has a tiny thickness too, so it is a very thin box. In everyday talk we call coins and paper flat. In maths, a truly flat shape has no thickness at all. You can draw one, but you cannot pick one up.
- e.g. A coin is a very short tin shape.
- e.g. A sheet of paper is a very thin box shape.
- e.g. A dosa is a short, wide tin shape, more or less.
### Other living beings use solid shapes too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings make solid shapes and use the way those shapes move.

Some dung beetles feed on the dung of cows and elephants. A beetle pats a lump of dung into a ball. Then they walk backwards and push the ball away with their back legs. A ball rolls any way you push it, so the food is soon far from other beetles. Such beetles live in India too. A young antlion lives in dry sand. They dig a pit shaped like a cone, with the point at the bottom, and wait there. An ant who steps over the edge slides down the loose sand.
- e.g. A dung beetle shapes dung into a ball and rolls it away.
- e.g. An antlion digs a cone-shaped pit in dry sand.
- e.g. An Indian pangolin curls up into a ball when they are in danger.
### Balls in the sky, boxes in the wall, tins under the bus · *defined*
**Definition:** Each solid shape turns up where its way of moving or staying is useful.

The Earth is a huge ball, and so are the Moon and the Sun. Told simply, each one pulls everything in it towards its middle, from every side. About 1,500 years ago the Indian mathematician Aryabhata wrote that the Earth is round. Bricks are box shapes, and their flat faces stack, so a wall stays up. A wheel is a short tin shape lying on its curved surface, and that is why a bus rolls. A wall that rolled would be no use, and nor would a bus that stayed. You will learn the fuller reasons in later years.
- e.g. The Earth, the Moon and the Sun are ball shapes.
- e.g. Bricks are box shapes, so they stack into walls.
- e.g. The wheels of a bus, a cycle and a suitcase are short tin shapes.

## Mostly agreed
- A ball has no flat face anywhere, so it rolls whichever way you push it. *How we know:* Run your palm over a ball. It never lies flat. On the slope in the Slope Test, the ball rolled however you put it down.
- A box has only flat faces, so it cannot roll. On a steep slope it slides. *How we know:* In the Slope Test the matchbox and the dice stayed on the gentle slope. On the steep slope they moved down with the same face on the tray.
- A round tin can roll and can stay. It rolls on its curved side and stays on its flat end. *How we know:* You tested the same tin both ways in the Slope Test. Nothing about the tin changed except the part that touched the tray.
- Some dung beetles shape dung into a ball and roll it away in a straight line. On dark, clear nights they keep straight by the glow of the Milky Way. *How we know:* In 2013 Marie Dacke and other scientists let beetles roll their balls under a real night sky and under a star show inside a dome. Under a cloudy sky, the beetles could not keep a straight path.
- The Earth is a ball, even though the ground looks flat from where we stand. *How we know:* Photographs taken from space show a ball from every side. Long before that, about 1,500 years ago, the Indian mathematician Aryabhata wrote that the Earth is round and turns.

## How we found out
**Tim Birkhead, Jamie Thompson, Robert Montgomerie and others, 2017 and 2018.** The common murre is a seabird of cold northern seas. She lays one egg on a bare rock ledge, high on a cliff, with no nest. Her egg is pointed at one end, like a pear. Why? In the 1800s, people said the pointed egg spins like a top in the wind and so stays on the ledge. That was wrong. From the middle of the 1900s, bird books gave another reason. A pointed egg rolls in a curve, as a cone does, so it would roll round and not off the edge. For many years nobody tested this properly. Then Tim Birkhead, a scientist in Britain who had watched murres for more than 40 years, tested real eggs with his team. In 2017 they reported that the egg does roll in a curve, but the curve is often wider than the ledge. In 2018 they put eggs on slopes. The more pointed eggs sat more steadily. So the shape may help the egg to stay still, more than to roll safely.  
*What people believed before:* A murre's pointed egg is safe because it spins like a top, or because it rolls in a tight circle. Tests with real eggs showed that it does not spin, and that its rolling circle is often wider than the ledge. On slopes, the pointed eggs were the ones that stayed put best.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Every thing is either a roller or a slider.** (Most of us, when we sort things into two heaps; *rejected*)  
  For: It works for many things. A ball always rolls. A matchbox never rolls. Two heaps are easy to remember.  
  Against: A tin, a cone and a coin each roll one way up and slide another way up. So the two heaps are about the part that touches the ground, not about the whole thing.
- **A ball is a circle.** (Many young children, and grown-ups in everyday talk; *rejected*)  
  For: Look at a ball from any side and its outline is a circle. We even say the Sun is a circle in the sky.  
  Against: A circle is flat. You can draw it, but you cannot pick it up. A ball is solid. You can hold it, and it rolls. In maths the two have different names.
- **A murre's pointed egg rolls in a circle, so that it does not fall off the cliff.** (Many bird books, from the middle of the 1900s onwards; *debated*)  
  For: A pointed egg does roll in a curve, as a cone does. On a wide, flat ledge that could keep it from the edge.  
  Against: Tim Birkhead and his team measured the curve. It is often wider than the ledge, so the egg would still fall. In 2018 they found that pointed eggs sit more steadily on a slope. The shape may help the egg stay still.

## Roots
We tested which things roll and which slide. Now let us think about the tin, which did both.

**Deep truth.** In the Slope Test, the tin went into two heaps. Lying on its side, it rolled, and standing on its end, it stayed. It was the same tin. So a thing is not just a roller or a slider. It depends on which part of it meets the ground.  
*Habit it breaks:* We like to give every thing one label: this is a roller, that is a slider. The tin shows that one label is not enough. The two heaps were our idea, made to keep things simple.  
*Evidence:* In the Slope Test, the same tin rolled when its curved surface was on the tray and stayed when its flat face was on the tray. The cone and the coin did the same.

- *who am i:* You made the heaps. You could have sorted the same things by colour or by size. You chose to sort by how they move.
- *what is life:* A dung beetle has never done a slope test. They still pat their food into a ball, and a ball is the shape that rolls best.
- *what is this world:* The Earth under your feet is a huge ball. From where you stand, the ground looks flat. How a thing looks depends on where you stand.

Take one tin. Lay it down, and it rolls. Stand it up, and it stays. Look at its end and you see a circle, and look at its side and you see a rectangle. It is one tin, and it shows itself in many ways.

- **Udana 6.4 (Tittha Sutta):** In this old Buddhist story, a king asks some people who were born blind to feel an elephant. Each one feels a different part. The one who feels the ear says an elephant is like a winnowing basket. The one who feels the foot says it is like a post. Then they quarrel. (One child sees the tin lying down and calls it a roller. Another sees it standing and calls it a stayer. Each of them has met one part of the tin.)
- **Thirukkural 667:** This Tamil verse says: do not look down on anyone because of how they look. Some people are like the small pin that holds the wheel on a great rolling chariot. (The verse says that the look does not tell you what someone can do. The Slope Test showed the same about things. The tin looked like a roller, and then it stood still.)
- Maths (settled): A thing with a curved surface and a flat face can roll or stay. It depends on which part touches the ground. Anyone can test this on a slope.
- Maths (debated): Why is a murre's egg pointed? For many years people said the shape makes it roll in a safe circle. Tests in 2018 found that pointed eggs sit more steadily on a slope. Scientists are still studying which reason matters most.
- Other view, Many scientists and mathematicians: They say the heaps are ours, but the shape is not. A tin has a curved side and two flat ends whether anyone sorts it or not. It would roll down a hill even if no person had ever seen it.
- Practice, Round in your hands (3 min): Hold a ball or a lemon in both hands. Close your eyes. Turn it slowly and breathe in. Keep turning and breathe out. Can your fingers find a place where it begins? Now hold a matchbox the same way. What can your fingers find this time?
- Ask: Is the tin a roller or a stayer? Who decides?
- Ask: You sorted things by how they move. What other ways could you sort the same things?
- Ask: The Earth is a ball, but the ground looks flat. Which one is true?
- Ask: A dung beetle makes a ball without a word for it. What does the beetle know?

## Story: The Blue Drum on the Hill Lane
*Kani, Chithi, Babu and Stella teacher are made up. Coonoor is a real hill town in the Nilgiris of Tamil Nadu, with sloping lanes.*

Friday was market day in Coonoor, and the lane to the market went uphill all the way.

Kani's Chithi had a stall on that lane. She sold everything a kitchen needs. There were steel tumblers and tiffin boxes. There were plastic balls for small children. And at the back, standing like a guard, there was one big blue drum for storing water.

"Can I help today?" asked Kani.

"You can set out the table," said Chithi. "Carrying is for big people." Babu anna was fourteen, and he did the carrying.

The table stood on the lane, so it leaned a little, like the lane.

Kani put six plastic balls in a row. Before she could count them, they ran off the low end of the table. They bounced, and then they rolled away down the lane. Babu ran after them. A goat watched him go.

Next Kani laid the tumblers down in a neat row, like children asleep. The tumblers rolled too. They rolled in a lazy curve, clinked into each other and dropped off the edge.

Kani sat down on an upturned crate and looked at the table for a while.

Then she tried the tiffin boxes. She set one down. It did not move. She set down another. It stayed where she had put it. She turned one over and looked underneath. It was flat. All of it was flat.

She picked up a tumbler. Its side was round, like the balls. But its bottom was flat, like the boxes.

She stood the tumbler up on its bottom. It stayed.

After that she worked fast. She stood every tumbler up, in rows of five. She built a little square wall out of tiffin boxes and tipped the balls inside it. The balls rolled as far as the wall and stopped.

Chithi came back with the tea and looked at the table. "Who showed you that?"

"The table did," said Kani.

At noon Stella teacher came by and bought the big blue drum. "Send it up soon," she said. "The water comes at one o'clock. I live in the last house at the top, with the green gate."

But Babu had gone to the bus stand with a parcel, and Chithi could not leave the stall.

Kani went and stood beside the drum. It was empty, so it was not heavy. But it came up to her chest, and it was far too wide to hug. She tried anyway. Her arms did not reach halfway round, and she could not see where she was going.

Carrying is for big people, she thought.

She looked at the drum again. It had a round side. It had a flat bottom and a flat lid.

"You are only a big tumbler," she told it.

She tipped the drum over on to its side. She put both hands on it and pushed. The drum rolled. It rolled up the lane, past the tea shop and past the tailor. People stepped aside to let it through.

Halfway up, Kani stopped to get her breath. She took her hands away.

The drum began to roll back down the lane, towards the stall and all the tumblers.

Kani ran round it. She pushed against it with her whole body. Then she heaved one end up, and up, until the drum stood on its flat bottom.

It stood on the slope as if it had always been there.

Kani sat down beside it and laughed. When she was ready, she laid it down again. She rolled it the rest of the way to the green gate and stood it up.

Stella teacher came out, wiping her hands. "Who carried this all the way up?"

"Nobody," said Kani. "It goes when it is lying down. It waits when it is standing up."

When she got back to the stall, Babu was there. He was tidying the table, and he was laying the tumblers down in a neat row.

"Not like that, anna," said Kani. "Let me show you."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kani finds that the balls and the lying tumblers roll off a sloping table, and the flat tiffin boxes stay. Then she sees that a tumbler has both a round side and a flat bottom, so she can choose what it does. She uses the same idea twice with the drum: she lays it down to move it and stands it up to stop it. A curved surface rolls and a flat face stays, and one thing can have both.

- Ask: Why did the balls run off the table when the tiffin boxes did not?
- Ask: Kani told the drum, 'You are only a big tumbler.' What did she mean?
- Ask: How did Kani stop the drum from rolling back down the lane?
- Ask: If the drum had been a big box, how could Kani have moved it up the lane?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Slope Test: which things roll and which slide? (25 min)
- **question:** A ball, a round tin, a matchbox, a dice, a paper cone and a coin sit at the top of a slope. Which will roll, which will slide, and why?
- **predict:** Before you let anything go, guess for each thing: will it roll, slide or stay? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every thing starts on the same line at the top. We let go without pushing. We use the same slope for all of them. We write the guess before the test, so the test cannot change the guess.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the paper for the cone. Keep marbles, coins and dice away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** A grown-up draws a table with four columns and eight rows. Write the guess first. Then write what happened on the gentle slope and on the steep slope: rolled, slid or stayed. At the end, the child says the rule and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** Thing, and how it is placed | My guess | Gentle slope | Steep slope
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 2 thick books under one end of the tray. 2. Draw a line across the top end. 3. Put a towel at the bottom. 4. Make the paper cone. 5. Lay the six things beside the slope.  
  *Parent says:* This is our slope. One end is high and one end is low. We will let each thing go from this line.  
  *Child mode:* Build a slope with a tray and 2 books. Put the six things next to it.
  - Note: For the cone, draw round a dinner plate on paper and cut the circle out. Fold it in half and cut along the fold. Roll one half into a cone and tape it. Use a smooth slope. A cloth cover grips too much.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold up each thing in turn. 2. Show how it will be placed. 3. Ask if it will roll, slide or stay, and why. 4. Write each guess. 5. Do not correct any guess.  
  *Parent says:* Will this one roll, slide or stay where it is? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess for each thing: roll, slide or stay? Say why.
  - Ask: What will the tin do when it stands on its end? Why? (good: It will roll, because it is round.; It will stay, because the bottom is flat.; I am not sure.)
  - Note: Many children say every round thing will roll, even the tin standing up and the coin lying flat. Accept each guess with its reason. The slope will do the correcting.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the ball on the top line. 2. Let go without pushing. 3. Write what happened. 4. Do the same for all eight tests. 5. Lay the tin across the slope, not pointing down it.  
  *Parent says:* Put it on the line and just let go. What did it do? Did it turn over and over, or stay?  
  *Child mode:* Put each thing on the line. Let go. Do not push. Watch what it does.
  - Ask: Which things moved on the gentle slope? Which stayed? (good: The ball and the tin lying down rolled.; The cone went round in a curve.; The matchbox, the dice and the coin stayed.)
  - Note: On 2 books, the ball, the tin lying down and the cone lying down should move. The cone swings round its point and may stop. The rest should stay. If something flat slides already, take one book away.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add 3 more books to make the slope steep. 2. Test again each thing that stayed. 3. Ask your child to watch which face is on the tray. 4. Write what happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now the slope is steep. Watch the matchbox closely. Does it turn over, or does one face stay down?  
  *Child mode:* Make the slope steep. Test the things that stayed. Do they turn over?
  - Ask: The matchbox moved this time. Did it roll? (good: No, it slid.; The same face stayed on the tray.; It did not turn over.)
  - Note: If a thing still stays, lift the high end slowly by hand until it moves. A tall thin tin may fall over when standing. Use a short one, or hold the slope a little lower.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to make two heaps: things that rolled and things that slid. 2. Hold up the tin and ask where it goes. 3. Your child runs a palm over each thing. 4. Ask what the rollers share.  
  *Parent says:* Make two heaps: rolled, and slid. Now, where does the tin go? Feel each thing with your palm.  
  *Child mode:* Make two heaps: rolled, and slid. Where does the tin go? Feel each thing.
  - Ask: The tin rolled lying down and slid standing up. Which heap is it in? (good: Both.; It needs to be in the middle.; It depends how you put it.)
  - Ask: Feel the ball and the matchbox. What is different? (good: The ball is curved all over.; The matchbox is flat everywhere.; My hand lies flat on the box but not on the ball.)
  - Note: The tin, the cone and the coin do not fit in one heap. Let your child feel the puzzle before you help. Put them between the two heaps.
  - Check: A round tin stands on its flat end on a gentle slope. What will it do? → **Stay**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they think makes a thing roll. 2. Ask what they saw that shows it. 3. Ask why. 4. Write their words under the table.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think makes a thing roll? What did you see? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Say what makes a thing roll. Say what makes a thing slide.
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why? (good: A thing rolls on its curved part.; A flat part slides or stays.; The tin did both, so it is about which part is down.; The matchbox never rolled, and it is flat all over.)
  - Check: Which thing can never roll, however you place it? → **A matchbox**

### a1 · Stack It: which shapes can go in a tower? (12 min)
- **problem:** You have 3 matchboxes, 2 round tins, 1 cone and 1 ball. How tall a tower can you build with all of them? Which ones cause trouble?
- **record:** Draw your tallest tower. Beside each shape write B for box, T for tin, C for cone. Write how many things are in it.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the 7 things. 2. Ask which ones will be easy to stack and which will be hard. 3. Ask why. 4. Write the guesses.  
  *Parent says:* We will build a tower with these. Which ones will stack well? Which will not? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the 7 things. Guess which will stack and which will not.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child builds the tallest tower they can. 2. Do not help with the order. 3. When something falls, ask what the two touching parts were.  
  *Parent says:* Build it as tall as you can. When something falls off, tell me which two parts were touching.  
  *Child mode:* Build the tallest tower you can. Use as many things as you can.
  - Ask: The ball fell off. What was touching what? (good: A curved part on a flat part.; Only a tiny bit of the ball was touching.; It rolled off.)
  - Note: Tins may be laid on their sides at first, and then roll away. Let it happen. Your child will soon stand them up.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to try the cone at the bottom, in the middle and on top. 2. Do the same with the ball. 3. Fill in yes or no in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Try the cone in three places: bottom, middle, top. Where does it work? Now try the ball.  
  *Child mode:* Try the cone at the bottom, in the middle and on top. Then try the ball.
  - Ask: Why can the cone go on top but not in the middle? (good: Its bottom is flat but its top is a point.; Nothing can sit on the point.)
  - Check: Which of these can go in the middle of a tower? → **A tin standing up**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the things in the tallest tower. 2. Ask which thing was left out. 3. Ask your child for the rule.  
  *Parent says:* How many things are in your tower? Which one could not join? What does a thing need so that it can stack?  
  *Child mode:* Count your tower. Which thing is left out? What does a shape need to stack?
  - Ask: What does a shape need so that another can sit on it? (good: A flat top.; A flat face on top and one underneath.)
  - Note: The tallest tower uses 6 things: 3 boxes and 2 tins in any order, with the cone on top. The ball is left out.
### a2 · The Feel Bag: name the shape without looking (10 min)
- **problem:** Can you tell a ball, a box, a tin and a cone apart with your eyes shut? What do your fingers look for?
- **safety:** Use only things with no sharp points or edges.
- **record:** Make one tally stroke for each right guess. Write how many you got right out of 6.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the 6 things in the bag while your child looks away. 2. Say the four families aloud together.  
  *Parent says:* There are six things in this bag. Each one is a ball, a box, a tin or a cone. You will find out with your fingers.  
  *Child mode:* Six things are hiding in the bag. Do not peep.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child puts one hand in and holds one thing. 2. Ask if it has a flat face, and how many. 3. Ask if any part is curved. 4. Your child names the family, then pulls it out.  
  *Parent says:* Hold one thing. Can you find a flat face? How many? Is any part curved? Which family is it?  
  *Child mode:* Hold one thing. Count its flat faces. Is any part curved? Name the family.
  - Ask: It has 2 flat round ends and a curved side. What is it? (good: A tin shape.; The glue stick.)
  - Note: The coin is the hard one. It feels flat, but it has 2 flat faces and a thin curved edge, like a very short tin.
  - Check: Your fingers find no flat face at all. Which family is it? → **Ball**

  *Parent does:* 1. Count the right guesses. 2. Ask which thing was hardest. 3. Swap: your child hides things for you.  
  *Parent says:* How many did you get right? Which one fooled your fingers? Now you hide some for me.  
  *Child mode:* Count your right guesses. Which thing was hardest? Now hide some for a grown-up.
  - Ask: Why is the coin hard to name? (good: It feels flat.; It is very thin.; It is like a tiny tin.)
### a3 · Maths in life: ball, rope and box from dough (15 min)
- **problem:** How do your hands have to move to make a ball? A tin shape? A box? And why is a rolling pin not a box?
- **safety:** Wash hands first. If you will cook the dough afterwards, a grown-up does the cooking.
- **record:** Draw the four dough shapes. Under each, draw how your hands moved: round and round, back and forth, or pressing flat.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child a lump of dough. 2. Ask them to make a ball, any way they like. 3. Ask how their hands moved. 4. Make one yourself.  
  *Parent says:* Make me a ball. Now tell me, how did your hands move?  
  *Child mode:* Make a ball from dough. How did your hands move?
  - Ask: How did your hands move to make the ball? (good: Round and round.; In circles.; Every way.)
  - Note: A ball needs the palms to circle, so every side gets pressed the same. This is how laddus and seedai are shaped.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a tin shape, like a short thick rope. 2. Ask how the hands moved this time. 3. Ask for a cone. 4. Ask what they did differently.  
  *Parent says:* Now make a tin shape, like a thick rope. How did your hands move this time? Now turn one into a cone.  
  *Child mode:* Make a tin shape. Then make a cone. How did your hands move?
  - Ask: What did you do to turn the tin shape into a cone? (good: I pressed one end more.; I rolled one end thinner.)
  - Note: Back and forth in one direction makes a tin shape. The dough only turns one way, so it stays the same width. Pat the two ends flat on the board.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a box. 2. Let your child find out that rolling does not work. 3. Show pressing each side flat on the board. 4. Count the 6 faces together.  
  *Parent says:* Now make a box. Can you roll it? What do you have to do?  
  *Child mode:* Make a box from dough. Can you roll it into shape? Count its faces.
  - Ask: Why can you not make a box by rolling? (good: Rolling makes it round.; A box needs flat faces, so I have to press.)
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tilt the plate a little. 2. Your child guesses what each dough shape will do. 3. Test all four. 4. Compare with the Slope Test.  
  *Parent says:* I will tilt the plate. What will each dough shape do? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Tilt the plate. Guess what each dough shape will do. Then test.
  - Note: Soft dough sticks a little, so tilt further than you did with the tray. Dust the plate with dry flour if nothing moves.
  - Check: You tilt the plate a little. Which dough shape stays where it is? → **The box**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press the ball a little flat. 2. Your child rolls it out with the rolling pin. 3. Then they try to roll it out with a matchbox. 4. Ask why the pin works.  
  *Parent says:* Roll the dough flat with the pin. Now try with the matchbox. Which works? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Roll dough flat with the rolling pin. Then try with a box. Which works?
  - Ask: Why is a rolling pin a tin shape? (good: It has to roll.; Its curved side rolls over the dough.; A box only pushes the dough.)
  - Note: The pin's curved surface rolls over the dough and presses it evenly. A box can only slide and scrape. If you are making chapatis today, let your child roll one.
### a4 · Maths in life: a rolling game and a sliding game (15 min)
- **problem:** From the same start line, which gets closer to the target line: a marble that rolls or a coin that slides?
- **safety:** Play on the floor, away from stairs. Keep marbles and coins away from babies and toddlers.
- **record:** Make two rows on paper: marble and coin. Make one tally stroke for each try that stops within one hand of the target line.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mark a start line on a smooth floor. 2. Mark a target line 2 big steps away. 3. Ask which will be easier to stop near the line, the marble or the coin, and why.  
  *Parent says:* We will send a marble and a coin to that line. Which will be easier to stop near it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two lines. Guess: which stops nearer the line, the marble or the coin?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rolls the marble from the start line, 5 times. 2. After each try, check with a flat hand. 3. Make a stroke if it stopped within one hand of the line.  
  *Parent says:* Roll it gently. Did it stop within one hand of the line? If yes, make a stroke.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the marble 5 times. Make a stroke each time it stops near the line.
  - Ask: What does the marble do after it passes the line? (good: It keeps going.; It rolls a long way.; It is hard to stop.)
  - Note: A marble keeps rolling on a smooth floor, so it often runs far past the line. That is the point of this step.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child slides the coin from the start line with a finger flick, 5 times. 2. Check each try with a flat hand. 3. Make a stroke for each near try.  
  *Parent says:* Now flick the coin so that it slides. Did it stop within one hand of the line?  
  *Child mode:* Slide the coin 5 times. Make a stroke each time it stops near the line.
  - Ask: The coin stopped sooner than the marble. Why might that be? (good: The floor grips its flat face.; Its whole face rubs on the floor.; I do not know.)
  - Note: A sliding coin rubs the floor with its whole face, so it slows down quickly. A carrom board is dusted with powder to make the coins slide further.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the strokes in each row. 2. Ask which was easier to send far. 3. Ask which was easier to stop near the line.  
  *Parent says:* Count the strokes. Which went further on a small push? Which was easier to stop near the line?  
  *Child mode:* Count your strokes. Which went further? Which was easier to stop?
  - Note: Scores differ from floor to floor. Most families find the marble goes further and the coin is easier to place. In a cricket match, the ball rolls all the way to the boundary for the same reason.
  - Check: On a smooth floor, which usually goes further on the same small push? → **A marble that rolls**
### a5 · Everyone does maths: push like a dung beetle (10 min)
- **problem:** You must move a ball and a box from one line to another with one finger. Which needs fewer pushes? Which is easier to keep straight?
- **record:** Write two numbers: pushes for the ball and pushes for the box.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mark two lines 4 big steps apart. 2. Put the ball and the box on the first line. 3. Ask which will need fewer pushes, and why.  
  *Parent says:* You can use only one finger. Which will need fewer pushes to reach that line? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess: which needs fewer pushes, the ball or the box?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child pushes the ball to the far line with one finger. 2. Count each push aloud. 3. Do the same with the box. 4. Write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Push and let go. That is one. Push again. Keep counting until it crosses the line.  
  *Child mode:* Push the ball with one finger. Count your pushes. Then push the box.
  - Ask: What happened to the ball between your pushes? (good: It kept going.; It rolled by itself.; It went the wrong way a little.)
  - Check: A ball crossed the floor in 3 pushes. A box needed 14. Which needed more pushes? → **The box**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the dung beetle note to your child. 2. Ask why a beetle might make a ball and not a box. 3. Ask what the hard part of pushing a ball was.  
  *Parent says:* Some beetles shape dung into a ball and roll it away. Why a ball, do you think? What was hard about pushing yours?  
  *Child mode:* A dung beetle rolls a ball of food away. Why a ball and not a box?
  - Ask: Why might a beetle make a ball and not a box? (good: A ball rolls easily.; It needs fewer pushes.; It rolls any way you push it.)
  - Note: Dung beetle note: the beetle walks backwards and pushes the ball with their back legs. A ball also wanders off line, as your child found. Scientists found that the beetles keep a straight path by looking at the sky: the Sun or the Moon, and on dark nights the glow of the Milky Way.

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: the cup that will not roll straight (20 min)
- **problem:** A tin rolls straight. A paper cup rolls in a curve. Find out which way it curves and why. Then make two paper cups roll in a straight line together.
- **your turn:** Now make two paper cones: a short one and a long one, measured from the point to the rim. Roll each on the floor. Which goes round in the smaller circle? Guess first, then test.
- **constraints:** Roll on a flat floor, not on a slope.; Push gently, once, and let go.; For the last part you may use only 2 paper cups and tape.
- **general:** Roll the cup and watch which end travels further.; Mark where the cup is after each push, to see the curve.; Compare it with the tin, which goes straight.
- **creative:** Tape two cups together bottom to bottom. The shape now rests on two wide rims of the same size, and it rolls straight.; Tape two cups together mouth to mouth. It rests on the join in the middle, tips to one side and curves again.; Put one cup inside the other. It still curves, because one end is still wider.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the tin and the cup on their sides. 2. Ask your child to guess the path of each. 3. Roll the tin first. 4. Then roll the cup and watch.  
  *Parent says:* The tin and the cup both have a curved side. Will they roll the same way? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how the tin will roll. Guess how the cup will roll. Then test.
  - Ask: What did the cup do that the tin did not? (good: It turned.; It went in a curve.; It came back round.)
  - Note: The cup curves towards its narrow end. Many children expect it to roll straight, like the tin.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Roll the cup again, slowly. 2. Put a bit of paper where the wide end is after each push. 3. Do the same for the narrow end. 4. Look at the two lines of paper.  
  *Parent says:* Let us mark where each end goes. Which end made the bigger curve?  
  *Child mode:* Roll the cup slowly. Mark where the wide end goes. Mark the narrow end too.
  - Ask: Which end travelled further? (good: The wide end.; The big end made the bigger circle.)
  - Note: The two paper trails are parts of two circles, one inside the other. The wide end is on the outside.
  - Check: A paper cup rolls on the floor. Which way does it curve? → **Towards its narrow end**
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child 2 cups and tape. 2. Ask them to join the cups so that they roll straight. 3. Let them try more than one way. 4. Test each on the floor.  
  *Parent says:* Here are two cups and some tape. Can you join them so that they roll straight? Guess first, then try.  
  *Child mode:* Join 2 cups with tape. Make them roll in a straight line.
  - Ask: Why does it roll straight now? (good: It rolls on two rims that are the same size.; Each end goes the same distance.; One cup wants to turn one way and the other turns it back.)
  - Note: Bottom to bottom works: the shape rolls on its two wide rims. Mouth to mouth does not: it tips on to one cup and curves. Let your child try both. Help with the tape.
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make a short cone and a long cone from paper. 2. Ask which will go round in the smaller circle. 3. Roll each. 4. Compare.  
  *Parent says:* One cone is short. One is long. Which will turn in the smaller circle? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make a short cone and a long cone. Which turns in the smaller circle?
  - Ask: What did you notice about the long cone's circle? (good: It is bigger.; The long cone needs more room to turn.)
  - Note: A cone turns round its own point, so a longer cone sweeps a bigger circle. For a short cone use half of a saucer-sized paper circle. For a long one use half of a dinner-plate circle. A grown-up cuts the paper.

## Worksheets
### Roll or Slide? (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is a solid shape?  
   Options: A circle drawn on paper / A ball / A line drawn on paper  
   **Answer:** A ball. *Why:* A ball. You can hold it, and it takes up room. A circle and a line on paper are flat.
2. (mcq) Which one has no flat face?  
   Options: Ball / Box / Tin  
   **Answer:** Ball. *Why:* The ball. It is curved all over. A box has 6 flat faces and a tin has 2.
3. (fill) A matchbox has ____ flat faces.  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* Top, bottom, front, back and two ends. That makes 6. Touch each one as you count.
4. (truefalse) A ball can roll whichever way you push it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A ball is curved all over, so any side of it can roll.
5. (sort) Each thing sits on a steep slope. Will it roll or slide?  
   **Answer:** marble → Rolls; book → Slides; football → Rolls; matchbox → Slides; lemon → Rolls; dice → Slides. *Why:* The marble, the football and the lemon rest on a curved surface, so they roll. The book, the matchbox and the dice rest on a flat face, so they slide.
6. (match) Match each thing to its family.  
   **Answer:** laddu → ball; brick → box; rolling pin → tin; party cap → cone. *Why:* A laddu is curved all over. A brick has 6 flat faces. A rolling pin has a curved side and 2 flat ends. A party cap has a point.
7. (fill) The squares are boxes and the circles are balls. There are ____ boxes.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Count the squares: 1, 2, 3. The two circles are balls.
8. (truefalse) A matchbox rolls down a gentle slope.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A matchbox has only flat faces. It stays on a gentle slope and slides on a steep one. It never rolls.
9. (mcq) A tin lies on its side on a slope. What does it do?  
   Options: It rolls / It stays / It stacks  
   **Answer:** It rolls. *Why:* It rolls. Its curved surface is on the slope.
10. (draw) Draw a tower of 2 boxes with a cone on top.  
   **Answer:** Two box shapes one above the other, with a pointed cone sitting on the top box.. *Why:* The boxes have flat faces above and below, so they stack. The cone sits on its flat face, with its point up.
11. (draw) Draw one thing that rolls and one thing that slides. Write R on the roller and S on the slider.  
   **Answer:** A thing with a curved surface marked R, such as a ball, and a thing with flat faces marked S, such as a box.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the roller resting on a curved part and the slider resting on a flat face.
12. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. Why does a ball roll when a matchbox does not?  
   **Answer:** A ball is curved all over. A matchbox has only flat faces.. *Why:* A strong answer talks about the curved surface and the flat faces. It does not say 'because it is a ball'.
### Which Part Is Down? (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (fill) A round tin has ____ flat faces.  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* The lid and the bottom. The side between them is curved.
2. (fill) A cone has ____ flat face.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* Only the round bottom is flat. The other end is a point.
3. (truefalse) A tin standing on its flat end rolls down a gentle slope.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A flat face is on the slope, so the tin stays. It rolls only when it lies on its curved side.
4. (mcq) A coin lies flat on a steep slope. What does it do?  
   Options: It slides / It rolls / It stacks  
   **Answer:** It slides. *Why:* It slides. Its flat face is on the slope. A coin rolls only when it is on its edge.
5. (order) Put these in order, fewest flat faces first.  
   **Answer:** ['Ball', 'Cone', 'Tin', 'Box']. *Why:* A ball has 0 flat faces. A cone has 1. A tin has 2. A box has 6.
6. (sort) Can it go in the middle of a tower?  
   **Answer:** matchbox → Yes; lemon → No; tin standing up → Yes; party cap → No; book → Yes; marble → No. *Why:* The matchbox, the tin and the book each have a flat face below and on top. The lemon and the marble have no flat face. The party cap has a point on top.
7. (mcq) Which thing can roll, and can also stand still on a slope?  
   Options: A ball / A glue stick / A dice  
   **Answer:** A glue stick. *Why:* A glue stick. It rolls on its curved side and stands on its flat end. A ball only rolls. A dice never rolls.
8. (fill) A tower has 3 boxes and 2 tins. How many things are in it? ____  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* 3 and 2 more make 5.
9. (perspective) A round tin sits on the table.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says both are right about one way of placing the tin. It rolls lying down and stays standing up.. *Why:* The tin has a curved side and two flat ends. What it does depends on which part is on the table.
10. (draw) Draw a tin lying down and a tin standing up. Colour the part that touches the floor.  
   **Answer:** Two tins. On the lying one a line along the curved side is coloured. On the standing one the flat bottom is coloured.. *Why:* The lying tin touches the floor with its curved surface, so it rolls. The standing tin touches with a flat face, so it stays.
11. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. Why can nothing sit on top of a cone?  
   **Answer:** The top of a cone is a point. There is no flat face to sit on.. *Why:* A strong answer says that stacking needs a flat face on top, and a cone has only a point there.
12. (match) Match each thing to what it does on a gentle slope.  
   **Answer:** ball → rolls any way it is put; cone lying down → rolls in a curve; matchbox → stays where it is. *Why:* The ball is curved all over. The cone turns round its point. The matchbox rests on a flat face.
### Solid Shape Puzzles (challenge) (challenge, 12 questions)
1. (fill) 2 matchboxes have ____ flat faces altogether.  
   **Answer:** ['12', 'twelve']. *Why:* Each matchbox has 6 flat faces. 6 and 6 make 12.
2. (fill) A tin and a cone together have ____ flat faces.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* A tin has 2 flat faces and a cone has 1. 2 and 1 make 3.
3. (mcq) A paper cup is wider at the top. How does it roll on the floor?  
   Options: In a curve / In a straight line / It cannot roll  
   **Answer:** In a curve. *Why:* In a curve. The wide end travels further in one turn than the narrow end, so the cup swings round.
4. (truefalse) A coin is a very short tin shape.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A coin has 2 flat round faces and a thin curved surface between them, just like a tin.
5. (order) Put these towers in order, fewest things first.  
   **Answer:** ['2 boxes', '1 box and 2 tins', '4 boxes']. *Why:* 2 boxes is 2 things. 1 box and 2 tins is 3 things. 4 boxes is 4 things.
6. (mcq) You can see only a flat round face. Which thing can it NOT be?  
   Options: A tin / A cone / A box  
   **Answer:** A box. *Why:* A box. All the faces of a box have straight sides and corners. A tin and a cone each have a flat round face.
7. (sort) Tin shape or box shape?  
   **Answer:** coin → Tin; book → Box; candle → Tin; brick → Box; drum → Tin; dice → Box. *Why:* The coin, the candle and the drum each have 2 flat round faces and a curved side. The book, the brick and the dice each have 6 flat faces.
8. (truefalse) Two cones can stack point to point.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A point is not a flat face. The top cone has nothing to rest on, and it falls.
9. (fill) A marble crossed the floor in 3 pushes. A box needed 9. The box needed ____ more pushes.  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* 9 take away 3 is 6. The box slides and stops, so it needs more pushes.
10. (draw) A cone is pushed on the floor. Draw its path. Mark where its point is.  
   **Answer:** A curved path or a circle, with the point of the cone at the middle of the circle.. *Why:* A cone turns round its own point. The wide end travels round and the point stays nearly still.
11. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. A dung beetle rolls food away as a ball. Why not as a box?  
   **Answer:** A ball rolls, so it is easy to push a long way. A box only slides and stops.. *Why:* A strong answer uses what the pushing test showed: a ball needs far fewer pushes than a box.
12. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. Is a sheet of paper flat or solid? Say why.  
   **Answer:** It is solid, but very thin. A pile of sheets shows the thickness.. *Why:* A strong answer says that paper has a tiny thickness, so it is a very thin box. 'Flat' is how we speak of it every day.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these can you pick up and hold?  
   Options: A circle drawn on paper / A ball / A square drawn on paper  
   **Answer:** A ball. *Why:* A ball. It is a solid shape. The circle and the square are flat shapes drawn on paper.
2. (mcq) Run your palm all over a matchbox. What do you feel?  
   Options: Only flat faces / Only a curved surface / One sharp point  
   **Answer:** Only flat faces. *Why:* Only flat faces. A matchbox has 6 of them and no curved surface.
3. (fill) How many flat faces does a ball have? ____  
   **Answer:** ['0', 'zero', 'none']. *Why:* None. A ball is curved all over. Your palm cannot lie flat on it anywhere.
4. (mcq) Which one will NOT roll on a gentle slope?  
   Options: The ball / The box / The tin lying down  
   **Answer:** The box. *Why:* The box. It rests on a flat face, so it stays. The ball and the tin lying down rest on a curved surface, so they roll.
5. (mcq) Why does a marble roll?  
   Options: It is small / It rests on a curved surface / It is made of glass  
   **Answer:** It rests on a curved surface. *Why:* It rests on a curved surface. A big football rolls too, so size is not the reason. A glass cube would not roll.
6. (mcq) A brick is in which family?  
   Options: Ball / Box / Cone  
   **Answer:** Box. *Why:* Box. A brick has 6 flat faces and no curved surface.
7. (mcq) A rolling pin is in which family?  
   Options: Tin / Box / Ball  
   **Answer:** Tin. *Why:* Tin. It has a curved side and 2 flat round ends.
8. (mcq) You want to put a box on top. Which thing can hold it?  
   Options: A ball / A cone standing up / A tin standing up  
   **Answer:** A tin standing up. *Why:* A tin standing up. Its top is a flat face. A ball has no flat face, and a cone has a point on top.
9. (truefalse) You can stack lemons into a tall straight tower.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A lemon has no flat face. One lemon on another touches at a tiny spot and rolls off.
10. (mcq) A glue stick stands on its flat end on a gentle slope. What does it do?  
   Options: It stays / It rolls / It spins  
   **Answer:** It stays. *Why:* It stays. A flat face is on the slope. It would roll if you laid it on its curved side.
11. (mcq) A coin stands on its edge. Then it lies flat. What has changed?  
   Options: The shape of the coin / The part that touches the floor / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** The part that touches the floor. *Why:* The part that touches the floor. On its edge the coin can roll. On its flat face it can only slide. The coin is the same.
12. (fill) A box has 6 flat faces. A tin has 2. How many altogether? ____  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* 6 and 2 more make 8.

## Applications
- A cook rolls dough flat with a rolling pin. The pin is a tin shape, so its curved side rolls over the dough.
- A mason builds a wall from bricks. Bricks are box shapes, so their flat faces stack.
- Wheels on a bus, a cycle and a suitcase are short tin shapes that roll on their curved sides.
- A shopkeeper stands tins and bottles on their flat ends, so that they stay on the shelf.
- A road roller has a huge, heavy tin shape in front. It rolls over the road and presses it flat.
- A carrom player slides a coin on its flat face. A cricket bowler sends a ball that rolls and bounces.
- Workers lay a heavy drum on its side and roll it, which is easier than carrying it.
- A funnel is a cone with the point cut off. Rice or oil slides down its sloping side into a narrow jar.

## Spotted around us
- A laddu on a tilted plate rolls to the edge. A piece of Mysore pak on the same plate stays where it is.
- A cooking gas cylinder stands on its flat end in the kitchen. The delivery person tilts it and rolls it along on its bottom rim.
- A tiffin carrier is a tower of tin shapes. Each one has a flat top and a flat bottom, so they stack.
- A temple chariot has huge wooden wheels. Each wheel is a short tin shape resting on its curved side.
- At a fruit stall, oranges sit in a heap that is wide at the bottom. Nobody stacks them in a straight tower.
- Pour rice slowly on to one spot on a plate. The grains slide down the sides, and the heap makes a cone by itself.
- A road roller pressing a new road has a giant tin shape in front, lying on its curved side.
- On a carrom board the coins slide. In a game of goli the marbles roll. Watch which ones travel further.

## Open questions
- **Why is a murre's egg pointed at one end?** For a long time people said it was so that the egg rolls in a circle. Tests in 2017 and 2018 pointed to other reasons, such as sitting steadily on a slope. Scientists are still studying it.
- **What does a dung beetle sense when they pat dung into a ball?** Scientists can film the beetle and measure the ball. Nobody has found a way to ask the beetle what they are aiming for.
- **Is there a perfect ball anywhere?** Every ball people have made has tiny bumps. Even the Earth is a little wider round its middle. Nobody knows whether a perfect ball exists outside our minds.
- **What does an antlion sense to get the slope of their pit right?** Scientists have watched how antlions dig, walking backwards and flicking sand out. What tells the antlion that the slope is steep enough is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- Kavya builds a tower with 3 matchboxes and 2 tins. She puts 1 cone on top. How many things are in her tower? **6 things.**. 3 matchboxes and 2 tins make 5. 1 cone more makes 6. So the tower has 6 things. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- On a gentle slope, 3 tests rolled and 5 tests stayed. How many more stayed than rolled? **2 more stayed.**. 5 stayed and 3 rolled. 5 take away 3 is 2. So 2 more stayed than rolled. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- In the bag are 1 box, 1 tin and 1 cone. How many flat faces can your fingers find altogether? **9 flat faces.**. The box has 6 flat faces. The tin has 2. The cone has 1. 6 and 2 make 8, and 1 more makes 9. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.number-facts.L1`)
- A tower goes box, tin, box, tin from the bottom up. What is the 6th thing? Could a cone be the 3rd thing? **The 6th thing is a tin. A cone cannot be the 3rd thing.**. Box and tin take turns. The 1st, 3rd and 5th are boxes. The 2nd, 4th and 6th are tins. A cone has a point on top, so nothing could sit on it. It can only be the last thing. (queues `math.patterns.repeating-patterns.L1`)
- Meenu feels a thing in the bag. It has a flat round face. Is it surely a tin? What must she check? **Not surely. She must feel the other end.**. A tin has a flat round face. So does a cone. Meenu must feel the other end. If there is a second flat round face, it is a tin. If there is a point, it is a cone. (queues `math.logic.puzzles.L1`)
