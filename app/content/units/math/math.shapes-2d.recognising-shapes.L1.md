# Shapes: count the sides and corners
`math.shapes-2d.recognising-shapes.L1`

**Summary.** Is an upside-down triangle still a triangle? What about a very thin one? Most of us pick out a shape by its look, and the look can fool us. You will cut out paper shapes, put a bean on every corner and run a finger along every side. That is how you will find the rule for a triangle, a square, a rectangle and a circle. Then you will find these shapes in your kitchen, in a kolam and in a game of paandi.

**Intro.** *How many triangles can you find here?* There are 4 triangles. Most people spot the first one at once, because it sits flat with its point on top. The upside-down one and the two leaning ones are triangles too. Each of them has 3 straight sides and 3 corners. In this unit you will find out why that is the only thing that counts.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name a circle, a triangle, a square and a rectangle when I see one.
1. I can count the sides and the corners of a shape.
2. I can tell a triangle by its 3 straight sides and 3 corners, whichever way it points.
3. I can tell a square from a rectangle by checking the lengths of the sides.
4. I can say what makes a circle: one curved edge, no corners, the same all the way round.
5. I can find these shapes around me, and say that a shape's name comes from a rule people agreed on.

## Terms
- **Shape**: The outline of a flat thing: what you get when you draw round its edge. *e.g.* Put a steel plate on paper and draw round it. The line you drew is the shape of the plate.
- **Side**: One piece of the edge of a shape, from one corner to the next. *e.g.* A postcard has 4 sides. Run your finger along each one.
- **Corner**: The point where two sides meet. *e.g.* A samosa's flat face has 3 corners. You can put one bean on each.
- **Straight**: Going one way without bending, like a tight thread. *e.g.* The edge of a ruler is straight. The edge of a bangle is not.
- **Curved**: Bending smoothly, with no corner. *e.g.* The rim of a tumbler is curved all the way round.
- **Triangle**: A flat shape with 3 straight sides and 3 corners. *e.g.* A slice cut from the corner of a sandwich is a triangle.
- **Square**: A flat shape with 4 straight sides of the same length, and 4 corners like the corners of a book. *e.g.* A carrom board is a square. So is one box on a chessboard.
- **Rectangle**: A flat shape with 4 straight sides and 4 corners like the corners of a book. In everyday talk it has 2 long sides and 2 short sides. *e.g.* A door, a ten-rupee note and a phone screen are rectangles.
- **Circle**: A flat round shape with one curved edge and no corners. It is the same all the way round. *e.g.* Draw round a bangle. The line is a circle.

## Explanations
### Every flat shape has sides and corners you can count · *defined*
**Definition:** A side is one piece of the edge of a shape. A corner is the point where two sides meet.

Here is a worked example. Take a postcard or a book. Put your finger on one corner and move it along the edge. It goes straight, and then it has to turn. Each straight run is one side: 1, 2, 3, 4. Each turn is one corner: 1, 2, 3, 4. So a postcard has 4 sides and 4 corners. A side can be straight, like the edge of a ruler. An edge can also be curved, like the rim of a tumbler. A curved edge bends smoothly and has no corner in it. Now you try with a steel box lid.
- e.g. A postcard: 4 straight sides, 4 corners.
- e.g. A samosa's flat face: 3 straight sides, 3 corners.
- e.g. A bangle: one curved edge, no corners.
### A triangle has 3 straight sides and 3 corners · *defined*
**Definition:** A triangle is a flat shape with 3 straight sides that join at 3 corners.

That is the whole rule. It says nothing about size, colour, or which way the point faces. So a triangle can be wide or thin, tiny or huge, pointing up, down or sideways. Here is a worked example. A long thin piece of cloth looks like a blade of grass. Is it a triangle? Run your finger along it: side 1, side 2, side 3, all straight. Put beans on the corners: 3 beans. Yes, it is a triangle. Now a piece shaped like a slice of a round cake. It has 3 corners, but one side is curved. It is not a triangle. The sides must also join up, with no gap.
- e.g. A samosa's face, a clothes hanger and a 'Give Way' road sign are all triangles.
- e.g. A triangle turned upside down still has 3 sides and 3 corners.
- e.g. A slice of a round dosa has a curved side, so it is not a triangle.
### Square or rectangle? Check the sides · *defined*
**Definition:** A rectangle has 4 straight sides and 4 corners like the corners of a book. A square is the same, and all 4 of its sides are the same length.

Counting is not enough here, because both shapes give 4 and 4. You have to compare the sides. Here is a worked example. Lay a strip of paper along one side of a carrom board. Mark where the side ends. Now lay the strip along the next side. The mark fits. It fits all 4 sides, so the board is a square. Try the same with a door. The mark fits the two long sides, but the top is much shorter. So a door is a rectangle with 2 long sides and 2 short sides. Rectangles can be long and thin or short and wide. One more thing for later: in maths, a square counts as a special kind of rectangle. In everyday talk, people keep the two names apart.
- e.g. A chessboard box, a carrom board and many floor tiles are squares.
- e.g. A door, a book cover and a cricket pitch are rectangles.
- e.g. A ten-rupee note is a rectangle. Fold one corner over to the far edge and you can see a square inside it.
### What is round? A circle is the same all the way round · *defined*
**Definition:** A circle is a flat shape with one curved edge and no corners. It is the same all the way round.

Here is how to test it. Draw round a bangle. Put the bangle back on its outline and turn it a little. It still fits. Turn it again. It fits at every turn. Now cut out an egg shape and draw round it. Turn it a little, and it sticks out of its outline. So an egg shape is round, but it is not a circle. A ball is round too, but it is not flat, so it is not a circle either. Look at a ball from any side and its outline is a circle. In everyday talk, round can mean all three. In maths, circle means only the flat one that is the same all the way round.
- e.g. The rim of a tumbler, a bangle and a coin's face are circles.
- e.g. The outline of an egg is round but longer one way, so it is not a circle.
- e.g. A lemon and a cricket ball are round like a ball. They are not flat.
### The look can fool you: turning does not change a shape · *defined*
**Definition:** A shape stays the same shape when you turn it, move it, make it bigger or smaller, or colour it.

Stand a book up. It is a rectangle. Lay it on its side. It is still a rectangle, with the same 4 sides and 4 corners. Now take a square of paper and turn it so that one corner points up. Many people call this a diamond. But nothing about the paper has changed. It still has 4 sides of the same length and the same 4 corners. So it is still a square. Our eyes are used to seeing shapes one way up. When a shape is turned, count its sides and corners again. They will tell you the truth.
- e.g. A standing rectangle and a lying rectangle are the same shape.
- e.g. A square standing on one corner is still a square.
- e.g. A red triangle and a blue triangle are both triangles.
### Who chose the names? · *defined*
**Definition:** A shape's name is a word people chose. Different languages name the same shape by different parts of it.

The English word triangle means three angles. An angle is the opening at a corner. The Tamil word mukkonam also means three corners. So does the Hindi word trikon. But Hindi has a second word, tribhuj, and it means three arms, which are the sides. Some people looked at this shape and counted its corners. Others counted its sides. They are all talking about the same shape, and both ways of counting give 3. The same thing happens with the square that stands on its corner. Some people call it a diamond, and in maths it is still a square. People choose the words. After that, anyone can check a shape against the rule by counting.
- e.g. Triangle, mukkonam and trikon name the shape by its 3 corners.
- e.g. Tribhuj names the same shape by its 3 sides.
- e.g. The words 'diamond' and 'square' can point at the same piece of paper.
### Is there a perfect circle anywhere? · *debated*
Look very closely at a circle you drew. The pencil line wobbles a little. It also has some thickness. A dosa has bumps at its edge. Even a coin has tiny dents. Nobody has ever made a circle that is perfectly the same all the way round. Yet we all know what a perfect circle would be, and we use that idea to judge every real one. So where is the perfect circle? People do not agree.
- e.g. A dosa is close to a circle. A coin is closer. Neither is perfect.
- e.g. A circle on a phone screen is made of tiny square dots.
- e.g. The full Moon looks like a circle, but its edge has mountains.
- **The view that the perfect circle is an idea:** The perfect circle lives in minds. People saw many round things and made one clear idea from them. We share that idea, the way we share a word.
- **Plato, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,400 years ago, and many mathematicians since:** The perfect circle is real, even though nobody can draw it. Real dosas and coins are rough copies of it. People found the circle. They did not make it.
### Other living beings make shapes too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings build things with a clear shape.

In the sea near Amami-Oshima island in Japan lives a small pufferfish. He is about 12 centimetres long, which is about as long as your hand. To make a nest, he swims along the sandy sea floor and pushes the sand with his fins. After 7 to 9 days he has made a circle about 2 metres wide, with ridges running out from the middle. That is wider than a grown-up is tall. A female comes to look, and if she chooses him she lays her eggs in the middle. Honeybees make shapes too. They build wax cells, and each cell has 6 sides and 6 corners. Nobody taught the fish or the bees the names of these shapes.
- e.g. A male white-spotted pufferfish makes a circle about 2 metres wide in the sand.
- e.g. Each cell in a honeybee's comb has 6 sides.
- e.g. Many spiders build round webs with threads running out from the middle.
### Shapes in the sky, on the road and at home · *defined*
**Definition:** Each shape turns up where it does a job well.

The Sun and the Moon are huge balls. From far away, each looks like a flat circle. Drop a pebble in a bucket of water, and the ripples spread out as circles, because they move at the same speed every way. Rectangles are mostly made by people. Bricks, books, doors and tiles are rectangles because rectangles fit side by side with no gaps. Triangles are used where things must not bend. Look at an electricity tower or the frame under a tiled roof, and you will see many triangles. Three sticks tied in a triangle cannot be pushed out of shape. Road signs in India use shapes too. Signs that give an order are mostly circles. Signs that warn you are triangles. Signs that tell you where things are, such as a hospital, are rectangles. These reasons are simple versions. You will learn more about each in later years.
- e.g. The full Moon looks like a circle in the sky.
- e.g. Floor tiles are squares or rectangles so that they fit with no gaps.
- e.g. An electricity tower is built from many metal triangles.

## Mostly agreed
- A flat shape with 3 straight sides that join at 3 corners is a triangle. It does not matter how big it is or which way it points. *How we know:* This is what the word means to mathematicians everywhere. Anyone can check a shape against it by counting, as you did in the Triangle Test.
- Young children usually know a shape by its whole look first. Counting its sides and corners comes later. *How we know:* In 1999, researchers sat with 97 children aged 3 to 6, one at a time, and asked them about shapes. Most of the children chose mainly by matching the look.
- Nobody has ever drawn or made a perfect circle. Every real circle is a little bit off somewhere. *How we know:* Look at a pencil circle through a magnifying glass. The line wobbles and has thickness. The same is true of a coin's edge under a stronger lens.
- A small male pufferfish near Japan makes a circle about 2 metres wide in the sand, as a nest. *How we know:* Divers had seen these circles on the sea floor without knowing who made them. In 2013, Hiroshi Kawase and two other scientists reported watching the fish build them over 7 to 9 days.
- More than 2,500 years ago, builders in India marked out exact squares, rectangles and circles on the ground with ropes and pegs. *How we know:* Their rules are written in old texts called the Sulba Sutras. The word sulba means rope. The oldest of them is linked to the name Baudhayana.

## How we found out
**Dina van Hiele-Geldof and Pierre van Hiele, and later Douglas Clements, Sudha Swaminathan, Mary Anne Hannibal and Julie Sarama, 1957 and 1999.** For a long time, schoolbooks showed one kind of triangle. It sat flat, with its point on top. Teachers thought that a child who could name it knew what a triangle was. In 1957 two Dutch teachers, Dina van Hiele-Geldof and her husband Pierre van Hiele, wrote about what they had seen in their own classes. They said that learners first know a shape by its whole look, the way we know a face. Only later do they notice its sides and corners. Dina died the next year, in 1958, and for many years few people outside the Netherlands knew of the work. In 1999 four researchers in the United States, Douglas Clements, Sudha Swaminathan, Mary Anne Hannibal and Julie Sarama, tested the idea. They sat with 97 children aged 3 to 6, one at a time, and asked them to pick out shapes. Most of the children chose mainly by matching the look. So children need to meet many kinds of triangle, and to count the sides and corners for themselves.  
*What people believed before:* A child who can name the usual triangle knows what a triangle is. The van Hieles in 1957, and the test with 97 children in 1999, showed that young children go mostly by the look. So they often leave out a thin or upside-down triangle.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A square is not a rectangle.** (Most children, and many grown-ups in everyday talk; *rejected*)  
  For: In everyday talk, 'rectangle' means a shape with 2 long sides and 2 short sides. A square does not look like that. Long ago, the Greek mathematician Euclid also listed them under two separate names, square and oblong.  
  Against: Mathematicians today say a rectangle is any shape with 4 straight sides and 4 corners like a book's. A square fits that rule. So in maths, a square is a special rectangle whose sides are all the same length.
- **A triangle must have a flat bottom and a point on top.** (Many children, because books usually draw a triangle that way; *rejected*)  
  For: Most triangles in picture books sit flat with the point on top. So that is the look we learn first.  
  Against: The rule for a triangle is 3 straight sides and 3 corners. It says nothing about which way is up. Turn any triangle, count again, and it still passes.
- **People are born with a sense of shape. Nobody has to teach it.** (Some scientists, after a 2006 study with the Mundurukú people of the Amazon forest; *debated*)  
  For: Mundurukú children and adults had little or no schooling, and their language has few words for shapes. They still picked the odd shape out of six about two times in three.  
  Against: Picking the odd one out may only need sharp eyes, not a sense of shape. And everyone, everywhere, grows up seeing round fruit and straight sticks, so they may have learned from those.

## Roots
We counted sides and corners on paper shapes. Now let us think about where a shape's name comes from.

**Deep truth.** In the Triangle Test, the thin piece and the upside-down piece did not look like triangles. But each had 3 straight sides and 3 corners. People agreed that this is what the word triangle means. So a shape gets its name from a rule that anyone can check by counting. Its size, its colour and the way it points are left out of the rule.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think we know a shape just by looking, the way we know a face. But the look can fool us. The name comes from a rule that people chose.  
*Evidence:* In the Triangle Test, five pieces that looked very different all had 3 beans and 3 straight sides. The piece with a curved side had 3 beans too, and still failed.

- *who am i:* You can see a triangle in a samosa, a flag and a hanger. Your mind picks out what is the same in all three.
- *what is life:* Other living beings make shapes too. A small pufferfish makes a big circle in the sand. Bees build wax cells with six sides.
- *what is this world:* No dosa is a perfect circle. Each one has small bumps. Real things only come close to the shapes we name.

A dosa, a bangle, a coin and the full Moon are four different things. We call all of them by one name: circle. So one shape shows up in many things. None of them is a perfect circle, and yet each one makes us think of it.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** The Chandogya Upanishad says: if you know one lump of clay, you know everything made of clay. A pot and a plate have different names, but both are clay. (Shape names work the other way round. A clay plate, a steel plate and a dosa are made of different things, and we give them one name, circle. A name picks out the part we choose to look at.)
- **Plato, Seventh Letter 342b-343a:** This old Greek letter is said to be by Plato, though scholars are not sure he wrote it. It says that every circle drawn by hand or made on a turning tool is a little bit wrong somewhere. The true circle is none of these. (The circle you drew round a bangle is very close, but it is not perfect. The letter says the perfect circle is real all the same. Others say it is an idea that minds make.)
- Maths (debated): In 2006, scientists showed sets of six pictures to Mundurukú children and adults in the Amazon forest of Brazil. They had little or no schooling, and their language has few words for shapes. They still picked the odd shape out about two times in three (Dehaene and others, 2006). Scientists do not agree on what this shows about where our sense of shape comes from.
- Maths (open): Is a perfect circle a real thing, or an idea that minds make? Nobody has settled this.
- Other view, Many mathematicians, from the Greek thinker Plato onwards: They say people did not choose what a triangle is. Triangles were there to be found, like a mountain. People only chose the word. Three straight sides meet at three corners whether anyone agrees or not.
- Practice, Once round the rim (3 min): Hold a steel tumbler or a bangle. Close your eyes. Put one finger on the rim. Breathe in slowly and move your finger half way round. Breathe out and come back to where you began. Do this five times. Then tell me. Where does the circle begin? Where does it end?
- Ask: Who decided that a triangle has three sides? Could they have chosen something else?
- Ask: No dosa is a perfect circle. So where is the perfect circle?
- Ask: A thin triangle and a wide triangle look different. Why do we give them the same name?
- Ask: A pufferfish has no word for circle. Does he still know what he is making?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A thin triangle and a wide triangle look very different. What is the same in both? (hint: Count something on each of them.)
- *deep:* Can a shape have 3 sides and 4 corners? Try to draw one. (hint: Each time two sides meet, you get one corner.)
- *deep:* If you turn a square on its corner, is it a new shape or the same shape? (hint: What changed about the paper? What did not?)
- *big:* No dosa is a perfect circle. So how do we all know what a perfect circle is? (hint: Have you ever seen one? Could you still spot a bumpy one?)
- *big:* A pufferfish makes a circle in the sand. Does he know it is a circle? (hint: He has no word for it. Do you need a word to know something?)
- *big:* Why are so many things people make rectangles, and so many things in the sky round? (hint: Think about bricks that must fit together, and about a ball of wet atta.)

## Story: The Flags Nobody Wanted
*Thenu, Surya, Paati and Fathima akka are made up. Strings of triangle flags are a common street decoration at festivals.*

Tomorrow was Pongal, and Thenu's street had no flags.

Paati had promised to sew a long string of them, long enough to cross the whole street. For that she needed twenty triangles of cloth. Fathima akka, the tailor at the corner, had given her a big bag of leftover pieces. Red, blue, green, yellow, all cut off from blouses and school uniforms.

"Sort them for me," Paati told Thenu and her big cousin Surya. "Triangles go in this basket. Everything else goes back in the bag."

Surya was ten, and he was quick. He tipped the bag out on the floor. He picked up a red piece. It had a flat bottom and a point on top, like a little hill. "Triangle," he said, and dropped it in the basket.

He picked up a blue piece. It was long and thin, like a blade of grass. "Not a triangle." Back in the bag.

A green piece, with its point hanging down. "Upside down. No."

A yellow piece that leaned to one side, as if it was tired. "No."

A purple piece as small as a biscuit. "Too small. No."

Soon the basket held nine pieces. All nine looked like little hills. The bag was nearly full again.

"Paati," Surya called, "there are only nine triangles. We need twenty."

Paati looked at the clock. Then she looked at the sky, which was already turning orange. Fathima akka's shop would be shut by now. "Then it will be a very short string," she said, and she went to find her needle.

Thenu did not want a very short string. She pulled the blue piece out of the bag, the one like a blade of grass. She did not ask herself what it looked like. She put her finger on one end and walked it along the edge. One side. Her finger turned a corner. Two sides. It turned again. Three sides, and her finger was back where it began.

"Surya anna, how many sides does a triangle have?"

"Three. Everybody knows that."

"And how many corners?"

"Three."

"Then count this one."

Surya counted the blue piece. He counted it again, more slowly. "Three sides," he said. "Three corners." He frowned at it. "But it is so thin."

"Is thin a number?" asked Thenu.

Surya did not answer. He took out the green piece that hung upside down, and he turned it round in his hands. It became a little hill. He turned it back again. Then he counted the tired yellow one. Three and three. He counted the purple one that was as small as a biscuit. Three and three.

After that they worked fast. Thenu counted, and Surya checked.

One piece nearly fooled them both. It was shaped like a slice of a round cake. It had two straight sides, and one side that bulged like a full tummy. "Three corners," said Surya, and he reached for the basket.

"But this side bends," said Thenu. She ran her finger over the bulge. "Paati can sew it, but it is not a triangle."

It went back in the bag. So did a piece with four sides, which looked like a hill with its top cut off.

When Paati came back with her needle, the basket was full.

"Twenty-three," said Surya. "Thin ones, small ones, tired ones. They are all triangles, Paati. We counted."

Paati sewed until the streetlight came on. She sewed each flag to the string by one side, so every single point hung down. Nobody on the street said they were not triangles.

In the morning the string went up, from Thenu's window to the neem tree across the street. The little hills hung quite still. But the long blue triangle caught every bit of wind. It flapped and snapped above the pongal pot, and the small children pointed up at it.

"That one is the best," said Surya. "And yesterday I threw it away."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Surya knows the rule for a triangle by heart, three sides and three corners, but he sorts by the look he is used to. Thenu ignores the look and counts. The thin, tiny and upside-down pieces all pass, and the piece with a curved side fails even though it has three corners. A shape's name comes from its sides and corners, not from its usual picture.

- Ask: Why did the blue piece look wrong to Surya at first?
- Ask: Thenu asked, 'Is thin a number?' What did she mean?
- Ask: Why was the piece like a slice of cake not a triangle, even with three corners?
- Ask: The flags hung with their points down. Were they still triangles? How could you check?

## Games
### g1 · Fill the Shape Mat (10 min)
- **rules:** Each player folds a sheet of paper into 8 boxes and writes the labels: 2 boxes each for circle, triangle, square and rectangle.; On your turn, roll the dice and read the key. Draw that shape in one of its empty boxes.; Your second shape of a kind must look different from the first: bigger, smaller, thinner or turned another way.; The next player checks your shape by counting its sides and corners. If it fails, rub it out.; If both boxes for your shape are full, you miss that turn.; The first player to fill all 8 boxes wins.; Playing alone: count how many rolls you need to fill the mat. Play again and try to need fewer.
- **Printable board: A mat for each player: a sheet folded into 8 boxes, with these labels:** Circle; Circle; Triangle; Triangle; Square; Square; Rectangle; Rectangle
- **Printable score sheet: The dice key:** 1 = circle; 2 = triangle; 3 = square; 4 = rectangle; 5 = any shape you need; 6 = any shape you need
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Each player folds a sheet in half three times and opens it. 2. Write the 8 labels, one in each box. 3. Copy the dice key where everyone can see it.  
  *Parent says:* Each of us needs a mat with 8 boxes. Two for each shape.  
  *Child mode:* Fold your paper into 8 boxes. Write two boxes for each shape.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one practice turn each. 2. Show how the checker counts the sides and corners aloud. 3. Draw a second triangle that looks different from the first.  
  *Parent says:* I rolled a 2, so I draw a triangle. Now check mine. Count my sides and corners.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the dice. Draw the shape. Let the next player check it.
  - Check: Your friend draws a triangle pointing down. It has 3 straight sides and 3 corners. Does it pass? → **Yes**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game. 2. Each time, the next player checks the shape. 3. Now and then, ask how many boxes a player still has to fill.  
  *Parent says:* You have filled 5 boxes. How many are still empty?  
  *Child mode:* Play the game. Check each other's shapes. Fill all 8 boxes.
  - Ask: You have filled 5 of your 8 boxes. How many are left? (good: 3.; 6, 7, 8. That is 3 more.)
  - Note: Hand-drawn shapes will wobble. A square passes if its sides look about the same length. The point is the counting, not neat drawing.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The game ends when one mat is full. 2. Ask each player which shape was hardest to draw two ways.  
  *Parent says:* Who filled the mat first? Which shape was hardest to draw in two different ways?  
  *Child mode:* The first full mat wins. Which shape was hardest to draw two ways?
  - Note: Most children say the circle, because two circles can only differ in size. That is worth noticing: a circle has only one look.

  *Parent says:* Each card is a riddle from a shape. Listen to the clue and tell me who is talking.

- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take turns. 2. Read the top clue aloud. 3. The player names the shape and touches one in the room. 4. Turn the card over to check.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the clue. Who am I? Now find me somewhere in this room.  
  *Child mode:* Read the clue. Say the shape. Touch that shape in the room. Then check.

  - Check: Clue: 'Put a bean on each of my corners. You need 3 beans.' Who am I? → **A triangle**
- **s3 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When the pile is gone, count each player's cards. 2. Ask which clue was the hardest.  
  *Parent says:* How many cards do you have? Which clue was the hardest?  
  *Child mode:* Count your cards. Which clue was the hardest?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Triangle Test: which of these are triangles? (25 min)
- **question:** Ten paper pieces lie on the floor. Which of them are triangles, and how can we be sure?
- **predict:** Before you count anything, put each piece in one of two heaps: triangle, or not a triangle. Why did you put it there?
- **fair test:** Every piece gets the same check. We put one bean on each corner and count the beans. Then we run a finger along each side and say straight or curved. We write the guess before we count, so the count cannot change the guess.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting. Keep beans and buttons away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** A grown-up draws the table with five columns. For each piece, write the guess first. Then write the number of beans, straight sides and curved sides. At the end, the child says the rule, and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** Piece | My guess: triangle or not | Corners (beans) | Straight sides | Curved sides
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut the ten pieces in the list. 2. Write a letter on each. 3. Lay them on the floor as the list says. 4. Draw the table with five columns.  
  *Parent says:* I have cut ten shapes. Some are triangles and some are not. Let us find out which.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts ten paper shapes. Each one gets a letter.
  - Note: The pieces need not be neat. For F, draw round a plate and cut one slice out of the circle, like a slice of cake. For G, cut a triangle and then snip its point off straight. Make D big enough to hold three beans.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to make two heaps, by looking only. 2. For each piece, ask why. 3. Write each guess in the table. 4. Do not correct any guess.  
  *Parent says:* Put the triangles in this heap. Put all the others in that heap. Tell me why for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Make two heaps: triangle, and not a triangle. Say why for each piece.
  - Ask: Why is this one not a triangle? (good: It is too thin.; It is upside down.; It is too small.; It has four sides.)
  - Note: Most six-year-olds leave out B, C and E, and many put F in. That is what the research found too. Accept every guess with its reason. The beans will do the correcting.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take piece A. 2. Your child puts one bean on each corner and counts the beans. 3. Your child runs a finger along each side and says straight or curved. 4. Write the numbers. 5. Do the same for B and C.  
  *Parent says:* Put a bean on every corner. How many beans? Now run your finger along each side. Is it straight or curved?  
  *Child mode:* Put one bean on each corner. Count the beans. Run your finger along each side.
  - Ask: How many beans did piece C need? How many sides did your finger find? (good: 3 beans.; 3 sides, all straight.)
  - Note: Let your child place the beans. If a corner is missed, ask, 'Does your finger turn anywhere else?'
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check pieces D to J in the same way. 2. Write the beans, the straight sides and the curved sides for each. 3. Go slowly on F and J.  
  *Parent says:* Now check the rest. Beans on the corners first. Then your finger along the sides.  
  *Child mode:* Check every other piece. Beans on corners. Finger along the sides.
  - Ask: Piece F has 3 beans. Is every one of its sides straight? (good: No, one side is curved.; Two are straight and one bends.)
  - Ask: How many beans does the circle need? (good: None.; Zero. It has no corners.)
  - Note: F has 3 corners, 2 straight sides and 1 curved side. G has 4 and 4. The circle has no corners and one curved edge.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask which pieces have 3 beans and 3 straight sides. 3. Put those pieces in one row. 4. Compare them with the first guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Which pieces have 3 beans and 3 straight sides? Put them together. Were they all in your triangle heap?  
  *Child mode:* Find every piece with 3 beans and 3 straight sides. Put them in a row.
  - Ask: These five look so different. What is the same about them? (good: They all have 3 sides.; 3 corners and 3 straight sides.; They all had 3 beans.)
  - Check: A piece is long and thin. It has 3 straight sides and 3 corners. Is it a triangle? → **Yes**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn piece B so that its point is up. 2. Ask if the beans changed. 3. Ask your child what they think now, what they saw and why. 4. Write their words under the table.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think makes a triangle? What did you see? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Turn piece B round. Count again. Now say what makes a triangle.
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why? (good: A triangle has 3 sides and 3 corners.; All of them had 3 beans, even the thin one.; Turning it did not change the beans.; The cake slice is out because one side bends.)
  - Check: A shape has 3 corners. One of its sides is curved. Is it a triangle? → **No, all 3 sides must be straight**

## Activities
### a1 · Kitchen Shape Hunt: which shape is hardest to find? (10 min)
- **problem:** Which of the four shapes will we find most often in the kitchen? Which will be hardest to find?
- **record:** Write the four shape names down the side of a sheet. Make one stroke beside a name each time you find that shape. Write the totals at the end.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand at the kitchen door. 2. Ask which of the four shapes will turn up most, and which least. 3. Write both guesses.  
  *Parent says:* We will hunt for circles, squares, rectangles and triangles. Which will we find most? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which shape is in your kitchen the most. Guess which is the least.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hunt for 5 minutes. 2. Your child traces each find with a finger and names the shape. 3. Make one tally stroke for each find. 4. Look at flat faces only.  
  *Parent says:* Trace it with your finger. What shape is this face? Does it have corners?  
  *Child mode:* Find a shape. Trace it with your finger. Say its name. Make one stroke.
  - Ask: This lid has 4 corners. Is it a square or a rectangle? How can we check? (good: Check if all the sides are the same length.; It has two long sides, so it is a rectangle.)
  - Note: Things are solid, so look at one flat face at a time: the top of a lid, the front of a box. For a square, compare two sides with a spoon handle or a strip of paper.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the strokes for each shape. 2. Write the four totals. 3. Compare them with the guesses. 4. Ask why triangles are so rare.  
  *Parent says:* Which shape won? Which was hardest to find? Why might that be?  
  *Child mode:* Count your strokes. Which shape won? Which was hardest to find?
  - Ask: Why do you think there are so many circles in a kitchen? (good: Round things are easy to stir in.; Plates and pots are round.; I do not know.)
  - Note: Most kitchens have many circles and rectangles, a few squares and hardly any triangles. There is no single right reason. Let your child wonder.
  - Check: In one kitchen there are 7 circles and 1 triangle. How many more circles than triangles? → **6**
### a2 · The Turn Test: square, rectangle or circle? (15 min)
- **problem:** A square and a rectangle both have 4 sides and 4 corners. An oval and a bangle are both round. How do we tell each pair apart?
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting.
- **record:** Make a table with two columns: the shape, and how many times it fits in one full turn.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay each of the four things on paper. 2. Your child draws round each one. 3. Put a pencil dot on one corner or edge of each thing.  
  *Parent says:* Draw round each one. Keep the pencil close to the edge.  
  *Child mode:* Draw round the postcard, the square, the bangle and the oval.
  - Note: Cut the square and the oval before you start. The oval need not be neat, as long as it is clearly longer one way.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the paper strip along one side of the square. 2. Mark where the side ends. 3. Try the mark on the other three sides. 4. Do the same with the postcard.  
  *Parent says:* Does the mark fit this side too? And this one? What about the postcard?  
  *Child mode:* Mark one side on the strip. Try the mark on every other side.
  - Ask: What did the strip show about the square? And about the postcard? (good: All 4 sides of the square are the same.; The postcard has 2 long sides and 2 short sides.)
  - Check: A shape has 4 straight sides and 4 corners like a book's. How do you know if it is a square? → **Check that all 4 sides are the same length**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the square back on its outline, dot at the top. 2. Ask for a guess: how many times will it fit in one full turn? 3. Turn it slowly and count each fit. 4. Do the same with the postcard.  
  *Parent says:* If we turn it all the way round, how many times will it fit its outline? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Then turn the square on its outline. Count each time it fits.
  - Note: The square fits 4 times in one full turn, counting the place where it started. The postcard fits 2 times. Stop the count when the dot is back at the top.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a guess about the bangle and the oval. 2. Turn the oval on its outline and count the fits. 3. Turn the bangle a little at a time. 4. Fill in the table. 5. Ask what your child found.  
  *Parent says:* Now the bangle and the oval. Both are round. Will they act the same? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the oval. Then turn the bangle. Which one always fits?
  - Ask: What is special about the circle? (good: It fits every time.; It is the same all the way round.; You cannot tell that it was turned.)
  - Note: The oval fits once or twice, depending on how evenly it was cut. The bangle fits wherever you stop. This is what 'the same all the way round' means.
  - Check: Which of these fits its outline at every turn? → **A circle**
### a3 · Maths in life: a dot kolam of squares and triangles (15 min)
- **problem:** How many squares and triangles can you make by joining 9 dots with straight lines?
- **record:** Draw both kolams on paper. Under each, write how many squares and how many triangles you found.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw 9 dots in 3 rows of 3. 2. Keep the gaps between the dots the same. 3. Ask your child to count the dots.  
  *Parent says:* A kolam often starts with dots. How many dots are here? How many in each row?  
  *Child mode:* Draw 3 dots in a row. Draw 2 more rows under it.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Join the outside dots with straight lines, all the way round. 2. Ask what shape it is and how to check. 3. Now join the middle dot to the four dots beside it. 4. Count the squares.  
  *Parent says:* What shape did we make round the outside? How many small squares are inside it now?  
  *Child mode:* Join the outside dots. Then join the middle dot to its four neighbours. Count the squares.
  - Ask: How many squares can you find altogether? (good: 4 small ones.; 5, with the big one round the outside.)
  - Note: There are 4 small squares and 1 big square, so 5 in all. Many children miss the big one. Trace it with a finger.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw 9 new dots. 2. Join the outside dots into a big square again. 3. Find the middle dot of each side. 4. Join those four dots to each other with straight lines. 5. Count the triangles in the corners.  
  *Parent says:* Join the middle of each side to the next. What shape is in the middle? What shapes are in the corners?  
  *Child mode:* On new dots, join the middle of each side to the next. What shapes appear?
  - Ask: Is the shape in the middle a square? How can you check? (good: Turn the paper and it looks like a square.; It has 4 sides the same length.)
  - Note: The middle shape is a square turned on its corner. Turn the paper to show it. There are 4 triangles, one in each corner.
  - Check: A square stands on one corner. How many sides does it have? → **4**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child add dots, lines or colour. 2. Draw the kolam at your door with powder or chalk. 3. Count the shapes in it together.  
  *Parent says:* Make it your own. Then tell me how many squares and triangles your kolam has.  
  *Child mode:* Add your own lines. Draw your kolam at the door. Count its shapes.
  - Note: If you have no doorstep, draw it on a slate or on paper and stick it by the door.
### a4 · Maths in life: draw a paandi court and play (15 min)
- **problem:** A paandi court is one big rectangle cut into 8 boxes. Are the boxes squares or rectangles? Check before you play.
- **safety:** Play on a flat, dry floor, away from the road and from stairs.
- **record:** After the game, draw the court on paper. Write whether your boxes were squares or rectangles, and how many footsteps each side was.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw one big rectangle, about 4 big steps long and 2 big steps wide. 2. Draw a line down the middle. 3. Draw 3 lines across. 4. Number the boxes 1 to 8, up one side and down the other.  
  *Parent says:* We need one big rectangle with 8 boxes in it. Help me keep the lines straight.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a big rectangle. Split it into 8 boxes. Number them 1 to 8.
  - Note: Families draw paandi courts in many ways. If you know another court, draw that one and check its shapes in the same way.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child walks heel to toe along each side of box 1 and counts the footsteps. 2. Write the four numbers. 3. Ask whether the box is a square or a rectangle.  
  *Parent says:* How many footsteps is each side? Are all four the same?  
  *Child mode:* Walk heel to toe along each side of box 1. Count your steps.
  - Ask: Is your box a square or a rectangle? How do you know? (good: A square. All 4 sides were 4 footsteps.; A rectangle. Two sides were 5 footsteps and two were 4.)
  - Check: The sides of a box are 4, 4, 4 and 4 footsteps. Its corners are like a book's. What shape is it? → **A square**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Throw your stone into box 1. 2. Hop over box 1 and through boxes 2 to 8 on one foot. 3. On the way back, pick up the stone in box 1 and hop out. 4. Next turn, throw into box 2. 5. If you touch a line, the turn passes.  
  *Parent says:* Hop through every box. Your foot must stay inside the four sides.  
  *Child mode:* Throw your stone into a box. Hop through the others on one foot. Stay off the lines.
  - Note: Use your own family's rules if you have them. For a first game, hopping with a rest on two feet in boxes 4 and 5 is fine.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stop when someone has thrown into all 8 boxes, or after 7 minutes. 2. Ask how many corners the whole court has.  
  *Parent says:* The whole court is one big rectangle. How many corners does it have?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the whole court. How many corners does the big rectangle have?
  - Ask: How many corners does the big rectangle have? (good: 4.)

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: sticks and shapes (20 min)
- **problem:** Use sticks as the sides of shapes. Find every different shape you can make with 6 sticks. Then find out which numbers of sticks can make a square.
- **your turn:** Now find out which numbers of sticks, from 3 to 12, can make a square. Try each number. Write yes or no for each, and look for the pattern.
- **constraints:** Every shape must be closed. The sticks touch end to end, with no gap.; Sticks may not cross or be broken.; You must use all the sticks you counted out for that shape.
- **general:** With 3 sticks, make a triangle.; With 4 sticks, make a square.; With 6 sticks, make a shape with 6 sides, like a cell in a honeycomb.
- **creative:** With 6 sticks, make a big triangle with 2 sticks on each side.; With 6 sticks, make a rectangle: 2 sticks, 1 stick, 2 sticks, 1 stick.; Push a 4-stick square sideways. It still has 4 equal sides, but its corners are no longer like a book's, so it is not a square.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child 3 sticks. 2. Ask for a closed shape. 3. Ask if 3 sticks can make any other shape. 4. Then give 4 sticks and ask for a square.  
  *Parent says:* Make a closed shape with 3 sticks. Can you make a different one with the same 3 sticks?  
  *Child mode:* Make a shape with 3 sticks. Then make a square with 4 sticks.
  - Ask: Can 3 sticks make anything but a triangle? (good: No. It is always a triangle.; I can turn it, but it is the same triangle.)
  - Note: Three equal sticks make only one triangle. Turning it does not make a new shape.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Gently push the 4-stick square sideways so that it leans. 2. Ask how many sides and corners it has now. 3. Ask if it is still a square.  
  *Parent says:* I pushed the square. It still has 4 sides the same length. Is it still a square? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Push your square so that it leans. Is it still a square?
  - Ask: What changed when it leaned? (good: The corners.; Two corners got pointy and two got wide.; The sides stayed the same.)
  - Note: This is why the rule for a square has two parts. A triangle of 3 sticks cannot be pushed out of shape in this way.
  - Check: A shape has 4 sides of the same length, but it leans. Its corners are not like a book's. Is it a square? → **No, a square needs corners like a book's**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child 6 sticks. 2. Ask for as many different closed shapes as they can find. 3. Draw each one. 4. Count the sides and corners of each.  
  *Parent says:* Use all 6 sticks each time. How many different shapes can you make?  
  *Child mode:* Use all 6 sticks. Make a shape. Draw it. Then make a different one.
  - Note: Look for a big triangle with 2 sticks a side, a rectangle with sides of 2 and 1, and a six-sided shape. Sides of 3, 2 and 1 sticks will not close into a triangle. They fall flat. Let your child find that.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask if 6 sticks can make a square. Let your child try. 2. Try 5, 7 and 8 sticks. 3. Write yes or no for each number. 4. Go on to 12 if there is time.  
  *Parent says:* Can 6 sticks make a square? Guess first, and say why. Then try 8.  
  *Child mode:* Can 6 sticks make a square? Try it. Then try 8 sticks.
  - Ask: Why does 8 work when 6 does not? (good: 8 gives 2 sticks to every side.; With 6, two sides would be longer.; The 4 sides must get the same number of sticks.)
  - Note: Only 4, 8 and 12 work. A square has 4 equal sides, so the sticks must share out equally among 4 sides.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the yes and no list. 2. Ask your child what they notice about the numbers that work. 3. Ask them to guess the next number after 12.  
  *Parent says:* Which numbers worked? What do you notice about them? What would come next?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the numbers that worked. What comes next after 12?
  - Ask: What comes after 4, 8, 12? (good: 16.; It goes up by 4 each time.)

## Worksheets
### Name the Shape (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these shapes has 3 straight sides?  
   Options: Circle / Triangle / Square  
   **Answer:** Triangle. *Why:* A triangle has 3 straight sides. A circle has none, and a square has 4.
2. (fill) A triangle has ____ corners.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Put a bean on each corner of any triangle. You always need 3 beans.
3. (fill) Count the triangles in the picture. There are ____ triangles.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Three shapes have 3 straight sides and 3 corners: one points up, one points down and one leans. The square, the circle and the rectangle are not triangles.
4. (truefalse) An upside-down triangle is still a triangle.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Turning a shape does not change its sides or corners. It still has 3 of each.
5. (mcq) Which shape has no corners?  
   Options: Circle / Triangle / Rectangle  
   **Answer:** Circle. *Why:* A circle has one curved edge and no corners. A triangle has 3 corners and a rectangle has 4.
6. (sort) Sort each thing by the shape of its flat face: circle or rectangle?  
   **Answer:** dosa → Circle; door → Rectangle; bangle → Circle; ten-rupee note → Rectangle; coin → Circle; phone screen → Rectangle. *Why:* The dosa, the bangle and the coin have a curved edge and no corners. The door, the note and the phone screen have 4 straight sides and 4 corners.
7. (match) Match each shape to its sides and corners.  
   **Answer:** Triangle → 3 sides, 3 corners; Square → 4 sides, all the same length; Rectangle → 2 long sides, 2 short sides; Circle → no corners at all. *Why:* Count the sides and corners of each shape. For the square and the rectangle, also compare how long the sides are.
8. (fill) Two squares lie on the table. How many corners altogether? ____  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* Each square has 4 corners. 4 and 4 more make 8.
9. (truefalse) A rectangle has 4 corners.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Put a bean on each corner of a postcard or a door picture. You need 4 beans.
10. (draw) Draw a triangle that points down. Put a dot on each corner.  
   **Answer:** A closed shape with 3 straight sides, pointing down, with 3 dots on its corners.. *Why:* A strong drawing has 3 straight sides that join up, and exactly 3 dots. It is a triangle whichever way it points.
11. (draw) Draw one long thin rectangle and one short wide rectangle.  
   **Answer:** Two closed shapes, each with 4 straight sides and 4 corners like a book's. One is clearly longer and thinner.. *Why:* Both are rectangles. Being long or short does not change the 4 sides and 4 corners.
12. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. How do you check that a shape is a triangle?  
   **Answer:** I count 3 straight sides and 3 corners.. *Why:* A strong answer talks about counting sides and corners, and says the sides must be straight. It does not talk about the look.
### Check the Sides (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (fill) Count the rectangles in the picture. There are ____ rectangles.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Two rectangles lie down and one stands up. All three have 4 straight sides and 4 corners. Standing up does not change the shape.
2. (truefalse) A shape with 3 corners is always a triangle.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A slice of a round cake has 3 corners, but one of its sides is curved. A triangle needs 3 straight sides.
3. (mcq) A shape has 4 sides, all the same length, and 4 corners like a book's. What is it?  
   Options: A square / A door-shaped rectangle / A triangle  
   **Answer:** A square. *Why:* 4 equal sides and 4 corners like a book's make a square. A door-shaped rectangle has 2 long sides and 2 short sides.
4. (fill) Two triangles lie on the table. How many sides altogether? ____  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* Each triangle has 3 sides. 3 and 3 more make 6.
5. (order) Put these shapes in order, fewest corners first.  
   **Answer:** ['Circle', 'Triangle', 'Square']. *Why:* A circle has 0 corners, a triangle has 3 and a square has 4.
6. (sort) Triangle or not a triangle?  
   **Answer:** 3 straight sides, very thin → Triangle; 3 corners, one curved side → Not a triangle; 4 straight sides → Not a triangle; 3 straight sides, pointing down → Triangle; 3 straight sides, tiny → Triangle; round, with no corners → Not a triangle. *Why:* A triangle needs 3 straight sides and 3 corners. Being thin, tiny or upside down does not matter. A curved side or a fourth side does.
7. (mcq) You turn each shape on its own outline. Which one fits at every turn?  
   Options: A square / A rectangle / A circle  
   **Answer:** A circle. *Why:* A circle is the same all the way round, so it fits however far you turn it. A square fits 4 times in a full turn.
8. (fill) A triangle, a rectangle and a circle. How many corners altogether? ____  
   **Answer:** ['7', 'seven']. *Why:* The triangle has 3 corners, the rectangle has 4 and the circle has 0. 3 and 4 and 0 make 7.
9. (draw) Draw a square. Then draw the same square standing on one corner.  
   **Answer:** Two shapes with 4 equal sides each. The second is turned so that a corner points up.. *Why:* Both drawings are squares. Turning a square does not change its 4 equal sides or its 4 corners.
10. (draw) Draw a long thin triangle. Put a dot on each corner.  
   **Answer:** A closed shape with 3 straight sides, much longer than it is wide, with 3 dots.. *Why:* A strong drawing still has exactly 3 straight sides and 3 corners. Thin triangles are triangles.
11. (write) Say aloud: a round dosa is cut from the middle into slices. Is one slice a triangle? Why?  
   **Answer:** No. The outside edge of the slice is curved.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a triangle needs 3 straight sides, and that the edge of the dosa is curved.
12. (perspective) A square piece of paper is turned to stand on one corner.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. It says that the 4 equal sides and 4 corners did not change, and also that people do call this look a diamond.. *Why:* Nothing about the paper changed, so in maths it is still a square. 'Diamond' is an everyday name for the way it now looks.
### Shape Puzzles (challenge) (challenge, 12 questions)
1. (fill) A kolam has 9 dots. You join the outside dots with straight lines. The shape has ____ sides.  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* The line goes along the top, down one side, along the bottom and up the other side. That makes 4 sides, and the shape is a square.
2. (fill) Now join the middle dot to its 4 neighbours. Count the big square too. There are ____ squares.  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* There are 4 small squares inside, and 1 big square round the outside. 4 and 1 make 5.
3. (truefalse) 8 sticks of the same length can make a square.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Put 2 sticks on each of the 4 sides. All 4 sides are then the same length.
4. (mcq) This goes past Class 1. One cell of a honeycomb has 6 straight sides. How many corners does it have?  
   Options: 5 / 6 / 7  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* Each time two sides meet, there is one corner. A closed shape with 6 straight sides has 6 corners.
5. (fill) An auto picture has 2 circle wheels and 1 rectangle body. How many corners altogether? ____  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* Each circle has 0 corners. The rectangle has 4. 0 and 0 and 4 make 4.
6. (order) Put one bean on every corner. Put these in order, fewest beans first.  
   **Answer:** ['3 circles', '1 square', '2 triangles', '2 rectangles']. *Why:* 3 circles need 0 beans. 1 square needs 4. 2 triangles need 3 and 3, which is 6. 2 rectangles need 4 and 4, which is 8.
7. (match) Match each thing to the shape of its flat face.  
   **Answer:** samosa → triangle; carrom board → square; chapati → circle; door → rectangle. *Why:* Trace each one in your mind. A samosa's face has 3 corners. A carrom board has 4 equal sides. A chapati has no corners. A door has 2 long sides and 2 short sides.
8. (sort) Each shape has 4 corners like a book's. The numbers are its sides in finger-widths. Square or not?  
   **Answer:** 4, 4, 4, 4 → Square; 5, 2, 5, 2 → Not a square; 3, 3, 3, 3 → Square; 6, 1, 6, 1 → Not a square; 2, 2, 2, 2 → Square; 7, 3, 7, 3 → Not a square. *Why:* If all 4 numbers are the same, the shape is a square. If there are 2 long sides and 2 short sides, it is a rectangle that is not a square.
9. (truefalse) A real dosa can be a perfect circle.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every real dosa has small bumps at its edge. It can be very close to a circle, but never perfect.
10. (draw) Draw a house or an auto using only circles, triangles, squares and rectangles. Write how many of each.  
   **Answer:** A picture built only from the four shapes, with a correct count of each.. *Why:* A strong drawing uses the shapes on purpose, such as circles for wheels. The numbers written match the picture.
11. (write) Say aloud: is the full Moon a circle or a ball? Why?  
   **Answer:** The Moon is a ball. From far away we see only its outline, which is a circle.. *Why:* A strong answer says the Moon is round like a ball, and that a ball looks like a flat circle from far away.
12. (write) Say aloud: can 6 sticks of the same length make a square? Why?  
   **Answer:** No. A square has 4 equal sides, and 6 sticks cannot be shared equally among 4 sides.. *Why:* A strong answer says that every side of a square must get the same number of sticks. 4 sticks work and 8 work, but 6 leave two sides longer.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is this shape?  
   Options: Triangle / Square / Circle  
   **Answer:** Triangle. *Why:* It has 3 straight sides and 3 corners, so it is a triangle. Pointing down does not change that.
2. (fill) How many corners does a square have? ____  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* A square has 4 corners. Put a bean on each one and count.
3. (mcq) Which of these is NOT a triangle?  
   Options: 3 straight sides, very thin / 3 straight sides, upside down / 3 corners, one curved side  
   **Answer:** 3 corners, one curved side. *Why:* A triangle needs 3 straight sides. The shape with a curved side fails, even with 3 corners. Thin and upside-down triangles pass.
4. (fill) How many triangles are in this picture? ____  
   **Answer:** ['5', 'five']. *Why:* Five shapes have 3 straight sides and 3 corners. They point different ways. The circle is the only shape that is not a triangle.
5. (mcq) A shape has 4 corners like a book's. Two sides are long and two are short. What is it?  
   Options: A square / A rectangle / A circle  
   **Answer:** A rectangle. *Why:* 4 corners with 2 long sides and 2 short sides make a rectangle. A square would have all 4 sides the same.
6. (truefalse) All 4 sides of a square are the same length.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. That is what makes a square different from a door-shaped rectangle. You can check with a marked strip of paper.
7. (mcq) Which of these is a circle?  
   Options: The outline of an egg / The rim of a tumbler / A cricket ball  
   **Answer:** The rim of a tumbler. *Why:* The rim of a tumbler is flat and the same all the way round. An egg's outline is longer one way. A cricket ball is round but not flat.
8. (mcq) How many corners does a circle have?  
   Options: 0 / 1 / 2  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* None. A circle has one curved edge. Your finger never has to turn a corner.
9. (mcq) Which thing has a flat face shaped like a rectangle?  
   Options: A ten-rupee note / A bangle / A samosa  
   **Answer:** A ten-rupee note. *Why:* A ten-rupee note has 4 straight sides and 4 corners. A bangle is a circle, and a samosa's face is a triangle.
10. (truefalse) A tiny triangle and a huge triangle are both triangles.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Size is not part of the rule. Both have 3 straight sides and 3 corners.
11. (mcq) A square of paper is turned to stand on one corner. What has changed?  
   Options: The number of sides / The number of corners / Only the way it faces  
   **Answer:** Only the way it faces. *Why:* Only the way it faces. It still has 4 equal sides and 4 corners, so it is still a square. People agreed that the name goes by sides and corners.
12. (fill) Three triangles lie on the floor. How many corners altogether? ____  
   **Answer:** ['9', 'nine']. *Why:* Each triangle has 3 corners. 3 and 3 and 3 make 9.

## Applications
- A tailor cuts a blouse piece into rectangles and triangles before stitching them together.
- A carpenter checks that a door frame is a true rectangle, or the door will not shut.
- A potter's wheel spins, so every pot that comes off it is round.
- Road signs use shape to speak fast: a circle gives an order, a triangle warns, a rectangle tells you where things are.
- A mason lays rectangular bricks because they fit side by side with no gaps.
- Builders of electricity towers and bridges join metal bars into triangles, because a triangle does not bend out of shape.
- A kolam artist starts from a grid of dots and joins them into squares, triangles and curves.
- A cricket ground keeper marks the pitch as a long rectangle with straight white lines.

## Spotted around us
- A dosa on the tawa is close to a circle. Fold it in half and the fold is a straight side.
- A samosa or a folded paper packet of sundal has triangle faces. Count the corners before you eat.
- A carrom board is a square. Its coins are circles, and so are the four pockets.
- From the front, a temple gopuram looks a little like a tall triangle. But its top is cut off flat, so the outline has 4 sides, not 3.
- Cut a ladies' finger across. The slice is not a circle. Count its corners. Many have five.
- An auto-rickshaw has circle wheels, a rectangle number plate and a windscreen that is nearly a rectangle.
- Strings of triangle flags hang across streets at festivals. Every flag hangs with its point down.
- The full Moon looks like a circle. A few nights later it does not. The Moon itself is always a ball.

## Open questions
- **Is a perfect circle a real thing, or an idea that minds make?** Thinkers have argued about this for more than two thousand years. No test has settled it.
- **Are we born with a sense of shape, or do we learn it from the things around us?** Tests with people who have little schooling suggest that some of it comes without teaching. Scientists do not agree on how much.
- **How do honeybees make the six sides of each cell so even?** Some scientists say the bees shape every wall themselves. Others say that warm wax also flows and pulls the walls straight. In 2016 a review said the question was still not settled.
- **What does a pufferfish sense or know as he makes his circle?** Scientists can watch what he does and measure the circle. Nobody has found a way to ask him.

## Connected maths
- Meena puts one bean on every corner of 2 triangles and 1 square. How many beans does she need? **10 beans.**. Each triangle has 3 corners, and the square has 4. 3 and 3 make 6. 6 and 4 more make 10. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
