# Will, might, won't: talking about chance
`math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L1`

**Summary.** Close your eyes and pick one folded chit from a cup. Will it be red? For one cup the answer is 'it will'. For another it is 'it won't'. For a third cup nobody can tell, and the answer is 'it might'. You will test three cups, toss a coin like a cricket captain, and sort the things in your day into will, might and won't. You will also find out that 'might' can change when someone peeks.

**Intro.** *You pick one chit without looking. From which cup will you get red for sure?* Cup A. Every chit in cup A is red, so you will pick red. Cup C has no red chit, so you won't pick red. Cup B has both colours. From cup B you might pick red, and you might not. Nobody can tell before you pick. In this unit you will test all three cups yourself. You will learn three words for what comes next: will, might and won't.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say if a thing will happen, might happen or won't happen.
1. I can give the reason, by looking at everything that can come out.
2. I can say that when a thing might happen, nobody can tell what comes this time.
3. I can change a cup so that 'won't' becomes 'might', or 'might' becomes 'will'.
4. I can use will, might and won't for things in my day, like the Sun, rain and dinner.
5. I can say that 'might' depends on what a person knows.

## Terms
- **Chance**: Chance is about things that can happen in more than one way, when we cannot tell which way it will go this time. *e.g.* You toss a coin. It can land heads or tails. Which one you get is chance.
- **Will**: It is sure to happen. Grown-ups also say 'certain'. *e.g.* Every chit in the cup is red. You will pick a red chit.
- **Won't**: It cannot happen. Grown-ups also say 'impossible'. *e.g.* A dice has the numbers 1 to 6. You won't roll a 7.
- **Might**: It can happen, and it can also not happen. We cannot tell which this time. Another word is 'maybe'. *e.g.* The cup has red chits and blue chits. You might pick red.
- **Sure**: You are sure when there is no other way it can go. *e.g.* You are sure the Sun will set this evening.
- **Guess**: To say what you think will happen before you find out. A good guess comes with a reason. *e.g.* You say, 'I think it will be blue, because I see only blue chits.'
- **Pick without looking**: To take one thing out with your eyes shut, so that you do not know which one you will get. *e.g.* You shut your eyes, shake the cup and take out one chit.
- **Chit**: A small folded piece of paper with something marked inside. *e.g.* Families often write names on chits and pick one to choose whose turn it is.
- **Toss**: To flip a coin into the air and see which side lands up: heads or tails. *e.g.* Before a cricket match, the two captains toss a coin.
- **Likely**: This word goes past Class 1. A thing is likely when it might happen and usually does. *e.g.* The cup has 5 red chits and 1 blue chit. Red is likely.

## Explanations
### Three words for what comes next · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing will happen if it is sure. It won't happen if it cannot happen. It might happen if it can go either way.

Look at the three cups in the picture. Cup A has only red chits. You pick one without looking, and it will be red. Cup C has only blue chits, so your chit won't be red. Cup B has both colours. Your chit might be red. Now try it with a plate of only idlis. Will you pick an idli?
- e.g. The Sun will set this evening.
- e.g. It might rain today.
- e.g. You won't wake up taller than a coconut tree.
### Look at everything that can come out · *defined*
**Definition:** First find everything that can come out. If all of them are what you want, it will happen. If none of them is, it won't. If only some are, it might.

Here is a worked example. A dice has six faces: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. Nothing else can come up. Will you roll a number smaller than 7? All six faces are smaller than 7, so you will. No face shows 8, so you won't roll an 8. Will you roll a 3? One face shows 3 and five faces do not, so you might.
- e.g. A coin has two sides. It might land heads. It won't land on a 5.
- e.g. A box has only laddus. You will pick a laddu.
- e.g. A spinner that is red all over will stop on red.
### Might means nobody can tell this time · *defined*
**Definition:** When a thing might happen, you cannot tell what you will get on the next try. You find out only by trying.

From the mixed cup, red came on some picks and blue on others. The order was jumbled. The picture shows one child's ten picks. Yours will look different. You put the chit back each time, so the cup is just the same on every pick. If you got blue three times, the next pick still might be blue.
- e.g. A coin landed tails twice. The next toss might be tails again.
- e.g. Two children test the same cup. Their picks come in a different order.
- e.g. You say 'it might rain'. Then you watch the sky to find out.
### One chit can change the answer · *defined*
**Definition:** Will, might and won't depend on what is in the cup. Change what is in the cup, and the answer can change.

A cup has 6 blue chits. You won't pick red. Now drop in one red chit. Red might come. One chit was enough to turn 'won't' into 'might'. To make it 'will', every chit must be red. So you take out all the blue chits. Shaking the cup harder or wishing changes nothing.
- e.g. A plate has only dosas. Put one idli on it. Now you might pick the idli.
- e.g. A week with no holiday: add one, and a day off might be tomorrow.
- e.g. A spinner is white all over. Colour one part red. Now it might stop on red.
### Might for you, sure for someone else · *debated*
The grown-up picked a chit from the mixed cup and peeked. For you, the chit might be red. For the grown-up it was sure. It was the same chit. So 'might' can depend on what a person knows. Is every 'might' like this? People do not agree.
- e.g. Amma has wrapped your birthday present. For you it might be a book. Amma is sure.
- e.g. The weather office has pictures of the clouds from space. They know more about today's rain than you do.
- e.g. Before a toss, nobody knows how the coin will land. Not even the one who tosses.
- **Pierre-Simon Laplace, a French mathematician, in 1814:** He wrote that someone who knew everything about the world at one moment would be unsure of nothing. So 'might' only shows what we do not know yet.
- **Many physicists today:** They say some things are a real 'might'. Take one tiny atom of the metal uranium. It can break apart at any moment. They say nobody could tell when, even with all the facts.
### Will, might and won't in the sky · *defined*
**Definition:** Some things in the sky are sure. Others are a 'might' that even experts cannot turn into 'will'.

The Sun will rise tomorrow. The Earth is a big ball that turns round once every day, and it does not stop. Will you see the Sun rise? You might, because clouds can hide it. Will it rain tomorrow? In India the weather office studies the clouds and the wind. Even so, its reports often say rain is 'likely' or 'very likely'. Those are careful ways of saying 'might'.
- e.g. Night will come after this day.
- e.g. You might see a shooting star tonight.
- e.g. The Moon won't fall into your garden.
### Other living beings weigh chances too · *debated*
In 2020 two scientists, Amalia Bastos and Alex Taylor, tested six kea. The kea had learned that a black token gets a treat and an orange token gets nothing. Then they saw two jars, one with more black tokens and one with more orange. A person took one token from each jar and hid each in a closed hand. The kea had to touch one hand. They mostly chose the hand from the jar with more black tokens. Scientists do not agree on how much this tells us.
- e.g. The kea could not see the hidden token. They could only see what was in each jar.
- e.g. It is like choosing between a cup with 5 red chits and a cup with 1 red chit.
- e.g. Only six kea were tested, and all six live in a wildlife park.
- **The scientists who did the test:** The kea looked at what was in each jar and picked the better chance. That is thinking about 'might', and they did it without words.
- **More careful scientists:** Six trained birds are very few. Nobody has yet shown how kea in the wild use this. It is safer to say that these six kea chose the better jar in this test.
### Stretch: likely and unlikely · *defined*
**Definition:** Not every 'might' is the same size. A thing is likely if it might happen and usually does. It is unlikely if it might happen but usually does not.

Here is a worked example. A cup has 5 red chits and 1 blue chit. Red might come, and blue might come. But there are many more red chits. So red comes much more often. We say red is likely and blue is unlikely. Unlikely is not 'won't'. The one blue chit is in the cup, and some day you pick it.
- e.g. In July in Kochi, rain is likely.
- e.g. In May in Chennai, rain is unlikely. It still might rain.
- e.g. A cup with 3 red and 3 blue: red and blue come about equally often.

## Mostly agreed
- If every chit in a cup is red, you will pick red every single time. *How we know:* You tested it with cup A. There was no other colour that could come out.
- If no chit in the cup is red, you won't pick red, however many times you try. *How we know:* You tested it with cup C. You cannot take out what is not there.
- When a cup has both colours, nobody can tell which colour comes next. *How we know:* Your ten picks from cup B came in a jumbled order. Cricket captains trust a coin toss for the same reason: neither of them can tell how it will land.
- Some birds choose the jar that gives them the better chance of a treat. *How we know:* In a test in 2020, six kea parrots mostly chose the hand that came from the jar with more treat tokens (Bastos and Taylor).

## How we found out
**Gerolamo Cardano, and later Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat, About 1565, and 1654.** For thousands of years people played with dice. Most of them thought that nothing could be worked out about dice. They said it was all luck. A hymn in the Rig Veda, about 3,000 years old, tells of a man who lost everything at dice. Around 1565 an Italian doctor, Gerolamo Cardano, wrote a small book about dice games. He began by listing everything the dice can show, and counted. His book was printed only in 1663, long after he died. In 1654 two French mathematicians, Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat, wrote letters to each other about a game that stops halfway. How should the players share the prize? They found a fair way by listing every way the game could go on. So a 'might' can be studied. The first step is the one you took with the cups: look at everything that can come out.  
*What people believed before:* Nothing can be worked out about dice, because it is all luck. Cardano, Pascal and Fermat showed this was wrong. They listed everything that could happen, counted, and got answers that others could check.

## Challenging perspectives
- **I got blue three times, so red must come next.** (Many children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels as if red is waiting for its turn.  
  Against: You put the chit back each time. So the cup is just the same on every pick. The next pick might be blue again.
- **If a thing has never happened, it won't ever happen.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: If something has never been seen in a very long time, it is fair to guess it will not happen tomorrow.  
  Against: Long ago, people in Europe had seen only white swans. A Roman poet, Juvenal, wrote of 'a rare bird, like a black swan'. He meant a thing nobody had ever seen. In 1697 a Dutch captain, Willem de Vlamingh, and his crew saw black swans on a river in Australia. 'Never seen' was not the same as 'won't'.
- **Nothing is really 'might'. If we knew everything, we would always say will or won't.** (Pierre-Simon Laplace, a French mathematician, in 1814; *debated*)  
  For: In the Closed Hand step, the one who peeked was sure. More knowing made the 'might' go away.  
  Against: Many physicists today say some things cannot be known ahead by anyone, such as the moment when one atom of uranium breaks apart.

## Roots
We picked chits from cups and said will, might and won't. Now let us think about what 'might' really tells us.

**Deep truth.** In the Three Cups Test, a grown-up picked a chit, peeked, and closed their hand. For you, the chit might be red. For the grown-up it was sure, because they had seen it. It was the same chit at the same time. So 'might' also tells what a person knows.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think 'might' is inside the thing, like its colour. But the chit in the hand was already red or blue. Only your knowing was missing.  
*Evidence:* In the Closed Hand step of the Three Cups Test, one chit was a 'might' for you and sure for the one who peeked.

- *who am i:* You do not know what tomorrow will bring. You still pack your bag and take your umbrella. You live with 'might' every day.
- *what is life:* Other living beings meet 'might' too. In a test, kea parrots chose the hand that had the better chance of holding a treat.
- *what is this world:* A few things in the world are sure, like the Sun rising. Many more are a 'might', like rain.

Before the hand opens, the chit could be red or it could be blue. Many things might be. Then the hand opens, and there is one colour. Every moment is like this. Out of all that might happen, one thing does happen.

- **Rig Veda 10.129.7:** This old hymn asks where the world came from. It ends by saying that the one who watches from the highest sky knows. Or maybe even he does not know. (The hymn does not pretend to be sure. It ends on a 'might'. Saying 'might' when you do not know is the honest answer.)
- **Tao Te Ching 71:** The Tao Te Ching, an old Chinese book, says: to know that you do not know is best. (In the Closed Hand step you had not seen the chit. You said 'it might be red'. You knew that you did not know.)
- **Rig Veda 10.34.13:** In this hymn, a man has lost everything by playing dice. He is told: do not play with dice. Plough your field. (About three thousand years ago, people already knew that nobody can tell how dice will fall. A dice is a 'might' that no wishing can turn into a 'will'.)
- Maths (settled): In a test in 2020, six kea parrots mostly chose the closed hand that came from the jar with more treat tokens. They could not see the token in the hand. They used what they knew about the jar (Bastos and Taylor, 2020).
- Maths (open): Is anything in the world a real 'might', even for someone who knows everything? Many physicists say yes. The question is still argued about.
- Other view, Many physicists today: They say some 'mights' are real, and have nothing to do with what a person knows. Take one tiny atom of the metal uranium. It can break apart at any moment. They say nobody could tell when, even someone who knew everything. If that is so, 'might' is in the world itself.
- Practice, The closed hand (3 min): Sit still. I will put one chit in your hand. Close your hand and do not look. Take three slow breaths. You do not know the colour. Notice how that feels in your body. Is it itchy? Is it fun? Now open your hand slowly and look. What changed just now: the chit, or you?
- Ask: When someone peeks at the chit, does the chit change, or does the person change?
- Ask: Is there anything you are fully sure of? How do you know?
- Ask: If someone knew everything, would they ever say 'might'?
- Ask: How does not knowing feel? Is it a bad feeling or a good one?

## Story: The Mat by the Door
*Ammu, Unni and Ammachi are made up. The monsoon really does reach Kerala around the start of June most years, and pappadams really are dried in the sun.*

It was the first week of June in Ammu's village near Alappuzha. Everyone was waiting for the monsoon.

Every morning Ammu ran into the yard and asked the same question. "Ammachi, will it rain today?"

Her grandmother looked at the sky for a long time. Then she always said the same thing. "It might."

"That is not an answer," said Unni. Unni was Ammu's cousin. He was nine, and he liked answers that were short and sure.

On Monday the clouds were grey and low. "It will rain today. Sure," said Unni. He carried his umbrella to school and back. Not one drop fell.

On Tuesday the sky was blue from end to end. "It won't rain today. Sure," said Unni. He left his umbrella at home. At four o'clock a cloud came in from the sea, and he walked home wet to the skin.

Ammachi said nothing. She only moved the umbrella stand a little closer to the door.

That night Ammu lay on her mat and thought. Unni said will, and it did not rain. Unni said won't, and it rained. Ammachi said might on both days, and on both days Ammachi was right. Was 'might' just a trick for never being wrong?

On Wednesday Ammachi had to go to the ration shop. Before she left, she rolled out forty pappadams, round and thin and pale. She gave Ammu a job.

"These must dry in the sun till I come back. A wet pappadam is a spoiled pappadam. Look after them."

Unni looked up. The sky was blue again. "It won't rain," he said. "Put them in the middle of the yard, where the sun is strongest. Then come and play." He had made a paper kite that morning. He left it on the yard wall for the glue to dry, and went down the lane to find string.

Ammu stood with the tray in her hands. She looked at the sky too. Then she tried to sort things, the way she sorted her beads into cups.

"The sun will go down this evening. That is sure. The pappadams won't dry inside the dark kitchen. That is sure too. And the rain?"

She could not put the rain in either cup. It was not a will. It was not a won't. However long she looked at the sky, the rain stayed in the middle.

"So I must be ready for rain, and ready for no rain," she said slowly. "Both at the same time."

She did not carry the tray to the middle of the yard. She dragged Ammachi's big grass mat to the sunny patch right beside the kitchen door. She laid the forty pappadams on it. Then she sat down on the step next to the mat.

Unni came back for his slippers and laughed. "You are guarding pappadams under a blue sky!"

"The sky might change," said Ammu.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the true one," said Ammu.

An hour went by. The pappadams began to curl at the edges. Then the wind turned around. It smelled of wet earth. The coconut leaves started to hiss. One fat drop hit the step.

Ammu took hold of two corners of the mat and pulled. One pull, and forty pappadams slid in through the kitchen door, dry. Then the rain came down so hard that the yard turned white.

Unni came running up the lane with the string in his fist. His kite was still on the wall, in the middle of the rain. When he reached it, it was two sticks and some wet paper.

They sat in the doorway and watched the water pour off the roof.

"How did you know it would rain?" asked Unni.

"I did not know it would rain," said Ammu. "I did not know it wouldn't. That is why the mat was by the door."

Ammachi came home under her umbrella. She counted the pappadams. Forty. She looked at the mat, and at the door, and at Ammu.

"Who told you to put the mat there?"

"Nobody. You say 'might' every day. So I thought about what to do with a might."

The next morning Unni was in the yard first. He looked at the sky for a long time. Then he turned around.

"Ammu," he said, "will it rain today?"

"It might."

Unni nodded, and went in to get his umbrella.

Ammu took her paper boat to the drain by the gate. After yesterday, there was one thing she could say for sure. There would be water in it.

**For the parent:** For the parent: 'might' is not a weak answer or a trick. It is the true answer when a thing can go either way. Ammu sorts what she knows into will, won't and might. Because rain is a 'might', she gets ready for both rain and sun. Unni keeps turning a 'might' into 'will' or 'won't', and is caught out each time.

- Ask: Unni said 'it will rain' and 'it won't rain'. Why was he wrong both times?
- Ask: Which things was Ammu sure about? Which thing could she not be sure about?
- Ask: Why did Ammu put the mat right beside the door?
- Ask: What is a 'might' in your day tomorrow? How could you be ready for both ways?

### g1 · Call It! (10 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the 12 cards. Put them in a pile with the sentences facing up. Put a heap of beans beside the pile.; On your turn, read the top card aloud, or ask a grown-up to read it. Call 'will', 'might' or 'won't'.; Turn the card over to check. If your call was right, keep the card. If not, slide it under the pile.; Then roll the dice once. If what the card said came true on your roll, take one bean.; The game ends when the pile is gone. Count one for each card and one for each bean. The biggest count wins.; Playing alone: count how many of the 12 cards you call right the first time. Play again and try to beat that.
- **Printable cards: 12 Call It cards. The sentence is on the front and the answer is on the back.:** You will roll a 7. (back: Won't. A dice goes only from 1 to 6.); You will roll a number smaller than 7. (back: Will. Every face is smaller than 7.); You will roll a 3. (back: Might. One face shows 3. Five faces do not.); You will roll a 0. (back: Won't. No face has zero dots.); You will roll 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. (back: Will. Those are all the faces.); You will roll a 6. (back: Might. One face shows 6.); You will roll a number bigger than 2. (back: Might. 3, 4, 5 and 6 are bigger. 1 and 2 are not.); You will roll a number bigger than 6. (back: Won't. 6 is the biggest face.); You will roll a number bigger than 0. (back: Will. Every face is bigger than 0.); You will roll a 1. (back: Might. One face shows 1.); You will roll a 4 or a 5. (back: Might. Two faces fit. Four faces do not.); You will roll a 10. (back: Won't. No face shows 10.)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 12 cards, or copy them on slips of paper with the answer on the back. 2. Shuffle them. Pile them with the sentences up. 3. Put the dice and the beans beside the pile.  
  *Parent says:* Each card says something about the next roll. You call will, might or won't.  
  *Child mode:* Pile the cards with the sentences up. Put the dice and the beans near.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take one practice turn each. 2. Read the top card. Call. Turn it over to check. 3. Roll once and see if the card came true.  
  *Parent says:* My card says: you will roll a 3. I call 'might'. Now I check, and then I roll.  
  *Child mode:* Read the top card. Call will, might or won't. Turn it over. Then roll.
  - Note: A 'might' card sometimes comes true and sometimes does not. A 'will' card comes true on every roll, and a 'won't' card never does. Let your child notice this.
  - Check: A card says 'You will roll a 3'. You roll a 5. Was 'might' the right call? → **Yes**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play until the pile is gone. 2. Now and then ask which faces would make the card come true.  
  *Parent says:* Which faces make this card come true? Point to them on the dice.  
  *Child mode:* Play the game. Keep each card you call right. Take a bean when a card comes true.
  - Ask: Which kind of card gives you a bean every time? (good: The will cards.; The ones that are sure.)
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Each player counts their cards and beans. 2. Sort all 12 cards into three heaps by their answers: will, might, won't.  
  *Parent says:* Count your cards and beans. Now let us put all the cards into three heaps.  
  *Child mode:* Count your cards and beans. Then sort the cards: will, might, won't.
### g2 · Red Road, Blue Road (10 min)
- **rules:** You need the red and blue chits from the Three Cups Test, one cup, two buttons and some beans.; One player owns the Red road. The other owns the Blue road. Put a button on each Start.; Take turns to be the cup maker. The cup maker puts any 6 chits in the cup and shows them.; The other player is the caller. The caller looks and calls: 'Red will win', 'Red might win' or 'Red won't win'.; Now race. Pick a chit without looking. Red chit: the Red button moves one square. Blue chit: the Blue button moves one. Put the chit back and shake.; The first button to reach Home wins the race. Its owner takes a bean. The caller takes a bean if the call was right. 'Might' is right if the cup had both colours.; Play 4 races. The player with the most beans wins.; Playing alone: put 3 red and 3 blue in the cup. Guess which road wins. Race. Then try 5 red and 1 blue.
- **Printable board: Two roads side by side, each with a Start square, 4 squares and a Home square:** Red road: Start; Red 1; Red 2; Red 3; Red 4; Red Home; Blue road: Start; Blue 1; Blue 2; Blue 3; Blue 4; Blue Home

  *Parent does:* 1. Draw two roads of 6 squares each, side by side. Colour one red and one blue. 2. Put a button on each Start. 3. Choose who owns Red and who owns Blue.

  *Child mode:* Draw two roads with 6 squares each. Put a button on each Start.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For a practice race, put 3 red and 3 blue chits in the cup. 2. Ask your child to call. 3. Race: pick, move, put back, shake.  
  *Parent says:* Three red and three blue are in the cup. Will Red win, might Red win, or won't Red win?  
  *Child mode:* The cup has 3 red and 3 blue. Call it. Then pick chits and race.
  - Check: The cup has 6 red chits and no blue. Which road wins the race? → **Red will win**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play 4 races. Swap the cup maker each time. 2. Let your child find out what happens when the cup maker uses only their own colour.  
  *Parent says:* You are the cup maker now. Put in any six chits you like, and show me.  
  *Child mode:* Play 4 races. Take turns to fill the cup. Call before each race.
  - Ask: You put in only red. Was that race fair to Blue? (good: No. Blue could not move.; Blue had no chance.)
  - Note: Sooner or later one of you fills the cup with one colour. Enjoy it. Then ask what cup would give both roads a chance.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the beans. 2. Ask which cup made the most exciting race.  
  *Parent says:* Count your beans. Which cup gave the best race? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your beans. Which cup made the best race?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Three Cups Test: will I pick red? (25 min)
- **question:** Before you pick, can you tell if the chit will be red? Does it depend on what is in the cup?
- **predict:** Look at what goes into each cup. Before you pick, say: I will pick red, I might pick red, or I won't pick red. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The chits are all the same size and folded the same way, so your fingers cannot tell them apart. You shut your eyes for every pick. You put the chit back and shake the cup before the next pick, so the cup is the same every time.
- **safety:** Keep small things such as chits, coins and beans away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** A grown-up draws the table. For each cup, write the guess first. Then draw a red or blue dot for each pick. At the end, the child says what they found, and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** Cup | What is inside | My guess: will, might or won't | What I picked
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tear the paper into 18 pieces, each about as big as a matchbox. 2. Your child draws a big red dot on 9 pieces and a big blue dot on 9. 3. Fold each piece twice, with the dot inside. 4. Check that no colour shows on the outside.  
  *Parent says:* We are making chits. Nine get a red dot and nine get a blue dot. Fold each one so the dot hides.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a red dot on 9 papers. Draw a blue dot on 9 papers. Fold each one two times.
  - Note: If the crayon shows through the paper, fold once more or use thicker paper. If you have a carrom board, 9 white and 9 black coins work as well. Then say white for red and black for blue.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child watch you put 6 red chits into cup A. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess. 3. Your child shuts their eyes, picks one chit, opens it, and draws a dot in the table. 4. Put the chit back and shake. Do this 6 times.  
  *Parent says:* Cup A has six red chits and nothing else. You will pick one without looking. Will it be red, might it be red, or won't it? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Cup A has only red chits. Guess: will you pick red? Now shut your eyes and pick. Do it 6 times.
  - Ask: Why did you guess that? (good: There are only red ones.; No blue is in the cup.; It has to be red.)
  - Note: Take any guess that comes with a reason. Do not say who is right. Let the six picks answer.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child watch you put 6 blue chits into cup C. 2. Ask for a guess about red, and a reason. Write the guess. 3. Your child picks 6 times with eyes shut, and draws a dot for each pick.  
  *Parent says:* Cup C has six blue chits and nothing else. Will you pick red, might you, or won't you? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Cup C has only blue chits. Guess: will you pick red? Shut your eyes and pick 6 times.
  - Ask: Could a red chit come out if you tried a hundred times? (good: No. There is no red in the cup.; You cannot pick what is not there.)
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child watch you put 3 red and 3 blue chits into cup B. 2. Ask for a guess about red, and a reason. Write the guess. 3. Before each pick, ask 'Red or blue this time?' 4. Your child picks 10 times with eyes shut, and draws a dot for each pick. Shake the cup each time.  
  *Parent says:* Cup B has three red and three blue. Will you pick red, might you, or won't you? Why? Before each pick, tell me the colour you think is coming.  
  *Child mode:* Cup B has red and blue chits. Guess: will you pick red? Pick 10 times. Each time, say the colour first.
  - Ask: Could you tell which colour was coming next? (good: No.; Sometimes I was right and sometimes wrong.; It kept changing.)
  - Note: Most children get red on some picks and blue on others, in a jumbled order. If all ten picks are the same colour, which is very rare, pick ten more.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick one chit from cup B yourself. 2. Open it so that only you can see, then close your hand over it. 3. Ask your child if the chit is red. 4. Then ask if YOU would say 'might'. 5. Show the chit. Swap places and do it again.  
  *Parent says:* I picked a chit and I peeked. Is my chit red: will, might or won't? Now tell me this. Am I unsure too?  
  *Child mode:* Your grown-up picks a chit and peeks. Is it red? Say will, might or won't. Does your grown-up say might too?
  - Ask: It is one chit. Why do you say 'might' when I am sure? (good: You saw it and I did not.; You know. I do not know.; Because you peeked.)
  - Note: Do not explain. The Roots card comes back to this moment. It is enough that your child sees you are sure and they are not.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 6 blue chits in a cup. 2. Ask your child to change the cup so that red MIGHT come, moving as few chits as they can. 3. Pick 10 times to see. 4. Then ask them to change the cup so that red WILL come. 5. Pick 6 times to check.  
  *Parent says:* This cup has six blue chits. Red won't come. Change the cup so that red might come. Move as few chits as you can.  
  *Child mode:* The cup has 6 blue chits. Change it so red might come. Then change it so red will come.
  - Ask: What is the smallest change that makes red a 'might'? (good: Put in one red chit.; Swap one blue for one red.)
  - Ask: What must you do so that red WILL come? (good: Take out all the blue ones.; Only red chits in the cup.)
  - Note: With one red chit among six blue, red may not come in ten picks. That is fine. Tip the cup out and show that the red chit is there. Red still might come.
  - Check: A cup has 6 blue chits. You drop in 1 red chit. Now, will you pick red? → **I might**
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child what they think, what they saw, and why. 3. Write their words under the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at our three cups. When could you be sure before you picked? When could you not be sure?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Which cup was a will? Which was a won't? Which was a might?
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why is it so? (good: Only red in the cup, so it will be red.; No red in the cup, so it won't be red.; With both colours I could not tell.; It depends on what is in the cup.)
  - Check: A cup has 2 red chits and 4 blue chits. You pick one without looking. Will it be red? → **It might**

### a1 · Jump to Your Answer: will, might or won't? (10 min)

  *Parent does:* Read each sentence. Your child jumps to a sheet and says why. 1. The Sun will set this evening. 2. A cow will fly past our window. 3. It will rain today. 4.   - Ask: Can the same sentence be a might in one place and nearly a will in another? (good: Yes. Rain in the monsoon is nearly sure.; It depends where you are and when.)
  - Check: Which of these will happen for sure? → **Night comes after today**
### a2 · Maths in sport: the cricket toss (10 min)
- **problem:** Two captains both want to choose. Why do they let a coin decide?
- **safety:** Keep small things such as chits, coins and beans away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Draw ten boxes in a row. For each toss, draw a tick if your call was right and a cross if it was wrong.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at both sides of the coin. The side with the lions is heads. The side with the number is tails. 2. Choose something real to decide, such as who picks tonight's story. 3. You toss. Your child calls while the coin is in the air. 4. Keep to what the coin says.  
  *Parent says:* Cricket captains toss a coin to see who chooses first. We will toss for who picks tonight's story. Call heads or tails!  
  *Child mode:* Find heads and tails on the coin. When the coin is in the air, call one. Did you win the toss?
  - Note: Keeping to the result matters. It shows your child that both sides accept a toss because neither can tell how it will land.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw ten boxes in a row. 2. Toss ten times. Your child calls each time before the coin lands. 3. Draw a tick for a right call and a cross for a wrong one. 4. Count the ticks.  
  *Parent says:* Now call ten tosses. Try as hard as you can to get every one right.  
  *Child mode:* Call ten tosses. Tick the box when you are right. How many ticks?
  - Ask: Did you find a way to get every call right? (good: No.; I got some right and some wrong.; You cannot tell.)
  - Note: Most children get between 3 and 7 right out of ten. Getting all ten right by luck is very rare.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the three questions below. 2. Ask why captains use a coin and do not just talk it over.  
  *Parent says:* Before a toss: will the coin show heads? Will it show heads or tails? Will it show a 6?  
  *Child mode:* Will the coin show heads? Will it show heads or tails? Will it show a 6?
  - Ask: Why do both captains trust the coin? (good: Nobody can tell how it lands.; It does not take sides.; Both have the same chance.)
  - Note: Heads is a might. Heads or tails is a will. A 6 is a won't. A coin can land on its edge, but that is so rare on a towel that we leave it out.
  - Check: You toss a coin once. Which of these will happen for sure? → **It shows heads or tails**
### a3 · Dice Talk: what can a dice show? (10 min)
- **problem:** To say will, might or won't about a dice, first find out everything the dice can show.
- **safety:** Keep small things such as chits, coins and beans away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Draw the six faces in a row. Under each face, make one mark every time that face comes up in 12 rolls.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child the dice. 2. Ask them to find every face and count the dots on each. 3. They draw the six faces in a row.  
  *Parent says:* Turn the dice around. How many faces does it have? What is on each face?  
  *Child mode:* Look at every face of the dice. Count the dots. Draw all six faces.
  - Ask: Is there a face with 7 dots? Is there a face with no dots? (good: No. It goes from 1 to 6.; The biggest is 6 and the smallest is 1.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Say each sentence. Your child looks at their drawing and says will, might or won't. 1. I will roll a 4. 2. I will roll a 7. 3. I will roll a number smaller than 7. 4. I will roll a 1 or a 2. 5. I will roll a 0. 6. I will roll 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your six faces. I say: I will roll a 7. Will I, might I, or won't I? How do you know?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your drawing. Your grown-up says a sentence. Say will, might or won't.
  - Note: Might: 1 and 4. Won't: 2 and 5. Will: 3 and 6. Ask 'Which faces make it true?' and let your child point at them.
  - Check: You roll one dice. Will you get a number smaller than 7? → **I will**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask: if we roll 12 times, will every face come up at least once? Take a guess and a reason. 2. Roll 12 times. Your child makes a mark under the face that comes up. 3. Look at which faces came often and which did not come.  
  *Parent says:* We will roll 12 times. Will every face come up? Will, might or won't? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Roll 12 times. Make a mark under the face that comes. Did every face come?
  - Ask: One face did not come at all. Does that mean it won't ever come? (good: No. It is on the dice.; It still might come next time.)
  - Note: It is a might. In 12 rolls, at least one face is usually missing. A missing face is still a might, because it is on the dice.

## Worksheets
### Will, Might, Won't (core) (core, 14 questions)
1. (mcq) Every chit in this cup is red. You pick one without looking. Will it be red?  
   Options: It will / It might / It won't  
   **Answer:** It will. *Why:* It will. Every chit in the cup is red, so no other colour can come out.
2. (truefalse) This cup has only blue chits. You pick one without looking. You won't pick red.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. There is no red chit in the cup. You cannot pick what is not there.
3. (mcq) Look at this cup. You pick one chit with your eyes shut. Will it be red?  
   Options: It will / It might / It won't  
   **Answer:** It might. *Why:* It might. The cup has red chits and blue chits, so either colour can come out.
4. (truefalse) The Sun will set this evening.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Earth keeps turning, so the Sun sets every evening. This is a will.
5. (sort) Sort these six things: will, might or won't?  
   **Answer:** The Sun rises tomorrow → Will; It rains today → Might; A cow flies past the window → Won't; A dropped spoon falls down → Will; You roll a 4 on a dice → Might; You roll a 9 on a dice → Won't. *Why:* The Sun rises every day, and dropped things fall, so those two are will.
Rain and a 4 on a dice can happen or not, so they are might.
Cows cannot fly and a dice has no 9, so those two are won't.
6. (fill) Count the faces of a dice. A dice has ____ faces.  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* A dice has 6 faces. They show 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 dots.
7. (mcq) You roll one dice. Which of these will you get for sure?  
   Options: A 6 / A number from 1 to 6 / A 7  
   **Answer:** A number from 1 to 6. *Why:* A dice shows only 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. So a number from 1 to 6 is sure. A 6 is only a might. A 7 is a won't.
8. (mcq) Every part of this spinner is shaded. Will it stop on a shaded part?  
   Options: It will / It might / It won't  
   **Answer:** It will. *Why:* It will. There is no white part to stop on.
9. (truefalse) No part of this spinner is shaded. It might stop on a shaded part.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. There is no shaded part to stop on. So this is a won't, not a might.
10. (truefalse) This cup has 3 red and 3 blue chits. You can tell which colour will come next.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both colours are in the cup, so either can come. Nobody can tell before the pick.
11. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** will → sure to happen; might → can happen or not happen; won't → cannot happen. *Why:* Will is for sure things. Won't is for things that cannot happen. Might is for everything in between.
12. (draw) Draw a cup of chits from which you won't pick blue.  
   **Answer:** Any cup with no blue chit in it, such as a cup of only red chits.. *Why:* If no blue chit is in the cup, blue cannot come out.
13. (draw) Draw a spinner that will stop on red for sure.  
   **Answer:** A circle with every part coloured red.. *Why:* If every part is red, the spinner has nowhere else to stop.
14. (write) Say it aloud. Cup A had only red chits. Why did you pick red every time?  
   **Answer:** Because every chit in cup A was red. There was nothing else to pick.. *Why:* A strong answer looks at everything in the cup. All of it was red, so red was sure.
### Look Inside, Then Say (stretch) (stretch, 13 questions)
1. (mcq) This cup has 1 red chit and 5 blue. You pick one without looking. Will it be red?  
   Options: It will / It might / It won't  
   **Answer:** It might. *Why:* It might. One red chit is in the cup, so red can come. Picking 'it won't' usually means that one chit seemed too few to count.
2. (mcq) This cup has 5 red chits and 1 blue. You pick one without looking. Will it be red?  
   Options: It will / It might / It won't  
   **Answer:** It might. *Why:* It might. Most chits are red, but one is blue, and blue can come. Picking 'it will' forgets the blue chit.
3. (truefalse) A cup has 6 blue chits. You add 1 red chit. Now red is a 'might'.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Before, red was a won't. One red chit is enough to turn it into a might.
4. (fill) This cup has 3 red and 3 blue. To make red a 'will', take out ____ blue chits.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three', 'all']. *Why:* Take out all 3 blue chits. Then only red chits are left, so nothing else can come out.
5. (truefalse) From a mixed cup you picked blue 3 times, putting it back each time. So the next pick will be red.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The cup is just the same on every pick. The next chit might be red, and it might be blue again.
6. (sort) You toss a coin once. Sort each one: will, might or won't?  
   **Answer:** It shows heads → Might; It shows heads or tails → Will; It shows a 5 → Won't; It shows tails → Might; It comes down again → Will. *Why:* Heads alone and tails alone are each a might.
One of the two must show, and the coin must come down, so those are will.
A coin has no 5, so that is a won't.
7. (mcq) One part of this spinner is shaded. Will it stop on the shaded part?  
   Options: It will / It might / It won't  
   **Answer:** It might. *Why:* It might. It can stop on the shaded part or on a white part.
8. (fill) This cup has 4 red chits. To make blue a 'might', add at least ____ blue chit.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* One blue chit is enough. Then blue can come out.
9. (order) Picking red: put the cups in order, from won't, to might, to will.  
   **Answer:** ['6 blue', '3 red and 3 blue', '6 red']. *Why:* With 6 blue, red won't come.
With both colours, red might come.
With 6 red, red will come.
10. (mcq) Amma peeks at the chit in her hand. You do not. Who says 'it might be red'?  
   Options: Only you / Only Amma / Both of you  
   **Answer:** Only you. *Why:* Only you. Amma has seen the chit, so she is sure. You have not seen it, so for you it is a might.
11. (draw) Draw a spinner with 4 parts that might stop on red.  
   **Answer:** A circle cut into 4 parts with 1, 2 or 3 parts coloured red.. *Why:* For a might, some parts must be red and some must not be red.
12. (write) Name one thing that will happen today, one that might, and one that won't.  
   **Answer:** For example: night will come, a friend might visit, I won't grow wings.. *Why:* A strong answer has a sure thing, a thing that can go either way, and a thing that cannot happen.
13. (perspective) The morning sky is clear and blue. Will it rain today?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. For example: it might rain, because the morning sky does not show the whole day. Meenakshi is right that a clear sky makes rain less likely.. *Why:* A clear morning is a clue, but it does not make rain impossible. For most days 'might' is the honest call.
### Chance Puzzles (challenge) (challenge, 14 questions)
1. (mcq) You roll one dice. Will you get a number bigger than 3?  
   Options: I will / I might / I won't  
   **Answer:** I might. *Why:* You might. The faces 4, 5 and 6 are bigger than 3. The faces 1, 2 and 3 are not.
2. (mcq) This goes past Class 1. From which cup is red more likely?  
   Options: Cup P / Cup Q / Both the same  
   **Answer:** Cup P. *Why:* Cup P. It has 5 red chits and cup Q has only 1. Red is a might in both cups, but it comes more often from cup P.
3. (truefalse) If a thing has never happened before, it won't ever happen.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. 'I have never seen it' is not the same as 'it cannot happen'. People in Europe had seen only white swans, until sailors saw black swans in Australia.
4. (mcq) Which change makes red a 'will'?  
   Options: Add 1 red chit / Take out all the blue chits / Shake the cup harder  
   **Answer:** Take out all the blue chits. *Why:* Take out all the blue chits. Then only red is left. Adding one red still leaves blue in the cup. Shaking changes nothing.
5. (mcq) Which change makes red a 'won't'?  
   Options: Add 1 blue chit / Shut your eyes tighter / Swap every red chit for a blue one  
   **Answer:** Swap every red chit for a blue one. *Why:* Swap every red chit for a blue one. Then no red is in the cup. Adding one blue makes red a might, not a won't.
6. (fill) A spinner has 4 parts, all shaded. For it to 'might stop on white', make at least ____ part white.  
   **Answer:** ['1', 'one']. *Why:* One white part is enough. Then the spinner can stop on white.
7. (sort) Sort these six: will, might or won't?  
   **Answer:** Tuesday comes after Monday → Will; The next toss is tails → Might; Ice left in the hot sun melts → Will; You grow taller than a coconut tree by tomorrow → Won't; A friend visits today → Might; A dice shows 0 → Won't. *Why:* The order of the days and ice melting in the sun are sure.
A toss and a visit can go either way.
Nobody grows that much in a day, and a dice has no 0.
8. (truefalse) This goes past Class 1. 'Might' always means it happens half the time.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. One red chit among six is a might. Five red chits among six is also a might. Red comes far more often from the second cup.
9. (truefalse) A cup has 1 red and 5 blue. In 10 picks, red never came. So red won't ever come.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The red chit is still in the cup, so red can come on the next pick. 'Might' never promised that red would come in ten picks.
10. (order) This goes past Class 1. Put the cups in order, from red least likely to red most likely.  
   **Answer:** ['1 red and 5 blue', '3 red and 3 blue', '5 red and 1 blue']. *Why:* Each cup has 6 chits.
The more of them are red, the more often red comes.
11. (fill) A cup has 6 chits. You will pick red for sure. So ____ of the chits are red.  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six', 'all']. *Why:* All 6. If even one chit were blue, blue might come, and red would not be sure.
12. (draw) This goes past Class 1. Draw a cup of 6 chits where red comes most, but blue still might.  
   **Answer:** A cup with 4 or 5 red chits and the rest blue.. *Why:* Blue must be in the cup to be a might. Red must be the bigger group to come most often.
13. (write) Say a 'might' sentence about tomorrow. Then change one thing to make it a 'will'.  
   **Answer:** For example: 'I might see Paatti tomorrow.' If we go to her house, 'I will see Paatti tomorrow.'. *Why:* A strong answer shows that a might turns into a will when the other way is taken away.
14. (perspective) Two thinkers argue about the word 'might'.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason from the unit. For example: the one who peeked at the chit was sure. Or: nobody can tell how a coin will land.. *Why:* This is a real argument with no agreed answer. Mathematicians and physicists have held both views.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) This cup has 4 chits. You pick one with your eyes shut. Will it be red?  
   Options: It will / It might / It won't  
   **Answer:** It will. *Why:* It will. All four chits are red, so only red can come out.
2. (mcq) You roll one dice. Will you roll an 8?  
   Options: I will / I might / I won't  
   **Answer:** I won't. *Why:* You won't. A dice shows only 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6.
3. (mcq) This cup has 4 red chits and 2 blue. Will you pick red?  
   Options: I will / I might / I won't  
   **Answer:** I might. *Why:* You might. Red and blue are both in the cup, so either can come out. Picking 'I will' forgets the blue chits.
4. (mcq) How do you find out if you will, might or won't pick red?  
   Options: Look at all the chits in the cup / Shake the cup very hard / Ask for your lucky colour  
   **Answer:** Look at all the chits in the cup. *Why:* Look at everything that can come out. All red means will. No red means won't. Some red means might.
5. (truefalse) A cup has red and blue chits. Before you pick, you can know the colour you will get.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. With both colours in the cup, nobody can tell which one comes this time.
6. (mcq) A coin landed tails 2 times. What about the next toss?  
   Options: It will be heads / It will be tails / It might be heads or tails  
   **Answer:** It might be heads or tails. *Why:* It might be heads or tails. The coin is just the same on every toss. Picking 'it will be heads' means thinking heads is waiting for a turn.
7. (mcq) A cup has 5 blue chits. Which change makes red a 'might'?  
   Options: Add 1 red chit / Add 1 blue chit / Shake the cup  
   **Answer:** Add 1 red chit. *Why:* Add 1 red chit. Then red is in the cup and can come out. More blue or more shaking puts no red in the cup.
8. (mcq) This cup has 2 red and 2 blue. Which change makes red a 'will'?  
   Options: Add 1 red chit / Take out the 2 blue chits / Pick very slowly  
   **Answer:** Take out the 2 blue chits. *Why:* Take out the 2 blue chits. Then only red is left. Adding a red chit still leaves blue in the cup.
9. (sort) Sort these four: will, might or won't?  
   **Answer:** Night comes after today → Will; It rains tomorrow → Might; The Moon falls into your garden → Won't; You eat something tomorrow → Will. *Why:* Night and eating come every day, so they are will.
Rain can come or not, so it is might.
The Moon stays far away in the sky, so that is won't.
10. (mcq) The weather report says 'rain is likely today'. Which word fits best?  
   Options: Will / Might / Won't  
   **Answer:** Might. *Why:* Might. 'Likely' means rain can well come, but it is not sure. So you carry an umbrella and hope for the best.
11. (mcq) Paatti hides a chit in her hand. She has seen it. You have not. Who is sure of its colour?  
   Options: Only Paatti / Only you / Nobody  
   **Answer:** Only Paatti. *Why:* Only Paatti. She has seen the chit. For you it is still a might.
12. (truefalse) The same chit can be a 'might' for one person and 'sure' for another.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It depends on what each person knows. The one who peeked is sure. The one who did not peek says might.

## Applications
- Two cricket captains toss a coin to see who chooses to bat or bowl first.
- The weather office tells fishing families when a storm is likely, so that boats stay on shore.
- A farmer cannot be sure of rain. So many farmers store water in a tank or a pond.
- Families pick chits from a bowl to choose fairly whose turn it is.
- A doctor says a medicine is likely to help, because it helped most people who took it before.
- Board games such as snakes and ladders use a dice, so that nobody can tell who will win.
- People carry an umbrella in June because rain might come, not because it will.

## Spotted around us
- The toss before a cricket match. Watch the two captains. Neither of them can tell how the coin will land.
- Snakes and ladders, a game that began in India. In Tamil Nadu it is called Paramapadam. Each roll of the dice is a might: a ladder, a snake or neither.
- The morning sky in the monsoon. Grey clouds are a clue, but rain is still a might.
- Chits in a bowl. Many families and classrooms pick a folded chit to choose a name fairly.
- Everyone does maths: kea parrots. In a test in 2020, six kea mostly chose the hand that came from the jar with more treat tokens.
- The Sun rising in the east every single morning. This is one of the few things in your day that is a will.
- A weather report in a newspaper or on a phone. Look for the words 'likely' and 'very likely'.
- A bus stop. The bus might come in two minutes or in twenty. People still wait, because it will come some time.

## Open questions
- **Is anything in the world a real 'might', or do we say might only because we do not know enough?** Thinkers have argued about this for hundreds of years. Many physicists say some tiny happenings cannot be known ahead by anyone. Others are not so sure.
- **How do kea and other animals use chance in their daily lives?** The 2020 test used six trained kea in a wildlife park. Watching wild birds weigh chances is much harder, and few such studies exist.
- **Can tomorrow's rain ever be told for sure?** Weather reports get better every year. But the air is always moving, and tiny changes can grow into big ones. Nobody knows how far ahead a sure report can ever reach.

## Connected maths
- A cup has 4 red chits and 2 blue chits. How many chits are in the cup? Will you pick red for sure? **6 chits. No, red is a might.**. Count on from 4: 5, 6. There are 6 chits. Two of them are blue, so blue can come. Red is a might, not a will. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- Kavya called 10 coin tosses. She was right 6 times. How many times was she wrong? **4 times.**. Ten tosses in all, and 6 calls were right. 10 take away 6 is 4. So 4 calls were wrong. Even a careful caller gets some wrong, because each toss is a might. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.number-facts.L1`)
- Cup P has 5 red chits and 1 blue. Cup Q has 2 red chits and 4 blue. Which cup has more red chits? From which cup does red come more often? **Cup P, for both questions.**. 5 is more than 2, so cup P has more red chits. Both cups hold 6 chits. The cup with more red in it gives red more often. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- A cup has 6 chits. Lakshmi says, 'I will pick red for sure.' She is right. How many of the chits are red? How many are blue? **6 red and 0 blue.**. If even one chit were blue, blue might come, and red would not be sure. So every chit is red. That makes 6 red and no blue. (queues `math.logic.puzzles.L1`)
- Farhan rolled a dice 12 times. The table shows how many times each face came. Which face did not come? Does that mean it won't ever come? **The face 3 did not come. No, it still might.**. The table shows 0 for the face 3. But 3 is on the dice, so it can come on the next roll. 'It has not come yet' is not the same as 'it won't come'. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L1`)
