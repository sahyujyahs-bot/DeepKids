# Sorting and counting: like with like, then count each group
`math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L1`

**Summary.** Tip out a heap of mixed things and ask: which kind is there most of? You cannot tell by looking. You will sort the heap into groups, count each group, and make one mark for each thing. Then you will sort the same heap by a different rule and see what changes and what stays the same. You will also find out that the groups come from the rule you choose.

**Intro.** *Which are there more of, circles or squares? Look first. Then find a way to check.* There are 6 squares and 5 circles. In a jumble it is hard to see. Put the squares together and the circles together, and it becomes easy to count each group. That is sorting. Now look at the colours. There are 7 blue shapes and 4 red shapes. They are the same shapes, sorted in a new way. In this unit you sort real things from your home, count each group, and see what a new sorting rule does.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can sort things into groups and say the rule I used.
1. I can count each group and say which has the most and which has the fewest.
2. I can sort the same things again in a different way.
3. I can show that sorting does not change how many things there are in all.
4. I can make one mark for each thing, and count the marks.
5. I can say that the groups depend on the rule we choose.

## Terms
- **Sort**: To put things that are alike together. *e.g.* You put all the spoons in one place and all the tumblers in another.
- **Group**: Things that have been put together because they are alike in one way. *e.g.* All the red crayons make one group.
- **Sorting rule**: The one thing you look at when you sort, such as colour, kind or size. *e.g.* If your rule is colour, a red clip and a red lid go together.
- **Alike**: The same in one way. Two things can be alike in one way and different in another. *e.g.* A banana and a lemon are alike in colour. They are different in shape.
- **Most**: The biggest number. The group with the most has more things than every other group. *e.g.* 6 clips, 5 crayons and 4 lids. Clips have the most.
- **Fewest**: The smallest number. The group with the fewest has less than every other group. *e.g.* 6 clips, 5 crayons and 4 lids. Lids have the fewest.
- **In all**: All the groups counted together. *e.g.* 6 clips, 5 crayons and 4 lids make 15 things in all.
- **Mark**: A short line you draw to stand for one thing. You draw one mark for each thing. *e.g.* Three autos go by. You draw | | | on your paper.
- **Table**: Boxes drawn in rows, where you write each group and how many it has. *e.g.* One row says 'spoons' with 6 marks. The next row says 'cups' with 4 marks.

## Explanations
### Sorting puts like with like · *defined*
**Definition:** To sort is to put things that are alike together. The way they are alike is your sorting rule.

A mixed heap is hard to use. First you choose a rule, such as shape. Then you look at each thing and ask which group it belongs to. Here is a worked example. The rule is shape. You pick up a red circle. It is a circle, so it goes with the circles. Its colour does not matter for this rule.
- e.g. In the kitchen, spoons go with spoons and tumblers go with tumblers.
- e.g. After the wash, each person's clothes go in that person's pile.
- e.g. A vegetable seller keeps tomatoes in one heap and onions in another.
### Count each group: most and fewest · *defined*
**Definition:** After sorting, count each group. The biggest number is the most. The smallest number is the fewest.

Look at the picture. There are 6 spoons, 4 cups and 5 lids. Spoons have the most and cups have the fewest. You can also see it without counting. Put each group in a row, one thing in each floor tile. The longest row has the most. Two spoons stick out past the cups. So there are 2 more spoons than cups.
- e.g. 3 idlis and 5 vadas on a plate. There are more vadas.
- e.g. In a family of 4 with 3 fans at home, there are more people than fans.
- e.g. Rows only work if the things are spaced the same. One thing in each floor tile is a good way.
### One heap can be sorted in many ways · *defined*
**Definition:** The same things can be sorted by different rules. Each rule gives different groups.

Take the shapes from the opening puzzle. Sort them by shape and you get circles and squares. Sort the same shapes by colour and you get red ones and blue ones. Both sorts are right. Each one answers a different question. 'How many circles?' needs the shape sort. 'How many red ones?' needs the colour sort. So before you sort, ask what you want to find out.
- e.g. Clothes can be sorted by whose they are, or by shirts, shorts and towels.
- e.g. Books can be sorted by size, or by what they are about.
- e.g. Children in a class can be sorted by age, by height, or by the first letter of their names.
### Sorting does not change how many there are in all · *defined*
**Definition:** Sorting only moves things. If nothing is added and nothing is taken away, the number in all stays the same.

You had 15 things in the heap. You sorted them by kind and got 6, 5 and 4. Count them all together and it is still 15. You sorted them by colour and got 7, 5 and 3, and it is 15 again. The groups changed. The number in all did not. This is a good way to check your work. If the groups do not add up to the number you started with, something was missed or counted twice.
- e.g. 12 clothes in the wash basket are still 12 clothes when sorted into three piles.
- e.g. 20 children stand in two lines or four lines. There are still 20 children.
- e.g. If your groups make 14 and the heap had 15, look under the plate for the lost one.
### One mark for each thing · *defined*
**Definition:** A mark is a short line that stands for one thing. One thing gets one mark.

Some things cannot be put in a pile. You cannot pile up the fans in your home, or the autos on the road. So you draw one mark for each. Here is a worked example. You see a fan and draw one mark beside the word fan. You see another fan and draw one more. At the end you count the marks. The marks stay on the paper, so you do not have to remember.
- e.g. A cricket scorer makes a mark in the book for every ball that is bowled.
- e.g. A milk seller may mark the wall or a card each day she brings milk.
- e.g. A table keeps the marks for each group in their own row, as in the picture.
### When a thing is hard to place · *defined*
**Definition:** If a thing fits two groups, or no group, the sorter decides where it goes and then does the same every time.

A clip that is half red and half blue is hard to sort by colour. A spoon made of wood and steel is hard to sort by what it is made of. Nothing is wrong with the thing. Your rule did not say what to do. So you add to the rule. You can say 'I go by the bigger part'. Or you can make a new group called 'two colours'. What matters is that you do the same every time, and that you can tell someone your rule.
- e.g. Is a tomato a fruit or a vegetable? Cooks say vegetable. Plant scientists say fruit.
- e.g. A book about a dog on a train can go with animal books or train books. The librarian chooses.
- e.g. A sock with no partner gets its own small group until the partner turns up.
### Ants sort too · *defined*
**Definition:** Some ants keep their young sorted by size, in rings.

A small ant called Temnothorax lives in thin cracks in rocks. The ants keep all their young in one flat cluster. Two scientists, Nigel Franks and Ana Sendova-Franks, looked closely at these clusters and wrote about them in 1992. The eggs and the tiniest young were in the middle. Bigger young lay in rings around them, with the biggest furthest out. When the ants moved to a new nest, they laid their young out in the same way again. Why they sort like this is still an idea. The scientists think it may give each young ant the room and the care they need.
- e.g. Middle: eggs and the tiniest young. Next ring: small young. Outside ring: the biggest young.
- e.g. Fox squirrels in America, given four kinds of nuts at one spot, buried each kind in its own area.
- e.g. Watch the ants at home. Do they carry one kind of food and leave another?
### Sorting helps us understand the world · *defined*
**Definition:** People sort and count to find out what is there and how much of each.

A shop sorts so that you can find the rice. Scientists sort too. About a hundred years ago, an American scientist named Annie Jump Cannon sorted the stars. She looked at the light of each star and put the star in a group. Hot blue-white stars went at one end and cooler red stars at the other. She sorted about 350,000 stars. People who study stars still use her groups today. Once the stars were sorted, people could count each group and ask why some kinds are rare.
- e.g. A doctor sorts blood into groups called A, B, AB and O before giving blood to a sick person.
- e.g. Waste is sorted into wet and dry so that each kind can be used again.
- e.g. A post office sorts letters by the place they are going to.

## Mostly agreed
- If you only move things into groups, the number of things in all stays the same. *How we know:* Anyone can test it. Count a heap, sort it, and count all the groups together. The Heap Test does this three times.
- The same things can be sorted by more than one rule, and each rule gives different groups. *How we know:* Sort clothes clips by colour and then by size. The groups change, but the clips do not.
- People were making marks to keep count a very long time before they could write. *How we know:* A bone with 29 cut marks was found in Border Cave, in southern Africa. It is about 42,000 years old. Nobody knows what its maker was counting.
- Some other animals sort things too. *How we know:* In 1992, scientists reported that Temnothorax ants keep their young sorted by size, in rings. In 2017, scientists reported that fox squirrels can bury each kind of nut in its own area.

## How we found out
**Denise Schmandt-Besserat, The 1970s to 1992.** For many years, people digging at very old villages in West Asia kept finding small pieces of baked clay. The pieces were shaped like cones, balls and eggs. Nobody was sure what they were for. A scientist named Denise Schmandt-Besserat, who was born in France, studied thousands of them in museums. In the 1970s she showed that they were counters. Each shape stood for one kind of goods. An egg shape stood for a jar of oil. A cone stood for a measure of grain. Three jars of oil were shown by three egg shapes. So people sorted by shape and kept count with one piece for each thing, long before anyone could write. The oldest pieces are about 9,500 years old. She also showed that the first writing in that part of the world grew out of these counters. Most scholars now agree that writing there began with keeping count of goods. They still argue about what some of the shapes meant.  
*What people believed before:* People thought that writing began as small pictures of things, and that counting on clay came later. Schmandt-Besserat showed that keeping count with clay pieces came first, thousands of years before writing.

## Challenging perspectives
- **There is one right way to sort a heap of things.** (Many children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: At home and in school we are often shown one way, such as spoons with spoons. For that job it is the best way.  
  Against: The same heap can be sorted by kind, colour, size or what it is made of. Each sort is right for a different question.
- **The group that takes up the most room has the most things.** (Many young children; *rejected*)  
  For: Often a bigger heap does have more things in it, so the guess works some of the time.  
  Against: Three big brinjals take up more room than six small lemons. Only counting, or matching one to one, tells you which group has more.
- **Some groups are made by nature, not by people.** (Aristotle, and many scientists today; *debated*)  
  For: A crow's chicks are always crows, never sparrows. Gold is gold wherever you find it. These groups seem to be there before anyone sorts.  
  Against: Even scientists often disagree about where one kind of living being ends and the next begins. And we still choose which likeness to sort by.

## Roots
We sorted heaps of things and counted the groups. Now let us think about where the groups come from.

**Deep truth.** In the Heap Test, you sorted 15 things by kind and found the biggest group. Then you sorted the same things by colour, and a different group was the biggest. Nothing was added and nothing was taken away. So a heap has no biggest group until someone picks a rule.  
*Habit it breaks:* We ask 'Which is the most?' as if the answer were sitting in the heap. But the heap only answers after we choose how to sort it.  
*Evidence:* Your table from the Heap Test. It shows three rules, three different sets of groups and three different biggest groups, and 15 things in all every time.

- *who am i:* You are in many groups at once. You are a child, a grandchild, a dosa eater and a person who lives on your street. No one group is all of you.
- *what is life:* Other living beings sort too. Some ants keep their eggs in the middle of the nest and their biggest young around the outside.
- *what is this world:* The world does not come in labelled boxes. People make the boxes, and people can make them in a different way.

Before you sorted, there was one heap. Your rule made it into three groups. A new rule made it into different groups. Push them together and it is one heap again. The many groups and the one heap are the same things.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** A father named Uddalaka tells his son: know one lump of clay, and you know everything made of clay. A pot and a cup have different names, but both are clay. (Sort by kind, and the pot and the cup go in two groups. Sort by what they are made of, and they go in one.)
- **Udana 6.4:** The Buddha tells of a king who had some blind men touch an elephant. One touched the ear, one the leg, one the tail. Each said the elephant was a different kind of thing, and they quarrelled. (Each sort is like one touch. It tells you something true about the heap, but not everything.)
- **Thirukkural 355:** Thiruvalluvar, the Tamil poet, says: whatever a thing is, and whatever kind it seems to be, wisdom is to see what it truly is. (A group name such as 'red things' tells you one fact about a thing. The thing is always more than its group.)
- Maths (settled): Ants of the kind called Temnothorax keep their young sorted by size, in rings, with the eggs in the middle (Franks and Sendova-Franks, 1992). So sorting is not only a human habit.
- Maths (open): Are some groups made by nature, or do people make them all? Thinkers and scientists still argue about this.
- Other view, Aristotle, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,300 years ago, and many scientists today: They say some groups are not our choice. A crow's chicks are crows whether or not anyone sorts them. Gold is gold and water is water. On this view, a good sorter does not make the groups. She finds the groups that are already there.
- Practice, One thing, three groups (3 min): Pick up one small thing, such as a spoon. Look at it. Say three groups it could belong to. Now close your eyes and hold it. Feel its weight and its coolness. It is in many groups, and it is still this one spoon. Open your eyes. Was it any different when you were not naming its groups?
- Ask: If nobody sorted the heap, would it still have groups in it?
- Ask: You belong to many groups. Which group feels most like you? Why?
- Ask: Can two things be alike in every way? What would that mean?
- Ask: Who chose the groups that you were put in, such as your class or your team?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* You sort a heap two ways and get different groups. Which sort is the right one? (hint: What did you want to find out each time?)
- *deep:* Can a thing belong to two groups at once? Can you? (hint: Think of a red spoon. Then think of yourself: a child, a cousin, a singer.)
- *deep:* If you sort the heap ten different ways, will the number in all ever change? (hint: Did you add any? Did you take any away?)
- *big:* Who decided that a tomato is a vegetable? Could it have been decided another way? (hint: Ask a cook. Then look inside a tomato for seeds.)
- *big:* Ants sort their young by size. Do they choose their rule the way you choose yours? (hint: Nobody knows yet. What could you watch for?)
- *big:* Is there anything in the world that cannot be put in any group? (hint: Try to think of one. Then ask: is 'things with no group' a group?)

## Story: The Stairway Library
*Nila, Kathir, Fathima Aunty and the library are made up. Mylapore is a real part of Chennai.*

Nila was seven. She lived on the second floor of a yellow building in Mylapore, in Chennai. On the landing outside her door stood an old shoe rack. It had no shoes on it. It had books.

This was Nila's library. Any child in the building could take a book home. They only had to bring it back.

On Saturday morning the books came back all at once. Eighteen of them lay in one heap on the landing.

'I will put them away nicely,' said Nila.

She put the tall books on the bottom shelf. She put the middle-sized books on the middle shelf. She put the small books on the top shelf. Nothing stuck out and nothing fell over. Nila was pleased.

Then Kathir came down the stairs. Kathir was five, and he lived on the third floor.

'Akka, do you have more animal books or more train books?' he asked. 'I want the kind you have the most of.'

Nila looked at her shelves. Tall, middle, small. She could not tell.

'Wait,' she said. She began to count the animal books with her finger. There was a cat book on the top shelf. There was a tiger book on the bottom shelf. Was that an elephant on the middle shelf? Had she counted the cat already? She lost her place and had to start again. Then she lost her place again.

'You sorted them the wrong way,' said Kathir.

'It is not wrong,' said Nila. 'It is a good way to make them stand up.' She thought for a moment. 'But it does not help with your question. Your question needs a different sort.'

She took all eighteen books down again. This time she did not look at how big they were. She looked at what each book was about. Animal books went here. Train and bus books went there. Books about people went in a third pile.

At the end, one book was left in her hand. It was about a dog who rides a train.

'Animal,' said Kathir.

'Train,' said Nila.

They looked at each other.

'It is my library,' said Nila slowly, 'so I have to choose. But I must choose the same way every time, or nobody will find anything.' She looked at the cover. The dog was big and the train was small. 'If an animal is the main one in the story, the book goes with the animal books.' She put the book on the animal pile.

Kathir nodded. That was fair.

Now the counting was easy. Nila found a piece of chalk. On the wall she drew a cat face, a train and a smiling face. She touched the first animal book and made one mark next to the cat. She touched the next one and made another mark. One book, one mark.

Kathir wanted the chalk, so he did the trains. Nila did the people books.

When they had finished, there were eight marks next to the cat. There were four marks next to the train. There were six marks next to the smiling face.

'Animals!' said Kathir. He had not even counted. The line of marks next to the cat was the longest. He took the tiger book and sat down on the stairs to look at it.

A little later Fathima Aunty came up from the ground floor. Her small daughter Sana was holding her hand.

'Nila, which of your books are thin?' she asked. 'Sana can only sit still for a thin book.'

Kathir looked up from his tiger. He looked at the three piles. Animals, trains, people. He began to laugh. 'Akka, now they are sorted the wrong way again!'

Nila laughed too. This time she did not mind at all. She had worked it out. The books did not have one right place. They had a right place for each question.

She went through the three piles and pulled out every thin book. It did not take long. 'Five thin books, Aunty. Sana can choose.'

That evening Nila put the books back on the shoe rack. She thought for a long time about how to do it. In the end she sorted them by what they were about, because that was what children asked most often.

She left the chalk marks on the wall. Next Saturday the books would come back in a heap again. Somebody would ask a new question. Then she would take the books down and sort them once more.

**For the parent:** For the parent: there is no single right way to sort. The right sort is the one that answers the question being asked. A hard case, like the dog on the train, needs a decision that is then kept the same every time. And one mark for each thing lets a child compare groups without holding numbers in her head.

- Ask: Why could Nila not answer Kathir's question from her neat shelves?
- Ask: The book about a dog on a train fits two piles. Where would you put it? Why?
- Ask: Kathir knew which pile had the most without counting. How?
- Ask: How would you sort your own books? What question would that sort answer?

## Games
### g1 · Guess My Rule (10 min)
- **rules:** Make the 12 cards. On each one, draw and colour the shape that its words say. Mix the cards and lay them face up.; The sorter thinks of a secret rule. It can be a colour, a shape or a size: for example 'red ones', 'triangles' or 'big ones'.; The sorter picks up one card at a time. A card that fits the rule goes on the YES pile. A card that does not fit goes on the NO pile.; After each card, the guesser may make one guess at the rule. The sorter says only 'yes' or 'not yet'.; When the guess is right, count the cards in the YES and NO piles together. That is the guesser's score. A low score is good.; Swap jobs. After two rounds each, the player with the lowest score in all wins.; Playing alone: lay out all 12 cards. Sort them by colour, then by shape, then by size. Each time, count the groups and the cards in each group.
- **Printable cards: 12 shape cards to draw, colour and cut out:** big red circle; small red circle; big blue circle; small blue circle; big red square; small red square; big blue square; small blue square; big red triangle; small red triangle; big blue triangle; small blue triangle
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the 12 cards together. 2. Lay them face up. 3. Ask your child to name three ways the cards differ.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the cards. In what ways are they different from each other?  
  *Child mode:* Lay out the 12 cards. How are they different? Find three ways.
  - Ask: In what ways do the cards differ? (good: Colour: red or blue.; Shape: circle, square or triangle.; Size: big or small.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. You are the sorter first. 2. Choose the rule 'red ones'. 3. Place cards one at a time on YES or NO. 4. After each card, your child guesses.  
  *Parent says:* I have a secret rule. This card goes on YES. This one goes on NO. What is my rule?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the YES pile and the NO pile. What is the secret rule? Guess.
  - Note: Start with a colour rule, which is the easiest to see. Then try a shape, then a size.
  - Check: A big red circle and a small red square are on YES. A big blue circle is on NO. Which rule fits? → **Red ones**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap jobs. 2. Your child chooses a secret rule and sorts. 3. You guess after each card. 4. Count the cards used.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are the sorter. Think of a rule. Do not tell me. I will guess.  
  *Child mode:* Now you sort. Think of a secret rule. Put each card on YES or NO.
  - Note: If a card lands on the wrong pile, do not correct it at once. Ask your child to say the rule at the end and check each card together.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add up each player's scores. 2. The lowest score wins. 3. Ask which rule was the hardest to guess.  
  *Parent says:* Who used the fewest cards? Which rule was the hardest to guess? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Add up your scores. The lowest score wins. Which rule was hardest?
### g2 · Roll and Mark (10 min)
- **rules:** Draw the sheet: six rows, numbered 1 to 6, with five boxes in each row.; Before the game, each player picks one number from 1 to 6 as their own. No two players pick the same number.; Take turns to roll the dice. Whatever number comes up, draw one mark in a box of that number's row.; The first row to fill all five boxes wins the race. If that row is your number, you win. If it is nobody's number, nobody wins that race.; After the race, count the marks in each row. Say which number came up the most and which came up the fewest.; Playing alone: guess which number will win, then roll and mark. Play three races. Does the same number win every time?

  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the sheet. 2. Each player picks a number and writes their name beside its row. 3. Read the rules aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Pick your number. Each time the dice shows a number, that row gets one mark.  
  *Child mode:* Draw six rows. Each row has five boxes. Pick your number.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take turns to roll. 2. The roller draws one mark in the row of the number rolled. 3. Stop when one row has five marks.  
  *Parent says:* You rolled a 4. Which row gets the mark? One roll, one mark.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the dice. Draw one mark in that number's row. One roll, one mark.
  - Note: A race takes at most 25 rolls and usually fewer. Now and then ask which row is ahead and how many more it needs.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child counts the marks in each row and writes the number at the end. 2. Ask which number came up the most and the fewest. 3. Ask how many rolls there were in all.  
  *Parent says:* Count the marks in each row. Which number came up the most? How many times did we roll in all?  
  *Child mode:* Count the marks in each row. Which number came up most? Which came up fewest?
  - Ask: How can we find out how many rolls there were, without rolling again? (good: Count all the marks.; Each roll made one mark.; Add up the rows.)
  - Check: In a race, the row for 3 has 5 marks and the row for 6 has 2 marks. Which number came up more often? → **3**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Heap Test: one heap, three ways to sort (25 min)
- **question:** If we sort the same heap by a new rule, do the groups change? Does the number of things in all change?
- **predict:** Before each sort, guess which group will be the biggest. Guess whether there will still be 15 things in all. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same 15 things every time. Nobody adds a thing or takes one away. After every sort we count each group, and then we count them all together.
- **safety:** Keep small things such as buttons, lids and beans away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** A grown-up draws the table. For each rule, write the guess, then draw one mark for each thing next to its group. Write how many in all. At the end the child says what they found, and the grown-up writes it under the table.
- **results table:** My sorting rule | My guess for the biggest group | The groups and how many in each | How many in all
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the 15 things in one mixed heap. 2. Ask which kind there is most of, by looking only. 3. Write the guess. 4. Your child counts the whole heap, moving each thing.  
  *Parent says:* Look, but do not touch. Which kind is there most of? Why do you think so? Now count them all.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the heap. Which kind is there most of? Guess. Then count them all.
  - Ask: Why is it hard to tell which kind there is most of? (good: They are all mixed up.; Some are hiding under others.; The lids are big, so they look like more.)
  - Note: Do not say the numbers of each kind. The heap should hold 15 things. If your child's count is different, count again together, moving each thing aside.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out three sheets of paper. 2. Ask your child to put each kind on its own sheet. 3. Your child counts each group. 4. Draw one mark for each thing in the table. 5. Ask which group has the most and the fewest.  
  *Parent says:* Put things that are the same kind together. Then count each group. Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Put each kind on its own paper. Count each group. Draw one mark for each thing.
  - Ask: Which group has the most? Which has the fewest? (good: Clips have the most.; Lids have the fewest.; There are 6 clips and only 4 lids.)
  - Note: Let your child name the groups. If the guess from step 1 was wrong, that is useful. Ask what made the other kind look like more.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask how many things there are in all now, before counting. 2. Ask for a reason. 3. Push the groups together and count them all. 4. Write the number in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We made three groups. How many things are there in all now? More than 15, fewer, or still 15? Why?  
  *Child mode:* You made three groups. How many things in all now? Guess. Then count them all.
  - Ask: Why is it still 15? (good: We did not add any.; We only moved them.; They are the same things.)
  - Note: Some children expect a new number, because the heap looks different now. Let the count answer.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix the heap again. 2. Say the new rule: colour. 3. Ask which colour group will be the biggest, and why. 4. Your child sorts by colour and counts each group. 5. Draw the marks and count them all.  
  *Parent says:* New rule: sort by colour. Which colour group will be the biggest? Why? Will there still be 15?  
  *Child mode:* Mix them up. Now sort by colour. Count each group. Then count them all.
  - Ask: What is different from the first sort? What is the same? (good: The groups are different.; Now clips and lids are together.; A different group is the biggest.; It is still 15.)
  - Note: Clips, crayons and lids now share groups. If a thing has two colours, let your child decide where it goes. Ask them to say their reason.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix the heap again. 2. Ask your child to think of a new rule and keep it secret. 3. Your child sorts. 4. You guess the rule. 5. Count each group and count them all.  
  *Parent says:* Now you choose the rule. Do not tell me. Sort them, and I will guess your rule.  
  *Child mode:* Think of your own rule. Keep it secret. Sort the heap. Can your grown-up guess it?
  - Ask: What was your rule? Did every thing fit a group? (good: Big ones and small ones.; Things that roll and things that do not.; Plastic and not plastic.; This one did not fit, so I made a new group.)
  - Note: Any rule is fine if your child can say it and uses it the same way for every thing. Ideas if they are stuck: big and small, rolls or does not roll, what it is made of.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick the thing that was hardest to place. 2. Ask where it went and why. 3. Ask whether it could have gone in another group.  
  *Parent says:* Which thing was the hardest to place? Where did you put it? Could it go somewhere else?  
  *Child mode:* Which thing was hard to place? Where did you put it? Say why.
  - Note: If nothing was hard, show a thing with two colours or two materials and ask where it would go. The point is that the sorter decides, and then keeps to it.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask what changed from sort to sort, and what did not. 3. Ask why. 4. Write your child's words under the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at our table. What changed each time we sorted? What never changed? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. What changed each time? What stayed the same?
  - Ask: What do you think now? What did you see? Why is it so? (good: The groups changed when the rule changed.; The biggest group was different each time.; It was 15 every time.; Because we only moved them. We did not add any.)
  - Check: You sort 12 buttons by colour. Then you mix them and sort them by size. How many buttons in all now? → **Still 12**

## Activities
### a1 · Line Them Up: see the most without counting (10 min)
- **problem:** Can you tell which group has the most without counting?
- **record:** Draw the three rows on paper as dots, one dot for each thing. Circle the longest row.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sort the things into three groups. 2. Ask your child to lay each group in a row, any way they like. 3. Ask which row has the most.  
  *Parent says:* Put each group in a row. Which row has the most? How do you know?  
  *Child mode:* Put each group in a row. Which row has the most?
  - Note: Your child may spread one row out and squash another. Then the longest row may not have the most. Let this happen. The next step sorts it out.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start all three rows at the same wall or line. 2. Put one thing in each floor tile. 3. Ask again which row has the most. 4. Check by counting.  
  *Parent says:* This time, one thing in each tile. All rows start at the wall. Now which has the most?  
  *Child mode:* Start each row at the wall. Put one thing in each tile. Which row is longest?
  - Ask: Why does each thing need its own tile? (good: So the rows are fair.; Big things take more room.; Then long means more.)
  - Note: With one thing in each tile, the longest row really does have the most. Without tiles, a few big tumblers can make a longer row than many spoons.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Point to the longest row and one other row. 2. Ask how many more the longer row has. 3. Show how to count only the things that stick out.  
  *Parent says:* How many more spoons than tumblers? Count only the ones that stick out past the tumblers.  
  *Child mode:* Look at two rows. Count the things that stick out. That is how many more.
  - Check: A row of 6 spoons lies next to a row of 4 tumblers, one in each tile. How many spoons stick out? → **2**
### a2 · Sort the Washing: whose pile is the biggest? (15 min)
- **problem:** The dry clothes are in one heap. Who has the most clothes in this wash? And how many pieces must go to be ironed?
- **record:** Write each person's name with one mark for each piece of clothing. Circle the name with the most marks.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tip the dry clothes onto the bed. 2. Ask who has the most clothes in this wash, by looking. 3. Your child counts all the pieces. A pair of socks counts as one piece.  
  *Parent says:* Who do you think has the most clothes in this wash? Now count all the pieces.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the heap. Who has the most clothes here? Guess. Then count every piece.
  - Note: Put each pair of socks together first, so that a pair is one piece. Write the number in all at the top of the paper.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to make one pile for each person. 2. Write each name on the paper. 3. Your child draws one mark for each piece beside the right name. 4. Count the marks.  
  *Parent says:* Make one pile for each of us. Then draw one mark for each piece, next to the name.  
  *Child mode:* Make a pile for each person. Draw one mark for each piece of clothing.
  - Ask: Who has the most? Who has the fewest? Do all the piles together still make the first number? (good: Amma has the most.; I have the fewest.; Yes, it is still 14.)
  - Note: A towel that everyone uses fits no pile. Let your child decide: a pile called 'everyone' is a good answer.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say that the clothes now need to be put away. 2. Ask your child to sort the same clothes by kind. 3. Count each kind. 4. Count all the pieces again.  
  *Parent says:* Now we sort a new way. All the shirts together, all the towels together. How many of each kind?  
  *Child mode:* Now sort by kind. Shirts with shirts. Towels with towels. Count each kind.
  - Ask: Which sort helps us know whose is whose? Which sort helps with ironing? (good: By person, for whose it is.; By kind, for ironing.; It depends what we want to do.)
  - Note: Both sorts are useful. The first tells each person what is theirs. The second shows how many shirts need ironing.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick out the pieces that need ironing. 2. Your child counts them. 3. Write the number on a slip for the ironing shop or the person who irons at home.  
  *Parent says:* The ironing person charges for each piece. How many pieces are we sending?  
  *Child mode:* Pick out the clothes for ironing. Count them. Write the number on a slip.
  - Note: Ironing shops in India usually charge for each piece, so they count the pieces too. If you iron at home, the count still tells you how big the job is.
  - Check: 14 clothes are sorted by person. Then the same clothes are sorted by kind. How many clothes are there now? → **14**
### a3 · A Mark for Each: count the things you cannot pile up (10 min)

- **record:** Keep the table. Write the number at the end of each row. Circle the row with the most marks.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a table with four rows. 2. At the start of each row, draw a fan, a door, a window and a tap. 3. Ask your child to guess which row will get the most marks.

  *Child mode:* Look at the four rows. Which thing do you have most of at home? Guess.

  *Parent says:* Each time you see one, draw one mark in its row. One thing, one mark.  
  *Child mode:* Walk through each room. See a fan? Draw one mark. One thing, one mark.
  - Ask: How do you make sure you do not mark the same door twice? (good: Go room by room.; Do not go back.; Touch it when I mark it.)

  *Child mode:* Count the marks in each row. Write the number. Which row has the most?
  - Check: The row for doors has these marks: | | | | |. How many doors is that? → **5**
### a4 · Pick the Dal Clean: sorting in the kitchen (10 min)
- **problem:** Three kinds of dal are mixed up on one plate. Can you get each kind back into its own bowl?
- **safety:** Wash your hands first, so that the dal can be cooked afterwards. Keep small things such as buttons, lids and beans away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Draw three bowls. Write how many of each kind you found. Circle the bowl with the most.

  *Parent does:* 1. Wash hands. 2. Put one small spoon of each dal on the plate and mix them. 3. Ask which kind there is most of, by looking.

  *Child mode:* Look at the mixed dal. Is there the same number of each kind? Guess.
  - Note: One spoon holds far fewer big rajma than small moong. Do not say so yet.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child slides each dal, one at a time, into its own bowl. 2. Watch for any that are broken or odd. 3. Ask where those should go.  
  *Parent says:* Slide each one to its own bowl. What will you do with a broken one?  
  *Child mode:* Slide each dal to its own bowl. One at a time. What about broken ones?
  - Note: A broken or spoiled piece fits none of the three bowls. A fourth group, 'not for cooking', is how cooks deal with it.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child counts each bowl. 2. Write the numbers. 3. Ask which has the most and why one spoon gave different numbers.

  *Child mode:* Count each bowl. Which kind has the most? Why are the numbers different?
  - Ask: Why did one spoon of moong hold more than one spoon of rajma? (good: Moong is smaller.; More small ones fit in a spoon.; Rajma is big.)
  - Check: One spoon holds 6 rajma. The same spoon holds 14 moong. Which is true? → **Each moong is smaller than each rajma**
### a5 · Ant Watch: what do the ants choose? (20 min)
- **problem:** You put out sugar and rava side by side. Which do your ants carry away more?
- **safety:** Watch the ants without touching them. Some ants bite. Do not put food where you do not want ants to come.
- **record:** Keep your table of marks. Write which food got more marks. Write one thing you saw an ant do.
- **s1 (13 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a pinch of sugar and a pinch of rava a hand-width apart, near where ants walk. 2. Draw a table with two rows. 3. Ask for a guess. 4. Wait about 10 minutes for the ants to find the food.  
  *Parent says:* Which will the ants carry away more, sugar or rava? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put out the sugar and the rava. Which will the ants take more? Guess. Then wait 10 minutes.
  - Note: Different kinds of ants like different foods, so the result is not the same in every home. While you wait, your child can draw an ant.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Watch for 5 minutes. 2. Each time an ant carries a grain away, your child draws one mark in that food's row. 3. Do not touch the ants.  
  *Parent says:* Each time an ant carries a grain away, draw one mark in the right row.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the ants. An ant carries a grain away? Draw one mark.
  - Note: The ants will not wait for your child, so marks are the only way to keep count. It is fine to miss one or two.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child counts the marks in each row. 2. Ask which food the ants chose more. 3. Ask what else they noticed.  
  *Parent says:* Count your marks. Which food did the ants choose more? What else did you see them do?  
  *Child mode:* Count the marks in each row. Which food did the ants choose more?
  - Ask: How do you think an ant tells sugar from rava? (good: By smell.; By taste.; They touch it with their feelers.)
  - Check: Your table shows 7 marks for sugar and 2 marks for rava. What does it tell you? → **More ants carried sugar**

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: count what will not stay still (20 min)
- **problem:** For five minutes, count the autos, two-wheelers, cars and buses that pass your window or gate. At the end you must be able to say how many of each kind passed, and which kind passed the most.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own way and try it for five minutes. Then give your paper to someone at home. If they cannot read the numbers from it, change your way and try again.
- **constraints:** You cannot stop anything or ask it to come back.; You must count four kinds at the same time.; At the end, someone else must be able to read your paper and get the same numbers.
- **general:** Draw a table with one row for each kind. Draw one mark for each one that passes.; Draw a small picture at the start of each row, so that you do not need to read words while you watch.; Work in a pair. One person watches and calls out the kind. The other makes the marks.
- **creative:** Tie the marks in bundles of five. Draw four marks, then draw the fifth one across them. Then you can count 5, 10, 15.; Drop one bean in a cup for each one that passes, one cup for each kind. Count the beans at the end.; Use a string for each kind and tie a knot each time one passes.
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go to the window or gate together. 2. Ask your child to count the autos, two-wheelers, cars and buses for 2 minutes, in any way. 3. Do not help. 4. Ask how many of each passed.  
  *Parent says:* For two minutes, count the autos, two-wheelers, cars and buses. Use any way you like.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the road for 2 minutes. Count each kind. Use any way you like.
  - Ask: What made it hard? (good: They went too fast.; I forgot the numbers.; I mixed up the autos and the cars.; Two came at once.)
  - Note: Stay with your child near any window, balcony or gate. Most children try to hold four numbers in their head and lose them. That is the point of this step.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what would make the counting easier. 2. Listen to your child's ideas. 3. Read the usual ways and the creative ways aloud, if your child wants ideas. 4. Your child draws their sheet.  
  *Parent says:* What could we use so that you do not have to remember four numbers?  
  *Child mode:* How can the paper remember for you? Think of a way. Draw your sheet.
  - Ask: Why is a picture at the start of each row useful? (good: I can find the row fast.; I do not have to read.; I can keep my eyes on the road.)
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show how to tie marks in fives: four marks, and the fifth across them. 2. Your child practises with 8 marks. 3. Start a timer for 5 minutes. 4. Your child marks every vehicle that passes.  
  *Parent says:* Four marks, and the fifth goes across. That is a bundle of five. Now watch for five minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Draw four marks. Draw the fifth across them. That is five. Now watch and mark.
  - Note: Bundles of five are usually taught in Class 2, so plain marks are fine too. On a busy road, count only for 2 minutes, or count only autos and buses.
  - Check: A row has 2 bundles of five marks and 3 loose marks. How many is that? → **13**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child counts the marks in each row and writes the numbers. 2. Ask which kind passed the most and the fewest. 3. Give the paper to someone else to read.  
  *Parent says:* Count each row. Which kind passed the most? Can Paatti read your paper and get the same numbers?  
  *Child mode:* Count each row. Which kind passed the most? Ask someone to read your paper.
  - Ask: If you counted again tomorrow at the same time, would you get the same numbers? (good: Maybe not.; Close, but not the same.; Sunday would be different.)

## Worksheets
### Sort It, Count It (core) (core, 13 questions)
1. (fill) How many circles are in this row? ____  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* Touch each circle once. There are 4 circles. The 3 squares are not counted.
2. (mcq) Which group has the most?  
   Options: spoons / cups / lids  
   **Answer:** spoons. *Why:* Spoons. 6 is more than 4 and more than 5. The row of spoons is also the longest.
3. (mcq) Which group has the fewest?  
   Options: idlis / vadas / dosas  
   **Answer:** dosas. *Why:* Dosas. 2 is less than 3 and less than 5. The row of dosas is the shortest.
4. (truefalse) You sort 10 buttons into two groups. Now there are still 10 buttons in all.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Sorting only moves the buttons. None was added and none was taken away.
5. (sort) Sort each thing: do we eat it or wear it?  
   **Answer:** idli → We eat it; chappal → We wear it; banana → We eat it; shirt → We wear it; cap → We wear it; dosa → We eat it. *Why:* Idli, banana and dosa are food. A chappal, a shirt and a cap are worn. The rule here is what we do with the thing.
6. (fill) Count the marks: | | | | | |   There are ____ marks.  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* Touch each mark once and count. There are 6 marks. Each mark stands for one thing.
7. (match) Match each thing to its group.  
   **Answer:** spoon → things for eating; crayon → things for drawing; chappal → things for feet; comb → things for hair. *Why:* A spoon is for eating, a crayon is for drawing, a chappal is for the feet and a comb is for the hair. Each thing is sorted by what it is used for.
8. (mcq) These shapes were sorted into two groups. What was the rule?  
   Options: By colour / By shape / By size  
   **Answer:** By colour. *Why:* By colour. Every shape in the first group is red, and every shape in the second group is blue. Each group has mixed shapes, so the rule was not shape.
9. (fill) How many more spoons than cups? ____  
   **Answer:** ['2', 'two']. *Why:* Match one spoon to one cup. Three spoons have a cup. Two spoons stick out. So there are 2 more spoons.
10. (order) Put the groups in order. Start with the fewest.  
   **Answer:** ['2 cups', '5 lids', '8 spoons']. *Why:* 2 is the fewest, then 5, then 8. So cups come first, then lids, then spoons.
11. (draw) Draw 3 bananas and 5 mangoes. Draw a ring around the group that has more.  
   **Answer:** Three bananas, five mangoes, and a ring around the mangoes.. *Why:* A strong drawing has exactly 3 and 5. The ring goes around the mangoes, because 5 is more than 3.
12. (draw) Draw one mark for each person who lives in your home. Write how many.  
   **Answer:** One mark for each person, and the number of marks.. *Why:* A strong answer has the same number of marks as people at home. Say each name as you touch each mark to check.
13. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. In the Heap Test, what rule did you make up for your own sort?  
   **Answer:** Any clear rule, such as big and small, or things that roll and things that do not.. *Why:* A strong answer names one thing to look at, and the rule works for every thing in the heap.
### Sort It Another Way (stretch) (stretch, 13 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at group 1 and group 2. What rule made these groups?  
   Options: By colour / By shape / By size  
   **Answer:** By shape. *Why:* By shape. The first group is all circles and the second is all squares. Each group has mixed colours, so the rule was not colour.
2. (fill) Sort these by colour in your head. How many red ones? ____  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* Look only at the colour. There are 4 red shapes: two circles, a square and a triangle.
3. (fill) Sort these by shape in your head. How many triangles? ____  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Look only at the shape. There are 3 triangles. Their colours do not matter for this rule.
4. (truefalse) The same toys can be sorted in more than one way.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. You can sort toys by colour, by size or by kind. Each rule gives different groups.
5. (truefalse) The group that takes up the most room always has the most things.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Three big brinjals take up more room than six small lemons. Count, or match one to one, to find which has more.

   Options: 11 / 12 / 13  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 12. Count on from 5: four more makes 9, and three more makes 12.
7. (match) Match each row of marks to its number.  
   **Answer:** | | | → 3; | | | | | → 5; | | → 2; | | | | → 4. *Why:* Count the marks in each row. One mark stands for one thing.
8. (sort) Sort each animal: where do they live?  
   **Answer:** fish → In water; cow → On land; whale → In water; goat → On land; octopus → In water; hen → On land. *Why:* A fish, a whale and an octopus live in water. A cow, a goat and a hen live on land. The rule here is the place where the animal lives.
9. (mcq) 12 clips are sorted by colour. Then they are mixed and sorted by size. How many clips in all now?  
   Options: Fewer than 12 / 12 / More than 12  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 12. The clips were only moved. The groups changed, but no clip was added or taken away.
10. (order) Put the rows of marks in order. Start with the fewest marks.  
   **Answer:** ['| |', '| | | |', '| | | | | |']. *Why:* The rows have 2, 4 and 6 marks. 2 is the fewest and 6 is the most.
11. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. Name one thing at home that could go in two groups. Say both groups.  
   **Answer:** For example: a red spoon can go with the spoons or with the red things.. *Why:* A strong answer names one thing and two different rules. The thing fits one group under each rule.
12. (draw) Draw 6 shapes. Use 2 colours. Then draw a ring around each colour group.  
   **Answer:** Six shapes in two colours, with one ring around each colour.. *Why:* A strong drawing has every shape inside one ring, and each ring holds only one colour.
13. (perspective) A red spoon is in a heap of mixed things. Which group should it go in?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other child has right.. *Why:* Both are right, for different rules. If the rule is kind, it goes with the spoons. If the rule is colour, it goes with the red things. The sorter must say the rule first.
### Tricky Sorts (challenge) (challenge, 13 questions)
1. (fill) Bananas and lemons are yellow. How many yellow fruits are there? ____  
   **Answer:** ['7', 'seven']. *Why:* Put the bananas and lemons together. 4 and 3 make 7 yellow fruits.
2. (mcq) 7 fruits are yellow and 2 are red. Which colour group is bigger?  
   Options: yellow / red / they are the same  
   **Answer:** yellow. *Why:* Yellow. 7 is more than 2. Sorted by kind, bananas were the biggest group with 4. Sorted by colour, yellow is the biggest with 7.
3. (fill) A heap has 10 things. 6 are red. The rest are blue. How many are blue? ____  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* The red and blue groups together make 10. 6 and 4 make 10, so 4 are blue.
4. (truefalse) A heap of 7 things can have 5 red things and 5 round things.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Some things can be both red and round. If 3 things are red and round, then 5 are red, 5 are round, and there are still only 7 things.
5. (mcq) Marks are tied in fives. How many marks are there?  
   Options: 5 / 13 / 23  
   **Answer:** 13. *Why:* 13. Two full fives make 10. Count on three more: 11, 12, 13.
6. (mcq) Which one does not fit this group: idli, dosa, vada, chappal?  
   Options: idli / dosa / vada / chappal  
   **Answer:** chappal. *Why:* The chappal. Idli, dosa and vada are all food. A chappal is not food.
7. (sort) Sort each thing by what it is made of.  
   **Answer:** steel tumbler → Steel; wooden spoon → Wood; steel plate → Steel; wooden ruler → Wood; glass bangle → Glass; glass jar → Glass. *Why:* The tumbler and the plate are steel. The spoon and the ruler are wood. The bangle and the jar are glass. This rule gives three groups.
8. (fill) 15 things are sorted into three groups. Two groups have 6 and 5. How many are in the third group? ____  
   **Answer:** ['4', 'four']. *Why:* 6 and 5 make 11. Count on from 11 to 15: 12, 13, 14, 15. That is 4 more, so the third group has 4.
9. (order) Put the colour groups in order. Start with the most.  
   **Answer:** ['red: 7', 'blue: 5', 'yellow: 3']. *Why:* 7 is the most, then 5, then 3. So red comes first, then blue, then yellow.
10. (write) Say your answer aloud. A shop keeps all its red things on one shelf. Is that a good way to sort a shop?  
   **Answer:** No. A shopper looks for a kind of thing, such as pens, and not for a colour. Red shirts, red pens and red apples would all be mixed together.. *Why:* A strong answer says what shoppers want to find. Sorting by colour is a real sort, but it does not answer the question 'Where are the pens?'
11. (draw) Draw 8 marks tied in fives: one bundle of five, and the loose ones.  
   **Answer:** Four marks with a fifth across them, then three loose marks.. *Why:* A strong drawing has one bundle of five and 3 loose marks. 5 and 3 make 8.
12. (perspective) Is a tomato a fruit or a vegetable?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other person has right.. *Why:* Both are right, by different rules. Sorted by how we cook and eat them, tomatoes are vegetables. Sorted by the part of the plant, tomatoes are fruits, because they hold the seeds.
13. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. Think of a rule that puts you and a crow in the same group.  
   **Answer:** For example: things that are alive, things with two legs, or things that eat rice.. *Why:* A strong answer names one way in which you and a crow are alike. There are many right answers.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does it mean to sort things?  
   Options: Put things that are alike together / Count things very fast / Put things in one long line  
   **Answer:** Put things that are alike together. *Why:* To sort is to put things that are alike together. Counting comes after sorting. A long line is not sorting unless like is put with like.
2. (mcq) Look at the two groups. What was the sorting rule?  
   Options: Colour / Shape / Size  
   **Answer:** Colour. *Why:* Colour. One group is all yellow and the other is all blue. Each group has mixed shapes, so the rule was not shape.
3. (mcq) Clips, crayons and lids were sorted and counted. Which group has the most?  
   Options: clips / crayons / lids  
   **Answer:** crayons. *Why:* Crayons. 7 is more than 4 and more than 5. The row of crayons is the longest.
4. (fill) How many more bananas than mangoes? ____  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* Match one banana to one mango. Four bananas have a mango. Three bananas are left over. So there are 3 more bananas.
5. (mcq) Autos, bikes and buses went past. Which group has the fewest?  
   Options: autos / bikes / buses  
   **Answer:** buses. *Why:* Buses. 2 is less than 6 and less than 8. The row of buses is the shortest.
6. (mcq) Red and blue circles and squares are sorted by colour. What is another way to sort them?  
   Options: By shape / By counting them / There is no other way  
   **Answer:** By shape. *Why:* By shape: circles in one group and squares in the other. The same things can be sorted by more than one rule.
7. (mcq) Sort these by shape. How many groups do you get?  
   Options: 2 / 3 / 8  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 3 groups: circles, squares and triangles. The colours do not matter when the rule is shape.
8. (truefalse) Sorting a heap into groups changes how many things there are in all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Sorting only moves things. If nothing is added or taken away, the number in all stays the same.
9. (mcq) Groups of 5, 4 and 3 are pushed back into one heap. How many things in all?  
   Options: 3 / 9 / 12  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 12. Count on from 5: four more makes 9, and three more makes 12.
10. (fill) Count the marks: | | | | | | | |   There are ____ marks.  
   **Answer:** ['8', 'eight']. *Why:* Touch each mark once and count. There are 8 marks.
11. (mcq) Four autos go by. You make one mark for each. How many marks do you draw?  
   Options: 1 / 4 / 5  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 4 marks. One auto gets one mark. Picking 1 means making a single mark for the whole group.
12. (mcq) Sorted by kind, clips are the biggest group. Sorted by colour, red is the biggest. What changed?  
   Options: The sorting rule / The number of things / Nothing changed  
   **Answer:** The sorting rule. *Why:* The rule changed. The things are the same and the number in all is the same. A new rule makes new groups.
13. (truefalse) There is only one right way to sort a heap of things.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A heap can be sorted by kind, colour, size and more. The best rule depends on what you want to find out.

## Applications
- A vegetable seller sorts her vegetables into heaps, so that you can find the tomatoes at once.
- A librarian sorts books by what they are about, so that a reader can find all the animal books together.
- An ironing shop counts each family's clothes piece by piece, because the price is for each piece.
- A cricket scorer makes a mark for every ball, then counts the marks to tell the runs in each over.
- Families sort waste into wet and dry, so that each kind can be used again.
- A doctor counts how many children had fever this week and how many had coughs, to see what is going around.
- At election time, the votes are sorted by who they are for, and each pile is counted.
- A bird watcher makes one mark for each bird of each kind, to find out which birds live in a place.

## Spotted around us
- A vegetable shop or cart. Each vegetable has its own heap or basket. Which heap is the biggest today?
- The spoon stand or drawer in your kitchen. Are the big spoons and small spoons kept apart?
- A flower seller's baskets in a South Indian market. Jasmine, marigolds and roses are kept in separate heaps.
- Chappals outside a temple or a function hall. A token counter sorts them into numbered racks.
- Wet and dry waste bins. Every time you throw something away, you sort.
- A line of ants carrying food. Do they all carry the same kind of thing?
- A cricket score book or a scoreboard. Each ball and each run is marked down as it happens.
- Squirrels hiding food. In America, scientists gave fox squirrels four kinds of nuts at one spot. The squirrels buried each kind in its own area.

## Open questions
- **Are some groups made by nature, or do people make all the groups?** Thinkers have argued about this for more than two thousand years. Even for living beings, scientists do not agree on one way to say where a kind begins and ends.
- **How do ants sort their young without anyone in charge?** Scientists have ideas, such as each ant following a simple habit. Nobody has shown for certain how the rings are made.
- **What was the person who cut 29 marks into the Border Cave bone counting?** The bone is about 42,000 years old and broken at one end. People have guessed days or moons, but there is no way to check.
- **Do other animals sort the world into the same groups as we do?** A squirrel can keep kinds of nuts apart, and a bee can tell flowers apart. But nobody knows what their groups are like for them.

## Connected maths
- You sorted a heap by kind and got 6 clips, 5 crayons and 4 lids. How many things are there in all? **15 things.**. Start with the 6 clips. Count on 5 for the crayons: 7, 8, 9, 10, 11. Count on 4 for the lids: 12, 13, 14, 15. So there are 15 things in all. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- After the wash, Amma's pile has 7 clothes and Kavin's pile has 4 clothes. How many more clothes does Amma have? **3 more.**. Match one of Amma's clothes to one of Kavin's. Four of Amma's have a partner. Three do not. So Amma has 3 more. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- A heap of 12 things is sorted by colour. There are 5 red things and 4 blue things. The rest are green. How many are green? **3 green things.**. Red and blue together are 5 and 4, which is 9. The heap has 12. Count on from 9 to 12: 10, 11, 12. That is 3 more. So 3 things are green. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.number-facts.L1`)
- A fruit bowl has 4 bananas, 3 lemons and 2 apples. Sorted by kind, bananas are the biggest group. Now sort by colour: yellow and red. Which group is the biggest, and how many does it have? **Yellow, with 7 fruits.**. Bananas and lemons are both yellow. 4 and 3 make 7 yellow fruits. The apples are red, and there are 2. So yellow is the biggest group. The rule changed, and so did the biggest group. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- In a game of Roll and Mark, the row for 2 has 5 marks, the row for 4 has 3 marks and the row for 6 has 1 mark. The other rows are empty. How many times was the dice rolled? **9 times.**. Every roll made one mark. So count all the marks. 5 and 3 make 8, and 1 more makes 9. The dice was rolled 9 times. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
