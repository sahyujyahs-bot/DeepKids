# Repeating patterns: what comes next?
`math.patterns.repeating-patterns.L1`

**Summary.** Red, blue, red, blue, red. What comes next? You know before you see it. How? You will guess beans before they are put down, clap patterns, hunt for patterns at home and make a thoranam for your door. You will learn to find the part that repeats, carry a pattern on and spot a wrong piece. You will also find out that clap, stamp, clap, stamp and red, blue, red, blue are the same pattern.

**Intro.** *What comes next: red or blue?* Blue comes next. Red and blue are taking turns, and it is blue's turn. You knew that without seeing the next bead. That is what a pattern does: it tells you what is coming. In this unit you will test how much of a row you need to see before you can know the rest.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can spot a repeating pattern and say what repeats.
1. I can say what comes next in a pattern, and how I know.
2. I can find the part that repeats and say how long it is.
3. I can copy a pattern, carry it on and make my own.
4. I can make the same pattern with different things: colours, shapes, sounds and movements.
5. I can find a wrong piece or a missing piece in a pattern.

## Terms
- **Pattern**: Things set out by a rule, so that you can tell what comes next. *e.g.* Red, blue, red, blue is a pattern. You can tell that red comes next.
- **Repeat**: To do the same thing again. *e.g.* You clap, then you clap again. You have repeated the clap.
- **Repeating pattern**: A pattern where one small part comes again and again, in the same order. *e.g.* Leaf, flower, leaf, flower, leaf, flower. The part leaf, flower comes three times.
- **Part that repeats**: The smallest group of things that makes the whole pattern when you do it again and again. Grown-ups also call it the unit of repeat. *e.g.* In red, red, blue, red, red, blue, the part that repeats is red, red, blue. It is 3 long.
- **Rule**: What the maker of a pattern follows. It tells you which thing comes next. *e.g.* The rule 'leaf, leaf, flower, then start again' makes the row leaf, leaf, flower, leaf, leaf, flower.
- **Carry on**: To add more things to a pattern, keeping the same rule. *e.g.* The row is clap, stamp, clap, stamp. You carry it on with clap, stamp.
- **Beat**: One count in music. Beats come one after another at a steady speed, like footsteps. *e.g.* When you clap along to a song, each clap is on a beat.
- **Tala**: A repeating pattern of beats in Indian music. Singers keep it with claps, waves and finger counts. *e.g.* One way to keep the tala called Rupaka is clap, clap, wave, again and again.
- **Thoranam**: A string of leaves and flowers hung across the top of a door on festival days. It is also called a toran. *e.g.* A thoranam of mango leaves and marigolds: leaf, flower, leaf, flower.
- **Border**: A strip along the edge of something, often filled with a pattern. *e.g.* The border of a sari has the same small shape again and again, all the way along.

## Explanations
### What a repeating pattern is · *defined*
**Definition:** A repeating pattern is a row of things where one small part comes again and again, in the same order.

Look at the row: red, blue, red, blue, red, blue. The part red, blue comes three times. Nothing else is in the row. That makes it a repeating pattern. A pattern does not have to be colours. It can be shapes, sounds, movements or days. Clap, stamp, clap, stamp is a repeating pattern. So is day, night, day, night. A row with no part that comes again is not a repeating pattern. Red, blue, green, yellow, pink has five colours and no repeat.
- e.g. Leaf, flower, leaf, flower on a thoranam.
- e.g. Black, yellow, black, yellow on the kerb stones along a road.
- e.g. Tick, tock, tick, tock from a wall clock.
### The part that repeats · *defined*
**Definition:** The part that repeats is the smallest group of things that makes the whole pattern when you do it again and again.

Here is how to find it. Say the row aloud: red, red, blue, red, red, blue. Listen for where it starts again. It starts again after the blue. So the part that repeats is red, red, blue. It is 3 things long. Now check. Cover the first part with your hand. The rest of the row should begin in the same way. Always look for the smallest part. In a long red and blue row, red, blue, red, blue also comes again. But the smaller part, red, blue, already makes the whole row.
- e.g. Clap, stamp, clap, stamp: the part is clap, stamp. It is 2 long.
- e.g. Leaf, leaf, flower, leaf, leaf, flower: the part is leaf, leaf, flower. It is 3 long.
- e.g. Monday to Sunday, then Monday again: the part is 7 days long.
### How to work out what comes next · *defined*
**Definition:** To find what comes next, find the part that repeats. Then see how far into the part the row has got.

Here is a worked example. The row is leaf, leaf, flower, leaf, leaf, flower, leaf. Step 1: find the part that repeats. It is leaf, leaf, flower. Step 2: look at the end of the row. After the last flower there is one leaf. So the row is one step into a new part. Step 3: the second thing in the part is a leaf. So a leaf comes next, and after it a flower. A common slip is to look only at the last thing. The last thing is a leaf. But in this pattern a leaf is sometimes followed by a leaf and sometimes by a flower. You need the whole part to know.
- e.g. Clap, stamp, clap, stamp, clap: the part is clap, stamp, so stamp comes next.
- e.g. Red, blue, blue, red, blue: the part is red, blue, blue, so blue comes next.
- e.g. Idli, idli, vada, idli, idli, vada: the part has ended, so it starts again with idli.
### The same pattern in different things · *defined*
**Definition:** Two rows are the same pattern when their things take turns in the same way, even if the things are different.

Clap, stamp, clap, stamp. Red, blue, red, blue. Circle, triangle, circle, triangle. Stand, squat, stand, squat. All four go like this: one thing, another thing, the first thing again, the other thing again. To check, do two of them together. Each time you clap, point at a red bead. Each time you stamp, point at a blue bead. If you reach the end without getting stuck, they are the same pattern. Grown-ups name patterns with letters. They call the first thing A and the second thing B. So all four rows are AB patterns. Clap, clap, stamp is an AAB pattern. Clap, stamp, pat is an ABC pattern.
- e.g. Tick, tock, tick, tock and left foot, right foot, left foot, right foot are both AB patterns.
- e.g. Rajma, rajma, chana and clap, clap, wave are both AAB patterns.
- e.g. Red, blue, yellow and ta, ka, di have three different things each. Both are ABC patterns.
### A part can have the same thing twice · *defined*
**Definition:** A part that repeats can have the same thing in it twice. Red, red, blue is a different pattern from red, blue.

In a red, blue pattern, the colour changes every time. In a red, red, blue pattern, it does not. Red comes twice, then blue comes once. What tells the two patterns apart is the part that repeats. Red, blue is 2 long. Red, red, blue is 3 long. Red, blue, blue is also 3 long. But it is a third pattern, because now blue is the one that comes twice. Patterns like these often catch people out at the second bead. They expect a change, and the same bean comes again.
- e.g. Clap, clap, wave: the clap comes twice.
- e.g. Idli, idli, vada on a plate, again and again.
- e.g. Step, step, hop: two steps and then one hop.
### Finding a wrong piece or a missing piece · *defined*
**Definition:** A wrong piece is one that does not fit the part that repeats. You find it by saying the part again and again along the row.

Here is a worked example. The row is red, blue, red, blue, blue, blue. Step 1: find the part from the start of the row. It is red, blue. Step 2: touch each bead and say the part over and over: red, blue, red, blue, red, blue. Step 3: stop where your word and the bead do not match. At place 5 you say red, but the bead is blue. Place 5 has the wrong piece. A missing piece is found in the same way. Say the part along the row. The word you say at the gap is the missing piece.
- e.g. Leaf, flower, leaf, leaf, leaf, flower: place 4 should be a flower.
- e.g. Clap, stamp, clap, (gap), clap, stamp: the gap should be a stamp.
- e.g. Tick, tock, tick, tick: the last sound should be tock.
### Patterns in time: day and night, the week, the year · *defined*
**Definition:** A pattern in time is something that happens again and again, in the same order.

Day, night, day, night is a repeating pattern. It happens because the Earth is a ball that spins. One full spin takes about 24 hours. When your side of the Earth faces the Sun, it is day. When your side faces away, it is night. The days of the week are a pattern with a part 7 long: Monday to Sunday, then Monday again. People chose to have 7 days in a week. The Earth did not choose it. The year is a pattern too. The Earth goes once round the Sun in about 365 days. So the hot months and the rains come back every year. These patterns are not perfectly even. The light part of the day is a little longer in summer than in winter. We still call each one a day.
- e.g. Your heart: lub, dub, lub, dub.
- e.g. Your breath: in, out, in, out.
- e.g. Breakfast, lunch, dinner, then breakfast again.
### Other living beings make patterns too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings make repeating patterns with their voices, their lights and their bodies.

A coppersmith barbet is a small green bird with a red forehead, who lives in many Indian towns. They call tuk, tuk, tuk at a steady speed, about two tuks each second. One call can go on for as many as two hundred tuks. A common hawk-cuckoo, also called the brainfever bird, sings a call of three notes. They repeat it five or six times, louder each time, and then stop suddenly. Fireflies make patterns with light. Each kind of firefly flashes in a pattern of their own. That is how fireflies of one kind find each other in the dark. Plants make patterns as they grow. A rice plant or a maize plant puts out one leaf on one side, the next leaf on the other side, and so on up the stem: left, right, left, right.
- e.g. A barbet's call: tuk, tuk, tuk, tuk.
- e.g. A hawk-cuckoo's call: three notes, then the same three notes again, louder.
- e.g. A maize plant's leaves: left, right, left, right.
- **What the barbet is doing:** The barbet keeps a steady beat for minutes at a time, as a drummer does. Other barbets hear the call and know who is there.
- **Some scientists:** They say the call is an automatic habit of the bird's body, and that the bird is not making a pattern on purpose.
### Can you ever be sure what comes next? · *debated*
**Definition:** A few beads can fit more than one rule. The maker of the pattern is the one who knows the rule.

You see red, blue, red, blue. What comes next? Almost everyone says red. That is the best guess. The simplest rule that fits is red, blue, again and again. But another rule also fits what you have seen: red, blue, red, blue, green, and then all five again. With that rule, green comes next. So a few beads fit more than one rule. Only the maker knows which rule she is using. The more of the row you see, the safer your guess becomes. In school questions, people agree to pick the simplest rule.
- e.g. Red, blue, red, blue, then green: the part could be 5 long.
- e.g. Idli on Monday, dosa on Tuesday, idli on Wednesday. Will Thursday be dosa? Only the cook knows.
- **Most school books:** There is one right answer. Find the smallest part that repeats and carry it on.
- **Many mathematicians:** Any rule that fits the beads you have seen is allowed. Picking the simplest rule is a sensible habit that people agreed on. It is not a law.

## Mostly agreed
- Once you know the part that repeats, you can say what comes next without seeing it. *How we know:* In Guess My Next Bean, the crosses come at the start of each row. After the part has come round twice, careful guessers get every bean right.
- The same pattern can be made with sounds, colours, shapes or movements. *How we know:* In One Pattern, Four Ways, you clap and stamp while pointing along a row of beans. Every clap lands on one kind of bean and every stamp on the other, with nothing left over.
- Day and night take turns because the Earth spins. *How we know:* Astronauts and satellites watch the Earth turn. One full turn takes about 24 hours. As the Earth turns, the line between day and night moves across the land.
- Small children who are good with patterns tend to do well in maths when they are older. *How we know:* Researchers in the USA followed 517 children from age 4 to age 11. How well a child did with patterns at age 4 helped to predict that child's maths marks in Class 5 (Rittle-Johnson and others, 2017). This does not prove that playing with patterns causes the better marks.

## How we found out
**Marina Papic, Joanne Mulligan and Michael Mitchelmore, 2011.** For many years, teachers gave small children rows of beads to copy and carry on. If a child got the beads right, people thought the child understood the pattern. Three researchers in Australia, Marina Papic, Joanne Mulligan and Michael Mitchelmore, tested this. They worked with 53 children from two preschools, in the year before the children started school. They asked each child to copy patterns, carry them on and talk about them. Many children saw a row only as things taking turns: red, then blue, then red. They did not see that one small part was being repeated. In one of the preschools, the teachers then spent six months helping children to find that part. Afterwards those children did much better on pattern tasks than the children at the other preschool. They were still ahead a year later, after their first year at school. The study came out in 2011.  
*What people believed before:* A child who can carry on a row of beads understands the pattern. The 2011 study showed this was wrong. Many children carried rows on by treating them as things taking turns, without seeing the part that repeats.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If a row goes red, blue, red, blue, the next bead must be red.** (Most pattern worksheets; *debated*)  
  For: Red is the simplest way to carry the row on. It is the answer nearly everyone expects.  
  Against: Four beads fit more than one rule. The maker could be using red, blue, red, blue, green. Red is the best guess, but only the maker knows the rule.
- **Patterns are only for decoration. They are not real maths.** (Many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Borders, rangoli and thoranams are made to look good. Nobody needs numbers to enjoy them.  
  Against: The English mathematician G. H. Hardy wrote in 1940 that a mathematician is 'a maker of patterns'. Finding the part that repeats, and using it to say what comes next, is the same thinking a child later uses in times tables and algebra.
- **Nothing ever really repeats, because no two things are exactly the same.** (An idea that follows from Heraclitus, a Greek thinker of about 2,500 years ago, who said that everything keeps changing; *debated*)  
  For: No two marigolds are the same size. No two claps are equally loud. Today is not the same as yesterday.  
  Against: A pattern does not need its things to be exactly alike. It needs us to agree what counts as the same: any marigold is a flower, any clap is a clap. With that agreement, a pattern tells us what comes next, and the guess comes true.

## Roots
We guessed beans, clapped, hunted for patterns and made a thoranam. Now let us think about what a pattern really is.

**Deep truth.** In One Pattern, Four Ways, you clapped and stamped, then laid rajma and chana in the same way. A clap is nothing like a bean. Still, you called them the same pattern. So a pattern is not the things. It is the way the things take turns.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a pattern is made of its things: these colours, these beads. But you can swap every thing for another, and the pattern is still there. And we are the ones who say the two rows are the same.  
*Evidence:* In One Pattern, Four Ways, one pattern was made with claps, beans, drawn shapes and your body. In Guess My Next Bean, you knew the next bean from the turns, before the bean was put down.

- *who am i:* Your heart goes lub, dub, lub, dub. Your feet go left, right, left, right. You are full of patterns. You are also the one who notices them.
- *what is life:* Many living beings make patterns. A coppersmith barbet calls tuk, tuk, tuk. A rice plant puts out leaves left, right, left, right.
- *what is this world:* Day follows night again and again, because the Earth keeps turning. But no two days are exactly alike. We choose to call each one a day.

Start with one thing: a clap. Do it again, and now there is a repeat. Put a stamp in between, and there is a pattern. From that one small part, a row can go on as long as you like. A long thoranam, a whole song and a year of days all grow from one small part, done again and again.

- **Bhagavad Gita 8.19:** The Gita says that the whole crowd of living beings comes out when Brahma's day begins and melts away when the night comes, again and again. (This is day and night seen as one huge repeating pattern. The same part, day and then night, comes round again and again.)
- **Ecclesiastes 1:5:** This old Hebrew book says that the sun rises and the sun goes down, and hurries back to the place where it rises. (People far from India watched the same pattern in the sky long ago, and wrote it down.)
- **Heraclitus, as reported by Plato in Cratylus 402a:** Heraclitus, a Greek thinker, said that you could not step twice into the same river, because the water keeps flowing. (This is the other side of every pattern. No two claps and no two days are exactly alike. To see a pattern, we agree to treat them as the same.)
- Maths (settled): Small children can copy a row of beads, and carry it on, before they can make the same pattern with new things. Researchers in the USA found this with 4-year-olds (Rittle-Johnson and others, 2013). Seeing that the pattern is not the things is the harder step.
- Maths (open): Is a pattern out in the world, or in the mind that notices it? Thinkers still argue about this.
- Other view, Many scientists and mathematicians: They say patterns are in the world, whether or not anyone notices them. Day and night took turns for a very long time before there were any people to call it a pattern. The Earth turns the same way with nobody watching. They say it is not up to us which things are the same. We find out.
- Practice, Listen to your own pattern (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Put one hand on your chest. Feel your heart beat, again and again. Now feel your breath go in and out, in and out. Stay with it for ten breaths. Then open your eyes. Which was faster, your heart or your breath? Was every breath exactly the same?
- Ask: Is the pattern in the beads, or in the one who looks at them?
- Ask: If nobody heard the barbet, would tuk, tuk, tuk still be a pattern?
- Ask: No two days are exactly alike. Why do we still say that day and night repeat?
- Ask: Can something happen only once and still be a pattern?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* How many beads do you need to see before you can call a row a pattern? (hint: Is one red and one blue enough? What about red, blue, red?)
- *deep:* Is clap, clap, clap, clap a pattern? What is the part that repeats? (hint: Can a part be just one thing?)
- *deep:* Can two people look at the same row and find different parts that repeat? (hint: Look at blue, red, blue, red, blue, red. Now cover the first bead. What does the row look like?)
- *big:* Why does it feel good to know what comes next? (hint: Think of a song you know well, or a story you have heard many times.)
- *big:* What would life be like if day and night did not take turns? (hint: When would you sleep? When would the birds sing?)
- *big:* Does a barbet know they are making a pattern? (hint: How could you find out? What would you watch for?)

## Story: Tak, Tak, Tok
*Yazhini, Kabilan and Selvi akka are made up, and so is this three-strike step. Kolattam is a real stick dance from Tamil Nadu. It is danced with two sticks, often at Pongal and Navaratri.*

Yazhini was eight, and this year she was in the kolattam line.

Kolattam is a dance with sticks. Each dancer holds two short wooden sticks, one in each hand. The dancers stand in two rows, each facing a partner, and strike their sticks in time with a song.

The dance was for Pongal evening, at the end of their street in Thanjavur. There were four days left.

Her cousin Kabilan was six. He had come from Tiruchi for the holidays, and he wanted to dance too.

"He is small," said Selvi akka, who taught the dance. "But there is a gap in the line, right across from you. If he can learn the step by Thursday, he is in."

The singer began. The sticks began. Tak, tak, tok. Tak, tak, tok.

Kabilan fixed his eyes on Yazhini's hands. When her sticks tapped each other, he tapped his. When her right stick came across, he sent his own across to meet it.

But he was always a little late. By the time he tapped his sticks together, she was already reaching over. Her stick met his fingers.

"Ow!"

"Sorry! Again."

Tak, tak. "Ow!"

On the second day it was the same. Kabilan watched harder. He watched so hard that he forgot to blink. The harder he watched, the later he was.

On the third day Selvi akka looked at his red knuckles and sighed. "Maybe next year, kanna."

Kabilan sat down on the temple step with his sticks across his knees. He did not cry, but only just.

Yazhini sat down next to him. She was hot and cross. She wanted a partner, and she did not want to tell him to go home.

How do I do it? she wondered. I never look at anyone's hands. So how do I know when to reach across?

She tried to catch herself knowing it. She could not. So she shut her eyes and listened to the others, who were still dancing.

Tak, tak, tok. Tak, tak, tok. Tak, tak, tok.

It was only three sounds. The same three, over and over. Nobody out there was dancing a long dance. They were dancing a very short one, again and again.

"Kabilan," she said. "Stop looking at me."

"Then how will I know what you are doing?"

"You already know. Close your eyes. Say it with me. Mine, mine, yours."

"Mine, mine, yours," said Kabilan.

"Again. Do not stop."

"Mine, mine, yours. Mine, mine, yours. Mine, mine, yours." His fingers began to tap on the step all by themselves. Two small taps, one big one.

"Now tell me. After mine, mine, what comes?"

"Yours."

"Do you need to see me move first?"

Kabilan opened his eyes. He thought about it. "No," he said slowly. "It is always yours."

They stood up. "Look at my nose, not my hands," said Yazhini. "And keep saying it."

Mine, mine. Their sticks came across at the very same moment and met in the middle. Tok! It was a clean, loud tok, the best one all week.

Mine, mine, tok. Mine, mine, tok. On the fourth time round, Kabilan grinned so widely that he lost his place and hit the air.

"Start the part again," said Yazhini. "It is always the same part."

On Pongal evening there was a kolam at every door, and mango leaves strung over every doorway. The singer sang faster and faster towards the end, the way she always did. Kabilan's lips moved. Mine, mine, yours. He did not look at Yazhini's hands once. The last tok rang out, and Selvi akka nodded at him from the side.

They walked home in the dark. Kabilan ran one stick along a gate. Tak, tak, tak.

"Akka, look," he said. "The street lights. Light, dark, light, dark."

"Mm."

"And the kerb. Black, yellow, black, yellow. And listen, somebody's cooker is whistling. Whistle, wait, whistle, wait."

"Now you will never stop," said Yazhini.

Kabilan pointed his stick down the long street, at the next light, and the dark after it, and the light after that.

"I know what comes next," he said. And he did.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kabilan fails while he copies one move at a time, because a copy always comes late. He gets it when he hears the small part that repeats: mine, mine, yours. Then he knows the next move before it comes. Knowing a pattern means knowing what comes next without being shown.

- Ask: Why was Kabilan always late when he watched Yazhini's hands?
- Ask: What was the part that repeated in the dance?
- Ask: How did saying 'mine, mine, yours' help Kabilan?
- Ask: On the walk home, Kabilan found patterns everywhere. Which patterns can you find on your street?

## Games
### g1 · Pattern Train (10 min)
- **rules:** You need a dice and three kinds of small kitchen things, a small bowl of each. For example: rajma for red, chana for blue and groundnuts for yellow.; Each player takes a rule card and keeps it face up. Everyone agrees which thing stands for which colour.; On your turn, roll the dice. Add that many pieces to your train. Say each piece aloud as you put it down.; Every new piece must follow your rule card. When you reach the end of the card, start the card again.; The other players watch. If someone spots a wrong piece, they say 'Check!'. You take back all the pieces from that turn.; The first train to reach 12 pieces wins. The winner reads the whole train aloud.; Playing alone: can you reach 12 pieces in four rolls with no wrong piece? Check by reading your train aloud against the card.; A harder game: turn your rule card face down after your first turn.
- **Printable cards: 8 rule cards to cut out:** 🔴🔵 red, blue; 🔴🔴🔵 red, red, blue; 🔴🔵🔵 red, blue, blue; 🔴🔵🟡 red, blue, yellow; 🔴🔴🔵🔵 red, red, blue, blue; 🔴🔴🔴🔵 red, red, red, blue; 🔴🔵🔴🟡 red, blue, red, yellow; 🔴🔴🔵🟡 red, red, blue, yellow
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the rule cards, or copy them on slips of paper. 2. Put out three bowls of small things. 3. Agree which thing stands for red, for blue and for yellow. 4. Each player takes a rule card. Read the rules aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Each of us gets a rule card. Our trains must follow our cards. First to 12 pieces wins.  
  *Child mode:* Take a rule card. Choose a thing for each colour. Your train must follow your card.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one practice turn each. 2. Roll, add the pieces and say each one aloud. 3. Show what to do when the card ends in the middle of a turn: start the card again.  
  *Parent says:* I rolled 4. My card is red, red, blue. So I put red, red, blue, and then red again.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the dice. Add that many pieces. Say each one aloud. Follow your card.
  - Note: The most common slip is to start the card from its beginning on every turn. Ask your child to read the whole train aloud before adding to it.
  - Check: Your card says red, blue, blue. Your train is red, blue, blue, red. You roll 2. Which pieces do you add? → **blue, blue**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game. 2. Watch each other's trains. 3. Now and then, ask a player what their next piece will be before they roll.  
  *Parent says:* Before you roll, tell me what your next piece will be. How do you know?  
  *Child mode:* Play the game. Watch the other trains. Say 'Check!' if you see a wrong piece.
  - Ask: Your train has 9 pieces. What will your 10th piece be? (good: I read my train to the end, then I say the next one on my card.)
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When a train reaches 12 pieces, the game ends. 2. The winner reads the whole train aloud. 3. Everyone checks it against the card.  
  *Parent says:* Read your whole train to us. Does every piece follow your card?  
  *Child mode:* The first train with 12 pieces wins. Read your whole train aloud.
### g2 · Which Card Did I Clap? (10 min)
- **rules:** Lay the 8 action cards face up where everyone can see them. A pat means patting your lap with both hands.; One player is the leader. The leader secretly chooses a card and does its actions three times round, without stopping.; The others watch and listen. Then each one points to the card they think it was.; The leader says which card it was. Everyone who pointed to it wins one bean.; Then all the players do that pattern together, two more times round.; If nobody found the card, the leader does it again, more slowly.; Take turns to be the leader. When everyone has led twice, the player with the most beans wins.
- **Printable cards: 8 action cards to cut out:** 👏🦶 clap, stamp; 👏✋ clap, pat; 👏👏🦶 clap, clap, stamp; 👏🦶🦶 clap, stamp, stamp; 👏🦶✋ clap, stamp, pat; ✋✋👏 pat, pat, clap; 👏✋✋ clap, pat, pat; 👏👏🦶🦶 clap, clap, stamp, stamp
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 8 action cards, or copy them on slips of paper. 2. Lay them face up. 3. Practise the three actions together: clap, stamp, pat your lap. 4. Read the rules aloud. Put a small heap of beans nearby for scoring.  
  *Parent says:* There are three actions: clap, stamp and pat your lap. Let us try each one.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the 8 cards face up. Practise the actions: clap, stamp, pat your lap.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Be the leader for a practice round. 2. Secretly choose the card clap, stamp, stamp. 3. Do it three times round at a steady speed. 4. Your child points to a card.  
  *Parent says:* Listen and watch. I will do one of these cards three times round. Which card is it?  
  *Child mode:* Watch and listen. The leader acts out one card. Point to the card you think it is.
  - Note: Keep the speed steady and do not pause between rounds. The player has to hear where the part starts again.
  - Check: You hear clap, stamp, stamp, clap, stamp, stamp. Which card is it? → **clap, stamp, stamp**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the game. 2. Take turns to be the leader. 3. After each round, everyone does the pattern together two more times.  
  *Parent says:* Your turn to lead. Choose a card in your head. Do it three times round.  
  *Child mode:* Take turns to lead. Do your card three times round. Do not stop in the middle.
  - Ask: How did you know which card it was? (good: I heard two stamps together.; It started again after three actions.; I said it in my head.)
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When everyone has led twice, count the beans. 2. Ask which card was the hardest to find, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Count your beans. Which card was the hardest to find? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your beans. Which card was the hardest to find?

## Experiments
### e1 · Guess My Next Bean: when can you know what is coming? (25 min)
- **question:** How many beans do you need to see before you can say what comes next, every time?
- **predict:** Before each row, guess: will this row be easy or hard to guess? Why do you think so? Then, before every bean, guess which kind is coming.
- **fair test:** The grown-up chooses each row's rule before starting and does not change it halfway. The child guesses before every bean, even the first ones. Every guess gets a tick or a cross, so no guess is forgotten.
- **safety:** Keep beans and buttons away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** The beans are laid on paper, and a tick or a cross is written under each one. In a table, write what took turns in each row and the bean number of the last cross. Under the table, a grown-up writes what the child found, in the child's own words.
- **results table:** Row | My guess: easy or hard? | A tick or a cross under each bean | The bean number of my last cross
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep the beans in a bowl on your side. 2. Say you will make a row, one bean at a time. 3. Before each bean, your child guesses which kind is coming. 4. Put the bean down on the paper. Write a tick or a cross under it. 5. Row 1 is rajma, chana, rajma, chana, up to 12 beans.  
  *Parent says:* I am making a row of beans. Before I put each one down, guess which bean is coming.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes a row, one bean at a time. Guess each bean first. Then look. A right guess gets a tick.
  - Note: Do not say the word 'pattern' yet. The first guess or two can only be luck. That is fine. Keep a straight face and keep the same speed for every bean.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the ticks and crosses together. 2. Ask where the crosses are. 3. Ask how your child knew the later beans. 4. Just listen. Do not explain yet.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your ticks and crosses. Where are the crosses? How did you know the later beans?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your ticks and crosses. Where are the crosses? How did you know the later beans?
  - Ask: How did you know what was coming? (good: They take turns.; It goes rajma, chana, rajma, chana.; It keeps doing the same thing.)
  - Note: Most children get a cross or two at the start, and then all ticks. Whatever your child's sheet shows, ask about it. Do not correct it.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear the paper. Show a rajma, a chana and a button. 2. Ask if this row will be easier or harder, and why. 3. Write the guess. 4. Row 2 is rajma, chana, button, then the same again, up to 12. 5. Your child guesses every bean. Mark ticks and crosses.  
  *Parent says:* This time I will use three kinds. Will this row be easier or harder to guess? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Now there are three kinds. Will it be easier or harder? Say why. Then guess each bean.
  - Note: Many children guess rajma for the third bean, because row 1 went back to rajma. The button is a surprise. Let it be one.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear the paper. Put the buttons away. 2. Ask if this row will be easier or harder than the button row, and why. 3. Row 3 is rajma, rajma, chana, then the same again, up to 12. 4. Your child guesses every bean. Mark ticks and crosses.  
  *Parent says:* Now only rajma and chana again. Will it be easier or harder than the button row? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Only rajma and chana now. Will it be easy or hard? Say why. Then guess each bean.
  - Note: The second rajma is the surprise in this row. Children who expect a change every time get crosses at beans 2 and 3. Some get one more cross at bean 5.
  - Check: A row goes rajma, rajma, chana, rajma, rajma, chana, rajma. What comes next? → **rajma**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find the last cross in each row. 2. Write its bean number in the table. 3. Ask which row had crosses for the longest, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Find your last cross in each row. Which row took you the longest to work out? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Find your last cross in each row. Which row took the longest to work out?
  - Ask: Why did that row take longer to work out? (good: Three things took turns, not two.; Rajma came twice, and I did not expect it.; I had to see more beans before I knew.)
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap places. Give your child the bowl. 2. Your child makes a secret row for you. 3. Guess each bean aloud before your child puts one down. 4. Guess wrong once or twice at the start.  
  *Parent says:* Now you make a row for me. Choose what takes turns. Do not tell me.  
  *Child mode:* Now you make the row. Choose what takes turns. Keep it the same all the way.
  - Note: If your child's row stops repeating, do not call it wrong. Ask: 'Can I ever guess this right? What would help me?' Children usually see that the guesser needs the same part to come again.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what your child found out. 2. Ask what they saw that makes them think so. 3. Write their words under the table. 4. Only then give the name: a repeating pattern.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find out? Tell me what you saw. Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found out. Say what you saw. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: When could you be sure of the next bean? (good: After it started again.; When I had seen the same part two times.; At the start I could only guess. Later I knew.)
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this: 'At the start I could only guess. When the same part had come round two times, I knew every bean.' After your child has said it in their own words, you can say that a row like this is called a repeating pattern.

## Activities
### a1 · One Pattern, Four Ways (10 min)
- **problem:** Can a sound pattern and a bean pattern be the same pattern?
- **record:** Draw the four rows one under the other: the sounds, the beans, the shapes and the body moves. Line them up so that each clap sits above one bean and one shape.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clap once, then stamp once. 2. Keep going: clap, stamp, clap, stamp. 3. Your child joins in. 4. Stop suddenly after a clap. Ask what comes next.  
  *Parent says:* Clap, stamp, clap, stamp. Join in. When I stop, tell me what comes next.  
  *Child mode:* Clap, stamp, clap, stamp. Keep going. Stop after a clap. What comes next?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put out the rajma and the chana. 2. Ask your child to make the clap and stamp pattern with beans. 3. Let your child choose which bean is the clap. 4. Clap and stamp slowly while your child points along the row.  
  *Parent says:* Now make the same pattern with beans. Which bean will be the clap? Which will be the stamp?  
  *Child mode:* Make the same pattern with beans. Choose a bean for clap. Choose a bean for stamp.
  - Ask: How did you decide which bean to put next? (good: Clap is rajma, so after rajma comes chana.; I said clap, stamp in my head.; They take turns like the sounds.)
  - Note: Some children lay all the rajma first and then all the chana. If so, clap and stamp slowly and ask them to point to a bean for each sound. They will find the row does not fit.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws the pattern with two shapes: a circle for clap and a triangle for stamp. 2. Then your child does it with the body: stand tall for clap, squat for stamp. 3. Do the body pattern together four times round.  
  *Parent says:* Now draw it. Use a circle for clap and a triangle for stamp. Then show me with your whole body.  
  *Child mode:* Draw it: a circle for clap, a triangle for stamp. Then stand for clap and squat for stamp.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Point to the bean row and clap once. 2. Ask whether the claps and the beans are the same pattern. 3. Ask what is the same about them. 4. Listen first. Then say the idea in one sentence.  
  *Parent says:* A clap is a sound. A bean is a thing. Are these two the same pattern? What is the same about them?  
  *Child mode:* A clap is a sound. A bean is a thing. Are they the same pattern? What is the same?
  - Ask: What is the same about the claps and the beans? (good: They both go one, the other, one, the other.; Two things take turns.; When I clap, I can point at a rajma every time.)
  - Note: After your child has answered, you can say it: the pattern is the way the things take turns. The things themselves can change.
  - Check: Which bean row is the same pattern as clap, clap, stamp, clap, clap, stamp? → **rajma, rajma, chana, rajma, rajma, chana**
### a2 · Pattern Hunt at Home (15 min)
- **problem:** Which things in your home are repeating patterns, and which are not?
- **record:** Draw three patterns you found. Draw a loop round the part that repeats in each one.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Walk round your home together. 2. Look at floors, window grills, towels, bedsheets, saris, shirts and railings. 3. Your child finds three things that repeat. 4. Write where each one was.  
  *Parent says:* Let us hunt for patterns. Find three things in our home that do the same thing again and again.  
  *Child mode:* Walk round your home. Find three things that repeat. Look at tiles, grills, stripes and borders.
  - Note: Good places to look: floor tiles, a window grill, a striped towel or shirt, the border of a sari, dupatta or bedsheet, a stack of bangles, the stairs.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go back to each pattern. 2. Your child touches along it and says it aloud. 3. Ask which part repeats and how long it is. 4. Your child draws the pattern and loops one part.  
  *Parent says:* Touch along it and say what you see. Which part comes again and again? How many things are in it?  
  *Child mode:* Touch along each pattern. Say it aloud. Draw it. Loop the part that repeats.
  - Ask: How long is the part that repeats? (good: Two: bar, gap.; Three: thin stripe, thin stripe, thick stripe.; It is one flower shape, then a dot.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now find one thing that is not a repeating pattern. 2. Try the vegetable basket, a shelf of books or the things on a table. 3. Ask why it is not a repeating pattern. 4. Ask how it could be made into one.  
  *Parent says:* Now find something that is not a repeating pattern. How do you know it is not one?  
  *Child mode:* Find one thing that is not a repeating pattern. Say why not.
  - Ask: How could you turn it into a repeating pattern? (good: Line up the vegetables: tomato, onion, tomato, onion.; Put the books tall, short, tall, short.)
  - Check: Which of these is a repeating pattern? → **Stripes on a towel: blue, white, blue, white**
### a3 · Maths in life: a thoranam for your door (20 min)
- **problem:** A thoranam looks right only if its pattern is kept all the way along. How do you plan it so that you cut the right number of each piece?
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting with sharp scissors, and all the work with a needle.
- **record:** Draw your thoranam. Write its rule under it, for example 'leaf, leaf, flower', and how many times the part comes.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child what a thoranam is: a string of leaves and flowers over a door on festival days. 2. Your child chooses a pattern for it. 3. Your child says the rule aloud, three times round. 4. Draw the plan on paper.  
  *Parent says:* We will make a thoranam for our door. Choose a pattern of leaves and flowers. Say it to me.  
  *Child mode:* Choose a pattern of leaves and flowers. Say it aloud three times round.
  - Note: Leaf, flower is the simplest. Leaf, leaf, flower and leaf, flower, flower are good next choices. Let your child choose.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Decide how many times the part will come. Four to six times fits most doors. 2. Ask how many leaves and how many flowers that needs. 3. Your child draws leaf and flower shapes about as long as a finger. 4. Cut them out.  
  *Parent says:* Your part will come six times. How many leaves do we need? How many flowers?  
  *Child mode:* How many times will your part come? Count the leaves you need. Count the flowers. Draw them.
  - Ask: Your pattern is leaf, leaf, flower and you want it 4 times. How many leaves must we cut? (good: 8.; Two leaves each time: 2, 4, 6, 8.)
  - Note: Let your child work it out by drawing the plan and counting, or by counting in twos. Do not give the number.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lays all the pieces in a row on the floor, in order. 2. Your child reads the row aloud from one end to the other. 3. Fix any piece that is out of place. 4. Lay the string along the top and tape each piece to it.  
  *Parent says:* Lay all the pieces in order first. Read the row to me. Is every piece in the right place?  
  *Child mode:* Lay the pieces in a row. Read the row aloud. Then tape each piece to the string.
  - Note: Laying the row first lets your child find and fix a wrong piece before the tape goes on. If a piece is wrong, ask your child to read the row aloud again and listen for it.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hang the thoranam across the top of a door. 2. Ask someone else at home to read the pattern aloud. 3. Ask them what would come next if it were longer.  
  *Parent says:* Ask someone to read your thoranam. Can they tell what would come next?  
  *Child mode:* Hang it over a door. Ask someone to read it. Can they say what comes next?
  - Check: A thoranam goes leaf, leaf, flower, leaf, leaf, flower, leaf. What are the next two pieces? → **leaf, flower**
### a4 · Maths in life: Ta Ka Di Mi, patterns you say and clap (10 min)
- **problem:** Musicians and dancers must all stay together. How does a repeating pattern of claps help them?
- **record:** Draw the hand pattern clap, clap, wave three times round. Use a hand shape for a clap and a wavy line for a wave.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit facing each other. 2. Say ta ka di mi slowly and evenly, round and round. 3. Clap your hand on your knee on every ta. 4. Go round eight times. Then stop and ask what the part that repeats is.  
  *Parent says:* Say it with me: ta ka di mi, ta ka di mi. Clap on every ta. Do not stop.  
  *Child mode:* Say ta ka di mi, round and round. Clap on every ta. Go round eight times.
  - Note: Saying rhythm sounds like these is called solkattu. Carnatic drummers and dancers learn their patterns this way. Keep the speed steady, like slow footsteps.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Change to ta ki ta, round and round. 2. Clap on the first ta each time. 3. Go round eight times. 4. Ask which pattern has the longer part: ta ka di mi or ta ki ta.  
  *Parent says:* Now a new one: ta ki ta, ta ki ta. Clap on the first ta. Which pattern has the longer part?  
  *Child mode:* Now say ta ki ta, round and round. Clap on the first ta. Which part is longer?
  - Ask: How many sounds are in the part ta ki ta? How many in ta ka di mi? (good: Three, and four.; Ta ka di mi is longer by one.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show a wave: turn your hand palm up beside your knee. 2. Keep clap, clap, wave, round and round, at a steady speed. 3. Your child joins in for eight rounds. 4. Ask which bean row from the bean test goes the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Carnatic singers keep time with their hands. Try this one: clap, clap, wave. Which bean row went the same way?  
  *Child mode:* Clap, clap, wave. Keep going for eight rounds. Which bean row went the same way?
  - Ask: Which bean row went like clap, clap, wave? (good: Rajma, rajma, chana.; The one where rajma came twice.)
  - Note: Many Carnatic students keep the tala called Rupaka in this way: two claps and a wave. If someone at home sings, ask them to show you.
  - Check: Clap, clap, wave, clap, clap, wave. Which is the part that repeats? → **clap, clap, wave**
### a5 · Everyone does maths: who else makes patterns? (10 min)
- **problem:** Who and what around your home makes a repeating pattern of sound?
- **record:** Write one sound pattern with marks: a dot for a short sound, a line for a long sound and a gap for a silence. Write who or what made it.

  - Note: There is always something: a wall clock, a ceiling fan, a dripping tap, a pressure cooker, a crow, a vendor's call, a pigeon. In the morning or evening you may hear a barbet or a koel.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child picks one sound from the list. 2. Your child writes it with marks: a dot for a short sound, a line for a long one. 3. Your child loops the part that repeats.  
  *Parent says:* Pick one sound. Write it with dots and lines. Then loop the part that repeats.  
  *Child mode:* Pick one sound. Write it with dots and lines. Loop the part that repeats.

  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child about the coppersmith barbet, a small green bird with a red forehead. 2. The barbet calls tuk, tuk, tuk at a steady speed, about two tuks each second. 3. Tap that speed on the floor together for ten seconds. 4. Ask what the part that repeats is.

  *Child mode:* A barbet calls tuk, tuk, tuk. Tap it: two taps each second. What part repeats?
  - Ask: What is the part that repeats in tuk, tuk, tuk? (good: Just one tuk.; Tuk and then a little gap.)

## Challenges
### c1 · Stretch: a bookmark with a part four squares long (20 min)
- **problem:** Design a bookmark of 12 squares in two colours. Its part that repeats must be exactly 4 squares long, and it must fit exactly 3 times.
- **your turn:** Now design your own part, 4 squares long, in your two colours. Colour it 3 times along the strip. Then check that no shorter part makes the same bookmark.
- **constraints:** Use only two colours.; The part that repeats must be exactly 4 squares long, not shorter.; The bookmark is 12 squares long, so the part must come exactly 3 times.
- **general:** red, red, blue, blue; red, red, red, blue; red, blue, blue, blue
- **creative:** red, blue, blue, red: the two ends of the part match; red, red, blue, red: the single blue is not at the end; Two bookmarks that are the same pattern, but one starts one square later than the other
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut a strip of squared paper 12 squares long, and a few spare strips. 2. Read the problem and the three limits aloud. 3. Your child colours the first 4 squares of a spare strip in any way, using both colours. 4. Your child repeats those 4 squares to the end.  
  *Parent says:* Colour the first four squares any way you like, with both colours. Then do the same four again, to the end.  
  *Child mode:* Colour 4 squares with two colours. Then colour the same 4 again, to the end.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On a spare strip, colour red, blue, red, blue, to the end. 2. Ask whether its part is really 4 squares long. 3. Cover all but the first 2 squares. Ask whether those 2 already make the whole strip.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a part: red, blue, red, blue. Is it really four squares long? Could a shorter part make the same strip?  
  *Child mode:* Look at red, blue, red, blue. Is the part really 4 long? Could a shorter part do it?
  - Ask: Why does red, blue, red, blue break the rule of this challenge? (good: Red, blue already makes it.; It is really 2 long, done twice.)
  - Note: This is the trap in the challenge. Red, blue, red, blue does come again and again, but the smaller part red, blue makes the same strip. People have agreed that the part that repeats means the smallest one.
  - Check: A strip goes red, blue, red, blue, red, blue. How long is the part that repeats? → **2 squares**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child finds as many different parts as they can that are truly 4 long, with two colours. 2. Your child colours each one on spare squared paper. 3. For each one, check that no shorter part makes the same strip.  
  *Parent says:* How many different parts can you find that are truly four long? Colour each one. Check each one.  
  *Child mode:* Find as many parts as you can that are truly 4 long. Colour each one.
  - Note: There are 12 parts that work. They come in three families: three of one colour and one of the other (4 ways for each colour), and two and two with the same colours side by side (4 ways). Your child does not need to find them all. Finding 4 or 5, and checking each one, is very good.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child chooses a favourite part and colours the real bookmark, 3 times round. 2. Your child gives it to someone at home. 3. That person says which 4 squares would come next if the strip were longer.  
  *Parent says:* Choose your best part. Colour the real bookmark. Then test it on someone.  
  *Child mode:* Choose your best part. Colour the bookmark. Ask someone what the next 4 squares would be.
  - Ask: Did your reader find the same part as you? Could they have found a different one? (good: Yes, the same four.; They started from the second square, so their part looked different but made the same strip.)

## Worksheets
### What Comes Next? (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) Which colour comes next?  
   Options: yellow / green / red  
   **Answer:** green. *Why:* Green. Yellow and green take turns, and the row stopped on yellow. So it is green's turn.
2. (fill) What comes next? clap, stamp, clap, stamp, clap, ____  
   **Answer:** ['stamp', 'a stamp']. *Why:* Clap and stamp take turns. The row stopped on a clap, so a stamp comes next.
3. (truefalse) Idli, dosa, idli, dosa, idli, dosa is a repeating pattern.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The part idli, dosa comes three times, in the same order each time.
4. (mcq) Which part repeats in this row?  
   Options: triangle, square / triangle, triangle / square, square, triangle  
   **Answer:** triangle, square. *Why:* Triangle, square. Say the row aloud. It starts again after every square.
5. (fill) How many beads are in the part that repeats?  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* The part is red, red, blue. Count its beads: 1, 2, 3. The row starts again after the blue.
6. (mcq) What comes next in this row of beads?  
   Options: red / blue / yellow  
   **Answer:** red. *Why:* Red. The part is red, red, blue. The row has just begun a new part with one red, so a second red follows. Picking blue means thinking the colours take turns one by one.
7. (sort) Sort each one: is it a repeating pattern or not?  
   **Answer:** day, night, day, night → Repeating pattern; the vegetables in a basket → Not a repeating pattern; tick, tock, tick, tock → Repeating pattern; clouds in the sky → Not a repeating pattern; leaf, flower, leaf, flower → Repeating pattern; 1 dot, 2 dots, 3 dots, 4 dots → Not a repeating pattern. *Why:* Day and night, tick and tock, and leaf and flower each have one part that comes again and again. The basket and the clouds have no such part. One dot, two dots, three dots is a pattern that grows. It does not repeat.
8. (match) Match each sound pattern to the bead row that goes the same way.  
   **Answer:** clap, stamp, clap, stamp → 🔴🔵🔴🔵; clap, clap, stamp, clap, clap, stamp → 🔴🔴🔵🔴🔴🔵; clap, stamp, stamp, clap, stamp, stamp → 🔴🔵🔵🔴🔵🔵; clap, stamp, pat, clap, stamp, pat → 🔴🔵🟡🔴🔵🟡. *Why:* Give each sound one colour. A clap is red, a stamp is blue and a pat is yellow. Then the beads take turns in the same way as the sounds.
9. (mcq) One piece of this thoranam is missing. Which piece is it?  
   Options: a leaf / a flower / nothing is missing  
   **Answer:** a flower. *Why:* A flower. Say leaf, flower along the row. At the gap you say flower.
10. (draw) Draw 8 circles in a row. Colour them red, yellow, red, yellow, to the end.  
   **Answer:** Eight circles. Red and yellow take turns. The row starts on red and ends on yellow.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps the turns all the way to the eighth circle. To check, say red, yellow aloud as you touch each circle.
11. (draw) Make your own pattern with two shapes. Draw it three times round. Loop the part that repeats.  
   **Answer:** Any part made of two shapes, drawn three times, with a loop round one part.. *Why:* A strong pattern has the very same part each time. A reader should be able to say what comes next.
12. (write) Say your answer aloud. A grown-up writes it. In Guess My Next Bean, why were your first guesses often wrong?  
   **Answer:** Because I had not seen enough beans yet. I did not know the part that repeats.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the first guesses were luck. You can only know the next bean after you have seen the part come round.
### Carry It On, Fix It, Swap It (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) What are the next two beads?  
   Options: blue, blue / blue, red / red, blue  
   **Answer:** blue, blue. *Why:* Blue, blue. The part is red, blue, blue. The row has just begun a new part with red, so two blues follow.
2. (mcq) This train should go red, blue, red, blue. Which place has the wrong piece?  
   Options: Place 3 / Place 4 / Place 5  
   **Answer:** Place 4. *Why:* Place 4. Say red, blue along the train. At place 4 you say blue, but the piece is red.
3. (fill) The part is leaf, leaf, flower. You make it 3 times. How many leaves do you need?  
   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* Each part has 2 leaves. Three parts: 2 and 2 and 2 make 6 leaves.
4. (truefalse) Clap, stamp, clap, stamp and red, blue, red, blue are the same pattern.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In both, two things take turns one by one. You can point at a red bead for every clap and a blue bead for every stamp.
5. (order) Put these rows in order. Start with the shortest part that repeats.  
   **Answer:** ['🔴🔵🔴🔵🔴🔵', '🔴🔵🟡🔴🔵🟡', '🔴🔴🔵🔵🔴🔴🔵🔵']. *Why:* Red, blue is 2 long. Red, blue, yellow is 3 long. Red, red, blue, blue is 4 long.
6. (sort) How long is the part that repeats: 2 or 3?  
   **Answer:** tick, tock, tick, tock → 2 long; clap, clap, wave, clap, clap, wave → 3 long; day, night, day, night → 2 long; ta, ki, ta, ta, ki, ta → 3 long; leaf, flower, leaf, flower → 2 long; idli, idli, vada, idli, idli, vada → 3 long. *Why:* Say each row aloud and listen for where it starts again. Tick, tock starts again after 2 sounds. Clap, clap, wave starts again after 3.
7. (mcq) Which bead row is the same pattern as jump, jump, clap?  
   Options: 🔴🔵🔴🔵🔴🔵 / 🔴🔴🔵🔴🔴🔵 / 🔴🔵🔵🔴🔵🔵  
   **Answer:** 🔴🔴🔵🔴🔴🔵. *Why:* Red, red, blue. A jump is red and a clap is blue: two the same, then one different.
8. (fill) The row carries on in the same way. What colour is the 8th bead?  
   **Answer:** ['blue']. *Why:* The part is red, blue, blue. The 7th bead begins a new part, so it is red. The 8th bead is blue.
9. (draw) Draw a thoranam with the pattern leaf, flower, flower. Make the part come 3 times.  
   **Answer:** Nine pieces: leaf, flower, flower, three times over.. *Why:* A strong drawing has 3 leaves and 6 flowers, and every part is the same.
10. (write) A row goes red, blue, red, blue. Can you be completely sure the next bead is red? Say why or why not.  
   **Answer:** Not completely. If the maker keeps the same rule, red is next. But only the maker knows the rule.. *Why:* A strong answer says red is the best guess, and that the maker decides. Seeing more beads makes the guess safer.
11. (perspective) A barbet calls tuk, tuk, tuk, tuk. Is that a repeating pattern?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other child has right.. *Why:* Mathematicians call it a repeating pattern whose part is 1 long. The first child is right that most patterns we make have two or more different things. And there is a second thing here: the short silence after each tuk.
12. (draw) Draw a pattern whose part is 4 long. Use only two colours.  
   **Answer:** For example red, red, blue, blue, drawn two or three times.. *Why:* A strong answer has a part that is truly 4 long. Red, blue, red, blue does not count, because the smaller part red, blue already makes it.
### Pattern Detective (challenge) (challenge, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) How many beads are in the smallest part that repeats?  
   Options: 2 / 4 / 6  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 2. The smallest part that makes the whole row is red, blue. Picking 4 means choosing two parts joined together.
2. (fill) A part is 3 pieces long. You make it 4 times. How many pieces in all?  
   **Answer:** ['12', 'twelve']. *Why:* Count 3 for each part: 3, 6, 9, 12. There are 12 pieces.
3. (mcq) Two shapes are missing. What are they?  
   Options: square, square / triangle, square / square, triangle  
   **Answer:** square, square. *Why:* Square, square. The part is triangle, square, square. Say it along the row. At the two gaps you say square, square.
4. (truefalse) A strip of 12 squares can hold a part that is 4 squares long exactly 3 times.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. 4 and 4 and 4 make 12. Three parts fill the strip with no square left over.
5. (truefalse) A row of 10 beads can be made from whole parts that are 3 beads long.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Three parts use 9 beads: 3, 6, 9. One bead is left over, so the row stops in the middle of a part.
6. (match) Match each row to its letter name. A is the first thing. B is the next new thing.  
   **Answer:** 🔴🔵🔴🔵 → AB; 🔴🔴🔵🔴🔴🔵 → AAB; 🔴🔵🔵🔴🔵🔵 → ABB; 🔴🔵🟡🔴🔵🟡 → ABC. *Why:* Give the first colour the letter A. Give the next new colour the letter B, and a third colour the letter C. Then read off one part.
7. (fill) Red, blue, red, blue goes on and on. What colour is the 10th bead?  
   **Answer:** ['blue']. *Why:* Blue. Every second bead is blue: the 2nd, 4th, 6th, 8th and 10th.
8. (sort) Does it repeat, or does it grow?  
   **Answer:** clap, stamp, clap, stamp → Repeats; 1 clap, then 2 claps, then 3 claps → Grows; leaf, flower, leaf, flower → Repeats; the steps of a staircase, each one higher → Grows; Monday to Sunday, then Monday again → Repeats; 2, 4, 6, 8 → Grows. *Why:* A repeating pattern does the same part again. A growing pattern gets bigger each time: one more clap, one step higher, two more.
9. (write) Why do you need to see a part come round twice before you call the row a repeating pattern?  
   **Answer:** Seeing the part once shows only a few things in a row. Seeing it come again shows that it repeats.. *Why:* A strong answer says that 'repeat' means 'again'. Until something has come again, you are only guessing that it will.
10. (draw) This bookmark carries on in the same way. Draw its next 4 squares.  
   **Answer:** Red, red, blue, red.. *Why:* The part is red, red, blue, red. It is 4 long and it has come twice. The next 4 squares are the same part again.
11. (perspective) A row goes red, blue, red, blue. What comes next?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other child has right.. *Why:* Red is the best guess, because red, blue is the simplest rule that fits. But four beads also fit other rules, and only the maker knows which one she is using.
12. (draw) Make up a body pattern with 3 different moves. Draw it twice round. Then teach it to someone.  
   **Answer:** Three moves in a fixed order, drawn two times, for example jump, clap, turn, jump, clap, turn.. *Why:* A strong pattern keeps the three moves in the same order both times. Your learner should be able to carry it on without you.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a repeating pattern?  
   Options: red, blue, red, blue, red, blue / red, blue, green, yellow, pink, white / red, red, red, blue, green, blue  
   **Answer:** red, blue, red, blue, red, blue. *Why:* Red, blue, red, blue, red, blue. The part red, blue comes three times. The other two rows have no part that comes again in the same order.
2. (mcq) Which bead comes next: yellow, green or red?  
   Options: yellow / green / red  
   **Answer:** yellow. *Why:* Yellow. The part is yellow, yellow, green. The row has just begun a new part with one yellow, so a second yellow follows. Picking green means thinking the colours take turns one by one.
3. (mcq) Clap, pat, clap, pat, clap. What comes next?  
   Options: a clap / a pat / a stamp  
   **Answer:** a pat. *Why:* A pat. Clap and pat take turns, and the row stopped on a clap.
4. (fill) How many shapes are in the part that repeats?  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* The part is triangle, square, star. It has 3 shapes. The row starts again after the star.
5. (mcq) Which part repeats in ta, ki, ta, ta, ki, ta?  
   Options: ta, ki / ta, ki, ta / ta, ta  
   **Answer:** ta, ki, ta. *Why:* Ta, ki, ta. It is 3 sounds long and it comes twice. Picking 'ta, ki' leaves out the last ta of each part.
6. (mcq) Which bead row is the same pattern as stamp, stamp, clap?  
   Options: 🔵🔴🔵🔴🔵🔴 / 🔵🔵🔴🔵🔵🔴 / 🔵🔴🔴🔵🔴🔴  
   **Answer:** 🔵🔵🔴🔵🔵🔴. *Why:* Blue, blue, red. A stamp is blue and a clap is red: two the same, then one different.
7. (truefalse) A pattern made of sounds can be copied with colours.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Give each sound one colour. The colours then take turns in the same way as the sounds.
8. (mcq) This thoranam should go leaf, flower, leaf, flower. Which place has the wrong piece?  
   Options: Place 4 / Place 5 / Place 6  
   **Answer:** Place 5. *Why:* Place 5. Say leaf, flower along the row. At place 5 you say leaf, but the piece is a flower.
9. (mcq) Which bead is missing?  
   Options: red / blue / yellow  
   **Answer:** blue. *Why:* Blue. The part is red, blue, yellow. Say it along the row. At the gap you say blue.
10. (mcq) Your rule is leaf, leaf, flower. You have put down leaf, leaf, flower, leaf. What do you add next?  
   Options: a leaf / a flower / nothing  
   **Answer:** a leaf. *Why:* A leaf. You have begun a new part with one leaf. The part needs a second leaf before its flower.
11. (truefalse) Day, night, day, night is a repeating pattern.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The part day, night comes again and again, because the Earth keeps spinning.

   **Answer:** ['6', 'six']. *Why:* Each part has 2 claps. Three parts: 2 and 2 and 2 make 6 claps.

## Applications
- A weaver sets up a loom so that the same small design comes again and again along a sari border.
- A tile layer follows a repeating plan, so that a whole floor fits together from a few kinds of tile.
- Singers, drummers and dancers keep a tala, a repeating pattern of beats, so that they all stay together.
- A doctor listens to your heartbeat. A steady lub, dub, lub, dub is a healthy pattern, and a change in it is a clue.
- A traffic light goes through the same colours in the same order all day, so that drivers know what is coming.
- A calendar uses the pattern of the week, so that you know that the day after Sunday is always Monday.
- Road workers paint kerb stones black and yellow by turns, so that drivers can see the edge of the road.
- People who write computer programs tell the computer to repeat one small set of steps again and again.

## Spotted around us
- A thoranam of mango leaves over a door at Pongal, Ugadi or a wedding. Often marigolds are strung between the leaves.
- Kerb stones along a road, painted black and yellow by turns, or black and white.
- Athangudi tiles on the floor of an old Chettinad house in Tamil Nadu. A few handmade tiles repeat across the whole room.
- The border of a sari or a veshti. One small shape, such as a temple tower or a mango, runs all the way along.
- A coppersmith barbet calling tuk, tuk, tuk from a tree in the hot months. Listen for how steady the call is.
- A maize or sugarcane plant in a field. The leaves come out on one side, then the other, all the way up.
- The sleepers under a railway track: sleeper, gap, sleeper, gap, as far as you can see.
- A zebra crossing: white stripe, black road, white stripe, black road.

## Open questions
- **Why do zebras have stripes?** Scientists have argued about this for more than a hundred years. The best evidence so far says the stripes help to keep biting flies away, but not everyone agrees.
- **Is there any pattern hidden in the digits of pi?** Pi is a number that begins 3.14159 and goes on for ever. In the 1760s Johann Lambert proved that its digits never settle into a repeating pattern. But nobody knows whether every digit turns up equally often in the long run.
- **What does a bird hear in another bird's song?** People hear a pattern of notes. Nobody can listen with a bird's ears, and scientists are still testing which parts of a song matter to the birds themselves.
- **How does a brain learn a pattern from only a few examples?** Babies only seven months old already notice simple patterns in sounds. Scientists do not yet know exactly how a brain does this.
