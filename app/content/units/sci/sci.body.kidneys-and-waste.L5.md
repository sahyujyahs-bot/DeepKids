# Water and salt: how humans, fish and desert animals keep the balance
`sci.body.kidneys-and-waste.L5`

**Summary.** A sea fish swims in water all day, yet she must keep drinking so that she does not dry out. A river fish hardly ever drinks. Why are they so different? In this lesson you will pull water out of cucumber slices using only salt, find out why sea water cannot quench thirst, and see how kidneys, gills and the white cap on a gecko's dropping all deal with the same two problems.

**Intro.** *A sailor's boat has run out of drinking water. The sea is all around her, as far as she can see. Should she drink the sea water? Say yes or no, and say why you think so.* Almost everyone answers no. Very few people can say the reason. Sea water is water, after all, and the sailor's body is short of water. First you will taste three waters. Then you will run a test with cucumber and salt, and the result will give you the reason. By the end you will be able to answer for the sailor. You will also be able to answer for a hilsa swimming from the sea into a river, and for a camel walking across the Thar.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain osmosis: which way water moves across a cell's membrane, and why.
1. I can explain why drinking sea water leaves a person with less water than before.
2. I can explain how my kidneys make urine stronger or weaker, and what the water-saving hormone does.
3. I can name the three nitrogen wastes, say which animals make each one, and say why.
4. I can explain the opposite water problems of a river fish and a sea fish, and how each fish solves hers.
5. I can name four ways a desert animal saves water.
6. I can run a fair test with cucumber and salt, and say what my results show.
7. I can explain why a rehydration drink needs both salt and sugar.

## Terms
- **Dissolve**: A solid dissolves when it breaks up into particles too small to see and spreads all through a liquid. *e.g.* Salt stirred into rasam dissolves. You cannot see it any more, but you can taste it in every spoonful.
- **Concentration**: Concentration is how much of something is dissolved in a set amount of liquid. A strong, or concentrated, liquid has a lot dissolved in it. A weak, or dilute, liquid has only a little. *e.g.* Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in each litre. That is nearly four times the concentration of salt in your blood.
- **Membrane**: A cell membrane is the very thin skin around every living cell. Water passes through it easily. Salt and sugar pass only through special doors in it. *e.g.* Each cell in a cucumber slice is wrapped in a membrane. Water leaves through it when you put salt outside.
- **Osmosis**: Osmosis is the movement of water through a membrane, from the side with less dissolved in it to the side with more dissolved in it. *e.g.* Dry raisins dropped into water swell up in a few hours. Water moves by osmosis into the sugary inside of each raisin.
- **Osmoregulation**: Osmoregulation is the work a living body does to keep the water and salt inside it at steady levels. *e.g.* After a salty meal of pickle and rice, you feel thirsty and pass less urine. Both are part of your osmoregulation.
- **Pump (in a cell)**: A pump is a protein in a cell membrane that uses energy to carry one kind of particle across, even towards the side that already has more of it. *e.g.* Pumps in the wall of a kidney tube push salt out of the urine and into the kidney around it.
- **Aquaporin**: An aquaporin is a water door: a protein in a cell membrane with a hole so narrow that only water can pass through it. *e.g.* Your kidney cells add more aquaporins to their membranes when your body needs to save water.
- **Nitrogen waste**: Nitrogen waste is the waste left over when cells break down protein. Protein contains nitrogen, and the body cannot store the extra. *e.g.* After you eat dal, paneer, egg or fish, your cells use the protein. The nitrogen they do not need leaves in your urine as urea.
- **Ammonia**: Ammonia is the first nitrogen waste every animal cell makes. It is poisonous, and it dissolves very easily in water. *e.g.* A rohu in a pond lets ammonia pass out through her gills, straight into the water around her.
- **Urea**: Urea is a nitrogen waste that the liver makes out of ammonia. It is far less poisonous than ammonia, so the body can carry it in the blood until the kidneys remove it. *e.g.* Humans, cows, dogs and adult frogs all send out most of their nitrogen waste as urea in urine.
- **Uric acid**: Uric acid is a nitrogen waste that hardly dissolves in water. An animal can send it out as a white paste, using very little water. *e.g.* The white cap on a house gecko's dropping is nitrogen waste made from uric acid.
- **Loop of Henle**: The loop of Henle is the hairpin-shaped part of each tiny kidney tube. It makes the deep part of the kidney very salty. *e.g.* A kangaroo rat has very long loops of Henle for the size of her kidneys. She can make urine nearly five times as strong as the strongest human urine.
- **Collecting duct**: A collecting duct is the last tube that urine passes through in the kidney. Here the kidney decides how much water to take back. *e.g.* On a hot day when you have drunk little, your collecting ducts let a lot of water go back into the blood. Your urine comes out dark and strong.
- **ADH, the water-saving hormone**: ADH is a chemical message made in the brain. It tells the collecting ducts in the kidneys to let more water go back into the blood. ADH is short for antidiuretic hormone. *e.g.* When your blood becomes a little too salty, your brain releases more ADH within minutes.
- **Gills**: Gills are the thin, feathery organs with which a fish takes oxygen from water. Their surface is very thin, so water and salt also cross it. *e.g.* Lift the gill cover of a fresh mackerel at the fish market. The dark red, comb-like gills are underneath.
- **Salt gland**: A salt gland is an organ that pumps extra salt out of the blood as a very salty liquid. Seabirds, sea turtles and some other sea animals have salt glands. *e.g.* A sea turtle on a beach seems to be crying. The thick tears are salt leaving through the salt glands beside her eyes.
- **Metabolic water**: Metabolic water is new water that cells make when they use food for energy. The hydrogen in the food joins with oxygen from the air and forms water. *e.g.* A kangaroo rat makes about 54 grams of water inside her body from every 100 grams of dry barley she eats.
- **Contractile vacuole**: A contractile vacuole is a tiny bag inside a one-celled pond creature. It fills with the water that keeps coming in, then squeezes the water out. *e.g.* A Paramecium in pond water empties their contractile vacuoles every few seconds, all day and all night.
- **Brackish water**: Brackish water is water that is saltier than a river but less salty than the sea. It is found where a river meets the sea. *e.g.* The backwaters of Kerala and the water among the mangroves at Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu are brackish.
- **Dehydration**: Dehydration is the state of a body that has lost more water than it has taken in. *e.g.* After a long day of cricket in May with nothing to drink, you have a dry mouth, a headache and very dark urine. These are signs of dehydration.
- **ORS**: ORS is a drink of clean water with measured amounts of salt and sugar. It replaces the water and salt a person loses in diarrhoea. ORS is short for oral rehydration solution. *e.g.* A health worker gives a packet of ORS to mix in one litre of water for a child with loose motions.

## Explanations
### A body has two jobs to do with water · *defined*
**Definition:** Excretion is sending out the wastes that the body's cells have made. Osmoregulation is keeping the water and salt in the body at steady levels.

Your kidneys filter your blood and take back what the body needs. What is left leaves as urine. That is the kidney's first job, removing waste. The kidneys have a second job that matters just as much. They keep your blood at almost exactly the same saltiness all the time. Your blood is about as salty as water with 9 grams of salt in each litre. Why must it stay there? Every cell in your body sits in liquid that comes from the blood. If that liquid turns too salty, water leaves the cells and they shrink. If it turns too watery, water enters the cells and they swell. Brain cells are packed inside the skull with no room to swell, so even a small change can make a person confused and ill. Every animal has to do both jobs. A fish, a camel and a gecko each do them in a different way, because each has a different amount of water to spare.
- e.g. After a salty meal you feel thirsty and pass less urine. Your body is bringing its saltiness back down.
- e.g. After two tumblers of water you pass a lot of pale urine within the hour. Your body is sending the extra water out.
- e.g. A camel, a hilsa and a house gecko all have to remove nitrogen waste. Each uses a different amount of water to do it.
### Osmosis: water moves towards the side with more dissolved in it · *defined*
**Definition:** Osmosis is the movement of water through a membrane, from the side with less dissolved in it to the side with more dissolved in it.

Every cell is wrapped in a membrane. Water passes through it easily, but dissolved salt mostly does not. Now put plain water on one side and salty water on the other. Water particles are always moving, and they cross the membrane in both directions. On the salty side, each salt particle holds a small crowd of water particles around it. Those water particles are less free to cross. So more water crosses from the plain side to the salty side than comes back. The salty side gains water, and nobody has pushed anything. This is a simplified picture. The full account uses a measure of how free the water particles are to move, which scientists call water potential. The rule you can rely on is simple: water moves towards the side with more dissolved in it. It does not have to be salt. Sugar, urea or anything else that cannot cross the membrane has the same effect.
- e.g. Salt sprinkled on cucumber slices makes a very salty film on the cut faces. Water leaves the cells and collects in the bowl.
- e.g. Raisins soaked in water swell up, because the inside of a raisin is full of sugar.
- e.g. Salt keeps lime pickle and dried fish from rotting. It draws water out of the cells of microbes by osmosis, so the microbes cannot grow.
### No cell pumps water: a body moves salt, and the water follows · *defined*
**Definition:** Animal cells have pumps for salt, but no pump for water has been found. To move water, a body moves salt first. Then water follows by osmosis.

A pump is a protein in a cell membrane that uses energy to carry a particle across. In 1957 the Danish scientist Jens Christian Skou found the pump that carries sodium, which is one half of salt. Your cells spend a large share of their energy running these sodium pumps. For water, cells have only doors. Water can also seep slowly through the membrane itself, and the doors make the crossing much faster. In 1992 Peter Agre and his team in the United States found the first water door, and it was named an aquaporin. A door lets water through in either direction, and osmosis decides which way it goes. So when a kidney tube needs to take water back from the urine, it pumps salt out of the tube. The space outside the tube becomes saltier, and water follows through the aquaporins. Your gut takes in water the same way after a drink. Sweat glands, tear glands and a fish's gills all work like this too. A few scientists think that some salt carriers drag a little water through with them. That is still being argued about. Either way, water moves only because something else was moved first.
- e.g. In the gut, cells pump salt from the food side to the blood side. The water you drank follows it.
- e.g. In a kidney tube, salt is pumped out and water follows it back to the blood.
- e.g. In a sweat gland, salt is moved into the tube of the gland first. Water follows it, and that water is your sweat.
### How a kidney makes urine stronger than blood · *defined*
**Definition:** The loop of Henle is a hairpin-shaped part of each kidney tube. It makes the deep part of the kidney very salty, so that water can be drawn out of the urine there.

To save water, a kidney must make urine that is stronger than blood. That means taking water out of the urine. But no cell can pump water. So the kidney builds a very salty zone deep inside itself, and lets osmosis do the work. Each kidney tube has a long loop shaped like a hairpin. It dips down into the deep part of the kidney and comes back up. The wall of the upward half pumps salt out into the kidney around it, and it has no water doors, so the water cannot follow. Salt builds up around the loop. Because the two halves of the hairpin lie side by side with liquid flowing in opposite directions, the effect is multiplied along the length of the loop. The deepest part of a human kidney becomes about four times as concentrated as blood. Part of that is urea, which the kidney also collects there. We call it the salty zone to keep things simple. Last of all, the urine runs down through this salty zone inside a collecting duct. If the wall of the duct has its water doors open, water leaves the urine by osmosis and goes back to the blood. What is left is strong urine. In 1951 Heinrich Wirz, Bartholomew Hargitay and Werner Kuhn in Basel showed that the salty zone is real. They froze thin slices of rat kidney and measured them from the outside of the kidney to its deepest tip. The slices grew steadily more concentrated towards the tip.
- e.g. The strongest human urine is about four times as concentrated as blood.
- e.g. A kangaroo rat's loops of Henle are very long for the size of her kidney. Her urine can be nearly five times as strong as the strongest human urine.
- e.g. A beaver lives in fresh water and has short loops. A beaver's strongest urine is weaker than a human's.
### The water-saving hormone sets how much water goes back · *defined*
**Definition:** ADH is a hormone, which means a chemical message carried in the blood. It tells the collecting ducts of the kidneys to open more water doors, so that more water returns to the blood.

A small part of your brain called the hypothalamus has cells that respond to how salty the blood is. When you sweat a lot or drink too little, your blood becomes slightly saltier. These cells shrink a little, and they signal the pituitary gland just below the brain. The pituitary releases ADH into the blood. Within minutes the ADH reaches the kidneys. The cells of the collecting ducts then move more aquaporins into their membranes. More water leaves the urine and returns to the blood, and you pass a small amount of dark urine. The same brain cells make you feel thirsty. When you drink a lot, the opposite happens. The blood becomes slightly more watery, less ADH is released, and the cells take their water doors back inside. Water stays in the urine, and you pass a lot of pale urine. In this way an adult's kidneys can make as little as about half a litre of urine in a day, or many litres. They cannot make none at all. The day's urea and other wastes need some water to carry them out.
- e.g. On a hot afternoon of play with little to drink, your urine is dark yellow and there is not much of it. Your ADH is high.
- e.g. After two tumblers of water on a cool day, your urine is pale and plentiful. Your ADH is low.
- e.g. Urine is usually darker first thing in the morning. ADH stays high during sleep, when you are not drinking.
### Why sea water cannot quench thirst · *defined*
**Definition:** Sea water has more salt in it than the strongest urine a human kidney can make. To send out the salt from sea water, the body must use more water than the sea water brought in.

Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in each litre. Your blood is about as salty as 9 grams in a litre. Suppose the sailor drinks one litre of sea water. Her gut takes in the salt, and the water follows it into the blood. Now her blood has 35 grams of extra salt, and her kidneys must send it out. Human urine at its strongest can hold only about 20 grams of salt in a litre. Much of the kidney's concentrating power is used up by urea and the other wastes that must also leave. So 35 grams of salt needs about one and three quarter litres of urine. She drank one litre and must pass one and three quarter litres. She ends up with about three quarters of a litre less water than before she drank. Sea water also contains magnesium and sulphate. The gut takes these in poorly, so they stay in the gut and hold water there. That can cause loose motions, which lose still more water. So the sailor should not drink. She should catch rain, and keep out of the sun.
- e.g. The English poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge wrote in 1798 about sailors stuck on a still sea: 'Water, water, every where, nor any drop to drink.'
- e.g. A kangaroo rat's kidneys can make urine much stronger than sea water. A human's kidneys cannot make urine that holds as much salt as sea water does.
- e.g. Ships and coastal cities make fresh water from sea water with machines. Chennai has large plants that do this.
### Three nitrogen wastes: ammonia, urea and uric acid · *defined*
**Definition:** Nitrogen waste is what is left when cells break down protein. Animals send it out as ammonia, as urea or as uric acid, depending on how much water they can spare.

Starch, oil and sugar are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. When cells use them for energy, they turn into carbon dioxide and water. Protein is the body-building part of food, found in dal, milk, eggs, fish and meat. Protein also contains nitrogen. When cells break down protein they do not need, the nitrogen comes off as ammonia. Ammonia is poisonous, so it cannot be allowed to build up. It dissolves very easily, so an animal who lives in water can let it wash away. Most fish and tadpoles do this. Their ammonia passes out through the gills, and it costs them almost no energy. An animal on land cannot spare that much water. So the liver joins ammonia with carbon dioxide and makes urea. Making urea costs energy. But urea is much less poisonous, so it can travel in the blood, and it needs roughly a tenth of the water. Mammals, adult frogs and sharks make urea. Birds, lizards, snakes and insects go one step further and make uric acid. It costs even more energy. But it hardly dissolves, so it can leave the body as a paste with very little water. A tadpole makes ammonia, and the same animal as a grown frog on land makes urea. In 1931 the biochemist Joseph Needham gave a reason why birds and reptiles use uric acid. Their young grow inside a shelled egg, where waste cannot be washed away. Solid grains of uric acid can sit in the egg without harming the chick. Most biologists still find this likely.
- e.g. Fish and tadpoles: ammonia, washed away through the gills.
- e.g. Humans, cows, dogs and adult frogs: urea, dissolved in urine.
- e.g. Crows, pigeons, geckos, snakes and cockroaches: a paste made from uric acid. It is the white part of a bird's or a gecko's dropping.
- e.g. Humans also make a little uric acid, from worn-out DNA. If too much builds up, it can form sharp crystals in a joint. That illness is called gout.
### A river fish has too much water coming in · *defined*
**Definition:** A river fish is saltier inside than the water around her. Water enters her body by osmosis all the time, and she must keep sending it out.

The blood of a bony fish is about one third as salty as the sea. River and pond water has almost no salt. So for a rohu or a catla in a pond, the inside is far saltier than the outside. Her gills must be thin, so that oxygen can cross them. Water crosses them too. It moves in by osmosis all day, and a little of her salt leaks out. If she did nothing, she would swell and her blood would become too watery. So she does three things. She hardly drinks at all. Her kidneys make a large amount of very weak urine, which sends the water out and keeps most of the salt in. And special cells in her gills pump salt in from the water, even though the water holds very little. The Danish scientist August Krogh showed in the 1930s that freshwater fish and frogs can take up salt from very dilute water in this way.
- e.g. A rohu, a catla and a murrel in a village tank all have this problem.
- e.g. A goldfish in a bowl passes a lot of very dilute urine all day.
- e.g. A frog sitting in a pond takes in water through her skin, and does not need to drink with her mouth.
### A sea fish has water leaking out all day · *defined*
**Definition:** A bony sea fish is less salty inside than the sea around her. Water leaves her body by osmosis all the time, so she must drink sea water and send the salt out.

A mackerel, a sardine or a pomfret has blood about one third as salty as the sea. So the outside is saltier than the inside. Water leaves through her thin gills by osmosis, all day. She lives in water and is always in danger of drying out. Her answer is to drink the sea. In 1930 the American scientist Homer Smith showed that bony sea fish swallow sea water steadily. In the gut, cells pump the salt into the blood, and the water follows it. Now she has the water, and far too much salt. Her kidneys cannot make urine saltier than her blood. So special cells in her gills pump the salt straight out into the sea. Ancel Keys and Edward Willmer described these cells in the gills of eels in 1932. Her kidneys make only a little urine, which carries out magnesium and sulphate. All this pumping costs energy. That is the reason a sea fish and a river fish cannot simply swap places. Each one's gills are set to pump salt in one direction only.
- e.g. Mackerel, sardines, seer fish and pomfret all drink sea water and pump the salt out at their gills.
- e.g. A sea fish put into fresh water takes in water and swells. Her gills are still pumping salt out when she needs it pumped in.
- e.g. A river fish put into sea water loses water and shrivels. Her gills are still pumping salt in.
### Sharks, hilsa and the fish who change waters · *defined*
**Definition:** Sharks keep urea in their blood so that they are as concentrated as the sea. Some fish, such as hilsa, can reverse the salt pumps in their gills and move between sea and river.

Sharks and rays solved the sea's problem in another way. They do not send out all their urea. They keep so much of it in the blood that their body is as concentrated as sea water, or slightly more. So water does not leave them by osmosis, and they do not need to drink. The extra salt from their food leaves through a salt gland near the end of the gut, called the rectal gland. When shark or ray meat is kept too long, bacteria turn the urea into ammonia. That is the sharp smell of stale shark. A few fish live in both waters. The hilsa, called ilish in Bengali and pulasa in Telugu, grows up in the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea. In the monsoon she swims up rivers such as the Hooghly, the Godavari and the Narmada to lay her eggs. On the way she passes through an estuary, where the river meets the sea and the water is brackish. There her body changes over. Her gill cells stop pumping salt out and begin to pump it in. She stops drinking, and her kidneys begin to make plenty of weak urine. Salmon make the same change, and eels make it in the other direction.
- e.g. Hilsa enter the Godavari when the monsoon floods come down, mainly from July to September.
- e.g. Pearl spot, called karimeen in Kerala, live in the brackish backwaters, where the saltiness changes with the tide and the season.
- e.g. Mudskippers on the mudflats of the Sundarbans cope with river water, sea water and air on the same day.
### Desert animals spend almost no water · *defined*
**Definition:** A desert animal survives by losing very little water. Some never drink, and live on the water in their food and on metabolic water, which is water their cells make from food.

When a cell uses food for energy, the hydrogen in the food joins with oxygen and makes new water. This is metabolic water. You make about a tumbler of it a day. The kangaroo rat of the North American deserts lives on it. In 1948 Bodil and Knut Schmidt-Nielsen and their co-workers showed that kangaroo rats can live on dry grain with nothing to drink. Every 100 grams of dry barley gives the rat about 54 grams of water made in her cells. That is enough only because she loses so little. Her urine is nearly five times as strong as the strongest human urine. Her droppings are almost dry. Her nose is cool inside, so water in her warm breath turns back to liquid there before the breath leaves. And she spends the hot day in a closed burrow, where the air is cooler and damper. In the Thar desert, Indian desert jirds and spiny-tailed lizards also shelter in burrows. A camel is too big to hide, so she saves water in other ways. Her hump is fat, and it holds no store of water. When she is short of water she lets her body warm up, from about 34 degrees Celsius at dawn to about 41 degrees in the afternoon. Then she cools off at night without sweating. She can lose water equal to about 30 parts in 100 of her body weight and still walk. Most mammals are in danger at less than half that. When she reaches water, she can drink back about a third of her body weight in one visit. For a large camel that is well over 100 litres.
- e.g. Kangaroo rat: dry seeds, no drinking, very strong urine, a cool nose and a closed burrow.
- e.g. Camel: a body temperature that swings by several degrees, dry dung, strong urine and a huge drink when water is found.
- e.g. Darkling beetles and other desert insects send out nitrogen waste as dry uric acid and take back water from their droppings before these leave.
### Salt glands: how seabirds and sea turtles drink the sea · *defined*
**Definition:** A salt gland is an organ that pumps salt out of the blood as a liquid much saltier than sea water. With salt glands, an animal can drink sea water and still gain water.

A bird's kidneys cannot make urine as salty as the sea. Yet gulls, cormorants and albatrosses live at sea for months and drink sea water. In 1958 Knut Schmidt-Nielsen and his co-workers found out how. They gave sea water to cormorants and collected clear drops that ran from the birds' nostrils and off the tip of the beak. The drops were saltier than sea water. They came from a pair of glands in the head, just above the eyes. The cells of these glands pump salt out of the blood, and a little water follows it. A seabird shakes the drops off her beak. Sea turtles have salt glands that empty beside the eyes. An olive ridley turtle laying her eggs on a beach in Odisha seems to weep thick tears. She is getting rid of salt. Marine iguanas sneeze their salt out. Whales and dolphins have no salt glands. They get most of their water from the fish and squid they eat and from metabolic water, and their kidneys make strong urine.
- e.g. A gull on the beach at Rameswaram shakes her head now and then, and clear salty drops fly off her beak.
- e.g. Olive ridley turtles nest in huge numbers at Gahirmatha and Rushikulya in Odisha, and their salt tears can be seen on the beach.
- e.g. Sharks have a salt gland too, near the end of the gut.
### Other bodies have other answers · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living body balances water and salt, with or without kidneys. One-celled creatures, worms, insects and even some plants each have their own way.

A Paramecium is a single cell living in pond water. They are saltier inside than the pond, so water enters by osmosis without stopping. Left alone, the cell would swell and burst. So they collect the incoming water in a small bag called a contractile vacuole, and squeeze it out through the membrane every few seconds. An earthworm has small coiled tubes, called nephridia, in almost every ring of their body. Some kinds of earthworm have one pair in each ring, and the common Indian earthworm has many tiny ones. Each tube takes in liquid from inside the body and takes back what is useful. The rest leaves through the skin or into the gut. An insect has fine threads called Malpighian tubules, which hang in the blood and empty into the gut. Cells of the tubule pump salts into it, water follows, and wastes are carried along. Then the last part of the gut takes most of the water and salt back. What leaves is nearly dry. The grey mangrove trees at Pichavaram stand in brackish water. They take in some salt with the water. Glands on their leaves push that salt out again, and you can see the crystals on the leaves on a dry day.
- e.g. Paramecium in a pond: a bag that bails water out every few seconds.
- e.g. Earthworm: small coiled tubes in nearly every ring of the body.
- e.g. Cockroach: threads that empty into the gut, and a gut that takes the water back.
- e.g. Grey mangrove: glands on the leaf that push out salt.
### Losing water and salt together: why ORS has sugar in it · *defined*
**Definition:** ORS is a drink of clean water with measured salt and sugar. The gut takes in salt much faster when sugar is there with it, and water follows the salt.

Sweat and loose motions carry away both water and salt. In bad diarrhoea, such as cholera, a person can lose many litres in a day, and that can kill. For a long time the only treatment was salt water dripped into a vein through a needle. In the early 1960s the American biochemist Robert Crane described a carrier in the wall of the gut. It takes sodium, from salt, into the gut cells, but only together with glucose, a simple sugar. Once the sodium is inside the body, water follows it by osmosis. This carrier keeps working even during cholera. So a drink with the right amounts of salt and sugar can put water back into a person through the mouth. In 1953 the Kolkata doctor Hemendra Nath Chatterjee reported in a medical journal that he had treated 186 cholera patients this way. Few doctors took notice. In 1971, during the Bangladesh war, cholera spread through the refugee camps of West Bengal and the drip bottles ran out. Dr Dilip Mahalanabis and his team had families give the patients a drink of salt, glucose and water. More than 3,000 patients were treated. In the camps using this drink, about 4 patients in 100 died. With drips alone the figure had been about 30 in 100. The amounts matter. Too much salt is harmful. Too much sugar holds water in the gut by osmosis and makes diarrhoea worse, which is why soft drinks and sweet juices are not ORS.
- e.g. Home ORS: one litre of clean water, six level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt.
- e.g. Salted lime juice with a little sugar, thin salted rice kanji and tender coconut water all give water with some salt and sugar.
- e.g. For anyone ill with loose motions, a packet of ORS from a chemist or a health centre is the right choice, and a doctor should be asked.
### Did the kidney's filter first appear in fresh water? · *debated*
A kidney filters a huge amount of liquid out of the blood and then takes nearly all of it back. That looks wasteful. Why would such an organ ever appear? Scientists do not agree. The answer depends on where the first animals with backbones lived, more than 450 million years ago, and the fossils do not settle it easily.
- e.g. A river fish uses her filters to send out a flood of water, which fits the first view below.
- e.g. Hagfish have filters in their kidneys and live only in the sea, which fits the second view.
- e.g. Some sea fish, such as the toadfish, have lost their filters altogether and make urine without them.
- **Homer Smith, American kidney scientist, in his book From Fish to Philosopher (1953):** The first animals with backbones lived in fresh water. Water poured into them by osmosis. The filter appeared as a way to bail that water out. Later animals, on land and in the sea, kept the filter and added ways to take water back.
- **James Robertson, British zoologist (1957), and many fossil scientists today:** The oldest fossils of animals with backbones are found in rocks laid down in the sea. Their living relatives, the hagfish, live only in the sea. So the filter probably appeared in the sea, and it was not built for bailing out fresh water.
- **Other biologists:** A filter is useful in any water. It lets the body throw out everything small and take back only what is wanted, including wastes the body has never met before. It did not need fresh water as a reason.

## Mostly agreed
- Water crosses cell membranes through water doors called aquaporins, and osmosis decides the direction. *How we know:* In 1992 Peter Agre's team put the protein into frog eggs, which normally take in water very slowly. In plain water the eggs with the protein swelled and burst within minutes. Agre shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2003.
- Bony sea fish drink sea water and send the salt out through their gills. *How we know:* Homer Smith showed in 1930 that sea fish swallow sea water and absorb most of it. Ancel Keys and Edward Willmer described the salt-moving cells in eel gills in 1932.
- Some desert rodents live their whole lives without drinking. *How we know:* In 1948 Bodil and Knut Schmidt-Nielsen and their co-workers kept kangaroo rats on dry grain with no water. They measured every gram of water the rats made and lost.
- The kidney concentrates urine by making its own deep part very salty. *How we know:* In 1951 Wirz, Hargitay and Kuhn froze slices of rat kidney and found that the tissue becomes steadily more concentrated from the outside to the deepest tip.
- Seabirds and sea turtles get rid of extra salt through glands in the head, and the kidneys are not involved. *How we know:* In 1958 Knut Schmidt-Nielsen and his co-workers gave sea water to cormorants and collected drops from the beak that were saltier than sea water.
- A drink of water, salt and glucose can replace the water lost in severe diarrhoea. *How we know:* In 1971 Dilip Mahalanabis and his team treated more than 3,000 cholera patients in refugee camps in West Bengal. About 4 in 100 died, against about 30 in 100 with drips alone.

## How we found out
**Bodil Schmidt-Nielsen and Knut Schmidt-Nielsen, with their co-workers A. Brokaw and H. Schneiderman for the kangaroo rats, and S. A. Jarnum and T. R. Houpt for the camels, The kangaroo rat study was published in 1948. The camel studies were done in the Sahara in 1953 and 1954 and published in 1956 and 1957..** For about two thousand years, travellers said that a camel carries a tank of water inside her, in the hump or in the stomach. It seemed obvious. An animal who goes without drinking must have a store. Bodil Schmidt-Nielsen grew up in Copenhagen, where both her parents studied how bodies work. In the 1940s she and her husband Knut went to the desert in Arizona to study kangaroo rats, small rodents who never seem to drink. They kept the rats on dry grain with no water at all. They measured the water in the grain, in the air the rats breathed out, in their urine and in their droppings. The rats stayed healthy. The sums showed where the water came from. The rats' cells made it from the food, and the rats lost almost none. The results were published in 1948. In 1953 and 1954 the couple and their co-workers lived in the Sahara and did the same careful measuring on camels. They found no water tank. The hump was fat. A camel lasts because she loses water slowly, lets her body warm up by day, and can bear a very large loss.  
*What people believed before:* That an animal who goes without drinking must carry a hidden store of water, such as a camel's hump. The Schmidt-Nielsens measured all the water going into and out of these animals. They found no store. The animals make some water from their food and lose very little.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A camel stores water in her hump.** (A common belief, at least two thousand years old; *rejected*)  
  For: A camel can walk for a week or more in the heat without drinking. Her hump shrinks and flops over on a long journey. Both facts look like a water tank being emptied.  
  Against: The hump is fat, which is a store of food. In 1953 and 1954 Knut and Bodil Schmidt-Nielsen and their team studied camels in the Sahara and found no water store anywhere in the body. A camel lasts because she loses water slowly and can bear losing a great deal.
- **A person lost at sea should drink small amounts of sea water from the first day.** (Alain Bombard, French doctor, after his voyage in 1952; *rejected*)  
  For: Bombard said he drank small amounts of sea water, and the liquid pressed from raw fish, and lived for 65 days. He argued that a little sea water, started early, is better than none.  
  Against: Human urine cannot hold salt as strongly as sea water does, so each drink costs more water than it gives. Navies and sea rescue services advise people never to drink sea water. Other sailors who tried Bombard's method reported that they needed fresh water as well.
- **All fish drink water all the time.** (A common saying; *rejected*)  
  For: A fish's mouth opens and shuts all day, and water keeps going in. Bony sea fish really do swallow sea water steadily.  
  Against: Most of the water a fish takes into her mouth goes out over the gills, for breathing. A river fish swallows hardly any, because water is already entering her by osmosis. Sharks do not need to drink either.
- **You cannot drink too much water. The more you drink, the healthier your kidneys are.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Too little water does cause harm, such as headaches and kidney stones. Healthy kidneys can send out a lot of extra water.  
  Against: Kidneys can send out only about one litre of extra water an hour. A person who drinks much faster than that for hours makes their blood too watery, and their brain cells swell. In a study of runners in the 2002 Boston Marathon, about 13 runners in 100 finished with too little sodium in their blood, mostly from drinking too much.
- **The white part of a bird's dropping is not uric acid by the time it leaves the bird.** (Nicholas Crouch, Vincent Lynch and Julia Clarke, scientists in the United States, in 2019; *debated*)  
  For: They tested the white part of droppings from six kinds of birds with X-rays. They found no uric acid. They found other white solids, such as ammonium urate and struvite. They think microbes in the bird's gut change the uric acid before it leaves.  
  Against: Older studies of many more kinds of birds did report uric acid. Six kinds of birds is a small sample. Everyone agrees that birds make uric acid in the body, and the argument is only about what happens to it on the way out.

## Roots
We have learned how bodies keep their water and salt steady in rivers, seas and deserts. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Salted Cucumber Test, nobody squeezed the slices. You put salt outside them, and water came out. Kidneys, fish gills and a seabird's salt glands all work in this way. They spend energy on moving salt, and the water follows without being pushed. So an animal steers her water by moving something else.  
*Habit it breaks:* We assume that to move a thing, you must push that thing. So we picture the kidneys pumping water, as a motor pumps water up to a tank. No pump for water has been found in any animal cell. Life sets up a difference in salt, and water does the rest.  
*Evidence:* In the Salted Cucumber Test, salt on the outside brought water out of the cells, and the bowl with no salt stayed almost dry. In Bring It Back, plain water outside sent the water in again. A kidney tube pumps salt out so that water returns to the blood. A sea fish's gut pumps salt in so that the sea water she drank follows it.

- *who am i:* You are mostly water, and you have never pushed a drop of it. Your cells move salt, and your water goes where the salt has gone.
- *what is life:* Every living being, from a Paramecium to a camel, keeps an inside that is different from the outside. Each one senses their own water and acts on it. Staying alive means keeping that difference going, every second.
- *what is this world:* The same water is too much for a river fish, too little for a sea fish and deadly for a sailor to drink. What water is to a body depends on the body it meets.

Water alone, with nothing in it, has no direction to go. Put a membrane in it, and a little more salt on one side than the other, and the water begins to move. One small difference is enough to start a flow. Living bodies are made of such differences, kept up by cells that never stop moving salt. The sea fish, the river fish, the gecko and you are many ways of holding the same kind of difference. A Paramecium senses the water coming in and bails it out. A hilsa senses the river and changes her gills. Each being decides, in their own way, what to do about their water. When the difference is gone, the flow stops, and the body is no longer separate from the water around it.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** “लवणमेतदुदकेऽवधायाथ मा प्रातरुपसीदथा इति / तं होवाच । यद्दोषा लवणमुदकेऽवाधा अङ्ग तदाहरेति / तद्धावमृश्य न विवेद / मध्यादाचामेति । कथमिति । लवणमिति / तं होवाचात्र वाव किल तत्सोम्य न निभालयसेऽत्रैव किलेति” Uddalaka asks his son to put a lump of salt in water. By morning the salt cannot be seen or picked out, yet every sip of the water tastes of it. (The salt in the cucumber bowl also vanished from sight. But the salt you could no longer see decided where the water went. What is dissolved and unseen is doing the work.)
- **Tao Te Ching 8:** “上善若水。 / 水善利萬物而不爭，處眾人之所惡，故幾於道。” The highest good is like water. Water benefits all things and does not strive. It settles in the low places that people avoid. (In a body, water is never forced. It goes wherever the difference in salt leads it, through doors that are simply left open.)
- **Thirukkural 20 (paraphrased):** “நீர்இன்று அமையாது உலகெனின் யார்யார்க்கும் / வான்இன்று அமையாது ஒழுக்கு.” Without water, the world cannot go on for anyone, however great they are. And without rain, there is no flow of water. (A kangaroo rat who never drinks still lives on water, which her cells make from seeds that grew after rain. No body has found a way to do without water. Bodies only differ in how little they can lose.)
- Science (settled): Water crosses cell membranes through aquaporins, which are doors and not pumps. The direction is set by osmosis.
- Science (debated): Some scientists report that a few carriers of salt and sugar take water through with them, even against osmosis. Others explain the same results by ordinary osmosis.
- Other view, Many physicists and physiologists: Water is not following anything, and a body is not steering anything. Water particles move at random in all directions. Where more is dissolved, slightly fewer of them cross, and the numbers do the rest. Words such as 'steer', 'follow' and 'decide' are our way of telling the story. On this view a kidney is a mechanism shaped by natural selection, and nothing in it chooses.

Within a few minutes some of that sip will be in your blood. Your gut is moving salt, and the water is following it. You are not doing anything, and it is happening.

## Story: The Good Water
*A made-up story. Thoothukudi is a real port town in Tamil Nadu, and its salt pans are real. Muthulakshmi, Selvam, Aachi and Mariammal Athai are imagined. It is true that a sea fish put into fresh water takes in water through her gills and is soon in danger. It is true that raw sea salt dissolved in water, about 35 grams to a litre, can keep small sea animals alive for some hours. People who keep sea fish for longer use a measured sea-salt mix and never plain kitchen salt. Wild animals borrowed from the shore should go back the same day, as they do here.*

On Friday evening, when the tide was far out, Muthulakshmi and her aunt waded into the shallows beyond the fishing harbour with a hand net and a blue plastic drum. Tomorrow was Sea Day at school. Every child in Class 6 was to bring something from the shore and tell the others about it.

Mariammal Athai knew where the small ones hid. By the time the lamps came on along the harbour wall, the drum held four silver fish with yellow stripes, each no longer than a finger, and two hermit crabs in borrowed shells. 'One day only,' said Athai. 'Tomorrow evening they go back to the same place.' Muthu promised.

They carried the drum home between them and set it on the veranda. Selvam, who was six and Athai's son, lay on his stomach beside it until he was called three times for dinner.

Aachi came in late from the salt pans. She had raked salt there for forty years, and her hands showed it. The skin of her palms was thick and split, with white lines where the brine had dried. She sat on the step and rubbed coconut oil into them, as she did every night, and looked into the drum. 'Pretty,' she said. 'Thirsty work, living in that.' Muthu did not know what she meant and was too sleepy to ask.

Selvam woke before anyone else. He went straight to the drum. In the grey light the water looked worse than it had at night. There was sand at the bottom and a shred of weed, and it smelled of the harbour. The fish hung in the middle of it, hardly moving.

Selvam knew about water. In their street the sweet water came on Tuesdays and Fridays, and everyone queued for it with pots. That water was for drinking and for rice. Nobody wasted a tumbler of it. It was the good water. And here were four fish and two crabs, guests in the house, sitting in dirty water from the harbour.

He fetched the steel bucket and filled it from the big pot of sweet water in the kitchen, tumbler by tumbler, without spilling. He caught the fish one at a time in the tumbler, which took a long while, and tipped each one gently into the bucket. He lifted the crabs across by their shells. Then he dragged the blue drum to the edge of the veranda and poured the harbour water into the drain. He sat down beside the bucket, very pleased, to watch his guests enjoy themselves.

That was how Muthu found him. He was not pleased any more.

'They don't like it,' he said. His voice was small.

Muthu knelt and looked. The fish were not hanging quietly now. Their gill covers were opening and closing fast, far too fast. One had tilted to the side and kept correcting herself. Another lay near the bottom, leaning against the steel. Both crabs had pulled so far into their shells that the shells looked empty.

'What did you put them in?'

'The good water. From the pot.' He began to cry. 'The other was dirty.'

Muthu's first thought was air. She swirled the water with her hand to stir some in. The fish went on gasping. Her second thought was the bucket, but it was the clean one, the one for rice. There was nothing in that water at all. It was the cleanest water in the house.

There was nothing in that water at all.

She thought of Aachi on the back step every March, laying sardines in a basket with a fistful of salt between each layer. By the next morning the basket always sat in a puddle, and the fish had gone thin and stiff. 'The salt calls the water out,' Aachi said every year. Muthu had thought it was only a way of talking. And she thought of the chickpeas her mother left in a bowl of water at night. By morning they were twice the size and tight in their skins.

The water went to whichever side had more in it. These fish had been made in the sea and they were salty inside, though not as salty as the sea. And she and Selvam had put them in water with nothing in it. The water would be going into them now. Through the gills, most likely, where the skin was thinnest. They were filling up like the chickpeas.

The sea was twenty minutes away at a run, and twenty back with a full pot. She looked at the fish leaning on the steel and ran to wake her grandmother instead.

Aachi listened without sitting up. Then she got up faster than Muthu had seen her move in years. 'You want me to make sea,' she said. 'I have been unmaking it for forty years.'

She took the bag of grey pan salt from under the kitchen shelf. It was the coarse kind she brought home from work, not the white powder from the shop. 'The sea is thinner than people think,' she said. 'At the pans we wait weeks for the sun to thicken it. For a bucket like this, three of my fistfuls, and a little more.' She would not put it in the bucket. She stirred the salt into a separate pot of water until the last grain had gone, then touched one finger to it and to her tongue. 'More.' Another pinch. She tasted again and nodded. 'That is the tide coming in.'

'Now slowly. A body does not like to be surprised.' She scooped half the sweet water out of the bucket, and Muthu poured the new water in, one tumbler at a time, with a count of twenty between tumblers. Selvam counted for her. He had stopped crying so that he could count properly.

For a long time nothing changed. Then the fish near the bottom lifted off the steel. The tilted one stopped tilting. Their gill covers slowed, a little and then a little more. When the sun reached the veranda, one hermit crab put out two legs and an eye on a stalk and walked across the bottom of the bucket.

They took the bucket to Sea Day on the back of Athai's cycle, with a wet cloth over the top. Three of the fish were swimming. The fourth swam too, but slowly, and Muthu watched her all morning.

In the evening the tide was out again. The three of them waded to the same place, and Selvam was allowed to tip the bucket. The fish were gone before the water had finished pouring. The crabs sat on the sand for a moment, then walked off in different directions.

Selvam stood looking at the place where they had been. Then he bent down, scooped up a handful of the sea and tasted it. He made a terrible face.

'Good water,' he said, and spat.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Selvam gives the sea fish the cleanest water in the house, and it nearly kills them. A sea fish is less salty than the sea but far saltier than tap water. In fresh water, water moves into her through her gills by osmosis, and her body is set up to push salt out and keep water in, which is now the wrong way round. Muthulakshmi works this out from two things she has seen at home: salt drawing water out of sardines, and chickpeas swelling in plain water. Water that is good for one body can be dangerous for another, because what matters is the difference between the inside and the outside.

- Ask: Water with nothing in it harmed the sea fish. Which way was water moving, and why?
- Ask: Would a pond fish, such as a rohu, have been harmed by the same bucket of tap water? Why or why not?
- Ask: Why is it safer to change the saltiness of a fish's water slowly than all at once?
- Ask: Sardines packed in salt lose water. Chickpeas in plain water gain it. What one rule explains both?
- Ask: Is there such a thing as water that is good for every living being?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Salted Cucumber Test: does more salt pull out more water? (40 min)
- **question:** When salt is sprinkled on cucumber slices, does liquid come out of them? Does twice as much salt bring out twice as much liquid?
- **predict:** Guess first. Four bowls each hold 15 cucumber slices. Bowl 1 gets no salt. Bowl 2 gets a quarter of a teaspoon, bowl 3 gets half a teaspoon and bowl 4 gets one whole teaspoon. After 18 minutes, how many millilitres of liquid will be in each bowl? Write a number for each bowl, and write the reason for your guess.
- **fair test:** Change only the amount of salt. Cut all the slices from the same cucumbers, about as thick as a 5 rupee coin, and put 15 in each bowl. Use four bowls of the same size. Toss every bowl 10 times, including the bowl with no salt. Wait the same 18 minutes for all four. Bowl 1 has no salt, so it shows how much liquid comes out from cutting alone.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the slicing with the sharp knife.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all four bowls. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each bowl. Then write three sentences: what you guessed and why, what you measured, and what you think moved the water.
- **results table:** Bowl | Salt added | My guess (ml) | Liquid after 18 minutes (ml) | How a slice feels when I bend it
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the question aloud and ask for a guess for each bowl, with a reason. 2. Write the guesses in the table. 3. Slice both cucumbers into rounds about as thick as a 5 rupee coin. 4. Your child counts 15 slices into each bowl and numbers the bowls 1 to 4.  
  *Parent says:* How much liquid do you think each bowl will have after 18 minutes? Give me a number for each. Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the liquid in each bowl. Say why. Ask a grown-up to slice the cucumbers. Count 15 slices into each bowl.
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect no liquid at all, or expect the salt to soak liquid up. Leave the skin on the cucumber. Slices of even thickness matter more than exact size.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Level one teaspoon of salt and tip it on a plate. 2. Divide the heap into four equal parts with the butter knife. 3. Toss bowl 1 ten times with the clean spoon, with no salt. 4. Sprinkle one part on bowl 2, two parts on bowl 3 and a fresh level teaspoon on bowl 4. 5. Toss each bowl ten times and start the timer for 18 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Bowl 1 gets no salt at all. We still toss it, so that tossing is the same for every bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Bowl 1 gets no salt. Toss it ten times. Salt bowls 2, 3 and 4. Toss each ten times. Start the timer.
  - Note: Toss bowl 1 first, while the spoon is still free of salt. One of the four small heaps is left over, and it can go back in the salt jar.
- **s3 (18 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave the bowls alone. 2. Look closely at 5, 10 and 15 minutes without stirring. 3. Draw the results table while you wait. 4. Ask what your child notices and where they think the liquid comes from.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the bottom of each bowl. What do you notice? Where do you think that liquid is coming from?  
  *Child mode:* Do not stir. Look at 5, 10 and 15 minutes. What is at the bottom of each bowl? Draw your table.
  - Note: Only listen at this stage. Common ideas are that the salt is melting, or that the salt has turned into water. Ask how the test could tell these apart. Bowl 1 and the taste of the liquid will help.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the slices of bowl 1 back with the spoon and tip the liquid into the medicine cup. 2. Read the millilitres and write the number. 3. Empty and rinse the cup, then do bowls 2, 3 and 4. 4. Bend one slice from bowl 1 and one from bowl 4.  
  *Parent says:* Read the mark at the top of the liquid. Now bend a slice from bowl 1 and a slice from bowl 4. What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Tip the liquid from each bowl into the cup. Read the millilitres. Write them down. Bend a slice from bowls 1 and 4.
  - Note: If a bowl holds more than the cup, measure it in two lots and add them. If bowl 4 gave less than 2 millilitres, the slices were thick or the cucumber was old. Wait 10 more minutes and measure again. Keep the slices from bowl 4 if you plan to do Bring It Back.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what your child claims, what they measured and why they think it happened. 2. Only then read the explanation in the parent note together. 3. Draw the bar chart and write three sentences.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me your claim, your evidence and your reason. Did water come out? Did twice the salt give twice the water? What moved it?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. Say what you measured. Say why you think it happened. Draw the bar chart.
  - Ask: Bowl 1 was cut and tossed like the others. What does its result tell you? (good: Cutting and tossing alone bring out almost no liquid.; So the liquid in the other bowls came out because of the salt.)
  - Ask: Nobody squeezed the slices. So how did the water get out? (good: It moved out of the cucumber towards the salt.; The outside became saltier than the inside, and water goes to the saltier side.)
  - Ask: Did twice the salt give twice the liquid? (good: More salt gave more liquid, but less than double.; The first bit of salt did the most.)
  - Note: After your child has answered: a cucumber is made of cells full of watery liquid with little dissolved in it. The salt dissolved in the wet film on each cut face and made a very salty layer outside the cells. Water moved out of the cells, through their membranes, towards the saltier side. This is called osmosis. More salt usually gives more liquid, but less than double. Once the cut faces are covered in strong salt water, extra salt adds little. If your numbers do not rise from bowl to bowl, the slices were probably uneven. Say so, and try again with thinner, even slices.
  - Check: Nobody squeezed the cucumber slices. What moved the water out of their cells? → **Water moved towards the saltier side**
### e2 · The Burrow Test: where does water last longest? (25 min)
- **question:** The same amount of water sits on three plates for two hours: in the sun or under a fan, in the shade, and under a cover. How much water is left on each?
- **predict:** Guess first. Each plate starts with 30 millilitres of water. After two hours, how many millilitres will be left on the open plate, on the shaded plate and on the covered plate? Write three numbers, and write the reason for each.
- **fair test:** Change only the place. Use three plates of the same size and put exactly 30 millilitres on each. Start and stop all three at the same time. Tilt each plate the same way when you pour the water back.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Work out the water lost from each plate. Then write three sentences: what you guessed and why, what you measured, and what this means for an animal in a burrow.
- **results table:** Plate | Where it stood | Water at the start (ml) | My guess (ml) | Water after 2 hours (ml) | Water lost (ml)
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for three guesses, with a reason for each, and write them down. 2. Measure 30 millilitres of water onto each plate. 3. Put plate 1 in the sun or under a fan. 4. Put plate 2 in the shade, away from the fan. 5. Put plate 3 beside plate 2 and cover it with the upturned bowl.  
  *Parent says:* Each plate has 30 millilitres. How much will be left on each after two hours? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how much water each plate will keep. Say why. Put 30 millilitres on each plate. Put them in their three places.
  - Note: Three measures of the 10 millilitre cup make 30 millilitres. So do two tablespoons. The cover should sit on the rim of the plate and close it fairly well.
  - Wait: Leave the plates for 2 hours.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 2 hours, carry each plate carefully to the table. 2. Tilt it over the cup and push the last drops in with a clean finger. 3. Read and write the millilitres left. 4. Work out the water lost from each plate.  
  *Parent says:* Pour slowly and push the last drops in. How much is left? How much has gone?  
  *Child mode:* Pour each plate back into the cup. Push the last drops in. Read the millilitres. Work out what was lost.
  - Note: About 1 millilitre always stays stuck to a plate. It is the same for all three, so the comparison is still fair. On a damp monsoon day the differences are small. Leave the plates for 4 hours instead.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what your child claims, what they measured and why they think it happened. 2. Only then read the explanation in the parent note together. 3. Write three sentences in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me your claim, your evidence and your reason. Which plate kept its water best? Why? What does that mean for a desert animal?  
  *Child mode:* Say which plate kept the most water. Say what you measured. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Where did the lost water go? (good: Into the air, as water vapour.; It evaporated.)
  - Ask: Why did the covered plate lose so little? (good: The air under the cover soon held as much water vapour as it could.; The damp air could not get away, so no more water could leave.)
  - Ask: A kangaroo rat plugs her burrow with sand during the day. Why might that help? (good: The air inside stays damp and cool, so she loses less water from her skin and breath.)
  - Note: After your child has answered: water leaves a wet surface by evaporating into the air. Heat, moving air and dry air all speed this up. Under the cover, the small amount of air soon holds as much water vapour as it can, and evaporation almost stops. An animal loses water in the same way from her skin and from every breath. In a closed burrow the air is cooler and damp, so she loses far less. Above ground at noon in the Thar, she would be the plate in the sun.
  - Check: Why does a small desert animal lose less water in a closed burrow? → **The air there is cooler and damper**

## Worksheets
### Osmosis, wastes and who lives where (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Plain water and salty water are on two sides of a cell membrane. Which way does water move?  
   Options: Towards the salty side / Towards the plain side / It does not move  
   **Answer:** Towards the salty side. *Why:* Water moves through a membrane towards the side with more dissolved in it. This is osmosis.
2. (fill) The movement of water through a membrane towards the side with more dissolved in it is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['osmosis']. *Why:* Osmosis needs a membrane that lets water through more easily than what is dissolved in the water.
3. (truefalse) True or false: your cells have pumps that push water across their membranes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. No pump for water has been found in animal cells. Cells pump salt, and water follows through water doors called aquaporins.
4. (match) Match each animal to what she does with her nitrogen waste.  
   **Answer:** Rohu → lets ammonia out at the gills; Human → sends out urea in urine; Crow → sends out a white paste; Shark → keeps urea in the blood. *Why:* A fish can wash ammonia away. A land mammal makes urea. A bird makes uric acid, which leaves as a paste. A shark keeps urea to match the sea.
5. (sort) Sort each living being: is water mostly coming in, or mostly leaving?  
   **Answer:** rohu in a pond → Water keeps coming in; mackerel in the sea → Water keeps leaving; Paramecium in a pond → Water keeps coming in; camel in the Thar → Water keeps leaving; tadpole in a puddle → Water keeps coming in; kangaroo rat in a desert → Water keeps leaving. *Why:* Beings in fresh water are saltier than the water, so water enters them. A sea fish is less salty than the sea, and a desert animal loses water to dry air.
6. (mcq) Why does a mackerel drink sea water all day?  
   Options: She loses water through her gills by osmosis / She likes the taste of salt / Her food is very dry  
   **Answer:** She loses water through her gills by osmosis. *Why:* A bony sea fish is less salty than the sea. Water leaves her by osmosis, so she drinks to replace it and pumps the salt out at her gills.
7. (write) In the Salted Cucumber Test, the bowl with no salt had almost no liquid. What does that tell you?  
   **Answer:** Cutting and tossing alone bring out almost no liquid. So the liquid in the salted bowls came out because of the salt, and not because the slices were cut.. *Why:* A strong answer says what the no-salt bowl rules out. It is the bowl that the others are compared with.
8. (order) Put these in order, for what happens after a very salty meal.  
   **Answer:** ['The blood becomes slightly saltier', 'Cells in the hypothalamus respond', 'The pituitary gland releases more ADH', 'Collecting ducts open more water doors', 'You pass a little dark urine']. *Why:* Saltier blood is detected in the brain. ADH travels to the kidneys, more water is taken back, and less leaves in the urine.
9. (truefalse) True or false: a camel carries a store of water in her hump.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The hump is fat. A camel lasts without drinking because she loses water slowly and can bear losing a great deal.
10. (fill) The white cap on a gecko's dropping is nitrogen waste made from ____ acid.  
   **Answer:** ['uric']. *Why:* Uric acid hardly dissolves, so it can leave the body as a paste with very little water.
11. (draw) Draw a fish in a pond. Add arrows to show where water enters her, where it leaves, and where salt enters.  
   **Answer:** A fish with water arrows going in at the gills, a large arrow of weak urine going out near the tail, and small salt arrows going in at the gills.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows water entering without the fish drinking, a lot of urine leaving, and salt being pumped in at the gills.
12. (mcq) A sailor drinks one litre of sea water. How much water does her body have afterwards?  
   Options: One litre more / The same as before / Less than before  
   **Answer:** Less than before. *Why:* Her kidneys need about one and three quarter litres of urine to send out the salt in one litre of sea water. She ends up with less.
13. (write) Name three ways in which a kangaroo rat saves water.  
   **Answer:** Any three of these. Her urine is very strong. Her droppings are nearly dry. Her cool nose takes water back from her breath. She spends the day in a closed, damp burrow. She does not sweat.. *Why:* Each way cuts one of the routes by which water leaves a body: urine, droppings, breath and skin.
14. (truefalse) True or false: a rehydration drink needs only water and salt.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The gut takes in sodium much faster together with glucose, and water follows the sodium. So the drink needs sugar too.
15. (draw) Draw two cells: one sitting in plain water, one in very salty water. Add arrows to show which way water moves.  
   **Answer:** The cell in plain water is large and round with arrows pointing in. The cell in very salty water is small and wrinkled with arrows pointing out.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows water going into the cell in plain water and out of the cell in salty water, with the cell's size changing to match.
16. (mcq) What is metabolic water?  
   Options: Water an animal stores in fat / New water that cells make from food / Water that enters through the skin  
   **Answer:** New water that cells make from food. *Why:* When cells use food for energy, the hydrogen in the food joins with oxygen and forms new water.
### Kidneys, gills and salt glands (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is the job of the loop of Henle?  
   Options: To filter the blood / To make the deep kidney very salty / To store urine  
   **Answer:** To make the deep kidney very salty. *Why:* The loop pumps salt into the deep part of the kidney. That salty zone later draws water out of the urine by osmosis.
2. (order) Put these in order, for how a kidney makes strong urine.  
   **Answer:** ['The loop of Henle pumps salt into the deep kidney', 'The deep kidney becomes very salty', 'Urine runs down through it in a collecting duct', 'ADH opens water doors in the duct wall', 'Water leaves the urine by osmosis', 'Strong urine flows on to the bladder']. *Why:* The kidney cannot pump water. It builds a salty zone first, and then lets water leave the urine by osmosis.
3. (write) Why can a kidney not simply pump water out of the urine?  
   **Answer:** No pump for water has been found in animal cells. Cells can pump salt. So the kidney pumps salt into the space around the tubes, and water follows by osmosis through water doors.. *Why:* A strong answer names what cells can pump, which is salt, and what they cannot, which is water.
4. (mcq) More ADH reaches the kidneys. What happens to the urine?  
   Options: There is more of it, and it is paler / There is less of it, and it is stronger / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** There is less of it, and it is stronger. *Why:* ADH makes the collecting ducts open more water doors. More water returns to the blood, so less urine leaves and it is stronger.
5. (truefalse) True or false: a hilsa's gills pump salt in the same direction all her life.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the sea her gills pump salt out. When she enters a river, they change over and pump salt in.
6. (match) Match each organ to the living being who has it.  
   **Answer:** Salt glands above the eyes → cormorant; Rectal gland for salt → shark; Contractile vacuole → Paramecium; Malpighian tubules → cockroach; Nephridia in nearly every ring → earthworm. *Why:* Each of these organs does part of the work that kidneys do in a mammal: moving salt, water or waste out of the body.
7. (fill) The liquid from a seabird's salt glands is ____ than sea water.  
   **Answer:** ['saltier', 'more salty', 'stronger']. *Why:* Because the liquid is saltier than what she drank, the bird sends out the salt and keeps some of the water.
8. (write) The table shows liquid from four bowls of cucumber. Did twice the salt give twice the liquid? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No. A quarter teaspoon gave 3 millilitres and a half teaspoon gave 5, which is less than double. A whole teaspoon gave 6, far less than double again. More salt gave more liquid, but each extra bit of salt added less.. *Why:* A strong answer compares pairs of numbers from the table and does not stop at 'more salt, more water'.
9. (mcq) Sea water has 35 grams of salt a litre. Strong urine holds 20. How much urine removes one litre's salt?  
   Options: About half a litre / Exactly one litre / About one and three quarter litres  
   **Answer:** About one and three quarter litres. *Why:* 35 divided by 20 is 1.75. The body must pass more water than the sea water brought in.
10. (sort) Sort each animal by the nitrogen waste she mainly sends out.  
   **Answer:** sardine → Ammonia; cow → Urea; pigeon → Uric acid; tadpole → Ammonia; dog → Urea; cobra → Uric acid; cockroach → Uric acid; adult frog → Urea. *Why:* Animals who live in water can wash ammonia away. Land mammals and frogs make urea. Birds, snakes, lizards and insects make uric acid, which needs the least water.
11. (draw) Draw one loop of Henle and a collecting duct inside a kidney. Shade the part of the kidney that is saltiest.  
   **Answer:** A hairpin loop dipping down from the outer kidney and coming back up, with a straight duct beside it. The shading is darkest at the bottom, around the bend of the loop.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the darkest shading at the deepest point. That is where the urine in the collecting duct loses the most water.
12. (perspective) A shark keeps a lot of urea in her blood. Two girls disagree about what urea is.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. For Thenmozhi: the urea makes the shark as concentrated as the sea, so she does not lose water or need to drink. For Nandini: the urea still comes from breaking down protein, and the extra does leave in the end.. *Why:* Both girls are using the science. The answer depends on whether 'waste' describes the substance itself or what a body does with it.
13. (truefalse) True or false: the main way desert animals survive is by storing a lot of water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Careful measuring found no water store in camels or kangaroo rats. They survive by losing very little water.
14. (write) A tadpole sends out ammonia. The same animal as a frog on land sends out urea. Why the change?  
   **Answer:** Ammonia is poisonous and needs a lot of water to carry it away. A tadpole lives in water, so the ammonia washes away at once. A frog on land cannot spare that much water, so the liver turns ammonia into urea, which is safer and needs far less water.. *Why:* A strong answer links the waste to the amount of water the animal has around her.
15. (mcq) In the Burrow Test, why did the covered plate lose the least water?  
   Options: The cover kept light out / The air under it became damp / The cover pressed the water down  
   **Answer:** The air under it became damp. *Why:* The trapped air soon held as much water vapour as it could, so evaporation almost stopped. A closed burrow works the same way.
16. (fill) Water that cells make from food is called ____ water.  
   **Answer:** ['metabolic']. *Why:* Metabolic water forms when hydrogen from food joins with oxygen. A kangaroo rat lives mainly on it.
### Testing claims about water and salt (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) A doctor claimed in 1952 that sips of sea water help a person lost at sea. What numbers would you check?

7. (order) Put these in order, for a hilsa leaving the sea to lay her eggs.

   **Answer:** A turtle with thick drops leaving beside her eyes, labelled salt from the salt glands, and waste leaving from the opening under her tail.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows two separate routes. Salt leaves through glands beside the eyes. Nitrogen waste leaves at the other end of her body.
9. (write) Textbooks say the white of a bird's dropping is uric acid. A 2019 study found none. What should scientists do next?  
   **Answer:** Test many more kinds of birds, including the ones in the older studies. Test droppings the moment they leave the bird and again some hours later, to see whether they change. It says which new measurements would settle the question.

   **Answer:** Two things were changed at once. *Why:* Thin slices have more cut surface for the same weight. If the bowls differ, nobody can tell whether the salt or the thickness caused it.
11. (fill) Water that is saltier than a river but less salty than the sea is called ____ water.  
   **Answer:** ['brackish']. *Why:* Brackish water is found in estuaries, backwaters and mangrove creeks, where river water and sea water mix.

   Options: Sea water is too cold / Removing its salt takes more water than it gives / The stomach cannot hold it  
   **Answer:** Removing its salt takes more water than it gives. *Why:* Sea water is saltier than the strongest human urine. The kidneys must use more than a litre of water to send out the salt from one litre.
4. (mcq) What does ADH, the water-saving hormone, tell the kidneys to do?  
   Options: Filter the blood faster / Take more water back from the urine / Make more urea  
   **Answer:** Take more water back from the urine. *Why:* ADH makes the collecting ducts open more water doors. More water returns to the blood, and less leaves as urine.
5. (mcq) Which part of a kidney tube makes the deep kidney very salty?  
   Options: The filter at the start / The loop of Henle / The bladder  
   **Answer:** The loop of Henle. *Why:* The loop of Henle pumps salt out into the deep part of the kidney. That salty zone draws water out of the urine.
6. (mcq) Which animal sends out nitrogen waste as a paste, with very little water?  
   Options: A cow / A sardine / A pigeon  
   **Answer:** A pigeon. *Why:* Birds make uric acid, which hardly dissolves. It leaves as the white part of the dropping. A cow makes urea and a sardine lets out ammonia.
7. (mcq) Ammonia is poisonous. Why can a pond fish still use it as her nitrogen waste?  
   Options: Fish are not harmed by poison / It washes away into the water at once / She stores it in her bones  
   **Answer:** It washes away into the water at once. *Why:* Ammonia dissolves very easily. It passes out through her gills into the water around her before it can build up.
8. (mcq) Which is true of a fish living in a river?  
   Options: She drinks all day / She passes a lot of weak urine / She pumps salt out at her gills  
   **Answer:** She passes a lot of weak urine. *Why:* Water enters a river fish by osmosis, so she sends it out as plenty of weak urine. She hardly drinks, and her gills pump salt in.
9. (mcq) A sea fish is put into a bucket of tap water. What happens to her?  
   Options: Water moves into her body / Water moves out of her body / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** Water moves into her body. *Why:* She is saltier than tap water, so water moves in by osmosis. Her gills are set to pump salt out, which now makes things worse.
10. (mcq) Which of these is NOT a way a kangaroo rat saves water?  
   Options: Very strong urine / A store of water in her tail / A closed burrow by day  
   **Answer:** A store of water in her tail. *Why:* No desert animal studied so far carries a tank of water. A kangaroo rat survives by losing very little.
11. (mcq) In the Salted Cucumber Test, why did one bowl get no salt at all?  
   Options: To save salt / To show what cutting alone does / To have something to eat  
   **Answer:** To show what cutting alone does. *Why:* The no-salt bowl is cut and tossed like the others. Whatever liquid it gives is not caused by salt, so the other bowls can be compared with it.
12. (mcq) A rehydration drink is made with salt but no sugar. What goes wrong?  
   Options: The gut takes in less salt and water / The drink becomes poisonous / Nothing: it works just as well  
   **Answer:** The gut takes in less salt and water. *Why:* The carrier in the gut wall takes in sodium together with glucose. Without sugar, less sodium enters, and less water follows it.

## Applications
- ORS, the drink of water, salt and sugar, treats the loss of water in diarrhoea. It works because the gut takes in salt together with sugar, and water follows.
- The salt water dripped into a vein in hospital has 9 grams of salt in a litre. That matches the blood, so the blood cells neither swell nor shrink.
- Salt keeps pickles and dried fish from rotting, and sugar does the same for jam and murabba. Both draw water out of microbes by osmosis.
- A water purifier marked RO pushes water through a membrane against the direction of osmosis, using a pump. RO is short for reverse osmosis. The salts stay behind.
- Large plants near Chennai make drinking water from sea water by reverse osmosis.
- Limp greens and carrots left in a bowl of plain water become crisp again, because water moves back into their cells.
- Fish farmers move young fish and prawns between waters of different saltiness slowly, over hours or days, so that their bodies can change over.
- People who work or play for hours in the heat drink salted buttermilk or salted lime juice. Sweat carries out salt as well as water.
- Urea is made in factories as a fertiliser. Plants take up its nitrogen and build it into new protein.

## Open questions
- **Does any cell move water directly, without moving salt first?** No pump for water has been found. But some scientists report that a few carriers of salt and sugar take water through with them, even against osmosis. Others think the measurements can be explained by ordinary osmosis. The argument is not over.
- **How does the very deepest part of the kidney become so concentrated?** The outer part of the salty zone is well understood. In the innermost part, the tube walls do not seem to pump salt in the same way. Several explanations have been offered since the 1970s, and none fits all the measurements.
- **How does a hilsa or a salmon sense that the water is changing, and start to change her gills in time?** Hormones that drive the change are known. How the fish first detects the saltiness of the water, and whether the change begins before she even reaches the river, is still being worked out.
- **How do tardigrades and some desert plants stay alive after losing almost all their water?** A tardigrade is a tiny animal who can dry out completely and start moving again when wetted. Special sugars and proteins seem to protect the dry cells. Exactly how they do it is not yet understood.
- **Can a small artificial kidney be worn on the body, or placed inside it?** A dialysis machine is the size of a small cupboard and uses a great deal of water. Teams of engineers are testing small devices. None is yet in ordinary use.

## Connected maths
- Sea water has 35 grams of salt in a litre. The strongest human urine holds about 20 grams in a litre. A sailor drinks 2 litres of sea water. How much urine is needed to carry that salt out? How much water has she lost or gained? **She needs 3 and a half litres of urine. She has lost 1 and a half litres of water.**. Two litres of sea water bring in 2 × 35 = 70 grams of salt. Each litre of urine carries out 20 grams. 70 ÷ 20 = 3.5 litres of urine. She drank 2 litres and passed 3.5 litres. 3.5 − 2 = 1.5 litres lost. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In a Salted Cucumber Test, a quarter teaspoon of salt gave 3 millilitres of liquid. If twice the salt always gave twice the liquid, how much would half a teaspoon and one teaspoon give? The real results were 5 and 8 millilitres. What does that tell you? **6 millilitres and 12 millilitres. The real results were smaller, so twice the salt gives less than twice the liquid.**. Half a teaspoon is twice a quarter, so 2 × 3 = 6. One teaspoon is four times a quarter, so 4 × 3 = 12. The real 5 is a little under 6, and the real 8 is well under 12. Each extra bit of salt pulls out less water than the bit before. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In Salty Sip, 3 level teaspoons of salt made half a litre of water as salty as the sea. How many teaspoons would a 10 litre bucket need? If one level teaspoon weighs about 6 grams, about how many grams is that? **60 teaspoons, which is about 360 grams.**. Half a litre needs 3 teaspoons, so one litre needs 6. Ten litres need 10 × 6 = 60 teaspoons. 60 × 6 grams = 360 grams. That is more than a third of a one kilogram packet of salt in one bucket. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A kangaroo rat's cells make about 54 grams of water from 100 grams of dry barley. Suppose she eats 5 grams of barley in a day. How much water does she make? An adult human loses about 2300 millilitres a day. About how many times more is that? **She makes about 2.7 grams of water, which is about 2.7 millilitres. A human's daily loss is roughly 850 times as much.**. 5 grams is one twentieth of 100 grams. 54 ÷ 20 = 2.7 grams. One gram of water is one millilitre. 2300 ÷ 2.7 is about 850. A whole day of her water is about half a teaspoon. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In Water Accounts, 23 beans of water leave an adult in a day, and 14 of them are urine. What fraction is urine? If the drinks are taken away, only 8 beans come in. Breath, skin and stool use 9 beans. Why can no change to the urine alone balance the account? **14 out of 23, which is a little more than half. Even with no urine at all, 9 beans would leave and only 8 would come in.**. 14 ÷ 23 is about 0.61, or about 61 parts in 100. Without drinks, the beans coming in are 6 + 2 = 8. The beans leaving by breath, skin and stool are 4 + 4 + 1 = 9. 9 is more than 8 before any urine is counted. So an animal who never drinks must also cut what she loses from breath and skin. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- Home ORS uses 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt in 1 litre of water. How much of each would one 250 millilitre tumbler need? Why is it better to mix a whole litre? **One and a half teaspoons of sugar and one eighth of a teaspoon of salt. One eighth of a teaspoon is too small to measure well.**. 250 millilitres is a quarter of a litre. A quarter of 6 is 1.5. A quarter of a half is one eighth. A small mistake with one eighth of a teaspoon could double the salt, and too much salt is harmful. In a whole litre, half a teaspoon can be levelled properly. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Which way does water move through a cell membrane? → Towards the side with more dissolved in it. This is called osmosis.
- Why can a thirsty sailor not drink sea water? → Sea water holds more salt than the strongest human urine can. Sending the salt out takes more water than the sea water gave.
- Does a river fish drink a lot or hardly at all? → Hardly at all. Water is already entering her through her gills by osmosis.
- What is the white part of a gecko's or a bird's dropping? → Nitrogen waste made from uric acid. It does the job that urine does for us, with very little water.
- What is in a camel's hump? → Fat. It is a store of food, and it holds no store of water.
- What does the water-saving hormone, ADH, do? → It tells the kidneys to take more water back from the urine. The urine becomes less and stronger.
- Where does a kangaroo rat get her water? → Her cells make it from her food. It is called metabolic water.
- Why does ORS have sugar in it as well as salt? → The gut takes in salt much faster together with sugar. Water then follows the salt.
