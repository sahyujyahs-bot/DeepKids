# Real or not? Dreams, sounds and air we cannot see
`sci.what-is-real.perception-and-reality.L2`

**Summary.** Last night's dream felt real until you woke up. The air in an 'empty' bottle is real, though nobody can see it. So how do we know what is really there? In this unit you hear sounds from behind a closed door and guess: was that a real tumbler, or a phone recording? You keep a dream notebook for five mornings. You find out that our senses give us clues, and that we check a clue by testing it another way.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes and listen for one minute. Count the sounds you hear. How many of the things that made them can you see when you open your eyes?* Most of the sounds came from things you could not see: a cooker in another flat, a bird, a vehicle on the road. You still felt sure they were there. In a dream, you can feel just as sure, and then wake up in bed. Perception means how we take in the world through our senses. In this unit you test your perception, and find out how to check what is real.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that a dream feels real while I dream, and how I find out it was a dream when I wake.
1. By the end I can say that a sound is real evidence that something happened, even when I cannot see what made it.
2. By the end I can test whether a sound comes from a real thing or a phone recording, and count how often my ears were right.
3. By the end I can say that air is real though we cannot see it, because it takes up space and pushes things.
4. By the end I can give two ways to check if something is real: test it with another sense, and ask someone else to check.
5. By the end I can guess first, give a reason, and say what my own test showed.

## Terms
- **Real**: Something real is there whether or not you think about it. You can check it in more than one way, and other people can check it too. *e.g.* The tumbler behind the door is real if you can open the door, see it, touch it and tap it again.
- **Perception**: Perception is how you take in the world through your senses, and make sense of what you get. *e.g.* Hearing a clink and thinking 'that is a spoon on a tumbler' is perception.
- **Sense**: A sense is one way your body notices the world, such as seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting or touching. *e.g.* You use your sense of smell when you know dosa is on the tava before you reach the kitchen.
- **Clue**: A clue is something you notice that tells you about something you cannot notice directly. *e.g.* A whistle from the next flat is a clue that someone is cooking in a pressure cooker.
- **Evidence**: Evidence is what you saw, heard or found in a test that shows something is true. *e.g.* A bubble rising from an 'empty' bottle under water is evidence that air was inside.
- **Recording**: A recording is a sound stored in a phone or another machine, so it can be played again later. *e.g.* A phone recording of a clinking tumbler sounds like a tumbler, but no tumbler is clinking.
- **Dream**: A dream is a story of pictures, sounds and feelings that your mind makes while you sleep. While you are in it, it usually feels real. *e.g.* You dream you are flying over a coconut grove. You feel the wind, until you wake in bed.
- **REM sleep**: REM stands for rapid eye movement. It is the part of sleep when the eyes move quickly under closed lids. Most vivid dreams happen in REM sleep. *e.g.* If you watch a sleeping person carefully, you may see their eyes moving under their eyelids.
- **Air**: Air is a gas all round us. It is made of tiny particles far too small to see. It takes up space, and it can push things. *e.g.* A balloon gets fat because the air you blow in takes up space inside it.
- **Vibrate**: To vibrate means to shake back and forth very fast. Every sound starts with something vibrating. *e.g.* When a spoon taps a tumbler, the tumbler vibrates, and you hear a clink.

## Explanations
### A sound is real evidence that something happened · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound is made when something vibrates. The vibration passes through the air to your ears. So a sound always means that something, somewhere, is vibrating.

When a spoon taps a tumbler, the tumbler vibrates. It shakes the air next to it, and that air shakes the air next to it, all the way to your ear. A phone speaker makes a sound by vibrating too. Your ears get the vibration, not the tumbler. So your ears tell you truly that a sound happened. What made the sound is a guess your mind makes from clues. Opening the door is how you check the guess.
- e.g. You hear a pressure cooker whistle through the wall. A cooker in the next flat is vibrating the air.
- e.g. You hear thunder before you see any rain. A lightning flash heated the air so fast that the air shook.
- e.g. A phone plays a recording of a crow. The phone's speaker is vibrating, and no crow is there.
### We know unseen things by what they do · *defined*
**Definition:** To infer means to work something out from clues. When we cannot see a thing, we can still infer that it is there from what it does to the things we can sense.

You cannot see air. But you see a dupatta flap in the breeze, and you feel the fan's air on your skin. You cannot see sound either. But you hear it, and loud sounds even make windows rattle. Scientists work this way all the time. Anna Mani, a scientist from Kerala, built instruments to measure the wind, sunlight and ozone, a gas high in the sky. Nobody can see ozone, but her instruments measured what it does to sunlight.
- e.g. Clothes on the balcony line flap, so wind is blowing.
- e.g. The road is wet in the morning, so it rained in the night.
- e.g. A bubble comes out of an 'empty' bottle under water, so air was inside.
### Air is real, though we cannot see it · *defined*
**Definition:** Air is a mix of gases, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. It is made of particles far too small to see. Air takes up space and can push.

An 'empty' bottle is full of air. Push the bottle, open end down, into a bucket of water. Water can hardly get in, because the air is in the way. Tip the bottle, and the air comes out as a big bubble. Only then can water fill the bottle. We breathe air in and out all day. Humans and other animals take oxygen from it, and plants give oxygen back into it.
- e.g. A balloon swells because the air you blow in takes up space.
- e.g. A kite rises on Makar Sankranti because moving air pushes up on the paper.
- e.g. Bubbles come out of a dry sponge when you squeeze it under water.
### A dream feels real while we are in it · *idea*
Most people dream every night, even on nights they remember nothing. Most vivid dreams happen in REM sleep, when the eyes move quickly under the lids. In REM sleep, most of the body's muscles go loose, so you do not act out your dream. Brain scans show that a front part of the brain, which helps us check and plan, is quieter while we dream. Many scientists think that is one reason we rarely notice a dream is a dream. Scientists only have ideas about this so far.
- e.g. You dream your school is inside your home, and it seems normal until you wake.
- e.g. In a dream you run from a dog, and wake up with your heart beating fast.
- e.g. A pet cat or dog twitches their paws and whiskers while fast asleep.
### Why do we dream? · *debated*
Nobody knows for sure why we dream. Scientists have three main ideas. Each idea has some evidence, and none has settled the question.
- e.g. After a day learning to ride a cycle, some children dream about cycling.
- e.g. Many people have dreams about being chased, or about being late for school.
- **Many sleep scientists:** Dreams come from the brain sorting and keeping the memories of the day. Rats that ran a maze in the day showed the same brain patterns again while they slept.
- **Antti Revonsuo, a scientist in Finland:** Dreams let us practise facing dangers in a safe place. That may be why so many dreams are about being chased or lost.
- **Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley, scientists in America:** In 1977 they said that dreams begin as signals that fire in the sleeping brain. The rest of the brain then makes a story out of them.

## Mostly agreed
- A sound is made by something vibrating, and the vibration travels through the air to our ears. *How we know:* A phone speaker and a tumbler both made sounds behind the door, and both were vibrating. Touch a speaker playing loud music, and you feel the buzz.
- Air is real. It takes up space and can push things, though we cannot see it. *How we know:* An 'empty' bottle pushed into water lets out a bubble. Wind moves clothes and kites. A balloon swells when you blow into it.
- Most people dream every night, and most vivid dreams happen in REM sleep. *How we know:* In 1953, Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman watched sleepers' eyes move fast under their lids several times each night. People woken at those times usually said they had been dreaming.
- Dreams are built mostly from people, places and things from the dreamer's own life, mixed up in new ways. *How we know:* In the 1890s, Mary Whiton Calkins wrote down her dreams as soon as she woke, for weeks. Most of them came from her own recent days.

## How we found out
**Mary Whiton Calkins, an American psychologist, with Edmund Sanford, Notes kept in the early 1890s, published in 1893.** For a long time, many people thought dreams came from somewhere far away from the dreamer's own life. In the early 1890s, Mary Whiton Calkins, a young teacher of psychology at Wellesley College in America, decided to look carefully. Dreams fade fast after waking, so she kept a notebook beside her bed. She set an alarm clock to wake herself at different hours of the night, and wrote down each dream at once. Edmund Sanford, who had taught her laboratory methods, kept his own notes in the same way. After several weeks, they looked at what their dreams were made of. Most dreams were built from people, places and things of their own recent days, mixed up in new ways. Calkins published this in 1893. Two years later, she finished all the work for a doctorate at Harvard University and passed every examination. Harvard refused to give her the degree, only because she was a woman. In 1905 she became the first woman president of the American Psychological Association. Sixty years after her notes, scientists found REM sleep, the dreaming sleep when the eyes move fast under the lids.  
*What people believed before:* Many people thought dreams came from somewhere far from the dreamer's own life. Calkins's careful notes showed that most dreams are built from the dreamer's own recent days.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If you cannot see it, it is not there.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Eyes are the sense we trust most. Most things we know about, we have seen.  
  Against: Air, sounds and smells are real, and we cannot see any of them. We know they are there from what they do: a bubble, a whistle, the smell of jeera frying.
- **If I heard it, I know what made it.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Our ears are good at telling sounds apart. Most of the time, our guess about what made a sound is right.  
  Against: A phone recording can sound like a real tumbler. The sound is real, but the thing we picture may not be there. We only know when we check another way.
- **A dream is real in its own way, because you truly saw and felt it.** (The Mandukya Upanishad, and many philosophers since; *debated*)  
  For: The fear or joy in a dream was really felt. The Mandukya Upanishad counts dreaming as one of the states that awareness passes through, alongside waking.  
  Against: The dream tiger left no footprints, and nobody else saw it. When you wake, nothing in the room agrees with the dream.
- **Other animals dream too.** (Many sleep scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Dogs, cats, rats and many birds have REM sleep. Rats that learned a maze showed the same brain patterns again in their sleep. Sleeping dogs twitch and make small running movements.  
  Against: Other animals cannot tell us in words what they experience while asleep, so we cannot ask about their dreams directly.

## Roots
We have guessed at sounds behind a closed door, found air in an 'empty' bottle, and caught our dreams in a notebook. Now we ask what these tell us about how we know what is real.

**Deep truth.** Behind the closed door, you heard clinks from a real tumbler and from a phone. Both times, the sound reaching your ears was real. Only opening the door showed whether a tumbler was there. So hearing tells us a sound happened, and another check tells us what made it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'I heard it, so I know what it was'. But the sound is all that reaches us. The thing we picture making it is our guess, and a guess needs another check.  
*Evidence:* In the door test, a phone recording and a real tumbler made the same kind of clink. The listener had to guess. Opening the door was the only way to know which it was.

- *who am i:* You are someone who builds a picture of the world from clues: sounds, smells, touches and sights. Even asleep, you build whole worlds in your dreams.
- *what is life:* Every living being reads clues about what is around them. An evening primrose flower senses a bee's buzz and makes sweeter nectar. A bat hears echoes in the dark.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of real things that eyes miss: air, sounds and smells. We find them by what they do, and check them in more than one way.

One air fills the bottle, the room and your lungs. The same air carries the cooker's whistle, the smell of jeera and the bee's buzz to every listener nearby. Everything is conscious, and every being meets this one world through their own senses. A bat, a flower and a child each take in the same air and come away knowing different things about it.

- **Nyaya Sutra 1.1.3, by Gautama (also called Akshapada):** There are four ways to know something truly: perception, inference, comparison, and the word of someone we can trust. The Nyaya teachers' favourite example: see smoke on a far hill, and you know there is fire, though you cannot see the flames. (In the door test, you heard a sound and inferred what made it. Opening the door was perception. The Nyaya teachers would say you used two of their four ways, one to check the other.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2 (the butterfly dream):** Zhuangzi, a Chinese thinker, dreamed he was a butterfly, flying happily, not knowing he was Zhuangzi. When he woke, he wondered: was he a man who had dreamed of being a butterfly, or a butterfly now dreaming of being a man? (Like Anju with her anklet, Zhuangzi felt the dream was real while he was in it. His question is the one the dream notebook asks: how do we tell a dream from waking?)
- **Mandukya Upanishad, verses 3 to 7:** The Upanishad describes states that awareness passes through: waking, dreaming, and deep sleep with no dreams. It speaks of a fourth, called turiya, which is aware of all three. (Your dream notebook caught the dreaming state, and you woke into the waking one. The Upanishad treats them as two states that the same awareness passes through.)
- Science (settled): Sound is a vibration that travels through air to the ear. A phone speaker and a tumbler both make real sounds this way.
- Science (open): Why we dream, and how the brain decides that something feels real, are still open questions.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India: They said only what we perceive directly can be trusted. A clue can mislead: what looks like smoke may be mist or dust. So, they said, inference does not give certain knowledge.
- Practice, Listen for what you cannot see (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Listen. Count the sounds you can hear but cannot see. Now notice the air moving in and out of your nose. Feel it, cool going in and warm coming out. Breathe slowly three times. Open your eyes. How many unseen things did you notice?
- Ask: If a sound can be real when the thing you pictured is not there, what else might you be picturing?
- Ask: In a dream, you were there and you felt things. In what way was the dream real?
- Ask: A bat and you hear the same room. Which of you hears the real room?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When you dream of a mango, where is that mango? (hint: Think about what was really in the room, and what was in your mind.)
- *big:* How do you know you are not dreaming right now? (hint: Think about the checks you could do: look twice, ask someone, test it another way.)
- *deep:* Behind the door, the phone sounded like a tumbler. Was the sound real, even though no tumbler was there? (hint: Think about what reached your ears both times.)
- *big:* A bat hears sounds too high for us, and a bee sees colours we cannot. Is the world each of us senses the whole world? (hint: Think about the things that are real but that your own senses miss.)
- *big:* If something is real for you but nobody else can check it, like a dream, how should we talk about it? (hint: Think about the difference between 'I really felt it' and 'it was really there'.)

## Story: The Anklet in the Dream
*Anju, Rahul and Ammachi are made up. Ammachi is a Malayalam word for grandmother. A kolussu is a silver anklet with tiny bells, worn by many girls in Kerala.*

Anju was seven, and in Class 2. She lived on the sixth floor of a building in Kochi, with her grandmother, Ammachi, and her cousin Rahul. Rahul was five.

Anju had a silver kolussu, an anklet with tiny bells. When she walked, it went chilum, chilum. One Sunday, after her bath, the kolussu was gone. She looked under the bed, behind the door and inside her school bag. It was nowhere.

That night, Anju had a dream. She was crawling under the sofa in the hall, and there was her kolussu, shining in the dark. She picked it up. She felt the cold silver in her hand. She heard the bells. She was so happy that she laughed out loud.

Then she woke up. It was morning. Her hand was empty.

"Never mind," thought Anju. "I know where it is now." She ran to the hall and lay flat on the floor. Under the sofa there was dust, one of Rahul's marbles and an old bus ticket. There was no kolussu.

"But I saw it!" said Anju. "I held it! I heard the bells!"

Ammachi came and sat on the sofa. "In your dream, did you check it any other way?" she asked. "Did you show it to me? Did you look away and look again?"

Anju thought. "No," she said. "In the dream I just knew."

"That is how dreams are," said Ammachi. "While you are in them, you just know. When you wake up, the room does not agree with them."

After lunch, Ammachi went out to the balcony to water her curry leaf plant. Anju was drawing at the table. Suddenly she heard Ammachi's voice from the kitchen. "Anju! Come and eat your banana!"

Anju ran to the kitchen. Nobody was there. Rahul was hiding behind the fridge, giggling, with Ammachi's phone in his hand. He had recorded her voice that morning, and played it.

Ammachi came in from the balcony, laughing. "So, Anju, were your ears wrong?"

"Yes!" said Anju. Then she stopped. "No. There really was a voice. It was your voice. But you were not in the kitchen. My ears were right about the sound. I was wrong about where it came from."

"That is a good answer," said Ammachi. "A sound tells you that something made it. To find out what made it, you have to check."

In the evening, Ammachi took the dry clothes off the balcony line. She dropped the pile on the bed for folding. Anju heard something very faint. Chilum.

She froze. "Did you hear that?"

"Hear what?" said Rahul.

Anju did not trust her ears yet. This time she wanted to check. She picked up the old bedsheet from the pile and shook it. Chilum, chilum. "I heard it!" shouted Rahul. Anju felt along the folds of the sheet with her fingers. There was a small hard lump, caught in the hem where the stitches had come loose. She worked it out with her fingers. It was her kolussu.

"It must have fallen into the bucket with the sheet," said Ammachi. "It went round in the washing, and then it hung on the line all day."

Anju put it on her ankle. Chilum. "The dream showed me the kolussu," she said slowly. "But the dream was wrong about where it was. This time I could check. I shook the sheet again, Rahul heard it too, and I felt it with my fingers."

Rahul was thinking about something else. "Ammachi," he said, "is air real? I can't see it."

Anju had an idea. She took a steel tumbler to the bathroom and pushed it, open end down, into a bucket of water. Then she tipped it. A big bubble burst up out of the water. Blub.

"That was the air inside," said Anju. "It was there all the time. It just does not show itself to eyes."

That night, Anju dreamed she was at school. The clock on the wall said seven o'clock. She looked away and looked back. Now it said three o'clock. Anju stood very still. "Clocks don't jump like that," she said. "I am dreaming!" In the dream, she smiled, and the classroom turned into a coconut grove.

**For the parent:** Anju sees that a dream cannot be checked, but a real thing can. The jingle came again when she shook the sheet, Rahul heard it too, and her fingers found the kolussu.

- Ask: Why did Anju feel so sure the kolussu was under the sofa?
- Ask: When Rahul played Ammachi's voice on the phone, what were Anju's ears right about, and what was she wrong about?
- Ask: Name three ways Anju checked that the jingle in the sheet was real.

## Games
### g1 · Clue Pairs: what is there, and how do we know? (15 min)
- **rules:** Before you play, draw a small sketch on each card, so a child who cannot read yet can play too.; Mix the cards and lay them face down in 4 rows of 4.; On your turn, turn over two cards and read them aloud.; If one card is a thing we cannot see, and the other is the clue that tells us it is there, you have a pair. Say why they go together.; If the other players agree that the two cards go together, you keep the pair and have another turn. If you cannot agree, ask a grown-up.; If they are not a pair, turn them back face down. The next player has a turn.; When all the cards are taken, the player with the most pairs wins.
- **Printable cards: 16 cards to cut out: 8 thing cards and 8 clue cards:** Air inside a balloon (back: Clue Pairs); The balloon is round and fat. (back: Clue Pairs); Wind outside (back: Clue Pairs); Clothes on the balcony line are flapping. (back: Clue Pairs); A pressure cooker in the next flat (back: Clue Pairs); You hear a whistle through the wall. (back: Clue Pairs); Jasmine flowers in the next room (back: Clue Pairs); A sweet smell comes through the door. (back: Clue Pairs); Rain in the night (back: Clue Pairs); The road is wet in the morning. (back: Clue Pairs); Air inside an 'empty' bottle (back: Clue Pairs); A bubble comes out when you tip the bottle under water. (back: Clue Pairs); A koel hidden in a tree (back: Clue Pairs); You hear 'koo-oo, koo-oo' from the leaves. (back: Clue Pairs); Someone frying vadas in the kitchen (back: Clue Pairs); You hear sizzling and smell hot oil. (back: Clue Pairs)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 16 cards together. 2. Draw a small sketch on each one. 3. Lay them face down in 4 rows of 4.  
  *Parent says:* Each card is either a thing we cannot see, or a clue that tells us it is there. Let's draw on them first.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Draw a sketch on each one. Lay them face down.
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take turns. 2. Each player turns over two cards and reads them aloud. 3. Ask why a pair goes together, and let the other players say if they agree.  
  *Parent says:* Is that a thing and its clue? Why do they go together?  
  *Child mode:* Turn two cards. Is it a thing and its clue? Say why.
  - Note: Some clue cards could fit more than one thing. That is a good thing to talk about. If players cannot agree, decide together which clue fits best.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the pairs. 2. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Which of these could you check another way? How?  
  *Child mode:* Count your pairs. Pick one. How else could you check it?
  - Check: You hear a whistle through the wall. What is the best way to check that it was a pressure cooker? → **Ask the neighbour, or listen for it again at meal time**
### g2 · Dream or Awake? Spot the dream sign (12 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the cards and put them in a pile, face up.; Take turns. Read the top card aloud.; Say 'dream sign', 'awake' or 'check again', and give a reason.; Turn the card over and read the back. If you were right and gave a reason, keep the card.; If you were not right, put the card at the bottom of the pile.; When the pile is gone, the player with the most cards wins.
- **Printable cards: 12 scene cards, with the answer on the back:** You open the fridge and it is full of stars. (back: Dream sign: fridges hold food, not stars.); Your school bag talks to you in your teacher's voice. (back: Dream sign: bags cannot talk. (Check: is there a phone inside?)); You read the clock: 7 o'clock. You look again: 3 o'clock. (back: Dream sign: awake, a clock moves on by a minute, not four hours.); You flap your arms and float up over Kochi. (back: Dream sign: arms cannot lift a person into the air.); Your classroom turns into your aunt's kitchen. (back: Dream sign: rooms do not change into other rooms.); An elephant fits in your pocket. (back: Dream sign: an elephant is far too big for a pocket.); Rain starts, and soon the road is wet. (back: Awake: this happens every monsoon.); You drop a steel tumbler and it clangs on the floor. (back: Awake: you can drop it again and hear the same clang.); A crow takes a piece of your dosa from the windowsill. (back: Awake: crows do this a lot.); A neighbour rings the bell with sweets for Pongal. (back: Awake: this can really happen.); On a hot day you see a puddle on the road ahead, but when you get there it is dry. (back: Awake: this is a mirage. Hot air bends light from the sky, so the road looks wet.); You press the light switch again and again, and the light stays off. (back: Check again: it could be a dream, or a power cut. Is the fan off too?)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 12 cards. 2. Explain the three answers: dream sign, awake, check again.  
  *Parent says:* A dream sign is something that could only happen in a dream. 'Check again' means you need another test first.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Learn the three answers: dream sign, awake, check again.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take turns reading cards. 2. Each player gives an answer and a reason. 3. Turn the card over to check.  
  *Parent says:* Could that happen when you are awake? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Read the card. Dream sign, awake or check again? Say why.
  - Note: The mirage card is tricky. The puddle looks real, but the light really is bent by hot air. It happens awake, and other people see it too.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the cards. 2. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Which card was hardest? What would you check if you were not sure?  
  *Child mode:* Count your cards. Which was hardest? What would you check?
  - Check: You press the light switch and the light stays off. What should you check? → **Whether the fan and other lights are off too**

## Experiments
### e1 · Real or Recorded? Guess what is making the sound behind the door (20 min)
- **question:** Behind a closed door, can your ears tell a real sound from a phone recording of the same sound? Which sounds are hardest to tell?
- **predict:** You will hear 8 sounds from behind the door. 4 will be real and 4 will come from a phone. How many do you think you will get right? Which sound will be hardest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each of the four sounds is made once for real and once from the phone, so every sound gets the same chance. The grown-up plays the phone from the same spot where the real sound is made, and about as loud. A shuffled set of slips decides the order, so nobody can guess from a pattern. The door stays shut every time.
- **record:** Write the numbers 1 to 8 down the page. After each sound, write R if you think it was real, or P if you think it was the phone. At the end, the grown-up reads out the answers and you tick each one you got right.
- **results table:** Turn | Sound | My guess: real or phone? | What it really was | Right?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. With your child watching, record four sounds on the phone, holding it close. 2. Tap the tumbler three times with the spoon. 3. Pour a little water from the tumbler into the steel bowl. 4. Crumple the newspaper sheet. 5. Say: 'Hello, can you hear me?'  
  *Parent says:* I am going to record four sounds on the phone. Later I will make each sound twice, once for real and once from the phone.  
  *Child mode:* Watch your grown-up record four sounds. Listen to each one.
  - Note: Record each sound as its own short clip, so you can play any one quickly. Play them back once to check they are clear. Keep the water well away from the phone.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shuffle the 8 slips face down and put them in a pile. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* You will hear 8 sounds. Four will be real and four will be the phone. How many do you think you will get right? Which sound will be hardest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* How many will you get right? Which sound will be hardest? Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think that sound will be the hardest? (good: The newspaper sounds the same on a phone.; The voice will be easy, because I know your real voice.; The door makes everything sound soft.)
  - Note: Write the guess and the reason down, word for word. Don't say whether it is likely to be right.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sits outside the closed door with the list. 2. Behind the door, turn over the top slip. 3. Choose a sound. If the slip says R, make it for real. If it says P, play its recording. 4. Call out the turn number first. 5. Keep the slips in order.  
  *Parent says:* Turn one. Listen carefully. Was that real, or the phone?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to each sound. Write R for real or P for phone.
  - Note: Use each sound once as real and once as phone, so keep a note of which you have used. Make the same small movements every time, so a rustle or a footstep gives nothing away. Set the phone's loudness so it sounds about as loud as the real thing.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the door. 2. Read out the slips in order: real or phone, and which sound. 3. Your child ticks each right guess and counts the ticks.  
  *Parent says:* Let's check. Turn one was the spoon on the tumbler, and it was real. Did you write R? How many did you get right altogether?  
  *Child mode:* Open the door. Tick the ones you got right. Count your ticks.
  - Ask: Which sound fooled you, or nearly fooled you? (good: The newspaper on the phone sounded real.; The pouring water was hard.; None fooled me, but the voice was close.)
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap places. 2. Your child goes behind the door with the slips and the phone. 3. The grown-up guesses all 8. 4. Count the grown-up's right answers too.  
  *Parent says:* Now you make the sounds, and I will guess. Shuffle the slips first.  
  *Child mode:* Now you make the sounds. Your grown-up guesses. Shuffle the slips first.
  - Note: Help your child play the right clip if needed, quietly through the door. It is fine if a grown-up gets fooled. That is part of the result.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what your child thinks now. 2. Ask what they heard. 3. Ask why it happened.  
  *Parent says:* When the phone played the tumbler, was there a real sound? Was there a tumbler clinking? How did you find out what made each sound?  
  *Child mode:* Was the phone's sound real? Was a tumbler clinking? How did you find out?
  - Ask: How did you find out what really made each sound? (good: We opened the door and looked.; We checked the slips.; I could not tell by ears alone. I had to look.)
  - Check: When the phone played the clinking tumbler, what reached your ears? → **A real sound, made by the phone's speaker vibrating**
### e2 · My Dream Notebook: are my dreams made from my own days? (30 min, 6 days)
- **question:** Are your dreams made of new things, or of people, places and things from your own days? Do you know you are dreaming while you dream?
- **predict:** Over five mornings, will most things in your dreams come from your own life, or will most be new? Will you know you were dreaming, while you were in the dream? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every morning is done the same way. You tell the dream before you get out of bed, and before anyone talks about anything else, because dreams fade fast. The grown-up writes down exactly what you say. Then you sort each thing in the dream with the same two questions every day.
- **record:** For each morning, write whether you remembered a dream. Draw the dream. Under it, make two lists: things from my own days, and new things. Tick yes or no for 'Did I know it was a dream while I was in it?'
- **results table:** Morning | Did I remember a dream? | Things from my own days | New things | Did I know it was a dream while I was in it?
- **schedule:** day 1: In the evening, make the dream notebook. Draw a table for five mornings. Put it beside the bed. (6 min); day 2: Before getting up, tell any dream. The grown-up writes it. Sort the things in it. (4 min); day 3: Tell, write and sort again. (4 min); day 4: Tell, write and sort again. (4 min); day 5: Tell, write and sort again. (4 min); day 6: Tell, write and sort for the last time. Then count everything up and say what you found. (8 min)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the evening, write 'My Dream Notebook' on the cover. 2. Draw a table with five rows, one for each morning. 3. Put the notebook and pencil beside the bed. 4. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* For five mornings we will catch your dreams before they fade. Will most things in your dreams come from your own days, or be new? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make your dream notebook. Will your dreams be new, or from your days? Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: My dreams are always strange, so they must be new.; I dream about school, so they come from my days.; Dreams mix things up.)
  - Note: Children often remember a dream on some mornings and not on others. A morning with no dream is still a result. Write 'no dream' and carry on.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. As soon as your child wakes, before they get up, ask: 'Did you dream?' 2. Write down exactly what they say. 3. Ask: 'While you were in the dream, did you know it was a dream?'  
  *Parent says:* Did you dream last night? Tell me everything you remember, even small bits. While you were in it, did you know it was a dream?  
  *Child mode:* Did you dream? Tell your grown-up everything you remember. Did you know it was a dream?
  - Note: Ask before anything else is said, because talking about the day makes a dream fade fast. Don't add to the dream or suggest what happened.
  - Wait: Do this every morning for five mornings
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the dream back. 2. For each person, place or thing in it, ask: 'Have you seen this in your own days?' 3. Write it in one of the two lists.  
  *Parent says:* Your dream had a bus full of cats. Have you been on a bus this week? Have you seen cats? Which list does each one go in?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each thing in your dream. Is it from your days, or new?
  - Note: A thing counts as 'from my days' if your child has really seen, done or heard about it. A mix, such as a talking cat, can go in both lists: cats from your days, talking as new.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On the last morning, count up the two lists for all five mornings. 2. Count how many times your child knew it was a dream. 3. Ask what your child thinks now, what the notebook showed, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count. How many things came from your own days? How many were new? How many times did you know you were dreaming? Why do you think that is?  
  *Child mode:* Count your two lists. Which list is longer? Did you know you were dreaming?
  - Ask: What were your dreams mostly made of? (good: Mostly things from my days, mixed up.; Mostly new things.; People I know, in strange places.)
  - Note: Most children find that most things in their dreams come from their own life, mixed up in strange ways, and that they rarely knew they were dreaming. If your child remembered no dreams at all, keep the notebook for a few more mornings.
  - Check: The scientist Mary Calkins wrote her dreams down as soon as she woke. Why? → **Dreams fade fast after waking**

## Worksheets
### Real Sounds, Real Air, Real Dreams? (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Riya, a girl in Class 2, hears a clink-clink from behind a closed door. What does the sound tell her for sure?  
   Options: Something behind the door vibrated / A steel tumbler is behind the door / Nothing, because she cannot see it  
   **Answer:** Something behind the door vibrated. *Why:* Every sound comes from something vibrating, so she knows something made it. It might be a tumbler, or a phone recording of one. Only opening the door can tell her which.
2. (truefalse) A phone recording of a tumbler is a real sound.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The phone's speaker vibrates and shakes the air, so a real sound reaches your ears. The tumbler in the recording is not there, but the sound is.
3. (match) Draw a line from each thing we cannot see to the clue that tells us it is there.  
   **Answer:** Air in a balloon → the balloon is round and fat; Wind → clothes flap on the line; A koel in a tree → 'koo-oo' from the leaves; Jeera frying → a smell from the kitchen. *Why:* Each clue reaches one of our senses: sight, hearing or smell. The thing itself stays out of sight.
4. (draw) Draw yourself outside the closed door, listening. Draw what was really making one of the sounds on the other side.  
   **Answer:** A child by a closed door, and on the other side either a tumbler and spoon, or a phone playing a recording.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that what was behind the door could only be found out by opening it.
5. (write) In the door test, which sound was hardest to tell, real or phone? Why do you think it was hard?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names one sound, such as the newspaper, and gives a reason, such as 'the phone made it sound just the same'.. *Why:* Some sounds come out of a phone speaker very like the real thing. Others change more. Your own result is the evidence.
6. (sort) Sort these: really there in your home now, or only in a dream or a story?  
   **Answer:** the air in your room → Really there; a dragon from a storybook → Only in a dream or story; a cooker whistle from the next flat → Really there; a monster you dreamed was under the bed → Only in a dream or story; the smell of jasmine from the next room → Really there; a talking cat from last night's dream → Only in a dream or story. *Why:* The things that are really there can be checked another way: smelled again, heard again, or found by someone else. The dream and story things cannot.
7. (mcq) While you are in a dream, how does the dream usually feel?  
   Options: Real / Like a film you know is pretend / Like nothing at all  
   **Answer:** Real. *Why:* Most of the time, a dream feels real until you wake. Picking 'a film you know is pretend' is how it feels only in a lucid dream, which is rare.
8. (fill) Something that is ____ can be checked in more than one way, and other people can check it too.  
   **Answer:** ['real']. *Why:* A real thing can be seen again, touched, heard or found by others. A dream cannot be checked by anyone else.
9. (perspective) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 2, and her friend Farhan, a boy in Class 2, talk about a scary dream.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, such as 'nobody else saw the tiger', and admits the other side's truth, such as 'the fear was really felt'.. *Why:* Both are partly right. The feeling in a dream really happens. The tiger was not really in the room.
10. (write) How could you check if you are awake right now? Write or draw two ways.  
   **Answer:** Two checks, such as 'read a word, look away and read it again', 'ask someone else if they see it too', or 'press the switch and see if the light comes on'.. *Why:* Awake, things stay the same when you look again, and other people can check them. In dreams, words and clocks often change.
### Clues, Air and Dreams (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Tsering, a girl in Class 2 in Leh, hears a dog bark behind a wall. How does the sound reach her ears?  
   Options: The bark shakes the air, and the shaking passes to her ears / The dog throws the sound to her like a ball / Sound can only come through an open window  
   **Answer:** The bark shakes the air, and the shaking passes to her ears. *Why:* The dog's throat vibrates and shakes the air. Each bit of air shakes the next, all the way to her ears. Picking the window answer misses that sounds come through walls too, only softer.
2. (truefalse) If you cannot see something, it cannot be there.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Air, sounds and smells are real, and nobody can see them. We know them by what they do.
3. (order) Put the steps of the door test in order.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3]. *Why:* The recordings must be made first, then the order is set by the slips, then you guess, and only then do you check.
4. (write) In the door test, you could not tell what made a sound until you opened the door. What did opening the door give you that your ears could not?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that opening the door let you check another way, with your eyes, what was really there.. *Why:* Your ears told you that a sound happened. Your eyes, after the door opened, told you what made it. One sense checked the other.
5. (count) Kavya, a girl in Class 2 in Madurai, sat at her window and heard 9 sounds. She could see what made 4 of them. How many came from things she could not see?

6. (draw) Draw what you think is inside a blown-up balloon, as if your eyes could see tiny things.  
   **Answer:** A balloon full of tiny dots or balls with space between them, moving about.. *Why:* Air is made of tiny particles, far too small to see, spread out and moving. A drawing like this is a model: a picture that explains.
7. (odd) Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: A steel spoon / The air in the room / The sound of a temple bell / The smell of rasam  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* You can see the spoon. Air, sound and smell are real too, but nobody can see them.
8. (perspective) Priya, a girl in Class 2, and Gurpreet, a girl in Class 3, talk about the door test.

### How Do We Know What Is Real? (challenge) (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (write) A flower called evening primrose makes sweeter nectar within minutes of hearing a bee's buzz. How can a flower know a bee is near, without seeing the bee?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the buzz is a vibration that reaches the flower, so the flower senses the bee by sound, the way we sense things behind a door.. *Why:* The bee's wings vibrate the air, and the flower senses the vibration. Like us, the flower knows something is there by a clue.
2. (perspective) Nandini, a girl in Class 3, and her aunt Revathi talk about knowing.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason and grants the other side something: for example, 'clues help, but smoke could also be steam, so it is good to check'.. *Why:* Long ago in India, two schools argued about this. The Charvaka school trusted only what we perceive. The Nyaya school said clues, like smoke, also give real knowledge.
3. (truefalse) Most people dream every night, even on nights they remember no dream.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Sleep scientists find REM sleep in nearly everyone, several times a night. Most dreams are forgotten soon after waking.
4. (write) In a dream, there is nobody else to ask, and nothing to check against. Why does that make it hard to notice that you are dreaming?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that awake we notice something is wrong when a check fails, but in a dream everything comes from our own mind, so nothing disagrees.. *Why:* Checking with another sense or another person is how we catch mistakes. A dream gives no outside check.
5. (sort) Sort these: do we see it directly, or know it from a clue?  
   **Answer:** the Moon in the sky → See it directly; air in an 'empty' bottle → Know it from a clue; your own hand → See it directly; wind outside → Know it from a clue; a koel hidden in a mango tree → Know it from a clue; a fire you cannot see, behind a building with smoke above it → Know it from a clue. *Why:* The Moon and your hand reach your eyes. The others are known by what they do: a bubble, flapping clothes, a call, smoke.
6. (mcq) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 2, says, 'The phone sounded exactly like the tumbler.' What is the best way to find out which it was?  
   Options: Open the door and look / Listen to it once more and decide / Ask which one she would like it to be  
   **Answer:** Open the door and look. *Why:* When one sense cannot tell, another sense can check. Listening again uses the same sense that was already fooled.
7. (draw) Draw a dream sign: something that could only happen in a dream. Then draw how you would check it.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of something impossible, such as a fridge full of stars, and a check, such as looking again or asking someone.. *Why:* A dream sign is a clue that you are dreaming. Checking is what turns a doubt into knowing.
8. (match) Draw a line from each animal to something real that they sense and we cannot.  
   **Answer:** A bat → very high squeaks; An elephant → very low rumbles; A bee → ultraviolet patterns on flowers; A pit viper, a kind of snake → the warmth of a mouse in the dark. *Why:* Each animal senses things that are really there but that our senses miss. So the world we sense is only part of what is real.
9. (fill) The dreaming part of sleep, when the eyes move fast under the lids, is called ____ sleep.  
   **Answer:** ['REM']. *Why:* REM stands for rapid eye movement. Most vivid dreams happen in REM sleep.
10. (mcq) Farhan's dream notebook had no dream on three of the five mornings. What should he do?  
   Options: Keep the notebook going for more mornings, and write 'no dream' when there is none / Make up dreams so the table is full / Stop, because he does not dream  
   **Answer:** Keep the notebook going for more mornings, and write 'no dream' when there is none. *Why:* A morning with no dream is still a result. Making dreams up would spoil the evidence. He almost surely dreamed, but forgot.

3. (mcq) A sound comes from behind a closed door. What do you know for sure?  
   Options: Something there vibrated / A person is there / A tumbler is there  
   **Answer:** Something there vibrated. *Why:* Every sound starts with something vibrating. What it was is a guess until you check. Picking 'a tumbler' means the guess has run ahead of the evidence.
4. (mcq) In the door test, the phone played a recording of crumpling newspaper. Was it a real sound?  
   Options: Yes, the phone speaker vibrated the air / No, because no newspaper was crumpling / Only if you guessed right  
   **Answer:** Yes, the phone speaker vibrated the air. *Why:* The sound was real, made by the phone. The newspaper was not there. Picking 'no' mixes up the sound with the thing it seemed to come from.
5. (mcq) In the door test, why did the grown-up use shuffled slips to choose real or phone?  
   Options: So you could not guess from a pattern / To make the test take longer / Because the phone needed the slips  
   **Answer:** So you could not guess from a pattern. *Why:* If the order went real, phone, real, phone, you could guess from the pattern instead of your ears. Shuffling keeps the test fair.
6. (mcq) Why can water hardly get into an 'empty' bottle pushed mouth down into a bucket?  
   Options: The bottle is full of air, which takes up space / Water does not like plastic / The bottle is too small  
   **Answer:** The bottle is full of air, which takes up space. *Why:* Air takes up space. Only when the bottle is tipped and the air bubbles out can water come in.
7. (mcq) Which of these shows that wind is real?  
   Options: Clothes flapping on a balcony line / A clear blue sky / A quiet room  
   **Answer:** Clothes flapping on a balcony line. *Why:* We cannot see the wind, but we see what it does to the clothes.
8. (mcq) Moumita, a girl in Class 2 in Kolkata, is not sure if she is dreaming. Which is the best check?  
   Options: Read a word, look away, and read it again / Close her eyes tightly / Decide she is awake  
   **Answer:** Read a word, look away, and read it again. *Why:* In dreams, words and clocks often change when you look again. Awake, they stay the same.
9. (mcq) Pooja, a girl in Class 2, hears a jingle in the washing and thinks it is her lost anklet. What is the best way to check it is her anklet?  
   Options: Shake the cloth again, ask someone else to listen, and feel for it / Decide it must be the anklet / Go to sleep and dream about it  
   **Answer:** Shake the cloth again, ask someone else to listen, and feel for it. *Why:* Testing it again, with another sense and another person, is how we check what is real.
10. (mcq) Before the door test, Fatima, a girl in Class 2, guessed she would get all 8 right. She got 5. What should she write?  
   Options: I guessed 8. I got 5. Some phone sounds fooled me. / I got 8, because I guessed 8. / Nothing, because my guess was wrong.  
   **Answer:** I guessed 8. I got 5. Some phone sounds fooled me.. *Why:* A good scientist writes the guess and the real result, even when they differ. A wrong guess is how we learn something new.

## Applications
- A doctor listens to a heart with a stethoscope. The heart is out of sight, but the sound tells the doctor how it is beating.
- Cooking gas has no smell of its own, so a strong smell is added to LPG. That way a family can smell a leak they cannot see.
- Weather scientists measure wind, air pressure and gases nobody can see, with instruments like the ones Anna Mani built in India.
- Mechanics listen to a scooter or car engine to find a problem hidden inside it.
- Sleep doctors record people's eyes and brain signals at night to study sleep and dreams.

## Spotted around us
- Pressure cooker whistles from neighbouring flats at breakfast and lunch time, in buildings from Chennai to Pune.
- A shimmering 'puddle' on a hot road in summer that dries up as you get close: a mirage, seen on highways in Rajasthan, Telangana and Tamil Nadu.
- Kites rising on Makar Sankranti over rooftops in Ahmedabad and Jaipur, lifted by wind nobody can see.
- Coconut palms bending and rustling on the Kerala and Goa coasts before the monsoon rain arrives.
- The call of a koel from a tree you cannot see, in almost every Indian city in spring and summer.

## Open questions
- **Why do we dream?** Scientists have three main ideas: sorting memories, practising for danger, or the brain making a story from signals. Each has some evidence. Nobody has settled it.
- **Do other animals dream the way we do?** Many animals have REM sleep, and rats replay their day's maze in their sleep. But they cannot tell us in words what their sleep is like.
- **Could a machine one day show us someone's dream?** In 2013, a team in Japan led by Yukiyasu Kamitani used brain scans to guess some kinds of things people saw in dreams, such as a car or a person. The guesses were rough, and nobody can yet watch a whole dream.
- **How does the brain decide that something feels real?** Brain scientists are still working out why a dream, a memory and the room around us each feel different, or sometimes the same.

- In the door test, Sneha got 6 of the 8 sounds right. How many did she get wrong? Did she get more right or more wrong? **2 wrong. She got more right: 6 is more than 2.**. 8 take away 6 is 2. She got 6 right and only 2 wrong. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)

If they just guessed with their eyes shut, about how many would each get right out of 8? **Leela got 3 more. Just guessing would give about 4 right, because each sound is real or phone, half and half.**. 7 take away 4 is 3. Half of 8 is 4, so 4 right is about what plain guessing gives. Leela did better than guessing. Kiran did about as well as guessing. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L2`)
