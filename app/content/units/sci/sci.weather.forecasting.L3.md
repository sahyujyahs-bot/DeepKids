# Forecasting the weather: using today's sky to tell tomorrow's
`sci.weather.forecasting.L3`

**Summary.** Nobody can see tomorrow, yet weather forecasts are right on most days. A forecast is a careful guess about the weather to come, made from what the air is doing now. Clouds, the wind and damp air are signs of what is coming. Old weather sayings are how people remembered these signs. Some sayings work, some work only in one place, and some do not work at all. Weather moves across the land and sea, so the weather heading your way is already somewhere else today. Weather offices, such as the India Meteorological Department, collect readings from all over the world and use computers to work out how the air will move. Forecasts get less sure the further ahead they look, because small things nobody measured grow bigger.

**Intro.** *Look out of the window now. Will it rain here in the next three hours? Say yes or no. Now say what you looked at to decide.* You have just made a weather forecast. A forecast is a guess about the weather to come, made from what you can see and measure now. Fishers, farmers and grandmothers have done this for thousands of years by reading the clouds and the wind. Today, weather offices do it with satellites and computers. In this unit you make a forecast every evening for a week, and check it the next day. You race your forecasts against the weather app, and against the simplest guess of all: 'tomorrow will be like today'. You also find out why some weather sayings work and others do not.

## Terms
- **Forecast**: A careful guess about the weather to come, made from what the air is doing now. *e.g.* The forecast for Madurai says, 'Sunny tomorrow, with thunder in the evening.'
- **Meteorologist**: A scientist who studies the weather and makes forecasts. *e.g.* Meteorologists at the weather office in Chennai warn fishers when a storm is coming.
- **Weather sign**: Something you can see or feel now that often comes before a kind of weather. *e.g.* Dark clouds growing very tall in the afternoon are a sign that a thunderstorm may come soon.
- **Weather saying**: A short old saying that people repeat to remember a weather sign. *e.g.* 'Red sky at night, shepherd's delight' is an old English weather saying.
- **Humidity**: How much water vapour is in the air. Water vapour is water in the form of an invisible gas. *e.g.* On a humid day in Kochi, washed clothes take a long time to dry, and your skin feels sticky.
- **Cumulus cloud**: A puffy white cloud with a flat bottom, like a heap of cotton. *e.g.* Small cumulus clouds drifting on a sunny morning usually mean fine weather.
- **Cumulonimbus cloud**: A huge, tall storm cloud. Its top can spread out flat, like the top of an anvil. It brings thunder, lightning and heavy rain. *e.g.* In Bengaluru in April and May, cumulonimbus clouds often build up in the afternoon and bring a thunderstorm by evening.
- **Cirrus cloud**: A thin, wispy cloud very high in the sky, made of tiny ice crystals. *e.g.* Long white streaks of cirrus high over the sea can show that a big storm is on its way, a day or more ahead.
- **Halo**: A ring of light around the Sun or Moon. It is made when light passes through tiny ice crystals in a thin, high cloud. *e.g.* Paati saw a pale ring around the Moon and said rain might come in a day or two.
- **Persistence forecast**: The simplest forecast: 'tomorrow will be like today'. Meteorologists use it to check whether their own forecasts are any better. *e.g.* In Chennai in March, when every day is hot and dry, 'tomorrow will be like today' is right almost every day.
- **Chance of rain**: How likely it is to rain, given as a percentage. A 60% chance of rain means that on 10 days like this one, rain would fall on about 6 of them. *e.g.* The app says 'Hyderabad: 30% chance of rain'. Rain is possible, but a dry day is more likely.
- **Weather satellite**: A machine high above the Earth that takes pictures of the clouds and measures the air from space. *e.g.* INSAT-3D, an Indian weather satellite, sends pictures of the clouds over India every half hour.
- **Weather model**: A computer program that uses the rules of how air moves and warms to work out what the weather will do next. *e.g.* The weather app's forecast for tomorrow comes from a weather model run on a very big computer.
- **Weather warning**: A message from the weather office telling people that dangerous weather is coming and what to do. *e.g.* The India Meteorological Department gives warnings in colours: yellow means be aware, orange means be prepared, and red means take action.
- **Cyclone**: A huge, swirling storm that forms over a warm sea, with very strong winds and heavy rain. *e.g.* Cyclones form over the Bay of Bengal and can bring floods to the coasts of Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha.

## Explanations
### What a forecast is · *defined*
**Definition:** A weather forecast is a guess about the weather to come, made from measurements of the weather now and knowledge of how weather changes.

A forecast is not magic and it is not a promise. Every forecast starts from the present: what the clouds, the wind, the warmth and the damp air are doing right now, here and in places nearby. Then the forecaster asks how all of this will change. The simplest forecast is 'tomorrow will be like today'. It is right surprisingly often, because weather usually changes slowly. A good forecaster has to beat this simple guess. The hard and useful part is catching the days when the weather is about to change.
- e.g. In a hot, dry Chennai March, 'tomorrow will be like today' is almost always right.
- e.g. The day before the monsoon rains reach Kerala, 'tomorrow will be like today' gets it wrong. Watching the clouds and the wind gets it right.
### Clouds as weather signs · *defined*
**Definition:** Different kinds of cloud form in different kinds of air, so the kind of cloud you see tells you something about the weather to come.

Clouds are made of tiny drops of water or tiny bits of ice, floating in the air. Small puffy cumulus clouds that stay small usually mean fine weather. Cumulus clouds that grow taller and taller during a hot afternoon can become a cumulonimbus, a storm cloud with thunder and heavy rain. A grey sheet of low cloud that covers the whole sky often brings long, steady rain. Thin wisps of cirrus, very high up, are made of ice. When cirrus slowly spreads and thickens into a sheet, a big storm system may be coming, a day or more ahead. No cloud is a sure sign. Each one makes some weather more likely.
- e.g. Fluffy clouds that stay small all afternoon: probably no rain today.
- e.g. A cloud tower building fast over the hills behind Coimbatore in the afternoon: a thunderstorm may come within an hour or two.
- e.g. High streaks of cirrus thickening into a milky sheet: rain may come in a day or two.
### Damp air as a weather sign · *defined*
**Definition:** Humidity is how much water vapour is in the air. Before rain, the air usually becomes more humid.

Water vapour is water as an invisible gas. Rain is made from water vapour in the air, so humid air is a sign that rain is possible. You can feel humidity. On a humid day, sweat dries slowly, so your skin feels sticky. Washed clothes stay damp for longer. Salt can take in water from damp air, so salt in an open dabba goes lumpy in the rainy season. Hair takes in water too, and curly hair often curls more on humid days. Humid air alone does not mean rain is coming. The damp air must also rise and cool to make clouds and rain.
- e.g. Salt in an open steel dabba turns lumpy in the monsoon months.
- e.g. Clothes on the balcony line dry in two hours on a dry day, and stay damp all day when the air is humid.
### Weather moves · *defined*
**Definition:** Clouds, rain and storms travel across land and sea, carried by winds high in the sky. So the weather coming to you tomorrow is often somewhere else today.

The rain that falls on Chennai today may have been out over the Bay of Bengal yesterday. A storm is a huge swirl of moving air, and the whole swirl travels. The wind you feel on the ground is the air swirling around the storm. That wind can blow one way while the storm itself moves another way. Benjamin Franklin found this in 1743. Clouds high up are often carried by winds that are different from the wind near the ground. This is why weather offices need readings from far away. To forecast tomorrow in Chennai, they need to know what the weather is doing over the sea today.
- e.g. Before the northeast monsoon rains reach Chennai in October and November, the clouds often build over the Bay of Bengal, to the east.
- e.g. A thunderstorm seen over the hills to the west of Bengaluru may reach the city an hour later.
### Do weather sayings work? · *debated*
Every part of the world has weather sayings. Some come from careful watching over many years and work well. Some work only in the place where they began. Some mix up a season with a day. And some do not work at all. The only way to know is to test a saying, many times, and count how often it is right. 'Red sky at night, shepherd's delight' comes from England, where most weather arrives from the west. A red sunset means the sky to the west is clear, so fine weather is coming. In places where the weather arrives from the east, the same sign can mean something else. Peacocks call loudly and dance in the monsoon. That is their breeding season, which comes with the rains. So the peacock tells you the season, not tomorrow's rain.
- e.g. 'Red sky at night, shepherd's delight. Red sky in the morning, shepherd's warning.' An old English saying.
- e.g. In Tamil Nadu people say 'Aippasi adai mazhai': in the month of Aippasi, which falls in October and November, the rain sets in and does not stop. This matches the northeast monsoon.
- e.g. Many people say that when ants carry their eggs to higher ground, rain is coming. Few careful tests of this have been done.
- **A farmer whose family has watched the sky for generations:** Our sayings come from hundreds of years of watching this land. Many of them are right more often than not, and they cost nothing.
- **A meteorologist:** Some sayings are right because they describe a real sign, such as high cloud before a storm. But people remember the times a saying worked and forget the times it failed. Only counting, many times over, shows how good a saying really is.
### How weather offices forecast · *defined*
**Definition:** Weather offices measure the air all over the world, then use computers to work out how the air will move and change over the next hours and days.

First, they measure. Thousands of weather stations on land, ships and buoys at sea, aircraft, and balloons that rise high into the sky all send readings. Satellites take pictures of the clouds from space, and radars see where rain is falling. Then a weather model, a computer program, uses the rules of how air moves and warms to work out the weather a few minutes ahead, then a few minutes after that, again and again, until it reaches tomorrow and beyond. This needs some of the biggest computers in the world. Finally, meteorologists look at the results, compare them with what they know, and write the forecast. Lewis Fry Richardson, an English scientist, tried to do this by hand during the First World War, and published it in 1922. One forecast, for just 6 hours ahead, took him weeks of work, and it came out very wrong. The first forecast made by an electronic computer was run in 1950.
- e.g. The India Meteorological Department, set up in 1875, runs weather stations all over India and sends up weather balloons every day.
- e.g. Indian satellites such as INSAT-3D photograph the clouds over India and the seas around it many times a day.
### What 'chance of rain' means · *defined*
**Definition:** A 60% chance of rain means that on 10 days with weather like this, rain would fall on about 6 of them.

The weather never gives a forecaster all the facts. So instead of saying 'it will rain' or 'it will not rain', many forecasts say how likely rain is. A 90% chance means rain is very likely. A 10% chance means rain is unlikely, but it can still happen. If the forecast says 30% and it rains, the forecast was not wrong. Rain on 3 of 10 such days is what 30% means. You only learn whether a forecaster's numbers are good by checking many days. If they say 30% on many days, it should rain on about 3 in 10 of those days.
- e.g. The app says '70% chance of rain in Mysuru'. Carry an umbrella.
- e.g. The app said '20% chance of rain', and it rained anyway. That will happen on about 1 in 5 days like this.
### Why forecasts get less sure further ahead · *defined*
**Definition:** In weather, tiny differences grow bigger over time. So a small mistake in today's measurements becomes a big mistake in a forecast many days ahead.

No one can measure every bit of air on Earth. Between weather stations there are gaps, and every reading is a little bit off. For tomorrow, these small errors hardly matter. But they grow. After a week, they can turn a sunny forecast into a rainy day. In 1961 Edward Lorenz, an American meteorologist, ran a weather program on his computer twice. The second time he typed in a number rounded off slightly, 0.506 instead of 0.506127. After a while, the two forecasts were completely different. Today a forecast for tomorrow is right most of the time. A forecast for 5 days ahead is good but less sure. For more than about two weeks ahead, nobody can forecast the details of the weather, and most scientists think no one ever will.
- e.g. Forecasts for 3 to 10 days ahead have improved by about one day each decade. A 6-day forecast today is about as good as a 5-day forecast was ten years earlier.
- e.g. Weather offices run the same model many times with tiny changes to the starting readings. If all the runs agree, the forecast is surer.
### Warnings that save lives · *defined*
**Definition:** A weather warning tells people that dangerous weather, such as a cyclone, very heavy rain or a heat wave, is coming, so they can get ready or move to safety.

Forecasts matter most when the weather is dangerous. In 1999 a very strong cyclone hit Odisha, and about ten thousand people died. In October 2013, the India Meteorological Department tracked Cyclone Phailin for days and forecast where and when it would cross the coast. Close to a million people in Odisha and Andhra Pradesh moved to shelters before the storm hit. The cyclone was about as strong as the one in 1999, but far fewer people died. The India Meteorological Department now gives warnings in colours: green means no warning, yellow means be aware, orange means be prepared, and red means take action. Fishers along the coast are told not to go out to sea when a storm is coming.
- e.g. A red warning for very heavy rain in Kerala: schools close and people living near rivers move to higher ground.
- e.g. Ports in India raise warning signals for ships when a storm is near.
### Can animals forecast the weather? · *debated*
Many people say animals know when rain is coming. Animals certainly sense changes in the air. Some insects and birds behave differently when the air becomes humid or the wind changes, and many animals take shelter as a storm comes close. Frogs croak loudly after the first monsoon rains, because that is when they find mates. But whether animals can sense rain a day or more ahead is not known. Few careful tests have been done, and people tend to remember the times the animals 'were right'.
- e.g. Ants pour out of their nest after rain. Do they also move their eggs before it? You could watch and keep count.
- e.g. Crows and mynas often go quiet and take shelter just before a thunderstorm reaches them.
- **Many farmers and fishers:** We have watched animals all our lives. Ants, birds and frogs change what they do before the rains, and they are often right.
- **Many scientists:** Animals sense the weather that is happening now, such as damp air and a rising wind, often more keenly than we do. Whether they can tell what will happen tomorrow has not been shown by careful tests.

## Mostly agreed
- Forecasts for the next day are right most of the time, and forecasts have got better every decade. *How we know:* Weather offices check every forecast against what really happened, and publish how often they were right.
- Weather travels. Storms, clouds and rain move across land and sea. *How we know:* Satellite pictures taken every half hour show clouds moving. Weather stations along a coast record a storm arriving at one town after another.
- Small differences in the air grow over time, so detailed forecasts many weeks ahead are not possible. *How we know:* Running the same weather model with tiny changes to the starting numbers gives very different weather after about two weeks.
- Some cloud signs really do help forecast the weather, such as tall cumulonimbus clouds before a thunderstorm. *How we know:* Weather observers have recorded clouds and the weather that followed for more than two hundred years.
- Warnings of cyclones and heavy rain save many lives. *How we know:* Far fewer people died in Odisha in Cyclone Phailin in 2013 than in the cyclone of 1999, which was about as strong, because people moved to shelters in time.

## How we found out
**Benjamin Franklin, a printer and scientist in Philadelphia, with letters and newspaper reports from Boston, 1743, the night of an eclipse of the Moon.** On a November night in 1743, Benjamin Franklin, a printer and scientist in Philadelphia in America, hoped to watch an eclipse of the Moon. A storm came instead. Thick clouds hid the sky, and a strong wind blew from the northeast. In those days, people thought a storm travels the way its wind blows. So Franklin guessed that this storm had come from the northeast, from the direction of Boston, where his brother lived. He thought Boston must have missed the eclipse too. Later, letters and newspapers from Boston surprised him. People there had seen the eclipse clearly, and the storm reached Boston hours after it hit Philadelphia. The storm had moved towards the northeast, against its own wind. Franklin collected reports of later storms from towns along the coast and found the same pattern. A storm's winds swirl around it, while the whole storm travels somewhere else. This also showed something useful. If storms travel, news of a storm can be sent ahead of it. After the telegraph was invented, towns could be warned before a storm arrived.  
*What people believed before:* People thought a storm travels in the direction its wind blows. Franklin found that a storm with a northeast wind moved towards the northeast, against its wind, because a storm is a swirl of air that travels as a whole.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A weather forecast is only a guess, so it is no use.** (Some people, after a forecast went wrong; *rejected*)  
  For: Forecasts are sometimes wrong, and people remember those days.  
  Against: A forecast for tomorrow is right on most days, much more often than chance. Even a forecast that is sometimes wrong helps you decide, and warnings of cyclones have saved many thousands of lives.
- **When the forecast said '30% chance of rain' and it rained, the forecast was wrong.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: The forecast seemed to say rain was unlikely, and then it rained.  
  Against: 30% means rain on about 3 of every 10 days like this one. Rain on one such day is what the forecast allowed. To judge the forecast, you need to check many days.
- **'Red sky at night, shepherd's delight' works everywhere.** (An old English saying, now repeated around the world; *debated*)  
  For: In England, where most weather comes from the west, a red sunset often means a clear sky to the west and fine weather coming.  
  Against: The saying depends on which way the weather usually travels. Where weather often arrives from the east, as in Chennai in the northeast monsoon, the sign may not work. Test it where you live.
- **Animals can sense rain a day or more before it comes.** (Many farmers, fishers and grandparents; *debated*)  
  For: Animals sense humid air and changes in the wind keenly, and many people have seen them act differently before rain.  
  Against: Careful tests that count the times the animals were wrong, as well as right, are rare. Animals may be reacting to weather that is already changing.
- **With big enough computers, we will one day forecast the weather a month ahead, day by day.** (A hope some people have held; *minority*)  
  For: Forecasts have improved by about a day each decade, and computers keep getting faster.  
  Against: Lorenz showed that tiny differences grow. Most meteorologists think day-by-day detail cannot be forecast beyond about two weeks, however big the computer. Forecasts of a whole season, such as 'a wetter monsoon than usual', are possible, but only as chances.

## Roots
We have learned to make forecasts from clouds, wind and damp air, and to check them. Now we ask what forecasting tells us about time, and about the world.

**Deep truth.** Every forecast in your contest was made from that evening's sky and readings. The weather that came next was already in the air somewhere, moving towards you. So the better we measure today, over a wider area, the further ahead we can see.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of tomorrow as hidden and cut off from today, something only a fortune-teller could see. But tomorrow's weather grows out of air that exists today, so knowing tomorrow comes from reading today carefully and widely.  
*Evidence:* In the forecast contest, every guess, even the weather app's, used only what could be seen or measured that evening. Franklin's storm and the Bay of Bengal rains show weather reaching one place after being somewhere else. Lorenz's rounded number shows the limit: what nobody measured grows.

- *who am i:* You are a forecaster. Every time you look at the sky and take an umbrella, you are reading today to guess tomorrow.
- *what is life:* Farmers, fishers, ants and birds all live by the weather. Knowing what is coming helps every living being get ready.
- *what is this world:* The air around the whole Earth is one moving ocean. The air on your balcony now was somewhere else this morning.

There is one layer of air wrapped around the whole Earth, always moving. It shows up as many weathers: a hot still afternoon in Madurai, a storm over the Bay of Bengal, snow in Ladakh. They are all the same air, warmed and cooled in different places. Because it is one moving whole, the weather in one place tomorrow grows out of the weather in other places today. That is why a forecast for one city needs readings from all over the world.

- **Tirukkural, chapter 2, 'The Excellence of Rain', verse 11:** The world goes on living because of rain. So rain should be thought of as amrita, the nectar of life. (Rain mattered so much to farmers that people watched the sky every day and made sayings to remember its signs. Forecasting began from that care.)
- **The Buddha's teaching of dependent arising, in the Samyutta Nikaya 12.21:** When this is, that comes to be. From the arising of this, that arises. (Tomorrow's rain arises from today's warm sea, rising damp air and wind. A forecast follows these causes forward, one step at a time.)
- **Heraclitus, the Greek philosopher, as reported by Plato in the Cratylus (402a):** Everything moves on and nothing stays still. You cannot step into the same river twice. (The air never stays still. The air you breathe on the balcony this evening will be far away tomorrow, and new air will have taken its place.)
- Science (settled): Weather models start from measurements of the air now and work forward using the rules of how air moves and warms.
- Science (settled): Tiny differences in the air grow over time, so detailed weather cannot be forecast more than about two weeks ahead.
- Science (open): Exactly how far ahead the weather could ever be forecast, even with perfect computers, is still argued about.
- Other view, Many scientists who study chaos: Tomorrow is not simply sitting in today's air waiting to be read. Tiny changes that no instrument could ever measure grow into big ones, so the weather a month from now is truly open. Today's air shapes the next few days, but it does not fix the far future.
- Practice, Where was this air this morning? (3 min): Let's stand at the window or on the balcony. Close your eyes. Feel the air on your face and arms. Is it moving? Which side of your face feels it most? Now take three slow breaths. This air was somewhere else this morning. Maybe it was over the sea, or over another street. Tomorrow it will be somewhere else again. Open your eyes. Which way do you think it is going?
- Ask: If you could measure every bit of air on Earth tonight, could you know tomorrow's weather for sure?
- Ask: The air on your balcony now was somewhere else this morning. Where does 'our' weather begin and end?
- Ask: Is a forecast that says '60% chance of rain' a way of knowing, or a way of not knowing?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* Tomorrow's weather grows out of today's air. So is tomorrow already decided, or still open? (hint: Think of Lorenz's two runs, which started almost the same and ended completely different.)
- *deep:* A storm's wind blows one way, but the whole storm travels another way. What, then, is a storm: the wind, or the swirl? (hint: Stir water in a bucket and then push the bucket. Which way does the water go round, and which way does the bucket move?)
- *deep:* If a forecast says 60% chance of rain, is the forecast right or wrong when it does not rain? (hint: Think of 10 days with the same forecast. On how many would you expect rain?)
- *big:* Grandparents' sayings and the weather office's computer both try to tell tomorrow. How are they alike, and how are they different? (hint: Both start from signs. Which one checks itself against what really happened, day after day?)
- *big:* Why do we remember the times a saying came true more than the times it failed? (hint: Think of a time you were sure a saying worked. Did you count the times it did not?)
- *deep:* A butterfly flapping their wings changes the air a tiny bit. Could that change, weeks later, change where a storm goes? (hint: Lorenz asked this question in a talk in 1972. Nobody can check it for one butterfly. What does his rounded number show?)

## Story: Three Guessers on the Balcony
*Kavya, Arjun, Paati and their flat are made up. The facts are real: Chennai gets most of its rain in the northeast monsoon, from October to December. Rain systems often form over the Bay of Bengal and move west to the coast, and high thin cloud can spread out ahead of them. Salt goes lumpy in damp air.*

Kavya was nine, and she lived on the sixth floor of a building in Besant Nagar, in Chennai. From the balcony, if she leaned a little, she could see a strip of grey-blue sea between two other buildings. Her little cousin Arjun was staying with them for the October holidays. He was seven, and he had one big wish. On Saturday it would be his birthday, and he wanted to fly his new kite on the beach.

On Thursday evening, Amma looked at the weather app on her phone. 'Saturday: seventy per cent chance of rain,' she read.

'That app is silly,' said Arjun. 'Look at the sky. It's all blue. Saturday will be sunny, like today.'

Paati was sitting on the balcony with her evening tea. She did not look at the phone. She looked east, over the strip of sea. High up, very high, there were thin white streaks, like hairs combed across the sky.

'Mazhai varudhu,' said Paati quietly. Rain is coming.

'How do you know?' asked Kavya. 'There isn't a single dark cloud.'

'Those thin clouds, high up, come before the big rain from the sea,' said Paati. 'My father was a fisherman in Pulicat. He watched them every evening. Sometimes they come and nothing happens. But when they get thicker, day after day, the boats stay on the beach.'

Kavya fetched her notebook. She drew three columns and wrote a name at the top of each: PAATI, THE APP, ARJUN. 'Every evening, all three of you make a forecast for tomorrow,' she said. 'Rain or no rain. The next evening, I check who was right and give a point.'

'I'll win,' said Arjun. 'Tomorrow will be like today. Easy.'

On Friday morning, Kavya ran out to the balcony before brushing her teeth. Thursday's forecasts had been for Friday: Paati said no rain yet, the app said no rain, and Arjun said sunny, like Thursday. Friday was dry and hot. Everyone got a point.

But the sky had changed. The thin streaks had spread into a pale milky sheet, and the sun looked weak through it. That night Kavya saw a big ring of light around the Moon. Paati nodded at it. 'Ice, very high up,' she said. 'The rain is getting closer.'

Something else had changed too. The wind had been blowing from the sea in the afternoons, as usual, but now it kept blowing in the evening, steady and cool, from the northeast. The air felt heavy and sticky on Kavya's arms. When Amma opened the salt dabba to make rasam, she frowned. 'Lumpy,' she said, tapping the spoon. 'The air is wet.'

On Friday evening, Kavya took the forecasts for Saturday. Paati said rain. The app now said ninety per cent chance of rain. Arjun crossed his arms. 'Today was dry,' he said. 'So tomorrow will be dry.'

It started to rain at five o'clock on Saturday morning. It was not a quick shower. It rained all day, steady and grey, and the road below turned into a brown stream. Arjun stood at the balcony door in his birthday shirt, holding his kite and looking at the water.

Kavya gave Paati a point and the app a point. Arjun got nothing. He looked as if he might cry.

'How did the app know?' he asked. 'It hasn't got a Paati.'

Amma showed them the weather office's website on her phone. There was a picture taken from a satellite. A huge white swirl of cloud sat over the Bay of Bengal, right next to the coast of Tamil Nadu. Amma pressed a button, and the picture played the last three days like a film. On Wednesday the swirl had been far out at sea. On Thursday it was closer. On Friday it was spreading towards the coast. Today it was over them.

Kavya stared at it. 'So Saturday's rain was already there on Thursday,' she said slowly. 'It was just out at sea. Paati saw the edge of it, the high thin clouds. The app saw the whole thing from space.'

'And I only looked at our sky,' said Arjun. 'Our sky was blue.'

'Your way isn't silly,' said Kavya, because she could see he was upset. 'Look at my notebook. On Friday you were right too. Weather doesn't change most days. When it doesn't, you win. You only lose on the days it changes.'

'And those are the days that matter,' said Paati, 'if you are a fisherman. Or a boy with a kite.'

The rain stopped on Monday. On Tuesday the sky was clear, and Arjun flew his kite on the beach. That evening he made his own forecast for Wednesday, but this time he looked east first, over the sea, before he said a word.

Kavya kept the contest going for two whole weeks. At the end, the app had the most points, Paati was close behind, and Arjun was third, but not by much. At the bottom of the page, Kavya wrote one line: 'Tomorrow's weather is today's weather somewhere else, on its way.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: the rain that fell on Saturday was already over the Bay of Bengal on Thursday, and it travelled to the coast. Paati read its first signs: high cirrus cloud spreading ahead of it, a halo around the Moon, a steady wind from the sea, and damp air that made the salt lumpy. The weather app used satellite pictures and computer models to see the whole system. Arjun's 'tomorrow will be like today' is a real forecast, called a persistence forecast. It wins on most ordinary days and loses on the days when the weather changes.

- Ask: What signs did Paati see before the rain came? Which one would you look for first?
- Ask: Why was Arjun right on Friday but wrong on Saturday?
- Ask: How could the app know about the rain before anyone in Chennai could see a rain cloud?
- Ask: At the end, Arjun looked east before making his forecast. Why east?
- Ask: Kavya kept score for two weeks, not just one day. Why was that fairer?

## Games
### g1 · Sky Signs: a forecasting card game (15 min)
- **rules:** Each player gets a set of 4 forecast cards. Shuffle the sign cards and put them face down, with the brackets hidden.; Turn over the top sign card and read it aloud, without the answer in brackets.; Every player chooses one forecast card and puts it face down. Then everyone turns their card over together.; Read the answer in brackets. Everyone who matched it scores 2 points.; If you chose a different card, you can still score 1 point by giving a good reason why your forecast could also happen.; Some cards say 'possible' or 'sometimes'. For those, everyone who says why the sign is not a sure thing scores 1 extra point.; After all 16 cards, the player with the most points wins. Playing alone, try to beat your own score next time.
- **Printable cards: 16 sign cards to cut out (the answer is in brackets at the bottom of each card):** Small puffy white clouds that stay small all afternoon. (Fine weather); Puffy clouds growing taller and taller on a hot afternoon. (Thunderstorm soon); A giant cloud with a dark bottom and a flat top spreading out. (Thunderstorm soon); Thin white streaks very high up, slowly thickening into a milky sheet. (Rain in a day or two); A pale ring of light around the Moon. (Rain in a day or two, sometimes); A low grey sheet of cloud over the whole sky. (Steady rain); No clouds at all, dry air, no wind, in March. (Fine weather); The air feels sticky, and washed clothes stay damp all day. (Rain is possible); Salt in the dabba has gone lumpy. (Rain is possible); A sudden cold, gusty wind and the smell of rain. (Thunderstorm soon); Thunder far away, getting louder. (Thunderstorm soon); The satellite film shows a big swirl of cloud over the Bay of Bengal, moving towards the coast. (Rain in a day or two); The weather office gives a red warning for very heavy rain. (Steady rain, and danger); Today was hot and dry, and the sky is still clear tonight. (Fine weather); Peacocks calling loudly in July. (The monsoon season, not a forecast for tomorrow); The forecast says 10% chance of rain. (Fine weather, most likely)
- **Printable cards: 4 forecast cards for each player:** Fine weather; Thunderstorm soon; Rain in a day or two; Steady rain
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player name; Points this round; Total
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the cards. 2. Give each player 4 forecast cards. 3. Shuffle the sign cards and put them face down.  
  *Parent says:* In this game, each card shows a sign in the sky or the air. You forecast the weather it points to.  
  *Child mode:* Get your 4 forecast cards. Mix up the sign cards. Put them face down.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first sign card, 'Puffy clouds growing taller and taller on a hot afternoon', as a practice round.  
  *Parent says:* Practice card. Puffy clouds are growing taller and taller on a hot afternoon. What's your forecast?  
  *Child mode:* Practice card. Puffy clouds are growing taller on a hot afternoon. What's your forecast?
  - Check: Puffy clouds are growing taller and taller on a hot afternoon. What is the best forecast? → **Thunderstorm soon**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the rest of the cards, keeping score.  
  *Parent says:* Next card. Choose your forecast. Ready? Turn it over. Who matched?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the next card. Choose your forecast. Turn them over together. Who matched?
  - Note: The peacock card is a trick worth talking about. Peacocks call and dance in the monsoon because it is their breeding season. So they tell you the season, not tomorrow's weather.

The towns were warned in time.; If the cyclone reaches the coast first, count the towns that were warned. Play again and try to warn them all.

- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns answering cards, placing flags and moving the cyclone.  
  *Parent says:* Your question. Right? Put a flag on a town. Now the storm moves. Will we warn everyone in time?  
  *Child mode:* Answer your card. Right? Put a flag on a town. Then roll for the storm.
  - Check: Why does warning towns early save lives? → **People have time to move to safety**
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the game.  
  *Parent says:* In the game, the earlier we saw the storm, the more time we had. How do real forecasters see a cyclone early?  
  *Child mode:* How do real forecasters see a cyclone early?
  - Ask: How do real forecasters see a cyclone early? (good: Satellites.; Radar.; Ships and buoys at sea.)

## Experiments
### e1 · Tomorrow Like Today? A week-long forecast contest (60 min, 8 days)
- **question:** Whose forecast for tomorrow is right most often: 'tomorrow will be like today', your own reading of the sky, or the weather app?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which of the three forecasters will get the most points in a week? Which will get the fewest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three forecasts are written at the same time each evening, before anyone knows what tomorrow brings. Nobody changes a forecast after writing it. All three are judged on the same two questions, by the same rules. We run the contest for 7 days, because one day can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Look at the sky, never straight at the Sun. If there is lightning, watch from indoors, away from the open balcony.
- **record:** Fill in one row of the table every evening. On day 8, add up each forecaster's points and draw a bar for each.
- **results table:** Day | Same as today: rain? sky? | My sky reading: rain? sky? | Weather app: rain? sky? | What really happened | Points: same as today | Points: me | Points: app
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up the score sheet. Write down today's weather. Make the first three forecasts for tomorrow. (15 min); day 2: Check yesterday's three forecasts and give points. Then make three new forecasts for tomorrow. (5 min); day 3: Check and score. Make tomorrow's forecasts. (5 min); day 4: Check and score. Make tomorrow's forecasts. Who is ahead so far? (5 min); day 5: Check and score. Make tomorrow's forecasts. (5 min); day 6: Check and score. Make tomorrow's forecasts. (5 min); day 7: Check and score. Make the last forecasts. (5 min); day 8: Check and score the last day. Add up the points, draw a bar chart and say what you found. (15 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the table in the notebook, or print the score sheet. 2. Write the three forecasters at the top: 'Same as today', 'Me', 'The app'. 3. Read out the two questions everyone will answer each evening.  
  *Parent says:* We're going to have a forecast contest for a week. Every evening, three forecasters guess tomorrow. The first just says tomorrow will be like today. The second is you, reading the sky. The third is the weather app. Each evening they answer two questions. Will it rain tomorrow? Will the sky be mostly sunny or mostly cloudy?  
  *Child mode:* You are running a forecast contest! Three forecasters guess tomorrow's weather. One says 'same as today'. One is you. One is the weather app. Each answers two questions. Will it rain? Sunny or cloudy?
  - Note: Agree on the rules now, and write them at the top of the page. Rain means enough rain to wet the balcony floor or the road, at any time from morning to bedtime. 'Mostly cloudy' means clouds cover more than half the sky for most of the day. For the app, a chance of rain of 50% or more counts as 'yes, rain'.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to predict the winner and give a reason. Write the guess and the reason at the top of the page.  
  *Parent says:* Before we start, which forecaster do you think will win the week? Which will come last? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the winner. Who will come last? Why do you think so? Write your guess at the top.
  - Ask: Why do you think that forecaster will win? (good: The app has satellites and computers.; Weather doesn't change much, so 'same as today' will be right a lot.; I can see the clouds myself.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is good. Don't say which one usually wins. Let the week answer it.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go to the window or balcony. 2. Write today's weather in the 'What really happened' column: rain or no rain, mostly sunny or mostly cloudy. 3. Write the 'Same as today' forecast for tomorrow: just copy today.  
  *Parent says:* First, what is today's weather? Did it rain today? Was the sky mostly sunny or mostly cloudy? The first forecaster just copies today for tomorrow.  
  *Child mode:* Look outside. Did it rain today? Was it sunny or cloudy? Write it down. Copy today into the 'Same as today' column for tomorrow.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to look at the sky for two minutes. They notice the clouds, which way they move, and how the air feels. 2. They write their own forecast for tomorrow. 3. Then open the weather app and write its forecast for tomorrow.  
  *Parent says:* Now it's your turn. Look carefully. Are there clouds? What kind? Are they growing or shrinking? Is there wind? Does the air feel sticky? Now make your forecast for tomorrow. Then we'll write what the app says.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the sky for two minutes. What clouds? Is there wind? Is the air sticky? Write your forecast for tomorrow. Then write the app's forecast.
  - Ask: What did you see that made you choose your forecast? (good: The clouds are getting bigger.; The sky is clear and there is no wind.; The air is sticky, so rain might come.)
  - Note: Always write your child's forecast before looking at the app, so the app does not change their mind. That keeps the test fair.
  - Wait: Every evening for the next 6 evenings, at about the same time, do steps s5 and then s3 and s4 again.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Each evening, look at yesterday's three forecasts. 2. Give each forecaster 1 point for a right rain answer, and 1 point for a right sunny or cloudy answer. 3. Then make the three new forecasts.  
  *Parent says:* Let's check yesterday's forecasts against what really happened today. One point for the right rain answer, and one point for the right sky answer. Who scored today?  
  *Child mode:* Check yesterday's forecasts. Was each one right about rain? About sun or cloud? Give 1 point for each right answer.
  - Note: On day 4, look at the totals together. Who is ahead? Is it what your child guessed?
  - Check: Yesterday, 'Same as today' said 'no rain, sunny'. Today it rained, and the sky was cloudy all day. How many points does 'Same as today' get? → **0 points**
- **s6 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 8, after the last check: 1. Add up each forecaster's points. The most anyone can get is 14. 2. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each forecaster.  
  *Parent says:* The contest is over. Let's add up the points. The most anyone could get is 14, because there were 7 days and 2 points a day. Now let's draw a bar for each forecaster.  
  *Child mode:* The contest is over! Add up each forecaster's points. The most is 14. Draw a bar for each one.
  - Note: If every day was the same weather, all three may have scored 14. That is a real result, not a failed test. In a settled spell, 'same as today' is very hard to beat. If you can, run the contest again when the weather is changing, for example at the start of the monsoon or in October and November.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a claim, evidence and reasoning. Then explain, if your child has not said it already, why 'same as today' does well and when it fails.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? Who won? Which days did each forecaster get wrong? What was happening on those days?  
  *Child mode:* Who won? Which days did each forecaster get wrong? What was the weather doing on those days?
  - Ask: On the days 'Same as today' was wrong, what had happened? (good: The weather changed.; It rained after a dry day.; The clouds came.)
  - Ask: How do you think the app knew about a change before it happened? (good: It sees the clouds coming from far away.; Satellites see the whole sky.; The weather was already somewhere else.)
  - Note: The usual pattern: weather changes slowly on most days, so 'same as today' scores well. It fails exactly when the weather changes. A good forecast earns its keep by catching those changes, and it can only do that by seeing the weather that is on its way, somewhere else. If your child's own forecasts beat 'same as today', ask which signs helped.
### e2 · Salt in the Steamy Bathroom: does damp air change salt? (25 min)
- **question:** Many people say salt goes lumpy before rain. Does damp air really change salt?
- **predict:** Guess first. One plate of salt goes into a steamy bathroom, and one goes into a dry room. After one hour, will they look and feel different? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same kind of salt, the same amount and the same kind of plate. Only the place changes: the steamy bathroom or a dry room. Leave both for the same time, then test both in the same way.
- **safety:** A grown-up runs the hot water. Hot water and hot taps can scald. Don't eat the salt afterwards: throw it away or use it to scrub a vessel.
- **record:** Fill in the table for both plates. Draw both plates after tilting, showing where the salt stayed.
- **results table:** Plate | Where it was | How the salt looks | Does it slide off when tilted? (yes, some, no) | How it feels when pressed with a finger
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 1 level teaspoon of salt on each steel plate. 2. Spread it into a thin, even patch about the size of a biscuit. 3. Tilt each plate a little to check the dry salt slides.  
  *Parent says:* Here are two plates with the same salt, spread the same way. Watch what dry salt does when I tilt the plate.  
  *Child mode:* Put one spoon of salt on each plate. Spread it thin. Tilt a plate. See how dry salt slides?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to predict, with a reason.  
  *Parent says:* One plate goes into the bathroom after a hot bath, where the air is full of steam. The other goes into a dry room. What will the salt be like after an hour? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* One plate goes in the steamy bathroom. One goes in a dry room. What will happen in an hour? Why?
  - Ask: Why might the bathroom change the salt? (good: The air is wet.; Salt soaks up water.; Steam makes things damp.)
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Just after a hot bath or shower, put plate 1 on a dry bathroom shelf, away from splashes. 2. Shut the door and window and turn off any exhaust fan. 3. Put plate 2 in the driest room, away from the kitchen.  
  *Parent says:* The bathroom air is full of water vapour from the hot water. We shut the door to keep it in. The other plate goes where the air is dry.  
  *Child mode:* Put plate 1 on a dry shelf in the steamy bathroom. Shut the door. Put plate 2 in a dry room.
  - Note: Put the plate where no water can splash or drip on it, so only the damp air reaches the salt. On a very humid monsoon day, the dry room may not be very dry. Then put plate 2 in a room with an air conditioner, or just compare with fresh salt from the dabba.
  - Wait: Wait one hour.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Bring both plates out together. 2. Look closely at each. 3. Tilt each plate slowly until it is nearly upright. 4. Press the salt gently with a finger.  
  *Parent says:* Let's compare. Look first. Now tilt each plate slowly. Does the salt slide off? Now press it gently with a finger. How does it feel?  
  *Child mode:* Bring both plates. Look closely. Tilt each one slowly. Does the salt slide? Press it gently. How does it feel?
  - Ask: What is different about the bathroom salt? (good: It is stuck to the plate.; It is lumpy and damp.; It doesn't slide off.)
  - Note: Usually the bathroom salt looks darker and wetter, sticks to the plate and clumps together, while the dry-room salt slides off. If you see no difference, put the bathroom plate back and leave it overnight with the door shut, or try on a very humid monsoon day.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a claim, evidence and reasoning. Then explain humidity and what this means for the saying.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? What took in the water? So, does lumpy salt tell us that rain is coming?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? Where did the water come from? Does lumpy salt mean rain is coming?
  - Note: The saying has a true part: salt really does show that the air is damp, and damp air often comes before rain. But damp air does not always bring rain. A strong answer says both.
  - Check: The bathroom salt went lumpy. Where did the water come from? → **Water vapour in the damp air**
### e3 · Ground Wind and Cloud Wind: do they go the same way? (25 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Does the wind on your balcony blow the same way as the clouds high above are moving?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the clouds move the same way as the wind on your balcony? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same spot each time. Check the ground wind with the same ribbon and the clouds against the same fixed edge. Check on 3 different days, because one day can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Hold the stick inside the balcony railing. Never lean over a railing or climb onto anything. Never look straight at the Sun.
- **record:** Fill in the table each day. Draw an arrow for the ground wind and an arrow for the clouds on a little compass drawing.
- **results table:** Day | Time | Ground wind comes from (north, south, east or west) | Clouds come from | Same or different?
- **schedule:** day 1: Make the wind ribbon. Check the ground wind and the cloud direction. Write both down. (10 min); day 2: Check both again, at about the same time. (5 min); day 3: Check both again. Compare all three days. (10 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie the plastic strip or ribbon to the end of the pencil. 2. Find north on the phone compass, or face where the Sun rises, which is east. 3. Write N, E, S and W on a page as a cross.  
  *Parent says:* We'll make a wind ribbon to see which way the air is moving on our balcony. First, let's find north, east, south and west.  
  *Child mode:* Tie the ribbon to the pencil. Find north with the phone. Draw a cross. Write N, E, S, W.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a prediction with a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Do you think the clouds high up will move the same way as the wind here? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Will the clouds move the same way as the wind here? Why?
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the ribbon out at arm's length, inside the railing. 2. The ribbon streams away from where the wind comes from. 3. Write where the wind comes from.  
  *Parent says:* Hold it still. The ribbon points away from where the wind comes from. So if it streams towards the west, the wind is coming from the east.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the ribbon out, inside the railing. Which way does it stream? The wind comes from the other side. Write it down.
  - Note: Stay with your child on the balcony. Buildings bend the wind, so if the ribbon flaps about in all directions, try a different side of the balcony or an open window on another side.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick a cloud close to a fixed edge, such as the top of a building. 2. Keep your head still and watch for one minute. 3. Which way does it move? Write where the clouds come from.  
  *Parent says:* Clouds move slowly, so we watch one against something that stays still. Keep your head still. Is the cloud moving away from the edge or towards it?  
  *Child mode:* Pick a cloud near the top of a building. Keep your head still. Watch for one minute. Which way does it move?
  - Note: Thin high clouds can be hard to read. Lower, puffy clouds are easiest. If there are no clouds at all, try again another day.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3, compare the three days. Ask for a claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* On how many days did the clouds go the same way as the ground wind? What does that tell you about the air high up?  
  *Child mode:* Look at all three days. Same or different? What does it tell you about the wind high up?
  - Note: In the afternoon in coastal cities like Chennai, a sea breeze often blows in from the sea near the ground, while clouds above move a different way. Any result is fine. The idea is that the air has layers that move differently.
  - Check: On your balcony the ribbon shows wind from the east, but the clouds are coming from the west. What does that tell you? → **The air high up is moving a different way from the air near the ground**

## Activities
### a1 · Read the Sky Now: your first forecast (12 min)
- **safety:** Never look straight at the Sun.
- **record:** Draw the sky now. Write your forecast and the time. Three hours later, write what really happened.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go to the window or balcony together. 2. Ask your child to say everything they notice about the sky and the air, without naming any weather yet.  
  *Parent says:* What do you notice about the sky right now? What colour is it? Are there clouds? What do they look like? Can you feel any wind? How does the air feel on your skin?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the sky. What colour is it? Are there clouds? What shape? Is there wind? How does the air feel?
  - Ask: What do you notice? (good: Small puffy clouds.; A big dark cloud over there.; No clouds and no wind.; It feels sticky.)
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to draw the sky. 2. Ask them for a forecast for the next three hours: rain or no rain, and what the clouds will do. 3. Write down the time.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the sky as it is now. Now make a forecast. In three hours, will it be raining? Will there be more cloud or less? What makes you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the sky now. What will it be like in three hours? Rain or no rain? More clouds or fewer? Why?
  - Ask: What made you choose your forecast? (good: The clouds are getting bigger.; It's clear, so it will stay dry.; The wind is getting stronger.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Three hours later, look again. Write what happened next to the forecast.  
  *Parent says:* It's been three hours. Let's look. Was your forecast right? What changed? What did you see earlier that could have told you?  
  *Child mode:* Three hours later, look again. Were you right? What changed?
  - Note: Don't worry about whether the forecast was right. The habit we want is to look closely, guess with a reason, and check.
  - Wait: Wait three hours, then do this step.
### a2 · Ask Paati: collect weather sayings (15 min)
- **safety:** None.
- **record:** Write each saying in the language it was said, then what it means in English. Mark each one: a sign we can test, or a season.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Help your child phone or visit an older relative or neighbour. 2. Your child asks: 'What signs did people look at to know rain was coming? Were there any sayings about the weather?' 3. Write down every answer.  
  *Parent says:* Older people often know signs and sayings about the weather. Let's ask Paati. You ask the question, and I'll help you write.  
  *Child mode:* Ask Paati or Thatha: what signs tell you rain is coming? Do you know any weather sayings? Write them down.
  - Note: Answers may be about clouds, wind, the smell of the air, ants, birds, the Moon, or the months. Write sayings in the language they come in, Tamil, Malayalam, Telugu, Kannada, Hindi or any other, and then what they mean.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sort the sayings into two groups on the page: 'a sign we could test this week' and 'about a season'.  
  *Parent says:* Some sayings are about a sign you can watch for, like a ring around the Moon. Others are about a time of year, like the rains in Aippasi. Let's sort them. Which ones could we test?  
  *Child mode:* Sort the sayings. Which ones are a sign you can watch for? Which ones are about a season?
  - Ask: How could we test one of these sayings? (good: Watch for the sign and see if rain comes the next day.; Count how many times it is right.; Write it down every time we see the sign.)
  - Note: Choose one testable saying to use in the project p1, the Family Book of Weather Sayings.
### a3 · Six Out of Ten: what 'chance of rain' means (15 min)
- **safety:** Children under 4 must not play with beans, because they can choke. Don't eat the raw beans.
- **record:** Draw 10 boxes for 10 days. In each box draw a rain cloud for a rajma and a sun for a chana.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 6 rajma and 4 chana in the tumbler. 2. Explain: each rajma is a day it rains, each chana is a dry day.  
  *Parent says:* Here are 10 beans. 6 red rajma stand for rain, and 4 white chana stand for no rain. This tumbler is like a forecast that says 60% chance of rain.  
  *Child mode:* Put 6 rajma and 4 chana in the tumbler. Rajma means rain. Chana means dry. This is a 60% chance of rain.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child shakes the tumbler, picks one bean without looking, and draws a cloud or a sun in box 1. 2. They put the bean back and shake again. 3. Repeat for all 10 boxes.  
  *Parent says:* Each pick is one day with the same forecast. Pick without looking. Rain or dry? Now put it back, so the chance stays the same, and pick again.  
  *Child mode:* Shake. Pick one bean without looking. Rajma? Draw a cloud. Chana? Draw a sun. Put it back. Do it 10 times.
  - Ask: Before you pick, can you tell whether this 'day' will be rainy? (good: No, but rain is more likely.; Rajma is more likely, because there are more of them.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the rain days. Talk about what happened.  
  *Parent says:* How many of your 10 days had rain? Was it exactly 6? If the forecast said 60%, and one day stayed dry, was the forecast wrong?  
  *Child mode:* Count your rain days. Was it exactly 6? If one day was dry, was the forecast wrong?
  - Note: Your child may get 5, 6, 7 or even 8 rain days out of 10. That is normal with chance. Doing it again gives a different count. Over many tries, it comes close to 6 in 10.
  - Check: The forecast said '60% chance of rain', and it did not rain. What does that tell you? → **Dry days happen on about 4 of 10 days like this, so it is not a surprise**
### a4 · Watch the Clouds Travel: a satellite film (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up chooses the website and stays with the child online.
- **record:** Draw a rough map of India and the seas. Draw today's big clouds and an arrow for which way they are moving.

### The Limits of Forecasting (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)

   **Answer:** Watch for the sign many times and count how often the weather it promises really comes. *Why:* One time can be luck. Counting the times a saying comes true and the times it fails, over many tries, shows how good it is.
4. (mcq) In a forecast contest, 'tomorrow will be like today' got most days right. On which kind of day did it go wrong?  
   Options: On days when the weather stayed the same / On days when the weather changed / On Sundays / On sunny days  
   **Answer:** On days when the weather changed. *Why:* 'Same as today' always predicts no change. So it is right when the weather stays the same and wrong every time it changes. Catching those changes is what makes a forecast useful.
5. (mcq) Farhan, a boy in Chennai, sees a big swirl of cloud on a satellite picture over the Bay of Bengal, moving towards the coast. What is the best forecast for Chennai?  
   Options: Rain may come soon, because the cloud is moving towards the city / No rain, because the sky over Chennai is blue now / Snow / The cloud will stay where it is  
   **Answer:** Rain may come soon, because the cloud is moving towards the city. *Why:* Weather travels. A storm moving towards the coast today may reach the city tomorrow, even if the sky over the city is still blue.
6. (mcq) Benjamin Franklin's storm had a wind from the northeast. Which way did the storm itself travel?  
   Options: Towards the northeast, against its own wind / Towards the southwest, with its wind / It did not move / Straight up  
   **Answer:** Towards the northeast, against its own wind. *Why:* Franklin found the storm reached Boston, to the northeast, hours after Philadelphia. A storm's winds swirl around it, while the whole storm travels another way.
7. (mcq) The forecast says '70% chance of rain'. What does it mean?  
   Options: It will rain for 70 minutes / 70% of the city will get wet / On 10 days like this, rain would fall on about 7 / It will certainly rain  
   **Answer:** On 10 days like this, rain would fall on about 7. *Why:* A 70% chance means rain on about 7 of 10 days like this one. It does not mean minutes or how much of the city gets wet.
8. (mcq) The forecast said '20% chance of rain', and it rained. Was the forecast wrong?  
   Options: Yes, it was completely wrong / Not necessarily: rain happens on about 2 of 10 days like this / Yes, because 20% means no rain / Forecasts are always wrong  
   **Answer:** Not necessarily: rain happens on about 2 of 10 days like this. *Why:* 20% means rain is unlikely but possible. Rain on 2 of every 10 such days is what 20% means. You would need to check many days to judge the forecaster.
9. (mcq) Why is a forecast for next week less sure than a forecast for tomorrow?  
   Options: Weather offices try less hard for next week / Small errors in today's measurements grow bigger over time / Computers switch off after one day / Next week's weather is decided by the Moon  
   **Answer:** Small errors in today's measurements grow bigger over time. *Why:* Nobody can measure every bit of air. The small errors grow day by day, as Edward Lorenz found with his rounded number. So forecasts get less sure the further ahead they look.
10. (mcq) What does a red weather warning from the India Meteorological Department mean?  
   Options: Be aware / Be prepared / Take action / No warning  
   **Answer:** Take action. *Why:* Green means no warning, yellow means be aware, orange means be prepared, and red means take action, for example moving to safety.
11. (mcq) Lakshmi, a girl in Kochi, leaves salt on a plate in a steamy bathroom for an hour. The salt goes lumpy. What does this show?  
   Options: Salt takes in water vapour from damp air / Salt makes rain / The bathroom is cold / Salt is melting  
   **Answer:** Salt takes in water vapour from damp air. *Why:* Salt takes in water vapour from humid air and goes lumpy. Lumpy salt shows the air is damp, which makes rain possible, though not certain.
12. (mcq) Why do weather offices need readings from far away to forecast your city's weather?  
   Options: Because the weather coming to your city is often somewhere else today / Because far-away readings are more exciting / Because your city has no weather / Because computers only work with far-away numbers  
   **Answer:** Because the weather coming to your city is often somewhere else today. *Why:* Weather travels across land and sea. To know what is coming, forecasters need to know what the air is doing in the places it is coming from.

## Open questions
- **How far ahead could the weather ever be forecast, even with perfect computers?** Most scientists say about two weeks for day-by-day detail, but the exact limit is still argued about, and it may be different for different kinds of weather.
- **Can animals really sense rain coming a day or more ahead?** Many people have seen animals behave differently before rain, but careful tests that count the misses as well as the hits are rare.
- **Why are some monsoon seasons much wetter or drier than others?** The sea temperatures of the Pacific and Indian Oceans play a part, but scientists cannot yet fully explain or forecast every monsoon.
- **Exactly where will a thunderstorm form this afternoon?** Forecasts can say storms are likely in an area, but the exact street where one will build up is still very hard to forecast.
- **Could a tiny change, like a butterfly's wings, really change where a storm goes weeks later?** Lorenz's work shows tiny differences can grow, but no one can test it for one real butterfly. Scientists still argue about how far this idea goes in the real atmosphere.

## Connected maths
- In the forecast contest, the app scored 12 points out of 14, and 'Same as today' scored 10. How many more points did the app score? **2 more points.**. The app scored 12 and 'Same as today' scored 10. 12 take away 10 is 2. So the app scored 2 more points. That means the app caught one more change in the weather, worth 2 points, or two changes worth 1 point each. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Draw a bar chart of these contest scores: Same as today 10, Me 11, The app 12. Which bar is tallest? How much taller is it than the shortest? **The app's bar is tallest. It is 2 points taller than 'Same as today'.**. Draw three bars, 10, 11 and 12 high. The app's is tallest at 12. The shortest is 'Same as today' at 10. 12 minus 10 is 2. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Priya, a girl in Thiruvananthapuram, put 6 rajma and 4 chana in a tumbler, then picked one bean without looking. Is she more likely to pick rajma or chana? What if she put in 2 rajma and 8 chana? **With 6 rajma and 4 chana, rajma is more likely. With 2 rajma and 8 chana, chana is more likely.**. With 6 rajma out of 10, rajma is more likely than chana. It is like a 60% chance of rain. With 2 rajma out of 10, chana is much more likely. That is like a 20% chance of rain: rain is unlikely, but it can still happen. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L3`)
- In one week of Kavya's contest it rained on 3 of the 7 days. What fraction of the days had rain? Did more than half the days have rain? **3 sevenths of the days had rain. That is less than half.**. 3 rainy days out of 7 days is 3 out of 7, written 3/7. Half of 7 is three and a half, and 3 is less than that. So fewer than half the days had rain. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- A thunderstorm is 30 km away and moving towards your town at 10 km each hour. How long until it arrives, if it keeps going the same way? **3 hours.**. Each hour it moves 10 km closer. After 1 hour it is 20 km away, after 2 hours 10 km, after 3 hours 0 km. So it arrives in 3 hours. 30 divided by 10 is 3. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
