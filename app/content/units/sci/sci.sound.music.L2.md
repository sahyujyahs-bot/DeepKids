# Home-made instruments: high and low sounds, and keeping a steady beat
`sci.sound.music.L2`

**Summary.** Tap a big carton and it booms. Tap a matchbox and it ticks. Both are made of card, so why do they sound so different? When you tap a box, its sides vibrate: they move in and out very fast. A big side vibrates more slowly, and slow vibrations make low sounds. In this unit you make instruments from boxes, bottles and rubber bands, and you test which ones sound high and which sound low. You also learn to keep a beat, a steady pulse like a clock ticking, and to clap a rhythm on top of it. When everyone follows one beat, all the different sounds fit together into music.

**Intro.** *Find an empty matchbox and a big empty carton. Tap the middle of each one with one finger. Listen. Both are made of card. So why does one tick and the other boom?* In this unit you find out. You make a drum from a box, a hooter from a bottle and a one-string instrument from a rubber band. You test big and small boxes to see which sounds high and which sounds low. Then you learn to keep a steady beat, like drummers all over India do, so that a whole family band can play together.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can make an instrument from a box, a bottle or a rubber band, and show which part vibrates to make the sound.
1. I can sort sounds into high and low, and say that fast vibrations make high sounds and slow vibrations make low ones.
2. I can test boxes of different sizes fairly, and say that a bigger box of the same material makes a lower sound.
3. I can keep a steady beat with claps or pats, and clap a rhythm, like the rhythm of my name, on top of it.
4. I can count one cycle of Adi talam, 8 beats, with claps, finger counts and waves.
5. I can name other animals that move in time to a beat, and say what scientists still argue about.

## Terms
- **Vibrate**: To vibrate means to move to and fro very fast, again and again. Every sound starts with something vibrating. *e.g.* Pluck a stretched rubber band. It goes blurry because it is vibrating.
- **Musical instrument**: A musical instrument is something made to vibrate on purpose, so that it makes sounds for music. *e.g.* A veena, a tabla, a flute, and a box drum you make yourself are all instruments.
- **Pitch**: Pitch is how high or low a sound is. Fast vibrations make a high pitch. Slow vibrations make a low pitch. *e.g.* A mouse's squeak has a high pitch. A big drum's boom has a low pitch.
- **High sound**: A high sound is thin and sharp, like a squeak or a whistle. It comes from fast vibrations. *e.g.* A small steel bowl tapped with a spoon gives a high 'ting'.
- **Low sound**: A low sound is deep, like a boom or a rumble. It comes from slow vibrations. *e.g.* A big delivery carton slapped with a palm gives a low 'dhum'.
- **Beat**: A beat is a steady pulse that repeats, with the same gap each time, like a clock ticking. *e.g.* When you march round the room, your steps make a beat: left, right, left, right.
- **Rhythm**: A rhythm is a pattern of sounds and gaps, some short and some long, that fits over the beat. *e.g.* Clap your name. 'Mee-nak-shi' is a rhythm of three claps.
- **Tempo**: Tempo is how fast the beat goes. A fast tempo has many beats in a short time. A slow tempo has few. *e.g.* A lullaby has a slow tempo. A dance song has a fast tempo.
- **Tala**: In Indian classical music, a tala is a cycle of beats that repeats again and again. Musicians count it with claps, finger counts and waves of the hand. *e.g.* Adi talam, used in many Carnatic songs, has 8 beats in each cycle.
- **Pattern**: A pattern is something that repeats in the same order. A rhythm pattern repeats its sounds in the same order. *e.g.* Clap, clap, pat. Clap, clap, pat. Clap, clap, pat.

## Explanations
### What makes an instrument sound · *defined*
**Definition:** A musical instrument makes sound when one part of it vibrates, moving to and fro very fast.

Every sound starts with something moving. In an instrument, the moving is very fast and very small. Pluck a rubber band, and the band vibrates. Tap a box, and its sides vibrate in and out. Blow across a bottle, and the air inside it vibrates. The vibrating part pushes the air around it, and the air carries the sound to your ears.
- e.g. A rubber band stretched over a tissue box: the band vibrates.
- e.g. A box drum: the side of the box you tap vibrates.
- e.g. A bottle hooter: the air inside the bottle vibrates.
- e.g. Your voice: two small folds inside your throat vibrate. Put your fingers on your throat and hum to feel them.
### High and low sounds · *defined*
**Definition:** Pitch is how high or low a sound is. Fast vibrations make high sounds. Slow vibrations make low sounds.

A high sound, like a squeak, comes from something vibrating very fast. A low sound, like a boom, comes from something vibrating more slowly. Both are still much too fast to count with your eyes. You can feel the difference, though. Hum a low note with your hand on your chest, and you feel a strong buzz. Hum a high note, and the buzz in your chest is much weaker.
- e.g. A mouse squeaks high. A cow moos low.
- e.g. A pressure cooker whistle is high. Thunder is low.
- e.g. A small bell in a temple rings high. The big temple bell rings low.
### Why a big box sounds lower · *defined*
**Definition:** When things are made of the same material and tapped the same way, the bigger one usually makes a lower sound.

When you tap a box, its side bends in and springs back, again and again. A small side is short and stiff, so it springs back very fast. That makes a high 'tik'. A big side is wide and floppy, so it moves in and out more slowly. That makes a low 'dhum'. The air inside the box vibrates too, and a big box holds more air, which also makes the sound lower. That is why big drums boom and small drums tick.
- e.g. A big delivery carton booms. A matchbox ticks.
- e.g. A tabla has two drums. The big one, called the bayan, gives the low sounds. The small one, the dayan, gives the high ones.
- e.g. In a set of steel bowls, the biggest bowl rings lowest and the smallest rings highest.
### Low is not the same as loud · *defined*
**Definition:** Loud or soft depends on how big the vibrations are. High or low depends on how fast they are.

Many children think a low sound is a loud sound, because big drums are often loud. But you can slap the big carton hard or tap it very gently. A gentle tap is soft, but it is still low. A hard tap on a matchbox is louder, but it is still high. How hard you hit changes how big the vibrations are. The size of the box changes how fast they are.
- e.g. Tap the big carton with one finger, very gently. You hear a soft, low sound.
- e.g. A whistle can be loud and high at the same time.
- e.g. A cat purring is soft and low.
### How a bottle hoots · *defined*
**Definition:** When you blow across the mouth of a bottle, the air inside the bottle vibrates. Less air makes a higher note.

A bottle that looks empty is full of air. When your breath crosses the top, it makes the air inside bounce up and down very fast. That is the hoot. Pour some water in, and there is less air left to vibrate. Less air vibrates faster, so the hoot goes higher. A big empty bottle has lots of air, so it hoots low.
- e.g. An empty bottle gives a low hoot.
- e.g. The same bottle, half full of water, gives a higher hoot.
- e.g. A bamboo flute works the same way: the air inside the tube vibrates.
### Beat and rhythm · *defined*
**Definition:** A beat is a steady pulse that repeats with the same gap each time. A rhythm is a pattern of sounds and gaps that fits over the beat.

The beat is like the ticking of a clock. It never speeds up or slows down in the middle. The rhythm is what you play on top of it. When you clap 'Mee-nak-shi' and then 'Kavya', the claps change, but the beat underneath stays the same. Drummers, dancers and singers all listen to the same beat. That is how they stay together.
- e.g. Your feet marching make a beat. Clapping your name while you march makes a rhythm.
- e.g. A clock ticks a beat.
- e.g. In a song, the words make rhythms over the beat.
### Tempo: how fast the beat goes · *defined*
**Definition:** Tempo is how fast the beat goes: how many beats there are in a fixed time.

You counted the beats of two songs for 15 seconds each. The lullaby usually gives far fewer pats than the dance song. A slow tempo calms us down, like rocking a baby. A fast tempo makes us want to jump and dance. Scientists who compared songs from many countries found that lullabies everywhere tend to be slow and soft.
- e.g. A lullaby: slow tempo.
- e.g. A fast film dance song: fast tempo.
- e.g. Rowers in a Kerala snake boat race pull their oars in time with the boat song.
### Tala: counting beats in a cycle · *defined*
**Definition:** In Indian classical music, a tala is a cycle of beats that repeats again and again.

Carnatic musicians, in South India, keep the tala with their hands. For Adi talam they clap, then count three fingers, then clap and wave, then clap and wave. That makes 8 beats. Then they start again. Hindustani musicians, in North India, use talas too. Teentaal has 16 beats. Drummers also speak their rhythms aloud with syllables like 'ta ka di mi'. This spoken drumming is called konnakol in South India.
- e.g. Adi talam: 8 beats in each cycle.
- e.g. Teentaal: 16 beats in each cycle.
- e.g. 'Ta ka di mi' fits four syllables into four beats. 'Ta ki ta' makes a group of three.
### Do other animals feel a beat? · *debated*
Humans and many other animals make sounds in rhythms. Frogs call in turns, and crickets chirp steadily. Moving in time to the beat of music is rarer. In 2009, scientists filmed a cockatoo called Snowball. He bobbed his head and lifted his feet in time with a song. When they played the song faster or slower, Snowball often changed his speed to match. Later films showed he made up many different dance moves of his own. Scientists still argue about which animals can do this, and why.
- e.g. Snowball, a cockatoo, dancing to a pop song.
- e.g. Ronan, a sea lion, bobbing her head to the beat.
- e.g. Frogs at night calling in turns, so each one can be heard.
- **Aniruddh Patel and his team:** They studied Snowball. They think animals that learn new sounds by copying, like parrots and humans, are the ones who can feel a beat and move to it.
- **Peter Cook and his team:** They trained Ronan, a sea lion. Sea lions are not known to copy sounds, yet she learned to bob her head to the beat. So they think many more animals may be able to feel a beat.
- **Other scientists:** They point out that a learned trick is not the same as enjoying music. Whether animals feel music the way we do is still an open question.

## Mostly agreed
- Every sound starts with something vibrating: a string, a drum skin, the side of a box, or air. *How we know:* You can see a plucked rubber band go blurry, and feel a box or your own throat buzz when it makes a sound.
- Faster vibrations make higher sounds, and slower vibrations make lower sounds. *How we know:* Scientists measure how many times a second things vibrate. High notes always have more vibrations each second than low notes.
- Of two things with the same material and shape, the bigger one vibrates more slowly and makes the lower sound. *How we know:* Big drums, big bells, big bowls and long strings all sound lower than small ones of the same kind. You tested this with boxes and steel bowls.
- Almost all the music people make, all over the world, has a steady beat. *How we know:* Scientists who compared hundreds of songs from many countries found a steady beat in most of them.

## How we found out
**Nicomachus, a Greek writer who told the story about Pythagoras, and Vincenzo Galilei, an Italian lute player and music writer who tested it, About 100 CE (the story), and about 1589 (the test).** About 1,900 years ago, a Greek writer called Nicomachus told a story about Pythagoras. Pythagoras was walking past a blacksmith's shop and heard the hammers ringing. Some of them sounded sweet together. Nicomachus said Pythagoras weighed the hammers and found simple numbers: 12, 9, 8 and 6. So, said the story, the weight of a hammer sets its note, in neat steps. For more than 1,000 years, people copied this story into their books. Then, around 1589 in Italy, a lute player and music writer called Vincenzo Galilei decided to test it. He hung weights on strings, the way the story said. The numbers did not work. To make a string's note jump up by one big step, called an octave, doubling the weight was not enough. He needed four times the weight. Hammers do not follow the story's numbers either, because a hammer's note depends on its size and shape. Vincenzo had shown that a famous old story was wrong, simply by trying it. His son, Galileo Galilei, grew up to test many old ideas in the same way.  
*What people believed before:* That the weight of a hammer, or of a weight hung on a string, sets its note in simple steps. When Vincenzo Galilei tried it, he needed four times the weight, not twice, to raise a string's note by an octave.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A low sound is a loud sound, and a high sound is a soft sound.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Big drums are often both loud and low, and small bells are often soft and high.  
  Against: Tap a big carton very gently. It is soft, but still low. A whistle is loud, but high. Loudness comes from how big the vibrations are. Pitch comes from how fast they are.
- **Only humans can move in time to a beat.** (Many people believed this until about 2009; *rejected*)  
  For: Most animals do not dance to music, even when they hear it every day.  
  Against: Snowball the cockatoo and Ronan the sea lion both moved in time to the beat, and changed speed when the music changed. Scientists still argue about how many other animals can do it.
- **Playing music to plants makes them grow better.** (Many gardeners, and some small studies; *debated*)  
  For: Plants do sense vibrations. Thale cress plants that were played the vibrations of caterpillars chewing made more of their bitter defence chemicals later, when caterpillars really ate them.  
  Against: Careful tests of music and plant growth have given mixed results. Plants clearly sense and respond to vibrations, but there is no clear proof that songs help them grow.
- **Pythagoras found the musical notes by weighing a blacksmith's hammers.** (An old story told by Nicomachus, a Greek writer, about 1,900 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: The story was copied in books for more than 1,000 years, and it does have some truth: bigger things often sound lower.  
  Against: Around 1589, Vincenzo Galilei tested the numbers in the story with weights on strings, and they did not work. A hammer's sound depends on its size and shape, not on its weight alone.

## Roots
We have tapped big and small boxes, hooted bottles and kept a beat together. Now we ask what the Big Box test tells us about sound, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the Big Box test, every box was card, tapped the same way. The big carton boomed low and the matchbox ticked high. The steel bowls did the same, from biggest to smallest. So one material can make a low note or a high note, and its size decides which.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think each material has its own sound: card goes 'tok' and steel goes 'ting'. But the same card made a deep boom and a high tick. The size and shape of a thing change its note as well as what it is made of.  
*Evidence:* Every box was cardboard, and every tap was one finger on the middle of the top. Yet the big carton went 'dhum', the shoebox 'tok' and the matchbox 'tik'. Three steel bowls from one set rang lowest for the biggest and highest for the smallest.

- *who am i:* Your voice comes from two small folds in your throat that vibrate. Grown-up men usually have longer folds, so their voices are usually lower.
- *what is life:* Many living beings keep time with each other. Frogs call in turns at night, and a cockatoo called Snowball danced to the beat of songs.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of beats that come round again and again: day and night, your breath and your heartbeat.

A tap is one quick push. A box answers it with a note of its own, set by its size and shape. The same tap gave a big carton, a small box and a steel bowl three different notes. When many players follow one beat, all those different notes join into one song.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2 ('The Adjustment of Controversies', also called 'All Things Are Equal'):** The Chinese thinker Zhuangzi describes the wind blowing through a great forest. The hollows in the trees are of every size and shape. When the wind blows, each hollow makes its own sound: some roar, some whistle, some hum. (Your bottle hooted when you blew across it, and hooted higher with less air inside. One breath, like one wind, made a different note in each different hollow.)
- **Natya Shastra, chapter 28 in most editions:** The Natya Shastra, an old Indian book on drama, music and dance, sorts instruments into four kinds: stringed ones (tata), drums covered with skin (avanaddha), solid ones that are struck (ghana), and hollow ones that are blown (sushira). (Your ektara is a tata instrument, and your bottle hooter is a sushira. Your box drum is struck, like a ghana instrument or a drum. In each kind, a different part vibrates: a string, a skin, a solid body or the air.)
- Science (settled): Every sound comes from something vibrating. For things of the same material and shape, bigger ones vibrate more slowly and make lower notes.
- Science (debated): Some other animals, such as Snowball the cockatoo and Ronan the sea lion, can move in time to a beat. Scientists still argue about which animals can do it, and why.
- Other view, Many physicists: Many physicists would say a box does not answer a tap at all. They say it simply vibrates in the way its size and shape allow, as a ball rolls down a slope, with no sensing involved.
- Practice, Find the Beat in the Quiet (3 min): Sit very still and close your eyes. Listen for one whole minute. Is anything making a steady beat? It could be a clock, a dripping tap, footsteps, or a fan. When you find a beat, pat it gently on your knee. Then put your hand flat on your chest. Can you feel your heart's own beat?
- Ask: Before you tapped it, did the big carton already have its low note? Where was the note while the box was silent?
- Ask: When a whole room claps together, who decides the beat?
- Ask: Is a beat in the drum, or in the people who hear it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Why does a big box make a lower sound than a small box made of the same card? (hint: Think about how fast each side can move in and out when you tap it.)
- *deep:* When you blew across the bottle, nothing seemed to move. So what was vibrating? (hint: Was the bottle really empty? What was inside it?)
- *deep:* A clock goes tick, tick, tick, every tick the same. Why do so many people hear 'tick-tock'? (hint: Our minds like to put beats into groups of two or three, even when every beat is the same.)
- *big:* Is a sound high or low out in the air, or only in your ears and mind? (hint: The box vibrates fast or slowly. Who decides that fast means 'high'?)
- *big:* Why do people everywhere make music with a beat, and want to move when they hear one? (hint: Think of rowers in a boat race, people marching, and a baby being rocked to sleep.)

## Story: Kavya's Carton Drum
*A made-up story. It is true that the ghatam is a clay-pot drum played in Carnatic concerts, that many ghatams are made in Manamadurai in Tamil Nadu, that Adi talam has 8 beats counted with a clap, three finger counts, and two claps with waves, and that Sukanya Ramgopal of Bengaluru is a famous ghatam player who began when very few women played it.*

Kavya lived on the fourth floor of a tall building in Chennai, with Amma, Appa, her little brother Arjun and their Paati. Kavya was seven. Arjun was four. Paati was their grandmother. She had sung Carnatic songs all her life.

It was the morning of Pongal. The kitchen smelled of jaggery, ghee and cardamom, because Paati was making sweet pongal. Her two sisters were coming for lunch. Paati wanted the whole family to sing her favourite Pongal song together.

"But we have no mridangam," said Appa. "A song needs a drum."

Paati wiped her hands and looked at the pile of empty boxes by the door. There was the huge carton that the new fan had come in. There was a shoebox, a tissue box, and a little toothpaste box. "There are drums all around us," she said.

"Those are just boxes," said Arjun.

"A ghatam is just a clay pot," said Paati. "Musicians play it in big concerts, with their fingers and palms. Many of the best ghatams are made in a town called Manamadurai, near Madurai. One of the most famous ghatam players is a woman from Bengaluru called Sukanya Ramgopal. When she began, hardly any women played the ghatam. Now she has played in many countries."

Arjun grabbed the toothpaste box and tapped it with one finger. Tik, tik, tik.

Kavya giggled. "That's a mouse drum." She knelt by the big carton and slapped its side with her flat palm. DHUM. The deep sound filled the whole room. Arjun's eyes went wide.

Kavya tapped the shoebox. Tok. She tapped the toothpaste box again. Tik. Then the carton. Dhum. She frowned.

"They are all made of card," she said slowly. "But the big one sounds deep, and the little one sounds high. The shoebox is in the middle."

"Good listening," said Paati. "When you tap a box, its side moves in and out very fast. We say it vibrates. A big side is floppy, so it vibrates more slowly. Slow vibrations make a low sound. A small side is stiff, so it vibrates fast and makes a high sound."

Kavya made a band. The big carton was her drum. Arjun filled a plastic bottle with a handful of rice and screwed the cap on tight. That was his shaker. Paati stretched a long rubber band round the tissue box, over the hole, and gave it to Appa. "Pluck it," she said. Amma found an empty glass bottle and blew across the top. It gave a low hoot, like an owl.

"Ready?" said Kavya. "Go!"

Everyone played at once. Kavya banged fast. Arjun shook slowly, then very fast, then stopped to look at the rice. Appa plucked whenever he remembered. Amma hooted and then laughed so much she could not hoot at all. It sounded like a lorry full of pots going over a bump.

Paati held up her hand, and everyone stopped. "You each have a lovely sound," she said. "But you are all going at your own speed. Music needs a beat first. A beat is a steady pulse, like a clock ticking. Everybody listens to the same beat."

She began to clap and count, slowly and evenly. Clap. Little finger. Ring finger. Middle finger. Clap, and turn the hand over to wave. Clap, and wave. "That is Adi talam," she said. "Eight beats. Then we start again, round and round."

Kavya copied her. After two rounds she had it. Appa kept getting lost at the waves. "Appa, clap, and then just turn your hand over," said Kavya, and showed him with her own hand. "Clap, wave. Clap, wave." On the fourth round, Appa got it, and he looked very pleased with himself.

"Now say it like a drummer," said Paati. "Ta ka di mi, ta ka di mi." Kavya said it with her and hit the carton on every 'ta'. Dhum, ka, di, mi. Dhum, ka, di, mi. Arjun shook his bottle once on every beat. Appa plucked his rubber band on the claps. Amma hooted at the end of every round.

This time, the sounds did not crash into each other. They fitted together, like the pieces of a kolam.

When the doorbell rang, the band was ready. The great-aunts sat down, Paati began the Pongal song, and the family band played under it. The carton boomed, the bottle hissed, the rubber band twanged and the bottle hooted. One great-aunt clapped the tala along with them and nodded at Kavya.

Afterwards, Kavya sat with the carton on her lap and tapped it softly. Dhum. Still low, even when it was quiet.

"Paati," she said, "the big box always sings low and the little box always sings high. But it was the beat that made us a band."

**For the parent:** Big boxes sound low and small boxes sound high, because big sides vibrate more slowly. But different sounds only become music together when everyone follows one steady beat.

- Ask: Why did the big carton sound deeper than the toothpaste box?
- Ask: What went wrong the first time everyone played at once?
- Ask: How did Paati's clapping help the family band?
- Ask: Which instrument would you play in Kavya's band, and why?

## Experiments
### e1 · Big Box, Small Box: which makes the deepest sound? (28 min)
- **question:** If boxes are all made of card and tapped the same way, does the size of a box change how high or low it sounds?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which box will make the deepest sound: the smallest, the biggest, or will they all sound the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every box is empty, with its lid taped shut, and lies on the floor on its biggest side. Every tap is the same: one finger, dropped onto the middle of the top. Only the size of the box changes. We tap each box twice, because one tap can sound different by mistake.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw the four boxes on a ladder: the deepest at the bottom, the highest at the top. Write how many boxes you named right in the eyes-shut round.
- **results table:** Box, from smallest to biggest | My guess: high, middle or low? | The sound word I heard (tik, tok, dum or dhum) | What I heard: high, middle or low?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the four boxes in a row, from smallest to biggest. 2. Draw the results table with four rows. 3. Ask your child to guess for each box.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to tap each box in exactly the same way. Which one will make the deepest sound? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the four boxes. Which will sound deepest? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The big one, because big drums are deep.; The small one, because it is hard.; All the same, because they are all card.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine if it has a reason. Write each guess in the table. Do not explain anything yet.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape each lid shut. 2. Lay each box on the floor on its biggest side. 3. Show the tap: one finger, dropped onto the middle of the top.  
  *Parent says:* Every box must get the same tap, in the same place. Why do you think that matters?  
  *Child mode:* Tape the lids shut. Lay each box flat. Tap the middle with one finger.
  - Ask: Why must every tap be the same? (good: So only the box is different.; So it is fair.)
  - Note: A tile or stone floor works well. On a soft mattress the boxes hardly sound at all.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child taps each box twice, from smallest to biggest. 2. After each box, they say a sound word for it: tik, tok, dum or dhum. 3. Write the word and 'high', 'middle' or 'low' in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Tap the smallest box. Listen. Is it high or low? Now the next one. Say a sound word for each.  
  *Child mode:* Tap each box twice. Listen hard. Is it high or low?
  - Ask: What did you hear as the boxes got bigger? (good: The sound got deeper.; The little one went tik and the big one went dhum.)
  - Note: The matchbox gives a high, short 'tik'. The big carton gives a deep 'dhum', like a big drum. If two boxes sound almost the same, they are probably close in size. Use boxes that are very different.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rests the fingertips of one hand lightly on the top of the big carton. 2. With the other hand, they slap the side of the carton once with a flat palm. 3. Do the same with the smallest box.  
  *Parent says:* Put your fingers lightly on top of the big carton. Now slap its side. What do your fingers feel?  
  *Child mode:* Fingers on top of the big box. Slap its side. What do you feel?
  - Ask: What did your fingers feel? (good: A buzz.; The box shook a little.; It tickled.)
  - Note: The carton's top buzzes under the fingers. That buzz is the card vibrating. The small box buzzes too, but too fast and too little to feel well.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child shuts their eyes and turns their back. 2. Tap one box, chosen at random, twice. 3. Your child names the box. 4. Do this 4 times, and count the right answers.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes shut. I will tap one box. Can you tell which one, just from the sound?  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Listen. Which box was it?
  - Note: Most children of 7 get 3 or 4 right, because big and small boxes sound so different. The two middle boxes are the easiest to mix up.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the three steel bowls on a folded towel. 2. Your child taps the rim of each gently with the spoon. 3. Ask which rings lowest and which highest.  
  *Parent says:* These bowls are all the same steel, from one set. Only the size is different. Which rings lowest?  
  *Child mode:* Tap each bowl with the spoon. Which rings lowest? Which rings highest?
  - Note: The biggest bowl rings lowest and the smallest rings highest. This shows the size rule works for steel too, not only card. Tap gently, so the spoon does not dent or chip anything.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they found, and how they know. 2. Only then, explain why bigger boxes sound lower.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find out? What did you hear that tells you so? Why do you think the big box sounded deeper?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? Which sounded deepest? Why, do you think?
  - Ask: What did you find, and how do you know? (good: The bigger the box, the deeper the sound. The carton went dhum and the matchbox went tik.; The biggest bowl rang lowest too.)
  - Note: After your child has said what they think, explain: when you tap a box, its side bends in and springs back again and again. That is vibrating. A big side is floppy, so it vibrates more slowly. Slow vibrations make a low sound.
  - Check: Farhan, a boy in Class 2, has a big carton and a tiny box, both made of card. He taps each the same way. Which sounds lower? → **The big carton**
### e2 · Sleepy Song, Dancing Song: how fast is the beat? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a lullaby have a slower beat than a dance song? How many beats does each have in 15 seconds?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which song will give you more pats in 15 seconds, the lullaby or the dance song? About how many for each? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We count for the same time, 15 seconds, for both songs. The same person pats, one pat on each beat. We count each song twice, because one count can go wrong.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw one bar for each song, as tall as its number of pats.
- **results table:** Song | My guess: how many pats? | Pats in 15 seconds, try 1 | Pats in 15 seconds, try 2
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose the lullaby and the dance song. 2. Draw the results table. 3. Ask your child to guess the number of pats for each.  
  *Parent says:* We will pat along to a lullaby and a dance song for 15 seconds each. Which will give more pats? How many? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which song has a faster beat? Guess how many pats for each. Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The dance song, because dancing is fast.; The same, because a beat is a beat.)
  - Note: Fifteen seconds is about as long as it takes to count slowly to fifteen. Write both guesses in the table.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the lullaby. 2. Pat both knees together on every beat, with your child. 3. Then play the dance song and do the same.  
  *Parent says:* Let's find the beat. It is the steady pulse you could rock a baby to, or march to. Pat it with me.  
  *Child mode:* Listen for the steady beat. Pat your knees on every beat.
  - Note: Some children pat on every word instead of every beat. Pat with them until they settle into a steady pulse.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the lullaby. 2. When the seconds hand reaches 12, say 'Go'. 3. Make one tally mark for each pat. 4. Say 'Stop' after 15 seconds. 5. Do the same for the dance song.  
  *Parent says:* Pat on every beat. I will make a mark each time you pat. Ready? Go!  
  *Child mode:* Pat on every beat until the grown-up says stop.
  - Note: A lullaby usually gives about 15 to 20 pats in 15 seconds. A fast dance song often gives 25 to 35. Your numbers will depend on your songs.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count each song a second time, the same way. 2. Add the tallies up and write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count each song once more. Do we get nearly the same number again?  
  *Child mode:* Count each song again. Is it nearly the same?
  - Note: The two counts for one song should be close. If one is about half the other, your child patted on every other beat that time. That is fine: count again.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what your child found, and how they know. 2. Then tell them the word for how fast a beat goes: tempo.  
  *Parent says:* Which song had more pats in 15 seconds? How many more? Why do you think a lullaby is slow?  
  *Child mode:* Which song had more pats? Why is a lullaby slow?
  - Ask: Why might a lullaby be slow? (good: To make the baby sleepy.; Slow rocking is calm.)
  - Note: How fast a beat goes is called its tempo. Scientists who compared songs from many countries found that lullabies everywhere tend to be slow and soft.
  - Check: Bidisha, a girl in Kolkata, counts 16 pats for a lullaby and 32 for a dance song, each in 15 seconds. Which song has the faster tempo? → **The dance song**

### a2 · Ta Ka Di Mi: speak like a drum, and count Adi talam (12 min)
- **record:** Draw the 8 hand shapes of Adi talam in a circle, numbered 1 to 8.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pat your knees slowly and evenly together. 2. Say one syllable on each pat: ta, ka, di, mi. 3. Repeat it again and again. 4. Then clap only on 'ta'.  
  *Parent says:* Drummers in South India say their rhythms out loud. Say it with me on every pat: ta, ka, di, mi.  
  *Child mode:* Pat your knees. Say ta, ka, di, mi. One sound on each pat.
  - Note: Speaking drum syllables like this is called konnakol. 'Ta ka di mi' is a group of four.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep patting. 2. Now say 'ta ki ta' over and over, one syllable on each pat. 3. Clap on every 'ta'.  
  *Parent says:* Now try a group of three: ta, ki, ta. Clap on the first 'ta' each time. Does the clap come at a new place?  
  *Child mode:* Now say ta, ki, ta. Clap on the first ta.
  - Note: In groups of four the clap comes every fourth pat. In groups of three it comes every third pat. The pats stay the same, but the pattern changes.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child Adi talam, slowly. 2. Clap. Touch your little finger to your thumb, then the ring finger, then the middle finger. 3. Clap, then turn your hand over to wave. 4. Clap, and wave again. 5. Do it together four times.  
  *Parent says:* This is Adi talam. Clap, one, two, three. Clap, wave. Clap, wave. That is 8 beats. Then start again.  
  *Child mode:* Clap. Little finger, ring finger, middle finger. Clap, wave. Clap, wave.
  - Ask: How many beats are in one round? (good: Eight.)
  - Note: The finger counts are done on the palm-up hand, touching each finger to the thumb, or tapping them on the other palm. Many children mix up the waves at first. Slow down and say the beat numbers aloud.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. You keep counting Adi talam. 2. Your child says 'ta ka di mi, ta ka di mi', one syllable on each beat.  
  *Parent says:* I will count the tala. You say ta ka di mi twice in each round. Do they fit?  
  *Child mode:* Say ta ka di mi twice. Does it fit one round?
  - Note: Eight syllables fit exactly into the 8 beats of Adi talam.
  - Check: Thenmozhi says 'ta ka di mi' two times while her Paati counts one round of Adi talam. How many syllables does she say? → **8**

### a4 · Bottle Hooter: make a bottle sing (10 min)
- **safety:** If you use a glass bottle, keep it on a towel on the floor, so it cannot fall and break.
- **record:** Draw the bottle with no water, a little water, and half full. Next to each, write 'low', 'middle' or 'high'.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up tries first. 2. Rest your lower lip on the edge of the bottle's mouth. 3. Blow gently, straight across the top, not into the bottle.

## Challenges
### c1 · A Band of Three: high, middle and low (20 min)
- **problem:** Can you make three instruments from boxes, bottles and rubber bands, so that one sounds high, one sounds in the middle and one sounds low?
- **your turn:** Make your own band of three. Ask someone to listen with their eyes shut and say which is high, middle and low. If two sound alike, change one: use a bigger box, or add or pour out water.

## Worksheets
### High, Low and the Beat (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) When you hoot a bottle, which part makes the sound?  
   Options: The air inside the bottle / The cap of the bottle / Your hand holding it  
   **Answer:** The air inside the bottle. *Why:* A strong answer says the air inside vibrates. The bottle only holds it.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a low sound is always a loud sound.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A gentle tap on a big carton is soft but still low. Low and high are about how fast things vibrate. Loud and soft are about how big the vibrations are.
3. (match) Draw a line from each instrument to the part that vibrates.  
   **Answer:** the tissue-box ektara → the rubber band; the box drum → the side of the box; the bottle hooter → the air inside the bottle; your voice → two small folds in your throat. *Why:* Every instrument has a part that vibrates. A strong answer can point to it on each one.
4. (draw) Remember the Big Box, Small Box test. Draw your four boxes on a ladder: the deepest sound at the bottom, the highest at the top. Write the sound word next to each.  
   **Answer:** The big carton at the bottom with 'dhum', then the shoebox, then the toothpaste box, and the matchbox at the top with 'tik'.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the boxes in order of size, biggest at the bottom, because bigger boxes sounded lower.
5. (write) Say it, and a grown-up writes it down: in the Big Box test, which box sounded deepest? What did you hear that shows it?  
   **Answer:** The big carton sounded deepest. It went 'dhum', and the matchbox went 'tik'.. *Why:* A strong answer names the box and gives evidence from the test: the sound words or the order heard.
6. (sort) Sort these sounds into high and low.  
   **Answer:** a mouse squeaking → High; the whistle of a pressure cooker → High; a big temple bell → Low; thunder → Low; a baby bird cheeping → High; a big carton drum → Low. *Why:* Squeaks, whistles and cheeps are thin and sharp, so they are high. Big bells, thunder and big drums are deep, so they are low.
7. (order) Three steel bowls come from one set: big, middle and small. Put them in order from the lowest ring to the highest.  
   **Answer:** ['the big bowl', 'the middle bowl', 'the small bowl']. *Why:* They are all the same steel, so only size changes the sound. The biggest bowl vibrates most slowly and rings lowest.
8. (fill) A beat is a ___ pulse that repeats, like a clock ticking.  
   **Answer:** steady. *Why:* A beat keeps the same gap every time. It does not speed up or slow down in the middle.
9. (count) Clap the name 'Ar-jun', and then the name 'Ka-ma-la'. How many claps is that altogether?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* Ar-jun is 2 claps and Ka-ma-la is 3 claps. 2 and 3 make 5.
10. (label) Label the tissue-box ektara: rubber band, box, hole, pencil.  
   **Answer:** ['rubber band: the part you pluck, which vibrates', 'box: it vibrates along with the band and makes it louder', 'hole: where you pluck, over the open box', 'pencil: it lifts the band off the box']. *Why:* A strong answer names each part and says what the band does: it vibrates.

Size changes both the sides and the amount of air inside.
2. (write) Your little cousin Arjun says, 'Small boxes are broken drums, because they don't go boom.' What would you tell him?  
   **Answer:** Small boxes are not broken. They vibrate faster, so they make a high sound instead of a low one. A band needs high sounds too.. *Why:* A strong answer explains that a small box's sides vibrate fast, which makes a high sound, and that high is just different from low.
3. (draw) Design one instrument that can play a high, a middle and a low sound. Draw it and label which part makes each sound.  
   **Answer:** For example, three boxes of different sizes taped in a row, labelled low for the biggest and high for the smallest. Or three bottles with different amounts of water.. *Why:* A strong design uses different sizes, or different amounts of air, and labels the biggest or emptiest one as low.
4. (mcq) A tabla has two drums, one small and one big. Which one gives the lower sounds?  
   Options: The big drum / The small drum / They both sound the same  
   **Answer:** The big drum. *Why:* The big drum, called the bayan, gives the deep, low sounds. The small drum, the dayan, gives the high ones. Bigger usually means lower.
5. (write) In the Sleepy Song, Dancing Song test, why did we count each song twice?  
   **Answer:** Because one count can go wrong, for example if you pat on every other beat. If two counts are close, we can trust them.. *Why:* A strong answer says that repeating a test checks for mistakes.
6. (order) Put these in order from the slowest beat to the fastest: a running person's footsteps, a sleeping person's breathing, a walking person's footsteps.  
   **Answer:** ["a sleeping person's breathing", "a walking person's footsteps", "a running person's footsteps"]. *Why:* Sleeping breaths come only every few seconds. Walking steps come about twice a second, and running steps come faster still.
7. (match) Match each change to what it does to the sound.  
   **Answer:** using a bigger box → the sound gets lower; adding water to the bottle → the hoot gets higher; tapping harder → the sound gets louder; patting faster → the tempo gets faster. *Why:* Size and the amount of air change high and low. How hard you hit changes loud and soft. How fast you play changes the tempo.
8. (truefalse) True or false: only humans can move in time to a beat.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Snowball the cockatoo and Ronan the sea lion both moved in time to music. Scientists still argue about how many other animals can.
9. (sort) Sort these changes: does each one make the sound higher or lower?  
   **Answer:** using a smaller box → Higher; adding water to a bottle → Higher; using a bigger steel bowl → Lower; using a bigger empty bottle → Lower. *Why:* Smaller things, and less air, vibrate faster and sound higher. Bigger things, and more air, vibrate more slowly and sound lower.
10. (odd) Which change does NOT make a sound higher or lower?  
   Options: Tapping harder / Using a smaller box / Adding water to the bottle / Using a bigger bowl  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* Tapping harder makes the sound louder, but not higher or lower. The other three change the size or the amount of air, so they change the pitch.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Meenakshi stretches a rubber band over a tissue box and plucks it. What vibrates to make the sound?  
   Options: The rubber band / The table under the box / Her ears / Nothing moves at all  
   **Answer:** The rubber band. *Why:* The band moves to and fro very fast. Look closely and it goes blurry. The box vibrates along with it, which makes the sound louder.
2. (mcq) Which of these makes a high sound?  
   Options: A mouse squeaking / Thunder rumbling / A cow mooing  
   **Answer:** A mouse squeaking. *Why:* A squeak is thin and sharp, so it is high. Thunder and a moo are deep, so they are low.
3. (mcq) Farhan taps a matchbox and a big delivery carton in exactly the same way. Which one sounds lower?  
   Options: The matchbox / The big carton / They sound the same  
   **Answer:** The big carton. *Why:* The carton's big sides vibrate more slowly, and slow vibrations make a low sound. Choosing 'the same' means thinking only about the material, not the size.
4. (mcq) Why does a big carton sound lower than a small box?  
   Options: Its big sides vibrate more slowly / The card in it is a different colour / It is always louder  
   **Answer:** Its big sides vibrate more slowly. *Why:* A big side is wide and floppy, so it moves in and out more slowly. Slow vibrations make low sounds. Loudness is a different thing.
5. (mcq) Lakshmi taps the big carton very, very gently with one finger. What does she hear?  
   Options: A soft, low sound / A loud, high sound / A soft, high sound  
   **Answer:** A soft, low sound. *Why:* A gentle tap makes small vibrations, so the sound is soft. The carton's sides still vibrate slowly, so it is still low. Low does not mean loud.
6. (mcq) Anjali hoots an empty bottle. Then she pours in water until the bottle is half full. What happens to the hoot?  
   Options: It goes higher / It goes lower / It stops for ever  
   **Answer:** It goes higher. *Why:* The hoot is the air inside vibrating. Water leaves less air, and less air vibrates faster, so the hoot goes higher.
7. (mcq) Which of these is a beat?  
   Options: A steady pulse with the same gap each time, like a clock ticking / The loudest sound in a song / A very high note  
   **Answer:** A steady pulse with the same gap each time, like a clock ticking. *Why:* A beat repeats steadily. Loudness and highness are about other things.
8. (mcq) Kavya claps her name: Ka-vya, 2 claps. How many claps is 'Mee-nak-shi'?  
   Options: 2 / 3 / 4  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Mee-nak-shi has three parts, so it is three claps. That pattern of claps is the name's rhythm.
9. (mcq) How many beats are in one round of Adi talam?  
   Options: 4 / 8 / 16  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Adi talam has 8 beats: a clap, three finger counts, a clap and a wave, and a clap and a wave. Teentaal, in Hindustani music, is the one with 16.
10. (mcq) Bidisha pats along to a lullaby and then to a dance song, for 15 seconds each. Which one usually gives more pats?  
   Options: The dance song / The lullaby / Every song gives the same number  
   **Answer:** The dance song. *Why:* A dance song usually has a faster tempo, so it has more beats in 15 seconds. Lullabies are slow, to help a baby calm down.
11. (mcq) Snowball is a cockatoo in America. What did scientists see Snowball do?  
   Options: Bob his head in time with music, and change speed when the music changed / Sing whole songs with words / Play a drum with sticks  
   **Answer:** Bob his head in time with music, and change speed when the music changed. *Why:* Snowball danced to the beat. When the song was played faster or slower, he often changed his speed to match.
12. (mcq) Arjun wants to find out if the size of a box changes its sound. Which test is fair?  
   Options: Tap every box the same way, in the same place / Tap the small box gently and the big box hard / Tap only the biggest box  
   **Answer:** Tap every box the same way, in the same place. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes, here the size of the box. If the taps are different, you cannot tell what made the sound change.

## Applications
- Drum makers make bigger drums for deeper sounds. The tabla's big bayan gives the low sounds and the small dayan gives the high ones.
- Temples hang big bells for deep sounds and small bells for high, bright sounds.
- Speakers that play deep bass sounds are bigger than the small speakers that play high sounds.
- Rowers in Kerala's snake boat races pull their oars in time to boat songs called vanchipattu, so the whole crew rows together.
- Music teachers use a metronome, a machine that ticks a steady beat, to help students keep time.
- People marching in a parade step on the same beat, so the whole group moves together.
- Doctors listen to the beat of the heart, and count how fast it is, to check that a body is well.
- Carnatic and Hindustani musicians keep the tala with their hands, so singers and drummers stay together.

## Spotted around us
- A big temple bell with a deep 'dong', and small hand bells with a high 'ting'.

- The dhakis playing the big dhak drum during Durga Puja in Bengal.
- The thavil and nadaswaram at a South Indian wedding: a deep drum and a high, loud pipe.

- Your family's steel bowls and dabbas: tap them and listen, from the biggest to the smallest.

## Open questions
- **Why do humans enjoy rhythm, and want to move to a beat?** Scientists have many ideas, for example that it helps groups work and stay together, or that it grew from the rocking of babies. Nobody knows for sure.
- **Which other animals can feel a beat and move to it?** Some parrots and one sea lion can. Scientists are still testing other animals, and argue about why some can and others seem not to.
- **Why do people hear identical clock ticks as 'tick-tock'?** Our minds group steady beats into twos or threes, even when every beat is the same. Scientists are still studying how the brain does this.
- **Why did music begin at all?** People have made music for a very long time: bone flutes found in Germany are more than 35,000 years old. But why people first made music is still argued about.

## Connected maths
- Thenmozhi's rhythm goes clap, clap, pat, clap, clap, pat, and keeps repeating. What is the 9th sound? **A pat.**. The pattern has 3 sounds: clap, clap, pat. So every 3rd sound is a pat: the 3rd, the 6th and the 9th. The 9th sound is a pat. (queues `math.patterns.repeating-patterns.L2`)
- One round of Adi talam has 8 beats. Paati counts 3 rounds. How many beats is that? **24 beats.**. Each round is 8 beats. Three rounds is 8, 16, 24. So 3 groups of 8 is 24 beats. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- In the Sleepy Song, Dancing Song test, Bidisha counted 16 pats for the lullaby and 32 for the dance song, each in 15 seconds. How many more pats did the dance song have? **16 more pats. The dance song had twice as many.**. 32 take away 16 is 16. And 16 and 16 make 32, so the dance song's beat was twice as fast. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In the eyes-shut round of the Big Box test, Kavya named 4 boxes right, Arjun named 2 and Appa named 3. Make a tally for each. Who named the most right? **Kavya named the most: 4.**. Kavya: IIII, 4. Arjun: II, 2. Appa: III, 3. 4 is the biggest number, so Kavya named the most right. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- Mee-nak-shi is 3 claps. Ka-vya is 2 claps. Far-han is 2 claps. If you clap all three names, one after the other, how many claps is that? **7 claps.**. 3 and 2 make 5. 5 and 2 make 7. So all three names make 7 claps. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
