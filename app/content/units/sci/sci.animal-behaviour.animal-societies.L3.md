# Animal societies: how elephants, ants and wolves live and decide together
`sci.animal-behaviour.animal-societies.L3`

**Summary.** Many animals live in groups that work together. A group like this is called a society. An elephant family is led by the oldest female, often a grandmother, because she remembers the most: where to find water in a dry year, and which dangers are real. An ant colony has a queen, but she does not give orders. Thousands of worker ants decide together, by leaving scent trails and following each other. A wolf pack is a family: a mother, a father and their young, who hunt together and raise the pups together. Humans are animals that live in societies too.

**Intro.** *Put one drop of sugar water near a line of ants and watch. One ant finds the drop. A few minutes later there are five, then twenty. Nobody can see an ant giving orders. So how does a crowd of ants agree where to go?* Ants are not the only animals who act together. Elephants walk in families led by a grandmother. Wolves hunt as a family. In this unit you test how ants choose between two foods, and you find out who leads an elephant family and a wolf pack, and why. You also find out about a famous mistake scientists made about wolves.

## Terms
- **Society**: A group of animals of one kind who live together and help each other, for a long time. Members of a society know each other. *e.g.* An elephant family, an ant colony and a wolf pack are all societies. So is a human village.
- **Herd**: A group of large animals, such as elephants, cows or deer, who live and move together. *e.g.* A herd of elephants walks to the river every evening in the dry season.
- **Matriarch**: The female who leads a family. In elephants, she is usually the oldest female, and often the grandmother of the calves. *e.g.* When a lion roars, the elephant family looks to the matriarch and gathers around her.
- **Calf**: A baby elephant. Cows, buffaloes, whales and giraffes also have calves. *e.g.* A newborn elephant calf walks under their mother's belly for shade.
- **Colony**: A large group of insects of one kind living together in one nest, such as ants, termites or honeybees. *e.g.* The ants in your kitchen probably all belong to one colony, with a nest inside a wall or under the floor.
- **Queen**: In an ant colony, the queen is the female who lays the eggs. She is usually the mother of all the other ants in the nest. She does not give orders. *e.g.* A queen ant may live for many years, laying eggs deep in the nest.
- **Worker**: A worker ant is a female ant who does the colony's jobs: looking after the young, cleaning, building, guarding and finding food. Worker ants do not lay the colony's eggs. *e.g.* The ants you see walking on the kitchen platform are workers, out looking for food.
- **Forage**: To search for food. An ant who goes out to look for food is a forager. *e.g.* Crows forage in the rubbish bin. Ants forage along the kitchen floor.
- **Pheromone**: A smell an animal makes to send a message to others of their kind. Ants use pheromones to mark a path to food and to warn of danger. *e.g.* An ant who finds sugar leaves a pheromone trail on her way home. Other ants smell it and follow it to the sugar.
- **Antennae**: The two feelers on an insect's head. Ants smell, taste and touch with their antennae. One feeler is called an antenna. *e.g.* When two ants meet on a path, they tap each other with their antennae.
- **Recruit**: To bring more members to a job. An ant who finds food recruits other ants to help carry it. *e.g.* One ant found the jaggery and recruited twenty more within ten minutes.
- **Pack**: A group of wolves or wild dogs who live and hunt together. A wolf pack is usually a family. *e.g.* An Indian wolf pack in the Deccan grasslands is usually a mother, a father and their young.
- **Pup**: A baby wolf or dog. *e.g.* In a wolf pack, the older brothers and sisters bring food to the pups.
- **Cooperate**: To work together towards something that all of you need. *e.g.* Wolves cooperate when they hunt, and ants cooperate when they carry a big crumb together.
- **Leader**: The one others follow. A leader may know the way, start a move or keep the group together. A leader does not always give orders. *e.g.* In an elephant family the matriarch leads, because the others follow where she goes.

## Explanations
### What an animal society is · *defined*
**Definition:** An animal society is a group of animals of one kind who live together for a long time, know each other, and help each other to survive and raise their young.

A crowd of pigeons at a grain shop is not a society. The birds just happen to be in the same place. A society is different: its members stay together, know each other and share the work. Elephants, ants, wolves, honeybees, crows, monkeys and humans all live in societies. Humans are animals, and a human family or village is a society too. Living together has costs as well as gains. Members must share food and can pass on germs. But together they find food, stay safe and raise their young better than one animal alone.
- e.g. An elephant family walks together, eats together and protects the calves together.
- e.g. An ant colony shares food: ants pass liquid food mouth to mouth to their sisters in the nest.
- e.g. A street dog group in your neighbourhood may sleep in the same spot every night and chase off strange dogs together.
### An elephant family is led by the oldest female · *defined*
**Definition:** An elephant family is a group of related females and their young: a grandmother, her daughters, her granddaughters and the small calves. The leader is called the matriarch. She is usually the oldest female.

Many people imagine that a big male with huge tusks leads the herd. He does not. Adult male elephants, called bulls, live alone or in loose groups of males. They visit families only for a short time. Young males leave their family as teenagers. So the family is a group of mothers, aunts, sisters and daughters. The family follows the matriarch. When she walks, they walk. When she stops to listen, they stop. When there is danger, they gather around her, with the calves in the middle. The scientist Cynthia Moss has studied the elephant families of Amboseli in Kenya since 1972, one of the longest studies of wild animals anywhere. In India, Asian elephant families live the same way. Female Asian elephants have no tusks, or only tiny ones.
- e.g. In the dry season, elephant families in Nagarhole and Bandipur in Karnataka walk to the Kabini river. Hundreds of elephants gather along its banks.
- e.g. When a calf squeals, the females nearby rush to them and rumble softly.
- e.g. A teenage male elephant starts to wander away from his family, and after a while lives on his own.
### Why the oldest female leads · *debated*
An old matriarch has lived through more dry years, more floods and more dangers than anyone else in her family. Scientists think her family follows her because her memory keeps them alive. Karen McComb and her team played recordings of lions roaring to elephant families in Amboseli. Families with an old matriarch reacted most strongly to the roar of male lions, who are the most dangerous to calves. Families with a younger matriarch often reacted less to them. In a bad drought in Tanzania in 1993, some families walked far out of the park to find water, and they lost fewer calves. The scientists think their old matriarchs remembered a drought from about 35 years before. Not everyone agrees on how much one leader decides. Some studies show that other females can start a move too.
- e.g. An old matriarch may remember a water pool that no younger elephant has ever seen.
- e.g. Elephants can tell the calls of their friends from the calls of strangers. Families with old matriarchs are better at it.
- e.g. Families who lose their old matriarch to poachers often show more signs of stress for years afterwards.
- **Karen McComb and Cynthia Moss, elephant scientists:** The matriarch matters most. Her age and memory are the family's store of knowledge. Families with old matriarchs make better choices about danger.
- **Some other elephant scientists:** The matriarch often leads, but other experienced females also start moves and help decide. The family decides together more than one leader does.
- **T. N. C. Vidya and her team, who study Asian elephants at Kabini in Karnataka:** Asian elephant families there often split into small groups and join up again. So what is true in Africa may not be exactly true in India. We need to study each kind of elephant.
### An ant colony: a queen and her worker daughters · *defined*
**Definition:** An ant colony is a family of ants living in one nest. A queen lays the eggs. Almost all the other ants are her daughters, the workers, who do all the other jobs.

Every worker ant you have ever seen is a female. Male ants hatch only at certain times of year. They have wings and fly out to mate, and they live only a short time. The queen stays deep in the nest and lays eggs. Workers look after the eggs and young, clean the nest, build, guard and bring in food. In many kinds of ants, the young workers work inside the nest. Older workers go out to forage. So the ants you see in your kitchen are probably older workers. The queen gives the workers no orders. Each worker senses what is going on around her and decides what to do next.
- e.g. The tiny ants on your kitchen platform belong to a nest you may never see, inside a wall or under a tile.
- e.g. Weaver ants, common in Kerala, Odisha and the Northeast, build nests in trees by pulling leaves together. They glue the leaves with silk from their young.
- e.g. Termites live in colonies too, but a termite colony has a king as well as a queen.
### How ants share news of food · *defined*
**Definition:** An ant who finds food leaves a scent trail, made of pheromone, on her way back to the nest. Other ants follow the trail to the food, and add their own scent on the way back.

The first ant to find food is often alone. She drinks or picks up a piece, and walks home, dabbing the floor with pheromone from her body. In the nest she meets her sisters and passes them some food. They follow her trail out. When they find the food, they lay scent on the way home too. So the trail gets stronger, and more ants follow it. When the food runs out, ants stop laying scent. The smell fades in a few minutes, and the ants stop coming. In lab tests with black garden ants, ants that found sweeter sugar laid more scent. So a sweet food builds a strong trail quickly.
- e.g. A dropped piece of laddu can have a line of ants within half an hour.
- e.g. If you wipe a short piece of the ant road with a wet cloth, the ants arriving there stop and wander, because the scent is gone.
- e.g. Ants passing each other on a trail tap antennae. They smell where the other ant has been.
### How a colony decides without a leader · *defined*
**Definition:** A colony makes choices as a group. Each ant makes small choices, such as which trail to follow and how much scent to lay. Together, these small choices become one choice for the whole colony.

Think of your sugar test. No ant tasted every drop and then gave orders. The ants that drank the strong sugar laid more scent on the way home. More ants followed that trail, so more ants laid scent there. The trail to the weak drop stayed faint. Soon most ants were at the strong drop. The scientist Deborah Gordon studied harvester ants in the deserts of Arizona for more than 30 years. She found that ants decide whether to go out for food by how often they meet ants coming back with food. Many quick meetings mean food is easy to find. Scientists call a group choice like this collective decision-making. Sometimes the crowd gets stuck. If ants find a weak drop first and build a strong trail, they may keep going there even after a sweeter drop appears.
- e.g. Honeybees choose a new home the same way: scouts dance for the places they found, and the best place gets the most dancers.
- e.g. A flock of starlings turns together because each bird watches the seven or so birds nearest to them.
- e.g. People on a busy railway platform make paths through the crowd without anyone in charge.
### Is a colony one mind? · *debated*
Each ant senses, remembers and chooses. A colony also seems to choose, as a whole, in ways no single ant could. Some scientists call a colony a superorganism. This means a group that works like one body, with each ant like a cell. Others say that is only a way of speaking. Scientists still argue about it.
- e.g. Army ants link their own bodies into living bridges across gaps, so the rest of the colony can cross.
- e.g. A colony moves house when the old nest floods, with workers carrying the eggs and the queen to safety.
- e.g. Your body is made of billions of cells, and each cell senses and responds too.
- **Scientists who use the word superorganism:** The colony is one living whole. The workers are like the cells of a body and the queen is like its egg-making organ. The colony thinks and chooses as one.
- **Many other biologists:** A colony is many ants, each following simple rules. Calling it one mind is a useful picture, but the choosing is done by the ants.
- **Others again:** Both are true at once. Each ant has her own small mind, and the colony has a larger one made of them.
### A wolf pack is a family · *defined*
**Definition:** A wild wolf pack is usually a family: a mother and a father and their young from the last one to three years.

The parents lead the pack. They choose where to travel and start the hunt. The young wolves follow them, as children follow their parents. Older young wolves help to feed and guard the new pups. Wolves bring meat back to the den in their stomachs and bring it up again for the pups to eat. When young wolves grow up, most of them leave to find a mate and start their own pack. Wolves howl to find each other when they are apart, and to warn other packs to keep away. Indian wolves live in the dry grasslands and farmland of the Deccan, Gujarat and Rajasthan. Their packs are small.
- e.g. In Velavadar National Park in Gujarat, wolf packs hunt blackbuck in the grasslands.
- e.g. A wolf alone can rarely catch a large deer. A pack hunting together often can.
- e.g. Street dogs in Indian cities are related to wolves. They often live in small groups too.
### Humans live in societies too · *idea*
Humans are animals who live in societies: families, villages, schools and cities. Like elephants, we learn a lot from older people. The scientist Kristen Hawkes studied the Hadza people in Tanzania. She found that grandmothers there gather food that helps their grandchildren grow. She suggested that grandmothers helped humans survive for thousands of years. This idea is called the grandmother hypothesis. Lauren Brent and her team found something like it in killer whales. Old females who no longer have calves lead their families to salmon, especially in years with little food. Scientists still argue about how much grandmothers explain.
- e.g. A grandmother who remembers which leaves are safe to eat, and which plants help a fever.
- e.g. Older killer whale females swim in front when food is hard to find.
- e.g. In many Indian homes, children learn recipes, songs and festival customs from their grandmothers.

## Mostly agreed
- Elephant families are groups of related females and their young, usually led by the oldest female. Adult males live apart. *How we know:* Scientists have watched and named the same elephant families for over 50 years, in Kenya, Sri Lanka, India and elsewhere.
- Old matriarchs know more about dangers than young ones, and their families do better. *How we know:* In playback tests, families with old matriarchs reacted more wisely to lion roars and to the calls of strange elephants.
- Worker ants are all females. The queen lays the eggs but does not give orders. *How we know:* Scientists have looked inside thousands of ant nests and watched the queen. Workers keep working even in nests where the queen is out of reach.
- Ants lay pheromone trails to food, and other ants follow them. *How we know:* Scientists can collect the scent from an ant's body and draw a line with the scent on paper. Ants follow the line even with no food at the end.
- A wild wolf pack is usually a family led by the parents. *How we know:* Years of watching wild wolves, and testing their genes, show that most pack members are parents and their young.

## How we found out
**Rudolph Schenkel, a Swiss animal scientist, and L. David Mech, an American wolf scientist, 1947 (Schenkel's zoo study), 1970 (Mech's book The Wolf), 1999 (Mech's paper on wild wolves).** In the 1940s, a Swiss scientist called Rudolph Schenkel watched wolves in a zoo in Basel. The wolves were strangers who had been put together in one enclosure. They fought often. Schenkel wrote that a top male and a top female, which he called the 'alpha' wolves, won their place by fighting. In 1970, the American scientist David Mech used the word in a popular book, The Wolf. Soon people everywhere believed that a wolf pack is ruled by the toughest fighter. Then Mech spent many summers on Ellesmere Island, in the far north of Canada, watching wild wolves who had little fear of people. He saw that a wild pack is a family: a mother, a father and their young from the last few years. The 'alpha' pair were simply the parents. The young wolves followed them as children follow their parents, and fights were rare. In 1999 Mech published what he had found. He asked his publisher to stop printing his old book. Scientists now think the zoo wolves fought because they were strangers forced together, not a family.  
*What people believed before:* People thought a wolf pack was ruled by an 'alpha' wolf who fought his way to the top. Watching wild wolves for many years showed that a pack is a family, and the so-called alphas are the parents.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A wolf pack is ruled by an 'alpha' wolf who fought his way to the top.** (A very common idea from older books, films and dog training; *rejected*)  
  For: Wolves put together as strangers in zoos did fight for rank.  
  Against: Wild packs are families, and the 'alpha' pair are simply the parents. The scientist who spread the word, David Mech, later showed it was wrong for wild wolves.
- **The queen ant rules the colony and tells the workers what to do.** (Many people, because of the word 'queen'; *rejected*)  
  For: The queen is the biggest ant and the mother of the others, and the workers feed and protect her.  
  Against: The queen gives no orders. Each worker decides what to do from what she senses around her. A colony keeps working even when the queen is far away in the nest.
- **The biggest male elephant leads the herd.** (Many people, and many cartoons; *rejected*)  
  For: Bull elephants are the largest and strongest, with the biggest tusks.  
  Against: Adult bulls live apart from the families. Families are led by an old female, the matriarch, who has the most experience.
- **Every insect queen keeps her place by bullying the others.** (An old idea about insect societies; *rejected*)  
  For: In some wasps, the queen does bully the others.  
  Against: Raghavendra Gadagkar and his team at the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru studied a paper wasp common across India, Ropalidia marginata. The queen there is calm and meek. When she is taken away, one worker becomes very aggressive for a while, and then becomes the next calm queen.
- **An ant colony is one mind, a superorganism.** (Some biologists; *debated*)  
  For: The colony makes good group choices that no single ant makes, such as picking the sweeter food or the best new nest.  
  Against: Others say the choosing is done by many ants, each following simple rules, and 'one mind' is only a picture.
- **Grandmothers helped humans survive, the way matriarchs help elephants.** (Kristen Hawkes and other scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Hadza grandmothers in Tanzania gather food that helps their grandchildren grow. Killer whale grandmothers lead their families to food.  
  Against: Other scientists think fathers, older children and the whole group mattered as much. It is hard to test what happened long ago.

## Roots
We have learned how elephant families, ant colonies and wolf packs live and decide together. Now we ask what animal societies tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your strong sugar drop ended up with far more ants than the weak one. No ant was in charge, and none gave orders. Ants that drank the sweeter sugar laid more scent on their way home. Those many small choices added up to one choice for the whole nest.  
*Habit it breaks:* We expect every group to have a boss who decides, like a captain or a head teacher. An ant nest has a queen, but she stays inside laying eggs and gives no orders. The choice is made by many ants together.  
*Evidence:* In the sugar drop test, the strong drop gained ants faster and faster, while the weak drop kept only a few. The queen was never there. The ants decided out on the floor, one ant at a time.

- *who am i:* You were born into a family, and you learned most of what you know from older people in it, just as an elephant calf learns from her grandmother.
- *what is life:* Many living beings survive by living together. An elephant calf, a worker ant and a wolf pup could not grow up alone.
- *what is this world:* Scientists estimate that about 20 million billion ants are alive on Earth. Almost all of them live in colonies, on nearly every piece of land.

One ant alone can find a drop of sugar. Thousands of ants, each sensing and choosing, find the best food and build roads to it. An elephant family carries the memory of their oldest member to every calf. In each society, many lives act together as one larger life, and each member is still herself.

- **Bhagavad Gita 3.21:** Whatever a great person does, others do too. The example they set, the world follows. (An elephant family follows the matriarch because of what she does: where she walks, where she digs, when she stops to listen. She leads by example, not by orders.)
- **Tao Te Ching 17:** The best leaders are hardly noticed. When the work is done, the people say, 'We did it ourselves.' (In your sugar test no ant was seen leading, yet the colony chose the sweeter drop. The choice came from the ants themselves.)
- **Ubuntu, a teaching of the Nguni-speaking peoples of southern Africa: umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu:** A person is a person through other people. (A worker ant, an elephant calf and a wolf pup each grow up through their family. What each one becomes depends on the others around them.)
- Science (settled): In lab tests, ant colonies end up mostly at the sweeter of two sugar drops, because ants lay more trail scent for sweeter food.
- Science (open): Whether a colony has a mind of its own, as well as each ant, is still argued about.
- Other view, Many biologists: A colony is many ants following simple rules, such as 'lay more scent for sweeter food'. Calling the colony's choice a decision is a handy way of talking. It does not show that the colony is one being with a mind of its own.
- Practice, Walk without a leader (3 min): Let's all stand up in the middle of the room. Nobody leads, and nobody talks. Each of us watches only the person next to us. If they move, we move a little the same way. Let's walk slowly for one minute. Now stop. Close your eyes and take five slow breaths. Did we move together? Who decided where we went?
- Ask: Your ants chose the strong drop together. Did the colony choose, or did each ant choose, or both?
- Ask: An elephant grandmother remembers water from long ago. Who in your family remembers things that no one else does?
- Ask: If no ant is in charge, why does the colony not fall apart?

## Story: The Grandmother Who Knew the Way
*Asian elephant families led by an old female, bulls living apart, and the summer gathering of elephants along the Kabini river at the edge of Nagarhole are real. Nila, Ravi, Kempamma, the elephant called Doddamma and this summer are made up.*

Nila lived in a small village at the edge of the Nagarhole forest in Karnataka. Behind her house was a field of ragi, and behind the field was the forest. In the evenings, elephants sometimes walked along the edge of the trees.

That April, her cousin Ravi came from Bengaluru for the holidays. Ravi was eleven, two years older than Nila. He knew a great deal about cars and nothing at all about elephants.

It was the hottest summer anyone could remember. The small pools in the forest had dried into cracked mud. The grass was brown. Even the stream near the village was only a line of wet sand.

"The elephants will come past soon," said Nila's grandmother, Kempamma. "When the forest dries, they walk to the river."

Kempamma had lived by the forest all her life. For many years she had cooked for the forest guards at the watchtower. She knew every path, and she knew the elephants who used them.

"Will the big tusker lead them?" asked Ravi. "The one with the giant tusks?"

Nila laughed. "Wait and see," she said.

That evening the three of them climbed onto the flat roof of the house. Kempamma brought a torch, but she did not switch it on. "We watch from far away," she said. "And we keep quiet. An elephant is a big, strong person, and she has a calf to protect."

Just as the sky turned orange, the leaves at the edge of the forest moved. A grey head came out of the trees. Then a second, and a third.

The first elephant was old. Her skin hung in loose folds, and one of her ears had a torn edge, like a leaf a goat had nibbled. She had no tusks at all.

"That's Doddamma," Nila whispered. "It means big mother. She's the oldest in her family."

"But where's the tusker?" Ravi whispered back.

"Not here," said Kempamma. "The grown-up males live on their own. This family is all mothers and daughters and little ones. Look."

Ravi counted. Behind Doddamma came two big females, then three smaller ones. Two tiny calves walked right under their mothers' bellies, in the shade. At the very end walked a young male with short new tusks. He kept stopping to pull at branches, then hurrying to catch up.

"He's a teenager," said Kempamma. "In a year or two he will wander off and live with other males. That is how it is with elephants. The daughters stay. The sons go."

At the dry stream, Doddamma stopped. The whole family stopped behind her. She lifted her trunk and held it in the air, turning it slowly, smelling the wind. Then she began to dig in the sand with her front foot, and with the tip of her trunk.

"What's she doing?" asked Ravi.

"Watch," said Nila.

After a while, the hole in the sand turned dark. Water was seeping into it from below. Doddamma drank first, sucking the water into her trunk and squirting it into her mouth. Then she stepped back, and a mother pushed her calf forward to drink.

"How did she know the water was there?" Ravi asked.

"She has done it before," said Kempamma. "Many times. Doddamma is older than your father, Ravi. She has lived through more dry summers than anyone in her family. The young mothers don't know where the hidden water is. She does."

There was not enough water in the hole for everyone. After a while Doddamma rumbled, a deep sound that Ravi felt in his chest more than heard. She turned away from the stream and set off down the long path towards the river. One by one, the family followed her.

"Why not stay and dig more?" said Ravi.

Kempamma was quiet for a moment. "Many years ago, when I was a young woman, the rains failed for two years," she said. "Even the streams dried to dust. The herds that stayed in the forest suffered. One old female led her family all the way to the river, many hours' walk. Her family lived. I think that old female was Doddamma's mother. And Doddamma walked behind her, as a young elephant, and learned the way."

Nila looked at her grandmother. "So Doddamma remembers what her own mother showed her."

"And her daughters will remember what she shows them," said Kempamma.

Ravi was quiet for a long time. "I thought the strongest one would be the leader," he said at last.

"The strongest one can push a tree over," said Nila. "But pushing a tree over doesn't find you water. The one who knows the way is the one they follow."

A week later, Kempamma took them both on the forest department's bus along the Kabini river. The water had shrunk back, leaving wide green grass along the banks. On the grass were elephants. Not one family, but dozens of families, hundreds of elephants, grazing and drinking and spraying mud over their backs.

Ravi pressed his face to the window. Then he grabbed Nila's arm.

"Look! The torn ear!"

At the edge of the water, an old female was spraying water over a calf. Her ear had a torn edge, like a leaf a goat had nibbled.

Doddamma had brought her family to the river again.

**For the parent:** For the parent: an elephant family follows the oldest female, the matriarch, because she has the most experience. Doddamma found water under the dry sand because she had done it in earlier dry summers. When there was not enough, she led her family to the river, a path she learned as a young elephant from her own mother. Knowledge passes down a family of females, from grandmother to mother to daughter. The young male will leave, as male elephants do. Size and strength did not decide who led. Memory did. Kempamma, who has watched the forest all her life, plays the same part for Nila and Ravi.

- Ask: Ravi expected the big tusker to lead the herd. Why was he not with the family at all?
- Ask: How did Doddamma find water in a dry stream?
- Ask: Why did the family follow Doddamma, and not the biggest or youngest female?
- Ask: Where did Doddamma learn the way to the river?
- Ask: Kempamma knew things about the forest that Ravi did not. Who in your family knows things nobody else knows?

### e1 · Weak Sugar or Strong Sugar: which drop does the colony choose? (40 min)
- **question:** If ants find a weak sugar drop and a strong sugar drop at the same time, will the colony end up at one of them? Which one, and how?
- **predict:** Guess first. After half an hour, will there be more ants at the weak drop, more at the strong drop, or the same at both? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Change only how sweet the drops are. Use the same amount of each drop, the same kind of square, the same distance from the ant road, and put both down at the same moment. Count both drops at the same time. If you can, do it again the next day with the two squares swapped, because one side might be nearer the nest.
- **record:** Fill in the table every 5 minutes. Then draw two bars for the last count: one for the weak drop and one for the strong drop.
- **results table:** Minutes since start | Ants on the weak drop | Ants on the strong drop
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look for ants along the kitchen platform, the window sill or near the dustbin. 2. Watch which way they walk for one minute. 3. Put the two squares a hand's width from the ant road, side by side, about 15 cm apart.  
  *Parent says:* First we need to find the ants' road. Let's watch where they walk, without touching them. We'll put both squares the same distance from the road.  
  *Child mode:* Find a line of ants. Watch where they walk. Put both squares near the line. Keep them the same distance away.
  - Note: If you can't find ants, leave a few grains of sugar on the kitchen platform for an hour first, then look. Ants are often busiest in the evening. Both squares must be the same distance from the ants, or one drop gets an unfair start.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the cup marked W, mix 2 teaspoons of water with one pinch of sugar. 2. In the cup marked S, mix 2 teaspoons of water with 2 teaspoons of sugar. Stir for one minute. 3. Write W on one square and S on the other. 4. Put half a teaspoon of each mix on its square, at the same moment. 5. Start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* W is weak, with just a pinch of sugar. S is strong, very sweet. Before we put them down, tell me your guess. After half an hour, where will most ants be? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Mix the weak cup and the strong cup. Put a drop on each square. Start the timer. Now guess. Where will most ants be? Why?
  - Ask: Where do you think most ants will be after half an hour, and why? (good: At the strong drop, because ants like sweeter food.; The same at both, because ants can't tell from far away.; At whichever drop they find first, because they will follow the first trail.)
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. Don't hint. The strong mix will look thick and may not all dissolve. That is fine.
- **s3 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every 5 minutes, count the ants on each square and write both numbers in the table. 2. In between, sit still and watch. 3. Follow one ant from the strong drop with your eyes. Does she walk straight back towards the ant road?

  *Child mode:* Every 5 minutes, count the ants on each square. Write the numbers down. In between, watch one ant. Where does she go?
  - Ask: What do two ants do when they meet on the path? (good: They touch each other with their antennae.; They stop for a moment, then walk on.)
  - Note: Ants that keep moving are hard to count. Count the ants touching the square, and count twice if you are unsure. If after 15 minutes no ant has come to either drop, move both squares closer to the ant road, together, and start the count again. Sometimes the ants find the weak drop first and keep crowding it. That is a real result too, and the explanation cards say why it happens.
  - Think: No ant has tasted both drops and told the others. So how do the ants end up at one more than the other?
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at your table together. 2. Ask your child to say what they found, what they saw, and why they think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look at the numbers. What did you find? What did you see that tells you so? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your numbers. Which drop has more ants? How did the number change? Why do you think so?
  - Note: Let your child explain before you read the explanation cards. If the weak drop won, ask which drop the ants found first.
  - Check: Most ants ended up at the strong drop. How did the colony make this choice? → **Ants that drank the strong sugar laid more scent, so more ants followed their trail**

## Worksheets
### Who Leads? (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Who leads an elephant family?  
   Options: The biggest male, with the longest tusks / The oldest female, called the matriarch / The youngest calf / Whoever is hungriest  
   **Answer:** The oldest female, called the matriarch. *Why:* An elephant family is led by the matriarch, usually the oldest female. Adult males live apart. A strong answer adds why: she remembers the most.
2. (truefalse) Every worker ant you see on the kitchen platform is a female.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Worker ants are all females, daughters of the queen. Male ants hatch only at certain times of year, have wings, and live a short time.
3. (fill) The smell an ant leaves on the floor to show the way to food is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pheromone', 'pheromone trail']. *Why:* A pheromone is a smell an animal makes to send a message to others of their kind. Ants lay pheromone trails to food.
4. (match) Match each society to who leads it.  
   **Answer:** Elephant family → the oldest female; Wolf pack → the mother and father; Ant colony → no single leader. *Why:* An elephant family follows the matriarch. A wolf pack follows the parents. In an ant colony, the queen lays eggs but does not lead, and the ants decide together.
5. (sort) Sort these animals of an elephant family: who stays with the family all their life, and who will leave?  
   **Answer:** the grandmother → Stays; a 12-year-old daughter → Stays; a 14-year-old son → Leaves; a baby daughter → Stays; a 10-year-old son → Leaves; an aunt → Stays. *Why:* Female elephants stay with their mothers and grandmother all their lives. Males leave as teenagers and live alone or with other males. The 10-year-old son will probably leave in a few years.
6. (write) Why does an elephant family follow the oldest female, and not the biggest one? Give one reason.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is an animal society?  
   Options: Any crowd of animals in one place / A group of animals of one kind who live together for a long time and help each other / Animals in a zoo / Only groups of humans  
   **Answer:** A group of animals of one kind who live together for a long time and help each other. *Why:* A society's members stay together, know each other and share the work. A crowd of pigeons at a grain shop is not a society: they just happen to be in one place.
2. (mcq) Why is an old matriarch useful to her family in a drought?  
   Options: She is the strongest / She may remember where water was in a drought long ago / She eats the least / She can run the fastest  
   **Answer:** She may remember where water was in a drought long ago. *Why:* Her long memory is the family's store of knowledge. Strength and speed don't find hidden water.
3. (mcq) Where do adult male elephants usually live?  
   Options: As the leaders of the families / Alone, or in loose groups of males / In the middle of the family, guarding the calves / With the wolves  
   **Answer:** Alone, or in loose groups of males. *Why:* Males leave their family as teenagers and live apart, visiting families only for a short time.
4. (mcq) What does the queen in an ant colony do?  
   Options: Gives orders to the workers / Lays the eggs, deep in the nest / Leads the line of ants to food / Guards the entrance  
   **Answer:** Lays the eggs, deep in the nest. *Why:* The queen lays eggs and gives no orders. The workers decide what to do from what they sense.
5. (mcq) How does an ant tell her sisters where food is?  
   Options: By drawing a map / By leaving a scent trail, made of pheromone, on the way home / By shouting / By carrying every sister to the food  
   **Answer:** By leaving a scent trail, made of pheromone, on the way home. *Why:* She dabs pheromone on the floor as she walks home. Other ants follow the smell.
6. (mcq) Priya, a girl in Class 3 in Hyderabad, put a weak and a strong sugar drop near an ant road. After 30 minutes the strong drop had 20 ants and the weak drop had 3. What is the best explanation?

   **Answer:** Ants that drank the strong sugar laid more scent, so more ants followed their trail. *Why:* A sweeter food makes ants lay more trail scent, so the trail grows stronger and pulls in more ants. No ant gave orders.
7. (mcq) In a sugar drop test, why should both drops be the same distance from the ant road?  
   Options: So the test looks tidy / So that only the sweetness is different, and neither drop gets an unfair start / Because ants can only see a short way / It does not matter  
   **Answer:** So that only the sweetness is different, and neither drop gets an unfair start. *Why:* In a fair test you change only one thing. If one drop is closer, ants may find it first, and then you can't tell whether sweetness or distance made the difference.
8. (mcq) Who leads a wild wolf pack, usually?  
   Options: The toughest fighter / The parents: a mother and a father / The youngest pup / Nobody  
   **Answer:** The parents: a mother and a father. *Why:* A wild pack is a family, so the parents lead and the young follow, as children follow parents.
9. (mcq) Where did the idea of a fighting 'alpha wolf' come from?  
   Options: From watching wild wolf families for many years / From watching strangers put together in a zoo / From old songs / From dogs  
   **Answer:** From watching strangers put together in a zoo. *Why:* Rudolph Schenkel watched unrelated wolves in a zoo in Basel in the 1940s. They fought for rank. David Mech later watched wild wolves and showed that packs are families.
10. (mcq) Why do wolves howl?  
   Options: Only at the full moon / To find each other when apart, and to tell other packs to keep away / Because they are sad / To scare the pups  
   **Answer:** To find each other when apart, and to tell other packs to keep away. *Why:* A howl carries far. Pack members use it to find each other, and other packs hear that the area is taken. The full moon has nothing to do with it.
11. (mcq) In what way is a human family most like an elephant family?  
   Options: Grown-up sons always leave / Children learn a lot from older relatives, such as grandmothers / The biggest person leads / Everyone eats grass  
   **Answer:** Children learn a lot from older relatives, such as grandmothers. *Why:* In both, the young learn from older family members. In many human families sons stay, so that part is different.

## Applications
- Forest departments plan elephant corridors, safe paths between forests, along the routes that matriarchs use year after year.
- Wildlife workers try hard to protect old matriarchs from poachers, because whole families depend on their memory.
- Computer scientists copy ant trails to find short routes for delivery vans and for data on the internet. This is called ant colony optimisation.
- Engineers build groups of small robots that work together with no leader, copying ants and bees.
- Dog trainers have moved away from 'be the alpha' training, because wolf packs turned out to be families.
- Farmers in parts of Asia have used weaver ants to guard fruit trees from pests for hundreds of years.

## Open questions
- **Is an ant colony one mind, or many minds working together?** Each ant senses and chooses, and the colony also makes choices. Scientists disagree on whether to call the colony a superorganism.
- **How does an old matriarch store a memory of water from 35 years ago?** Elephants have large brains with a big area for memory, but nobody knows how such long memories of places are kept.
- **Do Asian elephant families work the same way as African ones?** Most studies are of African savanna elephants. Studies at Kabini and in Sri Lanka show Asian families split and join more often, but much is still unknown.
- **How does a colony of thousands of ants decide to move house?** Scientists understand this well in a few kinds of ants, but there are more than 14,000 known kinds, and most have never been studied.
- **Are Indian wolves a separate kind of wolf?** Gene studies suggest that wolves in India are one of the oldest wolf lineages. Scientists still argue about whether they are a separate species.
