# Insects and small creatures: counting legs, and how a tiny ant carries so much
`sci.animals.insects.L2`

**Summary.** An ant can carry off a crumb bigger than her own head. Ants, bees and butterflies are insects: they have six legs, three body parts and two feelers. A spider has eight legs and two body parts, so a spider is not an insect. An earthworm has no legs at all. Your child counts legs, then puts biscuit crumbs of four sizes beside an ant line and watches which ones the ants carry home.

**Intro.** *A crumb is moving across the kitchen floor by itself. Look closer. An ant is carrying the crumb. How can something so small carry so much?* Small creatures live all around us, even in a flat. Ants walk in lines in the kitchen. A spider waits in a corner of the bathroom. A butterfly visits the curry leaf plant on the balcony. Some of these small creatures are insects, and some are not. You can tell them apart by counting their legs. In this unit you count legs on ants, spiders and butterflies. Then you test how big a crumb an ant can carry.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that an insect has six legs, three body parts and two feelers, and name some insects: ants, bees and butterflies.
1. By the end I can say why a spider is not an insect: a spider has eight legs and two body parts.
2. By the end I can say that an earthworm has no legs, and moves with muscles and tiny bristles.
3. By the end I can count the legs on a small creature, or on a picture, and sort small creatures by their legs.
4. By the end I can make a guess with a reason, test which crumbs ants carry, count what is left and say what I found.
5. By the end I can say that small animals are very strong for their size, and that ants, bees, spiders and worms sense the world and make choices.

## Terms
- **Insect**: An insect is a small animal with six legs and a body in three parts: the head, the middle and the back part. *e.g.* Ants, bees, butterflies, mosquitoes and cockroaches are all insects. Each one has six legs.
- **Antennae**: Antennae are the two feelers on an insect's head. Insects use them to smell and to touch. *e.g.* When two ants meet, they tap each other with their antennae. That is how they smell who the other ant is.
- **Thorax**: The thorax is the middle part of an insect's body. All six legs grow from the thorax, and so do the wings. *e.g.* Look at a bee. Her legs and her wings all join on to her middle part, the thorax.
- **Abdomen**: The abdomen is the back part of an insect's body. Food is digested there. *e.g.* A honeybee's abdomen has yellow and black stripes. Her sting is at the tip of her abdomen.
- **Spider**: A spider is a small animal with eight legs and a body in two parts. Spiders are not insects. *e.g.* The thin-legged spider in the bathroom corner has eight legs. Count them: four on each side.
- **Silk**: Silk is a strong, thin thread that some animals make inside their bodies. Spiders make silk, and so do silkworms. *e.g.* A spider's web is made of silk. A Kanchipuram silk saree is made from the silk of silkworms.
- **Earthworm**: An earthworm is a long, soft animal with no legs, whose body is made of many rings. Earthworms live in damp soil. *e.g.* After the first rain of the monsoon, earthworms often come up out of the soil onto the path.
- **Colony**: A colony is a big family of animals who live together and share the work. Ants and honeybees live in colonies. *e.g.* One ant colony can have thousands of ants. Almost all of them are sisters, and one or more queens lay the eggs.

## Explanations
### What makes an insect · *defined*
**Definition:** An insect has six legs, a body in three parts (head, thorax and abdomen) and two antennae. Most insects also have wings.

Count the legs first. Every grown-up insect has six legs, three on each side, and all six join the middle part, the thorax. On the head are two antennae, which smell and touch. Many insects have wings: a bee has four, and a housefly has two. Some butterflies seem to have only four legs, but the front two are tiny and folded up under the head. So they still have six.
- e.g. An ant: six legs, three body parts, two antennae, and no wings on most ants.
- e.g. A honeybee: six legs, four wings, two antennae.
- e.g. A butterfly: six legs, four big wings, two antennae with a little knob at the tip.
- e.g. A cockroach and a mosquito are insects too.
### A spider is not an insect · *defined*
**Definition:** A spider has eight legs, a body in two parts and no antennae. Insects have six legs, three body parts and two antennae.

Count a spider's legs and you get eight, four on each side. A spider's body has two parts: a front part with the legs, eyes and mouth, and a round back part. Spiders have no antennae and no wings. Some spiders build webs of silk to catch insects. Others, like the small jumping spiders on our walls, have no web. They creep up and jump on their food. Spiders, scorpions and ticks are all arachnids, and all have eight legs.
- e.g. The thin-legged spider in a bathroom corner: eight legs, two body parts.
- e.g. A jumping spider on a window frame: eight legs and two very big front eyes.
- e.g. A scorpion: eight legs, so a scorpion is in the spider group too.
### A worm has no legs · *defined*
**Definition:** An earthworm has no legs. Muscles squeeze and stretch the body, and tiny bristles grip the soil.

An earthworm's body is made of many rings, with muscles under the skin. First the front end gets long and thin and pushes forward. Then the body gets short and fat, and pulls the back end along. On each ring there are tiny stiff hairs called bristles. They grip the soil so the worm does not slide back. Earthworms breathe through their skin, so the skin must stay damp.
- e.g. Earthworms in the soil of a big potted plant.
- e.g. Earthworms on a wet path after the first monsoon rain.
- e.g. Earthworms in a vermicompost bin, turning vegetable peels into soil.
### Small bodies are strong for their size · *defined*
**Definition:** A small animal can carry a much bigger load, for the size of their body, than a big animal can.

Many ants can carry things heavier than their own bodies. A person cannot carry a load as heavy as a car. Here is why size matters. A muscle's strength depends on how thick it is, and a body's weight depends on how much of it there is. Imagine an ant made twice as long, twice as wide and twice as tall. Her muscles would be about 4 times as strong, but her body would be about 8 times as heavy. So a small body is very light for the strength of its muscles, and ants can carry so much.
- e.g. An ant carrying a sugar grain bigger than her own head.
- e.g. A group of ants dragging a crumb too big for one ant.
- e.g. A person can carry a school bag, but not a load as big as the person.
### Insects sense, learn and choose · *debated*
Ants, bees, spiders and worms sense the world, remember and make choices. A honeybee learns which flower colours give her food, and remembers the way back. She then dances in the hive to tell her sisters where the flowers are. A jumping spider watches her food, then plans a path to creep up on it. Scientists agree about what these animals do. They still argue about what, if anything, the animals feel while they do it.
- e.g. A honeybee dances to show her sisters where the flowers are.
- e.g. Ants call more ants to a big crumb.
- e.g. In a 2022 test, bumblebees rolled small balls again and again, with no food as a reward.
- **Hiruni Samadi Galpayage Dona, a scientist from Sri Lanka working in London, and her team:** In 2022 they gave bumblebees small wooden balls. Many bees rolled the balls again and again, though there was no food for it. Young bees rolled them most. The team said this looks like play, and that insects may have good and bad feelings.
- **Many other scientists who study insects:** We can see what insects do, but not what they feel. Many of their actions are built into their bodies from birth. We should be careful before saying that an insect enjoys something the way a child does.

## Mostly agreed
- Every grown-up insect has six legs, a body in three parts and two antennae. *How we know:* Scientists have described about a million kinds of insects. Ants, bees, butterflies, beetles, flies and all the rest have six legs as grown-ups.
- Spiders have eight legs and two body parts, so spiders are not insects. *How we know:* Every kind of spider ever found has eight legs. Their bodies, eyes and silk are built differently from any insect's.
- Small animals are much stronger for their size than big animals. *How we know:* In 1638 Galileo, an Italian scientist, worked out that when a body grows, the weight grows faster than the strength. Scientists who weigh ants and their loads find many ants carry loads heavier than themselves.

## How we found out
**Charles Henry Turner, an African American scientist and teacher, testing an idea of Albrecht Bethe, a German scientist, Bethe wrote his idea in 1898. Turner published his ant tests in 1907, and his bee colour tests in 1910..** About 125 years ago, many scientists thought insects were like tiny machines. In 1898, Albrecht Bethe, a German scientist, wrote that ants and bees only react to smells and other signals. He said they cannot learn. Charles Henry Turner, an African American scientist, decided to test this. People were often unfair to Black scientists then, and he did much of his research while teaching in a school. Turner built a small platform with little paths and bridges leading back to an ants' nest. He put ants on the platform and watched them find their way home, again and again. At first they wandered. Then they found their way faster each time, as if they had learned the route. In 1907 he wrote that ants learn and remember the way home. Later he showed that bees learn colours too.  
*What people believed before:* That insects are like machines, who only react and cannot learn. Turner's tests showed that ants learn their way home and get better with practice.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Spiders are insects.** (A very common idea. Even Carl Linnaeus, a famous Swedish scientist, put spiders in his group of insects in 1758.; *rejected*)  
  For: Spiders are small, have many legs and no bones, and live in the same places as insects.  
  Against: Count the legs: a spider has eight, an insect has six. A spider has two body parts, not three, and no antennae. Scientists later moved spiders into their own group, with scorpions and ticks.
- **If an ant were as big as a person, she could lift a car.** (A common idea in cartoons and quiz books; *rejected*)  
  For: An ant can carry a load heavier than her own body. So a person-sized ant sounds as if she could lift something very heavy.  
  Against: When a body gets bigger, the weight grows much faster than the strength. A person-sized ant would be very heavy, and would struggle even to hold up her own body. She would not lift a car.
- **Cut an earthworm in two, and you get two worms.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Some earthworms can grow back a lost tail end, so a worm with a missing tail can live on.  
  Against: In most earthworms, the back half dies. The front half, with the mouth and the important parts inside, can sometimes grow a new tail. So you do not get two worms. And cutting harms the worm, so never cut one.
- **Insects are like little machines. They only react, and cannot learn.** (Albrecht Bethe, a German scientist, in 1898, and others after him; *debated*)  
  For: Many insect actions are the same every time, and are built into the body from birth. A newly hatched ant knows how to walk and clean herself without being taught.  
  Against: Charles Henry Turner showed in 1907 that ants learn their way home, and later that bees learn colours. Bees also learn to tell their sisters where food is, and bumblebees can learn to pull a string to get food.

## Roots
We have counted legs, met insects, spiders and worms, and watched ants carry crumbs. Now we ask what the crumb test tells us about size, strength and ourselves.

**Deep truth.** An ant can carry a crumb bigger than her own head. You cannot carry a load as heavy as your own body. A small body weighs very little for the strength of its muscles. So smaller animals are usually stronger for their size.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of big as strong and small as weak. But strength depends on size in a surprising way: as a body grows, the weight grows faster than the strength. So a small body is not a weaker copy of a big one. Being small brings its own powers.  
*Evidence:* In the crumb test, ants carried the tiny crumbs, and teams sometimes moved bigger ones. In 1638 Galileo worked out that when a body is made bigger, the weight grows faster than the strength.

- *who am i:* You are a big animal, millions of times heavier than an ant. Your size decides what you can lift, and what you cannot.
- *what is life:* Life comes in every size. Each size has its own strengths: an ant lifts big loads, and a person can carry a baby a long way.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of small creatures, far more of them than of us. Ants, bees, spiders and worms do huge work together, one small load at a time.

An ant colony carries home far more food than any one ant could. Each ant brings a crumb, and thousands of sisters together feed the whole nest. A honeybee brings home a tiny drop of nectar, and many trips make a comb of honey. Worms move a little soil each, and together they turn over whole fields. The work of one small life joins the work of millions, and the colony, the hive and the soil are what the many build together.

- **Acharanga Sutra 1.2.3:** This old Jain text says that all beings love their life. They want to be happy and do not want to be hurt. To all of them, life is dear. (An ant who carries a crumb home is working for her own life and her sisters' lives. The Jain teaching asks us to care for the smallest beings, which is why this unit says: watch the ants, but do not spray or squash them.)
- **Proverbs 6:6-8:** The Bible says: go to the ant and watch her ways. She has no ruler or boss, yet she gathers her food in summer. (In the crumb test, no ant gave orders. Each ant carried what she could, and the colony's food arrived anyway.)
- **The Quran, Surah An-Naml 27:18:** The Quran tells of an ant who sees King Solomon's army coming. She calls to the other ants to go into their homes, so that they are not crushed. (In this story an ant notices danger, thinks of her sisters and warns them. Real ants also warn and call each other, with smells and touches of their antennae.)
- Science (settled): When a body is made bigger, the weight grows faster than the strength. This is why small animals can carry more for their size. Galileo worked this out in 1638.
- Science (debated): Bumblebees rolled small balls with no food as a reward, and some scientists think this is play. Whether insects have feelings like ours is still argued about.
- Other view, Many scientists who study insects: An ant carrying a crumb is following ways of acting built into her body from birth. In this view, we should not say that the ant cares about her sisters, or chooses her crumb, the way a person would.
- Practice, Ant's-Eye View (3 min): Lie down on your tummy and put your cheek near the floor. Look along the floor, the way an ant would see the room. What looks huge from down here? Breathe in slowly and out slowly, three times. Now think of the ant carrying her crumb all the way home.
- Ask: Is a small life less strong than a big one, or strong in a different way?
- Ask: If you were as small as an ant, what could you do that you cannot do now?
- Ask: An ant carries one crumb, and the nest is fed. What do you carry that helps your family?

## Story: The Crumb That Walked
*A made-up story. The facts in it are true: ants carry crumbs bigger than their heads, spiders have eight legs, and plain tiger butterflies, like many other butterflies, hold their two small front legs folded up.*

Meenakshi lived on the fourth floor of a building in Chennai. She was seven. Her brother Arjun was five. Their grandmother, Paati, lived with them.

One evening after dinner, Meenakshi was sweeping the kitchen floor. She stopped. A white crumb was moving across the floor, all by itself.

'Arjun!' she called. 'Come and see. The crumb is walking!'

Arjun ran in and squatted down. 'Crumbs cannot walk,' he said. Then he looked closer. 'There is an ant underneath!'

The ant was tiny and black. She held the crumb up in her jaws and walked fast, as if the crumb weighed nothing. The crumb was bigger than her whole head.

Paati came in with her reading glasses on. She bent down and watched. 'That crumb is from your biscuit,' she said. 'She is taking it home to her nest. Look, there is the line of her sisters, going under the door.'

'How do you know they are sisters?' asked Arjun.

'Nearly all the ants in a nest are sisters,' said Paati. 'They have one mother, the queen. She stays in the nest and lays the eggs. Her daughters do all the work.'

Meenakshi fetched the magnifying glass from Paati's sewing box. She held the glass over the ant line. The ants grew big and shiny.

'Let me count her legs,' she said. 'One, two, three on this side. One, two, three on that side. Six legs!'

'And two feelers on her head,' said Arjun. 'They are waving.'

'Those are her antennae,' said Paati. 'She smells with them. Every insect has six legs and two antennae.'

Arjun jumped up. 'Let's count another creature. Come!' He ran to the bathroom and pointed at the corner of the ceiling. A spider with long, thin legs hung there, very still, in a messy web.

Meenakshi stood on the step stool and looked through the glass, without touching. She counted slowly. 'One, two, three, four on this side. One, two, three, four on the other side. That is eight!'

'Eight?' said Arjun. 'But insects have six.'

'So the spider is not an insect,' said Meenakshi. She counted again, to be sure. Still eight. 'And look. Her body is in two parts, not three. And she has no antennae.'

'Spiders are spiders,' said Paati. 'They have their own group, with scorpions. That spider has lived in our bathroom for weeks. She catches mosquitoes in her web.'

Arjun looked up at the spider with new respect. 'Thank you, spider,' he said.

The next morning, the children went out to the balcony. Paati grew pink nithyakalyani flowers in an old paint tin. A big orange butterfly with black edges and white spots was sitting on a flower.

'That is a plain tiger butterfly,' said Paati. 'Watch her drink.' The butterfly uncurled a long, thin tongue, like a straw, and pushed it into the flower.

'Six legs?' asked Arjun.

Meenakshi crept close and held her breath. 'I can see four legs holding on to the flower,' she whispered. 'Only four.'

'Look near her head,' said Paati.

Meenakshi looked hard. There, folded up under the butterfly's head, were two tiny legs, like little arms. 'Six!' she said. 'She keeps two folded up.'

'Many butterflies do that,' said Paati. 'They walk on four legs, but they still have six. That is why we look twice.'

The butterfly opened her wings and flew away over the street.

That afternoon it rained hard. When it stopped, Paati took the children down to the building's garden. On the wet path were three long, pink-brown earthworms.

Arjun bent down. 'How many legs does a worm have?'

Meenakshi looked through the glass. She saw rings all along the worm's body, but no legs at all. 'None,' she said. 'Zero legs.'

'Then how does the worm move?' asked Arjun.

They watched. The worm's front end grew long and thin and pushed forward. Then the body bunched up, short and fat, and the back end slid along.

'Like squeezing toothpaste,' said Meenakshi.

Paati gently moved the worms onto the soil with a leaf, so that nobody would step on them.

That night, Meenakshi drew three pictures in her notebook: an ant with six legs, a spider with eight, and a worm with none. But she kept thinking about the walking crumb.

'Paati,' she said, 'the crumb was bigger than the ant's head. How can she carry something so big?'

Paati smiled. 'Could you carry a sack of rice as big as you?'

Meenakshi thought about the big rice sack in the kitchen. She could not even drag it. 'No,' she said.

'So how big a crumb can an ant carry?' asked Paati. 'Do you think she could carry any crumb at all?'

Meenakshi looked at the biscuit tin. 'I don't know,' she said. 'But I can find out.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: Meenakshi learns to sort small creatures by counting carefully: six legs for an insect, eight for a spider, none for a worm. Counting twice, and looking closely at the butterfly's folded legs, teaches her not to trust a first look. The story ends on a real question the crumb test will answer: how big a load can an ant carry?

- Ask: How did Meenakshi find out that the spider is not an insect?
- Ask: Why did the butterfly seem to have only four legs?
- Ask: How does an earthworm move without legs?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Crumb Test: how big a crumb can an ant carry? (40 min)
- **question:** Which sizes of crumb can ants carry away, and which are too big?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which crumbs will the ants carry away: the tiny ones, the rice-sized ones, the chana-sized ones, or even the big ones? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the crumbs come from the same biscuit, so they taste the same. There are 5 crumbs of each size, because one crumb can be luck. All 20 crumbs go in one line, with the sizes mixed up, so every size is the same distance from the ants. We count at 10 minutes and again at 20 minutes.
- **safety:** Some red ants bite or sting. Watch the ants, but do not touch them or put your hand in the ant line. When you finish, wipe the place with a damp cloth so the ants go home. Do not spray or squash them.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. A grown-up can write the numbers while your child counts.
- **results table:** Crumb size | Crumbs at the start | Left after 10 minutes | Left after 20 minutes | How the ants moved them: one ant, a team, or bit by bit
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a line of ants in the kitchen, on the balcony or by a window. 2. Look at the ants with the magnifying glass. 3. Count the legs on one ant.  
  *Parent says:* Let's find some ants. Look at one through the glass. How many legs does she have? How big is her head?  
  *Child mode:* Find a line of ants. Look at one through the glass. Count her legs.
  - Note: If you cannot find ants, put a pinch of sugar on a plate on the kitchen platform. Look again after an hour. In most Indian homes, ants come within a few hours. If none come, try again the next day.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Break the biscuit with the spoon. 2. Make 5 tiny crumbs, the size of sugar grains. 3. Make 5 crumbs the size of rice grains. 4. Make 5 crumbs the size of a chana. 5. Make 5 big pieces, the size of your thumbnail.  
  *Parent says:* We need four sizes of crumb. Five tiny ones, five like rice, five like chana and five as big as your thumbnail.  
  *Child mode:* Make 5 crumbs of each size. Tiny, rice, chana and thumbnail.
  - Note: The sizes do not need to be exact. What matters is four clearly different sizes, with the smallest about as small as a sugar grain.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child which crumbs the ants will carry away, and why. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* The ants will find these crumbs soon. Which sizes do you think they will carry away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which crumbs will the ants carry away? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Only the tiny ones, because ants are tiny.; The rice ones too, because I saw an ant carry a big crumb.; None of the big ones, because they are heavier than an ant.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine, as long as there is a reason. Do not tell your child what will happen.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a pencil line across the paper, two fingers from one edge. 2. Put the 20 crumbs along the line, mixing the sizes. 3. Put the paper next to the ant line, with the pencil line nearest the ants. 4. Drop a few sugar grains between the ants and the paper.

  *Child mode:* Put the crumbs along the line. Mix the sizes. Put the paper by the ants.
  - Note: The sugar grains lead the ants onto the paper. If no ant has reached the paper after 10 minutes, move the paper so that it touches the ant line.
- **s5 (11 min)**

  - Note: Usually the tiny crumbs go first, each carried by one ant. Rice-sized crumbs are carried by one big ant, or pulled by a team of small ants. Chana-sized and thumbnail crumbs are usually too big to move. The ants crowd on them and bite off small bits. Different kinds of ants act differently, so your results may differ.

### e2 · Damp or Dry: where does an earthworm go? (if you can get earthworms) (20 min)
- **question:** If an earthworm can choose, will they go to damp paper or dry paper?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the worms go to the damp side, the dry side, or stay in the middle? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both sides are the same paper and the same size. Both are covered, so both are equally dark. Only one thing is different: one side is damp. We use 3 or more worms, because one worm could go either way by chance.
- **safety:** Wash your hands with soap after touching soil and worms. Handle the worms gently, with wet hands or a leaf, and put them back in the soil afterwards.
- **record:** Draw the tray, the damp side and the dry side, and where each worm was after 10 minutes.
- **results table:** Worm | Where at the start | Where after 10 minutes
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put two paper towels side by side on the tray. 2. Sprinkle water on one, until damp but not dripping. 3. Leave the other dry.  
  *Parent says:* One side is damp and one is dry. Everything else is the same.  
  *Child mode:* Make one side damp. Keep the other side dry.
  - Note: A grown-up should help collect and hold the worms. Wet your hands first, because dry hands can harm a worm's skin.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess where the worms will go, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Where do you think the worms will go? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Damp side or dry side? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The damp side, because worms live in wet soil.; The dry side, because worms come out when it rains.)
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the worms gently across the middle line. 2. Cover the tray. 3. Wait 10 minutes. 4. Lift the cover and see where each worm is.  
  *Parent says:* Let's cover them, so it is dark on both sides. In ten minutes we will look.  
  *Child mode:* Cover the tray. Wait ten minutes. Then look.
  - Note: Most worms usually move to the damp side. An earthworm breathes through the skin, and the skin must stay damp to do that. If the worms stay in the middle or go to the dry side, write that down too. Worms sometimes do not move much.
  - Check: Why do earthworms choose damp places? → **They breathe through their skin, and the skin must stay damp**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the worms back on damp soil, in a pot or in the garden. Wash hands.  
  *Parent says:* Thank you, worms. Let's put them back in the soil, where they live.  
  *Child mode:* Put the worms back in the soil. Wash your hands.

### c1 · Web Builder: make a web that catches the most paper flies (25 min)
- **problem:** Can you build a thread web on a clothes hanger that catches some of the 10 paper balls thrown at it? Count the catches, change one thing, and try to catch more.
- **your turn:** Design your own web. Throw 10 paper flies and count how many it catches. Change one thing, then test again. Did it catch more?
- **constraints:** Use one clothes hanger and one reel of sewing thread, or wool.; Use cello tape to make the threads sticky, but no paper or cloth in the middle of the web.; The paper flies are 10 small balls of paper, each about the size of a grape.; Throw each fly gently from three big steps away.

- **creative:** Wind a thread round and round in a spiral over the spokes, like a real spider's web.

  - Note: Let your child try their own idea first, even if the gaps are big. The test will show what works.
- **s3 (10 min)**

  *Child mode:* Throw 10 flies. Count the catches. Change one thing. Try again.
  - Ask: What made your web catch more? (good: Putting the threads closer together.; Wrapping more sticky tape round the threads.)
  - Check: Why does a spider's web catch insects so well? → **The threads are thin, close together and sticky**

## Worksheets
### Six Legs, Eight Legs, No Legs (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (count) Look at the drawing of an ant. Count her legs. How many legs does she have?  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* An ant has three legs on each side. 3 and 3 make 6. Every insect has six legs.
2. (label) Label the ant's body: head, thorax, abdomen, antennae.  
   **Answer:** ['head: the front part, with the eyes and mouth', 'thorax: the middle part, where the six legs join', 'abdomen: the back part, the biggest', 'antennae: the two feelers on the head']. *Why:* An insect's body has three parts. The legs join the middle part, the thorax, and the antennae are on the head.
3. (sort) Sort these small creatures: six legs, or eight legs?  
   **Answer:** an ant → Six legs; a spider → Eight legs; a honeybee → Six legs; a scorpion → Eight legs; a butterfly → Six legs; a tick → Eight legs. *Why:* Ants, honeybees and butterflies are insects, with six legs. Spiders, scorpions and ticks belong to the spider group, with eight legs.
4. (draw) Remember the crumb test. Draw the four sizes of crumb on the paper, and draw what the ants did with each size.  
   **Answer:** Four sizes of crumb. Ants carrying the tiny ones, a team pulling a rice-sized one, and many ants crowded on the big ones.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the four sizes, and shows that the small crumbs were carried away and the big ones were not.
5. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: an ant has six legs and a spider has eight. Say one more way a spider's body is different from an ant's.  
   **Answer:** A spider's body has two parts, not three, and a spider has no antennae.. *Why:* A strong answer says two body parts, or no antennae. It may also say that a spider has no wings.
6. (truefalse) A butterfly drinks from a flower with a long, thin tongue, like a straw.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A butterfly uncurls her long tongue and puts the tongue into the flower to drink.
7. (truefalse) An earthworm has many tiny legs.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. An earthworm has no legs. Tiny stiff bristles on each ring of the body grip the soil, and muscles squeeze and stretch the body.
8. (match) Draw a line from each creature to the way they move.  
   **Answer:** Butterfly → flies with four big wings; Earthworm → squeezes and stretches, with no legs; Ant → walks on six legs; Snail → glides on one slimy foot. *Why:* A butterfly flies, an ant walks on six legs, an earthworm squeezes and stretches, and a snail glides on one foot.
9. (perspective) Kavya, aged 7, lives in Kochi. A thin-legged spider has made a web in the corner of her bathroom.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one view and gives a reason. The mother is right that house spiders catch mosquitoes and flies. Kavya is right that her feelings matter. The aunt finds a way that helps both Kavya and the spider.. *Why:* Common house spiders are not dangerous and they catch insects. Moving a spider gently, or leaving her, both avoid harming her.
10. (fill) Ants and honeybees live together in big families called ________.  
   **Answer:** colonies. *Why:* A colony is a big family who live together and share the work. In an ant colony, nearly all the ants are sisters.
### Looking Closer (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (odd) Three of these are insects. Which one is not?  
   Options: A mosquito / A cockroach / A spider / A ladybird beetle  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A spider has eight legs and two body parts, so a spider is not an insect. The others all have six legs.
2. (draw) Draw a spider and an insect side by side. Show the right number of legs and body parts on each.  
   **Answer:** An insect with three body parts, six legs on the middle part and two antennae. A spider with two body parts, eight legs on the front part and no antennae.. *Why:* A strong drawing gets the legs right (6 and 8), the body parts right (3 and 2), and gives antennae only to the insect.
3. (truefalse) Every spider builds a web to catch food.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Jumping spiders have no web. They creep up on their food and jump on it.
4. (order) Put these small creatures in order, from the fewest legs to the most legs.  
   **Answer:** ['an earthworm', 'an ant', 'a spider', 'a centipede']. *Why:* An earthworm has no legs, an ant has six, a spider has eight and a centipede has lots.
5. (truefalse) All the worker ants in a colony are female.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The worker ants are sisters, daughters of the queen. Male ants hatch only at some times of year, and do not do the work of the nest.
6. (mcq) Silk is a strong, thin thread made inside an animal's body. Which of these animals makes silk?  
   Options: A spider / A honeybee / A cockroach  
   **Answer:** A spider. *Why:* A spider makes silk at the back end of her body, and uses it for her web.
7. (label) Label the spider: front part, back part, legs, eyes.  
   **Answer:** ['front part: where the legs, eyes and mouth are', 'back part: the round part, where the silk comes out', 'legs: eight, all joined to the front part', 'eyes: on the top of the front part, often eight']. *Why:* A spider's body has two parts.    **Answer:** Honeybee → carries pollen from flower to flower; Earthworm → turns dead leaves into rich soil; House spider → catches mosquitoes and flies; Ant → carries away fallen crumbs and dead insects. *Why:* Bees carry pollen while they collect food, worms eat dead leaves and soil, spiders catch insects, and ants clear up crumbs and dead insects.

10. (fill) The two feelers on an insect's head are called ________.  
   **Answer:** antennae. *Why:* Antennae are the two feelers on an insect's head. Insects smell and touch with them.
### Small and Strong (challenge) (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (perspective) Lakshmi lives in a flat in Mysuru. A line of ants walks from a crack in the kitchen wall to the sugar tin.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and gives a reason. The grandmother is right that food must stay clean. Lakshmi is right that the ants are doing their own work and mean no harm. The mother's way keeps food clean without killing ants, by removing what draws them.. *Why:* Ants come where they find food. Closing tins and wiping crumbs works well, and is kinder than spray. 2. (write) Imagine an ant made as big as a dog. Would she still carry a crumb bigger than her own head? Say why. (Say it out loud, or a grown-up can write it for you.)  
   **Answer:** No. When a body gets bigger, the weight grows much faster than the strength of the muscles. A dog-sized ant would be very heavy, and would have little strength left for a load.. *Why:* A strong answer says that weight grows faster than strength as a body gets bigger. It may use numbers: twice as big each way means about 4 times the strength but about 8 times the weight.
3. (mcq) Which of these gives the best reason why a small ant can carry a big crumb?  
   Options: Ants eat a lot of sugar / A small body is very light for the strength of the muscles / Ants have more legs than people  
   **Answer:** A small body is very light for the strength of the muscles. *Why:* A small body weighs very little compared with how strong the muscles are, so there is strength left over for a load. Sugar and the number of legs do not explain it.

   **Answer:** The ants were different kinds and sizes. A big black ant is stronger than a tiny red ant, so she can carry a bigger crumb alone. 5. (draw) Draw how an earthworm moves, in three pictures: long and thin, then short and fat, then long and thin again, further along.  
   **Answer:** Three drawings of a worm. In the second, the body is short and fat. In the third, the front end has moved further forward.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the worm stretching forward, then bunching up and pulling the back along, so the whole worm moves forward.
6. (sort) Sort these: insect, spider group, or neither?  
   **Answer:** a dragonfly → Insect; a scorpion → Spider family; an earthworm → Neither; a centipede → Neither; a housefly → Insect; a tick → Spider family. *Why:* Dragonflies and houseflies have six legs, so they are insects. Scorpions and ticks have eight, so they belong to the spider group. Earthworms have no legs, and centipedes have many, so they are neither.
7. (truefalse) If an ant grew as big as a person, she could lift a car.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. When a body gets bigger, the weight grows much faster than the strength. A person-sized ant would be very heavy and would have little strength left for a load.
8. (label) Label the honeybee: antennae, wings, thorax, abdomen, legs.  
   **Answer:** ['antennae: two feelers on the head', 'wings: four, joined to the thorax', 'thorax: the middle part', 'abdomen: the striped back part', 'legs: six, joined to the thorax']. *Why:* Both the wings and the legs of an insect join the thorax, the middle part. The abdomen is at the back.
9. (odd) Three of these live in colonies and share the work. Which one usually lives alone?  
   Options: An ant / A honeybee / A termite / A house spider  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Ants, honeybees and termites live in colonies. Most spiders, like a house spider, live alone and build their own webs.
10. (write) Bumblebees in a test rolled small balls again and again, with no food as a reward. Some scientists say this is play. Others are not sure. What do you think? Give a reason. (Say it out loud, or a grown-up can write it for you.)  
   **Answer:** Any answer with a reason. For example: 'I think it is play, because they did it again and again with no food.' Or: 'Maybe they were only checking the balls, so I am not sure.'. *Why:* A strong answer gives a view and a reason from what the bees did. 5. (mcq) How does an earthworm move without legs?  
   Options: The body squeezes and stretches, and tiny bristles grip the soil / The worm rolls like a ball / The worm jumps  
   **Answer:** The body squeezes and stretches, and tiny bristles grip the soil. *Why:* Muscles make the body long and thin, then short and fat. Tiny bristles on each ring hold on to the soil, so the worm moves forward.
6. (mcq) Roshni, a girl in Kolkata, sees a butterfly standing on four legs. What is true?  
   Options: The butterfly has lost two legs / The butterfly has six legs, but keeps two folded up / Butterflies have four legs  
   **Answer:** The butterfly has six legs, but keeps two folded up. *Why:* Many butterflies keep their two small front legs folded up near the head. They still have six legs.
7. (mcq) In the crumb test, what did the ants usually do with the biggest crumbs?  
   Options: Carried them away fast / Crowded on them and bit off small bits / Ran away from them  
   **Answer:** Crowded on them and bit off small bits. *Why:* The biggest crumbs were too big for one ant to carry, so many ants crowded on them and took small bits home. If you picked 'carried them away', look again at how many big crumbs were left.
8. (mcq) Why did we put all the crumbs along one line, with the sizes mixed up?  
   Options: So every size was the same distance from the ants / So the paper looked nice / So the ants could not find them  
   **Answer:** So every size was the same distance from the ants. *Why:* Mixing the sizes along one line makes the test fair. No size was closer to the ants than another.
9. (mcq) Why can a small ant carry a load bigger than her head, when a person cannot carry a load bigger than the person?  
   Options: A small body is very light for the strength of the muscles / Ants eat more food than people / Ants have stronger bones  
   **Answer:** A small body is very light for the strength of the muscles. *Why:* A small body weighs very little compared with the strength of the muscles. Ants have no bones inside at all. Their hard skin is on the outside.
10. (mcq) What does a honeybee's dance tell her sisters?  
   Options: That she is tired / Which way to fly, and how far, to find flowers / That it will rain  
   **Answer:** Which way to fly, and how far, to find flowers. *Why:* The waggle dance shows which way to fly and how far. A bee learns where the flowers are, and tells her sisters.

## Applications
- Farmers in many parts of India keep honeybee boxes near crops like mustard, sunflower and coffee. The bees carry pollen from flower to flower, which helps the plants make seeds and fruit.
- Silk for sarees comes from silkworms, who are caterpillars of a moth. Mulberry silk is made in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, and golden muga silk in Assam.
- Vermicompost bins use earthworms to turn kitchen peels into rich soil for plants on balconies and farms.
- Engineers study how ants share a heavy load, to build small robots that carry things together.
- Scientists study spider silk, which is very strong for how thin it is, to make new strong threads.

## Spotted around us
- Huge hanging honeycombs of rock bees under water tanks and balconies of tall buildings in Bengaluru and Chennai.
- Weaver ants' nests of leaves, stitched together with silk, in mango and cashew trees in Kerala and Odisha.
- In Mayurbhanj in Odisha, some families make a chutney from red weaver ants, called kai chutney.
- Earthworms on the roads of Mumbai after the first heavy monsoon rain.
- Plain tiger butterflies drinking from flowers in a park in Hyderabad.

- A small jumping spider on a window frame in a flat in Guwahati, turning to look at you.

## Open questions
- **Why do earthworms come up out of the soil after rain?** Scientists have several ideas. The soil may have too little air when it is full of water. Or the wet ground lets worms travel far without drying out. Nobody is sure which matters most.
- **What do insects feel?** Scientists can see what insects do, and that bees seem to play. What being a bee or an ant feels like is still unknown, and scientists argue about it.
- **How many kinds of insects are there?** Scientists have named about a million kinds. Many more have never been found or named. Guesses for the real number go from about 5 million to many more.
- **Why are insects disappearing from some places?** In some countries, scientists have counted fewer insects than before. Loss of wild places, poisons sprayed on crops and changes in weather may all play a part. In many places, including much of India, nobody has counted for long enough to know.
