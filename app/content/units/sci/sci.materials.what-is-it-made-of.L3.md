# What is it made of? Where materials come from
`sci.materials.what-is-it-made-of.L3`

**Summary.** Pick up anything and follow it backwards, and you always reach something nature made. A steel spoon began as rock dug from a mine. A plastic bucket began as oil deep underground. Some things are natural, like a stone, a mango or a crow's feather. Other things are made by people, but never from nothing. People take natural raw materials and change them by cutting, spinning, melting, baking and mixing. Your child makes paper, salt, milk plastic, plant colours and a clay diya with their own hands. Then they zoom in: natural and made things are built from the same few kinds of atoms, and those atoms were made long ago inside stars.

**Intro.** *Pick up anything near you. A pencil, a spoon, a button. Now ask it a question. Where were you before you were this? And before that? How far back can you go?* The wood of your pencil was once a tree. Its grey middle is graphite, a mineral dug out of the ground, mixed with clay. The tree drank rain that fell from clouds, and most of that rain came from the sea. Follow any object backwards, and you always end up at the Earth. In this unit you become a material detective. You trace things back to where they came from. You find out which things nature made and which things people made. Then you make a few materials yourself, from natural ones.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what an object is made of, and trace its material back to where it came from: a plant, an animal, the ground, the sea or the air.
1. I can tell natural things from things made by people, and explain tricky ones like a wooden chair, a cotton kurta or a potted plant.
2. I can describe how people change natural raw materials into made materials, by cutting, spinning, melting, baking, mixing and heating.
3. I can make a made material from a natural one, like paper, salt, milk plastic, a dye or a clay diya. I can say what I think, what I saw and why.
4. I can explain that plants and animals make materials too, and that some made materials are copies of natural ones.
5. I can explain that natural and made things are built from the same kinds of atoms, and that those atoms were made long ago in stars.
6. I can say what scientists agree on and still argue about, and name some of the hidden people who dig, grow, spin and make our things.

## Terms
- **Object**: A thing you can see, touch and use. A tumbler, a chair and a kite are all objects. *e.g.* A spoon is an object. You can hold it and eat with it.
- **Material**: What an object is made of. One material can make many objects. One object can be made of many materials. *e.g.* A tumbler can be made of steel, glass or plastic. A school bag is cloth, plastic and metal together.
- **Natural**: Made by nature, not by people. A natural thing can be living, like a tree or a bird. It can be non-living, like a rock, water or air. *e.g.* A neem tree, a pebble and rainwater are all natural.
- **Made by people (artificial)**: Made by people from other materials. Scientists and books also call it artificial, human-made or manufactured. *e.g.* A steel spoon, a brick, a plastic bucket and a cotton kurta are all made by people.
- **Raw material**: A natural material before people change it. It is what people start with. *e.g.* Cotton fluff is the raw material for cotton thread. Clay is the raw material for a brick.
- **Source**: The place a material comes from. *e.g.* The source of wool is a sheep. The source of most of India's salt is sea water.
- **Ore**: A rock with a metal hidden inside it. People dig it out of the ground and heat it until the metal comes out. *e.g.* Iron ore is a reddish rock. It is dug from mines in Odisha, Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh.
- **Plant fibre**: A long, thin thread that grows inside or on a plant. People spin plant fibres into thread and rope. *e.g.* Cotton, jute and coir from coconut husks are plant fibres.
- **Spin**: To twist short fibres together into one long, strong thread. *e.g.* A charkha, a spinning wheel, twists cotton fluff into thread.
- **Weave**: To cross threads over and under each other to make cloth. *e.g.* A weaver on a handloom weaves thread into a sari.
- **Kiln**: A very hot oven for baking clay. Baking makes soft clay into hard pottery and bricks. *e.g.* Bricks are baked in a brick kiln with a tall chimney.
- **Furnace**: A very hot fire inside a closed chamber. It is hot enough to melt sand into glass, or ore into metal. *e.g.* Glass bangles in Firozabad are made from glass melted in furnaces.
- **Petroleum (crude oil)**: A thick, dark oil found deep under the ground and the sea floor. It formed over millions of years from tiny living things in the sea. Most plastic is made from it, and from natural gas. *e.g.* Petrol for scooters and the plastic of a bucket both come from petroleum.
- **Plastic**: A made material that can be shaped when it is soft and warm, then sets hard. Most plastic is made from petroleum and natural gas. *e.g.* Buckets, toothbrushes and pens are usually plastic.
- **Synthetic**: Made by people through chemistry, by joining atoms into new patterns. Many synthetic materials are not found in nature at all. *e.g.* Nylon and polyester are synthetic fibres. Cotton and silk are natural fibres.
- **Process**: The steps people use to change a raw material into something useful. *e.g.* Clay is dug, mixed with water, shaped, dried and baked. That is the process for making a pot.
- **Dye**: A coloured material that soaks into cloth and colours it. *e.g.* Turmeric dyes cloth yellow. Indigo dyes it blue.
- **Recycle**: To break down an old made thing and use its material to make a new thing. *e.g.* Old newspapers can be soaked, mashed and made into new paper.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see with an ordinary microscope. Only very special microscopes can show them. *e.g.* A wooden spoon, a plastic spoon and your hand are all made of atoms.
- **Element**: A material made of only one kind of atom. About 90 kinds of atom are found in nature, so there are about 90 natural elements. Every material in the world is made from them. *e.g.* Carbon, oxygen, iron and gold are elements.
- **Carbon**: An element found in every living thing. Wood, cotton, sugar, plastic and you are full of carbon atoms. *e.g.* The grey core of a pencil is graphite, which is a form of carbon, mixed with clay.
- **Stardust**: A name for the atoms that were made inside stars long ago and spread into space. The atoms in you and your things are stardust. *e.g.* Most of the oxygen and carbon in your body was made inside stars that exploded or puffed off their outer layers before the Sun was born.
- **Decompose**: To be broken down slowly by tiny living things, like bacteria and fungi, back into soil and air. *e.g.* A fallen leaf decomposes into soil in a few months.

## Explanations
### Every object is made of a material · *defined*
**Definition:** An object is a thing. A material is what it is made of. The same material can make many objects, and one object can be made of many materials.

Ask 'What is it?' and you get the object. Ask 'What is it made of?' and you get the material. A tumbler is an object. Steel is its material. Many objects are made of more than one material. Each part has its own job. A pressure cooker has a metal body, a rubber ring and a plastic handle. The unit on Properties of materials explores why each part needs its own material.
- e.g. A wooden spoon and a wooden door are made of the same material.
- e.g. A pencil is wood outside. Inside is graphite, a form of carbon, mixed with clay.
- e.g. A school bag is cloth, a plastic zip and a metal buckle.
### Where materials come from · *defined*
**Definition:** Every material comes from somewhere on Earth. Most come from plants, from animals, or from the ground. Some come from the sea or even the air.

Follow any material back far enough, and you reach a source in nature. Wood, cotton, jute, rubber and paper come from plants. Wool, silk, leather, honey and lac come from animals. Stone, clay, sand, metals and oil come from the ground. Most of India's salt comes from sea water. Some salt, like sendha namak, is rock salt dug out of the ground. Even the air is a source. Factories take nitrogen out of the air to make fertiliser for farms. Nothing that people make comes from nowhere.
- e.g. Paper comes from trees, or from old paper.
- e.g. Glass comes mostly from sand.
- e.g. A rubber band comes from the milky sap of a rubber tree, grown in Kerala and the north-east.
- e.g. A silk sari comes from the cocoon of a silkworm.
### Natural, and made by people · *defined*
**Definition:** Natural things are made by nature. They can be living, like a tree, or non-living, like a rock or water. Things made by people are called artificial, or human-made.

A mango, a crow, a pebble and the wind are natural. Nobody made them. A bicycle, a brick and a plastic bottle are made by people. Here is the tricky part. Every made thing is made from natural things. A wooden chair is made by people, from a natural material, wood. So we ask two questions. First, what is the material? Second, did people make this thing from it?
- e.g. A cotton boll on the plant is natural. A cotton kurta is made by people from it.
- e.g. A potted plant is natural. The pot is made by people.
- e.g. A concrete road is made by people, from cement, sand and stones. Cement is made from natural limestone and clay.
### How people change natural materials · *defined*
**Definition:** People change raw materials by cutting, shaping, spinning, weaving, melting, baking, mixing and heating. The steps are called a process.

Some materials are changed only a little. A carpenter cuts and shapes wood, but it is still wood. Others are changed a lot. Clay baked in a kiln becomes hard pottery that never turns back into clay. Sand melted in a furnace becomes glass. Ore heated with charcoal or coke gives out metal. Petroleum is changed in a factory into tiny plastic beads. These are melted and pressed into buckets.
- e.g. Cotton is picked, cleaned, spun into thread and woven into cloth.
- e.g. Clay is dug, mixed with water, shaped, dried and baked into a matka.
- e.g. Sand is mixed with soda ash and limestone, melted, and blown or pressed into glass.
### Is anything really unnatural? · *debated*
Everyone agrees on the easy cases. A pebble is natural, and a plastic bucket is made by people. But where exactly is the line? Thinkers give different answers.
- e.g. Plastic is made from oil, and oil formed from tiny living things in the sea that died millions of years ago.
- e.g. A beaver's dam is built by an animal. A farmer's mud dam is built by people. Are both natural?
- **The everyday view, used in most school books:** If people made it or changed it a lot, it is made by people, or artificial. Otherwise it is natural.
- **Chemists:** What matters is the substance, not who made it. Indigo dye made in a factory is the very same substance as indigo from the plant. So a material can be both made by people and exactly like a natural one.
- **Some philosophers and ecologists:** People are animals and part of nature. Birds build nests and termites build towers. So perhaps a city is as natural as an anthill. Others reply that people change the Earth far more than any other animal, so the word 'artificial' still helps.
### Animals make materials too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals make materials. Some grow them on their bodies, and some build with them.

A sheep grows wool, and people only cut it. A silkworm, the caterpillar of a moth, spins a cocoon of one long silk thread. Bees make wax for their honeycomb. In Jharkhand and nearby states, tiny lac insects on trees make a resin. People scrape it off to make bangles, varnish and sealing wax. Spiders spin silk that is very strong for its weight. Paper wasps chew wood and spit it out as paper for their nests. In 1719 a French scientist, René Réaumur, watched these wasps. He wrote that people could learn to make paper from wood, as wasps do. In the 1840s, more than 120 years later, people in Germany and Canada did.
- e.g. Wool from sheep, silk from silkworms, honey and wax from bees.
- e.g. Lac from lac insects makes the bright lac bangles of Rajasthan.
- e.g. A weaver bird weaves its nest from strips of grass.
### Plants are built of a material called cellulose · *defined*
**Definition:** Cellulose is the material that the walls of plant cells are made of. Wood, cotton, jute, coir and paper are mostly cellulose.

Every plant is made of tiny boxes called cells. The walls of these cells are made of cellulose. It is made of long chains of sugar joined end to end. Cotton is almost pure cellulose. Wood is cellulose fibres glued together by another plant material, called lignin. Scientists think cellulose is the most common material made by living things on Earth. When you make paper in this unit, you are tangling cellulose fibres into a mat.
- e.g. Cotton fluff is nearly pure cellulose.
- e.g. Paper is a mat of cellulose fibres pressed flat.
- e.g. Cows can digest grass because tiny living things in their stomach break cellulose down.
### Materials from deep in the ground: metals, sand, clay and oil · *defined*
**Definition:** Metals come from ores, rocks with metal inside. Glass comes from sand. Bricks and pots come from clay. Most plastic comes from petroleum and natural gas.

Metals hide inside ores. People dig the ore out of mines and heat it in a furnace until the metal comes out. Most steel is iron with a little carbon. A steel tumbler or spoon is stainless steel, which also has chromium mixed in, so it does not rust. Clay is a kind of soil with very tiny grains. It becomes sticky when wet, and hard when baked. Petroleum is a thick, dark oil from deep underground. It formed over millions of years from tiny living things in ancient seas. They were buried in mud and slowly squeezed and warmed. So most plastic is made from the remains of living things that died millions of years ago.
- e.g. Bauxite, a reddish ore from Odisha and other states, gives aluminium for pressure cookers and foil.
- e.g. Sand from riverbeds and quarries is melted into glass.
- e.g. A plastic bucket started as oil pumped up from deep underground or under the sea.
- e.g. A 'tin' can is mostly steel, with a very thin coat of tin to stop it rusting.
### The first made materials that copied nature · *defined*
**Definition:** Some made materials were invented to copy natural ones, or to take their place. Often they are made by chemistry, so they are called synthetic.

In 1862 Alexander Parkes, in England, made one of the first plastics from cotton cellulose. In 1907 Leo Baekeland, in the United States, made Bakelite. It was the first plastic made completely from chemicals, not from plants or animals. Old black telephones and radio cases were made of it. In 1935 Wallace Carothers and his team made nylon. People used it in place of silk for stockings and ropes. In 1856 an English teenager, William Perkin, made the first synthetic dye by accident. He was trying to make a medicine.
- e.g. Nylon took the place of silk for many things.
- e.g. Rayon is made from wood pulp. It was once called 'artificial silk'.
- e.g. Lab-grown diamonds are real diamond, made by people from carbon.
### Blue from a plant, blue from a factory: the story of indigo · *defined*
**Definition:** Indigo is a blue dye. It used to come only from plants. Since 1897 it has also been made in factories, and the factory indigo is the very same substance.

For thousands of years, India was famous for indigo. It was made from the leaves of the indigo plant. In the 1800s, British planters forced many farmers in Bengal and Bihar to grow indigo instead of food. The farmers were paid very little. In 1859 and 1860, indigo farmers in Bengal rose up against the planters. In 1878 a German chemist, Adolf von Baeyer, worked out how to make indigo from chemicals. In 1897 a German company began selling factory indigo. It was cheaper, so the trade in plant indigo fell sharply. In 1917, a farmer called Rajkumar Shukla persuaded Gandhi to come to Champaran in Bihar. He came to support the indigo farmers. Today almost all the blue in jeans is factory indigo.
- e.g. Blue jeans are dyed with indigo.
- e.g. Some craftspeople in India still dye with plant indigo.
### Zoom in: the same tiny bricks build everything · *defined*
**Definition:** Everything, natural or made, is built from atoms. There are about 90 kinds of atom found in nature, called elements.

Imagine a box of building blocks with about 90 colours. With them you can build anything at all. Atoms work like that. Wood, cotton and sugar are made mostly of three elements: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Most plastics are made mostly of carbon and hydrogen. Things are different because their atoms are joined in different patterns. A diamond and the graphite in your pencil are both carbon, joined in different ways. When people make a material in a kitchen or a factory, they never make new atoms. They only move atoms around and join them in new ways.
- e.g. A wooden spoon and a plastic spoon are both built from carbon and hydrogen atoms. The wood also has many oxygen atoms. Most plastic has few or none.
- e.g. Diamond and graphite are both made of carbon atoms, joined in different patterns.
- e.g. Water is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom.
### Where atoms come from: made in stars · *debated*
Scientists agree on most of this story. Right after the universe began, there was almost only hydrogen and helium, the two lightest elements. Stars are giant furnaces. Inside them, light atoms are squeezed together into heavier ones, like carbon, oxygen and iron. When big stars die, they blow these atoms out into space. New stars and planets, like our Sun and Earth, form from that dust. So the iron in your spoon was made in a star. The open question is about the heaviest atoms, like gold.
- e.g. The carbon in your pencil was made inside a star.
- e.g. The iron in your blood and in a kadhai was made in stars.
- **The older textbook idea:** Gold and other heavy atoms were made mostly in supernovas, the giant explosions of dying big stars.
- **Many scientists since 2017:** In 2017, telescopes watched two dead stars, called neutron stars, crash together. The crash threw out heavy atoms, perhaps including gold. So many scientists now think crashes like this make much of the gold.
- **Other scientists:** Crashes alone may not explain all the gold, especially in the oldest stars. Some rare kinds of exploding star may make it too. The answer is still being worked out.
### Hidden hands: the people inside our things · *defined*
**Definition:** Every made thing passes through many people's hands, from the field or the mine to the shop.

Think of a cotton kurta. A farmer grew the cotton, often in great heat. Pickers picked it by hand. Workers in a mill cleaned and spun it. A weaver wove it. A tailor stitched it. Drivers carried it at every step. Most of these people are never named. Some were paid very little. Long ago, the cloth of Bengal, called Dhaka muslin, was so fine it was famous across the world. It was spun by hand from a special cotton plant. The skill was lost in the 1800s. Today people in Bangladesh are trying to bring it back. From the 1920s, Gandhi asked Indians to spin their own thread on a charkha and wear khadi. He said that if Indians made their own cloth, they would not need cloth from British mills.
- e.g. A steel spoon passed through the hands of miners, furnace workers and factory workers.
- e.g. Recycled paper and plastic in India are often collected by kabadiwalas and waste pickers, many of them women.
### People are now changing the Earth's materials · *debated*
In 2020, scientists in Israel added up the weight of everything people have made. That means buildings, roads, machines and plastic. They found it now weighs about as much as all the living things on Earth put together. In 1900 it weighed only about 3 parts for every 100 parts of living things. Scientists have also found a new kind of rock on a beach in Hawaii, made of melted plastic, sand and stones. So some scientists asked: should we name a new age of the Earth after people?
- e.g. New rocks of melted plastic and stone, called plastiglomerate, were described in 2014.
- e.g. Concrete is the made material people use most. Only water is used more.
- **Many scientists:** People now change the Earth so much that we live in a new age. Some call it the Anthropocene, which means 'the age of humans'.
- **The scientists who officially name Earth's ages, in 2024:** They voted not to name the Anthropocene as an official age, for now. Some said the start date was not clear. Others said the idea is still useful, even if it is not official.

## Mostly agreed
- Every material comes from a source on Earth: plants, animals, the ground, the sea or the air. *How we know:* Every material we know can be traced back to one of these. People have followed these journeys in mines, fields and factories for thousands of years.
- Made things are made from natural raw materials, changed by people. *How we know:* You can watch it happen. Clay becomes a pot in a kiln. Cotton becomes thread on a charkha. Old paper becomes new paper in your own kitchen.
- In everyday making, like cooking, melting and mixing, people do not make new atoms. They join atoms in new ways. *How we know:* Chemists have weighed what goes into and comes out of their work for over 200 years. In the 1770s and 1780s the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier, helped by Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, showed that in a chemical change the total weight stays the same.
- A made substance can be exactly the same as a natural one. *How we know:* Chemists have shown that factory indigo and plant indigo are the very same substance, built from the same atoms in the same pattern. Lab-grown and mined diamonds are both carbon atoms in the same pattern.
- Apart from hydrogen and most of the helium, the atoms around us were made in stars. *How we know:* Telescopes split the light of stars into colours, and each element leaves its own pattern of colours. These patterns show which atoms each star holds. Old stars have fewer heavy atoms than young stars, which shows that heavy atoms have built up over time.

## How we found out
**Antoine Lavoisier, 1768 to 1770.** For a long time, many people believed that water could slowly turn into earth. When water was boiled for many days in a glass pot, a little white powder appeared at the bottom. Where else could it come from? In 1768, a young French chemist, Antoine Lavoisier, decided to test the idea by weighing. He used a closed glass vessel called a pelican. He weighed it empty, then filled it with very pure water and sealed it. Then he kept the water hot, just below boiling, for 101 days. The powder did appear. But when he weighed everything again, the total weight had not changed. The empty vessel was now lighter, by about the weight of the powder. So the powder had not been made from water. It was glass, slowly worn away from the inside of the vessel. He reported this in 1770. Nothing new had been made. The material had only moved from one place to another.  
*What people believed before:* Water can be turned into earth by boiling it for a long time. Lavoisier's weighing showed that the powder came from the glass vessel, not from the water.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Natural things are always safe and good. Made things are always harmful.** (A common belief, often used in adverts; *rejected*)  
  For: Some made things, like plastic waste and some chemicals, do harm living things.  
  Against: Many natural things are poisonous, like dhatura seeds or snake venom. Asbestos is a natural rock fibre that harms lungs. Many made things save lives, like medicines and clean water pipes. We have to ask about each thing, not about where it came from.
- **Plastic is completely unnatural. It has nothing to do with nature.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: Plastic is made in factories, by people, through chemistry. Nothing like most plastics is found in nature.  
  Against: Plastic is made from petroleum and natural gas. These formed from tiny living things in ancient seas. So plastic is made by people, but from a natural raw material. Its atoms are ordinary carbon and hydrogen.
- **Cotton cloth was first made in England, where the big cotton mills were.** (A common guess, because England's cotton mills were so famous; *rejected*)  
  For: England's mills made huge amounts of cotton cloth in the 1800s and sold it all over the world.  
  Against: Some of the oldest cotton ever found comes from Mehrgarh, in present-day Pakistan. Cotton fibres were preserved inside a copper bead more than 7,000 years old. Cotton cloth was made in the Indus Valley more than 4,000 years ago. For centuries, the finest cotton cloth in the world came from India.
- **A diamond made in a laboratory is a fake diamond.** (Some jewellery sellers and many shoppers; *rejected*)  
  For: Mined diamonds took millions or billions of years to form deep in the Earth. Lab diamonds take only weeks.  
  Against: A lab diamond is made of carbon atoms in exactly the same pattern as a mined diamond. Experts need special machines to tell them apart. It is a real diamond that was made by people. A fake would be glass or another stone pretending to be a diamond.
- **Recycling solves the problem of made materials we throw away.** (A common belief; *debated*)  
  For: Recycling turns old paper, metal and some plastic into new things, and saves raw materials.  
  Against: In 2017, three scientists, Roland Geyer, Jenna Jambeck and Kara Lavender Law, counted all the plastic waste ever made. Only about 9 parts in every 100 had been recycled. Most was buried or ended up on land and in the sea. Scientists agree recycling helps, but most say using less and reusing more matter too.
- **People are separate from nature.** (A common way of talking: 'nature' means places without people; *debated*)  
  For: People change the Earth far more than any other animal. Cities, plastic and new rocks look very different from forests.  
  Against: People are living things, made of the same atoms as every other living thing. We breathe, eat and die like other animals. Many Adivasi and other traditions have long said that people belong to nature, not outside it. Scientists and philosophers still argue about how best to say this.

## Roots
We have been learning where every material comes from, and how people change natural materials. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The salt on your plate was in the water before the sun dried it. The sun took the water into the air and left the salt behind. Paper, steel and plastic were also something else first. So people never make a material from nothing: they always change one that was already there.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say people 'make' paper, plastic or salt, as if they made the material itself. But making is always changing something that was already there. 'Made by people' tells us what people did, not where the material began.  
*Evidence:* In the salt experiment, salt appeared only because the water left. In the Journey Backwards, every chain ended at something nature made. In the paper experiment, old paper became new paper.

- *who am i:* Your body is built from atoms in the food you eat. Most of those atoms were made inside stars, long ago.
- *what is life:* Living things are makers too. Silkworms make silk, bees make wax and paper wasps make paper.
- *what is this world:* In this world, nothing is made from nothing. Every made thing is the Earth's material, changed by someone's work.

A steel spoon, a plastic bucket and a mango look as if they have nothing in common. Follow each one back, and they all lead to the same few kinds of atoms, made in stars. DeepKids believes that everything comes from one source. This unit's science shows something like it on a small scale. About 90 kinds of atom take the form of salt, clay, silk and you. When people make things, the same atoms simply take one more form.

- **Mundaka Upanishad 1.1.7:** A spider spins its thread out of itself. Plants grow up out of the earth. In the same way, everything comes out of the one unchanging source. (In this unit, spiders and silkworms spin silk out of their own bodies, and every material we traced came out of the Earth. The Upanishad uses the same picture for the whole world: it all comes out of one source.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper. You can see the cloud, the rain and the tree in it. Take them away, and there is no paper. (In the paper experiment, you saw plant fibres at the torn edge of paper. Those fibres grew in a tree that needed rain and sun. So the paper really does carry the tree, the rain and the sun in its story.)
- Science (settled): When people cook, melt or mix materials, no atoms are made or lost. They are only rearranged.
- Science (settled): Almost every atom in your body, apart from hydrogen, was made inside a star.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of India: Charvaka taught that everything is made of four elements: earth, water, fire and air. Even awareness comes from them, and it ends when the body ends.
- Practice, Look deeply (3 min): Let's pick up one thing near us, maybe a sheet of paper or a cotton cloth. Hold it and look at it quietly for a minute. Now let's close our eyes and picture where it came from. A tree, or a cotton plant. The rain that fed it. The sun. The people who made it. Breathe slowly. Open your eyes. Is it still just paper?
- Ask: If people only change what the Earth gives, is anything really unnatural?
- Ask: When people make a material, what exactly do they make?
- Ask: The atoms in your body were made in stars. In what way are you part of the stars' story?

## Deep and big questions

- *big:* If every made thing is made from natural things, is anything really unnatural? (hint: Is a bird's nest natural? Is a house? What is the difference?)
- *deep:* The atoms in your body were made in stars. Does that make you a little bit of a star? (hint: Your iron, your carbon and your oxygen were all made in stars long ago.)
- *big:* Who owns the sea's salt, the river's sand, or the iron in a hill? (hint: Think of the people who live near them, the people who dig them, and the people who sell them.)
- *deep:* When people make a material, they make no new atoms. So what exactly do they make? (hint: Think of building blocks. Is a tower made of something new, or of the same blocks in a new pattern?)
- *big:* Your kurta passed through many hands before it reached you. Should we know their names? (hint: Who grew it, spun it, wove it and stitched it?)
- *big:* Is a made copy of a natural thing worth less than the natural one? (hint: Think of factory indigo, a lab diamond, or a photo of a painting.)
- *deep:* Animals like wasps and silkworms make materials. What else might we learn from them? (hint: Wasps were making paper long before people learned to make it from wood.)
- *big:* Should we choose things made from materials that can go back to the Earth? (hint: What happens to a clay kulhad after you drink chai from it? What happens to a plastic cup?)
- *big:* Where is 'away', when we throw something away? (hint: Every material has to go somewhere.)
- *deep:* Could people one day make materials from things in space, like rocks on the Moon? (hint: Moon dust has oxygen and metals in it. Some space agencies are testing ways to use it.)

## Story: The Salt in the Stone Hollow
*The Salt March is real. From 12 March to 6 April 1930, Gandhi and 78 marchers walked about 385 kilometres from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi, on the coast of Gujarat. At Dandi he picked up a lump of salty mud, breaking the British law that forbade Indians to collect or make salt. Accounts say salt deposits on the shore had been trampled into the mud before he arrived. In the weeks after, people across India, many of them women, made salt. Kashi, her grandmother and the stone hollows are made up.*

In the village where Kashi lived, on the coast of Gujarat, the sea came in twice a day and went out twice a day. Each time it went, it left something behind.

Kashi's grandmother, whom everyone called Ba, knew a place among the black rocks where the sea left the most. There were little hollows in the stone, round as cupped hands. At high tide the sea filled them. At low tide the water stayed behind, warm and still, while the sun stared down at it.

'Don't go to the hollows,' Ba told her. 'Not this month. The salt man is walking the shore.'

The salt man wore a khaki uniform and carried a stick. His job was to make sure nobody took the salt. In 1930 the law said that salt belonged to the British government. Only the government could collect it or sell it, and it added a tax to the price. Kashi's family bought salt from the shop in a twist of paper. It cost more than it should, for something so plain.

Kashi had always imagined that the government made the salt. She pictured a great building somewhere, with chimneys and fires and machines, pouring out white grains. That was why it cost money. That was why it was theirs.

Then the news came along the road, from village to village, faster than any bus. An old man was walking from Ahmedabad to the sea. He had nearly eighty people walking with him, and every day more joined. He was coming to make salt.

In the village, people laughed. 'Make salt?' said the shopkeeper. 'With what? He has no factory. He has a walking stick and a shawl.' Kashi laughed too, a little. You could not make salt by walking. You needed fire and machines.

But that afternoon, when the salt man had gone home for his lunch, Kashi slipped down to the black rocks.

She knelt beside the biggest hollow. The water in it was low, much lower than at high tide. All around its edge there was a white rim, like frost on a winter window. She touched it. It was crisp. She touched her finger to her tongue. Salt.

Kashi sat there a long time. The sun was hot on her neck. She watched the water. She could not see it moving, but she knew it was going somewhere. Each time she looked, the white rim had crept a little further in. Tiny white squares were growing on the surface, like boats, and sinking, and new ones growing.

Nobody was making this salt.

There was no chimney and no machine. There was only the sea, and the sun, and the stone. The salt had been in the sea all along, hidden in the water the way sugar hides in tea. The sun took the water away into the air, a little at a time. What was left behind was salt. All a person had to do was wait, and then pick it up.

Kashi stood up so fast that her knees cracked. If nobody made salt, then nobody had a right to sell it as if they had made it. The government had not made the sea. It had not made the sun. It had only put a man with a stick between the people and the shore.

She ran home and told Ba. Ba did not laugh. She only nodded slowly, as if she had known it for seventy years and had been waiting for someone to say it out loud.

The marchers reached Dandi, further down the coast, at the beginning of April. Kashi's father walked there, and Kashi walked beside him, and half the village came too. The shore was crowded. There were people sitting on the dunes, and people standing in the shallow water, and people singing.

Early on the morning of the sixth of April, after a prayer, the old man walked down to the shore. Kashi could see that the white crust had been broken up and trampled into the mud by many boots. Someone had tried to hide the salt.

The old man bent down. He picked up a lump of salty mud in his hand and held it up.

That was all. It was so small. A lump of mud. Somebody near Kashi breathed out, and somebody else began to cry. Kashi understood. He had not made salt. Nobody could make salt. He had only picked up what the sea had left for everyone.

In the weeks that followed, the salt men could not walk every shore. Women carried brass plates and clay pots down to the water in towns and villages all along the coast. They filled them with sea water and set them in the sun on their rooftops. Kashi's mother did it. Ba did it, sitting on the rocks with her sari tucked up, not hiding at all.

Kashi set out a plate of her own, on the flat stone by their door. All day she watched it. The water shrank. The white rim crept inwards. By the evening the plate was a ring of salt, glittering, like a small full moon that had come down to stay.

She did not sell it. She gave a pinch to everyone who passed.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn of the story is Kashi's discovery that salt is a natural material. People do not make it. The sea already holds it, and the sun takes away the water, leaving the salt behind. People only collect it. This is the heart of the unit: made things are natural things that people collect and change, and nobody makes a material from nothing. The story also asks who can own a natural material, and shows the many ordinary people, many of them women, who made salt in 1930.

- Ask: At first, where did Kashi think salt came from? What did she find out at the stone hollow?
- Ask: Where was the salt before it appeared on the rim of the hollow?
- Ask: The old man picked up a lump of salty mud. Why did that small thing matter so much?
- Ask: Is salt from the sea natural, or made by people? What do people do to it?
- Ask: Who do you think should own the salt in the sea?

- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do the same with a fresh cup of hot milk and 2 teaspoons of vinegar. 2. Then with a third cup and 4 teaspoons. 3. Pour each bowl through the strainer. 4. Count how many teaspoons of lumps each bowl gave.  
  *Parent says:* The white lumps are a material called casein. It was hidden in the milk all along, and the vinegar makes it come out. Now, same milk, same warmth, same stirring. Only the vinegar changes. Which bowl gives the most lumps?  
  *Child mode:* Try again with 2 spoons of vinegar. Then 4 spoons. Strain each bowl. Count the spoons of lumps.
  - Note: This is how paneer is made too, with lemon juice. The lumps are casein, a natural material from milk, with some milk fat. Let the lumps cool before your child touches them.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press the cooled lumps between paper towels to squeeze out the water. 2. Knead them like dough. 3. Press them into a shape or a cookie cutter. 4. Leave the shapes to dry for 2 days.  
  *Parent says:* Now shape your plastic. As it dries, it will go hard.  
  *Child mode:* Squeeze the lumps dry in paper towels. Knead them. Press them into a shape. Leave them to dry and harden.
  - Check: Is milk plastic natural, or made by people? → **Made by people, from a natural material in milk**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 2 days, feel the shapes. Tap one on the table.  
  *Parent says:* About 125 years ago, factories in Germany and France made a hard plastic like this from milk. They called it Galalith, which means 'milk stone'. People made buttons, combs and fountain pens from it.  
  *Child mode:* Feel your shape. Is it hard now? Long ago, factories made buttons from milk plastic like this!
  - Note: Galalith was invented in 1897 and made into buttons, buckles and jewellery for decades. It soaks up water and can crack, so most factories later switched to plastics made from oil. If your shapes are still soft after 2 days, leave them 2 more days. Toned milk gives a harder plastic than full-cream milk, because fat keeps it soft.
  - Think: Today most plastic is made from oil. Why do you think factories stopped making plastic from milk?
  - Wait: Leave the shapes to dry for 2 days.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Before we finish, tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: We can make a plastic from milk, and more vinegar made more lumps, up to a point. What I saw: 1 teaspoon gave a few lumps. 4 teaspoons gave the most. The shapes went hard after 2 days. Why: Milk has a natural material called casein hidden in it. Vinegar made it come out in lumps. When it dried, it set hard, like plastic.

### p1 · From Soil to Diya (60 min, 5 days)
- **safety:** Dig only where a grown-up says you may. Wear old clothes. Wash hands after handling soil. A grown-up lights any diya, and never leaves a lit diya alone.

## Worksheets
### Material Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A steel tumbler. Which word names its material?  
   Options: Tumbler / Steel / Kitchen / Round  
   **Answer:** Steel. *Why:* Steel is what the tumbler is made of, so it is the material. 'Tumbler' is the object's name. Picking 'tumbler' usually means mixing up the object and the material. Ask: if you broke it into tiny pieces, what would the pieces be?
2. (sort) Sort these. Natural, or made by people?  
   **Answer:** a pebble → Natural; a glass bangle → Made by people; a mango → Natural; a brick → Made by people; a crow's feather → Natural; a plastic bucket → Made by people; rainwater → Natural; a steel spoon → Made by people. *Why:* Nature made the pebble, the mango, the feather and the rain. People made the bangle, brick, bucket and spoon, from natural materials.
3. (match) Match each material to its source.  
   **Answer:** Cotton → a plant; Wool → a sheep; Glass → sand; Salt → the sea; Iron → ore from a mine. *Why:* Every material comes from somewhere in nature: a plant, an animal, the ground or the sea.
4. (mcq) Where does most plastic come from?  
   Options: From petroleum, an oil deep underground / From trees / From nothing: it is invented in factories / From sand  
   **Answer:** From petroleum, an oil deep underground. *Why:* Most plastic is made from petroleum and natural gas, which formed from ancient sea life. Picking 'from nothing' usually means stopping the journey at the factory. Factories change materials. They never make a material from nothing.
5. (fill) A natural material before people change it is called a ____ material.  
   **Answer:** ['raw']. *Why:* Raw materials are what people start with, like cotton fluff, clay or iron ore.
6. (truefalse) A natural thing can be living or non-living.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A tree and a bird are natural and living. A rock and water are natural and non-living.
7. (mcq) A wooden chair. Which is right?  
   Options: It is natural, because wood is natural / It is made by people, from a natural material / It is made by people, from a made material / It is not made of anything  
   **Answer:** It is made by people, from a natural material. *Why:* Wood is a natural material, but a carpenter cut and joined it into a chair. So the chair is made by people from a natural material. Picking 'natural' means looking only at the material, not at what people did with it.
8. (match) Match each raw material to what people do to it.  
   **Answer:** Cotton fluff → spin it into thread; Clay → bake it in a kiln; Sand → melt it in a furnace into glass; Sea water → dry it in the sun to get salt. *Why:* People change raw materials in different ways: spinning, baking, melting and drying.
9. (mcq) Which of these comes from an animal?  
   Options: Silk / Jute / Coir / Rubber  
   **Answer:** Silk. *Why:* Silk comes from the cocoon of a silkworm, a moth's caterpillar. Jute, coir and rubber all come from plants. Picking 'rubber' is a common mix-up, because many people do not know where rubber comes from. It comes from the milky sap of a tree.
10. (fill) Most of India's salt comes from the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['sea']. *Why:* Sea water has salt hidden in it. In salt pans, the sun dries the water away and the salt is left.
11. (mcq) In the salt experiment, where did the salt come from?  
   Options: The sun made it / It was hidden in the water all along / The plate made it / It fell from the air  
   **Answer:** It was hidden in the water all along. *Why:* The salt was dissolved in the water from the start. The sun only took the water away into the air. Picking 'the sun made it' is a very common idea. The sun helps us collect salt, but it does not make it.
12. (truefalse) Paper is made from plant fibres.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Paper is a flat mat of tangled fibres, usually from trees. You can see them at a torn edge with a magnifier.
13. (write) Choose one object in your home. Write its journey backwards, from the shop to nature, in at least three steps.  
   **Answer:** For example: my cotton hanky. Before the shop, it was woven from thread in a mill. Before that, the thread was spun from cotton fluff. Before that, the cotton grew on a plant in a field.. *Why:* Any journey that ends in nature, with at least three correct steps, is right.
14. (write) Is a cotton kurta natural, or made by people? Explain your answer.  
   **Answer:** It is made by people, from a natural material. The cotton grew on a plant, but people spun it, wove it and stitched it.. *Why:* A good answer names both the natural material and what people did to it.
15. (draw) Draw one thing that nature made and one thing that people made. Label what each is made of.  
   **Answer:** For example: a leaf, made of plant material, and a steel spoon, made of metal from iron ore.. *Why:* Any correct pair with labels is right.
### Journeys and Makers (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (order) Put the journey of a cotton kurta in order, from nature to you.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Cotton grows on a plant. The fluff is picked, spun into thread, woven into cloth and stitched by a tailor.
2. (order) Put the journey of a steel spoon in order, from nature to you.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* The iron is in ore in the ground. Miners dig it out. A furnace melts out the iron. The iron is made into steel, and for a steel spoon some chromium is mixed in so it does not rust. A factory shapes the spoon.
3. (mcq) Paper wasps make their nests out of paper. What do they make it from?  
   Options: Chewed wood / Old newspapers / Mud / Spider silk  
   **Answer:** Chewed wood. *Why:* Paper wasps chew wood and mix it with their spit. It dries as paper. In 1719 René Réaumur watched them and said people could make paper from wood too. Picking 'old newspapers' is a fun guess, but wasps were making paper long before people had newspapers.
4. (mcq) Is the milk plastic you made natural, or made by people?  
   Options: Natural, because it came from a cow / Made by people, from a natural material in milk / Made by people, from oil  
   **Answer:** Made by people, from a natural material in milk. *Why:* Casein came from milk, a natural material. But you heated it, added vinegar, strained and shaped it. So it is made by people. Picking 'from oil' mixes it up with most modern plastics, which are made from petroleum.
5. (mcq) Look at the chart from the salt experiment. Why did the sunny plate dry first?  
   Options: The sun's warmth made the water go into the air faster / The sunny plate had less water / The sun made extra salt / The shady plate was colder, so salt stopped the water leaving  
   **Answer:** The sun's warmth made the water go into the air faster. *Why:* Both plates had the same water. The only change was the sun. Warmth makes water evaporate, or go into the air, faster. Picking 'less water' would make the test unfair, and we kept the water the same.
6. (sort) Sort these by their source.  
   **Answer:** jute sack → Plant; silk sari → Animal; glass bottle → Ground or sea; coir doormat → Plant; woollen shawl → Animal; salt → Ground or sea; lac bangle → Animal; clay matka → Ground or sea. *Why:* Jute and coir are plant fibres. Silk, wool and lac come from animals. Glass comes from sand, clay from the ground, and salt from the sea.
7. (mcq) Factory indigo and plant indigo are…  
   Options: The very same substance, made two ways / Completely different colours / Both fake / Both made from oil only  
   **Answer:** The very same substance, made two ways. *Why:* Chemists showed that factory indigo is exactly the same substance as plant indigo. It is made by people, but it is not different. Picking 'completely different' usually means thinking made things can never be the same as natural things.
8. (truefalse) Before 1856, almost every dye in the world came from plants, animals or minerals.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 1856 William Perkin made mauve, a purple. It was the first dye made from chemicals to be sold all over the world. Before that, almost all colours came from nature, like turmeric, indigo and lac.
9. (mcq) In the colour experiment, why did we soak every cloth for the same time?  
   Options: So the only difference was the dye / Because 30 is a lucky number / So the cloths would dry faster / To use up the water  
   **Answer:** So the only difference was the dye. *Why:* In a fair test you change only one thing. Here that was the dye. If one cloth soaked longer, it might be darker for that reason. Picking 'dry faster' is a guess about time, but soaking time is about fairness. The unit on Testing materials has much more about fair tests.
10. (mcq) A diya dried in the air goes soft when water drips on it. A kiln-baked diya stays hard. Why?  
   Options: A kiln heats clay so much that it changes for ever / Air-dried clay is a different material / Kiln diyas are made of plastic / Water is stronger than clay  
   **Answer:** A kiln heats clay so much that it changes for ever. *Why:* A kiln heats clay until its tiny grains change and fuse. Then it cannot turn back into soft clay. Air drying only removes water. Picking 'a different material' is close, but both started as the same clay. The kiln changed it.
11. (fill) In the bottle of shaken soil and water, the finest grains settled on top. These grains are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['clay']. *Why:* Clay grains are the tiniest, so they sink slowest and settle last, on top.
12. (match) Match each made material to the natural material it was made from.  
   **Answer:** Glass → sand; Paper → wood; Brick → clay; Plastic bucket → petroleum; Milk plastic → casein from milk. *Why:* Every made material starts as a natural one.
13. (write) Kashi first thought the government made salt in a factory. What did she find out at the stone hollow?  
   **Answer:** She found out that nobody makes salt. It is hidden in sea water. The sun takes the water into the air, and the salt is left behind. People only collect it.. *Why:* A good answer says the salt was already in the sea, and the sun left it behind.
14. (write) Name three people whose work is hidden inside a cotton kurta. Say what each one did.  
   **Answer:** A farmer grew the cotton. A spinner spun the thread. A weaver wove the cloth. A tailor stitched it.. *Why:* Any three correct workers with their jobs are right.
15. (draw) Draw the layers you saw in the bottle of shaken soil and water. Label them.  
   **Answer:** Pebbles and sand at the bottom, then silt, then smooth clay, then clear water at the top.. *Why:* Heavier grains sink first, so they are at the bottom. The finest grains, clay, are on top.
### Stardust and Tricky Cases (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A cultured pearl is grown by an oyster on a farm, after people put a tiny bead inside it. Who made it?  
   Options: Only people / Only the oyster / Both the oyster and people  
   **Answer:** Both the oyster and people. *Why:* The oyster made every layer of pearl, but people started it and looked after the oyster. So both played a part. Picking only one leaves out the other's part. This shows the line between natural and made is not always sharp.
2. (mcq) A wooden spoon and a plastic spoon are both made mostly of which atoms?  
   Options: Carbon and hydrogen, with oxygen in the wood / Iron and gold / Wood atoms and plastic atoms / Salt and water  
   **Answer:** Carbon and hydrogen, with oxygen in the wood. *Why:* Wood and plastic are both built mostly from carbon and hydrogen, and wood has lots of oxygen too. Only the patterns differ. Picking 'wood atoms and plastic atoms' is very common. But no such atoms exist. Everything is made of the same 90 or so kinds.
3. (truefalse) When people cook, melt or mix materials, they make new atoms.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Cooking, melting and mixing only join existing atoms in new patterns. New atoms are made in stars, and in a few special machines like nuclear reactors, not in kitchens or factories.
4. (mcq) Where were the iron atoms in a steel kadhai made?  
   Options: In the kadhai factory / In the mine / Inside stars, long before the Earth formed / In the furnace  
   **Answer:** Inside stars, long before the Earth formed. *Why:* Iron atoms were made inside stars. The Earth formed from dust that held them. Picking 'the furnace' or 'the mine' stops the journey too soon. The furnace melts iron out of ore, and the mine digs up the ore, but neither makes the atoms.
5. (mcq) Scientists still argue about where most gold atoms were made. Which is one of the main ideas?  
   Options: In crashes between two dead stars called neutron stars / In gold mines / In volcanoes / In the ocean  
   **Answer:** In crashes between two dead stars called neutron stars. *Why:* In 2017 telescopes saw two neutron stars crash, throwing out heavy atoms. Many scientists think crashes like this make much of the gold. Others think rare exploding stars also make some. Picking 'gold mines' confuses where we find gold with where it was made.
6. (mcq) Is plastic completely unnatural?  
   Options: Yes, it has nothing to do with nature / It is made by people, but from petroleum, which formed from ancient living things / No, plastic grows on trees  
   **Answer:** It is made by people, but from petroleum, which formed from ancient living things. *Why:* Plastic is made by people, through chemistry. But its raw material, petroleum, formed over millions of years from tiny living things in the sea. Picking 'nothing to do with nature' means stopping the journey at the factory.
7. (mcq) A diamond grown in a laboratory is…  
   Options: A real diamond, made by people / Glass pretending to be a diamond / A natural diamond / Not made of carbon  
   **Answer:** A real diamond, made by people. *Why:* A lab diamond is pure carbon in the same pattern as a mined diamond. It is real diamond, made by people. Picking 'glass' confuses it with a fake. A fake is a different material that only looks like diamond.
8. (fill) In 2017, scientists counted all the plastic waste ever made. About ____ parts in every 100 had been recycled.  
   **Answer:** ['9', 'nine']. *Why:* Geyer, Jambeck and Law found that only about 9 out of every 100 parts had been recycled. Most was buried or ended up on land and in the sea.
9. (truefalse) By about 2020, everything people have made weighed about as much as all the living things on Earth.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Scientists in Israel, led by Emily Elhacham and Ron Milo, worked this out in 2020. In 1900, made things weighed only about 3 parts for every 100 parts of living things.
10. (mcq) A new kind of rock was found on a beach in Hawaii. What is it made of?  
   Options: Melted plastic mixed with sand and stones / Pure gold / Frozen sea water / Old bones  
   **Answer:** Melted plastic mixed with sand and stones. *Why:* Scientists described it in 2014 and called it plastiglomerate. Fires on the beach melted plastic onto sand, shells and stones. Picking 'frozen sea water' is a fun guess, but Hawaii's beaches are warm. The rock shows that made materials are now mixing into the Earth itself.
11. (mcq) Some of the oldest cotton ever found, more than 7,000 years old, was found…  
   Options: At Mehrgarh, in South Asia / In a factory in England, 200 years ago / In the Arctic / On the Moon  
   **Answer:** At Mehrgarh, in South Asia. *Why:* Cotton fibres preserved inside a copper bead were found at Mehrgarh, in present-day Pakistan. They are more than 7,000 years old. Picking England is a common idea, because England's mills made huge amounts of cloth in the 1800s. But cotton cloth began in South Asia thousands of years earlier.
12. (write) Kashi gave her salt away and did not sell it. Who do you think should own the salt in the sea? Give a reason.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer with a reason. For example: everyone, because nobody made it. Or: the people who live by the sea and do the work of collecting it.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. What counts is a clear reason.
13. (write) Birds build nests and people build houses. Is a house natural or made by people? Is a nest? Explain your thinking.  
   **Answer:** A good answer might say: a nest is natural, because a bird built it from natural things. A house is made by people. But people are animals too, so perhaps a house is natural in a way. It depends on what we mean by 'natural'.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Good answers give reasons for both sides.
14. (write) Factory indigo was cheaper, so plant indigo was hardly grown any more. Was that good or bad for the farmers? Give one reason for each side.  
   **Answer:** Good: farmers were no longer forced to grow indigo, and could grow food. Bad: people who earned money making plant indigo lost their work.. *Why:* A balanced answer gives a reason on each side.
15. (write) Plan a test to find out whether a 'natural' cloth or a 'made' cloth dries faster. Say what you would change and what you would keep the same.  
   **Answer:** Take a cotton cloth and a polyester cloth of the same size. Wet them with the same amount of water. Hang them in the same place. Change only the cloth. Check every 10 minutes which is dry.. *Why:* A good test changes only one thing, the cloth, and keeps everything else the same. The unit on Testing materials goes further with fair tests.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A tumbler can be made of steel, glass or plastic. Steel, glass and plastic are…  
   Options: Objects / Materials / Sources / Atoms  
   **Answer:** Materials. *Why:* They are materials, what objects are made of. Picking 'objects' mixes up the thing and what it is made of.
2. (mcq) Where does wool come from?  
   Options: A sheep / A cotton plant / Oil / Sand  
   **Answer:** A sheep. *Why:* Wool is the coat of a sheep, cut and spun. Picking 'a cotton plant' mixes up two soft fibres. Cotton grows on plants, wool on animals.
3. (mcq) Glass is made mostly from…  
   Options: Sand / Ice / Plastic / Salt  
   **Answer:** Sand. *Why:* Glass is made by melting sand with soda ash and limestone in a furnace. Picking 'ice' is a natural guess, because glass and ice both look clear. But ice melts at room heat and glass does not.
4. (mcq) Which of these is natural?  
   Options: A neem tree / A brick / A plastic bottle / A steel gate  
   **Answer:** A neem tree. *Why:* Nature made the neem tree. People made the others from natural materials. Picking 'a brick' means remembering that clay is natural, but forgetting that people baked it.
5. (mcq) A potted tulsi plant. Which is right?  
   Options: The plant is natural and the pot is made by people / Both are made by people / Both are natural  
   **Answer:** The plant is natural and the pot is made by people. *Why:* The tulsi grew by itself from a seed, so it is natural. The pot was made by people. Picking 'both made by people' usually means thinking that whatever people look after is made by them.
6. (mcq) How do people get glass from sand?  
   Options: Melt it in a furnace / Spin it / Dry it in the sun / Soak it in water  
   **Answer:** Melt it in a furnace. *Why:* Sand must be melted at a very high heat in a furnace. Picking 'dry it in the sun' mixes glass up with salt. The sun is far too cool to melt sand.
7. (mcq) What is the raw material for a matka?  
   Options: Clay / Glass / Steel / Wool  
   **Answer:** Clay. *Why:* A matka is made from clay, shaped and baked in a kiln. Picking 'glass' mixes it up with a bottle.
8. (mcq) In the salt experiment, which plate gave more salt?  
   Options: About the same, because both had the same salt hidden in them / The sunny plate, because the sun makes salt / The shady plate, because shade makes salt  
   **Answer:** About the same, because both had the same salt hidden in them. *Why:* The salt was in the water from the start. The sun only made the water leave faster. Picking 'the sun makes salt' is the most common idea. Salt is collected, not made.
9. (mcq) Why did the wet paper pulp hold together as a sheet?  
   Options: The plant fibres tangled and gripped each other / The net was sticky / Water is a glue  
   **Answer:** The plant fibres tangled and gripped each other. *Why:* Paper is a mat of tangled plant fibres. As the water left, the fibres tangled and gripped. Picking 'water is a glue' is a fair guess, but wet paper falls apart. It is the drying fibres that hold.
10. (mcq) Which animal makes a material that people use for bangles and varnish?  
   Options: The lac insect / The cow / The crow / The fish  
   **Answer:** The lac insect. *Why:* Lac insects on trees make a resin. People scrape it off for lac bangles, varnish and sealing wax. Picking 'the cow' usually means thinking of milk, which makes paneer and milk plastic, not lac.
11. (mcq) Factory indigo is…  
   Options: The same substance as plant indigo / A different colour from plant indigo / Made from sand  
   **Answer:** The same substance as plant indigo. *Why:* Chemists showed they are exactly the same substance. Picking 'a different colour' means thinking made copies can never match nature.
12. (mcq) Everything, natural or made, is built from…  
   Options: About 90 kinds of atom / Thousands of kinds of atom, one for each material / Only one kind of atom  
   **Answer:** About 90 kinds of atom. *Why:* About 90 kinds of atom are found in nature. They join in different patterns to build everything. Picking 'one for each material' is a very common idea. But there is no 'wood atom' or 'plastic atom'.
13. (mcq) Where were the carbon atoms in your pencil made?  
   Options: Inside stars, long ago / In the pencil factory / In the tree  
   **Answer:** Inside stars, long ago. *Why:* Carbon atoms were made in stars before the Earth formed. Picking 'in the tree' is close: trees take carbon from the air to build wood. But they collect carbon atoms. They do not make them.

   Options: No. Many think neutron star crashes, others think exploding stars too / Yes, gold is made in mines / Yes, gold is made in volcanoes

## Applications
- Farmers grow cotton, jute and silk for cloth, and rubber trees for tyres and chappals.
- Miners dig iron ore, bauxite and coal, and furnaces turn ore into iron, steel and aluminium.
- Salt makers in Gujarat and Tamil Nadu dry sea water and salty ground water in the sun.
- Potters and brick makers dig clay and bake it in kilns.
- Glass makers in Firozabad melt sand into bangles and bottles.
- Paper mills and handmade paper makers turn wood, bamboo, rags and old paper into new paper.
- Recyclers, kabadiwalas and waste pickers collect old paper, metal and plastic so they can be made into new things.
- Chemists invent synthetic materials, like nylon and polyester, often to copy or replace natural ones.
- Dyers use plants like indigo, turmeric and madder, and also factory dyes, to colour cloth.
- Scientists who study meteorites and starlight work out where the atoms in our materials were made.
- Engineers are testing ways to make bricks and oxygen from Moon dust for future Moon bases.
- Archaeologists study old materials, like the cotton at Mehrgarh, to learn how people lived thousands of years ago.

## Open questions
- **Where were most gold atoms made: in exploding stars, or in crashes of dead stars?** Telescopes saw one neutron star crash in 2017, but it is not clear whether such crashes happen often enough to make all the gold.
- **How long does plastic really last in the sea and soil?** Most plastic has existed for less than a human lifetime. The long numbers you hear are estimates, and scientists are still measuring how fast it breaks into smaller bits.
- **How did the hand-spinners of Bengal make Dhaka muslin so fine?** The skill was lost in the 1800s. People today are trying to bring back the special cotton plant and the spinning skill, and they are still finding out how it was done.
- **Could we make strong materials the way living things do, like spider silk?** Scientists understand parts of how spiders spin silk, but nobody can yet make spider silk as well as a spider, in large amounts.
- **Should we name a new age of the Earth after people, the Anthropocene?** In 2024 the scientists who name Earth's ages voted no, for now. Many others still use the idea, and the argument goes on.
- **Can we build bases on the Moon from Moon dust?** Engineers have made test bricks from pretend Moon dust on Earth, but nobody has yet built anything on the Moon itself.
- **Where exactly should the line between natural and made be drawn?** It depends on what we mean by nature. Scientists, philosophers and different traditions give different answers.

## Connected maths
- One litre of sea water has about 35 grams of salt hidden in it. A salt maker dries 4 litres in a pan. About how much salt does she get? **About 140 grams.**. Each litre gives 35 grams. 4 litres is 4 lots of 35 grams. 35 + 35 = 70. 70 + 70 = 140. So 4 × 35 = 140 grams. That is about half a cup of salt. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- On the Three-Heap Walk, Meena found 7 once-living things, 4 never-living things and 5 things made by people. Draw a bar chart. How many things did she find altogether? How many were natural? **16 things altogether. 11 were natural.**. Altogether: 7 + 4 + 5 = 16. The natural things are the once-living and the never-living ones: 7 + 4 = 11. The bar chart has three bars: 7, 4 and 5 tall. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Your wet clay diya weighed 120 grams. After 2 days of drying, it weighed 95 grams. How much water went into the air? **25 grams of water.**. The clay did not go anywhere. Only the water left. So the water is the difference: 120 − 95 = 25 grams. Check: 95 + 25 = 120. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L3`)
- Bakelite, the first plastic made completely from chemicals, was invented in 1907. Galalith, the milk plastic, was invented in 1897. How many years apart were they? How many years ago was Bakelite invented, if this year is 2026? **10 years apart. Bakelite was invented 119 years ago.**. 1907 − 1897 = 10 years. For the second part: 2026 − 1907. From 1907 to 2007 is 100 years. From 2007 to 2026 is 19 more. 100 + 19 = 119 years. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- You spun a cotton thread 10 centimetres long in 1 minute. If you kept spinning at the same speed, how long would your thread be after 5 minutes? After 10 minutes? **50 centimetres after 5 minutes. 100 centimetres, which is 1 metre, after 10 minutes.**. Each minute adds 10 cm. After 5 minutes: 5 × 10 = 50 cm. After 10 minutes: 10 × 10 = 100 cm. 100 cm is 1 metre. A cotton sari is woven from many kilometres of thread, so hand spinning takes a very long time. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)

## Warm-up
- Is a steel spoon an object or a material? → An object. Steel is its material.
- Where does cotton come from? → From the cotton plant.
- Glass is made mostly from what? → Sand.
- Natural or made by people: a brick? → Made by people, from natural clay.
- Which animal gives us silk? → The silkworm, a moth's caterpillar.
- Where does most plastic come from? → Petroleum, an oil from deep underground.
- In the salt experiment, did the sun make the salt? → No. The salt was in the water. The sun took the water away.
- What are the tiny hairs at the edge of torn paper? → Plant fibres.
- Where were the iron atoms in a kadhai made? → Inside stars, long ago.
- What is a raw material? → A natural material before people change it.
- Which insect makes the material of lac bangles? → The lac insect.
