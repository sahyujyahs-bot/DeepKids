# Swings and pendulums: what changes how fast they swing
`sci.motion.swings-and-pendulums.L3`

**Summary.** A swing, a hanging bunch of keys and the weight in an old wall clock all go back and forth in the same way. Each one is a pendulum: a weight hanging from a fixed point, free to swing. In this unit you build one from a thread and a bag of coins, and time 10 swings. You find that more coins or a wider start change the time very little, but a shorter thread makes the swings quicker. That steady beat is why pendulums were used to make the first accurate clocks.

**Intro.** *Hold a bunch of keys by its ring and let the keys hang. Pull them a little to one side and let go. They swing back and forth by themselves. Now try a tiny push and then a big push. Does the big push make each swing quicker, or only wider?* Anything that hangs from one point and swings back and forth is a pendulum. Swings, cradles, jhumkas and the keys in your hand are all pendulums. In this unit you find out what makes a pendulum swing quickly or slowly. Make a guess now, and keep it. You will test it soon.

## Terms
- **Pendulum**: A weight that hangs from a fixed point and can swing freely back and forth. *e.g.* A playground swing is a pendulum. So is a bag of coins hanging on a thread.
- **Pivot**: The fixed point a pendulum hangs from. The pendulum swings around it. *e.g.* On a swing, the pivot is the hook or bar at the top of the chains.
- **Bob**: The weight at the bottom of a pendulum. *e.g.* In this unit the bob is a small bag of coins. On a swing, the bob is you.
- **One full swing**: The pendulum goes from where you let it go, over to the far side, and back again. Scientists call the time this takes the period. *e.g.* A 50 cm pendulum takes about one and a half seconds for one full swing.
- **Oscillate**: To move back and forth again and again, over the same path. *e.g.* A swing oscillates. So does a guitar string, much faster.
- **Gravity**: The force that pulls everything towards the Earth. *e.g.* Gravity pulls the bob down, so it always swings back towards the middle.
- **Air resistance**: The push of the air against anything that moves through it. It slows moving things down. *e.g.* A pendulum with a big card taped to it stops swinging much sooner, because the card pushes against more air.
- **Friction**: The grip between two things that rub together. It slows movement down. *e.g.* A squeaky swing loses its swing faster, because its hooks rub on the bar.
- **Resonance**: When small pushes come at the same beat as a thing's own swing, each push adds to the last, and the swing grows big. *e.g.* Pushing a friend on a swing each time they come back to you makes them go higher and higher.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* To test the weight, change only the number of coins. Keep the same thread length and the same start.
- **Metronome**: A tool that ticks at a steady beat, to help musicians keep time. Many have a swinging arm that works like an upside-down pendulum. *e.g.* A veena student practises with a metronome so that each note comes exactly on the beat.

## Explanations
### What a pendulum is · *defined*
**Definition:** A pendulum is a weight, called the bob, that hangs from a fixed point, called the pivot, and is free to swing back and forth.

A pendulum has three parts. The pivot is the fixed point at the top. The string, chain or rod hangs from the pivot. The bob is the weight at the bottom. When the bob is still, it hangs straight down. Pull it to one side and let go, and it swings back and forth over the same path. One full swing is there and back, from where you let go to the far side and back again.
- e.g. A playground swing: the bar is the pivot, the chains are the string and the child is the bob.
- e.g. A thottil, the cloth cradle that families in Kerala and Tamil Nadu hang from the ceiling.
- e.g. A jhumka earring swinging as you turn your head.
- e.g. The long swinging rod with a brass disc in an old wall clock.
### Why a pendulum swings back and forth · *defined*
**Definition:** Gravity pulls the bob down towards the middle. When it reaches the middle it is moving fast, so it carries on up the other side until gravity slows it to a stop. Then it falls back, and the swing repeats.

When you pull the bob to one side, you lift it a little. Let go, and gravity pulls it down and back towards the middle. It goes faster and faster on the way down. At the very bottom it is moving fastest. A moving thing keeps moving unless something slows it, so the bob does not stop in the middle. It swings up the other side. Now gravity slows it down, until it stops for a moment at the far end. Then it falls back the other way. Each swing, its speed turns into height, and its height turns back into speed.
- e.g. On a swing, you feel your tummy flip at the bottom, where you move fastest.
- e.g. At the very top of each swing, a swing stops for a moment before it comes back.
- e.g. A bag of coins on a thread comes back almost to the same height it started from.
### What changes the swing time: the length · *defined*
**Definition:** The time for one full swing depends on the length of the pendulum. A shorter pendulum swings quicker. A longer one swings slower. For small swings, the weight of the bob and how wide it starts change the time very little.

In the swing test, 5 coins and 1 coin both took about 14 seconds for 10 swings. A small start and a wide start took about 14 seconds too. But a thread one quarter as long took only about 7 seconds. Make a pendulum 4 times shorter, and it swings twice as fast. Weight does not matter for the same reason that a heavy and a light ball fall together. Gravity pulls a heavier bob harder, but a heavier bob also needs more force to get moving. The two cancel out. A wider start also takes nearly the same time, because the bob falls from higher and so moves faster over its longer path. Length is measured from the pivot to the middle of the bob, because that is where the weight is.
- e.g. A pendulum 25 cm long takes about 1 second for one full swing. One 1 metre long takes about 2 seconds.
- e.g. A small child and a grown-up on the same swing take about the same time for each swing.
- e.g. The swing for little children, with short ropes, swings back and forth quicker than a tall swing.
### Very wide swings take a little longer · *defined*
**Definition:** For swings up to about a hand-width or two, the time hardly changes. A very wide swing, high up to the side, takes a little longer than a small one.

Galileo thought every swing takes exactly the same time, however wide. Later, Christiaan Huygens worked out that this is only nearly true. A pendulum let go from far out to the side, almost level with the pivot, takes about one sixth longer for each swing than a small one. For the swings in your test, the difference is only a few tenths of a second in 10 swings. That is about as much as a stopwatch press can be off by. This is why clock pendulums swing only a little way each side.
- e.g. In the swing test, the wide start may take 14.4 seconds and the small start 14.2 seconds.
- e.g. A wall clock's pendulum swings only a few centimetres each way.
### Why a swing slows down and stops · *defined*
**Definition:** A pendulum slows down because air resistance pushes against the bob and string, and friction acts at the pivot. Each swing loses a little movement, which ends up as a tiny bit of warmth in the air and the pivot.

If nothing slowed it, a pendulum would swing for ever. But it moves through air, and the air pushes back. A bob with a big flat card on it pushes more air out of the way, so it slows down much sooner. At the top, the string or the swing's hooks rub on the pivot, and that friction slows it too. A heavy, small bob on a thin thread keeps swinging longest. The swing gets narrower and narrower, but each swing still takes about the same time.
- e.g. A swing in a park stops on its own after a minute or two if nobody pushes it.
- e.g. A bag of coins on a thread swings for minutes. The same bag with a postcard taped on it stops in under a minute.
### Pushing at the right moment: resonance · *defined*
**Definition:** Every pendulum has its own beat, set by its length. Small pushes that come at that same beat add up, and the swing grows big. This is called resonance.

When you push a friend on a swing, you push each time they come back to you. Each push adds a little to the swing. Because the pushes come at the swing's own beat, they add up, and your friend goes higher and higher. If you push at the wrong moment, while the swing is still coming towards you, you slow it down instead. You can try this with your coin pendulum. Hold the top of the thread and move your hand a tiny bit side to side, in time with the swing. The swing grows. Move your hand too fast or too slow, and it stays small.
- e.g. Pushing a swing each time it comes back to you.
- e.g. Children on an Onam oonjal swing higher by leaning and pulling in time with each swing.
- e.g. Soldiers break step when they march across a bridge, so their steps don't add up to shake it.
### How pendulums keep time in clocks · *defined*
**Definition:** A pendulum of a fixed length always takes the same time for each swing, so a clock can count its swings to measure time. A small part of the clock gives the pendulum a tiny push on each swing, so it never dies down.

In 1656, Christiaan Huygens made the first pendulum clock. Each swing of the pendulum lets a toothed wheel turn on by one tooth, and that is the tick. The wheel, in turn, gives the pendulum a tiny push each time, so it keeps swinging. The wheels count the ticks and turn the hands. If a pendulum clock runs slow, you shorten the pendulum a little. Most have a small nut under the bob. Turn it to move the bob up, and the clock goes faster. A pendulum about 1 metre long takes one second to swing one way. Tall floor clocks use pendulums this long.
- e.g. Old wall clocks in many Indian homes have a long pendulum behind a glass door.
- e.g. A metronome is a pendulum turned upside down. Sliding its weight up makes it tick more slowly.
- e.g. The pendulum in a tall floor clock is about 1 metre long, so each tick is one second.
### Living things that swing like pendulums · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals move by letting parts of their body swing like a pendulum, because a swing moves them forward without much effort.

Hoolock gibbons are India's only apes. They live high in the forests of Assam and the north-east. They need to travel far through the treetops to find ripe fruit, without coming down to the ground, where it is less safe. Their arms are very long and strong. A gibbon hangs from one arm and swings forward like a pendulum, then grabs the next branch with the other hand. Gravity does much of the work, so this way of travelling saves energy. Your own legs swing like pendulums when you walk. A short leg swings forward quicker than a long one, so small children take quicker steps than grown-ups.
- e.g. A gibbon swinging from branch to branch, one arm at a time.
- e.g. A child walking next to a grown-up takes more steps to cover the same path.

## Mostly agreed
- A pendulum's time for one swing depends on its length. A shorter pendulum swings quicker. *How we know:* Anyone can time a short and a long pendulum at home. Scientists have measured it very exactly since the 1600s.
- For small swings, the weight of the bob does not change the time of a swing. *How we know:* Galileo swung lead and cork balls side by side. Isaac Newton tested pendulum bobs filled with gold, silver, lead, glass, sand, salt, wood, water and wheat, and found the same time for all.
- Small swings and slightly wider swings take nearly the same time. Very wide swings take a little longer. *How we know:* Christiaan Huygens worked this out in 1673, and careful timing shows it.
- A pendulum slows down because of air resistance and friction at the pivot. *How we know:* A pendulum swinging in a box with the air pumped out keeps swinging far longer than one in air.
- The same pendulum swings more slowly where gravity is weaker. *How we know:* Pendulum clocks run slightly slow near the equator and on high mountains, where gravity is a little weaker. On the Moon, a pendulum would swing about two and a half times more slowly.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, and Christiaan Huygens, a Dutch scientist, 1602 (Galileo's letter) to 1673 (Huygens's book on pendulum clocks).** In 1602, Galileo Galilei was teaching in Padua, in Italy. He wrote to a friend, Guidobaldo del Monte, about his tests with pendulums. He had hung two balls on threads of the same length, one of heavy lead and one of light cork. He pulled them aside together and let them go. They kept swinging back and forth together, the heavy one and the light one in step. Galileo also said that a swing takes the same time whether it is wide or small. He thought this was exactly true for every swing, even very wide ones. In 1656, the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens used this steady beat to build the first pendulum clock. It kept time far better than any clock before it. But Huygens worked carefully through the mathematics of the swing. In his book of 1673, he showed that Galileo was not quite right. A very wide swing takes a little longer than a small one. For small swings the difference is tiny. That is why the pendulum in a clock swings only a little way each side.  
*What people believed before:* Galileo thought every swing of a pendulum takes exactly the same time, however wide. Huygens showed that very wide swings take a little longer.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A heavier bob swings faster.** (A very common first guess, from children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Heavy things feel more powerful. A heavier swing is also harder to stop.  
  Against: In the swing test, 1 coin and 5 coins took about the same time for 10 swings. Gravity pulls a heavy bob harder, but it also needs more force to move, and the two cancel.
- **A bigger push makes each swing quicker.** (Many children, when they first push a swing; *rejected*)  
  For: A big push makes the swing move faster at the bottom, so it seems each swing should take less time.  
  Against: The swing moves faster, but it also travels further. The two balance out, so each swing takes nearly the same time. Only very wide swings take a little longer.
- **Every swing, however wide, takes exactly the same time.** (Galileo Galilei, in about 1602; *rejected*)  
  For: For small swings, timing by hand shows no difference at all.  
  Against: Christiaan Huygens showed in 1673 that very wide swings take a little longer. Clockmakers keep swings small for this reason.
- **Once a pendulum starts, it should keep swinging for ever.** (Some people who see a clock pendulum that never seems to stop; *rejected*)  
  For: A clock's pendulum keeps swinging day after day.  
  Against: A clock gives its pendulum a tiny push every swing. Without it, air resistance and friction at the pivot slow any pendulum down until it stops.

## Roots
We have learned that a pendulum's beat is set by its length, and how clocks count that beat. Now we ask what swings tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Ten swings took about the same time with one coin or five, from a small start or a wide one. Only a shorter string made the swings quicker. The length and the Earth's pull set the beat, and your push only sets how wide it swings. So every pendulum of one length keeps the same time here, and that is how clocks were made.  
*Habit it breaks:* We expect a bigger push or a heavier weight to make things go faster, as if speed always comes from effort. A pendulum keeps its own beat whatever we do to it. The steady time of a clock was not made by people. Clockmakers found a beat that the Earth's pull and a length of string already keep, and counted it.  
*Evidence:* In the Swing Test, 1 coin and 5 coins both took about 14 seconds for 10 swings, and so did a wide start. A thread a quarter as long took about 7 seconds.

- *who am i:* Your legs swing like pendulums when you walk. Your heart and your breathing keep their own beats too.
- *what is life:* Hoolock gibbons in the forests of Assam swing through the trees like pendulums, letting gravity carry them to the next branch.
- *what is this world:* The Earth's pull sets the beat of every pendulum on it. On the Moon, the same pendulum would swing about two and a half times more slowly.

A pendulum at rest hangs straight down, perfectly balanced. Pull it to one side and let go, and it swings across to the other side and back. Every swing to the left is matched by a swing to the right, around that still middle. Out of one still balance comes a rhythm that repeats again and again. People counted that rhythm and turned it into hours, days and calendars that a whole world shares.

- **Tao Te Ching 40:** Returning is the movement of the Tao. (A pendulum always turns back towards its middle. Its whole movement is made of going out and returning.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.14:** Heat and cold, pleasure and pain, come and go. They do not last. Bear them patiently. (A swing goes up on one side and up on the other, but its resting point stays where it is. The swing changes all the time around a middle that does not move.)
- Science (settled): Every clock counts a steady back-and-forth beat. A quartz watch counts a crystal that vibrates 32,768 times a second. The second itself is defined as 9,192,631,770 waves of a kind of light given out by caesium atoms.
- Science (open): What time itself is, and whether it flows on its own, is still argued about by physicists and philosophers.
- Other view, Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz: Newton thought time flows evenly by itself, even if nothing moves at all, and clocks only measure it. Leibniz disagreed. He said there is no time apart from things happening, and time is only the order in which they happen. Many scientists today say a pendulum is just gravity and a string, and the rhythm is in our counting, not in nature.

Breathe out slowly while it makes three more. Let's do this five times. Notice that the pendulum keeps its own beat. Our breathing slowed down to follow it.

## Story: The Onam Swing
*The story is made up. The swing science in it is true: a heavy and a light rider take about the same time for each swing, a shorter rope swings quicker, and a pendulum clock is put right by moving its bob up or down with the nut beneath it. Families in Kerala really do hang an oonjal, a swing, for Onam.*

Every Onam, Ammamma's house in Thrissur fills up with cousins. This year Meenu was nine, and her cousin Arjun was seven.

On the first morning, Valyachan, their uncle, climbed a ladder into the old mango tree. He tied two thick ropes to a high branch and fixed a flat wooden plank between them. It was the Onam oonjal, the swing that families in Kerala hang up for the festival.

Arjun jumped on first. Meenu pushed him, and back and forth he went, while the mango leaves shook above him. "Higher!" he shouted.

"My turn next," said Valyachan. "I'm the heaviest, so I'll swing the fastest."

Arjun shook his head. "No, you'll be the slowest. Heavy things are slow, like a loaded lorry."

Meenu had an idea. "Let's find out," she said. "Ammamma's clock in the hall ticks once every second. I'll count the ticks while the rider counts swings." She stood by the open window, where she could hear the clock.

Arjun went first. Meenu let him go from her hands and said, "Zero!" Each time the swing came back to her, Arjun counted one more. When he shouted "Ten!", Meenu had counted 30 ticks.

Then Valyachan sat on the plank. The ropes creaked. Meenu pulled him back to the same place and let go. When he reached ten swings, she had counted 29 ticks.

"One less! So I am faster," said Valyachan.

"One tick is only me counting a bit late," said Meenu. "Twenty-nine and thirty are almost the same. Let's try Ammamma."

Ammamma laughed, but she sat down carefully on the plank, holding the ropes. Ten swings took 30 ticks.

Arjun frowned. "Then it must be the push. Push me really hard this time, Meenu." Meenu pulled the swing back as far as she could reach and let go. Arjun flew high, squealing. Ten swings took 31 ticks.

"A tiny bit slower," said Meenu, "even though you went much higher. The big push made you go further and faster. But each swing still took about the same time."

The next day, the little cousins wanted a swing of their own. Valyachan tied a second swing to a low branch, with ropes only as long as Meenu was tall. Arjun sat on it, and Meenu counted again. Ten swings took only 20 ticks.

"It's the rope!" said Meenu. "A short rope makes a quick swing. A long rope makes a slow swing. Heavy or light, big push or small push, the beat hardly changes. Only the length changes it."

That evening, Ammamma looked up at the clock in the hall and sighed. "This clock has been losing time," she said. "By Sunday it is five minutes behind. Your Appooppan, your grandfather, used to fix it. I never learned how."

Meenu opened the clock's glass door. Inside, a long rod swung slowly back and forth, with a round brass disc at the bottom. Under the disc was a small nut on a screw.

"The clock counts the swings of this pendulum," said Meenu. "Each swing makes one tick. If the clock is slow, the pendulum must be swinging too slowly. So we need it a little shorter."

Ammamma held the rod still while Meenu turned the nut. The brass disc moved up, just a few millimetres.

"Such a tiny change?" asked Arjun.

"The swing ropes changed a lot, so the beat changed a lot," said Meenu. "The clock is only a little slow, so it needs only a little change."

Ammamma set the hands to match her phone, and Meenu gave the pendulum a small push to start it again. Over the next two days, they checked the clock against the phone every morning and every night. The first day it was still a minute slow, so Meenu turned the nut once more. After that, it kept time with the phone.

On the last night of Onam, Arjun lay on his mat in the hall and listened to the clock. Tick, tock. Tick, tock.

"Meenu," he whispered. "The clock is a swing too. It's just a very small one, swinging all night."

"And it keeps the same beat, whoever is listening," said Meenu, half asleep.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a pendulum's time for each swing is set by its length. A heavier rider or a bigger push changes it very little, and a very wide swing takes only slightly longer. A pendulum clock is put right by making the pendulum a tiny bit shorter, to speed it up, or longer, to slow it down.

- Ask: Why did the little cousins' swing go back and forth so much quicker?
- Ask: The clock was slow. Why did Meenu move the brass disc up and not down?

  - Check: The card says HEAVIER BOB! What should your swing do? → **Keep the same beat**
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try the real thing. Hold your arm straight and let it swing, then bend it at the elbow and let it swing.  
  *Parent says:* Now swing your straight arm, then your bent arm. Which one swings quicker by itself?  
  *Child mode:* Swing your arm straight. Then bent. Which is quicker?
  - Ask: Why does a bent arm swing quicker? (good: Bending makes it shorter.; A shorter pendulum swings quicker.)
  - Note: A bent arm is a shorter pendulum, so it swings quicker on its own. This is why runners bend their arms.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Swing Test: what changes how long a swing takes? (35 min)
- **question:** Does the weight of the bob, how wide it starts, or the length of the string change how long a pendulum takes to swing?
- **predict:** We will change three things, one at a time: the number of coins, how wide the swing starts, and the length of the thread. Which ones will change the time for 10 swings? Will it get quicker or slower? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Change only one thing in each test. Keep the same thread, the same kind of coins and the same pivot. Always count 10 full swings, and do each test twice, because one try can be off.
- **safety:** Keep faces away from the swinging bag. Put the books on firmly so the ruler cannot tip off the table.
- **record:** Fill in the table as you go. At the end, circle the one change that made a big difference. Then write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Test | What we changed | Our guess | Try 1 (seconds for 10 swings) | Try 2 (seconds for 10 swings) | A big change, or about the same?
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the ruler on the table with 10 cm sticking out over the edge. 2. Put the three books on the rest of it. 3. Put 5 coins in the bag, twist it tight and tie the thread round the twist. 4. Tie the other end to the tip of the ruler and tape it so it cannot slide. 5. Make the thread 50 cm long, from the ruler to the middle of the coins.  
  *Parent says:* We are building a pendulum. The ruler's tip is the pivot. The bag of coins is the bob. We measure the length to the middle of the coins, because that is where the weight is.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the coin bag from the ruler. Make it 50 cm long. Measure to the middle of the coins.
  - Note: Any coins work, but use one kind all through. The bag should hang about 20 cm above the floor. If the ruler bends or wobbles, add another book.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show how to count. Pull the bag one hand-width to the side and let go. Say 'zero' as you let go. Say 'one' when it comes back to your hand, 'two' the next time, and so on up to 10.  
  *Parent says:* One full swing is there and back. We say zero as we let go. Then we count one each time it comes back to where it started.  
  *Child mode:* Let go and say zero. Count one when it comes back. Count up to ten.
  - Check: You let go of the bag. It swings to the far side and back to your hand. How many full swings is that? → **One full swing**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for guesses and reasons for each of the three tests, and write them in the table.  
  *Parent says:* First we will change the coins, then the start, then the length. Which will change the time for 10 swings? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Will more coins change the time? A wider start? A shorter thread? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen with 1 coin instead of 5? Why? (good: Any guess with a reason, such as: it will swing quicker because it is lighter.)
  - Note: Don't say what will happen. Most children guess that weight or a bigger push changes the time. That is a good guess to test.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. With 5 coins, pull the bag one hand-width out. 2. Start the stopwatch as you let go, and stop it at 'ten'. 3. Do it twice and write both times. 4. Take out 4 coins, leaving 1. Check the length is still 50 cm to the middle of the coin. 5. Time 10 swings twice again.  
  *Parent says:* Test one changes only the weight. Same length, same start. That makes it a fair test.  
  *Child mode:* Time 10 swings with 5 coins. Then with 1 coin. Do each one twice.
  - Note: Each should take about 14 seconds. If two tries differ by more than 1 second, do a third. One person lets go and counts, the other works the stopwatch.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the 5 coins back. 2. Time 10 swings from one hand-width out, twice. 3. Time 10 swings from two big hand-widths out, about 25 cm, twice.  
  *Parent says:* Test two changes only how wide it starts. The wide swing goes much further. Will it take longer?  
  *Child mode:* Time a small start. Then a wide start. Write both times.
  - Note: Both should take about 14 seconds. The wide start may be a few tenths of a second longer. That small difference is real, and the explanation cards come back to it.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wind the thread round the ruler's tip and tape it. 2. Stop when it is 12 and a half cm to the middle of the coins, a quarter of 50 cm. 3. Time 10 swings twice.  
  *Parent says:* Test three changes only the length. The thread is now a quarter as long. Count quickly, because it will be fast.  
  *Child mode:* Make the thread short. Time 10 swings again. Count fast!
  - Note: This should take about 7 seconds, half the time. Count out loud with your child, because the swings come quickly.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the whole table together. Circle the change that made a big difference.  
  *Parent says:* Which change made a big difference? Tell me what you think, what you saw and why.  
  *Child mode:* Which change made a big difference? Say what you think, what you saw and why.
  - Ask: What did you find out? (good: What I think: only the length changes the swing time. What I saw: 1 coin and 5 coins both took about 14 seconds, but the short thread took 7. Why: the length sets the beat.)
  - Check: In your test, which change made the swings much quicker? → **Making the thread shorter**
### e2 · The One-Second Pendulum (20 min)
- **question:** How long must the thread be so that one full swing takes exactly 1 second?
- **predict:** 50 cm took about 14 seconds for 10 swings. 12 and a half cm took about 7 seconds. What length will take exactly 10 seconds? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same 5 coins and the same small start every time. Change only the length. Always count 10 swings, so that small timing mistakes matter less.
- **safety:** Keep faces away from the swinging bag.
- **record:** Write every length you tried and its time. Draw a star by the length that took 10 seconds.
- **results table:** Length (cm, to the middle of the coins) | Seconds for 10 swings | Too slow, too quick or just right?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set up the coin pendulum as in the Swing Test. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* We want 10 swings to take exactly 10 seconds. What length do you think we need? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the length for 10 swings in 10 seconds. Why that length?
  - Ask: What length do you guess, and why? (good: Between 12 and 50 cm, because 10 seconds is between 7 and 14.; About 30 cm, because it is in the middle.)
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set the guessed length. 2. Time 10 swings. 3. If it took more than 10 seconds, make the thread shorter. If less, make it longer. 4. Repeat until it takes 10 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Too slow? Make it shorter. Too quick? Make it longer. Change it a little at a time.  
  *Child mode:* Time 10 swings. Too slow? Shorter. Too quick? Longer. Try again.
  - Note: The answer is about 25 cm. Many children guess about 30 cm, halfway between. The length is not halfway, because halving the time needs a quarter of the length.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Use your one-second pendulum to time something. Count swings while your child hops on one foot, or while they say a times table.  
  *Parent says:* Now our pendulum is a clock. Each full swing is one second. How long can you hop on one foot?  
  *Child mode:* Your pendulum is a clock now. Count swings while you hop!
  - Check: You made a pendulum that takes 1 second for each full swing. Your friend wants one that takes 2 seconds. What should she do? → **Make the thread longer**
### e3 · What Makes a Swing Die Down? (25 min)
- **question:** Does a pendulum that pushes more air stop swinging sooner?
- **predict:** We will tape a postcard to the thread just above the coins. Will the pendulum with the card stop swinging sooner, later or at the same time? Will each swing take longer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same length, the same coins and the same start for both. Change only the card. Stop the stopwatch at the same mark both times.
- **safety:** Keep faces away from the swinging bag.
- **record:** Write both times. Draw the two pendulums and show with arrows where the air pushes.
- **results table:** Pendulum | Seconds until it no longer reaches the 10 cm mark | Seconds for 10 swings at the start
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set up the 50 cm coin pendulum. 2. Put a shoebox under it, with a paper strip about 50 cm long on top. 3. Move the box until the strip's middle is right under the resting bag. 4. Mark 10 cm and 20 cm each side of the middle with a pencil.  
  *Parent says:* We let go from the 20 cm mark. We stop the clock when the bag no longer swings out as far as the 10 cm mark.  
  *Child mode:* Let go from 20 cm. Watch it swing. When does it stop reaching 10 cm?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for guesses and reasons.  
  *Parent says:* Next we will tape a card on. Will the card make it die down sooner? Will each swing take longer? Why?  
  *Child mode:* With a card, will it stop sooner? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What will the card do? Why? (good: Any guess with a reason, such as: it will stop sooner because the card pushes the air.)
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let go from 20 cm and start the stopwatch. 2. Stop it when the bag no longer reaches 10 cm. 3. Write the time.  
  *Parent says:* This one may take a few minutes. Keep watching the 10 cm mark.  
  *Child mode:* Let go. Watch the 10 cm mark. Stop the clock when it falls short.
  - Note: Without a card it may take a minute or more. If your child gets bored, count swings out loud together.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the card flat to the thread just above the coins, facing the way it swings. 2. Time 10 swings at the start. 3. Then time how long until it no longer reaches 10 cm.  
  *Parent says:* Now the card has to push the air out of the way on every swing.  
  *Child mode:* Tape on the card. Time it again. What changed?
  - Note: With the card it should die down in well under a minute. Each swing still takes about the same time, about 14 seconds for 10.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the times.  
  *Parent says:* What did the card change, and what did it not change? Why?  
  *Child mode:* What did the card change? What stayed the same?
  - Check: Why did the pendulum with the card die down sooner? → **The air pushed against the big flat card**

## Worksheets
### Swing Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (label) Label the three parts of this pendulum: pivot, string and bob.  
   **Answer:** ['pivot at the top', 'string in the middle', 'bob at the bottom']. *Why:* The pivot is the fixed point at the top. The bob is the weight at the bottom. A strong answer also says that the length is measured from the pivot to the middle of the bob.
2. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Pendulum → a weight hanging from a fixed point, free to swing; Pivot → the fixed point a pendulum hangs from; Bob → the weight at the bottom of a pendulum; Air resistance → the push of air against a moving thing; Resonance → small pushes at the swing's own beat adding up. *Why:* These words come up all through the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
3. (sort) Sort these things. Is each one a pendulum, or not?  
   **Answer:** a playground swing → Pendulum; a thottil cradle → Pendulum; a jhumka earring → Pendulum; a ceiling fan → Not a pendulum; a bunch of keys hanging on a ring → Pendulum; a ball rolling down a slope → Not a pendulum; a temple bell's clapper → Pendulum; a spinning top → Not a pendulum. *Why:* A pendulum hangs from one fixed point and swings freely. A ceiling fan turns in circles, and a ball rolling down a slope does not swing back and forth.
4. (truefalse) Kavya says, 'A heavier bob makes a pendulum swing faster.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the Swing Test, 1 coin and 5 coins took about the same time for 10 swings. A strong answer gives the numbers from the test.
5. (truefalse) A pendulum stops for a moment at each end of its swing.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. At each end it has climbed as high as it can go, so it stops for a moment before gravity pulls it back.
6. (fill) A pendulum moves fastest at the ______ of its swing.  
   **Answer:** ['bottom', 'middle']. *Why:* It speeds up on the way down and slows down on the way up, so it is fastest at the bottom.
7. (fill) To make a pendulum swing quicker, make its string ______.  
   **Answer:** ['shorter']. *Why:* A shorter pendulum swings quicker. Adding weight hardly changes it.
8. (mcq) Sneha's pendulum takes 14 seconds for 10 swings. She starts it from twice as far out to the side. About how long will 10 swings take now?  
   Options: About 7 seconds / About 14 seconds / About 28 seconds  
   **Answer:** About 14 seconds. *Why:* A wider start makes the bob travel further, but also faster, so the time stays about the same. It may be a few tenths of a second longer.
9. (order) Put the steps of the Swing Test for weight in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Build a 50 cm pendulum with 5 coins', 'Time 10 swings, twice', 'Take out 4 coins and keep the length 50 cm', 'Time 10 swings again, twice, and compare']. *Why:* First build it, then time it, then change only the weight, then time and compare.
10. (write) Explain why a pendulum keeps swinging past the middle instead of stopping there.  
   **Answer:** At the middle the bob is moving fastest. A moving thing keeps going unless something slows it, so it carries on up the other side until gravity slows it to a stop.. *Why:* A strong answer says the bob is moving fast at the middle, and that gravity slows it on the way up.
11. (write) Your friend counts each side of a swing as one swing. You count there and back as one. Whose count of 10 swings will take longer? Why?  
   **Answer:** Mine, because each of my swings is two of my friend's. My 10 swings take twice as long as theirs.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that the two ways of counting differ by two times, and says why everyone must count the same way to compare.
12. (draw) Draw a pendulum at the start, the middle and the far end of one swing. Draw a long arrow where it moves fastest and no arrow where it stops.  
   **Answer:** A long arrow at the bottom and no arrows at the two ends.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the bob fastest at the bottom and still for a moment at each end.
13. (perspective) Two cousins in Hyderabad are on the swings in their building's play area.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the Swing Test, where weight changed the time very little, so Ayesha is right that the beat stays the same. It also gives Rohan his due: he is right that his swing goes higher with a big push.. *Why:* This question checks that the child can tell 'goes higher' apart from 'swings quicker', and uses evidence.
14. (mcq) Why does a swing slow down and stop if nobody pushes it?  
   Options: Gravity gets tired / Air resistance and friction at the hooks slow it a little on each swing / The chains get longer  
   **Answer:** Air resistance and friction at the hooks slow it a little on each swing. *Why:* Each swing, the air pushes back and the hooks rub on the bar. Gravity does not tire. Picking 'gravity gets tired' means thinking of gravity as something that runs out.
15. (odd) Three of these change how long a pendulum takes to swing very little. Which one changes it a lot?  
   Options: Adding more coins / Starting it a little wider / Making the string much shorter / Using a heavier key bunch  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The length sets the beat. The weight, a small start and a slightly wider start change it very little.
### Pendulum Puzzles (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Rani has a pendulum 1 metre long. Each full swing takes about 2 seconds. How long should she make it so each swing takes about 1 second?  
   Options: 50 cm / 25 cm / 2 metres  
   **Answer:** 25 cm. *Why:* To halve the time, make the pendulum a quarter as long. A quarter of 1 metre is 25 cm. Many people guess half the length, 50 cm, but that makes it only a little quicker.
2. (truefalse) A very wide swing, let go from almost level with the pivot, takes a little longer than a small swing.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Christiaan Huygens worked this out in 1673. For a swing let go from level with the pivot, each swing takes about one sixth longer. For small swings the difference is tiny.
3. (write) In the One-Second Pendulum, many children first guess about 30 cm, halfway between 12 and a half cm and 50 cm. Why is the right length shorter than halfway?  
   **Answer:** The time does not change in step with the length. A quarter of the length gives half the time, so to get from 7 seconds to 10 seconds you need less extra length than you might think. The answer is about 25 cm.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the numbers from the experiments and says that length and time do not change by the same amount.
4. (sort) Sort these changes. Will each one make a swing die down sooner, or make little difference?  
   **Answer:** taping a postcard to the bob → Dies down sooner; a rusty, rubbing pivot → Dies down sooner; swinging under a running fan → Dies down sooner; using a small, heavy bob → Little difference or longer; using a thin, smooth thread → Little difference or longer; a big, light balloon as the bob → Dies down sooner. *Why:* Things that push more air, or rub more at the pivot, make it die down sooner. A heavy, small bob is slowed least.
5. (mcq) Two pendulums hang from one loose string. Both are 30 cm long. You start one. What will the other one do?  
   Options: Stay still / Swing the opposite way at once / Slowly start swinging, while the first one slows down  
   **Answer:** Slowly start swinging, while the first one slows down. *Why:* Each small tug on the string comes at the first pendulum's beat, which is also the second one's own beat. So the tugs add up, and the swing passes across. This is resonance.
6. (write) Why does pushing a swing at the wrong moment, while it is still coming towards you, make it go lower?  
   **Answer:** The push is against the way the swing is moving, so it slows the swing down instead of adding to it.. *Why:* A strong answer says the push and the movement are in opposite directions.
7. (draw) Draw a parent pushing a child on a swing at the best moment. Draw an arrow for the push and an arrow for the way the swing is moving.  
   **Answer:** The parent pushes at the back of the swing, as it starts to move away. Both arrows point the same way.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the push and the movement in the same direction.
8. (perspective) Two scientists from long ago disagree about pendulums.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says Galileo was right for small swings: weight hardly matters and small swings take nearly the same time. It says Huygens was right that very wide swings take a little longer, and that clockmakers keep swings small. Both used careful thinking and tests.. *Why:* This checks that the child sees that an idea can be nearly right, and how a better test or better maths improves it.
9. (fill) When small pushes come at a swing's own beat and add up, it is called ______.  
   **Answer:** ['resonance']. *Why:* Resonance is why a few small pushes can make a swing go very high.
10. (match) Match each pendulum to about how long it takes for one full swing.  
   **Answer:** A jhumka on a 3 cm hook → quickest swing; A 25 cm coin pendulum → about 1 second; A 1 metre floor clock pendulum → about 2 seconds; A swing with 2 metre chains → about 3 seconds. *Why:* A longer pendulum takes longer for each swing. A jhumka is tiny, so it swings quickest.
11. (order) Put these pendulums in order, from the one that swings quickest to the one that swings slowest.  
   **Answer:** ['String 10 cm, 5 coins', 'String 25 cm, 1 coin', 'String 50 cm, 5 coins', 'String 1 metre, 1 coin']. *Why:* Only the length decides the order. The coins do not change it.
12. (label) On this drawing of a swing, label where the swing moves fastest, and the two places where it stops for a moment.  
   **Answer:** ['fastest at the bottom', 'stops at the left end', 'stops at the right end']. *Why:* A strong answer puts 'fastest' at the lowest point and 'stops' at each end.
13. (truefalse) On the Moon, where gravity is weaker, the same pendulum would swing more slowly.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Gravity pulls the bob back towards the middle. Weaker gravity pulls it back more gently, so each swing takes longer, about two and a half times as long.
14. (mcq) Your legs swing like pendulums when you walk. Who takes quicker steps when walking relaxed, a small child or a tall grown-up?  
   Options: The small child / The tall grown-up / Both the same  
   **Answer:** The small child. *Why:* A child's legs are shorter pendulums, so they swing forward quicker. That is why a child takes more steps to keep up.
15. (write) Megha's wall clock is 2 minutes fast every day. What should she do to the pendulum, and why?  
   **Answer:** Make it a tiny bit longer by moving the bob down with the nut. A longer pendulum swings slower, so the clock ticks slower and stops gaining time.. *Why:* A strong answer says 'longer' and gives the reason: longer swings slower.
### Clockmaker's Questions (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Gravity pulls a heavy bob harder than a light one. So why does the heavy bob not swing faster?  
   **Answer:** A heavy bob also needs more force to get moving, just as a full trolley is harder to push than an empty one. The extra pull and the extra mass cancel out, so both swing at the same beat.. *Why:* A strong answer names both sides: the bigger pull and the bigger mass, and says they cancel.
2. (write) A wide swing travels much further than a small one. How can it take almost the same time?  
   **Answer:** Let go from further out, the bob falls from higher, so it moves faster. It travels further, but faster, so the time stays almost the same.. *Why:* A strong answer links the longer path to the faster speed.
3. (perspective) Three people talk about why a swing in a park stopped.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees most with Selvi, because the card test showed air slowing a pendulum, and a squeaky swing loses movement at its hooks. It gives Nisha her due: gravity does keep pulling, but it pulls the swing back to the middle, it does not stop it. And it gives Ravi his due: the swing did move less and less, but the movement went somewhere, into warmth.. *Why:* This checks that the child can use the card test as evidence and judge three ideas fairly.
4. (mcq) In the pendulum wave, the longest pendulum makes 12 swings in 15 seconds and the shortest makes 16. Why do they all line up again at 15 seconds?  
   Options: Because they get tired / Because each one has made a whole number of swings and is back at the start / Because the air pushes them back together  
   **Answer:** Because each one has made a whole number of swings and is back at the start. *Why:* Each pendulum makes a whole number of swings in 15 seconds, so at that moment every one is back where it started. In between they are out of step.
5. (write) Santorio Santorio, an Italian doctor in the early 1600s, measured his patients' pulse with a pendulum. He changed its length until each swing matched a heartbeat. How could the length tell him how fast the heart was beating?  
   **Answer:** A shorter pendulum swings quicker. So a quick pulse matched a short pendulum, and a slow pulse matched a long one. By reading the length, he could compare pulses from day to day.. *Why:* A strong answer links short to quick and long to slow, and says the length becomes a measure.
6. (draw) Draw a graph with length on the bottom and the time for 10 swings up the side. Plot 12 and a half cm at 7 seconds, 25 cm at 10 seconds and 50 cm at 14 seconds. Join the points. Is it a straight line?  
   **Answer:** The points rise, but the line bends and gets flatter. It is not a straight line.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that doubling the length does not double the time.
7. (mcq) A clock pendulum swings only a few centimetres each way. Why do clockmakers keep the swing so small?  
   Options: Small swings all take nearly the same time, so the clock stays accurate / Small swings are quieter / Small swings use a lighter bob  
   **Answer:** Small swings all take nearly the same time, so the clock stays accurate. *Why:* Very wide swings take a little longer, and the time changes if the width changes. Small swings all take nearly the same time, so the clock stays steady.
8. (truefalse) Equal-length pendulums hanging from one string pass their swing back and forth, but a short and a long one hardly do.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The tugs through the string come at one pendulum's beat. Only a pendulum with the same beat takes them up and grows. This is resonance.
9. (write) Many people think a bigger push makes a swing go back and forth quicker. Plan a test to find out if they are right. Say what you change, what you keep the same and what you measure.  
   **Answer:** Change only how far out you let it go. Keep the same length and the same bob. Time 10 swings from each start, twice each.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing, keeps the rest the same, measures 10 swings and repeats.
10. (sort) Sort these into what sets a pendulum's beat a lot, and what changes it very little.  
   **Answer:** the length of the string → Changes the beat a lot; taking it to the Moon → Changes the beat a lot; a brass bob instead of a steel one → Changes it very little; 1 coin instead of 5 → Changes it very little; a small start or a slightly wider one → Changes it very little. *Why:* The length and gravity set the beat. The weight, the material and the size of a small start change it very little.
11. (fill) A pendulum about ______ long takes one second to swing one way, so tall floor clocks use one this long.  
   **Answer:** ['1 metre', 'one metre', 'a metre']. *Why:* A pendulum about 1 metre long takes 1 second each way, and 2 seconds for a full swing there and back.
12. (mcq) A metronome is a pendulum turned upside down, with a sliding weight on its arm. A music teacher slides the weight up. What happens?  
   Options: It ticks quicker / It ticks more slowly / It stops  
   **Answer:** It ticks more slowly. *Why:* Sliding the weight up makes the metronome tick more slowly, a bit like making a pendulum longer.
13. (label) On a drawing of a pendulum clock, label the pendulum, the bob, the nut that moves the bob, and the wheel that ticks.  
   **Answer:** ['pendulum rod', 'bob', 'nut under the bob', 'toothed wheel at the top']. *Why:* A strong answer also says which way to turn the nut, up for faster and down for slower.
14. (odd) Three of these use a swing or a back-and-forth beat to keep time. Which one does not?  
   Options: A pendulum clock / A metronome / A sundial / A quartz watch  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A sundial uses the Sun's shadow. A pendulum clock, a metronome and a quartz watch, whose crystal vibrates 32,768 times a second, all count a steady back-and-forth beat.
15. (write) Hoolock gibbons swing through the treetops like pendulums. Why does this way of travelling help them find fruit high in the forest?  
   **Answer:** They can cross from tree to tree fast without coming down to the ground. Swinging lets gravity do much of the work, so it saves energy for a long day of looking for fruit.. *Why:* A strong answer says what problem the gibbon has, staying high and travelling far, and how swinging helps.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) On a playground swing, which part is the pivot?  
   Options: The seat / The bar or hooks at the top / The child / The ground  
   **Answer:** The bar or hooks at the top. *Why:* The pivot is the fixed point the swing hangs from, at the top. The child on the seat is the bob. Picking the seat means mixing up the bob and the pivot.
2. (mcq) Divya lets go of a swing. It goes to the far side and comes back to her hands. How much swinging has it done?  
   Options: Half a full swing / One full swing / Two full swings / Ten full swings  
   **Answer:** One full swing. *Why:* One full swing is there and back again. Counting each side as a swing gives twice the right number.
3. (mcq) Where does a pendulum move fastest?  
   Options: At the two ends / At the bottom, in the middle of its swing / It moves at the same speed all the way / Only when you push it  
   **Answer:** At the bottom, in the middle of its swing. *Why:* The bob speeds up as it falls towards the middle, and slows down as it climbs. At each end it stops for a moment. If you picked the ends, watch a swing again: it pauses there.
4. (mcq) Why does a pendulum not stop at the bottom, where it hangs at rest?  
   Options: The string pulls it past / It is moving fast there, so it carries on up the other side / The air pushes it on / It wants to reach the other side  
   **Answer:** It is moving fast there, so it carries on up the other side. *Why:* A moving thing keeps moving unless something slows it. At the bottom the bob is moving fastest, so it carries on up the far side until gravity slows it.
5. (mcq) Farhan wants to test if the weight of the bob changes the swing time. What should he change?  
   Options: The weight and the length together / Only the weight, keeping the same length and start / Only the length / Everything, to see what happens  
   **Answer:** Only the weight, keeping the same length and start. *Why:* In a fair test you change one thing only. If Farhan changed the length too, he would not know which change made the difference.
6. (mcq) Why do we time 10 full swings instead of just one?  
   Options: Because 10 is a nice number / One swing is too quick to time well, so a small stopwatch mistake matters less over 10 / Because the first nine don't count / Because the pendulum gets faster  
   **Answer:** One swing is too quick to time well, so a small stopwatch mistake matters less over 10. *Why:* One swing takes about a second, and pressing a stopwatch can be off by a few tenths of a second. Over 10 swings, the same mistake is a much smaller part of the time.
7. (mcq) Lakshmi swings 5 coins on a 50 cm thread. Then she takes out 4 coins, leaving 1, and keeps the length the same. What happens to the time for 10 swings?  
   Options: It gets much shorter / It gets much longer / It stays about the same / The pendulum won't swing  
   **Answer:** It stays about the same. *Why:* The weight changes the time very little. Gravity pulls the heavier bob harder, but it also needs more force to move. Thinking it changes a lot is the most common first guess.
8. (mcq) Meena makes her pendulum's thread a quarter as long. Before, 10 swings took 14 seconds. About how long do they take now?  
   Options: About 3 and a half seconds / About 7 seconds / About 14 seconds / About 28 seconds  
   **Answer:** About 7 seconds. *Why:* Making a pendulum 4 times shorter makes it swing twice as fast. Half of 14 seconds is 7 seconds. Many people expect a quarter of the time, but the length and the time do not change in step.
9. (mcq) Arjun pushes his little sister on a swing, first gently and then much harder. What happens to the time for each swing?  
   Options: It gets much quicker / It stays nearly the same, but the swing goes higher / It gets much slower / The swing stops  
   **Answer:** It stays nearly the same, but the swing goes higher. *Why:* A harder push sends the swing further and faster, and the two balance out. Each swing takes nearly the same time. Only very wide swings take a little longer.
10. (mcq) Why does a pendulum with a big card taped on it stop swinging sooner?  
   Options: The card is heavy / The air pushes against the big flat card / The card makes the string shorter / The card makes it swing quicker  
   **Answer:** The air pushes against the big flat card. *Why:* The card pushes a lot of air out of the way, and the air pushes back. That is air resistance. A card weighs very little.
11. (mcq) You push a friend on a swing. When should you push to make them go higher?  
   Options: While they are still coming towards you / Just as they start to move away from you / Any time at all / Only when they are at the bottom, moving fast  
   **Answer:** Just as they start to move away from you. *Why:* Pushes that come at the swing's own beat, as it moves away, add up and make it grow. This is resonance. A push while they come towards you slows them down.
12. (mcq) A pendulum wall clock is running slow. What should you do to the pendulum?  
   Options: Make it a little shorter / Make it a little longer / Add a heavier bob / Push it harder  
   **Answer:** Make it a little shorter. *Why:* A shorter pendulum swings quicker, so the clock ticks faster and catches up. Most clocks have a small nut under the bob that moves it up or down.
13. (mcq) Hoolock gibbons live high in the trees of Assam. How do they travel fast from tree to tree without coming down?  
   Options: They glide like a kite / They swing from one long arm to the other, like a pendulum / They jump like frogs / They walk along the ground  
   **Answer:** They swing from one long arm to the other, like a pendulum. *Why:* Gibbons hang from one long arm and swing forward like a pendulum to the next branch. Gravity does much of the work, so it saves energy.

## Applications
- Pendulum clocks were the most accurate clocks in the world for nearly 300 years, from 1656 until the 1930s.
- Musicians practise with metronomes, which keep a steady beat with a swinging arm.
- Playground swings, cradles and the Onam oonjal are pendulums. Children pump them higher by moving in time with the swing.
- Engineers design tall buildings and bridges so that wind and footsteps do not push them at their own beat and make them sway.
- Some tall buildings hang a huge heavy weight near the top. It swings against the sway and calms the building in strong winds.
- Scientists once measured the strength of gravity in different places by timing very exact pendulums.
- Doctors in the early 1600s used pendulums to compare how fast a patient's pulse was beating.
- Every clock counts a steady back-and-forth beat: a pendulum, the quartz crystal in a watch, or atoms in an atomic clock.

## Open questions
- **What is time, really? Does it flow on its own, or is it only the order in which things happen?** Physicists and philosophers still argue about this. Clocks measure time very exactly, but nobody agrees on what it is.
- **Why do some swinging things, like metronomes on a shared board, fall into step with each other?** The basic idea is understood, but how large groups of swinging things fall into step is still being studied. In 1665, Christiaan Huygens saw two of his pendulum clocks on one beam fall into step, and scientists still study this today.
- **How do our bodies keep their own beats, like the heartbeat and the daily sleep rhythm, so steady?** Scientists know many of the cells and signals involved, but not everything about how they stay so steady over a whole lifetime.
- **Two pendulums joined together, one hanging from the other, swing in wild patterns that never repeat. Can we ever predict them exactly?** This double pendulum follows simple rules, but the tiniest change at the start makes a big difference later. So in practice, nobody can predict it for long.

## Connected maths
- In the Swing Test, 10 full swings of the 50 cm pendulum took 14 seconds. About how long did one full swing take? **About 1 and a half seconds. More exactly, 1.4 seconds.**. One swing is one tenth of 10 swings. 14 seconds shared into 10 equal parts is 1.4 seconds each. That is a little less than one and a half seconds. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- A pendulum takes 1 second for each full swing. How many full swings does it make in 3 minutes? **180 swings.**. There are 60 seconds in a minute, so 60 swings in a minute. In 3 minutes there are 60 + 60 + 60 = 180 seconds, so 180 swings. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Here is a pattern. A pendulum 25 cm long takes 1 second for each swing. One 4 times as long, 1 metre, takes 2 seconds. One 4 times longer again, 4 metres, takes 4 seconds. How long would a 16 metre pendulum take? **8 seconds.**. Each time the length is multiplied by 4, the time is multiplied by 2. 4 metres times 4 is 16 metres. 4 seconds times 2 is 8 seconds. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Ishani timed 10 swings with 5 coins: 14.1 and 14.4 seconds. With 1 coin: 14.3 and 14.0 seconds. Her brother says the 1 coin was quicker, because 14.0 is less than 14.1. Is he right to say so? **No. The two tries with 1 coin added up to 28.3 seconds, and with 5 coins 28.5 seconds. That is only 0.2 seconds apart, which is less than the difference between two tries of the same pendulum. So the weight made no difference she could measure.**. 14.1 + 14.4 = 28.5. 14.3 + 14.0 = 28.3. The two 5-coin tries differ by 0.3 seconds on their own. A difference smaller than that is just the stopwatch, not the coins. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In the Pendulum Wave, the longest pendulum makes 12 swings in 15 seconds and the shortest makes 16. How many more swings does the shortest make in 30 seconds? **8 more swings.**. In 30 seconds, twice as long, the longest makes 24 swings and the shortest makes 32. 32 - 24 = 8. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
