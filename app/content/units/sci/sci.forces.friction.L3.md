# Friction: the force that lets the world hold on
`sci.forces.friction.L3`

**Summary.** When two surfaces touch, they grip each other. That grip is a force called friction, and it always pushes against sliding. Friction is why you can walk, write, tie a knot and stop a bicycle, and also why moving things slow down, wear out and get warm. Rough or soft surfaces usually grip more; oil, water or wheels let things slide or roll more easily. Scientists can measure friction and use it, but they still argue about exactly what happens where two surfaces meet, and even about why ice is slippery.

**Intro.** *Hold a pencil between two fingers, as lightly as you can. Now imagine your fingers and the pencil were perfectly slippery. Could you write your name?* Nearly four thousand years ago, Egyptian workers dragged a stone statue as heavy as ten elephants across the desert on a wooden sledge. A painting shows a man at the front doing something strange: pouring water onto the sand. Was it magic? A prayer? Or did he know something about the force we are going to explore?

## Learning outcomes
0. Explain friction as a force between two touching surfaces that pushes against sliding.
1. Predict and test how the kind of surface changes friction, in a fair test.
2. Read a forcemeter or a ruler to compare frictions, and show results in a table and bar chart.
3. Give examples where we want more friction and where we want less, and how people change it.
4. Explain that friction turns movement into heat and slowly wears things away.
5. Say what scientists agree about friction and what they still do not know.
6. Explain that friction comes from atoms pushing and pulling, and trace it out into tides, our bodies, superfluids and the open questions of quantum physics.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. *e.g.* You push a door open; a magnet pulls a pin.
- **Surface**: The outside layer of a thing, the part that touches other things. *e.g.* The bottom of your shoe; the top of the floor.
- **Friction**: A force where two surfaces touch, that pushes against sliding. *e.g.* Your chappal grips the road, so you don't slip.
- **Grip**: When surfaces hold each other well because there is a lot of friction. *e.g.* Rubber soles grip a wet floor better than smooth plastic soles.
- **Slide**: When one surface moves over another while touching it. *e.g.* A book sliding across a table.
- **Roll**: When a round thing turns as it moves, so only a tiny part touches at a time. *e.g.* A ball rolls; a wheel rolls.
- **Starting friction (static friction)**: The friction that stops something from beginning to slide. It is usually bigger than the friction once it is moving. *e.g.* A heavy almirah is hardest to move at the very start.
- **Sliding friction**: The friction on something that is already sliding. *e.g.* Once the box starts moving, it is a little easier to keep pushing.
- **Lubricant**: Something slippery (oil, water, powder, soap) put between surfaces to lower friction. *e.g.* Oil on a squeaky door hinge; powder on a carrom board.
- **Rough**: A surface with bumps you can feel or see. *e.g.* Sandpaper, a coconut shell, a rough road.
- **Smooth**: A surface with only very tiny bumps, too small to feel. *e.g.* Glass, a polished marble floor. Under a microscope even these have bumps.
- **Forcemeter**: A tool with a spring that measures how big a force is. *e.g.* Pulling a shoe with a forcemeter shows how much friction it has.
- **Newton (N)**: The unit we use to measure force, named after Isaac Newton. *e.g.* Lifting an apple takes about 1 newton.
- **Heat from friction**: When surfaces rub, some of the movement turns into warmth. *e.g.* Rub your hands fast: they get warm.
- **Wear**: Friction slowly rubbing tiny bits off a surface. *e.g.* Old temple steps worn smooth and dipped in the middle by millions of feet.
- **Tread**: The pattern of grooves on a tyre or sole that helps it grip. *e.g.* Bicycle tyres have tread; worn-out tyres are bald and slippery.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block of everything. A grain of sand has more atoms than there are grains of sand on all of India's beaches. *e.g.* Your shoe, the floor and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Electron**: An even tinier part on the outside of every atom, carrying electric charge. When two surfaces touch, it is really their electrons pushing and tugging. *e.g.* Friction, the floor holding you up and a balloon sticking to a wall all come from electric charge.
- **Energy**: What it takes to make something move, warm up or change. Energy never disappears; it only changes form. *e.g.* A rolling ball's movement energy becomes heat when friction stops it.
- **Superfluid**: A very strange liquid, made by cooling helium almost to the coldest possible temperature, that flows with no friction at all. *e.g.* Superfluid helium can creep up the walls of its cup and drip out over the rim.
- **Tide**: The daily rise and fall of the sea, caused mainly by the Moon's gravity. *e.g.* At Mumbai's beaches you can walk far out at low tide.

## Explanations
### What friction is · *defined*
**Definition:** Friction is a force that acts where two surfaces touch. It pushes against sliding, or against the start of sliding.

Friction appears only when surfaces touch and something is trying to slide. It never makes things start moving by itself; it resists. If nothing is pushing a book on a flat table, there is no friction on it at all.
- e.g. A rolling ball on grass slows down and stops.
- e.g. Your feet push back on the ground and friction pushes you forward, so you can walk.
- e.g. A bicycle brake rubs the wheel and slows it.
### Why surfaces grip each other · *debated*
Scientists agree on what friction does, but the deep 'why' has had several answers, and the full story at the level of atoms is still being researched (the science is called tribology).
- e.g. Sandpaper grips because its bumps catch on wood.
- e.g. Two very flat, clean pieces of metal can grip so strongly in space that they stick together (cold welding).
- **Leonardo da Vinci (around 1493) and Guillaume Amontons (1699):** Surfaces are covered in tiny bumps that catch on each other, like the teeth of two combs; to slide, the bumps must ride up over each other.
- **John Desaguliers (1734):** Surfaces stick because their particles attract each other; he showed that two very smooth lead balls pressed together stick. So smoother is not always more slippery.
- **Scientists today:** Both are part of it. Surfaces really touch only at the tips of their tiny bumps. At those tips, atoms of one surface stick to atoms of the other and have to be torn apart as they slide. Exactly how this happens is still being studied.
### Starting is harder than keeping going · *defined*
**Definition:** Static (starting) friction is usually bigger than sliding friction.

When surfaces rest together, their bumps settle into each other and more atoms stick. Once sliding begins, they don't have time to settle, so the grip drops a little.
- e.g. Pushing a heavy box: the first shove is the hardest.
- e.g. A forcemeter pulling a shoe jumps higher just before the shoe moves, then drops back.
### What changes friction · *defined*
**Definition:** Friction depends mainly on the two materials touching and on how hard they are pressed together.

Rubber on stone grips more than plastic on ice. Pressing harder (a heavier box) gives more friction. Surprisingly, for hard things like wooden blocks, the size of the touching area hardly matters: a brick slides about as easily on its big side as on its thin edge. This surprised people for centuries; it doesn't hold for soft, sticky things like rubber tyres or tape.
- e.g. An empty school bag is easier to drag than a full one.
- e.g. A brick flat or on its edge: about the same pull.
- e.g. Racing cars use wide, soft tyres, where area does matter.
### Rolling, lubricants and less friction · *defined*
**Definition:** Rolling and lubricants both reduce friction.

A rolling wheel doesn't scrape along the ground: each bit touches and lifts off. A lubricant (oil, water, soap, powder) keeps surfaces apart so their bumps don't catch as much.
- e.g. Logs or rollers under a heavy stone.
- e.g. Oil on a bullock cart's axle.
- e.g. Powder on a carrom board.
### Friction makes heat and wears things · *defined*
**Definition:** When surfaces rub, some of the energy of movement becomes heat, and tiny bits of the surfaces are rubbed off (wear).

The movement doesn't vanish when something stops: it turns into a little warmth in the surfaces and the air. Over a long time, friction wears down even stone.
- e.g. Rubbing hands warms them.
- e.g. A match lights by friction.
- e.g. Brake pads and eraser tips get used up.
### Why ice is slippery · *debated*
You'd think scientists knew this. They don't fully agree.
- e.g. Skates glide on ice.
- e.g. Very cold, dry ice (around -30 °C) is less slippery than ice near 0 °C.
- **The old textbook idea:** The pressure of a skate melts the ice into water. Most scientists now think this is far too weak to explain it, except perhaps for skates.
- **Michael Faraday (1850) and many scientists today:** Ice always has a very thin wet-like layer on its surface, even without pressure, and that layer is slippery.
- **Other scientists:** Friction itself warms and melts a thin film as you slide; recent experiments also suggest the top molecules of ice roll and tumble like tiny marbles. The full answer is still open.
### Friction is really electricity between atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** All friction comes from the electromagnetic force: the electric pushes and pulls between the electrons and atoms of two surfaces.

Zoom into the tips of the bumps where two surfaces touch and you reach atoms. Their outer electrons repel, and atoms of the two surfaces also tug on each other and briefly bond. Sliding means tearing those little bonds again and again. It is the same force that makes the floor push up on you (the 'normal force' from the Gravity unit) and makes a rubbed balloon stick to a wall. Gravity pulls you down; electricity between atoms holds you up and gives you grip.
- e.g. The floor holding you up and your shoe gripping it are the same kind of force.
- e.g. Two flexible fridge magnets pressed face to face slide with a clicking, sticking feel: a big model of atoms grabbing and letting go.
### Where the movement goes: jiggling atoms, and the arrow of time · *idea*
Heat is atoms jiggling. Friction takes the neat, all-together movement of a sliding box and spreads it into the messy jiggling of trillions of atoms in the box and the floor. That is easy to do and almost impossible to undo: a warm floor never pushes a box back across the room by itself. Physicists agree on this rule (the second law of thermodynamics). Many also think it is why time only seems to go one way, forwards; that bigger idea is still argued about.
- e.g. A film of a box sliding to a stop looks wrong when played backwards.
- e.g. Stir milk into tea: it never un-mixes by itself.
- **Most physicists:** Time's one-way direction comes from things spreading from order into mess, and friction is one of the ways that happens.
- **Other physicists and philosophers:** That explains which way things change, but not what time itself is; the full answer is open.
### Zero friction exists: superfluids · *defined*
**Definition:** A superfluid is a liquid that flows with no friction at all, made by cooling helium to about −271 °C.

At such cold temperatures the rules of quantum physics take over at a size we can see: the helium atoms stop behaving like separate balls and move together, like one single wave. With nothing rubbing against anything, the liquid flows through gaps far too tiny for water, climbs up the walls of its container and keeps swirling for ever once stirred. It was discovered in 1937–38 by Pyotr Kapitsa in Moscow, and separately by John Allen and Don Misener in Cambridge.
- e.g. Stir a superfluid and it keeps going round with no slowing at all.
- e.g. It creeps up and over the rim of its cup as a thin film.
### Friction from empty space: quantum friction · *idea*
Quantum physics says empty space is never perfectly empty: it fizzes with tiny flickers of energy. Some physicists (starting with John Pendry in 1997) worked out that two perfectly smooth surfaces sliding past each other without even touching should still feel a very, very tiny drag through those flickers. Others have argued about whether it really happens. Nobody has measured it yet; experiments are being designed to test it.
- e.g. Two plates sliding past each other a hair's width apart, never touching, in a perfect vacuum.
- **John Pendry and others:** It should exist, but it is far too small to notice in everyday life.
- **Some other physicists:** Some versions of the calculation are wrong, and the effect may be zero or different from predicted.
### Tidal friction is slowing the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** As the oceans' tides sweep over the sea floor, friction slowly brakes the Earth's spin, so the day is getting longer.

The Moon pulls the oceans into bulges, the spinning Earth drags them along, and the water rubs the sea floor. That friction takes a little spin from the Earth: each day is about 2 thousandths of a second longer than a day a hundred years ago. Over hundreds of millions of years it adds up: corals that lived 400 million years ago grew about 400 daily rings a year, so a day was only about 22 hours. The energy goes to the Moon too, which drifts about 3.8 cm further away each year (measured by bouncing lasers off mirrors left by Apollo astronauts).
- e.g. A day in the time of early fish was about 22 hours.
- e.g. The Moon moves away about as fast as your fingernails grow.
### Friction inside living things · *debated*
Living things are friction engineers. Your knee joints rub for a whole lifetime, but cartilage soaked in slippery joint fluid gives them less friction than ice sliding on ice. Gecko and tree-frog feet grip and let go in a moment. And the ridges on your fingertips (your fingerprints)? People used to say they help us grip, but experiments surprised scientists.
- e.g. Knees, hips and fingers move millions of times without wearing out for decades.
- e.g. A gecko runs up glass.
- **The old idea:** Fingerprint ridges help us grip, like the tread on a tyre.
- **Peter Warman and Roland Ennos (2009):** On smooth, dry surfaces, ridges actually lower the grip a little, because less skin touches.
- **Other scientists:** Ridges may help on wet or rough surfaces by letting water escape, and may mostly help us feel textures by vibrating as we touch. The question is still open.

## Mostly agreed
- Friction acts where surfaces touch and pushes against sliding. *How we know:* Every sliding thing on Earth slows down unless something keeps pushing it; in space, with nothing to rub on, things keep moving.
- Friction depends on the materials and on how hard they are pressed together. *How we know:* Measured with forcemeters since Amontons' experiments in 1699 and repeated in classrooms every day.
- Starting friction is usually bigger than sliding friction. *How we know:* The forcemeter reading jumps just before an object starts to slide, then drops.
- Friction turns movement into heat and wears surfaces. *How we know:* Rubbed surfaces get warm; brake pads, shoe soles and steps wear away.
- Friction costs the world a lot of energy. *How we know:* Engineers estimate that nearly a quarter of all the energy people use is lost to friction and wear in machines (Holmberg and Erdemir, 2017).

## Challenging perspectives
- **Moving things stop by themselves because stopping is natural.** (Aristotle, about 2,350 years ago, and most of us without thinking about it; *rejected*)  
  For: It is what we see every day: balls, carts and bicycles all stop if nobody keeps pushing.  
  Against: Galileo noticed that the smoother the surface, the further a ball rolls. Things stop because friction (and air) slows them. In space a pushed ball would keep going for ever.
- **Friction is a nuisance that we should get rid of.** (A common way of thinking about machines; *rejected*)  
  For: Friction wastes energy and wears out engines, shoes and roads.  
  Against: With no friction you could not walk, hold food, tie a knot, light a fire, stop a cycle, play a violin or sit on a chair without sliding off. We need the right amount in the right place.
- **Amontons discovered the laws of friction in 1699.** (Many physics books; *debated*)  
  For: Amontons published the laws, and published work is what science usually credits.  
  Against: Leonardo da Vinci had written the same rules in his notebooks about 200 years earlier, but the notebooks were not published, so his work was forgotten for centuries.
- **The Egyptian painting shows water being poured as a religious ritual, not for any practical reason.** (Some Egyptologists in the past; *debated*)  
  For: Pouring water was also a part of rituals in Egypt.  
  Against: In 2014 physicists in Amsterdam showed that the right amount of water on sand can roughly halve the force needed to pull a sledge. The workers seem to have known something scientists only explained 3,900 years later.
- **Ice is slippery because pressure melts it.** (Many older textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: Very strong pressure can lower ice's melting point.  
  Against: The pressure from a shoe is far too small to melt ice, yet ice is slippery even when you stand still on it.
- **Meteors and returning spacecraft get hot because of air friction.** (Many books and films; *rejected*)  
  For: They get extremely hot moving through air, and rubbing makes heat.  
  Against: Most of the heat comes from squashing the air in front of them so fast that it heats up enormously (like a bicycle pump getting hot, but far stronger). Friction with the air plays a much smaller part.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If you rolled a marble in empty space, would it ever stop? Why does everything on Earth stop? (hint: What is the marble rubbing against on Earth that isn't there in space?)
- *deep:* When a rolling ball stops, where has its movement gone? (hint: Rub your hands. What do you feel?)
- *deep:* Is there anything in the world with no friction at all? (hint: Scientists have found a few very strange cold liquids that flow with almost none. Why is it so rare?)
- *big:* Would a world without friction be a better world or a worse one? What would you miss first? (hint: Imagine breakfast, the walk to the park, writing, hugging.)
- *big:* Friction slows things down, but it also lets us move forward when we walk. Can the same thing be both a help and a hindrance? (hint: Can you think of other things in life that are both?)
- *big:* People say two friends have 'friction' when they disagree. Is anything useful made when people rub against each other's ideas? (hint: Rubbing makes heat and grip. What can disagreement make?)
- *big:* The pullers were praised and the water-pourer was laughed at, yet his small job made the statue move. Who gets remembered for big achievements, and who gets forgotten? (hint: Who poured the water in your family or school today?)
- *big:* Friction turns a sliding box's movement into jiggling atoms, and it never turns back. Is that why we remember yesterday but not tomorrow? (hint: Play a video of a ball rolling to a stop backwards. Why does it look wrong?)
- *deep:* Your knees rub for eighty years without wearing out. How might a body make something more slippery than ice? (hint: Think about what's wet, soft and keeps repairing itself.)
- *big:* If even 'empty' space might cause friction, is anything truly empty? (hint: What do you think is in the space between two hands that aren't quite touching?)
- *big:* The ocean's friction is pushing the Moon slowly away and making days longer. Is anything in the universe permanent? (hint: What was the length of a day when dinosaurs lived? Will people one day have 25-hour days?)

## Story: The Water-Pourer
*The tomb painting and the 2014 experiment are real. Mery, Hapu and their days are imagined.*

Nearly four thousand years ago, at the edge of the Egyptian desert, a stone giant lay on its back on a wooden sledge. It was a statue of a governor called Djehutihotep, carved from a single block of stone, taller than four men standing on each other's shoulders and as heavy as ten elephants. Between the giant and the river lay a whole day of sand.

Mery was ten, and she had not been chosen to pull. The pullers were the strong ones: more than a hundred and seventy men in four long lines, ropes over their shoulders, toes dug into the sand. Mery's job was to carry jars of water from the river to her father, Hapu. And Hapu's job, the whole of it, was to walk at the very front of the sledge and pour water onto the sand.

The pullers laughed at him. 'Look, Hapu is watering the desert,' they called. 'Will you grow us some cucumbers, Hapu?' Hapu only smiled and kept pouring, not a flood, not a trickle, but a steady curl of water that turned the pale sand just darker, the colour of a ripe date. Mery noticed that he never let it turn into mud. When it puddled, he waited. When it dried, he poured.

On the first day, the giant moved. The foreman beat the rhythm on a drum, the men leaned and heaved together, and the sledge slid forward, one long breath at a time, across the darkened sand.

That night Hapu shook with fever. In the morning he could not stand. 'No matter,' said the foreman. 'Pouring water is not real work. Today the men will pull harder.'

So they pulled harder. The drum beat. The ropes creaked. The sledge did not move. The front of it pressed into the dry sand and pushed up a little hill in front of itself, like a cow trying to walk through a heap of grain. The more the men pulled, the deeper it dug. One rope snapped with a crack like a whip, and three men tumbled into each other. Someone said the statue was cursed.

Mery stood with her jar and her heart thumping. The jar was heavy and she was only ten, and nobody had asked her anything. But she had watched her father for a whole day. She walked to the front of the sledge, past the men glaring at her, and poured.

She poured too much. The sand turned to mud, the sledge sank into it, and the men groaned. 'Get away, child,' the foreman snapped.

Mery's face burned. She stepped back and looked at the mud and at the dry sand beside it, and she thought about the colour of a ripe date. Not too wet, not too dry. She moved a little to the side, tipped the jar gently, and drew a steady curl of water across the sand, just enough to darken it. She waited. She poured again.

The sand changed under her feet. Dry, it had shifted and slid away like sugar; now it held together, firm as a path, with no little hill rising in front of the sledge. 'Pull,' whispered Mery, and then, louder, 'Pull now!'

The drum beat. The men leaned. And the stone giant, as heavy as ten elephants, slid forward over a thin skin of darkened sand, as smoothly as a fish through a river.

All that day Mery walked at the front, pouring and waiting and pouring, while the giant followed her like a patient animal. Nobody laughed. Late in the afternoon one of the pullers, the one who had asked about cucumbers, came and carried her empty jar to the river and filled it for her.

When the statue was finally standing, the governor's painters painted the whole story on the wall of his tomb: the giant on its sledge, the four long lines of pullers, the drummers, the foreman. And at the very front, so small you could miss him, a single figure pouring water from a jar onto the sand.

Nobody wrote down Hapu's name, or Mery's. But somebody painted them. And nearly four thousand years later, in a laboratory in Amsterdam, scientists pulled a tiny sledge across trays of sand, some dry, some damp, some soaked, and measured what a water-pourer at the edge of the desert had known all along.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the statue moves not because people pull harder but because the surface where sledge meets sand changes. Dry sand piles up and resists; just-damp sand holds together and the sledge slides; too much water makes mud. Friction lives at the meeting place of two surfaces, and a small change there matters more than a big change in pulling. The story also quietly asks who gets credit for great work.

- Ask: Why didn't pulling harder work?
- Ask: What did the right amount of water do to the sand? What did too much do?
- Ask: Why do you think the pullers laughed at Hapu's job?
- Ask: Who is the 'water-pourer' in your home, the person whose small job makes everything else work?
- Ask: Why do you think the painters included the water-pourer, even though he was small?

## Worksheets
### Friction Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Friction is a force that acts…  
   Options: only on moving cars / where two surfaces touch / only in water / in empty space  
   **Answer:** where two surfaces touch. *Why:* Friction happens wherever two surfaces touch and something tries to slide.
2. (mcq) Friction always pushes…  
   Options: the same way something is moving / against sliding / upwards / towards the Sun  
   **Answer:** against sliding. *Why:* Friction pushes against the sliding, so it slows things down or stops them starting.
3. (fill) A ball rolling on grass slows down because of ____.  
   **Answer:** ['friction']. *Why:* The grass rubs against the ball and slows it.
4. (sort) Sort: do we want MORE or LESS friction?  
   **Answer:** shoe on a wet floor → More friction; playground slide → Less friction; bicycle brake → More friction; carrom striker → Less friction; holding a cricket bat → More friction; squeaky door hinge → Less friction. *Why:* We want grip where we need to hold, stop or push off; we want slip where things should glide.
5. (mcq) Which surface will a toy car roll furthest on?  
   Options: carpet / sand / smooth tiles / grass  
   **Answer:** smooth tiles. *Why:* Smooth tiles have the least friction, so the car slows down least.
6. (truefalse) Rough surfaces usually have more friction than smooth ones.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* Bumps catch on each other more, so it is harder to slide.
7. (truefalse) Friction only causes problems.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* We need friction to walk, hold things, write and stop.
8. (match) Match each thing to how it changes friction.  
   **Answer:** oil on a hinge → less friction; grooves on a tyre → more grip; powder on a carrom board → less friction; sand on an icy path → more grip. *Why:* Lubricants lower friction; grooves and grit raise grip.
9. (mcq) Rub your hands together fast. They feel…  
   Options: colder / warmer / wetter / the same  
   **Answer:** warmer. *Why:* Friction turns movement into heat.
10. (fill) Something slippery put between surfaces to lower friction is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lubricant']. *Why:* Oil, water, soap and powder are lubricants.
11. (mcq) Why do bicycle tyres have bumpy patterns (tread)?  
   Options: to look nice / to grip the road better / to make the tyre lighter / to make a noise  
   **Answer:** to grip the road better. *Why:* Tread lets the tyre grip, and lets water escape on wet roads.
12. (mcq) Which is usually hardest?  
   Options: starting to push a heavy box / keeping it moving once it slides / they are always exactly the same  
   **Answer:** starting to push a heavy box. *Why:* Starting friction is usually bigger than sliding friction.
13. (fill) A forcemeter measures force in ____.  
   **Answer:** ['newtons', 'N', 'newton']. *Why:* The newton (N) is the unit of force.
14. (write) Why can't you walk on very slippery ice easily? Use the word friction.  
   **Answer:** There is very little friction between my shoes and the ice, so when I push my foot back it slides instead of pushing me forward.. *Why:* Walking needs friction: your foot pushes back on the ground and friction pushes you forward.
15. (write) Name two places at home where you are glad there is friction, and say why.  
   **Answer:** For example: the bathroom mat stops me slipping; my pencil grips the paper so I can write; a knot holds my hair tie.. *Why:* Any two correct examples with a reason.
16. (draw) Draw a shoe on a slope. Draw an arrow for the way it wants to slide and an arrow for friction.  
   **Answer:** Slide arrow pointing down the slope; friction arrow pointing up the slope along the surface.. *Why:* Friction acts along the surface, against the sliding.
### Friction in the Real World (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Riya pulls a box of books with a forcemeter. Full box: 8 N. Half-empty box: 4 N. Why?  
   Options: the full box is pressed down harder, so more friction / the half box is rougher / forcemeters lie / the floor changed  
   **Answer:** the full box is pressed down harder, so more friction. *Why:* Pressing harder together gives more friction.

   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* 12 ÷ 4 = 3.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* With no friction or air to slow it, nothing stops it (until it hits something or is pulled by gravity).
8. (mcq) Which helps a heavy stone move most easily?  
   Options: dragging it on dry sand / rolling it on logs / pushing it uphill / tying it to another stone  
   **Answer:** rolling it on logs. *Why:* Rolling has much less friction than sliding.
9. (match) Match the person to their idea about friction.  
   **Answer:** Leonardo da Vinci → wrote rules of friction in his notebooks around 1493; Guillaume Amontons → published the laws of friction in 1699; Galileo → realised things stop because of friction, not because stopping is natural. *Why:* Leonardo came first but did not publish, so Amontons usually gets the credit.
10. (mcq) In the Egyptian painting, why might the man be pouring water on the sand?  
   Options: to cool his feet / damp sand lets the sledge slide with less friction / to make mud bricks / to wash the statue  
   **Answer:** damp sand lets the sledge slide with less friction. *Why:* The right amount of water makes sand firm, so the sledge doesn't dig in.
11. (mcq) What happens if you pour too MUCH water on the sand?  
   Options: it gets even easier / it becomes mud and the sledge sinks / nothing changes / the sand catches fire  
   **Answer:** it becomes mud and the sledge sinks. *Why:* Too much water makes mud, and the sledge sinks in.
12. (write) A heavy almirah won't move. Give two different ways to make it easier to push, and explain each using the word friction.  
   **Answer:** Take things out of it so it presses down less and has less friction; put a cloth or rollers under it so it slides or rolls with less friction.. *Why:* Less pressing force, a slippery layer, or rolling all lower friction.
13. (write) Why do your hands get warm when you rub them? Where does the movement go?  
   **Answer:** Friction between my hands turns the movement into heat, so the movement doesn't vanish, it becomes warmth.. *Why:* Friction changes movement energy into heat.
14. (forces) Show the forces on a box being pushed to the right across the floor.  
   Options: push / friction / gravity / floor pushes up  
   **Answer:** push right, friction left, gravity down, floor pushes up up. *Why:* Friction acts against the push; gravity pulls down; the floor pushes up.
15. (draw) Design a shoe sole for walking on wet, mossy temple steps. Label two features that increase grip.  
   **Answer:** Soft rubber; deep grooves to let water out; small bumps or studs; a wide flat area.. *Why:* Rubber and grooves increase grip on wet surfaces.
### Friction Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 19 questions)
1. (mcq) A brick is pulled lying flat (big side down), then on its thin edge. The forcemeter shows about the same both times. What does this suggest?  
   Options: friction depends a lot on how much area touches / for a hard brick, friction hardly depends on the touching area / the forcemeter is broken / edges have no friction  
   **Answer:** for a hard brick, friction hardly depends on the touching area. *Why:* Amontons' surprising law: for hard materials, area hardly matters; what matters is the materials and how hard they press.
2. (mcq) Then why do racing cars have very wide tyres?  
   Options: the law only works for hard materials; soft sticky rubber grips more with more area / for decoration / wide tyres are lighter / to go slower  
   **Answer:** the law only works for hard materials; soft sticky rubber grips more with more area. *Why:* The 'area doesn't matter' rule breaks down for soft, sticky materials like rubber.
3. (truefalse) Scientists completely understand why ice is slippery.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* It is still debated: a slippery surface layer, melting by friction, loose rolling molecules.
4. (mcq) Aristotle thought moving things stop because stopping is natural. What would change his mind?  
   Options: seeing a ball roll further on smoother surfaces, as if it would never stop with no friction / seeing a ball stop on grass / reading a book / nothing could  
   **Answer:** seeing a ball roll further on smoother surfaces, as if it would never stop with no friction. *Why:* Galileo saw that less friction means longer rolling, so friction, not nature, stops things.
5. (mcq) Which is NOT caused by friction?  
   Options: a match lighting / a cycle stopping when you brake / a magnet pulling a pin from across the table / your shoe wearing out  
   **Answer:** a magnet pulling a pin from across the table. *Why:* A magnet pulls without touching; friction needs touching surfaces.
6. (fill) Engineers estimate that nearly a ____ of all the energy people use is lost to friction and wear in machines.  
   **Answer:** ['quarter', 'fourth', '1/4']. *Why:* Holmberg and Erdemir (2017) estimated about 23%.
7. (order) Put these in order from MOST friction to LEAST: ice, rubber mat, polished floor, sandpaper.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Rough sandpaper grips most; ice is slipperiest.
8. (mcq) Two notebooks with their pages laid in between each other are very hard to pull apart. Why?  
   Options: glue / hundreds of pages each add a little friction, and pulling presses them together more / magnetism / the books are heavy  
   **Answer:** hundreds of pages each add a little friction, and pulling presses them together more. *Why:* Many small frictions add up, and pulling squeezes the pages, adding even more.
9. (mcq) Why might Leonardo da Vinci's friction rules have been forgotten for 200 years?  
   Options: they were wrong / his notebooks were not published, so other scientists never read them / he wrote in code on purpose to hide them / nobody cared about friction  
   **Answer:** his notebooks were not published, so other scientists never read them. *Why:* Credit in science usually goes to whoever publishes; unpublished work can be lost.
10. (write) Hapu's job looked unimportant but made the statue move. Think of a 'small' job in your home or school that makes everything else work. What would happen without it?  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer (e.g. the person who fills the water bottles, sweeps, cooks) with a clear 'what would happen'.. *Why:* Connects the science (small change at the meeting place) to who is noticed and who isn't.
11. (write) Invent a machine that would work better WITHOUT friction and one that could not work without it. Explain both.  
   **Answer:** e.g. A frictionless wheel bearing would waste no energy; brakes could not work without friction because they need to rub to stop the wheel.. *Why:* Shows understanding that friction is useful in some places and wasteful in others.
12. (write) Some scientists think atoms of two surfaces stick to each other; others first thought bumps catch like comb teeth. How could you design a test to tell which matters more?  
   **Answer:** e.g. compare very rough and very polished pieces of the same material: if polishing lowers friction, bumps matter; if very polished pieces stick more, sticking atoms matter.. *Why:* A good answer changes one thing (roughness) and keeps the rest the same.
13. (mcq) In space, astronauts must hold on to handles. Why?  
   Options: there's no gravity to keep them pressed to the floor, so there's little friction between feet and floor / the floor is wet / handles are fun / space is cold  
   **Answer:** there's no gravity to keep them pressed to the floor, so there's little friction between feet and floor. *Why:* Friction depends on how hard surfaces press together; floating astronauts barely press on the floor.
14. (write) Is friction a 'good' or a 'bad' force? Give your opinion with two reasons, one for each side.  
   **Answer:** A balanced answer: good because we can walk and stop; bad because it wastes energy and wears things; perhaps 'it depends where'.. *Why:* There is no single right answer; reasons from both sides are what count.
15. (draw) Draw a close-up of two 'smooth' surfaces touching, as a microscope would show them. Mark where they really touch.  
   **Answer:** Two bumpy, mountain-like edges touching only at a few peaks, with the peaks marked.. *Why:* Even smooth surfaces are bumpy up close and touch only at a few tiny points.
16. (mcq) Deep down, where does friction come from?  
   Options: atoms tugging on each other with electric force / tiny glue on everything / gravity pulling things sideways / magic  
   **Answer:** atoms tugging on each other with electric force. *Why:* Friction is electromagnetic: electrons and atoms of the two surfaces push and pull.
17. (mcq) Superfluid helium, stirred once, keeps swirling for ever. Why?  
   Options: it has no friction at all / it is very heavy / it is very hot / someone keeps stirring  
   **Answer:** it has no friction at all. *Why:* With zero friction, nothing turns its movement into heat, so it never slows.
18. (truefalse) Friction in the oceans is making Earth's days very slowly longer.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* Tides rubbing the sea floor brake the Earth's spin; the Moon drifts away about 3.8 cm a year.
19. (write) A film shows a box sliding across a floor and stopping. How could you tell if the film was playing backwards?  
   **Answer:** Backwards, the box would start moving all by itself from a warm floor, which never happens: friction only turns movement into heat, not heat back into movement.. *Why:* Friction is one-way; this is linked to the 'arrow of time'.

## Applications
- Brakes on bicycles, scooters, cars and trains
- Shoe soles, tyre tread and anti-slip mats
- Matches and the first fires made by rubbing sticks
- Writing with pencils, chalk and crayons
- Knots, threads and woven cloth (threads hold by friction)
- Violin, sarangi and esraj bows (rosin makes them grip the strings)
- Oil and grease in engines, cycle chains and bullock-cart axles
- Ball bearings in fans, wheels and skateboards
- Carrom powder, ski wax and playground slides
- Sandpaper, files and grinding stones
- Earthquakes: rocks stick and suddenly slip along faults
- Maglev trains (like Shanghai's) float on magnets to beat wheel friction
- Air hockey and hovercraft ride on a cushion of air
- Hip and knee replacements, designed for very low friction
- Superfluid helium cools the giant magnets of the Large Hadron Collider

## Open questions
- **Exactly what happens between the atoms of two surfaces as they slide?** It happens in tiny spaces we can hardly see; scientists use special microscopes and computer models and still argue.
- **Why is ice slippery?** Several explanations compete: a surface layer, melting by friction, loose rolling molecules.
- **Can we make friction almost disappear?** Some materials like layers of graphene slide with almost no friction (superlubricity), but only in tiny pieces so far.
- **Can we predict earthquakes from friction on faults?** Faults stick and slip in ways that are still too complex to predict.
- **How do gecko and frog feet grip and let go so quickly?** Scientists understand parts of it and keep copying it in new materials.
- **Does 'quantum friction' between surfaces that never touch really exist?** It is predicted by some calculations, argued about, and has never been measured.
- **Is friction's one-way nature the reason time flows forwards?** Physicists agree on the rule that mess grows; whether that is what time's direction really is, is still debated.
- **What exactly do fingerprints do?** Experiments show they don't simply add grip; they may help on wet surfaces or mainly help us feel.

## Connected maths
- The shoe started sliding when the end of the board was 12 cm high. The sock slid at 4 cm. How many times higher did the shoe go? **3 times**. 12 ÷ 4 = 3: the shoe stayed on a slope 3 times as high. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Read the forcemeter scale: the pointer is 2 small marks past 4 N, and each small mark is half a newton. What is the reading? **5 N**. 4 + ½ + ½ = 5 N. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- Make a bar chart: rubber chappal 14 cm, school shoe 10 cm, sock 4 cm, slipper 7 cm. Which is the second grippiest? **the school shoe**. In order 14, 10, 7, 4: the school shoe (10 cm) is second. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Two notebooks of 40 pages each are interleaved. Each touching pair of pages adds a little friction. About how many page-to-page touching places are there? **about 79**. 80 pages laid alternately make 79 meeting places (one fewer than the pages). (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- The Moon moves about 4 cm further from Earth every year. About how far will it move in a lifetime of 80 years? Give your answer in metres and centimetres. **about 320 cm, which is 3 m 20 cm**. 80 groups of 4: 4 × 80 = 320 cm; 100 cm = 1 m, so 3 m 20 cm. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
