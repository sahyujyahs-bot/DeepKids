# Friction: the grip that works against sliding
`sci.forces.friction.L3`

**Summary.** Friction is the grip between two surfaces that touch, and it always works against sliding. It is why your chappal does not slip on the road, and why a rolling ball slows down and stops. Friction also turns movement into heat and slowly wears things away. Rough surfaces, rubber and pressing harder give more friction, while oil, water and wheels give less. Scientists can measure friction well, but they still argue about exactly why surfaces grip, and even why ice is slippery.

**Intro.** *Try this. Hold a pencil between two fingers, as lightly as you can. Now imagine your fingers and the pencil were perfectly slippery, like wet soap. Could you still write your name?* Nearly four thousand years ago, workers in Egypt moved a huge stone statue across the desert. It was about as heavy as ten elephants. They dragged it on a wooden sledge, a big flat sled with no wheels. An old wall painting shows the scene. At the front, one man is doing something strange. He is pouring water onto the sand. Why would he do that? By the end of this unit, you will know. It has to do with friction, the grip between things that touch.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces make things start moving, stop, speed up, slow down or turn. *e.g.* You push a door to open it. A magnet pulls a pin towards it.
- **Surface**: The outside of a thing. It is the part that touches other things. *e.g.* The bottom of your shoe is a surface. So is the top of the floor.
- **Friction**: The grip between two things that touch. Friction always works against sliding. *e.g.* Your chappal grips the road, so you don't slip when you walk.
- **Grip**: When two surfaces hold on to each other well. Lots of friction means good grip. *e.g.* Rubber soles grip a wet bathroom floor better than smooth plastic soles.
- **Slide**: When one thing moves across another while still touching it. *e.g.* A book sliding across a table. A child going down a playground slide.
- **Roll**: When a round thing turns over and over as it moves. Only a tiny part of it touches the ground at any moment. *e.g.* A ball rolls. A wheel rolls. Rolling a heavy thing is much easier than dragging it.
- **Starting friction (static friction)**: The grip that stops a thing from starting to slide. It is usually a little stronger than the grip once the thing is moving. Scientists call it static friction. *e.g.* A heavy almirah is hardest to move at the very first push.
- **Sliding friction**: The grip on a thing that is already sliding. It is usually a little weaker than starting friction. *e.g.* Once the almirah starts moving, it is a bit easier to keep pushing.
- **Lubricant**: Something slippery put between two surfaces to make the friction smaller. Oil, water, soap and powder are lubricants. *e.g.* Oil on a squeaky door hinge. Powder on a carrom board.
- **Rough**: A surface with bumps you can feel or see. *e.g.* Sandpaper, a coconut shell and a stony road are all rough.
- **Smooth**: A surface whose bumps are too tiny to feel. Under a microscope, even smooth things are bumpy. *e.g.* Glass and a polished marble floor feel smooth.
- **Forcemeter**: A tool that measures how strong a pull is. Inside it is a spring. The harder you pull, the more the spring stretches, and a pointer moves along a scale of numbers. *e.g.* Hook a forcemeter to a shoe and pull the shoe along the floor. The number shows how much friction you are pulling against.
- **Newton (N)**: The unit for measuring force, just as the centimetre is a unit for length. It is named after the scientist Isaac Newton. The short way to write it is N. *e.g.* Holding up a small apple takes a force of about 1 newton.
- **Heat from friction**: When two things rub together, some of their movement turns into warmth. *e.g.* Rub your palms together fast. They get warm.
- **Wear**: Friction very slowly rubbing tiny bits off a surface. *e.g.* Old temple steps have a dip in the middle. Millions of feet rubbed the stone away over hundreds of years.
- **Tread**: The pattern of grooves on a tyre or a shoe sole. It helps them grip, and lets water escape on wet ground. *e.g.* New bicycle tyres have deep tread. Old tyres worn smooth are called bald, and they slip more easily.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. One grain of sand has more atoms in it than there are grains of sand on all of India's beaches. *e.g.* Your shoe, the floor and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Electron**: An even tinier particle on the outside of every atom. Electrons carry electric charge, so they push and pull on each other. When two surfaces touch, it is really their electrons pushing and tugging. *e.g.* Friction, the floor holding you up, and a rubbed balloon sticking to a wall all come from electric pushes and pulls.
- **Energy**: What it takes to make something move, warm up or change. Energy never disappears. It only changes from one kind into another. *e.g.* A rolling ball has movement energy. When friction stops the ball, that energy becomes a tiny bit of heat.
- **Superfluid**: A very strange liquid that flows with no friction at all. Scientists make it by cooling helium, a gas, until it is almost as cold as anything can ever be. *e.g.* Superfluid helium can creep up the inside wall of its cup, over the rim, and drip off the outside.
- **Tide**: The sea rising and falling, about twice a day. It happens mainly because the Moon's gravity pulls on the oceans. *e.g.* At Mumbai's beaches, you can walk far out on the sand at low tide.

## Explanations
### What friction is · *defined*
**Definition:** Friction is a force that acts where two surfaces touch. It works against sliding, or against something starting to slide.

Friction only appears when two things touch and one of them is sliding, or is being pushed or pulled to slide. It always acts against the sliding. Think of a book lying still on a flat table. Nobody is pushing it, so there is no friction on it at all. Now give the book a gentle push. Friction pushes back the opposite way, and the book soon stops.
- e.g. A ball rolling on grass slows down and stops. The grass rubs against it.
- e.g. When you walk, your foot pushes back on the ground. Friction pushes your foot forward, so you move ahead.
- e.g. A bicycle brake rubs against the wheel. The rubbing slows the wheel down.
### Why surfaces grip each other · *debated*
Everyone agrees on what friction does. The harder question is why two surfaces grip each other at all. Over the centuries, scientists have given different answers. The study of friction even has its own name: tribology. At the level of atoms, the full story is still being worked out.
- e.g. Sandpaper grips wood because its sharp bumps catch on the wood.
- e.g. In space, two very flat, very clean pieces of metal can stick together when they touch. Scientists call this cold welding.
- **Leonardo da Vinci, an Italian artist and inventor, around 1493, and Guillaume Amontons, a French scientist, in 1699:** Every surface is covered in tiny bumps, even if it feels smooth. When two surfaces touch, the bumps catch on each other, like the teeth of two combs pushed together. To slide, the bumps must climb up over each other.
- **John Desaguliers, a scientist in London, in 1734:** Surfaces stick because their tiny particles pull on each other. He pressed two very smooth lead balls together, and they stuck. So smoother does not always mean more slippery.
- **Scientists today:** Both ideas are partly right. Two surfaces really touch only at the tips of their tiny bumps. At those tips, atoms of one surface stick to atoms of the other. As the surfaces slide, those little joins are torn apart again and again. Exactly how this happens is still being studied.
### Starting is harder than keeping going · *defined*
**Definition:** It is harder to start something sliding than to keep it sliding. The grip that stops things starting is called static friction. Once a thing is sliding, the grip is a little weaker. That weaker grip is called sliding friction.

When two surfaces rest together, their tiny bumps settle into each other, and more of their atoms stick. So at the start, you have to pull hard to break them free. Once the thing is sliding, the bumps have no time to settle. So the grip drops a little, and pulling gets easier. The graph shows this. A forcemeter, a spring tool that measures pulls, is pulling a shoe across the floor. The pull rises while the shoe stays still. The moment the shoe starts to slide, the pull drops. Then it stays about the same.
- e.g. Pushing a heavy box: the very first shove is the hardest. After that it gets a little easier.
- e.g. Pull a shoe with a forcemeter. The number climbs, then drops back a little the moment the shoe starts to slide.
### What changes friction · *defined*
**Definition:** Friction depends mainly on two things. First, what the two surfaces are made of. Second, how hard they are pressed together.

Rubber on stone grips much more than plastic on ice. That is the first thing: the materials. Pressing harder also gives more friction. A full bag presses on the floor harder than an empty one, so it is harder to drag. Here is the surprise. For hard things like bricks and wooden blocks, the size of the touching part hardly matters. A brick slides about as easily on its big flat side as on its thin edge. The pictures show made-up readings for a brick pulled both ways. People were puzzled by this for hundreds of years. The rule does not work for soft, sticky things like rubber tyres or sticky tape.
- e.g. An empty school bag is easier to drag than a full one.
- e.g. A brick lying flat and the same brick standing on its edge need about the same pull.
- e.g. Racing cars use wide, soft tyres. For soft rubber, more touching area does give more grip.
### Rolling, lubricants and less friction · *defined*
**Definition:** Rolling and lubricants both make friction smaller. A lubricant is something slippery, like oil, water, soap or powder, put between two surfaces.

A wheel does not scrape along the ground. As it rolls, each part touches the ground and then lifts off again. So there is very little rubbing. A lubricant works in a different way. It makes a thin, slippery layer between two surfaces. The layer keeps them a little apart, so their tiny bumps do not catch as much.
- e.g. Round logs or rollers under a heavy stone make it far easier to move.
- e.g. Oil on a bullock cart's axle, the rod the wheel turns on, helps the wheel spin.
- e.g. Powder on a carrom board lets the striker glide.
### Friction makes heat and wears things · *defined*
**Definition:** When surfaces rub, some of the movement turns into heat. Tiny bits of the surfaces also get rubbed off. This rubbing away is called wear.

The movement does not vanish when a thing stops. It turns into a little warmth in the surfaces and in the air around them. Rubbing also scrapes off tiny bits, far too small to see one at a time. Over a long time, this wears away even stone.
- e.g. Rub your hands together fast and they get warm.
- e.g. A match lights because rubbing it on the box makes its tip very hot.
- e.g. Brake pads, shoe soles and the tips of erasers slowly get used up.
### Why ice is slippery · *debated*
You might think scientists worked this out long ago. They have not. There are a few main ideas, and scientists still argue about which ones matter most.
- e.g. Ice skates glide easily over ice.
- e.g. Very cold ice, at about 30 degrees below zero, is less slippery than ice that is close to melting.
- **The old textbook idea:** The weight of a skate presses on the ice and melts it into water. Most scientists now think this pressure is far too weak to explain it, except perhaps under thin skate blades.
- **Michael Faraday, an English scientist, in 1850, and many scientists today:** Ice always has a very thin, almost wet layer on its surface, even when nothing presses on it. That layer is slippery.
- **Other scientists:** Sliding itself makes heat, and the heat melts a thin film of water. Some recent experiments also suggest the top layer of ice molecules can roll and tumble like tiny marbles. The full answer is still open.
### Friction is really electricity between atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** All friction comes from electric pushes and pulls between the atoms of two surfaces. Scientists call this the electromagnetic force.

Zoom in to where two surfaces touch, and keep zooming. You reach atoms. The outer parts of atoms are electrons, which carry electric charge. Electrons of the two surfaces push each other away. Atoms of the two surfaces also tug on each other and hold on for a moment. Sliding means breaking those little holds again and again. This same electric force makes the floor push up on you, so you don't sink into it. It also makes a rubbed balloon stick to a wall. Gravity pulls you down. Electric forces between atoms hold you up and give you grip.
- e.g. The floor holding you up and your shoe gripping the floor are the same kind of force.
- e.g. Press two thin fridge magnets face to face and slide one. You feel a clicky, sticky slide. It is a giant model of atoms attracting each other and letting go.
### Where the movement goes: jiggling atoms, and the arrow of time · *idea*
Heat is really atoms jiggling. Think of a box sliding across the floor. All its atoms move together, neatly, in one direction. Friction takes that neat movement and spreads it out. It becomes the messy jiggling of countless atoms in the box and the floor. That is easy to do, and almost impossible to undo. A warm floor never pushes a box back across the room by itself. Physicists agree on this rule. They call it the second law of thermodynamics. Many think it is also why time only seems to go one way, forwards. That bigger idea is still argued about.
- e.g. Film a box sliding to a stop, then play it backwards. It looks wrong straight away.
- e.g. Stir milk into tea. It never un-mixes by itself.
- **Most physicists:** Time seems to go one way because things always spread from neat order into mess. Friction is one of the ways that happens.
- **Other physicists and philosophers:** That explains which way things change. It does not explain what time itself is. That question is still open.
### Zero friction exists: superfluids · *defined*
**Definition:** A superfluid is a liquid that flows with no friction at all. Scientists make one by cooling helium to about 271 degrees below zero.

That is colder than anywhere in nature on Earth, and close to the coldest anything can ever be. At this cold, the strange rules of quantum physics show up at a size we can see. Quantum physics is the science of the very tiniest things. The helium atoms stop acting like separate little balls. They move together, like one single wave. Because the atoms move as one, there is nothing inside the liquid to rub. So the liquid can flow through gaps far too tiny for water. It can climb up the walls of its cup. Once stirred, it keeps swirling without slowing down. It was discovered in 1937 and 1938. Pyotr Kapitsa found it in Moscow. John Allen and Don Misener found it separately in Cambridge, England.
- e.g. Stir a superfluid and it keeps going round and round without slowing.
- e.g. It creeps up and over the rim of its cup as a very thin layer.
### Friction from empty space: quantum friction · *idea*
Quantum physics is the science of the very tiniest things. It says empty space is never perfectly empty. It fizzes with tiny flickers of energy. Some physicists have worked out what this means for sliding. One of the first was John Pendry, in 1997. Two perfectly smooth surfaces sliding past each other, very close but not touching, should still feel a tiny drag. The drag would come through those flickers. Other physicists have argued about whether this really happens. Nobody has measured it yet. Experiments are being planned to test it.
- e.g. Imagine two plates sliding past each other a hair's width apart, never touching, with no air between them.
- **John Pendry, a British physicist, and others:** It should exist, but it is far too small to notice in everyday life.
- **Some other physicists:** Some ways of calculating it are wrong. The real drag might be zero, or different from what was predicted.
### Tidal friction is slowing the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Tides are the sea rising and falling, about twice a day. As tides move over the sea floor, friction slowly brakes the Earth's spin. So each day is getting very slightly longer.

The Moon's gravity pulls the oceans into a bulge. The spinning Earth drags that bulge along, and the water rubs against the sea floor. This friction takes away a little of the Earth's spin. Because of it, a day gets about 2 thousandths of a second longer every 100 years. That is far too small to notice. But over hundreds of millions of years, it adds up. Corals that lived 400 million years ago grew one thin line each day. They grew about 400 lines a year. So a year then had about 400 days, and each day lasted only about 22 hours. Some of Earth's spin is passed on to the Moon. The Moon drifts about 3.8 centimetres further away every year. Scientists measure this by bouncing lasers off mirrors that astronauts left on the Moon.
- e.g. About 400 million years ago, long before the dinosaurs, a day lasted about 22 hours.
- e.g. The Moon moves away from us about as fast as your fingernails grow.
### Friction inside living things · *debated*
Living things are very good at controlling friction. Your knee joints rub every time you move, for your whole life. The ends of the bones are covered in smooth, soft cartilage, soaked in slippery joint fluid. Together they slide with less friction than ice sliding on ice. Gecko and tree-frog feet can grip and let go in a moment. And what about the ridges on your fingertips, your fingerprints? People used to say they help us grip. A 2009 experiment found that on smooth, dry surfaces, they do not.
- e.g. Knees, hips and finger joints move millions of times, for many years, before they wear.
- e.g. A gecko, a small lizard, can run straight up a glass window.
- **The old idea:** Fingerprint ridges help us grip, like the tread on a tyre.
- **Peter Warman and Roland Ennos, two scientists in England, in 2009:** They tested fingers on smooth, dry surfaces. The ridges actually lowered the grip a little, because less skin touched the surface.
- **Other scientists:** Ridges may help on wet or rough surfaces, by letting water escape. They may also mainly help us feel textures, because they vibrate as we touch things. The question is still open.

## Mostly agreed
- Friction acts where two surfaces touch, and it works against sliding. *How we know:* On Earth, every sliding thing slows down unless something keeps pushing it. In space, with nothing to rub on, a pushed thing keeps moving.
- Friction depends on what the surfaces are made of, and on how hard they press together. *How we know:* Scientists have measured this carefully since 1699, when a French scientist, Guillaume Amontons, did careful tests. Children repeat these tests in classrooms every day.
- It is usually harder to start something sliding than to keep it sliding. *How we know:* Pull a shoe with a forcemeter, a spring tool that measures pulls. The number climbs until the shoe starts to slide, then drops a little.
- Friction turns movement into heat, and slowly wears surfaces away. *How we know:* Rubbed surfaces get warm. Brake pads, shoe soles and stone steps wear away over time.
- Friction costs the world a lot of energy. *How we know:* In 2017, two engineers, Kenneth Holmberg and Ali Erdemir, added up the friction and wear in the world's machines. They estimated it uses up about 23 out of every 100 units of energy people use. That is nearly a quarter.

## How we found out
**Benjamin Thompson (Count Rumford), and later James Joule, 1798, and the 1840s.** For a long time, many scientists believed heat was an invisible fluid, called caloric. They thought every object held a certain amount of it. Rubbing, they said, only squeezed out caloric that was already inside. In 1798, Benjamin Thompson, known as Count Rumford, was in charge of making cannons in Munich, in Germany. Cannons were made by drilling a long hole into a solid block of metal. The drilling made the metal very hot. Rumford tried an experiment. He put a cannon in a box of cold water and turned a blunt drill inside it, using horses. After about two and a half hours, the water boiled. The people watching were amazed. As long as the horses kept turning, the heat kept coming. If heat were a fluid stored in the metal, it should have run out. So Rumford said heat must be a kind of movement instead. In the 1840s, James Joule measured how much heat a set amount of stirring makes. It was always the same, which showed that Rumford was right.  
*What people believed before:* Heat is an invisible fluid, called caloric, stored inside things. Rumford's blunt drill made heat for as long as the horses turned, so heat could not be a fluid that runs out.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Moving things stop by themselves, because stopping is natural.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,350 years ago. Most of us also think this without noticing.; *rejected*)  
  For: It matches what we see every day. Balls, carts and bicycles all stop if nobody keeps pushing them.  
  Against: About 400 years ago, Galileo, an Italian scientist, noticed something. The smoother the surface, the further a ball rolls. So things stop because friction and air slow them down. In space, a pushed ball would keep going for ever.
- **Friction is a nuisance, and we should get rid of it.** (A common way of thinking, especially about machines; *rejected*)  
  For: Friction wastes energy. It wears out engines, shoes and roads.  
  Against: With no friction at all, you could not walk or hold your food. Knots would come undone. You could not light a match, stop a bicycle or play a violin. You would even slide off your chair. We need the right amount of friction, in the right places.
- **Guillaume Amontons discovered the rules of friction, in 1699.** (Many physics books; *debated*)  
  For: Amontons, a French scientist, published the rules in 1699. Science usually gives the credit to whoever publishes first.  
  Against: Leonardo da Vinci, the Italian artist and inventor, wrote the same rules in his notebooks about 200 years earlier. But his notebooks were not published, so his work was forgotten for centuries.
- **In the old Egyptian painting, the man pours water on the sand only as a religious ritual, not for any practical reason.** (Some experts on ancient Egypt, in the past; *debated*)  
  For: Pouring water was part of religious ceremonies in ancient Egypt.  
  Against: In 2014, scientists in Amsterdam tested this with a small sledge on trays of sand. The right amount of water roughly halved the pull needed. So the workers seem to have known something useful. Scientists only explained it about 3,900 years later.
- **Ice is slippery because pressure melts it.** (Many older textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: Very strong pressure can make ice melt at a slightly lower temperature.  
  Against: The pressure from a shoe is far too small to melt ice. Yet ice is slippery even when you are standing still on it.
- **Meteors and returning spacecraft get hot because of air friction.** (Many books and films; *rejected*)  
  For: They get extremely hot while racing through the air. And rubbing makes heat.  
  Against: Most of the heat comes from squashing the air in front of them. They push into it so fast that the air heats up enormously. A bicycle pump gets warm in the same way when you squash air in it, but far less. Rubbing against the air plays a much smaller part.

## Roots
We have been learning about friction, the grip between things that touch. Now we ask what that grip tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** When you rub your palms fast and then stop, they are warm. Friction has turned their movement into the faster jiggling of countless tiny atoms. That warmth spreads into the air, and it never turns back into your hands moving. So when something stops, its movement has not ended, only spread out where we cannot see it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a moving thing 'stops', as if its movement simply ends. Friction shows that the movement is still there, only shared out among atoms where we cannot see it.  
*Evidence:* Rub your palms and they get warm. The warmth fades into the air, and it never turns back into your hands moving. A film of a box sliding to a stop looks wrong when it is played backwards.

- *who am i:* When you rub your hands, the warmth you feel is your own movement, now shared with your skin and the air.
- *what is life:* Living things control friction all the time. A gecko's feet grip a wall, and your knee joints stay slippery for a lifetime.
- *what is this world:* On Earth, every sliding thing slows down and warms what it rubs against. Even some of the Earth's spin is slowly being turned into warmth by the tides.

Friction does not destroy movement. It takes the neat movement of one thing and shares it out among countless atoms in the box, the floor and the air. DeepKids believes that everything comes from one whole and keeps flowing back into it. Friction is a small, everyday example: the movement of one thing becomes the warmth shared by many.

- **Isha Upanishad, opening verse (also Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.1.1):** That is whole, this is whole. From the whole the whole comes out. Take the whole from the whole, and the whole remains. (When friction stops a box, the box loses its movement, but the world loses no energy. The total stays the same, only spread out as warmth.)
- **Dhammapada 277:** The Buddha taught that everything made of parts keeps changing. Nothing stays the same for ever. (Friction shows this slowly. Shoe soles, brake pads and stone temple steps wear away, and even the length of a day is changing because of the tides.)
- Science (settled): When friction stops a sliding box, its energy is not lost. It becomes the jiggling of atoms, which is heat. In the 1840s, James Joule showed that the same amount of stirring always makes the same amount of heat.
- Science (debated): Many physicists think this one-way spreading is why time seems to go only forwards. Others say it explains which way things change, but not what time is.
- Other view, Many physicists: Many physicists say there is nothing deeper here. There are far more messy ways for atoms to jiggle than neat ways, so things almost always end up spread out. To them, this is a matter of counting, not a sign of one whole.
- Practice, Warm hands (2 min): Let's rub our palms together fast while I count to ten. Now stop and hold them still, a little apart. Feel the warmth in both palms. Close your eyes and just notice it fade. Where did the warmth come from? Where is it going now?
- Ask: When the warmth leaves your hands, is it gone, or somewhere else?
- Ask: A ball rolls to a stop and warms the floor a tiny bit. Why does the warm floor never push the ball back?
- Ask: If movement never ends but only spreads out, where is the movement of a ball you rolled yesterday?

## Story: The Water-Pourer
*The tomb painting is real, and so is the 2014 sand experiment in Amsterdam. Mery, Hapu and what happened to them are made up.*

Nearly four thousand years ago, at the edge of the desert in Egypt, a stone giant lay on its back. It was a statue of a governor called Djehutihotep. It had been carved from a single block of stone. It was taller than four men standing on each other's shoulders, and as heavy as ten elephants. It lay on a wooden sledge, a big flat sled with no wheels. Between the giant and the river lay a whole day of sand.

Mery was ten years old, and she had not been chosen to pull. The pullers were the strong ones. There were more than a hundred and seventy of them, in four long lines. Each man had a rope over his shoulder and his toes dug into the sand. Mery's job was to carry jars of water from the river to her father, Hapu. And Hapu's job, the whole of it, was to walk at the very front of the sledge and pour water onto the sand.

The pullers laughed at him. 'Look, Hapu is watering the desert!' they called. 'Will you grow us some cucumbers, Hapu?' Hapu only smiled and kept pouring. He did not pour a flood, and he did not pour a trickle. He poured a steady curl of water that turned the pale sand just a little darker, the colour of a ripe date. Mery noticed that he never let it turn to mud. When the water made a puddle, he waited. When the sand dried, he poured again.

On the first day, the giant moved. The foreman, the man in charge, beat the rhythm on a drum. The men leaned and heaved together. The sledge slid forward, one long breath at a time, across the darkened sand.

That night, Hapu shook with fever. In the morning, he could not stand. 'No matter,' said the foreman. 'Pouring water is not real work. Today the men will just pull harder.'

So they pulled harder. The drum beat. The ropes creaked. The sledge did not move. Its front edge pressed into the dry sand and pushed up a little hill in front of it, like a cow trying to walk through a heap of grain. The harder the men pulled, the deeper the sledge dug in. One rope snapped with a crack like a whip, and three men tumbled into each other. Someone muttered that the statue was cursed.

Mery stood holding her jar, her heart thumping. The jar was heavy, she was only ten, and nobody had asked her anything. But she had watched her father for a whole day. She walked to the front of the sledge, past the men glaring at her, and she poured.

She poured too much. The sand turned to mud, the front of the sledge sank into it, and the men groaned. 'Get away, child,' the foreman snapped.

Mery's face burned. She stepped back. She looked at the mud, and at the dry sand beside it, and she remembered the colour of a ripe date. Not too wet. Not too dry. She moved a little to one side and tipped the jar gently. She drew a steady curl of water across the sand, just enough to darken it. She waited. She poured again.

The sand changed under her feet. When it was dry, it had shifted and slid away like sugar. Now it held together, firm as a path. No little hill rose in front of the sledge. 'Pull,' whispered Mery. Then, louder: 'Pull now!'

The drum beat. The men leaned. And the stone giant, as heavy as ten elephants, slid forward over a thin skin of darkened sand, as smoothly as a fish through a river.

All that day, Mery walked at the front, pouring and waiting and pouring. The giant followed her like a patient animal. Nobody laughed. Late in the afternoon, one of the pullers came over to her. It was the man who had joked about cucumbers. He took her empty jar, carried it to the river, and filled it for her.

When the statue was finally standing, the governor's painters painted the whole journey on the wall of his tomb. They painted the giant on its sledge, the four long lines of pullers, the drummers and the foreman. And at the very front, so small that you could miss him, they painted a single figure pouring water from a jar onto the sand.

Nobody wrote down Hapu's name, or Mery's. But somebody painted them. And nearly four thousand years later, in a laboratory in Amsterdam, scientists pulled a tiny sledge across trays of sand. Some trays were dry, some were damp and some were soaked. They measured what a water-pourer at the edge of the desert had known all along.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the statue does not move because people pull harder. It moves because the surface where sledge meets sand changes. Dry sand piles up in front of the sledge and holds it back. Just-damp sand holds together, so the sledge slides over it. Too much water makes mud, and the sledge sinks. Friction lives where two surfaces meet. A small change there can matter more than a big change in pulling. The story also quietly asks who gets the credit for great work.

- Ask: In the story, the men pulled harder when Hapu was ill. Why didn't that work?
- Ask: What did just the right amount of water do to the sand? What did too much water do?
- Ask: Why do you think the pullers laughed at Hapu's job of pouring water?
- Ask: On her first try, Mery made mud. What did she change on her second try, and why did it work?
- Ask: The painters put the tiny water-pourer at the front of their painting. Why do you think they did?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Slope Test: which sole grips best? (30 min)
- **question:** Which of our shoes and socks grips best?
- **predict:** Before you test anything, line up the shoes and socks. Put the one you think grips best first, and the one you think grips least last. Then say why you put them in that order.
- **fair test:** To make it fair, keep everything the same except the shoe. Use the same board, and lift it at the same slow speed every time. Test each shoe 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Use light shoes, like chappals or slippers. Keep your fingers out of the way when the shoe slides off.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart. Draw one bar for each shoe. Make each bar as tall as that shoe's middle height.
- **results table:** Shoe or sock | Try 1: height when it slid (cm) | Try 2: height (cm) | Try 3: height (cm) | Middle value (cm)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Collect 3 or 4 different shoes or socks. Try to include a rubber chappal and a sock. 2. Ask your child to put them in a line, best grip first.  
  *Parent says:* Which of these do you think will grip best? Put them in a line. Put the best gripper first and the most slippery last.  
  *Child mode:* Find 3 or 4 different shoes or socks. Put them in a line. Which do you think grips best? Put that one first.
  - Ask: Why did you put that one first? (good: It is made of rubber.; It has grooves underneath.; It feels rough.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the board flat on the floor. 2. Put the first shoe on the board, sole down, near one end. 3. That is the end you will lift.  
  *Parent says:* We'll lift this end slowly until the shoe slides. The higher we can lift it before the shoe slides, the better the shoe grips.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the board flat. Put a shoe near one end, sole down. You will lift that end slowly. Higher before sliding means better grip.
- **s3 (11 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the shoe's end slowly, about one finger-width each second. 2. Stop the moment the shoe starts to slide. 3. Hold the board still. Measure from the floor to the top of the lifted end. 4. Write the number in the table. 5. Do this 3 times for each shoe.  
  *Parent says:* Lift slowly and steadily. Stop the moment it slides. Now let's measure how high the end is, from the floor up.  
  *Child mode:* Lift slowly. When the shoe slides, stop! Measure how high the end is. Write it in the table. Test each shoe 3 times.
  - Note: Keep the lifting speed the same each time, so the test is fair. If one try is very different, that's fine. That's why we do three. To find the middle value, put the three numbers in order and take the middle one. The heights depend on how long your board is, so use the same board for every test. If a sock crumples, fold it into a flat pad.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the middle values, with one bar for each shoe.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn the numbers into a picture. Each shoe gets one bar. The taller the bar, the higher the board went before that shoe slid.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each shoe. Make it as tall as its middle number. A taller bar means better grip.
  - Check: In the slope test bar chart, a taller bar means the shoe… → **Grips more**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the results with the line of guesses you made at the start. 2. If you have time, wipe the board with a damp cloth and test the best gripper once more. See if the height changes.  
  *Parent says:* Were your guesses right? Look at the shoes that gripped best. What do they have in common?  
  *Child mode:* Were your guesses right? Look at the best gripper. What is it made of? What pattern is on its bottom?
  - Think: If you had to climb wet rocks at a waterfall, which shoe would you wear? Why?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Now say or write three things. What do I think? What did I see? Why did it happen?
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the rubber chappal grips best. ### Friction Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Friction is a force. Where does it act?  
   Options: Only on things that are moving fast, like cars / Where two surfaces touch / Only when things are wet / Anywhere, even in empty space  
   **Answer:** Where two surfaces touch. *Why:* Friction happens wherever two surfaces touch and one is trying to slide over the other. Picking 'moving fast' usually means friction is linked only with speed. But friction also holds still things in place, like a shoe on a slope. Picking 'empty space' usually mixes friction up with gravity, which pulls without touching.
2. (mcq) Which way does friction push?  
   Options: The same way the thing is moving / Against the sliding / Always downwards, like gravity / Nowhere, because friction is not a push or a pull  
   **Answer:** Against the sliding. *Why:* Friction pushes against sliding. So it slows moving things down, or stops still things from starting to slide. Picking 'the same way' often means friction is seen as a helper that carries things along. Picking 'downwards' usually mixes friction up with gravity.
3. (fill) A ball rolling on grass slows down and stops because of ____.  
   **Answer:** ['friction']. *Why:* The grass rubs against the ball. That rubbing is friction, and it slows the ball down.
4. (sort) Sort these moments. Do we want MORE friction or LESS friction?  
   **Answer:** a shoe on a wet floor → More friction; a playground slide → Less friction; a bicycle brake → More friction; a carrom striker on the board → Less friction; holding a cricket bat → More friction; a squeaky door hinge → Less friction. *Why:* We want more friction where we need to hold on, stop or push off. We want less where things should glide smoothly.
5. (mcq) You give a toy car the same push on four different floors. Which floor will it roll furthest on?  
   Options: Carpet / Sand / Smooth tiles / Grass  
   **Answer:** Smooth tiles. *Why:* Smooth tiles have the least friction. So the car slows down the least and rolls furthest. Picking carpet or grass often means soft feels smooth. But soft surfaces squash under the wheels and hold them back.
6. (truefalse) Rough surfaces usually have more friction than smooth ones.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The bumps on rough surfaces catch on each other more. So it is harder to slide.
7. (truefalse) Friction only causes problems.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. We need friction to walk, hold things, write and stop. It does cause some problems too, like wearing things out.
8. (match) Match each thing to what it does to friction.  
   **Answer:** Oil on a door hinge → less friction; Grooves on a tyre → more grip; Powder on a carrom board → less friction; Sand on an icy path → more grip. *Why:* Slippery things like oil and powder lower friction. Grooves and gritty sand give more grip.
9. (mcq) Rub your hands together fast for 10 seconds. How do they feel?  
   Options: Colder, because air rushes past them / Warmer / Wetter / The same as before  
   **Answer:** Warmer. *Why:* Friction turns the movement of your hands into heat. Picking 'colder' usually means the child is thinking of a fan, where moving air cools us. Rubbing is different: it makes heat.
10. (fill) Something slippery that we put between two surfaces to lower friction is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lubricant']. *Why:* Oil, water, soap and powder are all lubricants.
11. (mcq) Bicycle tyres have bumpy patterns called tread. Why?  
   Options: To look nice / To grip the road better / To make the tyre lighter / To make the tyre roll faster  
   **Answer:** To grip the road better. *Why:* Tread helps the tyre grip the road. On wet roads, its grooves also let water escape. Picking 'roll faster' often means the child thinks less touching is always better. Tread is about grip, not speed.
12. (mcq) Think of a heavy box on the floor. Which is usually harder?  
   Options: Starting to push it / Keeping it moving once it slides / They are always exactly the same  
   **Answer:** Starting to push it. *Why:* It is usually harder to start something sliding than to keep it sliding. The starting grip, called static friction, is a little stronger. Picking 'keeping it moving' often comes from feeling tired after pushing for a long time. That tiredness is real, but the biggest push is needed right at the start.
13. (fill) A forcemeter is a tool that measures how strong a pull is. It measures in ____.  
   **Answer:** ['newtons', 'N', 'newton']. *Why:* The newton is the unit of force. Its short form is N.
14. (write) Why is it hard to walk on very slippery ice? Use the word friction in your answer.  
   **Answer:** There is very little friction between my shoes and the ice. When I push my foot back to step forward, it slides instead of gripping. So I can't push myself forward.. *Why:* Walking needs friction. Your foot pushes back on the ground, and friction pushes you forward.
15. (write) Name two places at home where you are glad there is friction. Say why for each one.  
   **Answer:** Any two good examples. For example, the bathroom mat stops me slipping when the floor is wet. My pencil grips the paper, so it leaves a line when I write.. *Why:* Any two correct examples, each with a reason, are right.
16. (draw) Draw a shoe resting on a slope. Draw one arrow for the way the shoe would slide. Draw another arrow for friction.  
   **Answer:** The slide arrow points down the slope. The friction arrow points up the slope, along the surface.. *Why:* Friction acts along the surface, against the way the shoe would slide.

   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Rough sandpaper grips most. A rubber mat grips well. A polished floor is slippery. Ice is the most slippery of all.
8. (mcq) Two notebooks have their pages laid over each other like this. They are very hard to pull apart. Why?  
   Options: The pages are glued together a little / Hundreds of pages each add a little friction, and pulling squeezes them together even more / The paper is magnetic / The books are too heavy to lift  
   **Answer:** Hundreds of pages each add a little friction, and pulling squeezes them together even more. *Why:* Each page grips only a tiny bit. But hundreds of tiny grips add up. Pulling also squeezes the pages tighter, which adds more friction. Picking glue or magnets usually means the child expects a hidden force. The surprise is that ordinary friction, added up many times, is enough.
9. (mcq) Why might Leonardo's friction rules have been forgotten for 200 years?  
   Options: His rules were wrong / His notebooks were not published, so other scientists never read them / Amontons copied him and kept it secret / Nobody cared about friction back then  
   **Answer:** His notebooks were not published, so other scientists never read them. *Why:* In science, the credit usually goes to whoever publishes. Work that is never published can be lost. Picking 'copied him' is a common guess when credit goes to the wrong person. There is no evidence for that. Amontons worked it out for himself.
10. (write) In the story, the men pulled harder, but the sledge would not move. Then Mery poured just enough water on the sand. Why did changing the sand work better than pulling harder? Use the word friction.  
   **Answer:** Dry sand piled up in front of the sledge and held it back. Damp sand stayed firm, so the sledge slid on top with less friction. Pulling harder only pushed the sledge deeper into the dry sand.. *Why:* Friction depends on both surfaces that touch. Changing the sand changed the friction, so a smaller pull was enough.
11. (write) Invent one machine that would work better WITHOUT friction, and one that could not work without it. Explain both.  
   **Answer:** For example: a wheel that turned with no friction would waste no energy, so a cycle would roll further. But brakes could not work without friction, because they must rub the wheel to stop it.. *Why:* A good answer shows that friction is useful in some places and wasteful in others.
12. (write) How could you design a test to find out which idea matters more?  
   **Answer:** For example: take two pieces of the same material. Make one very rough, and polish the other very smooth. Pull each with a forcemeter. If the rough piece grips more, bumps matter more. If the very polished piece grips more, sticking atoms matter more.. *Why:* A good test changes only one thing, here the roughness, and keeps everything else the same.
13. (mcq) Astronauts inside a space station float. They hold on to handles to stay in place. Why can't they just stand on the floor?  
   Options: They float instead of pressing down on the floor, so their feet get almost no friction / The floor is wet / There is no air in the space station / Space is very cold  
   **Answer:** They float instead of pressing down on the floor, so their feet get almost no friction. *Why:* Friction depends on how hard two surfaces press together. Floating astronauts hardly press on the floor at all, so their feet get almost no grip. Picking 'no air' is a common mix-up. The space station is full of air, so astronauts can breathe.
14. (write) Is friction a 'good' force or a 'bad' force? Give your opinion, with one reason for each side.  
   **Answer:** A balanced answer. For example: friction is good because it lets us walk and stop. It is bad because it wastes energy and wears things out. So perhaps it depends on where it is.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. What counts is giving a reason for each side.
15. (draw) Draw a close-up of two 'smooth' surfaces touching, as a very strong microscope would show them. Mark the places where they really touch.  
   **Answer:** Two bumpy edges, like mountain ranges, touching only at a few peaks. The touching peaks are marked.. *Why:* Even smooth surfaces are bumpy close up. They touch only at a few tiny points.
16. (mcq) Deep down, where does friction come from?  
   Options: Atoms tugging on each other with electric force / Tiny hooks on every surface / Gravity pulling things sideways / Air trapped between the surfaces  
   **Answer:** Atoms tugging on each other with electric force. *Why:* Friction comes from electric pushes and pulls between the atoms of the two surfaces. Picking 'tiny hooks' is close to the old bumps idea, and bumps do play a part. But deep down, even bumps grip through electric forces. Picking gravity mixes up two different forces.
17. (mcq) Superfluid helium, once stirred, keeps swirling without slowing down. Why?  
   Options: It has no friction at all / It is very heavy, so it keeps going / It is very cold, and cold things move faster / Someone keeps stirring it  
   **Answer:** It has no friction at all. *Why:* With no friction, nothing turns its movement into heat. So it does not slow down. Picking 'very heavy' usually means heavy things are thought to keep going longer. What really stops moving things is friction.
18. (truefalse) Friction from the ocean tides is very slowly making Earth's days longer.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The tides rub on the sea floor, and this slowly brakes the Earth's spin. About 400 million years ago, a day lasted only about 22 hours.
19. (write) A film shows a box sliding across a floor and stopping. How could you tell if the film was playing backwards?  
   **Answer:** Played backwards, the box would start moving all by itself, pushed by a warm floor. That never happens. Friction turns movement into heat, but it never turns heat back into movement like that.. *Why:* Friction only works one way. Some scientists link this to the 'arrow of time', the fact that time only goes forwards.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Where does friction act?  
   Options: Where two surfaces touch / Only on things that are moving fast / Only when things are wet / Between magnets far apart  
   **Answer:** Where two surfaces touch. *Why:* Friction needs two surfaces touching each other. Picking 'moving fast' usually means friction is linked only with speed. But it also holds still things in place. Magnets pull without touching, so that is a different force.
2. (mcq) Which way does friction push?  
   Options: The same way as the movement / Against the sliding / Always downwards  
   **Answer:** Against the sliding. *Why:* Friction works against sliding. Picking 'the same way' often means friction is seen as a helper that carries things along. Picking 'downwards' mixes it up with gravity.
3. (mcq) Which floor gives your feet the most grip?  
   Options: Ice / A rubber mat / A shiny, polished floor that is wet  
   **Answer:** A rubber mat. *Why:* Rubber grips well. Picking the shiny floor often means shiny is thought of as clean and safe. Shiny, wet floors are actually slippery.
4. (mcq) You test different shoes on the slope. To make it a fair test, what should change?  
   Options: Only the shoe / The shoe and the slope / Everything  
   **Answer:** Only the shoe. *Why:* In a fair test, you change only one thing. Then you know that one thing caused the difference. Picking 'the shoe and the slope' usually means wanting to test more at once. Then you can't tell which change mattered.
5. (mcq) You pull two shoes across the floor with a forcemeter. Shoe A needs 3 newtons. Shoe B needs 6 newtons. Which has more friction?  
   Options: Shoe A / Shoe B / They are the same  
   **Answer:** Shoe B. *Why:* Shoe B needed a bigger pull, 6 newtons instead of 3. So it has more friction. Picking A usually means a small number was read as a strong grip. Here, the number is how hard you had to pull, so bigger means more friction.
6. (mcq) Look at the bar chart of slope test heights. Which shoe has the tallest bar?  
   Options: The most slippery shoe / The grippiest shoe / The heaviest shoe  
   **Answer:** The grippiest shoe. *Why:* The tallest bar means that shoe stayed on the steepest slope. So it grips best. Picking 'slippery' often means the bar was read as 'how fast it slid'. The bar shows how high the board went before sliding.
7. (mcq) Where do we want LESS friction?  
   Options: Bicycle brakes / A playground slide / Shoes on a wet floor  
   **Answer:** A playground slide. *Why:* A slide should be slippery, so you can whizz down it. Picking brakes or shoes means GRIP and SLIP got swapped. Those two need lots of friction.
8. (mcq) Which of these lowers friction?  
   Options: Sand on an icy path / Oil on a door hinge / Rubber soles  
   **Answer:** Oil on a door hinge. *Why:* Oil is a lubricant, a slippery layer that lowers friction. Picking sand often means tiny grains are pictured rolling like marbles. On ice, sand grains dig in and add grip.
9. (mcq) Why do old temple steps have a dip in the middle?  
   Options: Friction from millions of feet wore them away / They were built that way / Heavy people squashed the stone down  
   **Answer:** Friction from millions of feet wore them away. *Why:* Each footstep rubs off a tiny bit of stone. Over hundreds of years, this wear makes a dip. Picking 'squashed' usually means stone is pictured as soft, like clay. Hard stone does not squash. It wears away.
10. (mcq) Rubbing your hands together makes them warm. Why?  
   Options: Friction turns movement into heat / Blood rushes into your hands / The air around you is warm  
   **Answer:** Friction turns movement into heat. *Why:* Friction turns the movement of your hands into heat. Picking 'blood rushes' is a sensible guess. But rub two sticks together and they warm up too, with no blood at all.
11. (mcq) Do scientists fully know why ice is slippery?  
   Options: Yes, it is because pressure melts it / No, they are still studying it / Yes, it is because ice is always wet  
   **Answer:** No, they are still studying it. *Why:* Scientists still argue about it. Picking 'pressure melts it' means the old textbook idea has been met. Scientists now think that pressure is far too weak to explain it.
12. (mcq) Who wrote down rules of friction first, but in notebooks that were never published?  
   Options: Guillaume Amontons / Leonardo da Vinci / Isaac Newton  
   **Answer:** Leonardo da Vinci. *Why:* Leonardo da Vinci wrote them around 1493. Amontons published them about 200 years later, in 1699. Picking Newton is a common guess, because he is famous for forces. But Newton was born more than 120 years after Leonardo died.
13. (mcq) Deep down, where does friction come from?  
   Options: Atoms pushing and pulling with electric force / Tiny hooks on every surface / Only gravity  
   **Answer:** Atoms pushing and pulling with electric force. *Why:* Friction comes from electric pushes and pulls between the atoms of the two surfaces. Picking 'tiny hooks' is close to the old bumps idea, which is part of the story. But even bumps grip through electric forces. Gravity is a different force.
14. (mcq) Which of these flows with no friction at all?  
   Options: Honey / Superfluid helium, cooled to about 271 degrees below zero / Water  
   **Answer:** Superfluid helium, cooled to about 271 degrees below zero. *Why:* Superfluid helium is so cold that it flows with zero friction. Picking water often means runny is taken to mean friction-free. Water flows easily, but it still has a little friction inside it.
15. (mcq) Friction from the tides rubbing on the sea floor is…  
   Options: Making Earth's days slowly longer / Making Earth's days slowly shorter / Pulling the Moon closer to us  
   **Answer:** Making Earth's days slowly longer. *Why:* Tidal friction slowly brakes Earth's spin. About 400 million years ago, a day lasted only about 22 hours. Picking 'Moon closer' is a natural guess, since the Moon's pull makes the tides. In fact the Moon is drifting away, about 3.8 centimetres a year.

## Applications
- Brakes on bicycles, scooters, cars and trains rub against the wheels to slow them down.
- Shoe soles, tyre tread and anti-slip mats are made to grip.
- Matches light by friction. The first fires were made by rubbing sticks together.
- Pencils, chalk and crayons leave marks because rubbing wears a little of them off onto the paper.
- Knots, threads and woven cloth hold together because of friction between the threads.
- Bows for the violin, sarangi and esraj are rubbed with rosin, a sticky resin, so they grip the strings.
- Oil and grease help engines, cycle chains and bullock-cart axles run smoothly.
- Ball bearings, rings of tiny steel balls, help fans, wheels and skateboards spin easily.
- Carrom powder, ski wax and smooth playground slides all lower friction.
- Sandpaper, files and grinding stones use friction to shape and smooth things.
- Earthquakes happen when rocks that were stuck together underground suddenly slip.
- Maglev trains, like the one in Shanghai, float above the track on magnets, so no wheels rub.
- Air hockey tables and hovercraft ride on a cushion of air, with almost no rubbing.
- Doctors fit new hip and knee joints that are made to slide with very little friction.
- Superfluid helium, a liquid with no friction, helps cool the giant magnets of the Large Hadron Collider, a huge science machine in Europe.

## Spotted around us
- Carrom powder, sprinkled on the board so the striker glides.
- Old temple and fort steps, with a smooth dip worn in the middle by millions of feet.
- Rubber bathroom mats, and rough tiles near taps and showers.
- The grooved patterns on chappals, tyres and cricket shoes.
- A squeaky door, gate or cycle chain that needs a drop of oil.
- Rangoli powder on a doorstep. Dry powder can make a smooth floor slippery.
- A doorknob or railing polished shiny by thousands of hands.
- Sand sprinkled on a slippery path, or dropped on railway tracks in the rain, to add grip.

## Open questions
- **Exactly what happens between the atoms of two surfaces as they slide?** It happens in spaces far too tiny to see. Scientists use special microscopes and computer models, and they still argue.
- **Why is ice slippery?** Several ideas compete. Maybe ice has a slippery layer on top. Maybe sliding melts a thin film. Maybe loose molecules on top roll like marbles.
- **Can we make friction almost disappear?** Some materials slide with almost no friction. One is graphene, a sheet of carbon just one atom thick, stacked in layers. Scientists call this superlubricity. So far it mostly works for very small pieces.
- **Can we predict earthquakes by studying friction underground?** Earthquakes happen when rocks that were stuck suddenly slip. How and when they slip is still too complicated to predict.
- **How do gecko and frog feet grip so well, and let go so fast?** Scientists understand parts of it. They keep copying it to make new sticky materials.
- **Does 'quantum friction' really exist? It is a tiny drag predicted between surfaces that never touch.** Some calculations predict it, others disagree, and nobody has measured it yet.
- **Is friction's one-way nature the reason time only goes forwards?** Physicists agree that things always spread from order into mess. Whether that is what makes time go forwards is still argued about.
- **What exactly do fingerprints do?** Experiments show they do not simply add grip. They may help on wet surfaces, or mainly help us feel what we touch.

## Connected maths
- A shoe started to slide when the end of the board was 16 cm high. A sock slid when the end was only 8 cm high. Look at the chart. How many times higher was the shoe's height than the sock's? **2 times.**. The shoe stayed put until the end was 16 cm high. The sock slid at just 8 cm. 16 divided by 8 is 2. So the shoe's height was 2 times the sock's. You can check: 8 + 8 = 16. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Look at the forcemeter in the picture. The big numbers are whole newtons. Each small mark between them is half a newton. What is the reading? **4 and a half newtons.**. The pointer is past the 4, but before the 5. It sits on the small mark exactly halfway between them. Each small mark is half a newton. So the reading is 4 newtons and half a newton more. That is 4 and a half newtons. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- Four shoes were tested. The board's end went this high before each one slid: rubber chappal 16 cm, school shoe 13 cm, sock 8 cm, slipper 11 cm. Draw a bar chart of these heights. Then answer: which one grips second best? **The school shoe grips second best. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each, as tall as its height. Then put the heights in order, from tallest to shortest: 16, 13, 11, 8. The tallest bar is the rubber chappal, at 16 cm. The next tallest is the school shoe, at 13 cm. So the school shoe grips second best. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Two notebooks have 40 pages each. You lay all their pages over each other, one from each book in turn. How many places are there where one page touches the next? **79 places.**. Altogether there are 40 + 40 = 80 pages in one pile. Between every two neighbouring pages there is one touching place. So there is always one fewer touching place than pages. 80 take away 1 is 79. Check with 3 pages: there are 2 touching places. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- The Moon moves about 4 cm further from Earth every year. About how far will it move in 80 years, a long human lifetime? Give your answer in metres and centimetres. **About 320 cm, which is 3 metres and 20 centimetres.**. Each year adds 4 cm. In 80 years, that is 80 lots of 4 cm. 4 × 80 = 320 cm. 100 cm make 1 metre, so 300 cm is 3 metres. That leaves 20 cm. So the answer is 3 metres and 20 centimetres. The real speed is a little less, about 3.8 cm a year. We rounded it to 4 cm to make the sum easier. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
