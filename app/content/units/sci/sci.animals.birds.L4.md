# Birds: how beaks and feet fit their food, and why birds are dinosaurs
`sci.animals.birds.L4`

**Summary.** A beak can crack a seed or sip nectar, and scientists say birds are dinosaurs. What shows that? In this unit you will watch a bird's beak and feet, test where a beak bites hardest with a lever, match clue cards, and find the dinosaur in a bird.

**Intro.** *A sparrow's beak is short and thick. A godwit's beak is as long as a pencil. Why can the sparrow crack a hard seed when the godwit cannot?* A beak has two halves joined at a hinge, like a pair of scissors. In this unit you cut stacks of newspaper with scissors, near the hinge and at the tips. You find out which part cuts hardest and which part reaches farthest. That explains why birds with different foods have different beaks. Then you read feet, nests and migration in the same way. Last, you look for a dinosaur's features on a bird at your window.

## Terms
- **Beak**: The hard mouth of a bird. It is made of bone with a covering of keratin, the material of your fingernails. Another word for beak is bill. *e.g.* A parakeet's beak is short and hooked. A kingfisher's beak is long and straight.
- **Lever**: A stiff bar that turns about a fixed point, called the hinge or pivot. A push near the hinge is strong, and a push far from the hinge is weak. *e.g.* Scissors are two levers. They cut thick card near the hinge and only thin paper at the tips.
- **Talon**: A long, sharp, curved claw on the foot of a bird who hunts. *e.g.* A black kite grips her prey with her talons and tears the meat with her hooked beak.
- **Webbed foot**: A foot with skin stretched between the toes. It pushes a lot of water, like a paddle. *e.g.* A spot-billed duck paddles across a tank with webbed feet.
- **Wader**: A bird with long legs who walks in shallow water or on wet mud to find food. *e.g.* A godwit is a wader. She walks on wet mud and pushes her long beak into it.
- **Perching foot**: A foot with three toes pointing forward and one pointing back. The toes wrap round a twig or a wire. *e.g.* A sparrow, a crow and a bulbul all have perching feet.
- **Incubate**: To keep eggs warm until they hatch. Most birds do it by sitting on the eggs. *e.g.* A bulbul incubates her eggs for about two weeks.
- **Migration**: A long journey that an animal makes at the same time every year, and then makes back again. *e.g.* Bar-headed geese fly from Mongolia and Tibet to India every autumn. They fly back in spring.
- **Winter visitor**: A bird who breeds in another country and comes to India only for the cold months. *e.g.* The black-tailed godwit is a winter visitor. She nests far to the north and feeds in Indian wetlands from about October to March.
- **Fossil**: The remains or the mark of a living being from long ago, kept in rock. *e.g.* A fossil found in Germany in 1861 shows a bird-like animal with feathers, teeth and a long bony tail.
- **Theropod**: A dinosaur from the group that walked on two legs and had hollow bones and three main toes. Most theropods ate meat. Birds belong to this group. *e.g.* Tyrannosaurus, Velociraptor and Rajasaurus from India were theropods. So is a hen.
- **Wishbone**: A bone shaped like the letter V at the front of the chest. It is made of the two collarbones joined together. *e.g.* A hen has a wishbone. So did Velociraptor and Tyrannosaurus.
- **Natural selection**: When the individuals whose bodies suit a place best live longer and have more young. Their features then become more common. *e.g.* In a drought, finches with deeper beaks could crack the hard seeds that were left. More of them lived, and their chicks had deeper beaks too.

## Explanations
### A beak is a tool that fits a way of feeding · *defined*
**Definition:** A beak is the hard mouth of a bird. Its shape fits the food the bird eats and the way the bird gets that food.

A bird has no hands and no teeth. The beak does the work of both. It is light, because it is thin bone with a covering of keratin, the material of your fingernails. The covering keeps growing, as your nails do, and wears down with use. Different foods need different tools. A sparrow has a short, thick cone that cracks seeds. A sunbird has a long, thin, curved beak that reaches the nectar deep in a flower. A kite has a sharp hook that tears meat. A duck has a flat beak with edges like a comb, which strain small food out of water. A woodpecker has a straight chisel that cuts into bark. A godwit has a very long beak that probes soft mud for worms. A crow has a strong, straight beak of middle length, and eats almost anything. A beak also does other jobs. Birds use beaks to clean and oil their feathers, to build nests, to feed chicks and to fight.
- e.g. Seed cracker: house sparrow, munia, parakeet.
- e.g. Nectar probe: purple sunbird.
- e.g. Meat hook: black kite, barn owl.
- e.g. Strainer: spot-billed duck, flamingo.
- e.g. Mud probe: godwit, sandpiper.
### A short beak bites hard, and a long beak reaches far · *defined*
**Definition:** A beak works as a pair of levers. A lever pushes hard near the hinge and weakly far from the hinge.

The two halves of a beak meet at a hinge at the back of the mouth. The muscles that close the beak pull near that hinge. This is the same plan as a pair of scissors. In the Beak Lever Test, scissors cut a thick stack of newspaper near the hinge. At the tips they cut only a thin stack. The muscle pulls with the same force the whole time, but the bite is not the same everywhere along the beak. Close to the hinge, the bite is strong. Far from the hinge, the bite is weak. So a short, deep beak gives a hard bite at the tip, because the tip is close to the hinge. A finch cracks her hardest seeds near the base of her beak, where the bite is strongest. A long beak has a weak bite at the tip. But the tip of a long beak moves farther and faster when the beak opens and shuts. It also reaches deep into mud, water or a flower. No beak can have the hardest bite and the longest reach together. That is why seed-crackers have short beaks and probing birds have long ones.
- e.g. A sparrow cracks a grain of bajra with a beak about one centimetre long.
- e.g. A godwit's beak is about ten centimetres long. She uses it to reach worms, and she swallows them whole.
- e.g. A kingfisher's long beak snaps shut very fast on a fish.
### Feet show where a bird lives and how they get food · *defined*
**Definition:** A bird's feet fit the surface the bird stands on and the way the bird catches or holds food.

Most small birds have perching feet. Three toes point forward and one points back, so the foot wraps round a twig. Woodpeckers, parakeets and barbets have two toes forward and two back. That grip holds on to an upright tree trunk. A parakeet also uses one foot as a hand, to hold a fruit while she bites it. Ducks have webbed feet, with skin between the toes, which push water like paddles. Herons and godwits have long legs and long toes. Long legs keep the body dry. Long toes spread the bird's weight, so she does not sink in mud. A jacana has the longest toes of all for her size, and walks on floating lotus leaves. Kites, eagles and owls have talons, which are long curved claws that grip and kill prey. A vulture has a hooked beak like an eagle's, but weak feet with blunt claws. Vultures eat animals who are already dead, so they do not need to grip prey. A hen and a peafowl have thick, strong toes that scratch the ground.
- e.g. Perching: sparrow, crow, bulbul.
- e.g. Climbing: woodpecker, parakeet, barbet.
- e.g. Paddling: duck, goose.
- e.g. Wading: heron, godwit, jacana.
- e.g. Grasping: kite, eagle, owl.
### How a sleeping bird stays on a branch · *debated*
**Definition:** A sleeping bird's toes stay closed round a branch. A lock in the toe tendons helps, and scientists argue about how much.

A myna sleeps all night on a thin branch and does not fall. Part of the reason is in the tendons of the foot. A tendon is a tough cord that joins a muscle to a bone. The tendons that close a bird's toes run down the back of the leg and under the toes. The underside of each tendon has tiny bumps. The tube around the tendon has tiny ridges. When the toes close round a branch, the bumps catch on the ridges. Then the toes stay closed with little effort. Scientists agree that this lock is there. They do not agree on how much this lock matters during sleep.
- e.g. A parakeet hangs upside down from a wire by her feet.
- e.g. A kite carries a fish in her talons for many minutes without dropping it.
- e.g. In The Sleeping Grip activity, your own fingers curl when your wrist is bent back.
- **The older view, found in many books:** When a bird squats, the bent ankle pulls the tendons tight, and the toes close by themselves. The bird's own weight keeps the lock shut all night.
- **Peter Galton and Jeffrey Shepherd, who tested starlings in 2012:** Bending the legs of starlings did not close their toes. Sleeping starlings often stood with their toes loose and kept their balance. So the grip may be less automatic than the books say.
### Beaks change over generations · *defined*
**Definition:** Natural selection means that the individuals whose bodies suit a place best live longer and have more young. Over generations, their features become more common.

No two sparrows have exactly the same beak, just as no two people have the same nose. Chicks get much of their beak shape from their parents. Rosemary Grant and Peter Grant studied finches on Daphne Major, a small island in the Galápagos, from 1973. They caught, ringed and measured almost every finch. In 1977 almost no rain fell. The small, soft seeds were soon eaten. Only large, hard seeds were left. Of about 1,200 medium ground finches, only about 180 lived through the drought. The finches who lived had deeper beaks than the average finch before the drought. A deeper beak cracks a harder seed. The chicks born the next year had beaks about half a millimetre deeper than the birds before the drought. Half a millimetre is small, but it is the difference between cracking a seed and starving. No finch changed their own beak. The share of deep beaks in the flock changed. This is how beaks come to fit foods.
- e.g. After the 1977 drought, the finches of Daphne Major had deeper beaks.
- e.g. After a very wet year in 1983, small seeds were plentiful, and finches with smaller beaks did better.
- e.g. House sparrows taken to North America in the 1850s now differ in size from place to place.
### A nest is a place built for eggs and chicks · *defined*
**Definition:** A nest is a place that a bird makes or chooses for eggs and chicks. A nest keeps them warm, dry and safe from hunters.

An egg must stay warm, and a chick cannot fly away from danger. Every nest is an answer to those two problems. A red-wattled lapwing lays her eggs in a shallow scrape on open ground. The eggs are speckled like the soil, so they are hard to see. A bulbul weaves a cup of thin twigs and roots in a bush. A baya weaver hangs a woven nest from the tip of a thin branch, often over water. Few snakes can climb out to such a nest. The male baya weaves the nest with his beak and feet, and the female inspects it before she accepts it. A red-rumped swallow builds a nest of mud pellets under a bridge or a roof. A great hornbill mother goes into a tree hole and seals the opening with her droppings and mud. She leaves a narrow slit, and the father passes food through it for weeks. An Asian koel builds no nest. The female lays her egg in a crow's nest, and the crows raise the chick. A nest is not a home for the whole year. Most birds use it only while there are eggs and chicks.
- e.g. Scrape on the ground: red-wattled lapwing.
- e.g. Woven hanging nest: baya weaver.
- e.g. Mud nest: red-rumped swallow.
- e.g. Sealed tree hole: great hornbill.
- e.g. No nest of their own: Asian koel.
### Migration: two places in one year · *defined*
**Definition:** Migration is a long journey that an animal makes at the same time every year, and then makes back again.

In the far north, summer days are very long. The ground thaws, and the lakes and mud fill with insects and worms. There is enough food to raise chicks quickly. In winter the same ground freezes hard and is covered with snow. In the Frozen Mud Test, tweezers found no beans in the frozen bowl, though all ten beans were still there. A godwit in frozen country has the same problem. Her food is still in the ground, and her beak cannot reach it. So she flies south to India, where the mud stays soft. In spring she flies back north to nest. Many birds come to India for the winter. Bar-headed geese fly from Mongolia and Tibet across the Himalayas. Scientists have tracked them at more than 6,000 metres above the sea. Demoiselle cranes come to Rajasthan from Central Asia. Amur falcons rest in Nagaland each October on their way from East Asia to southern Africa. Birds find their way by the Sun, the stars, landmarks and the Earth's magnetic field. How they sense the magnetic field is still being worked out.
- e.g. Bar-headed goose: Mongolia and Tibet to Indian lakes.
- e.g. Black-tailed godwit: Central Asia and Siberia to Indian marshes.
- e.g. Demoiselle crane: Central Asia to Khichan in Rajasthan.
- e.g. Amur falcon: East Asia to southern Africa, by way of Nagaland.
### Birds are living dinosaurs · *defined*
**Definition:** Birds are the one group of dinosaurs still alive. They belong to the theropods, the dinosaurs who walked on two legs.

Dinosaurs first appear in rocks about 230 million years old. One group, the theropods, walked on two legs. They had hollow bones, three main toes and a wishbone. Tyrannosaurus was a theropod. So was Rajasaurus, whose bones were found in Gujarat. Some small theropods had feathers. They laid eggs with hard shells in nests, and sat on them. Fossils from Mongolia show a dinosaur called Citipati sitting on a nest. The arms are spread over the eggs, as a hen spreads her wings. Another small dinosaur, Mei long, was found curled up with the head tucked under one arm. A sleeping duck tucks her head under a wing in the same way. By about 150 million years ago, some small feathered theropods could fly. Archaeopteryx, found in Germany in 1861, had feathered wings, and also teeth, clawed fingers and a long bony tail. About 66 million years ago a large asteroid hit the Earth. Every dinosaur died out except some of the birds. Today there are more than 10,000 kinds of birds. So a crow is as much a dinosaur as Tyrannosaurus was.
- e.g. A hen's scaly feet and three front toes are theropod feet.
- e.g. A hen's wishbone is the same bone as the wishbone of Velociraptor.
- e.g. Citipati sat on a nest of eggs, as a hen does.
### Feathers came before flight, and how flight began is argued · *debated*
**Definition:** The first feathers grew on dinosaurs who could not fly. Flight came later, and scientists argue about how it began.

In 1996 a farmer in Liaoning, in China, found a fossil of a small theropod. The animal had a coat of short, hair-like feathers. This kind of dinosaur was named Sinosauropteryx, and lived about 125 million years ago. They had short arms and could not fly. So feathers did not begin as tools for flying. The first feathers probably kept small dinosaurs warm, and may have been used for display. Scientists have even found the colour of some feathers. Tiny grains that held the colour are kept in the fossils. Sinosauropteryx had a tail with reddish-brown and pale rings. Long wing feathers and flight came later. Scientists do not agree on how flight began.
- e.g. Sinosauropteryx had feathers and could not fly.
- e.g. A peacock's train is made of feathers and is used for display, not for flying.
- e.g. A partridge chick flaps tiny wings to run up a steep slope.
- **The 'trees down' idea:** Small feathered dinosaurs climbed trees and glided down. Gliding slowly turned into flapping flight.
- **The 'ground up' idea:** Small feathered dinosaurs ran fast and leapt after prey. Feathered arms helped them leap higher and steer.
- **Kenneth Dial, who studied partridge chicks in 2003:** Chicks who cannot yet fly flap their wings to run up steep slopes and tree trunks. Small dinosaurs may have used half-grown wings in the same way.
### Why only the birds lived through the asteroid · *idea*
**Definition:** Scientists have ideas, but no proof, about why some birds lived through the asteroid strike when all other dinosaurs died.

The asteroid that struck 66 million years ago set off fires and filled the sky with dust. Plants stopped growing for many months, and forests fell. Every large land animal died. Many kinds of birds died too, including all the birds who still had teeth. Scientists have ideas about why some birds lived, and none is proved. The birds who lived were small, so they needed little food. They had beaks without teeth, and some may have eaten seeds. Seeds stay good to eat in the soil for years after plants stop growing. Many of the birds who lived seem to have lived on the ground, which helped when the forests were gone.
- e.g. Seeds buried in soil can last for years.
- e.g. A small body needs less food than a large one.
- e.g. The birds with teeth all died out.

## Mostly agreed
- The shape of a beak fits what the bird eats and the way the bird feeds. *How we know:* Scientists have watched what birds eat and measured their beaks in thousands of kinds of birds.
- A beak bites hardest near the hinge, and a deeper beak bites harder. *How we know:* In 2005 Anthony Herrel's team let wild Galápagos finches bite a small force meter. Finches with deeper beaks and bigger heads bit harder.
- Beaks can change within a few years by natural selection. *How we know:* Rosemary and Peter Grant measured the finches of Daphne Major every year from 1973. After the 1977 drought, the next generation had deeper beaks.
- Birds are theropod dinosaurs. *How we know:* Fossils show theropods with feathers, wishbones, hollow bones, nests of eggs and bird-like sleeping poses. Dozens of kinds of feathered dinosaur have been found since 1996.
- An asteroid strike about 66 million years ago ended all dinosaurs except birds. *How we know:* In 1980 Luis Alvarez, Walter Alvarez, Frank Asaro and Helen Michel studied rocks of that age. They found a thin layer rich in iridium, a metal that is common in asteroids. The crater was later found in Mexico.
- Many birds fly thousands of kilometres between a summer place and a winter place. *How we know:* Rings on birds' legs and small trackers on their backs show the routes. Tracked bar-headed geese crossed the Himalayas at more than 6,000 metres.

## How we found out
**Thomas Huxley in England, Gerhard Heilmann in Denmark, John Ostrom in America, and Li Yumin, Ji Qiang and Ji Shu'an in China, Archaeopteryx was found in 1861. Huxley wrote in 1868 and Heilmann in 1926. Ostrom found Deinonychus in 1964, and Sinosauropteryx was found in 1996..** In 1861, quarry workers in Germany found a fossil with feathered wings, teeth and a long bony tail. The animal was named Archaeopteryx. In 1868 the English scientist Thomas Huxley compared the bones with those of small dinosaurs. He said that birds had come from dinosaurs. In 1926 the Danish artist Gerhard Heilmann wrote a careful book that disagreed. Birds have a wishbone, which is made of two collarbones. No dinosaur seemed to have collarbones. Heilmann reasoned that a lost bone could not come back, so birds must have come from older reptiles. For about forty years most scientists agreed with him. Then, in 1964, John Ostrom found a quick hunting dinosaur in Montana, in America, and named it Deinonychus. The wrist and hand were built like a bird's. Wishbones were later found in many theropods. In 1996 a farmer named Li Yumin found Sinosauropteryx in Liaoning, in China. The fossil showed a small dinosaur with a coat of feathers. Since then, dozens of kinds of feathered dinosaur have been found.  
*What people believed before:* That birds could not have come from dinosaurs, because dinosaurs had no wishbone. Wishbones were later found in many theropods, and feathered dinosaurs were found from 1996.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The dinosaurs are extinct.** (Everyday speech and many older books; *rejected*)  
  For: Tyrannosaurus, the long-necked giants and every other large dinosaur died out about 66 million years ago.  
  Against: Birds are theropod dinosaurs, and more than 10,000 kinds are alive. The sentence is true only of the other dinosaurs.
- **Birds cannot have come from dinosaurs, because dinosaurs had no wishbone.** (The Danish artist and scientist Gerhard Heilmann, in a book of 1926; *rejected*)  
  For: In 1926 no dinosaur wishbone had been recognised, and a lost bone was thought never to come back.  
  Against: Wishbones have since been found in many theropods, including Velociraptor and Tyrannosaurus.
- **You can always tell a bird's food from the beak.** (Many school books; *debated*)  
  For: The rule works for most birds, and the Beak Lever Test shows why.  
  Against: A crow's plain beak takes almost any food. A toucan's huge beak is used for fruit, and also gives off body heat to cool the bird. Beaks do several jobs.
- **Archaeopteryx was the first bird.** (Most books since the fossil was found in 1861; *debated*)  
  For: Archaeopteryx had wings with flight feathers and lived about 150 million years ago.  
  Against: Small feathered dinosaurs of about the same age have been found in China. In 2011 Xu Xing's team argued that Archaeopteryx sits beside the first birds, and not among them. Others disagree.
- **Tyrannosaurus was covered with feathers.** (Some scientists, after a large feathered relative named Yutyrannus was found in China in 2012; *debated*)  
  For: Yutyrannus was about 9 metres long and had long, simple feathers. So a giant relative of Tyrannosaurus did have them.  
  Against: Skin prints from Tyrannosaurus show small scales on several parts of the body. A full-grown Tyrannosaurus may have had few feathers or none.

## Roots
In this unit you tested a model beak and looked for a dinosaur's features on a bird. Here we ask what it means that a kind of animal can change, and still be the same family.

**Deep truth.** In Find the Dinosaur in a Bird, you saw scaly feet, three front toes and a walk on tiptoe. Fossils show small meat-eating dinosaurs with the same feet, and with feathers and wishbones too. Birds are the one branch of the dinosaur family that lived through the asteroid strike. So dinosaurs are still alive, and one may be sitting on your window grill now.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the dinosaurs died out' and treat birds as a separate kind of animal. The family tree puts birds inside the dinosaurs. So that sentence is true only of the other dinosaurs.  
*Evidence:* In Find the Dinosaur in a Bird, you checked eight features on a bird. All eight are also found in fossils of small theropods. In the game Bird, Old Dinosaur or Both, 11 of the 16 feature cards belong to both.

- *who am i:* You belong to a very old family of animals. Your backbone, your four limbs and your five fingers come from animals who lived before the first dinosaur.
- *what is life:* Life does not start from nothing with each new kind of animal. A hen still has scaly feet, feathers and a nest, which came from her dinosaur ancestors and changed a little.
- *what is this world:* The animals you see are not a finished set. Each kind is one stage in a change that is still going on, as the finches showed after one drought.

Every bird hatched from an egg laid by a mother who hatched from an egg. Follow that line of mothers back for 150 million years, and they are feathered dinosaurs. Follow the line further, and it reaches the first animals and then the first cells. Many wise teachers taught that all living beings share one life. The fossils tell a similar story in their own way. Every kind of living being belongs to one family, and that family has been changing slowly for a very long time.

- **Udana 6.4, a Buddhist text:** “ediso dhammo, nediso dhammo; / nediso dhammo, ediso dhammo. / ediso, deva, hatthī seyyathāpi kumbho. / ediso, deva, hatthī seyyathāpi thūṇo. / ediso, deva, hatthī seyyathāpi sammajjanī.” Some blind men each touch one part of an elephant. One says an elephant is like a pot, another like a pillar, another like a broom. Each is sure, and they quarrel. (In 1836 Edward Hitchcock studied three-toed footprints in rock and said giant birds made them. Later scientists said dinosaurs made them. Each side held part of the truth, like the men with the elephant. The three-toed feet belong to dinosaurs, and birds are a kind of dinosaur.)
- **Heraclitus, as quoted by Plato in Cratylus 402a:** “πάντα χωρεῖ καὶ οὐδὲν μένει, / δὶς ἐς τὸν αὐτὸν ποταμὸν οὐκ ἂν ἐμβαίης.” Everything moves and nothing stays still. You cannot step twice into the same river. (A kind of animal is like a river, which never stands still. The finches of Daphne Major had deeper beaks after one drought. The name 'finch' stayed the same, but the birds had changed.)
- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), the last sentence:** From a simple beginning, endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved. (Darwin wrote 'are being'. The change from a small feathered dinosaur to a sparrow has not stopped. Beaks are still being fitted to foods today.)
- Science (settled): All living things share one ancestor. The same chemical code is used in the cells of a sparrow, a neem tree and you.
- Science (debated): Scientists do not agree on what a 'kind' or species really is. Some say kinds are real units in nature. Others say they are lines that people draw across slow change.
- Other view, Many people of faith, in several religions: Every kind of creature was made in the form they have now, and a bird has always been a bird. On this view, the likeness between a hen and a small dinosaur shows one maker's plan, and not one family.
- Practice, One minute with a living dinosaur (3 min): Find a bird you can see well. Watch her for one minute without speaking. Look at the scaly feet, the quick turns of the head and the eye. Now stand on your toes and fold your arms like wings. Feel your backbone, your ankles and your fingers. She has the same bones as you, in different lengths. How does it feel to share a skeleton with her?
- Ask: In Find the Dinosaur in a Bird, you ticked the same features for a hen and for Velociraptor. Where does 'dinosaur' stop and 'bird' begin?
- Ask: The finches' beaks changed after one drought. Is a kind of animal a fixed thing, or something that is happening?
- Ask: A godwit, a sparrow and you share a backbone from one ancestor. What does 'family' mean to you now?

## Story: The Long Beak on the Terrace
*Kayal, her father and Shanthi are made up, and so is this godwit's landing on a terrace. These facts are real. Black-tailed godwits breed in Central Asia and Siberia and spend the winter in Indian wetlands, including marshes near Chennai. A godwit feeds by pushing her long beak deep into soft mud. The tip of the beak can feel a worm move. Bird rescuers do ask people to keep a tired wild bird in a dark box with air holes. They also ask people not to push food into the beak.*

The storm blew over Velachery all night. In the morning the terrace was covered with neem leaves, and there was one bird.

Kayal saw her when she went up to bring down the clothes clips. The bird stood beside the water tank on legs like two thin grey sticks. She was brown, and as tall as a hen, but much slimmer. Her beak was the strange part. It was straight, pink near the face and dark at the tip, and almost as long as Kayal's hand.

The bird did not fly away. She took three steps and sat down.

"Appa!" Kayal called down the stairs. "Come and see. Come slowly."

Appa came up with the phone already in his hand. He rang the bird rescue number that was painted on the wall near the bus stop. A woman called Shanthi answered. She said that after a storm, tired birds came down in all sorts of places.

"Put her in a big box with air holes," Shanthi said. "Keep it dark and quiet. Give her a flat dish of water. Do not push food into her beak. I am in Tambaram with a hurt owl, so I can come only in the evening."

They found the box that the mixer had come in. Appa lifted the bird in a towel. She weighed almost nothing. Kayal saw her feet as they went past. Each foot had three long toes spread wide, and one small toe behind. There was no skin between the toes.

All morning Kayal sat beside the box. No sound came from it. At noon Appa could not bear it any longer.

"She must be hungry," he said. "Shanthi madam told us not to push food. She did not tell us not to offer it."

He put a spoon of raw rice in a jar lid and slid it into the box. An hour later he tried soaked chana, broken small. Then he tried a piece of banana. The bird looked at each one with a dark eye and did not move.

"Maybe she is too sick to eat," said Appa.

Kayal looked through an air hole at the long beak. She thought about the sparrows at the ration shop. They cracked spilt grain with short, fat beaks, crick, crick, like tiny nutcrackers. Then she thought about the scissors in her craft box. They cut thick card only near the screw. At the tips they could hardly cut two sheets.

"Appa, she cannot eat rice," Kayal said. "Look at her beak. It is as long as a pencil. A beak like that is weak at the tip. It cannot crack anything. It is made for reaching."

"Reaching what?"

"Something deep, and something soft." Kayal thought about the long legs and the wide, bare toes. This bird was not a swimmer, because she had no skin between her toes. She was not a tree bird either. Legs like those would be no use on a twig.

"She walks in shallow water, Appa. She walks on soft mud. She pushes that beak down into the mud for worms."

Appa laughed. "So I should cook worms."

"No. We should not feed her at all. We should take her where her food is. Not the park, as you said this morning, and not the temple tank. There is nothing for her in a tree, and the tank is deep, with stone steps. She needs mud that her beak can go into."

When Shanthi rang at four o'clock, Kayal took the phone and said all of it in one breath. For a moment Shanthi said nothing.

"A long straight beak, pink near the face?" she asked. "Long legs? Did you see white on the wing?"

"I saw white when Appa lifted her."

"Then she is a black-tailed godwit," said Shanthi. "She has flown here from far to the north, beyond the Himalayas. I think the storm brought her down before she reached the marsh. You are right about the mud. If she is standing up and holding both wings level, meet me on the Pallikaranai marsh road at five."

The marsh lay between the big road and the glass office towers. The water was low at the edges, and the mud shone. Shanthi looked at the bird's wings and eyes, and nodded. Kayal opened the flap of the box and stepped back.

For a long moment the godwit did nothing. Then she walked out, lifting her feet high. Her wide toes did not sink. At the edge of the water she put her head down and pushed her beak into the mud, right up to her face. She did it again and again, as fast as the needle of a sewing machine. On the fourth push she pulled out something small and swallowed.

"She found that without seeing," Kayal whispered.

"The tip of her beak can feel," said Shanthi. "She feels the worm move in the mud."

Further out, forty or fifty birds with the same long beaks were feeding in a line. The godwit fed for a few minutes more. Then she opened her wings, showed the white bar that Kayal had seen, and flew low over the water to join them.

Appa was still holding the jar lid of rice. He looked at it. Then he tipped it out on the bund for the sparrows.

**For the parent:** Kayal read the bird's food from her body. A long, thin beak is weak at the tip, so it is made for reaching and not for cracking. Long legs and wide, bare toes are for soft mud. So Kayal knew the bird needed a mudflat, and that no food from the kitchen would do.

- Ask: Why could the long beak not crack a grain of rice?
- Ask: Which two things about the feet showed that the bird belonged on mud?
- Ask: Why would the park or the deep temple tank have been the wrong place to let the bird go?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Beak Lever Test: where does a beak bite hardest? (30 min)
- **question:** Scissors have two blades joined at a hinge, like the two halves of a beak. Do the blades cut a thicker stack of paper near the hinge, in the middle or at the tips? How wide do they open at each place?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Where will the scissors cut the thickest stack of newspaper: near the hinge, in the middle or at the tips? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same scissors and the same newspaper for every cut. The same person makes every cut, with one hand and one squeeze. Use the same three places on the blades each time, and mark them first. Check each result twice. Only one thing changes: the place on the blades where the paper sits.
- **safety:** A grown-up stays beside the child for every cut. Keep the fingers that hold the paper well away from the blades. If a stack does not cut with one firm squeeze, stop and record that. Do not force the scissors.
- **record:** Make a table with three rows: near the hinge, middle and tips. In each row write the thickest stack the scissors cut in one squeeze, and the gap between the open blades.
- **results table:** Place on the blades | Thickest stack cut in one squeeze (layers) | Gap between the open blades (centimetres)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the scissors. Open and shut them slowly. Point to the screw, which is the hinge. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* These scissors are our model beak. The screw is the hinge, like the joint at the back of a bird's mouth. Where will the blades cut the thickest stack: near the hinge, in the middle or at the tips? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Open and shut the scissors. Look at the hinge, the middle and the tips.
  - Ask: Why do you think that place will cut best? (good: The tips are sharp and pointed.; Near the hinge feels stronger when I cut card.; The middle is where I usually cut.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children choose the tips because they look sharp.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark one blade at 2 centimetres from the screw, in the middle, and 1 centimetre from the tip. Tear the newspaper into five pieces, each about the size of a notebook page. Fold the first piece once and the second twice. Fold the third 3 times, the fourth 4 times and the fifth 5 times.  
  *Parent says:* Every fold doubles the layers. One fold makes 2 layers. Two folds make 4. How many layers will five folds make?  
  *Child mode:* Mark the blade in three places. Fold five stacks: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 layers.
  - Note: Five folds make 32 layers. Press each fold flat with a thumbnail. Write the number of layers on each stack.
- **s3 (9 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Start with the tips. Slide the stack of 2 layers between the tips only. Squeeze once with one hand. Then try 4, 8, 16 and 32 layers. Write down the thickest stack that was cut right through. Check that stack once more. Then do the same in the middle, and then near the hinge.  
  *Parent says:* Put the paper only at the mark we are testing. Squeeze once, with one hand, as hard as is comfortable. A cut counts only if every layer is cut through.  
  *Child mode:* Cut with the tips first. Then the middle. Then near the hinge.
  - Note: At the tips, a thick stack often slides forward out of the blades without being cut. That is a real result, so record it. Near the hinge most scissors cut 16 layers, and some cut 32.
  - Check: In the Beak Lever Test, why do you use the same hand and one squeeze for every cut? → **So only the place on the blades changes**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Open the scissors as wide as they go and lay them on the table. Measure the gap between the two blades at the mark near the hinge. Then measure the gap at the tips. Write both numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now we measure how far the blades open. Look at the gap near the hinge and the gap at the tips.  
  *Child mode:* Open the scissors wide. Measure the gap at the hinge and the tips.
  - Ask: Which part of the blades moves fastest when the scissors snap shut? (good: The tips, because they travel farthest in the same time.)
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. Wait for all three. Then read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Tell your grown-up what you found.
  - Ask: Where was the cut strongest, and where did the blades open widest? (good: Strongest near the hinge.; Widest at the tips.; The strong place and the wide place are at opposite ends.)
  - Note: The explanation: each blade is a lever, which is a stiff bar that turns about a hinge. Your hand's squeeze is passed along the blade. Close to the hinge it arrives as a strong push over a short distance. Far from the hinge it arrives as a weak push over a long distance. So the blades cut hardest near the hinge, and open widest and move fastest at the tips.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to use the result on two birds: a sparrow with a beak about 1 centimetre long, and a godwit with a beak about 10 centimetres long.  
  *Parent says:* A sparrow's whole beak is as short as the part of the scissors near the hinge. A godwit's beak is as long as the whole blade. Which bird can crack a hard seed? What can the other bird do that this one cannot?  
  *Child mode:* Match each bird to a part of the scissors.
  - Check: A bird has a very long, thin beak. What is this beak best at? → **Reaching deep and snapping fast**
  - Think: Why does a finch move a very hard seed to the back of her beak before she bites?
### e2 · The Frozen Mud Test: can a probing beak find food in frozen ground? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Ten beans are hidden in a bowl of soft mud, and ten in a bowl of frozen mud. How many can tweezers find in 30 seconds in each bowl? How long must frozen mud stand in the room before the tweezers can find food again?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many of the ten beans will you find in the soft mud in 30 seconds? How many in the frozen mud? How many after the frozen bowl has stood in the room for 20 minutes? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two bowls of the same size, with the same amount of mud and ten beans in each. A grown-up hides the beans at the same depth in both. The same person searches each time, with the same tweezers, one hand and 30 seconds on a timer. Only one thing changes: whether the mud is soft or frozen.
- **safety:** Wash your hands with soap after touching the soil. Cover the bowl before it goes into the freezer, and keep it away from food.
- **record:** Make a table with four rows: soft mud, frozen mud, frozen mud after 20 minutes in the room, and frozen mud after 40 minutes. Write the number of beans found in 30 seconds in each row.
- **results table:** Bowl | Beans found in 30 seconds | What the tweezers could do
- **schedule:** day 1: Make two bowls of mud with ten hidden beans each. Put one bowl in the freezer overnight. (10 min); day 2: Search both bowls for 30 seconds each. Search the frozen bowl again after 20 and 40 minutes in the room. (15 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the tweezers and the soil. Ask for the three guesses and the reasons. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* A godwit feeds by pushing her long beak into soft mud. The tweezers are her beak. I will hide ten beans in soft mud and ten in mud that I freeze. How many will you find in each in 30 seconds? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The tweezers are a godwit's beak. Guess how many beans you will find. Why?
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. Mix the soil with water until it is thick, shiny mud. Fill both bowls to about 3 centimetres deep. Send your child out. Push ten beans into each bowl, about 2 centimetres down, and smooth the top. Cover one bowl with a plate and put it in the freezer. Leave the other bowl in the room, covered with a damp cloth.  
  *Parent says:* Please wait outside for two minutes. I am hiding ten beans in each bowl. One bowl goes into the freezer until tomorrow.  
  *Child mode:* Help to mix the mud. Then wait outside while the beans are hidden.
  - Note: If you have no soil, wet sand or a thick paste of used tea leaves works. The mud must be wet enough to shine, or it will not freeze hard.
  - Wait: Leave the bowl in the freezer overnight, or for at least 4 hours
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2. Take the frozen bowl out. Give your child the tweezers and the soft bowl first. Time 30 seconds. Count the beans found. Then time 30 seconds on the frozen bowl. Write both numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* Use one hand and only the tweezers. You have 30 seconds for each bowl. Push the tips in, feel for a bean and lift the bean out.  
  *Child mode:* Search the soft bowl for 30 seconds. Then search the frozen bowl.
  - Check: In the Frozen Mud Test, are the ten beans still inside the frozen bowl? → **Yes, but the tweezers cannot reach them**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave the frozen bowl on the counter. Search it again for 30 seconds when 20 minutes have passed, and again when 40 minutes have passed. Write down each count.  
  *Parent says:* The bowl is warming up. Feel the top with the tweezers. Has anything changed? You have 30 seconds again.  
  *Child mode:* Wait 20 minutes. Search again. Then wait 20 more and search again.
  - Note: Use the waits for the term cards or a game. On a hot day the top thaws faster, so the counts will be higher.
  - Wait: Wait 20 minutes between the searches
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. Wait for all three. Then read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your four counts. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Then tell me what a godwit must do when the ground where she lives freezes.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Tell your grown-up what you found.
  - Ask: The food was in the frozen bowl all the time. Why did the beak go hungry? (good: The mud was too hard to push into.; A thin beak cannot break ice.)
  - Ask: Would a sparrow, who picks seeds off the top of the ground, have the same problem? (good: Less so, unless snow covers the seeds.; She does not need to push into the ground.)
  - Note: The explanation: godwits nest in Central Asia and Siberia, where the summer mud is soft and full of worms. In winter that ground freezes for months. The worms are still there, and a thin beak cannot reach them. So the godwits fly to India, where the mud stays soft, and fly back in spring. Tip the mud back into the plant pot when you finish.

## Worksheets
### Beaks, feet, nests and dinosaurs (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which beak is best for cracking hard seeds?  
   Options: A short, thick cone / A long, thin curve / A flat, wide scoop / A long, straight dagger  
   **Answer:** A short, thick cone. *Why:* A short, thick beak has its tip close to the hinge, so the bite is hard. Long beaks reach far, and bite weakly at the tip.
2. (mcq) Where along a beak is the bite strongest?  
   Options: Near the hinge / At the very tip / Exactly in the middle / The same everywhere  
   **Answer:** Near the hinge. *Why:* A beak is a lever. The closing muscles pull near the hinge, so the bite is strongest there and weakest at the tip.
3. (fill) A foot with skin stretched between the toes is called a ____ foot.  
   **Answer:** ['webbed']. *Why:* A webbed foot pushes a lot of water, like a paddle. Ducks and geese have webbed feet.
4. (truefalse) A vulture needs sharp talons to get food.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A vulture eats animals who are already dead, so there is nothing to catch. Her claws are blunt and her feet are made for walking.
5. (match) Match each kind of foot to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Three toes forward, one back → Wraps round a twig; Two toes forward, two back → Climbs a tree trunk; Skin between the toes → Paddles through water; Long legs and long, bare toes → Walks on soft mud; Long, curved talons → Grips live prey. *Why:* Ask what surface the foot meets and what it must hold. A twig, bark, water, mud and prey each need a different foot.
6. (sort) Sort these birds by beak: built for a hard bite, or built for a long reach?  
   **Answer:** house sparrow → Short beak, hard bite; munia → Short beak, hard bite; parakeet → Short beak, hard bite; godwit → Long beak, long reach; sunbird → Long beak, long reach; painted stork → Long beak, long reach. *Why:* Sparrows, munias and parakeets crack seeds. Godwits, sunbirds and storks reach into mud, flowers or water.
7. (mcq) Why does a godwit leave Siberia in autumn?  
   Options: The mud freezes over her food / She dislikes the long nights / Her feathers stop growing / Her chicks chase her away  
   **Answer:** The mud freezes over her food. *Why:* The worms are still in the ground, and a thin beak cannot enter frozen mud. She flies to places where the mud stays soft.
8. (truefalse) A nest is a home where a bird lives all year.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most birds use a nest only while there are eggs and chicks. For the rest of the year they sleep on branches, ledges or water.
9. (order) Put these in order, from longest ago to now.  
   **Answer:** ['The first dinosaurs appear', 'Archaeopteryx flies in what is now Germany', 'An asteroid hits the Earth', 'Sparrows nest in a city']. *Why:* The first dinosaurs lived about 230 million years ago, and Archaeopteryx about 150 million years ago. The asteroid struck 66 million years ago.
10. (fill) Birds belong to the group of two-legged dinosaurs called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['theropods']. *Why:* Theropods walked on two legs and had hollow bones, three main toes and a wishbone. Tyrannosaurus, Velociraptor and all birds are theropods.
11. (mcq) Which feature do birds share with Velociraptor?  
   Options: A wishbone / Teeth in the jaws / A long bony tail / Four walking legs  
   **Answer:** A wishbone. *Why:* A Velociraptor wishbone has been found. Velociraptor also had teeth and a long bony tail, and birds have lost both.
12. (write) A sunbird has a long, thin, curved beak. Explain in two sentences what this beak is for.  
   **Answer:** It reaches the nectar deep inside a flower, where a short thick beak could not go. The thin beak is weak at the tip, and nectar does not need a hard bite.. *Why:* A strong answer links the shape to the job, and says that reach matters here and bite strength does not.
13. (write) Scissors cut 16 layers near the hinge and 4 layers at the tips. What does that tell you about beaks?  
   **Answer:** A beak is hinged like scissors, so the bite is strong near the hinge and weak at the tip. A short beak bites hard, and a long beak has a weak tip.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the numbers as evidence and moves from the scissors to a real beak.
14. (draw) Draw a nest for a tree where snakes climb. Label how your nest keeps the eggs safe.  
   **Answer:** Any nest that a snake cannot easily reach or enter. One example is a nest hanging from a thin branch tip with the door at the bottom. Another is a hole with a very narrow opening.. *Why:* A strong drawing names the danger and shows one feature that deals with it. A baya weaver's hanging nest and a hornbill's sealed hole are two real answers.
15. (mcq) What does 'incubate' mean?  
   Options: Keep eggs warm until they hatch / Build a nest from grass / Carry food to a chick / Fly south for winter  
   **Answer:** Keep eggs warm until they hatch. *Why:* A chick grows inside an egg only if the egg stays warm. Most birds incubate by sitting on the eggs.
### Read the body, find the food (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A kingfisher's long beak bites weakly at the tip. What is that beak good at?  
   Options: Snapping shut fast on a fish / Cracking hard nuts / Straining tiny food from water / Chiselling into wood  
   **Answer:** Snapping shut fast on a fish. *Why:* The tip of a long beak moves far and fast. A fish needs speed and reach, and does not need a hard bite.
2. (mcq) A parakeet has two toes pointing forward and two back. What does this help her do?  
   Options: Climb, and hold food / Paddle through water / Walk on soft mud / Run fast on sand  
   **Answer:** Climb, and hold food. *Why:* Two toes each way make a strong grip on bark, and let one foot hold a fruit like a hand.
3. (mcq) After a drought, finches had deeper beaks on average. Why?  
   Options: Deep-beaked finches lived and had chicks / Each finch stretched their own beak / Shallow-beaked finches flew away / The seeds became softer  
   **Answer:** Deep-beaked finches lived and had chicks. *Why:* Only big, hard seeds were left. Finches with deeper beaks could crack them, so more of them lived and passed deep beaks to their chicks.
4. (mcq) Sinosauropteryx had feathers and could not fly. What does this show?  
   Options: Feathers came before flight / All dinosaurs could fly / Feathers grow only on wings / Birds are older than dinosaurs  
   **Answer:** Feathers came before flight. *Why:* A dinosaur with short arms and a coat of feathers could not fly. So feathers first did another job, such as keeping the body warm.
5. (truefalse) In the Frozen Mud Test, the tweezers found no beans because the beans were gone.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. All ten beans were still in the bowl. The mud was too hard for the tweezers to enter.
6. (truefalse) Winter visitors come to India to lay their eggs.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Winter visitors nest in the north during the northern summer. They come to India to feed while the north is frozen.
7. (sort) Sort these features: found in birds and in small theropods, or only in the old theropods?  
   **Answer:** feathers → Birds and small theropods; a wishbone → Birds and small theropods; hollow bones → Birds and small theropods; sitting on eggs → Birds and small theropods; teeth → Only the old theropods; a long bony tail → Only the old theropods. *Why:* Fossils show feathers, wishbones, hollow bones and nesting in small theropods. Birds alive today have no teeth and only a short tail stump.
8. (odd) Three of these birds are waders. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Godwit / Painted stork / Heron / Parakeet  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Godwits, storks and herons have long legs and feed in shallow water. A parakeet climbs trees and eats seeds and fruit.
9. (count) Each fold doubles the layers of a newspaper stack. How many layers are there after 4 folds?  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* One fold gives 2 layers, two folds give 4, three give 8 and four give 16.
10. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how a drought changed the finches' beaks.  
   **Answer:** ['Almost no rain falls', 'The small, soft seeds are eaten up', 'Finches with deeper beaks crack the big, hard seeds', 'More deep-beaked finches live through the drought', 'Their chicks have deeper beaks too']. *Why:* No single finch changed. The drought changed which finches lived, and the chicks took after their parents.
11. (write) A koel builds no nest. Explain how a koel's egg is still kept warm.  
   **Answer:** The female koel lays her egg in a crow's nest. The crows sit on it with their own eggs, and then feed the koel chick.. *Why:* A strong answer says who does the incubating. It may add that the egg must look enough like a crow's egg to be accepted.
12. (write) Why is a short, thick beak poor at getting nectar from a long flower?  
   **Answer:** The nectar lies deep inside the flower, further than a short beak can reach. A hard bite is no help, because nectar is a liquid.. *Why:* A strong answer names reach as the problem. It shows that a beak that suits one food suits another badly.
13. (write) A bird has long legs, long bare toes and a long straight beak. Where does this bird feed, and why?  
   **Answer:** On wet mud or in shallow water. Long legs keep the body dry, and long toes stop the bird sinking. The long beak reaches worms deep in the mud.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason from each of the three clues. Bare toes with no web show that the bird walks and does not paddle.
14. (perspective) People disagree about whether to call a hen a dinosaur.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that feathers, wishbones and nests place birds inside the theropod group. Another is that a word is useful only if people share its meaning.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer keeps the facts about the family tree apart from the question of which word to use.
15. (label) Label three parts of this hen's leg: the toes, the ankle and the knee.  
   **Answer:** ['The toes: the only part that touches the ground', 'The ankle: the joint that points backward, held high off the ground', 'The knee: close to the body, hidden in the feathers']. *Why:* A hen stands on her toes. So the joint that looks like a backward knee is the ankle, and the real knee is higher up.
### Evidence and arguments (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A bird has a large hooked beak and weak feet with blunt claws. What does she most likely eat?  
   Options: Animals who are already dead / Animals she catches alive / Nectar from flowers / Seeds strained from water  
   **Answer:** Animals who are already dead. *Why:* The hook tears meat, and weak feet with blunt claws cannot kill. This is the plan of a vulture.
2. (mcq) Several wet years bring plenty of small, soft seeds. What may happen to finch beaks over the generations?  
   Options: Smaller beaks become more common / Deeper beaks become more common / Beaks can never change again / Every finch gets the same beak  
   **Answer:** Smaller beaks become more common. *Why:* A big beak costs more to grow and is clumsy with small seeds. When small seeds are plentiful, finches with smaller beaks raise more chicks.
3. (mcq) Gerhard Heilmann said dinosaurs had no wishbone, so birds could not come from them. What showed he was wrong?  
   Options: Wishbones found in theropod fossils / A living Velociraptor / Birds losing their wishbones / Older drawings of dinosaurs  
   **Answer:** Wishbones found in theropod fossils. *Why:* Wishbones have been found in many theropods. Heilmann's reasoning was careful, and the fossils he needed had not yet been recognised.
4. (mcq) Which is the best evidence that some dinosaurs sat on their eggs?  
   Options: A fossil dinosaur found on a nest / Dinosaurs were very large / Hens sit on eggs today / Dinosaur eggs were round  
   **Answer:** A fossil dinosaur found on a nest. *Why:* Fossils of Citipati show the animal sitting on the nest with the arms spread over the eggs. What hens do today is only a hint.
5. (truefalse) A crocodile is a closer relative of a sparrow than of a lizard.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Crocodiles and dinosaurs, including birds, share a more recent ancestor than the one that they share with lizards.
6. (truefalse) In a drought, each finch grows a deeper beak by cracking hard seeds.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A finch's beak depth is set once the bird is grown. The drought changed which finches lived.
7. (match) Match each nest to the problem it solves.  
   **Answer:** A woven nest hanging from a branch tip → Snakes cannot easily reach it; Speckled eggs in a scrape on stony ground → Hunters cannot easily see the eggs; A mud nest stuck under a bridge → Rain and climbing animals cannot reach it; A tree hole sealed up to a narrow slit → Nothing wider than a beak can get in. *Why:* Every nest answers a danger in the place where it is built. The same bird in another place would need another answer.
8. (count) Force × distance from the hinge is always 24. What is the force 6 cm from the hinge?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 24 divided by 6 is 4. At 2 centimetres the force would be 12, which is three times more. So the bite at 6 centimetres is three times weaker, because that place is three times as far from the hinge.
9. (count) About 1,200 finches lived on an island. About 180 lived through a drought. How many did not?  
   **Answer:** 1020. *Why:* 1,200 minus 180 is 1,020. More than 8 finches in every 10 died, so the few who lived shaped the next generation strongly.
10. (fill) The V-shaped bone made of two joined collarbones is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['wishbone']. *Why:* Birds have a wishbone, and so did many theropods, including Velociraptor and Tyrannosaurus.
11. (write) Many people say, 'The dinosaurs died out.' Rewrite the sentence so that it is true, and explain your change.  
   **Answer:** One true sentence is this. All the dinosaurs except birds died out about 66 million years ago. Birds are theropod dinosaurs, and more than 10,000 kinds are alive.. *Why:* A strong answer keeps what is right in the old sentence and adds the one exception, with a reason.
12. (write) A fossil beak is short and thick. What does this show about the food, and what does it not show?  
   **Answer:** It shows that the beak could bite hard, so the animal could crack hard food such as seeds. It does not show what the animal really chose to eat each day, or how they behaved.. *Why:* A strong answer separates what the shape allows from what the animal did. A beak shows what was possible.
13. (write) The Frozen Mud Test used beans as worms. How is a bean unlike a worm? Does that change the result?  
   **Answer:** A worm can move and a bean cannot, and a worm is soft. It does not change the main result, because tweezers could not enter frozen mud at all.. *Why:* A strong answer names a real difference and then judges whether that difference could change the finding.
14. (perspective) Each October, huge flocks of Amur falcons rest near a village in Nagaland on their way to Africa.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that a migrating bird depends on every resting place along the route. Another is that people who protect the birds must also be able to live.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses the fact that one resting place serves birds from a whole continent. It also takes the villagers' needs seriously.
15. (draw) Draw a beak for a bird who eats water snails with hard, round shells. Label two features.  
   **Answer:** One good design is a long beak that reaches into shallow water. The beak also needs a way to hold a round shell, such as a gap or a strong tip. Each label says what the feature does.. *Why:* A strong drawing deals with both problems: reaching the snail and holding the slippery shell. The Asian openbill, a stork, has a gap between the two halves of the beak.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A bird has a flat, wide beak with edges like a comb. How does she feed?  
   Options: She strains food from water / She cracks hard nuts / She tears meat / She drinks nectar  
   **Answer:** She strains food from water. *Why:* The comb-like edges let water out and keep small food in. Ducks feed this way.
2. (mcq) Why can a sparrow crack a seed that a godwit cannot?  
   Options: Her beak tip is close to the hinge / Her beak is more pointed / She is always hungrier / A godwit has no jaw muscles  
   **Answer:** Her beak tip is close to the hinge. *Why:* A short beak keeps the tip near the hinge, where the bite is strong. The tip of a godwit's long beak is far from the hinge.
3. (mcq) In the Beak Lever Test, where did the scissor blades open widest?  
   Options: At the tips / Near the hinge / In the middle / The same everywhere  
   **Answer:** At the tips. *Why:* The tips are farthest from the hinge, so they travel farthest. That is why a long beak reaches far and snaps fast.
4. (mcq) Which feet belong to a bird who catches live prey?  
   Options: Feet with long curved talons / Webbed feet / Feet with long thin toes / Feet with thick blunt claws  
   **Answer:** Feet with long curved talons. *Why:* Talons grip and kill. Webbed feet paddle, long thin toes spread weight on mud, and thick blunt claws scratch the ground.
5. (mcq) Why does a jacana have very long toes?  
   Options: To spread her weight on floating leaves / To swim faster / To grip live prey / To dig nest holes  
   **Answer:** To spread her weight on floating leaves. *Why:* Long toes spread her weight over a wide area, so a floating leaf can hold her up.
6. (mcq) Why does a baya weaver hang the nest from a thin branch tip?  
   Options: Snakes cannot easily reach it / The eggs get more sun / It needs less grass / Rain cannot wet it  
   **Answer:** Snakes cannot easily reach it. *Why:* A thin tip will not hold a climbing hunter, and the entrance points down. The nest answers the danger of that place.
7. (truefalse) Every kind of bird builds a nest of their own.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A koel lays her egg in a crow's nest, and a lapwing lays on almost bare ground.
8. (mcq) Why do godwits spend the winter in India?  
   Options: Northern mud freezes, and Indian mud stays soft / India has no hunters / They come here to lay eggs / They follow the monsoon rain  
   **Answer:** Northern mud freezes, and Indian mud stays soft. *Why:* A godwit feeds by probing mud. When the northern ground freezes, the food is locked away, so she flies to soft mud.
9. (mcq) Which of these birds is a winter visitor to India?  
   Options: Bar-headed goose / House crow / Common myna / Rose-ringed parakeet  
   **Answer:** Bar-headed goose. *Why:* Bar-headed geese nest in Mongolia and Tibet and fly to India for the winter. Crows, mynas and parakeets live here all year.
10. (mcq) Which group of dinosaurs do birds belong to?  
   Options: Theropods, who walked on two legs / Long-necked plant-eaters / Horned plant-eaters / Armoured plant-eaters  
   **Answer:** Theropods, who walked on two legs. *Why:* Theropods had hollow bones, three main toes and a wishbone. Small feathered theropods were the ancestors of birds.
11. (mcq) A fossil found in 1996 showed a small dinosaur with a coat of feathers. Why did that matter?  
   Options: It was strong evidence that birds are dinosaurs / It proved all dinosaurs could fly / It showed dinosaurs were mammals / It showed feathers are scales  
   **Answer:** It was strong evidence that birds are dinosaurs. *Why:* Before 1996, feathers were known only on birds. A feathered dinosaur who could not fly tied the two groups together.
12. (mcq) After a drought, finches had deeper beaks on average. What had changed?  
   Options: Which finches lived and bred / Each finch's own beak / The size of the island / The colour of the seeds  
   **Answer:** Which finches lived and bred. *Why:* Natural selection changes the mix of individuals in a group. No finch's own beak grew deeper.

## Applications
- Tool makers use the same lever rule that beaks follow. Pliers for cutting thick wire have short jaws close to the hinge. Tweezers and long-nosed pliers have long jaws for reaching.
- Scientists who rebuild dinosaurs look at living birds. A hen's bones, eggs and nesting help them work out how Velociraptor grew, bred and moved.
- People who protect wetlands use rings and trackers on migrating birds. The routes show which marshes in India must be kept wet for birds from Siberia.
- The villagers of Pangti in Nagaland once trapped Amur falcons. Since 2013 they have guarded the resting flocks, after local people such as the journalist Bano Haralu reported the trapping.
- Farmers welcome barn owls, because one owl family eats hundreds of rats in a year. The talons and hooked beak show what the owl is good at.
- Bird rescuers read the beak and feet of a tired bird. This tells them what kind of place to let the bird go in.

## Open questions
- **How did flight begin?** Some scientists think small feathered dinosaurs glided down from trees. Others think they ran and leapt from the ground, or flapped to climb slopes. Fossils have not settled it.
- **How does a migrating bird sense the Earth's magnetic field?** One idea is that a chemical in the eye responds to the field. Another is that tiny grains of iron in the beak do. Tests have not settled which is right.
- **Why did only some birds live through the asteroid strike?** Small size, seed-eating and living on the ground may each have helped. Very few bird fossils are known from just after the strike.
- **How much does a sleeping bird rely on the lock in the toe tendons?** The lock is there, but tests on starlings found sleeping birds standing with loose toes. Few kinds of bird have been tested.
- **What colours were most dinosaurs?** Colour grains have been found in only a few feathered fossils. For most dinosaurs, including Tyrannosaurus, nobody knows.

## Connected maths
- In one Beak Lever Test, the scissors cut 16 layers near the hinge. They cut 8 in the middle and 4 at the tips. The three places were 2, 4 and 8 centimetres from the screw. What happens to the layers each time the distance doubles? **Each time the distance doubles, the number of layers halves.**. From 2 to 4 centimetres, the layers fall from 16 to 8. From 4 to 8 centimetres, they fall from 8 to 4. Double the distance gives half the cut. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- For one beak, the bite force multiplied by the distance from the hinge is always 24. Find the force at 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 centimetres from the hinge. At which of these distances is the force more than 5? **The forces are 12, 8, 6, 4 and 3. The force is more than 5 at 2, 3 and 4 centimetres.**. 24 divided by 2 is 12. 24 divided by 3 is 8. 24 divided by 4 is 6. 24 divided by 6 is 4. 24 divided by 8 is 3. So a seed that needs a force of 5 can be cracked only within 4 centimetres of the hinge. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- About 1,200 finches lived on Daphne Major before the 1977 drought. About 180 lived through it. About how many finches out of every 20 lived? **About 3 out of every 20.**. 1,200 divided by 20 is 60, so the flock is 60 groups of 20. 180 divided by 60 is 3. So about 3 finches in every 20 lived, and about 17 in every 20 died. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A wader flies 60 kilometres every hour for 8 hours each night. Her winter marsh is 4,800 kilometres away. How many nights of flying does she need? If she rests for 2 days after every 2 nights of flying, how long is the whole journey? **10 nights of flying. With the rests, the journey takes 18 days.**. 60 times 8 is 480 kilometres each night. 4,800 divided by 480 is 10 nights. Ten nights are five pairs of nights. She rests after the first four pairs, which is 4 rests of 2 days, or 8 days. 10 plus 8 is 18 days. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In one Frozen Mud Test, the tweezers found 9 beans in soft mud. In the frozen bowl they found 0 at the start, 2 after 20 minutes and 6 after 40 minutes. If the thawing goes on in the same way, about when will the count reach 9? Why can you not be sure? **At about 55 to 60 minutes. You cannot be sure, because the count did not rise by the same amount each time.**. The count rose by 2 in the first 20 minutes and by 4 in the next 20. Three more beans are needed. At 4 or more beans every 20 minutes, that takes about 15 minutes more. The speed of thawing is changing, so this is an estimate. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- Birds first flew about 150 million years ago. The asteroid struck 66 million years ago. For how many million years did birds live beside the other dinosaurs? Our own kind of human is about 300,000 years old. How many times longer is that shared time? **84 million years. That is 280 times as long as our kind of human has existed.**. 150 minus 66 is 84 million years. 84 million divided by 300,000 is the same as 840 divided by 3, which is 280. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
