# Pollution: what makes air, water and land dirty, and where it comes from
`sci.environment.pollution.L3`

**Summary.** Air can look clear and still carry dust and smoke too small to see one by one. Pollution means harmful things added to air, water or land faster than nature can clear them away. Most of it comes from everyday things: vehicles, burning rubbish, building work, cooking smoke, sewage and plastic. In this unit you catch the air's dust on a sticky card at your window and count it. You find out how your nose catches dust all day, why dust on a leaf is a problem for the plant, and why soil holds back mud but lets salt through to the water underground.

**Intro.** *Wet a white cloth and wipe the window grill on the side that faces the road. Then wipe a shelf inside a closed cupboard. Look at the two cloths. Where did the black on the first cloth come from?* Nobody poured dirt on the window grill. It came out of the air, a tiny speck at a time, over days. In this unit you find out what those specks are, where they come from, and where else they land, including inside your own nose.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what pollution is: harmful things added to air, water or land faster than nature can clear them away.
1. I can name where air pollution comes from in an Indian city, such as vehicles, burning rubbish, building dust and cooking smoke.
2. I can catch dust from the air on a sticky card, count the specks in a square and compare places fairly.

6. I can suggest changes at home and in the city that make less pollution, and say who they help.

## Terms
- **Pollution**: Harmful things added to air, water or land faster than nature can clear them away. *e.g.* Smoke from burning rubbish makes the air dirty. That is air pollution.
- **Pollutant**: One harmful thing that causes pollution, such as smoke, soot, sewage or plastic. *e.g.* Soot from a diesel lorry is a pollutant in the air.
- **Particle**: A very small piece of something. Dust and smoke are made of particles floating in the air. *e.g.* Each speck on your sticky card is a particle that fell or blew out of the air.
- **Soot**: Black particles left when something burns without enough air, such as diesel, wood, kerosene or rubbish. *e.g.* The black mark above an oil lamp's flame is soot.
- **Fine particles (PM2.5)**: Particles so small that about 30 of them side by side are as wide as one hair. Scientists call them PM2.5. They float in the air for days and can reach deep into the lungs. *e.g.* Smoke from a mosquito coil or incense stick is full of fine particles.
- **Mucus**: The wet, sticky layer inside your nose and airways. It catches dust and germs from the air you breathe. *e.g.* After a day near a busy road, the mucus you blow into a tissue can come out grey.
- **Sewage**: Used water from toilets, kitchens and bathrooms, carrying waste, soap and germs. *e.g.* In many towns, some sewage still flows into rivers and lakes without being cleaned.
- **Groundwater**: Water that has soaked down through soil and rock and is stored under the ground. Wells and borewells pump it up. *e.g.* Many flats in Chennai and Bengaluru get some of their water from a borewell, which pumps up groundwater.
- **Air Quality Index (AQI)**: A number that says how polluted the outside air is today. Low numbers mean cleaner air. High numbers mean dirtier air. *e.g.* On the morning after Bhogi, Chennai's AQI often jumps much higher than on an ordinary day.
- **Haze**: A dull, grey or yellow look in the air, made by many fine particles. Far-away buildings look blurry in haze. *e.g.* On a hazy winter morning, you may not see a water tank you can usually see clearly from your window.

## Explanations
### What pollution is · *defined*
**Definition:** Pollution is the adding of harmful things to air, water or land faster than nature can clear them away. Each harmful thing is called a pollutant.

Nature cleans up all the time. Rain washes dust out of the air. Microbes in soil and water break down dead leaves and dung. Wind spreads smoke out. Pollution happens when people add more than these can deal with. A little smoke from one lamp spreads out and is hard to notice. Smoke from thousands of vehicles and fires, all day, piles up in the air of a city. Some pollutants, such as plastic and some metals, are hardly cleared away at all, so even small amounts build up over years.
- e.g. One cooking fire in a village spreads its smoke into a wide sky. Thousands of fires in a city on the same evening make a haze.
- e.g. A river can break down a little waste from a few houses. Sewage from a whole city is far more than the river's microbes can deal with.
- e.g. Plastic in a drain does not rot, so every bag thrown in adds to the pile.
### Where air pollution comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** Air pollution is smoke, dust and harmful gases in the air. In Indian cities, most of it comes from vehicles, road and building dust, burning rubbish, factories and power plants, and cooking or heating with wood, dung or kerosene.

Anything that burns gives off smoke. Smoke is made of particles, such as soot, and gases. Diesel lorries, buses and old two-wheelers give off a lot of soot. Burning rubbish and dry leaves on the roadside adds smoke too, and burning plastic adds poisonous gases. Building sites and dusty roads throw dust into the air whenever vehicles pass or the wind blows. In winter, farmers in parts of North India burn the stubble left in their fields after the harvest, and the smoke spreads over many cities. Firecrackers at Diwali, and bonfires of old things at Bhogi in Tamil Nadu, add a lot of smoke in a few hours. Some harmful gases have no colour at all, so dirty air can look quite clear.
- e.g. On Bhogi morning in Chennai, smoke from bonfires of old mats and tyres has delayed flights at the airport.
- e.g. Smoke from burning stubble in Punjab and Haryana adds to Delhi's haze in late October and November.
- e.g. A building site next door leaves a grey layer on every window sill nearby.
### Indoor air: smoke inside the home · *defined*
**Definition:** Indoor air pollution is smoke, dust and harmful gases in the air inside a building. It comes from cooking, incense, mosquito coils, candles and dust brought in from outside.

Many people think the air inside is always cleaner. It is not always so. A tadka of mustard seeds in hot oil, frying papads or a burning incense stick all fill a kitchen with fine particles. A burning mosquito coil gives off a lot of fine smoke for hours, in a closed bedroom. Cooking on a wood or dung fire inside a home is one of the biggest causes of illness in the world, which is why gas stoves help. An exhaust fan or an open window while cooking lets the smoke out. On days when the outside air is very dirty, keeping windows closed for a while can help.
- e.g. After frying, the kitchen smells of oil for an hour. That smell is tiny droplets and gases still floating in the air.
- e.g. Your sticky card in the kitchen catches oily specks, not only dust.
- e.g. Switching on the exhaust fan while cooking carries the smoke outside.
### Tiny particles and your body · *defined*
**Definition:** Your nose and airways clean the air you breathe. Hairs in your nose catch bigger dust. Sticky mucus catches smaller dust. The smallest particles, the fine particles called PM2.5, can slip past and reach deep into the lungs.

You breathe in and out about 20,000 times a day. All that air passes over the wet, sticky mucus in your nose and airways, the way air passed over the jelly on your card. Dust sticks to the mucus. Tiny hairs in your airways, called cilia, sweep the dirty mucus up and out, and you swallow it or blow it out. That is why a tissue can come out grey after a dusty day. The finest particles are about 30 times thinner than a hair. They can float in the air for days and reach the smallest air sacs of the lungs. Breathing them for years can cause coughs, asthma and heart illness. Children breathe more air for their size than grown-ups, so dirty air affects them more.
- e.g. After Diwali crackers, many people blow grey or black mucus into a tissue.
- e.g. A cloth mask or handkerchief stops big dust. A well-fitted N95 mask stops most fine particles too.
- e.g. People who walk to school along a busy road breathe more soot than those who walk along a quiet lane.
### Dust on leaves: a problem for the plant · *defined*
**Definition:** A leaf makes the plant's food from sunlight, air and water. Dust on a leaf blocks some of the sunlight, and can clog the tiny holes in the leaf that let air in and out.

A plant cannot walk away from a dusty road, so their leaves meet whatever the air brings. A coat of dust means less light reaches the leaf, so the plant makes less food. Plants deal with this in several ways. Rain washes most dust off. Smooth, waxy leaves, like those of a money plant or a banana plant, let dust slide off easily. Hairy or rough leaves, like tulsi and many roadside trees, hold on to dust. That is hard for the plant. Still, less of that dust reaches people. Trees along a road catch a lot of dust on their leaves, which is one reason streets with trees often have cleaner air.
- e.g. Just before the monsoon, the leaves of roadside trees look grey. After the first heavy rain, they are bright green again.
- e.g. A damp cotton ball wiped over the top of a balcony leaf comes away grey.
- e.g. Banana and money plant leaves, which are smooth and waxy, shed water drops and dust quickly.
### Water and land pollution · *defined*
**Definition:** Water pollution is harmful things in rivers, lakes, the sea or groundwater. Land pollution is harmful things on and in the ground, such as rubbish, plastic and poisons.

Sewage is the biggest cause of dirty rivers in India. When sewage pours into a river, microbes feed on the waste and use up the oxygen in the water, so fish can run short of oxygen. Soap, oil, paint and factory waste make water harmful too. Rubbish heaped on open ground, and plastic in drains, pollute the land. Rain soaks through the rubbish and carries some of what is in it down into the soil. Soil is a good filter for pieces, like mud and peels. But things dissolved in water, such as salt and many chemicals, pass through the soil with the water. They can reach the groundwater that borewells pump up.
- e.g. Foam on some city lakes, such as Bellandur Lake in Bengaluru, comes from sewage with soap and detergent in it.
- e.g. In your soil test, the muddy water came out clearer, but the salt in it went straight through.
- e.g. A plastic bag in a drain stops the water flowing, and the drain floods in the rain.
### Do house plants clean the air in a room? · *debated*
Plant leaves and the microbes in potting soil take in some pollutant gases from the air. That much is agreed. The question is how much difference a few pots make in a real room, where air comes in and goes out all the time.
- e.g. Many shops sell money plants and snake plants as 'air-purifying plants'.
- e.g. Opening a window for ten minutes changes all the air in a room.
- **A study for NASA in 1989, led by Bill Wolverton:** Plants in small sealed chambers took some harmful gases out of the air within a day. Many people took this to mean that house plants clean a home.
- **Later reviews of many studies, such as one by Bryan Cummings and Michael Waring in 2019:** A real room is not a sealed box. To clean the air as much as ordinary airflow through windows and doors does, a room would need to be packed with plants, far more than anyone keeps. Plants are good for people in other ways, but they are not an air filter.

## Mostly agreed
- Breathing dirty air for a long time harms the lungs and heart. *How we know:* Studies of millions of people around the world, gathered by the World Health Organization, show more asthma, lung illness and heart illness where the air has more fine particles.
- Fine particles, PM2.5, are the most harmful part of air pollution. *How we know:* They are small enough to reach the deepest air sacs of the lungs. Doctors have found them in lung tissue, and illness rises as their amount in the air rises.
- Most air pollution in Indian cities comes from burning and from dust. *How we know:* Scientists have collected particles from city air, studied what they are made of, and matched them to vehicles, road dust, burning rubbish, industry and cooking fires.
- Sewage is the main cause of dirty rivers in India. *How we know:* The Central Pollution Control Board tests rivers across India. Stretches downstream of cities have far less oxygen and far more germs than stretches upstream.

## How we found out
**Clair Patterson, an American scientist, with his co-workers Masayo Murozumi and Tsaihwa Chow; Robert Kehoe, who held the opposite view, 1950s (the clean lab and the Earth's age), 1965 (Patterson's warning about lead), 1969 (the Greenland ice results).** In the 1950s, Clair Patterson, an American scientist, set out to measure the age of the Earth from tiny amounts of lead in rocks. He kept getting wrong results, because lead from the air was getting into his samples. It was on the dust, his tools, even his hands. So he built one of the world's first very clean labs, with filtered air and scrubbed surfaces, and found the Earth's age: about 4.55 billion years. But a question stayed with him. Where was all that lead in the air coming from? Most experts, such as Robert Kehoe, who worked with the makers of leaded petrol, said the lead in air and in people's bodies was natural and had always been there. Patterson tested this. With his co-workers Masayo Murozumi and Tsaihwa Chow, he measured lead in ice from deep in the Greenland ice sheet. Each year's snow is a layer, so old layers hold old air. Snow from long ago had almost no lead. Recent layers had far more, and the rise was steepest after leaded petrol came into use. The lead was not natural. Countries slowly stopped selling leaded petrol. India stopped in 2000, and the whole world had stopped by 2021.  
*What people believed before:* Most experts said the lead in the air and in people's bodies was natural and had always been there. Layers of old Greenland ice showed that there had been almost no lead in the air before people began burning leaded petrol.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Air that looks clear is clean.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: On a clear blue day we cannot see any dust or smoke.  
  Against: Your sticky card caught specks from air that looked clear. Fine particles are too small to see one by one, and some harmful gases have no colour at all.
- **The air inside a home is always cleaner than outside.** (Many families; *rejected*)  
  For: Walls and windows keep out a lot of road dust.  
  Against: Cooking smoke, incense and mosquito coils add fine particles inside. A kitchen during frying can have dirtier air than the road outside.
- **A few pot plants clean the air in a room.** (Many advertisements, based on a 1989 NASA study; *debated*)  
  For: In small sealed boxes, plants took harmful gases out of the air.  
  Against: Later studies found that a real room needs far more plants than anyone keeps to match what fresh air from a window does.
- **Burning dry leaves is natural, so it does no harm.** (Some people who sweep and burn leaves; *rejected*)  
  For: Leaves are natural, and forests sometimes burn by themselves.  
  Against: Burning leaves gives off thick smoke full of fine particles, right where people live and breathe. Leaves left to rot, or put in compost, become soil instead.

## Roots
We have caught the air's dust on sticky cards, on leaves and in our own noses. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** After one day, your window card still looked white. After three days, its square was grey with specks. No single speck was big enough to notice, but together they covered the card. Pollution grows the same way, from small amounts added every day, until rain and wind cannot clear them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We look for pollution in big, dramatic things, like a black cloud from a chimney. But most of it builds up from tiny amounts, from every vehicle, stove and fire, each too small to notice on its own.  
*Evidence:* On day 2 the cards still looked almost white. By day 3 the window card was grey, while the card in the closed dabba stayed white, so the specks came from the air and had piled up over the days. Clair Patterson's Greenland ice showed the same thing over many years: lead from petrol, too little to see in one year's snow, building up layer after layer.

- *who am i:* You are a body that air flows through all day. Some of what the air carries stays in you.
- *what is life:* Every living thing that meets the air, from a crow to a tulsi plant, meets the same dust.
- *what is this world:* The air over a city is shared. Smoke made in one lane ends up in the breath of people far away.

There is one air over a city, the same air for every creature in it. Every breath is drawn from it: yours, the crow's on the window sill, and the air taken in by the neem tree's leaves. When one person burns a tyre, the smoke does not stay theirs. It spreads into that one air and comes back as many breaths, in many noses and on many leaves. One air, shared by countless lives, is a plain, everyday picture of the One becoming the many.

- **Atharva Veda 12.1.12 (the Prithvi Sukta, the hymn to the Earth):** The Earth is my mother, and I am her child. (A child's body is built from what the mother gives. Our bodies are built from the Earth's air, water and food, so what we put into the air and water comes back into us.)
- **Acharanga Sutra, Book 1, Lecture 1 (Jain teaching on not harming):** There are living beings in earth, in water, in fire, in plants and in the air. A careful person does not harm any of them. (Jain teachers saw the air itself as full of life to be cared for. Smoke harms the beings who live by breathing, from the smallest insect to us.)
- **Rachel Carson, Silent Spring (1962):** In nature, nothing exists alone. (The smoke from one fire never stays alone. It lands on window sills, on leaves, and on the inside of every nose nearby, as your sticky cards showed.)
- Science (settled): The nose, the airways and their mucus remove most of the bigger particles from the air we breathe. The finest particles reach the deepest air sacs of the lungs.
- Science (open): Some of the finest particles pass from the lungs into the blood. How much harm they do to the heart and brain over a lifetime is still being studied.
- Other view, Many scientists: Air is a mixture of gases and particles. Sharing it does not make us one, any more than sharing a road does. Pollution is a problem of chemistry and health, to be solved with cleaner fuels, filters and fair laws, with no need for ideas about oneness.
- Practice, The breath you share (3 min): Let's sit by the window and close our eyes. Breathe in slowly through your nose while I count to four. Now breathe out while I count to four. Feel the air go in, cool, and come out, warm. Do it five more times. Now think: where was this air a minute ago? Who will breathe it after you? Open your eyes.
- Ask: No single speck made your card grey. So who made it grey?
- Ask: The air you breathed out a minute ago is in the room now. Is it still yours?
- Ask: Your nose cleans the air for your lungs. Who cleans the air for the city?

## Story: The Grey Card on Bhogi Morning
*The story is made up. The facts in it are true: Bhogi is the first day of Pongal, families in Tamil Nadu burn old things in bonfires that morning, and the smoke in Chennai has delayed flights at the airport. Tamil Nadu's Pollution Control Board asks people not to burn tyres, plastic or rubber.*

Kavya lived on the fourth floor of a block of flats in Chennai. She was nine. Her bedroom window looked down on a lane, and past the lane, on the main road with its buses and autos.

Two days before Pongal, her cousin Arjun came from Coimbatore to stay. Arjun was seven, and he had one plan. 'On Bhogi morning,' he said, 'we make the biggest bonfire in the lane. Uncle downstairs has an old tyre. Tyres burn for ages.'

Kavya's mother, Revathi, was ironing in the next room. She did not say yes and she did not say no. She said, 'Before you decide, would you two like to do an experiment?'

She cut three cards from the inside of an empty biscuit box. On each one, Kavya drew a square with a ruler, as big as her thumbnail. Then Amma gave them a small tin of petroleum jelly. 'Spread a thin layer inside each square,' she said. 'The jelly is sticky. Anything that touches it stays.'

Arjun spread his very thick. Kavya made hers so thin that the card only looked shiny.

They taped the first card flat on the window sill, jelly side up. They taped the second card upright on the window grill, facing the road, so nothing could fall onto it. The third card went inside a steel dabba, with the lid shut tight.

'Why the dabba?' asked Arjun.

'So we know what a card looks like when the air cannot reach it,' said Kavya. 'Otherwise how will we know the specks came from the air?'

That evening, the cards on the window looked white. Arjun was disappointed. 'Nothing,' he said.

'Wait,' said Amma.

The next day was Bhogi. Before the sun came up, Kavya woke to the smell of smoke. She went to the window. Down in the lane, and in every lane she could see, small fires were burning. People were throwing on old mats, broken baskets and torn clothes. On the main road, one fire was black and thick. Someone had put a tyre on it.

The sky was not blue that morning. It was a flat grey, and the tall water tank at the end of the road, which she could always see, had gone pale and blurry. On the radio, a man said that flights at Chennai airport were waiting, because the pilots could not see the runway well enough.

Arjun came to the window and coughed. 'My eyes are itching,' he said.

At lunchtime they took down the cards. The one in the dabba was as white as the day they made it. The upright card on the grill had a grey haze in its square. And the flat card on the sill was the worst. Its square was grey all over, with black specks in it, and a few shiny bits, and one tiny fibre from someone's cloth.

Amma gave them the magnifying glass from the drawer. Kavya counted the specks in one corner of the flat card's square. 'Forty-three,' she said. 'Just in this corner.'

Arjun looked at the upright card. 'But nothing could fall on this one,' he said. 'It was standing up. How did the dust get on it?'

'Some specks are so tiny they do not fall,' said Amma. 'They float in the air for days, and the wind blows them against anything in the way.' She set down the iron. 'Like the inside of your nose.'

Arjun touched his nose.

'Your nose is sticky inside, like that jelly,' Amma said. 'Every breath goes past it, and the sticky mucus catches dust. Blow your nose tonight into a white tissue, and look.'

That night, Arjun did, and he looked, and he made a face. The tissue was grey.

'So the smoke from the bonfires went into my nose,' he said slowly. 'And the tyre smoke too.'

'Some of it,' said Amma. 'Your nose catches the bigger specks. The smallest ones go further in, into your lungs. That is why the doctors say tyres and plastic must never be burned. Their smoke has poisons in it, as well as soot.'

Arjun was quiet for a long time. Then he said, 'But Bhogi is for getting rid of old things. If we don't burn them, what do we do with them?'

Kavya had been thinking about that too. 'Paati says Bhogi means letting go of old things to make space for new ones,' she said. 'It doesn't say you have to set them on fire.'

So the next year, on Bhogi morning, Kavya and Arjun did something different. They went round the flats with a big cloth bag. The torn clothes went to the kabadiwala, who sells them on to be shredded and made into new cloth. The old grass mats went into the compost pit at the corner of the park. The broken toys went in a box for a woman who mends them. And on the window sill, they taped up three fresh cards.

At lunchtime they compared them with last year's. The new flat card was grey too, because many people in the city still had bonfires. But it was less grey than last year's, and Arjun said his eyes did not itch at all.

'Our lane made less smoke,' said Kavya. 'And our noses caught less of it.'

**For the parent:** The dust that lands on a sticky card is the same dust that lands inside your nose, because both catch whatever is in the air you breathe.

- Ask: Why did Kavya put the third card in a closed dabba?
- Ask: Nothing could fall onto the upright card. How did specks get on it?
- Ask: Why was Arjun's tissue grey that night?
- Ask: What else could a family do with old things on Bhogi, instead of burning them?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Sticky Card Test: what is in the air at your window? (30 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Does the air at our window leave dust on a sticky card, even where nothing can fall on it? How much, compared with a card the air cannot reach?
- **predict:** Guess first. After three days, which card will have the most specks: the flat one on the sill, the upright one on the grill, or the one in the closed dabba? Will the upright card catch anything at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three cards are the same size, with the same square and the same thin layer of jelly, and stay out for the same three days. Only the place changes. The card in the closed dabba is the control: it shows what a card looks like when the air cannot reach it, so we know the specks on the others came from the air.
- **safety:** A grown-up tapes the card to the outside sill or grill. Children never lean out of a window or climb on the sill. Wash hands after using the jelly.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw what the specks looked like under the glass. Stick the three cards in your notebook with clear tape over them.
- **results table:** Card | Where it was | Colour of the square: white, light grey or dark grey | Specks counted in one corner of the square | What the specks looked like
- **schedule:** day 1: Make three sticky cards. Put one flat on the window sill, one upright on the grill, and one in a closed dabba. Write your guess. (15 min); day 2: Look at the cards without touching them. Can you see anything yet? (2 min); day 3: Bring the cards in. Look with the magnifying glass. Count the specks in one corner of each square. Fill in the table and say what you found. (13 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut three cards. 2. With a ruler, draw a square 2 cm on each side in the middle of each card. 3. Turn them over and write A, B and C.

  *Child mode:* Cut three cards. Draw a square on each one. Make it 2 cm on each side. Write A, B and C on the back.

  *Child mode:* Spread a little jelly in each square. Make it thin and shiny. Then wash your hands.
  - Note: If you use coconut oil, it may soak into the card and leave a see-through patch. That is fine. Specks still stick to it. Solid coconut oil in cool weather works just like jelly.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess which card will catch most, and why. Write the guess in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* One card lies flat on the sill. One stands upright on the grill, so nothing can fall on it. One goes in a closed dabba. Which will be dirtiest after three days? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which card will be dirtiest after three days? Will the standing card catch anything? Say why.
  - Ask: Why might the upright card catch less than the flat one? (good: Dust falls down, so it lands on flat things.; Nothing can drop onto a card that stands up.)
  - Note: Wait for a guess and a reason. Do not explain anything yet. Many children say the upright card will stay clean. That is a good guess to test.
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape card A flat on the window sill, jelly up, on the outside if you can reach safely. 2. Tape card B upright on the grill or the window frame, jelly facing outdoors. 3. Put card C in the steel dabba and close the lid. 4. Write the date and time.  
  *Parent says:* Card A can catch dust that falls and dust that blows. Card B can only catch dust that blows into it. Card C is shut away from the air. It shows us what a card looks like with nothing on it.  
  *Child mode:* A goes flat on the sill. B stands up on the grill. C goes in the dabba. Close the lid tight. Write today's date.
  - Note: Do the taping yourself if the sill is outside the grill or the flat is high up. Tape all four edges of A, or the wind can lift it. If rain is likely, choose a spot under the sunshade, because rain washes dust off. Card C is the control: it shows the specks did not come from the box or the jelly.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, look at the cards without touching them.  
  *Parent says:* Can you see anything on the cards yet? Don't touch them, or your finger will leave its own marks.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the cards. Don't touch. Can you see anything yet?
  - Note: Often the cards still look nearly white after one day. That is part of the lesson: the specks are there, but too few to see without a lens.
  - Wait: Leave the cards for 1 day
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3: 1. Untape the cards carefully, holding the edges. 2. Put the three cards side by side on a white sheet. 3. Look at each square with the magnifying glass. 4. Count the specks in the top left corner of each square, a quarter of the square.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look closely. Count only the specks in the top left corner, so the counting doesn't take all day.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the three cards side by side. Look with the glass. Count the specks in one corner of each square.
  - Ask: What colours and shapes are the specks? (good: Black specks, like soot.; Brown and grey bits, like dust from the road.; Little threads from cloth.; Shiny bits.)
  - Note: Black specks are mostly soot from burning. Brown and grey bits are soil and road dust. Threads are fibres from clothes and curtains. You may also see a tiny insect. If there are too many specks to count even in a corner, rate the colour of each square instead: white, light grey or dark grey.
  - Wait: Leave the cards for 1 more day
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill in the table together. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Let's write down what we saw. Then let's see what it tells us.  
  *Child mode:* Fill in the table. Which card is dirtiest? Which is cleanest?
  - Check: Card B stood upright on the grill, so nothing could fall onto it. It still caught some specks. What does that show? → **Some dust floats in the air and is blown against things**
- **s8 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write a claim, the evidence, and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you found out, what you saw that shows it, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I found. What I saw. Why I think so.
  - Ask: What did you find out? (good: The air at our window carries dust, even when it looks clear.; The flat card caught the most, but the upright card caught some too.; The card in the dabba stayed clean, so the specks came from the air.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses the dabba card as proof that the specks came from the air. Now is the time to read the explanation cards.
### e2 · The Leaf Wipe: where does dust sit on a plant? (20 min)
- **question:** On a potted plant near a window, is there more dust on the top of a leaf or underneath it? Is there more on an outer leaf or an inner one?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the top of a leaf be dirtier than the underside? Will a leaf on the outside of the plant, facing the window, be dirtier than one tucked inside? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same size of cotton ball, damp the same amount, and wipe each leaf with three gentle strokes. Wipe leaves of about the same size and age. Only the side or the place of the leaf changes.
- **safety:** Wipe gently, and hold each leaf from below so it doesn't tear. Wash hands afterwards.
- **record:** Lay the cotton balls on the labelled paper. Draw them, shading each one as grey as it is. Write which side of the leaf was dirtiest.
- **results table:** Leaf | Top or underside | Colour of the cotton: white, light grey or dark grey
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the plant together. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This plant has stood by the window for days. Where on their leaves do you think the dust has landed? Top, underside, outside or inside? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the plant. Where is the dust? Top of the leaf, or underneath? Say why.
  - Note: Wait for a guess and a reason. Most children guess the top, which is a good start. Fewer think about outer and inner leaves.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick three leaves: one facing the window, one on the far side, and one from the middle. 2. For each, wet a cotton ball, squeeze it out, and wipe the top with three gentle strokes. 3. Use a fresh ball for the underside. 4. Lay each ball on the paper by its label.  
  *Parent says:* Three gentle strokes each, the same every time. If we wiped one leaf harder, the test wouldn't be fair.  
  *Child mode:* Wipe the top of the leaf three times. Use a new ball for underneath. Put each ball on its label.
  - Note: Hold each leaf flat on your palm while your child wipes, so the stem doesn't snap. On a hairy leaf like tulsi, dab rather than wipe.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the six cotton balls. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which ball is greyest? Which is whitest? What does that tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Which ball is the greyest? Which leaf did it come from?
  - Check: The cotton from the top of the leaf is grey. The cotton from underneath is nearly white. Why? → **Most dust falls from above and lands on top**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about what the dust means for the plant.  
  *Parent says:* A leaf makes the plant's food from sunlight. What does a layer of dust do to the sunlight? How does this plant get the dust off on their own, out in the rain?  
  *Child mode:* Leaves need sunlight to make food. What does dust do to the light?
  - Ask: Why is dust a problem for the plant? (good: It blocks some sunlight, so the leaf makes less food.; It can block the tiny holes the leaf breathes through.)
  - Note: Rain washes most dust off leaves. A plant on a covered balcony gets no rain, so a gentle wipe or a spray of water now and then helps them. You can offer that as a kindness, not a chore.
### e3 · Through the Soil: what does soil stop, and what goes through? (25 min)
- **question:** When dirty water soaks into the ground, does the soil clean it? Does it stop the mud? Does it stop the salt?
- **predict:** Guess first. We pour muddy, salty water through a bottle of soil. Will the water that comes out be clear? Will the salt stay in the soil, or come through? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Dry one spoonful of the water that came through the soil, and one spoonful of plain tap water, side by side on the same steel plate, in the same place. Only the water changes. The tap water spoonful is the control.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the bottle, because the cut edge is sharp. Never drink or taste the water in this experiment. Wash hands after handling soil.
- **record:** Draw the soil filter, with the dirty water going in and the water coming out. Draw the two dry spots on the plate and label them.
- **results table:** Water | How it looked: cloudy or clear | What was left on the plate after drying
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts the bottle in half. 2. Push the cotton into the neck of the top half. 3. Turn it upside down inside the bottom half. 4. Fill it two-thirds full of soil.  
  *Parent says:* Rain soaks down through soil before it reaches the water under the ground. This bottle is a small piece of ground.  
  *Child mode:* Push cotton into the bottle neck. Turn it upside down. Fill it with soil.
  - Note: Cover the cut edge with a strip of cello tape so nobody gets scratched.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir 2 teaspoons of salt into a glass of water until you can't see it. 2. Add 1 spoon of soil and stir to make it muddy. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This is our dirty water. It has mud we can see, and salt we can't see, because the salt has dissolved. Dissolved means broken up into pieces too small to see, spread all through the water. What will come out of the bottom? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Stir salt into the water. Stir in some soil too. What will come out of the bottle? Say why.
  - Ask: Will the salt come through? (good: Yes, because it is dissolved in the water.; No, because the soil will catch it.)
  - Note: Either guess is fine, as long as there is a reason. The salt is standing in for the many harmful things that dissolve in water, such as some chemicals from factories and farms.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour the dirty water slowly onto the soil. 2. Wait for the water to drip through, about 5 minutes. 3. Compare the water that came out with a little of the dirty water you kept back.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the water dripping out. Is it clearer than what went in?  
  *Child mode:* Pour the water in slowly. Wait. Is the water that comes out clearer?
  - Note: The first drips may be brown, because some fine soil washes through. Pour the first half cup back through once, and it comes out clearer. Clean soil stops most of the mud.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one teaspoon of the water that came through on one side of the steel plate. 2. Put one teaspoon of plain tap water on the other side. 3. Leave the plate in a sunny window until both are dry, a few hours.  
  *Parent says:* The mud was stopped. What about the salt? We can't see it in the water, but if the water dries up, the salt gets left behind.  
  *Child mode:* Put a spoon of each water on the plate. Leave it in the sun. Look when it is dry.
  - Note: On a cloudy day, drying can take the whole day. A grown-up can dry the spoonfuls faster by warming the steel plate on a tawa over a low flame.
  - Wait: Leave the plate in the sun for a few hours
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the dry plate together. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* What is left where each spoonful dried?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the plate. What is left where the water dried?
  - Check: The water that came through the soil left a thick white crust. The tap water left only a faint ring. What does this show? → **The soil stopped the mud, but the dissolved salt went through**

### a2 · Smoke and Dust Hunt: where does our air pollution come from? (15 min)
- **safety:** Look from the window or balcony. Stay inside the grill.
- **record:** Make a tally chart: one row for each source, one tally mark each time you spot it.
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look out of each window for five minutes. Note every source of smoke or dust: vehicles, a building site, a rubbish fire, a factory chimney, a dusty road.  
  *Parent says:* Let's be air detectives. Every time you see something making smoke or dust, write it down and put a tally mark.  
  *Child mode:* Look out of the window. What makes smoke or dust? Write it down. Add a tally each time.
  - Note: Diesel buses and lorries often give off a visible puff of black smoke when they start moving. Count those puffs.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Walk through the home. Note sources inside: the stove, incense, a mosquito coil, a candle, dust on the floor.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's look inside. Does anything in our home make smoke?  
  *Child mode:* Now look inside. Does anything here make smoke?
  - Note: Children are often surprised that incense, coils and frying count. Don't make it a telling-off. It is a list of what is there.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the tally chart together.  
  *Parent says:* Which source did you see most often? Which one do you think makes the most smoke?  
  *Child mode:* Which did you see most? Which do you think is worst? Why?
  - Ask: Is the source you saw most often the one that makes the most smoke? (good: Not always. A tyre fire is rare but makes a lot of smoke.; Buses pass all day, so they add up.)
  - Note: This is a good moment to notice that how often and how much are two different things.
### a3 · Your Nose, the Dust Catcher (8 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** Look, don't poke. Never put anything inside your nose. Wash hands after using a tissue.
- **record:** Draw what you saw inside your nose. Write the colour of each tissue: white, light grey or dark grey.
- **schedule:** day 1: Look inside your nose with a mirror and torch. In the evening after a day out on busy roads, blow your nose into a white tissue and look. (4 min); day 2: In the evening after a day at home, blow your nose into a white tissue and compare. (4 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold a mirror. Shine a torch towards one nostril and look inside.  
  *Parent says:* Can you see tiny hairs inside your nose? The inside is wet and sticky too. What do you think they are for?  
  *Child mode:* Look in the mirror with the torch. Can you see hairs inside your nose?
  - Note: The hairs catch bigger dust. The wet lining is mucus, which catches smaller dust.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On an evening after a trip on busy roads, blow your nose into a white tissue and look at it.  
  *Parent says:* Your nose has been catching dust all day, like the sticky card. What colour is the tissue?  
  *Child mode:* Blow your nose into a white tissue. What colour is it?
  - Wait: Do this on an evening after a trip out
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On an evening after a day at home, do the same. Compare.  
  *Parent says:* Which day did your nose catch more? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Do it again after a day at home. Which tissue is greyer?
  - Note: Not every child's tissue will look grey. In cleaner air, or after a short trip, it may stay white. That is good news, not a failed test.
  - Check: Divya's tissue is greyer after a day on busy roads than after a day at home. Why? → **Her nose caught more dust from the dirtier air**
  - Wait: Do this on an evening after a day at home
### a4 · Haze Watch: can you see the far tower today? (10 min, 5 days)
- **safety:** Look from inside the window or grill.
- **record:** A table with the date, the time, your rating, and the AQI number if you can find it.
- **schedule:** day 1: Choose a far landmark. Rate it: sharp, a little blurry, very blurry, or can't see it. (2 min); day 2: Rate the landmark at the same time of morning. (2 min); day 3: Rate the landmark again. (2 min); day 4: Rate the landmark again. (2 min); day 5: Rate the landmark, then compare all five days. (2 min)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose a landmark at least a few hundred metres away. Rate it on a scale: sharp, a little blurry, very blurry, can't see it.  
  *Parent says:* Haze is made of fine particles in the air between us and the tower. The more particles, the blurrier it looks.  
  *Child mode:* Find something far away. How clear is it today? Sharp, blurry or can't see?
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each morning, at about the same time, rate the landmark again. If you have an air quality app, write the AQI too.  
  *Parent says:* Same window, same time, every day. Why do you think the time has to be the same?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the same thing at the same time each day. Write how clear it is.
  - Note: Morning mist after rain is water drops, not pollution. Note if it rained. In winter, haze is often worst early in the morning, when cold air near the ground keeps smoke from rising.
  - Wait: Repeat each morning for 4 more days
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare all five days.  
  *Parent says:* Which day was clearest? Did anything happen the day before, like rain, wind or a festival?  
  *Child mode:* Which day was clearest? Which was haziest? What happened before?
  - Ask: Why might the air be clearer after rain? (good: Rain washes the dust out of the air.; The rain drops catch the particles and bring them down.)

### c1 · The Window Dust Filter (30 min)

## Worksheets
### Air Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 3 in Madurai, wipes her window grill with a damp white cloth. The cloth comes away grey. Where did the grey come from?  
   Options: Dust and soot that came out of the air / The paint on the grill / The water in the cloth  
   **Answer:** Dust and soot that came out of the air. *Why:* Nobody put dirt on the grill. Dust and soot came out of the air, a speck at a time, and stuck to it. A strong answer says the dirt came from the air, and names a source such as vehicles or a building site.
2. (fill) Harmful things added to air, water or land faster than nature can clear them away are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pollution']. *Why:* Pollution is the word for it. One harmful thing, such as smoke, is called a pollutant.
3. (sort) Sort each one: does it mostly pollute the air, the water or the land?  
   **Answer:** smoke from a diesel lorry → Air; sewage poured into a river → Water; a rubbish heap on an empty plot → Land; dust from a building site → Air; washing a scooter in a lake → Water; plastic bags buried in a field → Land. *Why:* Smoke and dust float in the air. Sewage and scooter washing go into the water. Rubbish and plastic stay on and in the ground. A strong answer notices that some things pollute more than one, such as burning rubbish, which dirties the air and leaves ash on the land.
4. (mcq) In the Sticky Card Test, one card was shut in a closed steel dabba. Why?  
   Options: To show what a card looks like when the air cannot reach it / To keep it safe for later / To catch dust from inside the dabba  
   **Answer:** To show what a card looks like when the air cannot reach it. *Why:* The dabba card is the control. It had the same jelly and stayed clean, so we know the specks on the other cards came from the air, not from the jelly or the card.
5. (truefalse) Lakshmi says, 'If the air looks clear, it must be clean.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Fine particles are too small to see one by one, and some harmful gases have no colour. The sticky card caught specks from air that looked clear.
6. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Soot → black particles from burning; Sewage → used water from toilets and kitchens; Mucus → the sticky layer inside your nose; Haze → a grey look in the air from many fine particles. *Why:* These four words come up all through the unit. A strong answer could also give an example of each.
7. (mcq) Divya blows her nose after a day on a busy road. The tissue is grey. Why?  
   Options: The sticky mucus in her nose caught dust from the air / Her nose made dust / The tissue was dirty  
   **Answer:** The sticky mucus in her nose caught dust from the air. *Why:* Mucus is sticky, like the jelly on the card. Every breath passes over it, and dust sticks. Dirtier air leaves more dust.
8. (write) Name two things in or near your home that put smoke or dust into the air. For each one, say one way to make less.  
   **Answer:** For example: frying in the kitchen, so switch on the exhaust fan. Vehicles on the road, so switch the engine off at a signal.. *Why:* A strong answer names real sources the child has seen, and a change that really reduces them, not just 'be careful'.
9. (mcq) Which of these makes no smoke at all?  
   Options: Putting dry leaves in a compost pit / Burning dry leaves in a small pile / Burning dry leaves at night  
   **Answer:** Putting dry leaves in a compost pit. *Why:* Any burning makes smoke, whatever the size of the pile or the time. Leaves in a compost pit rot into soil.
10. (draw) Draw your three sticky cards after three days: the flat one on the sill, the upright one on the grill, and the one from the dabba. Shade each square as grey as it was.  
   **Answer:** The flat card darkest, the upright card lighter, the dabba card white.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the difference clearly and labels each card with where it was.
11. (truefalse) Smoke from a mosquito coil in a closed bedroom is a kind of air pollution.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A burning coil gives off fine smoke for hours. In a closed room, that smoke stays in the air you breathe all night. A mosquito net keeps mosquitoes off without any smoke.
12. (fill) The water stored under the ground, which borewells pump up, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['groundwater']. *Why:* Groundwater is water that soaked down through soil and rock. Pollution on the ground can reach it.
13. (mcq) Anjali's tulsi plant is on a covered balcony beside a busy road. Their leaves look grey. Why is the dust a problem for the plant?  
   Options: It blocks some of the sunlight the leaves use to make food / It makes the plant heavier / It makes the leaves taste bitter  
   **Answer:** It blocks some of the sunlight the leaves use to make food. *Why:* A leaf makes food from sunlight, air and water. Dust blocks some of the light, so the plant makes less food. On a covered balcony, no rain washes it off.
14. (write) Your upright card on the grill caught some specks, though nothing could fall onto it. Explain how they got there.  
   **Answer:** Some specks are so tiny that they hardly fall. They float in the air, and the wind blows them against the card, where they stick.. *Why:* A strong answer says the specks were floating and carried sideways by the air, not falling.
15. (perspective) Two sisters in Kochi talk about their neighbour, who burns the dry leaves from his garden every week.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses evidence: smoke from any burning carries soot and fine particles, and the sticky card shows that what is in the air lands near and inside us. It also gives the other side its due: burning plastic is worse, because its smoke has poisons in it as well.. *Why:* Both sisters have a point. Plastic smoke is more poisonous, but smoke from leaves is still full of fine particles that harm lungs. Composting leaves avoids the smoke altogether.
### Follow the Specks (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Kavya counts 40 specks in one corner of her sticky card's square. The square has four equal corners, and the specks are spread evenly. About how many specks are in the whole square?  
   Options: About 160 / About 44 / About 10  
   **Answer:** About 160. *Why:* One corner is a quarter of the square. If one quarter holds 40, the whole holds about 4 times 40, which is 160. Scientists count part of a sample and multiply in the same way.
2. (order) Put the steps of the Sticky Card Test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Draw a square on each card', 'Spread a thin layer of jelly in each square', 'Put the cards in their places for three days', 'Count the specks in one corner of each square']. *Why:* First mark where to look, then make it sticky, then let the air reach it, then count.
3. (write) Your nose and your sticky card both catch dust. Write two ways they are alike, and one way they are different.  
   **Answer:** Alike: both are sticky, and both catch dust from the air passing over them. Different: the card keeps the dust, but the nose sweeps the dirty mucus out and makes fresh mucus.. *Why:* A strong answer compares how they catch dust and what happens to the dust afterwards.
4. (odd) Three of these put fine particles into the air in a home. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: An electric fan / A burning incense stick / A mosquito coil / Frying papads  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* The incense stick, the coil and the frying all give off smoke. A fan only moves the air that is already there.
5. (truefalse) The smallest particles in smoke are the most harmful to our lungs.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The nose catches bigger dust. The finest particles, about 30 times thinner than a hair, can slip past and reach the deepest parts of the lungs.
6. (label) Label the picture of the soil filter: soil, cotton plug, muddy salt water going in, water coming out.  
   **Answer:** Muddy salt water poured in at the top, soil in the upper half, cotton plug in the bottle neck, water dripping into the lower half.. *Why:* A strong answer shows the water going down through the soil, and labels what the soil stops (mud) and what it lets through (dissolved salt).
7. (mcq) In the soil test, the water came out clearer, but it left a white crust when it dried. What did the soil stop?  
   Options: The mud, but not the dissolved salt / The salt, but not the mud / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** The mud, but not the dissolved salt. *Why:* Soil catches pieces like mud in the gaps between its grains. Salt was dissolved, in pieces far too small to catch, so it went through with the water.
8. (write) A factory spills a chemical that dissolves in water onto the ground. A family nearby drinks water from a borewell. Use the soil test to explain why they should worry.  
   **Answer:** In the soil test, dissolved salt went straight through the soil with the water. A dissolved chemical could do the same and reach the groundwater, which the borewell pumps up.. *Why:* A strong answer connects the dissolved salt in the test to the dissolved chemical, and follows the water down to the borewell.
9. (sort) Sort each change: does it make less smoke, or does it only move the smoke somewhere else?  
   **Answer:** putting leaves in a compost pit → Makes less smoke; burning rubbish behind the building instead of in front → Only moves the smoke; switching off the engine at a signal → Makes less smoke; a taller factory chimney → Only moves the smoke; cooking on gas instead of wood → Makes less smoke. *Why:* A taller chimney or a fire round the back still makes the same smoke, so somebody else breathes it. Composting, switching off and cleaner fuel make less smoke in the first place.
10. (mcq) Sana in Pune notices that the far hill is blurry every winter morning, but clear after rain. Why is it clearer after rain?  
   Options: Rain washes particles out of the air / Rain makes the hill closer / Rain makes new air  
   **Answer:** Rain washes particles out of the air. *Why:* Raindrops catch dust and soot as they fall and bring them to the ground. That is one way nature cleans the air.
11. (truefalse) Opening the kitchen window or running the exhaust fan while frying helps the air in the kitchen.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The fan or the open window carries the smoke outside, so less stays in the kitchen for people to breathe.
12. (perspective) A family in Hyderabad argues about the mosquito coil in the bedroom.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both dangers are real: dengue is serious, and coil smoke is full of fine particles. It may choose the net because it solves both problems, and it gives each person credit for their point.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. The mother is right that mosquitoes are dangerous. Ishita is right about the smoke. Paati's net removes both problems, though a net must be tucked in well to work.

   **Answer:** Put one sticky card in the kitchen and one on the window sill for the same three days, with the same jelly and square. Count the specks in one corner of each and compare.. *Why:* A strong answer keeps everything the same except the place, says how long, and says how to compare the results.
14. (fill) Black particles from burning diesel, wood or rubbish are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['soot']. *Why:* Soot is what the black specks on the sticky card mostly are. It forms when something burns without enough air.
15. (mcq) On the cotton from Gurpreet's leaf wipe, the top of the leaf was grey and the underside was nearly white. What does this tell us about most of the dust?  
   Options: It falls down and settles on surfaces that face up / It is made by the leaf / It floats up from the ground  
   **Answer:** It falls down and settles on surfaces that face up. *Why:* Most dust is heavy enough to settle slowly, so it lands on tops. Only the finest specks float about and stick underneath.
### Who Breathes It? (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Your card on the window sill was dark grey after three days. A friend's card, on the 14th floor in a quiet lane, was light grey. Give two reasons the results could be different.  
   **Answer:** For example: her lane has fewer vehicles, so less soot. Road dust is kicked up near the ground, so less reaches the 14th floor. It may have rained on her side of the city.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two different, testable reasons, not just 'her home is cleaner'.
2. (mcq) A factory builds a taller chimney, and the air near the factory gets cleaner. What has happened to the smoke?  
   Options: The wind carries it further away, so other people breathe it / The tall chimney destroys the smoke / The smoke turns into clean air higher up  
   **Answer:** The wind carries it further away, so other people breathe it. *Why:* A taller chimney puts the smoke higher, where the wind spreads it over a wider area. The same amount of smoke is still made. It only lands somewhere else.
3. (perspective) A building in Chennai is planning its Bhogi morning.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer weighs the meaning of the tradition, the evidence from the sticky cards, and the smaller harm of a small, clean fire. It might choose a smokeless Bhogi or Farhan's middle way, and it respects Lakshmi's feeling for the tradition.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement. Traditions matter to people. Smoke harms people. A small fire with no tyres or plastic makes far less harm, and a fire-free Bhogi makes none.
4. (write) Some people say, 'The air belongs to nobody, so nobody has to keep it clean.' Others say, 'The air belongs to everybody, so everybody has to keep it clean.' What do you think? Use your nose and the sticky card in your answer.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer might say the air passes through every breathing body, so smoke made by one person ends up in other people's noses and lungs, which is why everyone has a share in keeping it clean.. *Why:* A strong answer takes a side, gives a reason from the unit, and fairly states the other view.
5. (mcq) Clair Patterson measured lead in layers of old Greenland ice. Snow from long ago had almost no lead, but recent snow had far more. What did this show?  
   Options: The lead in the air came from people, not from nature / Ice makes lead / Lead has always been in the air in the same amount  
   **Answer:** The lead in the air came from people, not from nature. *Why:* If the lead were natural, old snow would hold as much as new snow. It did not. The lead rose after people began burning leaded petrol.
6. (draw) Draw the path of one speck of soot from a bus exhaust to the inside of a child's nose. Show at least three places it passes.  
   **Answer:** For example: out of the exhaust pipe, up into the air over the road, blown towards a building, in through an open window, into the nose with a breath, caught in the mucus.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the soot floating and carried by the air, and ends with it caught by the mucus.
7. (truefalse) If a city puts all its rubbish in one big dump far away, the pollution is solved.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The rubbish is still there. Rain soaks through it into the groundwater, fires in the dump fill the air with smoke, and the people who live near the dump breathe it. Moving waste is not the same as making less of it.
8. (write) Dust on a tree's leaves is hard for the tree. It also means less dust reaches people walking below. Is that fair to the tree? Say what you think and why.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer starts with the tree's own problem: dust blocks the light the leaves need to make food. It might say the tree pays a cost for people, and suggest ways people can help, such as making less dust or watering roadside trees.. *Why:* A strong answer puts the tree's life first, then thinks about what people owe in return.
9. (order) Sewage from a town pours into a river. Put what happens next in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Sewage pours into the river', 'Microbes in the water feed on the waste', 'The microbes use up oxygen in the water', 'Fish in the river run short of oxygen']. *Why:* The waste is food for microbes. As the microbes multiply and feed, they use the oxygen dissolved in the water, and fish need that same oxygen.
10. (mcq) A shop says, 'Put two money plants in your bedroom and the air will be clean.' What do most recent studies say?  
   Options: A room would need far more plants than anyone keeps, so fresh air does much more / Two plants clean a room completely / Plants make the air dirtier  
   **Answer:** A room would need far more plants than anyone keeps, so fresh air does much more. *Why:* In small sealed boxes, plants did take some gases out of the air. In a real room, air flows in and out all the time, and a few plants make only a tiny difference. Plants are good to live with for other reasons.
11. (fill) Fine particles are about ____ times thinner than a hair.  
   **Answer:** ['30']. *Why:* A hair is about 70 micrometres thick. The largest fine particles, PM2.5, are 2.5 micrometres. 70 divided by 2.5 is 28, so about 30 times.
12. (match) Match each source to the main pollutant it adds.  
   **Answer:** A diesel lorry → soot in the air; A building site → dust in the air; A town's toilets with no treatment plant → sewage in the river; A rubbish dump in the rain → dirty water soaking into the ground. *Why:* Each source adds something different. A strong answer could add a second pollutant for some of them, such as plastic from the dump.
13. (write) Children breathe more air for their size than grown-ups. Explain why this means dirty air affects children more.  
   **Answer:** More air for their size means more dust and soot passes into a child's nose and lungs for the size of their body. So the same dirty air puts a bigger load on a child.. *Why:* A strong answer links more air breathed to more particles taken in, compared with body size.
14. (odd) Three of these help clean the air by themselves, without people doing anything. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A taller chimney / Rain / Wind / Leaves of roadside trees  
   **Answer:** 0. *Why:* Rain washes particles down, wind spreads smoke out, and leaves catch dust. A taller chimney does not clean anything. It sends the same smoke further away.
15. (mcq) Revathi puts a sticky card on top of her kitchen cupboard for two days. It catches oily, sticky specks. Where did they most likely come from?  
   Options: Frying and tadka on the stove / Dust from the road / The cupboard wood  
   **Answer:** Frying and tadka on the stove. *Why:* Hot oil gives off tiny droplets of oil and smoke. They float in the kitchen air and stick to high surfaces. That is why kitchen tops get sticky.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is pollution?  
   Options: Harmful things added to air, water or land faster than nature can clear them / Anything that smells bad / Only smoke from factories  
   **Answer:** Harmful things added to air, water or land faster than nature can clear them. *Why:* Pollution covers air, water and land, and it comes from many sources, not only factories. Picking 'only smoke from factories' misses vehicles, burning rubbish, cooking smoke and sewage.
2. (mcq) Which of these puts the most soot into a city's air?  
   Options: Diesel lorries and buses / Bicycles / Electric trains  
   **Answer:** Diesel lorries and buses. *Why:* Diesel engines burn fuel and give off soot. Bicycles burn nothing. Electric trains make no smoke on the street, though the power station that makes their electricity may.
3. (mcq) Arjun, a boy in Coimbatore, leaves a sticky card on his window sill for three days. Another card stays in a closed dabba. The dabba card stays white. What does that show?  
   Options: The specks on the sill card came from the air / The dabba made the card cleaner / The jelly turns grey by itself  
   **Answer:** The specks on the sill card came from the air. *Why:* Both cards had the same jelly. Only the sill card met the air, and only it got specks. So the air brought them.
4. (mcq) Why do scientists count the specks in only one corner of the square?  
   Options: It is quicker, and they can multiply by 4 for the whole square / The other corners are always empty / The corner is the dirtiest part  
   **Answer:** It is quicker, and they can multiply by 4 for the whole square. *Why:* When specks are spread fairly evenly, a quarter of the square holds about a quarter of them. Counting a part and multiplying saves time.
5. (mcq) What catches dust inside your nose?  
   Options: Tiny hairs and sticky mucus / Your teeth / Your tongue  
   **Answer:** Tiny hairs and sticky mucus. *Why:* Hairs catch the bigger dust, and sticky mucus catches smaller dust. The mucus is then swept out.
6. (mcq) Which particles in smoke are the most harmful to lungs?  
   Options: The finest ones, too small to see / The biggest ones, which you can see / They are all equally harmful  
   **Answer:** The finest ones, too small to see. *Why:* The nose catches big dust. The finest particles slip past it and reach deep into the lungs. Picking 'the biggest' is a natural guess, because we can see those.
7. (mcq) In the soil test, muddy salt water went through soil. What came out?  
   Options: Clearer water that still had the salt in it / Pure clean water / Only mud  
   **Answer:** Clearer water that still had the salt in it. *Why:* Soil stops pieces like mud, but dissolved salt goes through. That is why dissolved chemicals on the ground can reach groundwater.
8. (mcq) What is the biggest cause of dirty rivers in India?  
   Options: Sewage from towns and cities / Leaves falling into the water / Rain washing mud in  
   **Answer:** Sewage from towns and cities. *Why:* Sewage pours into many rivers without being cleaned. Microbes feeding on it use up the oxygen that fish need.
9. (mcq) Why is a coat of dust a problem for a plant?  
   Options: It blocks light the leaves need to make food / It makes the plant grow too fast / It makes the leaves fall in one day  
   **Answer:** It blocks light the leaves need to make food. *Why:* Leaves make food from sunlight. Dust blocks some light and can clog the tiny holes in the leaf.
10. (mcq) On a dusty balcony, which kind of leaf lets dust slide off most easily in the rain?  
   Options: A smooth, waxy leaf, like a money plant leaf / A hairy leaf, like a tulsi leaf / A leaf with no water on it  
   **Answer:** A smooth, waxy leaf, like a money plant leaf. *Why:* Water runs off a smooth, waxy leaf and carries dust with it. Hairs hold on to dust.
11. (mcq) Which change makes less smoke, instead of moving it somewhere else?  
   Options: Cooking on gas instead of wood / Burning rubbish behind the building / Building a taller chimney  
   **Answer:** Cooking on gas instead of wood. *Why:* Gas burns much more cleanly than wood. Burning round the back or through a taller chimney still makes the same smoke.
12. (mcq) Clair Patterson found almost no lead in very old Greenland ice, and much more in recent ice. What did this prove wrong?  
   Options: That the lead in the air was natural and had always been there / That snow falls in layers / That lead is a metal  
   **Answer:** That the lead in the air was natural and had always been there. *Why:* If the lead were natural, old ice would hold as much as new ice. The rise came after leaded petrol, so people had put it there.

## Applications
- Air quality monitors on city streets, which measure fine particles every hour and give the AQI.
- Masks for people who work on building sites, in traffic or in dusty factories.
- Sewage treatment plants, which clean used water before it goes into a river or the sea.
- Filters in air purifiers and in the air intake of buses and cars.
- Rules that stopped leaded petrol, and rules for cleaner diesel and fuel.
- Trees planted along busy roads to catch dust.

## Spotted around us
- A grey layer on the top of a ceiling fan blade, which you can see from a ladder.
- Black soot round the end of an old scooter's exhaust pipe.
- Roadside tree leaves that look grey before the monsoon and bright green after the first rain.
- Foam floating on some city lakes, such as Bellandur Lake in Bengaluru, from sewage with soap in it.
- A hazy sky on the morning after Diwali or Bhogi, when the far buildings look pale.
- A black smudge on the wall above where a lamp or a candle burns every evening.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how do fine particles harm the brain and heart?** Studies show more illness where there are more fine particles, and particles have been found in blood. How they cause the harm inside the body is still being worked out.
- **How much of a city's air pollution comes from each source?** Different studies of the same city give different answers, because sources change with the season, the weather and the year.
- **How do plants that live by busy roads cope with dust year after year?** Some roadside plants do well and others struggle. Scientists are still finding out which leaf features and other ways of living help them most.
- **Is there any amount of fine particles in the air that is completely safe?** Health studies have found harm even at low amounts. Scientists have not found a level below which there is no harm at all.

## Connected maths
- Kavya counts 35 specks in the top left corner of her window card's square. The square has 4 equal corners, and the specks look evenly spread. About how many specks are in the whole square? **About 140 specks.**. One corner is a quarter of the square. If one quarter holds 35, four quarters hold 4 times 35. 4 times 35 is 140. Check: 35 + 35 + 35 + 35 = 140. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In one corner of each square, Meenakshi counted: card A on the sill, 35 specks. Card B upright on the grill, 12 specks. Card C in the dabba, 1 speck. How many more specks did card A catch than card B? Why might card C have 1 speck, not 0? **23 more specks. Card C may have caught a speck while the card was being made, before it went in the dabba.**. 35 minus 12 is 23. Card C shows what gets on a card without the outside air, such as a speck from the room while the jelly was spread. That is why a control is useful: it tells us how much to ignore. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Sana checked her city's AQI each morning. Monday 85, Tuesday 92, Wednesday 160, Thursday 110, Friday 70. It rained on Thursday night. Which morning had the dirtiest air? How much lower was Friday than Wednesday? What might explain Friday? **Wednesday had the dirtiest air. Friday was 90 lower. The rain on Thursday night probably washed particles out of the air.**. The highest number, 160, is the dirtiest. 160 minus 70 is 90. Rain catches particles as it falls, so the morning after rain is often clearer. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Resting quietly, a child of 8 breathes about 20 times a minute. How many breaths is that in 10 minutes? If every breath passes over the sticky mucus in the nose, which would catch more dust in 10 minutes: a child sitting still or a child running? **About 200 breaths in 10 minutes. The running child, because they breathe faster and deeper, so more air and more dust pass through their nose and lungs.**. 20 breaths a minute for 10 minutes is 20 times 10, which is 200. Running makes you breathe faster and take in more air with each breath, so more dust comes in. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
