# Magnet poles: why two magnets attract or repel
`sci.magnets.poles.L3`

**Summary.** Hold two magnets end to end. One way round, they snap together. Turn one round, and they push each other apart. Every magnet has two ends where its force is strongest, called poles: a north pole and a south pole. A north pole and a south pole attract each other. Two north poles, or two south poles, repel each other. Iron that is not a magnet is attracted by both poles, so only a magnet can repel a magnet. Cut a magnet in two, and each piece has its own north and south pole. Nobody has ever found a north pole on its own. In this unit you float two magnets on water and find out what a free magnet does when it is pushed away.

**Intro.** *Try this. Hold one bar magnet in each hand. Bring their ends slowly together. Now turn one magnet round and try again. One way, they jump together. The other way, you feel a push, though nothing is touching. Why does the same magnet do two different things?* The answer is in the two ends of every magnet, its poles. In this unit you find which end is north and which is south, and you learn the rule for when magnets attract and when they repel. Then you float two magnets on water, push one away, and watch what it does next. A French scholar called Petrus Peregrinus did the same test with magnet stones in 1269.

## Terms
- **Pole**: One of the two places on a magnet where its force is strongest. On a bar magnet, the poles are at the two ends. *e.g.* Roll a bar magnet in paper clips. Most clips cling to the two ends, the poles, and very few to the middle.
- **North pole**: The end of a magnet that turns to point north when the magnet can swing freely. Its full name is the north-seeking pole. *e.g.* Float a bar magnet on a plastic lid in a tub of water. When it stops turning, the end pointing north is its north pole.
- **South pole**: The end of a magnet that turns to point south when the magnet can swing freely. *e.g.* The floating bar magnet's other end points south. That end is its south pole.
- **Attract**: To draw something closer. Two magnets attract when a north pole faces a south pole. *e.g.* Bring the north pole of one magnet near the south pole of another. They jump together and stick.
- **Repel**: To push something away without touching it. Two magnets repel when two north poles, or two south poles, face each other. *e.g.* Push two north poles towards each other. You feel a push, and the magnet on the table slides away.
- **Like poles**: Poles of the same kind: two north poles, or two south poles. Like poles repel. *e.g.* Two south poles brought together push apart.
- **Unlike poles**: Poles of different kinds: a north pole and a south pole. Unlike poles attract. *e.g.* A north pole and a south pole snap together.
- **Magnetic force**: The push or pull between magnets, or between a magnet and iron. It acts across a gap, and gets weaker quickly as the gap grows. *e.g.* A floating magnet starts to move when another magnet comes within a few centimetres.
- **Magnetic field**: The space around a magnet where its force acts. Scientists draw it as lines that come out of the north pole and go into the south pole. *e.g.* A compass needle near a bar magnet turns to follow the field lines.
- **Temporary magnet**: A piece of iron or steel that acts as a magnet only while a magnet is near it or touching it. *e.g.* A paper clip hanging from a magnet can hold a second clip. Take the magnet away, and the second clip falls.
- **Bar magnet**: A magnet shaped like a straight bar, with a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other. *e.g.* School magnet sets usually have two bar magnets with red or N on the north end.

## Explanations
### Every magnet has two poles · *defined*
**Definition:** Every magnet has at least one north pole and one south pole. The poles are where its force is strongest.

On a bar magnet the poles are at the two ends, so paper clips cling there and hardly at all in the middle. A magnet that can turn freely, hanging on a thread or floating on water, always settles with one end pointing north. That end is called the north pole, and the other end is the south pole. Many school magnets are painted red at the north end, or marked N.
- e.g. A bar magnet floating on a plastic lid turns until it points north and south.
- e.g. A round fridge magnet has its poles on its two flat faces, not on its edge.
### Unlike poles attract, like poles repel · *defined*
**Definition:** A north pole and a south pole attract each other. Two north poles repel each other, and so do two south poles.

This is the rule for every magnet, big or small. The force works across a gap, so the magnets do not need to touch. The closer the poles are, the stronger the force. Move them a little further apart, and the force drops quickly. That is why a floating magnet sits still until the other magnet comes close, and then suddenly moves.
- e.g. The magnetic catch on a cupboard door holds because unlike poles, or a magnet and steel, attract.
- e.g. A toy train with magnet links will not join if one carriage is turned round, because like poles face each other.
### A magnet that is free to turn ends up attracting · *defined*
**Definition:** When like poles face each other and one magnet is free to turn, it swings round until its other pole faces the first magnet. Then the two attract.

Repelling pushes a magnet away, but it also twists it. Unless something holds the magnet straight, it turns round, so a north pole ends up facing a south pole. Then the two magnets come together. This is what you see when two magnets float on water. Scientists have shown that ordinary magnets on their own can never hold another magnet floating still in the air. Something else must hold it in place, like a pencil through ring magnets or a spinning top.
- e.g. Two floating magnets pushed north to north turn round and stick together.
- e.g. Ring magnets on a pencil float one above another. Pull the pencil out, and the top ring flips over and sticks.
### Iron is attracted by both poles · *defined*
**Definition:** Iron and steel that are not magnets are attracted by the north pole and by the south pole. They are never repelled.

When a magnet comes near a steel paper clip, the clip becomes a temporary magnet. The end nearest the magnet becomes the opposite pole, so it is always attracted. That means attraction cannot tell you whether a thing is a magnet or plain iron. Only repelling can. If something pushes away one end of your magnet, it must be a magnet too.
- e.g. A steel spoon sticks to both ends of a bar magnet.
- e.g. Two wrapped objects look the same. The one that pushes away one end of your magnet is the magnet.
### Cut a magnet, and you get two magnets · *defined*
**Definition:** If you cut or break a magnet, each piece has its own north pole and south pole.

Inside a magnet, countless tiny magnets point the same way, each with its own north and south. The north pole of a magnet is just where the north ends of these tiny magnets come out at the surface. Cut the bar anywhere, and new poles appear at the cut, one north and one south. Even an electron, the tiniest magnet known, has a north and a south. Nobody has ever found a north pole on its own.
- e.g. Cut a bendy fridge magnet strip in half. Both halves still stick to the fridge.
- e.g. Petrus Peregrinus broke a lodestone in 1269 and found that both pieces had a north and a south pole.
### The Earth's far north is a magnetic south pole · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth is a giant magnet. Its magnetic pole near the North Pole is a south pole, because it attracts the north poles of compass needles.

A magnet's north pole is named for the direction it points. Unlike poles attract, so the place it points to must be a south pole. Maps call that place the North Magnetic Pole, which can be confusing. It sits in the Arctic, not exactly at the North Pole, and it moves a little every year.
- e.g. A compass needle's north pole points north, towards the Earth's magnetic south pole.
- e.g. Over hundreds of thousands of years, the Earth's magnetic poles have swapped places many times.
### Bendy fridge magnets have many poles · *defined*
**Definition:** A bendy fridge magnet has thin stripes of north and south poles side by side on its magnetic face.

The stripes are a few millimetres wide. Press two bendy magnets face to face and slide one across the other. In one direction it slides smoothly. In the other, it bumps, because it crosses stripes that attract and repel in turn. Having many poles close together makes the magnet hold strongly to the fridge from very close, while its force dies away very close to its face. Its back face hardly attracts at all.
- e.g. Slide two fridge magnets face to face. Feel the bumps.
- e.g. The back of a bendy fridge magnet does not stick to the fridge.
### Could a north pole exist on its own? · *idea*
Some physicists' theories say a single north pole, without a south pole, could exist. They call it a magnetic monopole. On 14 February 1982, a detector run by Blas Cabrera at Stanford University recorded one signal that looked like a monopole passing through. It never happened again. Many larger searches since have found none. So nobody knows yet whether monopoles exist.
- e.g. Every magnet ever tested has had both a north and a south pole.
- e.g. Scientists still search for monopoles in rocks, in space and in giant machines.

## Mostly agreed
- Every magnet has a north pole and a south pole. Unlike poles attract, and like poles repel. *How we know:* Anyone can check it with two magnets. Petrus Peregrinus wrote it down from his own tests in 1269.
- Cutting a magnet gives two magnets, each with both poles. *How we know:* It has been tested on magnets of every size, down to single atoms and electrons, which are themselves tiny magnets with two poles.
- Plain iron and steel are attracted by both poles, never repelled. *How we know:* Test a steel spoon on each end of a magnet. A magnet turns the nearby iron into a temporary magnet with the opposite pole facing it.
- The Earth is a magnet, and its magnetic pole in the Arctic is a magnetic south pole. *How we know:* The north poles of compass needles all over the world point towards it, and unlike poles attract.
- Ordinary magnets on their own cannot hold another magnet floating still in the air. *How we know:* Samuel Earnshaw proved this in 1842. Every magnet that seems to float is held by something else, such as a pencil, a spin or an electric current.

## How we found out
**Petrus Peregrinus, also called Pierre de Maricourt, a French scholar of the 1200s., His letter on the magnet is dated 8 August 1269..** In August 1269, a French scholar called Petrus Peregrinus was in an army camp outside the town of Lucera, in southern Italy. While the army waited, he wrote a long letter about magnets to a friend. At that time, some people said a compass needle points north because there are great iron mines in the far north. Peregrinus said this could not be right. Magnet stones are found in many lands, yet needles everywhere point the same way. He also tested magnets himself. He put a lodestone, a natural magnet stone, in a wooden dish, and floated the dish in a big vessel of water. The stone turned until one end pointed north. He called that end its north pole. Then he brought a second lodestone close. A north pole drove the floating north pole away, but drew a south pole closer. In the end, he wrote, the north pole joins the south pole. He also broke a lodestone, and found that each piece had both poles. Peregrinus had a wrong idea too. He drew a wheel with iron teeth and a lodestone, and said it would turn forever, because the south pole would push the iron teeth away. But a magnet attracts iron with both poles. The wheel cannot work, and nobody has made one turn.  
*What people believed before:* People said compasses point north because of iron mines in the far north. Peregrinus showed this could not be right. He himself thought a magnet wheel could turn forever, because a south pole would repel iron. But iron is attracted by both poles, so such a wheel stops.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If a thing sticks to a magnet, it must be a magnet.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Two magnets do stick together.  
  Against: A steel spoon sticks to a magnet too, and it is not a magnet. Only repelling proves that something is a magnet.
- **You can cut a magnet so that one piece is all north and the other all south.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: The north pole is at one end and the south pole at the other, so cutting in the middle seems to split them.  
  Against: Every piece has both poles. New poles appear at the cut.
- **The north pole of a magnet is the stronger end.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Many sets paint the north end red, which looks more important.  
  Against: Both poles of a bar magnet are equally strong. Test it: count the paper clips each end picks up.
- **The compass points to the Pole Star, or to iron mines in the far north.** (Many people in the Middle Ages; *rejected*)  
  For: The needle points north, where the Pole Star shines.  
  Against: In 1269 Petrus Peregrinus argued against the iron mines, because magnet stones are found in many lands. Later, William Gilbert showed that the Earth itself is a magnet.
- **A wheel of magnets can turn forever and power a machine.** (Many inventors, including Petrus Peregrinus in 1269; *rejected*)  
  For: Magnets attract and repel without being pushed, so it seems they could keep a wheel turning.  
  Against: Every such wheel stops. A magnet's force pulls a wheel forward in one place and holds it back in another, so it gives no free energy. Peregrinus also thought the iron teeth would be repelled, but iron is attracted by both poles.
- **Single magnetic poles, monopoles, exist somewhere in the universe.** (Some physicists, from Paul Dirac in 1931 onwards; *debated*)  
  For: Some theories of physics predict them. One possible signal was seen in 1982.  
  Against: No search since has found one, and that signal was never repeated.

## Roots
We have learned that every magnet has a north and a south pole, and that a pushed-away magnet turns round and attracts. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** When two north poles faced each other on the water, the magnets pushed apart. Then one turned round, and they snapped together, north to south. Magnets that are free to turn always end up attracting. Repelling lasts only while something holds them facing the wrong way.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of attract and repel as two equal and opposite things a magnet does. But they are one force, and which one you get depends only on which way the magnets face. Left free, magnets turn to attract.  
*Evidence:* In the Magnet Boats test, boats that started north facing north pushed apart, turned and stuck together. Ring magnets float on a pencil only while the pencil stops them turning. Samuel Earnshaw proved in 1842 that ordinary magnets on their own cannot hold a magnet floating still.

- *who am i:* You felt the push between two magnets with your own hands. You can sense a force you cannot see.
- *what is life:* Some bacteria live in mud and need to find the parts low in oxygen. They carry a chain of tiny magnet crystals, joined north to south. The chain turns them along the Earth's magnetic field, which slopes down into the ground, so swimming along it takes them deeper.
- *what is this world:* The Earth is a giant magnet with two poles. Every compass needle on Earth turns to line up with them.

A magnet's north and south poles are never apart. Cut a magnet, and each piece has both again. Inside, countless tiny magnets point the same way and add up to one force. That one force shows up in many ways: a push, a pull, a turn, a needle pointing north. Underneath the many things magnets do is one force, and it settles into attraction when nothing holds it back.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 2:** Long and short shape each other. High and low lean on each other. Front and back follow each other. (A north pole never exists without a south pole. Cut a magnet, and both appear again at the cut. Each pole needs the other.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment B51 (Diels-Kranz numbering), about 500 BCE:** People do not see how something pulling apart also holds together, like the bow and the lyre, held in shape by strings pulling against them. (Two repelling magnets are a balance of push and twist that holds only while something keeps them straight. Let go, and the balance turns into attraction.)
- Science (settled): Every magnet ever tested has both a north and a south pole.
- Science (settled): Ordinary magnets on their own cannot hold another magnet floating still. Samuel Earnshaw proved this in 1842.
- Science (open): Whether a single north or south pole, a monopole, can exist is not known.
- Other view, Many physicists: A free magnet turns to attract because that is the arrangement with the least stored energy, the same reason a ball rolls downhill. It is a useful rule, not a lesson about the world, and reading meaning into it says more about us than about magnets.
- Practice, Feel the turn (3 min): Hold one magnet in each hand, north facing north. Bring them close until you feel the push. Hold them there and breathe in slowly three times. Notice how your fingers must work to keep them straight. Now loosen your grip a little. Feel one magnet start to twist. Let it turn, and let the two come together. Breathe out.
- Ask: When the magnets repelled, your fingers had to work to keep them straight. When they attracted, your fingers could rest. Which one is the magnets' own way?
- Ask: A north pole is never found without a south pole. Can anything exist on its own, with nothing to go with it?
- Ask: If attracting and repelling are one force, why do we give them two names?

## Story: The Carriage That Would Not Join
*Channapatna, a town between Bengaluru and Mysuru in Karnataka, really is famous for its bright lacquered wooden toys. Lakshmi, her grandparents and the toy train are made up.*

In Channapatna, the town of wooden toys, Lakshmi's grandparents had a small workshop at the back of their house. The air smelled of fresh wood and warm lac, the coloured wax that makes the toys shine. Paati turned the wood on the lathe. Thatha painted it. Lakshmi was nine, and in the summer holidays she was allowed to help.

That summer, a shop in Bengaluru had ordered fifty toy trains. Each train had an engine and three carriages, painted red, yellow and green. Paati had a clever plan for joining them. At each end of every carriage, she set a small round magnet into the wood, flat side out. Then the carriages would click together when they touched, with no hooks to break.

On Monday, Thatha fitted the magnets. On Tuesday, he lined up the first train on the workbench. The engine clicked onto the red carriage. The red carriage clicked onto the yellow one. But when he pushed the green carriage towards the yellow one, it would not join. It slid away across the bench, as if the yellow carriage had pushed it.

Thatha frowned. He pushed it again. It slid away again. 'This magnet is broken,' he said. 'I will glue a hook on instead.'

Lakshmi watched the green carriage. When Thatha pushed it, it did not just slide. It also twisted a little, sideways, as if it wanted to turn round. 'Wait, Thatha,' she said. 'Don't glue it yet.'

She picked up the green carriage, turned it round, and brought its other end to the yellow carriage. Click. The two joined at once.

Thatha stared. 'So the magnet works after all. But why did it push away the first time?'

Lakshmi had learned this from Paati, who had read it in an old science book. 'Every magnet has two poles, a north pole and a south pole,' she said. 'A north pole and a south pole attract. Two north poles repel. So do two south poles. When you put the magnets in, Thatha, some went in with north facing out, and some with south facing out. The green carriage had its north facing the yellow carriage's north.'

'Then I must take every magnet out and test it,' said Thatha, looking at the forty-nine trains still waiting.

Lakshmi shook her head. 'No. We only need one rule. Every carriage must have north facing out at the front, and south facing out at the back. Then every front joins every back, whichever carriage you pick.'

'But how do I know which side of a round magnet is north?' asked Thatha. 'They all look the same.'

Lakshmi thought about it. Then she fetched a plastic tiffin lid and the big plastic tub that Paati used for soaking cloth. She filled the tub with water and floated the lid in the middle. On the lid she stood one of the round magnets on its edge, held up by a little lump of clay, so that its flat faces pointed sideways.

Slowly, the lid began to turn. After a minute it stopped. One flat face pointed towards the front door, and the other towards the back wall. Lakshmi checked Paati's old compass. 'This face points north,' she said, and put a dot of red paint on it. 'That is the north pole.'

'Now we have one magnet we know,' she said. 'For all the others, we do not need the tub. Hold each new magnet near the red dot. If it is pushed away, the face towards the dot is north too. If it is pulled in, it is south.'

Thatha tried it with a new magnet. It jumped and stuck to the red dot. 'South,' he said, and marked it blue. The next one pushed away from the red dot and tried to flip over in his fingers. 'North,' he said, and marked it red. By the evening, every magnet had a dot.

That night, Thatha found one magnet with a crack across it. It snapped into two half moons in his fingers. 'Now I have a north half and a south half,' he said.

Lakshmi held each half near the red dot. One face of each half was pushed away, and the other face was pulled in. 'No, Thatha,' she laughed. 'Each half has its own north and its own south. You cannot get a north pole on its own.'

On Saturday, the fifty trains went to Bengaluru. Every carriage could join every other carriage, front to back, in any order. Paati gave the last train to Lakshmi, with a red dot painted under the engine, for the girl who had noticed the twist.

**For the parent:** For the parent: every magnet has a north pole and a south pole. Unlike poles attract and like poles repel. The green carriage was facing north to north, so it was repelled. It also twisted, because a magnet that is pushed away tends to turn round until it faces the other way and is attracted. Lakshmi found north by letting one magnet float and turn freely, as Petrus Peregrinus did in 1269. After that, she used repelling to sort the rest, because only a magnet can repel a magnet. The broken magnet showed that each piece of a magnet has both poles.

- Ask: Why did the green carriage slide away from the yellow one?
- Ask: What small thing did Lakshmi notice that Thatha missed?
- Ask: How did Lakshmi find out which face of the first magnet was north?
- Ask: Why did pushing away tell Thatha more than sticking did?
- Ask: When the magnet broke, Thatha expected a north half and a south half. What did Lakshmi find instead?

- **rules:** Each player tapes an N badge on their front and an S badge on their back.; Players walk slowly around the room. ### e1 · Magnet Boats: what does a pushed-away magnet do? (35 min)
- **question:** When two floating magnets repel each other, do they stay apart?
- **predict:** Guess first. Float two magnets with their north poles facing each other, and let go. Will they stay apart, drift away, or end up stuck together? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same two magnets, the same lids and the same tub every time. Start each try with the magnets 10 cm apart, and let go gently without pushing. Only change which poles face each other. Do each pairing 3 times, because one try can be a fluke. Switch off the fan, because moving air pushes the lids.
- **safety:** Wipe up spilled water so nobody slips. Keep magnets away from phones, cards and small children's mouths.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each try. Then draw what the two boats did after you let go, in three pictures.
- **results table:** Poles facing | Try | Gap when the boat first moved (cm) | What happened after you let go
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off the fan. 2. Fill the tub with water nearly to the top. 3. Lay one magnet in the middle of a lid. 4. Float the lid in the middle of the tub. 5. Wait about a minute until it stops turning.

   **Answer:** It is the same magnetic force. Which one you get depends only on which way the magnets face. The boats repelled, turned round, then attracted, with no change except the direction they faced.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the evidence that turning a magnet changes repel into attract.
9. (write) Design a test to find out whether a magnet's north pole and south pole are equally strong. Say what you would keep the same.  
   **Answer:** Count how many paper clips each pole can hold in a chain, using the same clips and the same magnet, 3 times each. Keep the clips, the magnet and the way you hang them the same.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only which pole is tested, and repeats it.
10. (write) A bendy fridge magnet has many poles in thin stripes. Why might that help it hold to the fridge but not attract things far away?  
   **Answer:** Neighbouring north and south stripes are close together, so their forces are strong right at the face. A little further away, the forces from the north and south stripes cancel out.. *Why:* A strong answer says the stripes act strongly close up and cancel out further away.
11. (write) A shop sells a 'north pole magnet' that it says has no south pole. What would you test to check the claim?  
   **Answer:** Hold it near the north pole of a marked magnet, then turn it round. If one side is repelled and the other attracted, it has both poles. Also float it to see if it points north and south.. *Why:* A strong answer uses repelling and attracting to find both poles.
12. (write) Why did the Repel Racer car often swing round and jump onto your hand magnet?  
   **Answer:** Repelling pushed the car but also twisted it. When it turned a little, its other pole faced my magnet and it was attracted.. *Why:* A strong answer links the car to the free magnet in the boats experiment.
13. (draw) Draw three pictures of two floating magnet boats that start north facing north: at the start, halfway and at the end. Label the poles.  
   **Answer:** Start: N facing N with a gap. Halfway: one boat turning. End: N of one touching S of the other, or side by side pointing opposite ways.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the turning step and labels every pole.
14. (label) Label the Earth drawing: the geographic North Pole, the magnetic pole in the Arctic, and what kind of magnetic pole it is (N or S).  
   **Answer:** The geographic North Pole at the top of the Earth's axis. The magnetic pole in the Arctic, a little away from it, marked S, because it attracts compass north poles.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the place on the map from the kind of magnetic pole.
15. (perspective) Three people talk about the magnet boats that always ended up stuck together.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses evidence from the boats, such as how often they turned. Meera is right that a perfectly straight push would not twist the magnet at first. Farhan is right that any tiny wobble grows, so in practice they turn. Kavya's 'prefer' is a way of saying what free magnets end up doing.. *Why:* This is a real question about balance. A balance that any wobble upsets is called unstable, and repelling magnets are in that kind of balance.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What are the two ends of a bar magnet called?  
   Options: Poles / Sides / Corners  
   **Answer:** Poles. *Why:* The ends, where the force is strongest, are called poles: a north pole and a south pole.
2. (mcq) Usha holds a north pole near another north pole. What happens?  
   Options: They repel / They attract / Nothing  
   **Answer:** They repel. *Why:* Like poles repel. Picking 'attract' mixes up the rule.
3. (mcq) Sundari holds a south pole near a north pole. What happens?  
   Options: They attract / They repel / Nothing  
   **Answer:** They attract. *Why:* Unlike poles attract.
4. (mcq) Gita's magnet is floating on a lid in water. When it stops, the end pointing north is called what?  
   Options: The north pole / The south pole / The middle  
   **Answer:** The north pole. *Why:* The north pole is the end that points north when the magnet is free to turn.
5. (mcq) Which one can push away the north pole of a magnet?  
   Options: An iron nail / The north pole of another magnet / A steel spoon  
   **Answer:** The north pole of another magnet. *Why:* Only a magnet can repel a magnet. A nail and a spoon are plain steel, which is attracted by both poles.
6. (mcq) Anu has two wrapped parcels. One pushes her magnet away. What is inside it?  
   Options: A magnet / Plain iron / Paper  
   **Answer:** A magnet. *Why:* Repelling is the sure test of a magnet.
7. (mcq) Two magnet boats start north facing north, and Bhavana lets go. What do they usually do in the end?  
   Options: Turn and stick together / Stay apart / Sink  
   **Answer:** Turn and stick together. *Why:* They push apart, but the push twists them. One turns until unlike poles face, then they attract.
8. (mcq) In the Magnet Boats test, why do you let go gently, without pushing?  
   Options: So only the magnets move the boats / So the water stays warm / So the boats go faster  
   **Answer:** So only the magnets move the boats. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. A push from your hand would mix up the result.
9. (mcq) Selvi cuts a magnet in two. What does each piece have?  
   Options: A north and a south pole / Only one pole / No poles  
   **Answer:** A north and a south pole. *Why:* Each piece is a whole magnet with both poles.
10. (mcq) The Earth's magnetic pole in the Arctic attracts the north pole of a compass. What kind of pole is it?  
   Options: A south pole / A north pole / Not a pole  
   **Answer:** A south pole. *Why:* Unlike poles attract, so it must be a magnetic south pole, even though it is in the north.
11. (mcq) Why does a bendy fridge magnet bump when you slide it across another one?  
   Options: It has stripes of north and south poles / It is wet / It is broken  
   **Answer:** It has stripes of north and south poles. *Why:* The face has thin stripes of poles. Crossing them you feel attract, repel, attract.
12. (mcq) Who wrote the rule that like poles repel and unlike poles attract, in 1269?  
   Options: Petrus Peregrinus / William Gilbert / Michael Faraday  
   **Answer:** Petrus Peregrinus. *Why:* Petrus Peregrinus wrote it in a letter from an army camp in Italy in 1269.

## Applications
- Toy trains and building toys join with magnets that only fit one way round.
- Cupboard and fridge doors stay shut with magnetic strips.
- A compass needle is a small magnet whose north pole points north.
- Some trains, like the airport train in Shanghai, float above their track on magnets.
- Electric motors in fans, mixers and washing machines turn because magnets attract and repel in turn.
- Loudspeakers and earphones move a thin cone with a magnet that is pushed and pulled.
- Magnetic clasps on bags, phone covers and necklaces.
- Magnetic name badges that hold through cloth.

## Spotted around us
- The black rubber strip around a fridge door. It is a bendy magnet that holds the door shut.
- A magnetic clasp on a handbag or a phone cover. Try turning one half round: does it still close?
- The ceiling fan's motor, which turns because magnets inside it attract and repel.
- Wooden toy trains from Channapatna or shops, with magnets at each end of the carriages.
- A fridge magnet from a sweet shop or a pizza delivery, with poles in stripes on its back.

## Open questions
- **Do magnetic monopoles, single north or south poles, exist anywhere?** Some theories predict them. One possible signal was seen in 1982 and never again. Searches go on.
- **Why do the Earth's magnetic poles sometimes swap places?** Scientists know it has happened many times from the rocks, and that the moving liquid iron in the core causes it, but cannot yet predict when it will happen next.
- **What is an electron's magnetism, underneath?** Every electron is a tiny magnet with two poles. Physicists can measure it very exactly, but nobody can say what it is made of, or whether that question even makes sense.

## Connected maths
- In the Magnet Boats test, Kavya found the boat moved at a gap of 6 cm, 5 cm and 7 cm in three tries, with north facing south. What is the middle value, and why use it? **6 cm.**. Put the three numbers in order: 5, 6, 7. The middle one is 6 cm. Using the middle value stops one odd try from changing the answer too much. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- A bar magnet is cut into 2 pieces, then each piece is cut in 2 again, and then each of those in 2 again. How many north poles are there now? **8 north poles.**. After the first cut there are 2 magnets. After the second, 2 times 2 is 4. After the third, 4 times 2 is 8. Each piece has one north pole, so there are 8 north poles and 8 south poles. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Divya slid two fridge magnets 5 cm across each other and felt 15 bumps. About how wide is one stripe of poles, in millimetres? (5 cm is 50 mm.) **About 3 mm for each bump.**. 50 mm shared among 15 bumps is a little over 3 mm each, because 15 times 3 is 45, close to 50. So the stripes of poles are about 3 mm apart. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the Magnet Boats test, Farhan let the boats go north facing north 10 times. 9 times they turned and stuck together. Once they drifted apart and stopped. Draw a bar chart and say what it shows. **A bar of 9 for 'turned and stuck' and a bar of 1 for 'stayed apart'. Almost every time, a free magnet turned round and attracted.**. The tall bar shows what usually happens. The one short bar is worth looking at: the boats may have drifted out of reach before they could turn. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
