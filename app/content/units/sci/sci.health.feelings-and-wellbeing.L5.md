# A happy place: how our words and actions change how other people feel and act
`sci.health.feelings-and-wellbeing.L5`

**Summary.** Drop 15 chana into a bottle and you may miss twice. Do it while someone counts down and says 'faster', and many people miss more. Why? In this lesson you will test how hurrying words change where your chana land, and practise kind and safe ways to step in.

**Intro.** *When someone counts down and says 'faster', a hand can miss the bottle more often. Why would another person's words change what your hand can do?* Most people think a skill belongs to one person. She is careful, or he is clumsy. But the same child reads aloud well at home and stumbles when classmates laugh. The same lane stays clean for months and then fills with litter in a week. In this lesson you find out how words, faces and actions pass from one person to the next. You run the Hurry Test on your own hands. You measure your doors as a wheelchair user would. You learn what to do when you see teasing or pushing. Then you can say what makes a school, a home or a street a happy place, and what your part in it is.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what makes a place happy: people are safe there, belong there, and care for the place.
1. I can explain how feelings and actions pass from one person to another.
2. I can run a fair test of how hurrying words change a person's hands, and say what the result shows.
3. I can explain what respect is, and why every honest job deserves it.
4. I can describe a day from another person's point of view, and find the barriers in a place.
5. I can choose a kind and safe action when I see teasing, pushing or damage, and say when to tell a trusted grown-up.
6. I can explain why a cared-for place stays cared for, and why one mess leads to more.
7. I can name things that calm a big feeling, and I know to tell a trusted grown-up when a feeling is too big.

## Terms
- **Well-being**: Being well in body, in feelings and with other people, all together. *e.g.* A child who sleeps enough, has friends to talk to and is safe at school has good well-being.
- **Emotion**: A feeling, such as joy, fear or anger, that happens in the body and the mind together. *e.g.* Before a race your heart beats fast and your tummy feels tight. That emotion is excitement or fear.
- **Emotional contagion**: The passing of a feeling from one person to another, mostly without either one choosing it. Contagion means spreading. *e.g.* One child starts to giggle in class, and soon the whole row is giggling.
- **Stress response**: The body getting ready for action when something seems urgent or unsafe. The heart speeds up and the muscles tighten. *e.g.* When someone shouts 'faster!', your heart beats quicker and your fingers tighten.
- **Adrenaline**: A substance that two small glands above the kidneys release into the blood. It makes the heart beat faster within seconds. *e.g.* A sudden loud bang sends adrenaline into your blood, and your heart thumps.
- **Social norm**: What most people in a place do, or what they think they should do there. *e.g.* At a temple, mosque or gurdwara door, everyone takes off their shoes. A visitor sees this and does the same.
- **Positive behaviour**: Actions that make things better for the people and the place around you. *e.g.* Waiting for your turn, putting a book back and helping a younger child are positive behaviour.
- **Respect**: Treating a person as someone who matters as much as you do. *e.g.* You listen until a classmate finishes speaking, though you disagree.
- **Empathy**: Feeling some of what another person feels, and caring about it. *e.g.* Your friend drops her tiffin, and you feel a pang as if the tiffin were yours.
- **Point of view**: What a person sees, knows, needs and feels from where they are. *e.g.* From a wheelchair, three steps at a doorway are a wall.
- **Barrier**: Anything in a place that stops some people from using it. *e.g.* A step with no ramp, a switch too high to reach and a notice in only one language are barriers.
- **Accessible**: Built so that everyone can get in and use it, whatever their body can do. *e.g.* A toilet with a wide door, a ramp and a handrail is accessible.
- **Inclusion**: Changing a place, and the way we act, so that every person can take part. *e.g.* The class learns five signs so that a deaf classmate can join the game.
- **Dignity of labour**: The idea that every honest job deserves equal respect. *e.g.* The person who cleans the school toilets is greeted by name, like the teachers.
- **Bullying**: Hurting, frightening or leaving out the same person again and again, when that person finds it hard to make it stop. *e.g.* Three children hide one boy's bag every day and laugh when he looks for it.
- **Bystander**: A person who sees something happen and is not the one doing it or the one it is done to. *e.g.* The children standing around when someone is teased are bystanders.
- **Trusted grown-up**: An adult you know well, who listens to you and keeps you safe. *e.g.* A parent, a grandparent, an aunt, a teacher or a school counsellor can be a trusted grown-up.
- **Shared place**: A place that many people use and that no single person owns. *e.g.* The stairs of a building, a classroom, a playground and a bus stop are shared places.
- **Charter**: A short list of rules that the people of a place agree on together and sign. *e.g.* A family writes five rules for their study corner, and everyone signs the page.

## Explanations
### What makes a place happy · *defined*
**Definition:** A happy place is one where people are safe, belong, and can do what they came to do. The people in it, and the way they treat each other, matter most.

Well-being means that a person's body, feelings and friendships are all doing well. A place can add to well-being or take away from it. Four things decide which. The first is safety: nobody is hurt, pushed or frightened there. The second is belonging: everyone is greeted, gets a turn and can take part. The third is care for the place: the room is clean, the water runs, and broken things get fixed. The fourth is something worth doing there, such as learning, playing or working. In Three Good Places, you sort your own reasons into people and things. Most children find that people come first. A new building full of unkind people is an unhappy place. A plain room with kind people is often a happy one. So the people who use a place make most of what it is like, and they make it again every day.
- e.g. A school where older children help the youngest find their classroom on the first day.
- e.g. A building where neighbours greet each other on the stairs and keep the landing clear.
- e.g. A playground where the swings are mended in the same week that they break.
- e.g. A classroom where a wrong answer is met with 'good try', and nobody laughs.
### Feelings pass from person to person · *defined*
**Definition:** Emotional contagion is the passing of a feeling from one person to another, mostly without either one choosing it.

When you watch a face, the small muscles of your own face begin to copy it. In 2000 the scientist Ulf Dimberg and his team measured this with sensors on people's cheeks and brows. The muscles moved within half a second. They moved even when the face was shown too briefly to notice. Your voice and the way you stand copy the people near you too. Your brain reads your own face and body, and takes the copied look for a feeling of your own. So one cheerful person can lift a whole room, and one cross voice can spoil it. The psychologist Elaine Hatfield and her colleagues named this emotional contagion in the early 1990s. In the Smile Count, you find out how often a smile is returned, and how often a plain face is. Each face in a room is partly made by the other faces in it.
- e.g. A baby in a clinic starts to cry, and the other babies start crying too.
- e.g. A cricket crowd groans together when a catch is dropped.
- e.g. In the Smile Count, you count how many smiles come back after a smile, and after a plain face.
- e.g. A teacher who comes in calm and smiling gets a calmer class.
### People copy what they see others do · *defined*
**Definition:** A social norm is what most people in a place do. People follow what they see being done more than what they are told to do.

A person in a new place looks around to find out what is done there. In 2008 Kees Keizer, Siegwart Lindenberg and Linda Steg tested this in an alley in Groningen, in the Netherlands, where people parked bicycles. The scientists hung a paper advert on every handlebar. There was no bin. When the walls were clean, 33 of every 100 riders dropped the paper on the ground. When the walls were covered in scribbles, 69 of every 100 dropped it. Nobody had told the riders anything. The walls had told them that people here break rules. Words can even work backwards. A park in the United States put up a sign saying that many visitors stole pieces of its ancient stone wood. Theft went up. So a wrapper on the ground is a message: people drop wrappers here. A line at a tap is a message too: people wait here. Younger children read these messages from older children. Everything you do in a shared place tells the next person what is done there.
- e.g. Passengers stand in a line for the bus in some cities and crowd the door in others.
- e.g. A small child who sees big children push at the water tap begins to push.
- e.g. One pair of shoes left on the stairs is soon joined by three more.
- e.g. When three people start to clap, a whole hall claps.
### How one person's words change another person's body · *defined*
**Definition:** The stress response is the body getting ready for action when something seems urgent or unsafe. Another person's words can start it.

When someone shouts 'faster!' or laughs at you, your brain treats the moment as urgent. Within a second or two, nerves signal the adrenal glands, which sit on top of the kidneys. They release adrenaline into the blood. The heart beats faster, breathing quickens, and the muscles tighten, ready to run or push. This helps in a race. It harms careful work. Tight muscles shake a little, and a hurried brain spends less time on aiming. In the Hurry Test, many people miss the bottle more often in the hurried runs, and say their body felt tighter. Teasing does the same to a child who is reading aloud, catching a ball or answering in class. Calm and friendly words do the opposite. The body stays loose, and the person can use all the skill they have. So the people around you are part of how well you do, and you are part of how well they do.
- e.g. A child who knows the answer forgets it when the class turns to stare.
- e.g. A bowler bowls a wide after the fielders jeer at her run-up.
- e.g. In the made-up results of the Hurry Test, the hurried runs had three times as many misses.
- e.g. A learner threads a needle more easily when the teacher says 'take your time'.
### Respect, and the dignity of labour · *defined*
**Definition:** Respect means treating a person as someone who matters as much as you do. Dignity of labour means that every honest job deserves equal respect.

Respect shows in small acts. You greet people, listen without cutting in, wait for your turn and ask before you touch someone's things. You use a person's name. Respect is owed to everyone, whatever their age, language, body, religion, caste, money or work. Yet some work is treated as low, although no place can run without it. A school with no sanitation workers would be unfit to use within days, and illness would spread. The people who do this work are often not greeted and not named. In India this work was forced on particular castes for centuries, and that injustice has not ended. Bezwada Wilson, who grew up in Karnataka, has campaigned for decades to end the cleaning of dry toilets by hand. He won the Ramon Magsaysay Award in 2016. Respect for work is also practical. Learn the worker's name. Make their work easier and safer: wrap broken glass, and keep wet and dry waste apart.
- e.g. Children greet the school's sanitation worker by name each morning.
- e.g. A family wraps a broken tumbler in newspaper and writes GLASS on it for the waste collector.
- e.g. A class thanks the cook after the noon meal.
- e.g. A child offers water to the worker who repairs the lift.
### Seeing a place from another person's point of view · *defined*
**Definition:** Taking someone's point of view means working out what they see, know and need from where they are. Inclusion means changing a place so that everyone can take part.

Your own point of view comes without effort. Another person's takes work, and people often skip that work. In The E on Your Forehead, many people draw the letter so that only they can read it. Places get built the same way. People who walk easily build steps, narrow doors and high switches. For a person who uses a wheelchair, each of these is a barrier. A common wheelchair is about 65 centimetres wide, and a door should be at least 90 centimetres wide for it to pass easily. One step of 15 centimetres stops a wheelchair. A safe ramp needs 12 centimetres of length for every centimetre of height, so that step needs a ramp 180 centimetres long. Language can be a barrier too. In Lost for Words, an instruction in an unknown language does not get clearer when it is said louder. Pointing and showing work. A change made for one person usually helps many. A ramp also helps a grandparent, a parent with a pram and a worker with a trolley.
- e.g. A ramp beside the steps of a railway station, used by wheelchairs, trolleys and suitcases.
- e.g. A lift button low enough for a seated person and a small child.
- e.g. A teacher who shows each step with her hands for a child new to the language.
- e.g. Raised dots in Braille beside the numbers in a lift.
### Teasing, bullying and the children who watch · *defined*
**Definition:** Bullying is hurting, frightening or leaving out the same person again and again, when that person finds it hard to make it stop. Friendly teasing is fun for both sides and stops when one side says stop.

Bullying is rarely a matter between two children. Films of school playgrounds in Toronto showed other children present in more than eight of every ten bullying episodes. Watchers give the bullying an audience, and laughing along joins it. The same films showed that when one child stepped in, the bullying stopped within 10 seconds more than half the time. Stepping in must be kind and safe. There are four ways. Say it calmly: 'Stop. That is not funny.' Stand beside the person, and take them away to play with you. Put right what you can yourself. Tell a trusted grown-up. Telling to get someone out of trouble is different from telling tales, which is done to get someone into trouble. Always tell a grown-up when someone is being hurt or threatened, or when the unkindness keeps happening. If it is happening to you, it is not your fault. Telling is the right thing to do, and you can keep telling until someone listens.
- e.g. A girl says 'come and play with us' to a classmate whose name others are making fun of.
- e.g. Two children refuse to laugh at a joke about a boy's stammer, and the joke falls flat.
- e.g. A child tells her teacher that a younger boy's lunch is taken from him every day.
- e.g. Two friends tease each other about a missed goal, and both are laughing.
### Why a cared-for place stays cared for · *defined*
**Definition:** A shared place belongs to everyone who uses it. Signs of care lead to more care, and signs of mess lead to more mess.

In the game Keep the Lane Clean, a passer-by copies the wrappers already lying in the lane. With 2 wrappers there, one passer-by in three adds another. With 4 wrappers, two in three add another. So a mess feeds itself, and the first wrapper matters most. Picking it up early costs one small act. Leaving it costs many acts later. The same holds for a scribble on a bench, a dripping tap and shoes left on the stairs. There are three ways to care for a shared place. Do your own part: use the bin, turn the tap off and put things back. Repair early: pick up the first wrapper, and report the drip on the day you see it. Make the right action easy to see and easy to do: a bin close by, a line to join, a sign that says what most people here do. Rules last longest when the people who use the place agree on them together.
- e.g. A classroom where the first torn poster is taped up the same day, and the walls stay neat all year.
- e.g. A staircase where one family's bag of rubbish is soon joined by others.
- e.g. A railway platform that stays clean after bins are placed every twenty steps.
- e.g. In Keep the Lane Clean, a lane that starts with 4 wrappers is much harder to save than a lane that starts with 2.
### Calming a big feeling, and asking for help · *defined*
**Definition:** A big feeling is a strong stress response. It settles sooner when you breathe out slowly, name the feeling, move your body or talk to someone you trust.

The body clears adrenaline from the blood within a few minutes, if nothing sets off more. Going over the upsetting thought again and again sets off more. Four things help the body settle. Breathe out slowly, for longer than you breathe in. The heart slows a little on every out-breath. Name the feeling exactly: 'I am embarrassed' says more than 'I feel bad'. Move: walk, stretch or wash your face. Talk to a person you trust. What works best differs from person to person, and the log called What Helped? shows what works for you. Every feeling is allowed. Hurting someone because of a feeling is not. Some feelings are too big to carry alone. A worry or a sadness may stay for many days. A secret may frighten you. Someone may be hurting you. When something feels too big, tell a trusted grown-up, and keep telling until one of them listens. People of every age ask for help, and it is a sensible thing to do.
- e.g. A child who lost a match takes ten slow out-breaths before shaking hands.
- e.g. A girl tells her aunt, 'I feel left out', and the two of them plan what to do.
- e.g. A boy who is angry with his brother walks round the building once before he speaks.
- e.g. A child tells her teacher about a worry that has kept her awake for a week.
### Other animals comfort and help each other · *defined*
**Definition:** Helping and comforting are found in many kinds of animals, including humans.

Humans are one helping animal among many. In 2011 Inbal Ben-Ami Bartal, Jean Decety and Peggy Mason put pairs of rats in a box. One rat was shut in a small clear tube, and the other was free. The free rats learned to open the tube and let their companions out. They did this even when a second tube held chocolate. Many of them opened both tubes and shared the chocolate. In 2014 Joshua Plotnik and Frans de Waal watched Asian elephants in Thailand. When one elephant was upset, others came to stand close. They touched the upset elephant with their trunks and made soft chirping calls. Chimpanzees put an arm round a companion who has lost a fight. So comforting others is far older than schools, rules or words. Scientists still disagree about exactly what the helper feels.
- e.g. A free rat opens a tube to let out a trapped companion.
- e.g. An elephant strokes an upset elephant with her trunk.
- e.g. A chimpanzee hugs a companion who has just lost a fight.
- e.g. A dog comes and sits against a person who is crying.
### Are we born kind, or do we learn to be kind? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that very young children help others. They do not agree on how much of human kindness is inborn and how much is taught.

Three kinds of evidence point in different directions. Toddlers help before anyone has taught them to. Yet people everywhere also look after themselves first, and need rules. And children grow up to share in the way their own community shares.
- e.g. A toddler of 18 months picks up a peg that a stranger dropped and hands it back.
- e.g. Children in different societies share differently from about the age of seven.
- **Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello, psychologists in Germany, in 2006:** They let a grown-up drop a peg or bump into a cupboard in front of toddlers of 18 months. Most toddlers helped within seconds, with no reward and no asking. Young chimpanzees helped in some of the tasks too. So helping begins before teaching.
- **Thomas Hobbes, an English philosopher, in his book Leviathan in 1651:** He wrote that people look after themselves first. Without rules and someone to enforce them, he said, people would fight over what they want. On this view kindness has to be taught and protected by rules.
- **Bailey House and a team of scientists, in 2013:** They tested children in six societies, from cities to small forest villages. Young children shared in much the same way everywhere. From about the age of seven, children began to share the way the grown-ups of their own society shared. So a community shapes the kindness a child is born with.

## Mostly agreed
- Feelings pass between people through faces, voices and bodies. *How we know:* In 2000 Ulf Dimberg's team recorded the face muscles of people looking at happy and angry faces. Their own muscles copied the faces within half a second, even when the faces were shown too briefly to notice.
- People break a rule more often in a place where they see that others have broken rules. *How we know:* In Groningen in 2008, 33 of every 100 bicycle riders dropped a paper advert in a clean alley. In the same alley with scribbled walls, 69 of every 100 did.
- Pressure from other people changes the body within minutes: the heart, the breathing and the muscles. *How we know:* Since 1993, scientists have asked volunteers to give a speech and do sums in front of silent judges. Heart rate and stress hormones rise in most people.
- Other children are present at most bullying, and one child who steps in often stops it. *How we know:* Lynn Hawkins, Debra Pepler and Wendy Craig studied films of playgrounds in Toronto, published in 2001. When a child stepped in, the bullying stopped within 10 seconds in 57 of every 100 cases.
- Talk in the background makes it harder to remember things in order, even when you try to ignore it. *How we know:* In 1982 Pierre Salamé and Alan Baddeley asked people to remember lists of digits. They remembered fewer when speech was played, even speech in a language they did not know.
- Very young children help other people without being asked and without a reward. *How we know:* In 2006 Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello tested toddlers of 18 months. Most of them picked up dropped things and opened cupboards for a grown-up they had only just met.

## How we found out
**Albert Bandura, Dorothea Ross and Sheila Ross at Stanford University, California, The report was published in 1961..** Until the 1960s, many psychologists taught that a child learns a behaviour only when she is rewarded or punished for it. Copying, they said, mattered little. In 1961 Albert Bandura and his colleagues Dorothea Ross and Sheila Ross tested this at a nursery school at Stanford University in California. They watched 72 children aged 3 to 6. One group saw a grown-up hit and shout at a big inflatable doll. A second group saw a grown-up play quietly with toys. A third group saw no grown-up at all. Then each child was left in a room with the same doll and toys. Nobody rewarded or praised any child. Yet the children who had watched the hitting hit the doll far more, and some used the same words and movements. Later work showed that children also notice whether the grown-up is praised or punished. But a child's learning could no longer be explained by her own rewards alone.  
*What people believed before:* That children learn only from what they are rewarded or punished for themselves. In Bandura's test, children copied a grown-up's actions and words with no reward at all.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Children who only watch bullying are not part of it.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: The watchers did not start it, and they said nothing unkind.  
  Against: Films of playgrounds in Toronto showed watchers at more than eight of every ten episodes. An audience keeps bullying going. When one watcher stepped in, it stopped within 10 seconds more than half the time.
- **When many people are watching, nobody helps.** (John Darley and Bibb Latané in 1968, and many textbooks since.; *debated*)  
  For: In their test, a person heard someone fall ill over an intercom. Of those who thought they were the only listener, 85 in 100 went for help. Of those who thought four others could hear, 31 in 100 did.  
  Against: In 2020 Richard Philpot's team studied street cameras in Amsterdam, Lancaster and Cape Town. They watched 219 real quarrels. In 9 of every 10, at least one bystander stepped in, and more bystanders made help more likely.
- **Telling people how many others break a rule will make them stop.** (Many notice boards and signs are written as if this were true.; *rejected*)  
  For: It shows that the problem is serious.  
  Against: Robert Cialdini's team tested signs in the Petrified Forest park in Arizona. A sign saying that many visitors took the ancient stone wood led to more theft: about 8 of every 100 marked pieces. A sign that simply asked visitors not to take wood led to fewer than 2 in 100.
- **Teasing toughens children up.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Friendly teasing between equals is a kind of play, and it can bring friends closer.  
  Against: Teasing that the other person does not enjoy sets off the stress response. In the Hurry Test, a countdown can make hands miss more often. Children who are bullied again and again enjoy school less and do worse at their work.
- **Cleaning is unskilled work, so it deserves less respect.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: The work needs no degree, and it is often poorly paid.  
  Against: A cleaner must know how to handle sharp and infected waste, and which chemicals must never be mixed. The work stops disease for everyone else. How long a person studied for a job does not measure how much others depend on it.
- **All kindness is hidden selfishness.** (Some philosophers and some biologists say this.; *debated*)  
  For: Helpers often get help back later, and helping feels good to the helper.  
  Against: Toddlers and rats help with no reward in sight. People return lost purses to strangers they will never meet. A good feeling afterwards does not show that the feeling was the aim.

## Roots
In the Hurry Test and the Smile Count, another person's words and face changed what your own body did. Here we ask what this tells us about where a skill, a mood and a place really come from.

**Deep truth.** In the Hurry Test, the same hand and the same chana did worse when someone said 'faster'. The words entered through the ears and reached the heart and the fingers within seconds. In the Smile Count, one face moved another face. So a skill or a mood is never made inside one person alone.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'she is careful' or 'he is clumsy', as if a skill sat inside one person like a coin in a pocket. Yet each body is being changed all the time by the faces, voices and actions of the people near it.  
*Evidence:* In the Hurry Test the helper changes only the words, and many people then miss more and have a quicker pulse. In 2000, Ulf Dimberg's sensors showed face muscles copying a face within half a second. In the Smile Count you see how often a smile is returned.

- *who am i:* You are partly made by the people around you. Your face, your voice and your aim all change with who is in the room.
- *what is life:* Living beings change each other's bodies all the time. Rats free trapped companions, elephants comfort an upset elephant, and a toddler hands back a dropped peg.
- *what is this world:* A place is made of the people in it. Its mood and its rules pass from one person to the next, so no place stands apart from the people who use it.

A mood, a habit or a skill passes from one body to another through faces, voices and actions. A giggling row, a line at a tap and a quiet room are each one shared state held by many people. Many sages taught that all beings share one awareness, even though each of us looks out from a different body. This lesson shows one small, measurable case of it. The state of a place is held by all of its people together.

- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** In Indra's net, a bright jewel hangs at every knot, and each jewel shows the reflection of all the others. (A face in a room shows the faces around it. A smile or a frown is reflected from one person to the next. The sutra is a picture from a Buddhist text, and the unit's measurements stand without it.)
- **Ubuntu, a saying of the Zulu and Xhosa peoples of southern Africa: 'umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu':** A person is a person through other persons. (The Hurry Test shows one exact case. A person's aim, pulse and mood are shaped by what other people say and do near them.)
- **Thirukkural 322, by Thiruvalluvar:** “பகுத்துண்டு பல்லுயிர் ஓம்புதல் நூலோர் / தொகுத்தவற்றுள் எல்லாந் தலை.” Share your food and look after all living beings. The wise count this as the first of all duties. (A place where people share and care for each other and for the place is a happy place. The unit shows how such habits pass from person to person, as a line at a tap passes from three people to many.)
- Science (settled): Seeing a face makes your own face muscles begin to copy it, often within half a second, even when you do not notice the face.
- Science (open): Whether copying a face creates the feeling itself, or only goes along with it, is still being measured. Some famous tests could not be repeated.
- Other view, Many psychologists who study personality: People differ from the start in how easily they feel stress, and in how steady their hands are. A skill belongs to a person, and a place adds only a little to it. Both views have evidence. How much comes from the person and how much from the people around is still being measured.
- Practice, Passing on a calm face (3 min): Sit facing your child. Look at each other's faces for a few breaths. Breathe out slowly and let your face rest. Notice whether the other face changes. Then each of you picks one person you will meet tomorrow who will get a calm face from you.
- Ask: In the Hurry Test, the helper changed only the words. Where did the change in your hand begin?
- Ask: A lane stays clean when the first few people keep it clean. Who began the habits in your own building?
- Ask: If a mood passes from one person to another, whose mood is it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the Hurry Test, the same hand did worse when someone hurried it. So does a skill belong to one person, or to the people in the room together? (hint: Think about who changed between the calm runs and the hurried runs, and what stayed the same.)
- *deep:* A new child copies what they see, whether it is pushing or a line. Who decides what is normal in a place? (hint: Think about the first three people at a tap, and about the hundredth.)
- *deep:* A place can teach a rule without any words. How does a clean wall or a scribbled wall tell you what to do? (hint: Think about what you look at first when you enter a place you have never been to.)
- *big:* The work that no place can run without is often paid and respected the least. Is that fair, and why does it happen? (hint: Think about who does this work, who decides the pay, and what would happen in a week if the work stopped.)
- *big:* A ramp built for one wheelchair user also helps many other people. Should every place be designed for the person who finds it hardest? (hint: Think about the cost, about who benefits, and about who is left out when nothing changes.)
- *big:* A rat frees a trapped companion and shares her chocolate. Did humans invent kindness, or is it older than humans? (hint: Think about elephants, chimpanzees and toddlers, none of whom were taught a rule about helping.)

## Story: Three in a Line
*Thenmozhi, Sasmita, Kavin, Poongodi, Fathima, Yamuna teacher, Rajathi akka and the school are made up. These facts are real. Tiruppur in Tamil Nadu is a town of knitwear factories, and many families from Odisha and other states work there. Schools in Tamil Nadu serve a free noon meal. 'Thanni' is the everyday Tamil word for water, and 'pani' is the Odia word. Tests by scientists show that people copy what they see others doing more than what they are told.*

At Thenmozhi's school in Tiruppur, lunch began at the taps. A long pipe ran behind the kitchen, with six taps on it. Every child had to wash there before Rajathi akka would fill a plate.

Nobody stood in a line. Two hundred children came round the corner in a bunch and pushed in from the sides. The small ones were squeezed to the back. Water ran on to the ground while people argued, and by one o'clock the mud was ankle-deep.

In June, Yamuna teacher made Thenmozhi the tap monitor. Thenmozhi was in Class 5, and she had the loudest voice in the school. She was sure that was why she had been chosen.

On the first day she stood on the kitchen step and shouted. "One line! One line! Don't push!" A few children looked up. Then the bunch closed again. She shouted louder. By Friday her throat hurt, and the mud was as deep as ever.

"They don't listen," she told Poongodi and Fathima. "I say it fifty times."

"You say it very well," said Fathima. "Nobody can hear anything else."

The new girl came in the second week. Her name was Sasmita. Her mother and father had come from Odisha to work in a knitwear factory, and at home she spoke Odia. She knew about ten words of Tamil. She sat in the front row of Class 4 and watched the teacher's hands.

At lunch Sasmita walked out with her plate, saw the bunch at the taps, and stopped. She stood at the edge for a long time. Then she did what everyone else was doing. She put her shoulder down and pushed in from the side.

"Hey! New girl! LINE!" Thenmozhi shouted from the step.

Sasmita turned round. She looked at Thenmozhi's mouth. Then she looked at the children on both sides of her, who were pushing too. Her face went stiff. She stepped back out of the bunch and stood by the wall, with her hands still dry.

Somebody laughed. "She doesn't even know the word 'line'."

Thenmozhi opened her mouth to shout again, and found that she had nothing to shout. The girl had not understood one word. But she had understood the taps perfectly. She had looked at what two hundred children were doing, and she had done it too.

Thenmozhi looked at the bunch for a while. Perhaps everybody here had learned the taps in the same way. Nobody at this school had ever seen a line. They had only heard about one, from a girl on a step.

That evening she thought about Kavin in Class 2. Last year Kavin used to wait at the back until the taps were free. This year he pushed. Nobody had told him to. He had spent a year watching Class 5.

The next day Thenmozhi did not go up on the step. She asked Poongodi and Fathima to leave class with her two minutes before the bell. They went to the first tap and stood one behind the other, with their plates under their arms.

"This is silly," said Poongodi. "Three people are not a line."

"Stand straight," said Thenmozhi. "And don't say anything."

The bell rang. The bunch came round the corner of the kitchen, loud as always, and went for the taps. Five taps disappeared under elbows. At the first tap, a few children slowed down. Three big girls stood in a row there, and it was not clear how to push past a row.

Sasmita came out last, as she did every day. She stopped at the edge and looked. Thenmozhi kept her eyes on the tap and did not call out. From the corner of her eye she saw the new girl look at the five bunches, and then at the one row. Sasmita walked over and stood behind Fathima.

Now there were four.

Kavin came next. A line has a place at the end, and that is easier for a small boy than a wall of backs. Then came two girls from Class 3. Then came a boy who looked round first, to see if anyone was laughing at him. Nobody was.

When Thenmozhi's turn came, she washed her hands, turned the tap off tightly and stepped aside. Fathima did the same, and so did Sasmita. Thenmozhi had not asked them to. At the other five taps the water was still running on to the ground.

She found Sasmita by the kitchen door afterwards and pointed back at the tap. "Thanni," she said. "Water. Thanni."

"Thanni," said Sasmita. She pointed at the tap too. "Pani."

"Pani," said Thenmozhi. It was the first Odia word she had ever said. Sasmita's face was not stiff any more.

By the next Friday the first tap had a line every day, and the second tap had grown a line that nobody had started on purpose. Yamuna teacher came out to look. "You have stopped shouting," she said.

"Nobody could see what I was shouting," said Thenmozhi.

On Monday she came out two minutes early again. Three children were already standing at the first tap, one behind the other. Kavin was at the front, standing very straight. Thenmozhi walked to the end of the line and stood there.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Thenmozhi thought that children would behave well if she told them loudly enough. Sasmita, who knew no Tamil, showed her the mistake. Sasmita pushed because she saw pushing, and joined a line as soon as she could see a line. People, and new people most of all, learn what is done in a place by watching what others do. So the surest way to change how a place behaves is to do the right thing where others can see it.

- Ask: Thenmozhi shouted 'one line' for a week. Why did the shouting change nothing?
- Ask: Sasmita did not know the word 'line'. How did she know what to do on the second day?
- Ask: Kavin waited last year and pushed this year. What had changed for him?
- Ask: Is there a place you use where people copy something unhelpful? What could three people do there?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Hurry Test: does another person's hurrying change where your chana land? (35 min)
- **question:** Do you miss the bottle more often when a helper hurries you than when the helper stays quiet? Does your pulse change too?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many of the 15 chana will miss in a calm run, and how many in a hurried run? Will your pulse change? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same bottle, the same 15 chana and the same hand height every time. Sit in the same place. Take turns between calm and hurried runs, so that getting better with practice does not fool you. The helper says only one kind of thing: nothing, or hurry words. Only what the helper says changes.
- **safety:** The helper uses only the words 'faster', 'hurry' and 'time is running out', in a playful voice. The helper never says anything unkind. Stop at once if the child is upset. Pick up every chana from the floor.
- **record:** Make a table. Fill one row for each run. Under it, write what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Run | What the helper said | Misses out of 15 | Pulse beats in 15 seconds | Body tightness, 1 to 5
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the bottle in the middle of the plate. Put 15 chana in the bowl. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* You will drop 15 chana into the bottle, one at a time. In some runs I stay quiet. In some runs I hurry you. How many will miss in each kind? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Drop 15 chana into the bottle. Guess how many miss when someone hurries you. Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why might hurry words change where a chana goes? (good: My hand might shake.; I might rush and aim badly.; My body might get tense or nervous.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. The helper only says 'faster', 'hurry' and 'time is running out'. Never say 'you are slow'. Stop at once if your child is upset.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child sits and holds each chana 30 cm above the bottle's mouth, about the length of a school ruler. They practise one round of 15 drops. The helper says nothing. Do not count this round.  
  *Parent says:* Hold each chana at the height of the ruler, then let go. Practise all 15 once. This round does not count.  
  *Child mode:* Hold each chana 30 cm above the bottle. Drop all 15 for practice.
  - Note: If every chana goes in, raise the hand to 45 cm, or use the 500 millilitre bottle, which has a smaller mouth. You want about 1 to 3 misses in a calm round. If chana keep missing, lower the hand to 20 cm.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the first pair of runs. Calm run: the helper says 'Go', then stays silent. Count the chana on the plate. Count the pulse for 15 seconds. Ask how tight the body feels, from 1 (loose) to 5 (very tight), and write it down. Then do a hurried run: the helper says 'Go', then calls 'Faster! Hurry! Time is running out!' all the way through. Count all three again.  
  *Parent says:* First a calm run. I say 'Go' and nothing more. Afterwards we count the chana on the plate, and your pulse for 15 seconds. Then the hurried run.  
  *Child mode:* Do one calm run and one hurried run. Count misses. Count your pulse for 15 seconds. Say how tight you feel, from 1 to 5.
  - Note: For the pulse, put two fingers on the inside of the wrist, below the thumb. Count the beats for 15 seconds. Write the misses, the pulse, and a body tightness number from 1 (loose) to 5 (very tight). Ask for the number straight after the run.
  - Check: In the Hurry Test, why do the calm and hurried runs take turns? → **So practice does not fool us**
- **s4 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do four more runs in this order: calm, hurried, calm, hurried. Write down the misses, the pulse and the tightness number after every run. Rest for a minute if your child's arm is tired.  
  *Parent says:* Now four more runs, taking turns: calm, hurried, calm, hurried. Write down the three numbers after each one.  
  *Child mode:* Do four more runs: calm, hurried, calm, hurried. Write three numbers each time.
  - Note: A few families see no difference. That is a real result, and you can say so in the last step. Do not hint to your child what you hope to see.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap places. Your child is the helper. The grown-up does one calm run and one hurried run. The child counts the misses and the pulse. The grown-up says how tight their own body felt.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are the helper. I will do one calm run and one hurried run. Hurry me as I hurried you, and no more. Then count my misses and my pulse.  
  *Child mode:* Now you are the helper. Hurry your grown-up in one run. Count their misses.
  - Note: Let your child hear how the hurry words feel from the helper's side. Ask them afterwards how it felt to hurry someone.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the misses of the three calm runs and of the three hurried runs. Compare the pulses and the tightness numbers. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Add up the misses in the three calm runs, and in the three hurried runs. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Add up the misses for each kind of run. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: Did the hurried runs have more misses than the calm runs? What else changed? (good: Yes, there were more misses, and my pulse was higher.; My body felt tighter.; Not much changed, and that is a real result too.)
  - Ask: How could you find out whether the words or only the speed caused the misses? (good: Hurry myself with no words, then compare.; Have the helper say 'faster' in a slow, calm voice.; Run the same test with a helper who says 'take your time'.)
  - Note: The reason: hurry words can set off the stress response. Adrenaline speeds the heart, and the muscles tighten. Tight, quick hands aim less well. The test cannot separate the words from the speed, and a grown-up can say so honestly. A small test of one family is only a hint. Scientists would repeat it with many people.
  - Think: Think of a time someone hurried you. What did your body do?
### e2 · The Smile Count: does a smile come back more often than a plain face? (30 min)
- **question:** Out of 10 people, how many smile back at a smile and a greeting, and how many smile back at a plain face?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many of 10 people will smile back at a smile? How many at a plain face? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Greet people one after another, taking turns: smile, plain, smile, plain. Use the same places and the same time of day. A plain face is a calm, ordinary face, never a cross one. Only the smile and the word change. Count a smile back only if it comes within 3 seconds.
- **safety:** A grown-up stays beside the child all the time. Greet only people who pass you in public places. Never stop anyone, follow anyone or ask anyone a question. If someone seems upset, do not greet them.
- **record:** Make a table with two rows: smile and word, plain face. Write the number of people and the number who smiled back.
- **results table:** How I greeted | People greeted | Smiled back within 3 seconds | Said something back
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Read the plan together. Choose the place. Ask your child for the guess and the reason, and write both down. Practise a plain face in a mirror, calm and not cross.  
  *Parent says:* You will greet 10 people with a smile and a word, and 10 with a plain face. How many will smile back each way? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will smile at some people and not at others. Guess who smiles back more. Why?
  - Ask: Why might someone smile back at a stranger's smile? (good: Smiling is catching.; It is polite.; Seeing a smile makes my own face copy it.)
  - Note: Choose a place where about 20 people pass in 15 to 20 minutes. A big building's stairs, a lane near a shop or a bus stop will do. If few people pass, do it over two days. The plain face is a calm resting face, not a frown.
- **s2 (18 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Stand with your child at the place. Your child greets passers-by, taking turns: smile and a word, then a plain face and no word, until 10 of each. Use a tally mark for each person. Mark a smile back only if it comes within 3 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Greet the first person with a smile and a word. The next person gets a plain face. I will make a tally mark for each person who smiles back.  
  *Child mode:* Greet people in turns: smile and a word, then a plain face. Your grown-up keeps the tally.
  - Note: Use the local greeting, such as 'good morning', 'vanakkam', 'namaste' or 'salaam'. Say it in an ordinary voice. Some people look at phones and do not see the child. Count them as people greeted only if they looked at the child.
  - Check: In the Smile Count, why must the plain face be calm and not cross? → **So only the smile is different**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the tally marks for each way of greeting. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Add up the smiles that came back each way. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Add up the smiles that came back. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: Did more people smile back at a smile than at a plain face? (good: Yes, about twice as many.; The numbers were about the same.; A few people smiled at both.)
  - Ask: How could someone smile back without wanting to? (good: Our face muscles copy a face we see.; The copying can happen very fast.)
  - Note: The reason: when we see a face, our own face muscles begin to copy it, often within half a second, and the copied look can start the feeling. So smiles are catching. A smile that comes back is a hint, not proof, because some people smile to be polite. If the numbers are about equal, say so: a big city, a hurry or a phone can stop people looking up.
  - Think: If one smile can start another, what might a frown start?
### e3 · The Doorway Test: which doors and switches in your home fit a wheelchair? (30 min)
- **question:** How many of the doors, thresholds and switches in your home can a person in a wheelchair use?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many of the five doors in your home will let a 65 cm wide wheelchair pass? Which will not? Why do you think so?

- **safety:** Do not climb on anything to reach a switch. Measure only. Do not switch on any heavy appliance or touch any open wire.
- **record:** Make a table with a row for each door, step, switch and handle. Write the measurement and tick yes or no. At the bottom, write the one change that would help the most.
- **results table:** Door or switch | Measurement in centimetres | Fits a seated person?

  *Child mode:* A wheelchair is about 65 cm wide. Which doors at home will it pass? Why?
  - Ask: Why might a bathroom door be narrower than a front door? (good: A bathroom is small, and people planned it for walking.; The builder saved space.)

### a4 · What Helped? Which calming move slows your pulse best? (25 min)

  *Parent says:* We will try three ways to settle your body. One is to sit still. One is to breathe out slowly. One is to say how you feel and tell me. Which will slow your pulse most? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Try three ways to settle. Which will slow your pulse most? Why?

  - Note: A few beats of difference are normal from one trial to the next. One trial is not enough to decide. If you want a fairer test, repeat the next day in the order C, B, A. The body is revved by marching, not by a feeling, but a strong feeling revs the body in a similar way.

  - Note: What science says: the body clears adrenaline within minutes, if nothing sets off more. A long out-breath slows the heart a little. ### c1 · The Ramp That Fits: a 15 cm step and only 120 cm of ground in front (30 min)
- **problem:** A library door has one step that is 15 cm high. Guides say a ramp may rise no more than 1 cm for every 12 cm of length. But the ground in front of the step is only 120 cm deep, because a wall stands there. The step itself is 600 cm wide. Design a ramp that a wheelchair can use.

It needs at least 12 cm of length for each 1 cm of rise. So the whole ramp needs at least 18 cm in your model.; The ramp must be at least 12 cm wide in the model, which stands for 120 cm.; Any turn needs a flat landing at least 15 cm long and 15 cm wide.

## Worksheets
### Happy places, feelings that spread, and kind and safe actions (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which three things make a place a happy one?  
   Options: New paint, big rooms and loud music / Rules, bells and silence / Prizes, uniforms and tall walls / Safety, belonging and care for the place  
   **Answer:** Safety, belonging and care for the place. *Why:* A happy place is one where people are safe, everyone belongs, and the place is cared for. Paint, size and prizes can help, but they do not make a place happy on their own.
2. (match) Match each action to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Say it calmly → Uses a steady voice and few words; Stand beside → Sits or stands with the person; Put it right → Does the helpful thing yourself; Tell a grown-up → Gets help from someone you trust. *Why:* These are four ways to step in that are kind and safe. Each does a different job, and a situation may need more than one.
3. (fill) A feeling that passes from one person to another, mostly without either choosing it, is called emotional ____.  
   **Answer:** ['contagion']. *Why:* Contagion means spreading. Giggles, yawns and fear can all spread between people, mostly without anyone choosing it.
4. (truefalse) In a fair test of hurry words, the bottle and the chana must change in every run.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In a fair test only one thing changes, which is what the helper says. The bottle, the chana and the hand height stay the same, so that any difference comes from the words.
5. (mcq) People copy what they see others do in a place. What is 'what most people here do' called?  
   Options: A stress response / A social norm / A barrier / A charter  
   **Answer:** A social norm. *Why:* A social norm is what most people in a place do, or think they should do. A stress response is the body getting ready for action. A barrier stops people, and a charter is a list of agreed rules.
6. (sort) Sort each one. Is it a barrier, or does it remove a barrier?  
   **Answer:** a step with no ramp → Barrier; a ramp beside the steps → Removes a barrier; a switch too high to reach → Barrier; a notice with pictures as well as words → Removes a barrier; a door 60 cm wide → Barrier; a wide door with a handrail → Removes a barrier. *Why:* A barrier is anything that stops some people from using a place. A ramp, pictures and a wide door let more people in.
7. (truefalse) Friendly teasing that both people enjoy is bullying.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bullying hurts, frightens or leaves out the same person again and again, and the person finds it hard to stop it. Friendly teasing is fun for both and stops when one says stop.
8. (mcq) What does the stress response do to the body?  
   Options: Slows the heart and loosens the muscles / Stops all breathing / Speeds the heart and tightens the muscles / Makes the body colder only  
   **Answer:** Speeds the heart and tightens the muscles. *Why:* When something seems urgent or unsafe, the body gets ready for action. Adrenaline speeds the heart, breathing quickens and the muscles tighten.
9. (fill) Dignity of labour means that every honest ____ deserves equal respect.  
   **Answer:** ['job', 'work']. *Why:* Dignity of labour means every honest job deserves equal respect, including jobs that are poorly paid, such as cleaning.
10. (write) A helper hurries a person who is dropping chana into a bottle. Explain why more chana might miss.  
   **Answer:** The hurry words make the body treat the moment as urgent. Adrenaline speeds the heart and tightens the muscles. Tight, quick hands aim less well.. *Why:* A strong answer says that words from another person can start the stress response, and links tight muscles to poor aim.
11. (write) Name two ways a place can teach a rule without any words. Say how each one works.  
   **Answer:** A clean wall tells people that litter is not done here, and a scribbled wall says the opposite. A line at a tap shows people to wait. People copy what they see others do.. *Why:* A strong answer says that people read a place and copy what they see. It gives two real examples.
12. (draw) Draw a doorway that a person in a wheelchair and a person with a pram can both use. Label three features.  
   **Answer:** A wide doorway of at least 90 cm. A gentle ramp instead of a step, or no step. A low door handle and a low switch, between 40 and 120 cm from the floor.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a clear width of at least 90 cm and no step. It also shows that the controls are within reach of a seated person.
13. (perspective) Who should clean a classroom at the end of the day?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For example: children should pick up their own things, because a mess is not only the cleaner's problem. But a child should not have to handle dirty or dangerous waste.. *Why:* A strong answer uses an idea from the unit, such as dignity of labour, safety, or the way a place stays cared for. It treats the other views fairly.
14. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, when someone shouts 'faster!' at you.  
   **Answer:** ['Brain treats the moment as urgent', 'Adrenaline enters the blood', 'Heart beats faster', 'Hands feel tight and aim worse']. *Why:* The brain first treats the words as urgent. The adrenal glands release adrenaline. The heart speeds up, and the muscles tighten, so hands aim less well.
15. (mcq) A friend says someone is hurting her, and asks you to keep it secret. What should you do?  
   Options: Keep the secret / Tell the whole class / Wait for a year / Tell a trusted grown-up  
   **Answer:** Tell a trusted grown-up. *Why:* A secret about someone being hurt is too big for a child to carry. A trusted grown-up can get help. Telling is how your friend gets help.
### Why people copy, what barriers do, and how to step in (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Riders dropped more paper beside scribbled walls than beside clean walls. What does this show?  
   Options: Scribbles make people hungry / Riders cannot read / People copy what they see others do / Clean walls are slippery  
   **Answer:** People copy what they see others do. *Why:* A scribbled wall says that people here break rules, so more riders dropped paper. Nobody told them anything. The place gave the message.
2. (mcq) Which is the kindest and safest first action when you see someone being teased?  
   Options: Join in so you are not next / Say 'Stop, that is not funny' calmly / Shout and push the teaser / Walk away and say nothing  
   **Answer:** Say 'Stop, that is not funny' calmly. *Why:* A calm, short sentence can stop teasing without anyone being hurt. Joining in or pushing adds to the harm, and saying nothing gives the teaser an audience.
3. (mcq) Why does one wrapper on the ground lead to more wrappers?  
   Options: People copy a sign that others have littered / Wind carries wrappers together / Wrappers attract insects / Bins are far away  
   **Answer:** People copy a sign that others have littered. *Why:* A wrapper on the ground tells people that dropping wrappers is done here. A mess that others have left makes more mess likely.
4. (truefalse) Telling a grown-up about bullying is the same as telling tales.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Telling to get someone out of trouble is not the same as telling tales, which is done to get someone into trouble. Always tell when someone is being hurt.
5. (truefalse) A ramp that helps a person in a wheelchair can also help a person with a pram.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A change made for one person often helps many, such as a parent with a pram, a grandparent with a stick and a worker with a trolley.
6. (odd) Three of these help a big feeling settle. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Breathe out slowly / Name the feeling exactly / Keep it secret for ever / Talk to someone you trust  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Breathing out slowly, naming the feeling and talking to someone you trust all help a feeling settle. Keeping a big feeling secret for ever does not.
7. (count) For each 1 cm of rise, a ramp needs 12 cm of length. A 15 cm step needs how many cm?  
   **Answer:** 180. *Why:* 15 times 12 is 180. So the ramp must be 180 cm long, which is 1.8 metres.
8. (sort) Sort each example. Does it show respect, or not?  
   **Answer:** greeting a cleaner by name → Shows respect; cutting in while someone is speaking → Does not show respect; waiting for your turn at the tap → Shows respect; laughing at the way someone speaks → Does not show respect; asking before touching someone's things → Shows respect; dropping a wrapper that a worker must pick up → Does not show respect. *Why:* Respect means treating a person as someone who matters as much as you do. Each of the first kind of actions does that, and each of the others does not.
9. (match) Match each example to the idea it shows.  
   **Answer:** One child giggles and the whole row giggles → Emotional contagion; The heart beats faster when someone shouts → Stress response; A step with no ramp → Barrier; Everyone takes off their shoes at a temple door → Social norm; Hurting the same child again and again → Bullying. *Why:* Each example is a clear case of its idea. Contagion is a feeling spreading, and a norm is what most people do in a place.
10. (write) Explain why a sign saying 'Many people drop litter here' can make litter worse.  
   **Answer:** The sign tells people that dropping litter is what most people do here. People copy what they see and read about others. A sign that asks people to keep the place clean works better.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the sign sets a social norm in the wrong direction. It says that people follow what most others do.
11. (write) Why does an audience keep bullying going? Say what a watcher can do instead.  
   **Answer:** An audience gives the bully attention, and laughing joins in. A watcher can say it calmly, stand beside the person, put it right or tell a grown-up. One child stepping in often stops it quickly.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the watchers are part of it, and names at least two of the four ways to step in.
12. (write) A ramp built for one wheelchair user helps other people too. Name three, and say why.  
   **Answer:** A parent with a pram, because wheels roll up a ramp. A grandparent with a stick, because a slope is easier than a step. A worker with a trolley, because a trolley cannot climb steps.. *Why:* A strong answer names three groups and links each to a barrier that a ramp removes.
13. (perspective) A school sees pushing at the water taps. What would work best?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For example: a line started by older students works because people copy what they see, and it costs nothing. But more taps remove the reason to push. A rule alone may not work if people see others pushing.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea that people copy what they see, or that the place makes the right action easy. It treats the other views fairly.
14. (label) On a drawing of a doorway, label these four things: threshold, clear width, door handle, light switch.  
   **Answer:** ['Threshold: the raised edge on the floor', 'Clear width: the narrowest gap with the door open', 'Door handle: low enough for a seated person, between 40 and 120 cm', 'Light switch: also between 40 and 120 cm from the floor']. *Why:* These are the four things that decide whether a doorway can be used by a person in a wheelchair. A strong label says what each is and what a good size is.
15. (fill) Breathe ____ slowly, for longer than you breathe in, to help a big feeling settle.  
   **Answer:** ['out']. *Why:* A long out-breath slows the heart a little. The body can then settle sooner.
### Testing ideas about feelings, norms and kindness (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A test finds that hurried hands miss more. How could you show that the words, not only the speed, caused it?  
   Options: Use a bigger bottle / Test one run only / Hurry yourself with no words, then compare / Use fewer chana  
   **Answer:** Hurry yourself with no words, then compare. *Why:* If the hand also misses more when it hurries itself in silence, the speed alone matters. If it misses more only with the hurry words, the words matter. Changing the bottle, the run count or the chana would not separate these.
2. (mcq) A park sign says 'Most visitors take wood.' Which sign would work better?  
   Options: Thieves will be caught / Many visitors take wood / Wood is very valuable / Please leave the wood where it is  
   **Answer:** Please leave the wood where it is. *Why:* A sign that says many people break a rule makes breaking it look normal. A simple, friendly request that shows the right action worked better in tests.
3. (mcq) Cameras filmed real street quarrels, and someone helped in most. What does this suggest about crowds?  
   Options: Crowds always help / Crowds do not always stop people helping / Cameras scare helpers / Quarrels are rare  
   **Answer:** Crowds do not always stop people helping. *Why:* An old idea says that nobody helps when many are watching. Real films suggest that in most quarrels someone steps in. Scientists still argue about how well the old idea fits real life.
4. (truefalse) People are kind only because they are taught rules and given prizes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Toddlers help others before anyone has taught them, and rats free trapped companions. Teaching and the community shape kindness, but they are not the only source.
5. (truefalse) Dignity of labour means every honest job must be paid exactly the same.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It means every honest job deserves equal respect. Pay is a separate question, which people argue about.
6. (count) In the lane game, a roll of 1, 2 or 3 adds a wrapper. About how many of 60 rolls add one?  
   **Answer:** 30. *Why:* 3 of the 6 numbers add a wrapper, which is half. Half of 60 is 30.
7. (order) Put the steps of a fair test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Decide what one thing to change', 'Keep everything else the same', 'Do each run and write the number', 'Say what the numbers show']. *Why:* A fair test starts with one thing to change. You keep the rest the same, run the test, and only then say what the numbers show.
8. (fill) A short list of rules that the people of a place agree on together and sign is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['charter']. *Why:* A charter is agreed together and signed, so people feel it is theirs. Rules last longest when the people who use the place agree on them.
9. (write) Telling people to form a line often fails, but standing in a line yourself often works. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** People copy what they see. A person shouting about a line is only words. A person standing in a line is a picture of what is done here, and others see that and join.. *Why:* A strong answer says people follow what they see more than what they are told, and uses the idea of a social norm.
10. (write) Is a narrow bathroom door a problem of the person who cannot get through, or of the building? Give evidence.  
   **Answer:** The building. The person cannot change the door by trying harder. A wider door would let every person through, so the door was made for people who walk.. *Why:* A strong answer says a barrier is in the place, and gives a measurement or an example.
11. (write) A classmate asks you to leave another child out. Write what you would say, and why.  
   **Answer:** 'I want to be friends with both of you.' A friend who asks you to leave someone out is asking for something unfair, and leaving the same person out again and again is a kind of bullying.. *Why:* A strong answer is calm and short, and says why in terms of fairness or bullying.
12. (write) Why might a calm voice and a smile help a hurried person do better work? Give two reasons from the body.  
   **Answer:** A calm voice does not start the stress response, so the heart stays slower and the muscles stay loose. A smile can be copied, so the other person's face and feelings relax too.. *Why:* A strong answer names two body effects: the stress response and the copying of faces.
13. (perspective) Is it fair that cleaning work is often paid the least?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For example: respect and pay are different questions. A place cannot run without cleaning, and the work protects everyone from illness, so that can be a reason to pay more. But pay is set by many things people disagree about.. *Why:* A strong answer separates respect from pay, and uses a fact such as that a school with no cleaners would soon be unfit to use. It treats the other views fairly.
14. (draw) Draw a school water tap area where children stand in a line without being told. Label three features.  
   **Answer:** A line marked on the ground, a footprint or an arrow at each tap, a short row of taps so no one waits long, a shaded place to stand, and a notice with a picture.. *Why:* A strong drawing makes the right action easy to see and easy to do, as in a line mark or a shorter wait.
15. (match) Match each situation to the best first action.  
   **Answer:** Someone is being hurt and threatened → Tell a trusted grown-up at once; A tap is left running → Turn it off yourself; A classmate is left out of a game → Stand beside her and invite her; A classmate laughs at how someone reads aloud → Say calmly, 'Let her finish'. *Why:* Someone being hurt must always be told to a grown-up. The others can be put right by a child, calmly and kindly.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which place is most likely to be a happy one?  
   Options: A grand hall where people ignore each other / A new building where older children push / A big field with no rules / A plain room where people greet and help each other  
   **Answer:** A plain room where people greet and help each other. *Why:* A happy place is made mostly by how the people in it treat each other. A plain room with kind people is happier than a fine one with unkind people.
2. (mcq) One child starts to giggle and the whole row soon giggles. What is happening?  
   Options: The row is learning a rule / A feeling is spreading from person to person / Everyone is tired / The teacher is cross  
   **Answer:** A feeling is spreading from person to person. *Why:* This is emotional contagion. A feeling spreads through faces and voices, mostly without anyone choosing it.
3. (mcq) Which substance enters the blood when the body gets ready for urgent action?  
   Options: Insulin / Calcium / Adrenaline / Chlorophyll  
   **Answer:** Adrenaline. *Why:* The two small adrenal glands above the kidneys release adrenaline. It speeds the heart within seconds.
4. (mcq) Why does a fair test take turns between two kinds of run?  
   Options: So nobody gets bored / So the helper can rest / So practice does not fool the result / So the numbers are bigger  
   **Answer:** So practice does not fool the result. *Why:* If one kind came first, practice would help the later kind and the result would be unfair. Taking turns spreads the practice evenly.
5. (mcq) Which shows respect for a person who cleans a school?  
   Options: Learn her name and greet her / Leave your rubbish on the floor / Ignore her / Tease her  
   **Answer:** Learn her name and greet her. *Why:* Respect means treating a person as someone who matters as much as you do. A greeting by name is a small, real act of it.
6. (mcq) A door is 60 cm wide. Which person is most likely to be stopped?  
   Options: A person in a 65 cm wide wheelchair / A child on foot / A grandfather with a stick / A person with a school bag  
   **Answer:** A person in a 65 cm wide wheelchair. *Why:* A 65 cm wide chair cannot pass a 60 cm gap. A barrier is anything that stops some people from using a place.
7. (mcq) A bigger child pushes a smaller child and says 'Tell anyone and you are next.' What is the best action?  
   Options: Push the bigger child / Tell a trusted grown-up at once / Laugh and walk away / Keep it secret  
   **Answer:** Tell a trusted grown-up at once. *Why:* Someone is being hurt and threatened. A child should not step in alone. A trusted grown-up can make it stop.
8. (mcq) Why is picking up the first wrapper better than picking up the tenth?  
   Options: Fewer wrappers mean fewer people copy / The first one is heavier / Bins are nearer then / Nobody sees the tenth  
   **Answer:** Fewer wrappers mean fewer people copy. *Why:* A mess feeds itself. With few wrappers few people add one, and with many wrappers most do. So the first wrapper matters most.
9. (mcq) Which of these can help a big feeling settle?  
   Options: Holding your breath / Breathing out slowly, longer than breathing in / Keeping the feeling secret / Shouting at someone  
   **Answer:** Breathing out slowly, longer than breathing in. *Why:* A slow, long out-breath slows the heart a little. Naming the feeling, moving and talking to someone you trust also help.
10. (truefalse) A feeling that is too big to carry alone should be told to a trusted grown-up.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. People of every age ask for help with big feelings, and it is a sensible thing to do. Keep telling until someone listens.
11. (mcq) When do people feel they belong in a place?  
   Options: Only the best get a turn / New children wait outside / Rules are never explained / Everyone is greeted and gets a turn  
   **Answer:** Everyone is greeted and gets a turn. *Why:* Belonging means that everyone is greeted, can take part and gets a turn. It is one of the four things that make a place happy.

## Applications
- Schools put ramps, wide doors and low handrails on their buildings so that every child can get in. India's Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act of 2016 asks public buildings to be accessible.
- Road signs use pictures and arrows, so that a driver who does not read the local language can still follow them.
- A teacher who greets each child at the door, calmly and by name, sets the mood for the whole class before the lesson begins.
- Many schools teach children four ways to step in when they see teasing: say it calmly, stand beside, put it right, or tell a grown-up.
- Towns put bins close to where litter is dropped, and fix a broken lamp or a scribble quickly, because a place that is kept up stays kept up.
- In 2013 India passed a law that bans employing people to clean dry toilets by hand. Campaigners such as Bezwada Wilson worked for decades to bring it about.
- A family charter, signed by every member, helps a household agree on how to share a kitchen, a study corner or a lift.

## Open questions
- **How much of kindness are we born with, and how much do we learn?** Toddlers help before anyone teaches them, and children in different places share in different ways. Both facts are real, and nobody knows how to weigh them.
- **When many people watch someone in trouble, do they help or hold back?** An old test in 1968 found that people helped less when others were near. Films of real street quarrels in 2020 found that someone helped in most. Scientists are still working out why the two differ.
- **Does copying a face create the feeling, or only show it?** Face muscles copy faces within half a second. But in 2016 a large team tried to repeat a famous pen-in-the-mouth test and did not find the effect. How much a face changes a feeling is still being measured.
- **How many people does it take to change what is normal in a place?** In 2018 Damon Centola and his team found that when about a quarter of a group held to a new way of naming things, the whole group switched. Nobody knows whether real places and real habits work the same way.
- **Do other animals feel for each other, or do they only act as if they do?** Rats free trapped companions and elephants touch an upset elephant. Scientists disagree about what the helper feels, because nobody can ask them.

## Connected maths
- In made-up Hurry Test results, the three calm runs had 2, 1 and 3 misses. The three hurried runs had 5, 6 and 7. How many times as many misses did the hurried runs have? **3 times as many.**. Calm runs: 2 plus 1 plus 3 is 6. Hurried runs: 5 plus 6 plus 7 is 18. 18 divided by 6 is 3. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the made-up Hurry Test, the first calm run had a pulse of 21 beats in 15 seconds. The first hurried run had 23. How many more beats in a minute was that? **8 more beats in a minute.**. A minute is 4 times 15 seconds. 21 times 4 is 84, and 23 times 4 is 92. 92 take away 84 is 8. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L5`)
- In the made-up Smile Count, 6 of 10 people smiled back at a smile, and 2 of 10 at a plain face. What percentage of each group smiled back? How many times as likely was a smile to be returned? **60 out of 100 and 20 out of 100, so 3 times as likely.**. 6 out of 10 is 60 out of 100, which is 60 percent. 2 out of 10 is 20 percent. 60 divided by 20 is 3. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the made-up Doorway Test, the bathroom door was 64 cm wide. A common wheelchair is 65 cm wide, and guides ask for 90 cm. By how many cm is the door too narrow for the chair? By how many cm is it short of the guide? **1 cm too narrow for the chair, and 26 cm short of the guide.**. 65 take away 64 is 1. 90 take away 64 is 26. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L5`)
- A ramp needs 12 cm of length for each 1 cm of rise. A step at a library door is 15 cm high. How long must the ramp be, in metres? **1.8 metres.**. 15 times 12 is 180 centimetres. 100 centimetres make a metre, so 180 centimetres is 1.8 metres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- A 90 cm ramp rises 7.5 cm. A guide asks for 12 cm of length for each 1 cm of rise. Does the ramp meet the guide? **Yes, just. It has exactly 12 cm of length for each 1 cm of rise.**. 90 divided by 7.5 is 12. The guide asks for 12 or more, so the ramp meets it exactly. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- In Keep the Lane Clean, with 2 wrappers in the lane a passer-by adds one when the die shows 1 or 2. With 4 wrappers, it shows 1 to 4. Out of 30 passers-by, how many would add a wrapper in each lane? **About 10 in the first lane and about 20 in the second.**. With 2 wrappers, 2 of the 6 numbers add one, which is a third. A third of 30 is 10. With 4 wrappers, 4 of 6 is two thirds. Two thirds of 30 is 20. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
