# Animal babies: how they grow up, and how they are cared for
`sci.animals.animal-babies.L2`

**Summary.** A sparrow chick in a dark hole in the wall cannot see the parent coming. Yet the parent finds the chick's mouth every time. Young animals grow and change until they are adults, and many need care on the way: food, warmth and safety. Some babies can walk within an hour of birth, and some need years of care, like human babies. Your child makes a dark nest of paper chick mouths and tests which mouth colours a parent finds and feeds first.

**Intro.** *Sparrow chicks live in a dark hole in a wall. When a parent flies in with food, how does the parent find the right place to put it?* Every animal starts small and grows into an adult. A calf can stand on the day she is born. A sparrow chick hatches with no feathers and shut eyes. A human baby cannot even sit up for months. Babies like these need someone to look after them. But babies are not only waiting to be cared for. They call, beg, cry and open their mouths wide. In this unit you find out how babies help their own care. You test it with a dark nest full of paper mouths.

## Terms
- **Adult**: An adult is an animal who has finished growing up. *e.g.* A hen is an adult. Her chicks are her young. Your parents and grandparents are adults.
- **Growth**: Growth is the way a young animal gets bigger and changes as they get older. *e.g.* A kitten's growth: she gets bigger, opens her eyes, grows teeth and learns to hunt.
- **Care**: Care is what parents and helpers do to keep young animals alive and well: feeding, keeping warm, keeping safe, cleaning and teaching. *e.g.* A mother cow licks her new calf clean and feeds her milk. That is care.
- **Begging**: Begging is how young animals ask for food. They call, open their mouths wide, flap their wings or cry. *e.g.* Sparrow chicks beg by stretching up, cheeping and opening their mouths wide when a parent arrives.
- **Gape**: The gape is a chick's open mouth. Many chicks have a bright colour inside the mouth, or bright edges around it. *e.g.* A sparrow chick's gape has bright yellow edges. A crow chick's mouth is red inside.
- **Brooding**: Brooding is when a parent bird sits over eggs or small chicks to keep them warm. *e.g.* A hen brooding her chicks spreads her wings, and the chicks tuck in underneath.
- **Mammal**: A mammal is an animal with hair or fur whose mother feeds the young on milk. *e.g.* Cows, cats, bats, elephants and humans are mammals. A newborn mammal drinks milk first.

## Explanations
### Growing up means getting bigger and changing · *defined*
**Definition:** Growth is how a young animal gets bigger and changes until they become an adult.

Young animals get bigger as they grow, and they also change. A kitten is born with her eyes shut. They open after about one to two weeks, and later she grows teeth and learns to hunt. A human baby has no teeth and cannot walk or talk. Then teeth grow, the baby walks at about one year old and starts to talk.
- e.g. A puppy opens their eyes after about two weeks.
- e.g. A chick's fluff is replaced by feathers.
- e.g. A human baby grows teeth, then walks, then talks.
- e.g. A tadpole grows legs and becomes a frog.
### Born ready, or born needing care · *defined*
**Definition:** Some young animals can walk and feed soon after birth. Others are born helpless and need a lot of care.

A calf stands up within about an hour of birth and soon follows her mother. A goat kid, a duckling and a hen's chick can walk within a day. A sparrow chick hatches with almost no feathers and shut eyes. A newborn kitten cannot see or walk properly. A human baby cannot even hold up their head at first, and needs years of care. Humans have one of the longest childhoods of any animal.
- e.g. Born ready: a calf, a goat kid, a hen's chick, a duckling, a baby gecko.
- e.g. Born needing care: a kitten, a puppy, a sparrow chick, a crow chick, a baby mouse, a human baby.
### How young animals are cared for · *defined*
**Definition:** Care means feeding the young, keeping them warm, keeping them safe, keeping them clean and teaching them.

Parents feed their young. Mammal mothers make milk, and sparrow parents bring insects to the nest many times an hour. A hen keeps her chicks warm under her wings, and a crow will dive at a person who comes near the nest. Often more than one carer helps. Both sparrow parents feed the chicks, and langur aunts take turns holding a baby. In a honeybee hive, older sisters feed the young. Human babies are cared for by parents, grandparents, aunts, older brothers and sisters, and friends.
- e.g. A hen keeps her chicks warm under her wings.
- e.g. Langur aunts take turns holding a baby.
- e.g. Worker bees feed the young bees in the hive.
- e.g. A grandmother feeds a baby ragi porridge.
### Babies help their own care · *defined*
**Definition:** Young animals send signals that tell carers what they need: begging calls, open mouths, crying, holding on and smiling.

A sparrow chick stretches up, cheeps and opens a wide yellow-edged mouth when a parent lands. A hungry chick begs harder than a full one. A puppy whimpers when cold or alone, and a baby langur holds on tight to the fur of whoever carries them. A human baby cries when hungry and grips a finger that touches their palm. By about six weeks old, most human babies smile at the faces around them. Each signal helps a carer know what to do next, so care is something the baby and the carer do together.
- e.g. A chick opens a bright mouth wide.
- e.g. A kitten mews when she is away from her mother.
- e.g. A human baby cries, grips and smiles.
- e.g. A baby langur clings to fur.
### Why are chick mouths so bright? · *debated*
Many chicks have bright mouths. Sparrow chicks have yellow edges around the mouth. Crow chicks are red inside the mouth. Scientists have tested two main ideas, and the answer may be different for different birds.
- e.g. House sparrow chicks: bright yellow mouth edges, in nests inside holes.
- e.g. Crow chicks: a red mouth inside, in open nests of sticks.
- e.g. In your dark nest test, yellow paper mouths were found more often than dark ones.
- **Scientists who study nests in holes:** Bright mouths are easy to find in a dark nest. In a test in 2003, scientists painted the mouths of great tit chicks yellow or red. In dim nests, the parents fed the yellow-painted chicks more. In bright nests, colour made no difference.
- **Scientists who study barn swallows and other birds:** The colour of the mouth tells the parent something about the chick, such as how hungry or how healthy they are. In some of these birds, parents feed chicks with redder mouths more.

## Mostly agreed
- Every kind of animal has young, and the young grow and change until they are adults. Humans are one kind of animal, and grow the same way. *How we know:* People have watched the young of thousands of kinds of animals grow up, from ants to elephants. A kitten always becomes a cat, and a human baby always becomes an adult human.
- Some young animals can walk and feed within hours of birth, and others are born helpless and need care for a long time. *How we know:* Farmers see calves and goat kids stand within an hour of birth. Sparrow and crow chicks hatch with shut eyes and almost no feathers, and stay in the nest for about two weeks or more.
- Young animals beg, call or cry, and parents answer these signals with food and care. *How we know:* Scientists who watch nests see that chicks beg when a parent arrives. When chicks are hungrier, they beg harder, and parents usually bring food more often.

## How we found out
**Mary Ainsworth, a scientist from Canada and the United States, working in Uganda (1954 to 1955); Rudolph Schaffer and Peggy Emerson in Glasgow, Scotland (1964), Ainsworth watched babies in Uganda in 1954 and 1955 and wrote her book in 1967. Schaffer and Emerson published their study in 1964..** About 70 years ago, many doctors and scientists thought babies love whoever feeds them. They said a baby holds on to the mother because she gives milk. Some even told parents not to pick up a crying baby too often. In the 1950s, a scientist called Mary Ainsworth went to villages in Uganda, near Kampala. She sat in homes and watched mothers and babies for many months. She saw babies crawl after their mothers, cry when they left and smile when they came back. Babies held on most to people who came quickly when they cried. In Glasgow, in Scotland, Rudolph Schaffer and Peggy Emerson visited 60 babies again and again through their first year. Many babies held on most to someone who played with them and answered them, even when another person did more of the feeding. So babies do not only want food. They want people who answer their signals.  
*What people believed before:* That babies love whoever feeds them, and that picking up a crying baby too often spoils them. Watching real babies showed that babies hold on most to the people who answer their cries and play with them.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only mothers look after babies.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: In most mammals, like cats and cows, the mother does almost all the care. Only mother mammals make milk.  
  Against: Sparrow fathers feed the chicks about as often as the mothers do. Langur aunts hold babies. Worker bees, who are sisters, feed young bees. Human babies are cared for by many people: fathers, grandparents, aunts and older children.
- **If a person touches a baby bird, the parents will smell the person and leave the chick.** (A very common belief, told to many children; *rejected*)  
  For: It is true that people should not touch nests or chicks. Disturbing a nest can scare the parents, and the chicks can get hurt.  
  Against: Most small birds have a weak sense of smell. Scientists who must handle chicks, to weigh them, find that the parents come back and keep feeding them. Still, leave nests alone and watch from far away.
- **A parent and a baby always want the same thing.** (A common idea about families; *debated*)  
  For: Parents and babies both need the baby to grow up safe and well.  
  Against: In 1974 an American scientist, Robert Trivers, pointed out that a chick may beg for more food than the parent wants to give. The parent also needs food, and may have other young. Scientists still argue about how much parents and young pull against each other.
- **Babies love whoever feeds them.** (Many baby doctors and scientists, about 70 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: Babies do feel calm and happy after a feed, with the person who fed them.  
  Against: Mary Ainsworth in Uganda, and Rudolph Schaffer and Peggy Emerson in Scotland, watched babies closely. Babies held on most to the people who answered them: who came when they cried, talked and played with them. That was often, but not always, the person who fed them.

## Roots
We have learnt how young animals grow up, how they are cared for, and how they beg to be fed. Now we ask what the dark nest test tells us about care, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In your dim nest, yellow mouths got the most chana, and dark mouths were often missed. In bright light, every colour was found. Sparrow chicks in dark holes have bright yellow mouth edges. So a chick helps to get fed, by being easy for a parent to find.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a baby as helpless, with care flowing one way, from the parent to the baby. But babies beg, call, cry, grip and show bright mouths, and the carer answers. Care is something the baby and the carer do together.  
*Evidence:* In the Find the Hungry Mouth test, yellow paper mouths were fed far more often than black ones in dim light, but not in bright light. Scientists found the same with real great tit chicks in 2003: in dim nests, parents fed yellow-painted mouths more than red-painted ones.

- *who am i:* You were once a baby who cried, gripped and smiled. People answered you, and you grew. You helped make your own care.
- *what is life:* A young life grows because others feed and protect them, and because the young one asks for what they need.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of babies asking and carers answering: sparrows in walls, langurs on temples, people in every home.

Every animal alive today, including you, was once young. Many were fed by someone else. A sparrow chick's yellow mouth and a parent's search for it work as one: neither alone gets the food where it must go. A human baby's cry and a grandmother's arms work the same way. The many babies of the world each grow up inside a web of others who answer them.

## Story: The Yellow Mouths in the Meter Box
*A made-up story. House sparrows really do nest in holes in walls and behind meter boxes. Both parents feed the chicks, the chicks have bright yellow mouth edges, and they leave the nest at about two weeks old.*

Kavya lived on the second floor of a building in Kochi. She was seven. Her cousin Rahul, who was six, came to stay for the summer holidays.

On the stairs outside their flat there was a grey electricity box, with a gap above it in the wall. One morning Kavya saw a small brown bird fly into the gap. The bird had a long piece of dry grass in her beak.

'That is a female sparrow,' said Ammachi, Kavya's grandmother. 'She is plain brown. The male has a black patch under his beak. They are making a nest in the wall.'

Ammachi gave Kavya an old notebook. 'If you want to watch them, sit on the top step. That is far enough. If you come close, they will be scared to go in.'

So every morning, Kavya and Rahul sat on the top step with the notebook. The two sparrows carried in grass, feathers and a bit of thread. Then, for many days, they were very quiet. One of them was usually inside.

'They are sitting on eggs,' said Ammachi. 'They keep the eggs warm, so that the chicks can grow inside.'

About two weeks later, Kavya heard a sound from the wall. It was a tiny, thin cheep, cheep, cheep. Now the parents never stopped. The mother flew in with a green caterpillar. The father flew in with a moth. Out they came, and off they went again.

'How many times do they come?' asked Rahul.

Kavya looked at the clock in the hall. For ten minutes, she made a mark in the notebook every time a parent went in. When the ten minutes were up, she counted. 'Eight times!' she said. 'Four for the mother and four for the father.'

'That is a lot of caterpillars,' said Rahul.

'Chicks grow very fast,' said Ammachi. 'They need a lot of food. When they hatch, they have no feathers, and their eyes are shut. They cannot even hold up their heads for long.'

Kavya frowned. 'It is so dark in that hole. The parents come in from the bright sunlight. How do they know where to put the food?'

Ammachi smiled. 'Watch carefully. Maybe the chicks will show you.'

A few days later, Ammachi held up her phone. She zoomed the camera into the gap, from the top step. On the screen, in the dark, Kavya saw three round shapes. Each had two bright yellow corners, like little yellow lamps. Then the mother landed, and all three shapes opened wide. They were mouths! The yellow edges shone around each open mouth. The mother pushed her caterpillar into the widest one.

'The yellow is the edge of their mouths,' said Kavya. 'In the dark, the yellow is the easiest thing to see.'

'So the chicks are helping,' said Rahul. 'They are showing the mother where to put the food.'

'Yes,' said Ammachi. 'And they cheep louder when they are hungry. The parents listen.'

Kavya wrote in the notebook: 'The chicks are not just waiting. They beg. They open yellow mouths. The parents follow the yellow.'

Each day the chicks grew. Soon their cheeps were loud enough to hear from inside the flat. One morning, Kavya saw a small head poking out of the gap. The chick had short brown feathers now. The yellow corners of the mouth were still there.

Then, one day, there was a chick sitting on the stair railing, fluffing out their feathers. The chick could fly a little, but not far. The father sparrow landed next to them. The chick shivered their wings and opened their yellow mouth wide. The father fed them a moth.

'The chick has left the nest, and the father is still feeding them,' said Kavya.

'Young birds keep begging for a while after they leave the nest,' said Ammachi. 'The parents keep feeding them until the young ones learn to find food by themselves.'

That evening, Rahul's mother rang from Bengaluru. Rahul told her all about the sparrows. 'Kavya says I was a baby who begged too,' he said. 'I cried, and you came.'

His mother laughed. 'You did. You cried very loudly. And you held my finger so tight, I could not let go.'

Kavya drew one last picture in the notebook. It showed a dark hole, three yellow mouths, and a sparrow flying in. Under it she wrote: 'Who did the caring? Everyone did.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kavya notices that the chicks are not only cared for. They beg, cheep and show bright mouths, and the parents answer. She also sees that both parents feed the chicks, and that the care goes on after the chicks leave the nest.

- Ask: Why were the yellow edges of the chicks' mouths easy to see?
- Ask: How did Kavya find out how often the parents brought food?
- Ask: How did Rahul help his own mother look after him when he was a baby?

## Experiments
### e1 · Find the Hungry Mouth: which chick gets fed in a dark nest? (20 min)
- **question:** In a dark nest, which colour of chick mouth does a parent find and feed most often?
- **predict:** Guess first. In the dim nest, which colour of mouth will you feed most: yellow, orange, brown or black? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the mouths are the same size, and there are two of each colour. The grown-up mixes them up before every round, so no colour is always in the best place. Each round lasts the same 10 seconds. We do three rounds in dim light, because one round can be luck. Then one round in bright light, to see if the light makes a difference.
- **safety:** Keep the chana away from toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Fill in the results table with the number of chana on each colour. Draw a block for each chana under each colour to make a block graph.
- **results table:** Colour of mouth | Round 1 | Round 2 | Round 3 | Total in dim light | Bright light round
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw round a bottle cap 8 times on paper. 2. Colour 2 circles yellow, 2 orange, 2 brown and 2 black. Colour them fully. 3. Cut them out.  
  *Parent says:* These circles are chick mouths. Real chicks have mouths of different colours. Let's make yellow, orange, brown and black ones.  
  *Child mode:* Draw 8 circles. Colour 2 yellow, 2 orange, 2 brown, 2 black. Cut them out.
  - Note: Colour the black and brown circles fully, so no white paper shows. A sketch pen works well.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess which colour they will feed most in the dark, and why. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Soon the room will be dim, like a nest in a hole. You are the parent bird. Which mouths will you feed most? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which colour will you feed most in the dark? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Yellow, because yellow is bright.; Black, because it looks like a dark mouth.; All the same, because I will look carefully.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine, as long as there is a reason. Do not tell your child what will happen.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Line the box or kadai with the dark cloth. 2. Your child looks away. 3. Scatter the 8 mouths on the cloth, apart from each other. 4. Close the curtains and switch off the lights.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes while I make the nest. Now the room is dim, like a hole in a wall. Can you still see a little?  
  *Child mode:* Look away while the nest is made. Now the room goes dim.
  - Note: The room should be dim, not pitch dark. Your child should just about see the box. Evening with the curtains shut and a light on in another room works well. In daytime, put the box under a table and cover the table with a bedsheet.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child 10 chana. 2. Say 'Go!' and count to 10 out loud. 3. Your child puts one chana on each mouth they find. 4. Switch on the light, count the chana on each colour, write the numbers in the table, and pick up all the chana. 5. Mix the mouths while your child looks away, and make the room dim again. 6. Repeat. Do 3 rounds in all.  
  *Parent says:* Ready, parent bird? You have ten seconds to feed every mouth you can find. One chana on each mouth. Go!  
  *Child mode:* Put one chana on each mouth you see. Be quick! Then count.
  - Ask: Which mouths were easy to find? (good: The yellow ones. They stood out.; The black ones were hard. They looked like the cloth.)
  - Note: A chana put on the cloth does not count. Mix the mouths well each time, with your child looking away. Usually yellow mouths get the most chana, then orange. Black and brown are often missed, because they are hard to see against the dark cloth. Each mouth can get only one chana, so a colour can get at most 2 in a round and 6 in three rounds. If every colour is found in every round, the room is too bright: make it dimmer. If your result is different, that is a real result too: talk about why.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch the lights on and open the curtains. 2. Mix the mouths. 3. Do one more round of 10 seconds in bright light. 4. Count the chana on each colour.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's do it once in bright light. Will the colour still matter?  
  *Child mode:* Now try in bright light. Is every mouth easy to find?
  - Note: In bright light, your child will usually find almost every mouth, whatever the colour. This shows that the colour matters most when the nest is dark.

## Applications
- People who rescue baby birds feed them with soft tweezers or a small brush, aiming into the open gape just as the parent would.
- In some zoos, keepers feed chicks using a puppet shaped like the parent's head, so the chicks grow up knowing their own kind.
- Nurses who care for newborn babies watch for the baby's signals, such as turning the head and sucking a fist, to tell when the baby is hungry.
- Nature Forever Society, started by Mohammed Dilawar in Nashik, gives out nest boxes so that house sparrows have dark holes to raise their chicks in city buildings.

## Open questions
- **Why do human children take so long to grow up?** Scientists have several ideas: big brains that take years to grow, a lot to learn, and many carers who share the work. They still argue about which matters most.
- **What does a parent animal feel when the young beg?** Scientists can see how parents act, and can measure some chemicals in the body. What the feeling is like for a sparrow or a cow is still unknown.
- **Why do chick mouths have the colours they do?** In some birds, bright mouths seem to help parents find chicks in the dark. In others, the colour seems to tell the parent how hungry or healthy the chick is. Scientists are still testing both ideas.
