# How a new person begins: egg cell, sperm cell, the first week, and families of many kinds
`sci.body.how-babies-grow.L5`

**Summary.** Only one sperm cell joins the egg cell. So why do many millions set out? In this unit you will run the Two Gates Test with beans and a die to find out. You will learn where egg cells and sperm cells are made, how they meet, and what happens in the first week. You will also learn how bodies get ready at puberty, how doctors can help a baby begin, and the many ways families are made.

**Intro.** *You began as one cell, smaller than a grain of salt. That cell was made when two cells joined: one egg cell and one sperm cell. Only one sperm cell was needed. Yet many millions of sperm cells set out. Why so many? Say what you think, and say why.* Most people guess that the sperm cells are racing, and the fastest one wins. That is not what scientists find. You will first make the egg cell and the sperm cell at a size you can see. Then you will run a test with beans and a die, and your own numbers will show why so many must start. After that you will follow the two cells from where they are made to where they meet, and through the first week of a new person.

## Learning outcomes

1. I can explain what fertilisation is, and why the egg cell and the sperm cell each carry half the instructions.
2. I can describe how sperm cells reach the egg cell, and what happens in the first week, until the embryo attaches in the womb.

4. I can explain how XX or XY is settled, and why nobody chooses it.
5. I can describe how bodies change at puberty, and say what a period is.
6. I can name several ways a family is made, including IVF and adoption.

## Terms
- **Egg cell**: An egg cell is the cell from a woman's body that can join with a sperm cell to start a baby. It carries half of the instructions for a new person, and a store of food and working parts. *e.g.* A human egg cell is about one tenth of a millimetre across. That is about a third of the width of a grain of table salt.
- **Sperm cell**: A sperm cell is the cell from a man's body that can join with an egg cell. They have a small head, which carries half of the instructions, and a long tail for swimming. *e.g.* A sperm cell is about one twentieth of a millimetre long. Twenty of them, end to end, would measure one millimetre.
- **Ovary**: An ovary is one of two small organs in a woman's lower belly where egg cells are kept and ripened. Each is about the size of an almond in its shell. *e.g.* A baby girl is born with all her egg cells already in her two ovaries.
- **Testis**: A testis is one of two oval organs where a man's body makes sperm cells. The two testes hang outside the belly, in a pouch of skin called the scrotum. *e.g.* The testes stay about 2 degrees cooler than the inside of the body. Sperm cells are made best at that cooler temperature.
- **Womb (uterus)**: The womb is a hollow organ of strong muscle in a woman's lower belly, where a baby grows before birth. Doctors call it the uterus. *e.g.* An empty womb is about the size of a small pear. It stretches to hold a baby, the placenta and the water around the baby.
- **Egg tube (fallopian tube)**: An egg tube is a thin tube, about as long as your hand is wide, that leads from near an ovary to the womb. There are two. Doctors call them fallopian tubes. *e.g.* The egg cell and the sperm cell meet inside an egg tube, not inside the womb.
- **Ovulation**: Ovulation is when a ripe egg cell leaves the ovary. In most women it happens about once a month. *e.g.* After ovulation the egg cell can be fertilised for only about one day.
- **Chromosome**: A chromosome is one long thread of DNA, wound up tightly. DNA is the chemical that holds the instructions for building a body. *e.g.* Most cells in your body have 46 chromosomes. An egg cell has 23, and a sperm cell has 23.
- **Fertilisation**: Fertilisation is when a sperm cell joins with an egg cell, and their two half-sets of chromosomes come together in one cell. *e.g.* In humans, fertilisation happens in an egg tube, usually within a day of ovulation.
- **Zygote**: A zygote is the single cell made by fertilisation. It has a full set of 46 chromosomes, 23 from the egg cell and 23 from the sperm cell. *e.g.* You were a zygote for about one day. Then you divided into two cells.
- **Embryo**: An embryo is a new living being in the earliest stages of growing. In humans the name is used from the first cell divisions to the end of week 8. *e.g.* On about day 5, a human embryo is a hollow ball of about 100 to 200 cells.
- **Implantation**: Implantation is when the embryo attaches to the soft lining of the womb and begins to grow into it. *e.g.* Implantation usually happens 8 to 10 days after ovulation. Until then the embryo is floating free.
- **Puberty**: Puberty is the time when a child's body slowly changes into an adult's body, and becomes able to make a baby one day. *e.g.* Puberty usually begins between 8 and 13 in girls and between 9 and 14 in boys. It takes several years.
- **Hormone**: A hormone is a chemical message that one part of the body makes and the blood carries to other parts. *e.g.* At puberty, a small gland under the brain sends hormones to the ovaries or the testes, and they start their adult work.
- **Period (menstruation)**: A period is a few days each month when the lining of the womb comes away and leaves through the vagina as blood. It is a healthy part of the monthly cycle. *e.g.* Each month the womb builds a soft lining. If no embryo arrives, the lining is not needed, and the body lets it go.
- **IVF**: IVF is a way doctors help a baby begin. The egg cell and the sperm cells are brought together in a dish, and the embryo is then placed in the womb. The letters stand for in vitro fertilisation, which means fertilisation in glass. *e.g.* The first baby born after IVF was Louise Brown, in England in 1978. The second was Durga, in Kolkata, 70 days later.
- **Adoption**: Adoption is when grown-ups become the parents of a child who was born to someone else. It is done by law, and it is for life. *e.g.* An adopted child began from an egg cell and a sperm cell like everyone else. Their parents are the people who raise them.
- **Mitochondria**: Mitochondria are tiny parts inside a cell that release energy from food. They carry a small piece of DNA of their own. *e.g.* All your mitochondria came from the egg cell. So they came from your mother, and from her mother before her.

## Explanations
### Two kinds of cell can start a new person: the egg cell and the sperm cell · *defined*
**Definition:** An egg cell and a sperm cell are sex cells. Each carries half of the instructions for a person. When one of each joins, a new person can begin.

Almost every cell in your body can only make more cells of the same kind. A skin cell makes skin cells. Two kinds of cell are different. An egg cell is large for a cell, about a tenth of a millimetre across. You could just see one as a speck. A sperm cell is far smaller, with a tail for swimming. Why are they so different? They have different work. The egg cell stays in place, and brings the food and the working parts that the new cell needs for the first days. The sperm cell has to travel, so they carry almost nothing but the instructions, a tail, and mitochondria to power the tail. Neither cell can grow into a baby alone. In humans and other mammals, an embryo needs instructions from both. Sex cells are alive. They sense chemicals around them and respond.
- e.g. If an egg cell were as big as a steel dinner plate, a sperm cell would be a grain of rice with a tail about as long as your finger.
- e.g. A hen's egg yolk also begins as one egg cell. It is huge because the chick grows outside the mother, so all the food must be packed in first.
- e.g. A human embryo is fed through the placenta. So a human egg cell needs to carry food for only the first week or so.
### Half and half: why each sex cell carries 23 chromosomes · *defined*
**Definition:** Most body cells have 46 chromosomes, in 23 pairs. An egg cell or a sperm cell has only 23, one from each pair.

Suppose egg cells and sperm cells had 46 chromosomes each. Then the first cell of a baby would have 92. That baby's own children would have 184. The number would double in every generation, and cells cannot work like that. So when a body makes sex cells, the cells divide in a special way that halves the number. This kind of division is called meiosis. At fertilisation, 23 and 23 make 46 again. So half of your instructions came from a woman, in her egg cell, and half from a man, in his sperm cell. Which one of each pair goes into a sex cell is settled by chance, and the pairs also swap pieces first. This is why no two egg cells are alike, and no two sperm cells. It is also why sisters and brothers are alike in some ways and different in others.
- e.g. You have two copies of chromosome 1. One came from the egg cell and one from the sperm cell.
- e.g. Two sisters with the same parents each got a different half from each parent.
- e.g. Identical twins are the exception. They began as one zygote, which split into two embryos.
### Where egg cells are made, and why they are older than they look · *defined*
**Definition:** Egg cells are kept in the two ovaries. They begin to form before a girl is born, and one usually ripens and leaves an ovary each month from puberty onwards.

Here is a fact that surprises most adults. A girl's egg cells start forming while she is still inside her own mother. At about 20 weeks of pregnancy, her tiny ovaries hold 6 to 7 million of them. Most die back naturally. At birth 1 to 2 million are left, and at puberty about 300,000 to 400,000. Only about 400 to 500 are ever released. So think about the egg cell that became you. They were already in your mother's ovary on the day your mother was born. They waited there for all the years until you began. Why make millions and release hundreds? Nobody is sure. One idea is that the body keeps choosing among them, and only some pass. From the middle of life, at about 45 to 55, the ovaries stop releasing egg cells. This is called menopause.
- e.g. When your grandmother was pregnant with your mother, the egg cell that became you was already forming inside that unborn baby girl.
- e.g. A woman who is 30 releases egg cells that have been waiting for a little over 30 years.
- e.g. In most months only one egg cell is released. When two are released and both are fertilised, twins who are not identical can begin.
- **Most scientists who study ovaries:** A woman is born with all the egg cells she will ever have, and no new ones are made later.
- **Jonathan Tilly and co-workers, from 2004:** They reported cells in adult ovaries of mice that may be able to make new egg cells. Other laboratories have not been able to show that this happens in a woman's body. The question is still argued over.
### Where sperm cells are made · *defined*
**Definition:** Sperm cells are made in the two testes, from puberty onwards. New ones are made every day, in millions.

This is the opposite of the egg cell's story. A boy's testes make no sperm cells until puberty. After that they make millions every day, usually for the rest of his life. Each sperm cell takes about ten weeks to make. Why do the testes hang outside the belly, where they are easier to hurt? Sperm cells are made best about 2 degrees cooler than the inside of the body. Outside, the testes stay cooler. The finished sperm cells are stored in coiled tubes beside each testis. When they leave the body, glands add liquid that feeds and protects them. This liquid, with the sperm cells swimming in it, is called semen. It leaves through a tube in the penis. Urine uses the same tube, but never at the same moment.
- e.g. A sperm cell that leaves the body today began to be made about ten weeks ago.
- e.g. A long, very hot bath can slow the making of sperm cells for a while, because the testes get too warm.
- e.g. Elephants and whales keep their testes inside the body. Scientists are still working out how their sperm cells cope with the warmth.
### How the sperm cells reach the egg cell · *defined*
**Definition:** Sperm cells have to get from the man's body into the woman's body, and then swim to the egg tube. This can happen when two adults have sex, or with a doctor's help.

This card says in plain words how sperm cells reach the egg cell. A parent may want to read it first. When a grown-up woman and a grown-up man want to make a baby, they lie very close together. The man's penis goes into the woman's vagina, and semen carrying sperm cells leaves his body there. This is called sexual intercourse, or sex. It is something for adults, and only when both of them want it. The vagina is the passage that leads from the outside of a woman's body to the womb. From there the sperm cells have a long way to go. They pass through the narrow neck of the womb, across the womb, and into the egg tubes. Tens of millions or more set out. By one estimate, about 5,000 reach the openings of the egg tubes, about 1,000 enter a tube, and about 200 get near the egg cell. The woman's body does much of the sorting. Thick liquid at the neck of the womb lets through only sperm cells that swim well. Squeezes of the womb's muscle carry them along. Sperm cells can stay alive in the egg tube for a few days, waiting. Sometimes sperm cells and egg cell cannot meet this way. Then doctors can help. They can place sperm cells in the womb, or bring the cells together in a dish, which is IVF.
- e.g. Half the sperm cells that get across the womb enter the egg tube on the side where no egg cell was released that month.
- e.g. The woman's body is not a passive road. Its muscle squeezes do much of the carrying, far faster than a sperm cell can swim.
- e.g. Some couples have sex and no baby begins for many months, or ever. This is common, and it is nobody's fault.
### Fertilisation: what happens when the two cells meet · *defined*
**Definition:** Fertilisation is when one sperm cell joins the egg cell and the two half-sets of chromosomes come together. The new single cell is called a zygote.

The egg cell is not just waiting. The egg cell and the smaller cells nearby release chemicals, and sperm cells swim towards them. The egg cell is wrapped in a clear coat. Many sperm cells reach the coat, and each one tries to work a way through. When the first one gets through and touches the egg cell, the two cells join into one. At once the egg cell changes the coat so that no other sperm cell can enter. Why does that matter? A second sperm cell would bring a third half-set of chromosomes, and the embryo could not grow properly. At this moment the egg cell also releases a burst of zinc. In 2016 scientists in the United States filmed this burst in human egg cells, using a dye that glows when it meets zinc. They named it the zinc spark. The sperm cell's tail and mitochondria are taken apart inside the egg cell. About a day later, the two half-sets of chromosomes are together, and the zygote divides into two cells.
- e.g. All of the zygote's working parts, and all of the mitochondria that last, came from the egg cell. The sperm cell brought half of the instructions.
- e.g. The zinc spark is a real flash of zinc. It is not light that you could see with your eyes. It shows up only with a special dye and a microscope.
- e.g. A pea flower does the same thing. A sperm cell from a pollen grain joins an egg cell, and that first cell grows into the baby plant inside a pea.
### The first week: from one cell to an embryo attached in the womb · *defined*
**Definition:** In the first week the embryo divides again and again while moving along the egg tube to the womb. Then the embryo attaches to the womb's lining. This is called implantation.

The zygote cannot swim. Tiny waving hairs on the wall of the egg tube, and gentle squeezes of the tube, carry the embryo towards the womb. On the way, one cell becomes 2, then 4, then 8, then 16. The embryo does not get bigger in these days, because the cells get smaller each time. By about day 5 the embryo is a hollow ball of 100 to 200 cells, and has reached the womb. The ball slips out of the old egg coat. Then the embryo sticks to the soft lining of the womb and grows into it. In most pregnancies this happens 8 to 10 days after ovulation. Only now does the mother's body get the chemical message that an embryo is there. Pregnancy tests look for that chemical. Many embryos never get this far. One study in 2020 estimated that 40 to 50 in every 100 fertilised egg cells are lost before they attach. Nobody can watch this happen inside a body, so the number is an estimate. Often the embryo had the wrong number of chromosomes. The woman usually never knows.
- e.g. On day 3 you were about 8 cells, and still no bigger than the egg cell you began as.
- e.g. Until about day 8 you were floating free. You were not yet attached to your mother.
- e.g. Doctors count a pregnancy from the first day of the mother's last period. That is about two weeks before fertilisation. So '40 weeks pregnant' means the baby has been growing for about 38 weeks.
### XX or XY: how it is settled · *defined*
**Definition:** One pair of chromosomes comes in two kinds, called X and Y. Most girls have two X chromosomes. Most boys have one X and one Y. Which it will be is settled by the sperm cell that joins the egg cell.

Every egg cell carries an X. Half of a man's sperm cells carry an X, and half carry a Y. If a sperm cell with an X joins the egg cell, the zygote is XX. If a sperm cell with a Y joins, the zygote is XY. Nobody chooses which sperm cell arrives. So nobody decides whether a baby is a girl or a boy. For the first weeks, XX and XY embryos look the same. At about week 6, a gene on the Y chromosome called SRY switches on. It was found in 1990. With SRY, the embryo's two small sex organs start to become testes. Without it, they become ovaries. In India some mothers have been blamed for having daughters. The science shows that this blame is wrong twice. The egg cell always brings an X. And a daughter is not a fault. Because some families wanted only sons, a law of 1994 forbids doctors in India from telling parents the sex of a baby before birth. Bodies are also more varied than two neat boxes. A small number of people have other sets, such as one X alone, or XXY. In some people the body develops in its own way between the usual patterns. And who a person knows themself to be is not read from chromosomes.
- e.g. A family with three daughters did not 'fail to have a son'. Three times in a row, a sperm cell with an X arrived first. Chance does that often.
- e.g. Birds do it the other way round. In a hen, the egg cell carries one of two kinds of chromosome, so the mother's cell settles the sex of the chick.
- e.g. In sea turtles there is no sex chromosome at all. Warmer nests in the sand hatch mostly females, and cooler nests hatch mostly males.
### Puberty: the body gets ready, years before the person is ready · *defined*
**Definition:** Puberty is the set of changes, over several years, that turn a child's body into an adult's body. The ovaries begin to release egg cells, or the testes begin to make sperm cells.

Puberty starts with a hormone message from a small gland under the brain. In girls it usually starts between 8 and 13. In boys it usually starts between 9 and 14. Starting early or late within that range is normal. Everyone grows faster for a while, grows hair under the arms and around the sex organs, and sweats more. In girls, breasts grow, hips widen, and periods begin. Here is what a period is. About once a month an egg cell ripens, and the womb builds a soft, thick lining with many blood vessels, ready for an embryo. If no embryo arrives, the lining is not needed. It comes away and leaves through the vagina as blood over about 3 to 7 days. Then the womb builds a new one. A period is not an illness, and the blood is not dirty. In boys, the testes and penis grow, the voice deepens, and the testes begin making sperm cells. Sometimes semen comes out during sleep. That is normal too. A body that can make a baby belongs to a person who is still a child. Being ready to raise a child comes many years later, and it is an adult's choice.
- e.g. A girl who has her first period at 10 and a girl who has hers at 14 are both healthy.
- e.g. In some homes a girl having her period is kept out of the kitchen or the puja room. Nothing in the science supports this. Period blood is the same blood and lining that would have fed an embryo.
- e.g. A boy's voice may crack for some months. His voice box is growing, and the cords in it are getting longer.
### When doctors help: IVF, and the people who were not believed · *defined*
**Definition:** In IVF, egg cells are taken from an ovary and joined with sperm cells in a dish. After a few days an embryo is placed in the womb, where the embryo may implant and grow as any other embryo does.

Some people cannot start a baby by having sex. The egg tubes may be blocked, there may be too few sperm cells, or the reason may be unknown. In 1944 Miriam Menkin, working in Boston with the doctor John Rock, was the first to fertilise a human egg cell in a dish. It had taken her six years of trying. In England, the scientist Robert Edwards, the nurse and embryologist Jean Purdy and the doctor Patrick Steptoe worked for ten more years. Their first IVF baby, Louise Brown, was born on 25 July 1978. Jean Purdy was the first person to see that embryo's cells dividing. Her part was left off plaques and reports for about thirty years. Just 70 days later, on 3 October 1978, a girl known as Durga was born in Kolkata. Dr Subhash Mukhopadhyay had done the work with Sunit Mukherji and Dr Saroj Kanti Bhattacharya, using simple equipment. He was not believed. A government committee dismissed his claim. He died in 1981 without credit. In 1997 another scientist, T. C. Anand Kumar, read his notes and concluded that he had truly done it. He is now credited as the maker of India's first IVF baby. Since then, millions of babies around the world have begun this way. An IVF baby grows in the womb exactly as other babies do.
- e.g. In IVF the zygote's first days, from one cell to a hollow ball, happen in a warm dish and not in the egg tube.
- e.g. Sometimes the egg cell or the sperm cells are given by a donor, a person who helps another family to have a child.
- e.g. Sometimes another woman carries the baby in her womb for the parents. She is called a surrogate.
### Families of many kinds · *defined*
**Definition:** Every person begins from one egg cell and one sperm cell. A family is the people who love and raise a child. These are two separate facts.

The egg cell and sperm cell tell you how a body began. They do not tell you who the family is. Some children are raised by the woman and the man whose cells they came from. Some are raised by one of them. Some are adopted, and their parents chose them by law, for life. Some live with a grandmother, an aunt and uncle, a step-parent, two mothers or two fathers, or in a big joint family where many adults share the raising. Some began with the help of IVF, a donor or a surrogate. All of these children began from an egg cell and a sperm cell, and all of these are real families. Humans have always raised children together. A human child needs care for far longer than a calf or a kitten does. One parent alone can rarely give all of it, so grandmothers, older children, neighbours and friends have always helped. Other animals raise young in many ways too. Elephant calves are looked after by aunts and older sisters. In India, adoption is arranged through a government body called the Central Adoption Resource Authority.
- e.g. A girl adopted as a baby has birth parents, whose cells she came from, and parents, who raise her. Both are part of her story.
- e.g. A boy raised by his grandmother in Madurai while his parents work in another city has a family of three generations.
- e.g. In a herd of elephants in Nagarhole, a calf is guarded and guided by several females, not only the mother.
### Life is handed on: every cell comes from a living cell · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell is only ever made by a living cell dividing, or by two living cells joining. No cell in any body has been seen to form from non-living material.

The egg cell was alive. The sperm cell was alive. When they joined, the zygote was alive. So at no point in your beginning was there something dead that came to life. Follow it backwards. Your mother's egg cell came from cells in her body, which came from her own zygote, which came from her mother's egg cell and her father's sperm cell. In 1852 the German scientist Robert Remak showed that new cells come only from the division of existing cells. Rudolf Virchow made the idea famous in 1855, at first without giving Remak credit. If every cell comes from a cell, the line behind you goes back without a break, through people, through earlier animals, to the first cells on Earth. Those lived more than three and a half billion years ago. How those very first cells arose from chemicals that were not cells is not known. One more thread runs through this. Your mitochondria all came from the egg cell. The father's are taken apart in the early embryo. So your mitochondria came down a line of mothers only.
- e.g. Your mitochondria are copies of your mother's, and hers are copies of her mother's. A father cannot pass his on.
- e.g. A cutting from a hibiscus plant grows into a new plant. No sex cells are involved, but the living cells are still handed on.
- e.g. A bacterium has no parents in our sense. One cell divides into two, and both are the old cell continuing.

## Mostly agreed
- A new human begins when one sperm cell joins one egg cell, and their chromosomes come together in one cell. *How we know:* In 1875 and 1876 Oscar Hertwig watched this in sea urchin eggs, which are clear enough to see into. Since 1944 it has been watched in human egg cells in a dish, and IVF depends on it.
- Egg cells and sperm cells each carry 23 chromosomes, and other body cells carry 46. *How we know:* Chromosomes can be stained and counted under a microscope. The count of 46 for human body cells was settled in 1956 by Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan.
- The sex chromosomes of the zygote are settled by whether the sperm cell carries an X or a Y. *How we know:* Nettie Stevens found the two kinds of sperm cell in mealworm beetles in 1905. The gene SRY on the human Y chromosome was found in 1990.
- A girl's egg cells begin forming before she is born, and their number falls from then on. *How we know:* Counts of egg cells in ovaries at different ages show 6 to 7 million at about 20 weeks of pregnancy, 1 to 2 million at birth, and far fewer by puberty.
- The embryo attaches to the womb about 8 to 10 days after ovulation in most pregnancies. *How we know:* In a study published in 1999, Allen Wilcox and co-workers tested the urine of women who were trying for a baby, every day, for the chemical an attached embryo makes.
- Mitochondria are passed on by the mother. *How we know:* The small DNA in mitochondria matches between mothers and their children, and not between fathers and their children. The sperm cell's mitochondria are destroyed in the early embryo.

## How we found out
**Lazzaro Spallanzani, Karl Ernst von Baer and Oscar Hertwig. Hermann Fol saw a sperm cell enter an egg cell at about the same time as Hertwig., Spallanzani's frog tests were done in the 1760s and later. Von Baer reported the mammal egg cell in 1827. Hertwig's work was done in 1875 and published in 1876..** For centuries many scholars thought only one parent gave the baby. Some said a tiny person lay ready in the egg. Others said it lay in the semen, and the mother was only the soil. In 1677 Antonie van Leeuwenhoek saw sperm cells with his microscope. Most people decided they were tiny worms living in the liquid. In the 1760s the Italian priest and scientist Lazzaro Spallanzani tested it. He dressed male frogs in tight little trousers of waxed silk. The eggs of their mates did not grow. When he painted those eggs with the liquid caught in the trousers, tadpoles grew. So the liquid had to touch the egg. He also found that filtered liquid did nothing. Even so, he went on thinking the 'worms' did not matter. In 1827 Karl Ernst von Baer found the egg cell of a mammal, in a dog. In 1875 Oscar Hertwig watched sea urchin eggs, which are clear like glass. He saw one sperm cell enter, and saw the nucleus inside join the egg's nucleus. Both parents give half.  
*What people believed before:* That only one parent gives the baby, either as a ready-made tiny person in the egg or as a seed in the semen, and that sperm cells are only worms living in the liquid. Spallanzani's own filter test pointed against this, and Hertwig then saw one sperm cell's nucleus join the egg cell's nucleus.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The fastest sperm cell wins the race, and the egg cell just waits.** (Many school books and films of the twentieth century; *rejected*)  
  For: Sperm cells do swim, and only one joins the egg cell. So it looks like a race with one winner.  
  Against: Squeezes of the womb carry sperm cells much of the way. The woman's body filters them and stores them. The egg cell releases chemicals that draw sperm cells in, and the egg cell's coat lets one through and then shuts. In 1991 the anthropologist Emily Martin showed how textbooks had told the story as a brave male and a passive female, against the evidence.
- **The mother decides whether the baby is a girl or a boy.** (A belief in many countries, including India, for centuries; *rejected*)  
  For: The baby grows in the mother, so people assumed everything about the baby came from her body, her food or her fate.  
  Against: Every egg cell carries an X. The sperm cell carries an X or a Y, and nobody controls which one arrives. Nettie Stevens found the two kinds of sperm cell in 1905.
- **Dr Subhash Mukhopadhyay's claim to have made India's first IVF baby in 1978 was false.** (A committee set up by the West Bengal government in 1978; *rejected*)  
  For: He worked with simple equipment, and he had not yet published full details in a journal.  
  Against: In 1997 T. C. Anand Kumar studied his handwritten notes and concluded that the work was real. The girl, Kanupriya Agarwal, was born on 3 October 1978. India's medical research council now credits him.
- **The egg cell takes part in choosing which sperm cell joins.** (John Fitzpatrick and co-workers, in a study published in 2020; *debated*)  
  For: In the laboratory, the liquid around one woman's egg cells drew in more sperm cells from some men than from others. Which men it drew in differed from woman to woman.  
  Against: This was measured in dishes, not inside a body, and with a small number of people. It shows attraction, but not yet that it changes which sperm cell fertilises the egg cell.
- **A father can sometimes pass on his mitochondria.** (Shiyu Luo and co-workers, in a study published in 2018; *debated*)  
  For: They reported three families in which some people seemed to carry mitochondrial DNA from both parents.  
  Against: Other scientists argue that the result could come from copies of mitochondrial DNA that sit in the ordinary chromosomes. In almost all people studied, mitochondria come only from the mother.

## Roots
We have learned how an egg cell and a sperm cell join to begin a new person. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Two Gates Test you asked which sperm cell arrives. But that sperm cell and the egg cell were both alive before they met. The egg cell was alive in your mother before she was born. So no life started when you started. A new person began, and life carried on.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a baby is 'the start of a new life', as if something that was not alive got switched on. But nothing was switched on. Two living cells became one living cell. What is new is the person, and the word 'beginning' hides how much was already there.  
*Evidence:* The egg cell that became you formed in your mother's ovary before her birth. Remak showed in 1852 that cells come only from living cells. Nobody has seen a cell in a body form from non-living material. Your mitochondria came unbroken down a line of mothers.

- *who am i:* You are a new person. But the living cells you are made of have never once been dead. They were handed to you, and they were handed to the people before you.
- *what is life:* Life in a body is not made fresh each time. Living cells pass it on by dividing and joining. Each of those cells senses the world and responds to it.
- *what is this world:* The world has been making living bodies without a break for more than three and a half billion years. You are where that long making has reached today.

Before the first cells there was a world with no cells in it. How the first living cells arose is not known. Since then, one unbroken stream of living cells has divided, joined and divided again. Out of that one stream came every bacterium, banyan, crow and person. You look like a separate thing with a start date. Seen from the cells, you are the same stream, in a new and never-repeated shape. The sperm cell and the egg cell each sensed the other and moved to join. That reaching towards another is as old as cells.

- Science (settled): Every cell comes from a living cell. No cell in a body has been seen to form from non-living material.

## Story: The Egg Chaser
*This story is about a real person. Miriam Menkin worked with Dr John Rock at the Free Hospital for Women in Boston from 1938. Her weekly routine, the six years without success, the night awake with her baby daughter, the three things she did differently, the two cells she saw, the lost first egg cell, the report in the journal Science in 1944 and the move to North Carolina are all recorded. Her thoughts, the words people say and the small details of the room are imagined. Nobody can be sure which of her three changes mattered most.*

Every Tuesday for six years, Miriam Menkin went looking for something almost too small to see.

She worked in the basement of a hospital in Boston. On Tuesdays the surgeons upstairs sent down a small piece of ovary from a woman who had needed an operation. Miriam bent over it with a fine glass tube and hunted, slowly, for an egg cell. An egg cell is a speck. Find one, and you have found a grain of dust that matters.

She had a name for herself. She was the egg chaser.

Miriam had wanted to be a doctor, but in those years medical schools took very few women, and none took her. She had done all the work for a doctor's degree in science, twice, and could not pay the fee to receive it. So she was called a technician. She knew more about egg cells than almost anyone alive.

Dr Rock, upstairs, had patients who longed for a child and could not have one. In many of them the egg tubes were blocked, so the egg cell and the sperm cell could never meet. He had asked Miriam a question. Could the two cells be made to meet outside the body, in a glass dish?

Nobody had ever done it with human cells. Nobody knew if it could be done.

So Miriam had her week. On Tuesday she found an egg cell and kept the dish warm. On Wednesday she added the sperm cells. On Thursday, she said, she prayed. On Friday she looked.

And every Friday for six years, she saw one cell. One round, quiet cell, exactly as on Wednesday. Nothing had happened.

She changed the liquid in the dish. Nothing. She changed the warmth. Nothing. She kept careful notes on every egg cell, and there were a great many of them now.

The method itself she did not change, because it was the proper one. First she washed the sperm cells three times, so that they were clean. Then she put them with the egg cell for half an hour. Then she moved the egg cell to a fresh dish. Clean, quick and neat.

One week in early February 1944, Miriam did not sleep. Her baby daughter was awake and crying, hour after hour, and Miriam walked the floor with her until the windows turned grey.

That Wednesday she came to the basement with her eyes aching. She washed the sperm cells once, and was too tired to do it twice more. The sample she used was thicker than usual, crowded with sperm cells. She put them in the dish with Tuesday's egg cell and sat down at the microscope to watch.

It was something to see, even after six years. The sperm cells swarmed around the egg cell. They pushed at its clear coat from every side, so many of them that the egg cell slowly turned in the liquid.

Miriam watched, and her eyes grew heavy. When she next looked at the clock, a whole hour had gone by. Half an hour was the rule. She had left them together twice as long.

She moved the egg cell to a fresh dish, went home and slept.

On Friday morning she came down the stairs expecting what she always saw. She put the dish under the microscope and turned the wheel until the picture was sharp.

There were two cells.

She looked away and looked again. Two round cells lay side by side inside the one clear coat. The egg cell had been fertilised, and the new cell had divided. For the first time, the first day of a human being had happened in a dish, where a person could see it.

She was afraid to take her eyes off it. She sent for Dr Rock, and soon the small room was full of people taking turns at the eyepiece.

Why that week, after six years? Miriam went back through what she had done. One wash, not three. More sperm cells, crowded close. One hour, not half. She had not been careful that day. She had only been tired. But being tired had given the two cells what the proper method never had. It had given them more of each other, and more time.

A sperm cell does not slip into an egg cell in a moment. The coat is thick. Many sperm cells must work at the coat, and it takes them a long while before one gets through. For six years Miriam had been pulling the cells apart just as the work was beginning.

In the bustle, nobody photographed those first two cells, and they were lost. So Miriam did it again, on purpose this time, with one wash, a crowded sample and a full hour. Three more egg cells were fertilised. She photographed every one.

That August the report appeared in the journal Science, under two names, John Rock and Miriam Menkin.

She did not get to go on. Her husband lost his post, and the family had to move far south to North Carolina, where there was no laboratory for her and no egg cells to chase. When she came back to Boston years later, the work had moved on to other things.

Thirty-four years after that Friday morning, in 1978, a baby girl was born in England who had begun in a glass dish. Then one was born in Kolkata. Then thousands, and then millions.

Each of them began the way Miriam first saw it. Two cells that were given enough time.

**For the parent:** Fertilisation is not instant. Many sperm cells have to work at the egg cell's coat for a long time before one gets through. When the two kinds of cell were left together for longer, and more closely, they joined.

- Ask: For six years the careful method failed, and the tired day worked. What does that tell you about following a method?
- Ask: Miriam changed three things at once that week. How could she find out which change mattered?
- Ask: Why does it help a body to release so many sperm cells near one egg cell?
- Ask: Miriam did the work with her own hands, and was called a technician. Who should be named when a discovery is made?
- Ask: The first two cells were lost before anyone took a photograph. Why did she need to do it again?

### e1 · The Two Gates Test: if one sperm cell is enough, why do millions start? (32 min)

## Worksheets
### Two cells, one beginning (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which two cells join to begin a new person?  
   Options: An egg cell and a sperm cell / Two egg cells / A skin cell and a blood cell  
   **Answer:** An egg cell and a sperm cell. *Why:* One egg cell and one sperm cell join. Each brings half of the instructions.
2. (fill) The joining of a sperm cell and an egg cell is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['fertilisation', 'fertilization']. *Why:* At fertilisation the two half-sets of chromosomes come together in one cell.
3. (mcq) How many chromosomes does a human egg cell carry?  
   Options: 23 / 46 / 92  
   **Answer:** 23. *Why:* An egg cell carries 23 and a sperm cell carries 23. Together they make the usual 46.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a baby girl is born with her egg cells already in her ovaries.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Egg cells begin forming before a girl is born. About 1 to 2 million are there at birth.
5. (mcq) Where are sperm cells made?  
   Options: In the testes / In the bladder / In the womb  
   **Answer:** In the testes. *Why:* The two testes make sperm cells from puberty onwards. The bladder only stores urine.
6. (match) Match each part of the body to its work.  
   **Answer:** Ovary → keeps and ripens egg cells; Testis → makes sperm cells; Egg tube → where the two cells meet; Womb → where the baby grows. *Why:* Egg cells ripen in an ovary and sperm cells are made in a testis. The two meet in an egg tube, and the embryo grows in the womb.
7. (order) Put these in order, for the first week of a new person.  
   **Answer:** ['Ovulation', 'Fertilisation', 'Two cells', 'A hollow ball of cells', 'Implantation']. *Why:* The egg cell leaves the ovary and is fertilised in the tube. The embryo divides on the way to the womb, and then attaches.
8. (mcq) In the Two Gates Test, which starting group most often had a bean arrive?  
   Options: 6 beans / 18 beans / 72 beans  
   **Answer:** 72 beans. *Why:* Each bean has the same small chance. With 72 beans there are many more tries, so one usually gets through.
9. (write) In the Two Gates Test, was the bean that arrived a special bean? Explain your answer.  
   **Answer:** No. Every bean had the same chance of 1 in 36. The die decided which one arrived, so it was chance.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the rule was the same for every bean. Which one arrived was not about being better.
10. (truefalse) True or false: the mother's egg cell decides whether a baby is a girl or a boy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every egg cell carries an X. The sperm cell brings an X or a Y, and nobody controls which one arrives.
11. (fill) A sperm cell carries either an X chromosome or a ____ chromosome.  
   **Answer:** ['Y']. *Why:* Half of a man's sperm cells carry an X and half carry a Y.
12. (sort) Sort each change at puberty: does it happen to girls, to boys, or to everyone?  
   **Answer:** periods begin → Girls; testes start making sperm cells → Boys; growing faster → Everyone; hair grows under the arms → Everyone; breasts grow → Girls; the voice deepens a lot → Boys. *Why:* Hormones start all of these changes. Some are shared, and some depend on whether the body has ovaries or testes.
13. (mcq) What is a period?  
   Options: The womb's unused lining leaving the body / Blood from an injury / A sign of illness  
   **Answer:** The womb's unused lining leaving the body. *Why:* Each month the womb builds a lining for an embryo. If none arrives, the lining comes away. Nothing is injured.
14. (draw) Draw an egg cell and a sperm cell side by side. Label what each one brings.  
   **Answer:** A large round egg cell labelled with half the instructions, food and mitochondria. A tiny sperm cell with a tail, labelled with half the instructions.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the difference in size, and that both bring half of the instructions.
15. (write) Name three different ways a family can be made.  
   **Answer:** Any three, for example: parents raising the child born to them, adoption, a single parent, grandparents raising a child, a step-family, or help from IVF.. *Why:* Every child begins from an egg cell and a sperm cell. A family is the people who love and raise the child.
### The journey, the cycle and chance (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why must sex cells carry half the usual number of chromosomes?  
   Options: So the number is 46 again after they join / Because sex cells are small / So that they can swim faster  
   **Answer:** So the number is 46 again after they join. *Why:* If each carried 46, the zygote would have 92, and the number would double in every generation.
2. (truefalse) True or false: the embryo gets bigger with every division in the first three days.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The cells get smaller each time they divide, so the embryo stays about the size of the egg cell.

   **Answer:** It changes to keep other sperm cells out. *Why:* A second sperm cell would bring too many chromosomes. The coat changes within moments to block it.
4. (fill) The embryo attaching to the lining of the womb is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['implantation']. *Why:* Implantation usually happens 8 to 10 days after ovulation.
5. (order) Put the journey of a sperm cell in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Made in a testis', "Leaves the man's body in semen", 'Passes the neck of the womb', 'Crosses the womb', 'Enters an egg tube']. *Why:* Only a tiny share of the sperm cells that start get as far as the egg tube.
6. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Zygote → the first single cell of a new person; Embryo → the early stages, up to week 8; Ovulation → an egg cell leaves the ovary; Semen → the liquid that carries sperm cells. *Why:* Each word names one step or one part of how a new person begins.

   **Answer:** mitochondria → Egg cell only; chromosomes → Both cells; food for the first days → Egg cell only; instructions for building a body → Both cells. *Why:* Both cells bring half of the chromosomes. The egg cell also brings the food store and the mitochondria that last.
8. (mcq) A family has four daughters. What is the chance that the next baby is a girl?  
   Options: About one in two / Very small / Certain  
   **Answer:** About one in two. *Why:* Each time, a sperm cell with an X or with a Y arrives by chance. Earlier babies do not change the next one.
9. (write) Explain why nobody chooses whether a baby is XX or XY.  
   **Answer:** Every egg cell carries an X. Half the sperm cells carry an X and half carry a Y. Which sperm cell joins the egg cell is chance, so nobody controls it.. *Why:* A strong answer names both cells and says that which sperm cell arrives is chance.
10. (truefalse) True or false: a baby who began by IVF grows in a glass dish for nine months.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Only the first few days happen in a dish. The embryo is then placed in the womb and grows there.

   **Answer:** If you leave out answers you did not expect, the results only show what you already believed. A fair survey keeps every answer.. *Why:* A strong answer says that choosing which answers to keep would change the result.

The facts do not settle it, because the answer depends on what we mean by 'a person'.

   **Answer:** Sperm cells are made best slightly cooler. *Why:* Sperm cells are made best about 2 degrees cooler than the inside of the body.

### Testing claims about how a baby begins (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Use the Two Gates Test to explain why millions of sperm cells start.  
   **Answer:** In the test each bean had a small chance, and a bean usually arrived only when many started. In a body the chance for each sperm cell is far smaller, so far more must start for a few to arrive.. *Why:* A strong answer links a small chance for each one to the need for a large number of starters.
2. (mcq) Which of these does the Two Gates Test leave out?  
   Options: Real sperm cells sense and respond / Chance plays a part / Many must start  
   **Answer:** Real sperm cells sense and respond. *Why:* Beans and a die show the part that chance plays. They cannot show living cells that swim and sense chemicals.
3. (truefalse) True or false: scientists have watched and counted embryos being lost inside a woman's body.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Nobody can see this happen inside a body. The figure of 40 to 50 in 100 is an estimate worked out from other measurements.
4. (perspective) Here are two ways of telling how the sperm cell and the egg cell meet.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary, but the evidence favours the second. The womb's squeezes carry the sperm cells, the neck of the womb filters them, the egg cell releases chemicals that attract them, and the egg cell's coat shuts after one enters.. *Why:* A strong answer uses evidence about what the woman's body and the egg cell actually do.
5. (write) Write two scientific reasons why a mother cannot be blamed for having only daughters.  
   **Answer:** First, every egg cell carries an X, so the egg cell never settles it. Second, which sperm cell arrives is chance, so nobody controls it. A daughter is also not a fault.. *Why:* A strong answer names what each cell carries and says that chance settles which sperm cell joins.
6. (order) Put these in order, from longest ago, for the egg cell that became you.  
   **Answer:** ['Your grandmother becomes pregnant', 'The egg cell forms in your unborn mother', 'Your mother is born', 'The egg cell ripens and is released', 'Fertilisation']. *Why:* Egg cells form in a baby girl's ovaries before she is born, and wait there for many years.
7. (sort) Sort each claim: is it settled, or still argued about?  
   **Answer:** Each sex cell carries 23 chromosomes → Settled; Fertilisation happens in an egg tube → Settled; Adult ovaries make new egg cells → Still argued about; The egg cell helps to choose the sperm cell → Still argued about; Mitochondria come from the mother → Settled; Why two parents are so common → Still argued about. *Why:* Some claims have been checked many times. Others rest on a few studies that scientists still question.
8. (match) Match each living being to how the sex of the young is settled.  
   **Answer:** Human → by the sperm cell's X or Y; Hen → by a chromosome in the egg cell; Sea turtle → by the warmth of the nest. *Why:* Living beings settle this in different ways. In humans it is the sperm cell, in birds the egg cell, and in sea turtles the temperature.
9. (mcq) Why is a hen's egg cell so much bigger than a human egg cell?  
   Options: All the chick's food must be packed inside / Hens are bigger than humans / A hen's cells are always larger  
   **Answer:** All the chick's food must be packed inside. *Why:* A chick grows outside the mother, so the egg holds all the food. A human embryo is fed through the placenta.
10. (write) A due date is 40 weeks after the last period began. Why has the baby been growing for only about 38 weeks?  
   **Answer:** Ovulation and fertilisation happen about two weeks after a period begins. So the count of 40 weeks starts about two weeks before the baby began.. *Why:* A strong answer says that doctors count from the last period because that day is known, and fertilisation comes about two weeks later.
11. (draw) Draw three generations as three nested shapes. Show where the egg cell that became you was.  
   **Answer:** A large shape for the grandmother, a smaller one inside for the unborn mother, and a dot inside that for the egg cell.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the egg cell inside the unborn mother, who is inside the grandmother.
12. (truefalse) True or false: a cell in your body can form from material that is not alive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every cell comes from a living cell that divided, or from two living cells that joined.
13. (fill) Tiny waving hairs called ____ help to carry the embryo along the egg tube.  
   **Answer:** ['cilia']. *Why:* The embryo cannot swim. Cilia and gentle squeezes of the tube move the embryo towards the womb.
14. (write) Is a family made by cells or by care? Give your view and one reason.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. One view: by care, because adopted children and children raised by grandparents have real families.    **Answer:** The embryo would have too many chromosomes. *Why:* Each sperm cell brings 23 chromosomes. Two would give the zygote 69, and the embryo could not grow properly.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does a sperm cell bring to the egg cell?  
   Options: Half of the instructions / All of the instructions / The food for the first week  
   **Answer:** Half of the instructions. *Why:* The sperm cell brings 23 chromosomes, which is half. The egg cell brings the other half and the food store.
2. (mcq) Where does a woman's body keep egg cells?  
   Options: In the ovaries / In the womb / In the egg tubes  
   **Answer:** In the ovaries. *Why:* Egg cells are kept and ripened in the two ovaries. The womb is where a baby grows.
3. (mcq) What does a zygote have that neither sex cell had alone?  
   Options: A full set of 46 chromosomes / A tail for swimming / A placenta  
   **Answer:** A full set of 46 chromosomes. *Why:* The egg cell's 23 and the sperm cell's 23 make a full set of 46 in the zygote.
4. (mcq) Where do the egg cell and the sperm cell usually meet?  
   Options: In an egg tube / In the womb / In the vagina  
   **Answer:** In an egg tube. *Why:* They meet in an egg tube. The embryo reaches the womb about five days later.
5. (mcq) About when does the embryo attach to the lining of the womb?  
   Options: About 8 to 10 days after ovulation / Within one hour / After three months  
   **Answer:** About 8 to 10 days after ovulation. *Why:* The embryo floats free for about a week and then attaches. This is implantation.
6. (mcq) Each traveller has only a small chance of arriving. What makes it likely that one arrives?  
   Options: Many of them starting / One very strong one / Starting earlier in the day  
   **Answer:** Many of them starting. *Why:* In the Two Gates Test a bean usually arrived only when many started. More starters means more chances.
7. (mcq) Which cell settles whether the zygote is XX or XY?  
   Options: The sperm cell / The egg cell / Neither. It is settled at birth  
   **Answer:** The sperm cell. *Why:* Every egg cell carries an X. The sperm cell carries an X or a Y.
8. (mcq) What starts the changes of puberty?  
   Options: Hormone messages from a gland under the brain / Eating more food / Reaching the age of thirteen  
   **Answer:** Hormone messages from a gland under the brain. *Why:* A small gland under the brain sends hormones to the ovaries or testes. The age differs from child to child.
9. (mcq) If no embryo arrives in a month, what happens to the womb's lining?  
   Options: It comes away as a period / It stays for the next month / It turns into an egg cell  
   **Answer:** It comes away as a period. *Why:* The lining is not needed, so it comes away and leaves as a period. Then a new lining is built.
10. (mcq) What is the same about a child born after IVF and every other child?  
   Options: Both began from an egg cell and a sperm cell / Both began in a glass dish / Nothing is the same  
   **Answer:** Both began from an egg cell and a sperm cell. *Why:* Every person begins from one egg cell and one sperm cell. In IVF the two cells meet in a dish.
11. (mcq) Who are an adopted child's parents?  
   Options: The people who raise the child / Only the birth parents / Nobody  
   **Answer:** The people who raise the child. *Why:* Adoption makes the people who raise a child their parents, by law and for life. Birth parents are part of the child's story too.
12. (mcq) Why can scientists only estimate how many embryos are lost before they attach?  
   Options: Nobody can see it happen inside a body / Nobody has tried to find out / It happens only in other animals  
   **Answer:** Nobody can see it happen inside a body. *Why:* The embryo is tiny and hidden, and the woman usually does not know. So the number is worked out from other measurements.

## Applications
- Fertility doctors use the timing of ovulation and the short life of the egg cell to help couples who want a baby.
- IVF brings egg cells and sperm cells together in a dish. It has helped millions of families who could not have a child otherwise.
- Pregnancy tests look for a chemical that an embryo makes only after attaching to the womb.
- Families use the knowledge of ovulation and fertilisation to choose when to have children, and how many.
- Schools and health workers teach about periods, so that no girl is frightened by her first one or kept away from school.
- Farmers and wildlife breeders use the same science. Many dairy calves in India begin when a vet places sperm cells in a cow's womb.
- Courts and adoption agencies keep apart two questions that science also keeps apart: whose cells a child came from, and who the child's parents are.

## Open questions
- **What sets the day a baby is born?** Signals from the baby, the placenta and the mother all seem to take part. Nobody can yet say what starts birth on one day and not another. This is why due dates are so often wrong, and why doctors cannot yet prevent most early births.
- **Why are so many embryos lost in the first week?** Many have the wrong number of chromosomes, but that does not explain all of the losses. The losses happen where nobody can see them, so even the number is an estimate.
- **Does the egg cell take part in choosing the sperm cell?** Laboratory studies show that the liquid around egg cells attracts some men's sperm cells more than others. Nobody has shown what this does inside a body.
- **Why do most animals and plants have two parents at all?** A living being who simply copies themself passes on all of their instructions, not half. So two parents look costly. The leading ideas are that mixing helps the young resist disease and clears out harmful changes in the DNA. None has been shown to be the whole answer.
- **Why are there so many twins in Kodinhi?** The village in Kerala has several times as many twins as the rest of India. Researchers have looked at food, water and family histories, and have not found a cause.
- **How did the first cells arise, if every cell comes from a cell?** The line of living cells must have had a first link, more than three and a half billion years ago. Chemists can make some of the building blocks of cells, but nobody has made a living cell from non-living material.

## Connected maths
- In the Two Gates Test a bean passes each gate only on a 6. What fraction of beans should pass both gates? If 72 start, how many should arrive, on average? **1 in 36 should pass both gates. Of 72 beans, 2 should arrive on average.**. A 6 comes up 1 time in 6. Passing two gates needs two sixes: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36. 72 ÷ 36 = 2. In any one run you may get 0, 1, 2, 3 or more, because chance varies. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- Suppose 200,000,000 sperm cells start and 200 get near the egg cell. What fraction get near? Compare it with 1 in 36 from the Two Gates Test. **1 in 1,000,000 get near the egg cell. That is far smaller than 1 in 36.**. 200,000,000 ÷ 200 = 1,000,000. So one in a million gets near. A chance of 1 in 36 needed about 72 starters. A chance of one in a million needs millions of starters. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- A salt grain is about 0.3 millimetres wide, and an egg cell about 0.1 millimetres. At 2,000 times life size, how wide is each? How tall would a child of 130 centimetres be? **The egg cell is 20 centimetres wide and the salt grain 60 centimetres. The child would be 2.6 kilometres tall.**. 0.1 mm × 2,000 = 200 mm = 20 cm. 0.3 mm × 2,000 = 600 mm = 60 cm. 130 cm × 2,000 = 260,000 cm = 2,600 m = 2.6 km. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- A girl has about 1,000,000 egg cells at birth. About 400 are released in her life. What fraction is released? Write it as 1 in something. **About 1 in 2,500.**. 1,000,000 ÷ 400 = 2,500. So for every egg cell released, about 2,499 are not. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A cycle is about 28 days. A woman has cycles from about age 13 to age 50. About how many cycles is that? Why might the real number be lower? **About 480 cycles. The real number is often lower, because cycles stop during pregnancy and often during breastfeeding.**. 365 ÷ 28 is about 13 cycles a year. 50 − 13 = 37 years. 37 × 13 = 481. This is close to the 400 to 500 egg cells released in a lifetime. (queues `math.time.calendar.L5`)
- In a made-up Due Date Survey of 20 births, 1 baby came on the due date, 11 before and 8 after. What percentage came on the date? Is this close to what large studies find? **5 percent came on the due date. Large studies find about 4 or 5 percent, so it is close.**. 1 out of 20 is 1 ÷ 20 = 0.05, which is 5 percent. With only 20 births, one more or one fewer would change the answer a lot, so a small survey gives only a rough figure. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
