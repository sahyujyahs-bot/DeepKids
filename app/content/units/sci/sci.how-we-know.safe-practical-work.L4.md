# Safe practical work: choosing equipment, naming risks, judging predictions
`sci.how-we-know.safe-practical-work.L4`

**Summary.** You want to know how long a spoon of sugar takes to vanish in warm water. Which spoon, which cup and which clock will you use, and what could go wrong before you even begin? In this lesson you choose your own equipment and learn to use it the right way. You name the risks of a test and plan how to make each one smaller. Then you write a prediction, which is a guess with a reason, and use your results to say how close it was.

**Intro.** *Put a ruler, a teaspoon, a big steel pot and a phone on the table. Your job is to find out how much water a small bottle cap holds. Which of these would you pick up, and which would you leave?* A test done with your hands is called practical work. Before any practical work, a scientist makes three choices. She chooses the tools that fit the job. She thinks about what could hurt someone, and how to stop that. And she says what she expects to happen, so that the result can surprise her. Today you make all three choices yourself, and then you check how good your expectation was.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can pick the right equipment for a job from a set, and say why it fits.
1. I can use a ruler, a measuring cup, a thermometer and a stopwatch the right way.
2. I can tell a hazard from a risk, and name the risks in a test before I start.
3. I can plan how to make each risk smaller, and carry out the test safely.
4. I can make a prediction with a reason, for something I know and for something new to me.
5. I can use my results to say how close my prediction was.
6. I can say what a missed prediction might be telling me.

## Terms
- **Practical work**: A test or an observation that you carry out yourself, with your own hands. *e.g.* Timing how long a wet cloth takes to dry on the line is practical work.
- **Equipment**: The things you use to carry out a test or an observation. *e.g.* A steel tumbler, a spoon and a phone stopwatch are the equipment for timing sugar in water.
- **Quantity**: Something you can measure and give a number to. *e.g.* How tall a plant is, how long a cloth takes to dry and how warm the water is are all quantities.
- **Length**: How far it is from one end of something to the other. A ruler or a tape measures it. *e.g.* A bean plant that is 12 centimetres tall has a length of 12 centimetres.
- **Volume**: How much space something takes up. For a liquid, a measuring cup measures it. *e.g.* A tumbler of water might hold 200 millilitres, and that is its volume.
- **Mass**: How much material a thing is made of. A weighing scale measures it, in grams or kilograms. *e.g.* One idli might have a mass of 40 grams on a kitchen scale.
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold something is. A thermometer measures it, in degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Cold water from the fridge is about 5 degrees Celsius, and warm tap water is about 40.
- **Millilitre**: A small unit of volume. There are 1,000 millilitres in one litre. *e.g.* A teaspoon holds about 5 millilitres of water.
- **Smallest mark**: The smallest amount a tool can show. A tool cannot tell apart amounts that are smaller than this. *e.g.* A kitchen jug marked every 100 millilitres cannot show 20 millilitres, but a medicine cup marked every 1 millilitre can.
- **Zero mark**: The mark on a tool where the counting starts. You line up what you measure with this mark. *e.g.* On many rulers the zero mark is a little way in from the end, so you line up the mark and not the edge.
- **Eye level**: Your eyes at the same height as the thing you are reading, so that you look straight across it. *e.g.* Bend down until your eye is level with the water line in the measuring cup before you read it.
- **Reading**: The number a tool shows when you use it. *e.g.* The thermometer's reading in the warm water was 41 degrees.
- **Thermometer**: A tool that measures temperature. It needs about a minute in the water before its number stops changing. *e.g.* Stand the thermometer in the warm water and read it after a minute, not at once.
- **Stopwatch**: A clock that you start and stop to measure how long something takes. A phone has one. *e.g.* Press start when the sugar touches the water, and press stop when the water is clear.
- **Senses**: The body's sensors, such as those in the eyes, skin, ears, nose and tongue, that report what is around you. The body has many more kinds than the few best known. *e.g.* Your skin senses warm and cold, and your inner ear senses balance.
- **Compare**: To put two things side by side and say how they are alike and different. *e.g.* Your two hands compare the middle bowl of water with the bowls they were in before.
- **Hazard**: Anything that could cause harm. *e.g.* A kitchen knife is a hazard, whether it is in a drawer or in a hand.
- **Risk**: How likely a harm is, together with how bad it would be. *e.g.* A knife in a closed drawer has a tiny risk. A knife carried by a running child has a high risk.
- **Harm**: Hurt or damage to a person, another living being or a thing. *e.g.* A scald from hot water and a slip on a wet floor are two kinds of harm.
- **Likely**: Something is likely if it will probably happen. It is unlikely if it will probably not. *e.g.* If you carry a full cup across a room while running, a spill is likely.
- **Risk assessment**: A short list made before a test of what could cause harm, who could be harmed, and how likely and how bad the harm would be. *e.g.* Before the sugar test, you write: warm water could scald, a spill could cause a slip, a cup could break.
- **Remove**: To take a hazard away completely, so that the test does not have it at all. *e.g.* If the test works with cold water, use cold water and the scald hazard is gone.
- **Swap**: To replace a hazard with a safer thing that does the same job. *e.g.* A plastic cup in place of a glass one is a swap.
- **Barrier**: Something put between people and a hazard, or distance kept from it, so that harm cannot easily reach them. *e.g.* A tray under the cups is a barrier, because a spill stays on the tray.
- **Protection**: Something you wear to guard your body from a hazard. It is the weakest kind of plan, because the hazard is still there. *e.g.* Tied-back hair and rolled-up sleeves keep loose things away from the work.
- **Hierarchy of controls**: The order of ways to reduce a risk, from strongest to weakest: remove, swap, separate, and protect. *e.g.* Using cold water (remove) is stronger than using warm water with a grown-up pouring (separate).
- **Spill**: Liquid that runs out of its cup or bowl by mistake. *e.g.* A spill on a tiled floor makes it slippery.
- **Scald**: A burn from hot water or steam. *e.g.* Water that is too hot can scald skin, which is why we use warm tap water and never boiling water.
- **Atom**: A tiny piece of matter, far too small to see. Everything solid, liquid and gas is built from atoms. *e.g.* A drop of water holds more atoms than anyone could count, and each is far smaller than a speck of dust.
- **Checklist**: A short written list of steps that must not be missed, read out and ticked at the moment of doing. *e.g.* Tray down, hair tied, cloth ready, grown-up near: tick each one before you start.
- **Radioactive**: Giving out invisible rays that can harm the body. Some materials do this all the time, such as radium. *e.g.* Marie Curie's notebooks are still radioactive today.
- **Acceptable risk**: A risk that people agree is small enough to take, because of what they gain. People do not all agree about how small that is. *e.g.* Most families accept the small risk of a cut when a grown-up slices an apple for a test.
- **Precaution**: A step taken before something happens, to keep harm away. *e.g.* Putting a tray under the cups is a precaution against a spill.
- **Living being**: A plant, an animal, a fungus, a microbe or any other someone who is alive. *e.g.* A potted plant, a snail on the balcony and the ants on the wall are all living beings.
- **Prediction**: A statement made before a test of what the result will be, with a reason for it. *e.g.* I predict the sugar will take 30 seconds in warm water, because cold took 70 and room-warm took 45.
- **Reason**: What you already know that makes you expect a result. *e.g.* My reason is that warm water was faster than cold water in the first two tests.
- **Inference**: A step of thinking from what you know to something you have not yet seen. *e.g.* Cold took longer than warm, so you infer that warm tap water will be even faster.
- **Familiar**: Something you have seen or done many times. *e.g.* Sugar going into tea is a familiar sight.
- **Unfamiliar**: Something you have not met before. *e.g.* Powdered jaggery in warm water is unfamiliar if you have never timed it.
- **Crystal**: A small solid piece with flat sides that meet in sharp corners, such as a grain of sugar or of rock salt. *e.g.* Look at a grain of sugar in torchlight. Its flat shiny sides are what make it a crystal.
- **Dissolve**: To spread out into a liquid until the pieces are too small to see and the liquid is clear. *e.g.* Sugar dissolves in water, and the water turns clear again.
- **Result**: What you find out when you do the test. *e.g.* The result was 41 seconds for the sugar in warm water.
- **Difference**: How far apart two numbers are. You find it by subtracting the smaller from the larger. *e.g.* You predicted 40 seconds and the result was 52 seconds, so the difference is 12 seconds.
- **Accuracy**: How close a prediction or a measurement is to the true result. *e.g.* A prediction that is 2 seconds off a 50-second result is more accurate than one that is 12 seconds off.
- **Hindsight**: Looking back at something after you know how it ended. It makes people feel they expected the result all along. *e.g.* After a match, many people say 'I knew he would get out', though they never said it before.
- **Spread**: How far apart your repeated results are, found by taking the smallest from the largest. *e.g.* Runs of 48, 52 and 56 seconds have a spread of 8 seconds.
- **Middle value**: The number in the middle when you line your results up from the smallest to the biggest. *e.g.* Runs of 56, 48 and 52 seconds line up as 48, 52, 56, so the middle value is 52 seconds.
- **Fluke**: A result that comes out oddly because of luck, not because of the idea you are testing. *e.g.* One run of the sugar test took 20 seconds longer because your finger pressed stop late. Three runs show whether that was a fluke.
- **Forecast**: A prediction about the future, such as tomorrow's weather, made from what is known now. *e.g.* The monsoon forecast says how much rain a year is likely to bring.
- **Eclipse**: When something in the sky passes in front of another and blocks its light, as the Moon does to the Sun. *e.g.* Astronomers can predict a solar eclipse centuries ahead, to the minute.
- **Miss**: How far a prediction was from the result. *e.g.* A prediction of 40 seconds for a 52-second result is a miss of 12 seconds, too short.
- **Assumption**: Something you take to be true without checking it. *e.g.* Assuming that both spoonfuls of sugar were the same size can hide the real reason for a miss.
- **Chaos**: When tiny differences at the start grow into big differences later, so that nothing can be predicted far ahead even though the rules are fixed. *e.g.* A tiny change in a weather sum makes the forecast for ten days later completely different.
- **Particle**: A very small piece of something, such as a grain of dust or an atom. *e.g.* A particle of sugar dissolving in water spreads out among the water.
- **Quantum physics**: The part of physics that describes very small things, such as atoms and light. For some of them it can say only the chances of a result, and not which result will come. *e.g.* It can say that half of a set of radioactive atoms will break up in a certain time, but not which atom will go next.
- **Matter**: Whatever things are made of. Scientists have working meanings of matter that let them calculate, and no final answer to what matter finally is. *e.g.* A stone, water and air are all matter, and so are the cells of your body.

## Explanations
### Practical work is finding out with your hands, and equipment is whatever helps you do it · *defined*
**Definition:** Practical work is a test or an observation that you carry out yourself. Equipment is the set of things you use to carry it out.

You can find some things out by thinking, and some by asking. Other things you can only find out by doing. Will a wet towel dry faster hung up or folded? You have to try it. That trying is practical work. The things you use are your equipment: a towel, a clothes line, a clock, a notebook. Equipment does not have to come from a laboratory. A steel tumbler is equipment when you measure with it. A window sill is equipment when you grow a seed there. What makes something equipment is the job you give it.
- e.g. A kitchen weighing scale becomes science equipment when you weigh a sponge dry and then wet.
- e.g. A phone becomes equipment three times over: a stopwatch, a torch and a camera.
- e.g. A cook testing if the dosa pan is hot enough by sprinkling water is doing practical work.
### Each measuring tool answers one kind of question · *defined*
**Definition:** A measuring tool is built to measure one quantity. Choosing equipment starts with asking which quantity you need.

A quantity is something you can measure and give a number to. Length is a quantity, and a ruler or a tape measures it. Volume is how much space a liquid takes up, and a measuring cup measures it. Mass is how much stuff is in a thing, and a weighing scale measures it. Temperature is how hot or cold something is, and a thermometer measures it. Time is measured with a clock or a stopwatch. So the first question when you choose equipment is 'What do I need to know?' Suppose you need to know how hot the water is. A ruler cannot help, however good the ruler is.
- e.g. How tall is the bean plant today? Length, so a ruler, in centimetres.
- e.g. How much water did the sponge soak up? Volume, so a measuring cup, in millilitres.
- e.g. How heavy is one idli before and after steaming? Mass, so a kitchen scale, in grams.
- e.g. How long does the ice take to melt? Time, so a clock, in minutes.
### The tool must fit the size of the thing: look at the smallest mark · *defined*
**Definition:** The smallest mark on a tool is the smallest amount it can measure. Choose a tool whose smallest mark is much smaller than the amount you are measuring.

Two tools can measure the same quantity and still not be equally good for your job. A one-litre kitchen jug often has a mark only every 100 millilitres. A medicine cup has a mark every 5 millilitres or less. If you need 10 millilitres of water, the jug cannot show it, and the medicine cup can. The same goes for time. A wall clock with no seconds hand cannot time a toy car that rolls for 3 seconds. A tool that is too fine is also a poor fit. Nobody measures a room 6 metres long with a 15 centimetre ruler, because laying it down 40 times adds 40 small mistakes.
- e.g. A school ruler has a mark every 1 millimetre. A tailor's tape has the same marks but bends round a tree trunk, so the tape fits that job.
- e.g. A kitchen scale that shows whole grams cannot weigh one grain of rice. Weigh a hundred grains together, then divide the total by a hundred.
- e.g. A phone stopwatch shows hundredths of a second, but your thumb takes about two tenths of a second to press. So the last digit means little when a person does the pressing.
### Using a tool the right way: start at zero, read at eye level, keep it steady · *defined*
**Definition:** A tool gives a true reading only when it is used the way it was made to be used.

The right tool, used the wrong way, gives a wrong number. Three habits fix most mistakes. First, start at zero. On many rulers the zero mark is a little way in from the end, so line up the mark and not the edge. Press the zero button on a kitchen scale after you put the empty bowl on. Second, read at eye level. Put a measuring cup on a flat table and bring your eye down to the water line. Looked at from above, the water seems to stand at a different mark. Third, keep the tool steady and wait. A thermometer needs about a minute in the water before its number stops changing.
- e.g. In a narrow clear tube, the surface of water curves up at the sides. Read the bottom of the curve.
- e.g. Hold a thermometer by its top end. If you hold the tip, the thermometer measures your fingers.
- e.g. Say 'three, two, one, go' so the hand that drops and the thumb that times start together.
- **Sushruta Samhita, Sutrasthana, chapter 9, an Indian book of surgery whose oldest parts are more than 2,000 years old:** A student must practise each tool before using it on a person. The book tells the student to practise cuts on gourds, watermelons and cucumbers, and stitching on cloth and soft leather. It says that even a well-read student is unfit for the work without this practice.
- **Sushruta Samhita, Sutrasthana, chapter 7:** The same book lists 101 blunt instruments. It then says that the hand is the most important instrument of all, because no other instrument can work without it.
### Your senses are equipment too, and they compare more than they measure · *defined*
**Definition:** Skin, eyes, ears, nose and tongue report how a thing differs from what they met just before. A measuring tool reports against a fixed scale.

Try this with three bowls of water: one cold, one warm from the tap and one in between. Keep one hand in the cold bowl and the other in the warm bowl for one minute. Then put both hands into the middle bowl. The same water feels warm to one hand and cool to the other. Your skin was not wrong. Skin reports change, and each hand came from a different starting point. A thermometer put in the middle bowl gives one number, whichever bowl it came from. That is why scientists use tools for numbers. It is also why they still use their senses to notice that something is worth measuring.
- e.g. A room feels dark when you walk in from the sun and bright when you wake at night, though the lamp is the same.
- e.g. After a sweet laddu, a sweet orange tastes sour.
- **John Locke, an English philosopher, in 'An Essay Concerning Human Understanding', book 2, chapter 8, in 1690:** He described this same test with two hands and one bowl. He concluded that warm and cold, as we feel them, are not in the water in the way that its amount is. They arise where the water meets the hand.
- **Skilled craftspeople, such as tea tasters in Assam and the Nilgiris, and potters who judge a kiln by the colour of its flame:** With years of practice, senses become fine instruments. A trained taster can tell teas apart that no simple machine can. Their view is that a sense is a tool that must be trained and checked, like any other.
- **Scientists who study other animals' senses:** Every being has their own equipment. A pit viper senses the warmth of a mouse from a distance, and a bee sees ultraviolet marks on a flower, a kind of light that human eyes cannot see. No being's senses report the whole world.
### A hazard is a thing that could hurt, and a risk is the chance that it will · *defined*
**Definition:** A hazard is anything that could cause harm. A risk is how likely the harm is, together with how bad it would be.

A kitchen knife is a hazard, because it is sharp. That is true when it lies in a closed drawer and when a running child carries it. But the risk is very different in the two cases. In the drawer the chance of a cut is tiny. In the running child's hand the chance is high, and the cut could be deep. So a hazard is about the thing. A risk is about the thing, the person, the place and what is being done. This is good news, because you often cannot change the hazard. Water for tea must be hot. You can nearly always change the risk.
- e.g. Hazard: hot water. Low risk: half a tumbler of warm tap water on a tray. High risk: a full pot of boiling water at the table's edge.
- e.g. Hazard: a glass jar can break. The risk rises on a tiled floor and falls on a mat.
- e.g. Hazard: the Sun can harm eyes. The risk is high if you look at the Sun, and low if you watch its shadow.
### Naming the risks before you start: what could hurt, whom, and how badly · *defined*
**Definition:** A risk assessment is a short list you make before a test. It says what could cause harm, who could be harmed, and how likely and how bad the harm would be.

Before a test, walk through it in your head from the first step to the cleaning up. At each step ask three questions. What here could hurt someone? Who could be hurt: me, a helper, a baby who crawls in, a pet, the plant? How likely is it, and how bad would it be? Write each answer in a line. Scientists call this list a risk assessment. Most home tests have the same few hazards. They are heat, sharp edges, glass, small things that could be swallowed, wall sockets, water on the floor, and the Sun in the eyes. Naming them takes two minutes. A risk that has a name is much easier to plan for.
- e.g. Test: dissolving sugar in warm water. Risks named: a scald from water that is too hot, a slip on spilled water, a broken glass.
- e.g. Test: growing moong seeds. Risks named: a toddler swallowing seeds, mouldy seeds touched and then fingers in the mouth.
- e.g. Test: making shadows. Risk named: looking straight at the Sun.
### Making a risk smaller: remove, swap, separate, protect · *defined*
**Definition:** There are four ways to make a risk smaller. You can remove the hazard, swap it for a safer one, put a barrier or distance between it and people, or wear protection. The first ways work better than the last.

Once a risk has a name, there are four kinds of plan, and they are not equally strong. The strongest is to remove the hazard. If the test works with cold water, use cold water. The next is to swap. Use a plastic or steel tumbler in place of glass, and warm tap water in place of boiling water. The third is to separate people from the hazard. Work on a tray in the middle of the table, and keep a baby in another room. The weakest is protection that you wear, such as an apron or glasses, because the hazard is still there and the protection can slip. Safety engineers call this order the hierarchy of controls.
- e.g. A torch in place of a candle removes the flame from a shadow test.
- e.g. A tray under the cups means a spill stays on the tray and not on the floor.
- e.g. Tied-back hair and rolled-up sleeves keep loose things away from the work.
- e.g. A grown-up pouring the warm water is separation too: the child is kept apart from the pouring.
### Carrying out the work safely: the habits that matter while your hands are busy · *defined*
**Definition:** Working safely means keeping to the plan while you work, and stopping when something happens that the plan did not cover.

A plan on paper protects nobody unless the hands follow it. A few habits do most of the work. Clear the table of everything the test does not need. Put each thing back in its own place after you use it. Never taste anything in a test unless the test is about taste and a grown-up has said the food is safe. To smell something, hold it away and wave a little air towards your nose. Wipe a spill at once. Wash your hands at the end. And the most important habit is this: if something happens that you did not expect, stop. Step back, tell someone, and think again before you carry on.
- e.g. The water from the tap comes out hotter than you planned. Stop, add cold water, and test the warmth with a thermometer or with a grown-up's hand before going on.
- e.g. A cup cracks. Stop, leave the pieces, and fetch a grown-up with a broom.
- e.g. At the end, pour sugar water down the sink and not into a bottle someone may drink from.
### A checklist catches the slips that skill does not · *debated*
**Definition:** A checklist is a short written list of steps that must not be missed, read out and ticked at the moment of doing.

Experts forget simple steps, most of all when the work is familiar. In October 1935 a new and very large aeroplane, the Boeing Model 299, crashed on a test flight in Ohio in the United States. The pilots were among the best in the country. They had forgotten to release a lock on the controls. Pilots then wrote the steps down on a card and read them before every flight. Aeroplanes have used checklists ever since. In 2008 the World Health Organization made a 19-step checklist for operations. It was tried in eight hospitals in eight cities, one of them New Delhi. Deaths after operations fell from 15 in every 1,000 to 8 in every 1,000.
- e.g. A test checklist for home: table clear, tray down, hair tied, grown-up near for the warm water, cloth ready, prediction written.
- e.g. A cook's habit of checking the gas knob before leaving the kitchen is a one-line checklist.
- **Atul Gawande, a surgeon, and the World Health Organization team, in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2009:** Work today has too many steps for memory alone. A short list read aloud catches the missed step, and it also makes the team speak to each other before they start.
- **David Urbach and colleagues, who studied 101 hospitals in Ontario, Canada, in 2014:** When hospitals across Ontario were required to use the checklist, deaths after operations hardly changed. Their view is that a list by itself does little. It works only when the people using it believe in it and really stop to read it.
- **Some experienced surgeons and pilots, when checklists were first brought in:** A list treats a skilled person like a beginner, and ticking boxes can take the place of thinking. Attention, they said, is the real safety.
### A hazard that nobody knows about cannot be put on the list · *defined*
**Definition:** A risk assessment can cover only the hazards that are known at the time. New materials and new methods can carry hazards that are discovered later.

Listing risks has a limit. You can name only what somebody already knows. Carl Wilhelm Scheele, a Swedish chemist, discovered several new substances in the 1770s. He smelled and tasted them, as chemists then did. He died in 1786 at the age of 43, and some historians think his chemicals shortened his life. Marie Curie carried tubes of glowing radium in her pocket. Her notebooks are still radioactive, and the national library of France keeps them in lead-lined boxes. In the 1920s, young women in American factories painted watch dials with radium paint. They were told to point their brushes with their lips. Many fell terribly ill. Five of them, led by Grace Fryer, took the company to court in 1927.
- e.g. The rule 'never taste anything in a test' exists because of people like Scheele.
- e.g. When scientists work with something new, they treat it as hazardous until tests show otherwise.
- **The dial painters, later called the Radium Girls, and the doctors who stood by them, such as Harrison Martland in 1925:** The company's scientists protected themselves with screens and tongs, while telling the painters that the paint was harmless. Their view is that risk is not only about knowledge. It is also about who is told, and who is listened to.
- **Historians of science:** Curie and Scheele were not careless by the standards of their day. Nobody could have listed a hazard that had not been discovered. The lesson they draw is to stay watchful when a thing is new.
### No test has zero risk, so people must decide how much is acceptable · *debated*
**Definition:** Risk can be made small but never zero. How small is small enough is a choice people make, and they do not all choose alike.

Even a test with a bowl of cold water has some risk, because someone can slip. Crossing the road to buy the sugar has more. So 'Is it safe?' is never a yes-or-no question. The real questions are how big the risk is, what we gain by doing the test, and who carries the risk. A test that teaches a lot may be worth a small risk. A test that teaches little is not worth a large one. People answer these questions differently, and that is a real disagreement, not a mistake by one side.
- e.g. Cutting an apple to watch it brown needs a knife. Most families accept that risk with a grown-up cutting.
- e.g. Boiling water for a test that works just as well with warm tap water is a risk with nothing gained.
- **The Rio Declaration of the United Nations, Principle 15, in 1992:** Where serious harm to the environment is possible, do not wait for full proof before you act to prevent it. This is called the precautionary approach. Suppose fish start dying in a river below a new factory. Waiting years for proof means years of harm, so the factory is asked to cut its waste now, while scientists study the river.
- **Chauncey Starr, an American engineer, in the journal Science in 1969:** Every benefit carries some risk, so compare the two with numbers. He found that people accept much larger risks when they choose them freely, such as skiing, than when others impose them.
- **People who carry the risk without getting the gain:** A sum of risk against benefit can look fine overall while one group gets the benefit and another gets the harm. They ask that the sum always says who is who.
### The other beings in your test are at risk too · *defined*
**Definition:** A risk assessment counts every living being the test touches, not only people.

Many tests at home use living beings. There may be a potted plant, sprouting seeds, ants on a wall, a snail from the balcony or the curd bacteria in a bowl. Each of them can be harmed by the test, so they go on the list. Ask what the test will do to them, and how to do less. Watch ants where they already walk, and do not shut them in a jar. If a test keeps a plant in the dark, do it for a few days at most, then bring the plant back to the light. Put a snail back where you found them within the hour. Plants and animals feel, remember and decide. Some scientists call these automatic responses.
- e.g. To learn what ants like to eat, place a grain of sugar and a grain of salt near their path and watch. No ant needs to be caught.
- e.g. To test whether seeds need light to sprout, put ten seeds on a damp cloth, some in light and some in a dark cupboard, and plant them all afterwards.
- **William Russell and Rex Burch, British scientists, in 'The Principles of Humane Experimental Technique' in 1959:** They gave three rules for any test on animals. Replace the animal with something else where you can. Reduce the number of animals. Refine the method so that the animals suffer less. Laboratories across the world now follow these three rules.
- **The Acharanga Sutra, book 1, lecture 4, lesson 1, one of the oldest Jain texts:** All breathing, existing, living beings should not be killed, treated with violence or tormented. Jain teaching counts plants, and the tiny beings in water and soil, among the living.
- **Scientists who use animals in medical research:** Some knowledge that saves many lives, human and animal, cannot yet be gained without tests on animals. They accept the three rules and hold that stopping all such tests would cause more suffering than it prevents.
### A prediction is what you expect to happen, with a reason taken from what you already know · *defined*
**Definition:** A prediction is a statement, made before a test, of what the result will be. A scientific prediction comes with a reason.

A guess says what will happen. A prediction also says why you think so. 'The sugar will dissolve faster in warm water' is a guess. Here is a prediction. 'The sugar will dissolve faster in warm water, because I have seen sugar vanish quickly in hot tea and slowly in cold lemon juice.' The reason matters for two things. It lets other people check your thinking. And when the result comes in, it tells you what to learn. If the result matches, the reason has passed one test. If the result does not match, something in the reason needs another look. Write the prediction down before the test, with a number if you can.
- e.g. I predict the wet towel will dry in about 2 hours on the line, because yesterday a wet shirt took about that long.
- e.g. I predict the bigger ice cube will take longer to melt, because there is more ice to warm up.
- **The Nyaya Sutra of Gautama, book 1, chapter 1, sutra 5, with Vatsyayana's commentary of about the 5th century CE:** This Indian text on reasoning names three kinds of inference. The first goes from a cause to what will follow. The commentary's example is seeing dark clouds gather and inferring that rain will come. That is a prediction with a reason.
- **The Charaka Samhita, Sutrasthana, chapter 11, an Indian book of medicine whose oldest parts are about 2,000 years old:** It lists four ways of knowing: trusted teaching, direct seeing, inference, and yukti. Yukti is reasoning that puts many causes together to say what will result. Its example is a crop, which comes from water, ploughing, seed and season together.
### Predicting something new: reason from the nearest thing you know · *defined*
**Definition:** To predict something unfamiliar, find a familiar case that is like it. Then ask what should stay the same and what should change.

It is easy to predict what you have seen many times. The real power of science is predicting what nobody has seen. You do it by stepping from the known to the new. You know that sugar dissolves faster in warm water. Now someone hands you rock salt, which you have never tested. Rock salt is like sugar in one way: both dissolve in water. So you predict that warm water will speed rock salt up too. In 1871 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev did this with a metal nobody had found. He reasoned from the metals around its empty place in his table. He predicted that each cubic centimetre of it would weigh about 5.9 grams.
- e.g. In 1875 the French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran found the metal, now called gallium. He first measured 4.7 grams. Mendeleev wrote to him that the figure was too low, and that the sample must be impure. Measured again on a purer piece, it came to about 5.9.
- e.g. You know a metal spoon in hot tea gets hot. Predict what a wooden spoon will do, and say what is the same and what is different.
- e.g. You know a ball rolls farther on tile than on a mat. Predict what a ball will do on sand.
### Judging a prediction: write it first, then find the difference from the result · *defined*
**Definition:** The accuracy of a prediction is how close it came to the measured result. It is found by taking the difference between the two.

After the test, put the prediction and the result side by side. Suppose you predicted 40 seconds and the sugar took 52. The difference is 12 seconds. Then compare the difference with the size of the result. Being 12 seconds off in 52 is a miss of nearly a quarter. Being 12 seconds off in 10 minutes would be a tiny miss. Say which way you missed as well: 'I predicted too short a time.' All of this works only if the prediction was written before the test. After people see a result, they honestly remember having expected it. Psychologists call this hindsight. A number written in ink does not change.
- e.g. Predicted: the towel dries in 2 hours. Result: 3 hours. Difference: 1 hour too short, which is one third of the result.
- e.g. Predicted: the big cube lasts longer. Result: the big cube did last longer. The direction was right, though no number was predicted.
### How close is close enough depends on the spread of your results and on what the answer is for · *defined*
**Definition:** A prediction counts as accurate when its miss is no bigger than the natural spread of repeated results, or small enough for the purpose it serves.

No test gives the same number twice. Do the sugar test three times and you may get 48, 52 and 56 seconds. The results spread over 8 seconds without anything being wrong. So a prediction of 50 seconds is as close as this test can judge. A prediction of 40 is outside the spread, and is a real miss. The purpose matters too. The India Meteorological Department predicts each year's monsoon rain as a percentage of the long-term average. It says that the forecast may be off by about 5 per cent either way. If it forecasts 60 centimetres of rain, the true rain may be anywhere from 57 to 63. A farmer can plan around that. An engineer cutting a part for a rocket engine cannot accept a miss of 5 per cent.
- e.g. Three results: 48, 52, 56 seconds. Prediction 50: inside the spread, so accurate. Prediction 40: outside, so a miss.
- e.g. A recipe that says 'about a cup' accepts a large miss. A medicine dose does not.
- e.g. A forecast of 'rain tomorrow afternoon' is close enough for carrying an umbrella, and not close enough for timing a kite festival to the minute.
### A missed prediction asks three questions: was the reason wrong, the tool wrong, or the doing wrong? · *defined*
**Definition:** When a result misses a prediction, look in three places, in this order: how the test was done, the equipment, and the idea behind the prediction.

Say you predicted 45 seconds, and the sugar took 90. A miss is information. But what does it tell you? There are three places to look. The doing: did you stir one cup and not the other? The tool: was the spoon heaped one time and level the next? The reason: is your idea about warm water wrong, or missing something? Check them in that order, because the first two are easier to fix.
- e.g. Your sugar took twice as long as predicted. You then notice that this spoonful had large crystals. The reason was missing something: crystal size matters too.
- e.g. A plant you predicted would bend to the window grows straight. You check and find the curtain was closed all week. The doing was at fault.
- **Pierre Duhem, a French physicist, in 1906:** No prediction tests one idea alone. It tests the idea together with the tools, the set-up and many smaller assumptions. A miss says that something in the whole bundle is wrong, and it cannot say which part.
### A miss alone cannot say whether a hidden cause or a wrong rule is to blame: Neptune and Vulcan · *debated*
**Definition:** When a prediction misses in a careful test, scientists may look for a hidden cause outside the idea, or change the idea itself. A miss alone does not say which.

Scientists meet the same puzzle as your sugar test, and they do not always agree. By the 1840s the planet Uranus was not where the sums predicted. Astronomers kept Newton's law and blamed an unseen planet. Neptune was found in 1846, close to where they said. Later, Mercury also strayed from the sums, by about one hundredth of a degree each century. Astronomers again predicted an unseen planet, and named it Vulcan. Nobody ever found it. This time the law itself needed changing, and Albert Einstein changed it in 1915.
- e.g. Your cloth dried much slower than predicted. A hidden cause may be at work, such as a very humid day. Or your rule 'sun means quick' may be too simple, because shade and wind matter too.
- e.g. A friend's sugar takes twice as long as yours. A hidden cause could be bigger crystals. Or you could ask whether warm water speeds up every kind of sugar equally.
- **Karl Popper, a philosopher born in Vienna, in 1934:** A scientific idea must risk being wrong. When its prediction fails in a careful test, the honest thing is to give the idea up, and not to keep rescuing it with excuses.
- **Imre Lakatos, a Hungarian philosopher, in 1970:** Scientists are right to protect a good idea for a while, as they did with Neptune. The test is what the protecting leads to. If it leads to new finds, carry on. If it leads only to more excuses, let go.
### Can everything be predicted? Some things, only a short way ahead · *debated*
**Definition:** Some systems, such as the weather, follow fixed laws and still cannot be predicted far ahead, because tiny differences at the start grow into large ones.

If you knew enough, could you predict anything? In 1961 the American weather scientist Edward Lorenz ran a weather sum on his computer twice. The second time he typed one starting number as 0.506 in place of 0.506127. That is a difference of about one part in 4,000. After a short while the two forecasts had nothing in common. He had found that in weather, small differences do not stay small. We can never measure today's air perfectly, so a forecast gets worse with each day ahead. Today's forecasts are useful for about a week, and most weather scientists think two weeks is near the limit. An eclipse, though, can be predicted centuries ahead to the minute. So the reach of prediction depends on the thing being predicted.
- e.g. A dropped steel spoon: easy to predict where it lands. A dropped sheet of paper: nearly impossible.
- e.g. Tomorrow's high tide at Chennai can be found in a table printed a year ago. Tomorrow's exact cloud shapes cannot.
- **Pierre-Simon Laplace, a French mathematician, in 'A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities' in 1814:** Imagine a mind that knew the place and motion of every particle at one moment. For that mind nothing would be uncertain, and the future would be as plain as the past. On this view, what we cannot predict is only what we do not know well enough.
- **Edward Lorenz, in 1963, and the scientists of chaos who followed:** The laws may be fixed, but perfect knowledge of the start is impossible. So for weather and many other things, long-range prediction is out of reach in practice, however good the computer.
- **Most physicists, since the 1920s:** Some events seem to have no cause that fixes their moment. In a lump of radioactive material, physics can predict that half the atoms will break up in a certain time. No one can predict which atom will go next. A smaller group of physicists thinks hidden causes exist that we have not found.
### Careful practical work is very old, and India's texts wrote down its rules · *defined*
**Definition:** Rules for testing things carefully, choosing instruments and learning from results were written in several ancient cultures, long before modern laboratories.

It is easy to think that careful testing began in Europe a few hundred years ago. The written record says otherwise. The Charaka Samhita, in its Vimanasthana section, chapter 8, tells a physician to examine in an ordered way before acting. It says to judge a claim by reasoning and by what is seen. In Cairo, about the year 1021 CE, Ibn al-Haytham wrote in his Book of Optics that a person who studies nature must test what earlier writers say. He added that you must suspect even your own judgement. In China, the Mozi, a book from about the 4th century BCE, gives three tests for any statement. One of them is to check it against what ordinary people's eyes and ears report. The tools have changed. The habits are the same ones this lesson teaches.
- e.g. The Sushruta Samhita asks that a sharp instrument be so well made and sharpened that it can split a hair.
- e.g. A potter in Manamadurai, a weaver in Kanchipuram and a cook testing sugar syrup between two fingers all use rules that were tested many times. The rules were passed from hand to hand, and most were never written down.
- **Historians who place the start of modern science in Europe in the 1600s:** What was new then, they say, was joining tests to mathematics and to instruments, and publishing results so that strangers could repeat them. Earlier work was careful but was not organised in this way.
- **Historians such as Joseph Needham, who studied science in China, and Dharampal, who studied science and technology in 18th-century India:** Careful, tested knowledge was built in many places by many kinds of people, among them craftspeople, healers and farmers. Much of it was passed on by doing and was never credited, because it was not written in books.

## Mostly agreed
- A good prediction, shared in time, is one of the strongest safety tools people have. *How we know:* In October 2013 Cyclone Phailin was heading for the Odisha coast. The India Meteorological Department, with cyclone forecasting led by Mrutyunjay Mohapatra, said days ahead where and how hard it would strike. About a million people were moved inland before the storm arrived. Fewer than 50 people died. A cyclone of similar strength struck the same coast in 1999, with much poorer warning, and about 10,000 people died.
- After people learn a result, they remember their earlier expectation as closer to the result than it was. *How we know:* In 1975 the psychologist Baruch Fischhoff gave people accounts of events with different endings. People who were told an ending said that ending had been far more likely all along. They did not notice that knowing it had changed their view. The finding has been repeated many times.
- A person needs about a fifth of a second to react to something seen, so hand timing cannot be trusted for very short events. *How we know:* Reaction-time tests since the 1800s, and the simple test of catching a dropped ruler, give about 0.2 seconds for most people. That is why races decided by hundredths of a second are timed by light beams and cameras.

## How we found out
**Bernardino Ramazzini, Alice Hamilton, Grace Fryer and the radium dial painters, Harrison Martland, 1700 to 1928.** For most of history, people who were hurt at work were said to be unlucky or careless. Sickness from a trade was treated as part of the trade. It fitted what everyone saw: miners coughed, potters shook, and it had always been so. In the year 1700 an Italian doctor, Bernardino Ramazzini, published a book on the diseases of workers. He had gone into workshops and asked a question doctors did not ask: 'What is your work?' He matched each trade to its own illness. So the harm had causes, and causes can be changed. In 1910 the American doctor Alice Hamilton went from factory to factory in the state of Illinois, tracing lead poisoning. Owners told her that the workers were to blame for not washing. She showed that the lead dust in the air was to blame. In 1927 five radium dial painters took their employer to court. The case brought out that the company had known the danger, and in 1928 they won a settlement. Out of work like this came the modern rule that risks are named before work starts, and that the person in charge must reduce them.  
*What people believed before:* Accidents and work sicknesses are bad luck or the worker's own carelessness, so nothing can be planned. It was believable because each injury looked like a separate event. Only when people counted the cases did they see that the same work gave the same harm again and again.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Children are kept too safe. A childhood with no risk harms them more than scrapes and small burns would.** (Ellen Sandseter and Leif Kennair, psychologists in Norway, in 2011, and Mariana Brussoni and colleagues in Canada, in 2015; *debated*)  
  For: Children who climb, use real tools and handle fire with care learn to judge danger for themselves. A review of studies in 2015 found that such play went with better health and with more active children. It found no clear sign of more serious injuries.  
  Against: The studies are mostly of outdoor play in rich countries. They cannot say how much risk is the right amount. Some injuries, such as to an eye, do not heal, so many doctors and schools prefer to stay on the cautious side.
- **Hot water can freeze faster than cold water.** (Erasto Mpemba, a schoolboy in Tanzania, in 1963. He published it with the physicist Denis Osborne in 1969. Aristotle wrote about something like it in his Meteorology, book 1, chapter 12, in the 4th century BCE.; *debated*)  
  For: Mpemba put a hot ice-cream mix in the freezer and saw it freeze before his classmates' cooler mixes. His classmates and his teacher laughed at him. He kept asking, and Osborne tested it and saw the same. In 2020 Avinash Kumar and John Bechhoefer in Canada showed a clear effect of this kind with a tiny glass bead cooling in water.  
  Against: In 2016 Henry Burridge and Paul Linden in Britain repeated the water test with great care and found no such effect. They showed that where the thermometer sits in the cup can create a false one. Nobody has an agreed explanation for water.
- **When ordinary people fear a risk more than experts do, the people are not simply mistaken. They are counting things that the experts' numbers leave out.** (Paul Slovic, an American psychologist, in the journal Science in 1987; *debated*)  
  For: Experts usually count expected deaths in a year. Slovic found that other people also ask more questions. Is the risk chosen or forced on them? Is it understood? Could it harm many at once? Does it fall on children? These are real features of a risk.  
  Against: Fears that ignore the numbers lead people to dread rare harms and overlook common ones, such as road accidents. Many safety scientists hold that spending should follow the count of lives saved.

## Roots
You have chosen tools, named risks, written a prediction and checked it against the result. Now we ask what it means that a prediction can come true at all, and what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You wrote a number in your notebook before the sugar touched the warm water. Then the water gave a number of its own, and the two were close. You built your number from water you had watched earlier. So a pattern from the world was already at work in your thinking. Where your number missed, your thinking had not yet met that part of the world.  
*Habit it breaks:* We speak of 'my mind in here' and 'the world out there'. It sounds as if a prediction is made in one place and tested in another. That split makes a correct prediction look like a lucky match between two strangers.  
*Evidence:* In the dissolving test, your prediction for warm water came from your own results for cold water and room-warm water. Your reason in ink pointed at those two results. A prediction for something you had never watched at all would have been a blind guess.

- *who am i:* You are someone who expects. Every step you take predicts that the floor will hold, and every sip predicts the taste. When a prediction of yours comes true, something of the world's pattern is living in you. So you are not looking in at the world through a window. You are a part of the world that has learned how other parts behave.
- *what is life:* Many living beings act ahead of what is coming. A sunflower bud faces east before dawn, expecting the Sun. Some crows drop a nut on the road and wait for a wheel to crack it. A being who expects wrongly gets a surprise, and learns or suffers. Life can be seen as matter that has learned to expect, and to lower its own risks.
- *what is this world:* The world can be predicted in part. The tide keeps its table, and sugar keeps its habit in warm water. No one knows why the world has patterns that a mind can hold. The world also keeps surprises, such as tomorrow's cloud. A world with both pattern and surprise is one you can learn from and never finish learning.

A risk and a prediction are the same act looked at from two sides. Both are a mind running ahead of the present moment, using patterns it took in earlier. This can happen only if the one who knows and the thing known are made to the same pattern. Consciousness is the ground from which both arise, so a true prediction is that one ground recognising its own ways. Many scientists explain the match differently: brains were shaped over millions of years by the world they had to survive in. Both accounts agree on the plain fact you tested. You come to know by attending closely, and you come to know only what you have attended to.

- **Bhagavad Gita, chapter 2, verse 47:** Your right is to the work itself, and never to its fruits. Do not let the result be your reason for acting, and do not fall into doing nothing. (In practical work you control the choosing, the care and the doing. The result belongs to the world, and comes out as it comes. The verse and the notebook agree on one point: do the work fully, write the prediction honestly, and then accept the answer that arrives.)
- **The Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65:** The Buddha told the Kalama people not to accept a teaching because it is repeated, or is in a scripture, or comes from a respected teacher. When you know for yourselves that a thing leads to harm, give it up. When you know for yourselves that it leads to good, take it up. (This is the rule Selvi follows in the story when she marks her grandmother and the phone in the same way. A prediction is judged by what happens and not by who made it. The sutta is about how to live, and the lesson borrows only its method.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 3, the story of Cook Ding, from China, about the 4th century BCE:** Cook Ding has used one knife for nineteen years and its edge is still like new. He explains that he no longer hacks. He lets the blade follow the natural gaps in what he is cutting. (A tool used the right way works with the thing and not against it. Reading at eye level and starting at the ruler's zero are small cases of the same fit between a hand, a tool and the world.)
- Science (idea): Several brain scientists, among them Karl Friston and Anil Seth, propose that a brain's main work is predicting what the senses will report next. The brain then corrects itself when it is surprised. On this account, seeing and hearing are themselves predictions that are being checked.
- Science (settled): Plants act ahead of events. Young sunflowers turn back to face east during the night, before the Sun rises. They keep doing so for a few days even under a lamp that does not move. Hagop Atamian and colleagues reported this in the journal Science in 2016.
- Other view, The Charvaka thinkers of ancient India, as reported in Madhava's Sarvadarshanasangraha, chapter 1, of the 14th century CE, and the Scottish philosopher David Hume in 1748: Only what is directly seen is certain. A prediction rests on the belief that tomorrow will be like yesterday, and nothing can prove that belief without using it. So a match between prediction and result shows a habit of the mind that has worked so far. It does not show any deep fit between mind and world.
- Practice, One breath of prediction (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Before you breathe in, guess how many slow counts your next breath out will last. Hold the number in your mind. Now breathe in, and breathe out slowly while I count. Was your guess close? Do it twice more. Notice the small moment of waiting between the guess and the answer. Notice who made the guess, and who gave the answer.
- Ask: Your body made the breath, and your mind made the guess about it. Are those two different makers, or one?
- Ask: When your prediction is wrong, is the surprise in the world or in you?
- Ask: A sunflower turns east before the Sun is up. Is the sunflower predicting? What would you look for to decide?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A knife is a hazard in a drawer and in a hand, yet the risk is different. So where is the danger: in the knife, in the person, or somewhere between them? (hint: Try changing only one of them at a time in your mind, and see if the danger changes.)
- *deep:* Your two hands disagreed about the same bowl of water, and the thermometer gave one number. Does that make the thermometer right and your hands wrong? (hint: Ask what each of them was built to report, and what the number leaves out.)
- *deep:* You moved the cup away from the table's edge and nothing was spilled. How can you ever know if moving the cup was what kept you safe? (hint: Think about what you would have to compare, and if you could ever see the thing that did not happen.)
- *deep:* If a prediction comes true, does that prove the reason behind it was right? (hint: Think of someone who predicts rain because a crow cawed, and then the rain comes. Was the cawing the reason?)
- *big:* Who should decide how much risk a test may have: the person doing it, the person hurt by it, or an expert with the numbers? (hint: Think of a case where these are three different people, and what each one knows that the others do not.)
- *big:* A snail, a plant or an ant cannot be asked if they agree to be in your test. Does that change what you may do? (hint: Think about how you would want a being much bigger than you to decide about you.)
- *big:* Marie Curie's work harmed her, and it also led to treatments that have saved many lives. Was the risk worth it, and who gets to say? (hint: Notice that she did not know the risk, and ask if that changes your answer.)
- *big:* If the world could be predicted perfectly, would your own choices be predicted too? (hint: Think about what happens if someone tells you their prediction of what you will do next.)
- *deep:* The sun has risen every day so far. Is that a good reason to predict that it will rise tomorrow, and can you say why without using the past again? (hint: Try to give a reason, then check if your reason quietly assumes that the future will be like the past.)

## Story: Two Forecasts on Pamban Shore
*A made-up story. The people are imagined. What is real: on the coast of Ramanathapuram district in Tamil Nadu, near Pamban and Rameswaram, women dive for seaweed around the small islands of the Gulf of Mannar, wearing goggles and wrapping cloth round their fingers against sharp rock. The numbers in the story are invented for the story.*

The boat was pulled half out of the water, and the sky over Pamban was still grey. Selvi, who was nine, sat on the edge of the boat with her school notebook. Her grandmother Muniyammal was winding strips of old sari cloth round each finger.

'Why the cloth, paatti?' asked Selvi, though she had asked before.

'Because the rock where the seaweed grows is sharp, and I need my fingers tomorrow also,' said Muniyammal. She held up her swimming goggles and looked at the strap. She pulled it twice and frowned. 'This one is tired.' She took a second pair from the bag. 'A diver with water in her eyes is a diver who cannot see the rock.'

Selvi's cousin Anthony, who was eleven, came down the sand with his phone held high. 'The weather office says wind from the north-east, 15 kilometres an hour now, 30 by two o'clock. Sea rough after two.'

Muniyammal did not look at the phone. She looked at the water for a long time. She watched how the waves folded over near the island, and which way the foam was dragged. 'The pull between the islands will turn before noon,' she said. 'When it turns, it runs hard out to the open side. We come up before that. The wind I do not know. It smells like any morning.'

'Two forecasts,' said Selvi. Her teacher had said that a prediction must be written before the test. She wrote on a clean page. Paatti: the current turns before 12. Phone: wind 30 by 2 o'clock. Paatti says nothing about the wind. The phone says nothing about the current.

Anthony laughed. 'You are marking paatti like an exam.'

'I am marking the phone also,' said Selvi.

Four women went out in the boat, and Selvi and Anthony went with them to mind the rope. At the island the divers slid into the water with their net bags. Selvi tied a coconut husk to a string and let the husk float beside the boat. For two hours the husk hung lazily on the island side.

At twenty minutes to twelve the string went tight. The husk swung round and pointed to the open sea. Selvi wrote: 11.40. She banged the side of the boat with the oar, three times, as she had been told. One by one the heads came up.

They were on the sand by half past twelve, with the bags only three-quarters full. Kaliammal, one of the divers, was not pleased. 'Another hour and my bag was full. The sea is like a plate.'

It was. At one o'clock the sea was a plate. At two o'clock Anthony held up a strip of cloth and counted how it flapped. 'This is not 30,' he said. 'This is nothing.' At three, the palm leaves began to hiss, and by four the waves were white all the way to the island.

Selvi marked her page. Paatti's current: predicted before 12, came at 11.40. Close. Phone's wind: predicted 2, came about 4. Two hours late, but it came.

'So paatti wins,' said Anthony.

'Paatti wins on the current,' said Selvi. 'She had nothing to say on the wind. The phone has never put its hand in this water. Paatti has, for forty years. The phone has watched the whole sky from far above, and she has not.' She chewed her pencil. 'Each one is good exactly where it has looked.'

Muniyammal was unwinding the cloth from her fingers. There was not one cut on them.

Kaliammal was still counting her loss. 'We came up at 11.40, and for four hours nothing happened. We could have stayed.'

'Could we?' said Muniyammal. 'You were on the open side. Did you feel the pull when you came up?'

Kaliammal said nothing for a moment. 'A little,' she said.

Selvi looked at her page. There was a line on it for what was predicted, and a line for what happened. There was no line for what would have happened if they had stayed. She did not know what to write there. Nobody did.

**For the parent:** A prediction is only as good as what its maker has watched. Selvi could mark the two forecasts because she wrote both down first and judged them by the sea, not by who made them. But she could not mark the safety. When a danger is avoided, the harm that did not happen leaves nothing to measure.

- Ask: Muniyammal's fingers had no cuts. Is that evidence that the cloth worked? What else would you need to know?
- Ask: The phone was two hours late about the wind. Would you call that prediction accurate? What would your answer depend on?
- Ask: Was coming up at 11.40 the right choice, even though nothing went wrong afterwards? How could anyone tell?

  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Read three plan cards. Guess their kind.
  - Note: Cutting is for a grown-up if the child is under 8. Do not read the card backs aloud during play. Keep the cards in an envelope for next time.
- **g1s2 (12 min)**

  *Child mode:* Finish the rounds. Add up the points.
  - Note: No test has zero risk. If someone says a plan 'makes it totally safe', ask what could still go wrong.

## Experiments
### e1 · The dissolving-time test: choose your tools, name the risks, predict, then judge your prediction (45 min)
- **question:** How long does a level teaspoon of sugar take to dissolve in cold, room-warm and warm water? Can you predict the warm time from the first two?
- **predict:** Before the warm test, use your cold and room-warm times to write a number for warm water. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** These stay the same. The cup is filled to the same line. The sugar comes from the same bag, one level teaspoon each time. The spoon, the stirring and the stop signal do not change. The same person starts and stops the clock. Only the warmth of the water changes. Cold and room-warm are each run twice and warm is run three times, because a finger on a stopwatch is never exactly on time. In the second round, only the solid changes.
- **safety:** Use warm tap water only, never boiling water, and let a grown-up pour it. Stand the cups on a tray. Use plastic and not glass. Do not taste anything in the test.
- **record:** Fill the table for cold, room-warm and warm water. Write your prediction and its reason before the warm test. After it, write the middle value, the spread and the miss. Then write one sentence each for what you thought, what you saw and why. Do the same for rock salt or jaggery.
- **results table:** Water | Temperature in degrees Celsius, if you have a thermometer | Run 1 (seconds) | Run 2 (seconds) | Run 3 (seconds) | Middle value (seconds)
- **e1s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put these on the table: a measuring cup, a one-litre jug, a ruler, a phone with a stopwatch and a wall clock. Add a thermometer if you have one. Ask your child to pick one tool for each question: how much water, how long, and how warm.  
  *Parent says:* We will time sugar in water. We need to know three things. How much water? How long does the sugar take? How warm is the water? Which tool answers each one, and why that one?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the tools. Pick one for each question. Say why you chose it.
  - Ask: Which tool will you use to time the sugar, and why not the wall clock? (good: The phone stopwatch, because it shows seconds, and the wall clock has no seconds hand.)
  - Note: Good picks are the measuring cup for the water, the phone stopwatch for the time and the thermometer for the warmth. The wall clock has no seconds hand, so it cannot show 20 seconds. A ruler measures length, which this test does not need. If you have no thermometer, skip the numbers and call the waters cold, room and warm. Never use a glass thermometer that holds mercury.
- **e1s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Walk through the whole test in your head, from setting up to cleaning up. In a notebook, write three columns: What could hurt, Who could be hurt, and My plan. Fill in a row for each risk you find.  
  *Parent says:* Before we start, let us ask what could go wrong. Take each step in turn. What could hurt someone? Who could it hurt? How likely is it? Then we write one plan for each.  
  *Child mode:* Think through each step. Write what could hurt, who, and your plan.
  - Note: Let your child name as many risks as they can. Then add the ones they miss. Warm water could scald, so we use warm tap water and never boiling water, and a grown-up pours it. A spill could make the floor slippery, so the cups stand on a tray and a cloth is ready. A glass cup could crack, so use plastic. Nothing here is meant to be eaten, so nobody tastes anything. A small child or a pet could knock things over, so keep them in another room. Sugar water draws ants, so wipe up at the end.
  - Check: Which plan cuts the scald risk the most? → **Use warm tap water and never boiling water**
- **e1s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set a tray in the middle of the table and tie back long hair. Put a dark cloth on the tray and a clear cup on the cloth. Keep a spill cloth beside you. Read this checklist aloud and tick each line: tray down, hair tied, cloth ready, grown-up near, prediction paper ready. Fill a teaspoon with sugar and slide a stiff card across the top to level it. Then do one practice run with cold water, and do not write the time down.  
  *Parent says:* Everything has its place. Now one practice run. Pour water to the line and add one level spoon of sugar. Start the clock as the sugar touches the water. Stir one slow circle each second, and say the count aloud.  
  *Child mode:* Set the tray and cup. Do one practice run. Stir one circle each second.
  - Note: The edge of a ruler works as well as a card for levelling. The run ends when no crystals show on the dark cloth or in the water, and the water looks clear. If the practice run is still cloudy after 2 minutes, pour it away and stir two circles each second in every cup from now on. If it clears in under 10 seconds, your sugar is very fine, so stir one circle every two seconds in every cup. Keep your chosen stirring the same for the whole test.
- **e1s4 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the cup to the line with cold water from the fridge bottle. Add one level teaspoon of sugar, start the clock and stir one circle each second. Stop the clock when the water is clear, and write the time. Do two runs with cold water and two with room-warm water from a jug that has stood out for an hour. Read each jug's temperature after a minute.  
  *Parent says:* Before the first run, which water do you think will be faster, cold or room-warm? Why do you think so? Now measure and see.  
  *Child mode:* Do two cold runs and two room-warm runs. Write each time.
  - Ask: Which water do you think will be faster, cold or room-warm, and why? (good: Room-warm, because sugar vanishes faster in warm tea than in cold juice.)
  - Note: Use fresh water and the same spoon each time, and rinse the cup between runs. Do not tell your child the answer. If two runs of the same water differ by a few seconds, that is normal. A finger on a stopwatch is never exactly on time. In winter the room water may be nearly as cold as the fridge water. Write the real numbers whatever they are.
- **e1s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover the results table. On a slip of paper, write: Warm tap water. I predict ___ seconds, because ___. Write in ink. Fold the slip and put it under the tray.  
  *Parent says:* Warm tap water is warmer than both of your cups. What time do you expect? Use your cold and room-warm times. Write the number and your reason in ink.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your two times. Write a number for warm water. Write your reason.
  - Ask: What number did you write, and what in your results made you choose it? (good: A number lower than the room-warm time, with a reason taken from the first two results.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A good reason uses the data. For example: 'Cold took 70 seconds and room-warm took 45. Warmer water should save about 25 more, so about 20.' Any honest number with a reason is fine. The ink matters. A number that can be changed after the result cannot be judged.
- **e1s6 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up fills a jug with warm tap water. Check it with the inside of a wrist or a thermometer. It must feel comfortably warm and read under 45 degrees Celsius. The grown-up pours one cup to the line. Time three runs, with fresh warm water for each run, and write each time.  
  *Parent says:* I will pour. You press start when the sugar touches the water. Same spoon, same stirring, same stop signal. We do three runs, because one run can be a fluke.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours the warm water. You do the timing. Do three runs.
  - Note: The child never carries or pours the warm water. If your tap gives hot water, add cold water until it feels comfortably warm on the inside of your wrist. Warm water cools a little between runs, so use fresh water each time. Whatever numbers you get are the real result. If warm water clears in under 10 seconds, the finger on the stopwatch matters more, so say so in the table.
- **e1s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Open the slip. Find the middle value of the three warm runs. Find the spread, which is the biggest time minus the smallest. Subtract your prediction from the middle value to get the miss. Write which way you missed, too short or too long.  
  *Parent says:* Your three runs differ from each other by this much. That wobble is the spread. Is your miss smaller than the wobble? If so, this test cannot tell your prediction from the truth.  
  *Child mode:* Find the middle time and the spread. Find how far off you were.
  - Ask: Was your miss bigger or smaller than the spread? What does that tell you? (good: If smaller, the prediction was as close as the test can judge. If bigger, the doing, the tool or the reason needs another look.)
  - Note: If the miss is inside the spread, the prediction is as accurate as this test can show. If it is outside, it is a real miss. Then ask three questions in this order. Did we do every run the same way? Was the tool the same each time? Is the reason about warmth missing something? A test like this cannot say why the numbers fall as they do. Ask your child what else they would test.
  - Think: If a friend did this test with the same sugar, would they get the same numbers? What would make them differ?
- **e1s8 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Choose rock salt or powdered jaggery, whichever you have. On a new slip, predict in ink how long one level teaspoon takes in warm water, and why. A grown-up pours warm water, and you time two runs. Stop at 3 minutes if the water is not clear.  
  *Parent says:* This one is new. Sugar and rock salt are alike in one way and different in another. What do you think will stay the same, and what will change? Write your number and your reason first.  
  *Child mode:* Write your guess and your reason. Time two runs. Stop at 3 minutes.
  - Note: Good reasons: both dissolve in water, so warmth should still help. Rock salt crystals are bigger, so it may be slower than sugar. Powdered jaggery has tiny grains, so it may be about as fast as sugar. Jaggery water stays brown, so stop when no grains show on the dark cloth. If the water is still cloudy at 3 minutes, write 'more than 180 seconds'. A prediction of 'slower than sugar' is then right about the direction. Pour the water down the sink, rinse the cups and wipe the table, because sugar water draws ants.
### e2 · The falling ruler: how late is your finger on a stopwatch? (25 min)
- **question:** How far does a ruler fall before you catch it, and what does that tell us about how late a finger on a stopwatch is?
- **predict:** Before you drop the first ruler, guess how many centimetres it will fall before you catch it. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** These stay the same: the ruler, the partner, the start position of your fingers and the eyes-on-zero rule. The partner lets go at a different time every drop, so that you cannot guess. For the second hand, only the hand changes. Ten drops are done for each hand, because one catch can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Use a plastic or wooden ruler, not a metal one with sharp edges. The partner lets the ruler fall straight down and never swings or flicks it. Keep faces well away from the ruler.
- **record:** Write ten numbers for each hand. Circle the middle value for each. Find the spread for each hand. Then write one sentence each for what you thought, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Drop | Right hand (centimetres) | Left hand (centimetres)
- **e2s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask a partner to hold a 30 centimetre ruler by its top end, with the zero end hanging down. Put your thumb and first finger around the zero mark, not touching the ruler. Look at the zero mark, not at your partner's hand.  
  *Parent says:* A ruler will drop when you least expect it. You must close your fingers as fast as you can. Before we start, how many centimetres do you think it will fall before you catch it, and why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Hold your fingers round the zero mark. Guess how far the ruler will fall.
  - Ask: How far do you think the ruler will fall, and why? (good: A small number such as 5 centimetres, with a reason such as 'my hand is fast'.)
  - Note: Write the guess down. Most children guess 2 to 5 centimetres. Do not say the real answer. Without a partner, ask any grown-up or friend to drop the ruler.
- **e2s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Practise three drops without writing anything. The partner lets go at a different moment each time, anywhere between 2 and 5 seconds after saying 'ready'. Do not count aloud.  
  *Parent says:* I will say 'ready', wait a little, and then let go. Close your fingers as soon as you see it move.  
  *Child mode:* Do three practice drops. Watch the zero mark.
  - Note: A random wait stops your child from guessing the moment. If the ruler falls right through, check that eyes are on the zero mark and fingers are open about 3 centimetres wide.
- **e2s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do ten real drops with the same hand. After each one, read the number on the ruler at the top of your thumb. Write it in the table. Slide the ruler back into place each time.  
  *Parent says:* Ten drops, ten numbers. Write each one. Some will be bigger and some smaller. That is normal.  
  *Child mode:* Do ten drops. Write each number.
  - Note: Typical numbers are between 12 and 22 centimetres. If a drop goes past 30 centimetres, do it again, because the child was probably looking away.
- **e2s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now swap hands. First write a guess for the other hand, and a reason. Then do ten drops with that hand and write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Will the other hand catch higher or lower, or about the same? Write your guess and your reason first. Then try it.  
  *Child mode:* Guess which hand is faster, and why. Try ten drops.
  - Ask: Which hand do you think is faster, and why? (good: Either answer is fine, with a reason. The test will show whether any difference is bigger than the spread.)
  - Note: Most children find the two hands differ by only a few centimetres, which is often smaller than the spread within one hand. If it is, the test cannot tell the hands apart.
- **e2s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Use the table to turn each number into a time. Here is the table. 5 centimetres is 0.10 seconds. 10 is 0.14 and 15 is 0.18. 20 is 0.20. 25 is 0.23 and 30 is 0.25. Find the middle number for each hand and its time. Find the spread for each hand.  
  *Parent says:* Your hand took about this many tenths of a second to answer your eyes. A stopwatch press has the same delay.  
  *Child mode:* Look up your middle number in the table. Write the time.
  - Note: The times come from the physics of falling things, and they are rounded. You do not need the sum, only the table.
- **e2s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Think of timing a toy car that rolls for 3 seconds by hand. Say how much of the time a delay of 0.2 seconds is. Then think of a snap that lasts 0.3 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* A finger is about a fifth of a second late. For a long event, that is small. For a very short one, it is most of the time. So when should you trust a hand-timed number?  
  *Child mode:* A finger is 0.2 seconds late. When does that matter?
  - Ask: When can you trust a hand-timed number, and when not? (good: For long events a fifth of a second is small. For very short events it is most of the result, so use a camera or a longer distance.)
  - Note: 0.2 out of 3 is about one in fifteen. 0.2 out of 0.3 is two thirds. Races decided by hundredths of a second use light beams and cameras for this reason.

## Activities
### a1 · Three bowls of water for two hands (12 min)
- **safety:** Use warm tap water only, never hot water. A grown-up pours it and checks it on the inside of a wrist first.
- **record:** Write what the left hand said and what the right hand said. Write the thermometer number if you have one.
- **a1s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Set three bowls in a row on a tray. A grown-up fills the left bowl with cold water. The right bowl gets warm tap water that feels comfortable on the inside of a wrist. The middle bowl gets half cold and half warm.  
  *Parent says:* We will use three bowls of water. One is cold, one is warm and one is in between. Do not touch them yet. What do you think will happen to your hands?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the three bowls. A grown-up fills them.
  - Note: Cold bowl: cold tap water, with four ice cubes in summer. Warm bowl: warm tap water, comfortable on the inside of your wrist and under 45 degrees Celsius. Middle bowl: one cup from each, with no ice cubes. If your tap is only slightly warm in the hot months, the test still works, because the cold bowl is much colder than the middle one.
- **a1s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what each hand will say about the middle bowl. Then put the left hand in the cold bowl and the right hand in the warm bowl. Keep them there for one minute.  
  *Parent says:* Left hand in the cold bowl, right hand in the warm bowl. Wait for one minute. Before you move them, guess what each hand will feel when it goes into the middle bowl. Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put one hand in each outer bowl. Wait one minute. Make a guess.
  - Ask: What do you think each hand will feel in the middle bowl, and why? (good: Any guess with a reason is fine. Many children think both hands will feel the same.)
- **a1s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put both hands into the middle bowl at the same time. Ask what each hand says. Write down what the left hand says and what the right hand says.  
  *Parent says:* Both hands into the middle bowl, now. What does the left hand say? What does the right hand say? Is it the same water?  
  *Child mode:* Put both hands in the middle bowl. What does each hand say?
  - Note: The left hand, which came from cold, feels warm. The right hand, which came from warm, feels cool. The same water feels different to each.
- **a1s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand a thermometer in the middle bowl and read it after one minute at eye level. Ask whose report your child trusts, and what they would use to check a baby's bath.  
  *Parent says:* The thermometer gives one number for the middle bowl. Your hands gave two answers. Is either hand wrong? What would you use to check that a baby's bath is safe?  
  *Child mode:* Read the thermometer. Compare it with what your hands said.
  - Ask: Which would you trust to check a baby's bath, and why? (good: A thermometer, because it gives one number that does not depend on where it just was.)
  - Note: Skin reports a change from where it just was. A thermometer reports against a fixed scale. Neither is wrong. Tools give numbers, and senses notice what is worth measuring. If you have no thermometer, a grown-up's wrist is a third opinion.
  - Think: Your hands compare. What else in your day is judged by comparing and not by a number?
### a2 · Match the tool to the job (20 min)
- **safety:** Rice grains and bottle caps are small enough for a toddler to swallow. Keep them away from babies and small children, and put them away when you finish. Wipe up any water at once.
- **record:** Fill a table with the job, the quantity, the tool you chose, its smallest mark and why another tool would not fit.
- **a2s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set out a ruler, a one-litre jug marked every 100 millilitres, and a medicine cup or a teaspoon. Add a phone stopwatch and a bottle cap. Job one is to find how much water one bottle cap holds. Ask your child to pick a tool.  
  *Parent says:* How much water fits in this bottle cap? Which tool will you pick? What is the smallest mark on each one? Which tool can show an amount as small as this?  
  *Child mode:* Pick a tool to measure the water in a cap. Check its smallest mark.
  - Ask: Why can the jug not measure the water in one cap? (good: Its smallest mark is 100 millilitres, which is far bigger than the water in a cap.)
  - Note: The jug cannot show it, because its smallest mark is far bigger than the cap's water. If your cup shows 1 millilitre marks, fill the cap to the brim ten times into the cup and read the total. Divide by 10. With only a teaspoon, count how many capfuls fill it. A teaspoon holds about 5 millilitres, so a teaspoon that takes 3 capfuls means a cap holds about 1.7 millilitres. Most caps hold between 1 and 3 millilitres.
- **a2s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Job two is the length of one grain of rice. Lay ten grains in a row, touching end to end, along the ruler. Read the whole length and divide by ten. Then measure one grain alone and compare.  
  *Parent says:* How long is one grain of rice? Measure one grain, then measure ten in a line and divide by ten. Which gives you the more careful answer? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Measure one grain. Then measure ten in a row and divide by ten.
  - Note: A grain is about 6 to 9 millimetres. One grain alone is read to the nearest millimetre, which is a big share of its length. Ten grains in a row spread that small error over ten grains. Line up the first grain at the zero mark, not the end of the ruler.
- **a2s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Job three is the time a marble or small ball takes to roll along a book ramp. Time it with the phone stopwatch. Then try the wall clock with no seconds hand. Say which tool fits the job and why.  
  *Parent says:* How long does the ball take to roll down? Time it with the phone, then try to time it with the wall clock. What happens? Which tool fits?  
  *Child mode:* Time the roll with the phone. Try the wall clock too.
  - Note: The roll takes about 1 to 2 seconds. A clock with no seconds hand cannot show a time this short. The phone shows tenths and hundredths of a second, though your finger is about 0.2 seconds late. Run it three times.
- **a2s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill a table with four columns: the job, the quantity, the tool you chose and the smallest mark of that tool. Add a fifth column for a tool that would not fit, and why.  
  *Parent says:* For each job, which quantity were you finding out? Which tool fitted, and which did not? Would you use this ruler to measure our room? Why not?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the table. Write why each unfit tool would not do.
  - Note: Do not rank tools as good or bad. A tool fits when its smallest mark is much smaller than your amount and it is not so fine that you must repeat it many times. A 15 centimetre ruler could measure a room, but you would lay it down about 40 times and each laying adds a small mistake.
  - Think: What job could you do today with no tool at all, only your senses? When would a tool do better?
### a3 · How many drops fit on a coin? Predict, test and judge (15 min)
- **safety:** Coins are small enough for a toddler to swallow, so keep them away from babies and small children. Wipe up spilled water at once so nobody slips.
- **record:** Write each guess and its reason. Write three counts for each coin. Find the middle count and the spread. Write whether each guess was inside or outside the spread.
- **a3s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wipe the small coin dry and lay it flat on a dry tray. Fill the cup with water. Ask your child to write how many drops will fit before the water spills over, and why.  
  *Parent says:* How many drops of water can sit on this coin before it spills over? Write your number and why you think so, before we try.  
  *Child mode:* Write how many drops you think will fit. Write why.
  - Note: Most families see between 10 and 40 drops on a small coin, so any guess is fine. A clean, dry coin is the key. A greasy coin makes the drops flatten and spill sooner. Wipe it with a dry cloth each time.
- **a3s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the dropper or straw just above the coin. Add drops one at a time and count aloud until the water spills over the edge. Dry the coin. Do this three times and write each count.  
  *Parent says:* Drop, count, drop, count. Stop when the water runs over the edge. Do it three times. Are the counts the same?  
  *Child mode:* Add drops one at a time. Count until it spills. Do three runs.
  - Note: To use a straw as a dropper, dip it in the water, put a finger over the top end and lift. Then ease your finger off to let drops fall. Keep the same height above the coin every time. The three counts will differ by a few drops. That is the spread.
- **a3s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the bigger coin on the tray. Ask your child to predict its count from the small coin's result, with a reason. Then do three runs the same way and write each count.  
  *Parent says:* This coin is wider. How many drops do you think it will hold? Use what you found out with the small coin. Write your number and your reason first.  
  *Child mode:* Guess for the bigger coin. Then do three runs.
  - Ask: What number did you write for the bigger coin, and why? (good: A larger number than the small coin's, with a reason about the bigger top.)
  - Note: A wider coin has a bigger top and holds more, though not in exact proportion. A good reason says that a bigger flat top has room for more water.
- **a3s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the middle count and the spread for each coin. Compare each miss with the spread. Say which prediction was closer, and whether you missed by more than the wobble of your own counts.  
  *Parent says:* Was your guess inside the spread or outside it? Which guess was closer, the first or the second? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Find the middle and the spread for each coin. Judge your two guesses.
  - Note: You may find the second guess was closer, because the child had data to build on. Do not push this. Let the numbers say it.
  - Think: Would a drop that is twice as big change your counts? Which tool would you change to get a fairer count?
### a4 · A risk hunt in your own kitchen (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up stays beside the child. Nothing in the kitchen is touched or used.
- **record:** Write five hazards, the risk of each today and a plan for the riskiest, with its kind.
- **a4s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Walk through the kitchen with your child and a grown-up. Point out five hazards, such as the gas stove, a sharp tool, a hot pan, a wet floor and a plug point. Write each on a card.  
  *Parent says:* Let us look for hazards in our kitchen. A hazard is anything that could cause harm. Can you find five? Do not touch them. Just point and name.  
  *Child mode:* Walk through the kitchen. Name five hazards. Do not touch them.
  - Note: Stay beside your child the whole time and keep them from touching anything on the list. The aim is to see, not to handle. Other good hazards are a glass jar on a high shelf, a steam cooker and a mat that slides.
- **a4s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take each card and ask: how likely is harm today, and how bad would it be? Write low, medium or high for the risk. Then say what would make the risk higher, and what would make it lower.

  *Child mode:* Rate each risk low, medium or high. Say what would change it.
  - Ask: What would make the risk of this one higher today, and what would make it lower? (good: Higher with a hurry, a child nearby or a wet floor. Lower with a barrier, a grown-up present or the thing put away.)

### p1 · A week of drying forecasts: how well can you predict a wet cloth? (85 min, 6 days)
- **safety:** Hang the cloth at head height or lower. Do not climb or lean over a balcony rail to reach a line. Wipe up any water on the floor.
- **record:** Keep a table with the day, the place, your prediction, your reason and the time the cloth took to dry. Add the spread and the miss for each day. Draw a graph of predictions and results.
- **schedule:** day 1: List the risks. Wet, wring and hang the cloth. Check every hour until dry. (25 min); day 2: Predict from Day 1. Hang the cloth again in the same place and check every hour. (10 min); day 3: Predict from Days 1 and 2. Hang the cloth and check every hour. (10 min); day 4: Predict for a new place. Hang the cloth there and check every hour. (15 min); day 5: Find the spread of Days 1 to 3 and judge each prediction. (10 min); day 6: Draw the graph and write what you learned. (15 min)
- **p1s1 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose one small cotton cloth, such as a handkerchief. Wet it in a bowl of water for one minute, then twist it ten times to wring it out. Hang it at head height in the spot you chose. Write the time. Check it every hour until it feels dry. Write down the time.  
  *Parent says:* How long will a wet cloth take to dry? Write a risk list first. Hang the cloth low, where nobody must climb or lean. Then guess the time, with a reason, and check every hour.  
  *Child mode:* List the risks. Wet the cloth, wring it and hang it. Check every hour.
  - Note: Risks to name: a wet floor under the line, climbing to reach a high line, and leaning over a balcony rail. Hang the cloth at head height or lower, with a cloth or tray underneath. A cloth is dry when it feels exactly like a dry cloth of the same kind, judged by the same person each day. With a kitchen scale you can weigh the cloth each hour, and it is dry when two weighings in a row match. In a very humid week the cloth may stay damp into the night. Then hang it in front of a fan, in the same place on every day, and write the time you checked. A chair back or a door handle also works for hanging.
  - Wait: Check once an hour until the cloth is dry
- **p1s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wet, wring and hang the cloth in the same spot in the same way. Before you hang it, write a prediction for the drying time using Day 1, with a reason. Check every hour. Write the time it dried.  
  *Parent says:* Yesterday the cloth took this long. How long do you think it will take today? Write your number and your reason in ink before you hang it.  
  *Child mode:* Write a prediction. Hang the cloth. Check every hour.
  - Note: Use the same cloth, the same twist and the same place. The weather may differ from day to day. That is what makes a spread.
- **p1s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Repeat Day 2 exactly. Predict first, using Days 1 and 2. Then write the time it dried.  
  *Parent says:* Now you have two days of data. Can you predict better? Write the number and reason first.  
  *Child mode:* Predict from two days. Hang the cloth. Check every hour.
  - Note: Do not tell your child how good the prediction was until the cloth is dry.
- **p1s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose a new place for the cloth, such as a shady corner or a window with sun. Predict the drying time there, with a reason from Days 1 to 3. Then wet, wring and hang the cloth in the new place, and check every hour.  
  *Parent says:* This place is new. What stays the same, and what changes? Write your number and your reason before you start.  
  *Child mode:* Predict for the new place. Hang the cloth there. Check every hour.
  - Note: A sunny place should be quicker and a shady one slower, but the size of the change is not obvious. This is a prediction about something unfamiliar, so a miss is expected and useful.
- **p1s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the middle time and the spread of Days 1 to 3. Compare each of your predictions with the result, and say which were inside the spread. Then compare the new place's prediction with its result.  
  *Parent says:* The three days differ from each other. That wobble is the spread. Which of your predictions were inside it? What did the new place teach you?  
  *Child mode:* Find the spread of Days 1 to 3. Judge each of your predictions.
  - Note: If the days differ a lot, the weather probably changed. A forecast cannot be sharper than the natural spread of what it forecasts.
- **p1s6 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a graph with the days across and the drying time up. Mark each prediction with a cross and each result with a dot. Write what you learned about predicting, and what you would measure differently.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your crosses and your dots. Were they close? Did your predictions get better? What would help you predict better next time?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the graph. Write what you learned.
  - Note: A good write-up says which of the three places, the doing, the tool or the reason, explained the biggest miss.
  - Think: Is a forecast of 'it will dry by evening' close enough, or do you need it to the minute? What decides?

### ch2 · Make your own measuring cup from a bottle (35 min)
- **problem:** Turn a clear plastic bottle into a measuring cup with a mark every 25 millilitres up to 200. Your marks must agree with a real measuring cup to within 10 millilitres. There is more than one good way.
- **safety:** Only a grown-up cuts a bottle, and the cut edge is covered with tape, because cut plastic can be sharp. Use no glass. Wipe up water as you go.

- **constraints:** Use a plastic bottle and no glass.; Use only tape, a pen, water and a trusted measure to mark it.; Check at least three of your marks against a real measuring cup.; Write down the error of each mark.
- **general:** Pour a trusted 25 millilitres in at a time and mark each line on tape.; Use a bottle with straight sides, so equal amounts make equal gaps.; Mark from the bottom up, and read at eye level each time.
- **creative:** Ask a grown-up to cut the top off a wide bottle to make a cup with a handle, then mark the inside.; Use a narrow clear tube or a long bottle, so each millilitre is a larger gap and the cup is more exact.; Mark a bottle in tablespoons or katoris, for a cooking cup in the family's own units.

  *Child mode:* Choose your trusted measure. Decide your smallest mark.

## Worksheets
### Tools, risks and predictions (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which tool answers 'How much water is in this tumbler?'  
   Options: A measuring cup / A ruler / A stopwatch  
   **Answer:** A measuring cup. *Why:* Water in a tumbler is a volume, and a measuring cup measures volume. A ruler measures length and a stopwatch measures time.
2. (fill) A thermometer measures ____.  
   **Answer:** ['temperature', 'how hot or cold']. *Why:* Each tool measures one kind of quantity. A thermometer is built for temperature.
3. (mcq) You press the zero button on a kitchen scale after putting the empty bowl on. Why?  
   Options: So the bowl weighs nothing in real life / To switch the scale on / So it shows only what you add  
   **Answer:** So it shows only what you add. *Why:* The bowl has a mass of its own. Setting zero with the bowl on lets the scale show the food alone.
4. (truefalse) A thermometer shows the true number the moment it touches the water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A thermometer needs about a minute for its number to stop changing. Read it after it settles.
5. (match) Match each job to the tool that fits it.  
   **Answer:** How long a toy car rolls → A phone stopwatch; How long a leaf is → A ruler; How much milk fits in a glass → A measuring cup; How hot the tea is → A thermometer. *Why:* Each job asks about one quantity: time, length, volume or temperature. Each tool is made to measure one of them.
6. (sort) Sort each one into hazard or plan to cut a risk.  
   **Answer:** warm water on a table → Hazard; a tray under the cups → Plan to cut a risk; a spill on the floor → Hazard; tied-back hair → Plan to cut a risk; a cracked glass → Hazard; a plastic cup in place of glass → Plan to cut a risk. *Why:* A hazard is a thing that could cause harm. A plan changes the set-up, so that harm is less likely or smaller.
7. (mcq) A spill on the floor is a hazard. Which makes the risk of a slip smaller?  
   Options: Leaving it until the test ends / Wiping it up at once / Walking round it faster  
   **Answer:** Wiping it up at once. *Why:* Wiping removes the hazard from where people walk. Leaving it or hurrying past keeps the chance of a slip high.
8. (order) Put these steps of a safe test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['List the risks', 'Set up the tray and tools', 'Do the test', 'Clear up and wash hands']. *Why:* Risks come first, so the set-up can include their plans. Then you work. Tidying and washing close the test.
9. (truefalse) If something unexpected happens in a test, keep going and fix it afterwards.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A plan cannot cover everything. Stop, step back, tell someone and think before you carry on.
10. (fill) A prediction says what you expect, and it also gives a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['reason']. *Why:* The reason lets others check your thinking. If the result differs, it shows what to look at again.
11. (mcq) You predicted 30 seconds. The result was 36 seconds. What is the difference?  
   Options: 66 seconds / 30 seconds / 6 seconds  
   **Answer:** 6 seconds. *Why:* Subtract the smaller from the larger. 36 minus 30 is 6. Adding the two numbers is a common slip.
12. (mcq) Two predictions missed by 12 seconds. One result was 30 seconds, the other 10 minutes. Which miss matters more?  
   Options: The 10-minute one / The 30-second one / They matter the same  
   **Answer:** The 30-second one. *Why:* 12 seconds is a large share of 30 seconds and a tiny share of 10 minutes. Compare a miss with the size of the result.
13. (write) The big ice cube was predicted to melt first, but the small one did. Name two places to look for the cause.  
   **Answer:** A good answer names two places. The doing: were the cubes in the same water and place? The tool: was the same clock used? The reason: is the idea that bigger means first wrong?. *Why:* A miss can come from how the test was done, from the equipment, or from the idea behind the prediction. Check the first two before blaming the idea.
14. (draw) Draw a tray, three cups and a spill cloth. Mark where a baby or pet should not be.  
   **Answer:** A tray in the middle of a table with three cups on it. A cloth beside it. A cross where a small child or pet could reach.. *Why:* A tray and a clear space are barriers. They keep a spill and small hands away from each other.
15. (write) Predict how long a wet sock takes to dry on a line, and give a reason from something you have seen.  
   **Answer:** A time such as 'about 3 hours'. A reason from something seen, such as 'a wet shirt took that long yesterday'.. *Why:* A prediction without a reason is only a guess. Reasons come from patterns you have watched before.
16. (perspective) Two ways to plan a test that needs warm water.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says removing the hazard is the strongest plan. It also says the warm test teaches more but needs a grown-up and a tray, so it is a trade-off.. *Why:* Both views are sensible. The first is the stronger kind of plan. The second accepts a small risk for a clearer result, and that is a choice people make differently.
### Plans, spread and misses (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) You want the mass of one grain of rice on a scale that shows whole grams. What works?  
   Options: Weigh one grain and round up / Weigh a hundred grains, then divide / Use a bigger scale  
   **Answer:** Weigh a hundred grains, then divide. *Why:* One grain is far below the scale's smallest mark. A hundred grains give a reading you can see, and dividing by a hundred gives one grain.
2. (count) 100 grains of rice weigh 2 grams. What is the mass of one grain, in grams?  
   **Answer:** 0.02. *Why:* 2 divided by a hundred is 0.02 grams. This is how a tool that is too coarse for one grain can still tell you about it.
3. (truefalse) Holding a thermometer by its tip gives the most exact reading.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The tip then feels your fingers and not the water. Hold the thermometer by its top end.
4. (mcq) A thermometer reads 44, then 42, then settles at 41 after a minute. Which do you write down?  
   Options: 41 / 44 / 42  
   **Answer:** 41. *Why:* The reading changes until the thermometer has taken on the water's temperature. The settled number is the real reading.
5. (mcq) A glass jar can break. In which set-up is the risk highest?  
   Options: Standing on a mat on a table / Carried quickly across a tiled floor / Inside a closed cupboard  
   **Answer:** Carried quickly across a tiled floor. *Why:* The hazard is the same in all three. The risk rises with speed, a hard floor and a chance of dropping it.
6. (match) Match each plan to its kind.  
   **Answer:** Use cold water because the test works without warmth → Remove; Use warm tap water in place of boiling water → Swap; Keep a baby in another room → Separate; Roll up your sleeves → Protect. *Why:* Remove takes the hazard away. Swap makes it safer. Separate keeps people away from it. Protect only guards your body.
7. (sort) Sort each plan by when it happens.  
   **Answer:** list the risks → Before the test; put the tray down → Before the test; tie back long hair → Before the test; wipe a spill at once → During the test; stop when the cup cracks → During the test; step back and tell someone about a surprise → During the test. *Why:* A plan on paper protects nobody unless the hands follow it. Some steps are done in advance, and some are habits for the moment.
8. (mcq) You have timed ice cubes melting. Which prediction for crushed ice of the same mass has the best reason?  
   Options: Faster, because more ice touches the water / The same, because ice is ice / Nobody can say before trying it  
   **Answer:** Faster, because more ice touches the water. *Why:* Step from the known case. Crushed ice has the same ice but much more surface touching the water, so it should melt sooner.
9. (mcq) Runs of 20, 24 and 22 seconds. You predicted 21. Was the prediction accurate?  
   Options: No, none of the runs was 21 / No, it was lower than the middle run / Yes, it lies inside the spread  
   **Answer:** Yes, it lies inside the spread. *Why:* The three runs spread over 4 seconds. A prediction inside that spread is as close as the test can judge.
10. (fill) You predicted 50 seconds and the result was 40 seconds. The prediction was too ____.  
   **Answer:** ['long']. *Why:* Saying which way you missed helps: 50 was longer than 40, so you expected the sugar to be slower than it was.
11. (count) You predicted 40 seconds and the result was 50. The miss is what fraction of the result?  
   **Answer:** 1/5. *Why:* The difference is 10. 10 out of 50 is 1/5, which is 20 in every 100.
12. (order) Put these in order for judging a prediction.  
   **Answer:** ['Write the prediction in ink', 'Run the test three times', 'Find the spread of the runs', 'Compare the miss with the spread']. *Why:* The prediction must come first, or hindsight changes it. Repeated runs give the spread, and only then can a miss be judged.
13. (write) Your sugar took twice as long as predicted. List your three checks in order, with one thing to look for in each.  
   **Answer:** The doing: were all the cups stirred the same? The tool: was the spoon level each time? The reason: is something other than warmth, such as crystal size, also at work?. *Why:* Check the doing and the tool first, since they are easier to fix. Then question the idea behind the prediction.
14. (truefalse) If a result matches your prediction, your reason is proved right.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A different reason could give the same number. A match means the reason passed one test, and the next test can still go against it.
15. (perspective) A farmer and an engineer both use a forecast. Each must decide how close is close enough.  
   **Answer:** Neither is wrong. A strong answer says the purpose decides, because a sowing plan can absorb a miss that a machine part cannot.. *Why:* How close is close enough depends on the natural spread of the results and on what the answer is for.
16. (draw) Draw a risk list with three columns for a test with a steel bowl of warm water. Fill one row.  
   **Answer:** Three columns headed What could hurt, Who could be hurt, and My plan. One row such as: a spill makes the floor slippery, anyone walking by, a tray and a cloth.. *Why:* A risk assessment asks what could hurt, who, and what to do. Writing it down makes each answer clear.
17. (write) To test which food ants like, name one risk to the ants and one plan to reduce it.  
   **Answer:** A risk such as crushing, trapping or flooding the ants. A plan such as watching them where they already walk, putting a tiny grain of food near their path, and not shutting them in a jar.. *Why:* The risk assessment counts every living being the test touches, not only people. Ants feel and decide too, so they are on the list.
### Faulty checks and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Both predicted 30 seconds. Child A got 29, 31 and 30. Child B got 18, 42 and 30. Who is better supported?  
   Options: Child A, whose runs cluster tightly / Child B, because 30 was one of the runs / Both the same, since the middle is 30  
   **Answer:** Child A, whose runs cluster tightly. *Why:* A tight spread lets the prediction be judged sharply. A wide spread means the test itself cannot tell 30 from many other numbers.
2. (truefalse) A prediction that comes true proves that its reason is right.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Another reason could give the same number. A true prediction is one test passed, not a proof.
3. (mcq) Warm water took longer than cold. The warm cup was stirred gently and the cold one hard. What is at fault?  
   Options: The reason, because warm water is slower / The doing, because stirring differed / The thermometer  
   **Answer:** The doing, because stirring differed. *Why:* Only the warmth should change. Different stirring is another change, so the test cannot say what caused the result.
4. (mcq) A forecast says 80 centimetres of rain, plus or minus 5 per cent. 77 fall. Is that within the stated error?  
   Options: No, it was not exactly 80 / No, because 77 is lower / Yes, 77 lies between 76 and 84  
   **Answer:** Yes, 77 lies between 76 and 84. *Why:* A forecast with a stated error is judged against that error. The range is 76 to 84, and 77 sits inside it.
5. (write) Why does it matter to write a prediction in ink before the test?  
   **Answer:** A good answer says that after seeing a result, people remember expecting it, which is hindsight. A number in ink cannot change, so the miss can be judged honestly.. *Why:* Hindsight makes a result feel more expected than it was. A written prediction beats memory.
6. (mcq) A risk list names a scald, a slip and a broken cup. The test uses an untested powder. What is missing?  
   Options: Nothing, because three risks are enough / Treating the powder as hazardous until tested / Nothing, because powders are always safe  
   **Answer:** Treating the powder as hazardous until tested. *Why:* A list covers only the hazards known at the time. A new material is treated as hazardous until tests show it is not, and that is why we never taste in a test.
7. (sort) Sort each test by whether it is worth its risk.  
   **Answer:** a grown-up cutting an apple to watch it turn brown → Worth a small risk; using water at full boil when warm tap water works just as well → Not worth the risk; growing seeds on a window sill → Worth a small risk; tasting an unknown powder to see what it is → Not worth the risk. *Why:* The question is never only 'is it safe'. It is how big the risk is, what the test gives, and whether a safer way does the same job.
8. (match) Match each job to how big a miss it can accept.  
   **Answer:** A recipe that says 'about a cup' → A large miss; Taking an umbrella for tomorrow's rain → A fairly large miss; A dose of children's medicine → A very small miss; Cutting a part for a rocket engine → A tiny miss. *Why:* What counts as close enough depends on what the answer is used for, and on how bad a wrong answer would be.
9. (mcq) Two weather computers start from nearly the same numbers. Ten days on, their forecasts differ greatly. Why?  
   Options: Tiny differences at the start grow in weather / One computer must be broken / Weather has no rules at all  
   **Answer:** Tiny differences at the start grow in weather. *Why:* Weather follows fixed laws, but small differences in the start grow into large ones. So the forecast gets worse with each day ahead.
10. (write) Give a reason to predict the Sun will rise tomorrow without saying 'it always has'.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A good one says the Earth spins and keeps spinning unless something stops it, and we understand why the Sun seems to rise. The prediction comes from a cause, not only from the past.. *Why:* A reason made only of past days could not tell a lucky run from a rule. A cause we understand gives a stronger prediction.
11. (perspective) A prediction missed. Two scientists look at it differently.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A good one says that finding the hidden cause would settle the first view, and that a new rule predicting better would settle the second. Until then both can be reasonable.. *Why:* History holds both: sometimes the hidden cause was found, and sometimes the rule needed changing. A miss alone cannot say which.
12. (draw) Draw one bowl of warm water twice, with a high-risk and a low-risk set-up. Label three differences.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Experts forget simple steps too, most of all when the work is familiar. Checklists catch the slips that skill does not.
15. (write) Think of a guess of yours that missed. Which place explains it best: the doing, the tool or the reason?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A good answer names a real moment, picks one of the three places, and gives a reason for the choice.. *Why:* Asking where a miss came from turns a surprise into something to learn.
16. (mcq) You must time a snap that lasts 0.3 seconds. Why is timing it by hand a poor choice?  
   Options: A 0.2 second reaction is most of 0.3 / Phones cannot show tenths of a second / Such short times do not exist  
   **Answer:** A 0.2 second reaction is most of 0.3. *Why:* A person needs about a fifth of a second to react. For a very short event, that delay is a large share of the whole time.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) You must find how much water fits in a bottle cap. Which tool fits best?  
   Options: A one-litre jug marked every 100 millilitres / A school ruler / A medicine cup marked every 1 millilitre  
   **Answer:** A medicine cup marked every 1 millilitre. *Why:* A tool's smallest mark must be much smaller than your amount. The jug's smallest mark is far bigger than a cap of water, and a ruler measures length, not volume.
2. (mcq) A 15 centimetre ruler is used to measure a 6 metre room. What is the main problem?  
   Options: Laying it down 40 times adds 40 small mistakes / Its marks are too big / A ruler cannot measure length  
   **Answer:** Laying it down 40 times adds 40 small mistakes. *Why:* A tool that is too fine for the job adds many small mistakes. A tape would fit the job. The ruler's marks are tiny, and it does measure length, but only well over short distances.
3. (mcq) Water in a measuring cup looks higher from above than from the side. Which reading is right?  
   Options: The one from above / The one with your eye level / The average of the two  
   **Answer:** The one with your eye level. *Why:* Looking from above makes the water seem to stand at a different mark. Eye level is the one position that gives the true mark.
4. (mcq) A ruler's zero is 2 millimetres in from its end. You start measuring a pencil at the end, not at zero. The reading is...  
   Options: 2 millimetres too big / 2 millimetres too small / exactly right  
   **Answer:** 2 millimetres too small. *Why:* The scale starts counting 2 millimetres in from the end. So the first 2 millimetres of the pencil lie before the zero mark and are never counted. The reading is 2 millimetres less than the true length. Always line up the zero mark and not the edge.
5. (mcq) Which sentence about a knife is true?  
   Options: A hazard only in someone's hand / A hazard in a drawer, with a low risk / A risk in a drawer only  
   **Answer:** A hazard in a drawer, with a low risk. *Why:* A hazard is what could cause harm, so the knife is one everywhere. The risk is the chance and size of the harm, and that changes with how and where it is used.
6. (mcq) Why list the risks of a test before starting it?  
   Options: It proves the test is perfectly safe / It makes the test finish sooner / A named risk is easier to plan for  
   **Answer:** A named risk is easier to plan for. *Why:* Naming a risk lets you plan a way to make it smaller. No test has zero risk, so a list can never prove perfect safety.
7. (order) Put these ways to cut a risk in order, from strongest to weakest.  
   **Answer:** ['Remove the hazard', 'Swap it for a safer one', 'Put a barrier or distance between', 'Wear protection']. *Why:* Removing the hazard leaves nothing to go wrong. Swapping lowers it. A barrier keeps it away from people. Protection is weakest because the hazard is still there and the protection can slip.
8. (mcq) A test works with cold water or with boiling water. Which plan cuts the risk most?  
   Options: Use cold water / Boiling water, poured slowly / Boiling water, with oven gloves  
   **Answer:** Use cold water. *Why:* Cold water removes the scald hazard, and nothing is lost. Pouring slowly and gloves only make a hazard that is still there a little less likely to hurt.
9. (mcq) Halfway through a test, a cup cracks. What do you do?  
   Options: Pick up the pieces quickly / Finish the run, then clear up / Stop, step back and fetch a grown-up  
   **Answer:** Stop, step back and fetch a grown-up. *Why:* A plan cannot cover everything. When something unexpected happens, the safe habit is to stop and think before the hands carry on.
10. (mcq) Which of these is a prediction and not only a guess?  
   Options: Warm water will be quicker / Quicker, because cold took longer than room-warm / It will take about 30 seconds  
   **Answer:** Quicker, because cold took longer than room-warm. *Why:* A prediction carries a reason taken from what you know. The reason lets others check your thinking and tells you what to rethink if the result differs.
11. (mcq) You have timed sugar but never rock salt. What is the best first step to predict for rock salt?  
   Options: Compare what rock salt shares with sugar / Say nobody can predict a new thing / Pick any number, because it is new  
   **Answer:** Compare what rock salt shares with sugar. *Why:* Predicting the new works by stepping from a familiar case. Both dissolve, so warm should help, and rock salt crystals are bigger, so it may be slower.
12. (mcq) You predict 25 seconds. Three runs give 31, 34 and 37 seconds. Judged by the spread, was the prediction accurate?  
   Options: No, 25 is outside the runs' spread / Yes, because 25 is near 31 / Yes, any number under 40 is fine  
   **Answer:** No, 25 is outside the runs' spread. *Why:* The runs differ by 6 seconds without anything being wrong. A prediction outside that spread is a real miss, and 25 is 6 seconds below the lowest run. A number is not fine only because it is small.
13. (truefalse) Writing your prediction after you see the result is as good as writing it before.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. After people see a result, they honestly remember having expected it. A number written before the test cannot change.
14. (mcq) A prediction missed by a lot. Which check comes first?  
   Options: Whether each run was done the same way / Whether the idea behind it is wrong / Whether the thermometer is lying  
   **Answer:** Whether each run was done the same way. *Why:* Look at the doing first, then the tool, then the reason. The first two are easier to fix, and each is a likelier cause than a wrong idea.
15. (mcq) Two planets strayed from the sums. For one, an unseen planet was found. For the other, none ever was. What does that show?  
   Options: A miss always means a hidden object / A prediction that missed is useless / A miss alone cannot say what is wrong  
   **Answer:** A miss alone cannot say what is wrong. *Why:* In one case the cause was hidden outside the idea. In the other the idea itself needed changing. A miss is a clue, and more tests are needed to tell which.

## Applications
- Each year the India Meteorological Department forecasts the monsoon rain as a percentage of the long-term average. It says the forecast may be off by about 5 per cent. Listen for the number on the news in June. Farmers plan sowing around it.
- Cyclone warnings on the Odisha and Tamil Nadu coasts rely on predicting where a cyclone will land. In 2013 a good forecast helped about a million people move inland before Cyclone Phailin. Look at a cyclone warning on your phone and notice the 'cone' that shows how wide the forecast spread is.
- A cough syrup comes with a small measuring cup because a kitchen spoon varies in size. Read the dose on a bottle and see which mark the cup shows. A miss here matters much more than in a recipe.
- Surgeons in many hospitals read a short checklist aloud before an operation. Ask a parent if they have seen a checklist at a hospital or in a clinic, and what it asked.
- Rocket launches everywhere use a long checklist during the countdown, because one forgotten step can end the mission. Watch a launch video and listen for the calls and replies.
- A tailor measures twice before cutting cloth, with a tape that fits the job. Watch a tailor and notice what the tape measures and what a mistake would cost.
- A weighing scale at a shop shows grams, and a grocer weighs 100 grams of a spice by sight first, then checks. Notice how close the first guess is each time.

## Spotted around us
- When a parent tests whether the dosa pan is hot by sprinkling a few drops of water, watch what the drops do. They sizzle and dance when the pan is ready. That is a quick test with a prediction inside it.
- Look at the marks on a milk pouch, a medicine bottle cap and a one-litre jug. Find the smallest mark on each and say which could measure 5 millilitres.
- On the way to school, look for something that keeps people away from a hazard. It might be a railing at a drain, a fence round a pond, a speed bump or a zebra crossing. Say whether it removes, swaps, separates or protects.
- Watch a cook check sugar syrup between two fingers, or a potter check a pot's dryness by touch. Each one has a test and a prediction learned from hands that have done it many times.
- Look at a packing list on a fridge or a school timetable stuck on a wall. Each is a kind of checklist. Notice what it saves you from forgetting.
- When the evening news gives tomorrow's forecast, write down what it says. Tomorrow, check the weather and say whether the forecast was close enough for what you needed to do.
- Watch how a grown-up checks the gas knob before leaving the kitchen. This one-line checklist guards against a hazard that skill alone can forget.

## Open questions
- **Why can the world be predicted at all?** Science finds patterns and uses them. It has no agreed account of why nature keeps to patterns that minds and mathematics can capture. In 1960 the physicist Eugene Wigner called this the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics. Some think the answer lies in how minds evolved, and some in the nature of mathematics. No test has yet decided it.
- **Is the future already fixed by the present, or is some of it truly open?** Chaos shows that we cannot predict some things in practice, which is a different matter from their being open. Quantum physics seems to show events without a deciding cause, but physicists still disagree about how to read it. No experiment so far settles the question.
- **Does hot water ever really freeze faster than cold, and if so, why?** Careful tests on water disagree with each other, and the effect, where reported, depends on the cup, the freezer and where the thermometer sits. An effect of this kind has been shown in other, simpler systems. For water there is no agreed result and no agreed explanation.
- **How safe is safe enough?** Measuring a risk is science. Deciding that a measured risk is acceptable is a judgement about what people value, and people value different things. No measurement can make that choice for them.
- **Do all living beings predict, and is predicting the same thing in a plant, an ant and a person?** Plants and many animals clearly act ahead of events. Scientists disagree on what to call this and on how it is done in beings with very different bodies. The idea that predicting is the main work of every brain is still a proposal being tested.

## Connected maths
- Three timed runs of the sugar test give 48, 52 and 56 seconds. Find the mean by adding and dividing by 3, and the spread, which is the biggest minus the smallest. Is a prediction of 50 inside the spread? **Mean 52 seconds. Spread 8 seconds. Yes, 50 lies between 48 and 56.**. 48 + 52 + 56 = 156. 156 ÷ 3 = 52. The biggest is 56 and the smallest is 48, and 56 − 48 = 8. A prediction between 48 and 56 is inside the spread, so the test cannot tell it from the truth. (queues `math.data.averages.L4`)
- One test predicted 40 seconds and got 52. Another predicted 9 minutes and got 10 minutes. Which miss is bigger in seconds, and which is bigger as a share of its result? **The 9-minute test misses by more seconds (60 against 12). The 40-second test misses by the bigger share, about 23 per cent against 10 per cent.**. First test: 52 − 40 = 12 seconds. 12 ÷ 52 is about 0.23, which is 23 per cent. Second test: 10 minutes is 600 seconds and 9 minutes is 540, so the miss is 60 seconds. 60 ÷ 600 = 0.1, which is 10 per cent. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A jug is marked every 100 millilitres, so a reading may be off by up to 50 millilitres. A medicine cup is marked every 5 millilitres, so a reading may be off by up to 2.5. How many times smaller is the cup's possible error? **20 times smaller.**. The error can be up to half of the smallest mark. 50 ÷ 2.5 = 20. For a 10 millilitre amount, an error of 50 is five times the amount itself, so the jug is useless for it. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L4`)
- A kitchen scale shows whole grams. 1,000 grains of rice show 21 grams. What is the mass of one grain? Why is weighing 1,000 grains better than weighing 100 grains that show 2 grams? **0.021 grams. The scale's rounding of up to 0.5 gram is about 2 per cent of 21 grams, but about 25 per cent of 2 grams.**. 21 ÷ 1,000 = 0.021. A reading of 2 grams may truly be anything from 1.5 to 2.5, a swing of 0.5 out of 2, which is 25 per cent. A reading of 21 swings 0.5 out of 21, about 2 per cent. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A test has a one-in-a-hundred chance of a small spill and a one-in-ten-thousand chance of a cut. If 200 families do it, how many spills and cuts do you expect? **About 2 spills and 0.02 cuts. A cut is unlikely: about one chance in 50 that anyone is cut at all.**. 200 × 1/100 = 2 spills. 200 × 1/10,000 = 0.02, which is about one chance in 50. A risk joins how likely a harm is with how bad it would be. Spills are likely and mild. A cut is unlikely and worse. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A hand-timed run has a finger error of 0.2 seconds at the stop only. How big is that error as a share of a 5-second event, and of a 0.5-second event? **4 per cent of 5 seconds, and 40 per cent of 0.5 seconds.**. 0.2 ÷ 5 = 0.04, which is 4 per cent. 0.2 ÷ 0.5 = 0.4, which is 40 per cent. The same error is small for a long event and huge for a short one. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)

## Warm-up
- Which tool tells you how hot water is? → A thermometer.
- Where should your eye be when you read a measuring cup? → Level with the water, looking straight across.
- What is the difference between a hazard and a risk? → A hazard is anything that could cause harm. A risk is how likely the harm is, and how bad.
