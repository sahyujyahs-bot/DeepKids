# Floating and sinking: why a heavy rolling pin floats and a light coin sinks
`sci.states.floating-and-sinking.L2`

**Summary.** A heavy wooden rolling pin floats in a bucket of water, but a light one-rupee coin sinks. Your child guesses first, then tests ten things from the kitchen to see which float and which sink. They find that how heavy or how big a thing is does not decide it. A tiny piece of potato sinks, just like the whole potato. What a thing is made of, and whether it holds air inside, decides if it floats.

**Intro.** *Hold a wooden rolling pin in one hand and a one-rupee coin in the other. Which one is heavier? Now guess: which one will sink in a bucket of water?* Most people guess that the heavy rolling pin will sink and the light coin will float. You are going to drop them in and see. Then you will test many more things from the kitchen. If weight does not decide what sinks, what does? This unit answers that question, and your own test will show you.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what float and sink mean, and test a thing to find out which it does.
1. By the end I can make a guess before a test and give a reason for it.
2. By the end I can show that a heavy thing can float and a light thing can sink.
3. By the end I can show that a big piece and a small piece of the same material both float, or both sink.
4. By the end I can say that water pushes up on things put into it, and that air inside a thing helps it float.
5. By the end I can name things around me in India that float or sink, and say why.

## Terms
- **Float**: A thing floats when it stays at the top of the water. If you push it under and let go, it comes back up. *e.g.* A lemon floats in a bucket of water. Push it down, let go, and it bobs back up.
- **Sink**: A thing sinks when it goes down to the bottom of the water and stays there. *e.g.* A one-rupee coin sinks to the bottom of a bucket.
- **Prediction**: A prediction is a guess about what will happen, made before you test it, with a reason. *e.g.* Nila predicts: 'The potato will sink, because it feels heavy and hard.'
- **Test**: A test is when you try something to find out what really happens. *e.g.* Putting the potato in the water is the test. It shows if your prediction was right.
- **Material**: A material is what a thing is made of, such as wood, steel, plastic or wax. *e.g.* A rolling pin is made of wood. A spoon is made of steel.
- **Upthrust**: Upthrust is the push of water upwards on anything put into it. You can feel it when you push a ball down into water. *e.g.* Push a ball into a bucket. The water pushes it back up. That push is upthrust.
- **Air space**: An air space is a tiny gap filled with air inside a thing. Many air spaces make a thing lighter for its size. *e.g.* An apple has lots of air spaces inside, so it floats.

## Explanations
### Floating and sinking · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing floats if it stays at the top of the water and comes back up when you push it under and let go. A thing sinks if it goes to the bottom and stays there.

In the bucket test, you pushed each thing under with a finger, then let go. The lemon and the rolling pin came back up, so they float. The coin, the spoon and the potato stayed on the bottom, so they sink. A bottle cap floats high, and an ice cube floats low. Both are floating.
- e.g. A lemon bobs back up when you let go, so it floats.
- e.g. A steel spoon stays at the bottom, so it sinks.
- e.g. An ice cube floats, but most of it stays under the water.
### Weight alone does not decide · *defined*
**Definition:** A heavy thing can float, and a light thing can sink. How heavy a thing feels in your hand does not tell you if it will float.

In your hand, the wooden rolling pin felt much heavier than the coin. Many children guess the rolling pin will sink. But in the bucket, the rolling pin floated and the coin sank. A big log floats down a river, while a small stone sinks. A huge ship floats, while a tiny steel pin sinks. So we cannot tell by weight alone.
- e.g. A wooden rolling pin is heavy and floats.
- e.g. A one-rupee coin is light and sinks.
- e.g. A whole coconut is heavy and floats.
### What it is made of decides · *defined*
**Definition:** For a solid lump of one material, what the material is decides if it floats. A big piece and a small piece of the same material do the same thing.

The whole potato sank, and so did a piece as small as your fingernail. The big rolling pin floated, and so did a tiny matchstick. Wood is lighter than the same amount of water, so wood floats. Steel and potato are heavier than the same amount of water, so they sink.
- e.g. A whole potato and a tiny piece of potato both sink.
- e.g. A big rolling pin and a small matchstick, both wood, both float.
- e.g. A steel spoon and a steel key both sink.
### Water pushes up: upthrust · *defined*
**Definition:** Water pushes up on anything put into it. This push is called upthrust.

When you pushed the ball down, you felt the water push back. When you let go, the ball jumped up. Water pushes up on a coin too, but the push is too small to hold the coin up, so the coin sinks. A thing floats when the push of the water is enough to hold up its whole weight.
- e.g. A ball pushed under water jumps back up when you let go.
- e.g. You can feel the water push up on a lemon when you press it down.
- e.g. A coin gets a tiny push up, but not enough, so it sinks.
### Air inside helps things float · *defined*
**Definition:** Air is very light. A thing with lots of air inside it is light for its size, so it is more likely to float.

An orange peel is full of tiny pockets of air. With its peel on, the whole orange floats. Peeled, the orange usually sinks. An apple floats because it has many air spaces between its cells. Thermocol is mostly air, so it floats very high. A life jacket is full of foam with air in it, so it helps a person float.
- e.g. A whole orange floats, and a peeled orange usually sinks.
- e.g. An apple floats because of the air spaces inside it.
- e.g. Thermocol is mostly air, and it floats high.
### Another way to stay on top: the skin of water · *defined*
**Definition:** The top of water holds together like a very thin, stretchy skin. Very small, light things can rest on this skin without floating in the usual way.

A steel pin laid very gently on still water can rest on top. Push it under, and it sinks, because steel is heavier than water. The skin of the water held it up, not upthrust. Agnes Pockels, a woman in Germany, studied this skin in her own kitchen with a tray of water and a button. She was not allowed to study at a university, yet in 1891 the science journal Nature printed her letter about it.
- e.g. A steel pin laid gently on still water can rest on top.
- e.g. Pond skaters, insects that walk on ponds, stand on the skin of the water.
- e.g. Pushed under, the pin sinks.

## Mostly agreed
- A thing floats or sinks depending on what it is made of, and on any air inside it, not on how heavy or how big it is. *How we know:* In the bucket test, the heavy rolling pin floated, the light coin sank, and a tiny piece of potato sank like the whole potato.
- Water pushes up on everything put into it. This push is called upthrust. *How we know:* You can feel it when you push a ball down into water, and see it when you let go and the ball jumps up.
- Air inside a thing makes it lighter for its size, and helps it float. *How we know:* A whole orange floats, and the same orange usually sinks once its air-filled peel is taken off.

## How we found out
**Archimedes of Syracuse, and the people who saved his book: Johan Ludvig Heiberg, and Abigail Quandt and her team at the Walters Art Museum, About 250 BCE (the book), 1229 (written over), 1906 (found again), from 1999 (read again).** About 2,250 years ago, Archimedes lived in Syracuse, a Greek city on the island of Sicily. Many people thought heavy things sink and light things float. But a big, heavy log floats, while a small pebble sinks. Archimedes worked out a better rule and wrote it in a book called On Floating Bodies. He said a floating thing sinks into the water until it has pushed aside water as heavy as itself. His book was nearly lost. In the year 1229, someone scraped the writing off its pages and wrote prayers over them. In 1906, the scholar Johan Ludvig Heiberg found the faint old words under the prayers. Later, a team at the Walters Art Museum in the United States read even more. Abigail Quandt, a book expert there, spent years taking the crumbling pages apart safely.  
*What people believed before:* That heavy things sink and light things float. Archimedes showed that what matters is how heavy a thing is compared with the water it pushes aside, so a heavy log can float and a small pebble can sink.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Heavy things sink and light things float.** (A very common guess, by children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Many heavy things do sink, like a brick or a steel vessel full of water. Many light things float, like a leaf or a feather.  
  Against: A heavy wooden rolling pin floats and a light coin sinks. A big ship floats. What a thing is made of, and the air inside it, matters more than its weight.
- **Big things sink and small things float.** (Another common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Big things are often heavier, so people expect them to sink.  
  Against: A tiny piece of potato sinks just like the whole potato, and a tiny matchstick floats just like a big rolling pin. Size does not decide.
- **Archimedes worked out his idea in the bath, and ran through the streets shouting 'Eureka!', which means 'I have found it!'** (A famous story, first written by the Roman writer Vitruvius about 200 years after Archimedes died; *debated*)  
  For: The story is old, and it fits Archimedes, who did write a real book about floating.  
  Against: Nobody wrote it down while Archimedes was alive. Historians are not sure it happened the way the story says.

## Roots
We have guessed and tested what floats and what sinks, cut a potato small, and peeled an orange. Now we ask what floating tells us about things, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In your test, the heavy wooden rolling pin floated and the light coin sank. A tiny piece of potato sank, just like the whole potato. So size and weight alone do not decide if a thing floats. For a solid lump, what it is made of decides.  
*Habit it breaks:* We judge things by how big they look and how heavy they feel in the hand. The bucket test showed that floating depends on the material all the way through, which the hand cannot feel.  
*Evidence:* In the bucket test, the heaviest thing, the rolling pin, floated, and one of the lightest, the coin, sank. A fingernail-sized piece of potato sank, and a matchstick floated like the rolling pin.

- *who am i:* Your body is about as heavy as the same amount of water. Breathe in deeply, and the air in your lungs helps you float.
- *what is life:* A coconut floats across the sea and grows into a palm on a new island. Floating is one way life travels.
- *what is this world:* The same water holds up a bamboo coracle on the Kaveri and a floating island on Loktak Lake, and lets a clay idol sink in Mumbai.

A coin, a rolling pin and a potato are different things, and the same water meets all of them. What each one does depends on what it is made of. Everything is conscious, and each kind of thing meets the water in its own way.

- **Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 (the Kalama Sutta):** The Buddha told the Kalama people not to believe something only because it is often said, or because it seems right. When you know for yourselves, he said, then accept it. (Many people say heavy things sink. You did not just believe it. You tested it, and the rolling pin floated.)
- **Udana 6.4, the blind men and the elephant:** Some blind men each touched one part of an elephant. One felt the leg and said an elephant is like a pillar. One felt the ear and said it is like a winnowing basket. Each was sure, and each knew only one part. (Your hand knew only one part of the rolling pin: how heavy it is. The bucket showed another part, what it is made of. You needed both to understand it.)
- Science (settled): Archimedes' rule: a floating thing sinks into the water until it has pushed aside water as heavy as itself.
- Science (settled): Water presses harder the deeper you go. So it pushes harder on the bottom of a thing than on its top, and that difference is upthrust.
- Other view, Many physicists: They say water, wood and coins have no awareness at all. They explain floating fully with weight and the push of the water, and they find no need for a mind to explain it.
- Practice, Float on your breath (3 min): Lie down on your back on the floor. Put one hand on your chest. Breathe in slowly and deeply. Feel your chest rise. Now breathe out slowly. Feel it fall. Do this three times. In water, a big breath in helps a person float. The air inside you makes you lighter for your size.
- Ask: The rolling pin felt heavy, but it floated. What else might feel one way and be another way?
- Ask: What else could you test to find out if size matters, using wood or potato?
- Ask: Your hand and the bucket told you different things. When two tests disagree, which one do you trust?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The rolling pin felt heavy in your hand, yet it floated. Why do our hands guess wrong about floating? (hint: Your hand feels how heavy the whole thing is. Does it feel how heavy it is for its size?)
- *deep:* A peeled orange sinks and a whole orange floats. What does the orange lose when its peel comes off? (hint: What is inside the peel that the juicy part does not have?)
- *deep:* When you are in water, you float a little better after a big breath in. What does that tell you about your own body? (hint: Where does the air go when you breathe in?)
- *big:* Coconuts can float across the sea to new islands and grow there. How do you think plants that cannot float travel to new places? (hint: Think of seeds that fly, seeds that stick to fur, and seeds that birds eat.)
- *big:* We guess before we test. Why is it worth guessing, if the test will show us the answer anyway? (hint: What did you learn when your guess was wrong?)

## Story: The Coconut That Would Not Sink
*Kanji is rice cooked soft in plenty of water, eaten in Kerala, often on rainy days. Ammamma and Appooppa are Malayalam words for a mother's mother and a mother's father. Nila, Hari and their grandparents are made up.*

It was raining hard in Kochi. Nila was seven, and her little brother Hari was five. They could not go out, so Ammamma, their grandmother, gave them a job.

"Wash the rice for the kanji," she said. She put a steel vessel of rice and water in front of them on the kitchen floor.

Hari swished the rice with his hand. Little brown flakes rose to the top and floated there.

"Those are husks," said Ammamma. "They are the thin dry coats of the rice. Scoop them off."

"The husks float because they are light," said Hari. "Light things float. Heavy things sink. Everyone knows that."

Nila looked at the rice grains lying on the bottom. Each grain was tiny and light. Yet it sank.

"Is that really true?" she asked.

Ammamma smiled. She took a big steel bucket and filled it half full with water from the tap. "Let us find out," she said. "But first, each of you must guess. And you must say why."

She put a row of things on the floor. There was a wooden rolling pin, a one-rupee coin, a lemon, a potato, a steel spoon and a whole coconut with its brown hair still on.

Hari picked up the coconut. It was the heaviest thing in the row. He needed both hands.

"The coconut will sink," he said, "because it is so heavy. And the coin will float, because it is so small."

Nila held the rolling pin in one hand and the coin in the other. The rolling pin was much heavier. "I think the rolling pin will sink," she said slowly. "But I am not sure."

"Good," said Ammamma. "Now test."

Hari dropped the coin in first. It wobbled down through the water and landed on the bottom with a little click.

Hari frowned.

Nila laid the rolling pin on the water. It dipped down, then rolled over and floated.

"It floated!" said Nila. "The heavy one floated, and the light one sank."

Then Hari lifted the big coconut and let it go. Splash! It went under, then bobbed straight back up and floated, turning slowly.

Hari's mouth fell open. "But it is so heavy," he said.

"Coconuts float very well," said Ammamma. "A coconut can float across the sea for weeks. When she lands on a new beach, she can grow into a new palm. That is how coconut palms reached many far islands."

They tried the rest. The lemon floated. The spoon sank. The potato sank.

"Maybe the potato is too big," said Hari. "A tiny piece will float."

"Guess first," said Nila. She was starting to like this game.

"A tiny piece will float," said Hari firmly.

Ammamma cut a piece of potato as small as Hari's fingernail. Hari dropped it in. It sank, just like the whole potato.

Nila thought hard. "So it is not how big it is," she said. "And it is not how heavy it is. It is what it is made of. Every piece of potato sinks. Wood floats, big or small."

To check, she found a used matchstick and put it on the water. The tiny stick floated, just like the big wooden rolling pin.

"Then why do the husks float?" asked Hari.

Nila picked one up and held it near the window. It was thin and dry, like paper. "Maybe husks are made of something lighter than water," she said. "Like the wood."

Just then, Appooppa, their grandfather, came in, shaking water off his umbrella.

Hari ran to him. "Appooppa, guess! Will this heavy rolling pin float or sink?"

"Sink, of course," said Appooppa. "It is heavy."

"Wrong!" said Hari. "Now guess the coin."

"The coin will float," said Appooppa. "It is so light."

Hari shook his head. He told Appooppa what Nila had found. It was not how heavy a thing was, and not how big. It was what the thing was made of.

Appooppa dropped the coin in, and it sank. He laughed. "I have lived for seventy years," he said, "and today a five-year-old taught me about floating."

"Nila taught me first," said Hari.

"And the bucket taught all of us," said Ammamma, and she put the rice on to cook.

**For the parent:** Whether a thing floats does not depend on how heavy or how big it is. A heavy rolling pin and a heavy coconut float, a light coin sinks, and a tiny piece of potato sinks like the whole potato. What a thing is made of decides. Guessing first, then testing, shows us when our guess was wrong.

- Ask: Why did Hari think the coconut would sink?
- Ask: What did the tiny piece of potato show Nila?
- Ask: Why did Ammamma make them guess before every test?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bucket Test: guess, then find out what floats (25 min)
- **question:** Which things float and which sink? Do heavy things always sink, and do small things always float?
- **predict:** Hold each thing in your hand. Will it float or sink? Why do you think so? Then guess: will a tiny piece of potato do the same as the whole potato, and why?
- **fair test:** We use the same bucket of water for every thing. We put each thing in gently, push it to the bottom with a finger, and let go. If it comes back up, it floats. If it stays down, it sinks. We test each thing twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **record:** Fill in the table as you go. At the end, draw the bucket with the floaters on top and the sinkers on the bottom. Count your right guesses with tally marks.
- **results table:** Thing | Heavy or light in my hand? | My guess: float or sink? | What it did | Was my guess right?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the bucket half full and put it on a towel. 2. Line up the ten things. 3. Your child holds each one, then sorts them into heavy and light.  
  *Parent says:* Hold each thing. Which feel heavy? Which feel light? Put the heavy ones on one side.  
  *Child mode:* Hold each thing. Is it heavy or light? Make two piles.
  - Ask: Which is heavier, the rolling pin or the coin? (good: The rolling pin, by a lot.)
  - Note: This step matters. Your child will use these feelings to guess. Write heavy or light in the table.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Point to each thing in turn. 2. Your child says float or sink, and why. 3. Write each guess in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now guess for each one. Will it float or sink? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess for each thing. Float or sink? Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think the rolling pin will sink? (good: Because it is heavy.; It is big and heavy.; Wood floats, so it will float.)
  - Note: Do not say if a guess is right. Most children say heavy things sink. Any guess with a reason is a good guess.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child puts in one thing at a time. 2. They push it to the bottom with a finger and let go. 3. Write what it did. 4. Take it out before the next one.  
  *Parent says:* Put it in gently. Now push it down to the bottom and let go. Did it come back up? Then it floats.  
  *Child mode:* Put it in. Push it down. Let go. Did it come up?
  - Ask: What did the rolling pin do? (good: It floated, even though it is heavy.)
  - Ask: What did the coin do? (good: It sank, even though it is light.)
  - Note: Pushing each thing down and letting go stops tiny things resting on the water's skin. Expect: rolling pin, lemon, bottle cap, apple, ice and candle float. Coin, potato, spoon and key sink. If a bottle cap fills with water, empty it and lay it flat side down. A few lemons and apples are denser than most and sink. If yours does, write what it did. That is a real result.
  - Check: The heavy rolling pin floated, and the light coin sank. What does that show? → **How heavy a thing is does not decide if it floats**
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts a piece of potato as small as a fingernail. 2. Your child guesses, then drops it in. 3. Then they guess and test a matchstick.

  *Child mode:* Guess: will a tiny piece of potato float? Say why. Now drop it in.
  - Ask: What did the tiny piece of potato do? (good: It sank, like the whole potato.)
  - Ask: What did the matchstick do? (good: It floated, like the rolling pin.)
  - Note: Many children think a small piece will float. It sinks, because potato is a little heavier than the same amount of water. Push it down if it rests on the top.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the right guesses with tally marks. 2. Ask the questions below. 3. Listen before you explain.  
  *Parent says:* How many guesses were right? Which ones surprised you? So what decides if a thing floats?  
  *Child mode:* Count your right guesses. Which ones surprised you? What decides if a thing floats?
  - Ask: What decides if a thing floats? (good: Not how heavy it is.; Not how big it is.; What it is made of.)
  - Note: After your child answers, say it plainly. Weight and size do not decide. For a solid lump, what it is made of decides. Wood is lighter than the same amount of water, so it floats. Steel and potato are heavier, so they sink.

  - Ask: What did the peel do? (good: It floated.)

1. (match) Draw a line from each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Float → stays at the top, and comes back up when pushed under; Sink → goes to the bottom and stays there; Prediction → a guess with a reason, made before a test; Upthrust → the push of water upwards. *Why:* These are the main words in this unit. A floater comes back up, a sinker stays down, and we guess before we test.
2. (mcq) In the bucket test, which one floated?  
   Options: The one-rupee coin / The wooden rolling pin / The steel spoon  
   **Answer:** The wooden rolling pin. *Why:* The rolling pin floated, though it was the heaviest. Picking the coin may mean thinking light things always float.
3. (sort) Sort these into float and sink.  
   **Answer:** a lemon → Float; a metal key → Sink; an ice cube → Float; a raw potato → Sink; a candle → Float; a steel spoon → Sink. *Why:* The lemon, ice and candle float. The key, potato and spoon sink. Metal and potato are heavier than the same amount of water.

   **Answer:** A bucket with the rolling pin, lemon and other floaters at the top, and the coin, spoon and potato on the bottom.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the heavy rolling pin at the top and the light coin at the bottom, and labels them.

## Applications
- Farmers put seeds in water before sowing. Full, healthy seeds usually sink, and empty or damaged seeds float, so they can be taken out.
- Fishers in Kerala, Odisha and Gujarat tie floats to the top edge of their nets, so the net hangs in the water like a curtain.
- Life jackets are full of foam with air in it, so they help a person float.
- Some recycling plants put chopped plastic in water. Some kinds of plastic float and others sink, so they can be sorted.
- Many cooks check an egg by putting it in water. A fresh egg sinks. A very old egg may float, because air has got inside.

## Spotted around us
- Round coracles, made of woven bamboo, carrying people on the Kaveri river at Hogenakkal, on the border of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka.
- Kettuvallam houseboats, made of wood, floating on the backwaters of Alappuzha in Kerala.
- Phumdis, floating islands of plants, soil and roots, on Loktak Lake in Manipur. Keibul Lamjao, a national park on the lake, floats on them, and the rare sangai deer live there.

## Open questions
- **When did people first make boats or rafts?** The oldest boat ever found is a dugout canoe about 10,000 years old, found in the Netherlands. But people reached Australia across the sea more than 50,000 years ago, so they must have floated on something much earlier. Those rafts were made of wood or plants that rotted away, so nobody knows what they looked like.
- **How exactly do phumdis, the floating islands of Loktak Lake, form, and why are they changing?** Scientists know phumdis are mats of plants, roots and soil. But they still study how fast new ones form, and why many have grown thinner or moved since a dam was built on the lake in the 1980s.
