# Magnetic materials: which things a magnet pulls, and which it does not
`sci.materials.magnetic-materials.L3`

**Summary.** This unit is about which materials a magnet pulls. A material is magnetic if a magnet pulls it. Iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt are magnetic. Wood, plastic, glass, paper, cloth and stone are not. The big surprise is that most metals are not magnetic either. Aluminium, copper, brass, gold and silver are all ignored by a magnet, and so are some steel tumblers. The reason is hidden inside the atoms, where tiny magnets line up in iron but not in brass. A magnet test can show that a thing contains iron. It can never show that a thing is gold. Scientists still cannot predict every new magnetic material.

**Intro.** *Try this. Take a fridge magnet to your kitchen. Touch it to a steel spoon, then to a steel tumbler. Both are grey and shiny and called steel. Does the magnet treat them the same?* In many kitchens, the answer is no. The spoon grabs the magnet, but the tumbler may not. A magnet will stick to the fridge door, but it will fall off the wooden cupboard door right beside it. So a magnet is choosy. It picks some things and leaves others, and it is not picking by colour or by shine. What is it picking by? This unit is about finding out. You will test thirty or more things and spot a pattern. Then you will meet a surprise that even many grown-ups get wrong.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what magnetic and non-magnetic mean. A magnet pulls a magnetic thing, and does not pull a non-magnetic one.
1. I can predict, test and record which objects a magnet pulls, and sort them into two groups.
2. I can say the pattern: a magnet pulls iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt, and not every metal is magnetic.
3. I can explain why two steel things can behave differently, because steel is an alloy and the mixture matters.
4. I can show that a magnet's pull goes through paper, cloth, plastic and water. I can show that it gets weaker as the gap grows.
5. I can say what a magnet test can prove and what it cannot, and use a magnet to sort scrap.
6. I can say, in simple words, why iron is magnetic: tiny magnets inside it line up. I can also give big examples: the Earth's iron core, magnetic bacteria, and a frog floating in a magnet.

## Terms
- **Magnet**: A thing that pulls iron and steel towards it, with no rope or glue. It has this pull all by itself. *e.g.* A fridge magnet holds a drawing on the fridge door.
- **Magnetic**: Pulled by a magnet. Iron and most steel are magnetic. *e.g.* An iron nail jumps towards a magnet. So the nail is magnetic.
- **Non-magnetic**: Not pulled by a magnet, or pulled so little that we cannot notice. *e.g.* A wooden spoon, a glass bangle and aluminium foil are all non-magnetic.
- **Attract**: To pull towards. A magnet attracts magnetic things. *e.g.* A magnet attracts a paper clip. The clip leaps up and sticks.
- **Magnetic force**: The pull that a magnet puts on magnetic things. It works across a small gap, so the magnet need not touch. *e.g.* Hold a magnet just above a paper clip. The clip jumps up before they touch.
- **Material**: What a thing is made of. *e.g.* A spoon can be made of steel, wood or plastic. Steel, wood and plastic are materials.
- **Metal**: A material that is usually shiny, feels cold, and rings when you tap it. Heat and electricity pass easily through metals. *e.g.* Iron, copper, aluminium, gold and silver are metals.
- **Iron**: A grey metal that a magnet pulls strongly. It goes brown and flaky with rust when it stays wet. *e.g.* An old gate, a tawa and a nail may be made of iron.
- **Steel**: Iron with a little carbon mixed in, which makes it much stronger. Most steel is magnetic, but some kinds are not. *e.g.* Train tracks, a bridge, a spoon and an almirah are made of steel.
- **Alloy**: A metal mixed with something else, usually other metals, to give it new properties. Steel, brass and bronze are alloys. *e.g.* Brass is copper mixed with zinc. A magnet ignores brass, because it ignores copper and zinc too.
- **Stainless steel**: A steel that hardly rusts, because another metal called chromium is mixed in. Some kinds also have nickel. *e.g.* Your steel tumbler is probably stainless steel. A magnet may stick to it, or may not.
- **Nickel and cobalt**: Two metals that a magnet pulls, just like iron. They are much rarer in everyday things. *e.g.* Nickel is in some coins. Cobalt is in some strong magnets and in phone batteries.
- **Lodestone**: A natural rock that is already a magnet. It is a form of a dark mineral called magnetite. *e.g.* People found lodestones thousands of years ago, and saw them pull iron.
- **Pattern**: Something that keeps happening in your results. *e.g.* Every object that stuck was made of iron or steel. That is a pattern.
- **Predict**: To guess what will happen before you try, and to have a reason for your guess. *e.g.* I predict the brass key will not stick, because brass is not iron.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* A nail, a wooden spoon and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Electron**: A tiny bit on the outside of every atom. Each electron spins, and a spinning electron acts like a very tiny magnet. *e.g.* In an iron nail, the tiny magnets from billions of electrons line up in groups. That is why the nail is magnetic.
- **Domain**: A tiny patch inside iron where countless atoms all act like little magnets pointing the same way. *e.g.* A nail is full of domains. A nearby magnet turns many of them to point the same way, so the nail gets pulled.
- **Magnetise**: To turn a magnetic thing into a magnet, at least for a while. *e.g.* Touch a nail to a magnet and it can pick up a paper clip. The nail has been magnetised.
- **Curie point**: The heat at which a magnetic material stops being magnetic. For iron it is about 770 degrees Celsius. It is named after Pierre Curie, a French scientist. *e.g.* A nail glowing red-hot is above its Curie point, so a magnet no longer pulls it.
- **Ferrite**: A hard, dark, brittle ceramic made from iron joined with oxygen, mixed with other powders. Most fridge magnets are made of it. *e.g.* A flat black fridge magnet is often ferrite powder held in rubber.
- **Diamagnetic**: Pushed away, very weakly, by a magnet. Almost everything is a little diamagnetic, including water, wood and you. *e.g.* A live frog was floated in the air by a very strong magnet, because the frog is diamagnetic.
- **Scrap**: Old metal and broken things that are thrown away. Scrap is melted and made into new things. *e.g.* A kabadiwala buys scrap iron, steel, aluminium and copper.
- **Kabadiwala**: A person who buys old paper, bottles and metal from homes, and sells them on to be recycled. *e.g.* The kabadiwala weighs your old newspapers and a broken iron pan, and pays you for them.

## Explanations
### What magnetic means · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is magnetic if a magnet pulls it. A material is non-magnetic if a magnet does not pull it, or pulls it too little to notice.

Hold a magnet near a paper clip, and the clip jumps up to meet it. Hold the same magnet near a wooden spoon, and nothing happens. We call the clip magnetic and the spoon non-magnetic. The pull works across a small gap, so the magnet does not need to touch. Be careful with the word. Magnetic means a magnet pulls it. It does not mean it is a magnet. A paper clip is magnetic, but it is not a magnet. It cannot pull other clips by itself, unless a real magnet has just given it some power.
- e.g. A paper clip is magnetic. It leaps to a magnet.
- e.g. A plastic comb is non-magnetic. It stays where it is.
- e.g. A steel almirah door is magnetic. That is why a fridge magnet can hold up a drawing on it.
### Which materials a magnet pulls · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnet pulls iron, most kinds of steel, nickel and cobalt. It does not pull wood, plastic, glass, paper, cloth, rubber or stone.

Test enough things and a pattern appears. The things that jump to a magnet are made of iron or steel, or a little nickel or cobalt. Nails, safety pins, paper clips, tawas and almirahs are the usual ones. Everything made of wood, plastic, glass, paper, cloth, rubber or stone is ignored. This works for thousands of everyday objects. Scientists have checked it for hundreds of years. But there are exceptions, and they are the best part of the unit.
- e.g. Pulled: an iron nail, a safety pin, a paper clip, a steel almirah.
- e.g. Not pulled: a wooden pencil, a plastic ruler, a glass bangle, a cotton hanky, a stone.
- e.g. A tin can is steel with a thin coat of tin, so a magnet sticks to it.
### Not every metal is magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** Only three common metals are magnetic: iron, nickel and cobalt. Aluminium, copper, brass, gold, silver, zinc, tin and lead are not.

Many people believe a magnet pulls every metal. It does not. Try an aluminium foil ball, a copper wire, a brass key and a gold ring. They are all shiny metals, and a magnet ignores every one. There are about ninety kinds of natural element. Only iron, nickel and cobalt are magnetic in an ordinary room. A fourth one, gadolinium, is magnetic only when it is colder than about 20 degrees Celsius. So being a metal is not the secret. Something about iron, nickel and cobalt is special. Later in this unit you will zoom in and find out what.
- e.g. Aluminium foil and aluminium pressure cookers are not pulled.
- e.g. A brass diya and a copper lota are not pulled.
- e.g. A gold ring and a silver anklet are not pulled.
### Steel is a mixture, so steel can differ · *defined*
**Definition:** Steel is an alloy, which means a mixture. It is iron with a little carbon. Stainless steel also has chromium, and often nickel. The mixture decides whether a magnet pulls it.

Here is the tumbler puzzle. Most steel is mostly iron, so a magnet pulls it. But the steel in many Indian tumblers, katoris and thalis has a lot of nickel mixed in. In every 100 parts, there may be about 18 parts of chromium and 8 parts of nickel. The nickel changes how the iron atoms sit next to each other. In that new pattern, the iron's tiny magnets do not line up. So the magnet barely pulls. Other stainless steels have chromium but little nickel. They do stick. Pots for induction stoves must be that sticking kind, because the stove works through magnetism. You may also find that a tumbler's bottom sticks but its sides do not. A bent corner may stick a little too, because bending can rearrange the atoms.
- e.g. A steel almirah, a tawa and many spoons stick to a magnet.
- e.g. Many tumblers and katoris do not stick, or stick only weakly.
- e.g. A pan that works on an induction stove always sticks to a magnet.
### Mixing can switch magnetism on or off · *defined*
**Definition:** Mixing metals can make a mixture that behaves nothing like its ingredients. A mix of magnetic metals can lose its magnetism. A mix of non-magnetic metals can gain it.

In 1903, a German scientist called Fritz Heusler made a mixture of copper, manganese and aluminium. None of those three metals is magnetic. But the mixture was. Heusler could not explain it, and nobody could for years. We now know the manganese atoms sit in a special pattern in the mixture. Their tiny magnets line up. It works the other way too. A stainless steel with nickel loses the magnetism that plain iron has. So you cannot work out magnetism by looking at the list of ingredients. You must test.
- e.g. Heusler's copper, manganese and aluminium mix is magnetic, though none of its metals is.
- e.g. Alnico is a mixture of aluminium, nickel and cobalt, with iron, used for strong magnets in loudspeakers. Aluminium alone is not magnetic.
- e.g. Nickel-rich stainless steel is not magnetic, though plain iron is.
### Tiny magnets inside: why iron is magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside iron, nickel and cobalt, the tiny magnets of many atoms line up in groups called domains. A nearby magnet turns the domains to point the same way, so the metal is pulled.

Zoom in past anything a microscope can show, and you find atoms. Zoom in further, and you find electrons. Every electron spins, and a spinning electron acts like a very tiny magnet. In most materials, the tiny magnets point every which way, so they cancel out. A wooden spoon has countless tiny magnets, but no pull. In iron, neighbouring atoms make each other point the same way, in patches called domains. A domain has billions and billions of atoms. A nail is full of domains, but they point in different directions, so the nail is not a magnet. Bring a real magnet close, and the domains swing round to match it. Now the nail has a pull of its own, and it is drawn to the magnet. Move the magnet away, and most domains drift back. In hard steel, many stay lined up, which is how steel can become a lasting magnet.
- e.g. A nail touched to a magnet can pick up paper clips. Pull it away and the clips fall, as the domains drift back.
- e.g. A steel needle stroked many times with a magnet keeps its pull for a long time.
- e.g. Hit a magnet hard, or drop it many times, and it weakens. The domains get shaken out of line.
### Why do iron's tiny magnets line up? · *debated*
You might expect tiny magnets to turn and point opposite ways, like two fridge magnets that snap together. That is not what happens in iron. Something stronger makes neighbours copy each other. The answer needs quantum physics, the science of the very tiniest things. It says that electrons find it easier to be near each other when they spin the same way. This rule has no everyday example, and it is not about magnets pulling at all. Scientists agree that this rule exists. They still argue about the best picture of how it works in a real piece of metal.
- e.g. Iron, nickel and cobalt all have electrons that can line up this way.
- e.g. Copper and aluminium have electrons that pair up and cancel out instead.
- **Werner Heisenberg, a German physicist, in 1928:** Each atom keeps its own tiny magnet. Quantum physics makes neighbouring atoms copy each other, like children in a line.
- **Edmund Stoner, a British physicist, in the 1930s:** The electrons are not stuck to single atoms. They roam through the whole metal, like a crowd. The whole crowd can find it easier if more of them spin one way.
- **Scientists today:** Both pictures are partly right. For iron, nickel and cobalt, the roaming picture fits better, but exact predictions are still hard.
### Everything answers a magnet a little · *defined*
**Definition:** Almost every material is pushed away, very weakly, by a magnet. This is called being diamagnetic. Some are pulled very weakly instead.

When we say wood, water or aluminium is non-magnetic, we really mean a fridge magnet cannot move it. A much stronger magnet can show a tiny reaction. In the 1990s, a physicist called Andre Geim floated a live frog in the air. He worked in the Netherlands and used a very strong magnet. The frog was not hurt. It just hovered, calmly. Frogs are mostly water, and water is pushed away from magnets a tiny bit. In a strong enough magnet, that tiny push can hold up the whole frog. Geim later won a Nobel Prize for a different material, graphene, a sheet of carbon one atom thick. So non-magnetic really means not pulled enough for us to notice.
- e.g. A very strong magnet can make a drop of water float in the air. A frog floats in an even stronger one.
- e.g. Aluminium is pulled by a magnet extremely weakly. Copper, gold and silver are pushed away very weakly.
- e.g. An MRI scanner in a hospital has a huge magnet. You lie inside it and feel no pull at all, unless you carry steel.
### Heat can switch magnetism off · *defined*
**Definition:** Every magnetic material stops being magnetic when it gets hot enough. The heat where this happens is called its Curie point.

Heat makes atoms jiggle. When iron is hot enough, the jiggling wins over the lining up, and the domains break apart. For iron, this happens at about 770 degrees Celsius, when it glows red. For nickel it is about 358 degrees. A French scientist, Pierre Curie, measured this carefully in 1895. Cool the iron again, and it becomes magnetic again. This is why you should never heat a magnet. It can also be why a magnet that is hit or dropped many times gets weaker. This is not safe to try at home, so we watch it in a video.
- e.g. A red-hot nail is not pulled by a magnet. When it cools it is pulled again.
- e.g. A magnet left on a hot stove will lose strength.
- e.g. Nickel loses its magnetism at about 358 degrees Celsius, iron at about 770 degrees.
### Magnets without metal: lodestone and ferrite · *defined*
**Definition:** Some magnetic materials are not metals. Lodestone is a rock. Ferrite is a ceramic, like a very hard pot. Both contain iron joined with oxygen.

Magnetite is a dark mineral made of iron joined with oxygen. A piece of it that is already a magnet is called a lodestone. People found lodestones thousands of years ago. In Sanskrit, the word for a magnet is ayaskanta, which means loved by iron. Today, many magnets on fridges are ferrite. Ferrite is a ceramic made by baking iron oxide, which is a bit like rust, with other powders. The thin bendy fridge magnet sheets are ferrite powder mixed into rubber. So a magnet can be made without any shiny metal. The rule that only metals are magnetic is a good one for kitchen objects, but not a law of nature. Ordinary brown rust is only very faintly pulled. A rusty nail still sticks, because there is iron under the rust.
- e.g. A black ceramic magnet from a loudspeaker is not metal.
- e.g. A bendy fridge magnet sheet is powder mixed into rubber.
- e.g. Black sand on some beaches contains magnetite grains, and a magnet picks them out.
### How did lodestones become magnets? · *debated*
A lodestone is a rock that is already a magnet, with no one having touched it. Most pieces of magnetite are not magnets. So what turned a few into magnets? Scientists have more than one idea, and they do not agree yet.
- e.g. Lodestones are rare. Most magnetite rocks do not pull iron at all.
- e.g. Many lodestones have been found near places where lightning has struck.
- **Peter Wasilewski and Gunther Kletetschka, American scientists, in 1999:** Lightning did it. A lightning bolt carries a huge electric current, which makes a very strong magnetic field for a moment. That could magnetise the rock it strikes.
- **Other geologists:** Slow cooling might do it. When hot rock cools deep underground, it can lock in a weak magnetic direction from the Earth's own magnetic field. That could explain weakly magnetic rocks, but not the very strong lodestones.
### The pull goes through things, and weakens with distance · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnet's pull goes through paper, cloth, plastic, wood, glass and water almost as if they were not there. But the pull gets weaker quickly as the gap gets bigger.

Put a paper clip on a table, and hold a magnet above a sheet of paper over it. The clip still jumps up. Add more sheets, and at some point the clip stops jumping. The paper did not block the pull. The thicker stack just put the magnet too far away. A stronger magnet can reach through more sheets. Non-magnetic materials let the pull pass through. But a magnetic material is different. A steel plate that sticks to the magnet grabs the pull for itself. Steel that does not stick lets the pull pass, like paper.
- e.g. A fridge magnet can hold a drawing, since the pull goes through paper to the steel door.
- e.g. A magnet under a table can drag a clip across the top.
- e.g. A strong magnet can drag a clip through the side of a glass of water.
### The Earth has an iron heart · *defined*
**Definition:** The centre of the Earth is mostly iron, with some nickel. The outer part of the core is liquid, and its flowing makes the Earth's magnetic field.

Deep below your feet, past the rock, lies a ball of iron and nickel. It is about as wide as the planet Mars. The outer part is liquid, and the inner part is solid, squeezed hard. In 1936 a Danish scientist called Inge Lehmann studied earthquake waves. She worked out that there is a solid ball inside the liquid. She kept her measurements on index cards in boxes. The core is far too hot to be a permanent magnet, since it is much hotter than iron's Curie point. Instead, scientists think its liquid iron flows and swirls, and that swirling makes the magnetic field which a compass feels. Iron also falls from space as meteorites. A magnet can even pull tiny bits of space dust off a rooftop.
- e.g. The Earth's field makes a compass needle point north.
- e.g. Iron meteorites are mostly iron and nickel. A magnet picks them up.
- e.g. Tiny space dust grains, called micrometeorites, can be found on roofs with a magnet.
### Iron comes from stars · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists think most of the iron in the universe was made inside stars, and scattered into space when stars exploded.

The early universe had almost only hydrogen and helium. Stars made heavier atoms inside them by squeezing lighter ones together. A big star can build atoms up to iron and nickel at its centre. After that, squeezing does not give out energy, and the star collapses and explodes. Some small, dead stars in pairs can blow up in another way. They make a lot of iron too. The iron flew into space, and became part of new stars, planets and, eventually, almirahs. So the iron nail in your hand contains atoms made in a star that died before our Sun was born. The iron in your blood has the same story.
- e.g. Iron meteorites carry iron that formed inside an old planet's core.
- e.g. The iron in your blood, in haemoglobin, was also made in stars.
### Living magnets: bacteria, and a debate about birds · *debated*
Some living things carry magnets inside them. Magnetotactic bacteria make tiny chains of magnetite crystals, like a string of beads. The chain acts as a compass needle. A scientist called Richard Blakemore described them in 1975. Some historians say an Italian scientist, Salvatore Bellini, saw them in 1963, but few people read his work. Many animals can also sense the Earth's magnetic field. Scientists have shown this in birds, turtles and fish. But how they do it is a puzzle, and scientists have more than one idea.
- e.g. Magnetotactic bacteria swim along the Earth's magnetic field lines.
- e.g. Migrating birds seem to use the magnetic field, along with the stars and the Sun.
- **Joseph Kirschvink, a scientist in America, and others:** Animals may have tiny magnetite crystals in their bodies that turn like compass needles.
- **Thorsten Ritz, Klaus Schulten and others, in 2000:** A light-sensitive protein in the eye, called cryptochrome, may let birds see the field, using quantum effects in its electrons.
- **Many scientists today:** Birds may use both ideas, one for direction and one for position. The question is still open.
### Super-strong magnets and why they are dangerous · *defined*
**Definition:** The strongest everyday magnets are made from a mixture of the metals neodymium, iron and boron. They can pinch skin, and they can badly hurt a child who swallows two.

In 1982, two scientists found a new kind of magnet. They worked separately. Masato Sagawa worked in Japan, and John Croat in the United States. They mixed neodymium, iron and boron, and made a magnet far stronger than any before. These magnets are inside headphones, phones, electric cars and wind turbines. Neodymium is a rare earth, a group of metals that is hard to dig up and to clean. These magnets are so strong that two swallowed apart can snap together through the gut wall. That needs an operation. That is why this unit uses only ordinary fridge magnets. If you find tiny shiny round magnets, keep them from little children.
- e.g. Neodymium magnets are in earphones and the motors of electric cars.
- e.g. A tiny neodymium magnet can lift a heavy steel tool. A fridge magnet cannot.

## Mostly agreed
- A magnet pulls iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. *How we know:* People have tested this for thousands of years. Children repeat it in classrooms every day with nails, pins and paper clips.
- Not every metal is magnetic. Aluminium, copper, brass, gold and silver are not. *How we know:* Foil, copper wire, brass diyas and gold rings are ignored by a fridge magnet. Scrap dealers use this every day to separate iron from other metals.
- Magnetism comes from electrons, which spin like tiny magnets. *How we know:* In 1915, Albert Einstein and Wander de Haas hung an iron rod and flipped its magnetism. The rod twisted a little, which showed that magnetism comes from the spin of electrons.
- In iron, nickel and cobalt, the tiny magnets line up in patches called domains. *How we know:* Pierre-Ernest Weiss proposed domains in 1907. In 1931, Francis Bitter used a fine magnetic powder to show the patches on a polished metal surface.
- Heat destroys magnetism. Every magnetic material has a heat, its Curie point, above which it is not magnetic. *How we know:* Pierre Curie measured it in 1895. The Curie point of iron is about 770 degrees Celsius.
- A magnet test can show that a thing contains iron, nickel or cobalt. It cannot show that a thing is a particular metal. *How we know:* A thing that sticks must contain a magnetic material. But brass, aluminium, copper, gold, silver and some steels all fail to stick, so not sticking tells us little.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Magnets pull all metals.** (Many children and grown-ups, and some old books; *rejected*)  
  For: Magnets and metals seem to go together. The magnets we use stick to the metal door of a fridge, and a metal nail.  
  Against: Aluminium foil, copper wire, brass diyas, gold and silver are all ignored by a fridge magnet. Out of about ninety kinds of natural element, only iron, nickel and cobalt are magnetic at room temperature.
- **Stainless steel is never magnetic.** (Many shopkeepers and some websites; *rejected*)  
  For: Many steel tumblers, katoris and thalis really do not stick to a magnet.  
  Against: Only the nickel-rich kinds of stainless steel are non-magnetic. Others, which have chromium but little nickel, stick well. Induction stove pans are made from those. Even the non-sticking kind can become a little magnetic where it has been bent.
- **A magnet can tell you whether a coin or a ring is real gold or silver.** (A common belief about jewellery; *debated*)  
  For: Gold and silver are not magnetic. If something sticks, it contains iron or a similar metal, so it is not pure gold.  
  Against: Many fake metals also do not stick, such as brass, copper or aluminium. A magnet test can catch some fakes, but it cannot prove that anything is real. Other tests are needed.
- **The iron in your blood makes your body magnetic.** (A popular joke and some websites; *rejected*)  
  For: Blood contains iron, and iron is magnetic.  
  Against: Your whole body holds only about as much iron as a small nail. It is locked inside molecules, such as haemoglobin, and is not lined up in domains. You are not pulled by a magnet. Hospitals put people inside huge magnets for MRI scans, and nothing pulls them, unless they carry steel.
- **The Earth is a giant bar magnet, with a big permanent magnet inside.** (Many older books and drawings; *rejected*)  
  For: A compass needle behaves as if there were a giant bar magnet inside the Earth.  
  Against: The Earth's core is much hotter than iron's Curie point, so it cannot hold a permanent magnet. Scientists think the field comes from flowing liquid iron. This also explains why the field has flipped direction many times in the Earth's history.
- **A magnet's pull shows that it is alive.** (Thales of Miletus, a Greek thinker, about 2,600 years ago, as reported by Aristotle; *rejected*)  
  For: A lodestone moves iron without anyone pushing it, which is what living things do.  
  Against: We now know the pull comes from electrons lined up inside the rock. Whether a stone has a soul is a different question, and the pull cannot answer it. But Thales was one of the first to look for a cause inside nature, instead of blaming a god. That was a big step.
- **Garlic makes a magnet lose its power.** (Sailors and writers in old times, believed for centuries; *rejected*)  
  For: Many people repeated it, and it was in respected books.  
  Against: William Gilbert, an English doctor, wrote a whole book on magnets in 1600, called De Magnete. He tested the garlic claim and found it was false. He said you should trust experiments over old books.
- **Birds find their way using tiny iron compasses in their beaks.** (Some scientists, and many science books; *debated*)  
  For: Iron-rich cells were found in the beaks of some birds, and iron can sense a magnetic field.  
  Against: Later work found that many of those iron-rich cells were white blood cells, which are not sensors at all. Other experiments point to a light-sensitive protein in the eye. Scientists still do not agree.

## Roots
We have been learning that a magnet picks some things and leaves others. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves, about life, and about the world.

**Deep truth.** An invisible pull reaches through empty space and through paper.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think things must touch to affect each other. A magnet moves a pin through a page without touching it.  
*Evidence:* A magnet pulls a paper clip through a book cover.

- *who am i:* You are made of atoms, and each atom has tiny spinning magnets inside. Almost every atom in your body, apart from hydrogen, was made inside a star.
- *what is life:* Life uses magnets too. Some bacteria carry a chain of tiny magnets as a compass, and many animals can feel the Earth's magnetic pull.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of pulls we cannot see. A magnet reaches across a gap and holds a nail, with nothing in between that our eyes can find.

Every electron is a tiny magnet. In wood, copper and brass, the tiny magnets point every which way. They cancel out to nothing, like the zero DeepKids talks about. In iron, nickel and cobalt, the tiny magnets agree, and the many become one big pull. DeepKids believes this is how the One shows up as the many all through the world. Zero holds everything in balance, and when many parts turn together, something new appears. A nail is a crowd that has learned to lean the same way.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** A teacher, Uddalaka, asks his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water and come back in the morning. The salt cannot be seen, but it is in every sip. The teacher says that what is unseen can be the very heart of things. (A magnet's pull is unseen, yet it fills the space around the magnet and holds the nail. The salt and the magnet both show that what we cannot see can be everywhere.)
- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** Imagine a net with a shining jewel at every knot. Each jewel shows the reflection of every other jewel. Nothing stands alone. (In a nail, each tiny magnet leans towards its neighbours, and every neighbour leans back. Each one is shaped by all of the others, like the jewels.)
- **Aristotle, On the Soul 405a, reporting Thales of Miletus:** Thales said that a lodestone has a soul, because it moves iron. He was among the first to look for a reason inside nature itself. (We now explain the pull with electrons and fields. The wonder Thales felt is still here. You feel it when a fridge magnet moves a clip with no hand.)
- Science (settled): Every electron acts like a tiny magnet. In most materials the tiny magnets cancel out. In iron, nickel and cobalt they line up in groups.
- Science (debated): Scientists do not yet agree on how birds feel the Earth's magnetic field. One idea uses tiny magnets in the body, and another uses a light-sensitive protein in the eye.
- Other view, Many physicists, and the Charvaka school of ancient India: When we say the tiny magnets agree, we are using a human word for a rule of physics. Electrons line up because of laws of quantum physics, and there is nothing like a mind in a nail. The Charvaka thinkers taught that there is only matter, and that we should trust only what we can perceive.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Out of about ninety natural elements, only three are magnetic in a room. Why do you think the universe made so few? (hint: In most atoms, the tiny magnets cancel out in pairs. What would the world be like if every metal were magnetic?)
- *deep:* If a wooden spoon has countless tiny magnets inside it, why is it not magnetic? (hint: Think of a crowd where everyone points a different way, and a crowd where everyone points the same way. Which crowd would you notice?)
- *deep:* A magnet pulls a nail across a gap with nothing touching it. What is in the gap? (hint: Scientists say a magnetic field fills the gap. A field is something that can push or pull things in the space around a magnet. Is a field a thing, or only an idea?)
- *deep:* A frog floated in a magnet, and frogs are not magnetic. So is anything truly non-magnetic? (hint: Maybe 'non-magnetic' is a word for 'too weak for me to notice'. What other words in science work that way?)
- *big:* The iron in your almirah was made in a star that exploded before the Sun was born. What does that make you and the almirah? (hint: Where did the iron in your own blood come from?)
- *big:* A magnet test can say 'no, this is not gold', but it cannot say 'yes, this is gold'. What other tests in life can only say no? (hint: Think about a smoke alarm, a fever, or a doctor's check. When can a no be trusted more than a yes?)
- *big:* The Earth's magnetic field comes from flowing iron deep below. Would life be different without it? (hint: The field helps push away fast particles from the Sun. Scientists argue about how much it matters for life. Mars has almost no field. Venus has none either.)
- *big:* Thales thought a lodestone must be alive because it moved iron. Is there something else that moves without being touched that you feel is alive? (hint: Think of the Moon moving the sea, or a plant turning to the Sun. Which things are alive, and how can we tell?)
- *big:* Waste pickers and kabadiwalas sort metal by hand every day, work that magnets and machines do in rich countries. Who should be paid for sorting the world's rubbish? (hint: Who uses the metal they sort? Who is paid, and how much?)
- *big:* Much of India's iron ore is dug from hills in Odisha and Jharkhand. Adivasi communities have lived there for generations. Who should decide whether a hill is dug up? (hint: Who gains from the iron? Who loses the forest? Is there a fair way to share?)
- *big:* Inge Lehmann found the Earth's solid inner core with a shoebox of cards, working almost alone. Why do you think great ideas sometimes come from quiet people with simple tools? (hint: Think of Salvatore Bellini, who saw magnetic bacteria in 1963 when few people read his paper. Who else gets forgotten?)
- *deep:* In a wooden spoon, the tiny magnets cancel out to nothing. Is 'nothing' always empty? (hint: A crowd where everyone pushes a different way can be perfectly still. Is it empty, or perfectly balanced?)

## Story: What the Magnet Said No To
*Moradabad in Uttar Pradesh really is called the Brass City, and brass plated onto steel is real. Zainab, her grandfather and the trader are made up.*

Zainab's grandfather could no longer see the colour of metal. A cloud had grown over both his eyes, like milk in tea. But he could still hear it.

Every morning, in the workshop in Moradabad, the city of brass, he held up a new sheet of metal. Then he flicked it with his fingernail. If the note came back long and warm, he nodded. If it came back short and dull, he frowned. 'Brass sings,' he would say. 'Everything else only talks.'

Zainab was ten, and she swept the floor. It was a big floor, covered in filings and sawdust and tiny bits of wire, and the worst part was the pins. The workers used pins and tacks and little nails to hold their patterns, and the pins fell everywhere. So Zainab had tied a small flat magnet to a string at her waist. She dragged it across the floor like a fishing line, and the pins leapt up and clung to it. At the end of each day she wiped them off into a tin. She called the magnet Chhoti Machhli, the little fish.

That week the workshop had a huge order. A shop in Delhi wanted two hundred brass lamp stands for Diwali. The sheets of brass would come on a truck on Monday morning. Her grandfather had been waiting for the order for three months, because money was short.

The truck came. The trader was a man in a clean white kurta with a gold ring on every other finger. His workers carried in the sheets, and they were beautiful. They were the colour of a ripe mango. Her grandfather held out his hand, and the trader put a sheet in it.

He flicked it. The note came back. It was short, but the workshop was loud with the truck, so who could be sure? He weighed it in his hand. He ran his thumb along the edge. 'Good,' he said at last, though he sounded like a man trying to convince himself. 'It will do.'

Zainab was standing by the door with her broom. Something bothered her, and she could not say what. So she did the only thing she knew how to do. She unwound Chhoti Machhli from her waist, and let it dangle over the top sheet in the pile.

The little magnet swung once, and then it jumped. It snapped onto the sheet with a click that everyone in the room could hear.

Nobody spoke. Zainab pulled the magnet off with both hands, because it was stuck quite hard, and held it over the next sheet. Click. And the next. Click. She went down the pile. It stuck to every one of them.

'That is a toy,' said the trader quickly. 'A child's toy. It means nothing.'

Her grandfather had gone very still. 'Zainab,' he said, 'go to the shelf. Bring me the old diya, the one my father made.' She ran. The diya was brass, black with age, and as heavy as a fist. She put it in his hands, and then she held Chhoti Machhli close.

It swung once, and it did not jump. It did not even twitch. It hung from its string, over the old lamp, and just turned slowly.

'Brass is copper and zinc,' her grandfather said, to nobody in particular. 'Neither of them cares for a magnet. But iron cares. Steel cares very much.' He turned his clouded eyes towards the trader. 'Underneath your lovely mango colour, there is steel, isn't there? A thin coat of brass on top, like a coat of butter on a roti.'

The trader said many things, some loud and some soft. But in the end he picked up his sheets and went back to his truck, because it is hard to argue with something that clicks.

That night, as the lamps in the lane were being lit, Zainab sat with her grandfather on the step. 'Dada,' she said. 'If the magnet had not stuck, would that have meant the sheets were real brass?'

He was quiet for a long time. A moth went round the lamp. 'No,' he said at last. 'It could have been aluminium with yellow paint on it. It could have been something I have never heard of. Your fish can only tell me what a thing is not. It cannot tell me what it is.' He smiled. 'But today I needed to hear a no more than I needed to hear a yes. A good test, Zainab, is a small honest voice. It says one true thing.'

The next morning he did not flick any sheets. He held out his hand, palm up, and said, 'Teach me to use the fish.'

So she taught him. She guided his fingers until he could feel the little tug of the string, or the lack of it. The new sheets, the real ones, came a week later from a different trader. One by one they went under the magnet in his old hands. Each time, the little fish hung there, calm and still, and did not stick at all.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a magnet pulls iron and steel, but not brass, which is made of copper and zinc. So when a magnet sticks to a sheet that is meant to be brass, the sheet must have steel underneath. The magnet cannot tell you what a thing is, only that it contains iron, nickel or cobalt. Zainab's grandfather also teaches a lesson about tests. When the magnet sticks, it gives a clear no to the question 'is this solid brass?' When it does not stick, it gives only a maybe. The story also asks who is trusted to know. The expert's ears failed him in a noisy room. The child who swept the floor, with a simple tool, saw what he could not.

- Ask: Why did the magnet stick to the sheets but not to the old diya?
- Ask: What was underneath the thin layer of brass on the sheets?
- Ask: Zainab's grandfather said a magnet could tell him what a thing is not, but not what it is. What do you think he meant?
- Ask: Why did the trader call the magnet a toy? Why did it still matter?
- Ask: Who in your home or school notices small things that others miss?

  *Child mode:* Touch a nail to the magnet. Can the nail pick up clips? Now pull the nail away. What happens?
  - Check: When the nail is pulled away from the magnet, most clips fall off. What does that tell us? → **The nail was a magnet only while the magnet was touching it**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do the same with the steel bolt, the steel spoon, the copper wire, the foil strip and the brass key. 2. For each one, touch it to the magnet for five seconds. 3. Count the clips it can pick up. 4. Write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now try the other things. Which ones can borrow the magnet's power? Which cannot?  
  *Child mode:* Try the bolt, spoon, copper wire, foil and brass key. Count the clips each can pick up. Write the numbers.

- **s4 (7 min) · grown-up needed**

At the end of each stroke, lift the needle away through the air. 4. Test if the needle can pick up a paper clip. 5. Do the same with an iron nail, and compare how long each keeps its pull.

  - Think: A fridge magnet keeps its pull for years. A nail loses it in minutes. What might be different inside?
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. What I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Finish like a scientist. What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. Say or write three things. What I think, what I saw, and why.

## Worksheets
### What does a magnet pull? (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these does a magnet pull?  
   Options: A wooden spoon / An iron nail / A glass bangle / A plastic comb  
   **Answer:** An iron nail. *Why:* A nail is made of iron, and a magnet pulls iron. Picking wood, glass or plastic usually means thinking that any small light thing will be pulled. But a magnet ignores all of these.

### Think like a magnet scientist (challenge, 16 questions)

   **Answer:** Even non-magnetic things are pushed a tiny bit by a magnet. *Why:* Almost everything is pushed away very weakly by a magnet. A huge magnet can push hard enough to hold up a frog.    Options: It is much hotter than iron's Curie point / It is too cold / It is made of copper / It is too far down  
   **Answer:** It is much hotter than iron's Curie point. *Why:* Iron stops being magnetic at about 770 degrees Celsius, and the core is far hotter. So the field must come from something else, which scientists think is flowing liquid iron. Picking 'too cold' gets the idea the wrong way round.
3. (write) Explain why a copper wire is not pulled but an iron nail is. Use the words tiny magnets.  
   **Answer:** Every atom has electrons that act like tiny magnets. In iron, the tiny magnets line up in patches, so a magnet can turn the patches and pull the nail. In copper, the tiny magnets cancel each other out, so there is nothing for a magnet to pull.. *Why:* The difference is not in the metals' colour or hardness. ## Applications
- Scrap yards and recycling plants use giant magnets to lift iron and steel out of mixed rubbish.
- Flour mills and food factories pass their products over magnets. The magnets catch tiny bits of iron before they reach our food.
- A fridge door has a flexible magnetic strip around its edge that holds it shut.
- Cupboard catches hold a door closed with a small magnet and a steel plate.
- Loudspeakers and headphones use a magnet and a coil to turn electric signals into sound.
- Electric fans, mixer grinders and car motors have magnets or electromagnets inside them.
- Induction stoves heat pans that stick to a magnet, by using a changing magnetic field.
- A compass needle is a small magnet that points north.
- Old cassette tapes, and the stripe on some cards, store information in tiny magnetic particles.
- Hospitals use huge magnets in MRI scanners to look inside the body without cutting.
- Magnetic whiteboards and notice boards hold papers with small magnets.
- Maglev trains in Japan and China float above the track on magnets and travel without wheels touching.
- Mining companies use magnets to separate iron ore from other rock.
- Pencil boxes and school bags often close with a small magnet and a steel plate.
- Cranes in steel works use big electromagnets that can be switched on to lift and off to drop.

## Open questions
- **Can scientists predict which new mixtures of metals will be magnetic?** Often they can only guess. Computers are getting better at it, but surprises such as Heusler's alloy still happen.
- **How do birds, turtles and fish sense the Earth's magnetic field?** Scientists have shown that they can, but not how. Ideas include tiny crystals of magnetite, and a light-sensitive protein in the eye.
- **Why does the Earth's magnetic field flip, with north and south swapping places?** Rocks show that it has flipped hundreds of times. The last big flip was about 780,000 years ago. Nobody knows exactly why, or when the next one will be.
- **When did the Earth's solid inner core form?** Estimates range from about half a billion to well over a billion years ago. It is very hard to find out, since the core is thousands of kilometres down.
- **Can we make strong magnets without rare earth metals such as neodymium?** Rare earths are hard to mine and clean, and are found in only a few places. Scientists are trying to find strong magnets made from more common metals, but have not yet matched neodymium.
- **Can a magnet be made from carbon and other light elements, with no metal?** Scientists have made magnets from molecules. Most work only when very cold. A cheap, strong one that works in a kitchen would be a surprise.
- **Can humans sense the Earth's magnetic field?** In 2019 a team in California found that human brain waves changed when the magnetic field around people was turned. Other scientists want the result checked again and again.
- **Why are only iron, nickel and cobalt magnetic in a room, when many metals have electrons that could line up?** Quantum physics can explain it for each metal after it is measured. But predicting it from scratch for a new material is still very difficult.

## Connected maths
- In the lending experiment, the iron nail picked up 6 clips. The steel bolt picked up 5, the steel spoon 2 and the copper wire 0. Draw a bar chart of these. How many more clips did the nail pick up than the spoon? **4 more clips. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each object, as tall as its number of clips. The nail's bar is 6 tall and the spoon's is 2 tall. To find how many more, take away: 6 minus 2 is 4. You can check: 2 and 4 more makes 6. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In the Great Magnet Test, 16 objects were tested. 6 of them stuck to the magnet. How many did not stick? **10 objects did not stick.**. There were 16 objects in all, and 6 of them stuck. The rest did not stick. 16 take away 6 is 10. You can check: 10 and 6 make 16. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- Out of 12 objects, 3 stuck to the magnet. What fraction of the objects stuck? Say it in words. **One quarter of the objects stuck.**. Share the 12 objects into 4 equal groups, and each group has 3 objects. So the 3 objects that stuck make one of the 4 equal groups. That is one quarter. You can check: 4 groups of 3 make 12. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- A sorting ramp has 4 magnets. Each magnet catches 6 clips. How many clips does the ramp catch in all? **24 clips.**. There are 4 groups of 6 clips. Add them up: 6 and 6 is 12. 12 and 6 is 18. 18 and 6 is 24. Or use the tables: 4 times 6 is 24. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- A stack of paper that a magnet reached through is measured with a ruler, as shown. How thick is the stack? **3 and a half centimetres.**. The top of the stack lines up halfway between the 3 and the 4. Each small mark on the ruler is half a centimetre. So the stack is 3 centimetres and half a centimetre more. That is 3 and a half centimetres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- A kabadiwala pays 25 rupees for each kilogram of iron scrap. This price is made up. Hira sells 4 kilograms of iron scrap that she sorted with a magnet. How many rupees does she get? **100 rupees.**. She gets 25 rupees for each of 4 kilograms. That is 4 groups of 25. 25 and 25 is 50. 50 and 25 is 75. 75 and 25 is 100. So she gets 100 rupees. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L3`)
- William Gilbert wrote his book about magnets in 1600. Pierre Curie measured how heat changes magnets in 1895. How many years later was Curie's work? **295 years later.**. Take 1600 away from 1895. 1895 minus 1600 is 295. You can check: 1600 and 295 make 1895. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What does 'magnetic' mean? → A magnet pulls it.
- Does a magnet pull every metal? → No. It pulls iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt only.
- Name two metals a magnet does not pull. → Aluminium and copper. Also brass, gold and silver.
- Brass is copper mixed with ____. → Zinc.
- Will a magnet pull a wooden spoon? → No. Wood is not magnetic.
- A steel spoon sticks, but a steel tumbler may not. Why? → Steel is a mixture, and the mixtures differ.
- Does a magnet's pull go through paper? → Yes. Paper does not block it.
- What does a kabadiwala use a magnet for? → To pull out iron and steel from a mixed heap of scrap.
- Deep down, iron is magnetic because its tiny magnets ____ up. → Line.
- What happens to a magnetic nail when it gets red-hot? → It stops being magnetic, until it cools.
- What is the Earth's core mostly made of? → Iron, with some nickel.
