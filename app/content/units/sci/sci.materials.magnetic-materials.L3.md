# Magnetic materials: what a magnet attracts, and why only some things
`sci.materials.magnetic-materials.L3`

**Summary.** A magnet sticks to a steel spoon, but often not to a steel tumbler. A magnet attracts iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt, and these are called magnetic materials. Most metals are not magnetic: a magnet does not attract aluminium, copper, brass, gold or silver. The reason is inside the atoms. Every electron is a tiny magnet. In most materials, the electrons are in pairs whose tiny magnets point opposite ways and cancel out. In iron, the tiny magnets of neighbouring atoms point the same way. A magnet test can show that a thing contains iron, but it can never show that a thing is gold.

**Intro.** *Take a fridge magnet into your kitchen. Touch it to a steel spoon, then to a steel tumbler. Both are grey, shiny and called steel. Will the magnet stick to both?* In many kitchens, the magnet sticks to the spoon but not to the tumbler. It also sticks to the fridge door, but falls off the wooden cupboard door right beside it. Colour and shine do not decide whether a magnet sticks. So what does? You will test more than thirty things and look for the pattern. Then you will find out what is different inside the things a magnet attracts.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what magnetic and non-magnetic mean. A magnet attracts a magnetic material, and does not attract a non-magnetic one.
1. I can guess, test and write down which objects a magnet attracts, and sort them into two groups.
2. I can say the pattern: a magnet attracts iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt, and most other metals are not magnetic.
3. I can explain why two steel things can behave differently: steel is a mixture, and the exact mixture decides whether it is magnetic.
4. I can show that a magnet's force acts through paper, cloth, plastic and water, and that it gets weaker as the gap grows.
5. I can say what a magnet test can prove and what it cannot, and use a magnet to sort scrap.
6. I can say in simple words why iron is magnetic: in iron, the tiny magnets of neighbouring atoms point the same way. I can give big examples: the Earth's iron core, bacteria that carry tiny magnets, and a frog floating in a magnet.

## Terms
- **Magnet**: An object that attracts iron and steel without touching them. It does this on its own, with no electricity. *e.g.* A fridge magnet holds a drawing on the fridge door.
- **Attract**: To draw something closer, even without touching it. A magnet attracts magnetic materials. *e.g.* A magnet attracts a paper clip. The clip moves up to the magnet and sticks to it.
- **Repel**: To push something away without touching it. Two magnets can repel each other. *e.g.* Push the same ends of two bar magnets towards each other. You can feel them push apart.
- **Magnetic force**: The force a magnet puts on a magnetic material, or on another magnet. It acts across a gap, so the magnet does not need to touch. *e.g.* Hold a magnet just above a paper clip. The clip jumps up to it before they touch.
- **Magnetic field**: The space around a magnet where its magnetic force acts. We cannot see it, but we can see what it does. *e.g.* Near a magnet, a compass needle turns and a paper clip moves. They are inside the magnet's magnetic field.
- **Magnetic**: A magnetic material is one that a magnet attracts. Iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt are magnetic. *e.g.* A magnet attracts a steel nail, and the nail moves to it. So steel is magnetic.
- **Non-magnetic**: A material that a magnet does not attract, or attracts so weakly that we cannot notice it. *e.g.* A wooden spoon, a glass bangle and aluminium foil are all non-magnetic.
- **Material**: What a thing is made of. *e.g.* A spoon can be made of steel, wood or plastic. Steel, wood and plastic are materials.
- **Metal**: A material that is usually shiny, feels cold, and rings when you tap it. Heat and electricity pass easily through metals. *e.g.* Iron, copper, aluminium, gold and silver are metals.
- **Iron**: A grey metal that a magnet attracts strongly. When it stays wet, it rusts and turns brown and flaky. *e.g.* An old gate and a tawa may be made of iron. A nail is made of steel, which is mostly iron.
- **Steel**: Iron with a little carbon mixed in, which makes it much stronger. A magnet attracts most kinds of steel, but not all. *e.g.* Train tracks, a bridge, a spoon and an almirah are made of steel.
- **Alloy**: A mixture of a metal with other metals or other substances, made to suit a job better, for example to be harder or to rust less. Steel, brass and bronze are alloys. *e.g.* Brass is copper mixed with zinc. A magnet does not attract brass.
- **Stainless steel**: Steel with a metal called chromium mixed in, so that it hardly rusts. Many kinds also have nickel in them. *e.g.* Your steel tumbler is probably stainless steel. A magnet attracts some stainless steels and not others.
- **Nickel and cobalt**: Two metals that a magnet attracts, like iron. Pure nickel and pure cobalt are rare in everyday things. *e.g.* Cobalt is mixed into some strong magnets. Nickel is mixed into stainless steel and many coins, but in those mixtures it is often not magnetic.
- **Lodestone**: A natural rock that is already a magnet. It is a piece of a dark mineral called magnetite. *e.g.* People found lodestones thousands of years ago, and saw that they attract iron.
- **Pattern**: Something that keeps happening in your results. *e.g.* Every object that stuck was made of iron or steel. That is a pattern.
- **Predict**: To guess what will happen before you try, and to have a reason for your guess. *e.g.* I predict the brass key will not stick, because brass is not iron.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* A nail, a wooden spoon and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Electron**: A tiny particle in every atom. Every electron acts like a very tiny magnet. Scientists call this property its spin. *e.g.* In most atoms, the electrons come in pairs that point opposite ways, so they cancel out. In iron, some electrons have no partner, so each iron atom is a tiny magnet.
- **Domain**: A tiny region inside iron where the tiny magnets of all the atoms point the same way. *e.g.* A nail is full of domains that point different ways, so the nail is not a magnet. A magnet nearby turns the domains to point its way. Then the magnet attracts the nail.
- **Magnetise**: To turn a magnetic thing into a magnet, at least for a while. *e.g.* Touch a nail to a magnet and it can pick up a paper clip. The nail has been magnetised.
- **Curie point**: The temperature at which a magnetic material stops being magnetic. For iron it is about 770 degrees Celsius. It is named after Pierre Curie, a French scientist. *e.g.* A red-hot nail is above its Curie point, so a magnet does not attract it.
- **Ferrite**: A hard, dark, brittle ceramic made from iron joined with oxygen, mixed with other powders. Most fridge magnets are made of it. *e.g.* A flat black fridge magnet is often ferrite powder held in rubber.
- **Diamagnetic**: Repelled very weakly by a magnet. Almost every material is a little diamagnetic, including water, wood and you. *e.g.* A giant magnet floated a live frog in the air. The frog is only very weakly repelled, but the magnet was strong enough to hold it up.
- **Scrap**: Old metal and broken things that are thrown away. Scrap is melted and made into new things. *e.g.* A kabadiwala buys scrap iron, steel, aluminium and copper.
- **Kabadiwala**: A person who buys old paper, bottles and metal from homes, and sells them on to be recycled. *e.g.* The kabadiwala weighs your old newspapers and a broken iron pan, and pays you for them.

## Explanations
### What magnetic means · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is magnetic if a magnet attracts it. It is non-magnetic if a magnet does not attract it, or attracts it too weakly to notice.

Hold a magnet near a paper clip, and the clip jumps up to it. Hold the same magnet near a wooden spoon, and nothing happens. So the clip is magnetic and the spoon is non-magnetic. The magnet does not need to touch the clip, because the magnetic force acts across a gap. Magnetic does not mean the same as 'a magnet'. A paper clip is magnetic, but it is not a magnet. On its own, it cannot attract other clips. It can only do that for a short time, while a magnet is touching it.
- e.g. A paper clip is magnetic. It jumps to a magnet.
- e.g. A plastic comb is non-magnetic. It does not move.
- e.g. A steel almirah door is magnetic. That is why a fridge magnet can hold up a drawing on it.
### Which materials a magnet attracts · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnet attracts iron, most kinds of steel, nickel and cobalt. It does not attract wood, plastic, glass, paper, cloth, rubber or stone.

Test enough things and you find a pattern. Everything a magnet attracts contains iron, steel, nickel or cobalt. At home these are usually nails, safety pins, paper clips, tawas and almirahs. A magnet does not attract anything made only of wood, plastic, glass, paper, cloth, rubber or stone. This pattern holds for thousands of everyday objects. But there are exceptions, and the next cards explain them.
- e.g. Attracted: a steel nail, a safety pin, a paper clip, a steel almirah.
- e.g. Not attracted: a wooden pencil, a plastic ruler, a glass bangle, a cotton hanky, a stone.
- e.g. A tin can is steel with a thin coat of tin, so a magnet sticks to it.
### Not every metal is magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** Only three common metals are magnetic: iron, nickel and cobalt. A magnet does not attract aluminium, copper, brass, gold, silver, zinc, tin or lead.

Many people think a magnet attracts every metal. It does not. Try an aluminium foil ball, a copper wire, a brass key and a gold ring. They are all shiny metals, and a magnet attracts none of them. An element is a pure substance made of only one kind of atom. About ninety elements are found in nature. At room temperature, only three of them are magnetic: iron, nickel and cobalt. A fourth, gadolinium, is magnetic only when it is colder than about 20 degrees Celsius. So being a metal does not make a thing magnetic. Something inside iron, nickel and cobalt is different. A later card explains what it is.
- e.g. A magnet does not attract aluminium foil or an aluminium pressure cooker.
- e.g. A magnet does not attract a brass diya or a copper lota.
- e.g. A magnet does not attract a gold ring or a silver anklet.
### Steel is a mixture, so one steel can differ from another · *defined*
**Definition:** Steel is an alloy, a mixture. It is mostly iron with a little carbon. Stainless steel also has chromium, and often nickel. The exact mixture decides whether a magnet attracts it.

This explains the spoon and the tumbler. Most steel is mostly iron, so a magnet attracts it. But many Indian tumblers, katoris and thalis are made of a stainless steel with nickel in it. In every 100 parts, about 18 are chromium and 8 are nickel. The nickel changes how the iron atoms are packed together. Packed this way, the tiny magnets of the iron atoms do not line up. So a magnet attracts this steel very weakly, or not at all. Other stainless steels have chromium but little nickel, and a magnet attracts them well. Pans for induction stoves are made of that kind, because an induction stove works well only with a pan that a magnet attracts. Bending can also change how the atoms are packed. So a bent rim of a tumbler may be attracted a little.
- e.g. A magnet attracts a steel almirah, a tawa and many spoons.
- e.g. A magnet does not attract many tumblers and katoris, or attracts them only weakly.
- e.g. A magnet always attracts the bottom of a pan that works on an induction stove.
### Mixing metals can make a material magnetic, or stop it being magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** A mixture of metals can behave quite unlike the metals in it. Some mixtures that contain iron are not magnetic. Some mixtures of non-magnetic metals are magnetic.

In 1903, a German scientist called Fritz Heusler mixed copper, manganese and aluminium. None of those three metals is magnetic. But a magnet attracted the mixture. Heusler could not explain why, and nobody could for many years. We now know that in this mixture, the manganese atoms sit in a regular pattern, and in that pattern their tiny magnets line up. It works the other way too. Iron is magnetic, but stainless steel with plenty of nickel in it is not. So you cannot tell whether a mixture is magnetic from the list of what is in it. You have to test it.
- e.g. Heusler's mixture of copper, manganese and aluminium is magnetic, though none of the three metals is.
- e.g. Alnico is a mixture of aluminium, nickel, cobalt and iron. It is used to make strong magnets for loudspeakers.
- e.g. Stainless steel with plenty of nickel is not magnetic, though iron is.
### Tiny magnets inside: why iron is magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** In iron, nickel and cobalt, each atom is a tiny magnet, and the tiny magnets of neighbouring atoms point the same way. Together they form regions called domains. A magnet nearby turns the domains to point its way, and then the magnet attracts the metal.

Everything is made of atoms, far too small to see. Every atom has electrons, and every electron acts like a very tiny magnet. In most atoms, the electrons come in pairs that point opposite ways. Each pair cancels out, so the atom is not a magnet. That is why a magnet does not attract a wooden spoon or a copper wire. In an iron atom, some electrons have no partner, so each iron atom is a tiny magnet. The tiny magnets of neighbouring iron atoms also point the same way. They form regions called domains, each with billions of atoms. Some other atoms, such as aluminium, also have unpaired electrons, but their neighbours do not line up with them. So a magnet attracts aluminium only very weakly. In an ordinary nail, the domains point in different directions and cancel out, so the nail is not a magnet. Bring a magnet close, and the domains turn to point the same way as the magnet. Now the nail is a magnet too, and the two attract each other. Take the magnet away, and most domains in the nail turn back. In hard steel, many domains stay lined up. That is how a steel needle can become a lasting magnet.
- e.g. A nail touched to a magnet can pick up paper clips. Take the nail away from the magnet, and the clips fall off, because its domains turn back.
- e.g. A steel needle stroked many times with a magnet stays a magnet for a long time.
- e.g. Hit a magnet hard, or drop it many times, and it can get weaker. The knocks turn some of its domains out of line.
### Why do iron's tiny magnets line up? · *debated*
Two bar magnets laid side by side turn to point opposite ways. So why do neighbouring iron atoms point the same way? The magnetic force between two atoms is far too weak to do it. The answer comes from quantum physics, the science of the very tiniest things. Electrons repel each other, because they all have the same electric charge. Quantum physics has a rule: two electrons that point the same way must keep further apart. Further apart, they repel each other less. In iron, nickel and cobalt, this makes pointing the same way the more settled arrangement. Scientists agree on this rule. They still argue about the best way to picture it in a real piece of metal.
- e.g. In iron, nickel and cobalt, the electrons' tiny magnets line up this way.
- e.g. In copper, the electrons stay in pairs that point opposite ways and cancel out.
- **Werner Heisenberg, a German physicist, in 1928:** Each electron belongs to one atom. The quantum rule makes each atom point the same way as its neighbours, all through the metal.
- **Edmund Stoner, a British physicist, in the 1930s:** Some electrons do not belong to one atom. They move freely through the whole metal. The quantum rule makes it easier for more of them to point one way than the other.
- **Scientists today:** Both pictures are partly right. For iron, nickel and cobalt, the free-moving picture explains more, but neither picture predicts everything exactly.
### Every material responds to a magnet, a little · *defined*
**Definition:** Almost every material is repelled very weakly by a magnet. This is called being diamagnetic. A few materials, such as aluminium, are attracted very weakly instead.

When we say wood, water or aluminium is non-magnetic, we mean that a fridge magnet cannot move it. A much stronger magnet does move it, a tiny bit. In 1997, a physicist called Andre Geim floated a live frog in the air, in the Netherlands. He used one of the strongest magnets in the world. The frog was not hurt. A frog is mostly water, and a magnet repels water very weakly. In a strong enough magnet, that weak force can hold up the whole frog. Geim later won a Nobel Prize for a different discovery, graphene, a sheet of carbon one atom thick. So non-magnetic really means that the magnetic force on a thing is too weak for us to notice.
- e.g. A very strong magnet can make a drop of water float in the air. A frog floats in the same way, because a frog is mostly water.
- e.g. A magnet attracts aluminium extremely weakly. It repels copper, gold and silver extremely weakly.
- e.g. An MRI scanner in a hospital has a huge magnet. You lie inside it and feel nothing, unless you carry something made of steel.
### Heat can stop a material being magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** Every magnetic material stops being magnetic when it gets hot enough. The temperature at which this happens is called its Curie point.

Heat makes atoms jiggle. When iron gets hot enough, the jiggling is stronger than the rule that lines up its tiny magnets. The domains break up, and a magnet no longer attracts the iron. For iron this happens at about 770 degrees Celsius, when it glows red. For nickel it is about 358 degrees. Pierre Curie, a French scientist, measured this carefully in 1895. When the iron cools down, it becomes magnetic again. Heat can also weaken a magnet for good, so never heat a magnet. Making iron red-hot needs a gas flame and a grown-up, so in this unit we only read about it.
- e.g. A magnet does not attract a red-hot nail. When the nail cools, the magnet attracts it again.
- e.g. A magnet left on a hot stove gets weaker.
- e.g. Nickel stops being magnetic at about 358 degrees Celsius, and iron at about 770 degrees.
### Magnets without metal: lodestone and ferrite · *defined*
**Definition:** Some magnetic materials are not metals. Lodestone is a rock. Ferrite is a ceramic, like a very hard pot. Both contain iron joined with oxygen.

Magnetite is a dark mineral made of iron joined with oxygen. A piece of magnetite that is already a magnet is called a lodestone. People found lodestones thousands of years ago. In Sanskrit, a magnet is called ayaskanta, which means 'beloved of iron'. Today, most fridge magnets are made of ferrite. Ferrite is a ceramic, made by baking iron oxide, which is iron joined with oxygen, with other powders. The bendy fridge magnet sheets are ferrite powder mixed into rubber. So a magnet can be made without any shiny metal. 'Only metals are magnetic' works for kitchen objects, but it is not true everywhere. Ordinary brown rust is only very weakly magnetic. A rusty nail still sticks to a magnet, because there is iron under the rust.
- e.g. The black ceramic magnet inside a loudspeaker is not a metal.
- e.g. A bendy fridge magnet sheet is ferrite powder mixed into rubber.
- e.g. Black sand on some beaches contains grains of magnetite, and a magnet picks them out.
### How did lodestones become magnets? · *debated*
A lodestone is a rock that is already a magnet, without anyone making it one. Most pieces of magnetite are not magnets. So how did a few of them become magnets? Scientists have more than one idea, and they do not agree yet.
- e.g. Lodestones are rare. A magnet attracts most magnetite rocks, but they are not magnets themselves.
- e.g. Lodestones are usually found near the ground's surface, where lightning can strike them.
- **Peter Wasilewski and Gunther Kletetschka, American scientists, in 1999:** Lightning made them. A lightning bolt carries a huge electric current, and for a moment this makes a very strong magnetic field. That could magnetise the rock it strikes.
- **Other geologists:** Lightning may not explain every lodestone. When hot rock cools, it locks in a weak magnetism from the Earth's magnetic field. Some kinds of magnetite also hold their magnetism much better than others. The full answer is still being studied.
### A magnet's force acts through things, and weakens with distance · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnet's force acts through paper, cloth, plastic, wood, glass and water as if they were not there. But the force gets weaker quickly as the gap gets bigger.

Put a paper clip on a table, and lay a sheet of paper over it. Hold a magnet on the paper, then lift it. The clip still jumps up. Add more sheets, and at some point the clip stays down. The paper did not block the magnetic force. The thick stack just kept the magnet too far from the clip. A stronger magnet works through more sheets. So non-magnetic materials let the magnetic force pass. A magnetic material is different. A steel plate that a magnet attracts carries the magnetic field inside itself, so little of it reaches the far side. A steel that a magnet does not attract lets the force pass, like paper.
- e.g. A fridge magnet holds a drawing, because its force acts through the paper on the steel door.
- e.g. A magnet held under a thin table can move a clip across the top.
- e.g. A strong magnet outside a glass of water can move a clip up the inside of the glass.
### The Earth has an iron heart · *defined*
**Definition:** The centre of the Earth is mostly iron, with some nickel. The outer part of the core is liquid, and its flowing makes the Earth's magnetic field.

Deep below your feet, under the rock, lies a ball of iron and nickel. It is about as wide as the planet Mars. Its outer part is liquid. Its inner part is solid, because it is squeezed so hard. In 1936, a Danish scientist called Inge Lehmann studied earthquake waves. She worked out that there is a solid ball inside the liquid. She kept her measurements on cards, stored in cardboard boxes. The core is much hotter than iron's Curie point, so it cannot be an ordinary magnet. Instead, scientists think its liquid iron flows and swirls. Electric currents flow in the moving iron, and these currents make the Earth's magnetic field. That is the field a compass needle turns to follow. Iron also falls from space, in meteorites. A magnet can even pick up tiny grains of space dust from a rooftop.
- e.g. The Earth's magnetic field turns a compass needle to point north.
- e.g. Iron meteorites are mostly iron and nickel. A magnet attracts them.
- e.g. Tiny grains of space dust, called micrometeorites, can be picked up from roofs with a magnet.
### Iron comes from stars · *defined*
**Definition:** Iron is made by stars, and thrown into space when stars explode.

The early universe was almost all hydrogen and helium. Stars make heavier atoms by squeezing lighter atoms together. A big star builds atoms all the way up to iron at its centre. Squeezing iron gives out no energy, so the star's centre collapses and the star explodes. Some small, dead stars, called white dwarfs, can also explode, and they make a lot of iron too. These explosions throw the iron into space. There it became part of new stars and planets, including the Earth. So the atoms in a steel nail were made in stars that exploded before our Sun was born. So were the iron atoms in your blood.
- e.g. Iron meteorites are pieces of the iron core of a small early planet that broke apart.
- e.g. The iron in your blood, inside a substance called haemoglobin, was also made in stars.
### Living magnets: bacteria, and a debate about birds · *debated*
Some living things carry magnets inside them. Magnetotactic bacteria make tiny chains of magnetite crystals, like a string of beads. The chain works like a compass needle. It helps the bacteria swim down into the mud, where they like to live. A scientist called Richard Blakemore described these bacteria in 1975. An Italian scientist, Salvatore Bellini, had already seen them in 1963, but his notes were not published until much later. Many animals can also sense the Earth's magnetic field. Scientists have shown this in birds, turtles and fish. How they do it is still a puzzle, and scientists have more than one idea.
- e.g. Magnetotactic bacteria line up with the Earth's magnetic field as they swim.
- e.g. Migrating birds seem to use the magnetic field, along with the stars and the Sun.
- **Joseph Kirschvink, a scientist in America, and others:** Animals may have tiny magnetite crystals in their bodies, which turn like compass needles.
- **Thorsten Ritz, Klaus Schulten and others, in 2000:** A protein in the eye called cryptochrome, which responds to light, may let birds see the magnetic field. It would work through quantum effects in its electrons.
- **Many scientists today:** Birds may use both ideas, one for direction and one for position. The question is still open.
### Super-strong magnets and why they are dangerous · *defined*
**Definition:** The strongest everyday magnets are made from a mixture of the metals neodymium, iron and boron. They can pinch skin, and two swallowed ones can badly hurt a child.

In 1982, two scientists, working separately, found a new kind of magnet. Masato Sagawa worked in Japan, and John Croat in the United States. They mixed neodymium, iron and boron, and made a magnet far stronger than any before. These magnets are inside earphones, phones, electric cars and wind turbines. Neodymium is one of the rare earth metals, which are hard to separate from rock and from each other. Two neodymium magnets attract each other so strongly that, if a child swallows two, they can clamp together through the walls of the gut. That makes holes, and needs an operation. That is why this unit uses only ordinary fridge magnets. If you find tiny shiny round magnets, keep them away from little children.
- e.g. Neodymium magnets are in earphones and the motors of electric cars.
- e.g. A tiny neodymium magnet can lift a heavy steel tool. A fridge magnet cannot.

## Mostly agreed
- A magnet attracts iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. *How we know:* People have tested this for thousands of years. Children test it in classrooms every day with nails, pins and paper clips.
- Not every metal is magnetic. A magnet does not attract aluminium, copper, brass, gold or silver. *How we know:* A fridge magnet does not attract foil, copper wire, brass diyas or gold rings. Scrap dealers use this every day to separate iron from other metals.
- Magnetism comes from electrons, which act like tiny magnets. *How we know:* In 1915, Albert Einstein and Wander de Haas hung an iron rod from a thin thread and reversed its magnetism. The rod turned a little. This showed that the magnetism of iron comes from its electrons. Later work showed it comes mostly from the electrons' spin.
- In iron, nickel and cobalt, the tiny magnets line up in regions called domains. *How we know:* Pierre Weiss proposed domains in 1907. In 1931, Francis Bitter spread a fine magnetic powder on polished iron. The powder gathered along the edges of the domains, so people could see them.
- Heat can stop a material being magnetic. Every magnetic material has a temperature, its Curie point, above which it is not magnetic. *How we know:* Pierre Curie measured it in 1895. The Curie point of iron is about 770 degrees Celsius.
- A magnet test can show that a thing contains iron, nickel or cobalt. It cannot show that a thing is a particular metal. *How we know:* If a magnet attracts a thing, it must contain a magnetic material. But a magnet does not attract brass, aluminium, copper, gold, silver or some steels. So when a magnet does not attract a thing, we learn little about what it is.

## How we found out
**Michael Faraday (1791 to 1867), an English scientist who started work at 13 as an errand boy for a bookseller. Before him, Anton Brugmans (1732 to 1789), a Dutch scientist., Brugmans noticed bismuth being pushed away in 1778. Faraday tested many materials in 1845..** For a long time, people thought a magnet acts only on iron and a few other metals, and that everything else is untouched. In 1778, a Dutch scientist, Anton Brugmans, noticed that a magnet pushed away a piece of the metal bismuth. Few people followed this up. In 1845, Michael Faraday tested it properly at the Royal Institution in London. He had a very strong electromagnet. He hung a bar of heavy glass between its two ends on a silk thread. When he switched the magnet on, the glass turned to lie across the gap, as if pushed away from both ends. An iron bar would have turned the other way, to point from one end to the other. Faraday then hung up everything he could find, such as wood, bismuth, an apple, bread and a piece of beef. Every one of them moved a little. Most were pushed away, and he called them diamagnetic. A few were drawn in, like a very weak iron. He concluded that every material responds to a magnet, in one way or the other.  
*What people believed before:* That a magnet acts only on iron and a few metals, and leaves everything else alone. Faraday's strong magnet showed that every material moves a little near a magnet. Most are pushed away, and a few are drawn in.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A magnet attracts all metals.** (Many children and grown-ups, and some old books; *rejected*)  
  For: Magnets and metals seem to go together. Fridge magnets stick to metal fridge doors and to metal nails.  
  Against: A fridge magnet does not attract aluminium foil, copper wire, brass diyas, gold or silver. Of about ninety elements found in nature, only iron, nickel and cobalt are magnetic at room temperature.
- **Stainless steel is never magnetic.** (Many shopkeepers and some websites; *rejected*)  
  For: Many steel tumblers, katoris and thalis really do not stick to a magnet.  
  Against: Only some stainless steels are non-magnetic: the kinds with plenty of nickel. Others have chromium but little nickel, and a magnet attracts them well. Pans for induction stoves are made from those. Even the non-magnetic kind can become a little magnetic where it has been bent.
- **A magnet can tell you whether a coin or a ring is real gold or silver.** (A common belief about jewellery; *debated*)  
  For: Gold and silver are not magnetic. If something sticks, it contains iron or a similar metal, so it is not pure gold.  
  Against: Many cheaper metals, such as brass, copper and aluminium, are not attracted either. So a magnet test can catch some fakes, but it can never prove that a thing is real gold or silver. Other tests are needed.
- **The iron in your blood makes your body magnetic.** (A popular joke and some websites; *rejected*)  
  For: Blood contains iron, and iron is magnetic.  
  Against: Your whole body holds only about as much iron as a small nail. That iron is spread out, one atom at a time, inside substances such as haemoglobin. The atoms are not lined up in domains, so a magnet does not attract you. In hospitals, people lie inside huge magnets for MRI scans and are not attracted, unless they carry steel.
- **The Earth is a giant bar magnet, with a big permanent magnet inside.** (Many older books and drawings; *rejected*)  
  For: A compass needle behaves as if there were a giant bar magnet inside the Earth.  
  Against: The Earth's core is much hotter than iron's Curie point, so it cannot be a permanent magnet. Scientists think the field is made by flowing liquid iron. This also explains why the field has reversed direction many times in the Earth's history.
- **A lodestone can move iron because it has a soul.** (Thales of Miletus, a Greek thinker, about 2,600 years ago, as reported by Aristotle; *rejected*)  
  For: A lodestone moves iron without anyone pushing it. Thales thought that only something with a soul could move another thing on its own.  
  Against: Scientists explain the magnetic force with electrons lined up inside the rock, and this explanation works without a soul. Whether a stone has some kind of awareness is a separate question, which the science of magnets does not answer. Thales also took a big step: he looked for the cause inside nature itself, not in a god.
- **Rubbing a magnet with garlic stops it attracting iron.** (Sailors and writers in old times, believed for centuries; *rejected*)  
  For: Many people repeated it, and it was in respected books.  
  Against: In 1558, Giambattista della Porta in Italy rubbed a magnet with garlic, and it still attracted iron. ## Roots
We have found that a magnet attracts only a few materials, mostly iron, nickel and cobalt. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves, about life and about the world.

**Deep truth.** A magnet acts on every material, not only on iron. In iron, the atoms' tiny magnets add up, so the attraction is strong. In water, wood and you, they cancel, and a magnet repels them only very weakly. So 'non-magnetic' means the effect is too small to notice, not that there is none.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort materials into two boxes, magnetic and non-magnetic, as if a magnet acts on one box and leaves the other alone. The science shows one scale instead: strong attraction, weak attraction and weak repulsion. The line between the two boxes is drawn by what our hands can feel.  
*Evidence:* In 1997, Andre Geim and Michael Berry floated a live frog in a very strong magnet, because the magnet repels the water in its body. A drop of water can be floated the same way. In 1845, Michael Faraday found that every material he tested moved a little near a strong magnet.

- *who am i:* You are made of atoms, and every electron in them is a tiny magnet. In your body they cancel out, so a strong enough magnet would repel you very weakly, as it repels a frog.
- *what is life:* Life uses magnetic materials too. Some bacteria grow a chain of tiny magnets and use it to find their way, and many animals sense the Earth's magnetic field.
- *what is this world:* Every material in the world responds to a magnet, strongly or very weakly. Our hands notice only the strongest cases.

Every electron is a tiny magnet. In wood and copper, the electrons come in pairs that cancel out to zero. In iron, the same kind of electrons add up, and strong magnetism appears. So wood and iron are not two separate kinds of thing for a magnet. They sit at two ends of one scale. DeepKids believes the whole world comes from one source, and that many of the differences we see are differences of amount, not of kind.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** A teacher, Uddalaka, asks his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water and come back in the morning. The salt cannot be seen, but it is in every sip. - Science (settled): Every electron acts like a tiny magnet. In most materials, the electrons come in pairs that cancel out. In iron, nickel and cobalt, the tiny magnets line up in domains.
- Science (settled): Every material is affected by a magnetic field. Most are repelled very weakly, a few are attracted weakly, and iron, nickel and cobalt are attracted strongly.

## Story: What the Magnet Said No To
*Moradabad in Uttar Pradesh really is called the Brass City, and brass plated onto steel is real. Zainab, her grandfather and the trader are made up.*

Zainab's grandfather could no longer see the colour of metal. A cloud had grown over both his eyes, like milk in tea. But he could still hear it.

Every morning, in the workshop in Moradabad, the city of brass, he held up a new sheet of metal. Then he flicked it with his fingernail. If the note came back long and warm, he nodded. If it came back short and dull, he frowned. 'Brass sings,' he would say. 'Everything else only talks.'

Zainab was ten, and she swept the floor. It was a big floor, covered in filings and sawdust and tiny bits of wire, and the worst part was the pins. The workers used pins and tacks and little nails to hold their patterns, and the pins fell everywhere. So Zainab had tied a small flat magnet to a string at her waist. She dragged it across the floor like a fishing line, and the pins leapt up and clung to it. At the end of each day she wiped them off into a tin. She called the magnet Chhoti Machhli, the little fish.

That week the workshop had a huge order. A shop in Delhi wanted two hundred brass lamp stands for Diwali. The sheets of brass would come on a truck on Monday morning. Her grandfather had been waiting for the order for three months, because money was short.

The truck came. The trader was a man in a clean white kurta with a gold ring on every other finger. His workers carried in the sheets, and they were beautiful. They were the colour of a ripe mango. Her grandfather held out his hand, and the trader put a sheet in it.

He flicked it. The note came back. It was short, but the workshop was loud with the truck, so who could be sure? He weighed it in his hand. He ran his thumb along the edge. 'Good,' he said at last, though he sounded like a man trying to convince himself. 'It will do.'

Zainab was standing by the door with her broom. Something bothered her, and she could not say what. So she did the only thing she knew how to do. She unwound Chhoti Machhli from her waist, and let it dangle over the top sheet in the pile.

The little magnet swung once, and then it jumped. It snapped onto the sheet with a click that everyone in the room could hear.

Nobody spoke. Zainab pulled the magnet off with both hands, because it was stuck quite hard, and held it over the next sheet. Click. And the next. Click. She went down the pile. It stuck to every one of them.

'That is a toy,' said the trader quickly. 'A child's toy. It means nothing.'

Her grandfather had gone very still. 'Zainab,' he said, 'go to the shelf. Bring me the old diya, the one my father made.' She ran. The diya was brass, black with age, and as heavy as a fist. She put it in his hands, and then she held Chhoti Machhli close.

It swung once, and it did not jump. It did not even twitch. It hung from its string, over the old lamp, and just turned slowly.

'Brass is copper and zinc,' her grandfather said, to nobody in particular. 'A magnet does not attract either of them. But it attracts iron, and steel is mostly iron.' He turned his clouded eyes towards the trader. 'Underneath your lovely mango colour, there is steel, isn't there? A thin coat of brass on top, like a coat of butter on a roti.'

The trader said many things, some loud and some soft. But in the end he picked up his sheets and went back to his truck, because it is hard to argue with something that clicks.

That night, as the lamps in the lane were being lit, Zainab sat with her grandfather on the step. 'Dada,' she said. 'If the magnet had not stuck, would that have meant the sheets were real brass?'

He was quiet for a long time. A moth went round the lamp. 'No,' he said at last. 'It could have been aluminium with yellow paint on it. It could have been something I have never heard of. Your fish can only tell me what a thing is not. It cannot tell me what it is.' He smiled. 'But today I needed to hear a no more than I needed to hear a yes. A good test, Zainab, is a small honest voice. It says one true thing.'

The next morning he did not flick any sheets. He held out his hand, palm up, and said, 'Teach me to use the fish.'

So she taught him. She guided his fingers until he could feel the little tug on the string, or no tug at all. The new sheets, the real ones, came a week later from a different trader. One by one they went under the magnet in his old hands. Each time, the little fish hung there, calm and still, and did not stick at all.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a magnet attracts iron and steel, but not brass, which is made of copper and zinc. So when a magnet sticks to a sheet that is meant to be brass, the sheet must have steel underneath. A magnet cannot tell you what a thing is made of. It can only tell you whether it contains iron, nickel or cobalt. Zainab's grandfather also teaches something about tests. When the magnet sticks, it gives a clear no to the question 'is this solid brass?' When it does not stick, it gives only a maybe. The story also asks who is trusted to know. In a noisy room, the expert's ears could not be sure. The child who swept the floor, with a simple tool, found the answer.

- Ask: Why did the magnet stick to the sheets but not to the old diya?
- Ask: What was underneath the thin layer of brass on the sheets?
- Ask: Zainab's grandfather said a magnet could tell him what a thing is not, but not what it is. What do you think he meant?
- Ask: Why did the trader call the magnet a toy? Why did it still matter?
- Ask: Who in your home or school notices small things that others miss?

  *Parent does:* 1. Try the same with a cloth, a piece of cardboard, a plastic plate and a wooden ruler in the gap. 2. Then put a clip in a glass of water, against the side. Move the magnet up the outside of the glass and see if the clip follows. 3. Write down yes or no for each.  
  *Parent says:* Now try other things in the gap. Cloth, cardboard, plastic, wood. Then try water. Does the magnet still attract the clip through each one?  
  *Child mode:* Try cloth, cardboard, plastic and wood in the gap. Then try a glass of water. Write yes or no.
  - Note: All of these should let the magnetic force through, unless they are very thick. The clip in the water must sit close to the glass. If it does not move, try a glass with thinner walls.
  - Check: A magnet attracts a clip through cloth, plastic and wood. What does that tell us? → **These materials do not block the magnetic force**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the clip on the table. 2. Stack 5 sheets over it, then bring the magnet down on top. 3. Add 5 more sheets and try again. 4. Keep adding until the clip no longer jumps. 5. Write the biggest stack where it still jumped.  
  *Parent says:* Now we find the limit. Add five sheets at a time. At some point, the clip will not jump. Write down the last stack where it did.  
  *Child mode:* Stack 5 sheets over the clip and try the magnet. Add 5 more sheets and try again. Keep going until the clip stays put. Write the last number.
  - Note: Many fridge magnets reach through 10 to 25 thin sheets. Weaker ones reach less. The result depends on the magnet, the paper and the clip. Your numbers may differ a lot from the example.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. If you have a second, stronger magnet, repeat the stack test with it. 2. Compare the two numbers. 3. Draw a bar for each magnet.  
  *Parent says:* If you have two magnets, test both. Which one reached through more sheets? That is the stronger magnet.  
  *Child mode:* Test a second magnet. Which one reached through more sheets? That one is stronger.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the steel thali. 1. Check whether a magnet sticks to the thali. 2. Put the clip on one side of the thali and the magnet on the other side. See if the clip moves. Then say or write what you think, what you saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Does the magnet attract the clip through the thali? What do you think, what did you see, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Try the steel thali. Put the clip on the far side. Does it move? Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: This depends on the steel. If a magnet sticks to your thali, the thali carries the magnetic field inside itself, and the clip on the far side hardly moves. If a magnet does not stick to your thali, the force passes through, as with paper. Both results are correct. They show again that the exact steel matters.
  - Check: Why does the clip stop jumping when the stack gets too thick? → **The magnet is now too far away to attract the clip**
### e3 · Make a Nail a Magnet: which materials can become magnets? (30 min)
- **question:** Can a magnet turn a steel nail into a magnet, and does it work for other materials?
- **predict:** Guess first. If you touch a nail to a magnet, can the nail pick up paper clips? What about a copper wire, or a strip of foil? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use the same magnet and the same touch each time, for five seconds. Use objects of about the same size. Change only the material. Count the clips three times and take the middle number.
- **safety:** A sewing needle is sharp. A grown-up does the needle step. Keep magnets away from phones and cards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then write one sentence about which materials could become magnets.
- **results table:** Object | Made of | Clips it held while touching the magnet | Clips it held after the magnet moved away
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the magnet up by its edge. 2. Touch one paper clip to it. 3. Touch a second clip to the first one, and a third to the second. 4. Keep going. Count how many hang in a chain.  
  *Parent says:* A paper clip is magnetic. Touch one to the magnet. Then touch another clip to the first one. Does it hang on?  
  *Child mode:* Touch a paper clip to the magnet. Touch a second clip to the first. Keep going. How long can your chain be?
  - Ask: The second clip does not touch the magnet. Why does it hang on? (good: The first clip became a magnet.; The magnet turned the first clip into a magnet.)
  - Note: Each clip in the chain becomes a weak magnet while it is near the magnet. This is the idea of the whole experiment. A chain of 3 to 6 is typical for a fridge magnet.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Touch the steel nail to the magnet and hold it there for five seconds. 2. Bring the free end of the nail close to the pile of clips. 3. Count how many clips it picks up. 4. Take the nail away from the magnet and watch the clips.  
  *Parent says:* Touch the nail to the magnet. Now see if the nail can pick up clips. Then take the nail away from the magnet. What happens to the clips?  
  *Child mode:* Touch a nail to the magnet. Can the nail pick up clips? Now take the nail off the magnet. What happens?
  - Check: When the nail is taken away from the magnet, most clips fall off. What does that tell us? → **The nail was a magnet only while the magnet was touching it**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do the same with the steel bolt, the steel spoon, the copper wire, the foil strip and the brass key. 2. For each one, touch it to the magnet for five seconds. 3. Count the clips it can pick up. 4. Write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now try the other things. Which ones become magnets? Which do not?  
  *Child mode:* Try the bolt, spoon, copper wire, foil and brass key. Count the clips each can pick up. Write the numbers.
  - Note: Expect clips on the nail and the bolt, and maybe a few on the steel spoon. Expect none on the copper, aluminium and brass. If your fridge magnet is weak, put the clips closer.
- **s4 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up does this step. 1. Hold a steel sewing needle. 2. Stroke it 30 times along one end of the magnet, always in the same direction. 3. At the end of each stroke, lift the needle away through the air. 4. Test if the needle can pick up a paper clip. 5. Do the same with a steel nail, and compare how long each stays a magnet.  
  *Parent says:* Now we try to make a lasting magnet. Stroke the needle many times in the same direction. Always lift it away at the end of each stroke.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to hold the needle. Stroke it 30 times with the magnet, the same way each time. Does it pick up a clip?
  - Note: The needle should pick up a clip or two, and keep doing so for hours or days. The nail may stop being a magnet within minutes. Results vary a lot. Steel with more carbon stays magnetised better than soft iron. Scientists call steel that stays magnetised a hard magnetic material, and iron that soon loses it a soft magnetic material.
  - Think: A fridge magnet stays a magnet for years. A nail loses its magnetism in minutes. What might be different inside them?
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. What I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things: what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: only magnetic materials can become magnets. What I saw: the nail and bolt picked up clips, but copper, foil and brass did not. Why: the big magnet can line up the domains inside the nail. Copper and brass have no domains to line up.

### a2 · Be the Atoms: the Domain Dance (15 min)
- **safety:** Leave enough space so nobody bumps into anyone.
- **record:** Draw the three scenes: wood, iron, and iron near a magnet. Draw each arm as an arrow.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone stands in a group, about a metre apart. 2. Each person is an atom, and each arm is an electron, which is a tiny magnet. 3. For wood, everyone stretches both arms out sideways, so the two arms point opposite ways. 4. The grown-up walks past holding the big paper arrow. Nobody turns.  
  *Parent says:* We are pretending to be atoms. Each arm is an electron, and every electron is a tiny magnet. In wood, the electrons come in pairs that point opposite ways. So stretch both arms out sideways.  
  *Child mode:* You are an atom inside wood. Your arms are two tiny magnets. Stretch them out sideways, pointing opposite ways. They cancel out.
  - Ask: When the big arrow walks past, does anyone turn? (good: No, because each person's two arms point opposite ways and cancel out.; Nobody points one way overall, so the magnet has nothing to turn.)
  - Note: Say that every arm is a tiny magnet, but each pair of arms cancels out. That is why a magnet does not attract wood.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now the group is iron. Everyone points both arms forward, the same way. 2. Split into two or three small groups. In each group, everyone points the same way. These are domains. 3. Different groups point different ways. 4. The grown-up walks past with the big arrow. Each group slowly turns to point the same way as the arrow.  
  *Parent says:* Now we are iron. In iron, some electrons have no partner, and each atom points the same way as its neighbours. Stand in small groups, all pointing one way. When the big arrow comes, turn to match it.  
  *Child mode:* Now you are iron. Point both arms forward. Your group points the same way. When the big arrow comes, turn to match it.
  - Check: In the dance, what makes the big arrow attract the iron? → **The small groups turn to point the same way as the big arrow**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about what the dance gets right and wrong. Then ask your child to draw the three scenes: wood, iron, and iron near a magnet.  
  *Parent says:* Real iron has trillions of atoms, not three people. And the tiny magnets are electrons, not arms. But the idea is real. What did the dance get wrong?  
  *Child mode:* Draw three pictures: wood, iron, and iron near a magnet. Draw each tiny magnet as an arrow.
  - Think: In the dance, the groups turn slowly. In a real nail, the domains turn in a tiny part of a second. Why do you think they can turn so fast?

### p1 · Build a Magnetic Sorting Ramp (60 min, 3 days)
- **safety:** Keep magnets away from phones, cards and small children. Sweep up any dropped clips so nobody steps on them.
- **record:** Write how many of the 20 clips your ramp caught in each test. Draw a bar for each design. Write what you changed.
- **schedule:** day 1: Plan your sorting ramp. Draw it. Build the first version. (20 min); day 2: Test the ramp with 20 clips mixed with 20 other bits. Count and record. (25 min); day 3: Change one thing. Test again. Compare the three designs. (15 min)
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the task: build a ramp that sorts a mixture. 2. Ask your child to draw a plan. 3. They should show where the mixture starts, where the magnet goes, and which bowl catches what.  
  *Parent says:* Scrap yards use giant magnets to sort metal. We'll build a small sorting ramp. The things that slide down go in one bowl. The clips should stay behind.  
  *Child mode:* Plan a sorting ramp. Draw a slope. Show where the magnet goes. Show which bowl catches what.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Prop one end of the cardboard on a stack of books, about 20 centimetres high. 2. Tape a magnet under the middle of the ramp, so it holds the clips from below. 3. Put one bowl at the bottom to catch what slides.  
  *Parent says:* Prop up the ramp. Tape the magnet under the middle. Place a bowl at the bottom. The clips should stop at the magnet. The rice should slide down.  
  *Child mode:* Prop up the ramp on books. Tape the magnet under the middle. Put a bowl at the bottom.
  - Note: If the magnet is too weak, clips slide past it. If the cardboard is too thick, the magnet is too far from the clips. Both are useful things to find out.
  - Check: Why does the magnet go under the ramp, not on top? → **The magnetic force acts through the cardboard, and the magnet does not attract the rice**
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix 20 clips with 20 non-magnetic bits. 2. Pour the mix at the top of the ramp, slowly. 3. Count how many clips stayed on the ramp and how many slid into the bowl. 4. Write the numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the mixture slowly at the top. Count the clips that stayed on the ramp, and the clips that slid down. How well did your ramp work?  
  *Child mode:* Pour the mix at the top. Count the clips that stayed. Count the clips that slid down. Write the numbers.

  *Parent says:* Change just one thing, so we know what made the difference. Test again. Which change catches the most clips?  
  *Child mode:* Change one thing. Test again. Count the clips. Do it again with a different change.
  - Think: Why must we change only one thing between tests?
- **s5 (13 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a bar chart of the three results. 2. Talk about which change helped most and why. 3. Say what you would change next.  
  *Parent says:* Which design caught the most clips? Why do you think it worked? What would you try next?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each design. Which caught the most? Why? What would you try next?

### What does a magnet attract? (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these does a magnet attract?  
   Options: A wooden spoon / A steel nail / A glass bangle / A plastic comb  
   **Answer:** A steel nail. *Why:* A nail is made of steel, which is mostly iron, so a magnet attracts it. Picking wood, glass or plastic often means thinking a magnet attracts any small, light thing. It attracts none of these.
2. (truefalse) A magnet attracts every metal.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A magnet attracts iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. It does not attract aluminium, copper, brass, gold or silver. Thinking it attracts all metals is the most common mistake about magnets.
3. (sort) Sort these things. Does a magnet attract them or not?  
   **Answer:** a steel nail → Attracted by a magnet; aluminium foil → Not attracted by a magnet; a steel paper clip → Attracted by a magnet; a brass key → Not attracted by a magnet; a wooden pencil → Not attracted by a magnet; a steel safety pin → Attracted by a magnet; a copper wire → Not attracted by a magnet; a steel almirah → Attracted by a magnet. *Why:* The nail, clip, pin and almirah contain iron, so a magnet attracts them. It does not attract foil, brass, copper or wood. Brass and copper are shiny metals, but shine is not what matters.
4. (fill) A material that a magnet attracts is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['magnetic']. *Why:* Magnetic means a magnet attracts it. It does not mean the thing is itself a magnet.
5. (mcq) Which of these metals does a magnet NOT attract?  
   Options: Iron / Nickel / Aluminium / Cobalt  
   **Answer:** Aluminium. *Why:* Aluminium is not magnetic. Iron, nickel and cobalt are. Picking nickel or cobalt often means thinking unusual metals are non-magnetic. But a magnet attracts those two, as it attracts iron.
6. (match) Match each alloy to what it is made from.  
   **Answer:** Steel → iron with a little carbon; Brass → copper and zinc; Bronze → copper and tin; Stainless steel → iron with chromium added. *Why:* An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other metals or other substances. A mixture can behave very differently from what is in it.

It can only tell us what the ring is not.

   **Answer:** Even non-magnetic things respond a tiny bit to a magnet. *Why:* A magnet repels almost everything very weakly, including water. 3. (write) Explain why a magnet attracts a steel nail but not a copper wire. Use the words 'tiny magnets'.  
   **Answer:** Every electron acts like a tiny magnet. In copper, the electrons are in pairs that point opposite ways, so they cancel out, and there is nothing for a magnet to turn. In iron, some electrons have no partner, so each atom is a tiny magnet. The tiny magnets of neighbouring atoms point the same way, in domains. A magnet turns the domains to point its way, and attracts the nail.. *Why:* The difference is not the metals' colour or hardness. It is how the tiny magnets inside them are arranged.
4. (mcq) Which statement about fridge magnets is true?  
   Options: Some magnets contain no shiny metal at all / All magnets are made only of iron / A ceramic can never be magnetic / Rust is the strongest magnet  
   **Answer:** Some magnets contain no shiny metal at all. *Why:* Many fridge magnets are ferrite, a ceramic made of iron joined with oxygen. Picking 'a ceramic can never be magnetic' usually means thinking only shiny metal can be magnetic.

12. (mcq) A key does not stick to a magnet. Which is the best thing to say?  
   Options: It might be brass, aluminium, copper or a steel that does not stick. We cannot tell which / It is brass / It is plastic / It is steel  
   **Answer:** It might be brass, aluminium, copper or a steel that does not stick. We cannot tell which. *Why:* A magnet only tells us whether it attracts a thing. A magnet does not attract many different metals, so a no tells us little. A magnet is best at saying yes to iron, nickel and cobalt.
13. (mcq) How does a scrap dealer use a magnet?  
   Options: To lift iron and steel out of a mixed heap / To find gold / To melt metal / To clean metal  
   **Answer:** To lift iron and steel out of a mixed heap. *Why:* A big magnet lifts iron and steel and leaves the rest. Aluminium, copper and brass, which are worth more, are sorted by other tests.
14. (mcq) Why is iron magnetic?  
   Options: The tiny magnets of its atoms point the same way, in domains / It is shiny / It is heavy / It is very hard  
   **Answer:** The tiny magnets of its atoms point the same way, in domains. *Why:* Each iron atom is a tiny magnet, and the tiny magnets of neighbouring atoms point the same way, in domains. A nearby magnet turns the domains to point its way, and then attracts the iron. Picking 'shiny' or 'heavy' often means choosing something we can see, not what is inside.
15. (mcq) What do scientists think makes the Earth's magnetic field?  
   Options: Flowing liquid iron in the outer core / A giant bar magnet at the centre / Magnets in the oceans / The Moon  
   **Answer:** Flowing liquid iron in the outer core. *Why:* The core is too hot to be a permanent magnet. Scientists think flowing liquid iron makes the field. Picking 'a giant bar magnet' is a common picture in old books.
16. (mcq) Why does a red-hot steel nail not stick to a magnet?  
   Options: Heat jiggles the atoms so much that the tiny magnets cannot line up / It is too bright / It has become aluminium / It has become a magnet  
   **Answer:** Heat jiggles the atoms so much that the tiny magnets cannot line up. *Why:* Above its Curie point of about 770 degrees Celsius, iron is not magnetic. Cool it down and it is magnetic again.

## Applications
- Scrap yards and recycling plants use giant magnets to lift iron and steel out of mixed rubbish.
- Flour mills and food factories pass their products over magnets. The magnets catch tiny bits of iron before they reach our food.
- A fridge door has a flexible magnetic strip around its edge that holds it shut.
- Cupboard catches hold a door closed with a small magnet and a steel plate.
- Loudspeakers and headphones use a magnet and a coil to turn electric signals into sound.
- Electric fans, mixer grinders and car motors have magnets or electromagnets inside them.
- Induction stoves use a changing magnetic field to heat pans that a magnet attracts.
- A compass needle is a small magnet that points north.
- Old cassette tapes, and the stripe on some cards, store information in tiny magnetic particles.
- Hospitals use huge magnets in MRI scanners to look inside the body without cutting.
- Magnetic whiteboards and notice boards hold papers with small magnets.
- Maglev trains in Japan and China float above the track, held up and moved by magnets, so no wheels touch the rails.
- Mining companies use magnets to separate iron ore from other rock.
- Pencil boxes and school bags often close with a small magnet and a steel plate.
- Cranes in steel works use big electromagnets that can be switched on to lift and off to drop.

## Open questions
- **Can scientists predict which new mixtures of metals will be magnetic?** Often they can only guess. Computers are getting better at it, but surprises such as Heusler's alloy still happen.
- **How do birds, turtles and fish sense the Earth's magnetic field?** Scientists have shown that they can, but not how. Ideas include tiny crystals of magnetite, and a light-sensitive protein in the eye.
- **Why does the Earth's magnetic field flip, with north and south swapping places?** Rocks show that it has flipped hundreds of times. The last big flip was about 780,000 years ago. Nobody knows exactly why, or when the next one will be.
- **When did the Earth's solid inner core form?** Estimates range from about half a billion to well over a billion years ago. It is very hard to find out, since the core is thousands of kilometres down.
- **Can we make strong magnets without rare earth metals such as neodymium?** Rare earths are hard to mine and clean, and are found in only a few places. Scientists are trying to find strong magnets made from more common metals, but have not yet matched neodymium.
- **Can a magnet be made from carbon and other light elements, with no metal?** Scientists have made magnets from molecules. Most work only when very cold. A cheap, strong one that works in a kitchen would be a surprise.
- **Can humans sense the Earth's magnetic field?** In 2019 a team in California found that human brain waves changed when the magnetic field around people was rotated. Other scientists want the result checked again and again.
- **Why are only iron, nickel and cobalt magnetic in a room, when many metals have electrons that could line up?** Quantum physics can explain it for each metal after it is measured. But predicting it from scratch for a new material is still very difficult.

## Connected maths
- In the nail experiment, the steel nail picked up 6 clips. The steel bolt picked up 5, the steel spoon 2 and the copper wire 0. Draw a bar chart of these. How many more clips did the nail pick up than the spoon? **4 more clips. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each object, as tall as its number of clips. The nail's bar is 6 tall and the spoon's is 2 tall. To find how many more, take away: 6 minus 2 is 4. You can check: 2 and 4 more makes 6. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In the Great Magnet Test, 16 objects were tested. 6 of them stuck to the magnet. How many did not stick? **10 objects did not stick.**. There were 16 objects in all, and 6 of them stuck. The rest did not stick. 16 take away 6 is 10. You can check: 10 and 6 make 16. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- Out of 12 objects, 3 stuck to the magnet. What fraction of the objects stuck? Say it in words. **One quarter of the objects stuck.**. Share the 12 objects into 4 equal groups, and each group has 3 objects. So the 3 objects that stuck make one of the 4 equal groups. That is one quarter. You can check: 4 groups of 3 make 12. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- A sorting ramp has 4 magnets. Each magnet catches 6 clips. How many clips does the ramp catch in all? **24 clips.**. There are 4 groups of 6 clips. Add them up: 6 and 6 is 12. 12 and 6 is 18. 18 and 6 is 24. Or use the tables: 4 times 6 is 24. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Through the Paper experiment, you measure the thickest stack of paper a magnet worked through. The ruler shows the stack. How thick is it? **3 and a half centimetres.**. The top of the stack lines up halfway between the 3 and the 4. Each small mark on the ruler is half a centimetre. So the stack is 3 centimetres and half a centimetre more. That is 3 and a half centimetres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- A kabadiwala pays 25 rupees for each kilogram of iron scrap. This price is made up. Hira sells 4 kilograms of iron scrap that she sorted with a magnet. How many rupees does she get? **100 rupees.**. She gets 25 rupees for each of 4 kilograms. That is 4 groups of 25. 25 and 25 is 50. 50 and 25 is 75. 75 and 25 is 100. So she gets 100 rupees. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L3`)
- William Gilbert wrote his book about magnets in 1600. Pierre Curie measured how heat changes magnets in 1895. How many years later was Curie's work? **295 years later.**. Take 1600 away from 1895. 1895 minus 1600 is 295. You can check: 1600 and 295 make 1895. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What does 'magnetic' mean? → A magnet attracts it.
- Does a magnet attract every metal? → No. Of the common metals, it attracts only iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt.
- Name two metals a magnet does not attract. → Aluminium and copper. Also brass, gold and silver.

- Does a magnet work through paper? → Yes. Paper does not block the magnetic force.

- Iron is magnetic because the tiny magnets of its atoms ____ up. → Line.
- What happens to a magnetic nail when it gets red-hot? → It stops being magnetic, until it cools.
- What is the Earth's core mostly made of? → Iron, with some nickel.
