# Caring for animals, soil and water: using them so they last
`sci.environment.conservation.L3`

**Summary.** A cow eats the same field of grass day after day, and the field stays green. Grass grows from the bottom of each leaf, close to the soil, so it grows back after grazing. Using something carefully, so that it lasts, is called conservation. Cows, buffaloes, hens, bees and silkworms give people milk, eggs, honey and silk, and each of them needs water, shade, food and kindness in return. Soil needs a cover of roots and leaves, or the rain washes it away. Water needs saving too: Indian cities now catch the rain from their roofs and send it back into the ground.

**Intro.** *A cow eats grass for about eight hours a day, often in the same field. If you cut a plant's stem, the plant usually does not grow taller from that stem again. So why does the field not run out of grass?* In this unit you find the answer by growing wheat and moong in a tub on your window, and cutting both. You find out what the animals who help us need from us, how a cover of roots keeps soil in place, and how cities like Chennai catch the rain. You also meet a bird park where people sent the buffaloes away to protect the birds, and the birds lost out.

## Terms
- **Conservation**: Using and protecting something carefully, so that it lasts for a long time. *e.g.* Cutting only the tops of the coriander, so the plant grows more leaves next week, is conservation.
- **Domestic animal**: An animal that lives with people and is cared for by them, such as a cow, a goat, a hen or a dog. *e.g.* A Kangayam bullock that pulls a cart in Tamil Nadu is a domestic animal.
- **Graze**: To eat grass and other low plants growing in a field, a little at a time. *e.g.* Buffaloes graze on the grass beside a village tank.
- **Overgrazing**: When too many animals eat in one place for too long, so the plants are eaten down to the ground and cannot grow back. *e.g.* A field where goats are kept all year can turn into bare, dusty ground.
- **Growing point**: The part of a plant where new cells are made, so the plant grows longer there. Scientists call it a meristem. *e.g.* A grass leaf's growing point is at the bottom, near the soil. A moong plant's growing point is at the tip of the stem.
- **Soil erosion**: When rain or wind carries away the top layer of soil. *e.g.* After heavy rain, brown water runs off a bare slope, carrying soil with it.
- **Groundwater**: Water that soaks down into the ground and fills the tiny spaces between grains of soil, sand and rock. Wells and borewells pump it up. *e.g.* Many flats in Bengaluru and Chennai get some of their water from a borewell.
- **Rainwater harvesting**: Catching rain from roofs and ground, and storing it in a tank or sending it into the ground through a pit. *e.g.* A pipe from the terrace of a Chennai building carries rain into a pit filled with stones and sand.
- **Pollinate**: To carry pollen from one flower to another, so the flower can make seeds and fruit. *e.g.* A honeybee pollinates the flowers of a mango tree while she drinks their nectar.
- **Vaccine**: A medicine that teaches the body to fight a disease before the disease arrives. *e.g.* Pet dogs get a rabies vaccine every year, so they cannot catch or spread rabies.
- **Wallow**: To lie and roll in water or mud to cool down. *e.g.* On a hot afternoon, buffaloes wallow in a pond with only their heads showing.

## Explanations
### What conservation means · *defined*
**Definition:** Conservation is using and protecting something carefully, so that it lasts: soil, water, forests, animals and plants.

Some things grow back, like grass, trees and fish. They last for ever if we take less than grows back. Other things take thousands of years to form, like good soil and the water deep under the ground. These must be used very slowly, or they run out. Conservation means knowing how fast something comes back, and taking less than that.
- e.g. Picking only a few curry leaves from each branch, so the plant can grow new ones.
- e.g. Fishing boats on India's east and west coasts stop fishing for some weeks each year, while many fish lay their eggs.
- e.g. Planting trees on a hillside, so their roots hold the soil.
### Why grass grows back after grazing · *defined*
**Definition:** A grass leaf grows from a growing point at the bottom, close to the soil. When an animal bites off the top, the growing point is left, and the leaf keeps growing.

A grass plant has a hard problem: cows, goats, buffaloes, deer and grasshoppers eat them all the time. The grass answers this by keeping their growing points low, near the ground, where teeth rarely reach. So the leaf just keeps pushing up from the bottom, like toothpaste from a tube. Most other plants, like moong, beans and tomatoes, grow from the tip of each stem. Cut the tip off and that stem stops getting taller. The plant can only grow again from a bud lower down, if one is left. This is why a lawn can be cut every week, and a field can be grazed every day. Grasses and grazing animals have lived together for millions of years.
- e.g. In the cutting test, the cut wheat grew taller within two days. The cut moong stems stayed short stumps.
- e.g. A lawn mower cuts the grass every week, and the lawn stays green.
- e.g. Sugarcane, bamboo, rice and wheat are all grasses.
### Overgrazing: when the field cannot grow back · *defined*
**Definition:** Overgrazing is when animals eat plants faster than the plants grow back, until the ground is bare.

Grass grows back, but only so fast. Grass plants need their leaves to catch sunlight and make food. If animals eat them again and again before they regrow, they use up the food stored in their roots. Too many hooves also trample the soil hard. Soon the roots die, the ground is bare, and the next rain washes the soil away. Herders who move their animals from field to field give each field time to grow back.
- e.g. In the bare tub test, rain washed far more soil out of the bare tub than out of the tub of wheat roots.
- e.g. In many dry places in India, village grazing land has turned bare where too many animals are kept.
- e.g. Herders in Rajasthan and Gujarat walk their sheep and camels to new places through the year.
### Animals who help us, and what they need · *defined*
**Definition:** Useful animals give people food, work and materials. In return, they need clean water, the right food, shade, a clean shelter, rest and care when they are ill.

Each animal has her own needs, which come from her own body. A buffalo has dark skin and fewer sweat glands than a cow, so she heats up fast in the sun. She wallows in water or mud to cool down. So a buffalo needs a pond, a shower or a wet floor on hot days. A hen needs a dry, clean coop, because damp and dirt bring disease. A bullock that pulls a cart needs rest in the hottest hours. Honeybees need flowers without poison sprays. A silkworm caterpillar eats mulberry leaves and almost nothing else, and needs fresh ones every day. Caring for an animal starts with asking what her life is like.
- e.g. Cows and buffaloes give milk. Their dung is used as manure and as fuel.
- e.g. Hens give eggs. Bees give honey and pollinate crops. Silkworms give silk. Sheep give wool.
- e.g. Bullocks pull ploughs and carts. Dogs guard homes and help people who cannot see.
### Caring for animals is also the law · *defined*
**Definition:** In India, the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act of 1960 makes it a crime to beat, overload, starve or neglect an animal.

Good care is simple to say. Give clean water every day, more in summer. Give shade and rest in the hottest hours. Keep the shed or coop clean and dry. Get vaccines, such as the rabies vaccine for dogs. Never feed animals plastic, or let them eat from rubbish. Cows that wander city streets often swallow plastic bags with food scraps inside. The bags cannot be digested and fill up their stomachs. Vets in Indian cities have taken many kilograms of plastic out of single cows.
- e.g. On Mattu Pongal, families in Tamil Nadu bathe their cattle, paint their horns and feed them pongal, to thank them.
- e.g. Tying food waste in a bag and throwing it on the road can harm a cow. A bin with a lid keeps it away from her.
- e.g. A dog bowl of fresh water in a shady spot helps street dogs in May.
### Soil needs a cover · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil stays in place when plants cover it. Roots hold the grains together, and leaves break the force of the rain.

Good soil forms very slowly, from broken rock and dead plants. A few centimetres can take hundreds of years. A raindrop hitting bare soil knocks grains loose, and the running water carries them off. Under grass or fallen leaves, the drops land softly, the water soaks in, and the roots hold the soil like a net. That is why a bare, overgrazed field or a cut forest loses its soil so fast in the monsoon.
- e.g. In the bare tub test, the water from the bare tub came out brown. The water from the wheat tub was much clearer.
- e.g. Tea gardens in the Nilgiris are planted in rows along the slopes, to slow the water down.
- e.g. Farmers leave crop stubble or grow a cover crop, so the soil is never bare.
### People who stood up for forests · *defined*
**Definition:** Forests hold soil and water, give homes to animals and plants, and give people food, fuel and medicine. Many forests in India were saved by ordinary people, often women.

In 1983, people in Uttara Kannada, in Karnataka, hugged the trees of their forest so the loggers could not cut them. This was the Appiko movement. Appiko means 'hug' in Kannada. They followed the Chipko movement of the Himalayas. In Kerala, scientists, writers and villagers campaigned for years to save Silent Valley, a rainforest where a dam was planned. The poet Sugathakumari was one of its leaders. The dam was dropped, and Silent Valley became a national park in 1984.
- e.g. The lion-tailed macaque, a monkey found only in the Western Ghats, lives in Silent Valley.
- e.g. Sacred groves, small forests kept by villages and temples, are found all over Kerala, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. A forest's roots and fallen leaves let rain soak into the ground and fill streams slowly.
### Saving water, and putting it back in the ground · *defined*
**Definition:** Water conservation means using less water, and helping rain soak back into the ground so wells and borewells refill.

Most Indian cities get water from rivers, lakes and reservoirs, and from the ground. When roads and buildings cover the soil, rain runs into drains and out to sea, so less soaks down to refill the groundwater. In 2003, Tamil Nadu made rainwater harvesting compulsory for buildings. In 2019, Chennai's main lakes went almost dry, and the city needed water from trains and tankers. Rain on a terrace can be sent down a pipe into a pit of stones and sand, and soak into the ground. At home, small habits save a lot: a mug instead of a running tap, fixing drips, and using rice-washing water for plants.
- e.g. 1 millimetre of rain on 1 square metre of terrace is 1 litre of water.
- e.g. Old tanks called eris in Tamil Nadu, and temple tanks across South India, catch rain and help it soak into the ground.
- e.g. Water used to wash vegetables can water a potted plant.
### Is it fair to use animals at all? · *debated*
People in India and everywhere disagree about this, and each side has good reasons. Many families have lived with cows, goats and hens for thousands of years, and care for them well. Others think animals should not be kept to give us things. Most silk is made by boiling the cocoons with the young moth still inside, and some people will not wear it for that reason.
- e.g. Ahimsa silk, also called peace silk, is made after the moth has come out of the cocoon.
- e.g. Some people eat no animal foods at all, and are called vegans.
- e.g. Many dairy farmers in Kerala and Karnataka keep only a few cows, and know each one by name.
- **A dairy farmer:** My cows are part of my family. I feed them, keep them cool and call the vet when they are ill. The milk feeds my children and pays for school.
- **A vegan:** A cow's milk is made for her calf. Animals have their own lives and feelings, so we should not keep them to take from them, however kindly.
- **An animal welfare scientist:** We can measure how well an animal lives: is she healthy, can she move, rest and be with others? Whatever people choose, the animal's life must go well.
### Do grazing animals help or harm a wild place? · *debated*
It depends on the place, the animals and how many there are. Too many animals strip the land bare. But some wild places stay healthy only because something keeps the plants short. In Keoladeo National Park, in Bharatpur, buffaloes were banned in 1982, and a wetland grass spread over the shallow water.
- e.g. In Keoladeo, the grass Paspalum spread after the buffaloes were sent out, and park staff had to cut it back.
- e.g. In grasslands, wild grazers like blackbuck and gaur keep some plants down and let others grow.
- e.g. On many Indian hills, too many goats have left bare slopes.
- **Some park managers and ecologists:** Village animals compete with wild animals for food and trample nests. Keep them out of protected areas.
- **Other ecologists:** Some places were shaped by grazing for centuries. Take the grazers away and the plants change. A small, managed number of animals can help.

## Mostly agreed
- Grass leaves grow from a growing point near their base, so grass can be grazed or mowed again and again. *How we know:* Plant scientists have mapped where the new cells are made in grass leaves. Farmers and park managers see it every time a lawn or field regrows.
- Bare soil washes away far faster than soil covered by plants. *How we know:* Measurements on farms and slopes all over the world show it. You can see it yourself in the bare tub test.
- Most flowering crops make more fruit and seed when bees and other insects visit them. *How we know:* Farmers and scientists compare crops visited by insects with crops covered by nets. The covered ones usually set less fruit.
- Rainwater harvesting helps refill groundwater in cities. *How we know:* After Tamil Nadu made it compulsory in 2003, studies in Chennai reported higher water levels in many wells over the next few years.

## How we found out
**Scientists of the Bombay Natural History Society, including Salim Ali, who first supported the ban, and the team whose long study came out in 1991, 1982 (grazing banned) to 1991 (the study's final report).** Keoladeo, near Bharatpur in Rajasthan, is a shallow wetland where thousands of water birds spend the winter, including painted storks, herons and, in the past, rare Siberian cranes. For hundreds of years, buffaloes from nearby villages grazed there. Many people, including scientists of the Bombay Natural History Society such as the bird expert Salim Ali, thought the buffaloes harmed the birds by eating their food and trampling the wetland. Keoladeo became a national park in 1981, and grazing was banned in 1982. Then something unexpected happened. A wetland grass called Paspalum, which the buffaloes used to eat, spread across the shallow water. It made thick mats where open water had been, and birds that feed in open water lost space. Park staff had to cut the grass by hand. Scientists of the same society studied the park for years. Their report in 1991 said that grazing had been keeping the wetland open, and suggested letting some grazing back. The wrong idea was that a wild place is always best protected by keeping its animals out.  
*What people believed before:* People thought the buffaloes only harmed the wetland, so keeping them out would help the birds. Instead, the grass they had been eating spread over the open water, and the long study showed that some grazing had kept the wetland healthy.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Keeping all people and their animals out of a forest or park is always the best way to protect it.** (An idea held by many park managers in the twentieth century; *debated*)  
  For: Fewer people and animals means less cutting, hunting and trampling.  
  Against: At Keoladeo, the wetland grew over with grass once the buffaloes were sent out. Many forest people protect their forests well, as the women of Chipko did.
- **A plant that has been eaten is damaged, and is worse off.** (A common belief; *debated*)  
  For: Losing leaves means less food made from sunlight.  
  Against: Grasses grow back from the base. Some grasses even make more shoots after grazing, and grazing stops tall plants from shading them out.
- **Saving water at home makes no difference, because farms use most of the water.** (Something many people say; *minority*)  
  For: Farms do use most of India's water.  
  Against: In cities, homes use most of the piped water. When Chennai's lakes dried up in 2019, every litre saved at home mattered.

## Roots
We have learned how grass grows back, what useful animals need, and how soil and water are kept. Now we ask what this tells us about taking from the world.

**Deep truth.** In your tub, the cut wheat grew back within days, and the cut moong stems stayed short. Both lost the same height. Wheat keeps their growing point low, below the cut, and moong keeps theirs at the tip. So taking from a living thing can go on only if the part that grows is left behind.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think taking always uses something up, like cutting a whole bunch of coriander. But the wheat lost as much as the moong and kept growing. What decides is not how much is taken, but whether the growing part is left.  
*Evidence:* In the Cutting Test, the cut wheat grew several centimetres in four days, with their flat cut tips pushed up from below. The moong stumps did not grow taller. In the Grazing Diary, wheat cut close to the soil again and again grew back less each time.

- *who am i:* Your hair and nails also grow from the bottom, so cutting the ends does not stop them.
- *what is life:* Grass and grazing animals have lived together for millions of years. The grass feeds the cow, and the cow's dung feeds the soil the grass grows in.
- *what is this world:* The same rain falls on the field, soaks into the ground and comes back up in a well. The water we save today is the water someone drinks later.

One grass seed becomes a plant, and that plant feeds cows, goats, grasshoppers and the soil below. The cow's milk feeds a family, and her dung goes back to the field. Each of these lives is separate, and each depends on the grass keeping their growing point low. Where people take only the top, the field keeps feeding all of them. One plant's way of growing holds up many lives.

- **Isha Upanishad, verse 1:** All this world is filled by the One. Enjoy it by letting go. Do not grab what belongs to others. (The cow eats only the top and lets the rest go, so the field lasts. Taking with a light hand is what keeps the growing part alive.)
- **Tirukkural, verse 20 (chapter on rain):** Without water, the world cannot go on. (Every animal on the farm, every field and every city depends on rain. Saving water and sending rain back into the ground keeps the world going.)
- Science (settled): Grass leaves grow from a growing point near their base, so grazing and mowing remove old leaf, not the part that makes new leaf.
- Science (debated): How many animals a piece of land can feed for ever depends on rain, soil and plants, and is still argued about for each place.
- Other view, Many biologists: Grass keeps their growing point low because, over millions of years, the grasses that did so survived grazing best. It is how they adapted, not a gift to the cow. Reading lessons about taking and giving into it says more about us than about grass.
- Practice, Take the top (3 min): Let's go to the curry leaf or tulsi plant. Look for the growing tips. Now pick just one leaf from lower down, and leave every tip. Smell it. Close your eyes and take five slow breaths. The plant will keep growing because we left the part that grows.
- Ask: When the cow eats the top of the grass, has the grass lost something, or shared something?
- Ask: What is the growing part of a forest, a river or a family? How could we leave it whole?
- Ask: Is there anything we take every day that cannot grow back?

## Story: The Field That Never Ran Out
*Made up: Kavya, Selvi Athai, Arun and their farm are invented. True: Kangayam cattle come from the Kangayam area of Tiruppur district in Tamil Nadu. On Mattu Pongal, the day after Thai Pongal, families bathe their cattle, paint their horns, put garlands on them and feed them pongal. Grass grows from the base of the leaf, and goats often eat plants down to the ground. Buffaloes wallow because they heat up fast.*

Kavya lived on the sixth floor of a building in Velachery, in Chennai. The only plants she knew were the tulsi and the money plant on the balcony. So when her family went to Selvi Athai's farm near Kangayam for Pongal, she did not know what to expect.

Athai's farm had four Kangayam cows, grey and white, with big curved horns. It had two black buffaloes, a few goats, and hens that ran everywhere. Behind the cow shed was a wide field of grass, with a small pond at one end.

On the first morning, Kavya watched Athai walk the cows out to the field. They put their heads down and began to eat. They tore off the grass with a jerk of the head, chewing and moving on, chewing and moving on.

"How long will they eat?" Kavya asked.

"Most of the day," said Athai. "A cow eats for hours and hours. Then she lies down and chews it all again."

The next morning, the cows went to the same field. And the next. Kavya began to worry. At home, when Amma cut coriander for rasam, the bunch was gone. "Athai, the cows eat this field every day. When will it be finished?"

Athai laughed. "Come and see." She knelt down at the edge of the field, where the cows had eaten the day before. The grass was short there, cut off flat, as if with scissors. "Look at the tips of these leaves."

Kavya looked. The leaves had flat, chewed ends. But they were not as short as she expected.

"Yesterday these were bitten right down," said Athai. "Today they are a little taller. Grass grows from the bottom of the leaf, not the top. The cow bites off the top, and the bottom keeps pushing up."

Kavya thought about this. "So the cow never eats the part that grows?"

"Not if she just eats the top. That is why a field can feed cows for a hundred years."

Just then, Kavya's cousin Arun came running. He was six, and he loved the goats. "Athai! The goats got into your bhindi plants!"

They ran to the vegetable patch behind the house. Two goats were eating happily. The bhindi plants had been bitten off at the top, and some were chewed right down to the soil. Athai shooed the goats away and tied them up.

"Will the bhindi grow back too?" asked Arun.

Athai shook her head slowly. "Not like grass. A bhindi plant grows from the tip of their stem. The goats ate the tips. A few may grow a new shoot from lower down. The ones chewed to the ground are finished."

Arun looked sad. Kavya put her arm round him. "It isn't the goats' fault," she said. "They don't know which plants grow from the bottom."

"Goats eat almost anything," said Athai. "That is why we keep them tied, and move them to a new place every few days. If they stay too long in one place, they pull the plants up by the roots. Then nothing grows back. The rain washes the bare soil away, and it takes hundreds of years to make more."

That afternoon it was hot, even in January. The buffaloes walked down to the pond and lay in the water, with only their heads and their backs showing. Kavya laughed. "They're having a bath!"

"They're keeping cool," said Athai. "A buffalo has dark skin and not many sweat glands, so she gets hot fast. If there were no pond, I would have to pour water on them. Every animal on this farm needs something different. The hens need a dry coop. The cows need clean water three or four times a day. The buffaloes need their pond."

"And the pond needs the rain," said Kavya.

Athai smiled. "That is right. We dug the pond deeper three years ago. Now the rain from the whole field runs into it, and soaks down slowly. My well has water even in May."

The next day was Mattu Pongal. Kavya and Arun helped Athai bathe the cows. They painted their horns blue and red, and put garlands of marigolds round their necks. They fed them sweet pongal from their hands. Kavya stroked the oldest cow's nose. "Thank you for the milk," she said.

Before they left, Kavya pulled up a few blades of grass from the field edge, very carefully, with the roots. She wanted to see the bottom of a grass leaf for herself. In the car, she held them up to the window. At the bottom of each leaf, near the roots, the grass was pale and soft, tucked inside the leaf below.

"That is where they grow," Kavya told Arun. "Right at the bottom, where the cows can't reach."

Back in Chennai, Kavya asked Amma for a handful of wheat and a tub of soil. She wanted to see a field grow back on her own balcony.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kavya worries that the cows will finish the field, because when you cut most plants, that part is gone. But grass grows from the bottom of each leaf, near the soil, so a cow that eats only the top leaves the growing part behind. The goats show the other side: plants that grow from the tip, like bhindi, do not grow back the same way, and animals kept too long in one place eat plants to the roots. Caring for a farm means knowing what each living thing needs, from the buffalo's pond to the grass's time to regrow.

- Ask: Why didn't the cows run out of grass, even though they ate the same field every day?
- Ask: Why did Athai keep the goats tied and move them every few days?
- Ask: What did each animal on the farm need from Athai?
- Ask: Kavya said it wasn't the goats' fault. Do you agree? What could Athai do so the goats and the vegetables both do well?

- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play 10 rounds by the rules. Before each move, the player reads a question card and answers it.  
  *Parent says:* Where should the herd go next? Which field has had the longest rest?  
  *Child mode:* Play 10 rounds. Answer a card to move. Try to keep every field green.
  - Note: Moving every round or two keeps all four fields alive. A wrong answer keeps the herd in place, which makes the game harder. That is fine. Talk about which field to choose.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what kept the fields growing.  
  *Parent says:* What did we do that kept the fields alive? Real herders in Rajasthan move their sheep and camels across the year for the same reason.  
  *Child mode:* What kept your fields alive? Why did moving the herd help?
  - Ask: Why did moving the herd help? (good: Each field got time to grow back.; The herd ate less than grew back in each field.; No field was eaten down to nothing.)
### g2 · Who Needs What: a matching game of animals and their care (15 min)
- **rules:** Cut the 16 cards and lay them face down in 4 rows.; Turn over 2 cards. If they are an animal and her need, say why she needs this, and keep the pair.; If they don't match, turn them back. The next player has a turn.; Playing alone: count how many turns you take to find all 8 pairs. Next time, try to beat it.
- **Printable cards: 16 cards: 8 animal cards and 8 need cards:** Buffalo (gives milk) | A pond, a shower or a wet floor on hot days, because she heats up fast; Hen (gives eggs) | A dry, clean coop, because damp and dirt bring disease; Silkworm (gives silk) | Fresh mulberry leaves every day; Honeybee (gives honey and pollinates crops) | Flowers that have not been sprayed with poison; Bullock (pulls a cart or plough) | Rest and water in the hottest hours; Dog (guards the home) | A rabies vaccine every year; Cow (gives milk) | Clean food, and never plastic bags from the road; Sheep (gives wool) | A haircut, called shearing, so her wool does not get too thick and hot
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write or print the 16 cards. 2. Shuffle them. 3. Lay them face down in 4 rows of 4.  
  *Parent says:* Each animal who helps us has her own needs. Let's find which need goes with which animal.  
  *Child mode:* Mix the cards. Lay them face down in 4 rows of 4.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns turning over 2 cards. A pair counts only if the player says why the animal needs this.  
  *Parent says:* You found the buffalo and the pond. Why does she need a pond?  
  *Child mode:* Turn over 2 cards. Is it an animal and her need? Say why she needs this.
  - Note: The reason matters more than the match. A buffalo has dark skin and few sweat glands, so she wallows to cool down. Sheep of wool breeds need shearing once or twice a year.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which need surprised you the most?  
  *Child mode:* Which need surprised you? Answer the question.
  - Check: Why does a hen need a dry coop? → **Damp and dirt bring disease**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Cutting Test: what grows back, wheat or moong? (52 min, 13 days)
- **question:** If we cut wheat and moong at the same height, which one grows back?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will cut the wheat and the moong 2 centimetres above the soil. After four days, which will be taller: the wheat, the moong, or both the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Grow both in the same tub, with the same soil, the same water and the same light. Cut both at exactly 2 centimetres. Only the kind of plant is different. Measure 3 plants of each kind, not 1, because one plant might be odd. Leave 5 wheat plants uncut, to compare.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the holes in the tub. Wash your hands after touching soil. Don't eat the soaked grains.
- **record:** Fill in the table each time. On the last day, draw one cut wheat plant and one cut moong plant, side by side, with their heights.
- **results table:** Day | Wheat 1 (cm) | Wheat 2 (cm) | Wheat 3 (cm) | Moong 1 (cm) | Moong 2 (cm) | Moong 3 (cm) | What I noticed
- **schedule:** day 1: Soak the wheat and the moong overnight, in separate bowls. (5 min); day 2: Fill the tub with soil. Sow wheat on one half and moong on the other. Cover lightly and water. Sow a second tub with only wheat, for the Bare Tub Test. (12 min); day 3: Water if the soil feels dry. Look for shoots. (2 min); day 5: Water and look. (2 min); day 7: Water and look. Measure the tallest plant of each kind. (2 min); day 9: Both should be about 8 to 12 centimetres tall. Measure 3 of each. Guess. Cut both at 2 centimetres, leaving 5 wheat uncut. (15 min); day 10: Measure the 3 cut wheat and the 3 cut moong. (3 min); day 11: Measure again. Look closely at the tips of the cut wheat. (3 min); day 13: Measure for the last time. Say what you think, what you saw and why. Keep the tub for the next experiments. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 2 tablespoons of wheat in one bowl and 2 tablespoons of moong in the other. 2. Cover each with water. 3. Leave them overnight.  
  *Parent says:* A cow eats the same field every day, and the field doesn't run out. Today we start a tiny field of our own, to find out why.  
  *Child mode:* Put wheat in one bowl. Put moong in the other. Cover them with water. Leave them till tomorrow.
  - Note: Soaking makes the seeds swell and start growing faster. Any whole wheat from a grocery shop works, but not atta or broken wheat (dalia).
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the tub with soil to about 2 centimetres from the top. 2. Put a label W on the left half and M on the right half. 3. Spread the wheat thickly over the W half. 4. Spread the moong over the M half, about a finger-width apart. 5. Cover with a thin layer of soil. 6. Water gently until the soil is damp, not muddy. 7. Put the tub on its plate by a bright window. 8. Fill the second tub the same way and spread wheat over all of it. Keep it next to the first. You will need it for the Bare Tub Test.  
  *Parent says:* We grow both in the same tub, so they get the same soil, water and light. Then the only difference is the kind of plant.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the tub with soil. Wheat goes on one side. Moong goes on the other. Cover them with a little soil. Water gently. Now sow a second tub with only wheat.
  - Note: A grown-up pokes the drainage holes with a heated skewer or the tip of a scissor. Wheat is a grass. Moong is not. Don't tell your child that yet.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every day or two, touch the soil. If it feels dry, add half a cup of water. On day 7, measure the tallest plant of each kind from the soil to the tip.  
  *Parent says:* Feel the soil first. We only water when it feels dry, because soggy soil rots seeds.  
  *Child mode:* Touch the soil. Dry? Give half a cup of water. Wet? Leave it.
  - Note: Wheat shoots usually show in 2 to 3 days and moong in 2 days. In about a week, both are usually 8 to 12 centimetres tall. In a cold room it may take 10 days. Moong grown indoors can lean towards the window, which is fine.
  - Wait: Water every day or two for about a week, until both kinds are 8 to 12 centimetres tall.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose 3 wheat plants and 3 moong plants near the middle of each half. 2. Measure each from the soil to the top. 3. Write the heights in the table as Day 9, before the cut. 4. Look closely at where each plant's leaves come from.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure first, so we can compare later. Now look at the wheat leaves. Where do they come out of the plant? And where do the moong leaves come out?  
  *Child mode:* Measure 3 wheat and 3 moong. Write the numbers down. Where do the wheat leaves start? Where do the moong leaves start?
  - Ask: Where do the leaves come from on each plant? (good: The wheat leaves come straight out of the soil.; The moong leaves are at the top of a stem.; The moong has two round leaves and a stem.)
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess, and to give a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to cut both kinds at 2 centimetres, like a cow biting off the top. In four days, which will be taller? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will cut both at 2 centimetres. Which will grow back? Why do you think so? Write your guess.
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. Many children think neither will grow back, or that both will. Don't correct the guess. The test will answer it.
- **s6 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the ruler upright in the soil. 2. Cut the 3 measured wheat plants, and all the other wheat except 5, exactly at the 2 centimetre mark. 3. Cut all the moong at 2 centimetres too. 4. Leave the 5 uncut wheat plants in one corner.  
  *Parent says:* Now we are the cow. Cut straight across, at the 2 centimetre mark, for both kinds.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the ruler up. Cut the wheat at 2 centimetres. Cut the moong at 2 centimetres too. Leave 5 wheat plants uncut.
  - Note: Help with the scissors. Moong seed leaves are the two thick, round leaves. If they sit lower than 2 centimetres, cut just below them, so no leaves are left on the stump. Write down which you did.
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 10, 11 and 13, measure the same 3 wheat and 3 moong from the soil to the top. Write each height in the table. Look at the tips of the cut wheat leaves.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure the same plants. Look at the tips of the wheat. Are they pointed, or flat where we cut them?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the same 3 wheat and 3 moong. Write the numbers. Look at the tips of the wheat. Pointed or flat?
  - Note: Cut wheat usually grows back 1 to 2 centimetres a day in a warm room. New, pointed leaves also come up from the middle. The moong stumps usually stay the same height and may dry up. If a moong stump kept their round seed leaves, a small side shoot may slowly grow beside them, from a bud. Write it down if you see it.
  - Check: The cut wheat leaves are taller, but their tips are still flat where you cut them. Where did the new part grow? → **At the bottom, near the soil, pushing the flat tip up**
- **s8 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make a bar for each plant on the last day. 2. Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: wheat grows back after cutting and moong doesn't. What I saw: the cut wheat grew from 2 to 7 centimetres in four days, and the moong stayed at 2. Why: wheat grows from the bottom of the leaf, so cutting the top doesn't stop them. Now read the card 'Why grass grows back after grazing'. Keep the tub for the Dot Test and the Bare Tub Test.
### e2 · The Dot Test: does a wheat leaf grow at the top or the bottom? (20 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Does a wheat leaf grow longer at the top, in the middle, or at the bottom?
- **predict:** Guess first. You put one dot 2 centimetres above the soil and one 4 centimetres above the soil. In two days, will the dots move apart, stay the same, or both move up? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Mark 3 leaves, not 1, in case one leaf is odd. Measure from the same place, the top of the soil, every time.
- **safety:** Press the pen gently, so the leaf doesn't tear.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw one leaf on day 1 and on day 3, with its dots.
- **results table:** Leaf | Day 1: soil to lower dot (cm) | Day 1: gap between dots (cm) | Day 3: soil to lower dot (cm) | Day 3: gap between dots (cm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Put two dots on 3 young leaves. Measure. Guess. (10 min); day 3: Measure again. Compare. (10 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose 3 young wheat leaves. 2. Hold the ruler upright in the soil beside each leaf. 3. Put a small dot on the leaf at 2 centimetres, and another at 4 centimetres. 4. Write down the heights and the gap, which is 2 centimetres.  
  *Parent says:* If a leaf grows at the top, the dots stay where they are. If the leaf grows in the middle, the dots move apart. What if she grows at the bottom? What do you think will happen? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Put a dot at 2 centimetres. Put another at 4 centimetres. Do it on 3 leaves. What will the dots do?
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure from the soil to the lower dot on each leaf. 2. Measure the gap between the two dots. 3. Write them in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Are the dots further apart? Are they higher? What does that tell us about where the leaf grows?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the soil to the lower dot. Measure the gap. Did the dots move apart, or just move up?
  - Note: The lower dot usually rises by 1 to 3 centimetres in two days while the gap stays about the same. If the lower dot was very close to the soil, the gap may grow a little, because the growing part is just there. That is a useful clue too.
  - Check: Both dots are higher above the soil, but the gap between them is still 2 centimetres. Where is the leaf growing? → **Below the lower dot, near the soil**
### e3 · The Bare Tub Test: does a mat of roots keep soil from washing away? (25 min)
- **question:** When rain falls on a slope, does a mat of grass roots stop the soil washing away?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which tub will lose more soil when we pour water on it: the bare tub or the wheat tub? What will the water look like? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same kind of soil in both tubs. Tilt them on the same books, to the same slope. Pour the same amount of water, from the same height, at the same speed. Only the cover is different.
- **safety:** Do this in the bathroom or on the balcony, where mud can be washed away. A grown-up makes the holes in the bottle cap. Wash your hands afterwards.
- **record:** Draw the two bowls, colouring the water as it looked. Fill in the table.
- **results table:** Tub | Colour of the water that ran off | Soil settled in the bowl after 10 minutes (a little, some, a lot)
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cover the books with the plastic bag. 2. Lean both tubs on the books, at the same slope, with the holes facing down the slope. 3. Put a bowl at the low end of each tub, under the holes and the edge.  
  *Parent says:* These two tubs are two little hillsides. One is bare, like a field eaten to the ground. One is covered in grass, like a field grazed just a little.  
  *Child mode:* Lean both tubs on the books. Same slope for both. Put a bowl under each one.
  - Note: Soil can slide out of the bare tub. A slope of about one book height is enough. The tubs drain through the holes and over the low edge, so place the bowls to catch both.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason before any water is poured.  
  *Parent says:* We will rain the same water on both. Which bowl will get more soil? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which bowl will get more soil? Why do you think so?
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the bottle with 2 cups of water. 2. Hold it about 20 centimetres above the top of the bare tub. 3. Squeeze gently so it sprinkles, for about 1 minute. 4. Do the same for the wheat tub.  
  *Parent says:* Rain gently, from the same height, for the same time. Watch where the water goes.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the bottle up high. Squeeze gently, like rain. Count to 60. Now do the other tub.
  - Note: If water pours straight out of the bare tub without sinking in, that is part of the result too. Bare soil often forms a crust that rain runs off.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the colour of the water in the two bowls. 2. Wait 10 minutes for the soil to settle. 3. Tip the bowls gently and look at the soil at the bottom.  
  *Parent says:* Which bowl is muddier? Which has more soil at the bottom? What did the roots do?  
  *Child mode:* Which water is browner? Wait 10 minutes. Which bowl has more soil at the bottom?
  - Note: The bare tub usually gives brown water and a clear layer of soil in the bowl. The wheat tub's water is usually much clearer, because the roots form a mat. Link it to the card 'Soil needs a cover': this is why an overgrazed field loses its soil.
  - Check: The bare tub's water was brown, and the wheat tub's water was much clearer. Why? → **The roots held the soil, and the leaves slowed the drops**

## Worksheets
### Grow Back or Run Out (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meena, a girl in Class 3 in Mysuru, cuts wheat and moong plants at 2 centimetres above the soil. Four days later, which will be taller?  
   Options: The moong / The wheat / Both the same, 2 centimetres  
   **Answer:** The wheat. *Why:* Wheat grows from the bottom of each leaf, so the cut leaves keep pushing up. Moong grows from the tip of each stem, and the tips were cut off. Picking 'both the same' means thinking no plant can grow after cutting.
2. (fill) Using and protecting something carefully, so that it lasts, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['conservation']. *Why:* Conservation means taking care that soil, water, forests and animals last. A strong answer could add an example, such as picking a few curry leaves from each branch.
3. (truefalse) Lakshmi says, 'When a cow eats grass, that grass plant is finished.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The cow bites off the top, but the growing point at the bottom of each leaf is left, so the grass grows again. A strong answer says where the grass grows from.
4. (match) Match each animal to one thing she gives people.  
   **Answer:** Buffalo → milk; Hen → eggs; Honeybee → honey; Silkworm → silk; Sheep → wool. *Why:* Each of these animals gives people something. A strong answer could also say one thing each animal needs in return.

## Applications
- Lawns, playgrounds and cricket grounds can be mowed every week because grass grows from the base.
- Herders and dairy farmers move animals between fields so the grass can grow back.
- Farmers cover soil with crop stubble, leaves or a cover crop so the monsoon does not wash it away.
- Tea and coffee estates on hills plant along the slopes and keep the ground covered.
- Buildings in Tamil Nadu and many other states must harvest rainwater.
- Fishing stops for some weeks each year on India's coasts, so fish can breed.
- Vets, animal shelters and animal welfare groups care for working and street animals.
- Beekeepers place hives near crops like mustard and sunflower, so the bees pollinate them.
- Silk farmers in Karnataka grow mulberry fields to feed their silkworms.

## Open questions
- **How many animals can a field or a hill feed for ever without being harmed?** It depends on the rain, the soil, the plants and the animals, and it changes from year to year. Scientists still argue about how to measure it.
- **Did grasses and grazing animals shape each other?** Grasses and grazers spread together over millions of years. How much each one changed the other is still being studied from fossils of teeth and plants.
- **How much does rainwater harvesting really refill a city's groundwater?** Some studies in Chennai found well levels rising after 2003, but many pits are not cleaned, and the amount is hard to measure across a whole city.
- **What is the best way to protect a wild place: keep people and their animals out, or let them share it carefully?** Keoladeo and other places show both ways can go wrong. The answer may be different for every place.

## Connected maths
- In the Cutting Test, a wheat plant was cut at 2 centimetres. Four days later it was 7 centimetres tall. How much did it grow in the four days, and how much is that each day? **5 centimetres in four days, which is about 1 and a quarter centimetres each day.**. 7 take away 2 is 5, so it grew 5 centimetres. 5 shared over 4 days is 1 centimetre each day, with 1 centimetre left over, shared as a quarter each day. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Rain falls 10 millimetres deep on a terrace of 50 square metres. How many litres of water fell on the terrace? **500 litres.**. 1 millimetre on 1 square metre is 1 litre. 10 millimetres on 1 square metre is 10 litres. On 50 square metres it is 50 times 10, which is 500 litres. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- A dripping tap fills 40 millilitres in 10 minutes. How much does it waste in one hour? **240 millilitres.**. An hour is 60 minutes, which is 6 groups of 10 minutes. 6 times 40 is 240 millilitres. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Four Fields game, a field has 5 tokens. Each round the herd eats 2 and the field grows 1 back. After how many rounds is it down to 2 tokens? What happens if the herd stays longer? **After 3 rounds. If the herd stays, the field soon reaches 0 and turns bare.**. Each round the field loses 2 and gains 1, so it loses 1 overall. 5, then 4, then 3, then 2. Staying on means 1, then 0. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Tamil Nadu made rainwater harvesting compulsory in 2003. Chennai's lakes went almost dry in 2019. How many years apart are these? **16 years.**. 2019 take away 2003. 2003 plus 10 is 2013, and 6 more is 2019. So it is 16 years. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
