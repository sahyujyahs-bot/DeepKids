# Symmetry in nature and art: moves that change nothing, and how nature breaks them
`sci.patterns-symmetry.symmetry-in-nature.L4`

**Summary.** A square card fits back into its own outline in eight different ways, but a plain triangle cut at random fits in only one. Count the ways, and a shape's symmetry becomes a number. Your child counts the fits, builds border patterns from five moves, and finds that a left hand cannot be turned into a right hand. Then a straight strip, pushed equally from both ends, bends to one side anyway. Nature uses this to give a body a left and a right, and physics uses it to explain why energy is never lost. A chemistry puzzle remains open: life uses only left-handed building blocks, and nobody knows why.

**Intro.** *Hold up your left hand, then your right. They are the same size, with the same fingers in the same order. Now try to lay your left hand exactly on top of your right, both palms down. You cannot, however you turn it. What is different between two things that match in every measurement?* In Class 3 you found lines of symmetry by folding and rotational symmetry by turning. Now we go one step deeper. A symmetry is any move that leaves a shape looking the same. You will count all the moves a shape allows, see why borders on sarees come in exactly seven kinds, and find out why hands, screws and shells have a handedness. Then you will push a strip and watch a perfectly even push make a lopsided bend.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name four moves that can leave a shape looking the same: a flip, a turn, a slide and a glide.
1. I can count every way a shape fits back into its own outline, and explain why a regular shape with n sides fits back in 2 times n ways.
2. I can make border patterns with a stencil and say how many kinds of border pattern exist.
3. I can explain why a left hand cannot be turned to fit a right hand, and name three things in nature or at home that have a handedness.
4. I can explain symmetry breaking using the pushed strip: an even push, a lopsided bend, and a tiny difference that decides the direction.
5. I can give two ways that a lopsided body part helps a living being, and say what is still not known about how a body gets its left and right.
6. I can say in simple words what Emmy Noether and Chien-Shiung Wu each found about symmetry in the laws of nature.

## Terms
- **Symmetry**: A move that leaves a shape looking exactly the same as before. The move can be a flip, a turn, a slide or a glide. *e.g.* A steel thali looks the same after any turn. So turning is a symmetry of the thali.
- **Reflection**: A flip over a line, like looking in a mirror. Every point goes to the same distance on the other side of the line. *e.g.* A butterfly's left wing is the reflection of the right wing.
- **Rotation**: A turn of a shape around a fixed point called the centre. *e.g.* A ceiling fan with three blades looks the same after one third of a turn.
- **Translation**: A slide of a shape in a straight line without turning or flipping it. *e.g.* The same flower is printed again and again along a border, each one slid a little to the right.
- **Glide reflection**: A flip and a slide done together. The shape is flipped over a line and slid along the line. *e.g.* Footprints in wet sand: the left footprint and the right footprint glide along the path.
- **Identity**: The do-nothing move. Every shape fits back onto itself if you leave it where it is, so the identity always counts as one symmetry. *e.g.* Even the letter F, which has no flip or turn that works, has the identity.
- **Mirror image**: What you see in a flat mirror. It looks like the thing, but with left and right reversed. *e.g.* The mirror image of your right hand looks like a left hand.
- **Handed**: A thing is handed if its mirror image cannot be turned to match it. Scientists also say the thing is chiral, from the Greek word for hand. *e.g.* A glove is handed. A steel katori is not, because it matches its mirror image.
- **Helix**: A shape that winds round like a spring or a screw thread. A helix can wind one way or the other. *e.g.* The thread on a jar lid is a helix. So is the shape of DNA.
- **Border pattern**: A pattern that repeats along a line, like the border of a saree or a band of carving on a temple wall. Mathematicians call it a frieze pattern. *e.g.* The row of small temples painted along the edge of a Kanchipuram saree is a border pattern.
- **Tiling**: A pattern that covers a flat surface completely with repeating shapes, with no gaps and no overlaps. *e.g.* A bathroom floor of square tiles is a tiling. So is a honeycomb.
- **Symmetry breaking**: When something that starts out perfectly even ends up lopsided. A tiny difference decides which way it goes. *e.g.* A tall pencil balanced on its tip must fall, and it falls one way, though nothing pushed it.
- **Buckle**: To bend suddenly sideways when pushed hard from the ends. *e.g.* A thin plastic ruler buckles when you push its two ends towards each other.
- **Embryo**: A living being at the very beginning of growth, before the body has taken shape. *e.g.* A chick embryo inside an egg, in the first days, is a tiny curled body.
- **Crystal**: A solid whose tiny particles are arranged in a pattern that repeats in every direction. *e.g.* A grain of salt is a tiny cube-shaped crystal.
- **Quasicrystal**: A solid with an orderly pattern of particles that never exactly repeats. The word means 'almost a crystal'. *e.g.* Some quasicrystals have a five-fold symmetry, which an ordinary crystal can never have.
- **Conserved**: Kept the same total, never made or lost. Scientists say energy is conserved, because it can move or change form but the total stays the same. *e.g.* A swinging swing slows down and stops. Its energy has gone into warming the air and the rope, not vanished.
- **Virus**: A tiny living being, far smaller than a bacterium, that lives inside the cells of other living beings. Many viruses build a shell from many identical pieces. *e.g.* Many viruses have a shell with 20 flat triangular faces.
- **Cilia**: Tiny hairs on the surface of a cell that wave or spin. Cilia can push fluid along. *e.g.* The cilia in your windpipe sweep dust and mucus upwards.

## Explanations
### Symmetry is a move that changes nothing · *defined*
**Definition:** A symmetry of a shape is a move, such as a flip, a turn, a slide or a glide, after which the shape looks exactly as it did before.

In Class 3 you tested for a fold line and for a turn. Those are two of four moves. A flip swaps the two sides of a line. A turn spins the shape round a centre. A slide moves it along without turning it. A glide flips it and slides it at the same time. A single shape, such as the outline of a butterfly, can only be flipped or turned back onto its own outline. But a pattern that goes on and on, such as a saree border, can be slid and glided onto itself as well. A move counts as a symmetry only if every part lands on a matching part. Mathematicians describe a shape or a pattern by listing all its symmetries.
- e.g. A steel thali: any turn, and a flip over any line through its centre.
- e.g. A saree border of identical mangoes: a slide to the next mango.
- e.g. Footprints along a path: a glide, because the left foot and the right foot alternate.
- e.g. The letter F: only the do-nothing move.
### Counting every way a shape fits back · *defined*
**Definition:** The symmetry count of a shape is the number of different ways it can fit back into its own outline, counting the do-nothing move as one.

Cut a square from card and trace around it. Now find every way to put it back in its outline. You can leave it, turn it a quarter, a half, three quarters, and you can flip it and turn it in the same ways. That is 4 turns and 4 flips, which makes 8. An equilateral triangle, with three equal sides, fits back in 6 ways. A rectangle that is not a square fits in 4. A triangle with three different sides fits in only 1, the do-nothing move. A regular shape with n equal sides always fits back in 2 times n ways: n turns and n flips. A circle fits back in endless ways. A shape with a higher symmetry count looks more regular.
- e.g. Square: 8 ways.
- e.g. Equilateral triangle: 6 ways.
- e.g. Rectangle that is not a square: 4 ways.
- e.g. Regular hexagon, like a honeycomb cell: 12 ways.
### Borders and floors: seven and seventeen · *defined*
**Definition:** There are exactly seven different kinds of border pattern, and exactly seventeen different kinds of pattern that cover a flat floor.

A border is a pattern that repeats along a line. You can build one from a single shape, such as the letter F, by repeating it with a slide, a glide, a flip over a line across the border, a flip over a line along the border, or a half turn. Mix these moves and you get exactly seven border patterns, and not an eighth. For a floor, the pattern repeats in two directions. Mathematicians showed there are exactly seventeen kinds. A Russian crystal scientist, Evgraf Fedorov, proved this in 1891, and the mathematician George Pólya found the same result again in 1924. Many designers in the world have used all kinds without knowing the count, and the count is a surprise: the possible patterns are not endless.
- e.g. The row of mangoes on a Kanchipuram saree border: a slide.
- e.g. A border of leaves that point alternately up and down: a glide.
- e.g. A row of diyas whose reflection is also a diya: a flip over a line along the border.
- e.g. A tiled floor of squares or hexagons.
### Left and right: why a hand cannot be turned into its twin · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing is handed, or chiral, if its mirror image cannot be turned to match the original. A left hand and a right hand are mirror twins.

Lay a cut-out of your left hand on your right hand outline, both facing the same way up. It does not fit. Flip it over and it fits. A flat cut-out can be flipped over to fit. A real hand lives in space and cannot be flipped. It can only be turned. So a left glove will never fit your right hand, however you turn it. In a flat drawing, any shape with no line of symmetry is handed. A screw thread is handed: nearly every jar lid tightens by turning to the right. Many climbing plants twine round a pole in one direction, and most snail shells coil the same way. Handedness is not the same as being uneven. A left hand is not worse than a right hand. They are exact twins that differ only in a way that no turning can change.
- e.g. Gloves, shoes and left and right hands.
- e.g. A jar lid or bolt: the thread winds one way.
- e.g. The tusk of a narwhal almost always spirals in one direction.
- e.g. The letters F, R, G and S, which cannot be flipped onto themselves by turning.
### Life uses one hand of many molecules · *debated*
Many molecules come in a left-handed and a right-handed form, like gloves. Chemists can make both forms in the laboratory, in equal amounts. But the living world mostly uses just one. The amino acids that build proteins are almost all left-handed. Common sugars in living bodies are right-handed. The twist of the DNA double helix is right-handed. Nobody is sure why life ended up with one hand. This is one of the open puzzles in science.
- e.g. Proteins are built from left-handed amino acids.
- e.g. The double helix of DNA winds to the right.
- e.g. Two forms of the same molecule can smell different. One form of a molecule called carvone smells of spearmint and the other of caraway.
- **Chance:** Early on, a tiny random excess of one hand in some place got copied and grew until life used only that hand. It could just as easily have been the other.
- **A bias from physics:** Chien-Shiung Wu showed that one force inside atoms treats left and right differently. A few scientists think this nudged molecules a very little towards one hand. The effect is very small and not shown to be enough.
- **A bias from space:** Some meteorites, such as the one that fell near Murchison in Australia in 1969, hold amino acids with a slight excess of the left-handed form. The idea is that such rocks seeded the young Earth. Whether this is the cause is not known.
### Symmetry breaking: an even start, a lopsided end · *defined*
**Definition:** Symmetry breaking happens when a system that is perfectly even at the start ends up lopsided, and a tiny difference decides which way it goes.

Stand a pencil on its sharp tip. It cannot stay up, and it falls. Before it falls, every direction is equal. After it falls, it points in one direction. Nothing pushed it harder from one side. A tiny draught, a speck on the table or a tiny wobble in the wood decided. In the same way, a thin straight ruler pushed equally from both ends buckles to the left or to the right. A tiny bend in the ruler or a tiny difference in your fingers decides which. Once it has gone one way, the symmetry is broken. A symmetric cause can have a lopsided result. The other way round, a very small difference can make a very big change in the shape of things. Physicists think the same idea shaped the early universe. They think the early universe was almost perfectly even, and tiny differences grew into stars and galaxies.
- e.g. A pencil balanced on its tip falls one way.
- e.g. A straight ruler buckles left or right.
- e.g. A pea on top of a smooth upturned bowl rolls down one side.
- e.g. A drinking straw bent by squeezing its ends goes one way or the other.
### How a body gets a left and a right · *debated*
A human embryo starts as a ball of cells that looks almost the same on every side. Later the body has a left and a right. Most people have the heart on the left, the liver on the right and the stomach tilted to the left. A few people have all their organs the other way round, and they are usually healthy. How does a ball of cells choose a side? The question is not settled. In mouse embryos, a small pit has tiny spinning hairs called cilia. They all spin the same way and sweep the fluid in the pit towards one side. This flow is thought to start the difference. How a first tiny difference gets to the pit is still being studied.
- e.g. In most people, the heart sits to the left and the liver to the right.
- e.g. In a few people, the organs are mirrored. This is rare.
- e.g. In an embryo, the gut tube loops round in a set direction.
- **The flow explanation:** In the mouse, spinning cilia sweep fluid to the left and start a chain of signals on that side. Many scientists think humans work in a similar way.
- **The early signal explanation:** In some animals, such as frogs and chicks, an earlier difference may be set before the cilia start, perhaps by electrical signals between the first cells. How much this matters in humans is not known.
### Asymmetry that helps a living being · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings are not even on purpose: a lopsided part can give them a skill that an even body could not have.

A flounder starts life as a baby that swims upright, with one eye on each side. As the baby grows, one eye slowly moves across the head to join the other. The adult lies on one side on the sea floor with both eyes looking up. A barn owl's ears sit at different heights on her head. A sound from above reaches one ear a tiny moment before the other, and she uses that to find a mouse she cannot see. A male fiddler crab grows one huge claw on one side, for fighting and for waving at females. Almost all narwhal tusks spiral the same way. A little unevenness is not a mistake in these cases. It is the way the body does a job.
- e.g. A flounder with both eyes on the same side.
- e.g. An owl with ears at different heights.
- e.g. A male fiddler crab waving one large claw.
- e.g. The narwhal's spiral tusk.
### Symmetry in the smallest things · *defined*
**Definition:** Crystals, viruses and many small living things are built from many identical copies of one piece, and this makes them symmetrical.

A grain of salt is a tiny cube because its particles stack in a cubic pattern that repeats in every direction. Ice makes hexagon patterns. Many viruses build their shell out of many copies of the same protein. Each copy fits into a spot among its neighbours, and the shell ends up as a 20-sided shape with 20 triangular faces. This saves the virus effort. A virus only has to carry the instructions for one small piece, and the copies join themselves. Tiny sea creatures called radiolarians make glassy skeletons with beautiful symmetry. In every case, building from identical copies makes symmetry almost automatic.
- e.g. A grain of salt: a cube.
- e.g. A snow crystal: six arms.
- e.g. Many viruses: a shell with 20 triangular faces.
- e.g. A pollen grain: often a round ball with a regular pattern of pores.
### Patterns that never repeat: quasicrystals · *defined*
**Definition:** A quasicrystal is an orderly solid whose pattern of particles never exactly repeats, even though it has a regular, five-fold or ten-fold symmetry.

A pattern that repeats by sliding, such as wallpaper, can only have 2-fold, 3-fold, 4-fold or 6-fold symmetry. A five-fold pattern cannot fill a floor with repeating tiles. Pentagons leave gaps. In 1982, Dan Shechtman, a scientist in the United States, saw a five-fold pattern in a diffraction picture of a metal mixture. Most crystal scientists said it was impossible. The famous chemist Linus Pauling is reported to have said there were no quasicrystals, only quasi-scientists. Shechtman was asked to leave his research group. But the evidence held. In 2011 he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The mathematician Roger Penrose had already drawn floor patterns with five-fold symmetry that never repeat, in the 1970s. Natural quasicrystals have since been found in a meteorite. Some medieval tilings in West Asia, such as those on a shrine in Isfahan, look like Penrose patterns.
- e.g. Penrose tiling: two shapes of tile make a never-repeating floor.
- e.g. A metal mixture of aluminium and manganese in which Shechtman saw a five-fold pattern.
- e.g. Girih tilings on old buildings in Iran and Central Asia, which some scientists say come close to quasicrystal patterns.
### Why the laws of nature have symmetry: Emmy Noether · *defined*
**Definition:** Noether's theorem says that every smooth symmetry in the laws of nature goes with something that is never used up. The laws do not change from one moment to the next, and that is why energy is conserved.

Suppose a physicist does the same experiment on Monday and on Tuesday and gets the same result. The laws of nature are the same at both times. That is a symmetry: sliding the experiment along time changes nothing. In 1918, the German mathematician Emmy Noether proved that this particular symmetry means energy can never be made or lost. A different symmetry, that the laws are the same in every place, means a thing called momentum is conserved. A third, that the laws are the same in every direction, means angular momentum, the amount of spinning, is conserved. Noether was not allowed to hold a paid university post for years because she was a woman. A famous mathematician, David Hilbert, argued for her, and she was finally allowed to lecture. She is now ranked among the great mathematicians of the twentieth century.
- e.g. The same experiment on any day gives the same result: energy is conserved.
- e.g. The same experiment in any room: momentum is conserved.
- e.g. The same experiment facing any direction: angular momentum is conserved.
### Nature is not the same in a mirror: Chien-Shiung Wu · *defined*
**Definition:** In 1957, an experiment led by Chien-Shiung Wu showed that one force inside atoms, the weak force, behaves differently in a mirror world. Nature can tell left from right.

Until 1956, most physicists believed that every law of nature looks the same in a mirror. Two theorists, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, suggested that the weak force, which makes some atoms fall apart, might break this rule. They needed someone to test it. Wu, a Chinese-American experimental physicist, cooled radioactive cobalt almost to absolute zero inside a magnet, so the tiny atoms lined up like small spinning tops. Then she and her team counted the electrons flung out by the atoms. More electrons came out in one direction than in the other. In a mirror world, the pattern would be reversed. So a mirror world would not follow our laws. Lee and Yang won the Nobel Prize in 1957. Wu was not included, and many people think she should have been. Her result means we could, in principle, tell someone far away which hand is the left one, with no pictures.
- e.g. Cobalt atoms line up in a magnet and throw out more electrons one way than the other.
- e.g. A mirror world would reverse this effect, so it would not be exactly like ours.
- e.g. Gravity, electricity and magnetism, in contrast, are the same in a mirror.

## Mostly agreed
- A symmetry of a shape is a move that leaves it looking the same. A regular shape with n equal sides fits back into its outline in 2 times n ways. *How we know:* Anyone can check with a cut-out: turn and flip it in every way and count.
- There are exactly seven kinds of border pattern and exactly seventeen kinds of floor pattern. *How we know:* These were proved by mathematics. Fedorov proved the seventeen in 1891 and Pólya found the same result in 1924.
- Living bodies use one hand of many molecules. Proteins are made of left-handed amino acids, and the DNA double helix winds to the right. *How we know:* Chemists have measured the handedness of these molecules in laboratories all over the world.
- In 1957, an experiment led by Chien-Shiung Wu showed that the weak force is not the same in a mirror. *How we know:* Other laboratories repeated the experiment and got the same result.
- Each smooth symmetry in the laws of nature goes with something that is conserved. For example, laws that do not change with time go with conservation of energy. *How we know:* Emmy Noether proved this in 1918. The conservation laws she explained have been tested many times.
- Most bodies of animals that move forward are nearly even on the outside but not on the inside. Most people have their heart on the left. *How we know:* Doctors see the position of organs in X-rays and scans. A small number of people have mirrored organs.

## How we found out
**Chien-Shiung Wu, an experimental physicist (1912 to 1997), with Ernest Ambler and colleagues, testing an idea of Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, 1956 to 1957.** For decades, almost every physicist believed that no law of nature could tell left from right. If you filmed any experiment in a mirror, they thought, the mirror film would obey the same laws. This was true for gravity, electricity and magnetism. In 1956, two theorists, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, noticed that nobody had ever tested it for the weak force, the force behind some kinds of radioactive decay. They needed an experimenter. Chien-Shiung Wu, working with a team at the United States National Bureau of Standards, cooled radioactive cobalt almost to absolute zero inside a magnet, so its atoms lined up. Then the team counted the electrons thrown out in each direction. More came out one way than the other. A mirror film of the experiment would show the opposite, so the mirror world was not the same as ours. The result was published in 1957. Lee and Yang received the Nobel Prize that year. Wu was not included.  
*What people believed before:* Physicists believed that every law of nature looks the same in a mirror. Wu's experiment showed that the weak force does not.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Nature always prefers the most symmetrical shape.** (A common idea, and the view of many older thinkers who thought the heavens must be perfect.; *rejected*)  
  For: Snowflakes, flowers and crystals are very even.  
  Against: Nature breaks symmetry all the time. A pencil falls, a heart sits on the left, a flatfish has both eyes on one side, and life uses one hand of its molecules.
- **A mirror swaps left and right.** (Most people. Philosophers and physicists have argued about how to say this properly.; *debated*)  
  For: When you raise your right hand, your reflection seems to raise its left hand.  
  Against: A mirror really swaps front and back, because your nose points towards the mirror and the nose in the mirror points back at you. The left-right swap comes from how you imagine turning round to stand where your reflection stands.
- **Quasicrystals are impossible, so Shechtman must have made a mistake.** (Many crystal scientists in the 1980s, and the famous chemist Linus Pauling.; *rejected*)  
  For: Textbooks said that a repeating pattern can only have 2, 3, 4 or 6-fold symmetry, and this is correct for repeating patterns.  
  Against: Quasicrystals do not repeat, so the rule does not apply to them. Other laboratories found more of them, and natural ones were found in a meteorite. Shechtman won the Nobel Prize in 2011.
- **The Alhambra palace in Spain has all 17 kinds of floor pattern.** (Many books and websites.; *debated*)  
  For: The Alhambra has many tiling patterns in tile, plaster and carved wood, and some mathematicians have spotted many of the 17 there.  
  Against: Mathematicians who checked carefully say some of the 17 are missing or hard to find, and the claim is more of a story than a count.
- **The left-handed amino acids of life were chosen by the weak force.** (A small number of scientists.; *minority*)  
  For: The weak force treats left and right differently, so it could give molecules a tiny push towards one hand.  
  Against: The push is extremely small, and no one has shown that it is big enough to matter. Chance, or a start from space, could explain it equally well.

## Roots
We have counted the moves that leave a shape unchanged, and we have seen that an even start can end lopsided. Now we ask what the pushed strip, the hands and the heart tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You pushed a straight strip equally from both ends. It still bent to one side. A tiny hidden curve in the strip chose which side. A small difference can turn an even start into a lopsided end.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a lopsided result must have come from a lopsided cause of about the same size, so we hunt for the big thing that tipped it. In the strip test the push was even, and the tipping came from a difference too small to see.  
*Evidence:* In the strip test, the push at both ends was equal. A flattened strip mostly bent the same way, and a strip curled by a coin's thickness bent that way every time. When the strip was turned over, the bend turned over with it. If your own results differ, write them down as they are.

- *who am i:* You began as a ball of cells that looked almost the same on every side. Now your heart is on the left, and scientists think tiny spinning hairs in the early embryo started that difference.
- *what is life:* The proteins in every living being are built from left-handed amino acids, and the DNA helix winds to the right. Life uses one hand, and nobody knows why.
- *what is this world:* In 1957 Chien-Shiung Wu showed that the weak force, one of the basic forces inside atoms, is not the same in a mirror. The world itself can tell left from right.

A pencil on its tip, a pushed strip, an early embryo and the early universe all begin even. Then one tiny difference grows, and a left and a right, a here and a there, appear. Physicists think the early universe was almost perfectly even, and tiny differences grew into stars and galaxies. That is an idea, and not yet settled. What is settled is that you can watch an even start become lopsided with a strip of card. The many shapes of the world come out of sameness through small breaks in it.

- **Tao Te Ching 42:** The Tao gives birth to One. One gives birth to Two. Two gives birth to Three. Three gives birth to all things. (One even start splits into two sides, and the many things come after. A flat strip becomes a bend to one side, and a ball of cells becomes a body with a left and a right.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.2.1 to 6.2.3:** In the beginning there was only Being, one without a second. Being thought, 'May I become many.' (An even beginning is one. The many different shapes and sides come later. Symmetry breaking is the science of how one even start can become many different things.)
- **Aristotle, On the Heavens 2.13, reporting the thinker Anaximander:** Anaximander is reported to have said that the Earth stays still because it is equally far from everything, so it has no reason to move one way rather than another. (Anaximander used a symmetry argument. In the strip test, equal pushes did leave a decision to be made, and a tiny difference made it.)
- Science (settled): Emmy Noether proved in 1918 that every smooth symmetry of the laws of nature goes with something conserved. For example, laws that stay the same over time go with energy that is never lost.
- Science (settled): In 1957 an experiment led by Chien-Shiung Wu showed that the weak force is not the same in a mirror.
- Science (open): Why life uses left-handed amino acids and right-handed sugars is not known.
- Other view, Many physicists: Nothing chooses in symmetry breaking. The laws stay perfectly even, and each possible result is equally allowed. The strip bends because it has to go somewhere, and a tiny difference decides where. No deeper story is needed about sameness becoming many.
- Practice, Even, then a lean (3 min): Stand with your feet together near a wall. Close your eyes. Feel your weight on both feet. Breathe in slowly. Now breathe out and notice that you sway a tiny bit. Which way do you start to lean? Let the lean happen, and then come back to the middle. Do this three times. Does it lean the same way each time?
- Ask: The strip was pushed evenly and still bent to one side. What decided which side?
- Ask: Your two hands are the same in every measurement, yet no turning makes one fit the other. Are they the same or different?
- Ask: If the early universe was perfectly even, what would it take for anything to be here at all?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When a ruler buckles to the left, was that an accident, or was it decided by something? (hint: Think about the tiny bend in the ruler, a tiny difference in your fingers, and whether those are 'causes'.)
- *deep:* Your left hand and right hand are the same in every measurement. In what way are they different? (hint: Try to fit your hand outline on top of its twin. What must you do?)
- *deep:* Why might life have chosen only left-handed amino acids? (hint: Think about gloves. Would a left-handed glove factory and a right-handed glove factory work well together in one room?)
- *big:* If someone on another planet describes 'left' in a message, how could they tell us which hand they mean? (hint: Photographs and drawings do not help, because we cannot tell which way they are flipped. Chien-Shiung Wu's result gives one way.)
- *big:* Left-handed people have lived in every age. The world is built mostly for right-handed people. Is that fair, and what could be changed? (hint: Think of scissors, desks, can openers and guitars. Who decides what is normal?)
- *deep:* If everything in the universe were perfectly even, would anything have a place or a direction? (hint: A line of identical bulbs along a wall. How would you tell one from another if they were truly identical?)
- *big:* Why do people everywhere make borders and patterns that repeat? (hint: Is it easier to make a repeat? Is it pleasing to the eye? Does a repeat tell a story like a rhythm?)
- *big:* Is it a mistake that your heart sits on the left, or is it how a good body is built? (hint: Think of a body with an exactly even inside. Where would the heart and the gut tubes go?)

## Story: The Scissors That Would Not Turn
*A made-up story. Left-handed people, scissors that suit one hand, shells that coil one way and the jar lids that tighten to the right are all real. The shop and the family are imagined.*

Kavya's grandmother had a tailor's shop in Fort Kochi. It was a narrow shop, with a sewing machine by the door and cloth in rolls up to the ceiling. Everyone in the street called her Ammamma.

Every holiday, Kavya's cousin Ayesha came from Hyderabad to stay. Ayesha was nine, the same age as Kavya, and she was left-handed.

This holiday, Ammamma had given them a job. They could cut out cloth squares for a patchwork quilt. Ammamma kept her big black scissors, the shears, on a nail by the machine. Their handles were as long as Kavya's hand.

Kavya cut three squares in a row, easy as paper. Then it was Ayesha's turn.

Ayesha took the shears in her left hand. She lined up the cloth, and squeezed. The cloth slid between the blades and crumpled. She tried again. The blades chewed a ragged edge.

'It's not cutting,' said Ayesha. 'It never cuts. These scissors hate me.'

'You are holding them wrong,' said Kavya. 'Turn them round.'

Ayesha turned them upside down. The blades still slid. She turned them front to back. The blades still slid.

'Try your right hand,' said Kavya.

Ayesha laughed. 'I can't write with my right hand. I can't do anything with my right hand. I can only waste cloth.'

Ammamma came over, looked at the shears, and looked at Ayesha's hand. 'Ah,' she said. 'You need a left-handed pair.' She went to the back of the shop and returned with an old cardboard box.

'A left-handed pair? They look the same,' said Kavya. 'The same handles, the same blades. Why can't she just turn them over?'

'Because turning them over doesn't make them left-handed,' said Ammamma. 'Look closely at the blades.'

Kavya looked. When you close a pair of scissors, one blade passes over the other. On the shears, the top blade was the one on the right side of the handle. In Ayesha's left hand, the squeeze pushed the blades apart, instead of together. The cloth slipped out.

Kavya fetched a piece of paper and traced round the shears. She cut out the shape. She laid it on the table beside the real scissors. Then she flipped her paper shape over, and the top blade jumped to the other side.

'The flip makes a left-handed pair,' she said slowly. 'But I can't flip a real pair of scissors. I can only turn it.'

'That is the trick of it,' said Ammamma. 'A thing and its mirror twin have the same parts, the same size, and the same weight. But you can never turn one into the other. You have to go and make the twin.'

She opened the box. Inside lay a pair of small shears with orange handles. 'My friend's daughter left these here forty years ago. She was left-handed too.'

Ayesha took them in her left hand. The blades came together with a soft snip. The cloth fell away in a clean line. She cut a whole square, then another, and then she kept cutting until the cloth pile was tall.

That evening Kavya found a shell on the windowsill, a small white snail shell from the beach. She turned it in her fingers. Its coil wound to the right, like the thread on a jar lid. She picked up another shell from the jar. It wound to the right too. She went through all twelve. They all wound the same way.

'Almost every snail shell coils the same way,' said Ammamma. 'But now and then a snail is born who coils the other way. Shell collectors prize such a shell.'

Kavya held the shell next to Ayesha's left hand and her own right. 'Everything has a twin,' she said. 'Even a shell.'

The next day, they sat at the shop door sewing the quilt. Ammamma's shears hung on their nail. The small orange pair lay on the table for Ayesha. 'Tomorrow,' said Kavya, 'we ask the whole street who is left-handed. We will find out how many pairs of scissors this street needs.'

Ayesha grinned. 'Start with Ammamma's tailor friends. I bet they have been cutting with the wrong hand for years.'

**For the parent:** A thing and its mirror twin have the same parts, the same size and the same weight, yet no turning can make one into the other. A left-handed person needs a left-handed tool, not the same tool turned round.

- Ask: Why could Ayesha not just turn the scissors round to cut with her left hand?
- Ask: What would you need to see to tell that two things are mirror twins?
- Ask: What is something at home or at school that is built for right-handed people only?
- Ask: Think of three things that come in a left-handed and a right-handed form.

## Games
### g1 · Fit-Back Guess: how many ways does it fit? (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 24 cards and put them in a pile with the shape side up. Every player cuts out their own set of ten number tokens.; One player is the reader. The reader takes the top card and shows only the front. If a shape is hard to imagine, draw it quickly on scrap paper.; Every other player chooses one number token and puts it face down. Think about turns first, and then flips.; Everyone turns their tokens over together. Then the reader reads the back of the card aloud.; A player with the exact number scores 2 points. A player whose token is the next one along in the row of tokens scores 1 point.; The reader passes the cards to the next player to be the reader. Play until all 24 cards are used, or for 15 minutes.; The player with the most points wins. A tie is a shared win.; Playing alone: be the reader and the player. Choose your token, turn the card, and score yourself. Try to beat your own score next time.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: a shape on the front, and the number of ways it fits back, with the reason, on the back:** A triangle with three equal sides. (back: 6. Three turns (counting no turn) and three flips.); A square. (back: 8. Four turns and four flips.); A rectangle that is not a square, such as a playing card. (back: 4. No turn, a half turn, and two flips. A quarter turn does not fit.); A regular pentagon, with five equal sides. (back: 10. Five turns and five flips.); A regular hexagon, like one cell of a honeycomb. (back: 12. Six turns and six flips.); A triangle with three sides of different lengths. (back: 1. Only the do-nothing move. No turn and no flip fits.); A triangle with two equal sides, but not three, like a Christmas tree outline. (back: 2. The do-nothing move and one flip down the middle.); A diamond shape with four equal sides that is not a square. (back: 4. No turn, a half turn, and two flips along the long and short lines.); A slanted rectangle, called a parallelogram, with no right angles and unequal neighbouring sides. (back: 2. The do-nothing move and a half turn. It has no flip that fits.); A kite shape, with two pairs of equal sides next to each other. (back: 2. The do-nothing move and one flip down the long middle.); A circle, such as a katori lid. (back: Endless. Every turn fits, and every flip over a line through the centre fits.); A regular octagon, like a stop sign. (back: 16. Eight turns and eight flips.); A plus sign with four equal arms. (back: 8. Four turns and four flips.); The capital letter H in plain, even lettering. (back: 4. No turn, a half turn, a flip down the middle and a flip across the middle.); The capital letter A in plain, even lettering. (back: 2. The do-nothing move and a flip down the middle.); The capital letter F. (back: 1. Only the do-nothing move. Its mirror twin is a different letter.); The capital letter S. (back: 2. The do-nothing move and a half turn. It has no flip that fits.); A long oval, like a rugby ball seen from the side. (back: 4. No turn, a half turn and two flips.); A five-petalled periwinkle flower with all petals alike. (back: 10. Five turns and five flips.); A three-bladed ceiling fan with its blades all twisted the same way. (back: 3. Three turns and no flip. A flip would make the blades twist the other way.); A four-bladed pinwheel with its blades all bent the same way round. (back: 4. Four turns and no flip. A flip would bend the blades the other way.); A regular five-pointed star. (back: 10. Five turns and five flips.); An ideal six-armed snowflake with six matching arms. (back: 12. Six turns and six flips.); The outline of your right hand with the fingers spread. (back: 1. Only the do-nothing move. A flip would make a left hand.)
- **Printable tokens: 10 number tokens for each player: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16 and Endless:** 1; 2; 3; 4; 6; 8; 10; 12; 16; Endless
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the tokens. Give each player a set of ten tokens. Put the cards in a pile.  
  *Parent says:* These cards show shapes. You will guess how many ways each shape fits back into its outline. Each player gets ten number tokens.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Take your ten number tokens.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first six cards. After each card, the reader reads the back aloud and everyone scores their points.  
  *Parent says:* The reader shows a shape. Pick a number token and put it face down. Turn them over together. Who is right?  
  *Child mode:* The reader shows a card. Pick a token. Turn it over. Check the back.
  - Ask: What do the shapes with the most ways have in common? (good: They have many equal sides.; They look the same from every side.; They are very regular.)
  - Note: Do not tell the count before the guesses. After six cards, the numbers will already look like they follow a pattern.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the next ten cards. Stop after card 12 and ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the cards so far. The triangle had 6 ways. The square had 8. What do you notice about the number of sides and the number of ways?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the triangle and the square. Count the sides and the ways.
  - Ask: What is the link between the number of equal sides and the number of ways? (good: The ways are double the sides.; A triangle has 3 sides and 6 ways. A square has 4 sides and 8 ways.; Turns and flips are each as many as the sides.)
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the last cards. Talk about the cards with a low count, such as the letter F, the letter S and the hand.  
  *Parent says:* The letter F and the hand fit in only one way. The S fits in two. Why do these have so few ways?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the F, the S and the hand. Why do they have so few ways?
  - Ask: Why does the fan with twisted blades have turns but no flips? (good: A flip would twist the blades the other way.; A flip makes the mirror twin, which is a different fan.)
  - Note: The fan and the pinwheel are handed. A flip turns them into their mirror twins, which are different shapes. Handed shapes have turns only.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the points and name the winner. Ask your child to make up a new card for the next game.  
  *Parent says:* Who won? Make a card for the next game. Draw a shape, and work out its count first.  
  *Child mode:* Add up your points. Make a new card with a shape of your own.
  - Check: A regular shape has 7 equal sides. How many ways does it fit back into its outline? → **14**

## Experiments
### e1 · Fit-Back Count: how many ways does each shape fit its outline? (35 min)
- **question:** How many different ways can each of six cut-out shapes fit back into its own outline? Is there a pattern in the numbers?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many ways will the square fit back into its outline? Which of the six shapes will have the most ways, and which the fewest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every shape is cut from the same card and labelled the same way, with a letter at each corner on both the front and the back. You find the ways the same way each time, by turning and flipping until no new letter pattern appears. Only the shape changes, so differences in the count come from the shape. You check each count twice.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the card and pushes the pin. Keep fingers behind the scissors.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For each shape, also write down one of the letter patterns you found, going round the outline from the star. Draw the outline of the square with the star and the letters.
- **results table:** Shape | Number of equal sides | My guess | Ways by turning only | Ways by turning and flipping
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a card square and its outline. Ask them to guess how many ways it fits back. Write the guesses for all six shapes in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a square and its outline. How many different ways can it fit back in? Which of our six shapes will have the most ways? Which will have the fewest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many ways the square fits its outline. Which shape fits most ways? Why?
  - Ask: Which shape do you think has the most ways, and why? (good: The circle, because it looks the same however you turn it.; The square, because it has four equal sides.; The one with the most corners.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write them at the top of the table.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts the six shapes from the card. First, a square 6 centimetres on each side. Second, a rectangle 8 centimetres by 4 centimetres. Third, a second square cut along its diagonal into two triangles, and keep one. Fourth, a second rectangle cut along its diagonal, and keep one half. Fifth, a circle traced round the katori lid. Sixth, a triangle with three sides of 8 centimetres each.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up cuts these shapes for us. The triangle has three equal sides. It is the one that needs care.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to cut six shapes. Look at each one when it is ready.
  - Note: For the triangle with equal sides, draw an 8 centimetre line. At each end, use the protractor to draw a 60 degree line upwards. They meet at the third corner. With no protractor, tie a thread to a pencil, hold the 8 centimetre end at one end of the line with a pin, and draw an arc. Repeat from the other end. The arcs cross at the third corner. The diagonal cuts of the square and the rectangle give a triangle with two equal sides and a triangle with all sides different.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On the front of each shape, write the letter A at one corner. Write B, C and D going round. Turn each shape over like a page. Write the same letters at the same corners on the back. Trace each shape on paper. Put a star at one corner of each outline.  
  *Parent says:* Every corner gets a letter on both sides. That way we can tell the fits apart. The star on the outline tells us where to start reading.  
  *Child mode:* Write letters at the corners on both sides. Trace each shape. Add a star.
  - Note: The circle only needs one dot on its edge. For the triangles, use only the letters A, B and C. Write the letters small and clear so they show at the corners.
  - Check: Why do we write the letters on both sides of each shape? → **So that we can tell the fits apart when the shape is flipped over**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the square in its outline. Read the letters clockwise from the star and write them down. Turn the square a quarter turn. Write the new letters. Keep going until you have all the fits from turning. Then flip it over and do the same again.  
  *Parent says:* Read the corner letters from the star, going clockwise. Turn it a quarter. Read again. How many different patterns do you find?  
  *Child mode:* Read the letters from the star. Turn the square. Read again. Then flip it.
  - Note: The four turns give ABCD, DABC, CDAB and BCDA, in some order of reading. The flipped square gives four more, which read in the opposite order, such as ADCB. A pattern counts once. The letters on the star corner and the order show which is new.
  - Check: You found 4 ways to fit the square by turning. You then flipped it over and found more. How many ways in all? → **8**
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same for the other five shapes. For each shape, write every different letter pattern. Stop when no new pattern appears after every turn and flip. Fill in the table. For the circle, write endless.  
  *Parent says:* Find the fits for each shape. Turn and flip it every way. Stop when nothing new appears. Write the count in the table.  
  *Child mode:* Find the fits for each shape. Turn and flip. Write the counts in the table.
  - Ask: Which shape surprised you? (good: The scalene triangle, which fits in only one way.; The circle, which has no end.; The rectangle, which has fewer than the square.)
  - Note: Expect: square 8, rectangle 4, equal-sided triangle 6, triangle with two equal sides 2, triangle with three different sides 1, circle endless. If a count is odd, check for a letter pattern counted twice, or a shape that did not fit properly. The triangle with three different sides has only the start pattern.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table. Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and the reason. Ask them to look at the square and the triangle with three equal sides.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? What did you see in the numbers? Why do you think the count is the way it is? Look at the equal-sided shapes. What is the link between sides and ways?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? What numbers show it? Why does it happen?
  - Ask: What is the link between the number of equal sides and the number of ways? (good: The number of ways is double the number of sides.; The triangle has 3 sides and 6 ways. The square has 4 sides and 8 ways.; There are as many turns as sides and as many flips as sides.)
  - Note: A strong answer says: the equal-sided shapes have as many fits by turning as they have sides, and the same number again by flipping. So the count is double the number of sides. Shapes with unequal sides have fewer. A shape that is not the same as its mirror twin has no flip that fits.
  - Check: A shape with 5 equal sides and 5 equal angles fits back into its outline in how many ways? → **10**
### e2 · The Pushed Strip: which way does it bend? (25 min)
- **question:** When you push a straight card strip evenly from both ends, which way does it bend? Is it the same each time? What decides?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. When you push the ends of a straight strip together, will the middle bend towards you, away from you, or sometimes each way? Will a curled strip behave differently? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use strips of the same card, the same length and the same width. Push slowly, with the same hands, in the same way each time. Do ten tries for each strip. Change only the strip. Flatten the strip between tries so the last bend does not set the next.
- **safety:** Keep the strip away from your face and eyes when you push it. Push slowly. Fold over any sharp corner of card.
- **record:** Make a tally mark in the table for every try. Draw one strip bent towards you and one bent away, seen from above, with the dot on the side facing you.
- **results table:** Strip | How it was prepared | Tries bent towards me | Tries bent away from me
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the card, a strip and the pencil. Ask your child to guess and give a reason. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will push a straight strip from both ends, evenly. Which way will the middle bend, towards you or away from you? Will it be the same every time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Push a straight strip from both ends. Which way will it bend? Why?
  - Ask: Which way will a straight strip bend, and why? (good: It will bend either way, because both sides are the same.; It will go towards me, because I am pushing.; It will go the same way each time.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say it will go in any direction at random. Others say it must go straight. All guesses with a reason are good.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut the three strips. Mark a dot in the middle of one face of each strip. Write A, B and C at one end. Flatten strip A by rubbing it along the edge of the table in both directions until it lies flat. Curl strip B over the pencil with the dot on the inside. Curl strip C the other way.  
  *Parent says:* Strip A is flat. Strip B is curled towards the dot. Strip C is curled away from the dot. Lay each on the table. A curl the thickness of a coin at the ends is enough.  
  *Child mode:* Cut three strips and mark a dot. Flatten A. Curl B and C gently.
  - Note: A gentle curl is enough. Lay the strip on the table. Its ends should lift about the thickness of a one-rupee coin. If the strip creases, throw it away and cut a new one. A crease is a sharp fold and spoils the test.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take strip A. Hold it upright with the dot facing you and an end in each hand. Push the ends slowly together until the middle bends. Note which way the middle goes. Make a tally mark. Lay the strip flat and rub it flat again. Do ten tries.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the strip with the dot facing you. Push the ends slowly together. Which way does the middle go? Mark it down. Flatten the strip each time.  
  *Child mode:* Hold strip A with the dot facing you. Push the ends together. Where does the middle go?
  - Ask: Did strip A bend the same way each time? (good: Mostly the same way.; Sometimes one way and sometimes the other.; Always towards me.)
  - Note: A flattened card strip is rarely perfectly flat. Many children find strip A goes mostly one way, which tells us it has a hidden tiny curve. If it goes both ways about equally, that is a fine result too. Write what you see.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test strip B ten times in the same way, with the dot facing you. Then test strip C ten times. Make a tally mark each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now the curled strips. B is curled with the dot inside. C is curled with the dot outside. Hold each with the dot facing you. Do ten tries each. Which way do they go?  
  *Child mode:* Test strip B ten times. Test strip C ten times. Tally each try.
  - Ask: What happened with the curled strips? (good: They went the same way every time.; B went one way and C went the other way.)
  - Note: Expect strip B to bend one way every time, and strip C the opposite way every time. The curl is the tiny difference that decides.
  - Check: Strip B is curled towards the dot and bends the same way ten times out of ten. What is the best explanation? → **The curl in the strip decides which way it bends**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take strip A again. Turn it over so the dot faces away from you. Push the ends together six times. Tally the bends.  
  *Parent says:* Now turn strip A over. The dot faces away from you. Does it bend towards you or away from you this time?  
  *Child mode:* Turn strip A over. Push it six times. Does it bend the same way?
  - Ask: When you turned strip A over, did it bend the same way as before? (good: No, it flipped to the other way.; Yes, it still goes towards me.)
  - Note: If strip A bends the opposite way after turning over, its own hidden curve decided. If it still bends the same way, your hands or the way you stand may be deciding. Both are real results.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table. Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and the reason. Then ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? What numbers show it? Why do you think a perfectly even push gave a lopsided bend? What decided which way?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? What numbers show it? What decided the bend?
  - Ask: The push was the same at both ends. Why did the strip bend to one side? (good: The strip has a tiny curve that decides.; A tiny difference in the strip made it go one way.; It had to go one way, and the tiny curve picked it.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses the numbers: strip A mostly went one way, B and C went opposite ways every time, and turning A over changed its direction. The reasoning is that an even push has no side to favour, so a tiny difference in the strip does. Scientists call this symmetry breaking. Some other words children use are fine.
  - Check: You push a flat strip evenly from both ends and it bows towards the dot 8 times out of 10. What is the most likely reason? → **The strip has a tiny hidden curve that favours that side**

## Activities
### a1 · Stamp a Border: five moves, five borders (30 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts out the F stencil.
- **record:** Stick your five borders in a column in your notebook. Write the name of the move beside each one: slide, flip across, flip along, half turn or glide.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts out an F stencil from card. Show your child the five moves with the stencil on the table. Ask them to say what each move does.  
  *Parent says:* This F can do five things. It can slide. It can flip over a line across the border. It can flip over a line along the border. It can turn halfway. It can glide, which is a flip and a slide together. What will a row of slid Fs look like?  
  *Child mode:* The F can slide, flip, turn and glide. What will a row of slid Fs look like?
  - Ask: What will a border made by sliding the F look like? (good: The same F again and again, all facing the same way.; Like a row of the same picture.; Like a train.)
  - Note: Cut the F from stiff card so it can be traced many times. The F has no symmetry of its own. That makes every move easy to see.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Border one: trace the F at the left of a strip. Slide the stencil 6 centimetres to the right. Trace it again. Do this five times. Border two: trace the F. Flip the stencil like a page, so it faces the other way, and trace it beside the first. Repeat.  
  *Parent says:* Border one is a slide. Every F faces the same way. Border two flips over a line across the strip, like a page. The Fs take turns facing left and right.  
  *Child mode:* Slide the F and trace it five times. Then trace an F, flip it, and trace again.
  - Check: A border has the same F again and again, all facing the same way, each one further along. Which move made it? → **A slide**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Border three: trace the F. Flip the stencil upside down, like turning a page over from top to bottom, and trace it beside the first. Repeat. Border four: trace the F. Turn the stencil halfway round and trace it beside the first. Repeat. Border five: trace the F. Flip it upside down and slide it along as well, then trace it. Repeat.  
  *Parent says:* Border three flips the F upside down. Border four turns it halfway round, so it is upside down but not flipped. Border five is the glide. It flips and slides in one move.  
  *Child mode:* Make three more borders. One flips the F upside down. One turns it halfway. One does both a flip and a slide.
  - Note: Children often mix up the flip and the half turn. A flip makes a mirror image. A half turn does not. Look at the F: a half-turned F is still a proper F, but upside down. A flipped F reads backwards. If two borders look the same, one may have been done wrong. Try again with the stencil.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stick the five borders in a column. Write the move beside each one. Ask your child which two borders look most alike and which move differs.  
  *Parent says:* Put your borders in a column. Write the name of the move next to each. Which two look most alike? Which move is different?  
  *Child mode:* Put your five borders in a column. Name the move for each.
  - Ask: Which borders look most alike, and what is different? (good: The half turn and the glide, but the half turn is not flipped.; The two flip borders, but one flips across and the other flips along.)
  - Note: There are seven kinds of border altogether. These five moves make five of them. The other two are made by mixing moves, such as a flip across and a half turn together.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at a saree border, a bedsheet, a temple picture or a floor tile edge. For each, find the repeating unit. Decide which move takes one unit to the next.  
  *Parent says:* Find a real border. What is the shape that repeats? Does it slide, flip, turn or glide? Try covering all but two units with your hand to see.  
  *Child mode:* Find a real border. What repeats? Is it a slide, a flip, a turn or a glide?
  - Ask: Which move does your real border use? (good: A slide. The same shape is repeated along.; A flip. Mangoes face one way and then the other.; A glide. Leaves point up and down alternately.)
  - Note: Most simple borders on cloth are slides. Borders with pairs of animals or birds facing each other use a flip across. Alternating up and down shapes use a glide. Do not worry if the real border looks messy. Pick the move that matches best.
### a2 · Two Mirrors: how many copies do you see? (25 min)
- **safety:** Cover the edges of the mirrors with tape, because the edges can scratch. Do not drop them. Do not put the mirrors where they could reflect strong sunlight into anyone's eyes.
- **record:** A table with the angle, your guess, the number of objects you counted, and 360 divided by the angle.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw four angles on the sheet, with the same corner point. Use 120, 90, 60 and 45 degrees. Ask your child to guess how many objects they will see at each angle.  
  *Parent says:* We will put one small object between two mirrors. How many objects will you see in all, counting the real one? Guess for 90 degrees, then 60, then 45. Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many objects you will see between two mirrors. Why?
  - Ask: How many objects will you see at 90 degrees, and why? (good: Two, one real and one in the mirror.; Four, because the mirrors also reflect each other.; More as the angle gets smaller.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children guess 2 for every angle. The answers are 3, 4, 6 and 8 for 120, 90, 60 and 45 degrees.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the two mirrors together along one edge, with the shiny sides facing each other. Cover the other edges with tape.  
  *Parent says:* Lay the mirrors face up side by side. Tape them along one edge. Now the mirrors can open and close like a book.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the two mirrors together. Cover the edges with tape.
  - Note: Press the tape firmly on both mirrors so the hinge is stiff but can still move. The mirrors must meet exactly at the hinge. A gap makes the pattern harder to see.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the mirrors on the sheet, with the hinge on the corner point and the mirrors along the 90 degree lines. Put the object between them, close to the hinge. Look in from above. Count the objects in all, including the real one.

## Worksheets
### Moves and Counts (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is NOT one of the four moves that can leave a pattern looking the same?  
   Options: A slide / A flip / A stretch / A glide  
   **Answer:** A stretch. *Why:* The four moves are the slide, the flip, the turn and the glide. A stretch changes the size of a shape, so the shape no longer looks the same.
2. (fill) A move that flips a shape and slides it along at the same time is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['glide', 'glide reflection']. *Why:* A glide is a flip and a slide together. Footprints along a path, with the left and right foot alternating, follow a glide.
3. (mcq) A rectangle that is not a square is traced and cut out. In how many ways does it fit back into its outline?  
   Options: 1 / 2 / 4 / 8  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* It fits with no turn, a half turn, and two flips along its two middle lines. That is 4. A quarter turn does not fit, because the sides are different lengths.
4. (truefalse) A shape with no line of symmetry can still fit back into its outline by turning.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The letter S has no fold line, but it fits back after a half turn. A pinwheel with twisted blades fits after a turn but not after a flip.
5. (mcq) Which of these shapes fits back into its outline in the most ways?  
   Options: A square / A triangle with three equal sides / A rectangle that is not a square / A circle  
   **Answer:** A circle. *Why:* A circle fits back after any turn, and after a flip across any line through its centre. That is endless. The square has 8, the equal-sided triangle 6 and the rectangle 4.
6. (sort) Sort each thing by whether its mirror twin can be turned to match it.  
   **Answer:** a left shoe → Handed (the mirror twin does not fit by turning); a steel katori → Not handed (it matches its mirror twin); a screw → Handed (the mirror twin does not fit by turning); a plain round plate → Not handed (it matches its mirror twin); a pair of scissors → Handed (the mirror twin does not fit by turning); the capital letter H → Not handed (it matches its mirror twin). *Why:* A handed thing has no mirror line, so its mirror twin is a different thing. A shoe, a screw and scissors are handed. A katori, a round plate and the letter H match their mirror twins.
7. (order) Put the steps of the Fit-Back Count in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Cut the shapes from card', 'Write letters at the corners on both sides', 'Trace each shape and put a star on the outline', 'Fit the shape back into its outline and read the letters from the star', 'Turn and flip the shape until no new letter pattern appears']. *Why:* First make the shapes, then label both sides so you can tell a flip from a turn, then trace and mark a start. Only then can you read the letters and keep turning and flipping until nothing new appears.
8. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Slide → The same shape moved along without turning; Flip → A mirror image across a line; Turn → Rotating a shape around a centre; Glide → A flip and a slide done together; Handed → Its mirror twin cannot be turned to match it. *Why:* These five words are the vocabulary of symmetry. A strong answer could give an example of each: mangoes along a border for a slide, a butterfly for a flip, a fan for a turn, footprints for a glide and a hand for handed.
9. (mcq) How many different kinds of border pattern are there?  
   Options: 3 / 7 / 17 / Endless  
   **Answer:** 7. *Why:* Mathematicians have proved there are exactly 7 kinds of border pattern, however clever the design. There are 17 kinds for covering a floor.
10. (fill) A regular shape with 9 equal sides fits back into its outline in ____ ways.  
   **Answer:** ['18']. *Why:* A regular shape with n sides has n turns and n flips. For 9 sides, 9 plus 9 is 18.
11. (mcq) Two mirrors are hinged at 60 degrees with an object between them. How many objects do you see in all, including the real one?  
   Options: 3 / 6 / 8 / 12  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* 360 divided by 60 is 6. The real object and its copies make 6 objects in all, arranged round the hinge.
12. (write) In the pushed strip test, the push was the same at both ends. Explain what decided which way the strip bent.  
   **Answer:** A tiny hidden curve in the strip decided. An even push has no side to favour, so something small and uneven, such as a slight curl in the card, made the strip go one way. When the strip was turned over, the bend turned over with it.. *Why:* A strong answer says the push was even, names a tiny difference as the decider, and uses the evidence of the curled strips or the turned-over strip.
13. (draw) Draw a badge that has exactly 2 symmetries and no mirror line. Say what it is.  
   **Answer:** For example the letter S or a yin-yang symbol, which fit back after the do-nothing move and a half turn only.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a shape that matches itself after a half turn but not after a flip, such as a pair of curved blades pointing opposite ways.
14. (label) Label the drawing of a square: draw one line of symmetry and write how many symmetries the square has in all.  
   **Answer:** A dotted line through the middle of two opposite sides or through two opposite corners, and the number 8.. *Why:* A square has 4 turns and 4 flips, which makes 8. The 4 flips are across 4 lines: two through the middle of opposite sides and two corner to corner.
15. (mcq) A left glove will never fit your right hand, however you turn it. Why?  
   Options: The glove and your right hand are mirror twins, and no turning changes a thing into its mirror twin / Left gloves are smaller / Gloves shrink when turned / Hands are all different sizes  
   **Answer:** The glove and your right hand are mirror twins, and no turning changes a thing into its mirror twin. *Why:* A left glove is the mirror twin of a right glove. Turning only moves a thing round in space and cannot reverse its handedness. To change it, you need a flip, which is not possible for a real glove.
16. (odd) Three of these are handed. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A pair of scissors / A screw / A left chappal / A round steel plate  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Scissors, a screw and a chappal each have a mirror twin that does not fit by turning. A round steel plate matches its mirror image, so it is not handed.
### Breaking and Keeping Symmetry (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A regular shape fits back into its outline in 20 ways. How many equal sides does it have?  
   Options: 5 / 10 / 20 / 40  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* The number of ways is double the number of sides, because a regular shape has as many flips as turns. 20 divided by 2 is 10.
2. (mcq) A badge looks the same after a quarter turn, a half turn and a three-quarter turn, but any flip changes it. How many symmetries does it have?  
   Options: 4 / 8 / 3 / 1  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* The badge fits back after no turn, a quarter turn, a half turn and a three-quarter turn. That is 4. The flips do not work, so none are added.
3. (truefalse) Because the push is the same at both ends, a card strip will bend towards each side equally often, even if one strip has a tiny curl.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A strip with a tiny curl bends the way its curl favours almost every time. The even push leaves the choice open, and the tiny difference makes it.
4. (mcq) A cobalt experiment in 1957 counted electrons thrown out in two directions. More came out one way. What does this show?  
   Options: The weak force behaves differently in a mirror world / All forces are the same in a mirror / Cobalt atoms are handed like gloves / Electrons are left-handed gloves  
   **Answer:** The weak force behaves differently in a mirror world. *Why:* If the weak force were the same in a mirror, equal numbers would come out both ways. Because the counts differed, a mirror version of the experiment would give a different result.
5. (fill) If the laws of nature do not change with time, Emmy Noether proved that ____ is never used up.  
   **Answer:** ['energy']. *Why:* Laws that do not change from one day to the next go with conservation of energy. Energy can move and change form, but the total stays the same.
6. (fill) The proteins in living bodies are built from ____-handed amino acids.  
   **Answer:** ['left']. *Why:* Life uses left-handed amino acids almost everywhere. Chemists can make both hands, but living bodies use mostly one. Nobody knows why.
7. (sort) Sort each body part by whether an even or a lopsided body helps.  
   **Answer:** a barn owl's ears at different heights → The lopsided part helps; a butterfly's matching wings → The even pair helps; a male fiddler crab's one huge claw → The lopsided part helps; a fish's matching fins → The even pair helps; the flounder's two eyes on one side → The lopsided part helps. *Why:* The owl locates sound by comparing the two ears, the crab uses one big claw for signalling, and the flounder lies on one side. 8. (match) Match each example to the move that makes it.  
   **Answer:** A row of identical mangoes on a saree border → Slide; Footprints with the left and right foot alternating → Glide; The letter S → Half turn; A butterfly → Flip; A right hand outline → Only the do-nothing move. *Why:* A border of identical shapes is a slide. Footprints are a glide. The letter S matches itself after a half turn. A butterfly's wings match after a flip. A hand outline matches itself only if left where it is.
9. (mcq) Mathematicians have shown there are exactly 17 kinds of repeating floor pattern. What does this tell a designer?  
   Options: Every repeating floor pattern is one of 17 kinds, however new it looks / Only 17 floor designs can be drawn / A floor needs 17 tiles / Nobody can invent a new floor design  
   **Answer:** Every repeating floor pattern is one of 17 kinds, however new it looks. *Why:* The 17 kinds describe the way a pattern repeats, not the pictures in it. There are endless pictures, but each repeating floor still repeats in one of 17 ways.
10. (truefalse) A scientist who sees a result that every textbook says is impossible should throw the result away.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A surprising result should be checked, repeated and tested by others. Dan Shechtman's five-fold pattern looked impossible, but it held up and won a Nobel Prize in 2011.
11. (order) Put these shapes in order, from the fewest to the most ways of fitting back into their outline.  
   **Answer:** ['A triangle with three different sides', 'A rectangle that is not a square', 'A square', 'A regular hexagon']. *Why:* The triangle with three different sides fits in 1 way. The rectangle fits in 4, the square in 8 and the hexagon in 12.
12. (write) Many viruses build their shell from copies of one small piece. Why does this mean the virus needs fewer instructions than if every piece were different?  
   **Answer:** The virus only has to carry the instructions for one piece. The copies of the piece then join together by themselves, and the shell ends up with symmetry. A shell of many different pieces would need instructions for every piece.. *Why:* A strong answer connects repeating a piece with saving instructions, and says that symmetry is a result of building from identical copies.
13. (write) A friend says, 'A mirror swaps left and right.' Give one reason this seems true and one reason to doubt it.  
   **Answer:** It seems true because your right hand looks like a left hand in the mirror. It can be doubted because the mirror only swaps front and back. The left-right swap comes from how you imagine turning round to stand where your reflection stands.. *Why:* A strong answer gives both sides. A mirror reverses the direction that points towards it, not left and right.
14. (draw) Draw a border made of three copies of the letter F using a glide. Mark the line each F is flipped across.  
   **Answer:** An upright F, then an upside-down mirror F slid along, then an upright F slid further along, with a dotted line running along the middle of the border.. *Why:* A glide flips each F across a line along the border and slides it to the next place. A strong drawing alternates upright and upside-down Fs, each one further along.
15. (label) Label the drawing of the two hinged mirrors: mark the hinge, mark the angle, and write how many objects you see in all if the angle is 90 degrees.  
   **Answer:** An arrow to the corner where the mirrors meet for the hinge, an arc inside it for the angle, and the number 4.. *Why:* The hinge is where the mirrors meet. At 90 degrees, 360 divided by 90 is 4, so you see 4 objects in all.
16. (perspective) Two friends test a flattened card strip and it bends the same way 9 times out of 10.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says how to test: turn the strip over. If the bend turns over with the strip, the strip's curve decides. If the bend stays the same, the hands may decide. Either friend could be right, and the test settles it.. *Why:* The turned-over test separates the two explanations. A strong answer proposes a test, and does not just pick a friend.
17. (mcq) A baby flounder swims upright with an eye on each side. Later one eye moves to the other side of the head. What does this show?  
   Options: A body can begin even and become lopsided as it grows / Flounders are born by mistake / All fish eventually have both eyes on one side / Eyes are the only lopsided part of the body  
   **Answer:** A body can begin even and become lopsided as it grows. *Why:* The baby starts out even and the adult is lopsided in a useful way. This is a body breaking its own symmetry as it grows, not a mistake.

1. (mcq) Which of these shapes has exactly 5 symmetries?  
   Options: A regular pentagon / A five-bladed pinwheel with blades all twisted the same way / A five-pointed star / A circle  
   **Answer:** A five-bladed pinwheel with blades all twisted the same way. *Why:* The pinwheel fits back after 5 turns and no flip works. A regular pentagon and a five-pointed star have 5 turns and 5 flips, which makes 10. A circle has endless.
2. (mcq) A badge fits back in 9 ways and has no flips. How many equal turns does it have?  
   Options: 9 / 18 / 4 / 3  
   **Answer:** 9. *Why:* With no flips, every symmetry is a turn. So the badge has 9 equal turns, counting the do-nothing move.
3. (truefalse) A shape with two mirror lines at right angles must also fit back after a half turn.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Flipping across one line and then across the other line gives a half turn. A rectangle shows it: two flips make a half turn.
4. (fill) A flat shape that looks reversed when you see it from behind has no line of ____.  
   **Answer:** ['symmetry']. *Why:* If a shape has a line of symmetry, its back view looks just the same as the front. If it looks reversed from the back, no fold line matches its halves.
5. (mcq) Which pair of moves, done one after the other, gives a half turn?  
   Options: A flip across one line, then a flip across a line at right angles to it / Two slides in the same direction / A slide, then a glide / A flip across a line, then a slide  
   **Answer:** A flip across one line, then a flip across a line at right angles to it. *Why:* Two flips across lines that cross at a right angle make a half turn about the point where the lines cross. You can check it with a cut-out F.
6. (mcq) A shape is flipped across a line, then flipped across the same line again. What is the result?  
   Options: The do-nothing move / A half turn / A glide / A slide  
   **Answer:** The do-nothing move. *Why:* The first flip swaps the two sides of the line. The second flip swaps them back, so the shape is exactly where it started.
7. (write) Cut out an F. Draw two lines that cross at a point on paper. Flip the F across the first line, then flip the result across the second line. Describe what happened.  
   **Answer:** The F ends up turned round the crossing point, not flipped. Two flips make a turn. The angle of the turn is double the angle between the two lines.. *Why:* A strong answer says that two flips make a turn and notices that the F is no longer a mirror image after two flips.
8. (write) In the pushed strip test, a tiny difference decided the direction. Give one more example from daily life where a tiny difference decides a big result, and say what the even start was.  
   **Answer:** For example a coin standing on its edge falls heads or tails. The even start is the coin balanced on its edge, and a tiny draught or a speck of dust decides the result.. *Why:* A strong answer names an even start and a tiny difference that tips it. A pencil on its tip, a ball on top of a dome and a rolling die all work.
9. (perspective) Scientists argue about why life uses left-handed amino acids.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason, and then says what evidence would help: a measurement that the nudge from the weak force is big enough to matter, or a way to see whether chance would give the same hand on another world.. *Why:* The question is open. A strong answer separates what is known from what is guessed and asks for evidence.
10. (mcq) A scientist says, 'Life uses left-handed amino acids because the weak force pushed molecules that way.' What would you most want to see before believing it?  
   Options: A measurement showing that the push is big enough to matter / A famous person repeating the claim / A beautiful picture of the molecules / Nothing, because it sounds right  
   **Answer:** A measurement showing that the push is big enough to matter. *Why:* The weak force does treat left and right differently, but the effect on molecules is extremely small. A strong claim needs a measurement showing the effect is big enough.
11. (sort) Sort each of these by whether it is its own mirror twin.  
   **Answer:** the capital letter R → Has a different mirror twin; the capital letter T → Its own mirror twin; a spiral staircase → Has a different mirror twin; a round dosa → Its own mirror twin; a corkscrew → Has a different mirror twin. *Why:* The letter T and a round dosa have mirror lines, so they match their own mirror images. The letter R, a spiral staircase and a corkscrew wind or lean one way, so their mirror twins are different things.
12. (match) Match each idea to an example.  
   **Answer:** Symmetry breaking → A pencil on its tip falling in one direction; Conserved → Energy that moves and changes form but is never lost; Quasicrystal → An orderly pattern that never repeats; Handed → A shape whose mirror twin cannot be turned to match it. *Why:* Each word names a different idea. A strong answer could give a second example of each, such as a straw buckling for symmetry breaking.
13. (draw) Draw one pattern with a half turn symmetry and no mirror line. Draw another pattern with a mirror line and no half turn.  
   **Answer:** For example the letter S for the first, and an isosceles triangle or a leaf shape for the second.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a dotted line on the second pattern and an arrow showing the half turn on the first. The two patterns show that turning and flipping are different kinds of symmetry.
14. (write) The thinker Anaximander is reported to have said the Earth stays still because it is equally far from everything, so it has no reason to move one way rather than another. Using what you found with the pushed strip, say what is missing from his argument.  
   **Answer:** In the strip test, equal pushes still gave a bend, because a tiny difference made one side win. A perfectly even situation can still end up lopsided, so an argument that there is no reason to move may not show that nothing moves.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the strip test as evidence: an even situation can still be tipped by a tiny difference, so 'no reason to move' is not the same as 'will not move'.
15. (mcq) You raise your right hand in front of a mirror and your reflection seems to raise its left hand. Which direction does a flat mirror really reverse?  
   Options: Front to back / Left to right / Up to down / Nothing  
   **Answer:** Front to back. *Why:* Your nose points towards the mirror and the nose in the mirror points back at you, so front and back are swapped. Left and right only seem swapped because you imagine turning round to stand where your reflection stands.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which move flips a shape over a line, like looking in a mirror?  
   Options: Reflection / Translation / Rotation / Stretching  
   **Answer:** Reflection. *Why:* A reflection is a flip over a line. A translation is a slide and a rotation is a turn. Stretching changes the size, so it is not a symmetry.
2. (mcq) A saree border shows the same mango again and again, all pointing the same way, with each one further along. Which move makes this border?  
   Options: A slide / A flip / A half turn / A glide  
   **Answer:** A slide. *Why:* When every copy faces the same way and each is moved along the border, the move is a slide. A flip or a half turn would make some copies face the other way.
3. (mcq) A regular octagon, with 8 equal sides, fits back into its outline in how many ways?  
   Options: 8 / 16 / 32 / 64  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* A regular shape has as many flips as turns. For 8 sides, 8 turns plus 8 flips is 16. The answer 8 forgets the flips.
4. (mcq) Which of these fits back into its outline in only one way?  
   Options: A square / The outline of a hand / A triangle with three equal sides / A rectangle  
   **Answer:** The outline of a hand. *Why:* A hand has no turn and no flip that fits, so only the do-nothing move works. The square has 8 ways, the equal-sided triangle 6 and the rectangle 4.
5. (mcq) A border has an F, then an upside-down mirror F slid along, then an F, then an upside-down mirror F, and so on. Which move made it?  
   Options: A glide / A slide only / A half turn / A stretch  
   **Answer:** A glide. *Why:* Each new F is a flip over a line along the border and a slide, done together. That is a glide.
6. (mcq) Which of these has a mirror twin that cannot be turned to match it?  
   Options: A steel thali / A pair of scissors / A round ball / A square tile  
   **Answer:** A pair of scissors. *Why:* Scissors have a handedness. The left-handed pair is the mirror twin of the right-handed pair, and no turning makes one into the other. A thali, a ball and a square tile match their mirror images.
7. (mcq) A tall pencil balanced on its tip falls over, though nobody pushes it. What is the best description of what happened?  
   Options: An even start ended lopsided, and a tiny difference decided the direction / The pencil chose where to fall / Gravity pulled it left / All pencils are lopsided  
   **Answer:** An even start ended lopsided, and a tiny difference decided the direction. *Why:* Before the fall, every direction was equally likely. A tiny draught, a speck or a wobble in the wood decided the direction. Scientists call this symmetry breaking.
8. (mcq) A card strip is pushed equally from both ends and bows to the same side 10 times out of 10. What does this most likely mean?  
   Options: The strip has a hidden tiny curve that favours that side / The push was perfectly even and strips always do this / The table is tilted / The card is too thick  
   **Answer:** The strip has a hidden tiny curve that favours that side. *Why:* An even push leaves both sides open, so something small must favour one side. A hidden curve in the card fits best. You can test it by turning the strip over.
9. (mcq) Why might a barn owl benefit from having her ears at different heights?  
   Options: She can tell how high a sound is from the small difference in when it reaches each ear / She hears less noise / It makes her look fierce / It is only an accident of growing  
   **Answer:** She can tell how high a sound is from the small difference in when it reaches each ear. *Why:* A sound from above reaches one ear a tiny moment before the other. The owl uses the difference to find a mouse she cannot see. A lopsided part can be an advantage.
10. (mcq) Most people have the heart on the left although the body looks even outside. What do scientists think helps start this difference?  
   Options: Tiny spinning hairs in the early embryo sweep fluid to one side / The mother's favourite hand / What the baby eats first / The pull of the Moon  
   **Answer:** Tiny spinning hairs in the early embryo sweep fluid to one side. *Why:* In mouse embryos, tiny hairs called cilia spin and sweep fluid to one side, which starts a chain of signals. Scientists think humans work in a similar way, but they are still studying it.
11. (mcq) Emmy Noether proved that a symmetry in the laws of nature goes with what?  
   Options: Something that stays the same, such as energy / A new force / A new planet / A left-handed molecule  
   **Answer:** Something that stays the same, such as energy. *Why:* Noether showed that each smooth symmetry of the laws, such as the laws being the same on every day, goes with something that is conserved, such as energy.
12. (mcq) What did the cobalt experiment led by Chien-Shiung Wu show?  
   Options: The weak force is not the same in a mirror / Magnets are handed / Cobalt is a crystal / Energy is lost  
   **Answer:** The weak force is not the same in a mirror. *Why:* More electrons came out in one direction than the other. So a mirror copy of the experiment would give a different result, and the weak force can tell left from right.

## Applications
- Designers use the seven border patterns and the seventeen floor patterns to plan sarees, tiles, carpets and wallpaper.
- Drug makers choose the right hand of a molecule for a medicine, because the body's proteins are handed and the two hands of one medicine can act differently.
- Crystal scientists use symmetry to work out where atoms sit from the pattern of spots in an X-ray picture.
- Physicists use symmetry to guess the form of new laws. Noether's theorem links each symmetry to something that is conserved.
- Makers of left-handed scissors, desks, guitars and can openers build the mirror twins of the usual tools.
- A kaleidoscope uses two or three mirrors at an angle to make a pattern with turn symmetry.
- Doctors read X-rays and scans knowing where organs should sit, so a heart on the other side is noticed at once.
- Computer artists draw one small piece of a design and let the machine repeat it with slides, flips and turns.
- Quasicrystals are hard and slippery, and engineers have tested them as coatings.
- Engineers build aircraft, bridges and gopurams with mirror symmetry so that loads are shared evenly.

## Spotted around us
- Footprints on a sandy beach or a muddy path, where the left and right foot alternate along the path in a glide.
- The rows of carved figures on a temple gopuram or the mango motifs along a saree border, each one slid to the next place.
- The thread of a jar lid, a bottle cap or a bulb holder, all tightened by turning to the right.
- A pair of chappals, shoes or gloves in the shoe rack, each left one the mirror twin of a right one.
- A kolam or rangoli border around a doorstep, with shapes that flip or repeat along the edge.
- A snail shell on a garden wall or a shankha at a temple, coiled one way.
- A crab with one big claw, or a flatfish with both eyes on one side, at a fish market in Kochi or Chennai.
- Your own heartbeat, felt on the left side of your chest, though your body looks even outside.

## Open questions
- **Why does life use left-handed amino acids and right-handed sugars?** Chemists can make both hands, and no one has shown how life ended up with just one. Chance, a push from the weak force and seeding from space are all being studied.
- **How does the human embryo first choose its left side?** Spinning cilia are shown in mouse embryos. Some animals seem to use earlier signals. How much each matters in people is still being studied.
- **Why do most snail shells coil the same way?** Scientists have found genes that decide the coiling in some snails, but no one has a settled reason why one direction is so much commoner.
- **Are there symmetries in the laws of nature that we have not found?** Physicists have proposed extra symmetries to explain puzzles in particle physics. None has been confirmed by an experiment.
- **Why do people find symmetrical patterns pleasing?** Some studies suggest that symmetrical shapes are easier for the eye to take in. Others find the link to beauty weak or different between cultures.
- **Are there quasicrystals in places we have not looked?** Natural quasicrystals were found in a meteorite, and scientists are looking for how they formed and where else they may be.

## Connected maths
- A regular shape has 12 equal sides. How many ways does it fit back into its outline? How many more ways is that than a square, which fits in 8 ways? **24 ways, which is 16 more than the square.**. A regular shape with n sides has n turns and n flips, so 2 times n ways. 2 times 12 is 24. The square fits in 8 ways, and 24 take away 8 is 16. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- A shape with equal sides fits back into its outline in 30 ways. How many sides does it have? How do you know? **15 sides.**. The number of ways is 2 times the number of sides, so the number of sides is half of 30. 30 divided by 2 is 15. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Two mirrors are hinged at 36 degrees with an object between them. How many objects do you see in all, including the real one? **10 objects.**. The number of objects is 360 divided by the angle. 360 divided by 36 is 10. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- A friend wants to see exactly 9 objects in all in the two mirrors. What angle should the mirrors make? What about 7 objects? **40 degrees for 9 objects. For 7 objects the angle is about 51.4 degrees, which is not a whole number of degrees.**. The angle is 360 divided by the number of objects. 360 divided by 9 is 40. 360 divided by 7 is about 51.4. It still works, but it is hard to set exactly with a protractor. Whole-number angles come from numbers that divide 360 exactly, such as 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10 and 12. (queues `math.factors-multiples.factors-and-multiples.L4`)
- Tara pushed strip A ten times. It bent towards her 7 times and away 3 times. Strip C bent away 10 times. What fraction of strip A's tries bent towards her? What percentage is that? **7 out of 10, which is 70 percent.**. The fraction is 7 over 10. As a percentage, 7 tenths is 70 per hundred, so 70 percent. Strip C is 0 towards and 10 away, so 100 percent away. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- Maha tossed a fair coin 10 times and got 7 heads. Does this show the coin is unfair? How could she find out? **No, not by itself. 7 heads in 10 tosses is quite common with a fair coin. She could toss it 100 times and see whether the heads stay near half.**. A fair coin often gives 6, 7 or 4 heads in 10 tosses. A small number of tries is too few to be sure. With 100 tosses, a fair coin gives heads close to 50 times. The same applies to the strip: 7 out of 10 could be chance, but 10 out of 10 on many tries is not. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- Each F in a border takes 6 centimetres along the strip, and the first F starts at 0. The strip is 40 centimetres long. How many whole Fs start on the strip? **7 Fs.**. The Fs start at 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30 and 36 centimetres. The next would start at 42, which is past the end. 40 divided by 6 is 6 with a remainder of 4, and the first F at 0 makes it 7. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A curvy tile started as a square, 6 centimetres on each side. A page is 24 centimetres by 18 centimetres. How many tiles cover the page, and why is the area of the curvy tile still 36 square centimetres? **12 tiles. The curvy tile has the same area as the square because the piece cut away was added back, so nothing was lost or gained.**. 24 divided by 6 is 4 tiles across, and 18 divided by 6 is 3 tiles down. 4 times 3 is 12. The area of the square is 6 times 6, which is 36 square centimetres. Sliding a piece from one side to the other does not change the total area. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
