# Caring for the place we share: litter, plants and small animals
`sci.environment.caring.L1`

**Summary.** A toffee wrapper dropped on the road does not stay there. Rain and wind carry light litter along, into drains, rivers and the mouths of animals. Your child puts clean litter on the bathroom floor, pours a mug of 'rain', and watches where each thing goes. Then we look after the beings who share our home: we water a plant only when the soil is dry, and we carry a small visitor outside gently. Our environment is everything around us, and every living being in it needs it clean.

**Intro.** *Drop a scrap of paper in front of the fan. Where does the paper land?* The paper does not stay where you dropped it. The moving air carries it across the room. Rain does the same thing to litter outside. Litter is rubbish left where it does not belong. In this unit we find out where litter goes, and who it meets on the way. Then we learn how to care for the plants and small animals who live near us.

2. By the end I can say how litter can hurt cows, birds and drains.

5. By the end I can move a small animal outside gently, without hurting them.

## Terms
- **Environment**: Your environment is everything around you. It has living beings, like plants, birds and people, and things that are not alive, like air, water and soil. *e.g.* Your balcony, the tulsi pot on it, the crow on the railing, the air and the rain are all part of your environment.
- **Litter**: Litter is rubbish left where it does not belong, like on a road, in a park or by a drain. *e.g.* A chips packet lying on the steps of a building is litter. The same packet inside a bin is not.
- **Drain**: A drain is a channel or pipe that carries rainwater and used water away from homes and roads. *e.g.* The hole with a grill on your bathroom floor is a drain. So is the channel along the side of your street.
- **Wet waste and dry waste**: Wet waste is food and plant rubbish that rots, like peels and leftover rice. Dry waste is rubbish that does not rot quickly, like plastic, paper, glass and metal. *e.g.* A banana peel goes in the wet waste bin. A milk packet goes in the dry waste bin.

## Explanations
### Our environment is everything around us · *defined*
**Definition:** The environment is everything around a living being: the air, water, soil and light, and all the other living beings nearby.

Your environment is not only forests far away. It is your room, your building, your lane and the sky above. Plants, crows, ants, cows and people all share it. Humans are animals too, so we need clean air, clean water and a safe place, just like the others. When one part of the environment changes, the beings living there feel the change.
- e.g. The air you breathe in your room is part of your environment.
- e.g. The neem tree by your gate, and the squirrels in the tree, are part of your environment.
- e.g. The rain that falls on your roof and runs into the drain is part of your environment.
### Litter does not stay where it is dropped · *defined*
**Definition:** Litter is rubbish left where it does not belong. Rain and wind move light litter from place to place.

A toffee wrapper, a plastic bag or a paper cup is very light. Wind lifts light litter and blows it along. Rainwater picks it up and carries it down the road. Plastic floats, so the water carries plastic a long way. Most litter ends up in a drain, a lake, a river or the sea. So the person who dropped it never sees where it ends up.
- e.g. In the Bathroom Rain Test, the wrapper rode the water to the drain, but the pebble stayed.
- e.g. On a windy day, plastic bags get caught in trees and on wires.
- e.g. After heavy rain, plastic and wrappers pile up at the mouth of a drain.
### How litter hurts living beings and drains · *defined*
**Definition:** Litter can hurt living beings who eat it or get stuck in it, and it can block drains so that rainwater floods the street.

Cows in Indian towns often eat plastic bags with food scraps inside. The plastic stays in the cow's stomach and can make her very ill. Birds and fish can swallow plastic, or get tangled in strings and bags. Litter piles up on drain grills, so rainwater cannot get away, and the street floods. Bottle caps and cups hold a little rainwater, and mosquitoes lay their eggs in it. Plastic does not rot away, so it stays for many years.
- e.g. A cow nosing at a plastic bag of leftover food by a gate.
- e.g. A street in the monsoon flooded because plastic blocked the drain.
- e.g. Mosquito eggs in rainwater inside an old paper cup on a terrace.
### Plants and small animals near us feel and choose · *debated*
Plants and animals near us sense what is around them and act on it. A plant whose soil is dry closes tiny holes in their leaves to keep water in. A money plant grows towards the window. An ant who finds sugar leaves a smell trail on the way home, and other ants follow it. Plants and ants feel, remember and decide. Some scientists say these are automatic actions, like a machine. So we care for them gently, as we would want to be cared for.
- e.g. A tulsi plant's leaves droop on a hot afternoon when the soil is dry, and lift again after watering.
- e.g. Ants walk in a line along a smell trail to sugar on a counter.
- e.g. A crow opens her beak and pants on a very hot day to cool down.
- **Many scientists who study plants and insects:** Plants and ants sense, remember and choose. A plant who meets dry soil keeps water in, and ants choose the shortest way to food. This is their own kind of thinking.
- **Other scientists:** These are automatic responses, built into the body, like a door that opens when you step near it. They say it is better not to call this thinking.

## Mostly agreed
- Rain and wind carry litter from where it is dropped to drains, rivers and the sea. *How we know:* People who clean beaches and rivers find wrappers and bags that came from towns far upstream. In the Bathroom Rain Test, one mug of water carried a wrapper to the drain.
- Plastic litter blocks drains and makes floods worse. *How we know:* City workers in India clear piles of plastic from drains before every monsoon. Where drains are blocked, rainwater stays on the streets.
- Plastic can hurt animals who eat it or get tangled in it. *How we know:* Vets in India have found plastic bags inside the stomachs of cows who roam the streets. Seabirds, turtles and fish around the world have been found with plastic inside them.

## How we found out
**Roy Barker, an American scientist who studied insects, and Rachel Carson, an American scientist and writer, The 1950s (Barker's report came out in 1958), 1962 (Silent Spring) and 1972 (most uses of DDT stopped in the USA).** About 70 years ago, towns in the USA sprayed their elm trees with a poison called DDT. The poison was meant to kill the beetles that spread a disease among the elms. People thought the poison would stay on the trees and harm only the beetles. Then, on lawns near the sprayed trees, many robins were found dead or shaking. Robins are birds who eat earthworms. A scientist called Roy Barker looked closely. In autumn, the sprayed leaves fell to the ground. Earthworms ate the fallen leaves, and the poison stayed inside the worms. In spring, the robins ate the worms. So the poison travelled from the tree to the leaf, to the worm, to the bird. In 1962, Rachel Carson, a scientist who studied life in the sea, put many stories like this into a book called Silent Spring. Chemical companies attacked her and said her book was wrong. Other scientists checked, and she was right. In 1972, the USA stopped most uses of DDT.  
*What people believed before:* That a poison sprayed on trees stays on the trees and harms only the pests. Barker followed it from the fallen leaves into the earthworms, and then into the robins who ate them.

## Challenging perspectives
- **One small wrapper does no harm.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: One wrapper is tiny. On its own, one wrapper will not block a big drain.  
  Against: Many people each drop one wrapper, and the rain carries them all to the same few drains. Together they block the grill. In the Bathroom Rain Test, even one wrapper stuck on the grill.
- **A banana peel is fine to throw anywhere, because it rots.** (Many people; *debated*)  
  For: A banana peel does rot, and it does not stay for years like plastic.  
  Against: A peel takes weeks to rot. On a road, someone can slip on it. In a lane, it brings flies and rats. Peels rot best in a wet waste bin or a compost pot.
- **Feeding bread to crows and pigeons is always kind.** (Many families; *debated*)  
  For: Birds are hungry, and feeding them can help in a hard season. Many families put out rice for crows.  
  Against: Bread fills birds up without giving them what their bodies need. Food left in piles brings rats, and very large flocks of pigeons can make people ill with their droppings. Clean water in a shallow plate on a hot day helps birds without these problems.

## Roots
We put clean litter on the bathroom floor and poured a mug of rain. Now we ask what that tells us about the places we share with other beings.

**Deep truth.** On the bathroom floor, one mug of water carried the toffee wrapper to the drain. The wrapper stuck on the drain's grill, while the pebble beside it stayed put. So light litter dropped on a road travels with the rain, to a drain or a river far away.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a wrapper on the ground stays where it fell, and only makes that one spot untidy. But wind and rain move it on, and the harm happens where it ends up, far from the person who dropped it.  
*Evidence:* In the Bathroom Rain Test, the wrapper and the plastic piece floated to the drain and stuck on the grill, try after try. The pebble stayed in the row. In front of the fan, the light, dry litter blew away too.

- *who am i:* You are one of many beings who share the same lane, the same drain and the same river. What you drop reaches them.
- *what is life:* Every living being needs clean water, food and a safe place. Caring for the place near you is caring for the lives there.
- *what is this world:* The world around your home is joined by moving water and moving air. They carry things from one place to the next.

Rain falls on every roof and road, and runs into the same drains, lakes and rivers. The same water passes the crow's ledge, the cow's lane and the fish's river. So what one person drops, the water and the wind carry to many others. Keeping one corner clean helps beings we will never meet.

- **Tirukkural 322 (Thiruvalluvar, in Tamil):** Share your food, and care for every living being. Of all the things wise people have taught, this is the greatest. (The Helping Pairs game and the Kind Hands activity ask what each neighbour needs, from a dry plant to a lost beetle. The Kural puts caring for many lives at the top of everything we learn.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra 5.21 (a Jain teaching):** 'Parasparopagraho jivanam': living beings help one another. Each life is held up by others. (The rain test shows that what we drop reaches others. This Jain teaching says the same about kindness: what we give to one being helps others too.)
- Science (settled): Rain and wind move light litter. In Indian cities, plastic and wrappers block drains, and in heavy rain the streets flood.
- Science (open): Plastic breaks into smaller and smaller pieces. Tiny bits have been found in rivers, seas, fish and even rain. What they do to living bodies is still being studied.
- Other view, Some people who think about why we care for nature: Some people say we should keep our surroundings clean mainly because dirt, floods and mosquitoes harm humans. In this view, caring for crows, cows and ants matters less than caring for people. Others answer that every living being matters for themselves, not only for what they do for us.
- Practice, A gentle hand on a leaf (2 min): Sit near a plant. Hold one leaf very gently between two fingers, without pulling. Feel how cool and soft it is. Breathe in slowly and out slowly, three times. The plant is feeling your fingers too. Now let the leaf go, just as gently.
- Ask: The wrapper went to the drain. Where does the water go after the drain?
- Ask: If the crow and the cow could tell us about our lane, what would they say?
- Ask: Is a lane clean when we cannot see litter, or when no one is harmed by it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Why does a wrapper ride on water, but a pebble stays on the floor? (hint: Think about which one is lighter, and which one floats.)
- *deep:* How does a plant know that the soil is dry? (hint: Think about the roots in the soil. What do they touch?)
- *big:* When we say 'our lane', whose lane is it: ours, the crow's, the cow's or the ant's? (hint: Who lives there? Who uses the lane every day? Was anyone there before the houses?)
- *big:* If nobody ever saw where their litter went, would they still care? (hint: Think about the wrapper in the drain. Who saw it there?)

## Story: The Red Wrapper
*Nila, Arun, Ammamma and Selvi akka are made up. What the rain, the wrappers, the drain and the cow do in the story really happens in Indian towns.*

Nila lived on the second floor of a yellow building in Chennai. She was seven. Her little brother Arun was five. Their grandmother, Ammamma, lived with them and grew tulsi and jasmine on the balcony.

One Monday afternoon, Arun ate a toffee on the balcony. He held the red wrapper up high. A gust of wind snatched it out of his fingers. The wrapper floated over the railing and down into the lane.

"Arun, your wrapper!" said Nila.

"It's only a tiny one," said Arun. "Nobody will notice."

On Thursday, the big rain came. The northeast monsoon rain fell all night. In the morning, the lane was a brown river. The water came right up to the first step of their building.

"The drain at the corner must be blocked again," said Ammamma. "Look how the water stays."

When the rain stopped, Ammamma, Nila and Arun went down with an umbrella. At the corner, a woman in a green coat was working at the drain with a long rake. Her name was Selvi akka, and she cleaned the drains on their street.

The drain grill was covered with a thick mat of litter. There were chips packets, plastic bags, paper cups and wrappers, all pressed flat by the water. Selvi akka pulled the mat away with her rake. With a loud gurgle, the water rushed into the drain. Slowly, the lane came back.

Nila looked at the pile. Right on top lay a red toffee wrapper.

"Arun! That could be yours," she said.

"It can't be," said Arun. "Mine fell outside our gate. That's far away."

Selvi akka laughed kindly. "Every wrapper on this street comes here in the end," she said. "The rain brings them all to my drain."

Next to the gate, a brown cow was sniffing a plastic bag with old rice inside. Ammamma gently pulled the bag away and put the rice on a banana leaf for her. She dropped the bag in the dry waste bin.

"Cows can't tell the plastic from the food," said Ammamma. "The plastic stays inside them and makes them ill."

At home, Nila had an idea. She took Arun into the bathroom. She put a clean wrapper and a pebble on the floor, a little way from the drain. "This is our lane," she said. "Now watch the rain."

She poured a mug of water on the floor. The pebble stayed where it was. But the wrapper floated, turned round once, and slid all the way to the drain. There it stuck flat on the grill.

Arun stared. "The rain took it for a ride," he said slowly. "My wrapper went to Selvi akka's drain."

"Maybe that one, maybe another one," said Nila. "But it could have been yours."

That evening, Arun made a pocket from an old envelope. He wrote BIN on it in big crooked letters. Every wrapper went in his pocket bin until he got home.

On the balcony, Ammamma's tulsi was drooping after the hot afternoon. Arun pushed his finger into the soil, the way Ammamma had shown him. It was dry. He gave the tulsi a little water. By night, the leaves had lifted up again.

"Now the tulsi is happy, and the drain is happy," said Arun.

"And the cow," said Nila.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Arun thinks a small wrapper stays where it falls and harms nobody. Nila shows him with a mug of water that rain carries light litter along the ground until it reaches a drain, where it piles up with everyone else's and blocks the water. What one person drops travels to places, and beings, they never see.

- Ask: Where did the wind take Arun's wrapper on Monday?
- Ask: Why did the lane fill with water on Thursday?
- Ask: What did Nila show Arun in the bathroom? What happened to the pebble, and to the wrapper?
- Ask: How did Arun know the tulsi needed water?

## Games
### g1 · Fly, Float or Stay: a moving game about litter (10 min)
- **rules:** Clear a space on the floor to move in.; The grown-up mixes the cards, picks one and reads the front aloud.; Then the grown-up calls 'Wind!' or 'Rain!'.; If the thing would fly in the wind, run round the room on tiptoe with your arms out.; If the thing would float on rain, sway and slide slowly to one wall, the drain.; If the thing would stay, freeze like a statue.; The grown-up reads the back of the card. Did you move the right way? Then pick the next card.
- **Printable cards: 10 litter cards to cut out, with what wind and rain do on the back:** Toffee wrapper (back: Wind: flies far. Rain: floats away to the drain.); Thin plastic bag (back: Wind: flies and catches on trees. Rain: floats far.); Paper cup (back: Wind: rolls and tumbles. Rain: floats, then goes soggy.); Chips packet (back: Wind: flies far. Rain: floats away to the drain.); Dry leaf (back: Wind: flies. Rain: floats a little way.); Scrap of newspaper (back: Wind: flies. Rain: goes soggy and sticks down.); Pebble (back: Wind: stays. Rain: stays.); Banana peel (back: Wind: stays. Rain: may slide a little.); Steel spoon (back: Wind: stays. Rain: stays.); Coconut shell (back: Wind: stays. Rain: may roll a little.)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 10 cards. 2. Read the front of each card aloud. 3. Clear a space and choose one wall to be the drain.  
  *Parent says:* Each card is a piece of litter. When I call 'Wind!' or 'Rain!', show me what that litter would do.  
  *Child mode:* Each card is litter. Listen for 'Wind!' or 'Rain!'. Then fly, float or freeze.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pick a card, read the front, and call 'Wind!' or 'Rain!'. Watch what your child does. Then read the back aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Toffee wrapper. Wind! Show me. Why did you fly?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the card. Is it light or heavy? Fly, float or freeze.
  - Ask: Why does the wrapper fly, but the pebble stays? (good: The wrapper is very light.; The pebble is too heavy for the wind.; The wind can lift light things.)
  - Note: If your child has done the Bathroom Rain Test, ask what they saw there. The answers on the backs come from that kind of test.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay all the cards out in two rows: the ones that fly or float, and the ones that stay.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the two rows. Which row should never be dropped outside? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Make two rows: fly or float, and stay. Which row travels far?
  - Check: Which litter will the rain carry furthest? → **A plastic bag**
### g2 · Helping Pairs: a memory game about caring for our neighbours (12 min)
- **rules:** Mix the 12 cards and lay them face down in rows.; Take turns. Turn over two cards and read them aloud.; If you find a neighbour and the help that fits, say why it helps. Keep both cards.; If they do not fit, turn them face down again in the same places.; When all the cards are gone, count your pairs.
- **Printable cards: 12 cards to cut out: 6 neighbours and 6 kind acts:** Money plant with dry soil; Water slowly at the roots, until the soil is damp; Ant on the kitchen counter; Wipe up the crumbs, and carry the ant out on a paper; Crow panting on a hot ledge; A shallow plate of water in the shade; Moth stuck at the window; Switch off the light and open the window; Snail on the wet path; Walk round her, and do not pick her up; Wrapper lying by the drain; Pick it up and put it in the dry waste bin
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 12 cards. 2. Read each one aloud once. 3. Mix them and lay them face down in rows.  
  *Parent says:* Some cards show a neighbour who needs help. Some show a kind thing to do. Let's match them.  
  *Child mode:* Some cards show who needs help. Some show what helps. Find the pairs.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns turning over two cards. When a pair fits, ask your child why.  
  *Parent says:* You found the crow and the plate of water. Why does a shallow plate in the shade help the crow?  
  *Child mode:* Turn over two cards. Do they fit? Say why.
  - Ask: Why do we carry the ant outside, instead of squashing the ant? (good: The ant is alive.; The ant was only looking for food.; The ant can feel.)
  - Ask: Why should the bird's water be shallow? (good: So small birds can stand in it.; So they do not fall in and drown.)
  - Note: Accept any pair your child can explain well. For the snail, the kind act is to leave her where she is. Snails can carry germs, so wash hands if anyone touches one.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the pairs. Then choose one kind act to do for real today.  
  *Parent says:* Which kind act can we do today, here at home?  
  *Child mode:* Count your pairs. Pick one kind act to do today.
  - Check: A money plant's soil is dry. What helps? → **Water slowly at the roots**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bathroom Rain Test: where does water carry litter? (25 min)
- **question:** When rain falls, does litter stay where it was dropped, or does the water carry it away? Which kinds go furthest?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which of the five things will the water carry to the drain? Which will stay where they are? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All five things start in one row, the same distance from the drain. We pour the same mug of water from the same place each time. Only the things are different.
- **safety:** A wet bathroom floor is slippery. Walk slowly, and wipe the floor dry at the end. A grown-up switches the fan on and off, and fingers stay away from the blades.
- **record:** Draw the bathroom floor and the drain. Draw an arrow for each thing that moved, showing where it went. Draw a circle round each thing that stayed.
- **results table:** Thing | My guess: go or stay? | Did the water carry it to the drain? | Did the fan blow it away?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the five things. 2. Let them hold each one. 3. Lay them in a row, half a big step from the drain. 4. Choose the side where water runs to the drain.  
  *Parent says:* These five things are litter on a road. This hole is the drain. Hold each one. Which feel light? Which feel heavy?  
  *Child mode:* Hold each thing. Which is light? Which is heavy? Put them in a row.
  - Note: Use only clean litter. Choose the side of the floor that slopes towards the drain. If your bathroom has no drain, tilt a big steel plate on a book over a bucket, and put the things on the plate.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess for each thing, and the reason. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* When I pour the rain, which things will the water carry to the drain? Which will stay? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess: which will go to the drain? Which will stay? Why?
  - Ask: Which do you think will reach the drain? Why? (good: The wrapper, because it is light.; The plastic, because it floats.; Not the pebble, because it is heavy.)
  - Note: Wait for a guess and a reason for each thing before you pour. Do not say what will happen.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour one mug of water slowly onto the floor, just behind the row. 2. Watch together. 3. Mark in the table what moved. 4. Put the things back in the row and pour again. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Here comes the rain. Watch each one. Where did the wrapper go? Where did the pebble go? Let's pour two more times, because one try can be a fluke.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the rain. What moved? What stayed? Try three times.
  - Ask: What happened at the drain? (good: The wrapper got stuck on the grill.; The plastic covered the holes.; The water had to go round it.)
  - Note: Usually the wrapper and the plastic float and ride the water to the drain, and stick flat on the grill. The newspaper gets soggy and sticks to the floor. The peel may slide a little. The pebble stays. If nothing reaches the drain, move the row closer and pour from a little higher.
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick up the five things and dry them on a cloth. 2. Move to a dry floor in another room. 3. Lay them in a row. 4. The grown-up switches the fan on low, about one big step away, or fans hard with folded newspaper.  
  *Parent says:* Now the wind. Which things will the fan blow away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Now the wind. Guess first. Then watch the fan blow.
  - Ask: Which things did the wind blow away? (good: The wrapper and the plastic.; The dry newspaper.; The light ones.)
  - Note: The light, dry things fly: the wrapper, the plastic and the dry newspaper. The peel and the pebble stay.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say three things: what they think, what they saw, and why. Then look back at their guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what happened, and why. Was your guess right? If a wrapper is dropped on our road, where could the rain take it?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you saw. Was your guess right? Where would the rain take a wrapper on our road?
  - Ask: Where could a wrapper on our road end up? (good: In the drain.; In the river or the sea.; Blocking the drain, so the road floods.)
  - Check: In our test, which stayed where it was put? → **The pebble**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the wrapper, plastic and newspaper in the dry waste bin. 2. Put the peel in the wet waste bin. 3. Wipe the bathroom floor dry. 4. Wash hands.  
  *Parent says:* Litter goes in a bin, where the rain cannot carry it. Which bin does the peel go in? Which bin for the wrapper?  
  *Child mode:* Put each thing in the right bin. The peel is wet waste. The wrapper is dry waste.
### e2 · The Sugar Test: do ants find food left out? (20 min)
- **question:** If we leave a few grains of sugar out, will ants find them? If we wipe across their path, what will the ants do?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will any ants come to the sugar in half an hour? If we wipe across their path, will they keep walking, or stop? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We put the same pinch of sugar in the same place, and look after the same time. For the wipe, we wipe only one strip across the path, and leave the rest of the path alone.
- **record:** Draw the sugar paper and the ants. Draw their path as a dotted line. Draw the wiped strip, and what the ants did there.
- **results table:** When we looked | How many ants? (none, a few, many) | Walking in a line, or all over? | What did they do at the wiped strip?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the sugar paper down where you have seen ants. 2. Note the time. 3. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* We are leaving a little food out, like a crumb after a snack. Will anyone find the sugar? How long will they take? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put the sugar down. Who will find the sugar? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Who might come to the sugar? (good: Ants.; Maybe a fly.; Nobody, because there is no smell.)
  - Note: Choose a place where you have seen ants before. Most Indian homes have them somewhere.
- **s2 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave the sugar for half an hour.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wait. The ants have to find the sugar by themselves.  
  *Child mode:* Now we wait for half an hour.
  - Note: If no ants come in half an hour, look again after two hours. If none come at all, try a window ledge or the balcony on another day. You can still talk about why food left out brings ants.
  - Wait: Leave the sugar for about half an hour.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look closely together. Count roughly: none, a few or many. Look for a line of ants coming and going.  
  *Parent says:* Who found the sugar? Are the ants walking anywhere, or in a line? Where does the line come from?  
  *Child mode:* Look closely. Are there ants? Are they in a line? Where does the line start?
  - Ask: Why do the ants walk in a line? (good: They follow each other.; They know the way.; They are going home with the sugar.)
  - Note: An ant who finds food leaves a smell trail on the way home. Other ants follow the smell to the food, and add their own smell. That is why they walk in a line.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wipe one strip across the ants' path with the soapy cloth, a hand's width away from the sugar. Do not wipe the ants themselves. Watch for five minutes.  
  *Parent says:* I am wiping away a strip of the path. What will the ants do when they reach the wiped part? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the ants at the wiped strip. What do they do?
  - Ask: What did the ants do at the wiped strip? (good: They stopped.; They turned round.; They waved their feelers and walked all over.; They found the path again.)
  - Note: The soap wipes away the smell trail. The ants stop, wave their feelers, called antennae, and wander about until they find the smell again. This shows the path is a smell, not a road they can see.
  - Check: How do ants find the way to the sugar? → **They follow a smell trail left by other ants**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Carry the sugar paper out to the balcony or a window ledge. 2. Wipe the counter where the sugar was. 3. Ask the last question.  
  *Parent says:* The ants were only looking for food. Let's give them the sugar outside. Why do ants come into homes? How can we keep them outside?

## Applications
- City workers clear plastic from drains before the monsoon, so that rainwater can flow away.
- Many Indian towns ask families to keep wet waste and dry waste in separate bins, so food waste can become compost and dry waste can be recycled.
- Saalumarada Thimmakka, a woman from Karnataka, planted banyan trees along a road near her village and watered them for years. They grew into a long, shady avenue.
- In 1974, Gaura Devi and the women of Reni village in the Himalayas stood by their trees to stop them being cut. This was part of the Chipko movement.

## Spotted around us
- Wrappers and bags piled up on a drain grill after rain, in almost any Indian city.
- Green and blue bins for wet and dry waste, outside homes and shops in Bengaluru and Indore.
- Clay pots of water for birds on balconies in Hyderabad and Ahmedabad in the hot months.
- Cloth bags and banana-leaf packing at vegetable markets in Kerala and Tamil Nadu.
- Sikkim, in the northeast, was one of the first states in India to ban plastic bags, in 1998.

## Open questions
- **Where does all the tiny plastic go when bags and wrappers break into small pieces?** Tiny bits of plastic have been found in rivers, seas, fish and even rain. Scientists are still finding out where they all end up, and what they do to living bodies.
- **How do the first ants find the sugar at all?** Scouts wander and smell their way, but how far they can smell food, and how they choose where to search, is still being studied.
- **What does a plant feel when the soil goes dry?** Scientists can measure the signals that travel from dry roots to the leaves. What that is like from inside the plant, nobody knows.

Why might that be? **The number keeps going up: 2, then 6, then 12.**. Each count is bigger than the one before. Every ant who finds the sugar adds to the smell trail, so more and more ants find the way. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- At the shop, Nila said no to 2 plastic bags. Her mother said no to 3. How many plastic bags did they not take altogether? **5 plastic bags.**. 2 and 3 more make 5. So 5 bags will never end up in a drain or a cow. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L1`)
