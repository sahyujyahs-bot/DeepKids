# Static electricity: why a rubbed balloon sticks to the wall, and what lightning is
`sci.electricity.static-and-charge.L3`

**Summary.** Rub a balloon on dry hair, and your hair rises up towards it. Everything is made of tiny particles with electric charge, plus and minus, in equal amounts. Rubbing moves some of the tiny minus particles, called electrons, from your hair onto the balloon. Now the balloon has extra minus charge and your hair has extra plus charge, and opposite charges attract. Charge that stays where it was put is called static electricity. Lightning is the same thing on a giant scale: a huge spark that jumps when a storm cloud holds too much charge.

**Intro.** *Rub a balloon on your hair, or on a woollen shawl, about ten times. Now hold it against the wall and let go. It stays there, with no tape and no glue. What is holding it up?* Nothing you can see is holding the balloon. The rubbing has given it an electric charge, and the charge attracts the wall. In this unit you rub balloons, combs and rulers with different cloths, and find out which ones work best. You make paper jump, water bend and an empty can roll without touching them. You find out why it all works badly on a damp monsoon day. And you learn how a storm cloud does the same thing until it makes lightning, and how to stay safe when it does.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that everything is made of tiny particles with plus and minus charge, in equal amounts, and that rubbing moves electrons from one thing to another.
1. I can say that opposite charges attract and the same charges repel, and use this to explain why my hair rises to a rubbed balloon and why the strands spread apart.
2. I can explain why a charged balloon attracts things that have no charge, like paper bits, a wall or a stream of water.
3. I can carry out a fair test of which cloth charges a balloon best, count my results, and say what I think, what I saw and why.
4. I can explain why static electricity works badly in damp air, and why a steel spoon held in my hand does not stay charged.
5. I can explain that lightning is a giant spark from a charged storm cloud, and that thunder is the sound of air heated by it.
6. I can say the rules for staying safe in a thunderstorm, and why each one helps.
7. I can tell how Thomas-François Dalibard and Benjamin Franklin showed that lightning is electricity.

## Terms
- **Electric charge**: Something that tiny particles carry, which makes them push or pull on each other. It comes in two kinds, plus and minus. *e.g.* A rubbed balloon has extra minus charge, so it attracts your hair.
- **Atom**: One of the tiny particles that everything is made of. It is far too small to see, even with a strong magnifying glass. *e.g.* A single hair is a few hundred thousand atoms thick.
- **Electron**: A very tiny particle in the outer part of every atom. Every electron has a minus charge. Electrons are the part of an atom that can be rubbed off. *e.g.* When you rub a balloon on dry hair, some electrons move from your hair onto the balloon.
- **Proton**: A tiny particle in the centre of every atom. Every proton has a plus charge. Protons stay put in the atom and are not rubbed off. *e.g.* An atom with 6 protons and 6 electrons has no overall charge, because the plus and minus cancel.
- **Neutral**: Having the same amount of plus and minus charge, so that they cancel. Most things around us are neutral. *e.g.* A balloon straight out of the packet is neutral. It does not lift paper bits.
- **Static electricity**: Electric charge that has gathered on something and stays there. 'Static' means staying still. It is different from the moving charge in a wire. *e.g.* The charge on a rubbed balloon is static electricity. It can stay for many minutes on a dry day.
- **Attract**: To pull towards. Opposite charges, plus and minus, attract each other. *e.g.* A balloon with minus charge attracts your hair, which has plus charge.
- **Repel**: To push away. Two of the same charge, two plus or two minus, repel each other. *e.g.* Hairs that all have plus charge repel each other and spread apart.
- **Insulator**: A material that does not let charge move through it. Charge stays where it is put on an insulator. *e.g.* Rubber, plastic, wool and dry hair are insulators, so they can hold static charge.
- **Conductor**: A material that lets charge move through it easily. Metals, water and your body are conductors. *e.g.* A steel spoon rubbed on wool does not stay charged in your hand, because the charge runs through the steel and through you.
- **Humid**: Having a lot of water vapour in the air. Humid air makes things feel sticky and damp. *e.g.* Chennai in the monsoon is humid. Static tricks work badly then.
- **Spark**: A tiny flash and snap when charge jumps across air from one thing to another. *e.g.* Pulling off a sweater in a dark, dry room can make tiny sparks you can see and hear.
- **Lightning**: A giant spark between a storm cloud and the ground, or between two parts of a cloud. *e.g.* In April and May, evening storms bring lightning to Kerala and Karnataka.
- **Thunder**: The loud sound made when lightning heats the air so fast that the air bursts outwards. *e.g.* You see the flash first and hear the thunder after, because light travels much faster than sound.
- **Lightning conductor**: A metal rod on top of a tall building, joined by a thick metal strip to the ground. Lightning goes down the strip instead of through the building. *e.g.* Tall apartment blocks and mobile phone towers usually have a lightning conductor on top.
- **Electric field**: The space around a charged thing where it can push or pull other charges, without touching them. *e.g.* Bees can feel the electric field around a flower with the tiny hairs on their body.

## Explanations
### Everything is full of charge · *defined*
**Definition:** Every atom has a centre with plus charges, called protons, and minus charges around it, called electrons. In most things, there are exactly as many of each, so the charges cancel.

You, the balloon, the wall and the air are all made of atoms. Every atom holds plus charge in its centre and minus charge in its electrons. So a balloon straight from the packet is packed with charge. But it holds exactly as much plus as minus. The pushes and pulls cancel out, so it does nothing. We call it neutral. Things only start to push and pull when the balance is upset, when one thing has a few more electrons than it should, or a few fewer.
- e.g. A new balloon is neutral. Hold it over paper bits, and none jump.
- e.g. A single grain of salt holds more electrons than there are people on Earth, and as many protons, so it is neutral.
- e.g. Your body is neutral most of the time, though it is full of charge.
### Rubbing moves electrons · *defined*
**Definition:** When two different materials rub together, some electrons move from one to the other. One ends up with extra minus charge, and the other with the same amount of extra plus charge.

Rubbing does not make charge. It only moves electrons. Some materials hold on to their electrons loosely, like hair, wool and glass. Others grab electrons, like rubber and many plastics. Rub a rubber balloon on dry hair, and electrons move from the hair to the balloon. The balloon now has extra minus charge. The hair has lost the same number of electrons, so it has extra plus charge. Rub two pieces of the same material together, and hardly any electrons move, because neither grabs harder. On an insulator like a balloon, the charge stays on the patch you rubbed. Turn the balloon round, and the other side does not lift paper.
- e.g. A plastic comb pulled through dry hair takes electrons from the hair.
- e.g. A plastic ruler rubbed on a woollen sweater takes electrons from the wool.
- e.g. Some materials give electrons to a balloon instead, such as a thin plastic carry bag. The balloon is charged either way.
### Opposite charges attract, the same charges repel · *defined*
**Definition:** Plus and minus charges attract each other. Two plus charges repel each other, and so do two minus charges. The closer they are, the stronger the push or pull.

After rubbing, your hair has plus charge and the balloon has minus charge. Opposites attract, so the hairs rise up towards the balloon. Each hair also has plus charge, and so do the hairs next to it. The same charges repel, so the hairs push away from each other and stand apart, like a fan. That is why rubbed hair looks fly-away. The forces grow quickly as things get closer. Lift the balloon a little, and the hair still follows. Lift it far away, and the hair falls back down.
- e.g. Rubbed hair stands up and spreads out, each strand pushing the next away.
- e.g. Strips of a plastic bag rubbed with wool spread out like a fan.
- e.g. Two rubbed balloons hung side by side lean away from each other.
### Why a charged balloon attracts things with no charge · *defined*
**Definition:** A charged thing attracts a neutral thing by pushing or pulling the charges inside it a tiny way, so the near side gets the opposite charge.

A paper bit is neutral. So why does it jump up to a rubbed balloon? The balloon's extra minus charge pushes the electrons in the paper a tiny way back. Now the side of the paper nearest the balloon has a little more plus charge, and the far side a little more minus. The near side is pulled, and the far side is pushed. But the near side is closer, so its pull is stronger. The paper is pulled up overall. A wall, a stream of water and an empty can are attracted in the same way. The thing must be light, because the force is small. A paper bit jumps, but a book does not move.
- e.g. A rubbed balloon sticks to a painted wall.
- e.g. A rubbed comb bends a thin stream of tap water.
- e.g. A rubbed balloon rolls an empty can along the floor without touching it.
### Why static fails on a damp day · *defined*
**Definition:** Water lets charge move. In humid air, a very thin layer of water forms on surfaces, and the charge leaks away through it.

Static electricity needs the charge to stay put. On a dry day, a rubbed balloon can stay charged for a long time. On a humid day, a very thin layer of water forms on the balloon, too thin to see. Water is a conductor, so the extra electrons slowly leak away into the air and onto your hands. That is why static tricks work well in a dry Bengaluru or Delhi winter, and badly in a sticky Chennai or Kochi monsoon. An air-conditioned room helps, because an AC takes water out of the air. Your body is a conductor too. A steel spoon rubbed on wool does not stay charged while you hold it, because the charge runs through the steel and through you.
- e.g. Static shocks from a car door or a metal railing are common in dry winters, and rare in the monsoon.
- e.g. Clean, dry hair charges well. Damp or oiled hair often charges badly.
- e.g. A rubbed comb that touches the water stream stops bending it, because the wet comb loses its charge.
### Sparks and static shocks · *defined*
**Definition:** When enough charge builds up, it can jump across a gap of air to a conductor. The jump heats the air so fast that it glows and snaps. That is a spark.

Air is an insulator, so usually charge cannot cross it. But when a lot of charge builds up close to a conductor, the push gets strong enough to tear electrons off the atoms of the air. For a moment, a thin line of air carries the charge across. It gets very hot and glows. That is the flash. The heated air expands with a snap. That is the crackle. You may feel it as a small sting, called a static shock. A static shock from a sweater or a car door is not dangerous, because only a small amount of charge moves. Mains electricity is different. It keeps pushing, and it can kill.
- e.g. Pulling off a sweater in a dark room on a dry night makes tiny crackling sparks.
- e.g. Touching a metal door handle after walking on a carpet can give a small shock.
- e.g. Pulling a woollen blanket off a bed on a dry winter night can make it crackle.
### How a storm cloud gets its charge · *debated*
**Definition:** A storm cloud gets charged as tiny ice crystals and soft bits of hail bump into each other inside it. Scientists agree on this much, but they still study exactly how each bump moves the charge.

A storm cloud can be more than 10 kilometres tall. Strong winds carry water drops up into the cold top of the cloud, where they freeze. Inside, tiny ice crystals and heavier soft hail, called graupel, crash into each other millions of times. Each bump moves a little charge from one to the other, like rubbing a balloon. The light ice crystals get plus charge and are carried up to the top of the cloud. The heavy graupel gets minus charge and stays lower down. So the cloud ends up with plus charge at the top and minus charge near the bottom. When the charge is big enough, a spark jumps. That spark is lightning.
- e.g. Most lightning jumps inside a cloud or between clouds, and never reaches the ground.
- e.g. In India, lightning is most common just before and during the monsoon.
- e.g. Lightning flashes about 40 to 50 times every second, somewhere on Earth.
- **Most cloud scientists today:** The main cause is bumping between ice crystals and graupel. Laboratory tests and measurements inside clouds show that these bumps move charge, and which piece gets plus depends on the temperature and the water in the cloud.
- **Some scientists:** Bumping is not the whole story. Rising and sinking air may also carry charge from the ground and the air into the cloud. And nobody yet knows exactly how the first spark starts, because the measured charge in clouds seems too small to tear the air.
### Lightning and thunder · *defined*
**Definition:** Lightning is a giant spark that heats the air in its path to nearly 30,000 degrees Celsius in a tiny fraction of a second. Thunder is the sound of that air bursting outwards.

The minus charge at the bottom of a storm cloud attracts plus charge on the ground below. When the charge is big enough, a spark jumps across kilometres of air. It is the same kind of spark as the crackle from a sweater, only millions of times bigger. The air in its path gets about five times hotter than the surface of the Sun. It glows white, and that is the flash. The hot air bursts outwards so fast that it makes a huge bang, and that is the thunder. Light reaches you almost at once, but sound takes about 3 seconds to travel 1 kilometre. So you see the flash first and hear the thunder later.
- e.g. A crackle from a sweater is a tiny flash and a tiny thunder.
- e.g. A rumble of thunder is the sound from different parts of a long bolt arriving one after another.
- e.g. Lightning tends to strike tall, pointed things, like trees, towers and poles.
### Staying safe from lightning · *defined*
**Definition:** Lightning can kill. The safest place in a thunderstorm is inside a building or a closed car with a metal roof.

In recent years, lightning has killed more than 2,000 people in India every year, more than any other force of nature. Most were outdoors, in fields, under trees or near water. Lightning strikes tall things and looks for an easy path to the ground. A person standing in an open field, or under a lone tree, can be that path. So when you hear thunder, go indoors at once and stay there until about 30 minutes after the last thunder. Inside, keep away from taps, bathing and corded appliances, because lightning can travel along pipes and wires. A closed car is safe because of its metal body, not its rubber tyres. Never fly a kite in a storm. India's weather scientists made a free phone app called Damini that warns when lightning is near.
- e.g. A family on a terrace goes indoors at the first rumble of thunder.
- e.g. A tall apartment block has a lightning conductor on its roof, joined to the ground.
- e.g. A farmer caught in the open crouches low with feet together, away from trees, if there is no shelter at all.
### Electric fields and living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals can sense electric fields, the space around a charged thing where it pushes and pulls other charges.

A bumblebee needs to find flowers that still have nectar, and not waste time on ones another bee has just emptied. A flying bee picks up a small plus charge from rubbing against the air. A flower usually has a small minus charge. When a bee lands, some of the charge moves, and the flower's electric field changes for a while. Scientists in Bristol showed in 2013 that bumblebees can feel this with the tiny hairs on their body, and learn which flowers to skip. The charge also helps pollen jump from the flower onto the bee. Young spiders have a problem too: they have no wings, but need to travel to new places. Many climb up high and let out silk threads. The Earth's electric field pulls on the charged threads, and helps lift the spiders into the air.
- e.g. A bee's charge pulls pollen grains across a small gap onto their body.
- e.g. Young spiders can float for kilometres on silk threads, which is called ballooning.
- e.g. Sharks and some fish sense the electric signals of the animals they hunt.

## Mostly agreed
- Everything is made of atoms, with plus charge in the centre and minus charge in the electrons. Most things have equal amounts, so they are neutral. *How we know:* J. J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897, and Ernest Rutherford found the atom's centre in 1911. Experiments since then have shown this for every material tested.
- Opposite charges attract, and the same charges repel. *How we know:* Anyone can see it with rubbed balloons, hair and plastic strips. Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured these forces carefully in the 1780s.
- Rubbing does not make charge. It moves electrons from one material to another, so one gains exactly what the other loses. *How we know:* When both rubbed things are measured, one always has as much extra plus charge as the other has extra minus. No experiment has ever found charge being made or destroyed.
- Lightning is a giant electric spark. *How we know:* In 1752, Thomas-François Dalibard in France drew sparks from a tall iron rod during a storm, as Benjamin Franklin had suggested. Scientists now measure lightning with instruments on the ground and on satellites.
- Lightning is dangerous, and being inside a building or a metal-roofed car is the safest place in a storm. *How we know:* Records of lightning deaths in India and around the world show that most victims were outdoors. Weather services everywhere give the same advice.

## How we found out
**Benjamin Franklin, a printer and scientist in Philadelphia, and Thomas-François Dalibard, a scientist in France, 1750 (Franklin's proposal) and 10 May 1752 (the sparks at Marly-la-Ville).** In the 1700s, people could make small sparks by rubbing glass tubes. Some wondered whether lightning was the same thing, only bigger. Many others thought it was something quite different, and in many towns church bells were rung during storms to drive lightning away. In 1750, Benjamin Franklin, a printer and scientist in Philadelphia in America, suggested a test. Put a tall iron rod on high ground, standing on something that does not conduct, and see whether a passing storm cloud charges it. In France, Thomas-François Dalibard read Franklin's idea and set up a tall iron rod in a garden at Marly-la-Ville, near Paris. On 10 May 1752, while Dalibard was away, a storm passed over. His helper brought a wire close to the rod, and sparks jumped across with a crackle, just like the sparks from rubbed glass. Lightning was electricity. Franklin later wrote that he had done a similar test with a kite, though some historians doubt the details. In 1753, Georg Richmann was killed by lightning in St Petersburg while doing a test like this, so nobody should ever try it. Franklin's idea became the lightning conductor, which slowly replaced bell ringing as protection.  
*What people believed before:* Many people thought lightning was a different thing from the small sparks of rubbed glass, and that ringing church bells would keep it away. The sparks drawn from the iron rod showed that lightning is electricity, and lightning conductors protected buildings where bells had not.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Rubbing makes electricity.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Before rubbing, the balloon does nothing. After rubbing, it lifts paper. It seems the rubbing created something new.  
  Against: The electrons were already in your hair. Rubbing only moved some of them to the balloon. Your hair is left with exactly as much plus charge as the balloon has extra minus, which is why your hair rises to it.
- **A paper bit jumps to a balloon because the paper is charged too.** (A natural guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Charged things attract each other, so it seems both must be charged.  
  Against: The paper was never rubbed, and it is neutral. The balloon pushes the electrons inside the paper a tiny way, so the near side is pulled more than the far side is pushed.
- **Lightning never strikes the same place twice.** (A common saying; *rejected*)  
  For: A strike seems so rare and random that it feels unlikely to happen twice in one spot.  
  Against: Lightning strikes tall, pointed things again and again. The Empire State Building in New York is struck about 20 to 25 times in a year.
- **The rubber tyres of a car keep you safe from lightning.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Rubber is an insulator, so it seems it would stop the lightning reaching the car.  
  Against: Lightning has just jumped kilometres through air, so a few centimetres of rubber cannot stop it. A closed car is safe because its metal body carries the charge around the outside and into the ground. A scooter or an open jeep is not safe.
- **Static electricity and the electricity in a wire are two different kinds of electricity.** (A common idea, from the two different names; *rejected*)  
  For: Static stays on a balloon and gives a small snap. Electricity in a wire flows all the time and runs a fan. They seem very different.  
  Against: Both are electric charge, carried by the same electrons. Static is charge that stays in one place. In a wire, the charge moves round a loop. A spark is static charge moving for a moment, like a current.

## Roots
We have rubbed balloons, bent water and learned what lightning is. Now we ask what static electricity tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your hair and the balloon were each full of plus and minus charge, in equal amounts. Rubbing moved only a tiny share of the electrons from your hair to the balloon. Yet that small imbalance lifted your hair against the pull of the whole Earth. So the electric force is huge, and it hides only because charges almost always balance.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of things as either electric or not electric, and of a plain balloon as having no electricity in it. But every object is packed with charge. What we call 'no charge' is a very exact balance of plus and minus.  
*Evidence:* In the Rubbing Test, the plain balloon lifted no paper at all. After only ten rubs, the same balloon lifted paper bits, and your hair rose up towards it, against gravity.

- *who am i:* You are made of countless plus and minus charges, held in balance. That balance holds every atom of you together.
- *what is life:* A bee feels the faint electric field of a flower, and uses it to choose which flower to visit.
- *what is this world:* The same force that lifts a paper bit builds the lightning in a monsoon storm.

A plain balloon looks as if nothing is going on inside it. In fact it holds huge amounts of plus and minus charge, which add up to exactly zero. Tip that balance a tiny way, by rubbing, and forces appear at once: hair rises, paper jumps, water bends and sparks crackle. In a storm cloud, the same tipping, on a giant scale, makes lightning. Out of one quiet balance come many different happenings.

- **Tao Te Ching 42:** All things carry the dark, yin, on their back and hold the light, yang, in their arms. Blending the two, they reach harmony. (Every atom holds plus and minus together, and their balance is what makes an ordinary, quiet object. Your balloon showed what happens when that balance is upset a little.)
- **Rig Veda 1.32:** The hymn tells how Indra struck the serpent Vritra with his thunderbolt, the vajra, and set free the waters held back in the clouds. (People who wrote this hymn saw that lightning and rain come together. Science agrees that lightning is made in tall rain clouds, by the bumping ice and water that also bring the rain. It explains the link with charge instead of with a god.)
- Science (settled): Electric charge is never made or destroyed. Rubbing only moves it from one thing to another.
- Science (settled): The electric force between two electrons is far, far stronger than the pull of gravity between them.
- Science (open): Exactly how the first spark of a lightning bolt starts inside a cloud is not yet known.
- Other view, Many physicists: Plus and minus are only names that Benjamin Franklin chose, and their balance is a plain fact about particles. Comparing it with yin and yang, or with a god's thunderbolt, is a nice picture, but it tells us nothing new about the balloon.
- Practice, Feel without touching (3 min): Rub the balloon on your hair ten times. Now close your eyes. Hold the balloon just above your bare arm, without touching it. Move it very slowly. Can you feel the tiny hairs on your arm move? Nothing is touching you. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly, three times. What did you feel?
- Ask: A plain balloon is full of charge, but does nothing. Is a perfect balance the same as nothing at all?
- Ask: Your arm felt the balloon without touching it. Where does your arm end and the balloon's field begin?
- Ask: If the electric force is so strong, why is the world mostly calm and quiet?

## Story: Sparks in Thatha's Sweater
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: in dry winter air, pulling off a woollen or synthetic sweater in the dark can make tiny sparks you can see and hear, and a balloon rubbed on wool attracts paper bits.*

It was January in Bengaluru, and the nights were cold and dry. Revathi was nine. She lived in a flat on the fourth floor with her mother and her grandfather, Thatha. Thatha had come from Madurai for the winter, and he was not used to the cold. Every evening he wore his thick brown sweater, the one Revathi's mother had knitted for him.

One night the power went off just before bedtime. The fan stopped and the whole building went dark. Outside, the street lights were off too.

"I will go to bed anyway," said Thatha. "It is too cold to sit up." He stood in the dark in the middle of the room and pulled his sweater up over his head.

Crackle, crackle, snap.

Revathi saw it. Tiny blue flashes, like the smallest stars, ran along the wool as it came off. They were gone in a moment.

"Thatha! Your sweater is sparkling!"

Thatha froze with the sweater in his hands. "What do you mean, sparkling?"

"There were little blue lights on it. And it went crackle. Do it again."

Thatha laughed, but he pulled the sweater back on, and then pulled it off again, slowly. Crackle. This time he saw the sparks too, tiny and blue, running across the wool near his face.

"Ayyo," said Thatha. "Is it burning? Is it the wiring?" He held the sweater far away from him.

"It can't be the wiring," said Revathi. "The power is off. There is no electricity in the house at all."

"Then where do the sparks come from?" asked Thatha.

Revathi did not know. But she wanted to. She remembered something her mother had shown her last week. They had rubbed a balloon on Revathi's hair, and her hair had stood straight up towards it. Her mother had said the balloon had a charge on it. The rubbing had moved tiny particles called electrons from her hair onto the balloon.

"Thatha," she said slowly, "when you pull off the sweater, the wool rubs on your shirt. Maybe the rubbing moves electrons, like with the balloon. And then they jump back. Maybe the jump is the spark."

"You think so?" said Thatha.

"I can test it," said Revathi. "Tomorrow."

The next afternoon, the power was back. Revathi tore a paper napkin into twenty tiny bits and put them in a pile on the dining table. She took a balloon from the drawer and blew it up. Her mother tied it.

"First, a test with no rubbing," said Revathi. She held the balloon just above the paper bits. Nothing moved.

Then she rubbed the balloon on Thatha's brown sweater, ten times, quickly. She held it above the paper bits again. Pit, pit, pit. The bits jumped up and clung to the balloon. She counted. "Eleven!"

"So the sweater gives the balloon a charge," said her mother.

"Now the cotton towel," said Revathi. She patted the balloon all over with a damp hand, waited a minute, and checked that it lifted nothing. Then she rubbed it on the towel, ten times. Only two bits jumped.

"The wool works much better than the cotton," she said. "Wool must give away its electrons easily."

Thatha was watching closely now, his glasses pushed up on his forehead. "And the sparks?"

"They come when the electrons jump back," said Revathi. "The sweater and your shirt get opposite charges when they rub. Opposite charges attract. When enough charge builds up, it jumps across the air in a spark. That is the crackle. Amma, isn't it the same as lightning?"

Her mother nodded. "Lightning is the same thing, only very, very big. A storm cloud builds up charge, and then it jumps in one giant spark."

"So your sweater makes tiny lightning, Thatha," said Revathi. "And the crackle is tiny thunder."

Thatha looked at his sweater as if he had never seen it before. "Seventy-two years I have worn sweaters," he said. "And nobody ever told me I was making lightning."

That night, Revathi switched off the lights on purpose. Thatha stood in the middle of the dark room and pulled off his sweater, very slowly.

Crackle, snap. The tiny blue sparks ran across the wool.

"Tiny lightning," said Thatha, and he laughed so loudly that the neighbour's dog started to bark.

In March, when Thatha went home to Madurai, he told Revathi on the phone that his sweater had stopped sparkling. "It is the hot, damp air here," Revathi told him. "Water in the air lets the charge leak away before it can build up. Wait until next January, Thatha. Come back to Bengaluru, and your sweater will sparkle again."

**For the parent:** For the parent: rubbing two different materials moves electrons from one to the other. The wool and the shirt rub as the sweater comes off, so they get opposite charges. When enough charge builds up, it jumps across the air as a tiny spark, which glows and crackles. Lightning is the same kind of spark on a giant scale. Revathi's test also shows two things a scientist does: she tested the balloon first with no rubbing, and she took the old charge off before each new cloth, so the test was fair.

- Ask: Why did Revathi know the sparks were not coming from the wiring?
- Ask: Why did she hold the balloon over the paper first, before rubbing it?
- Ask: Why did she pat the balloon with a damp hand before rubbing it on the towel?
- Ask: Which worked better, the wool or the cotton? What does that tell you about wool?
- Ask: Why did the sweater stop sparkling in Madurai?

### g1 · Plus or Minus: the charge card game (15 min)

## Experiments
### e1 · The Rubbing Test: which cloth charges a balloon best? (30 min)
- **question:** If you rub a balloon with different things, which one gives it the most charge? Does a balloon rubbed with another balloon get charged at all?
- **predict:** Look at the things you will rub the balloon with: dry hair, a woollen sweater, a cotton towel, a newspaper and a second balloon. Which one will make the most paper bits jump up? Which will make the fewest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same balloon, the same 20 paper bits, the same 10 rubs and the same height every time. Only the thing you rub with changes. Before each new rub, take the old charge off the balloon, and check that it lifts no bits. Do each one twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Pick up every piece of a popped balloon at once, because small children can choke on them. A grown-up blows up the balloons if the child finds it hard.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar for each thing you rubbed with, as tall as the number of bits it lifted.
- **results table:** Rubbed with | My guess | Try 1: bits lifted | Try 2: bits lifted | Most or fewest?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shut the windows. If you have an AC, run it for 30 minutes first. 2. Put the 20 paper bits in a small heap on the dark plate. 3. Hold the plain balloon 2 cm above them for 3 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* First, a test with no rubbing at all. Hold the balloon just above the paper. Watch closely. Does anything jump?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the balloon just above the paper bits. Do any jump?
  - Note: No bits should jump, because a new balloon is neutral. This is the starting test that every other result is compared with. Static works best in dry air. In the monsoon, or in Chennai or Kochi, an AC room helps a lot, because an AC takes water out of the air.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the five things: dry hair, wool, cotton, newspaper and the second balloon. 2. Ask your child to put them in order, from most bits to fewest. 3. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will rub the balloon with each of these, ten times. Which will make the most bits jump? Which the fewest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which thing will make the most bits jump? Which the fewest? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will work best? (good: Because the sweater made sparks in the story.; Because hair stood up to the balloon before.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Any reason your child gives is good. It tells you what they think now.
- **s3 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rub one side of the balloon 10 times on the first thing, firmly, in one direction. 2. Hold the rubbed side 2 cm above the heap for 3 seconds. 3. Count the bits that cling. 4. Pick off the bits. 5. Pat the balloon all over with a damp hand, wait one minute, and check it lifts no bits. 6. Go on to the next thing.  
  *Parent says:* Rub ten times on one side. Hold that side over the paper. Count the bits that stick. Then pat it with a damp hand to take the charge off.  
  *Child mode:* Rub ten times. Hold it over the paper. Count the bits. Then pat it with a damp hand.
  - Note: Hair, if it is clean, dry and not oiled, and wool usually lift the most bits. Cotton and newspaper usually lift fewer. A balloon rubbed with another balloon usually lifts very few or none, because both are the same material, so neither grabs electrons harder. Results vary from day to day, so a different order is fine, as long as you saw it. If nothing lifts at all, the air is too damp. Try in an AC room, make the bits smaller, or do the water-bending test instead, which works in damper air.
  - Check: Why do we pat the balloon with a damp hand and check it before each new rub? → **To take away the old charge, so each result shows only the new rubbing**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rub one side of the balloon on wool. 2. Hold the other side, the side you did not rub, over the bits. 3. Then turn the rubbed side to the bits.  
  *Parent says:* Now a puzzle. I rub only this side. What will the other side do over the paper? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Rub one side only. Hold the other side over the paper. What happens?
  - Note: The unrubbed side lifts few or no bits. The rubbed side lifts many. Rubber is an insulator, so the charge stays on the patch where it was put. That is why it is called static, which means staying still.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: some things give the balloon much more charge than others. What I saw: after wool it lifted 12 bits, after cotton only 3, and after the other balloon none. Why: rubbing moves electrons. Wool lets go of its electrons easily, and the balloon grabs them. Two balloons are the same material, so neither grabs harder.
  - Check: A balloon rubbed with a second balloon lifted no paper bits. What is the best reason? → **Both are the same material, so hardly any electrons moved from one to the other**
### e2 · Bend the Water: can every rubbed thing do it? (20 min)
- **question:** A rubbed plastic comb can bend a thin stream of water. Can a rubbed plastic ruler do it? Can a rubbed steel spoon?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will a comb that has not been rubbed bend the water? Will a rubbed ruler? Will a steel spoon rubbed on wool? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Keep the stream the same size the whole time. Rub each thing 10 times on the same dry hair or wool. Hold each one the same distance from the stream, about 1 cm, without touching the water.
- **safety:** Keep away from any switches or sockets near the tap. Dry your hands before you rub anything, and turn the tap off when you finish.
- **record:** Draw the stream of water four times. Next to each, draw the thing you held near it, and show how much the water bent.
- **results table:** Thing | Rubbed? | My guess | Did the water bend? | A lot, a little or not at all?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the tap slowly until the water runs in a thin, smooth stream, about as thick as a pencil lead. 2. Bring the comb, without rubbing it, close to the stream.  
  *Parent says:* First we need a very thin stream, as thin as a pencil lead. Now let's hold the comb near it, with no rubbing. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Make a very thin stream. Hold the comb near it. Does it bend?
  - Note: The stream must be thin and smooth, not dripping. The unrubbed comb should not bend it. This is the starting test.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dry your hands. 2. Comb dry hair 10 times fast, or rub the comb on wool. 3. Hold the comb about 1 cm from the stream, without touching it.  
  *Parent says:* Now rub the comb ten times. Bring it close to the water, but don't touch. Watch the stream. What do you think will happen? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Rub the comb ten times. Hold it close. Don't touch the water!
  - Ask: The water has no charge. Why did it bend towards the comb? (good: The comb pulled the near side of the water.; The comb's charge pushed the water's electrons back, so the near side got pulled.)
  - Note: The stream should bend towards the comb, often clearly, by a few millimetres or more. This works even on many days when balloons do not, because the water is so light and so close. If the comb touches the water, it gets wet and stops working. Dry it and rub again.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rub the plastic ruler 10 times on wool. 2. Hold it 1 cm from the stream. 3. Now rub the steel spoon 10 times on wool, holding its handle. 4. Hold it 1 cm from the stream.  
  *Parent says:* Now the ruler. Then the steel spoon. The spoon is rubbed just as hard. Will it bend the water too?  
  *Child mode:* Try the rubbed ruler. Then try the rubbed steel spoon. What happens?
  - Note: The ruler bends the water like the comb. The steel spoon does not, however hard you rub it. Steel is a conductor. Any charge that moves onto it runs through the steel, through your hand and away through your body.
  - Check: A rubbed comb bends the water, but a rubbed steel spoon does not. Why? → **Steel is a conductor, so the charge runs through the spoon and into your hand**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rub the comb again. 2. Let it touch the stream for a moment. 3. Hold it near the stream again without drying it.  
  *Parent says:* Last test. Let the rubbed comb touch the water once. Now does it still bend the stream?  
  *Child mode:* Let the comb touch the water once. Does it still work?
  - Note: After touching the water, the wet comb bends the stream much less, or not at all. Water is a conductor, so the charge leaks away. This is also why static works badly on damp days.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me what you think, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: rubbed plastic can bend water, but rubbed metal cannot. What I saw: the comb and the ruler bent the stream, the unrubbed comb and the spoon did not, and the wet comb stopped working. Why: plastic is an insulator and keeps its charge. Steel and water are conductors, so the charge runs away.
  - Think: Your body is a conductor. So how can your hair stay charged after rubbing?

## Worksheets
### Charge Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (fill) The tiny particles with minus charge that move when you rub a balloon are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['electrons']. *Why:* Electrons are in the outer part of every atom, and they are the part that can be rubbed off.
2. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Attract → to pull towards; Repel → to push away; Neutral → equal plus and minus charge; Insulator → a material that keeps charge where it is put; Conductor → a material that lets charge move through it. *Why:* These five words come up all through the unit.
3. (sort) Sort these pairs. Do they attract or repel?  
   **Answer:** a plus charge and a minus charge → Attract; two plus charges → Repel; two minus charges → Repel; a rubbed balloon and a paper bit → Attract; a rubbed balloon and your rubbed hair → Attract; two rubbed plastic strips → Repel. *Why:* Opposites attract and the same charges repel. A charged thing also attracts a neutral thing, like a paper bit.
4. (truefalse) A balloon that has not been rubbed has no electric charge inside it at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It is full of plus and minus charge, in equal amounts, so they cancel. It is neutral, not empty.
5. (mcq) Kavitha rubs only one side of a balloon on wool. She holds the other side over paper bits. What happens?  
   Options: Many bits jump / Few or no bits jump / The balloon pops  
   **Answer:** Few or no bits jump. *Why:* Rubber is an insulator, so the charge stays on the patch that was rubbed. The other side has hardly any. That is why it is called static.
6. (write) In the Rubbing Test, which thing made your balloon lift the most paper bits? Write what you think, what you saw, and why.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names the winner and the number, for example: wool lifted 12 bits and cotton only 3. It says that rubbing moves electrons, and that wool gives its electrons away easily to the balloon.. *Why:* This is claim, evidence and reasoning, using the child's own results.
7. (draw) Draw a balloon over your head, just after you rubbed it on your hair. Put minus signs where the extra electrons are, and plus signs where electrons are missing. Draw arrows to show the pulls and pushes.  
   **Answer:** Minus signs on the rubbed side of the balloon. Plus signs on the hairs. Arrows from the hairs up towards the balloon, and small arrows between the hairs pointing apart.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows both rules: opposites attract, so the hair rises, and the same charges repel, so the hairs spread.
8. (mcq) Which is the best place in your home to do static tricks on a sticky monsoon day?  
   Options: The bathroom after a hot bath / A room with the AC running / The balcony in the rain  
   **Answer:** A room with the AC running. *Why:* An AC takes water out of the air, so the charge leaks away more slowly. The bathroom and the balcony are full of damp air.
9. (truefalse) Lightning is the same kind of thing as the crackle you hear when you pull off a sweater, only much bigger.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both are sparks: charge jumping across air and heating it, which makes light and sound.
10. (order) Put these in order, to tell how lightning happens.  
   **Answer:** ['Wind carries water drops up into the cold top of the cloud', 'Ice crystals and hail bump into each other', 'Plus charge gathers at the top and minus near the bottom', 'A giant spark jumps']. *Why:* The water must freeze, then the bumps move charge, then the charge separates, and only when it is big enough does the spark jump.
11. (sort) Sort these. Is each one safe or unsafe in a thunderstorm?  
   **Answer:** inside a building with the door shut → Safe; under a lone tree → Unsafe; in a closed car → Safe; swimming in a pond → Unsafe; flying a kite → Unsafe; sitting in a room away from taps → Safe. *Why:* Buildings and closed cars give the charge a path around you. Tall lone things, water and kite strings can bring lightning to you.
12. (write) Your little cousin wants to stand under a big tree in a field to keep dry in a thunderstorm. What would you tell him, and why?  
   **Answer:** Tell him not to, because lightning often strikes tall things standing alone, like a tree, and the lightning can jump to a person sheltering below. He should go inside a building at once.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the rule and the reason.
13. (fill) Things that let charge move easily, like metals and water, are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['conductors']. *Why:* Conductors let charge move. That is why a steel spoon does not stay charged in your hand.
14. (mcq) The plastic strips of a rubbed tassel spread out like a fan. What does this show?  
   Options: The strips have the same kind of charge, and the same charges repel / The strips are wet / Plastic is magnetic  
   **Answer:** The strips have the same kind of charge, and the same charges repel. *Why:* All the strips were rubbed with the same wool, so they got the same kind of charge, and they push each other away.
15. (write) Write one question you still have about static electricity or lightning, and say how you might find out the answer.  
   **Answer:** Any real question with a way to find out. For example: does a plastic spoon charge as well as a comb? I could test both over the water stream.. *Why:* A strong answer asks something that can be tested, and says what the test would be.
### Sparks and Storms (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (perspective) Nandini and her brother Vikram watch paper bits jump up to a rubbed balloon. They disagree about why.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sides mostly with Vikram: the paper was never rubbed, and it is neutral. But it gives Nandini her due: the near side of the paper does get a little opposite charge, because the balloon pushes the paper's electrons a tiny way back. So both are partly right.. *Why:* This tests the hardest idea in the unit: how a charged thing attracts a neutral one.
2. (mcq) Ranjini rubs a balloon on her hair. The balloon now has 1,000 extra electrons. How many electrons has her hair lost?  
   Options: 1,000 / 500 / None  
   **Answer:** 1,000. *Why:* Rubbing does not make or destroy charge. Every electron the balloon gained came from her hair, so her hair lost exactly 1,000. Real numbers are far bigger, but the rule is the same.
3. (write) Arun says, 'Rubbing a balloon makes electricity.' Use what you saw in this unit to explain what rubbing really does.  
   **Answer:** Rubbing does not make charge. It moves electrons from one thing to the other. The balloon gets extra minus charge, and the hair is left with the same amount of plus. My hair rose to the balloon, which shows the hair was charged too.. *Why:* A strong answer corrects the idea and uses the rising hair as evidence that both things got charged.
4. (truefalse) A steel spoon can never be charged at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Steel can carry charge, but held in your hand, the charge runs away through the steel and through your body. A steel spoon on a plastic stand, not touched, can hold charge for a while.

6. (draw) Draw a paper bit just below a rubbed balloon. Inside the paper, draw where the plus and minus charges move. Draw an arrow to show which way the paper moves.  
   **Answer:** Minus on the balloon. Plus signs crowded on the top of the paper, near the balloon, and minus signs on the bottom. An arrow pointing up.. *Why:* The near side of the paper gets the opposite charge and is pulled more strongly than the far side is pushed.
7. (mcq) Why does a charged balloon lift a paper bit but not a book?  
   Options: The electric force is small, so it can only lift very light things / Books are conductors / Books have no atoms  
   **Answer:** The electric force is small, so it can only lift very light things. *Why:* The force on a neutral thing is small, so it lifts only very light things like paper bits, pepper and hair. A book is far heavier.
8. (order) Put these in order, from the one that charged the balloon most to least, using these made-up results: cotton 3 bits, wool 12 bits, newspaper 5 bits, dry hair 14 bits.

10. (fill) The sound made when lightning heats the air so fast that it bursts outwards is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['thunder']. *Why:* Thunder is the sound of air heated by lightning to nearly 30,000 degrees Celsius.
11. (mcq) You see a lightning flash. You count 6 seconds before you hear the thunder. About how far away was it?  
   Options: About 2 kilometres / About 6 kilometres / About 18 kilometres  
   **Answer:** About 2 kilometres. *Why:* Sound travels about 1 kilometre in 3 seconds. 6 seconds divided by 3 is 2, so about 2 kilometres. That is close enough to be dangerous.
12. (sort) Sort these materials. Does each one keep its charge in your hand, or let it run away?  
   **Answer:** plastic comb → Keeps charge; steel spoon → Lets it run away; rubber balloon → Keeps charge; aluminium foil → Lets it run away; plastic ruler → Keeps charge; a wet cloth → Lets it run away. *Why:* Insulators like plastic and rubber keep the charge where it is put.    Options: The flower's electric field has changed for a while / The flower tells her with a sound / The bee can see the nectar is gone  
   **Answer:** The flower's electric field has changed for a while. *Why:* When a charged bee lands, the flower's electric field changes for a while. Bumblebees can feel this with the tiny hairs on their body, and skip that flower, which saves them time.
14. (write) Why is it dangerous to fly a kite when a storm is coming? Use the words conductor and lightning.

15. (draw) Draw a storm cloud. Show where the plus charge gathers, where the minus charge gathers, and a lightning bolt to the ground.  
   **Answer:** Plus signs at the top of the cloud, minus signs near the bottom, and a bolt from the bottom of the cloud down to the ground.. *Why:* Light ice crystals carry plus to the top, and heavy hail keeps minus lower down. The bottom attracts plus charge on the ground below.

   **Answer:** It has equal plus and minus charge, so they cancel. *Why:* Every atom in the balloon has plus and minus charge, in equal amounts, so they cancel. We call it neutral. Picking 'no charge at all' is a very common idea, but the balloon is full of charge. It is only balanced.

   Options: Extra minus charge / Extra plus charge / No charge

3. (mcq) Two balloons are both rubbed on wool and hung side by side on threads. What do they do?  
   Options: Swing towards each other / Lean away from each other / Hang straight down  
   **Answer:** Lean away from each other. *Why:* Both balloons got the same kind of charge from the wool, and the same charges repel. Picking 'towards each other' would be right only if they had opposite charges.

   Options: Each strand has the same plus charge, and the same charges repel / The wind blows them / The strands are pulled by gravity

   Options: Light travels much faster than sound / Thunder is made later / The cloud is slow  
   **Answer:** Light travels much faster than sound. *Why:* The flash and the bang happen together. Light reaches you almost at once, but sound takes about 3 seconds to travel 1 kilometre. Picking 'made later' is a natural guess, but the hot air bursts outwards at the same moment as the flash.
12. (mcq) You are playing in a park when you hear thunder. What should you do?  
   Options: Shelter under the biggest tree / Go inside a building at once / Keep playing until it rains  
   **Answer:** Go inside a building at once. *Why:* If you can hear thunder, lightning is close enough to reach you. A building is safe. A lone tree is one of the most dangerous places, because lightning often strikes tall things.
13. (mcq) Why is a closed car a safe place in a thunderstorm?  
   Options: Its rubber tyres stop the lightning / Its metal body carries the charge round the outside to the ground / Lightning cannot see cars  
   **Answer:** Its metal body carries the charge round the outside to the ground. *Why:* The metal body carries the charge around you and into the ground. Picking 'tyres' is the most common belief, but a few centimetres of rubber cannot stop a spark that has crossed kilometres of air.
14. (mcq) In 1752, what did Thomas-François Dalibard's helper in France get from a tall iron rod during a storm?  
   Options: Rain water / Electric sparks / A magnet  
   **Answer:** Electric sparks. *Why:* Sparks jumped from the iron rod, which showed that storm clouds are electrically charged and that lightning is electricity. Benjamin Franklin had suggested the test.

## Applications
- Photocopiers and laser printers use static charge to make black toner powder stick to paper in the shape of the words.
- Factory chimneys use charged plates to catch smoke and dust before it reaches the air.
- Car factories give paint drops a charge, so the paint is pulled evenly onto the car body and less is wasted.
- Lightning conductors protect tall buildings, towers and temples.
- Petrol tankers are joined to the ground with a wire before they unload, so no spark can jump near the fuel.
- Cling film sticks to bowls partly because of static charge.
- Electronics workers wear a strap joined to the ground, so a static spark from their body does not damage a computer chip.
- Weather services track lightning with sensors and satellites to warn people, as the Damini app does in India.

## Open questions
- **How does a lightning bolt start?** The charge measured inside storm clouds seems too weak to tear the air and start a spark. Some scientists think tiny particles from space, called cosmic rays, may help. Nobody knows for sure.
- **Why does rubbing two materials move electrons one way and not the other?** Scientists can list which materials usually gain electrons and which lose them, but the list does not always hold. Even two pieces of the same material can charge each other. Nobody yet fully understands why.
- **What exactly happens to the charge in each bump between ice and hail in a cloud?** Laboratory tests show the bumps move charge, but which piece gets plus depends on temperature, water and speed, in ways that are still being worked out.
- **How many animals can sense electric fields in the air?** Scientists found that bumblebees can sense them only in 2013, and spiders in 2018. Many other insects have not been tested yet.

## Connected maths
- In the Rubbing Test, Fathima's balloon lifted these paper bits: dry hair 14, wool 12, newspaper 5, cotton 3, another balloon 0. How many more bits did hair lift than cotton? How many bits were lifted in all? **Hair lifted 11 more than cotton. 34 bits were lifted in all.**. 14 take away 3 is 11. For the total, 14 + 12 = 26, 26 + 5 = 31, 31 + 3 = 34, and 34 + 0 = 34. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Sound travels about 1 kilometre in 3 seconds. Gayathri counts 12 seconds between a flash of lightning and the thunder. About how far away was the lightning? **About 4 kilometres.**. Every 3 seconds is about 1 kilometre. 12 divided by 3 is 4. So the lightning was about 4 kilometres away. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Lightning flashes about 45 times every second somewhere on Earth. How many flashes is that in 10 seconds? In one minute? **About 450 in 10 seconds, and about 2,700 in a minute.**. 45 times 10 is 450. One minute is 60 seconds, which is 6 lots of 10 seconds. 450 times 6 is 2,700. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In her Static Weather Diary, Meera's balloon lifted 15, 13, 4, 2 and 14 bits on five days. On two of the days it rained. Which two days were probably the rainy ones? Why? **Days 3 and 4, with 4 and 2 bits.**. Damp air lets charge leak away, so a rainy day gives a low count. The counts 4 and 2 are much lower than the others, which are all 13 or more. (queues `math.logic.puzzles.L3`)
- Lightning can heat the air to about 30,000 degrees Celsius. The surface of the Sun is about 6,000 degrees Celsius. About how many times hotter is lightning? **About 5 times hotter.**. How many 6,000s make 30,000? 6,000 times 5 is 30,000. So lightning is about 5 times as hot as the Sun's surface. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
