# Parasites and hosts: an arms race, and parasites who change what a host does
`sci.microorganisms.parasites.L5`

**Summary.** A praying mantis lives on bushes and keeps away from water. Yet a mantis with a hairworm inside walks to a pond and steps in. Why would any animal do that? In this lesson you will run a pond of seed snails for eight generations and find out why the best-protected snail does not stay the best. You will learn how parasites and hosts keep answering each other's moves, and how some parasites change what a host does.

**Intro.** *A rat who smells a cat runs away. A rat who carries a certain parasite in the brain walks towards the smell. Who gains from that walk?* A parasite is a living being who lives on or inside another living being, the host, and takes food from them. The host fights back. Then the parasite changes, and the host has to change again. This goes on for millions of years, and scientists call it an arms race. Some parasites have gone further. They change what the host does, so that the host carries them to their next home. We will test the arms race with seeds and a die. Then we will look at the evidence for parasites who steer their hosts.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what an arms race between a parasite and a host is, and give two examples of a move and an answer.
1. By the end I can explain, from the Pond Race, why the commonest kind of host does not stay the best protected, and why variety protects a group.
2. By the end I can describe how a body learns to know a parasite, and how some parasites escape by changing their coat.
3. By the end I can name four parasites who change what a host does, and say who gains from each change.
4. By the end I can tell apart a change that helps the parasite, a change that helps the host and a side effect of illness, and say how to test which one it is.
5. By the end I can say which claims about parasites are tested, which are only seen, and which scientists still argue about.
6. By the end I can say what to do after a dog bite or scratch, and how to keep safe from parasites carried by cats and soil.

## Terms
- **Parasite**: A living being who lives on or inside another living being and takes food from them. The parasite gains and the other one loses something. *e.g.* A head louse on a scalp, a malaria parasite in the blood and a hairworm inside a mantis are all parasites.
- **Host**: The living being that a parasite lives on or in. The host gives the parasite food and a home, without choosing to. *e.g.* A person is the host of a head louse. A snail is the host of a young fluke.
- **Arms race**: A long contest in which two sides keep answering each other's changes. Each new defence is followed by a new way round it. *e.g.* Reed warblers learned to throw out strange eggs. Cuckoos now lay eggs that look like reed warbler eggs.
- **Coevolution**: Two kinds of living beings changing over many generations because of each other. An arms race is one kind of coevolution. *e.g.* Snails and the flukes who infect them have changed together for a very long time.
- **Generation**: All the young born at about the same time. Parents are one generation and their children are the next. *e.g.* You, your parents and your grandparents are three generations.
- **Variation**: The small differences between living beings of one kind. Many of these differences are passed from parents to young. *e.g.* Children in one class differ in height, in blood group and in which illnesses they catch easily.
- **Natural selection**: What happens when some living beings leave more young than others because of how they are built. Their kind becomes more common in the next generation. *e.g.* Flukes who fit the commonest snail find more hosts and leave more young.
- **Clone**: A living being who is an exact copy of their one parent. A group of clones has almost no variation. *e.g.* Some New Zealand mud snails are clones. A mother snail has daughters without a father, and each daughter is a copy of her.
- **Rare advantage**: The protection that a kind of host gets from being uncommon. Few parasites fit a rare kind, because parasites who fit the common kind do better. *e.g.* In the Pond Race, the kind of snail with the fewest seeds was infected least often.
- **Red Queen idea**: The idea that living beings must keep changing only to stay as well off as before, because their enemies are changing too. *e.g.* In a story by Lewis Carroll, the Red Queen tells Alice that here you must run as fast as you can just to stay in the same place.
- **Immune system**: All the parts of a body that find and fight germs and parasites. It includes skin, special blood cells and the chemicals they make. *e.g.* When a cut gets red and warm, cells of the immune system have arrived to fight microbes.
- **Antibody**: A tiny Y-shaped protein that the body makes to grip one particular germ or parasite. Each antibody fits one shape only. *e.g.* After chickenpox, the body keeps cells that can quickly make chickenpox antibodies again.
- **Coat**: The outer layer of proteins on a parasite. It is the part that the host's antibodies learn to grip. *e.g.* A sleeping-sickness parasite is covered in millions of copies of one coat protein.
- **Coat changing**: A parasite switching to a different coat protein, so that the antibodies made for the old coat no longer fit. Scientists call it antigenic variation. *e.g.* Sleeping-sickness parasites carry about two thousand coat genes and use one at a time.
- **Resistance**: The ability of a germ or parasite to survive a medicine that used to kill their kind. *e.g.* Many malaria parasites are now resistant to the old medicine chloroquine.
- **Brood parasite**: A bird who lays her eggs in the nest of another kind of bird. The other bird then feeds her chick. *e.g.* The Asian koel lays her eggs in the nests of house crows.
- **Final host**: The host in which a parasite becomes an adult and has young. Many parasites must reach one particular final host. *e.g.* Toxoplasma can live in many animals, but has young by mating only inside cats.
- **Manipulation**: A change in what a host does that is caused by a parasite and that helps the parasite to spread or to reach the next host. *e.g.* A mantis with a full-grown hairworm inside walks into water, where the worm needs to be.
- **Side effect**: A change that comes with being ill but helps nobody in particular. It is not a defence and not a trick. *e.g.* An animal with many worms may move slowly only because the worms have taken much of the food.
- **Rabies**: A deadly disease caused by a virus in the saliva of an infected animal. The virus reaches the brain and changes how the animal behaves. *e.g.* In India most people who get rabies got the virus from a dog bite. Washing the wound and a vaccine given in time prevent the disease.
- **Control**: The part of a test where nothing is changed, so that there is something to compare with. *e.g.* In the Starling Test, the round with all the paper bugs hidden is the control.

## Explanations
### An arms race: a move, an answer, and another move · *defined*
**Definition:** An arms race is a contest between two kinds of living beings in which each change on one side is followed, over generations, by a change on the other side.

A parasite takes food from a host, so a host who can keep parasites out leaves more young. That is why hosts have defences such as skin, grooming and an immune system. But a parasite who cannot get past the defence leaves no young at all. So any parasite who is born a little different, and gets through, has many young. After some generations most parasites can get through, and the host's defence is worth less. Now any host who is born with a new defence does well. The contest has no finish line. Nobody plans the moves ahead. Each move is a small difference that some were born with, and that helped them to leave more young. The name arms race was first used for countries that keep building more weapons because the other country does.
- e.g. A louse has claws that grip one hair. People answer with fine combs. Lice in many places are now resistant to common lice lotions.
- e.g. Reed warblers in Europe throw out eggs that look unlike their own. Cuckoos who use reed warbler nests lay eggs that look like reed warbler eggs.
- e.g. Bacteria are attacked by viruses. Many bacteria keep small pieces of old viruses in their own genes, and use them to recognise and cut the same virus next time. Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna turned this defence, called CRISPR, into a tool for changing genes.
### How a change spreads without anyone planning it · *defined*
**Definition:** Natural selection means that the living beings whose bodies suit their surroundings leave more young, so their kind becomes more common.

Three things are needed. First, the members of one kind differ a little from each other. This is variation. Second, some of those differences are passed to the young. Third, some differences change how many young survive. Put these together and the group changes from one generation to the next. In the Pond Race you did not tell any fluke to change. You only let the flukes who found a snail have more young than the flukes who did not. The fluke card changed all the same. For parasites this happens fast, because many parasites have a new generation in days or weeks, and one host may have a new generation only after many years. This is one reason hosts can never win the race for good.
- e.g. Chloroquine once cured almost all malaria. A few parasites had a small change in one gene that kept the medicine away from where it works. They survived, and their young filled the place of the others.
- e.g. In your Pond Race, the fluke who fitted the commonest snail took four squares of the card after one generation.
### Lock and key: why a parasite fits some hosts and not others · *defined*
**Definition:** A parasite gets into a host's cell, or hides from the host's defences, only when molecules on the parasite fit molecules on the host.

Every cell is covered in proteins. A protein is a long chain of atoms folded into one exact shape. A parasite also carries proteins on the outside. To enter a cell, a protein on the parasite must fit a protein on the cell, somewhat as a key fits a lock. If the shapes do not fit, the parasite stays outside. The shape of each protein is written in a gene, and genes differ a little from one snail to the next. So one fluke may fit one family of snails well and another family badly. This is a simplified picture. Real infection takes many steps, with many locks and keys, and scientists have worked out the exact molecules for only some parasites. In the Pond Race we used three kinds of seed to stand for three kinds of lock.
- e.g. One kind of malaria parasite, Plasmodium vivax, enters a red blood cell by gripping a protein called Duffy. Most people in West and Central Africa lack that protein on their red cells, and this malaria is rare there.
- e.g. A dog flea and a cat flea can each bite a person, but a head louse from a person cannot live on a dog. The fit is close.
### Why the commonest kind does not stay on top · *defined*
**Definition:** When parasites fit one kind of host at a time, the commonest kind of host is attacked most and a rare kind is attacked least. This is called rare advantage.

Think about a fluke looking for a snail. If most snails in the pond are of one kind, a fluke who fits that kind finds a host easily and has many young. A fluke who fits a rare kind mostly meets the wrong snails. So after a few generations, most flukes fit the commonest snail. Now the commonest snails are infected most, and have fewer young. A rare kind is hardly touched, has many young, and becomes common. Then the flukes who fit that kind do well, and it begins again. Curtis Lively, Mark Dybdahl and Jukka Jokela watched this in a lake in New Zealand. The snails there are attacked by a fluke who stops an infected snail from having young. Over 7 to 10 years, the commonest families of clone snails were almost completely replaced by families that had been rare. In a laboratory, Britt Koskella and Curtis Lively showed that the commonest kind of snail went down only in tanks that had flukes.
- e.g. In the Pond Race, rajma snails began with 8 of the 12 places and usually lost the lead within a few generations.
- e.g. Farmers who plant one variety of a crop over a huge area sometimes lose the whole crop to one disease. Mixed fields usually lose less.
### The Red Queen: is this why living beings mix their genes? · *debated*
**Definition:** The Red Queen idea says that living beings must keep changing just to stay as safe as before, because their parasites and other enemies keep changing too.

Leigh Van Valen gave the idea its name in 1973, from the Red Queen in Lewis Carroll's book Through the Looking-Glass. Other scientists then used it for a hard question. A mother who makes copies of herself, as a clone snail does, has twice as many daughters as a mother who needs a father for her young. So why do most animals and plants need two parents? One answer is parasites. Young with two parents get a fresh mix of genes, so many of them are a rare kind that few parasites fit. In your second pond, the clone snails had no rare kind to fall back on. Scientists agree that this happens in some animals. They do not agree that it is the main reason for two parents everywhere.
- e.g. In New Zealand lakes, snails with two parents are more common in the shallow water, where the flukes are common.
- e.g. The clone pond in the Pond Race usually shrank to a few snails or none in eight generations.
- **William Hamilton, Curtis Lively and others:** Parasites are the main reason. They change so fast that a host family must keep making new mixes of genes or be caught.
- **Alexey Kondrashov and others:** Mixing genes mainly helps to get rid of harmful mistakes in genes. Young who get many mistakes die, and the mistakes go with them.
- **Many biologists today:** Both matter, and their share differs from one kind of living being to another. Some clones have lasted a very long time, and nobody is sure how.
### How a body learns to know a parasite · *defined*
**Definition:** The immune system is the body's defence. One part of it acts at once against anything strange. Another part learns the exact shape of each invader and remembers it.

The first part is fast and general. Skin keeps most things out. Certain white blood cells swallow microbes, and the body may raise its temperature, which is a fever. The second part is slower and exact. White blood cells called B cells each make one shape of antibody. An antibody is a Y-shaped protein whose tips grip one shape and nothing else. When a B cell's antibody happens to fit a new parasite, that cell divides into thousands of copies, and they pour out antibodies. This takes one to two weeks the first time. Antibodies stick to the parasite and mark them for other cells to destroy. Some of the B cells stay for years as memory cells, so the second meeting is answered in days. A vaccine works by giving the body this first meeting safely. Notice that the same three things are at work as in natural selection: many different cells, one that fits, and many copies of the one that fits. This time it happens inside one body, in days.
- e.g. A child who has had measles, or the measles vaccine, almost never gets measles again.
- e.g. People who grow up where malaria is common get milder malaria as adults, because their bodies have met many coats of the parasite.
### Coat changers: parasites who cannot be learned · *defined*
**Definition:** Some parasites keep ahead of antibodies by changing the proteins on their surface. Scientists call this antigenic variation.

The parasite of sleeping sickness in Africa is a single cell called a trypanosome, who lives in the blood. They are covered in a thick coat made of millions of copies of one protein. The body learns that coat and kills nearly all the parasites. But the parasite carries about two thousand genes and pieces of genes for other coats, and uses one at a time. Now and then one parasite switches to another coat. The old antibodies do not fit. That parasite multiplies, until the body learns the new coat too. So the number of parasites in the blood goes up and down in waves, and the infection can last for months or years. A malaria parasite, Plasmodium falciparum, does something similar. They put a protein on the outside of the red blood cell they live in, and they have about 60 genes for it. A relative of the sleeping-sickness parasite lives in India. Trypanosoma evansi causes a disease called surra in camels, horses and cattle, and is carried by biting flies. Griffith Evans first saw this parasite in the blood of sick horses in 1880, at Dera Ismail Khan, which is now in Pakistan.
- e.g. A person with sleeping sickness has fever that comes and goes as each new coat grows and is beaten.
- e.g. The flu virus also changes surface proteins from year to year. That is why a flu vaccine is made new each year.
### Medicines join the race · *defined*
**Definition:** When people use a medicine against a parasite, the parasites who survive the medicine leave the young. Over time the medicine works less well. This is resistance.

Humans have added a new kind of move to the arms race, which is medicine. Chloroquine cured malaria cheaply from the 1940s. Parasites that it could not kill were first suspected near the border of Thailand and Cambodia in 1957. In India they were first reported in 1973, in the Karbi Anglong district of Assam. In 1972 Tu Youyou and her team in China separated a new medicine, artemisinin, from the sweet wormwood plant, after reading old Chinese books of medicine. It has saved millions of lives, and she shared the Nobel Prize in 2015. In 2008 and 2009, doctors in western Cambodia found parasites that artemisinin cleared more slowly than before. So today artemisinin is always given together with a second medicine. A parasite would need to be born with the answer to both at once, and that is far less likely. This is also why a person must finish every tablet the doctor gives. Stopping early kills the easy parasites and leaves the tough ones.
- e.g. India's malaria programme gives artemisinin together with other medicines for falciparum malaria, never alone.
- e.g. Head lice in many countries now survive lotions that killed them thirty years ago. A fine comb still works, because no louse egg can become too small for the comb quickly.
### Egg tricks: cuckoos, koels and their hosts · *defined*
**Definition:** A brood parasite is a bird who lays eggs in another bird's nest. The host bird loses food and work, and often loses chicks, so hosts have defences and brood parasites have answers.

In the 1980s Nick Davies and Michael Brooke put painted model eggs into reed warbler nests in England. The reed warblers accepted model eggs that looked like their own and threw out most of the others. So a cuckoo's egg must look right, and the eggs of cuckoos who use reed warblers do look like reed warbler eggs. The cuckoo also lays very quickly, while the hosts are away. In India the best-known brood parasite is the Asian koel, whose rising call you hear from March. The female koel lays in the nests of house crows and jungle crows. The koel chick does not push the crow chicks out. They grow up together. People long said that koel eggs copy crow eggs. A study in Bangladesh in 2021 measured the eggs in the way a bird's eye sees them, and found no close copying of any one host. So how crows and koels answer each other is still being worked out.
- e.g. The koel's egg hatches a few days sooner than the crow's eggs, so the koel chick is fed first.
- e.g. The pied cuckoo, who arrives in North India before the monsoon, lays in the nests of babblers.
### Hosts fight back with what they do · *defined*
**Definition:** Not every defence is inside the body. Many animals, including people, keep parasites away by how they behave.

A bird draws each feather through the beak. This is preening, and it removes lice and mites. Cows swish their tails at flies. Monkeys pick through each other's fur. Ants carry their dying and dead nest-mates far from the nest, which keeps fungus spores away from the young. Some animals even seem to treat themselves. At Mahale in Tanzania, Michael Huffman and his colleagues watched chimpanzees fold rough, hairy leaves and swallow them whole, without chewing. In the dung they found the leaves, with gut worms among them. People do all of this too. We comb, wash, cook food, wear footwear, sleep under nets and stay away from dung. Even the feeling of disgust is a defence. So a host's behaviour is also part of the arms race, and that is exactly why a parasite who can change a host's behaviour gains so much.
- e.g. A crow on a wire spends many minutes a day preening. Count them in the Preening Count activity.
- e.g. A fever is a defence that the whole body takes part in. Many microbes grow more slowly when the body is hotter.
### Parasites who change a host so that the host is eaten · *defined*
**Definition:** Many parasites must get from one kind of host into another kind that eats the first. Some of them change what the first host does, so that this host is caught more easily.

A worm who lives in a pill bug cannot walk to a starling. But starlings eat pill bugs. In 1983 Janice Moore found that pill bugs with this worm inside spent more time in open, dry, light places, and that starlings ate more of them. On the coast of California, a fluke forms tiny cysts on the brain of a small fish called a killifish. Kevin Lafferty and Kimo Morris found in 1996 that infected fish flash their bright sides and jerk near the surface four times as often as healthy fish. Birds, the fluke's final host, ate the infected fish 10 to 30 times as often. A single-celled parasite called Toxoplasma can live in the brain of a rat, but can mate only inside a cat. Healthy rats stay away from the smell of cat urine. Manuel Berdoy, Joanne Webster and David Macdonald showed in 2000 that infected rats lose this fear, and some even go towards the smell. In 2007 Ajai Vyas and his colleagues found that the rats' other fears stayed the same. Only the fear of cats had changed.
- e.g. Infected pill bugs also moved about more, so they were not simply weak.
- e.g. Nobody has yet measured how many more infected rats are really caught by cats outdoors.
### Parasites who change a host to reach the right place · *defined*
**Definition:** Some parasites do not need the host to be eaten. They need the host to go to one particular place, at one particular time.

A hairworm grows inside a cricket or a praying mantis until the worm fills most of the body. But adult hairworms live and mate in water, and a mantis keeps away from water. When the worm is full grown, the mantis walks into a pond or stream, and the worm comes out. In 2021 a team in Japan found a reason. Light that bounces off flat water is polarised, which means its waves all lie flat in one direction. Mantises with a hairworm inside walked towards this kind of light. Healthy mantises did not. In forests in Thailand, Brazil and elsewhere, a fungus grows inside carpenter ants. An infected ant leaves the nest, climbs a plant, and bites hard into a vein under a leaf at about midday. The ant dies there. A stalk grows from the head and drops spores on the path below, where other ants walk. In 2017 scientists looked inside infected ants. The fungus had spread around and into the muscles all through the body, but they did not find fungus inside the brain. How the fungus steers the ant is still not known.
- e.g. In nine streams in Japan, crickets and their relatives made up nearly half of what the trout had in their stomachs, on average. Streams with more hairworms had trout who had eaten more of them.
- e.g. In India, hairworms have been recorded from praying mantises. About twenty kinds of hairworm are known from the country so far, most from the east and north-east.
### Rabies: a virus who changes how a dog behaves · *defined*
**Definition:** Rabies is a disease caused by a virus that passes in the saliva of an infected animal, usually through a bite. The virus travels to the brain and changes behaviour.

After a bite the virus does not go into the blood. The virus enters the nerves near the wound and moves along them towards the brain. This is slow. It usually takes two to three months, and sometimes only a week or as long as a year. Once in the brain, the virus multiplies and moves out to the salivary glands. Many infected dogs become restless and bite at things they would have left alone. The animal cannot swallow well, so saliva full of virus stays in the mouth. Each of these changes helps the virus to reach the next animal. Once signs of rabies begin in a person, the disease is almost always fatal. But it can be stopped completely in the weeks before that, because the virus travels slowly. According to the World Health Organization, dogs pass on the virus in up to 99 out of 100 human cases, about 4 in 10 deaths are children under 15, and India has about a third of the world's rabies deaths. So every child should know the steps. Wash the bite or scratch with soap and running water for at least 15 minutes. Tell a grown-up at once, even if the wound is small and even if the dog is a pet. See a doctor the same day for the vaccine, and take every dose.
- e.g. A lick on broken skin or a scratch from an infected animal can also pass the virus.
- e.g. Putting chilli, oil or turmeric on a bite does not stop the virus. Soap and water, and then the vaccine, do.
### Trick, defence or side effect? How to tell · *debated*
**Definition:** When an infected animal behaves oddly, there are three possible reasons. The change may help the parasite, it may help the host, or it may be a side effect of illness that helps nobody.

A sneeze throws a cold virus across a room, and it also clears the nose. Is the sneeze the virus's trick or the body's defence? It may be both. Scientists use several tests before they call a change manipulation. First, do infected animals really behave differently from healthy ones, in a fair comparison? Second, does the change happen at the time when the parasite is ready to move on, and not before? Third, does the change really carry more parasites to the next host? Fourth, is the change exact, like losing only the fear of cats, or is the animal just slow and ill? Janice Moore's pill bugs passed these tests. Many famous stories have passed only the first one. In 2023 scientists went back to the California killifish and infected young fish on purpose. The infected fish did become more showy, so the fluke does cause the change.
- e.g. A lizard with a fever sits in the sun for longer. That helps the lizard, so it is a defence.
- e.g. A goat with many gut worms eats less and moves slowly. That may be only a side effect.
- **Richard Dawkins, in The Extended Phenotype (1982):** A parasite's genes can show their effects in the host's body and behaviour. The mantis walking to water is the worm's genes at work, in the same way that a web is a spider's genes at work.
- **Robert Poulin and other careful parasitologists:** Many changes are small, and many have never been tested against the next host. Call a change manipulation only when the tests have been done.
- **Scientists who study the host's side:** Some changes are the host's own answer. An infected bumblebee who stays out of the nest in the cold slows the parasite down and may protect her sisters.
### Does Toxoplasma change people? · *debated*
**Definition:** Toxoplasma gondii is a single-celled parasite who can live in almost any warm-blooded animal, including people, but mates only in cats. Whether the parasite changes human behaviour is not settled.

A cat who catches the parasite passes tiny egg-like cysts in the dung for a short time. The cysts get into soil and water, and on to unwashed vegetables. Other animals swallow them, and the parasite forms resting cysts in muscle and brain. People also get the parasite from undercooked meat. Scientists estimate that around a third of all people carry these resting cysts, with large differences between countries. Most healthy people never feel ill. The parasite can seriously harm a baby before birth if the mother is infected for the first time while pregnant, and can harm people whose immune system is very weak. Some studies have found that infected people are a little slower to react, or have more road accidents. Other studies have found no difference. Humans are a dead end for the parasite, since cats almost never eat people today. So if there is any effect on us, it may be left over from what the parasite does in rats. To keep safe, wash your hands after touching soil, sand or a cat's litter, wash fruit and vegetables, and eat meat only when it is cooked through.
- e.g. A cat's litter tray should be cleaned every day, because the cysts need one to five days outside the cat before they can infect anyone.
- e.g. Cats are safe to stroke. The parasite is in dung and soil, not in fur or purring.
- **Jaroslav Flegr and some other researchers:** Infected people differ a little in reaction time and in some personality tests, and this fits what the parasite does to rats.
- **Many other researchers:** Large studies have not found the same differences. People who are infected may differ in other ways, such as where they live and what they eat.
### How can a parasite reach into behaviour at all? · *idea*
**Definition:** Behaviour is run by signals in nerves and by chemicals in the blood. A parasite who makes or changes those chemicals can change what the host does.

A signal in a nerve is a wave of charged atoms, called ions, moving in and out of the nerve cell. Where one nerve cell meets the next, the signal is carried across by small molecules such as dopamine. Hormones in the blood carry slower messages, such as hunger and fear. All of these are chemicals, and any living being can make chemicals. The jewel wasp, who lives in South Asia and Africa, is the clearest case. She stings a cockroach twice. The second sting goes into the cockroach's head, and scientists have shown that her venom reaches two exact places in the brain. The cockroach can still walk, but no longer starts walking alone. The wasp leads the cockroach by one antenna to a burrow and lays an egg on them. For most other parasites we have only ideas. Hairworms make proteins that look like the insect's own brain proteins. Toxoplasma cysts were found more often in the part of the rat brain that handles fear. Nobody yet knows the full chain from a parasite's molecule to a mantis stepping into water.
- e.g. Caffeine in tea changes how awake you feel. It is a plant chemical that fits a lock in human nerve cells.
- e.g. The rabies virus travels inside nerve cells, which is how the virus reaches the brain without being met by antibodies in the blood.
### Do parasites sense and decide? · *debated*
**Definition:** A parasite senses the host's body and acts on what is sensed. Parasites decide when to multiply, when to rest and when to move on.

Sleeping-sickness parasites sense how crowded they are in the blood. When the crowd is thick, many of them stop dividing and change into a short, stout form that waits for a tsetse fly. This keeps the host alive for longer. Malaria parasites in the blood keep time with the host's day and night. A hairworm senses when they are full grown, and only then is the mantis drawn to water. A tick waits for the smell of breath and the warmth of skin. These are living beings who take in news of their world and act on that news, which is what sensing and deciding mean. A parasite is not a simple or lower being. A fluke who passes through a snail, a fish and a bird has to solve three different bodies in one life.
- e.g. A young filarial worm is in the blood near the skin mostly at night, when the mosquitoes who carry them bite.
- e.g. Cuckoo chicks call like a whole nest of hungry reed warbler chicks, and the foster parents bring more food.
- **The view this lesson takes:** Sensing, choosing and acting are found in every living being, with or without a brain. A parasite does them in a parasite's way.
- **Many biologists:** They call these automatic responses. In their view, genes that happened to produce the right response were kept by natural selection, and nothing is chosen.
### Parasites hold the web of life together · *idea*
**Definition:** Parasites are a large part of all living beings, and they shape who eats whom, who is common and who is rare.

It is easy to think of parasites as extras that the living world could do without. The evidence points the other way. When Kevin Lafferty and his colleagues weighed everything living in three river mouths in California and Mexico, the parasites together weighed more than all the birds. In streams in Japan, the trout ate more crickets wherever there were more hairworms, so a parasite was feeding the fish. Parasites also keep any one kind of host from taking over, as the flukes did in your pond. Some old partnerships may even have begun as a kind of infection. Lynn Margulis showed that the mitochondria who release energy in each of your cells were once free-living bacteria who moved into a larger cell. Whether they came in as food, as guests or as parasites, nobody knows. So the line between a parasite and a partner can move over time.
- e.g. A cuckoo, a koel, a louse, a fluke, a mistletoe on a mango tree and a virus are all living off another living being.
- e.g. Gut worms take food, but people with no worms at all seem to get more allergies. Scientists are still working out why.

## Mostly agreed
- Parasites and their hosts change because of each other, generation after generation. *How we know:* In a New Zealand lake, the commonest families of clone snails were almost completely replaced within 7 to 10 years by families that had been rare. In laboratory tanks, the commonest snail kind went down only when flukes were present.
- Some parasites change the proteins on their surface, so that the host's antibodies stop fitting. *How we know:* Scientists have read the genes of sleeping-sickness parasites and found about two thousand coat genes and pieces of genes. In infected animals, a new coat appears in the blood each time the old one is beaten.
- Using a medicine selects the parasites who can survive it. *How we know:* Malaria parasites that survive chloroquine were suspected in 1957 and reported in India in 1973. Parasites that artemisinin clears slowly were found in western Cambodia in 2008 and 2009.
- Some parasites change what their hosts do, in ways that carry the parasite to the next host. *How we know:* Starlings ate more infected pill bugs than healthy ones when given equal numbers. Birds ate infected killifish 10 to 30 times as often as healthy ones. Mantises with hairworms walked towards the kind of light that water reflects, and healthy mantises did not.
- Rabies can be prevented after a bite by washing the wound and giving the vaccine in time. *How we know:* The virus travels slowly along nerves, usually taking two to three months to reach the brain. The World Health Organization reports that washing with soap and water for at least 15 minutes, followed by the vaccine, prevents the disease.
- Parasites are a large and ordinary part of the living world. *How we know:* When scientists weighed all the living beings in three river mouths in California and Mexico, the parasites together weighed more than the top hunters, such as the birds.

## How we found out
**Janice Moore, and before her John Holmes and William Bethel, 1972 and 1983.** For a long time scientists thought parasites were simple and lazy. In 1880 the English zoologist Ray Lankester called them 'degenerate', which means fallen back. People also thought that an infected animal behaved oddly only because of being ill and weak. In 1972, in Canada, John Holmes and William Bethel reported something that did not fit. Small pond animals called amphipods usually dive into the mud when disturbed. Those with a certain worm inside swam up to the light and clung to floating plants. Ducks, the worm's next home, ate them easily. In 1983 the American biologist Janice Moore tested pill bugs. Pill bugs with a worm inside spent more time in open, dry, light places, and the healthy ones hid. Then she gave starlings equal numbers of both, and the starlings ate more of the infected ones. The infected pill bugs were not weak. They moved about more, not less. Their behaviour had changed in exactly the way that carried the worm into a bird.  
*What people believed before:* Parasites are simple, degenerate beings, and an infected animal behaves oddly only because of being ill and weak. Moore showed that infected pill bugs were more active than healthy ones, and that their new behaviour took them to the birds the worm needed.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Parasites are simple, lazy beings who have lost what other animals have.** (Many scientists of the 1800s. Ray Lankester wrote in 1880 that parasites were 'degenerate'.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many parasites have no eyes, no legs or no gut. A tapeworm soaks up food through the skin.  
  Against: A parasite gives up what is not needed and builds what is: hooks, coats that change, and ways to pass through three different hosts. A fluke's life needs more separate skills than a snail's.
- **An infected animal behaves oddly only because the animal is ill and weak.** (Most biologists before the 1970s; *rejected*)  
  For: Illness does make animals slow, and many odd behaviours are only that.  
  Against: Infected pill bugs moved about more than healthy ones, and went to the very places where starlings hunt. Infected rats lost one fear, the fear of cat smell, and kept their other fears.
- **The zombie-ant fungus takes over the ant's brain.** (Many films, games and news stories; *debated*)  
  For: The ant's behaviour changes completely, and behaviour is run by the brain.  
  Against: When scientists looked inside infected ants in 2017, the fungus was all through the muscles of the body and legs, and they did not find the fungus inside the brain. The fungus may work the muscles directly, or send chemicals to the brain from outside.
- **Toxoplasma, a parasite carried by cats, changes the personality of people.** (Jaroslav Flegr and some other researchers; *debated*)  
  For: The parasite clearly changes rats. Some studies of people found slower reactions and more road accidents in those infected.  
  Against: Other large studies found nothing. Infected and uninfected people may differ in food, work and home as well, and those could explain the results.
- **Koel eggs are copies of crow eggs, to fool the crows.** (Many bird books, for more than a hundred years; *debated*)  
  For: To a human eye, a koel egg and a house crow egg look alike. Egg copying is real in European cuckoos.  
  Against: A 2021 study in Bangladesh measured the eggs as a bird's eye sees colour, and found no close copying of crows or of two other hosts. Nobody has yet tested how often crows throw out strange eggs.
- **The best plan is to kill every parasite everywhere.** (A common first thought; *debated*)  
  For: Parasites cause great suffering. Wiping out the Guinea worm and treating children for gut worms have saved many people from pain.  
  Against: Parasites feed fish and birds, keep any one kind of host from taking over, and are a large share of all living beings. Removing the ones who harm people most is very different from removing them all.
- **A dog bite is safe if the dog is a pet or the wound is small.** (A common and dangerous belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Most pet dogs who are vaccinated every year do not carry rabies.  
  Against: Nobody can tell by looking. A scratch or a lick on broken skin can pass the virus, and the disease cannot be cured once it begins. Washing and seeing a doctor the same day costs little.

## Roots
We have run a pond of snails and flukes, and looked at parasites who steer their hosts. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves, about life and about the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Pond Race, the commonest snail never stayed the commonest for long. The fluke who fitted her had the most young, so being common made her the main target. No kind of snail was the best. Each kind was only the best for a while, until the flukes caught up.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk about the strongest, the fittest or the best as if this were a fact about one living being, like weight or colour. The Pond Race shows that 'best' is not inside the snail. It depends on which flukes are in the pond this year, and they depend on which snails were common last year.  
*Evidence:* In the Pond Race, rajma snails started with 8 of the 12 places and usually lost the lead within a few generations, while the fluke card kept turning to whichever snail was commonest. In a real New Zealand lake, the commonest clone snails were almost completely replaced by rare ones within 7 to 10 years. If your own pond came out differently, write down what you got and run it again.

- *who am i:* Your body was shaped by parasites long before you were born. Your fever, your antibodies and your wish to wash dirty hands are all answers to them. And some things you do, such as a sneeze, serve a microbe as well as you.
- *what is life:* No living being is finished. Each one is an answer to the others around them, and the others keep changing, so the answer keeps changing too.
- *what is this world:* In this world a host and a parasite make each other what they are. A snail has her shape partly because of flukes, and a fluke has hers because of snails.

We usually picture a living being as one separate thing with fixed strengths of their own. The Pond Race showed something else. What a snail is worth depends on the flukes, and what the flukes are depends on the snails. Neither can be described fully without the other. The mantis and the hairworm show the same thing in one body: two living beings, and one walk to the pond. Life began as one family and became many kinds, but the many kinds never stopped shaping each other. So the many are still tied together, and this tie is something scientists can measure in a lake.

- **Tao Te Ching 2:** Being and non-being create each other. Difficult and easy complete each other. Long and short shape each other. Before and after follow each other. (Laozi says that opposites are made by each other. In the Pond Race, the snail's defence and the fluke's key were made by each other in just this way. Neither came first, and neither makes sense alone.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 3.27:** All actions are carried out by the qualities of nature. But a person confused by the sense of 'I' thinks, 'I am the doer.' (The Gita asks who really does an action. The mantis who walks to the pond would feel that she is the doer. The science of this lesson shows that her walk had more than one author.)
- **Samyutta Nikaya 22.59 (Anattalakkhana Sutta):** The Buddha says: if this body were truly myself, it would not lead to trouble, and I could say 'let my body be like this' and it would obey. But the body does not obey in that way. (The Buddha points out that we do not fully command our own bodies. A host carrying a parasite shows this plainly, and so does every fever, sneeze and itch.)
- Science (settled): In snails and their flukes, the commonest kind of host is attacked most, and rare kinds take over. This has been watched in a lake and tested in tanks.
- Science (debated): The Red Queen idea says that this endless chase is the main reason most animals and plants mix their genes from two parents.
- Science (open): Nobody knows what it is like to be a host whose behaviour a parasite has changed, or whether the new wish feels like the host's own.
- Other view, Many evolutionary biologists, for example Richard Dawkins: They would say there is no tie or togetherness here, only a contest. Each gene is copied more or less often, and that is all. The fluke's genes spread when snails suffer, and the snail's genes spread when flukes fail. On this view nothing is shared and nothing is decided. Words like 'answer' and 'author' are only ways of speaking about which genes get copied.
- Practice, What did my body do without asking me? (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. For one minute, do nothing on purpose. Only notice what your body does. You may blink, swallow, breathe, shift or scratch. Count each thing on your fingers. Now open your eyes. How many things did your body do that you did not plan? Who did them?
- Ask: In the Pond Race, was any kind of snail the best? What would 'best' have to mean?
- Ask: The mantis walked to the pond and the worm needed the pond. Whose walk was it?
- Ask: Your fever and your antibodies are answers to parasites. How much of you was shaped by living beings who are not you?
- Ask: If hosts and parasites make each other what they are, where does one end and the other begin?

## Story: The Mantis Who Walked into the Pond
*Junali, her family and their mantis are made up. These facts are real. Hairworms grow inside praying mantises and crickets, and the adults live in water. Hairworms have been recorded from mantises in India, and most Indian records are from the east and north-east. In many countries people once believed that horse hairs came alive in water, which is why the worms are called horsehair worms. In 2021 a team in Japan showed that mantises with hairworms walk towards light reflected from flat water, and healthy mantises do not. Whether a mantis lives after the worm has left is not well known.*

The mantis had lived on the hibiscus bush since the month of Jeth, and Junali had watched her nearly every day.

The bush stood by the kitchen wall of the house, in a village a little way from Jorhat in Assam. The mantis was green, and as long as Junali's middle finger. She stood so still among the leaves that Junali's little brother Bhaskar could never find her, even when Junali pointed.

Junali knew three things about her. She ate flies, and held each one in her folded arms like a girl holding a mango. She turned her whole head to look at you, which no other insect did. And she hated water. When the rain came she moved under a leaf. When a drop landed on her she shook it off, one leg at a time.

In the month of Bhado, when the rains were ending, the mantis began to do strange things.

On Monday Junali found her on the lid of the steel water drum by the well. The lid was flat and shone in the sun. The mantis was walking on it in small circles. Junali carried her back to the bush on a stick.

On Wednesday she was on the drum again. On Thursday she was on the tin sheet that covered the firewood. The sheet was wet from the night's rain and shone like a mirror.

'She is looking for something,' Junali said.

'She is looking for flies,' said Bhaskar. 'Flies like the drum.'

But the mantis was not hunting. She was thin in the chest and thick in the belly, and she did not hold her arms ready.

On Friday evening the sun came low under the clouds. The pond behind the house, the pukhuri where the ducks slept, lit up like a brass plate. Junali was shelling peas on the step. She saw the mantis climb down the hibiscus stem to the ground.

A mantis on the ground is a mantis in danger. The hens were not ten steps away. But the mantis did not hide. She walked across the swept yard in a straight line. She walked past the tulsi, past the hens, past the upturned boat. She walked down the bank.

'Bhaskar,' Junali said. 'Come and look.'

The mantis reached the edge of the water. She did not stop. She stepped on to the pond as if it were the steel lid, and then she was in it, with her legs working slowly.

'She is thirsty,' said Bhaskar.

'She has never once gone to water,' said Junali. 'She shakes it off her feet.'

'Then she is stupid.'

Junali broke off a piece of banana leaf and slid it under the mantis. And then she saw the other thing. A dark thread was coming out of the end of the mantis's body. It kept coming. It was as thin as a wire and longer than Junali's forearm. It left the mantis altogether and went winding away through the water, tying and untying itself.

Bhaskar ran for their grandmother.

Aita came down the bank slowly, wiping her hands. She looked at the thread for a long time.

'When I was a girl we called that a horse's hair,' she said. 'People said that if a hair from a horse's tail fell into the water, it came alive.'

'There are no horses here,' said Junali.

'No,' said Aita. 'There never were. I wondered about that, too.'

The mantis lay on the banana leaf. She was alive, but she was flat and light, like an empty pea pod. Junali put her back on the hibiscus, in the fork of two stems.

That night Junali could not sleep. She lay and put the pieces side by side, as she did with sums.

The thread was a worm. The worm had been inside the mantis. That was why the mantis had grown thick in the belly and eaten so much. A worm that long must have been growing there for weeks.

The worm had swum off into the pond as if it were home. Junali had seen threads like that before, in other years, in the shallow end. So the worm was a water animal.

But the mantis was a bush animal. The bush was forty steps from the pond. A worm inside a mantis on a bush has no way to get to a pond. The worm has no legs.

Junali sat up.

The mantis had legs.

She thought of the steel lid, and the wet tin sheet, and the pond at sunset. All three were flat, and all three shone. The mantis had not wanted flies. She had not been thirsty. All week she had been walking towards anything that shone like water. And she had only begun in the week that the worm was full grown.

In the morning she told Bhaskar.

'The mantis did not walk to the pond,' she said. 'The worm did. The worm used her legs.'

'A worm cannot make a mantis walk,' said Bhaskar.

'Then why did she go to the lid on Monday, and the tin on Thursday, and never once before this week? Find me another reason.'

Bhaskar thought about it all through breakfast, and could not.

They went to the hibiscus. The fork of the two stems was empty. Junali looked under every leaf, and Bhaskar looked too, properly this time. They did not find the mantis, and Junali never learned whether she had lived.

But she went on thinking about the walk across the yard. The mantis had gone so straight, and had looked so sure. If you had asked the mantis, Junali thought, she would have said: I want to go to the shining place. And she would have meant it.

**For the parent:** The worm lives in water and has no legs. The mantis lives on a bush and has legs. Junali worked out that the walk to the pond served the worm, and that the mantis had been drawn to anything that shone like water only once the worm was full grown.

- Ask: Which clues told Junali that the mantis was not simply thirsty?
- Ask: Why did it matter that the strange walks began only in the last week?
- Ask: How could you test whether the shine draws the mantis, and not the wetness?
- Ask: The mantis looked sure of where she was going. Was the wish to reach the shining place her own?

### e2 · The Starling Test: how much does a small change in behaviour matter? (35 min)
- **question:** Pill bugs with a worm inside walk in the open. If 10 bugs stand in the open and 10 hide, how many times as many of the open ones does a hunter catch? And does the worm alone, with no change in behaviour, make any difference?
- **predict:** Guess first. Ten paper bugs lie in the open on pale paper and ten hide among leaves on a dark cloth. In 10 seconds, how many of each will the hunter catch? Then guess what happens when all twenty hide in the same way. Write both guesses and why you think so.
- **fair test:** Every round has 20 bugs of the same size and colour, the same hunter, one hand and 10 seconds. Only the place of the 10 dotted bugs changes: in the open, on the dark cloth without cover, or hidden like the others.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps with the scissors.
- **record:** Fill in one row of the table for each of the six rounds. Add up the dotted bugs and the plain bugs caught in each set-up. Draw a bar chart with two bars for each set-up.
- **results table:** Round | Where the dotted bugs stood | Dotted bugs caught | Plain bugs caught
- **s1 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut 20 circles of dark paper, each the size of a one-rupee coin. 2. Put a pencil dot on one side of 10 of them. 3. Spread the dark cloth on the floor and scatter the leaves on it. 4. Lay the white paper beside the cloth. 5. Draw the results table. 6. Ask for the guesses and the reasons.  
  *Parent says:* Each circle is a pill bug. A dot underneath means a worm inside. Healthy bugs hide. Bugs with a worm walk in the open. How many of each will a hunter catch in 10 seconds? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to help you cut 20 dark circles. Dot 10 underneath. Spread the cloth and the leaves. Now guess. How many of each kind will be caught? Write why.
  - Note: Keep the dots small and underneath, so that the hunter cannot tell the bugs apart from above. Write the guesses down. Many children expect only a small difference.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The hunter turns away. 2. Hide the 10 plain bugs among the leaves on the dark cloth. 3. Put the 10 dotted bugs on the white paper, dot side down. 4. Say go and count slowly to 10. 5. The hunter picks up one bug at a time with one hand and drops each in the cup. 6. Count the dotted and plain bugs in the cup. Write them down. 7. Do the round once more.  
  *Parent says:* You are a starling with ten seconds to feed. Use one hand, and take one bug at a time. Go. Now turn them over. How many have a dot?  
  *Child mode:* Hunter, turn away. Setter, hide the plain bugs and put the dotted bugs in the open. Hunt for 10 seconds with one hand. Count dotted and plain bugs. Do it twice. Working alone? Set the bugs, leave the room, count to 30, then hunt.
  - Ask: How many times as many dotted bugs as plain bugs did you catch? (good: Any number worked out from the counts, such as 15 and 4, which is nearly 4 times.; Many more dotted ones, because they were easy to see and reach.)
  - Note: Expect most of the open bugs to be caught and only a few hidden ones. If the hunter catches every bug in 10 seconds, cut the time to 6 seconds for all rounds.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the white paper away. 2. Put the 10 dotted bugs on the dark cloth, in the gaps between the leaves and not under them. 3. Hide the 10 plain bugs among the leaves as before. 4. Hunt for 10 seconds. Count and write. 5. Do the round once more.  
  *Parent says:* Now the dotted bugs are on the dark cloth, but they are not under the leaves. This is a smaller change in behaviour. Will it still matter? Go.  
  *Child mode:* Take away the white paper. Put the dotted bugs on the cloth, between the leaves. Hide the plain bugs. Hunt for 10 seconds. Count. Do it twice.
  - Ask: Was a small change in behaviour enough to change who was caught? (good: Yes, the dotted bugs were still caught more often, but by less.; It depended. Dark bugs on a dark cloth were harder to see.)
  - Note: Results vary here, and that is fine. Dark bugs on a dark cloth are harder to see, so the gap usually shrinks. Write down what you get.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix all 20 bugs. 2. Hide all of them among the leaves on the dark cloth in the same way. 3. Hunt for 10 seconds. Count dotted and plain. 4. Do the round once more.  
  *Parent says:* This is the control. Now the dotted bugs behave exactly like the others. If the worm alone made a bug easy to catch, what would we see? Go.  
  *Child mode:* Mix all the bugs. Hide them all in the same way. Hunt for 10 seconds. Count dotted and plain. Do it twice.
  - Ask: Were dotted bugs caught more often when they hid like the others? (good: No. About the same number of each was caught.; The dot made no difference. The place did.)
  - Note: Expect about equal numbers, give or take one or two. This round shows that the difference in the first rounds came from where the bugs stood.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add up the two rounds for each set-up. 2. Draw the bar chart. 3. Ask for three sentences: what I thought, what I saw, and why it happened. 4. Then read the true story of Janice Moore's pill bugs on the card How we found out.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the three set-ups. When were dotted bugs caught most? When were they caught no more than the others? What did you think, what did you see, and why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Add up each set-up. Draw the bars. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Note: This is a model. Real pill bugs with the worm were also found on paler ground and in drier air, and real starlings did eat more of them. Paper cannot show whether a real infected bug is weaker, which Janice Moore checked separately.
  - Check: In the control round, dotted and plain bugs were caught equally. What does that show? → **Where a bug stands decides who is caught**
  - Think: The worm cannot see a starling. So how can a worm's host end up where starlings hunt?

- **record:** Make a table with a row for each day from 1 to 13, and columns for coat A, coat B, coat C, coat D and the total. Then draw a line graph of the total, with the day along the bottom.

  *Parent says:* Draw the line. What shape do you see? The real sleeping-sickness parasite has about two thousand coat genes. What does that tell you about how long the illness can last?

## Challenges
### c1 · The Mixed Field: plant 36 plots so that one disease cannot take them all (40 min)
- **problem:** Lakshmi grows bananas near Tiruchirappalli. Her field has 36 plots, in 6 rows of 6. She grows 12 plots each of three varieties: Poovan, Rasthali and Nendran. A fungus in the soil fits one variety at a time. It starts in one plot and spreads to every plot of the same variety that touches it side to side, and then onward from those. Plan where to plant each variety so that an outbreak takes as few plots as possible.
- **your turn:** Now draw your own 6 by 6 field and colour it. For each outbreak, roll the die twice to choose a row and a column. Shade that plot and every plot of the same variety joined to it side to side. Count the plots lost. Do five outbreaks, starting fresh each time, and work out the average. Then try to beat your own plan.
- **constraints:** Use exactly 12 plots of each variety.; Every plot must touch at least one plot of its own variety, side to side, so that Lakshmi can walk through each variety at harvest.; Plots that touch only at a corner do not count as touching.; Test your plan with five outbreaks and work out the average loss.
- **general:** Three big blocks of 12 plots are the easiest to plant and harvest. One outbreak takes a whole block of 12.; Rows of one variety, in the order Poovan, Rasthali, Nendran and again, lose 6 plots in an outbreak.; Smaller groups lose less. The smallest group the rules allow is 2 plots.
- **creative:** Plant pairs of plots, and colour the pairs so that no two pairs of the same variety touch side to side. Then any outbreak takes only 2 plots.; Change which variety grows in which plot every few years, so that the fungus in the soil of a plot meets a new variety.; Keep a few plants of older, rarer varieties in a corner. If a new fungus beats all three main ones, a rare one may survive.; Farmers in several villages can each grow a different mix, so that the whole district is one large mixed field.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the problem aloud. 2. Draw a 6 by 6 grid and colour it in three blocks of 12. 3. Roll twice to pick a plot. Shade the plot and all joined plots of that variety. 4. Count the loss.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the easiest field to plant, in three big blocks. Roll for a row and a column. That plot falls ill. Which other plots will the fungus reach? How many is that?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a 6 by 6 grid. Colour three blocks of 12. Roll for a row and a column. Shade that plot and all joined plots of its variety. Count them.
  - Ask: Why does the whole block go? (good: Every plot in the block is the same variety and joined side to side.; It is like the pond where every snail was the same.)
- **s2 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a better plan that keeps to the rules. 2. Your child colours a new grid. 3. Check that there are 12 plots of each variety and that every plot touches one of its own. 4. Test with five outbreaks. Write each loss.  
  *Parent says:* Now plan your own field. Keep 12 plots of each variety, and let every plot touch one of its own kind. What will you change to make the loss smaller? Test it five times.  
  *Child mode:* Colour your own field. Use 12 plots of each variety. Every plot must touch one of its own kind. Test it with five outbreaks. Write each loss.
  - Ask: What is the smallest loss the rules allow, and why? (good: 2 plots. Every plot must touch one of its own variety, so the smallest group is a pair.)
  - Note: Add the five losses and divide by 5 for the average. Rows give 6 every time. A plan of pairs, with no two pairs of one variety touching, gives 2 every time. One way is to lay all pairs across the rows and repeat the colours in the order 1, 2, 3 along each row, starting one colour later in each new row.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the average losses of blocks, rows and your child's plan. 2. Ask what the smaller groups cost the farmer. 3. Ask what else she could do over several years.  
  *Parent says:* Which plan lost least? What extra work does that plan give Lakshmi? The fungus stays in the soil for years. What could she change from year to year?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the plans. Which lost least? What extra work does it give the farmer? What could she change each year?
  - Note: A mixed field means more walking and sorting at harvest, and that is a real cost. This is why large farms often plant one variety, and why they are at risk.
  - Check: Why does a field of one variety lose more to a disease than a mixed field? → **A disease that fits one plant fits all of them**
### c2 · Trick or Only Ill? Design a fair test by watching (30 min)
- **problem:** This case is made up. On a beach near Kochi, most small crabs stay in their burrows at noon. A few stand out on the open sand. A student finds that many of the crabs in the open carry a worm. She thinks the worm makes them stand there, so that shore birds eat them. Design a test that could show whether she is right.
- **your turn:** Now write your own one-page plan with four parts: what I will compare, what I will count, what result would mean a trick, and what result would mean only ill. Then write the same four parts for an animal you can really watch, such as ants on a wall or snails after rain.
- **constraints:** You may only watch, count and gently look. No crab may be hurt or kept for more than 10 minutes.; Compare crabs who carry the worm with crabs who do not.; Use at least 20 crabs of each kind, at the same place and the same time of day.; Say beforehand what result would mean a trick, and what result would mean the crabs are only ill.
- **general:** Count how many crabs with the worm, and how many without, stand in the open at noon.; Watch whether shore birds take more crabs from the open sand than from near the burrows.; Check the time. Do crabs with young worms behave normally, and only those with full-grown worms stand in the open?

If the crabs with the worm are just as fast, they are not simply weak.; Put small clay model crabs on open sand and near burrows, and count peck marks, so that no real crab is risked.; Look at whether the change is exact. - **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the problem aloud. 2. Ask for three possible reasons why the crabs stand in the open. 3. Write each as a short line.

## Worksheets
### Hosts, parasites and the race between them (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is a parasite?  
   Options: A louse feeding on a scalp / A crow eating a grain of rice / A bee visiting a flower  
   **Answer:** A louse feeding on a scalp. *Why:* A louse lives on another living being and takes food from them. A crow eating rice and a bee at a flower do not live on or in a host.
2. (fill) The living being that a parasite lives on or in is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['host']. *Why:* The host gives the parasite food and a home. A person is the host of a head louse, and a snail is the host of a young fluke.
3. (truefalse) In an arms race between a host and a parasite, one side wins for good.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each defence is followed by a way round it, and then by a new defence. Neither side stays ahead, because both keep changing.
4. (match) Match each move to the other side's answer.  
   **Answer:** Hosts throw out strange eggs → Cuckoos lay eggs that look like the host's; The body makes antibodies to a coat → The parasite switches to a new coat; Doctors use one medicine → Resistant parasites survive and spread; Flukes fit the commonest snail → Rare kinds of snail take over. *Why:* In each pair the second line only makes sense as an answer to the first. That is what an arms race looks like: a move, and then a move that gets round it.
5. (mcq) Each fluke fits one kind of snail. Which kind of snail is infected most?  
   Options: The commonest kind / The rarest kind / All kinds equally  
   **Answer:** The commonest kind. *Why:* Flukes who fit the commonest snail find hosts most easily and have the most young. So most flukes come to fit the commonest snail. Choosing 'all kinds equally' treats the flukes as fixed, but they change with the snails.
6. (sort) Sort each behaviour by who gains from it.  
   **Answer:** A crow preening → Helps the host; A mantis with a hairworm walking into water → Helps the parasite; A lizard with an infection basking longer → Helps the host; A rat with Toxoplasma walking towards cat smell → Helps the parasite; Monkeys grooming each other → Helps the host; An ant with a fungus biting a leaf at midday → Helps the parasite. *Why:* Preening, basking and grooming remove or slow parasites. The mantis, the rat and the ant each do something that carries the parasite to the place or the host needed next.
7. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, for a body meeting a new parasite.  
   **Answer:** ['The parasite gets in', "One white blood cell's antibody fits the parasite", 'That cell divides into thousands of copies', 'Antibodies mark the parasites to be destroyed', 'Memory cells stay for years']. *Why:* The body does not design an antibody. It already has many kinds, and the one that fits is copied. Memory cells make the second meeting much faster.
8. (truefalse) A bite from a pet dog never needs a doctor.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Nobody can tell by looking whether a dog carries rabies. Wash any bite or scratch for 15 minutes, tell a grown-up and see a doctor the same day.
9. (fill) When parasites survive a medicine that used to kill their kind, this is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['resistance']. *Why:* The medicine kills the others, and the few who survive leave the young. After some years most parasites are of the surviving kind.
10. (mcq) A hairworm grows inside a mantis but must mate in water. What is the hairworm's problem?  
   Options: The worm cannot cross dry ground alone / The worm is afraid of the mantis / The mantis lives under water  
   **Answer:** The worm cannot cross dry ground alone. *Why:* A hairworm has no legs and would dry out on land. The mantis has legs. The mantis walking to water solves the worm's problem.
11. (write) A pond holds only one kind of snail, all clones. Explain why one kind of fluke could empty it.  
   **Answer:** Every snail has the same lock, so a fluke who fits one snail fits them all. That fluke has many young, and soon most snails are infected. There is no rare kind of snail left to carry on.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the snails are all alike, and that there is no rare kind for the flukes to miss.
12. (label) Label the four steps to take after a dog bite, in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Wash with soap and running water for 15 minutes', 'Tell a grown-up at once', 'See a doctor the same day', 'Take every dose of the vaccine']. *Why:* The virus travels slowly along the nerves, so there is time to stop the virus. Washing removes much of the virus, and the vaccine readies the body before the virus reaches the brain.
13. (draw) Draw a line graph of three kinds of snail over eight generations, when flukes chase the commonest kind.  
   **Answer:** Three lines that rise and fall in turn. When one line is highest it soon drops, and a line that was low rises. The lines cross at least once.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that no line stays on top. The commonest kind is infected most, so a rare kind takes over.
14. (perspective) Should people try to wipe out every kind of parasite?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer separates parasites who cause great human suffering, such as the Guinea worm and the malaria parasite, from the many who are part of lakes and forests.. *Why:* This question has no single right answer. What matters is a reason that uses evidence, and a fair reply to another view.
15. (count) A parasite doubles every day. On day 1 there is 1. How many are there on day 6?  
   **Answer:** 32. *Why:* The numbers go 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Doubling five times gives 32. This is why a body has only a few days to learn a new coat.
### Coats, medicines and parasites who steer (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why can most parasites change faster than their hosts?  
   Options: Parasites have new generations much more often / Parasites are cleverer than hosts / Hosts never change at all  
   **Answer:** Parasites have new generations much more often. *Why:* Change spreads from one generation to the next. A parasite may have a new generation in days and a host in years. Hosts do change, but more slowly.
2. (truefalse) An antibody made for one coat of a parasite grips every other coat too.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each antibody fits one shape. When the parasite switches coat, the body must start learning again.
3. (odd) Three of these are a host's defences. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Preening / Fever / Coat changing / Grooming  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Coat changing is a parasite's move against antibodies. Preening, fever and grooming are all things a host does against parasites.
4. (sort) Sort these claims by how strong the evidence is.  
   **Answer:** Starlings ate more infected pill bugs → Tested; Cats catch more infected rats outdoors → Not yet tested; Infected mantises walk towards light from water → Tested; A Guinea worm's blister sends people to water → Not yet tested; Birds ate more infected killifish → Tested; Dogs with rabies bite more than other dogs → Not yet tested. *Why:* A claim is tested when someone has compared infected and healthy hosts fairly and counted. The three untested claims fit what we know, but nobody has made the comparison.
5. (count) Birds took 2 of every 100 healthy fish. They took infected fish 10 times as often. How many of 100 infected fish?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* 2 times 10 is 20. In the real study birds took infected killifish 10 to 30 times as often, so the number could be as high as 60.
6. (mcq) A sneeze clears your nose and also spreads a cold virus. What is the fairest conclusion?  
   Options: It may serve both you and the virus / It serves only the virus / It serves only you  
   **Answer:** It may serve both you and the virus. *Why:* The sneeze does two things at once. Scientists still argue about how much of it is defence and how much helps the virus. One behaviour can serve two living beings.
7. (order) Put these in order, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['A medicine cures nearly everyone', 'A few parasites survive the medicine', 'Resistant parasites spread widely', 'Doctors give two medicines together']. *Why:* The medicine removes the ordinary parasites, so the few who survive leave the young. Two medicines together are much harder to get round.
8. (fill) The idea that living beings must keep changing just to stay as safe as before is the ____ idea.  
   **Answer:** ['Red Queen']. *Why:* It is named after the Red Queen in Through the Looking-Glass, who tells Alice that here you must run as fast as you can to stay in the same place.
9. (match) Match each parasite to what the parasite needs next.  
   **Answer:** Hairworm in a mantis → Water; Fungus in an ant → A leaf above the ants' path; Toxoplasma in a rat → The inside of a cat; Worm in a pill bug → The inside of a starling. *Why:* In each case the host's changed behaviour takes the parasite to exactly this place. That close fit is one sign of manipulation.
10. (truefalse) Scientists have found the zombie-ant fungus growing all through the ant's brain.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In 2017 the fungus was found around and inside the muscles, and was not found inside the brain. How the ant is steered is still unknown.
11. (write) Pill bugs with a worm inside moved about more than healthy ones. Why did that matter?  
   **Answer:** It showed that the infected pill bugs were not simply weak or tired. A weak animal moves less. Their behaviour had changed in a particular way that took them into the open.. *Why:* A strong answer says that more movement rules out the old idea that infected animals only behave oddly from weakness.
12. (write) Explain why stopping a medicine early helps resistant parasites.  
   **Answer:** The first tablets kill the parasites that are easiest to kill. The toughest ones are still alive. If the medicine stops, they multiply and are passed on.. *Why:* A strong answer says that an unfinished course removes the easy parasites and leaves the tough ones to have young.
13. (perspective) A mantis with a hairworm inside walks into a pond. Whose walk is it?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer uses the evidence: healthy mantises do not walk to water, the walk begins only when the worm is full grown, and the worm has no legs.. *Why:* This question has no single right answer. What matters is a reason that uses the evidence, and a fair reply to another view.
14. (label) Label the three parts of this picture of a body meeting a parasite.  
   **Answer:** ['Antibody', "The parasite's coat", 'Memory cell']. *Why:* An antibody grips the coat. A memory cell keeps the pattern, so that the same coat is answered within days next time.
15. (mcq) Which is true of a koel chick in a crow's nest?  
   Options: The koel chick grows up beside the crow chicks / The koel chick is fed by the koel mother / The koel chick builds a new nest  
   **Answer:** The koel chick grows up beside the crow chicks. *Why:* The koel mother lays the egg and leaves. The crows feed the koel chick together with their own chicks. The koel chick does not push the crow chicks out.
### Testing, arguing and working it out (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Animals with a parasite sit in the open. What must be shown before calling it the parasite's trick?  
   Options: More of them reach the parasite's next host / They look unusual / They are thinner than the others  
   **Answer:** More of them reach the parasite's next host. *Why:* A change is manipulation only if it carries more parasites onward. Looking unusual or being thin could be side effects of illness.
2. (mcq) In a hunting test with paper bugs, which round is the control?  
   Options: All bugs hide in the same way / Dotted bugs stand in the open / The hunter uses both hands  
   **Answer:** All bugs hide in the same way. *Why:* In the control nothing differs between the two groups, so it shows whether the dot alone matters. It did not. Only the place did.
3. (truefalse) If a claim fits everything we know, it has been tested.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Fitting is not testing. A claim is tested when someone compares and counts, and the result could have come out the other way.
4. (count) A pond has 12 clone snails. Two thirds are infected. Each healthy snail has one baby. How many snails are there now?  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Two thirds of 12 is 8 infected, so 4 are healthy. Each has one baby, and 4 plus 4 is 8. The pond has shrunk from 12 to 8 in one generation.
5. (count) A model parasite shows a new coat every 4 days and has 60 coats. How many days can the infection last?  
   **Answer:** 240. *Why:* 60 times 4 is 240 days, which is about 8 months. A real malaria parasite has about 60 genes for their marker protein, and infections can last many months.
6. (mcq) Why might any effect of Toxoplasma on people be a leftover?  
   Options: Cats almost never eat people / People cannot carry the parasite / The parasite mates in people  
   **Answer:** Cats almost never eat people. *Why:* The parasite gains when a rat is eaten by a cat. A person will not be eaten by a cat, so a change in a person carries the parasite nowhere. It may be left over from what works in rats.
7. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Clone → An exact copy of one parent; Control → The part of a test where nothing is changed; Final host → The host where a parasite has young; Side effect → A change that helps nobody in particular. *Why:* These four words are needed to talk about the arms race and about testing. A control and a side effect are the two that people most often forget.
8. (order) Put a hairworm's journey in order, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['A mantis eats an insect carrying a young worm', "The worm grows until the mantis's body is nearly full", 'The mantis walks to shining water', 'The worm leaves and mates in water']. *Why:* The mantis is drawn to water only once the worm is full grown. A change that begins exactly when the parasite is ready is one sign of manipulation.
9. (fill) Light that bounces off flat water has waves lying in one direction. It is called ____ light.  
   **Answer:** ['polarised', 'polarized']. *Why:* Mantises with a hairworm inside walked towards this light in a test in Japan in 2021. Healthy mantises did not.
10. (write) Rats with Toxoplasma lost their fear of cat smell but kept their other fears. Why does that matter?  
   **Answer:** If the rats were only ill, all their behaviour would be dulled. Losing one exact fear, the one that keeps them away from the parasite's final host, points to a particular change and not general illness.. *Why:* A strong answer says that an exact change is unlikely to be a mere side effect of feeling ill.
11. (write) Some snails sit on top of leaves in daylight. Plan a test of whether a parasite causes this.  
   **Answer:** Compare snails who carry the parasite with snails who do not, in the same place and at the same time. Count how many of each sit on top of leaves. Then count whether birds take more snails from the leaf tops. Say beforehand what result would mean a trick.. *Why:* A strong answer has a comparison with healthy snails, a count, and a check that the change helps the parasite reach the next host.
12. (draw) Draw a graph of parasites in the blood for a coat-changing parasite. Mark where the body has learned a coat.  
   **Answer:** A line that rises, drops sharply, rises again and drops again, in several waves. An arrow at each drop is labelled: the body has learned this coat.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows waves, with each fall caused by new antibodies and each rise caused by a parasite in a new coat.
13. (perspective) Hosts and parasites have changed each other for millions of years. Is this a war?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer notices that in a war one side hopes to end the other, and that in the pond the flukes die out if the snails do.. *Why:* This question has no single right answer. What matters is a reason that uses the evidence, and a fair reply to another view.
14. (truefalse) A field with several varieties usually loses less to one disease than a field of one variety.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A disease that fits one variety is stopped when the next plant is a different variety. It is the same reason that the mixed pond lasted longer than the clone pond.
15. (sort) Sort these host defences.  
   **Answer:** Antibodies → Inside the body; Preening → Something the host does; Memory cells → Inside the body; Sleeping under a net → Something the host does; Fever → Inside the body; Ants carrying their dead away → Something the host does. *Why:* Hosts defend themselves with their bodies and with their behaviour. Because behaviour is a defence, a parasite who can change behaviour gains a great deal.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which best describes an arms race between a host and a parasite?  
   Options: Each side keeps changing in answer to the other / The host changes and the parasite stays the same / The stronger side wins and it ends  
   **Answer:** Each side keeps changing in answer to the other. *Why:* Each defence is followed by a way round it. Choosing 'the stronger side wins' misses that neither side stays ahead, because both keep changing.
2. (mcq) A medicine against a parasite is used widely. After years it works less well. Why?  
   Options: Parasites who survive it left the young / The tablets became weaker / People's bodies got used to it  
   **Answer:** Parasites who survive it left the young. *Why:* The medicine removes the ordinary parasites. The few who can survive it multiply. The tablets and the people have not changed. The parasites have.
3. (mcq) Flukes each fit one kind of snail. One kind of snail becomes very common. What happens next?  
   Options: Flukes who fit that kind multiply, and it goes down / That kind stays on top for ever / The flukes die out  
   **Answer:** Flukes who fit that kind multiply, and it goes down. *Why:* Being common makes a snail kind the easiest host to find, so her flukes do best. Choosing 'stays on top for ever' treats the flukes as fixed.
4. (mcq) Why is a group of clones in more danger from a parasite than a mixed group?  
   Options: A parasite who fits one clone fits them all / Clones are always smaller / Clones cannot have young  
   **Answer:** A parasite who fits one clone fits them all. *Why:* Clones are copies, so they share the same locks. A mixed group always has some rare kind that few parasites fit.
5. (mcq) What does an antibody do?  
   Options: Grips one particular shape on a germ or parasite / Carries oxygen in the blood / Kills every microbe nearby  
   **Answer:** Grips one particular shape on a germ or parasite. *Why:* An antibody is a Y-shaped protein that fits one shape only. It marks the parasite for other cells to destroy. It does not fit every microbe.
6. (mcq) How does a coat-changing parasite stay in the blood for months?  
   Options: Some switch to a coat the body has not learned / They hide in the stomach / They stop multiplying  
   **Answer:** Some switch to a coat the body has not learned. *Why:* Antibodies clear one coat, but a few parasites have already switched to another. The body has to start learning again, so the numbers rise and fall in waves.
7. (mcq) A fluke must get from a fish into a bird. Which change in the fish helps the fluke?  
   Options: The fish flashes near the surface / The fish hides in deep weed / The fish swims faster from birds  
   **Answer:** The fish flashes near the surface. *Why:* A fish who flashes near the surface is easier for a bird to see and catch. Birds ate infected killifish 10 to 30 times as often as healthy ones.
8. (mcq) A mantis with a full-grown hairworm inside walks into water. Who needs the water?  
   Options: The hairworm, who mates in water / The mantis, who is thirsty / Neither of them  
   **Answer:** The hairworm, who mates in water. *Why:* Adult hairworms live and mate in water. Healthy mantises keep away from water. Choosing 'the mantis is thirsty' does not explain why only infected mantises do it.
9. (mcq) An ill goat lies still all day. What must be shown before calling this a parasite's trick?  
   Options: That lying still helps the parasite spread / That the goat has a parasite / That the goat looks unhappy  
   **Answer:** That lying still helps the parasite spread. *Why:* Lying still could be a side effect of being weak, or the goat saving strength. It is a trick only if the change carries more parasites to the next host.
10. (truefalse) Every odd behaviour of an infected animal is caused by the parasite to help the parasite.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Some odd behaviours are the host's own defence, such as a fever or basking in the sun. Some are side effects of illness. Each case has to be tested.
11. (mcq) Which of these claims do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Toxoplasma changes human personality / Infected rats lose their fear of cat smell / Birds eat more infected killifish  
   **Answer:** Toxoplasma changes human personality. *Why:* The rat and killifish findings were tested by comparing infected and healthy animals. Studies of people and Toxoplasma disagree with each other.
12. (mcq) A dog's tooth scratches your hand and breaks the skin. What do you do first?  
   Options: Wash with soap and running water for 15 minutes / Cover it with turmeric / Wait to see if the dog falls ill  
   **Answer:** Wash with soap and running water for 15 minutes. *Why:* Washing at once removes much of the virus. Then tell a grown-up and see a doctor the same day. Waiting is dangerous, because rabies cannot be cured once signs begin.
13. (truefalse) You can catch Toxoplasma by stroking a healthy cat's fur.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The parasite is passed in cat dung and gets into soil, sand and unwashed vegetables. Wash your hands after touching soil or a litter tray, and wash vegetables.

## Applications
- Malaria programmes give two medicines together and watch for places where a medicine has begun to work less well, so that the parasites cannot get round one medicine at a time.
- Farmers mix varieties, change crops from year to year and keep old varieties as seed, so that one disease cannot run through a whole district.
- Doctors prevent rabies after a bite by washing the wound and giving the vaccine, using the weeks that the virus needs to travel along the nerves.
- Vaccine makers study which parts of a parasite's surface cannot be changed easily, and aim vaccines at those parts.
- Scientists turned CRISPR, the defence that bacteria use against viruses, into a tool for changing genes in plants, animals and people.
- People who protect rare animals keep many unrelated families, because a small group of near-copies is easily wiped out by one disease.
- Health workers treat whole villages for worms at the same time, so that the worms have no untreated host to wait in.

## Open questions
- **How does the zombie-ant fungus steer an ant?** The fungus was found in the muscles and was not found inside the brain. Nobody knows whether the fungus works the muscles directly or sends chemicals to the brain.
- **Does Toxoplasma change how people behave?** Some studies find small differences in infected people and others find none. Infected and uninfected people differ in many other ways too, which are hard to separate.
- **Is the chase between hosts and parasites the main reason that most animals and plants have two parents?** It has been shown in some snails. But some clones have lasted a very long time, and other explanations fit other animals better.
- **What is it like to be a host whose behaviour a parasite has changed?** We can measure what a mantis does. We have no way yet to find out whether the pull towards water feels to the mantis like her own wish.
- **Do crows have any defence against koels?** Nobody has yet tested how often crows throw out strange eggs, and the 2021 study found no close copying of crow eggs by koels.
- **Did the mitochondria in our cells begin as parasites?** They were once free-living bacteria. Whether the first ones entered a larger cell as food, as guests or as parasites happened too long ago to see directly.
- **Can a medicine or vaccine be made that parasites cannot get round?** Every medicine so far has met resistance in the end. Scientists are looking for parts of a parasite that cannot change without killing the parasite.

## Connected maths
- In the clone pond of the Pond Race, about 2 of every 3 snails are infected each generation, and each healthy snail has one baby. So the pond is multiplied by two thirds each time. Starting from 12, after how many generations is there less than 1 snail? **After 7 generations.**. One third stay healthy and each has a baby, so the pond becomes two thirds of what it was. The numbers go 12, 8, about 5.3, about 3.6, about 2.4, about 1.6, about 1.1 and about 0.7. The eighth number is below 1, and it comes after 7 generations. A real pond of seeds jumps about, because the die decides, but it shrinks in the same way. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A fluke card has R in 4 boxes, C in 1 box and M in 1 box. A die picks the box. What is the chance that a rajma snail is infected? What is the chance for a chana snail? How many times as likely is the rajma snail to be infected? **4 out of 6, which is two thirds. 1 out of 6. Four times as likely.**. The die has 6 faces. Four of them point to R, so a rajma snail is infected on 4 rolls out of 6. One face points to C, so a chana snail is infected on 1 roll out of 6. 4 divided by 1 is 4. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- In a lagoon, birds ate infected killifish 10 to 30 times as often as healthy ones. Suppose birds take 2 of every 100 healthy fish. How many of 100 infected fish would they take at 10 times the rate, and at 30 times? **20 fish at 10 times the rate, and 60 fish at 30 times.**. 2 times 10 is 20. 2 times 30 is 60. So between a fifth and three fifths of the infected fish would be eaten, against 2 in 100 of the healthy ones. The 2 in 100 is made up for this problem. The 10 to 30 times comes from the real study. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the Starling Test, Kavya's hunter caught 9 of 10 dotted bugs in the open and 3 of 10 plain bugs in hiding. In the control round, with all bugs hidden, the hunter caught 3 dotted and 3 plain. Write the first two as percentages. What does the control round tell you? **90 percent and 30 percent. The control shows that the dot alone made no difference.**. 9 out of 10 is 90 out of 100, which is 90 percent. 3 out of 10 is 30 percent. In the control, dotted and plain bugs were caught equally. So in the first round the place where the bugs stood made the difference, and not the dot. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In Kenya, children carrying the mosquito stage of malaria drew about 10.2 mosquitoes each in a test. Children without the parasite drew about 5.3. About how many times as many is that? After medicine the numbers were about equal. What does that tell you? **About twice as many. The parasite caused the difference.**. 10.2 divided by 5.3 is about 1.9, which is close to 2. When medicine removed the parasites, the difference went away. So the extra pull on mosquitoes came from the parasite, and not from something else about those children. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A parasite doubles every day, starting from 1 on day 1. On which day does the number first pass 1,000? **On day 11.**. The numbers go 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024. The eleventh number is 1,024, which is 2 multiplied by itself ten times. Doubling starts slowly and then grows very fast. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- Here are made-up results from one Pond Race. Rajma snails: 8, 9, 6, 3, 2, 2, 2, 4. Chana snails: 2, 2, 4, 7, 9, 9, 8, 6. In which generation did chana first pass rajma? What had happened to the fluke card just before chana began to fall? **In generation 4. The card had turned to chana.**. In generation 3 rajma had 6 and chana had 4. In generation 4 rajma had 3 and chana had 7, so the lines crossed between them. Chana was then the commonest snail, so the chana fluke found the most hosts and took four boxes of the card. After that chana went from 9 down to 6. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
- A sleeping-sickness parasite has about 2,000 coat genes. A malaria parasite has about 60 genes for their marker. About how many times as many is 2,000 as 60? **About 33 times as many.**. 2,000 divided by 60 is 33 and a bit. Both parasites use one at a time. The parasite with more coats can keep ahead of the body for much longer. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is an arms race between a host and a parasite? → Each side keeps changing in answer to the other, and neither stays ahead.
- Flukes fit one kind of snail at a time. Which kind of snail is safest? → A rare kind, because few flukes fit a rare snail.
- Why is a group of clones easy for a parasite to wipe out? → A parasite who fits one of them fits them all.
- What does an antibody grip? → One particular shape on the coat of a germ or parasite.
- How does a coat-changing parasite escape antibodies? → A few switch to a coat that the body has not learned yet.
- Why does a hairworm need the mantis to walk into water? → The adult worm mates in water and cannot cross dry ground alone.
- Name one behaviour that is a host's own defence. → Preening, grooming, a fever or basking in the sun.
- What are the first two steps after a dog bite or scratch? → Wash with soap and running water for 15 minutes, and tell a grown-up at once.
- What must be shown before an odd behaviour is called a parasite's trick? → That the change carries more parasites to the next host.
