# Measuring the weather: thermometers, rain gauges and wind vanes
`sci.weather.weather.L3`

**Summary.** Hang a thermometer in the sun and it can read many degrees more than one in the shade, in the same air. A thermometer shows how warm the thermometer itself is, and sunlight heats it. So weather stations keep their thermometers in the shade, inside a white box with slatted sides. A rain gauge measures rain as a depth in millimetres. A wide pot and a narrow jar with straight sides catch the same depth of rain. A wind vane turns to point to where the wind is coming from. Write these readings down every day in a weather diary, and patterns appear: the warmest time of day, the wettest week, the usual wind.

**Intro.** *The radio says it is 34 degrees in Chennai today. The thermometer on Nila's sunny balcony says 45. Both thermometers work. So which one is telling the truth about the air?* Weather is what the air is doing right now: how warm it is, whether rain is falling, and which way the wind blows. To compare one day with another, or one town with another, people measure it. In this unit you measure temperature with a thermometer, rain with a rain gauge and wind with a wind vane. You find out why a thermometer must be kept in the shade. Then you keep your own weather diary, like the weather scientists of India.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can measure the temperature of the air with a thermometer, and explain why it must be kept in the shade.
1. I can run a fair test that compares a thermometer in the sun, in the shade and under a white paper roof, and say what my results show.

3. I can make a wind vane, and use it to say where the wind is coming from.
4. I can keep a weather diary for a week and find a pattern in it, such as the warmest time of day.

6. I can read a weather report: the highest and lowest temperature, the rain in millimetres and the wind.

## Terms
- **Weather**: What the air is doing at one place and time: how warm it is, whether it rains, how windy and how cloudy it is. *e.g.* This morning in Kochi the weather was warm, cloudy and still. By evening it was raining.
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold something is. We measure it in degrees Celsius. *e.g.* On a summer afternoon in Hyderabad, the air temperature can be 40 degrees Celsius.
- **Thermometer**: A tool that measures temperature. The red liquid in a glass thermometer takes up more space as it warms, so it climbs the tube. *e.g.* A weather thermometer hangs in the shade, so it measures the air.
- **Degree Celsius**: The unit for temperature. It is written °C. Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at about 100 °C. *e.g.* A comfortable room is about 25 °C. Your body is about 37 °C.
- **Shade temperature**: The temperature of the air, measured with the thermometer kept out of the sunlight. This is the temperature in a weather report. *e.g.* The weather report says 36 °C. That was measured in the shade at a weather station.
- **Weather screen**: A white box with slatted sides that holds thermometers. It keeps sunlight off them but lets the air flow through. It is also called a Stevenson screen. *e.g.* Weather stations all over India keep their thermometers in a weather screen on legs, above the grass.
- **Rain gauge**: A container that collects rain so that we can measure how much fell. *e.g.* A straight-sided plastic jar with a ruler beside it is a simple rain gauge.
- **Millimetre**: A small unit of length, written mm. Ten millimetres make one centimetre. Rain is measured in millimetres. *e.g.* After a heavy shower, the water in the rain gauge was 12 mm deep.
- **Rainfall**: How much rain fell, measured as the depth of water it would make on flat ground if none soaked in or ran away. *e.g.* Chennai gets most of its rainfall between October and December.
- **Wind**: Air that is moving. *e.g.* The wind makes the clothes on the line flap and the coconut leaves sway.
- **Wind direction**: The direction the wind is coming from. A wind that comes from the west is called a west wind. *e.g.* The southwest monsoon is named for where it comes from: the southwest, over the Arabian Sea.
- **Wind vane**: A tool that turns in the wind, so that its arrow points to where the wind is coming from. *e.g.* A wind vane on a rooftop points west when a west wind blows.
- **Anemometer**: A tool that measures how fast the wind blows. Most have three or four cups that spin faster when the wind is stronger. *e.g.* An anemometer on an airport tower tells pilots how strong the wind is.
- **Weather diary**: A notebook where you write the weather at the same times every day. *e.g.* In her weather diary, Kavya writes the temperature at 8 in the morning and 3 in the afternoon.
- **Weather station**: A place where weather is measured with standard tools, in a standard way, at set times. *e.g.* The weather station at Chennai airport, in Meenambakkam, measures the temperature, rain and wind every day.
- **Meteorologist**: A scientist who studies the weather. *e.g.* Anna Mani, a meteorologist from Kerala, helped India make its own weather instruments.
- **Maximum and minimum temperature**: The highest and the lowest temperature of the air in one day. They are often called the max and the min. *e.g.* Today in Madurai the maximum was 37 °C, in the afternoon. The minimum was 26 °C, just before sunrise.

## Explanations
### What we measure when we measure weather · *defined*
**Definition:** Weather is the state of the air at one place and time. The main things people measure are temperature, rain, wind direction, wind speed and cloud.

Words like 'hot' or 'very rainy' mean different things to different people. A child from Shimla and a child from Chennai would not agree on what 'cold' means. Numbers let everyone compare. Each measurement needs its own tool. A thermometer measures temperature. A rain gauge measures rain. A wind vane shows wind direction, and an anemometer measures wind speed. Clouds are still mostly judged by eye, by how much of the sky they cover.
- e.g. 'Very hot' in Delhi in May might be 45 °C. 'Very hot' in Bengaluru might be 36 °C.
- e.g. A weather report gives the maximum and minimum temperature, the rainfall in millimetres, and the wind.
### A thermometer shows its own temperature · *defined*
**Definition:** A thermometer can only show how warm the thermometer itself is. It shows the air's temperature only when the air is the only thing warming or cooling it.

In the shade, the thermometer gives heat to the air or takes heat from it until both are equally warm. Then its reading is the air's temperature. In sunlight, something else happens. The thermometer takes in sunlight, and that light warms it, on top of the air. So it climbs above the air's temperature. The air around it has not changed. Only the thermometer has. This is why a thermometer in the sun can read 5, 10 or even more degrees above one in the shade nearby.
- e.g. In the Sun or Shade test, a thermometer hung in the sun read 44 °C. In the shade, a metre away, it read 33 °C.
- e.g. Digital temperature signs at bus stops often stand in the sun. On sunny days they can show much higher numbers than the weather report.
### Why sunlight warms things more than it warms the air · *defined*
**Definition:** Clear air lets most sunlight pass straight through it. The ground, walls and other solid things take in much more sunlight, and they warm up first. The air is warmed mostly by touching the warm ground.

Air is see-through, so most sunlight goes through it without warming it much. When sunlight lands on a floor, a wall or a thermometer, much of it is taken in, and the thing gets warm. A dark thing takes in more light than a white or shiny thing, so it warms more. This is why a sunny wall or ledge can be too hot to touch while the air beside it feels only warm. A thermometer near a sunny wall is warmed by the wall too.
- e.g. At noon, a black car roof can be too hot to touch while the air beside it is only warm.
- e.g. A white paper roof over a thermometer takes in less sunlight than a black one would.
### The weather screen: a shaded house for thermometers · *defined*
**Definition:** A weather screen is a white box with slatted sides, on legs. It holds thermometers about 1.25 to 2 metres above short grass. It keeps sunlight off them and lets air flow through.

Every part of it has a reason. White takes in little sunlight, so the box itself stays cool. The slats let the air blow through, so the air inside is the same as the air outside. The legs keep it away from the ground, which gets hot in the day and cold at night. It stands in an open place, away from walls. The design was made in Scotland in the 1860s by Thomas Stevenson, a lighthouse engineer, so it is often called a Stevenson screen. Today almost every country follows the same rules, so a reading in Chennai can be fairly compared with one in Nairobi or Tokyo.
- e.g. Weather stations in India keep their thermometers in white screens on legs.
- e.g. Your white paper roof in the Sun or Shade test was a very simple weather screen.
### How a rain gauge measures rain · *defined*
**Definition:** A rain gauge catches rain, and we measure the depth of water in it in millimetres. That depth is the rainfall.

Rain falls over a whole area. A wide container catches more water than a narrow one, but it spreads that water over a bigger floor. So, if both have straight sides, the water rises to the same depth in both. That is why the width of a rain gauge does not matter, but its shape does. A container that is narrow at the bottom and wide at the top fills deeper than the true rainfall. Many steel tumblers are like that. Real rain gauges have a funnel on top that leads into a measuring tube, which makes small amounts easy to read. One millimetre of rain on one square metre of ground is one litre of water.
- e.g. In the Wide or Narrow test, a steel dabba and a straight glass under the same shower both filled to about 20 mm.
- e.g. If 10 mm of rain falls on a flat terrace of 50 square metres, that is 500 litres of water.
- e.g. An old Indian book, the Arthashastra, describes a bowl set up in front of a storehouse as a rain gauge.
### Which way the wind blows · *defined*
**Definition:** A wind is named for the direction it comes from. A wind vane turns until its arrow points into the wind, towards where the wind comes from.

A wind vane has a small arrowhead at one end and a big, flat tail at the other. The wind pushes harder on the big tail. So the vane swings round until the tail is blown away from the wind, downwind. Then the arrow points the other way, into the wind. If the arrow points west, the wind is coming from the west. In India, the big summer winds come from the southwest, over the sea, and bring the southwest monsoon. In Chennai, much of the year's rain comes in October to December, with winds from the northeast. This is called the northeast monsoon.
- e.g. Hold a hand fan to the east of a wind vane and wave it. The arrow turns to point east, at the fan.
- e.g. An airport windsock is the other way round. Its open end faces the wind, and the sock blows away from it.
### How strong is the wind? · *defined*
**Definition:** Wind speed is how fast the air is moving. An anemometer measures it. You can also judge it by eye, from what the wind does to things around you.

In a cup anemometer, the wind pushes the open side of each cup harder than the round back, so the cups spin. The faster they spin, the stronger the wind. Long before machines, sailors judged the wind from what they saw. In 1805, Francis Beaufort, an officer in the British navy, wrote down a scale from 0, calm, to 12, a hurricane. At first, each number was described by what the wind did to a ship's sails. Later, signs on land were added, such as smoke rising straight up or branches swaying. Weather offices still use a version of his scale.
- e.g. Calm: smoke rises straight up and leaves do not move.
- e.g. A strong breeze: big branches sway and it is hard to hold an umbrella.
### The warmest time of day · *defined*
**Definition:** On a clear day, the air is usually warmest in the middle of the afternoon, around 2 to 4 o'clock, not at noon. It is usually coolest just before sunrise.

The Sun is highest at about noon, but the air keeps warming after that. The ground and buildings keep taking in sunlight and passing warmth to the air. Until mid-afternoon, they take in more heat than they lose. After that, the Sun is lower and they lose more than they take in, so the air starts to cool. All night, heat keeps leaving, so the coolest time is just before the Sun comes back. Clouds change this. On a cloudy or rainy day, the temperature may hardly change at all.
- e.g. A shade thermometer in Madurai might read 28 °C at 7 in the morning, 35 °C at noon and 37 °C at 3 in the afternoon.
- e.g. A pot of water on the stove keeps getting hotter for a while even after you turn the flame down a little.
### India's weather watchers · *defined*
**Definition:** The India Meteorological Department, IMD, measures and reports the weather for the whole country. It was started in 1875.

Every day, hundreds of IMD weather stations measure temperature, rain and wind in the same way, at the same times. Many are now automatic weather stations, which send readings by themselves. For a long time, India bought its weather instruments from abroad. Anna Mani, a scientist born in Peermade in Kerala in 1918, changed that. She joined IMD in 1948 and led the work to design and make weather instruments in India. She also measured sunlight and wind across India, to find good places for solar and wind power.
- e.g. Chennai's weather reports come from two stations: Nungambakkam in the city and Meenambakkam at the airport.
- e.g. Thousands of rain gauges across India are read every morning, and the rain is added up for each district.
### The 'feels like' temperature · *idea*
Many weather apps show two numbers: the temperature, and what it 'feels like'. The first is measured with a thermometer in the shade. The second is worked out from the temperature, how damp the air is, and how windy it is. Damp air makes it harder for sweat to dry, so a humid 34 °C in Chennai can feel worse than a dry 38 °C in Jaipur. There is no single agreed way to work out the 'feels like' number. Different countries and apps use different formulas, so they can give different answers for the same weather.
- e.g. A weather app may say 33 °C, feels like 41 °C, on a sticky Chennai afternoon.
- e.g. A fan makes you feel cooler, but it does not change what a thermometer reads.

## Mostly agreed
- To measure the temperature of the air, a thermometer must be kept out of direct sunlight, with air flowing round it. *How we know:* A thermometer in the sun reads well above one in the shade nearby. Weather offices around the world follow the same rules from the World Meteorological Organization, so their readings can be compared.
- Rainfall is measured as a depth, in millimetres, in a container with straight sides or a funnel of known size. *How we know:* Straight-sided containers of different widths under the same rain fill to the same depth. Rain gauges have been compared side by side for centuries.
- A wind is named for the direction it comes from. *How we know:* This is the rule used by weather offices, sailors and pilots everywhere, so a 'west wind' means the same thing to everyone.
- On clear days, the air is usually warmest in the mid-afternoon and coolest just before sunrise. *How we know:* Hourly readings from weather stations show this pattern on most clear days, all over the world.

## How we found out
**Crown Prince Yi Hyang, who later became King Munjong, at the court of his father, King Sejong, in Joseon Korea, 1441, with rain gauges sent across the country soon after.** Nearly 600 years ago, in Korea, farmers needed rain for their rice, and the king's court kept records of the rain. To measure it, officials dug into the ground after the rain and looked at how deep the soil was wet. But this did not work well. Dry soil soaks up rain fast, while soil that is already wet takes in little more. Sandy soil and clay soil soak differently too. So the same rain gave different numbers in different places. The crown prince, who later became King Munjong, tried a better way. In 1441, he set a metal vessel in the palace courtyard and measured the depth of the rain in it with a ruler. A vessel soaks up nothing, so it shows only the rain. Soon after, the court sent rain gauges of the same size to towns all over the country. They were called cheugugi. Now the readings from different towns could be fairly compared. The wrong idea was that wet soil shows how much rain fell. The soil was also showing how dry it had been before.  
*What people believed before:* People measured rain by how deep it soaked into the soil. But the soil's own dryness, and the kind of soil, changed the answer. A metal vessel that soaks up nothing measured the rain alone.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A thermometer on a sunny wall tells you how hot the day is.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: It is a real thermometer, and it is outside in the weather.  
  Against: Sunlight heats the thermometer, and the hot wall heats it too. So it reads far above the air. The Sun or Shade test shows the difference in a few minutes.
- **A wider rain gauge measures more rain.** (Many children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: A wider container does catch more water.  
  Against: It also spreads that water over a wider floor. With straight sides, the depth is the same. Rain is measured as depth, not as how much water was caught.
- **A wind vane points where the wind is going.** (A common mix-up; *rejected*)  
  For: Many arrows point the way something moves.  
  Against: A wind vane's arrow points into the wind, to where it comes from. Wave a fan at a vane, and the arrow points at the fan.
- **Noon is the hottest time of the day, because the Sun is highest.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: The sunlight is strongest at noon.  
  Against: The ground and buildings keep warming the air for hours after noon. Shade readings through a day usually peak at about 2 to 4 in the afternoon.
- **My street is hotter than the weather report says.** (Many people living in big cities; *accepted*)  
  For: Roads, roofs and walls soak up sunlight and give off heat into the evening. Fewer trees means less shade.  
  Against: The report gives the temperature at one weather station, often at an airport with open ground. That is a fair reading of that place, not of every street.

## Roots
We have measured the temperature, the rain and the wind. Now we ask what measuring tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the sun, your thermometer read many degrees higher than in the shade, in the same air. Sunlight was heating the thermometer itself. A thermometer can only show how warm it is. So to measure the air, we shade it, so that only the air can warm or cool it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a measuring tool as a window that simply shows the world. But a thermometer is a thing in the world too. It takes part in what it measures, and its reading is what happened to it.  
*Evidence:* Under the white paper roof, in the same sunny spot, the reading fell back close to the shade reading. The air had not changed. Only the sunlight landing on the thermometer had changed.

- *who am i:* Your skin is a kind of thermometer too. In a sunny patch it feels hot, even when the air is the same as in the shade.
- *what is life:* Plants, birds and ants all sense the weather. A crow pants and sits in the shade on a hot afternoon, just as you would.
- *what is this world:* The same sunlight falls on the air, the ground and the walls. Each one takes in a different amount and warms in its own way.

On one balcony there is one Sun and one air. Yet your thermometer gave three numbers: one in the sun, one in the shade and one under paper. Each number was true. Each told what happened to the thermometer in that place. Each thing on the balcony meets the same sunlight and air in its own way. Many readings together, taken the same careful way, give a fuller picture than any one of them.

- **Udana 6.4, in the Buddhist Pali Canon: the blind men and the elephant:** Several blind men each touched one part of an elephant. One felt the trunk, one the leg, one the ear. Each was sure the elephant was what he had touched. (Each thermometer reading was true for its own place, like each man's touch. Weather offices put many readings, taken the same way, together to see the whole.)
- **Bhagavad Gita, chapter 13, opening verses:** The body is called the field. The one who knows the field is called the knower of the field. (A thermometer stands inside the field it measures. The sunlight on it is part of its reading, so we have to know where it stands to know what it is telling us.)
- Science (settled): A thermometer in direct sunlight can read far above the true air temperature. So the World Meteorological Organization asks for thermometers to be shaded and kept where air can flow round them.
- Science (settled): In physics, every measurement involves the measuring tool meeting the thing measured. For everyday things like air, the tool's effect can be made very small, but not zero.
- Other view, Many scientists and engineers: A thermometer in the sun is simply badly placed. Shade it properly, and it gives the air's temperature well enough for any weather use. There is no lesson about knowing here, only good and bad ways to set up a tool.
- Practice, Be the thermometer (3 min): Let's stand in a patch of sunlight by the window. Close your eyes. Hold out your arms. Feel the warmth on your skin for ten slow breaths. Now step into the shade. Keep your eyes closed. Feel your arms for ten more breaths. The air did not change. What changed?
- Ask: Your thermometer gave three numbers in one place. Can more than one of them be true?
- Ask: Your skin felt the sunlight, not only the air. Is your skin a good thermometer? Why, or why not?
- Ask: Is there anything we can measure without the measuring tool taking part?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your thermometer gave three different numbers in three places, all in the same air. Which number was the 'real' temperature? (hint: Think about what each number really measured: the air, the sunlight, or both.)
- *deep:* Can we ever measure something without changing it, or without the tool taking part? (hint: A cold thermometer put into a small cup of hot tea cools the tea a tiny bit. Is that true of every measurement?)
- *deep:* Why do we need numbers for weather at all? Isn't 'hot' good enough? (hint: Think of a farmer deciding when to sow, or a city deciding whether to store more water.)
- *big:* The air you breathe today was over another country last week. Whose weather is it? (hint: The southwest monsoon winds cross the Arabian Sea before they reach Kerala.)
- *big:* A crow, a neem tree and an ant feel the weather without any tools. What do they notice that we might miss? (hint: Watch how birds and ants behave before a rain, and on a very hot afternoon.)
- *big:* If every home kept a weather diary, what could a whole city learn? (hint: Some streets are hotter than others. Who would want to know that, and why?)

## Story: The Balcony That Said 45
*Nila, Arun, their mother and their flat are made up. The facts are real: a thermometer in the sun reads well above the air, weather reports give the shade temperature, and Chennai's readings come from stations at Nungambakkam and Meenambakkam.*

Nila was nine, and she had a new thermometer. It was a long glass one with red liquid inside, and it came in a cardboard tube. Her aunt in Madurai had sent it for her birthday, with a note: 'For the scientist of the family.'

Nila lived on the third floor of a block of flats in Mylapore, in Chennai. The flat had one small balcony, with a grill all round it and a clothesline across the top. In the mornings the balcony was in shade. After eleven o'clock, the sun moved round and poured onto it.

On the first Saturday of May, Nila tied her thermometer to the balcony grill with a piece of thread. Then she went inside to help her mother make lemon rice.

At one o'clock, the radio in the kitchen gave the weather. 'Chennai today. Nungambakkam, maximum 36 degrees Celsius. Meenambakkam, 37 degrees.'

Nila ran to the balcony. The sun was blazing on the grill. She bent close to the thermometer and read it carefully, with her eye level with the top of the red line. It said 45.

'Amma!' she shouted. 'The radio is wrong! It is 45 degrees, not 36!'

Her little brother Arun, who was six, came running too. He looked at the red line, and then at the radio. 'Maybe our balcony is just hotter than Nungambakkam,' he said.

Their mother came out with a ladle still in her hand. She read the thermometer. 'It does say 45,' she said. 'So, Nila, who is right? Your thermometer, or the weather office?'

'Mine,' said Nila. 'I can see it.'

'Then can you show me?' said Amma. 'Not by saying it. By testing it.'

Nila thought about it all through lunch. If the balcony was really 45 degrees, then the air everywhere on the balcony should be 45 degrees. But one corner of the balcony, behind the big pot of tulsi, was still in shade.

After lunch she untied the thermometer and tied it again in the shady corner, with the same thread. She waited, and she made Arun wait too, for five whole minutes. Then she read it. 35.

'It went down by ten!' said Arun. 'The corner is colder.'

Nila frowned. The two places were only a metre apart. The same air blew across the whole balcony. She could feel it on her arms. It did not feel ten degrees different. But her arms in the sunny part felt hot, as if the sunlight itself was pressing on them.

Then she had an idea. She took a sheet of white paper from her school bag and folded it in half, like a little roof. She tied the thermometer back in the sunny spot, and taped the paper roof to the grill just above it. Now the thermometer hung in the sun's place, in the sun's air, but in the paper's shadow.

Five minutes later, she read it. 36.

Arun stared. 'The paper made the air cold?'

'No,' said Nila slowly. 'The air is the same air. The paper only stopped the sunlight landing on the thermometer.' She looked at the glass tube. 'When the sun shone on it, the sunlight was heating the thermometer itself. It was telling me how hot the thermometer was.'

'So is it 45 or 36?' asked Arun.

'The air is about 36,' said Nila. 'The thermometer in the sun was 45. They are both true. They are just about different things.'

Amma smiled. 'And the weather office?'

'They must keep their thermometers in the shade,' said Nila. 'Otherwise every town would get a different number depending on where the sun was.'

That evening, her mother showed her a picture on her phone. It was a white wooden box with slatted sides, standing on legs on a patch of grass. 'This is a weather screen,' she said. 'Weather stations keep their thermometers inside one. The white keeps the sunlight off. The slats let the air through.'

'It's my paper roof,' said Nila, 'only better.'

The next morning, Nila made a new page in her notebook. At the top she wrote 'Weather Diary'. Underneath she drew a table: Date. Time. Temperature in the shade. Sky. Wind.

And next to the word 'Temperature' she added a small note to herself, in red pencil: 'Shade only.'

**For the parent:** A thermometer in the sun was heating up in the sunlight, so it showed its own temperature, not the air's. In the shade, the air was the only thing warming it, so it showed the air's temperature.

- Ask: Why did Nila's thermometer read 45 in the sun and 36 under the paper roof, in the same air?
- Ask: Arun thought the shady corner was colder. What did Nila do to test that idea?
- Ask: Why did Amma ask Nila to test it, instead of just telling her the answer?
- Ask: Where would you hang a thermometer in your home to measure the air?

### e1 · Sun or Shade: where does a thermometer tell the truth about the air? (38 min)
- **question:** Does a thermometer give the same reading in the sun, in the shade, and in the sun under a white paper roof? Which one shows the temperature of the air?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the thermometer read the same in all three places, or different? Which place will read highest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same thermometer, hung from the same thread, at the same height, in places less than two metres apart. Wait the same 5 minutes in each place before reading. Only the sunlight changes. If you can, do the whole test again the next day at the same time, because one day can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A glass thermometer breaks easily. Tie it firmly, and keep it away from the edge of the balcony. If it breaks, a grown-up sweeps up the glass. Never look at the Sun.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw your balcony or window from above, and mark the three places with their readings.
- **results table:** Place | My guess (°C) | Reading after 5 minutes (°C) | Reading 1 minute later (°C)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the thermometer. 2. Show the sunny spot and the shady spot. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason for each of the three places.  
  *Parent says:* We will hang the thermometer in three places: in the shade, in the sun, and in the sun under a paper roof. What will it read in each? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the sunny spot and the shady spot. Guess the reading in each place. Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Will the thermometer read the same everywhere on the balcony? (good: Any guess with a reason is good, for example 'It will be higher in the sun because the sun is hot.')
  - Note: Don't explain anything yet. Write down your child's three guesses. The best time is late morning or early afternoon on a sunny day, when part of the balcony or window is in full sun. No thermometer yet? A straw thermometer, a bottle of coloured water with a straw in it, shows the same change as a rising level, without degrees.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the white A4 sheet in half, like a roof. 2. Keep it ready with two pieces of tape. 3. Tie the thread round the top of the thermometer.  
  *Parent says:* This paper roof will make a shadow. We'll use it in the third test.  
  *Child mode:* Fold the white paper in half like a roof. Tie the thread to the thermometer.
  - Note: With a digital probe thermometer, hang the probe tip in the air, not touching anything. Keep the display box in the shade if you can.
- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie the thermometer to the grill in the shady spot. 2. It must hang in the air, touching nothing. 3. Wait 5 minutes. 4. Read it, then read again 1 minute later. 5. Write both readings.  
  *Parent says:* We read it twice, a minute apart. If both are the same, the thermometer has finished warming or cooling.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the thermometer in the shade. Don't let it touch the wall. Wait 5 minutes. Read it. Read it again after 1 minute.
  - Note: Read with your eye level with the top of the red line, not from above or below. If the second reading is still changing, wait another minute. Touching the wall or the grill would let them warm the thermometer, which is why it must hang free.
  - Check: Why do we read the thermometer twice, a minute apart? → **To check it has stopped changing**
- **s4 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Move the thermometer to the sunny spot, less than two metres away, at the same height. 2. Again it hangs in the air, touching nothing. 3. Wait 5 minutes. 4. Read it twice, a minute apart.  
  *Parent says:* Same thermometer, same thread, same height. Only the sunlight is different. What does it say now?  
  *Child mode:* Hang it in the sun now. Same height. Wait 5 minutes. Read it twice. Was your guess right?
  - Note: Expect a much higher reading, often 5 to 15 degrees above the shade. If it climbs near the top of its scale, take it out of the sun. On a cloudy day the difference is small: try again on a sunny day.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave the thermometer hanging in the sun. 2. Tape the paper roof to the grill just above it, so it hangs in the roof's shadow. 3. Leave both ends of the roof open for air. 4. Wait 5 minutes. 5. Read it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Now the thermometer is in the same sunny air, but the paper stops sunlight landing on it. What do you think it will read?  
  *Child mode:* Tape the paper roof above the thermometer. It is in the shadow now. Wait 5 minutes. Read it twice.
  - Note: The reading usually falls back to within a degree or three of the shade reading. It may stay a little higher, because the paper itself warms up in the sun.
  - Check: The thermometer in the sun read 44 °C. Under the paper roof, in the same spot, it read 35 °C. What did the paper roof change? → **It stopped sunlight landing on the thermometer**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open a weather app, or look at today's newspaper weather box. 2. Find the temperature for your town right now. 3. Compare it with your three readings.  
  *Parent says:* Weather stations measure the air in the shade. Which of our three readings is closest to the weather report?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the weather app. Which of your readings is closest to it?
  - Check: Which of your readings is likely to be closest to the weather report? → **The one in the shade, or under the paper roof**
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a thermometer in the sun reads too high, so to measure the air it must be in the shade. What I saw: it read 33 °C in the shade, 44 °C in the sun and 35 °C in the sun under the paper roof. Why: sunlight heats the thermometer itself, so in the sun it shows its own warmth, not the air's.
### e2 · Wide or Narrow: does a bigger rain gauge catch deeper rain? (25 min)
- **question:** If a wide container and a narrow container stand side by side in the same rain, which one fills deeper?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which will have the deeper water, the wide one or the narrow one, or will they be the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both containers have straight sides and stand side by side, the same distance from the middle of the shower. They get the same 'rain' for the same time. Then swap their places and do it again, because the spray may be heavier on one side.
- **safety:** Bathroom floors get slippery. Wear chappals and move slowly. Glass containers can break: use plastic or steel if you can.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the two containers side by side, with the water level in each.
- **results table:** Container | How wide (cm) | Depth, try 1 (mm) | Depth, try 2, swapped (mm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show both containers. 2. Check that each one is as wide at the top as at the bottom. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Rain will fall on both of these for one minute. Which one will have the deeper water? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the wide one and the narrow one. Which will fill deeper? Why?
  - Note: Many steel tumblers are narrower at the bottom than at the top. Those fill deeper than the true rain, so don't use them. Write down your child's guess before you start.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand both containers side by side on the bathroom floor. 2. Put them the same distance from the spot right under the shower. 3. Turn the shower on for exactly 1 minute. 4. Turn it off.  
  *Parent says:* This is our rain. Both containers stand in the same rain for the same minute.  
  *Child mode:* Put both side by side under the shower. Turn it on for 1 minute. Then turn it off.
  - Note: No shower? Hold a steel chalni high over both containers. Pour three mugs of water through it, moving it slowly back and forth over both. Afterwards, pour the water into the bucket and use it for mopping.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the ruler straight up inside each container, touching the bottom. 2. Read the depth of the water in millimetres. 3. Write both depths.  
  *Parent says:* Look at where your ruler's 0 is. Many rulers have a little gap before the 0, so we line up the 0 with the bottom.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the ruler in each one. Read how deep the water is, in millimetres. Write it down.
  - Check: The wide dabba and the narrow glass both have water about 20 mm deep. Which one caught more water? → **The wide dabba**
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty both containers into the bucket. 2. Swap their places. 3. Run the shower for 1 minute again. 4. Measure both depths again.  
  *Parent says:* Maybe the shower sprays more on one side. If we swap them and get the same answer, we can trust it.  
  *Child mode:* Empty them. Swap their places. Shower for 1 minute again. Measure again.
  - Note: The two depths are usually within a few millimetres of each other. A bigger difference usually means the spray is uneven, which is why we swap and repeat.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a wide and a narrow container fill to the same depth in the same rain, as long as both have straight sides. What I saw: the dabba and the glass both had about 20 mm of water, both times. Why: the wide one catches more rain but spreads it over a bigger floor, so the depth is the same. That is why rain is measured as a depth.
### e3 · When Is the Air Warmest? One day of readings (30 min)

  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.

What I saw: it was 28 °C at 7, 34 °C at noon and 36 °C at 2, then it went down to 31 °C by 7 in the evening. Why: the ground and walls keep warming the air after noon, until the Sun gets low.

## Applications
- Farmers decide when to sow and water using rainfall and temperature records.
- Airports measure the wind so that planes can take off and land into the wind.
- Cities warn people about heat waves using shade temperatures.
- Dams and water boards use rain gauge readings to plan how much water to store.
- Fishing boats check wind forecasts before going out to sea.
- Wind farms are built where years of wind records show strong, steady winds.
- Doctors and schools use temperature warnings to keep children safe on very hot days.
- Weather records from many years show whether a place is getting hotter or wetter.

## Open questions
- **How much hotter will Indian cities get in the coming years?** Scientists agree cities are warming, but the amount depends on how much the world warms and how cities are built, with or without trees and shade.
- **Why does the monsoon reach Kerala on different dates each year?** It depends on the oceans, the winds and the land all working together. Forecasts are getting better, but the exact date is still hard to predict.
- **How can we measure rain fairly in strong wind?** In strong wind, rain blows sideways and some misses the gauge, so gauges catch less than really fell. Scientists use shields and corrections, but no method is perfect.
- **What is the best way to work out how hot weather 'feels' to a person?** Different countries and apps use different formulas. Bodies differ too: a child, an old person and a labourer in the sun all feel the same weather differently.

A sudden drop like that often means clouds blocked the sunlight, so her sky drawing for that day is the place to check. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- A family's flat terrace in Chennai is 30 square metres. One millimetre of rain on one square metre is one litre. How many litres fall on the terrace in a 10 mm shower? **300 litres.**. 10 mm on one square metre is 10 litres. On 30 square metres it is 30 groups of 10 litres. 30 times 10 is 300 litres. That is about 15 big buckets. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the day of readings, the thermometer read 28 °C at 7 am and 36 °C at 2 pm. How many hours passed, and how many degrees did it rise? **7 hours, and 8 degrees.**. From 7 in the morning to 12 noon is 5 hours, and from noon to 2 pm is 2 more, so 7 hours. 36 minus 28 is 8 degrees. So the air warmed by a little over 1 degree each hour. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
