# Digestion: the journey of food from mouth to stomach to intestines
`sci.body.digestion.L4`

**Summary.** Bend down until your head is lower than your stomach, and swallow a bite of banana. The bite still reaches your stomach. Food does not fall down inside you. Rings of muscle squeeze it along a food tube that is about 8 metres long in a grown-up. On the way, teeth, acid, bile and other juices break the food into very small pieces. Only pieces that small can pass through the gut wall into the blood. Your child counts chews to find what makes a bite ready to swallow. They also shake oil with soap to see what bile does to fat.

**Intro.** *An astronaut on the space station floats, and so does her dinner. Nothing falls there. So how does the food she swallows reach her stomach?* Many people say that food falls into the stomach. In space nothing falls, yet astronauts eat three meals a day. In this unit you test the idea on your own body. You swallow a bite of banana with your head lower than your stomach. You count your chews to find what makes a bite ready to swallow. You lay out a food tube of wool across the floor. You also shake oil with soap to see how your body deals with fat.

## Terms
- **Digestion**: Breaking food down into pieces small enough to pass through the gut wall into the blood. *e.g.* The starch in rice is digested into sugar, and the sugar passes into your blood.
- **Food tube (gut)**: The one long tube that food travels through, from the mouth to the anus. Its proper name is the digestive tract. *e.g.* The food pipe, the stomach and the intestines are all parts of the food tube.
- **Saliva**: The watery liquid your mouth makes. It wets food and starts to digest starch. *e.g.* Your mouth fills with saliva when you smell hot sambar.
- **Bolus**: A soft, wet ball of chewed food that is ready to swallow. *e.g.* After you chew a bite of dosa, your tongue rolls it into a bolus.
- **Oesophagus (food pipe)**: The tube of muscle that carries food from the throat to the stomach. In a grown-up it is about 25 centimetres long. *e.g.* A swallowed bite takes less than 10 seconds to pass down the oesophagus.
- **Peristalsis**: A wave of squeezing that travels along the food tube. A ring of muscle tightens behind the food and pushes it forward. *e.g.* Peristalsis carries water to your stomach even when your head is down.
- **Stomach**: A stretchy bag of muscle below the ribs on the left. It stores food, churns it and mixes it with acid. *e.g.* After a full meal a grown-up's stomach holds about 1 litre.
- **Enzyme**: A chemical made by living cells that speeds up one kind of change. A digestive enzyme cuts one kind of food molecule into smaller pieces. *e.g.* An enzyme in saliva cuts starch into sugar.
- **Chyme**: The thick liquid that food becomes after the stomach has churned it with acid. *e.g.* Idli and sambar leave the stomach as chyme, a little at a time.
- **Small intestine**: The long, narrow, coiled part of the food tube after the stomach. Most digestion happens here, and nearly all digested food is absorbed here. *e.g.* A grown-up's small intestine is about 6 metres long.
- **Bile**: A yellow-green liquid made by the liver. It breaks fat into tiny droplets. *e.g.* Bile flows into the small intestine after you eat something oily, such as a vada.
- **Villi**: Tiny finger-shaped bumps that cover the inside of the small intestine. Digested food passes through them into the blood. One of them is called a villus. *e.g.* Each villus is about 1 millimetre tall, and there are millions of them.
- **Absorb**: To take in. The gut wall absorbs digested food and water, and passes them to the blood. *e.g.* The small intestine absorbs sugar. The large intestine absorbs water.
- **Large intestine**: The wider, shorter last part of the food tube. It takes back water, and it is home to trillions of bacteria. *e.g.* A grown-up's large intestine is about 1.5 metres long.
- **Faeces**: The solid waste that leaves the body through the anus. It is also called stool or poo. *e.g.* Faeces are about three quarters water. Much of the rest is bacteria and fibre.

## Explanations
### What digestion is for · *defined*
**Definition:** Digestion means breaking food down into pieces small enough to pass through the gut wall into the blood.

Your body cannot use a piece of idli as it is. The piece is far too big to enter the blood. Food is made of molecules, which are tiny particles too small to see. Many food molecules are long chains. Starch is a chain of thousands of sugar units. A protein is a chain of units called amino acids. Fat is made of smaller units too. Digestion cuts these chains into single units, and only those units can cross the gut wall. The body breaks food in two ways. Teeth and muscles break it by force into smaller pieces. This is called mechanical digestion. Enzymes break it by chemistry into smaller molecules. This is called chemical digestion. An enzyme is a chemical made by cells that cuts one kind of molecule. Water, salt and vitamins are already small, so they need no digesting.
- e.g. Starch from rice is cut into sugar.
- e.g. Protein from dal or egg is cut into amino acids.
- e.g. Fat from groundnut oil is cut into smaller units called fatty acids.
### The mouth: teeth, saliva and the bolus · *defined*
**Definition:** In the mouth, teeth break food into small pieces and saliva wets it. The tongue rolls the wet pieces into a soft ball called a bolus.

Chewing does two jobs. The first job is breaking. Front teeth cut the bite, and back teeth grind it. Small pieces have more surface for the juices to work on. The second job is wetting. Three pairs of glands make saliva, about 1 litre every day. Saliva makes the pieces slippery and sticks them together. It also holds an enzyme called amylase, which begins to cut starch into sugar. A bite is ready to swallow only when the pieces are small and also wet enough to hold together. A dry biscuit breaks easily but needs many chews, because the mouth has to add water. A raw carrot is wet inside but needs many chews, because the pieces are hard to break.
- e.g. A bite of dry rusk needs many chews. A bite of rusk dipped in tea needs few.
- e.g. Chew plain cooked rice slowly, and after a minute it begins to taste a little sweet.
- e.g. Your mouth waters when you smell lemon pickle, before you eat any.
### Swallowing and peristalsis · *defined*
**Definition:** Peristalsis is a wave of squeezing that travels along the food tube. A ring of muscle tightens just behind the food and pushes it forward.

When you swallow, the tongue pushes the bolus to the back of the mouth. A small flap called the epiglottis folds over the top of the windpipe. This keeps food out of the lungs. For that moment you stop breathing. The bolus enters the oesophagus, the food pipe. In a grown-up the oesophagus is about 25 centimetres long. Its wall is muscle. A ring of muscle tightens behind the bolus, and the ring in front relaxes. The tightening moves along the tube as a wave. The bolus reaches the stomach in less than 10 seconds. Food is pushed, so the direction does not matter. You can swallow with your head down. Astronauts swallow in space, where nothing falls. A cow drinks with her head far below her shoulders. Peristalsis does not stop at the stomach. It moves food along the whole tube, to the very end.
- e.g. An astronaut eats a meal while floating.
- e.g. A cow with her head down at a trough swallows water up along her neck.
- e.g. A snake moves a whole egg along the body by squeezing behind it.
### The stomach: store, churn, add acid, let out slowly · *defined*
**Definition:** The stomach is a stretchy bag of muscle. It stores a meal and churns it with acid and enzymes. Then it lets the meal out into the small intestine a little at a time.

An empty stomach is about as big as a fist. After a full meal a grown-up's stomach stretches to hold about 1 litre. A ring of muscle at the top closes, so food does not go back up. The stomach wall has three layers of muscle, and it squeezes about three times a minute. Glands in the wall make gastric juice. This juice holds hydrochloric acid, which is about as strong as the acid in lemon juice or stronger. The acid kills many microbes that were swallowed with the food. Gastric juice also holds an enzyme called pepsin, which starts to cut proteins. After 2 to 4 hours the meal has become a thick liquid called chyme. A ring of muscle at the stomach's exit opens a little, again and again. Each time, a spoonful or so of chyme passes into the small intestine. The stomach does not digest its own wall, because the wall is coated with thick, slippery mucus. The cells of the lining are also replaced every few days.
- e.g. Your stomach feels tight after a wedding feast because its wall has stretched.
- e.g. A burp is swallowed air coming back up from the stomach.
- e.g. A stomach growls when its muscles squeeze gas and liquid along.
### The small intestine: where most digestion happens · *defined*
**Definition:** The small intestine is a narrow tube about 6 metres long in a grown-up. Most chemical digestion happens in it.

Many people think the stomach does most of the digesting. The small intestine does far more. It is called small because it is narrow, about 2.5 centimetres wide. The chyme from the stomach is sour with acid. In the first part of the small intestine, two juices are added through a small tube. One is bile. The liver makes bile, and a small bag called the gall bladder stores it. Bile is not an enzyme. It works as a detergent works on a greasy plate. It breaks large drops of fat into tiny droplets, so that enzymes can reach the fat. The other juice comes from the pancreas, an organ behind the stomach. Pancreatic juice holds enzymes for starch, protein and fat. It also holds a base, which cancels the stomach's acid. The wall of the small intestine adds enzymes of its own. Food never passes through the liver or the pancreas. They are helper organs, and they send their juices in. Food spends 3 to 5 hours in the small intestine.
- e.g. After a meal with ghee, the gall bladder squeezes bile into the small intestine.
- e.g. Starch that saliva did not finish is cut into sugar by pancreatic juice.
- e.g. A person whose stomach has been removed by surgeons can still digest food.
### Absorption: how digested food enters the blood · *defined*
**Definition:** To absorb means to take in. The wall of the small intestine absorbs digested food and passes it into the blood.

The inside of the small intestine is not smooth. It has folds, and the folds are covered with millions of tiny fingers called villi. Each villus is about 1 millimetre tall. The cells on each villus have even smaller bumps of their own. All this folding gives the lining a very large surface. A smooth tube of the same size would have about half a square metre. The real lining has about 30 square metres, as much as the floors of three small bedrooms. Inside every villus are tiny blood vessels. Sugars and amino acids pass through the wall of the villus into this blood. Fats pass into a nearby set of vessels called lymph vessels, and join the blood later. The blood from the intestine goes first to the liver. The liver stores some sugar, changes some molecules and breaks down many harmful ones. Then the blood carries the food to every cell of the body.
- e.g. Sugar from a banana can be in your blood within about 15 minutes.
- e.g. A child with loose motions is given a drink of sugar, salt and water. The sugar helps the gut absorb the salt and the water.
- e.g. A terry towel soaks up more water than a flat cloth, because its loops give it more surface.
### The large intestine: water, bacteria and faeces · *defined*
**Definition:** The large intestine is a wide tube about 1.5 metres long in a grown-up. It takes back water and salts, and it is home to trillions of bacteria.

What reaches the large intestine is watery. It holds fibre, which is the part of plants that human enzymes cannot cut. It also holds water, salts and worn-out cells from the gut lining. Each day the body pours about 7 litres of juices into the food tube. Nearly all of that water is absorbed again, most of it in the small intestine and the rest here. Tens of trillions of bacteria live in the large intestine. They feed on the fibre. They make some vitamins, such as vitamin K, and some gases. The leftovers move slowly and stay for 1 to 2 days. What remains is faeces. Faeces are about three quarters water. Much of the solid part is bacteria, and the rest is mostly fibre. The brown colour comes from bile. Faeces are stored in the last part, the rectum, and leave through the anus. This last step is one that you control.
- e.g. After eating plenty of vegetables and whole grains, faeces are soft and bulky, because fibre holds water.
- e.g. With loose motions, food moves too fast, so too little water is absorbed.
- e.g. Gas passed from the bottom is made mostly by gut bacteria.
### The whole journey: how far and how long · *defined*
**Definition:** The food tube of a grown-up is about 8 metres long from the top of the oesophagus to the anus. A meal usually takes between 1 and 3 days to pass through.

Add up the parts. The oesophagus is about 25 centimetres. The stomach is about 25 centimetres. The small intestine is about 6 metres. The large intestine is about 1.5 metres. Together they make about 8 metres. A child's food tube is shorter, because a child is smaller. These lengths differ from one person to another. They are also hard to measure in a living body, because the muscles of the tube keep it pulled short. The times differ too. A bite spends less than 10 seconds in the oesophagus. It spends 2 to 4 hours in the stomach and 3 to 5 hours in the small intestine. It spends 1 to 2 days in the large intestine. So the fastest part of the journey is the first, and the slowest is the last. Fatty meals leave the stomach more slowly than meals of rice or fruit.
- e.g. A skin of sweetcorn swallowed at dinner often appears in the faeces one or two days later.
- e.g. A drink of water leaves the stomach in minutes, but a meal with fried food stays for hours.
- e.g. The small intestine makes up about three quarters of the length of the tube.
### Who runs the food tube? · *defined*
**Definition:** The wall of the food tube holds a net of hundreds of millions of nerve cells. This net is called the enteric nervous system, and it runs digestion.

You decide when to swallow, and you decide when to go to the toilet. Everything between those two moments happens without your deciding. In 1899 two English scientists, William Bayliss and Ernest Starling, studied the intestine of a dog. They found that it squeezes behind a lump of food and relaxes in front of it. It went on doing this after its nerves to the brain were cut. So the intestine senses the food and moves it by its own nerve net. This net is sometimes called the second brain. The gut and the brain also send messages to each other all day. Most travel along the vagus nerve. That is how you feel hungry, full or sick. It is also why a fright before an exam can upset your stomach. The gut sends chemical messages too. In 1902 Bayliss and Starling found that the small intestine releases a chemical into the blood when acid touches it. The chemical tells the pancreas to pour out juice. Starling later named such messengers hormones.
- e.g. An empty stomach growls about every one and a half to two hours. Waves of squeezing are sweeping the leftovers along.
- e.g. Your mouth waters and your stomach starts making juice when you only smell food.
- e.g. You feel 'butterflies' in your belly before going on stage.
### Do the microbes in your gut affect how you feel? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that gut bacteria help digest fibre and make some vitamins. They disagree about how much gut bacteria affect mood and behaviour.

Tens of trillions of bacteria live in the large intestine. That is about as many as the cells of your own body. They make many chemicals, and some of these reach the nerves of the gut and the blood. Scientists are testing whether these chemicals change how animals, including humans, feel and act.
- e.g. Mice raised with no microbes at all behave differently from ordinary mice.
- e.g. Some people feel low when their gut is upset, and some feel sick when they are worried.
- **Scientists who study mice, such as Rochellys Diaz Heijtz and her team in Sweden in 2011:** Mice raised without any microbes moved about more and were less cautious than ordinary mice. Giving them microbes early in life changed this. So gut microbes can shape a growing brain, at least in mice.
- **Scientists who urge care:** A mouse with no microbes at all is very unlike any human. Studies in people are small and often disagree. We do not yet know whether changing gut bacteria changes a person's mood.

## Mostly agreed
- Digestion is chemical as well as mechanical. Juices change food, and grinding alone does not explain it. *How we know:* In 1752 René de Réaumur fed a kite small metal tubes with meat inside. The tubes could not be crushed, yet the meat came back partly dissolved.
- The stomach makes an acid juice that dissolves meat, and it churns food as it does so. *How we know:* From 1825 to 1833 William Beaumont watched digestion through a hole in the stomach of Alexis St. Martin. He took out gastric juice and saw it digest meat in a glass tube kept warm.
- Food is moved along the tube by waves of squeezing, and the gut's own nerves run them. *How we know:* In 1896 and 1897 Walter Cannon, then a medical student, used the newly found X-rays. He watched food move in living animals. In 1899 Bayliss and Starling showed the intestine still does this with its nerves to the brain cut.
- Most digestion and nearly all absorption of food happen in the small intestine. *How we know:* People whose stomachs have been removed can live and digest food. People who lose most of the small intestine cannot absorb enough, and need special feeding.
- The lining of the small intestine has a surface of about 30 square metres. *How we know:* In 2014 Herbert Helander and Lars Fändriks measured folds, villi and the bumps on cells under microscopes. Their figure is much smaller than the 'tennis court' that older books give.
- Sugar helps the small intestine absorb salt and water. *How we know:* In 1971 Dr Dilip Mahalanabis and his team worked in refugee camps near Bangaon in West Bengal. They gave people with cholera a drink of sugar and salt. Far fewer of them died than of those treated in the usual way.

## How we found out
**René de Réaumur in France, Lazzaro Spallanzani in Italy, and William Beaumont with Alexis St. Martin in North America, Réaumur's tests were in 1752. Spallanzani published in 1780. St. Martin was shot in 1822, and Beaumont's book came out in 1833..** For a long time, many doctors thought the stomach worked only by grinding, as a mill grinds grain. Others thought food was cooked or rotted there. In 1752 the French scientist René de Réaumur tested this with a kite. A kite is a bird of prey who brings up what she cannot digest. He put meat inside small metal tubes with open ends and let her swallow them. The tubes came back unharmed, so nothing had ground the meat. Yet the meat was partly dissolved. Some juice had done it. In Italy, Lazzaro Spallanzani repeated such tests on many animals. He even swallowed small linen bags of food himself. In 1822, at a trading post in North America, a young man named Alexis St. Martin was shot in the side. The wound healed with a hole that led into his stomach. From 1825 to 1833 the army doctor William Beaumont lowered pieces of food into the hole on silk threads. Later he pulled them out to look. He collected gastric juice and watched it digest meat in a warm glass tube. St. Martin was poor, and he did not always want to be tested.  
*What people believed before:* That the stomach digests only by grinding, or by cooking or rotting the food. Réaumur showed that meat dissolved inside a metal tube that nothing could grind. Beaumont showed that gastric juice digests meat outside the body.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Food falls down into the stomach, so you must sit up to swallow.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: We usually eat sitting up, and things do fall downwards.  
  Against: Food is pushed by peristalsis. People swallow with the head down, and astronauts swallow where nothing falls. Sitting up is still wise, because it makes choking less likely.
- **The stomach does most of the digesting.** (Many people, and the everyday meaning of the word 'stomach'.; *rejected*)  
  For: The stomach is where we feel a meal, and its acid is strong.  
  Against: The stomach mainly stores, churns and starts on protein. Most digestion and nearly all absorption happen in the small intestine.
- **Stomach ulcers come from worry and spicy food.** (Most doctors until the 1980s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Ulcer pain often gets worse with worry and with some foods.  
  Against: Barry Marshall and Robin Warren in Australia found a bacterium, Helicobacter pylori, in the stomachs of ulcer patients. In 1984 Marshall drank the bacteria and his stomach became inflamed. Most ulcers are caused by this bacterium or by some pain medicines. They won the Nobel Prize in 2005.
- **Swallowed chewing gum stays in the stomach for seven years.** (A common playground saying.; *rejected*)  
  For: Gum base is not digested by any human enzyme.  
  Against: Peristalsis moves gum along like any other thing that is not digested. It leaves in the faeces within a few days.
- **The appendix is a useless leftover.** (Many biology books of the last century.; *debated*)  
  For: People live well after the appendix is removed.  
  Against: In 2007 William Parker's team at Duke University proposed that the appendix is a safe store of helpful bacteria. After an illness empties the gut, the appendix can supply bacteria to fill the gut again. The idea is not yet proven.
- **The inside of the food tube is outside the body.** (Many biologists who study how bodies are built.; *debated*)  
  For: The tube is open at both ends. A swallowed seed can pass through without crossing any wall of the body.  
  Against: The gut is plainly an organ inside the belly. The body pours juices into it and controls what happens there. 'Outside' is true of the shape, but it can mislead.

## Roots
In this unit you followed a bite of food along a tube about 8 metres long. Here we ask where the food stops being food and starts being you.

**Deep truth.** In the Sweetcorn Clock, the skins of the corn came out looking as they did when they went in. They travelled the whole food tube and never passed through its wall. The food tube is one long passage, open at the mouth and at the anus. So food you have swallowed is still outside your body until it crosses the gut wall.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say food is 'inside us' as soon as we swallow it, because it is under the skin. The food tube is a passage through the body. Only what crosses its wall enters the blood and the cells.  
*Evidence:* In the Sweetcorn Clock, the skins pass through whole in 1 to 3 days. In the Food Tube on the Floor, the wool is one unbroken line with two open ends. Digestion exists because whole food cannot cross the gut wall, and only small molecules can.

- *who am i:* Your body is built from what crossed your gut wall. The sugar in your muscles this evening was part of a banana or a bowl of rice this morning.
- *what is life:* Every animal, including you, takes other living things apart and builds a body from the pieces. A cow, a snake and a child all do this along a tube of muscle.
- *what is this world:* Rice, dal and fish are cut into the same few kinds of small molecules. One set of building units is shared by everything that eats and everything that is eaten.

A bite of idli is rice and urad dal, and those were sunlight, water, air and soil. In your small intestine the idli is cut into sugars and amino acids. They cross the wall of a villus, and after that they are your blood, your muscle and your warmth. Many sages taught that the eater and the eaten are one. The science here says something close and plain. Your body and your food are built from the same kinds of molecules. That is why one can become the other.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.5.1:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu that food, when eaten, divides into three. The coarsest part becomes faeces, the middle part becomes flesh, and the finest part becomes mind. (This is an old account of the same journey: part of a meal leaves, and part becomes the body. Today we would say that what is absorbed also feeds the brain. The text's 'three parts' are a teaching, and not a measurement.)
- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.10.6:** The verse ends in a song: 'I am food, I am food, I am food. I am the eater of food.' (Everything you digest was alive, and your own body will feed other living things one day. The verse says in a song what a food chain says in a diagram. The Upanishad means it as an insight about the self, which science does not test.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, 'interbeing', in The Heart of Understanding (1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the forest there. Nothing exists by itself alone. (Look at your hand in the same way. Your hand was built from what crossed your gut wall. So the idli, the paddy field and the rain are all in your hand.)
- Science (settled): The lining of your small intestine is worn away and replaced every few days. The new cells are built from the food that the old cells absorbed.
- Science (settled): All living things use the same kinds of sugars, fats and amino acids, because all share one ancestor. That is why almost any living thing can be food for some other.
- Science (debated): Whether the bacteria in the gut count as part of 'you' is argued. Some biologists treat a human and their microbes as one unit, called a holobiont. Others say the microbes are separate beings who live with us.
- Other view, Many doctors, and people who study anatomy: Calling the inside of the gut 'outside the body' is only a way of speaking about shape. The gut is an organ of yours. Your body pours 7 litres of its own juices into it each day and controls what happens there. On this view a swallowed bite is inside you from the moment you swallow. Digestion is your body working on what it already holds.
- Practice, One slow bite (3 min): Take one small bite of fruit. Chew slowly and notice the pieces getting smaller and wetter. Notice the moment when the bite feels ready. Now swallow, and follow the bite down for as long as you can feel it. Put your hand on your belly. The work goes on there for a day or more. Sit quietly for five breaths.
- Ask: In the Sweetcorn Clock, the corn skins travelled through you and never crossed the gut wall. Were they ever inside you?
- Ask: The sugar from this morning's breakfast is now in your blood. Is it still breakfast, or is it you?
- Ask: You chose when to swallow. After that, your gut did the work for a day. Who was doing it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A swallowed sweetcorn skin passes through you and comes out unchanged. Was the skin ever inside you? (hint: Think about a marble rolling through a pipe. Is the marble inside the pipe's wall?)
- *deep:* At what moment does a bite of banana stop being banana and start being you? (hint: Is it when you swallow, when the sugar crosses the gut wall, or when a cell uses it?)
- *deep:* Your gut runs most of digestion without your deciding. Who is doing that work? (hint: The gut has hundreds of millions of nerve cells of its own. Are they part of 'you'?)
- *big:* Tens of trillions of bacteria live in your large intestine and eat with you. Is a meal eaten by one being or by many? (hint: They digest fibre that your own enzymes cannot cut, and they make vitamins you use.)
- *big:* A cow, a snake and you all move food by peristalsis. Why might such different animals share one way of doing it? (hint: Think about what animals had in common long ago, before there were cows, snakes or humans.)
- *big:* Alexis St. Martin did not choose to have a hole in his stomach. Who should get the credit for what was learned through it? (hint: Dr Beaumont did the tests and wrote the book. St. Martin's body made them possible, and he bore the pain.)

## Story: The Lump That Climbed
*Kayal, Senthil, Paati and the cow Lakshmi are made up. These facts are real: on Mattu Pongal in Tamil Nadu, families bathe their cattle and paint their horns. A cow swallows grass quickly and later brings it back up, a mouthful at a time, to chew again. That mouthful is called the cud, and you can see it travel up the left side of her neck. Cows drink with their heads down. The stalk of a papaya leaf is hollow, and children do use it as a straw.*

On the morning of Mattu Pongal, Kayal's cousin Senthil said something that sounded right. Kayal was sure it was wrong, but she could not show it.

They were eating pongal on the thinnai, the raised ledge at the front of Paati's house near Melur. Kayal had leaned back against a pillar with her banana leaf on her lap.

"Sit up straight," said Senthil. He was eleven, and he liked to know things. "Food falls into your stomach like a stone into a well. If you lean back, it gets stuck in your neck."

Kayal swallowed. "Nothing got stuck," she said.

"That is because your neck is still higher than your stomach. Try it with your head lower than your stomach. You cannot do it. Nobody can. If you can, I will give you my piece of sugarcane."

Kayal wanted the sugarcane. But she had never swallowed anything with her head down, and she did not like to lose. "By this evening," she said.

"Done," said Senthil.

In the yard, Paati was painting the horns of Lakshmi the cow, one blue and one yellow. Lakshmi lay under the neem tree with her legs folded, chewing. She had not taken a bite of anything since sunrise. Yet her jaw went round and round.

"Paati, what is she chewing? Her mouth was empty a minute ago."

"Watch her neck," said Paati. "The left side. Do not blink."

Lakshmi swallowed, and her jaw stopped. For a moment nothing happened. Then a lump as big as a lemon appeared low on her neck, near her chest. The lump travelled up under the skin, all the way to her jaw. Her cheek filled, and she began to chew again.

"It went up," said Kayal.

"Every cow does this," said Paati. "She swallows her grass quickly in the morning. Later she brings it back, one mouthful at a time, and chews it properly. That mouthful is called the cud."

"But how does it come up? Nothing falls upwards."

Paati put down her brush. She pulled Thatha's old shirt off the clothes line and took a lemon from the plate beside the paint. She dropped the lemon into one sleeve at the shoulder, and held the sleeve out level.

"I will not tip it," she said. She closed her fingers around the sleeve just behind the lemon, and squeezed. The lemon slid forward. She squeezed behind it again, and again, and the lemon dropped out at the cuff. Then she put it back in at the cuff and squeezed from that side. The lemon went back the way it had come.

"A cow's food pipe is a tube of muscle," said Paati. "So is yours. A ring of the tube squeezes just behind the food. Then the next ring squeezes, and then the next. The food goes where the squeezing sends it. Down, up or along."

Kayal looked across at Senthil, who was tying bells to a calf's rope. She said nothing yet. She wanted to be sure.

In the afternoon her uncle led Lakshmi to the stone trough beside the well. The trough was low, almost at the ground. Lakshmi spread her front legs a little and put her head right down. Her mouth was far below her shoulders.

Kayal crouched and watched the neck. One gulp after another ran along it, from the mouth up towards the chest. The water was climbing.

Now she was sure.

She cut a leaf stalk from the papaya tree behind the kitchen. The stalk was hollow and as long as her forearm. She filled a steel tumbler with thin buttermilk and set it on the ground below the thinnai. Then she called Senthil.

She lay on her stomach on the thinnai, with her head and shoulders hanging over the edge. Her plait fell forward and touched the ground. Her head was lower than her stomach, and anyone could see it.

She put the stalk into the tumbler, sucked, and swallowed.

She felt the cool buttermilk leave the back of her tongue. She felt it travel along her throat and into her chest. She took three more swallows. Then she sat up, red in the face.

"That is a trick," said Senthil. "The straw did it."

"The straw only brings it to my mouth. After that I swallow, the same as Lakshmi." She held out the stalk. "You try."

Senthil lay down and hung his head over the edge. He sucked, and he swallowed. He stayed there a moment, very still.

"It went up," he said.

"It was squeezed up," said Kayal. "The tube does it. Ask Lakshmi."

He gave her the sugarcane without a word. She broke it across her knee and handed him the smaller half.

They sat on the thinnai and chewed. Neither of them spoke. Each was paying attention to the same thing: the moment at the back of the tongue when you let go, and something else carries the food on from there.

**For the parent:** Senthil was sure that food falls into the stomach. Kayal saw a lump of cud climb a cow's neck, and saw water climb the same way. Food is pushed along by rings of muscle, so it can travel in any direction.

- Ask: Nothing was lifting the cud. So why could the cud travel up the cow's neck?
- Ask: Senthil said the straw did the work. What did the straw do, and what did the food pipe do?
- Ask: After you swallow, can you stop the bite halfway down? What does that tell you?

### e1 · Dry or Wet: what makes a bite ready to swallow? (25 min)
- **question:** How many chews does a bite need before you can swallow it? Does dipping the bite in water first change the number? Is the answer the same for a biscuit and for a raw carrot?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which of the four bites will need the most chews, and which the fewest? Will dipping in water help the biscuit, the carrot, both or neither? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use half a plain biscuit for every biscuit bite. Cut every carrot piece to the same size, about as big as the top joint of your thumb. Dip for the same time, a slow count of three. Chew in your usual way, and stop counting when the bite feels ready to swallow. Take a sip of water and wait half a minute between bites. Then your mouth starts the same each time. Do every kind of bite twice. Only two things change: the food, and whether it was dipped.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the carrot. Sit up straight while you chew, and do not talk or laugh with food in your mouth. Do not use nuts, in case of an allergy.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows: dry biscuit, dipped biscuit, dry carrot and dipped carrot. Write the number of chews for try 1 and try 2 in each row.
- **results table:** The bite | Chews, try 1 | Chews, try 2
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the biscuits, the carrot and the bowl of water on the table. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will count the chews for four kinds of bite. They are a dry biscuit, a dipped biscuit, a dry carrot and a dipped carrot. Which will need the most chews? Will dipping help both foods? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the four bites. Which needs the most chews? Will dipping help? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think dipping will or will not help? (good: Water makes the biscuit soft.; A carrot is hard, so water will not change it.; A wet bite slides down more easily.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* I will cut the carrot. All four pieces must be the same size. Why does the size matter?  
  *Child mode:* Break the biscuits in half. A grown-up cuts the carrot. Draw the table.
  - Note: A bigger piece needs more chews, so equal pieces keep the test fair.
- **s3 (6 min)**

  *Child mode:* Chew the dry biscuit and count. Sip water. Then chew the dipped biscuit and count.

  - Check: In Dry or Wet, why do you sip water and wait between bites? → **So my mouth starts the same each time**
- **s4 (6 min)**

  *Child mode:* Chew the dry carrot and count. Then the dipped carrot. Do both twice.

### e2 · The Oil and Soap Bottles: what does bile do to fat? (25 min)

  *Parent does:* At 5 minutes after shaking, measure the oil layers again without moving the bottles. Try the headline again. Record the results.  
  *Parent says:* Five minutes have passed. Measure the oil on top of each bottle. Can you read the headline through A? Through B? Through C?  
  *Child mode:* Five minutes have passed. Measure the oil layers. Try to read the headline.
  - Wait: Wait until 5 minutes after shaking

  *Parent says:* Fifteen minutes now. This is the last measurement. Which bottle changed the most since the start? Which changed the least?

  - Note: Use the waiting time for the term cards or the story.
  - Wait: Wait until 15 minutes after shaking

  - Ask: An enzyme can only work on the outside of a drop of fat. Why would many tiny drops be digested faster than one big drop? (good: Tiny drops have much more outside in total.; The enzyme can reach more of the fat at once.)

### a3 · Squeeze It Along: a sleeve for a food pipe (10 min)
- **record:** Write how many squeezes moved the lemon 25 centimetres on the level, and how many when the sleeve pointed uphill.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the lemon in the sleeve at the shoulder end. Hold the sleeve level. Ask your child to move the lemon to the cuff without tipping the sleeve and without touching the lemon itself.  
  *Parent says:* The sleeve is a food pipe, and the lemon is a bite of food. Move the lemon to the other end. You may not tip the sleeve or hold the lemon. Where will you squeeze?  
  *Child mode:* Put the lemon in the sleeve. Move it along without tipping. Where do you squeeze?
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child makes a ring with thumb and fingers just behind the lemon and squeezes, hand after hand. Count the squeezes for 25 centimetres. Then they try squeezing in front of the lemon.  
  *Parent says:* Squeeze just behind the lemon, and then behind it again. Count your squeezes for 25 centimetres. Now squeeze in front of the lemon instead. Which way does it go?  
  *Child mode:* Squeeze behind the lemon. Count the squeezes. Now squeeze in front. What happens?
  - Check: In peristalsis, where does the food pipe squeeze to move food forward? → **Just behind the food**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the cuff end high, so that the sleeve points uphill. Your child squeezes the lemon up to the cuff. Then talk about the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Now the sleeve points uphill. Can you still move the lemon to the top? What does this tell you about the Head-Down Swallow, when your bite of banana went up to your stomach?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the sleeve uphill. Squeeze the lemon to the top. Does it still work?
  - Ask: Your hand moved along the sleeve. What moves along a real food pipe? (good: The squeezing moves, as a wave.; One ring of muscle tightens, then the next ring, then the next.)
  - Note: In the real food pipe nothing travels along the outside. Each ring of muscle in the wall tightens in turn, and the ring ahead relaxes. In a grown-up the wave takes less than 10 seconds to cover 25 centimetres.

## Worksheets
### The journey of a bite (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is digestion for?  
   Options: Breaking food small enough to enter the blood / Keeping food warm inside the belly / Storing food until the next meal / Turning food into air  
   **Answer:** Breaking food small enough to enter the blood. *Why:* Food pieces are far too big to pass through the gut wall. Digestion cuts them into small molecules that can cross into the blood.
2. (order) Put these parts in the order that food passes through them.  
   **Answer:** ['Mouth', 'Oesophagus', 'Stomach', 'Small intestine', 'Large intestine']. *Why:* Food goes from the mouth down the oesophagus to the stomach. Then it passes through the small intestine and last the large intestine.
3. (fill) The wave of squeezing that pushes food along the food tube is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['peristalsis']. *Why:* In peristalsis a ring of muscle tightens just behind the food, and the tightening travels along the tube.
4. (truefalse) Food reaches the stomach only because it falls downwards.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Rings of muscle push the food. That is why you can swallow with your head down, and why astronauts can eat in space.
5. (match) Match each part to its main job.  
   **Answer:** Teeth → Break food into small pieces; Stomach → Churns food with acid; Small intestine → Absorbs digested food; Large intestine → Takes back water. *Why:* Each part does a different job. Breaking comes first, absorbing food comes in the small intestine, and taking back water comes last.
6. (mcq) Which liquid in the mouth wets food and starts to digest starch?  
   Options: Saliva / Bile / Stomach acid / Blood  
   **Answer:** Saliva. *Why:* Saliva makes food slippery and holds an enzyme that cuts starch into sugar. Bile and stomach acid are added further along.
7. (sort) Sort these. Does food pass through each one, or does it only send in a juice?  
   **Answer:** stomach → Food passes through; small intestine → Food passes through; oesophagus → Food passes through; liver → Only sends in a juice; pancreas → Only sends in a juice. *Why:* The liver and the pancreas are helper organs. They send bile and pancreatic juice into the small intestine, but food never enters them.
8. (truefalse) The small intestine is called small because it is short.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It is the longest part, about 6 metres in a grown-up. It is called small because it is narrow.
9. (mcq) What do villi do?  
   Options: Absorb digested food into the blood / Grind food into pieces / Make stomach acid / Push food back up  
   **Answer:** Absorb digested food into the blood. *Why:* Villi are tiny fingers on the lining of the small intestine. Digested food passes through them into tiny blood vessels inside.
10. (fill) The yellow-green liquid made by the liver, which breaks fat into tiny droplets, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['bile']. *Why:* Bile is stored in the gall bladder and flows into the small intestine. It works on fat as dish soap works on oil.
11. (write) A dry biscuit needs many chews. A dipped biscuit needs few. Explain why in two sentences.  
   **Answer:** A bite can be swallowed only when the pieces are small and wet enough to stick together. A dry biscuit breaks easily, but the mouth must add saliva to wet it. Dipping adds the water at once, so fewer chews are needed.. *Why:* A strong answer names both jobs of chewing, breaking and wetting. It says that the dry biscuit was waiting for wetting.
12. (write) Explain how a bite of banana can reach your stomach when your head is lower than your stomach.  
   **Answer:** The oesophagus is a tube of muscle. A ring of muscle tightens behind the bite and pushes it forward, and the squeezing moves along the tube. A push works in any direction, so the bite can travel upwards.. *Why:* A strong answer says that food is pushed by muscle. A push does not depend on which way is down.
13. (draw) Draw the food tube inside an outline of a body. Show the oesophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine.  
   **Answer:** One continuous tube. A straight oesophagus runs from the throat to a bag-shaped stomach under the ribs on the left. A long coiled small intestine fills the middle of the belly. A wider large intestine runs around the coils and ends at the bottom.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows one unbroken tube, with the small intestine coiled and much longer than the rest.
14. (perspective) Is food in your stomach inside your body?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that a sweetcorn skin passes through without crossing the gut wall. Another is that the body pours its own juices into the tube and controls what happens there.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses a fact about the food tube. Two such facts are its open ends and the wall that food must cross.
15. (mcq) What are faeces mostly made of?  
   Options: Water / Undigested rice / Stomach acid / Blood  
   **Answer:** Water. *Why:* Faeces are about three quarters water. Much of the solid part is bacteria, and the rest is mostly fibre.
### Why each part works as it does (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Dipping a raw carrot piece in water hardly changes its chew count. Why?  
   Options: The carrot needs breaking, not wetting / Water makes carrots harder / Carrots contain no water / The teeth were tired  
   **Answer:** The carrot needs breaking, not wetting. *Why:* A carrot is mostly water already. Its chews are spent breaking hard pieces, and a dip in water does not break anything.
2. (mcq) Why does the stomach let chyme out a little at a time?  
   Options: So the small intestine can deal with it / Because the stomach is too weak / To keep the stomach warm / So that food can go back up  
   **Answer:** So the small intestine can deal with it. *Why:* The small intestine must cancel the acid, add bile and enzymes, and absorb the food. It can only handle a small amount at once.
3. (mcq) Why does the stomach not digest its own wall?  
   Options: Thick mucus coats the wall / The acid is very weak / The wall is made of bone / It has no enzymes  
   **Answer:** Thick mucus coats the wall. *Why:* A layer of slippery mucus keeps the acid and pepsin off the wall. The cells of the lining are also replaced every few days.
4. (mcq) Blood from the small intestine goes to one organ first. Which one?  
   Options: The liver / The lungs / The brain / The kidneys  
   **Answer:** The liver. *Why:* The liver receives the absorbed food first. It stores some sugar and breaks down many harmful molecules before the blood goes on.
5. (truefalse) Bile is an enzyme that digests fat.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bile only breaks fat into tiny droplets. Enzymes from the pancreas then digest the droplets.
6. (truefalse) A person can live without a stomach.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Surgeons sometimes remove a stomach. The small intestine does most digestion, so the person can still digest food, eating small meals.
7. (sort) Sort each change. Is it mechanical digestion or chemical digestion?  
   **Answer:** teeth grinding a groundnut → Mechanical; the stomach churning → Mechanical; bile breaking fat into droplets → Mechanical; saliva cutting starch into sugar → Chemical; pepsin cutting protein → Chemical; pancreatic juice cutting fat → Chemical. *Why:* Mechanical digestion makes smaller pieces of the same substance. Chemical digestion makes new, smaller molecules. Bile only makes smaller droplets of the same fat.
8. (odd) Three of these are added to food in the small intestine. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Bile / Pancreatic juice / Enzymes from the intestine wall / Saliva  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Bile, pancreatic juice and the wall's own enzymes all enter the small intestine. Saliva is added in the mouth.
9. (count) A stomach squeezes 3 times a minute. How many times does it squeeze in 10 minutes?  
   **Answer:** 30. *Why:* 3 squeezes each minute for 10 minutes is 3 times 10, which is 30 squeezes.
10. (order) Put these in order, to show what happens to the fat in a vada.  
   **Answer:** ['Teeth break the vada into pieces', 'The stomach churns the pieces into chyme', 'Bile breaks the fat into tiny droplets', 'Enzymes cut the fat into smaller units', 'Villi absorb the units']. *Why:* Fat must first be made into tiny droplets by bile. Only then can enzymes reach it. Absorbing comes last.
11. (write) In the Oil and Soap Bottles, the bottle with 10 drops stayed milky. What does this show about bile?  
   **Answer:** Soap kept the oil in tiny droplets spread through the water. Bile does the same to fat in the small intestine. Tiny droplets have far more surface, so enzymes can digest the fat faster.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the oil is still there as tiny droplets. It links droplets to more surface for enzymes.
12. (write) With loose motions, food moves through the gut very fast. Explain why the child loses a lot of water.  
   **Answer:** The gut absorbs water as food passes slowly along it. When everything rushes through, there is too little time to absorb the water, so it leaves with the faeces. The child must drink water with sugar and salt to replace it.. *Why:* A strong answer links speed to absorbing: less time in the tube means less water taken back.
13. (write) A snake swallows an egg wider than the snake's own head. How does the egg move along the snake's body?  
   **Answer:** Muscles in the wall of the snake's food tube squeeze just behind the egg and push it along. This is peristalsis, the same way your own food pipe moves a bite.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea of squeezing behind the food, and sees that one idea explains both animals.
14. (perspective) An old rule says: chew every bite 32 times. Three people talk about it.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. The chew counts in Dry or Wet differ from food to food, which supports the scientist. The grandmother is right that small, wet pieces are digested more easily. The child is partly right that the stomach churns. But the stomach has no teeth, and large pieces can make a person choke.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the child's own numbers, and takes what is true in another view seriously.
15. (label) Label four parts on this picture of the food tube: oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine.  
   **Answer:** ['Oesophagus: the straight tube from the throat down through the chest', 'Stomach: the bag under the ribs on the left side', 'Small intestine: the narrow coiled tube in the middle of the belly', 'Large intestine: the wider tube that frames the small intestine']. *Why:* The parts come in the order of the journey. The small intestine is narrow and coiled, and the large intestine is wider and runs around it.
### Testing ideas about digestion (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) René de Réaumur fed a kite meat inside metal tubes. The meat still dissolved. What did this show?  
   Options: Juices digest food without any grinding / Kites cannot digest meat / Metal helps digestion / The stomach does nothing  
   **Answer:** Juices digest food without any grinding. *Why:* The tube could not be crushed, so nothing ground the meat. Something liquid entered the tube and dissolved it. Digestion is chemical as well as mechanical.

   Options: The gut has its own nerve net / The brain is not needed for anything / The intestine has no nerves / Food moves by falling  
   **Answer:** The gut has its own nerve net. *Why:* The intestine sensed the food and squeezed behind it without the brain. The nerve net in the gut wall runs peristalsis.
3. (mcq) A smooth tube would have half a square metre of lining. The small intestine has 30. What makes the difference?  
   Options: Folds and villi / A longer tube / Thicker muscles / More acid

4. (mcq) Fibre cannot be cut by human enzymes. What happens to it in the large intestine?  
   Options: Gut bacteria feed on the fibre / It is absorbed whole / It turns into bile / It goes back to the stomach  
   **Answer:** Gut bacteria feed on the fibre. *Why:* Gut bacteria break down part of the fibre and make gases and some vitamins. The rest leaves in the faeces and helps them hold water.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed that most ulcers come from a bacterium called Helicobacter pylori. Some pain medicines cause ulcers too.
6. (truefalse) Your stomach can start making gastric juice before any food reaches it.

   **Answer:** 1.5. *Why:* 6 metres in 4 hours is 6 divided by 4, which is one and a half metres each hour. That is slower than a snail.
9. (fill) The thick liquid that leaves the stomach is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['chyme']. *Why:* The stomach churns food with gastric juice for 2 to 4 hours. The result is a thick, sour liquid called chyme.
10. (write) A child says: 'Milk is a liquid, so it needs no digesting.' Is this right? Explain.  
   **Answer:** It is not right. Milk holds protein, fat and milk sugar, and their molecules are too big to cross the gut wall. Enzymes must cut them first. Being liquid only means that milk needs no chewing.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the size of the pieces you can see from the size of the molecules. Digestion is about molecules.

   **Answer:** Eat two meals of the same size at the same time of day. One has little fat and one has more. Change only the amount of fat. Note the time when you first feel hungry again after each meal. Repeat on several days.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the fat. It keeps the size of the meal and the time of day the same, and repeats the test. Feeling hungry is a rough measure, and a strong answer may say so.
12. (write) Why is a cut-up vada a poor model of what bile does? Why is oil shaken with soap better?  
   **Answer:** Cutting a vada makes smaller pieces of food, which is the job of teeth. Bile works on liquid fat and keeps it in tiny droplets that do not join up again. Oil shaken with soap shows exactly that.. *Why:* A strong answer says what a model must show: tiny droplets of fat that stay apart in water.
13. (write) The Pleated Cloth Test is a model of villi. Name one way it is like villi and one way it differs.  
   **Answer:** It is like villi because folding packs more surface into the same space, so more is taken up. It is unlike villi because a cloth only holds the water. Villi pass the food on into the blood, so they never fill up.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a true likeness about surface and a true difference, such as blood carrying the food away.

   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer weighs the knowledge gained against St. Martin's pain and his lack of free choice. It may say that both men should be credited.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. A strong answer does not treat the knowledge as worthless, and does not treat St. Martin's wishes as unimportant.

   **Answer:** Liver and pancreas. *Why:* The liver makes bile and the pancreas makes pancreatic juice. Both juices flow into the small intestine through a small tube.
4. (mcq) What are the two jobs of chewing?  
   Options: Breaking food small and wetting it / Warming food and cooling it / Adding acid and adding bile / Absorbing sugar and water  
   **Answer:** Breaking food small and wetting it. *Why:* Teeth break the food, and saliva wets the pieces so they stick together and slide. A bite needs both before it can be swallowed.
5. (mcq) How does a bite move along the oesophagus?  
   Options: Muscle rings squeeze behind it / It falls under its own weight / Air from the lungs blows it / Saliva washes it down  
   **Answer:** Muscle rings squeeze behind it. *Why:* This squeezing is peristalsis. It pushes food in any direction, so it does not depend on falling.
6. (truefalse) An astronaut floating in space is able to swallow food.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Peristalsis pushes the food to the stomach. Nothing needs to fall.
7. (mcq) Which of these is a job of the stomach?  
   Options: Churning food with acid / Absorbing most of the food / Making bile / Taking back most of the water  
   **Answer:** Churning food with acid. *Why:* The stomach stores, churns and adds acid and pepsin. Most absorbing happens in the small intestine, and bile comes from the liver.
8. (mcq) What does bile do to the fat in a meal?  
   Options: Breaks it into tiny droplets / Turns it into sugar / Absorbs it into the blood / Makes it solid  
   **Answer:** Breaks it into tiny droplets. *Why:* Bile keeps fat in tiny droplets, as soap did to the oil in the Oil and Soap Bottles. Enzymes then digest the droplets.
9. (mcq) Where is nearly all digested food absorbed into the blood?  
   Options: In the small intestine / In the stomach / In the mouth / In the large intestine  
   **Answer:** In the small intestine. *Why:* The villi of the small intestine absorb sugars, amino acids and fats. The large intestine absorbs mainly water and salts.
10. (mcq) What is the main job of the large intestine?  
   Options: Taking back water / Grinding food / Making stomach acid / Cutting protein  
   **Answer:** Taking back water. *Why:* The large intestine absorbs water and salts from what is left. Its bacteria also feed on fibre.
11. (mcq) About how long does a meal take to travel the whole food tube?  
   Options: 1 to 3 days / 10 seconds / 1 hour / 2 weeks  
   **Answer:** 1 to 3 days. *Why:* The oesophagus takes seconds and the stomach and small intestine take hours. The large intestine takes 1 to 2 days, so the whole journey takes 1 to 3 days.
12. (mcq) A grown-up's food tube is about 8 metres long. Which part makes up most of that length?  
   Options: The small intestine / The stomach / The oesophagus / The large intestine  
   **Answer:** The small intestine. *Why:* The small intestine is about 6 metres of the 8. The large intestine is about 1.5 metres, and the oesophagus and stomach are about 25 centimetres each.

## Applications
- Oral rehydration solution, or ORS, is water with sugar and salt in set amounts. Sugar helps the small intestine absorb salt and water, so ORS saves children with loose motions.
- Doctors can ask a patient to swallow a camera the size of a large tablet. Peristalsis carries it through the whole food tube while it takes pictures.
- For an X-ray of the food tube, the patient drinks a white liquid that blocks X-rays. Doctors then watch the swallow and the waves of squeezing on a screen.
- Some tablets have a coat that stomach acid cannot dissolve. The coat dissolves only in the small intestine, so the medicine is released there.
- Cooks pound, grind, soak and ferment food. Idli batter is ground and fermented, so part of the work of breaking down is done before the first bite.
- Vets treat cows whose rumen has stopped moving. They listen to the belly, because a healthy rumen squeezes once or twice every minute.

## Spotted around us
- A cow or a buffalo lying down and chewing with nothing in front of her. She is chewing the cud, which she has brought back up.
- A packet of ORS powder at the chemist or in the home medicine box.
- Your own mouth watering when mustard seeds and curry leaves hit hot oil.
- A stomach growling in a quiet room before lunch.
- Dish wash liquid turning the greasy water in a kadai milky. Bile does this to fat inside you.
- A crow or a pigeon swallowing a grain whole with a jerk of the head. Birds have no teeth, and a muscular part of the stomach grinds the food.

## Open questions
- **Do the bacteria in the gut change how we feel and think?** In mice, gut microbes change behaviour. In people the studies are small and often disagree, and nobody yet knows how strong the effect is.
- **What does the appendix do?** One idea is that it keeps a store of helpful bacteria for refilling the gut after an illness. People live well without an appendix, so the idea is hard to test.
- **Why do some people's guts move food much faster or slower than others'?** Food, water, exercise, microbes and the gut's own nerves all play a part. Scientists cannot yet predict one person's time from these.
- **How do the hundreds of millions of nerve cells in the gut wall work together?** Scientists can describe single reflexes such as peristalsis. How the whole net senses a meal and plans hours of work is still being mapped.
- **How does the gut tell food from a harmful microbe, when both arrive the same way?** The gut wall holds a large share of the body's defence cells. How they learn to leave food and helpful bacteria alone is only partly understood.

## Connected maths
- In Dry or Wet, a dry half biscuit took 22 chews and then 20 chews. A dipped half biscuit took 9 and then 8. Find the middle value for each kind of bite. About how many times as many chews did the dry bite need? **Dry: 21 chews. Dipped: 8 and a half chews. The dry bite needed about two and a half times as many.**. 22 and 20 add to 42, and half of 42 is 21. 9 and 8 add to 17, and half of 17 is 8 and a half. Two lots of 8 and a half make 17, and two and a half lots make just over 21. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In Dry or Wet, a dry carrot piece took 30 chews and then 28. A dipped carrot piece took 29 and then 27. Did dipping change the carrot? Explain how you can tell. **No, there is no clear change.**. The dry tries differ from each other by 2 chews. The dry and dipped counts differ by only 1 chew. A difference smaller than the difference between two tries of the same bite could be chance. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In the Food Tube on the Floor, the four pieces of wool were 25, 25, 600 and 150 centimetres long. How long is the whole tube? What fraction of it is the small intestine? **800 centimetres, which is 8 metres. The small intestine is three quarters of it.**. 25 + 25 + 600 + 150 = 800 centimetres. The small intestine is 600 out of 800. Divide both by 200 to get 3 out of 4. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A smooth tube the size of the small intestine would have half a square metre of lining. The real lining has 30 square metres. How many times as much is that? **60 times as much.**. Two halves make 1 square metre. So 30 square metres hold 30 times 2 halves, which is 60 halves. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In the Pleated Cloth Test, the flat cloth took up 1 teaspoon. The pleated cloth has 4 times as much cloth, but it took up 3 teaspoons. Give one reason why it took less than 4. **The inside layers may not have been fully wet after a count of 10.**. Four times the cloth could hold 4 teaspoons. Water reaches the outer layers first. The inner layers are pressed together and get less water in a short time. A longer soak would bring the number closer to 4. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- In the Sweetcorn Clock, the corn was eaten on Monday at 7:30 in the evening. The first skins were seen on Wednesday at 7:00 in the morning. How long did the journey take? **35 hours and 30 minutes.**. From Monday 7:30 in the evening to Tuesday 7:30 in the evening is 24 hours. From then to Wednesday 7:00 in the morning is 11 hours and 30 minutes. 24 hours and 11 and a half hours make 35 hours and 30 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
