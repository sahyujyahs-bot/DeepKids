# Chlorophyll and starch: how a leaf catches sunlight and keeps it as food
`sci.plants.photosynthesis.L5`

**Summary.** Leaves picked at sunset and at sunrise look the same, but an iodine test shows the sunset leaf holds far more starch. Where does the starch go at night? In this lesson you will test real leaves for starch, check a white patch, and trace your meal back to the Sun.

**Intro.** *Think of everything you ate yesterday. Was there one food that did not need sunlight? Say what you think, and why.* Most people find this hard to answer. Rice and dal are plants, so they needed sunlight. But what about an egg, a fish or a mushroom? In the first activity, Plate to Sun, you follow each food on a real plate backwards and see where the trail ends. After that you test real leaves. You find out what chlorophyll does, where a leaf keeps the food it makes, and what happens to that food at night. You also meet the few living beings on Earth whose food does not start with sunlight.

7. By the end I can say what scientists still do not know about chlorophyll and starch.

## Terms
- **Photosynthesis**: Photosynthesis is the way plants, algae and cyanobacteria make sugar from carbon dioxide and water, using the energy of light. Oxygen is left over. *e.g.* A hibiscus leaf in the morning sun is photosynthesising.
- **Chlorophyll**: Chlorophyll is the green pigment in leaves. It catches light, which is the first step of photosynthesis. *e.g.* Palak leaves are dark green because their cells are packed with chlorophyll.
- **Pigment**: A pigment is a substance that takes in some colours of light and sends back others. The colours it sends back are the colour we see. *e.g.* Haldi is yellow because of a pigment that sends back yellow light.
- **Chloroplast**: A chloroplast is a tiny green body inside a plant cell. All of the cell's chlorophyll is inside its chloroplasts, and photosynthesis happens there. *e.g.* One cell near the top of a leaf can hold dozens of chloroplasts.
- **Photon**: A photon is one tiny packet of light energy. Light always arrives as photons. *e.g.* A photon leaves the Sun and reaches a leaf on Earth about eight minutes later.
- **Electron**: An electron is a very small particle with an electric charge. Every atom has electrons. *e.g.* When chlorophyll catches a photon, one of its electrons gains the photon's energy.
- **Glucose**: Glucose is a simple sugar. It is the sugar most living cells use for energy. *e.g.* The glucose powder sold at a medical shop is the same sugar a leaf makes.
- **Starch**: Starch is a long chain of thousands of glucose units joined together. Plants make it to store food, because starch does not dissolve in water. *e.g.* Rice, potato, tapioca and ragi are full of starch.
- **Sucrose**: Sucrose is the sugar that travels inside a plant. It is the same sugar as the white sugar in your kitchen. *e.g.* Sugarcane stems are sweet because the plant stores sucrose in them.
- **Phloem**: Phloem is the set of thin living tubes that carry sucrose from the leaves to every other part of a plant. *e.g.* Sucrose made in a mango leaf travels through the phloem to the growing mango.
- **Iodine test**: The iodine test is a test for starch. Brown iodine solution turns blue-black where it touches starch. *e.g.* A drop of iodine on a slice of raw potato turns blue-black within seconds.
- **Variegated leaf**: A variegated leaf is a leaf with patches of different colours, often green and white. The white patches have no chlorophyll. *e.g.* Many hibiscus and bougainvillea plants in nurseries have green and white leaves.
- **Producer**: A producer is a living being that makes their own food from things that are not food. Plants, algae and cyanobacteria are producers. *e.g.* A paddy plant is a producer. They make rice grains from air, water and sunlight.
- **Consumer**: A consumer is a living being that gets food by eating other living beings or what they made. All animals, including humans, are consumers. *e.g.* A hen eating grain and a child eating the hen's egg are both consumers.
- **Algae**: Algae are simple living beings that photosynthesise. Most live in water, and seaweeds are large algae. One of them is called an alga. *e.g.* The green film on a wet wall in the monsoon is made of algae.
- **Cyanobacteria**: Cyanobacteria are bacteria that photosynthesise. They have chlorophyll but are far smaller than a plant cell. *e.g.* Cyanobacteria floating in the sea do a large share of the photosynthesis on Earth.
- **Fluorescence**: Fluorescence is light given out by a substance just after it has caught light of another colour. *e.g.* Chlorophyll dissolved in spirit glows dull red when violet light shines on it.
- **Chemical energy**: Chemical energy is energy held in the way atoms are joined together. Food, wood and petrol all hold chemical energy. *e.g.* The chemical energy in a banana lets you run across the playground.
- **Chemosynthesis**: Chemosynthesis is a way of making food using energy from chemicals, not from light. Some bacteria do this. *e.g.* Bacteria at hot springs on the deep sea floor make food by chemosynthesis.

## Explanations
### Chlorophyll: the green pigment that catches light · *defined*
**Definition:** Chlorophyll is the green pigment in plants, algae and cyanobacteria. It catches light, and that is the first step of photosynthesis.

A pigment takes in some colours of light and sends back others. Chlorophyll takes in mostly red and blue light. It sends back more of the green light, and that is why leaves look green to us. A leaf still catches a good part of the green light, only less than the red and the blue. Chlorophyll is a molecule, which means a group of atoms joined together. One chlorophyll molecule has about 137 atoms of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, arranged around one single atom of magnesium. So a plant needs a little nitrogen and magnesium from the soil to make chlorophyll. A plant that is short of either turns yellow. Chlorophyll is not spread all through a leaf. It sits inside chloroplasts, the tiny green bodies inside leaf cells. Leaves have two main kinds, called chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b. They take in slightly different shades of red and blue, so together they catch more of the light.
- e.g. In the starch test, the spirit turns green. That green is chlorophyll that has dissolved out of the leaf.
- e.g. Before an old leaf falls, the plant takes the chlorophyll apart and moves much of the nitrogen to younger leaves. The leaf turns yellow because the yellow pigments are left behind.
- e.g. A moong sprout grown in a dark cupboard is pale yellow. Most plants make chlorophyll only after they sense light.
### What happens when chlorophyll catches light · *defined*
**Definition:** When a chlorophyll molecule catches a photon, one of its electrons gains the photon's energy. In a living leaf that energy is passed on and used. In a jar it comes straight back out as heat and red light.

Light arrives in tiny packets of energy called photons. When chlorophyll catches a photon, one of its electrons is lifted to a higher energy level. The electron now holds the photon's energy, but only for an instant. What happens next depends on where the chlorophyll is. In a living leaf, chlorophyll molecules sit close together in thin skins inside the chloroplast. A few hundred of them work as one team around a spot called a reaction centre. The energy hops from one chlorophyll to the next in trillionths of a second, until it reaches the reaction centre. There an electron leaves its chlorophyll and is handed along a chain of other molecules. Its energy is used to build sugar. The chlorophyll takes a new electron from water. This splits the water, and oxygen is left over. Now take chlorophyll out of the leaf and dissolve it in spirit. It has no neighbours to hand the energy to. The electron falls back within a few billionths of a second, and the energy comes out as heat and as a dull red light. This red light is called fluorescence.
- e.g. In the Red Glow activity, green leaf liquid glows red in violet light. The chlorophyll is catching light and has nowhere to send the energy.
- e.g. A living leaf glows red too, but very faintly. Only about 1 or 2 of every 100 photons a leaf catches come back out as red light.
- e.g. The oxygen you are breathing now was left over when chlorophyll took electrons from water.
### Chlorophyll catches the light, but it does not build the sugar · *defined*
**Definition:** Chlorophyll only catches light energy. The sugar is built by other parts of the chloroplast, using that energy and carbon dioxide from the air.

Many books say 'chlorophyll makes food'. That is a shortcut, and it is not quite true. A chloroplast has two parts that work together. In the thin green skins, chlorophyll catches light and water is split. The energy is loaded onto small carrier molecules. These carriers move into the watery part of the chloroplast. There, special proteins called enzymes take carbon dioxide from the air and join it into sugar, using the energy from the carriers. An enzyme is a protein that makes one chemical change happen quickly. So making food needs the whole living chloroplast: chlorophyll, water, enzymes, carbon dioxide and light. Take any one away and no sugar is made.
- e.g. A rooftop solar panel catches light, but the pump does the lifting of water. In a leaf, chlorophyll is the catcher and the enzymes are the builders.
- e.g. Chlorophyll in a jar of spirit catches light and makes no sugar at all, because the rest of the chloroplast is missing.
- e.g. A leaf kept in bright light in a sealed bag with no carbon dioxide makes no starch, even though the chlorophyll is working.
### Where the energy is kept · *defined*
**Definition:** Photosynthesis turns light energy into chemical energy. Chemical energy is energy held in the way atoms are joined.

Carbon dioxide and water are low-energy substances. They do not burn, and no living being can get energy from them. Photosynthesis uses the energy of light to pull them apart and join their atoms in a new way, as sugar and oxygen. Sugar and oxygen together hold more energy than the carbon dioxide and water did. When sugar and oxygen meet again and react, they turn back into carbon dioxide and water, and the energy comes out. This happens quickly in a flame. It happens slowly and gently in every living cell, where it is called respiration. People often say 'the energy is in the sugar'. That is nearly right. To be exact, the energy is in sugar and oxygen kept apart from each other. The plant makes both. The sugar stays in the plant, and the oxygen waits in the air.
- e.g. A dry coconut shell burns with a hot flame. The heat is sunlight energy that the palm's leaves caught years ago.
- e.g. You feel warm after running. Your cells are joining sugar with oxygen, and part of the energy comes out as heat.
- e.g. A sealed tin of sugar keeps its energy for years, because the sugar and the oxygen are not reacting.
### Sugar for now, starch for later, sucrose for travel · *defined*
**Definition:** A leaf makes sugar in the light. The plant uses some at once, stores some in the leaf as starch, and sends some to the rest of the plant as sucrose.

On a sunny day a leaf makes sugar faster than the plant can use it. Sugar dissolves in water. A cell full of dissolved sugar would pull in more and more water and swell. So the chloroplast joins thousands of glucose units into long chains of starch. Starch does not dissolve. It sits in the chloroplast as tiny hard grains that take up little room. This leaf starch is what your iodine test finds. For travel, the plant makes a different sugar called sucrose. Sucrose moves through thin living tubes called phloem to every part that makes no food: roots, buds, flowers, fruits and seeds. Those parts use the sucrose to live and grow, or they store it again. Rice grains, potatoes and tapioca roots store it as starch. Sugarcane stems and ripe fruits store it as sugar. Coconuts and groundnuts store it as oil.
- e.g. A tapioca root grows underground in the dark. Every grain of starch in the root was made as sugar in the leaves above.
- e.g. In the Banana Clock activity, a raw banana turns blue-black with iodine and a ripe one hardly changes. The fruit turns stored starch back into sugar as it ripens.
- e.g. White sugar is sucrose taken from sugarcane. It is the same travelling sugar that flows in the phloem of every tree.
### How a leaf makes the day's starch last all night · *debated*
**Definition:** A plant makes food only in the light, but their cells need energy all night too. So the leaf breaks down its starch slowly through the night and sends the sugar to the rest of the plant.

At dusk a leaf is full of starch. At dawn most of it is gone. This is what the Dawn Leaf, Dusk Leaf experiment tests. Scientists have measured it hour by hour in a small plant called thale cress, a relative of mustard. The leaf uses up starch at a steady rate all night. By dawn about 95 parts of every 100 are gone, and the plant has not run short. Then the scientists switched off the lights four hours early. The plants used their starch more slowly that night, and it still lasted until dawn. Plants whose inner daily clock is damaged get this wrong. They run out of starch before dawn and grow less. So a plant measures how much starch they have and how long the night will be, and sets the speed to match. Everyone agrees that this happens. How the plant does it is still argued over. Other plants may do it differently, and some store sugar instead of starch.
- e.g. If a leaf has 60 units of starch and 12 hours of night, a steady rate of 5 units an hour uses all 60 by dawn.
- e.g. A potted tulsi plant kept in a dark cupboard for two days uses up nearly all the starch in their leaves. That is why school books say to 'destarch' a plant in the dark before a test.
- e.g. You do something like this too. Your liver stores sugar after dinner and lets it out slowly while you sleep.
- **Alison Smith, Martin Howard and their team at the John Innes Centre in England (2013):** The plant does a kind of division. Two chemicals in the leaf keep track of the amount of starch and the time left until dawn. The rate of use is the first divided by the second.
- **Other plant scientists, including Akiko Satake's group in Japan:** The plant needs no division. The amount of sugar in the leaf nudges the plant's daily clock forward or back, and the clock controls how fast starch is used. That simple loop gives nearly the same result.
### Why the starch test has four steps · *defined*
**Definition:** The starch test on a leaf uses hot water, then spirit, then a rinse, then iodine. Each step does one job.

First the leaf goes into very hot water for a minute. This kills the leaf cells, so they stop using up starch. It also breaks the thin skin around each cell, so that liquids can get in and out. Second, the leaf sits in warm spirit. Chlorophyll does not dissolve in water, but it does dissolve in spirit. The spirit turns green and the leaf turns pale. Now a colour change will be easy to see. Third, the leaf is rinsed in warm water, because spirit leaves it stiff and brittle. Fourth, brown iodine solution is poured on. Starch is a long chain that coils like a spring. Iodine particles slip inside the coil and line up in a row. A row of iodine inside a starch coil takes in light differently from loose iodine, and looks blue-black. Where there is no starch, the iodine stays yellow-brown. The test shows starch, not sugar. Sugar gives no colour with iodine.
- e.g. A drop of iodine on cooked rice goes blue-black at once. A drop on a pinch of sugar stays brown.
- e.g. If you skip the spirit, a dark green leaf looks almost the same before and after iodine.
- e.g. Onion leaves stay brown in the test. Onion plants store sugar in their leaves, not starch.
### No chlorophyll, no starch · *defined*
**Definition:** A part of a plant that has no chlorophyll cannot catch light, so no sugar or starch is made there. It lives on sugar sent from the green parts.

Some leaves have white or cream patches. These are called variegated leaves. The cells in the white patches are alive, but their chloroplasts have no chlorophyll. In the White Patch experiment, iodine turns the green parts blue-black and leaves the white parts brown. The white cells are kept alive by sucrose that flows in from the green cells beside them. Roots, flower petals and the inside of a tree trunk live the same way. Now and then a seed sprouts into a seedling that is white all over. This is called an albino seedling. They grow well for a few weeks on the food stored in the seed. When the seed is empty they die, however much light, water and manure they are given. A few plants have given up chlorophyll and live by taking food from others. Dodder, called amarbel in Hindi, is a tangle of yellow threads on roadside hedges. Dodder pushes tiny suckers into a green plant and drinks their sucrose.
- e.g. A variegated hibiscus grows more slowly than an all-green one. They have less green to feed the same size of plant.
- e.g. The bright yellow or red ball of a moon cactus has no chlorophyll. Nurseries join it onto a green cactus stem, and the green stem feeds both.
- e.g. You have no chlorophyll either. Like the white patch, every animal is fed by green cells somewhere else.
### Hidden green: red, purple and yellow leaves · *defined*
**Definition:** A leaf that looks red or purple still has chlorophyll. A second pigment covers the green.

Leaves hold other pigments besides chlorophyll. Red and purple pigments sit dissolved in the watery sap of the cells. Yellow and orange pigments, called carotenoids, sit in the chloroplasts next to the chlorophyll. Carotenoids catch some blue and green light and pass the energy on to chlorophyll. They also take up extra energy in very bright light, so the chlorophyll is not harmed. In a red amaranth leaf there is so much red pigment that we cannot see the green under it. The Green Under Red activity shows this. Hot water takes out the red pigment, because the red pigment dissolves in water. The chlorophyll stays behind, because it does not, and the leaf turns green. Red leaves photosynthesise and give a blue-black starch test. Only the truly white parts of a leaf do not.
- e.g. Red cheera in Kerala and lal saag in Bengal are red amaranth leaves. The cooking water turns pink and the cooked leaves look green.
- e.g. Young mango and peepal leaves are coppery red. Scientists think the red pigment shades the new chlorophyll while the leaf is still soft.
- e.g. A coleus leaf can be purple, red and green at once. All three parts usually pass the starch test.
### Almost all food starts with caught sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** Producers make food from things that are not food. Almost all producers on Earth use sunlight to do it. Every consumer lives on what producers made.

Follow any food backwards and ask each time, 'and what did they eat?' The trail nearly always ends at a producer. On land the producers are mostly plants. In the sea they are mostly algae and cyanobacteria, which are tiny living beings that float near the surface. Cyanobacteria are bacteria that photosynthesise. The sea's producers do about half of all the photosynthesis on Earth. So 'food comes from plants' is a simplification. The fuller picture is that food comes from every living being that has chlorophyll. A sardine ate tiny animals that ate algae. A hen ate grain. A mushroom fed on dead straw. Each step passes on only a part of the energy, because every eater uses most of their food to stay alive. Producers catch only a small share of the sunlight that reaches the Earth, roughly one part in a thousand. All animals, fungi and most bacteria live on that share. Animals complete the balance of the air. In the light, a plant takes in carbon dioxide and gives out oxygen. Animals breathe the other way round: they take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide. A plant also breathes in the dark, using oxygen, as every living cell does. Over time the air's oxygen and carbon dioxide stay in balance, because each kind of living being gives out what the other one uses.
- e.g. Curd: sunlight was caught by grass, the cow ate the grass and made milk, and bacteria turned the milk into curd.
- e.g. Fish curry in Kochi: sunlight was caught by algae in the Arabian Sea, small animals ate the algae, and the fish ate them.
- e.g. A cow in a village field breathes out carbon dioxide, and the grass beside it takes that carbon dioxide in to make sugar.
- e.g. Salt and water are on your plate too, but they give no energy. They are not food in this sense, and they do not come from sunlight.
### The 'almost': living beings fed by chemicals · *defined*
**Definition:** A few living beings get their energy from chemicals that come out of rock, not from light. This is called chemosynthesis.

Deep in the sea, far below where any sunlight reaches, hot water full of dissolved chemicals pours out of cracks in the sea floor. Bacteria there make sugar from carbon dioxide, as plants do. But their energy comes from a chemical in the hot water called hydrogen sulphide, the gas that smells of rotten eggs. Giant tubeworms, clams and crabs live around these hot springs. The tubeworms have no mouth. Bacteria live inside their bodies and feed them. Even here the Sun plays a part. Most of these bacteria need oxygen to use the hydrogen sulphide, and that oxygen was made by photosynthesis far above. A few microbes do without the Sun completely. In 2008 scientists described bacteria living in water inside rock 2.8 kilometres down a South African gold mine. They live on hydrogen that forms when natural radioactivity in the rock splits water. This is why we say 'almost all' food starts with sunlight, and not 'all'.
- e.g. Giant tubeworms at deep-sea hot springs grow up to about 2 metres long, fed only by the bacteria inside them.
- e.g. In the gold mine, the bacteria have been cut off from sunlight for millions of years.
- e.g. Some scientists think life on Earth may have started at such hot springs, before any living being could use light. Nobody knows yet.
### Seeing photosynthesis from space · *defined*
**Definition:** Living leaves give off a very faint red glow while they photosynthesise. Satellites can measure this glow from space.

You read that chlorophyll in a jar glows red, and that a living leaf glows red very faintly. The glow from one leaf is far too dim for your eyes in daylight. But a whole forest or a field of paddy has billions of leaves. Since 2011, scientists have used satellites to measure this faint red glow over the whole Earth. Where the glow is strong, leaves are catching a lot of light and making a lot of sugar. Where it fades, the plants are short of water, too cold or too hot. So the glow works as a health report for the green cover of the Earth. Scientists use it to watch crops, forests and droughts. In India the glow rises across the country after the monsoon arrives and the fields turn green.
- e.g. The same red glow that you see in a glass in the Red Glow activity is what the satellites measure.
- e.g. The glow over the paddy fields of the Kaveri delta rises as the crop grows and falls after harvest.
- e.g. The sea's algae glow as well, and satellites map them too.
### Who worked this out · *defined*
**Definition:** Our picture of chlorophyll and starch was built by many people over about 200 years.

In 1817 two French chemists, Pierre-Joseph Pelletier and Joseph Bienaimé Caventou, took the green substance out of leaves and named it chlorophyll, from Greek words for 'green' and 'leaf'. In 1845 the German doctor Julius Robert Mayer wrote that plants take in the energy of light and store it as chemical energy. In the 1860s Julius von Sachs, also in Germany, used the iodine test on leaves. He showed that starch grains appear inside chloroplasts, and only when the leaf is in light. Richard Willstätter showed that every chlorophyll molecule holds one atom of magnesium, and won a Nobel Prize in 1915. Jagadish Chandra Bose in Kolkata built instruments to record photosynthesis in water plants, and wrote a book on it in 1924. Govindjee, born in Allahabad in 1932, spent his working life at the University of Illinois studying how chlorophyll handles light and why it glows. Rajni Govindjee worked on photosynthesis in the same laboratory. In 2013 Alison Smith and her team in England showed how carefully a leaf shares out its starch through the night.
- e.g. The word chlorophyll is just over 200 years old. The pigment itself is more than two billion years old.
- e.g. The iodine test you do at home is the test Sachs used on leaves in the 1860s.
- e.g. Colleen Cavanaugh was a student in 1981 when she worked out how tubeworms at deep-sea hot springs are fed.

## Mostly agreed
- Chlorophyll catches mostly red and blue light, and this is the first step of photosynthesis. *How we know:* When light is split into colours and shone on algae, the most oxygen is made in the red and the blue. Theodor Engelmann showed this in 1882, and it has been measured many times since.
- Every chlorophyll molecule has one atom of magnesium at its centre. *How we know:* Richard Willstätter found magnesium in the chlorophyll of every plant he tested, before 1915. The full shape of the molecule was worked out by 1940, and chemists built it in a laboratory in 1960.
- Starch forms in a leaf only where there is chlorophyll and only when there is light. *How we know:* Iodine tests on covered leaves and on variegated leaves show this. Julius von Sachs did the first such tests in the 1860s, and you can repeat them at home.
- A leaf builds up starch by day and uses most of it up by the next dawn. *How we know:* Scientists have measured the starch in leaves hour by hour in many plants. In thale cress about 95 parts in 100 are used by dawn.
- The sea's algae and cyanobacteria do about half of the photosynthesis on Earth. *How we know:* Satellites measure the chlorophyll in sea water by its colour. From this, Christopher Field and others worked out in 1998 that the sea and the land each do about half.
- A few living beings are fed by chemosynthesis, with energy from chemicals and not from light. *How we know:* Animals crowded around deep-sea hot springs were first seen in 1977. Bacteria that feed them were found inside tubeworms in 1981.

## How we found out
**The geologists who dived in Alvin, led by Jack Corliss. Colleen Cavanaugh, who worked out how the tubeworms are fed. Sergei Winogradsky, who first found chemosynthesis in bacteria., 1887 (Winogradsky), February 1977 (the dives near the Galápagos Islands) and 1981 (Cavanaugh's paper).** Until 1977, scientists believed that the deep sea floor was almost empty. No sunlight reaches it, so no plants or algae can grow there. They thought the few animals living there ate only scraps that sank from the sunlit water far above. In February 1977, geologists went down 2,500 metres in a small submarine called Alvin, near the Galápagos Islands. They were looking for hot springs. They found them. Around the springs they also found crowds of animals: clams as big as dinner plates, crabs, and tubeworms taller than a child. Sinking scraps could never feed so many. In 1981 Colleen Cavanaugh, a student at Harvard University, showed that the tubeworms' bodies are packed with bacteria. The bacteria make food using energy from a chemical in the hot water, not from light. This is called chemosynthesis. Sergei Winogradsky had found it in bacteria in 1887, but nobody had expected it to feed whole communities of animals.  
*What people believed before:* That every community of animals must be fed by sunlight, so the dark sea floor could hold only a few animals living on scraps. The crowded hot springs, and the bacteria inside the tubeworms, showed that chemicals from rock can feed a community too.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Chlorophyll makes the plant's food.** (Many school science books; *rejected*)  
  For: Without chlorophyll no food is made. White patches on a leaf make no starch.  
  Against: Chlorophyll only catches the light. Enzymes in another part of the chloroplast build the sugar. Chlorophyll in a jar catches light and makes no food at all.
- **All life on Earth depends on the Sun.** (A common saying; *rejected*)  
  For: Nearly every food chain starts with sunlight caught by a plant, an alga or a cyanobacterium. Even most deep-sea bacteria use oxygen that photosynthesis made.  
  Against: Some microbes deep inside rock live on hydrogen made by natural radioactivity. They need nothing from the Sun. So the true word is 'almost all'.
- **A red or purple leaf has no chlorophyll.** (What many people think when they see a red plant; *rejected*)  
  For: The leaf does not look green at all.  
  Against: Hot water takes the red pigment out of a red amaranth leaf, and the leaf turns green. Red leaves also give a blue-black starch test. The chlorophyll was there, under the red.
- **A plant does arithmetic at night to share out their starch.** (Alison Smith, Martin Howard and their team, 2013; *debated*)  
  For: Thale cress plants use starch at a steady rate that lasts almost exactly until dawn. When night comes four hours early, they slow down at once and still last until dawn.  
  Against: Other scientists agree with the measurements, but say a simpler loop between sugar and the plant's daily clock explains them. They say no division is needed.
- **A green sea slug can live on sunlight like a plant.** (Reports about the sea slug Elysia chlorotica; *debated*)  
  For: This slug eats algae and keeps the algae's chloroplasts alive in their own body for months. The slug turns green, and the chloroplasts go on photosynthesising.  
  Against: In 2014, scientists kept related green slugs in the dark, and they lived as long as slugs kept in the light. So the slugs may use the chloroplasts mainly as a food store.
- **Plants are the only living beings that make food from light.** (A common way of speaking; *rejected*)  
  For: On land, nearly all the photosynthesis we can see is done by plants.  
  Against: Algae and cyanobacteria do about half of all photosynthesis, and they are not plants. Reef corals are animals, but algae live inside their bodies and make most of their food.

## Roots
We have learned how chlorophyll catches light, and how a leaf keeps that energy as starch and spends it through the night. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In Dawn Leaf, Dusk Leaf, the leaves picked at dusk went dark with iodine, and the leaves picked at dawn stayed pale. The starch made by day was spent in the dark, at a speed that made it last until morning. Sunlight comes only by day, but a living being needs energy every hour. So catching light is only half of what a leaf does. The other half is keeping the light's energy and sharing it out through the night.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say that a plant 'makes food from light', and we picture this happening at noon. That leaves out the night, which is half of a plant's life. We also tend to think that saving and sharing out are things only animals with brains do. A leaf does both every night.  
*Evidence:* In Dawn Leaf, Dusk Leaf, leaves from one plant held much more starch at sunset than at sunrise. Scientists who measured thale cress hour by hour found a steady use of starch that ends almost exactly at dawn. When they made the night come four hours early, the plants slowed down and still lasted until dawn.

- *who am i:* You have no chlorophyll. Every bit of energy you use today was caught by chlorophyll in a leaf or an alga, and kept as starch, sugar or oil until you ate it.
- *what is life:* Living beings do not only take energy in. They keep it and spend it later. A seed, a potato, a leaf at dusk and the fat under your own skin all hold energy from sunlight, kept for a time with no light.
- *what is this world:* Half of the Earth is always in the dark. The whole living world gets through every night on light that was caught during the day.

Light leaves the Sun as one stream of energy. A chlorophyll molecule catches one tiny packet of it. From there the same energy takes many forms. It is in the sugar and starch of a leaf, in a grain of rice, in the muscles of the child who eats the rice, and in the warmth of her skin. The energy is never made and never lost on the way. Each living being holds it for a while and passes it on.

- Science (settled): Energy is never made or destroyed. A plant does not create energy. They change the energy of light into chemical energy, as Julius Robert Mayer wrote in 1845.
- Science (settled): Thale cress plants use their leaf starch at a steady rate that lasts until dawn, and change the rate at once when the night is made longer.
- Science (debated): How a plant sets that rate, and whether it should be called measuring or dividing, is still argued over.
- Other view, Many plant scientists: They say a plant does not plan the night. The speed of starch use is set by chemical reactions tied to the plant's daily clock, which was shaped over millions of years. In this view, 'saving' and 'sharing out' are words that describe people, and we only borrow them for plants.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Chlorophyll in a jar catches light and makes no food. So what really makes the food: the chlorophyll, or the whole living leaf? (hint: Think about what the leaf has that the jar does not.)
- *deep:* A leaf uses up its starch at a speed that just lasts until dawn. Is that planning? How would you decide? (hint: What would a plant with no way of measuring do on a night that came early?)
- *deep:* You have no chlorophyll. Are you more like the green part of a variegated leaf or the white part? (hint: Think about where the white patch gets sugar from, and where you get yours.)
- *big:* Producers catch about one part in a thousand of the sunlight that reaches the Earth. Humans take about a quarter of what land plants grow each year. Is that a fair share for one kind of animal? (hint: Think about who else has to live on the rest.)
- *big:* A few microbes deep in rock have never used sunlight. If we find life on another world, should we look for green leaves, or for something else? (hint: Think about places with no sunlight, such as the sea under the ice of Jupiter's moon Europa.)
- *big:* People named chlorophyll only about 200 years ago. Leaves had been green for hundreds of millions of years before that. What else might be in plain sight that nobody has named yet? (hint: Think about things you see every day and have never asked about.)

## Story: The White Seedling
*A made-up story. Devika, Shobha Ammayi and their nursery are imagined. Mannuthy, near Thrissur in Kerala, is a real place with many plant nurseries. The science is real. Seedlings with no chlorophyll, called albino seedlings, turn up now and then in many kinds of plants. They live only as long as the food in the seed lasts. The moon cactus is a real plant sold in nurseries. Its coloured top has no chlorophyll and is joined onto a green cactus stem.*

The jackfruit seeds came up in the third week of June, when the rain had settled over Mannuthy. Devika counted them every morning before school. She had pushed forty seeds into forty black bags with her own thumb. Thirty-six had come up.

Thirty-five were green. One was white.

Not pale green. White, like the inside of a tender coconut. The stem was white. The two small leaves were white, with a little pink where the veins ran. Among the rows of ordinary seedlings they stood out like a candle in a dark room.

'Ammayi! Come and see!'

Her aunt Shobha came down the path between the benches, wiping her hands on her sari. She had run this nursery for twenty years. She bent over the bag and looked for a long time.

'I have seen four of these,' she said. 'In twenty years.'

'Are they sick?'

'No. They were born this way.'

'Can I keep them?'

'You planted them. They are yours.' Ammayi straightened up. 'Look after them well. And watch closely.'

Devika named the seedling Nilavu, which means moonlight. She moved the bag to the best place in the nursery, the bench by the gate, where the morning sun came first. She gave Nilavu rainwater from the brass uruli and a pinch of old cow dung manure. She turned the bag a little each day, so that both leaves had their share of the light.

For two weeks Nilavu grew as fast as any of the green ones. A third leaf opened, as white as the first two. Customers stopped at the gate to look. A woman from Thrissur offered five hundred rupees. Devika said no before Ammayi could answer.

In the fourth week, Nilavu stopped.

The green seedlings opened a fourth leaf and then a fifth, dark and shining. Nilavu's third leaf began to hang down. Devika gave more water. The leaf hung lower. She carried the bag into the open yard for more sun, and by evening the edges of the leaves had gone brown. She carried the bag back into the shade. She soaked manure in a bucket and poured the brown water round the stem. Nothing helped.

On Saturday she stood in front of her aunt with the bag in both hands.

'I did everything. The same soil as the others. The same water. More sun than anyone.'

'You did,' said Ammayi. 'Everything you could give, you gave.'

'Then what is missing?'

Ammayi did not answer. She spread a newspaper on the bench. She took Nilavu's bag and the bag of a green seedling, and tipped each one out gently, soil and all.

'Look at the seeds.'

Under each seedling the jackfruit seed was still there, joined to the bottom of the stem. Devika had last seen those seeds fat and smooth and heavy. Now both were brown and wrinkled. She pressed one with her finger. It was hollow, like a pod with nothing in it.

'Both of them have been eating from the seed,' said Ammayi. 'The mother tree packs every seed full, like a tiffin box. Now both boxes are empty.'

'But the green one is still growing.'

'So where is their food coming from now?'

Devika looked at the green seedling lying on the newspaper. Five leaves, held out flat. She knew this from school. She had only never needed it before.

'From the leaves. The leaves make it, from the light.'

'And Nilavu's leaves?'

Devika looked. The sun was on the white leaves at that moment. It lit them right through, like paper held up to a window. There was nothing in them to stop the light and hold it.

'I gave them more sun than anyone,' she said slowly. 'And they could not catch any of it.'

'Whose sunlight was in the seed?'

'The mother tree's. From last summer.' So Nilavu had lived four weeks on last year's light. All the water and all the manure had been beside the point. It was a hard thing to know. Devika sat down on the upturned bucket and stayed there for a while.

When she looked up, Ammayi was holding a small clay pot from the cactus shelf. In it stood a green stem with three sides, as long as a finger. On top of the stem sat a round ball, bright yellow, with not one speck of green.

'Moon cactus,' said Ammayi. 'How old, do you think?'

Devika shook her head.

'Six years. And in six years the yellow one has not made a single grain of food.'

Devika took the pot and turned it. Where the yellow met the green there was a thin line, like an old scar.

'Somebody joined them,' she said.

'When the yellow one was smaller than your thumbnail. Everything they have grown since then, the green stem caught for them.'

Devika put the pot down very carefully. 'Can we join Nilavu to a green one?'

'A jackfruit this young and this soft? I have never managed it.' Ammayi looked at the white seedling on the newspaper. 'I think it will fail. But we can try tomorrow, with the sharpest blade, if you hold the lamp.'

At one o'clock they ate on the back step, out of the rain. Rice, moru curry, and red cheera from the bed behind the well. Devika took a handful of rice and then stopped with it halfway to her mouth. She was looking at her own hand. Brown skin, pink nails. No green anywhere.

'Ammayi. I am like Nilavu.'

'Hm?'

'No green. I cannot catch any light either. Somebody has to catch it for me.'

Ammayi went on eating. 'And who is your green stem?'

Devika looked at the rice in her hand and the cheera on her plate. She looked at the thirty-five seedlings on the bench, and the old jackfruit tree by the gate, and the wet hills beyond the road, every one of them holding leaves up to the grey sky.

'All of that,' she said.

She ate the rice slowly. Then she went to find the blade.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a plant with no chlorophyll cannot catch light, so no amount of sun, water or manure can feed them. The white seedling lived for four weeks on food that the mother tree had packed into the seed, which was the mother tree's caught sunlight. When that ran out, the seedling had nothing. The green seedlings had begun to make their own food by then. The moon cactus lives for years with no chlorophyll, because a green stem joined to them catches light for both. Every animal, including a child eating rice, is in the same position as the white seedling. We live on light that green cells caught.

- Ask: Devika gave the white seedling more sun than any other. Why did the extra sun not help?
- Ask: Both seeds were empty after four weeks. Why was the green seedling still growing?
- Ask: How does the yellow moon cactus stay alive with no chlorophyll?
- Ask: Devika said, 'I am like Nilavu.' In what way is that true? In what way is it not?
- Ask: Whose sunlight was in your breakfast this morning?

- **s4 (6 min)**

## Worksheets
### Chlorophyll, starch and food chains (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What does chlorophyll do in a leaf?  
   Options: It catches light / It keeps the leaf stiff / It carries water / It joins sugar into starch  
   **Answer:** It catches light. *Why:* Chlorophyll is the pigment that catches light. Choosing 'joins sugar into starch' mixes up the catcher with the builders. Enzymes do the building.
2. (truefalse) True or false: chlorophyll is spread evenly through every part of a leaf cell.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Chlorophyll sits only inside the chloroplasts, the small green bodies in the cell. The rest of the cell has no colour.
3. (fill) Brown iodine solution turns ____ where it touches starch.  
   **Answer:** ['blue-black']. *Why:* Iodine particles slip inside the coiled starch chain, and the two together look blue-black. 'Dark blue' or 'black' are also right.
4. (order) Put the steps of the starch test on a leaf in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Dip the leaf in very hot water', 'Warm the leaf in spirit until pale', 'Rinse the leaf in warm water', 'Cover the leaf with iodine']. *Why:* Hot water kills the cells and lets liquids in. Spirit takes out the green. The rinse softens the leaf. Iodine then shows the starch.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Chlorophyll → the green pigment that catches light; Chloroplast → the tiny green body where photosynthesis happens; Starch → a long chain of glucose that stores food; Sucrose → the sugar that travels inside a plant; Producer → a living being who makes their own food. *Why:* These words are used all through the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
6. (mcq) Leaves are picked from one plant at sunrise and at sunset. Which usually go darker with iodine?  
   Options: The sunrise leaves / Both the same / Neither changes colour / The sunset leaves  
   **Answer:** The sunset leaves. *Why:* A leaf builds up starch through the day, so it holds the most at sunset. Choosing 'both the same' usually comes from how the leaves look. Starch cannot be seen without the test.
7. (write) In Dawn Leaf, Dusk Leaf, you test two leaves from each time of day. Why not one?  
   **Answer:** One leaf can be odd. It may have been shaded, old or damaged. If two leaves from the same time agree, the result can be trusted more.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a single result can be a fluke, and that a repeat checks it.
8. (sort) Sort these living beings into producers and consumers.  
   **Answer:** paddy plant → Producer; hen → Consumer; tiny sea algae → Producer; cow → Consumer; mango tree → Producer; sardine → Consumer; cyanobacteria → Producer; you → Consumer. *Why:* Plants, algae and cyanobacteria make food from light, so they are producers. Animals, including humans, eat food that was made by others.
9. (draw) Draw a green and white leaf after the iodine test. Label the parts with starch and without.  
   **Answer:** A leaf outline with the parts that were green coloured blue-black and labelled 'starch', and the parts that were white coloured yellow-brown and labelled 'no starch'.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the blue-black exactly where the green was. Colouring the whole leaf dark shows the idea that light alone is enough.
10. (truefalse) True or false: a red amaranth leaf has no chlorophyll.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The chlorophyll is under the red pigment. Hot water takes out the red, and the leaf turns green.
11. (mcq) Why is the leaf warmed in spirit during the starch test?  
   Options: To add starch to the leaf / To make the leaf sweet / To kill germs on the leaf / To take the green colour out  
   **Answer:** To take the green colour out. *Why:* Chlorophyll dissolves in spirit. With the green gone, the blue-black of the iodine is easy to see. Spirit adds nothing to the leaf.
12. (fill) The sugar that travels through a plant's phloem is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['sucrose']. *Why:* Sucrose is the travelling sugar. It is the same sugar as kitchen sugar. Glucose is made first, and starch is the stored form.
13. (odd) Three of these hold energy that a plant caught from sunlight. Which one does not?  
   Options: Rice / Jaggery / Salt / Coconut oil  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Rice, jaggery and coconut oil were all made by plants using sunlight. Salt is a mineral from sea water or rock, and gives no energy.
14. (write) Follow a bowl of curd back to the Sun. Name each living being on the way.  
   **Answer:** Curd bacteria turned milk into curd. A cow made the milk. The cow ate grass. The grass made food from sunlight.. *Why:* A strong answer names all three living beings in order and ends at a plant catching light.
15. (label) Draw a plant with leaves, a fruit and a root. Label where sugar is made, travels and is stored.  
   **Answer:** ['Made: in the green leaves', 'Travels: through the phloem in the stem', 'Stored: in the fruit and the root']. *Why:* Sugar is made only in green parts. It travels as sucrose and is stored again, often as starch. Seeds and stems are also right for 'stored'.
16. (mcq) A white patch on a leaf makes no food. How do its cells stay alive?  
   Options: They need no food / They eat the iodine / Green cells nearby send them sugar / They take food from the soil  
   **Answer:** Green cells nearby send them sugar. *Why:* Sucrose flows from the green cells to the white ones. Choosing 'from the soil' is an old idea about plants. Soil gives water and minerals, not food.
### Light, starch and who feeds whom (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Which colours of light does chlorophyll take in most?  
   Options: Only green / Only white / Green and yellow / Red and blue  
   **Answer:** Red and blue. *Why:* Chlorophyll takes in mostly red and blue light. Choosing 'green' is a common mix-up. Green is the colour a leaf sends back most, which is why we see it.
2. (write) Explain why a leaf looks green.  
   **Answer:** Chlorophyll takes in mostly red and blue light. More of the green light is sent back or passes through, and that green light reaches our eyes.. *Why:* A strong answer says the colour we see is the light the leaf does not keep. An answer such as 'because it uses green light' has it the wrong way round.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a leaf catches no green light at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A leaf catches a good part of the green light too, only less than the red and the blue.
4. (count) A leaf holds 60 units of starch at dusk. The night is 12 hours. How many units an hour last until dawn?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* 60 ÷ 12 = 5. At 5 units an hour the starch lasts exactly 12 hours.
5. (mcq) Scientists made night come four hours early for thale cress plants. What did the plants do?  
   Options: Used their starch faster / Used their starch more slowly / Stopped using starch / Made starch in the dark  
   **Answer:** Used their starch more slowly. *Why:* The plants slowed down at once, and the starch still lasted until dawn. A plant makes no starch in the dark, because photosynthesis needs light.
6. (fill) Every chlorophyll molecule has one atom of ____ at its centre.  
   **Answer:** ['magnesium']. *Why:* Magnesium sits in the middle of the chlorophyll molecule. A plant short of magnesium turns yellow.
7. (sort) Sort these by how the plant mainly stores food in them.  
   **Answer:** rice grain → As starch; sugarcane stem → As sugar; coconut → As oil; potato → As starch; groundnut → As oil; ripe mango → As sugar; tapioca root → As starch. *Why:* All of this food arrived as sucrose from the leaves. Each part then stores it in its own way. An iodine test would turn only the starch group blue-black.
8. (order) Put this food chain in order, starting with the producer.  
   **Answer:** ['Tiny sea algae', 'Tiny sea animals', 'Sardine', 'A child eating fish fry']. *Why:* The algae catch sunlight. Tiny animals eat the algae, the sardine eats them, and the child eats the sardine.
9. (perspective) Three people talk about who makes a plant's food.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses evidence: the white patch for the textbook, a food chain for the physicist, or the Red Glow for the plant scientist. It also gives a fair point to another view.. *Why:* Each is right about one part. Chlorophyll is needed, the energy is the Sun's, and only the whole chloroplast can turn that energy into sugar.
10. (fill) When animals breathe, they take in oxygen and give out ________.  
   **Answer:** carbon dioxide. *Why:* Animals breathe the other way round from a plant in the light. The plant takes in carbon dioxide and gives out oxygen, so the two living things keep the air in balance.
11. (mcq) In the Banana Clock, the very ripe slice hardly changed colour with iodine. Why?  
   Options: It had dried out / Its food had been used up / Iodine was too weak / Its starch had turned into sugar  
   **Answer:** Its starch had turned into sugar. *Why:* A ripening banana breaks starch into sugars, and sugar gives no colour with iodine. The food is still there, as sugar.
12. (draw) Draw what happens to a photon's energy in a leaf, and in a jar of chlorophyll. Use two boxes.  
   **Answer:** Box 1: a photon lands on chlorophyll in a leaf, and an arrow leads on to 'sugar'. Box 2: a photon lands on chlorophyll in a jar, and arrows lead back out as 'red light' and 'heat'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the energy being passed on in the leaf and coming straight back out in the jar.
13. (truefalse) True or false: plants store starch because starch dissolves easily in water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Starch does not dissolve. That is why it is good for storing. It sits in the cell as small grains and does not pull water in.
14. (odd) Three of these photosynthesise. Which one does not?  
   Options: Seaweed / Cyanobacteria / Mushroom / Paddy plant  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A mushroom is a fungus with no chlorophyll, and feeds on dead plants. Seaweed, cyanobacteria and paddy all make food from light.
15. (write) A pure white seedling grows well for three weeks and then dies. Explain both parts.  
   **Answer:** For three weeks the seedling lives on food stored in the seed. With no chlorophyll they catch no light and make no new food. When the seed is empty, they starve.. *Why:* A strong answer explains the good start by the seed's store, and the death by the missing chlorophyll.
16. (match) Match each living being to the way they are fed.  
   **Answer:** Dodder (amarbel) → takes sucrose from a green plant; Giant tubeworm → fed by bacteria that use chemicals; Reef coral → fed by algae living inside their body; Mushroom → feeds on dead plants; Hibiscus → makes food from light. *Why:* Only the hibiscus and the coral's algae catch light themselves. The others live on food that someone else made.
17. (label) List the four liquids used in the starch test on a leaf. Beside each, write its job.  
   **Answer:** ['Very hot water: kills the cells and lets liquids in', 'Warm spirit: takes out the chlorophyll', 'Warm rinse water: softens the leaf', 'Iodine: turns blue-black with starch']. *Why:* Each step does one job. Skipping the hot water or the spirit gives a result that is hard to read.
### Energy, the night and the 'almost' (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) People say 'the energy is in the sugar'. What is the more exact way to say it?  
   **Answer:** The energy is in sugar and oxygen kept apart. It comes out when the two react and turn back into carbon dioxide and water.. *Why:* A strong answer brings in the oxygen. Sugar gives energy only when it reacts with oxygen, in a flame or in a cell.
2. (mcq) Most bacteria at deep-sea hot springs still need one thing that photosynthesis made. What is it?  
   Options: Sunlight / Starch / Oxygen / Chlorophyll  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* They use oxygen to get energy from hydrogen sulphide. That oxygen was made by algae and plants far above, and was carried down in the sea water.
3. (count) A square metre of paddy gives 300 grams of rice. You eat 150 grams a day. How many days does it last?  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 300 ÷ 150 = 2. One square metre of field feeds you rice for only two days.
4. (truefalse) True or false: every living being on Earth depends on sunlight.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Some microbes deep inside rock live on hydrogen made by natural radioactivity. So the true word is 'almost every'.
5. (perspective) Two groups of scientists talk about a leaf's starch at night.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both explain the same results, and suggests a test where the two would differ, such as changing the sugar level without changing the starch.. *Why:* This is a real, open argument. A good scientist can say what would change their mind.
6. (mcq) How do satellites measure photosynthesis from space?  
   Options: By counting trees / By the faint red glow of leaves / By weighing forests / By the smell of leaves  
   **Answer:** By the faint red glow of leaves. *Why:* Chlorophyll in living leaves gives off a very faint red glow while it works. Satellites have measured this glow over the whole Earth since 2011.
7. (fill) Making food with energy from chemicals, not from light, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['chemosynthesis']. *Why:* Chemo means 'chemical' and photo means 'light'. Some bacteria make food by chemosynthesis.
8. (write) You wrap one tulsi leaf in foil for two days. Plan a fair starch test. What do you compare it with?  
   **Answer:** Test the wrapped leaf together with an unwrapped leaf of the same size from the same plant, picked at the same time. Use the same spirit and iodine for both.. *Why:* A strong answer names the comparison leaf and keeps everything else the same. Without the second leaf, a pale result could mean the test failed.
9. (order) Put in order what happens to light energy in a leaf.  
   **Answer:** ['Chlorophyll catches a photon', 'An electron gains the energy', 'The energy reaches a reaction centre', 'Enzymes build sugar', 'Sugar is stored as starch']. *Why:* The light is caught first. The energy is passed on, then used to build sugar, and part of the sugar is stored as starch.
10. (mcq) A plant short of magnesium turns yellow. Why?  
   Options: They cannot make enough chlorophyll / They have too much starch / They get too much light / Magnesium is yellow  
   **Answer:** They cannot make enough chlorophyll. *Why:* Every chlorophyll molecule needs one magnesium atom. With too little magnesium, less chlorophyll is made, and the yellow pigments show.
11. (draw) Sketch a line graph of the starch in a leaf over 24 hours, from sunrise to the next sunrise.  
   **Answer:** A line that starts low at sunrise, rises through the day to a peak at sunset, then falls in a straight line through the night to nearly zero at the next sunrise.. *Why:* A strong sketch shows a steady fall at night, not a sudden drop. The line should reach almost zero at dawn, not hours before.
12. (odd) Three of these need oxygen made by photosynthesis. Which one does not?  
   Options: A fish / A child / Tubeworm bacteria / Rock microbes living on hydrogen  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Fish, children and most hot spring bacteria use oxygen that photosynthesis made. The microbes deep in rock use hydrogen and need no oxygen.
13. (write) Name one thing about chlorophyll or starch that scientists still argue about.  
   **Answer:** One answer: how a plant sets the speed of starch use at night. Another: whether green sea slugs really live on the chloroplasts they take from algae.. *Why:* Any open question from this lesson is right, as long as it is truly unsettled.
14. (truefalse) True or false: chlorophyll on paper fades in sunlight, but chlorophyll in a living leaf is kept in repair.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A living leaf passes the caught energy on and replaces harmed parts all day. On paper nothing replaces them.
15. (sort) Sort these into 'settled' and 'still argued about'.  
   **Answer:** Chlorophyll holds a magnesium atom → Settled; Leaves use up most of their starch by dawn → Settled; Plants do division to share out starch → Still argued about; A green sea slug lives on sunlight → Still argued about; The sea does about half of all photosynthesis → Settled; Life began at deep-sea hot springs → Still argued about. *Why:* Science has both kinds of statement. Saying which is which is part of being honest about what we know.
16. (mcq) Of 1,000 units of sunlight energy on a field, the crop stores about 10. What share is that?  
   Options: 1 in 1,000 / 1 in 10 / 1 in 100 / Half  
   **Answer:** 1 in 100. *Why:* 10 out of 1,000 is the same as 1 out of 100. Most of the light warms the field, passes through or is sent back.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Where in a leaf cell is the chlorophyll?  
   Options: In the cell's outer wall / Dissolved in the cell's water / In the veins / In the chloroplasts  
   **Answer:** In the chloroplasts. *Why:* Chlorophyll sits in thin skins inside the chloroplasts. Choosing 'dissolved in the water' is a fair guess, but chlorophyll does not dissolve in water.
2. (mcq) A chlorophyll molecule catches a photon. What happens first?  
   Options: An electron gains the energy / Starch appears / The leaf gets heavier / Oxygen is caught  
   **Answer:** An electron gains the energy. *Why:* The photon's energy lifts an electron in the chlorophyll. Sugar and starch come later, after the energy is passed on.
3. (mcq) In the starch test, what is the job of the very hot water?  
   Options: It turns starch blue / It adds sugar / It takes the green out / It kills the cells and lets liquids in  
   **Answer:** It kills the cells and lets liquids in. *Why:* Hot water stops the cells using starch and breaks their thin skins. Taking the green out is the job of the spirit.
4. (mcq) When does a leaf on a sunny balcony hold the most starch?  
   Options: At midnight / The same all day / Just before sunrise / Late afternoon  
   **Answer:** Late afternoon. *Why:* Starch builds up all day and is used up all night. So it is highest late in the afternoon and lowest just before sunrise.
5. (mcq) A green and white leaf is tested with iodine. Which parts turn blue-black?  
   Options: Only the parts that were white / The whole leaf / Only the parts that were green / No part  
   **Answer:** Only the parts that were green. *Why:* Starch is made only where there is chlorophyll. Choosing 'the whole leaf' usually means thinking that light alone is enough.
6. (mcq) A purple coleus leaf turns blue-black in the starch test. What does that show?  
   Options: Purple pigment makes starch / The test went wrong / Purple leaves need no light / It has chlorophyll under the purple  
   **Answer:** It has chlorophyll under the purple. *Why:* Starch needs chlorophyll. So the leaf must have chlorophyll, hidden by the purple pigment.
7. (mcq) How does food made in a tapioca leaf reach the root?  
   Options: It is made in the root / As sucrose, through the phloem / As starch grains, through the soil / As chlorophyll, through the veins  
   **Answer:** As sucrose, through the phloem. *Why:* Starch does not dissolve, so it cannot travel. The leaf turns it into sucrose, which flows through the phloem. The root then stores it as starch again.
8. (mcq) Why does a leaf store food as starch and not as dissolved sugar?  
   Options: Starch is sweeter / Starch is green / Starch does not dissolve or pull in water / Starch catches light  
   **Answer:** Starch does not dissolve or pull in water. *Why:* A lot of dissolved sugar would draw water into the cell and swell it. Starch sits as small grains and causes no such trouble.
9. (truefalse) True or false: the fish in a fish curry needed sunlight.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The fish ate smaller animals, who ate tiny sea algae, and the algae made food from sunlight.
10. (mcq) What comes first in nearly every food chain?  
   Options: A producer that catches light / A fungus / Soil / A small animal  
   **Answer:** A producer that catches light. *Why:* Nearly every chain begins with a plant, an alga or a cyanobacterium making food from light. Soil gives minerals and water, not energy.
11. (mcq) A leaf is in a dark room all night. What does it take in and give out?  
   Options: Oxygen in, carbon dioxide out / Carbon dioxide in, oxygen out / Nothing at all, because the leaf is resting  
   **Answer:** Oxygen in, carbon dioxide out. *Why:* Every living cell breathes all day and night, taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide in and oxygen out needs light, so it stops in the dark.
12. (mcq) What feeds the giant tubeworms at hot springs on the deep sea floor?  
   Options: Nothing at all / Bacteria that use chemicals for energy / Seaweed growing on the rocks / Sunlight shining down  
   **Answer:** Bacteria that use chemicals for energy. *Why:* No light reaches the deep sea floor, so no seaweed grows there. Bacteria inside the tubeworms make food using energy from chemicals in the hot water.
13. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: How a plant sets the speed of starch use at night / Whether leaves hold chlorophyll / Whether iodine shows starch / Whether plants need light  
   **Answer:** How a plant sets the speed of starch use at night. *Why:* Everyone agrees that leaves use starch at a steady rate that lasts until dawn. How the plant sets that rate is not settled.

## Applications
- Farmers and gardeners check leaf colour to see whether a crop needs more nitrogen. Paler leaves have less chlorophyll. Many paddy farmers in India hold a printed colour card against the leaf to decide.
- Apple growers use the iodine test on a cut apple to decide when to harvest. Less blue-black means more starch has turned into sugar.
- Satellites measure the faint red glow of chlorophyll to watch crops, forests and droughts across whole countries.
- Sago, cornflour and the starch used to stiffen cotton saris are all plant starch, washed out of tapioca roots, maize grains or rice.
- Hay and green fodder cut in the afternoon hold more sugar and starch than fodder cut at dawn. Some farmers time their cutting by this.
- Scientists are trying to copy chlorophyll's light catching in 'artificial leaves' that would make fuel from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide. None is cheap and long-lasting yet.
- Sugar mills and jaggery makers collect the sucrose that sugarcane leaves made and sent down to the stem.

## Spotted around us
- A tangle of yellow threads over a roadside hedge. This is dodder, called amarbel in Hindi, a plant with almost no chlorophyll who lives on the hedge's sugar.
- Pink water in the pot when red cheera or lal saag is cooked, and green leaves left behind.
- Coppery red new leaves on mango and peepal trees in spring, which turn green in a few weeks.
- Green and white hibiscus, bougainvillea and money plant leaves at any plant nursery.
- A small bright red or yellow ball on a green cactus stem at a nursery. This is a moon cactus, and the coloured ball has no chlorophyll.
- Yellow lower leaves on a pot plant that has not been fed for a long time. The plant has moved the nitrogen from those leaves to new ones.
- A green film on a wet wall, a water tank or a temple pond in the monsoon. These are algae and cyanobacteria, catching light with chlorophyll.
- Raw green bananas at the vegetable shop and ripe yellow ones at the fruit shop. They are the same fruit before and after the starch turned into sugar.

## Open questions
- **How does a plant set the speed at which they use starch at night?** The steady rate has been measured many times. Scientists have at least two different explanations for how the plant does it, and the chemicals involved have not all been found.
- **Do all plants share out their night food in the same way as thale cress?** Most of the careful work was done on one small plant in laboratories. Rice and wheat store more sugar and less starch in their leaves, and far less is known about them.
- **Does the green sea slug really live on the chloroplasts taken from algae?** The stolen chloroplasts keep working for months. But related slugs lived just as long in the dark, so the chloroplasts may be mainly a food store.
- **Did life on Earth begin with chemosynthesis at deep-sea hot springs, before any living being could use light?** The oldest traces of life are hard to read, and nobody has yet made a living cell from chemicals in a laboratory.
- **Why did life settle on chlorophyll, which leaves much of the green light uncaught?** There are several ideas. One is that catching every colour would make the energy supply too jumpy to handle safely. None has been proved.

## Connected maths
- In Dawn Leaf, Dusk Leaf, the two dusk leaves scored 4 and 3 for starch. The two dawn leaves scored 1 and 0. What is the mean score for each time? What is the difference? **Dusk: 3.5. Dawn: 0.5. The difference is 3.**. Dusk: 4 + 3 = 7, and 7 ÷ 2 = 3.5. Dawn: 1 + 0 = 1, and 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5. Then 3.5 − 0.5 = 3. So the dusk leaves scored 3 points higher on a scale of 4. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- A leaf holds 60 units of starch at dusk. The night is 12 hours long. What steady rate uses it all exactly by dawn? In December the night is 15 hours. What rate is needed now? **5 units an hour for the 12-hour night. 4 units an hour for the 15-hour night.**. 60 ÷ 12 = 5. For the longer night, 60 ÷ 15 = 4. A longer night means the same starch must be spread more thinly, so the plant has less to grow on each hour. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- In the Starch Bank game you have 14 beans. You choose to spend 4 in each part of the night. Then the card says the night has 5 parts. Do your beans last? What is the biggest number you could safely have chosen? **No. The beans run out during the fourth part. The biggest safe number was 2.**. 4 beans in each of 5 parts needs 4 × 5 = 20 beans, and you have 14. After 3 parts you have spent 12, and only 2 are left for the fourth. For a safe number, divide: 14 ÷ 5 = 2 with 4 left over. So 2 in each part is safe, and 3 is not, because 3 × 5 = 15. (queues `math.logic.if-then.L5`)
- Scientists found that a thale cress leaf uses about 95 parts in every 100 of its starch by dawn. A leaf starts the night with 80 units. About how many units are used, and how many are left? **76 units are used and 4 are left.**. 95 parts in 100 is 95 per cent. 10 per cent of 80 is 8, so 5 per cent is 4. Then 80 − 4 = 76. The 4 units left are a small safety margin, in case dawn is a little late. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In Measure the Sun Catcher, three leaves had areas of 12, 18 and 24 square centimetres. The plant has 45 leaves. The round pot is 20 centimetres wide. About how many times bigger is the leaf area than the top of the pot? **About 2.7 times.**. Mean leaf area: 12 + 18 + 24 = 54, and 54 ÷ 3 = 18 square centimetres. All the leaves: 18 × 45 = 810 square centimetres. Pot: 20 × 20 = 400, and three quarters of 400 is 300 square centimetres. Then 810 ÷ 300 = 2.7. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L5`)
- One square metre of paddy field gives about 300 grams of rice in one crop. A child eats about 150 grams of rice a day. About how many square metres of field does the child's rice for one year need? **About 183 square metres, if the field gives one crop a year.**. 300 ÷ 150 = 2, so one square metre feeds the child for 2 days. A year has 365 days, and 365 ÷ 2 = 182.5. That rounds up to 183 square metres, which is about the floor of a large flat and a half. The numbers are rounded averages for India. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
