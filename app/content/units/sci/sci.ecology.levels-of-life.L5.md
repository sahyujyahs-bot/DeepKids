# From one living being to the whole Earth: how the levels of life depend on each other
`sci.ecology.levels-of-life.L5`

**Summary.** The air of Mars and the air of Venus are almost all carbon dioxide. The Earth sits between them, yet one fifth of the Earth's air is oxygen. Nearly all of that oxygen was made by living beings. Life is arranged in levels: one organism, a population, a community, an ecosystem and the whole biosphere. Each level depends on the others, and the living beings of the Earth have changed the air, the soil and the rain that they all depend on. Your child runs a paper planet of dark and pale daisies under a Sun that keeps getting hotter. They find out what the daisies do to the planet's temperature. Then they meet the Gaia idea, which says the Earth works as one self-steadying system, and the scientists who doubt it.

**Intro.** *Take one slow breath in. About one fifth of the air you just took in is oxygen. Who made that oxygen, and where? Say what you think, and why.* Most people answer 'the trees near my home'. That is only a small part of the answer. The oxygen in your breath was made over a very long time by green plants, by seaweeds and by tiny green cells who float in the sea. So one breath joins you to living beings far away, and to living beings from long ago. In this unit you find out how one living being depends on the whole living Earth. You also test whether living beings can keep a planet steady.

## Terms
- **Organism**: An organism is one living being. A crow, a neem tree, a mushroom, a bacterium and you are each one organism. *e.g.* The crow on your window ledge is one organism.
- **Species**: A species is one kind of living being. Members of a species can have young together, and their young can have young too. *e.g.* All house crows are one species. Jungle crows are a different species.
- **Population**: A population is all the members of one species who live in one place. *e.g.* All the house crows of Mylapore in Chennai are one population.
- **Community**: A community is all the populations of every species who live in one place. It includes the plants, animals, fungi and microbes. *e.g.* The crows, mynas, squirrels, neem trees, ants and soil bacteria of one street are a community.
- **Ecosystem**: An ecosystem is a community together with the non-living things around them. The non-living things are the water, air, soil, rock, sunlight and warmth. *e.g.* A temple tank is an ecosystem. It has fish, frogs, water plants and microbes, and also water, mud, air and sunlight.
- **Biosphere**: The biosphere is the part of the Earth where living beings are found. It is all the ecosystems of the Earth taken together. *e.g.* The deep sea, the Thar desert, a paddy field in Thanjavur and the air where a vulture flies are all parts of the biosphere.
- **Atmosphere**: The atmosphere is the layer of air around a planet. *e.g.* The Earth's atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen. The atmosphere of Mars is mostly carbon dioxide.
- **System**: A system is a set of parts that affect each other. Because the parts affect each other, a change in one part changes the others. *e.g.* A cycle is a system. The pedals turn the chain, the chain turns the wheel, and the brake slows the wheel.
- **Emergent property**: An emergent property is a feature that a whole has, and that none of its parts has alone. *e.g.* A heap of rice has a slope. One grain of rice has no slope.
- **Feedback**: Feedback happens when the result of a change comes back round and affects that same change. *e.g.* You feel hot, so you sweat. The sweat cools you, so you sweat less. The result came back and changed what started it.
- **Balancing feedback**: Balancing feedback works against a change and brings things back towards where they were. Scientists also call it negative feedback. Here 'negative' means 'against the change'. It does not mean 'bad'. *e.g.* When the room gets warm, an air-conditioner with a thermostat cools it. When the room gets cold, the cooling stops.
- **Reinforcing feedback**: Reinforcing feedback adds to a change, so the change grows bigger and bigger. Scientists also call it positive feedback. Here 'positive' means 'adds to the change'. It does not mean 'good'. *e.g.* When white ice melts, dark sea is left. Dark sea takes in more sunlight, so the place warms and more ice melts.
- **Homeostasis**: Homeostasis means keeping conditions inside steady while conditions outside change. *e.g.* Your body stays close to 37 degrees Celsius on a hot day in May and on a cold night in December.
- **Albedo**: Albedo is the share of the sunlight falling on a surface that the surface reflects back. A pale surface has a high albedo. A dark surface has a low albedo. *e.g.* Fresh snow reflects most of the sunlight that falls on it. The dark sea reflects very little.
- **Model**: A model is a simple copy of a real thing, made to find out how the real thing might work. A model can be a drawing, a set of rules or a computer program. *e.g.* The Daisy Planet is a model. It has only daisies, a Sun and three rules.
- **Hypothesis**: A hypothesis is a possible explanation that can be tested against evidence. *e.g.* 'Living beings keep the Earth's temperature steady' is a hypothesis. Scientists test it against what the rocks and the air show.
- **Gaia hypothesis**: The Gaia hypothesis says that the living beings of the Earth, together with the air, sea and rocks, form one system. It says this system keeps the Earth fit for life. Gaia is said 'guy-ah'. *e.g.* James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis put the Gaia hypothesis forward in the 1970s. Scientists still argue about it.
- **Phytoplankton**: Phytoplankton are tiny living beings who float in the sea and in lakes. They make their food from carbon dioxide and water, using sunlight. Most are too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* Phytoplankton make about half of all the oxygen that living beings make each year.

## Explanations
### The five levels of life · *defined*
**Definition:** Ecologists study life at five levels. They are one organism, a population, a community, an ecosystem and the biosphere. Each level contains all the levels below it.

An ecologist is a scientist who studies how living beings live with each other and with their surroundings. One living being is an organism. All the organisms of one species in one place are a population. All the populations in that place, of every species, are a community. The community together with the water, air, soil and sunlight is an ecosystem. All the ecosystems of the Earth together are the biosphere. The levels are a way of looking, like zooming out with a camera. Nothing new is added to the world at each level. You only see more of what was already joined together.
- e.g. One lion-tailed macaque in a tree in Silent Valley, Kerala, is an organism. All the lion-tailed macaques of Silent Valley are a population.
- e.g. The macaques, the trees, the hornbills, the frogs, the fungi and the soil microbes of Silent Valley are a community.
- e.g. Add the streams, the rain, the soil and the mist, and Silent Valley is an ecosystem. Silent Valley is one small part of the biosphere.
### No level can live alone · *defined*
**Definition:** Every level depends on the level above it for what it needs, and every level is made by the beings in the level below it.

Look upward first. One organism needs a population, because most animals and many plants need a mate to have young. A population needs a community, because its members need food, and many need other species to carry their pollen or seeds. A community needs an ecosystem, because every living being needs water, air and warmth. An ecosystem needs the biosphere, because its rain and its air come from far away. Now look downward. A population is only its organisms. A community is only its populations. The biosphere is only its ecosystems. So when the organisms change, every level above them changes too. Changes travel both ways, up and down.
- e.g. A single wild fig tree cannot make seeds alone. Each kind of fig needs their own kind of tiny fig wasp to carry the pollen.
- e.g. A forest in the Western Ghats cannot make the monsoon. The wet wind comes from the Arabian Sea, thousands of kilometres of ocean away.
- e.g. About 2,400 million years ago, tiny bacteria began to fill the air with oxygen. Organisms too small to see changed the whole biosphere.
### Emergent properties: what a whole has that its parts do not · *defined*
**Definition:** An emergent property is a feature that a whole has and that none of its parts has alone. It comes from the way the parts act on each other.

Each level of life has properties that the level below does not have. One crow cannot have a birth rate, but a population of crows can. One species cannot have a food web, but a community can. A community cannot have a water cycle, but an ecosystem can. These properties are real, and they can be measured. Yet you will not find them by looking at one part, however closely you look. That is why ecologists study each level in its own right. The reason is simple. The property comes from many parts acting on each other, so it needs many parts.
- e.g. Honeybees keep the part of the hive where their young grow at about 32 to 36 degrees Celsius. They shiver to warm it and fan their wings to cool it. One bee alone cannot hold any place at a steady temperature.
- e.g. Pour rice slowly on a plate, and the heap always makes about the same slope. No single grain has a slope.
- e.g. You can ride a cycle. No single cell of your body can ride one.
### The biosphere is a very thin layer · *defined*
**Definition:** The biosphere is the part of the Earth where living beings are found. Compared with the size of the Earth, it is very thin.

The word biosphere was first used by the geologist Eduard Suess in 1875. In 1926 the Russian scientist Vladimir Vernadsky wrote a book called The Biosphere. He said that living beings are a force that shapes the planet, as strong as wind or water. Living beings have been found at the bottom of the deepest sea, about 11 kilometres down. A vulture has been recorded flying about 11 kilometres up. Microbes also live in rock several kilometres under the ground. So nearly all life is in a layer about 20 kilometres thick. The Earth is about 12,700 kilometres across. If the Earth were a ball 1 metre across, that layer would be less than 2 millimetres thick.
- e.g. A football is about 22 centimetres across. On an Earth that size, the biosphere would be thinner than half a millimetre.
- e.g. Mount Everest is less than 9 kilometres high. Bar-headed geese cross the Himalayas flying more than 7 kilometres up.
### Energy passes through the biosphere, but matter stays and goes round · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth takes in energy as sunlight and sends almost exactly the same amount back out to space. Almost no matter enters or leaves, so the same atoms are used again and again.

Sunlight reaches the Earth all the time. The Earth sends energy back out to space all the time, partly as reflected light and mostly as heat. If more came in than went out, the Earth would keep getting hotter. Matter is different. Apart from a little dust from space and a little gas that escapes, the Earth keeps the same atoms. So the biosphere has to recycle. The waste of one living being must be the food of another. Animals breathe out carbon dioxide, and plants take carbon dioxide in. Plants give out oxygen, and animals take oxygen in. From 1991 to 1993, eight people lived sealed inside a huge glass building in Arizona, in the United States, called Biosphere 2. It held a small rainforest, a small sea and a farm. Within sixteen months the oxygen in its air fell from about 21 parts in 100 to about 14. The soil had been made very rich in compost, and the microbes in the soil used up oxygen faster than the plants made it. Oxygen had to be pumped in. A working biosphere is much harder to build than anyone expected.
- e.g. A sealed glass jar with damp soil and a small plant can live for months on a windowsill. Light goes in and heat comes out, but no water or air is added.
- e.g. The water you drank today has been drunk before, many times, by other animals and by plants.
### Living beings made the air · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's air is very different from the air of the planets next to it, and living beings are the reason. Nearly all the oxygen in the air was made by photosynthesis.

Photosynthesis is the way plants, seaweeds and some bacteria make food from carbon dioxide and water, using light. Oxygen is given out as they do it. The young Earth had almost no oxygen in its air. About 2,400 million years ago, bacteria called cyanobacteria had made so much oxygen that it began to stay in the air. Scientists call this the Great Oxidation Event. They know about it from rocks. Rocks older than that hold minerals that cannot last where there is oxygen. Today oxygen is 21 parts in every 100 of the air. Oxygen reacts easily. It rusts iron and it burns wood. So if living beings stopped making oxygen, the oxygen in the air would slowly be used up. No life has been found on Venus or Mars. Their air shows what a planet's air is like with no life at work. On both, 95 parts or more in every 100 are carbon dioxide.
- e.g. Phytoplankton in the sea do about half of all the photosynthesis on the Earth. Plants on land do the other half.
- e.g. In 1986, Sallie Chisholm and her team found Prochlorococcus in the sea. They are the smallest and the most numerous of all the cells who photosynthesise.
- e.g. The oxygen in the air built up over many millions of years. Each year, living beings use up almost as much oxygen as they make.
### Living beings also move water, make soil and change the weather · *defined*
**Definition:** Living beings change the non-living parts of their ecosystems. Some of these changes reach far beyond the place where those beings live.

A tree draws water up from the soil and gives it out from her leaves as water vapour. A whole forest gives out so much that the vapour falls again as rain somewhere else. One study from the Indian Institute of Technology Bombay in 2018 used a computer model of the monsoon. It estimated that the forests of the Western Ghats supply 25 to 40 parts in every 100 of the monsoon rain that falls on Tamil Nadu. Soil is made by life too. Rock is broken up by roots, fungi and microbes, and mixed with dead leaves by earthworms and termites. Life even links ecosystems across an ocean. Wind carries dust from the Sahara desert in Africa to the Amazon rainforest in South America. The dust holds phosphorus, which plants need. Much of that dust comes from the dry bed of an old lake, and it is rich in the remains of tiny beings who lived in that lake.
- e.g. Stand under a big banyan on a hot afternoon. The air is cooler there, partly because the leaves are giving out water.
- e.g. A satellite study in 2015 estimated that about 22,000 tonnes of phosphorus reach the Amazon in Sahara dust every year.
- e.g. Coral animals built the islands of Lakshadweep. The land there was made by living beings.
### Feedback: balancing and reinforcing · *defined*
**Definition:** Feedback happens when the result of a change comes back and affects that change. Balancing feedback works against the change. Reinforcing feedback adds to it.

A system stays steady when it has balancing feedback. Your body is one example. When you get hot, you sweat, and the sweat cools you. When you get cold, you shiver, and the shivering warms you. In both cases the result works against the change. Reinforcing feedback does the opposite. A small change causes more of the same change, so things run away in one direction. Ice shows this. Ice is pale, so it reflects sunlight. If some ice melts, the dark ground or sea takes in more sunlight. That warms the place, and more ice melts. The Earth has many loops of both kinds, running at the same time. Whether the Earth stays steady depends on which loops are stronger.
- e.g. Balancing: more rats in a paddy field means more food for rat snakes. More snakes then eat more rats, so the rats become fewer again.
- e.g. Reinforcing: a fire dries the leaves next to it. Dry leaves catch fire easily, so the fire spreads faster.
- e.g. Balancing, but very slow: warm, wet weather wears rock away faster. Wearing rock takes carbon dioxide out of the air, and that cools the Earth over about a million years.
### The Gaia hypothesis · *debated*
In the 1960s, the scientist James Lovelock was asked to help look for life on Mars. He noticed how strange the Earth's air is compared with the air of Mars and Venus. In the 1970s he and the biologist Lynn Margulis put forward the Gaia hypothesis. It says that living beings, together with the air, the sea and the rocks, form one system that keeps the Earth fit for life. The name Gaia comes from an old Greek name for the Earth. Lovelock's neighbour, the writer William Golding, suggested it. Almost all scientists now agree with one part of the idea: life changes the planet a great deal. The part they argue about is whether life keeps the planet steady, and whether it is right to say the Earth acts as one whole.
- e.g. For: when life began, the Sun gave only about three quarters of the light it gives today. Yet the Earth has had liquid water for nearly all that time.
- e.g. Against: between about 720 and 635 million years ago, the Earth froze almost completely, at least twice. The system did not hold steady then.
- **James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis (1974):** Living beings and their surroundings together form one system with balancing feedback. That system has kept the temperature and the air of the Earth within the range that life can bear, for more than three thousand million years.
- **Ford Doolittle (1981) and Richard Dawkins (1982):** Living beings are shaped by natural selection. This means that those who leave more young pass on their features. Natural selection helps an organism or their family. It cannot make organisms work for the good of a whole planet. There is also only one Earth, so planets cannot have been selected against each other.
- **James Kirchner (1989 and 2002):** The hypothesis is said in many different ways. The weak forms are true but not new. The strong forms cannot be tested. Life makes reinforcing feedback as well as balancing feedback, so life can make conditions less steady too.
- **Peter Ward (2009):** Life has often harmed life. When bacteria first filled the air with oxygen, that oxygen was probably a poison to most living beings of the time. Ward calls his idea the Medea hypothesis, after a mother in an old Greek story who harms her own children.
- **Toby Tyrrell (2013):** The Earth may have stayed fit for life partly through luck. We could only be here to ask the question on a planet where things went well. So a long steady record is not proof of a steadying system.
- **Earth system scientists (Amsterdam Declaration, 2001):** Scientists from four international research programmes agreed on this statement: the Earth behaves as a single, self-regulating system made of physical, chemical, biological and human parts. They did not say the system is sure to stay good for us.
### The Daisy Planet: what a model can show, and what it cannot · *idea*
Critics said Gaia would need living beings to plan together for the good of the planet. In 1983, Andrew Watson and James Lovelock answered with a model called Daisyworld. Their planet has only dark daisies and pale daisies, under a Sun that slowly gets brighter. Dark daisies take in more sunlight, so they warm the ground. Pale daisies reflect more, so they cool it. Daisies only grow where the warmth suits them. Yet in the model, the planet's temperature stays almost level for a long time. The Daisy Planet Test in this unit is a simple paper version of that model. The model shows one thing clearly. Steadying can come from living beings who only grow where it suits them. No planning together is needed. But a model shows what could happen. It does not show what does happen on the real Earth. The real Earth has millions of species, and many of them push conditions away from steady.
- e.g. In the Daisy Planet Test, the bare planet warms from 8 to 47 degrees. The planet with daisies stays between 20 and 27 degrees for twelve rounds.
- e.g. The model has a limit. When every square is full of pale daisies, the daisies can cool the planet no further, and the temperature starts to climb.
- **Andrew Watson and James Lovelock (1983):** The model proves that a planet can regulate its temperature without foresight or planning. The daisies only follow their own needs.
- **Critics such as James Kirchner:** The model was built so that what is good for each daisy is also good for the planet. On the real Earth the two often differ. So the model shows that regulation is possible, not that it is likely.
### Is the Earth alive? · *debated*
This is a question about what the word 'alive' means, as well as a question about the Earth. Living organisms take in energy, keep themselves steady, grow and have young. The Earth takes in energy and may keep itself steady. The Earth does not have young, and has no parents. People who think carefully about this do not agree, and the answer depends on which signs of life you count as the important ones.
- e.g. A honeybee colony keeps the hive's warmth steady, gathers food and founds new colonies. Many biologists call a colony a 'superorganism'.
- e.g. A candle flame takes in fuel and keeps its shape, but almost nobody calls a flame alive.
- **James Lovelock:** The Earth regulates itself in the way a living body does. So in his books he often wrote of the Earth as alive. He also said this was a way of speaking, to help people see the Earth as one system.
- **Lynn Margulis:** She did not like calling the Earth one organism. She pointed out that no organism eats its own waste, but the biosphere recycles everything. She saw Gaia as a huge set of ecosystems acting on each other.
- **Richard Dawkins and many biologists:** Living beings come from parents and are shaped by natural selection. The Earth has no parents, has no young and does not compete with other planets. So the Earth is a home for life, and not a living being.
- **The Atharva Veda and many peoples who live close to forests and rivers:** The Earth is a mother, and living beings are her children. Many communities across India treat a hill, a river or a grove as a living someone, and protect them for that reason.
### People in the biosphere · *defined*
**Definition:** Humans are one species among millions, but today humans change every level of life, up to the whole biosphere.

Other beings have changed the whole Earth before. Cyanobacteria did it with oxygen. Humans are now doing it with carbon dioxide. Coal, oil and gas are the remains of ancient living beings, and burning them puts their carbon into the air. Before factories, the air held about 280 parts of carbon dioxide in every million. Now it holds more than 420. Carbon dioxide traps heat, so the Earth is warming. The change is fast. Forests, seas and rocks do take carbon dioxide out of the air, but far more slowly than people are adding it. People have also mended a harm that reached the whole biosphere. In the 1970s, scientists found that gases from fridges and spray cans were destroying the ozone in the upper air. Ozone shields living beings from harmful rays of the Sun. In 1987 the countries of the world agreed to stop making those gases, and the ozone is slowly recovering.
- e.g. James Lovelock built the instrument that first measured those fridge gases in air far out at sea. It showed that they had spread across the world.
- e.g. When a forest in the Western Ghats is cut, one hornbill loses her nest tree. Farms far to the east may also lose some of their rain.

## Mostly agreed
- Life can be studied at five levels: organism, population, community, ecosystem and biosphere. Each level has properties the level below does not have. *How we know:* A birth rate can be measured only for a population. A food web can be drawn only for a community. A water cycle can be traced only through an ecosystem.
- Nearly all the oxygen in the Earth's air was made by living beings through photosynthesis. *How we know:* Rocks older than about 2,400 million years hold minerals that cannot last in air with oxygen. Younger rocks do not. Mars and Venus, with no known life, have almost no oxygen in their air.
- Living beings change the air, water, soil and climate around them, sometimes across the whole Earth. *How we know:* Measurements show forests returning rain to the air, microbes and roots turning rock into soil, and phytoplankton doing about half of the Earth's photosynthesis.
- The Earth has both balancing and reinforcing feedback loops, and living beings take part in both kinds. *How we know:* Melting ice speeds up warming, which is reinforcing. Warm weather wears rock faster and that removes carbon dioxide from the air, which is balancing. Both have been measured.
- The Earth has had liquid water and living beings for more than 3,500 million years, though the young Sun gave only about three quarters of today's light. *How we know:* The oldest signs of life in rocks are about 3,500 million years old or older. Rocks formed in water are found from every age since.
- The Earth has not always been steady. It has been almost completely frozen, and it has been much hotter than today. *How we know:* Rocks dropped by ice are found in places that were near the equator between about 720 and 635 million years ago.

## How we found out
**James Lovelock and Dian Hitchcock, who worked out how to find life from a planet's air. Lynn Margulis, who showed which microbes make which gases., 1965 (Lovelock's first paper on the idea), 1967 (Hitchcock and Lovelock's paper) and 1974 (Lovelock and Margulis's paper on the Gaia hypothesis).** Sixty years ago, most scientists treated a planet's air as the work of volcanoes, rocks and sunlight. They knew that plants give out oxygen. But few thought that living beings decide what the whole air is made of. In the 1960s, the American space agency NASA was planning to look for life on Mars. It asked the scientist James Lovelock to help. Lovelock and the philosopher Dian Hitchcock said nobody needed to land a machine to dig in the soil. They said to look at the air of Mars through a telescope. On a planet with no life, the gases in the air will have reacted with each other until nothing more can happen. The Earth's air is not like that. It holds oxygen and methane together, and those two gases destroy each other. Living beings must be making both all the time. The air of Mars turned out to be almost all carbon dioxide, with nothing still reacting. Hitchcock and Lovelock published the idea in 1967. So on the Earth, living beings decide what the air is made of. Scientists now use the same test to look for life on planets around other stars.  
*What people believed before:* That volcanoes, rocks and sunlight decide what a planet's air is made of, and living beings only fit themselves to it. The Earth's air holds gases that destroy each other, such as oxygen and methane. They can only stay there because living beings keep making them.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Earth regulates itself for the good of life.** (James Lovelock, in his early writing on Gaia in the 1970s; *minority*)  
  For: The air and the temperature have stayed within the range life can bear for a very long time, while the Sun grew brighter. Living beings are part of many balancing loops.  
  Against: Natural selection cannot make living beings work for a planet's good. Life has also caused disasters, such as the first spread of oxygen. Lovelock later said that the regulation is not done on purpose.
- **The Amazon rainforest makes 20 parts in every 100 of the oxygen we breathe.** (Many news reports and posters; *rejected*)  
  For: The Amazon is huge, and the trees there do a great deal of photosynthesis.  
  Against: The trees, animals and microbes of the forest use up almost all the oxygen that the forest makes. So the forest adds almost nothing to the oxygen in the air. The Amazon matters greatly for rain, for carbon and for the millions of beings who live there.
- **Living beings fit themselves to their surroundings, and the surroundings stay as they are.** (Many school science books; *rejected*)  
  For: A camel is suited to the desert and a fish to the water. Living beings clearly are shaped by where they live.  
  Against: Living beings also shape where they live. Bacteria and plants made the oxygen in the air. Earthworms and microbes make soil. Forests make some of their own rain. The fitting goes both ways.
- **Life makes the Earth worse for life as often as it makes it better.** (Peter Ward, in his book The Medea Hypothesis, 2009; *debated*)  
  For: Oxygen from the first photosynthesising bacteria poisoned most life of the time. Several of the great die-offs in Earth's history may have been set off by living beings.  
  Against: After each disaster, life spread again in new forms. Over the whole record, the Earth has stayed fit for life. Most scientists think Ward shows that Gaia is too simple, not that the opposite is true.
- **The Daisy Planet model proves that Gaia is real.** (Some popular books and websites; *rejected*)  
  For: The model does regulate its temperature, with no planning by the daisies.  
  Against: A model shows what could happen under its own rules. The rules were chosen so that each daisy's needs match the planet's needs. Whether the real Earth works that way has to be tested on the real Earth.

## Roots
We have learned that life is arranged in levels, and that living beings have changed the air and the warmth of the whole Earth. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Daisy Planet Test, the planet's warmth decided which daisies grew. The daisies then changed the planet's warmth. The oxygen in the air was made by living beings, and that oxygen decides who can live here now. So living beings and the place they live in keep making each other.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say that living beings 'adapt to their environment'. That makes the environment sound like a fixed stage that was there first. The air, the soil and some of the rain were made by living beings, so the stage is partly made by the actors.  
*Evidence:* In the Daisy Planet Test, the bare planet warmed from 8 to 47 degrees, but the planet with daisies stayed between 20 and 27 degrees for twelve rounds. On the real Earth, rocks older than about 2,400 million years show that the air once had almost no oxygen. Bacteria and plants made it.

- *who am i:* You are one organism. You are also part of a population, a community, an ecosystem and the biosphere, all at the same moment. Each breath you take was prepared by other living beings.
- *what is life:* Life does not only live on the Earth. Life has changed the Earth's air, soil and rain, and those changes have changed life in turn.
- *what is this world:* The world around you is partly made by the living. The air in this room would be a different air if nothing had ever lived on the Earth.

A crow looks like one separate thing. Zoom out, and the crow is part of a population, then a community, then an ecosystem, then the biosphere. At no step do you reach a place where the crow stops and the rest begins. The crow's breath is the forest's air, and the forest's air is the whole Earth's air. Consciousness is in everything, and so it is in the Earth as a whole. Many scientists who accept everything else in this unit say the Earth is a system and not a mind.

- **Atharva Veda 12.1.12:** The Earth is my mother, and I am her son. (This hymn was sung long before anyone could measure the air. The science agrees with the plain part of it. Every atom of your body, and every breath, came to you from the Earth and the living beings on the Earth.)
- **Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.40:** Always think of the universe as one living being, with one substance and one soul. See how all things work together to cause all that happens. (Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor who wrote this about 1,850 years ago. The Gaia hypothesis asks a smaller question about just one planet: do the living and the non-living parts of the Earth work together as one system?)
- **Thirukkural 20:** If the world cannot go on without water, then water cannot go on without rain. (Thiruvalluvar saw that life depends on something far above any one field or village. Today we can add a step. Part of the rain depends on forests, so the rain also depends on life.)
- Science (settled): Nearly all the oxygen in the Earth's air was made by living beings.
- Science (settled): The Earth behaves as one system in which living beings, air, sea and rock all affect each other.
- Science (debated): Whether that system keeps the Earth fit for life, as the Gaia hypothesis says, is still argued about.
- Other view, Richard Dawkins and many other biologists: The Earth is not a living being and has no aims. Each organism lives for themself and their family. If the planet has stayed fit for life, that is partly the result of many separate struggles and partly good luck. Calling the Earth one whole hides how much living beings compete.

## Story: The Line Nobody Drew
*A made-up story. Koodu, the farm near Kotagiri and all the people in it are imagined. The science is real, and it comes from a real event. From 1991 to 1993, eight people lived sealed inside a glass building called Biosphere 2 in Arizona, in the United States. The oxygen in their air fell from about 21 parts in 100 to about 14. The cause was the microbes in the rich soil, who were using up oxygen faster than the plants could make it. Oxygen had to be pumped in. A compost heap really does warm up and steam on a cold morning, because the microbes in the heap give out heat as they respire.*

Every evening at six, Kayal walked up the tea slope to the glass house and pressed her palm flat against the wall. On the other side, her mother pressed back. The glass was cold, and as thick as a thumb.

The glass house was called Koodu, which means nest. Three women had been sealed inside it for fifty-two days. Nothing went in and nothing came out, except sunlight, and voices on the telephone. Their water went round and round. Their air went round and round. They grew their food in long beds of black soil, and the plants in the beds made the oxygen the women breathed. If three people could live a hundred days like that on a hillside near Kotagiri, people might one day live like that much further away.

Kayal was ten. She lived for these months with Shanthi Athai, her aunt, who ran the compost yard at the bottom of the farm.

"You look tired, Amma," Kayal said into the telephone.

"I climbed the ladder to the bean vines," her mother said, and laughed. "Eleven steps. I had to sit down at the top." She laughed again, but Kayal saw her hand go to her chest.

On the wall by the door was a board with numbers, for anyone outside to read. Kayal read them every day, as other children read cricket scores. Water: steady. Carbon dioxide: steady, because a machine inside caught the extra and held it. Oxygen: 17.9.

On the first day the oxygen had been 21, the same as on the hillside. Kayal had the whole column in her notebook. It went down like stairs.

Prakash sir, the engineer, was on his knees by the wall again with a brush and a tin of soapy water. He painted the soap along every joint in the glass and watched for bubbles.

"Still looking for the leak, sir?"

"There must be a leak." He sat back on his heels. "The plants are healthy. The lamps are fine. I counted the leaves myself. Those plants make enough oxygen for three people, with some to spare. So where is the oxygen going?"

Kayal thought about it on the way down the slope. If there was a hole, air would come in from the hillside. The air on the hillside was 21. A hole would push the number up, and the number was going down. She did not say so. He was a kind man, and he had not slept much.

The next morning was the coldest of the year. There was frost on the tea, white and thin, like sugar on a plate. Athai woke her before the sun was up. "The big heap must be turned today. Come and hold the lamp."

The heap stood taller than Kayal, a long dark hill of leaves, cow dung and kitchen peelings from the whole farm. Athai drove the fork in and pulled.

Steam poured out. It rose in the lamplight, thick as the steam off a pot of rice. Kayal pulled off her glove and held her hand over the hole. The air coming out was warm. The inside of the heap was hot, on a morning of frost.

"Who lit a fire in there?"

"Nobody lit anything." Athai turned another forkful. "They are eating. More of them in one handful than there are people in India. Eating all night, and breathing all night. That is where the heat comes from."

"Breathing?"

"Why do you think I turn the heap? If I leave them packed tight, they use up the air inside and the heap goes sour. They need air as much as you do." She pushed the fork in again. "More than you do. There are more of them."

Kayal stood very still, with the lamp in her hand and the steam going past her face.

She was remembering a morning two months ago. A line of people with sacks on their shoulders, walking up the slope to Koodu before the door was sealed. Sixty sacks. Athai had stood at the gate, counting them out, proud. Her best compost, two years old, black as coffee powder, for the vegetable beds.

"Athai. How many people are breathing inside Koodu?"

"Three. You know that."

"And the hens?"

"Four hens."

"And who else?"

Athai stopped with the fork in the air. She looked at the heap. Then she looked up the hill at the glass, which was just turning pink.

Prakash sir did not laugh. He listened to all of it with his tea going cold. Then he fetched a big glass jar with a tight lid, and a small meter on a wire. He asked for a spadeful of soil from the same sacks. There were two left over in the shed. He put the soil in the jar with the meter, screwed the lid down and wrote the time on it.

"Twenty-one," he said. "Now we wait."

By evening the meter in the jar said nineteen. By the next morning it said sixteen. There was no plant in the jar and no person. There was only soil, sitting quietly in the dark.

He took the jar up to the glass and held it against the wall for the three women to see. Kayal's mother read the number. She looked at the long black beds behind her, and then she put both hands over her mouth.

That afternoon she unrolled the big plan of Koodu on the floor inside, where Kayal could see it through the glass. It was a drawing of everything that breathed in and everything that breathed out, with an arrow for each. There was an arrow for each woman. There was one for the hens, and one for the beans, the tomatoes and the greens.

"We counted everybody," her mother said into the telephone. Her voice was thin. "We were so careful."

"You counted everybody you could see," said Kayal.

Her mother took a pencil. Under the beds on the plan she drew one more arrow. It was thicker than all the others.

They did not break the seal. A cylinder of oxygen went in through the small hatch that week, and half the compost came out in the same sacks. Athai counted them back at the gate, and said nothing, and patted each one as it passed.

The numbers on the board began to climb, slowly, like someone going up a ladder and stopping to rest.

On the morning it reached 20, Kayal went down to the yard before school. She pulled off her glove and held her hand over the big heap. It was warm.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the three women, the hens and the plants were not the only beings breathing the air of the sealed house. The soil held countless microbes, and they were using oxygen too. A living place is a whole system, and the beings nobody sees are part of the system. Kayal works it out from the steaming compost heap. Let your child reach this before you say it.

- Ask: Why did Kayal think a hole in the glass could not explain the falling oxygen number?
- Ask: What did the steam from the compost heap show?
- Ask: The plan had an arrow for everyone who breathes. Who was left out, and why?
- Ask: Who breathes the air in your home, besides the people?
- Ask: The Earth is sealed too. Nothing much comes in except sunlight. What do you think keeps the Earth's oxygen from running down?

- **safety:** Do this where direct sunlight falls, but never look at the Sun. Put glass tumblers where they cannot be knocked over.
- **record:** Draw the two tumblers. Write which one felt warmer, and the two temperatures if you measured them. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Tumbler | How the water feels at the start | How the water feels after 30 minutes | Temperature after 30 minutes, if you have a thermometer
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wrap one tumbler in black cloth and the other in white cloth. Cover the sides fully, in one layer. 2. Hold each cloth on with a rubber band. 3. Pour 100 millilitres of tap water into each tumbler. That is about half a small tumbler. 4. Ask your child to dip a finger of each hand into the two tumblers. They should feel the same.  
  *Parent says:* Both tumblers have the same water from the same tap. Dip a finger in each. Do they feel the same now?  
  *Child mode:* Wrap one tumbler in black cloth. Wrap the other in white cloth. Pour the same water into each. Dip a finger in each. Do they feel the same?
  - Note: If you have a thermometer, measure both now. They should agree to within one degree.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand both tumblers side by side in direct sunlight. 2. Check that no shadow falls on either one. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write them down. 4. Note the time.  
  *Parent says:* Both will stand in the same sun for 30 minutes. Which water will be warmer at the end? Or will they be the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Stand both tumblers in the sun. Which water will be warmer after 30 minutes? Write your guess. Write why you think so.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The black one, because dark clothes feel hotter in the sun.; The same, because the same sun falls on both. (A fair guess. The test will show.))
- **s3 (30 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave the tumblers in the sun for 30 minutes. Do not move them. 2. While you wait, lay your hand on a dark thing and a pale thing that are already in the sun. Try a dark and a pale tile, or two parked scooters. 3. Use the rest of the time to draw the two tumblers in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* While we wait, let's feel things that are already in the sun. Find one dark thing and one pale thing. Which is hotter to touch?  
  *Child mode:* Leave the tumblers for 30 minutes. Find a dark thing and a pale thing in the sun. Touch each one quickly. Which is hotter? Draw the two tumblers.
  - Note: Metal in strong sun can be hot enough to hurt, so touch it first yourself. You need not stand by the tumblers for the whole 30 minutes.
  - Wait: Leave the tumblers in the sun for 30 minutes.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 30 minutes, ask your child to dip a finger of each hand into the two tumblers at the same moment. 2. If you have a thermometer, measure the black one, then the white one. 3. Write the results in the table. 4. Ask your child what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 5. Ask how this result fits the Daisy Planet rules.  
  *Parent says:* Dip a finger in each at the same time. Which is warmer? What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Dip a finger in each tumbler at the same time. Which is warmer? Say what you guessed. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Why did the water in the black cloth get warmer? (good: Black cloth takes in more of the sunlight and turns it into heat.; White cloth reflects more of the sunlight away.)
  - Ask: How does this fit the Daisy Planet rules? (good: Dark daisies take in more sunlight, so they warm the ground.; Pale daisies reflect sunlight, so they cool the ground.)
  - Note: In strong sun the black tumbler is usually 3 to 8 degrees warmer, which fingers can feel easily. If the sky was hazy and you feel no difference, leave them for another 30 minutes. On a cloudy day, wait for a sunny one. An LED lamp gives too little heat for this test.
  - Check: Black cloth and white cloth lie in the same sunlight. Why does the black cloth get hotter? → **Black cloth takes in more of the light**
### e3 · The Balancing Stick: what does a steady system need? (22 min)
- **question:** You balance a stick upright on your palm. Does the stick stay up longer when you watch the top of the stick, when you watch your hand, or when your eyes are closed?
- **predict:** Guess first. In which of the three ways will the stick stay up longest: watching the top, watching your hand, or eyes closed? In which will it fall soonest? Why do you think so? Write your guesses and your reason.
- **fair test:** Use the same stick, the same hand and the same spot in the room every time. Start every try the same way, with the stick held upright and then let go. Do 3 tries of each way, because one try can be a fluke. Change only where your eyes look.
- **safety:** Do this in a clear space, away from windows, lamps and other people's faces. With eyes closed, stand still and let the stick fall.
- **record:** Fill in the table with 9 times. Circle the middle value for each way. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Where the eyes look | Try 1 (seconds) | Try 2 (seconds) | Try 3 (seconds) | Middle value (seconds)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear a space. 2. Show how to stand the stick upright on a flat palm, hold it with the other hand, and then let go. 3. Let your child practise for two minutes, looking wherever they like. 4. Ask what their hand does when the stick starts to tip.  
  *Parent says:* Stand the stick on your palm and let go. Keep it up for as long as you can. What does your hand do when the stick starts to tip?  
  *Child mode:* Stand the stick on your flat palm. Let go with the other hand. Keep the stick up. What does your hand do when the stick tips?
  - Ask: What does your hand do when the stick starts to tip? (good: My hand moves the same way as the tip, to get back under the stick.; I see the stick lean and I move to catch it.)
  - Note: A longer stick is easier, because it tips more slowly. If the stick falls at once, use a longer one.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the three ways: watching the top of the stick, watching the hand, and eyes closed. 2. Ask which way will keep the stick up longest, which will be shortest, and why. 3. Write the guesses and the reason down.  
  *Parent says:* You will try three ways. Watch the top, watch your hand, or close your eyes. Which will keep the stick up longest? Which will be shortest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* There are three ways: watch the top, watch your hand, eyes closed. Which will be longest? Which will be shortest? Write your guesses and why.
- **s3 (11 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child balances the stick while watching the top. Time from letting go until the stick falls or is caught. 2. Do 3 tries and write each time. 3. Do 3 tries watching only the hand. 4. Do 3 tries with eyes closed. Stand close, and catch the stick if it falls towards anyone. 5. Stop any try at 30 seconds and write '30 or more'.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? Let go. I am timing. Keep your eyes where we agreed for the whole try.  
  *Child mode:* Do 3 tries watching the top. Do 3 tries watching your hand. Do 3 tries with eyes closed. A grown-up times each try. Write down all 9 times.
  - Note: Most children keep the stick up for 5 to 20 seconds watching the top, a few seconds watching the hand, and about 1 second with eyes closed. Times vary a lot from try to try, which is why there are three.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each way, put the 3 times in order and circle the middle one. 2. Ask your child what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 3. Ask what the hand needed in order to keep the stick up.  
  *Parent says:* Circle the middle time for each way. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why was closing your eyes so much harder?  
  *Child mode:* Circle the middle time for each way. Say what you guessed. Say what you saw. Say why closed eyes made it so hard.
  - Ask: Why did the stick fall so quickly when your eyes were closed? (good: My hand could not tell which way the stick was tipping.; The news about the tip did not reach me, so I could not correct it.; I could only feel the tip when it was already too late.)
  - Ask: Why was watching the top better than watching the hand? (good: The top moves furthest, so I saw the tip earlier.; I got the news sooner, so my correction was in time.)
  - Note: Balancing feedback needs three things: a way to notice the change, a way to act against it, and enough speed. With eyes closed the first is nearly gone. Watching the hand, the news comes too late.
  - Check: A stick balanced on your palm falls quickly when your eyes are closed. Why? → **You cannot notice the tip in time**

### a1 · Zoom Out: one living being at five levels (10 min)
- **record:** Keep the drawing of five circles. Beside each circle, write one thing your living being gets from that level.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look out together and choose one living being. A crow, a pigeon, an ant, a street tree or a potted tulsi will do. 2. Draw five circles on the paper, one inside the next. 3. Draw your being in the smallest circle and write ORGANISM beside it. 4. Ask what this one being needs in order to stay alive and have young.  
  *Parent says:* Choose one living being you can see. That one being is called an organism. What does this one organism need to stay alive and to have young? What do you notice? What do you wonder?  
  *Child mode:* Choose one living being outside. Draw five circles, one inside the next. Draw your being in the middle. Write ORGANISM. What does this being need to live?
  - Ask: What does this one organism need to stay alive and have young? (good: Food, water and air.; A place to rest or nest.; A mate of the same kind.)
  - Note: If nothing is in sight, your child can choose themself. A person is an organism too.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the second circle, draw more of the same kind, and write POPULATION. 2. In the third circle, add four other kinds of living being from the same place, and write COMMUNITY. 3. In the fourth circle, add four non-living things from the same place, and write ECOSYSTEM. 4. In the biggest circle, draw the Earth, and write BIOSPHERE.  
  *Parent says:* Now zoom out. Who else of the same kind lives here? Which other kinds of living being live here? What non-living things are here? The last circle is every living place on the Earth.  
  *Child mode:* Circle 2: more of the same kind. Write POPULATION. Circle 3: four other kinds. Write COMMUNITY. Circle 4: four non-living things. Write ECOSYSTEM. Circle 5: the Earth. Write BIOSPHERE.
  - Note: Non-living things are water, air, soil, rock, sunlight and warmth. Remind your child that a community includes plants and beings too small to see.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Point to each circle in turn. 2. Ask what your being gets from that level. 3. Write one thing beside each circle. 4. Ask which circle your being could do without.  
  *Parent says:* What does your being get from each circle? Is there any circle your being could do without?  
  *Child mode:* Point to each circle. What does your being get from it? Write one thing beside each. Could your being do without any circle?
  - Ask: What does a crow get from each level? (good: From the population: a mate, and others to warn of danger.; From the community: food, and a tree to nest in.; From the ecosystem: water, air and warmth.; From the biosphere: rain and oxygen that come from far away.)
  - Check: All the house crows of one town, taken together, are which level of life? → **A population**
### a2 · Three Planets' Air: which planet is the odd one out? (15 min)
- **record:** Keep the three coloured strips. Under them, write one sentence saying which planet's air is the odd one out, and what makes it so.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say that Venus, the Earth and Mars are three rocky planets in a row, with the Earth in the middle. 2. Say that the air of Venus and the air of Mars are each about 95 parts carbon dioxide in every 100. 3. Ask what your child would expect the Earth's air to be, and why. 4. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Venus is on one side of us and Mars is on the other. Their air is almost all carbon dioxide. So what would you expect the Earth's air to be made of? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Venus and Mars have air that is nearly all carbon dioxide. The Earth is between them. What would you expect our air to be? Write your guess and why.
  - Ask: What would you expect the Earth's air to be? (good: Mostly carbon dioxide too, because the Earth is between them. (A sensible guess from the pattern.); Something different, because we can breathe it.)
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw three strips, each 20 squares long. Label them Venus, Earth and Mars. 2. Choose a colour for carbon dioxide, one for nitrogen and one for oxygen. 3. Venus: colour 19 squares as carbon dioxide and 1 as nitrogen. 4. Mars: colour 19 squares as carbon dioxide and 1 as nitrogen. 5. Earth: colour 16 squares as nitrogen and 4 as oxygen. 6. Beside the Earth's strip, make one tiny dot for carbon dioxide.  
  *Parent says:* Each square is 5 parts in every 100. Colour the three strips. The Earth's carbon dioxide is so little that it gets only a dot.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three strips of 20 squares. Venus: 19 carbon dioxide, 1 nitrogen. Mars: the same. Earth: 16 nitrogen, 4 oxygen. Add one tiny dot of carbon dioxide for the Earth.
  - Note: The measured figures are these. Venus: carbon dioxide about 96.5, nitrogen about 3.5. Mars: carbon dioxide about 95, nitrogen about 3, argon about 2, which we colour with the nitrogen. Earth: nitrogen 78, oxygen 21, argon about 1, carbon dioxide 0.04. The squares round these off.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the three strips one under the other. 2. Ask which planet is the odd one out. 3. Remind your child that oxygen reacts easily: it rusts iron and it burns wood. 4. Ask why so much oxygen is surprising, and who could be making it.  
  *Parent says:* Which planet is the odd one out? Oxygen rusts iron and burns wood, so it keeps getting used up. Then why is there still so much oxygen in our air?  
  *Child mode:* Which strip is the odd one out? Oxygen gets used up by rusting and burning. So why does our air still have so much?
  - Ask: Why is there still so much oxygen in the Earth's air? (good: Plants and other green beings keep making it.; Someone must be putting it back all the time.; Venus and Mars have nobody to make it.)
  - Note: This is the reasoning James Lovelock and Dian Hitchcock used in the 1960s. A planet with no life has air in which nothing is left to react. The Earth's air is kept far from that state by living beings.
  - Check: The Earth's air has far more oxygen than the air of Venus or Mars. What is the best explanation? → **Living beings keep making oxygen**
### a3 · The Rice Heap: a property that only the whole has (12 min)
- **record:** Write the height and the width of both heaps, and the height divided by the width for each. Then write one sentence about what a heap has that a grain does not have.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one grain of rice on the newspaper. 2. Ask whether one grain has a slope. 3. Say that you will pour a small heap and then a heap twice as big. 4. Ask whether the big heap will be steeper, flatter or the same, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Look at one grain. Does one grain have a slope? Now think of a small heap and a big heap. Will the big heap be steeper, flatter or the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at one grain of rice. Has it got a slope? You will pour a small heap and a big heap. Which will be steeper? Write your guess and why.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour half a tumbler of rice slowly onto one spot, from about 5 centimetres above the paper. 2. Measure the height of the heap and its width across the bottom. 3. Divide the height by the width. 4. Sweep the rice back and pour a full tumbler in the same way. 5. Measure and divide again.  
  *Parent says:* Pour slowly onto one spot. Now measure how high the heap is and how wide. Divide the height by the width. Let's see what the bigger heap gives.  
  *Child mode:* Pour half a tumbler slowly onto one spot. Measure the height and the width. Divide height by width. Now pour a full tumbler. Measure and divide again.
  - Note: Both heaps give nearly the same number, usually between 0.25 and 0.35. The big heap is higher and wider, but just as steep. If the heap slides flat, the paper is too smooth, so pour onto a cotton cloth.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask where the slope is. Is it in any one grain? 2. Say that a feature of the whole that no part has is called an emergent property. 3. Ask for one emergent property of a population, one of a bee colony, and one of your child's own body.  
  *Parent says:* Both heaps had the same slope, but no grain has a slope. So where does the slope come from? Can you think of something a group of living beings has that one member does not have?  
  *Child mode:* No grain has a slope, but every heap has one. Where does the slope come from? What can a group of living beings do that one cannot?
  - Ask: What does a group of living beings have that one member does not? (good: A population has a birth rate.; A bee colony keeps the hive warm.; A forest makes shade and damp air.; I can ride a cycle, but no single cell of my body can.)
  - Check: Which of these is an emergent property of a population of deer? → **The number of fawns born each year**
### a4 · When the Daisies Run Out of Room: how far can a system steady itself? (12 min)
- **record:** Add rounds 15 to 20 to your table and to your graph. Write one sentence about what happened to the temperature, and why.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set out the planet as it was after round 14, with 20 pale daisies and no dark ones. 2. Add 6 rows to the table. The Sun numbers are 50, 53, 56, 59, 62 and 65. 3. Add one new rule: no daisy can live above 40 degrees. If the temperature goes above 40, take every daisy off. 4. Ask what will happen to the temperature now, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Every square is full of pale daisies, and the Sun is still getting hotter. There is one new rule. Above 40 degrees, all the daisies die. What do you think will happen in the next six rounds? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Put 20 pale daisies on the planet. Add 6 rows to the table. New rule: above 40 degrees, all daisies die. What will happen now? Write your guess and why.
  - Note: If you have not done the Daisy Planet Test, do that first. This activity needs its three rules.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play rounds 15 to 20 with the same rules as before. 2. Write the temperature each round. 3. In the round when the temperature goes above 40, take off every daisy. 4. Add the six new points to the graph from the Daisy Planet Test.  
  *Parent says:* Work out the temperature. Can any more pale daisies grow? Is it above 40 yet?  
  *Child mode:* Play rounds 15 to 20. Write the temperature each round. Above 40 degrees, take off all the daisies. Add the new points to your graph.
  - Note: The temperatures for rounds 15 to 20 are 30, 33, 36, 39, 42 and 65. The daisies die after round 19. In round 20 the planet is bare, so the temperature is the Sun number, 65.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the graph together. 2. Ask why the temperature began to climb in round 15. 3. Ask why it jumped by 23 degrees between rounds 19 and 20, when the Sun number rose by only 3.  
  *Parent says:* Why did the temperature start to climb after round 14? And why did it jump so far in the last round, when the Sun only got 3 degrees hotter?  
  *Child mode:* Why did the temperature climb after round 14? Why did it jump so far in the last round?
  - Ask: Why did the temperature jump by 23 degrees in the last round? (good: The pale daisies had been cooling the planet by 20 degrees. When they died, that cooling was lost all at once.; Losing the daisies made the planet hotter, and a hotter planet cannot grow daisies.)
  - Note: A system that steadies itself can do so only up to a limit. Past the limit, the loss of the living beings makes the change worse. That is reinforcing feedback.
  - Check: A paper planet is full of pale daisies and the Sun keeps getting hotter. What happens to the temperature? → **It starts to rise again**

### a6 · One Change, Five Levels: follow a change up and down (15 min)
- **record:** Keep the two ladders. On each rung, write what changed at that level.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a ladder with five rungs. From the bottom, label them organism, population, community, ecosystem and biosphere. 2. Start at the top. Write on the top rung: the monsoon wind brings very little rain one year. 3. Think of a village tank where egrets nest. Egrets are white water birds who eat fish and frogs. 4. Ask what happens on each rung below, one at a time, and write the answers.  
  *Parent says:* The rain comes from far away, so it belongs to the top rung. One year very little rain comes. What happens to the tank? Then to the beings who live there? Then to the egrets? Then to one egret chick?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a ladder with five rungs. Label them from organism up to biosphere. Top rung: little rain this year. Now write what happens on each rung below.
  - Ask: What happens on each rung when little rain comes? (good: Ecosystem: the tank shrinks and the mud dries.; Community: there are fewer fish, frogs and water plants.; Population: fewer egrets nest, and fewer chicks are raised.; Organism: one egret chick gets less food.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a second ladder. 2. Start at the bottom. Write on the bottom rung: one big wild fig tree in a forest is cut down. 3. Say that fig trees fruit when few other trees do. Hornbills, macaques and bats all eat the figs and carry the seeds. 4. Ask what happens on each rung above, and write the answers. 5. Ask how far up the ladder this change reaches.  
  *Parent says:* Now start at the bottom. One big fig tree is cut. Who was eating those figs? What happens to them? What happens to the seeds they used to carry? Does this change reach the top rung?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a second ladder. Bottom rung: one big fig tree is cut. Write what happens on each rung above. Does the change reach the top?
  - Ask: How far up does the loss of one fig tree reach? (good: Population: there is one fig tree fewer, and their fig wasps have nowhere to breed.; Community: hornbills, macaques and bats lose food in the lean months.; Ecosystem: fewer seeds are carried, so fewer young trees grow.; Biosphere: one tree is too small a change to notice.)
  - Note: One tree does not change the biosphere. A change at the bottom reaches the top only when a very great number of organisms do the same thing for a long time.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what would have to be true for a change at the bottom rung to reach the top. 2. Give two real cases. Bacteria filled the air with oxygen. People burning coal, oil and gas are adding carbon dioxide to the air. 3. Ask what the two cases have in common.  
  *Parent says:* One tree did not change the whole Earth. But bacteria once did, and people are doing so now. What do those two cases have in common?  
  *Child mode:* One tree did not change the Earth. Bacteria once did. People are doing it now. What do those two have in common?
  - Ask: What do the bacteria and the people have in common? (good: There were a very great number of them, all doing the same thing.; They went on doing it for a long time.; What they gave out spread through the air of the whole Earth.)
  - Check: When can a change made by single organisms reach the whole biosphere? → **When very many do the same for long**

### p1 · A World in a Jar: a sealed ecosystem for three weeks (66 min, 21 days)
- **safety:** A glass jar can break if it falls, so keep it on a low, steady shelf. Wash your hands after touching soil.
- **record:** Keep a jar diary. On each day in the plan, write the date, where the water drops are, how the plant looks, and anything new. Write the weight on day 1 and day 21.
- **schedule:** day 1: Build the jar: pebbles, soil, plant and water. Seal the lid. Weigh the jar if you can. Guess what you will see. (25 min); day 2: Look for mist and drops on the glass. If the glass is running wet all over, open the jar for an hour and seal it again. (5 min); day 4: Look at the glass in the morning and in the afternoon. Note when it is misty. (5 min); day 7: Draw the jar. Look for new growth, yellow leaves, mould or tiny animals. (8 min); day 14: Draw the jar again. Compare the drawing with day 7. (8 min); day 21: Weigh the jar again. Say what went round inside the jar and what crossed the glass. (15 min)
- **s1 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a layer of pebbles 2 centimetres deep in the jar. 2. Add soil until the jar is about one third full. 3. Make a hole with a finger, set the plant's roots in, and press the soil round them. 4. Sprinkle 3 tablespoons of water on the soil. The soil should be damp, not muddy. 5. Wipe the inside of the glass, screw the lid on tightly and write the date on it. 6. Weigh the jar if you have a scale, and write the weight. 7. Ask what will happen to the water, the plant and the weight in three weeks, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Once the lid is on, nothing goes in or out except light and heat. What do you think will happen to the water? To the plant? Will the jar get lighter, heavier or stay the same? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Put in pebbles, then soil. Plant the plant. Add 3 tablespoons of water. Seal the lid and write the date. Weigh the jar. Guess what will happen to the water, the plant and the weight.
  - Ask: Will the jar's weight change in three weeks? Why? (good: It will get lighter, because the plant drinks the water. (A fair guess. The scale will show.); It will stay the same, because nothing can get out.)
  - Note: Put the jar where it gets bright light but no direct sun, for example a metre back from a window. Direct sun can heat a closed jar enough to kill the plant.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the inside of the glass. 2. Ask where the drops came from. 3. If the glass is running wet all over, there is too much water. Open the lid for one hour, then seal it again. This is the only time the jar may be opened.  
  *Parent says:* There is water on the glass. We did not put any there. Where do you think it came from?

### c1 · The Faster Sun: tune the Daisy Planet (30 min)

## Worksheets
### Levels of life and feedback (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A temple tank holds fish, frogs, water plants, water and mud. Which level of life is the whole tank?  
   Options: A population / A community / An ecosystem / The biosphere  
   **Answer:** An ecosystem. *Why:* An ecosystem is the living beings of a place together with the water, mud, air and sunlight. Choosing 'community' leaves out the non-living things.
2. (fill) All the members of one species who live in one place are called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['population']. *Why:* A population is one species in one place, such as all the house sparrows of Mysuru.
3. (match) Match each level of life to an example.  
   **Answer:** Organism → one neem tree; Population → all the neem trees of a village; Community → all the living beings of the village; Ecosystem → the village's living beings with its soil, water and air; Biosphere → every living place on the Earth. *Why:* Each level contains the one before. A strong answer can give a second example of each from a place the child knows.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a community includes the water and the soil of a place.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A community is only the living beings. When the water, soil, air and sunlight are counted too, the level is an ecosystem.
5. (sort) Sort these parts of a pond ecosystem.  
   **Answer:** frog → Living part; mud → Non-living part; sunlight → Non-living part; fungus → Living part; water → Non-living part; bacteria → Living part; air → Non-living part; moss → Living part. *Why:* Frogs, fungi, bacteria and moss are living beings. Mud, sunlight, water and air are not alive, but every living being in the pond needs them.
6. (mcq) A forest gets its monsoon rain from winds that crossed the sea. Which level supplies that rain?  
   Options: The organism / The population / The community / The biosphere  
   **Answer:** The biosphere. *Why:* The wet wind comes from far outside the forest, so no level inside the forest can supply it. An ecosystem depends on the biosphere for its rain and its air.
7. (write) In the Daisy Planet Test, the bare planet reached 47 degrees. Explain why the planet with daisies stayed near 26.  
   **Answer:** Whenever the planet went above 25 degrees, three pale daisies replaced three dark ones. Pale daisies reflect sunlight, so each one cooled the planet by a degree. That cancelled the extra heat from the Sun.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the daisies changed the temperature and the temperature changed the daisies. It uses numbers from the test.
8. (fill) A feature that a whole has, and that no part has alone, is called an ____ property.  
   **Answer:** ['emergent']. *Why:* The slope of a rice heap and the birth rate of a population are emergent properties.
9. (odd) Three of these are non-living parts of an ecosystem. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Water / Soil microbes / Sunlight / Rock  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* Soil microbes are living beings, so they belong to the community. Water, sunlight and rock are non-living.
10. (truefalse) True or false: balancing feedback works against a change.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Balancing feedback brings things back towards where they were, as sweat brings your body's heat down.
11. (mcq) Which gas is about one fifth of the Earth's air, and was made by living beings?  
   Options: Oxygen / Carbon dioxide / Nitrogen / Argon  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* Oxygen is 21 parts in every 100 of the air, and nearly all of it came from photosynthesis. Nitrogen is the biggest part of the air, at 78 in 100.
12. (draw) Draw one living being from near your home inside five circles. Label the five levels.  
   **Answer:** Five circles, one inside the next. From the centre: one organism, their population, their community, their ecosystem with water and soil, and the Earth as the biosphere.. *Why:* A strong drawing adds something new in each circle. The ecosystem circle must show at least one non-living thing.
13. (order) Put these in order, from the smallest level to the biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['One lion', 'All the lions of the Gir forest', 'All the living beings of the Gir forest', 'The Gir forest with its soil, streams and air', 'Every living place on the Earth']. *Why:* The order is organism, population, community, ecosystem, biosphere. Each one contains the one before.
14. (label) Label each loop 'balancing' or 'reinforcing'. Loop A: hot, sweat, cooler. Loop B: ice melts, dark sea warms, more ice melts.  
   **Answer:** ['Loop A: balancing', 'Loop B: reinforcing']. *Why:* In loop A the result works against the change. In loop B the result adds to the change.
15. (perspective) Is a population a real thing, or only a word? Two friends disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the rice heap. The slope is real and can be measured, yet no grain has one. In the same way a birth rate is real, yet no single crow has one. It also grants the other side its point: a population is made of nothing but crows.. *Why:* Both views hold something true. The parts are all there is, and yet the whole has features the parts lack.
### How living beings change the Earth (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Very old rocks hold minerals that oxygen destroys. Younger rocks do not. What does this show?  
   Options: The early air had almost no oxygen / Old rocks are harder / Oxygen comes from rocks / The early Earth had no rocks  
   **Answer:** The early air had almost no oxygen. *Why:* Those minerals could only last in air without oxygen. So the air changed, about 2,400 million years ago, when bacteria had made enough oxygen.
2. (write) Rusting and burning keep using up oxygen. Explain how the air can still be one fifth oxygen.  
   **Answer:** Plants, seaweeds and tiny cells in the sea give out oxygen when they photosynthesise. They keep putting oxygen back as fast as rusting, burning and breathing take it away.. *Why:* A strong answer says that oxygen is being made all the time, not that it was made once and stayed.
3. (truefalse) True or false: the Amazon rainforest adds most of the oxygen to the Earth's air.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The living beings of the forest use up almost all the oxygen the forest makes. The forest matters for rain, for carbon and for the beings who live there.
4. (match) A year of poor rain reaches a village tank. Match each change to the level where it happens.  
   **Answer:** The monsoon wind brings little rain → biosphere; The tank dries to cracked mud → ecosystem; Fish, frogs and water plants all become fewer → community; Fewer egrets nest this year → population; One egret chick goes hungry → organism. *Why:* A change at the top passes down through every level. A strong answer could trace a second change upward.
5. (fill) The share of sunlight that a surface reflects is called its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['albedo']. *Why:* Snow has a high albedo, because it reflects most of the light. Dark sea has a low albedo.
6. (mcq) A black car and a white car stand in the same sun. Which gets hotter inside, and why?  
   Options: Black: it takes in more light / White: it reflects more light / The same: the sun is the same / Black: it is heavier  
   **Answer:** Black: it takes in more light. *Why:* A dark surface takes in more of the light, and that light becomes heat. Reflecting light keeps the white car cooler, not hotter.
7. (order) Put this loop in order, starting with 'The air warms'.  
   **Answer:** ['The air warms', 'Sea ice melts', 'Dark water is uncovered', 'The water takes in more sunlight']. *Why:* After the last step, the air warms again, so the loop goes round and grows. This is reinforcing feedback.
8. (sort) Sort these loops into balancing and reinforcing.  
   **Answer:** A pressure cooker's whistle lets out steam when the pressure rises → Balancing; Each person who hears a rumour tells two more → Reinforcing; You are thirsty, you drink, and the thirst goes → Balancing; A rolling snowball picks up more snow as it grows → Reinforcing; The pupils of your eyes shrink in bright light → Balancing; Money in a bank earns interest, and the interest earns interest → Reinforcing. *Why:* In a balancing loop the result works against the change. In a reinforcing loop the result adds to it, so the change grows.
9. (write) Daisy Planet: the Sun number is 32, with 2 dark and 9 pale daisies. What is the temperature? What happens next?  
   **Answer:** 32 + 2 - 9 = 25 degrees. That is inside the range from 20 to 25, so no daisies are added or taken off this round.. *Why:* A strong answer shows the sum, and then uses the rules to say what the daisies do.
10. (draw) Draw a balancing loop from your own day, with arrows. Mark where the change is pushed back.  
   **Answer:** A loop of at least three steps that returns to its start, such as: I feel hungry, I eat, my stomach is full, I stop eating. The step that works against the change is marked.. *Why:* A strong drawing goes all the way round, and shows which step pushes back.
11. (mcq) The Daisy Planet filled up with pale daisies. Why did its temperature then start to rise?  
   Options: The pale daisies turned dark / There was no room for more pale daisies / The Sun number fell / The rules changed  
   **Answer:** There was no room for more pale daisies. *Why:* Each new pale daisy had cancelled one degree of extra heat. With every square full, nothing was left to work against the hotter Sun.
12. (truefalse) True or false: if a model works, the real world must work the same way.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A model shows what could happen under its own rules. Whether the real world follows those rules has to be tested in the real world.
13. (odd) Three of these are emergent properties. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A hive's steady warmth / A population's birth rate / A rice heap's slope / One bee's wing length  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A wing length belongs to one bee. The other three belong only to a whole made of many parts.
14. (write) A sealed jar with a plant weighs the same after three weeks. What crossed the glass, and what did not?  
   **Answer:** Light went in and heat came out. No water, air or soil crossed the glass, so the weight stayed the same. Inside, the water went round and round.. *Why:* A strong answer separates energy, which crosses the glass, from matter, which does not.
15. (perspective) Does life keep the Earth fit for life? Two scientists disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer for the first view uses the long record of liquid water under a brightening Sun, or the Daisy Planet. A strong answer for the second uses the frozen Earth, or the point that a daisy planet was built so that each daisy's needs match the planet's. It then says what the other side has right.. *Why:* This is a real argument among scientists. Both sides accept that life changes the planet. They differ on whether those changes steady it.
### Gaia, models and evidence (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) One scientist says our steady Earth may be luck. Explain his point by imagining 100 planets with life.  
   **Answer:** Suppose life starts on 100 planets and only a few happen to stay fit for life. Beings who can ask questions will only ever be found on those few. They will all see a long steady record, even if luck alone made it.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that we could not be looking from a planet where things went badly. So our own steady record is weak evidence.
2. (mcq) Astronomers find a far planet whose air holds oxygen and methane together. Why is that exciting?  
   Options: Those gases smell / Something must keep making both gases / Those gases are heavy / Those gases make the planet blue  
   **Answer:** Something must keep making both gases. *Why:* Oxygen and methane react with each other. If both are there, something must be making them all the time. On the Earth, living beings do that.
3. (write) In the Faster Sun, changing 6 daisies a round did worse than changing 5. Suggest why.  
   **Answer:** With 6, each correction was bigger than the change it was correcting. The temperature went past the comfortable range on the other side, and then had to be corrected back. With 5, the correction matched the Sun's step.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea of overshooting: correcting by more than the change.
4. (truefalse) True or false: living beings have always made the Earth better for other living beings.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. When bacteria first filled the air with oxygen, that oxygen was a poison to most living beings of the time. Life changes the Earth, sometimes for the worse.
5. (order) Put this slow loop in order, starting with 'The Earth warms'.  
   **Answer:** ['The Earth warms', 'More rain falls and rock wears away faster', 'Wearing rock takes carbon dioxide out of the air', 'The air traps less heat', 'The Earth cools']. *Why:* This is a balancing loop that takes about a million years. Roots and fungi make the rock wear away faster, so living beings take part in it.
6. (mcq) The young Sun was about a quarter dimmer, yet the early seas stayed liquid. What is the likeliest reason?  
   Options: The early air trapped more heat / The Earth was nearer the Sun / Sea water cannot freeze / The old rocks are wrong  
   **Answer:** The early air trapped more heat. *Why:* Most scientists think the early air held more heat-trapping gases, such as carbon dioxide and methane. Exactly how much of each is still being worked out.
7. (label) A graph from the Daisy Planet Test has two lines. Label the bare planet's line and the daisy planet's line.  
   **Answer:** ['The straight, steep line: the bare planet', 'The line that climbs and then runs level: the daisy planet']. *Why:* The bare planet follows the Sun number, which rises by 3 every round. The daisy planet levels off near 26 degrees.
8. (fill) Keeping conditions inside steady while conditions outside change is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['homeostasis']. *Why:* Your body does this with its warmth, and a honeybee colony does it with the warmth of the hive.
9. (sort) Sort each statement: agreed by almost all scientists, or still argued about.  
   **Answer:** Living beings made most of the oxygen in the air → Agreed; Forests return water to the air → Agreed; Life keeps the Earth's temperature steady → Argued about; The Earth is alive → Argued about; The Earth has both kinds of feedback loop → Agreed; Life's changes to the Earth mostly help life → Argued about. *Why:* That life changes the Earth is well measured. Whether those changes keep the Earth steady, and whether the Earth is 'alive', are still argued about.
10. (perspective) Is the Earth alive? Three people disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says which signs of life it is counting. It uses evidence such as the steady record, the lack of young, or the recycling of waste. It then answers one of the other views fairly.. *Why:* The disagreement is partly about the Earth and partly about what the word 'alive' should mean. A strong answer notices both.
11. (write) Bacteria changed the air with oxygen. People are changing it with carbon dioxide. Give one likeness and one difference.  
   **Answer:** Likeness: in both cases a very great number of organisms gave out the same gas for a long time, and it spread through the whole air. Difference: the bacteria took many millions of years, and people have taken about two hundred. People also know what they are doing and can choose to change it.. *Why:* A strong answer compares the scale and the speed, and notices that only one of the two can know and choose.
12. (draw) Draw the Earth with one arrow for energy coming in, one for energy going out, and a loop for matter.  
   **Answer:** The Earth with a sunlight arrow coming in, a heat arrow going out, and a closed loop on the surface, for example water or carbon going round.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that energy passes through, while matter stays and is used again.
13. (mcq) In the sealed building Biosphere 2, oxygen fell though there were many plants. What had the builders not counted?  
   Options: The microbes in the soil / The size of the windows / The weight of the glass / The colour of the leaves  
   **Answer:** The microbes in the soil. *Why:* The rich soil held huge numbers of microbes, who used oxygen faster than the plants made it. Every being who breathes has to be counted.
14. (mcq) The Earth is 12,700 km across. Nearly all life is in a layer 20 km thick. What fraction is that?  
   Options: About 1/6 / About 1/60 / About 1/600 / About 1/6000  
   **Answer:** About 1/600. *Why:* 12,700 divided by 20 is 635. So the layer is about one six-hundredth of the Earth's width.
15. (odd) Three of these are evidence that life changes the Earth. Which is not?  
   Options: Oxygen in the air / Soil made from rock and dead leaves / Rain returned to the air by forests / The Earth's distance from the Sun  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The Earth's distance from the Sun has nothing to do with living beings. The other three were made by living beings.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) All the species living in one mangrove, without the water or the mud, are which level?  
   Options: A population / A community / An ecosystem / The biosphere  
   **Answer:** A community. *Why:* Many species in one place are a community. Add the water and the mud, and the level is an ecosystem.
2. (mcq) Which level counts the living beings of a place and the non-living things together?  
   Options: Organism / Population / Community / Ecosystem  
   **Answer:** Ecosystem. *Why:* An ecosystem is a community with the water, air, soil and sunlight around them.
3. (mcq) A lone tigress lives in a forest with plenty of prey and water. What can she not do alone?  
   Options: Hunt / Find water / Have cubs / Breathe  
   **Answer:** Have cubs. *Why:* To have cubs she needs a mate, so she needs a population. This is one way an organism depends on the next level up.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a change made by tiny organisms can never reach the whole biosphere.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Cyanobacteria are too small to see, yet they filled the Earth's air with oxygen. It took a very great number of them and a very long time.
5. (mcq) Which of these belongs to a honeybee colony, but not to one bee?  
   Options: Having six legs / Keeping the hive at a steady warmth / Having wings / Drinking nectar  
   **Answer:** Keeping the hive at a steady warmth. *Why:* Thousands of bees shivering and fanning together hold the hive near 35 degrees Celsius. One bee cannot do that. It is an emergent property.
6. (mcq) A loop makes a small change grow bigger and bigger. What is it called?  
   Options: Balancing feedback / Reinforcing feedback / Homeostasis / Albedo  
   **Answer:** Reinforcing feedback. *Why:* Reinforcing feedback adds to a change. Balancing feedback works against a change, and homeostasis is the steadiness it produces.
7. (mcq) Wild dogs eat deer. When deer increase, the dogs increase and eat more deer. What kind of loop is this?  
   Options: Reinforcing / Balancing / No loop / Emergent  
   **Answer:** Balancing. *Why:* More deer leads to more dogs, and more dogs brings the deer down again. The result works against the change.
8. (mcq) Mars has almost no oxygen in its air. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Mars is red / Oxygen is too heavy for Mars / Nothing there keeps making oxygen / Mars has no air at all  
   **Answer:** Nothing there keeps making oxygen. *Why:* Oxygen reacts with rocks and is used up unless something keeps making it. On the Earth, living beings do that. Mars does have air, and most of it is carbon dioxide.
9. (mcq) On the Daisy Planet, what cools the planet when it gets too hot?  
   Options: The Sun dims / Pale daisies spread and reflect sunlight / Dark daisies spread / Rain falls  
   **Answer:** Pale daisies spread and reflect sunlight. *Why:* Above 25 degrees, pale daisies replace dark ones. Pale daisies reflect sunlight, so the planet cools. The model has no rain.
10. (truefalse) True or false: the Daisy Planet can stay steady however hot the Sun gets.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. When every square is full of pale daisies, no more cooling can be added. A balancing loop works only up to a limit.
11. (mcq) Which of these is a real criticism of the Gaia hypothesis?  
   Options: The Earth is too big to study / Natural selection cannot make beings serve a planet / Plants do not make oxygen / The Sun never changes  
   **Answer:** Natural selection cannot make beings serve a planet. *Why:* Natural selection favours what helps an organism and their family. Critics ask how it could ever favour what helps a whole planet.

## Applications
- Astronomers look for life on planets around other stars by studying the air of those planets. They look for gases that would vanish unless something kept making them.
- People who work to protect the forests of the Western Ghats point to the rain those forests send on to farms in Tamil Nadu.
- Engineers who plan homes on the Moon or Mars must close every loop of air, water and waste. Biosphere 2 showed how hard that is.
- Climate scientists use computer models of the whole Earth, with air, sea, ice and living beings all acting on each other. The Daisy Planet is a very small relative of those models.
- Doctors use the idea of balancing feedback to understand how the body holds its warmth and its blood sugar steady.
- In 1987, the countries of the world agreed to stop making the gases that were destroying ozone in the upper air. That agreement treated the atmosphere as one system shared by all.
- Farmers who grow many crops together, with trees and ponds, are looking after a whole community and ecosystem, and not just one population of one crop.

## Spotted around us
- A crow on a wire is an organism. The flock gathering at dusk on one big tree is part of a population.
- A temple tank or a village pond is an ecosystem you can walk round in five minutes.
- Mist rising from a forested hill after rain, in Munnar, Coorg or Cherrapunji, is water that the trees are sending back to the air.
- A black umbrella in the sun gets hot to touch. A white cotton veshti or dupatta stays cooler. That is albedo.
- A pressure cooker's whistle lifts when the pressure is high and drops when it falls. That is balancing feedback in the kitchen.
- A steaming compost heap or a warm heap of cut grass shows that the microbes inside are respiring.
- A termite mound by a roadside in Telangana or Karnataka is built and cooled by a whole colony. No single termite could build one.
- The green layer on a pond or an old tank in summer is made of algae and cyanobacteria. The ancestors of cyanobacteria were the first makers of the oxygen in the air.

## Open questions
- **Do living beings, taken all together, make the Earth's climate steadier or less steady?** Life is part of balancing loops and of reinforcing loops. Nobody has yet added them all up for the whole Earth.
- **Did the Earth stay fit for life through a steadying system, or partly through luck?** We have only one planet with life to study. A fair test needs other planets with life, and none has been found yet.
- **How do the tiny beings of the sea affect the clouds above them?** In 1987, four scientists proposed that gases from phytoplankton help clouds to form and so cool the sea. Later measurements suggest the effect is weaker and more tangled than they thought.
- **How much of the rain over land was given out by plants somewhere else?** It can only be worked out with computer models and special measurements of water, and the answers differ from region to region.
- **Can people build a small sealed world that keeps going for many years?** Biosphere 2 needed oxygen from outside. Smaller tests have kept people alive for months, but always with some help from machines and stores.

## Connected maths
- On the Daisy Planet, the Sun number is 35. There are 9 pale daisies and no dark daisies. What is the temperature? **26 degrees.**. Start with the Sun number, 35. Add 1 for each dark daisy: there are none. Take away 1 for each pale daisy: 35 - 9 = 26. So the temperature is 26 degrees. (queues `math.algebra.input-output.L5`)
- The bare planet was between 20 and 27 degrees in 3 rounds. The daisy planet was in that range in 12 rounds. How many times as many rounds is that? **4 times as many.**. Divide the daisy planet's rounds by the bare planet's rounds: 12 ÷ 3 = 4. So the planet with daisies stayed comfortable for 4 times as many rounds. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- From round 10 to round 13 the Sun number rose by 3 each round, and the temperature stayed at 26. How many pale daisies were added each round? Explain how you know. **3 pale daisies each round.**. Each pale daisy cools the planet by 1 degree. The Sun added 3 degrees each round. For the temperature to stay the same, the cooling must also grow by 3 degrees. So 3 pale daisies were added each round. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L5`)
- The Earth is about 12,700 kilometres across. Nearly all life is in a layer about 20 kilometres thick. On a model Earth 1 metre across, how thick is that layer? **About 1.6 millimetres.**. The model is 1 metre, which is 1,000 millimetres, for 12,700 kilometres. Divide 1,000 by 12,700 to find what 1 kilometre becomes: about 0.08 millimetres. Multiply by 20: 0.08 × 20 = 1.6 millimetres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- In Three Planets' Air, one square stands for 5 parts in 100. The Earth's carbon dioxide is 0.04 parts in 100. What fraction of one square is that? **0.008 of a square, which is less than one hundredth of a square.**. Divide the gas by the size of one square: 0.04 ÷ 5 = 0.008. One hundredth of a square would be 0.01, and 0.008 is smaller than that. So the carbon dioxide gets only a tiny dot. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Faster Sun, the scores were 3 rounds with 3 daisies, 4 with 4, 8 with 5 and 6 with 6. Do the scores fit the rule 'the bigger the number, the better'? **No. The score rises up to 5 daisies and then falls at 6.**. Put the scores in order of the number of daisies: 3, 4, 8, 6. They rise from 3 to 4 to 8, but then drop to 6. So the best number is 5, in the middle, and not the biggest one. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Name the five levels of life, from smallest to biggest. → Organism, population, community, ecosystem, biosphere.
- What do you add to a community to make an ecosystem? → The non-living things around them: water, air, soil, rock and sunlight.
- Which living beings made most of the oxygen in the air? → Plants, seaweeds, phytoplankton and bacteria, through photosynthesis.
- What is an emergent property? → A feature a whole has that none of its parts has alone, such as the slope of a rice heap.
- Which kind of feedback keeps things steady? → Balancing feedback, which works against a change.
- Which gets hotter in the sun, a dark cloth or a pale cloth? → The dark cloth, because it takes in more of the light.
- What does the Gaia hypothesis say? → That living beings and the non-living Earth form one system that keeps the Earth fit for life.
- Give one reason some scientists doubt the Gaia hypothesis. → Natural selection cannot make living beings work for a planet. Also, the Earth has frozen over in the past.
