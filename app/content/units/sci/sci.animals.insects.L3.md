# Insects: six legs, changing bodies, and who is a friend or a foe
`sci.animals.insects.L3`

**Summary.** A bee can sting you, and the same bee helps put mangoes, brinjals and mustard oil on your plate. So is a bee a friend or a foe? In this lesson you find out what makes an animal an insect: six legs, three body parts, a skeleton on the outside and feelers that smell. You follow insects as they grow up, and meet the bees, silk moths, ladybirds and dung beetles whose work people depend on. Then you look hard at the word 'pest', and at ways of protecting crops and homes that do not harm bees.

**Intro.** *Find an ant, or remember the last one you watched. How many legs did the ant have? Most people say 'lots'. Almost nobody has counted.* The answer is six, always. Six legs is the first sign that you are looking at an insect. Insects are the animals you meet most often and look at least. There are more kinds of insects than of all other animals put together. Today you will set out food for the ants near your home and watch what they choose. Then you will find out how an insect's body works. You will also see why the same insect can be a helper in one place and a pest in another.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can tell an insect from a spider or a centipede by counting legs and body parts.
1. I can name the three body parts of an insect and say what antennae, compound eyes and wings do.
2. I can describe two ways insects grow up: in four stages, as a silk moth does, and in three stages, as a grasshopper does.
3. I can explain how bees and other insects pollinate flowers, and name foods that depend on them.
4. I can say how silk moths, ladybirds and dung beetles help people and other living beings.
5. I can explain why 'pest' describes what an insect is doing in one place, and why the same insect may help somewhere else.
6. I can suggest three ways to protect plants or a home that do not harm bees.
7. I can give two views on a question scientists still argue about, such as whether insects feel.

## Terms
- **Insect**: An animal whose adult body has three parts and six legs. *e.g.* An ant has six legs and a body in three parts, so she is an insect. A spider has eight legs, so she is not.
- **Thorax**: The middle part of an insect's body, where the six legs and the wings join. *e.g.* On a housefly, both wings and all six legs join the thorax.
- **Abdomen**: The back part of an insect's body. It holds the stomach and, in females, the eggs. *e.g.* A grasshopper's abdomen moves in and out when she breathes.
- **Antennae**: The two jointed feelers on an insect's head. They smell, taste, touch and feel the air. *e.g.* An ant taps her antennae on the ground to follow a smell trail.
- **Exoskeleton**: A hard cover on the outside of an animal's body that does the job of a skeleton. *e.g.* The brown empty shell stuck to a tree trunk after the rains is a cicada's old exoskeleton.
- **Chitin**: The tough, light material that an insect's exoskeleton is made of. *e.g.* A beetle's wing covers are made of chitin. They are light and very strong.
- **Moulting**: Shedding the old exoskeleton so that a bigger one can harden. *e.g.* A silkworm moults four times before she spins her cocoon.
- **Arthropod**: An animal with jointed legs and an exoskeleton. Insects, spiders, centipedes and prawns are all arthropods. *e.g.* A prawn, a spider and a beetle are all arthropods, though only the beetle is an insect.
- **Tripod**: A stand with three legs. Three legs always rest steadily on a flat surface. *e.g.* A camera stand has three legs and never rocks. An ant walks on two tripods in turn.
- **Pheromone**: A smell that an animal gives off as a message to others of her own kind. *e.g.* A female silk moth gives off a pheromone that a male can sense from far away.
- **Compound eye**: An eye made of many tiny lenses packed together, each looking in a slightly different direction. *e.g.* A dragonfly has up to about 30,000 lenses in each compound eye.
- **Ultraviolet**: A kind of light that bees can see and our eyes cannot. *e.g.* A flower that looks plain yellow to you may have ultraviolet lines that a bee sees pointing to the nectar.
- **Spiracle**: A small breathing hole along the side of an insect's body. *e.g.* A row of small dots along a caterpillar's side are her spiracles.
- **Tracheae**: The thin air tubes inside an insect that carry air from the spiracles to every part of the body. *e.g.* A grasshopper's tracheae branch again and again, until air reaches every muscle.
- **Fossil**: The mark or the remains of a living being from long ago, kept in rock. *e.g.* A fossil dragonfly-like insect found in rock has wings about 70 centimetres across, much wider than any dragonfly alive.
- **Larva**: The young stage of an insect who grows up in four stages. She looks nothing like the adult. *e.g.* A silkworm is the larva of the silk moth, and a maggot is the larva of a fly.
- **Pupa**: The stage in which a larva's body is rebuilt into the adult's body. *e.g.* A silkworm becomes a pupa inside her cocoon, and a moth comes out about two weeks later.
- **Cocoon**: A case of thread that a larva spins around herself before she becomes a pupa. *e.g.* A silkworm spins her cocoon from one thread that can be several hundred metres long.
- **Complete metamorphosis**: Growing up in four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult. *e.g.* A butterfly, a beetle, a fly and a mosquito all grow up in four stages.
- **Nymph**: The young stage of an insect who grows up in three stages. She looks like a small adult with no working wings. *e.g.* A grasshopper nymph looks like a small grasshopper with tiny wing buds.
- **Incomplete metamorphosis**: Growing up in three stages: egg, nymph and adult. *e.g.* Grasshoppers, cockroaches, crickets and dragonflies all grow up in three stages.
- **Pollen**: A fine powder that a flower makes. It must reach another flower of the same kind to make seeds. *e.g.* A bee comes away from a mustard flower with yellow dust on her body.
- **Pollination**: The moving of pollen from one flower to another flower of the same kind, so that seeds and fruits can grow. *e.g.* A fly moving pollen between mango flowers makes the fruit grow.
- **Pollinator**: An animal who carries pollen from flower to flower. *e.g.* A bee, a fly, a butterfly and a beetle can all be pollinators.
- **Nectar**: The sweet drink that a flower makes to pay visitors. *e.g.* A bee pushes into a flower to drink nectar and gets dusted with pollen on the way.
- **Queen**: In a honeybee family, the one large bee who lays all the eggs. *e.g.* A honeybee queen is the mother of every worker in her hive.
- **Worker**: In a honeybee family, a female bee who builds the comb, feeds the young, guards the door and collects food. *e.g.* Every worker honeybee is a daughter of the queen.
- **Drone**: A male honeybee. His one job is to mate with a new queen. *e.g.* A drone does not collect food or guard the hive.
- **Comb**: The wall of six-sided wax cells that honeybees build to hold eggs, honey and pollen. *e.g.* The Indian rock bee hangs one huge comb from a cliff or a tall tree.
- **Swarm**: A very large crowd of insects who move together. A honeybee swarm is a crowd of bees looking for a new home. *e.g.* A locust swarm can cover many square kilometres of sky.
- **Waggle dance**: A movement a honeybee makes on the comb to show her sisters the direction and distance of flowers. *e.g.* A longer waggle means flowers farther away, and the direction shows the way compared with the Sun.
- **Silk**: A thread that a silkworm makes to spin her cocoon. *e.g.* A silk sari is made of the thread of thousands of cocoons.
- **Sericulture**: Rearing silk moths for their thread. *e.g.* In Karnataka, families grow mulberry trees to feed silkworms for their thread.
- **Aphid**: A small, soft insect who sucks the juice of a plant. *e.g.* Look under a rose leaf and you may see a crowd of green aphids.
- **Predator**: An animal who hunts and eats other animals. *e.g.* A ladybird is a predator of aphids, and a dragonfly is a predator of mosquitoes.
- **Biological control**: Protecting a crop by helping the animals who hunt the insects who eat it. *e.g.* In 2010, scientists released a tiny wasp that hunts the papaya mealybug, and the fields recovered.
- **Decomposer**: A living being who breaks down dead plants, dead animals and dung so that what they are made of returns to the soil. *e.g.* A dung beetle buries a ball of dung, and the soil gets the food.
- **Vector**: An animal who carries a germ from one body to another. *e.g.* A mosquito who carries the malaria germ from one person to another is a vector.
- **Parasite**: A living being who lives in or on another one and takes food from her. *e.g.* The tiny living beings that cause malaria are parasites that live in a mosquito's stomach wall and in a person's blood.
- **Pest**: A word people use for an insect who eats or spoils something they want, or carries illness. It describes what an insect is doing in one place. *e.g.* Ants in the sugar tin are called pests. The same ants carrying away a dead moth are doing a helpful job.
- **Pesticide**: A poison made to kill animals who damage crops. *e.g.* A spray meant for aphids can also kill ladybirds and bees.
- **Decline**: A fall in numbers over time. *e.g.* Fewer fireflies in a street than there were years ago would be a decline, if counted the same way each year.

## Explanations
### An insect is an animal with six legs and a body in three parts · *defined*
**Definition:** An insect is an animal whose adult body has three parts, called the head, the thorax and the abdomen, with six legs joined to the thorax.

Look closely at an ant. The body has three parts with narrow waists between them. The front part is the head. It carries the eyes, the mouth and two feelers called antennae. The middle part is the thorax. All six legs join the body here, and so do the wings in insects who have wings. The back part is the abdomen. It holds the stomach, and in females the eggs. Count six legs and three body parts on an adult, and you have found an insect. Ants, bees, butterflies, beetles, flies, mosquitoes, grasshoppers and cockroaches all pass this test.
- e.g. An ant: three clear parts, six legs, two bent antennae.
- e.g. A housefly: six legs and two wings, all joined to the thorax.
- e.g. A beetle's three parts are hidden under hard wing covers, but turn a beetle picture over and the six legs show.
- e.g. Scientists have named about ten lakh kinds of insects so far. That is more than half of all the kinds of animals with names.
### An insect wears the skeleton on the outside, and must shed it to grow · *defined*
**Definition:** An exoskeleton is a hard cover on the outside of an animal's body that does the job of a skeleton. Moulting is shedding the old exoskeleton so that a bigger one can harden.

Your bones are inside you, with muscles around them. An insect is built the other way round. The hard part is on the outside, like a suit of armour, and the muscles are inside. This outside skeleton is called an exoskeleton. It is made of a tough, light material called chitin. It keeps the insect from drying out and holds the body in shape. But armour cannot stretch. So a growing insect grows a new soft skin underneath, splits the old one and climbs out. Then the insect swells up with air and waits for the new skin to harden. This is called moulting. Until the new skin hardens, the insect is soft and easy to hurt.
- e.g. The brown, empty shell clinging to a tree trunk in the rains is the moulted skin of a cicada.
- e.g. A silkworm moults four times before spinning a cocoon.
- e.g. A cockroach who has just moulted is white for a few hours.
- e.g. A beetle's wing covers are chitin. They are so light and strong that engineers study them for building materials.
### Not every small crawling animal is an insect · *defined*
**Definition:** Insects belong to a larger group called arthropods, the animals with jointed legs and an exoskeleton. Spiders, centipedes, crabs and prawns are arthropods who are not insects.

Count before you name. A spider has eight legs and a body in two parts, so a spider is not an insect. A centipede has dozens of legs. A woodlouse, who rolls into a ball, has fourteen and is closer to a prawn than to a beetle. All of these have jointed legs and a skeleton outside. Scientists put them, with insects, in a big group called arthropods. An earthworm and a snail have no legs at all and are not arthropods. In daily talk we lump them together as 'keeda', 'poochi' or 'bug'. That word tells you the animal is small. It does not tell you who the animal is.
- e.g. Spider: 8 legs, 2 body parts, no antennae.
- e.g. Centipede: one pair of legs on almost every part of a long body.
- e.g. Scorpions, ticks and mites have 8 legs, like spiders.
- e.g. A caterpillar seems to have many legs. Only the front six are true legs. The soft ones behind are grips that are lost when the caterpillar becomes a moth or butterfly.
- e.g. Studies of DNA show that insects are closer kin to crabs and prawns than to spiders.
- **Scientists who classify animals, following Carl Linnaeus in 1758 and DNA studies since the 1990s:** Animals are sorted by how their bodies are built and by how closely they are related. On this sorting, an ant is nearer to a prawn than to a spider.
- **The Jain text Tattvartha Sutra by Umasvati, chapter 2, sutras 23 and 24:** Living beings are sorted by how many senses they have. Worms have two, touch and taste. Ants add smell. Bees add sight. Beings such as humans add hearing. This is a sorting by experience, and its aim is to help people avoid harming any of them. Scientists today find that ants and bees have more senses than this list gives them.
- **Farmers and gardeners:** They often sort small animals by what they do: who eats the crop, who eats the eaters, who carries pollen. This sorting is useful in a field, and one animal can move from one group to another.
### Six legs walk as two tripods, so a walking insect never tips over · *defined*
**Definition:** In the tripod walk, an insect moves three legs at a time while the other three stay on the ground, making a steady triangle.

A stool with three legs never wobbles. Insects walk using this fact. A walking ant lifts three legs together: the front and back legs on one side and the middle leg on the other. The other three legs stay down and make a triangle, like a stool. Then the two sets swap. So at every moment the insect stands on a steady tripod. This works on a wall, a ceiling or a shaking leaf. Feet with tiny claws and sticky pads do the rest. Six is the smallest number of legs that gives a tripod on the ground and a tripod in the air.
- e.g. A cockroach can run about fifty body lengths in one second using the tripod walk.
- e.g. Engineers build six-legged robots that walk on tripods over rubble where wheels get stuck.
- e.g. A fly on a window pane holds on with pads on the feet that are covered in tiny hairs.
- e.g. A praying mantis walks on four legs and keeps the front two for catching prey.
- **Engineers who build walking robots, such as Robert Full's group in California since the 1990s:** They filmed running cockroaches and copied the tripod walk in machines. They found the legs act like springs, so the insect stays steady without the brain correcting every step.
- **Scientists who study insect walking closely:** Insects do not use the tripod all the time. A slow or heavily loaded insect keeps four or five feet down, and an insect who loses a leg changes the pattern at once. The walk is chosen from moment to moment.
### Antennae smell, taste, touch and feel the air · *defined*
**Definition:** Antennae are the two jointed feelers on an insect's head. They carry thousands of sensors for smell, taste, touch and movement of the air.

An insect has no nose. The two antennae do that job and several more. They are covered in tiny hairs and pits, and each one is a sensor. Some catch smells floating in the air. Some taste what they touch. Some feel wind, warmth or dampness. An ant who finds food lays a trail of smell on the way home. Her sisters follow it with their antennae, tapping the ground. Rub a finger across an ant trail and the ants stop and search, because you have wiped the smell away. Male moths have feathery antennae, with more room for sensors.
- e.g. A female silk moth gives off a smell that a male can detect from far away. In 1959 the German chemist Adolf Butenandt worked out what this smell is made of. It was the first such signal smell, called a pheromone, ever identified.
- e.g. Two ants who meet touch antennae to check that they smell of the same nest.
- e.g. A mosquito finds you partly by the gas you breathe out, sensed by her antennae and mouthparts.
- e.g. A cricket hears with ears on the front legs. Insects carry their sensors in places we do not expect.
### Compound eyes: thousands of small eyes, and colours we cannot see · *defined*
**Definition:** a compound eye is an eye made of many tiny lenses packed together, each one looking in a slightly different direction.

Your eye has one lens. A dragonfly's eye has up to about 30,000, packed like the cells of a honeycomb. This is a compound eye. Each lens sees one small patch, and the brain joins the patches. The picture is less sharp than yours. It is far better at catching movement, which is why a fly is gone before your hand lands. Insects also see different colours. Bees do not see red well, but they see ultraviolet, a colour our eyes cannot detect. Many flowers that look plain yellow to us have ultraviolet marks that point to the nectar. A bee sees the marks.
- e.g. A worker honeybee has about 5,000 lenses in each compound eye, and three small single eyes on top of the head as well.
- e.g. In 1914 the Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch trained bees to come to blue cards among grey cards, and so showed that bees see colour.
- e.g. A fly can tell apart flashes of light that are too quick for our eyes to separate.
- e.g. Engineers have built small cameras shaped like compound eyes to see all around at once.
- **Scientists who measure eyes:** A flower gives off light of many kinds. A human eye picks up some of it and a bee's eye picks up a different part. Both are correct measurements of the same flower.
- **Jakob von Uexküll, an Estonian-German biologist, in 1934:** Each animal lives in a world built by the animal's own senses. He called this world the Umwelt. The meadow of a bee and the meadow of a child are, in experience, two different meadows.
- **Jain thinkers, in the teaching called anekantavada:** Any one view of a thing is partial. A full account needs many viewpoints together. The bee's flower and the child's flower are both true and both incomplete.
### Insects breathe through holes along their sides, with no lungs · *defined*
**Definition:** Spiracles are small breathing holes along the sides of an insect's body. They open into thin air tubes called tracheae, which carry air to every part of the body.

An insect does not breathe through the mouth, and has no lungs. Look at a caterpillar's side and you may see a row of small dots. These are breathing holes, called spiracles. Each one opens into a tube. The tubes branch again and again, growing thinner, until they reach every muscle. Air goes straight to where it is needed. In your body, blood carries oxygen from the lungs. In an insect, the tubes deliver the air directly, and the blood has other work. An insect can close the spiracles to save water. Many insects also pump the abdomen to push air through.
- e.g. A grasshopper's abdomen can be seen pumping in and out as the insect breathes.
- e.g. Mosquito young live under water and hang from the surface, breathing through a short tube like a snorkel.
- e.g. A diving beetle carries a bubble of air under the wing covers and breathes from it.
- e.g. On a large grasshopper you can find the spiracles with a magnifying glass, as small dots along her side.
### Why is no insect as big as a dog? Scientists give three answers · *debated*
**Definition:** The size of an animal is limited by how the body gets air, holds itself up and escapes being eaten.

The heaviest insects alive weigh less than a small mango. Yet rocks hold fossils of an insect like a dragonfly with wings about 70 centimetres across. Their name is Meganeura, and they lived about 30 crore years ago. So insects were once much larger. Why not now? Scientists have three main answers. One is breathing. Air creeps slowly down long tubes, and a big body needs long tubes. A second is moulting. A big insect who has just shed the old skin would sag under their own weight. A third is birds, who hunt large flying insects. The answers may all be partly right.
- e.g. The Atlas moth of Indian forests has wings about 25 centimetres across, among the widest of any living insect, yet weighs only a few grams.
- e.g. The giant weta of New Zealand, one of the heaviest insects, can weigh more than a sparrow.
- e.g. The smallest insects, fairy wasps, are smaller than a full stop.
- **Jon Harrison and Alexander Kaiser, American scientists, in 2007:** They X-rayed beetles of many sizes. In bigger beetles the air tubes took up a larger and larger share of the room inside the legs. In the age of Meganeura the air held more oxygen than today, so thinner tubes were enough and bodies could be larger.
- **Matthew Clapham and Jered Karr, in 2012:** They measured more than 10,000 fossil insect wings. Insect size followed oxygen for a long time. Then, about 15 crore years ago, birds appeared, and the largest flying insects grew smaller even though oxygen rose. Being hunted mattered.
- **Scientists who study the exoskeleton:** An outside skeleton must get much thicker as a body gets larger. A large insect, soft just after a moult, could not stand up on land. Large relatives of insects, such as crabs, live in water, which holds them up.
### Insects were the first animals to fly, and nobody is sure where wings came from · *debated*
**Definition:** Insect wings are thin, stiff sheets of exoskeleton moved by muscles in the thorax. They are not changed legs, as the wings of birds and bats are.

Insects were flying more than 30 crore years ago. That is long before any bird, bat or flying reptile. A bird's wing is a changed arm. An insect's wing is something else. An insect kept all six legs and grew wings as well. So where did the wings come from? No fossil shows the first small wing. Scientists have argued about this for more than a hundred years. Wings changed everything for insects. A flying insect can escape, find a mate and reach a new plant far away. Most kinds of insects alive today have wings.
- e.g. A housefly beats her wings nearly two hundred times a second. That is the buzz you hear.
- e.g. A dragonfly moves the four wings separately and can hover, fly backwards and turn on the spot.
- e.g. A beetle's front wings are hard covers. The thin back wings unfold from under them for flight.
- e.g. Worker ants have no wings, but new queens and males grow wings and fly once, in the rains.
- **Scientists who support the 'body wall' idea, first suggested in the 1800s:** Wings began as flat flaps growing out from the back of the thorax. The flaps first helped insects glide down from tall plants, and later gained hinges and muscles.
- **Scientists who support the 'gill' idea, such as Jarmila Kukalová-Peck from the 1970s:** Wings began as movable flaps near the legs of water-living ancestors, like the gills of young mayflies today. These flaps already had joints and muscles.
- **Scientists studying genes since about 2010, such as Yoshinori Tomoyasu's group:** In winged insects, the genes that build a wing switch on in two places: on the back wall and near the leg. This suggests that wings were made by two older body parts joining. Many researchers now favour this, and it is still being tested.
### Growing up in four stages: the silk moth · *defined*
**Definition:** Complete metamorphosis is growing up in four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult. The larva and the adult look entirely different.

A silk moth begins as an egg the size of a mustard seed. Out comes a larva, the silkworm, only a few millimetres long. The silkworm does one thing: eat mulberry leaves. In about four weeks the silkworm moults four times and grows to about ten thousand times the first weight. Then the silkworm spins a cocoon from one unbroken thread. Inside, the silkworm becomes a pupa, and the body is rebuilt. About two weeks later a moth comes out. The moth has wings and feathery antennae and no working mouth. The moth never eats. The moth's work is to mate and lay eggs.
- e.g. Butterflies, beetles, flies, ants, bees and mosquitoes all grow up in these four stages.
- e.g. A fly's larva is called a maggot. A beetle's is called a grub. A mosquito's is called a wriggler.
- e.g. The thread of one silk cocoon is often several hundred metres long, and can be about a kilometre.
- e.g. Caterpillars and butterflies eat different food, so the young do not take food from their parents.
- **Most biologists today:** The larva is built for eating and growing, and the adult for travelling and breeding. Dividing a life in two lets each stage do one job very well, and may be why insects with four stages have so many kinds.
- **Aristotle, in his History of Animals, about 350 BCE:** He thought a caterpillar was a kind of soft egg that had not finished forming, and that the butterfly was the real animal. For about two thousand years many scholars agreed. Modern studies show the larva is a full animal with senses and memory of their own.
### Growing up in three stages, and young who live in water · *defined*
**Definition:** Incomplete metamorphosis is growing up in three stages: egg, nymph and adult. A nymph is a young insect who looks much like the adult but has no working wings.

Not every insect has a pupa. A grasshopper's egg hatches into a nymph. The nymph looks like a small grasshopper with tiny wing buds. With each moult the nymph grows, and the wings grow too. After the last moult the wings work. Cockroaches, crickets, bugs and termites grow this way. Dragonflies do too, with a surprise. A dragonfly nymph lives under water, breathing with gills and hunting tadpoles. After months or years the nymph climbs a reed and moults one last time. An adult dragonfly flies away. Mosquitoes grow up in four stages, and three of them are spent in still water.
- e.g. The empty skins on reeds beside a pond are left by dragonflies who have just become adults.
- e.g. A mosquito can grow from egg to adult in about eight to ten days in warm weather. That is why standing water is emptied every week.
- e.g. Some cicadas in North America live under the ground as nymphs for 13 or 17 years and come out all together.
- e.g. A termite nymph looks like a small, pale adult from the first day.
### Bees and other insects carry pollen, and much of our food depends on them · *defined*
**Definition:** Pollination is the moving of pollen, a fine powder, from one flower to another of the same kind. This lets the plant make seeds and fruits. An animal who does this is called a pollinator.

A flower makes a fine powder called pollen. For a seed to form, pollen must reach another flower of the same kind. Plants cannot walk, so many pay insects to do this job. The payment is nectar, a sweet drink. A bee pushes into a flower for nectar and gets dusted with pollen. At the next flower some pollen rubs off. A fruit can now grow. Of the main kinds of crops that people grow for food, about three out of four give more or better food when animals pollinate them. Most of those animals are insects. Honeybees do part of this work. Wild bees, flies, moths, beetles and wasps do the rest.
- e.g. Mango flowers are pollinated mostly by flies. Cocoa, for chocolate, is pollinated by midges smaller than a sesame seed.
- e.g. Mustard, sunflower, apple, cardamom, coffee, brinjal and pumpkin all give more with insect visits.
- e.g. Rice, wheat and maize do not need insects. Their pollen stays in the same flower or travels on the wind.
- e.g. India has several native honeybees. The rock bee builds one huge comb on cliffs and tall trees. The others are the Indian hive bee, the little bee and the tiny stingless bees.
- e.g. Each wild kind of fig tree, including the banyan and the peepal, is pollinated by its own kind of tiny wasp, who lives nowhere else.
- **Alexandra-Maria Klein and colleagues, in 2007:** They checked the world's leading food crops one by one. Of 115 crops, 87 depend at least partly on animal pollinators. By weight, though, most food comes from grains that do not need insects. Insects matter most for fruits, vegetables, nuts, spices and oils.
- **Renee Borges and other ecologists in India:** They study figs and fig wasps and find that neither can have young without the other. Pollination is a deal that both sides have shaped over a very long time, and each side also tries to get more than they give.
- **Christian Konrad Sprengel, a German teacher, in 1793:** He was the first to show that flowers' colours, scents and nectar are there to draw insects. His book was ignored for decades. Charles Darwin later praised his work.
### A beehive is a family of sisters, and for two thousand years people thought a king ruled it · *defined*
**Definition:** A honeybee colony is one family. It has one queen, who lays the eggs, thousands of workers, who are her daughters, and some males, called drones.

A hive holds one large bee, thousands of workers and a few hundred males. The large bee is the mother of all the others. She lays the eggs. Every worker is female. Workers build the wax comb, feed the young, guard the door and collect nectar and pollen. The males do none of this work. Their one job is to mate with a new queen. The queen does not give orders. Workers decide most things together, such as where to build a new home. For about two thousand years, scholars wrote that the large bee was a king. They assumed that a leader must be male.
- e.g. Ants and many wasps also live as families of sisters with one or more egg-laying queens. Termite families have a queen and a king.
- e.g. A worker honeybee in summer lives about six weeks. A queen can live several years.
- e.g. Most kinds of bees do not live in hives at all. A carpenter bee nests alone in a hole in wood.
- e.g. Workers make honey by drying nectar in the comb with their wings.
- **Aristotle, in his History of Animals, about 350 BCE:** He called the large bee the king or leader, and many scholars repeated this after him.
- **Luis Méndez de Torres in Spain in 1586, Charles Butler in England in 1609 and Jan Swammerdam in the Netherlands in the 1670s:** Méndez de Torres wrote that the large bee lays the eggs. Butler called his book 'The Feminine Monarchie'. Swammerdam cut open the large bee under a microscope and found eggs inside. The king was a queen.
- **The Qur'an, chapter 16, 'The Bee', verses 68 and 69:** The verses speak to the bee who builds homes in hills and trees and makes a drink of many colours. In the Arabic, the words that address the bee are in the female form.
- **Thomas Seeley, an American scientist, in his 2010 book 'Honeybee Democracy':** When a swarm needs a new home, a few hundred scout bees visit different places and each dances for the one she found. Support builds for the best place until enough scouts agree. The queen takes no part in the choice.
### The waggle dance: how a bee tells her sisters where the flowers are · *defined*
**Definition:** The waggle dance is a movement a honeybee makes on the comb. Its direction shows the direction of food compared with the Sun, and its length shows the distance.

A bee who finds a field of flowers flies home and dances on the comb in the dark hive. She runs in a straight line, waggling her abdomen, then circles back and does it again. Her sisters crowd round and follow with their antennae. If she runs straight up the comb, the flowers are towards the Sun. If she runs at an angle to the left, they are that angle to the left of the Sun. The longer the waggle, the farther the flowers. Karl von Frisch worked this out in the 1940s. Many scientists did not believe it, and the argument lasted forty years.
- e.g. For one kind of honeybee, a waggle lasting about one second means roughly a kilometre. Different kinds of honeybees use different scales.
- e.g. The Indian rock bee dances on the open face of the comb, in the light.
- e.g. Stingless bees do not waggle-dance. Many of them lay scent trails.
- e.g. In 1973 Karl von Frisch shared a Nobel Prize for this work.
- **Karl von Frisch, in the 1940s:** He put out dishes of sugar water at measured distances and directions and watched the dances through a glass wall. The dance matched the place. He concluded that the dance is a message that other bees read.
- **Adrian Wenner and Patrick Wells, American scientists, from 1967:** They agreed the dance matches the place. They doubted that other bees use the dance. In their tests, new bees seemed to find food by the smell on the dancer's body. They argued that a dance 'language' was more than the evidence showed.
- **Axel Michelsen in Denmark in 1989, and Joe Riley's team in England in 2005:** Michelsen built a small model bee that danced with no flower smell on the model. Real bees flew where the model pointed. Riley's team fixed tiny radar tags on bees who had followed a dance and tracked them flying straight towards the right place. Most scientists now accept that bees read the dance, and that smell helps them at the end.
### Silk is a thread spun by a caterpillar, and making silk raises a hard question · *defined*
**Definition:** Silk is the thread a silkworm spins to make a cocoon. Rearing silk moths for their thread is called sericulture.

A silkworm spins a cocoon from one long thread of protein, pushed out near the mouth. People learnt to unwind that thread and weave it. Silk threads have been found at Harappa and Chanhu-daro, from more than 4,000 years ago, made by wild silk moths. In China, people were keeping silk moths about 5,000 years ago. India makes four kinds of silk. Mulberry silk comes mostly from Karnataka. Tasar is gathered by Adivasi families in the forests of Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh. Eri and golden muga come from Assam. Here is the hard part. To unwind one whole thread, the cocoon is heated before the moth comes out, and the pupa inside dies.
- e.g. It takes about 2,500 to 3,000 cocoons to make one mulberry silk sari.
- e.g. The mulberry silk moth has lived with people for so long that the moths can no longer fly or find food alone.
- e.g. Eri cocoons are open at one end. The moth can leave, and the short threads are spun like cotton.
- e.g. In Assam and other parts of the Northeast, eri pupae are also cooked and eaten, so little is wasted.
- **Silk-rearing and weaving families, from Kanchipuram to Sualkuchi:** Sericulture feeds lakhs of families in India, many of them women working at home. The moths are cared for closely from egg to cocoon. A whole unbroken thread gives the smooth, strong cloth that people have valued for thousands of years.
- **Jain teaching, and Mahatma Gandhi, who wrote against ordinary silk:** Taking a life for cloth breaks ahimsa, the rule of not harming. Many Jains do not wear silk. Gandhi asked people to choose cloth that needed no killing.
- **Kusuma Rajaiah, a handloom officer from Telangana, since the 1990s:** He developed 'Ahimsa silk'. The moth is allowed to cut a way out, and the broken cocoon is spun into yarn. The cloth is less glossy and costs more, because the thread is in pieces and extra cocoons are needed.
- **Scientists who study insect minds:** Whether a pupa feels anything during the heating is not known. A pupa's nervous system is being rebuilt, and nobody has a test that settles it.
### Insects who hunt other insects: ladybirds, dragonflies and tiny wasps · *defined*
**Definition:** A predator is an animal who hunts and eats other animals. Biological control is protecting crops by helping the predators of the insects who eat the crop.

Look under a rose leaf and you may find aphids, small soft insects who suck the plant's juice. Look again in a week and there may be a ladybird. A ladybird and her larvae eat aphids, dozens in a day. Dragonflies catch mosquitoes in the air, and their nymphs eat mosquito young in the water. Tiny wasps lay their eggs inside caterpillars and aphids. A farmer who protects these hunters needs less spray. In 2008 the papaya mealybug reached Tamil Nadu and ruined papaya and mulberry fields. Sprays failed. In 2010 scientists in Bengaluru released a tiny wasp whose young grow inside the mealybug, and within about a year the fields recovered.
- e.g. In 1888 orange trees in California were dying from a scale insect. Albert Koebele brought the vedalia ladybird from Australia, and within two years the trees were saved.
- e.g. Not every ladybird hunts. The hadda beetle, a ladybird with 28 spots, eats brinjal and potato leaves.
- e.g. A praying mantis, a lacewing and a hoverfly larva are hunters too.
- e.g. Weaver ants are placed in orange trees in parts of Asia to guard the fruit, a practice written about in China in 304 CE.
- **Farm scientists who practise biological control:** A released hunter keeps working for years at no cost, leaves no poison and is safe for bees. After the wasp spread, farmers could stop paying for sprays that had failed.
- **Ecologists who study introductions that went wrong:** A hunter brought from far away may start eating local insects. The harlequin ladybird, released in Europe and North America to eat aphids, spread widely and pushed out native ladybirds. They ask for long testing before any release.
### Insects who clean up: dung beetles, termites, ants and maggots · *defined*
**Definition:** A decomposer is a living being who breaks down dead plants, dead animals and dung, so that what they are made of returns to the soil.

Every day, animals leave dung, leaves fall and bodies die. Insects do much of the clearing. Dung beetles shape dung into balls, roll them away and bury them as food for their young. That feeds the soil and lets air and water in. Termites eat dead wood, with the help of tiny living beings in their guts who digest it. Ants carry away dead insects. Fly maggots clean a dead animal down to the bones. Without these workers, fields and forests would lie buried in waste. Australia found this out. Its native dung beetles could not handle cow dung, and pats covered the pastures until other dung beetles were brought in from the 1960s.
- e.g. In 2013 Marie Dacke and colleagues showed that some dung beetles roll their balls in a straight line at night by steering by the Milky Way.
- e.g. A cow pat in an Indian field can be taken underground by beetles within days in the rains.
- e.g. Hospitals sometimes use specially raised, clean maggots to clear dead tissue from wounds that will not heal.
- e.g. Ants and termites turn and loosen soil, much as earthworms do.
### Some insects carry illness: the mosquito, and how the proof was found in India · *defined*
**Definition:** A vector is an animal who carries a germ from one body to another. Some mosquitoes are vectors of malaria and dengue.

Only female mosquitoes bite. A female needs the protein in blood to make her eggs. The bite itches and does little harm. The harm comes from tiny living beings who ride inside some mosquitoes. One kind causes malaria, which kills about six lakh people a year across the world. For centuries people blamed bad air from swamps. In 1897, in Secunderabad, Ronald Ross found malaria parasites in the stomach wall of a mosquito who had fed on a patient. The surest protection needs no poison. Mosquito young grow in still water. Tip out every tray, pot and old tyre once a week, and sleep under a net.
- e.g. The mosquitoes who carry dengue bite by day and breed in small pools of clean water, such as a flowerpot tray or a cooler.
- e.g. Guppy and gambusia fish are put in ponds and wells to eat mosquito young.
- e.g. A dragonfly nymph in a pond eats many mosquito young each day.
- e.g. Male mosquitoes drink only nectar and plant juice, and they pollinate some flowers.
- **Ronald Ross, a British army doctor born in Almora, in 1897 and 1898:** He received the Nobel Prize in 1902 for showing that mosquitoes carry malaria. He worked in Secunderabad and then in Calcutta.
- **Historians who have studied his notebooks:** Ross relied on Indian helpers who are missing from most accounts. Husein Khan, a patient, let mosquitoes feed on him for a small payment. Kishori Mohan Bandyopadhyay worked beside Ross in the Calcutta laboratory. Giovanni Battista Grassi in Italy showed in 1898 which mosquito carries human malaria. He did not share the Nobel Prize.
- **Ecologists:** There are about 3,500 kinds of mosquitoes, and only a small share bite people or carry illness. Mosquitoes and their young are food for fish, frogs, bats, birds and dragonflies.
### 'Pest' describes what an insect is doing in one place, and the same insect may help elsewhere · *debated*
**Definition:** People call an insect a pest when the insect eats or spoils something people want, or carries illness. It is a word about a situation.

A caterpillar eats holes in the curry-leaf plant. Three weeks later the same being is a butterfly carrying pollen. Termites eat a door, and in the forest termites turn dead wood into soil. A desert locust usually lives alone and harms nobody. When rains bring plenty of grass, locusts crowd together. The touch of other locusts changes their colour, their bodies and their behaviour, and they fly as a swarm. In 2020 such swarms reached Rajasthan, Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh. A swarm covering one square kilometre can eat as much in a day as about 35,000 people. A farmer's loss is real. So is the question: is 'pest' a kind of insect, or a meeting between insects and our plans?
- e.g. Ants in the sugar tin are called pests. The same ants on the balcony are carrying away a dead moth.
- e.g. A field of one crop planted edge to edge is a feast laid out for the insects who eat that crop.
- e.g. Cockroaches in a kitchen can carry germs on their feet. In a forest, cockroaches break down fallen leaves.
- e.g. A wasp near a picnic is a nuisance. The same wasp feeds caterpillars to her young all summer.
- **Farmers:** When a family's food and income for the year are being eaten, the insect is an enemy in that field, whatever the insect does elsewhere. Any other way of speaking must still protect the harvest.
- **Ecologists, such as those who shaped 'integrated pest management' from 1959 onwards:** No insect is a pest by nature. An insect becomes one when numbers pass a level that causes real loss. Below that level, the insect is food for hunters who will be needed later, and spraying does more harm than good.
- **Stephen Simpson and colleagues, who studied locusts from the 1990s:** They showed that a calm, lone locust becomes a swarming locust within hours of being crowded, and can change back. The 'plague insect' and the harmless one are the same animal in two states.
- **Jain teaching, in the Acharanga Sutra, book 1:** All beings wish to live and none wishes to suffer, so no being should be harmed. Jain monks and nuns sweep the ground before them so as not to step on small beings. On this view, 'pest' is not a reason that permits killing.
### Sprays that kill insects also reach bees, and there are bee-safe ways · *debated*
**Definition:** A pesticide is a poison made to kill animals who damage crops. Bee-safe ways protect plants while keeping pollinators unharmed.

A poison sprayed on a crop reaches every insect. It kills the aphid, and also the ladybird and the bee. Then the aphids, who breed fastest, return first, with no hunters left. In 1962 the American scientist Rachel Carson set this out in her book 'Silent Spring'. There are other ways. Pick caterpillars off by hand. Cover young plants with a net. Wash aphids off with a jet of water. Grow marigold, coriander and mustard among the vegetables, to feed hunters and confuse leaf-eaters. If a spray is truly needed, neem is gentler than most. Spray at dusk, when bees have gone home, and never on open flowers.
- e.g. Indian farmers have long mixed dried neem leaves into stored grain to keep weevils away.
- e.g. Yellow sticky cards and scent traps catch particular insects without poison.
- e.g. Sikkim was declared India's first fully organic state in 2016, with no chemical pesticides sold there.
- e.g. A patch of wild flowers left at a field's edge gives bees food when the crop is not in bloom.
- **Rachel Carson in 1962, and many bee scientists since, such as Dave Goulson:** Poisons travel through soil, water and food chains far beyond the field. Studies since 2012 found that a group of pesticides called neonicotinoids harms bees, even in tiny amounts. The bees get worse at finding their way home and at raising young. In 2018 the European Union banned three of them from outdoor use.
- **Pesticide makers and many farmers:** Without pesticides, harvests of some crops fall sharply and food costs more. They point to field studies that found little harm to honeybee hives at real farm doses. They say that mites, disease and loss of wild flowers harm bees more.
- **Farm scientists who promote integrated pest management:** Count the insects first. Help the hunters and change the crop each season. Use the mildest spray, on the smallest area, only when the count passes a set level. Farmers in Asia who learnt this in field schools cut their spraying greatly and kept their rice harvests.
### Are insects disappearing? The counts disagree · *debated*
**Definition:** A decline is a fall in numbers over time. To know if insects are declining, the same places must be counted the same way for many years.

Older people often say there were more fireflies and butterflies when they were young. Is it true? Memory is a poor counter. In Germany, members of an insect society in the town of Krefeld set the same traps in nature reserves for 27 years. In 2017 they reported that the weight of flying insects caught had fallen by about three-quarters. The news went round the world. Then a larger study pooled counts from many countries. It found land insects falling by about 9 in every 100 each ten years, and water insects rising. For India, almost no long counts exist. So nobody can yet say what is happening here.
- e.g. The Krefeld counters were mostly unpaid volunteers. Their careful records are the longest of their kind.
- e.g. Likely causes of decline include loss of wild land, pesticides, bright lights at night and a warming climate.
- e.g. Fireflies need dark nights to find each other by their flashes. Street lights hide the signal.
- e.g. A count of butterflies in the same park, on the same date each year, becomes useful science after a few years.
- **Caspar Hallmann and the Krefeld Entomological Society, in 2017:** Across 63 protected sites in Germany, the weight of flying insects fell by about 76 in every 100 over 27 years. If insects are falling inside reserves, the wider land may be worse.
- **Roel van Klink and colleagues, in 2020:** Pooling 166 long-term surveys, they found a slower fall on land and a rise in freshwater insects, perhaps because rivers have been cleaned. The picture differs from place to place, and a single alarming number misleads.
- **Critics of the phrase 'insect apocalypse', such as Manu Saunders:** Most long counts come from Europe and North America. Places that started with unusually high numbers are more likely to be studied and to show a fall. Insects need protecting, and overstating what is known can damage trust in science.
### Do insects feel? The evidence is growing, and scientists are divided · *debated*
**Definition:** To feel is to have an experience, such as pain, fear or pleasure. Scientists cannot observe an experience directly, so they look for behaviour that is hard to explain without it.

A bee's brain is smaller than a sesame seed and holds about ten lakh nerve cells. Bees learn colours, count up to small numbers and remember routes. Their choices depend on what has happened to them before. Some scientists say these are automatic responses with nobody feeling them. The evidence is hard to brush aside. In 2022 a team in London gave bumblebees small wooden balls. The bees rolled them again and again, with no food as reward. Young bees rolled more than old ones, as happens with play in mammals. In 2011 honeybees who had been shaken, as if a badger were attacking the hive, treated uncertain smells as bad news. They behaved like worried animals.
- e.g. Fruit flies who lose a fight are less willing to fight again for hours.
- e.g. An injured insect often keeps walking on a damaged leg. Some scientists take this as a sign of no pain. Others point out that hiding weakness is common in animals who are hunted.
- e.g. In 2024 scientists finished a map of every nerve cell in a fruit fly's brain: about 1,40,000 cells and about 5 crore connections between them.
- e.g. Paper wasps recognise the faces of other wasps in their nest.
- **Lars Chittka, in his 2022 book 'The Mind of a Bee', and Samadi Galpayage Dona, who led the ball-rolling study:** Bees show play, mood-like states and flexible choices. The simplest explanation is that bees have some kind of inner experience. We should treat insects with care while the question is studied.
- **Shelley Adamo, a Canadian scientist, and others:** Insect brains are small, and each of these behaviours can be produced by a fairly small group of nerve cells with no need for feeling. A robot can be built to avoid damage without feeling pain. They ask for stronger tests before deciding.
- **Jain teaching, in the Tattvartha Sutra, chapter 2:** Every living being has awareness, from one-sensed beings upward. Ants and bees are named as beings with three and four senses, who feel pleasure and pain.
- **Thomas Nagel, a philosopher, in his 1974 essay 'What Is It Like to Be a Bat?':** Even with every measurement of a brain, we could not know what the experience of another kind of animal is like from the inside. Science describes from outside, and experience is had from inside.

## Mostly agreed
- Ants alone outnumber people by about 25 lakh to one. *How we know:* In 2022 Patrick Schultheiss and colleagues pooled 489 studies and estimated about 20 quadrillion ants alive at one time. Shared out, that is about 25 lakh ants for every person.
- Insects live on every continent, including Antarctica. *How we know:* A wingless midge, only a few millimetres long, lives on the Antarctic Peninsula and spends most of each year frozen. This midge is the largest animal who lives wholly on land there.
- Insects were living on land long before the first dinosaurs. *How we know:* Fossils of insect relatives and early insects are found in rocks about 40 crore years old, from before there were trees, and long before the dinosaurs.
- Almost every insect kind does us no harm at all. *How we know:* Farm scientists who list the insects causing real loss count a few thousand kinds worldwide. Set beside the lakhs of kinds that exist, that is well under one in a hundred.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, Francesco Redi, Maria Sibylla Merian, About 350 BCE to 1679.** For about two thousand years, most scholars believed that insects grew by themselves out of mud, dew, dung and rotting meat. Aristotle wrote this in his History of Animals, about 350 BCE. It fitted what everyone saw. Leave meat out, and in a few days it crawls with maggots, though nobody put them there. In 1668 Francesco Redi, a doctor in Florence, tested it. He put meat in jars. Some he left open. Some he sealed. Some he covered with fine cloth that let air in. Flies went into the open jars, and maggots appeared there. On the cloth, flies laid eggs that he could see, and no maggots appeared in the meat below. Maggots came from flies' eggs. In 1679, in Germany, Maria Sibylla Merian published her paintings of caterpillars she had reared, each shown with their food plant, cocoon and moth. She had begun this work at the age of thirteen. Together they showed that every insect has parents.  
*What people believed before:* People believed insects arose by themselves from mud, dung and rotting meat. It was believable because the eggs are too small to notice and the maggots seem to come from nowhere. Redi's covered jars showed that no flies meant no maggots.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Insects feel pain and should be protected as other animals are.** (Lars Chittka, Matilda Gibbons and colleagues, in a 2022 review; *debated*)  
  For: Insects show learned avoidance, trade-offs between harm and reward, wound-tending in some kinds, and mood-like states. Their nervous systems can turn pain signals up and down, as ours can.  
  Against: Shelley Adamo and others say that a small group of nerve cells can explain each of these behaviours, with no feeling needed. They add that nobody has a test for feeling in a brain so different from ours.
- **Keeping more honeybee hives is the way to save pollinators.** (Beekeepers' associations and the honey companies that run 'adopt a hive' schemes; *debated*)  
  For: Honeybees pollinate many crops and are easy to move to where they are needed. More hives mean more pollination and income from honey.  
  Against: Jonas Geldmann and Juan González-Varo argued in 2018 that kept honeybees are farm animals. Large numbers of hives can take food from wild bees and pass diseases to them. Many crops are pollinated better by wild bees and flies.
- **Bees do not read the waggle dance. They find food by smell alone.** (Adrian Wenner and Patrick Wells, from 1967; *rejected*)  
  For: In their experiments, recruited bees went to the smell they had met on the dancer, even when the dance pointed elsewhere.  
  Against: In 1989 a model bee with no flower smell sent real bees in the right direction. In 2005 radar followed new bees as they flew the direction and distance that the dance showed.
- **People should eat insects in place of much of the meat they eat now.** (The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, in a 2013 report, and food scientists such as Arnold van Huis; *debated*)  
  For: About two hundred crore people already eat insects. They include communities in Nagaland, Assam and Odisha. Farmed insects need far less land, water and feed than cattle do for the same amount of protein.  
  Against: If insects feel, farming them by the crore could cause suffering on a huge scale. Some researchers also doubt the savings once heating and feed are counted.

## Roots
You have learnt how insects are built, how they grow and what they do. Now we ask what the words 'friend' and 'foe' really point to, and what insects show us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You put sugar on a card, and ants came and carried it home. In the kitchen tin, the same ants doing the same thing are called pests. The caterpillar who eats the leaf is the butterfly who carries the pollen. So 'friend' and 'foe' name where we are standing when we look. The insect is one being all along.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort insects into helpful ones and harmful ones, as if each insect carried the label inside. That sorting makes us feel that some lives are for us and some are against us. The lesson's findings show the same insect moving between the two lists as the place and the watcher change.  
*Evidence:* Ants carry sugar home in just the same way from a card on the floor and from a tin in the kitchen. Only our name for them changes. A lone locust and a swarming locust are one animal. A leaf-eating caterpillar and a pollinating butterfly are one individual.

- *who am i:* A bee's flower and your flower look different, because your eyes and hers pick up different light. So what you see is the world as your own eyes build it. Then who is doing the seeing in you? And does a bee wonder about this, or does she simply have no way to tell us?
- *what is life:* A fig tree cannot make seeds without one kind of wasp, and the wasp cannot have young without the fig. A hive decides with no ruler. Life is not a set of separate beings who sometimes help each other. Each life is made partly of other lives.
- *what is this world:* Insects were flying, pollinating and clearing waste for crores of years before any human lived. The soil, the fruit and the clean ground that we take as given are their work. The world is something countless small beings are doing, every moment, all together.

Follow any insect far enough and the line between 'that insect' and 'the rest' gets hard to draw. The bee is in the mustard seed, the dung beetle is in the soil, and the mosquito's young are in the fish. We name an insect friend or foe at the point where her life touches our plans. That is a fact about our plans. The Upanishads below go one step further. They say that one consciousness is the ground from which the bee, the flower and the child who watches them all arise. Many scientists do not accept this last step, and their view is given below.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.9:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu: bees gather the juices of many different trees and make them into one honey. In the honey, no juice can say 'I am the juice of this tree' or 'I am the juice of that one'. In the same way, he says, all beings merge in one being and do not know it. (The lesson shows bees doing exactly this gathering. The Upanishad uses it as a picture of many becoming one. Science can confirm the honey, and has no test for the oneness the sage speaks of.)
- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.5, the 'honey teaching':** Yajnavalkya says: this earth is honey for all beings, and all beings are honey for this earth. He repeats it for water, fire, wind and the sun. Each thing is the nourishment of all the others. (Pollination, dung beetles and fig wasps show the same pattern in small: each life feeding and being fed by others. The teaching says this is true of everything. The lesson's science shows it for insects, flowers and soil.)
- **Kuruntokai, poem 2, by Iraiyanar, from the Tamil Sangam collections:** The poet speaks to a bee as to an expert. 'You whose life is to study the pollen of flowers, tell me truly, and not to please me. Among all the flowers you know, is any sweeter than my beloved's hair?' (About two thousand years ago a Tamil poet treated a bee as someone who knows things a human does not. The lesson's science agrees that a bee senses a flower in ways we cannot.)
- Science (settled): All insects, and all other living beings studied so far, share one common ancestor. A child and an ant are distant relatives.
- Science (debated): Whether insects have inner experience is being actively tested, with evidence of play and mood-like states in bees.
- Other view, Many neuroscientists, and the philosopher Daniel Dennett: Awareness is something large, complex brains do. An insect's small brain may run skilful behaviour with nobody at home, as a thermostat keeps a room warm without feeling cold. On this view, kinship with insects is a fact about ancestry alone, and says nothing about inner experience.
- Practice, Five minutes with one insect (5 min): Find one insect with your child: an ant on a wall, a fly on a window, a bee on a flower. Sit an arm's length away. Say: 'For five minutes we will watch this one insect and nothing else. Breathe slowly. Do not touch or name what you see. Only watch what the insect does next.' Afterwards ask: 'What was the insect trying to do? Did you feel like a friend, a foe or neither while you watched?'
- Ask: The ants at your sugar drop and the ants in the sugar tin did the same thing. Why do we give them different names?
- Ask: If a bee and you see different flowers, is there one flower or two?
- Ask: A hive has no ruler and still decides. Is a hive one being or many?
- Ask: How would you find out if an ant feels anything?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A bee sees ultraviolet lines on a flower that looks plain yellow to you. Which is the flower's real colour? (hint: Ask where a colour is: in the flower, in the light, or in the one who is looking.)
- *deep:* A caterpillar's body is taken apart and rebuilt inside the pupa. Is the moth the same individual as the caterpillar, or a new one? (hint: Think about what would have to stay the same for you to say 'the same one'.)
- *deep:* No bee is in charge of the hive, yet the hive chooses a home, keeps warm and stores food. Who decides? (hint: Compare with your own body, where no single cell is in charge.)
- *deep:* If the caterpillar who eats your plant becomes the butterfly who carries pollen between flowers, at what point did a 'pest' become a 'helper'? (hint: Ask whether the insect changed, or only your reason for looking.)
- *big:* Scholars believed for two thousand years that a king ruled the hive. What might we be getting wrong today because of what we expect to see? (hint: Think about the words we use for insects, such as 'worker', 'queen' and 'pest', and where those words came from.)
- *big:* Is it fair to end a silkworm's life for a sari? Does your answer change for a mosquito, a cockroach or a bee? (hint: Notice what you are weighing each time: need, harm, numbers, or how the insect looks.)
- *big:* We cannot ask a bee what she feels. How sure do we need to be that insects feel before we change how we treat them? (hint: Think about what it costs to be wrong in each direction.)
- *big:* If every insect vanished tomorrow, what would be missing from your plate, your street and your nearest park within a year? (hint: Go through the jobs one at a time: pollen, cleaning, food for birds, soil.)
- *big:* A locust is harmless alone and joins a swarm when crowded. Do the places and the company we keep change who we are, too? (hint: Think of how you behave alone, and in a noisy group.)

## Story: The Guard of the Som Tree
*A made-up story set in a village near Sualkuchi, Assam. The family is imagined. What is real: muga silkworms are reared outdoors on som trees and guarded against birds, the full-grown silkworms crawl down the trunk in the evening and are collected to spin on bundles of dry leaves, most cocoons are heated so the thread can be unwound whole, and some moths are kept to lay the eggs for the next rearing. Nobody knows what a silkworm or a pupa experiences.*

For the Bihu holidays, Rishabh, who was eight, came from Bengaluru to his grandmother's village in Assam. On the first morning his cousin Junmoni, who was nine, handed him a long bamboo pole with a rag tied to the end.

'What is this for?' asked Rishabh.

'Guarding,' said Junmoni. 'Come.'

Behind the house stood a row of som trees. At first Rishabh saw only leaves. Then one of the leaves moved. It was a caterpillar as long as his hand and as thick as his thumb, bright green, with small red dots along the side. Then he saw another, and another. The trees were full of them, all chewing.

'Aita!' he shouted to their grandmother. 'Your trees have pests! Hundreds!'

Aita came out wiping her hands. She was laughing. 'Those are my muga, child. Those pests are your mother's wedding mekhela. Each one will spin a golden cocoon.'

A bulbul landed on the top branch, looked about and hopped lower. Junmoni waved the pole and clapped. The bulbul flew to the fence and sat there, watching.

'That is the pest,' said Junmoni. 'She has been at it since sunrise. Yesterday she took four.'

Rishabh frowned. He looked at the caterpillars stripping the som branches bare. He looked at the bulbul. 'But the worms are eating the tree,' he said. 'If I were the tree, I would say the bulbul is my friend.'

Junmoni opened her mouth and shut it again.

'He is right,' said Aita. She sat down on the step. 'Ask the tree, and the muga is the pest. Ask me, and the bulbul is. Ask the bulbul.' She pointed with her chin. In the hedge behind the fence was a small cup of a nest, and three open beaks. 'She has those to feed. To her, I am the pest. I am the big animal with the pole who stands between her children and their breakfast.'

'So who is right?' said Rishabh.

'I guard my worms,' said Aita. 'She feeds her chicks. The tree grows new leaves after the worms come down. Everybody is doing what they must. Keep waving the pole.'

They guarded all day, in turns. In the evening something happened that Rishabh had not expected. The biggest caterpillars stopped eating. One by one they began to walk down the trunks, head first, in a slow line.

'They are ready,' said Junmoni. 'They come down to find a place to spin.' She and Aita lifted each one gently into a bamboo tray. In the shed they set them on bundles of dry leaves. By lamplight Rishabh watched one caterpillar swing her head from side to side, laying a thread as fine as a hair, back and forth, back and forth, until a pale gold wall hid her.

'What happens inside?' he whispered.

'The body is taken apart and made again,' said Junmoni. 'In about three weeks a moth comes out.'

Aita was quiet for a moment. 'From a few of them,' she said. 'I keep the best cocoons for moths, so there will be eggs for the next rearing. The rest I put in the hot sun before the moth can cut the thread. If the moth comes out, the thread is in pieces.'

Rishabh understood slowly. 'So the ones inside...'

'Yes,' said Aita. She did not look away from him. 'All day I chase the bulbul for taking four. Then I take four hundred. My mother did it this way, and her mother. Some weavers now wait for every moth to leave and spin the broken thread instead. Their cloth is rougher and costs more. I have thought about it. I have not decided.'

Nobody said anything. In the dark outside, a bat went over the som trees.

'Junmoni,' said Rishabh at last, 'tomorrow, when the bulbul comes, are we the guards or the pests?'

Junmoni picked up the bamboo pole and turned it over in her hands. 'I think,' she said, 'it depends who is telling the story.'

**For the parent:** The silkworm is a pest to the tree and a treasure to the weaver. The bulbul is a thief to the weaver and a mother to her chicks. Friend and foe are places to stand, and each being stands in a different one.

- Ask: Rishabh said that if he were the tree, the bulbul would be his friend. Whose side would you have taken at the start, and did it change?
- Ask: Aita chases the bulbul for taking four silkworms, and takes four hundred herself. Is that different? Why, or why not?
- Ask: Aita said she has not decided about waiting for the moths. What would help her decide?
- Ask: Think of an insect in your home that you call a pest. Tell the story from that insect's side.

## Games
### g1 · Growing-Up Families: a card game about insect stages (25 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the 28 cards. With two players, deal 7 cards each. With three or four players, deal 5 each. Put the rest face down as the draw pile.; On your turn, ask any player for one stage card. Name the insect and the stage. For example: 'Do you have the pupa of the ladybird?' You may ask only for an insect whose card you already hold.; If the player has the card, they hand it over and you ask again. If not, they say 'Go and grow!' and you take the top card of the draw pile. If it is the card you asked for, show it and ask again. If not, your turn ends.; When you hold every stage of one insect, lay the cards in life order on the table and read the back of each aloud. That is 4 cards for the silk moth, ladybird, mosquito and dung beetle, and 3 cards for the grasshopper, dragonfly, cockroach and cricket. Score 1 point for each card.; Take 1 bonus point if you can say how the young stage looks different from the adult. The silk moth's young is a worm and the adult has wings. The grasshopper's young looks like the adult, only smaller.; If you have no cards in your hand on your turn, draw 3 cards from the pile, or all that are left.; The game ends when all eight insects have been laid down. The player with the most points wins. If you tie, share the win.; Playing alone: spread all 28 cards face up. Sort them into the eight insects and put each set in life order. Time yourself, and try to beat your time.

- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet, to copy by hand: one row for each player:** Player name. Cards laid down, one point for each card.; Bonus points, one for each set where you said how the young look different from the adult.; Total points.
- **g1s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child one four-stage set and one three-stage set, and read out the backs. Ask what is different.  
  *Parent says:* Some insects grow up in four stages, with a pupa. Others grow in three stages, with no pupa. In this game you collect them all.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the silk moth and the grasshopper. How many stages does each have?
  - Note: Four-stage growth is called complete metamorphosis. Three-stage growth is called incomplete metamorphosis. Both words are in the terms, and you do not need them to play.
- **g1s2 (18 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the game by the rules. Take turns to ask for cards. Read the back of each card aloud when a set is laid down.  
  *Parent says:* Which insect do you want next? Which stage are you missing?  
  *Child mode:* Ask for a card. Collect all the stages of one insect.
  - Note: With two players, the draw pile starts with 14 cards. Everyone can always move, because a player with an empty hand draws 3 cards. Let your child ask first if you are playing a younger child.
- **g1s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When the game ends, ask which insects grow up in three stages and which in four. Ask where each young one lives.  
  *Parent says:* Which young ones live in water? Which ones are nothing like their parents?  
  *Child mode:* Which insects grow up in three stages? Which in four?
  - Ask: Which insects in the game live in water when they are young? (good: The mosquito and the dragonfly.; Mosquito wrigglers and dragonfly nymphs.)
  - Think: A caterpillar and a butterfly eat different foods. What could be good about that for the whole family?
### g2 · Friend, Foe or Both? A game of arguing both sides (20 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the 14 cards into a pile, face down. One player is the speaker and takes the top card. Read the front aloud, but do not read the back yet.; The speaker says 'friend', 'foe' or 'both' for the insect in this place, and gives one reason. Score 1 point for the reason.; Then the speaker must name a different place or a different time where the same insect would get the opposite name. Score 2 points for a place that makes sense to the others.; If the speaker cannot find a place, any other player may offer one. That player scores 2 points.; Any other player may add a view that no one has said yet, such as the plant's side, the insect's side or the farmer's side. Score 1 point for each new view.; Now read the back of the card aloud. Talk about whether you agree.; Pass the pile to the next player. The game ends when all 14 cards have been read. The player with the most points wins. If you tie, share the win.; No one may say 'pest' or 'helper' without saying where and when the insect is doing the thing.; Playing alone: read a card, say your answer, then read the back. Score 1 point for each idea on the back that you said before reading it. Play the pile twice and see if your score goes up.
- **Printable cards: 14 scene cards, to cut out, with the other side of the story on the back:** Ants walk into the sugar tin in your kitchen. (back: In the kitchen, a foe to your sugar. On a balcony, the same ants carry away a dead moth and loosen the soil. Same ants, two places.); A caterpillar eats holes in a curry-leaf plant. (back: A foe to the leaves. Three weeks later the same insect is a butterfly who carries pollen. The name changes with what she is doing.); Termites chew the wooden frame of a door. (back: A foe to the door. In a forest, the same termites turn fallen wood back into soil.); A lone desert locust sits in the grass at the edge of a field. (back: Alone, she harms nobody. In a crowded swarm, the same animal changes colour and behaviour, and a swarm can eat in a day what thousands of people eat.); A mosquito bites you at night. (back: A foe to you, because some carry malaria or dengue. Her young feed fish and dragonfly nymphs, and male mosquitoes drink nectar and pollinate some flowers.); A ladybird sits on a rose covered with aphids. (back: A friend to the rose, because she eats the aphids. Not every ladybird hunts. One kind with 28 spots eats brinjal leaves instead.); A wasp hovers over a picnic. (back: A nuisance at the picnic. All summer, the same wasp hunts caterpillars to feed her young.); Flies crowd onto the flowers of a mango tree. (back: Many people shoo them away. Flies are the main pollinators of mango, so no flies could mean few mangoes.); Dung beetles roll a ball of dung along a village path. (back: A messy sight, and the dung is buried, which feeds the soil. Australia's pastures were covered in cow pats until the right dung beetles were brought in.); A silkworm spins her cocoon on a rearing tray. (back: A friend to the weaver's family who depends on the thread. When the cocoon is heated for the thread, the pupa inside dies. Eri silk lets the moth leave. Who should decide?); Maggots work on a dead animal in a field. (back: Unpleasant to look at, and they are the clean-up crew. Some hospitals use clean maggots to clear dead tissue from wounds.); A cockroach walks across the kitchen floor at night. (back: A foe in the kitchen, because she can carry germs on her feet. In a forest, the same kind of insect helps to break down fallen leaves.); Bees have built a comb under the water tank on your roof. (back: Some people want the bees gone because they might sting. Others say rock bees pollinate the whole neighbourhood. People who know bees can move a comb safely, so nobody should poke at it.); A dragonfly lands on a reed beside a pond. (back: A friend to people, because she eats mosquitoes. She also eats flies and small butterflies. So who is her friend, and who is her foe?)
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet, to copy by hand: one column for each player:** Player name.; Points for each reason given, one for each.; Points for each opposite place found, two for each.; Points for each extra view added by another player, one for each.; Total points.
- **g2s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read out the first card to your child. Say friend, foe or both, and give a reason. Then ask your child to find a place where the same insect would get the other name.  
  *Parent says:* We call an insect a friend or a foe by where she is and what she is doing. Let us try it with a card.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the first card. Say friend, foe or both. Say why.
  - Note: Model the answer once in a short sentence. Children often say 'foe' for stinging or biting insects and 'friend' for pretty ones. Ask them to give the reason, not only the word.
- **g2s2 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play through the cards by the rules. Keep score on the sheet.  
  *Parent says:* What is the insect doing? Where is she? Where would she get a different name?  
  *Child mode:* Read a card. Say friend, foe or both. Find a place where she would get the other name.
  - Note: There is no wrong word, only a better or weaker reason. When someone says 'foe', ask what the insect would be doing in a forest or on a flower. A real discussion matters more than the points.
- **g2s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When the game ends, ask which insect was the hardest to name, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which insect was the hardest? Did you change your mind about anyone?  
  *Child mode:* Which insect was hardest to name? Did you change your mind about anyone?
  - Think: If the name changes with the place, where does the 'friend' or 'foe' live: in the insect or in us?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Ant Café: what do your ants choose, and how fast does the news spread? (42 min)
- **question:** Four small foods sit in a row beside an ant trail: sugar water, salt water, plain water and an oily crumb. Which one do the ants visit most, and how quickly do more ants come once one ant has found the best food?
- **predict:** Before you set anything out, guess which food will get the most ants and which will get the fewest. Say why you think so. Then guess how many minutes the first ant will take to find the best one.
- **fair test:** The four foods sit on four identical squares of tissue, in one row, each the same distance from the trail. Only the food changes. Each food is counted every 2 minutes for 10 minutes. Then the row is reversed and the foods are counted for 10 minutes more, because ants may simply like the square nearest to where they arrive. A food that wins in both rounds is a real winner.
- **safety:** Do not touch the ants, because some kinds bite. Look from a hand's length away. Wash your hands with soap afterwards. Wipe up every drop of sugar water at the end, so that no trail leads into the kitchen.
- **record:** Fill in the table for both rounds. Add up each food's ants across the two rounds and draw four bars, one for each food. Write the minute when the first ant touched any square, and the minute when one square first held 5 ants. Then write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Round and minute (2, 4, 6, 8, 10) | Ants on the sugar water | Ants on the salt water | Ants on the plain water | Ants on the oily crumb
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look for ants. Try the kitchen floor near the sink, the foot of a wall, a window sill or the pots on a balcony. Pick a place where ants are already walking in a line. Sit one arm's length away. Ask your child to guess which of the four foods the ants will visit most, and why. Write the guess on the table sheet.  
  *Parent says:* Four small foods are coming to the ant café. They are sugar water, salt water, plain water and a crumb of fried snack. Which will get the most ants? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Find a line of ants. Sit an arm's length back. Guess which food gets the most ants, and say why.
  - Ask: Which food will get the most ants, and why? (good: Sugar water, because ants like sweet things.; The oily crumb, because it is real food.; Any guess with a reason. The ants will give the answer.)
  - Note: A guess is never marked right or wrong. If you see no ants, put one drop of sugar water on a card near a kitchen corner, a window sill or a plant pot. Wait up to 15 minutes. If none come, try early morning, evening or another day. Nobody touches the ants.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the foods. Stir 1 teaspoon of sugar into 2 tablespoons of water in one katori until it is clear. Do the same with 1 teaspoon of salt in a second katori. Pour 2 tablespoons of plain water into a third. Crush a pinch of fried snack into the fourth. Put the four tissue squares on the card in a row, 8 centimetres apart. Wet the first with half a teaspoon of sugar water. Wet the second with half a teaspoon of salt water. Wet the third with half a teaspoon of plain water. Put the crushed snack on the fourth. Write A, B, C and D under the squares and note which food is where.  
  *Parent says:* Each square is a small plate. We give every plate the same amount, so that only the food is different. Which square do you think the ants will find first?  
  *Child mode:* Make the three liquids. Wet three tissue squares, one with each. Put crushed snack on the fourth. Put the squares in a row.
  - Note: Use the same amount of liquid on each square. Very salty water may put ants off, and that is a result too. Keep the katoris covered afterwards so that they do not attract ants to the wrong place.
- **s3 (11 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the card to the floor about 10 centimetres from the ant trail. Run the row of squares along the trail, so that each square is the same distance from the trail. Start the timer. At 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 minutes, count the ants standing on each square and write the numbers in the table. Note the minute when the first ant touches any square, and the minute when one square first holds 5 ants.  
  *Parent says:* Count only ants who are standing on a square, not ants walking past. Which square gets an ant first? Do more ants come after the first one?  
  *Child mode:* Tape the card near the trail. Every 2 minutes, count the ants on each square. Write the numbers down.
  - Ask: What happens on the winning square after the first ant arrives? (good: Many more ants come. The first ant leaves a smell trail and her sisters follow the trail.; It gets crowded fast, much faster than the first ant took to arrive.)
  - Note: Dot each ant on scrap paper as you count, so that your child can count again if the ants move. If no ant touches a square in 10 minutes, move the card up to 3 centimetres from the trail and start again. If still none, leave the card for an hour and count once then. Do not brush ants away, and do not block the trail.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Reverse the row. The food at the left end goes to the right end, and the food at the right end goes to the left. The two in the middle swap places. Write which food is now on which square.  
  *Parent says:* Now every food is in a new place. Why would we move them?  
  *Child mode:* Reverse the row of squares. Every food gets a new place.
  - Note: This round checks whether ants just like the place nearest to where they arrive. If one food wins in both rounds, the food is the reason.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count again at 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 minutes, and write the numbers in the second part of the table.  
  *Parent says:* Is the same food winning? Or did the winner change when we moved the squares?  
  *Child mode:* Count the ants on each square again, 5 times. Write the numbers down.
  - Note: The ants know the way by now, so more may arrive earlier in this round. That is the trail at work and a good thing to notice.
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add each food's numbers from both rounds. Draw four bars, one for each food. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then wipe the card and the floor with a damp cloth, throw away the tissue squares and wash hands.  
  *Parent says:* Which food got the most ants in both rounds? Was your guess right? What do you think the ants were doing with the sugar?  
  *Child mode:* Add up the ants for each food. Draw four bars. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: This test cannot tell us why the ants chose as they did, or whether the ants like the taste. It tells us about one ant family on one day. Ants of other kinds, or the same ants in another season, may choose differently. Some ants are hungry for fat or protein and ignore sugar. What else could your child test? A weaker or stronger sugar water, a different time of day, or a crumb of rice are good next questions.
  - Think: If a family found these same ants at the sugar in a tin, what would they call them? What did the ants do differently?
### e2 · The Wobble Test: why do walking insects never tip over? (20 min)
- **question:** A table on four legs can rock if the floor is a little uneven. A table on three legs never does. Is that true? What does it show about six-legged walkers?
- **predict:** Guess which will rock when you tap it, a card on four caps or a card on three caps, when one cap is a little taller. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same card, the same caps, the same coins and the same table are used every time. Only the number of caps under the card changes, four or three. One cap is lifted by a small stack of coins, and each cap takes a turn at being the lifted one. Each setup is tapped 5 times in the middle with one finger.
- **record:** Fill in the table for 4 caps and then for 3 caps. Write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why. Draw the card on three caps from the side, and mark where an insect's three steady legs would be.
- **results table:** Number of caps | Which cap is lifted (1 to 4) | Does the card rock? (yes or no) | Clicks in 5 taps
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the table on a flat, hard surface. Set the 4 caps upside down at the four corners of the card, under it, so that the card rests on them. Check that the card sits flat.  
  *Parent says:* This is a small table with four legs. When the floor is flat, it stands. What do you think will happen when one leg is slightly taller?  
  *Child mode:* Rest the card on four caps, one at each corner.
  - Note: Use caps that are all the same. Put the card on a flat, hard table, never a cloth or a bed.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess. Then lift one cap with two coins stacked under it and write the number of the lifted cap on the table.  
  *Parent says:* Tap the middle of the card with one finger. Will the card rock, or stay still? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess if the card will rock. Say why.
  - Ask: Will the card on four caps rock when one cap is higher? (good: Yes, because the card can tip over the two caps that touch at opposite corners.; No, because the card is heavy. Any guess with a reason is a good start.)
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tap the middle of the card 5 times with one finger. Count the clicks and note whether it rocks. Then lift each of the other three caps in turn and tap 5 times each. Write all four results.  
  *Parent says:* Is it the same every time? Which cap you lift does not seem to matter. Why might that be?  
  *Child mode:* Tap the card 5 times. Count the clicks. Do it with each cap lifted in turn.
  - Note: A rock sounds like a click-clack. A lifted cap leaves the card touching only two caps at opposite corners, and it tips back and forth over them.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take one cap away, so that the card rests on three caps placed in a triangle under it. Lift one cap with two coins. Tap 5 times and write the result. Do this with each of the three caps lifted in turn.  
  *Parent says:* Now there are only three legs. Which do you think will rock?  
  *Child mode:* Use three caps. Lift one with coins. Tap 5 times. Does the card rock?
  - Note: Place the three caps so that the card's middle sits inside the triangle they make. The card may tilt a little, and that is fine. Tilting is not rocking. A rock means the card tips back and forth.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Draw the card on three caps from the side.  
  *Parent says:* Which table never rocked? Why can three points always hold a flat card steady? An ant lifts three legs and keeps three down. What shape do her feet make on the ground?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw and why. Draw the card on three caps from the side.
  - Note: Three points always make a flat triangle, and a card can rest on a triangle without rocking. This test cannot show how a real insect walks. Real feet have sticky pads and claws, and real insects also run so fast that they glide. Engineers who copy the tripod walk in six-legged robots have to test them on real rubble.
  - Think: Could a walker with four legs lift two at a time and stay steady? What would you test?

## Activities
### a1 · Be an antenna: find the matching smells with your eyes shut (20 min)
- **record:** Draw the six cups and join the matching pairs with lines. Write the distance in steps at which you could first smell the clove.
- **a1s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put two cloves, crushed, into two cups. Put a pinch of tea leaves into two more cups. Put a piece of squeezed orange or lemon peel into the last two. Cover each cup with cloth and a rubber band. Shuffle the cups so that you do not know which are pairs.  
  *Parent says:* An insect has no nose on her face. She smells with her antennae. Today you will smell with your nose, and match pairs the way an ant checks that another ant smells like her nest.  
  *Child mode:* Make six covered cups, two of each smell. Shuffle them.
  - Note: Do the shuffling out of your child's sight, so that the pairs are a surprise. If you have no orange or lemon peel, use a pinch of turmeric or a small piece of jaggery.
- **a1s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to close their eyes or wear the scarf. Hand over one cup at a time. They sniff it and put it into a pile. They find the three pairs by smell alone. Then they open their eyes and check.  
  *Parent says:* Take a good sniff. Which two cups smell the same? Do not peek. How did you decide?  
  *Child mode:* Sniff each cup with your eyes shut. Put matching cups together. Then open your eyes and check.
  - Ask: How did you tell the smells apart? (good: By how strong they were, and by whether they were sweet or sharp.; I remembered the first smell and compared the next one with it.)
  - Note: If your child struggles, give a break after 3 or 4 sniffs. The nose gets tired and then smells everything the same. A breath of fresh air resets it.
- **a1s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Crush a fresh clove and hold it in your open palm. Ask your child to stand at one end of a room with eyes shut. Walk slowly towards them from the far end of a room until they say they can smell it. Count the steps between you.  
  *Parent says:* A male silk moth can smell a female silk moth from very far away. How far can you smell the clove?  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Say 'now' when you smell the clove. Count the steps between you.
  - Note: A room with a fan or open windows moves the smell around, so your child may smell it from the side before you reach them. That is a good thing to talk about. Moths use the wind too.
  - Think: If you could smell as well as a moth, how would your day change?
### a2 · A fly's-eye picture: draw it in a few big squares and in many small ones (25 min)
- **record:** Keep both pictures. Write one sentence on which picture shows the flower better, and one on which one a moving object would be easier to notice in.
- **a2s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the picture to copy. Draw a square 12 centimetres wide on paper. Divide it into 16 equal squares, 4 across and 4 down.  
  *Parent says:* A compound eye is made of many tiny lenses, and each lens sees one small patch. Your brain joins the patches. We will make a picture the same way.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a big square. Divide it into 16 equal squares.
  - Note: On squared paper, a block of 3 small squares by 3 small squares counts as one big square. A 12 centimetre square split into 4 by 4 gives squares of 3 centimetres.
- **a2s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill each of the 16 squares with one colour only. Use the colour that fills most of that square in the real picture. Do not draw lines inside a square.  
  *Parent says:* Each square is one lens of an eye. It can show only one colour. Can you still see a flower?  
  *Child mode:* Fill each square with one colour only. Make a flower picture.
  - Ask: Can you tell it is a flower? (good: Yes, a rough one. I can see red in the middle and green below.; Barely. The squares are too big to show a petal.)
- **a2s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a second square of the same size, divided into 64 equal squares, 8 across and 8 down. Fill each with one colour. This will take a while, so take turns with a grown-up if your child likes.  
  *Parent says:* Now each lens is much smaller. There are four times as many lenses. What can you see now that you could not before?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the second square with 64 small squares. Fill each with one colour.
  - Note: This is a rough model. A real compound eye has thousands of lenses, so the world is made of tiny patches too. A fly or a dragonfly sees movement very well because a moving thing passes from one unit to the next.
- **a2s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the two pictures side by side. Ask your child which shows the flower better. Then ask how a moving bee would look in each: crossing one big square, or 4 small ones.

  *Child mode:* Compare the pictures. Which shows more? Why?
  - Note: A bee sees ultraviolet light, which our eyes cannot see, and is poor at seeing red. Compound eyes make a less sharp picture than ours but catch movement very well. We cannot know exactly what an insect sees, only what the eye can and cannot sense.
  - Think: A bee has about 5,000 lenses in each eye and a dragonfly up to about 30,000. What does a dragonfly see that a bee does not?
### a3 · Wear an outside skeleton: why an insect must moult (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting with scissors. Keep the card tube loose on the arm.
- **record:** Draw your arm in its card tube with the joints marked. Write one sentence about what happens to a hard tube when the arm inside it grows.
- **a3s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Roll a stiff card around your child's forearm, from the wrist to the elbow, loosely enough to slide off. Tape the edge so the card stays a tube. Slide it back on. Ask your child to bend the elbow.  
  *Parent says:* An insect wears her skeleton on the outside, like a suit of armour. This tube is armour for your arm. Can you bend your elbow?  
  *Child mode:* Put the tube on your forearm. Try to bend your elbow.
  - Ask: What happens when you try to bend your arm? (good: The tube is in the way. It does not bend.; I can only bend where there is no tube.)
  - Note: Keep the tube loose so your child can slide the arm in and out. A tube that is tight can pinch.
- **a3s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Take the tube off. Draw two lines around it, dividing it into three equal rings. Cut along the lines, with the adult doing the cutting, to make three rings. Tape the three rings together on one side. Use strips of tape or cloth, and leave a gap of about 1 centimetre between rings. Slide the tube back on and bend the elbow.  
  *Parent says:* Insect legs have hard tubes with soft joints in between. Now where can your arm bend?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to cut the tube into three rings. Join the rings with soft strips. Bend your elbow.
  - Note: Cutting card with scissors is an adult job in this step. The child can hold the card and draw the lines. A gap of 1 centimetre between rings is what lets the arm bend. This is how an insect leg works: hard sections joined by soft skin.
- **a3s3 (5 min)**

  *Parent says:* The arm grew. The old tube is too small. What does a growing insect have to do?  
  *Child mode:* Wrap a sock around your arm. Try the old tube. Make a bigger tube.
  - Ask: What does a growing insect need to do with her old skin? (good: Get out of it and make a new, bigger one. That is moulting.; She has to split it, climb out and grow a bigger one.)
  - Note: The insect makes a new soft skin under the old one, splits the old skin, climbs out and swells with air. The new skin is soft and easy to hurt for a few hours until it hardens. A card tube cannot stretch, and nor can a hard exoskeleton.
- **a3s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at both tubes. Draw your arm in the joined tube with the joints marked. Write one sentence about what happens to a hard tube when the arm inside it grows.  
  *Parent says:* What is the good thing about a hard outside skeleton? What is the problem with it?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your arm in the joined tube. Write what a growing arm needs.
  - Note: Hard armour protects and stops the insect drying out. The price is that she must moult, and she is soft and easy to hurt until the new skin hardens. Many insects moult in hiding, at night or under leaves.
  - Think: While a newly moulted insect is soft, what could happen to her? How could she stay safe?
### a4 · Walk the thread: how far is one silk cocoon's thread? (20 min)
- **record:** Write the steps and the seconds for 10 metres. Write how many steps and how many minutes it would take to walk 800 metres, and 1 kilometre.
- **a4s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure 10 metres along a floor or path, and mark the start and the end. A strip of paper tape at each end will do.  
  *Parent says:* A silkworm spins one long thread to make her cocoon. It can be several hundred metres, and sometimes about a kilometre. We will find out how long a walk that is.  
  *Child mode:* Measure 10 metres. Mark the start and the end.
  - Note: No tape measure? Use a string marked every 1 metre against a metre ruler or a tailor's tape. Or count 10 long strides of an adult and call that about 10 metres.
- **a4s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to walk the 10 metres at an ordinary pace and count the steps. Time the walk with the phone. Do it 3 times and use the middle value of the three.  
  *Parent says:* Walk like you are going to school. Count each step. Do we get the same number each time?  
  *Child mode:* Walk 10 metres. Count your steps. Time it. Do it 3 times.
  - Note: Three tries are done because one try can be a fluke. Choose the middle value of the three. If your child's steps are different each time, that is real, and it is why we take the middle.
- **a4s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out together. Divide 800 metres by 10 to find how many lots of 10 metres, then multiply by the steps for 10 metres. Do the same for the seconds. Then do the same for 1 kilometre.  
  *Parent says:* How many steps would it take to walk the thread of one cocoon, if it is 800 metres long? How many minutes?  
  *Child mode:* Work out the steps and the minutes for 800 metres. Then for 1 kilometre.
  - Ask: About how long would you walk to follow one cocoon's thread? (good: Around 10 minutes or more, depending on how fast I walk.; A lot more steps than I thought, around a thousand.)
  - Note: Example: 14 steps for 10 metres and 9 seconds. For 800 metres, 80 lots of 10 gives 1,120 steps and 720 seconds, which is 12 minutes. A cocoon's thread is often several hundred metres, and can reach about a kilometre.
- **a4s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Say the answer in a sentence. Ask where in your lane or school the walk would end.  
  *Parent says:* Where would you be after one cocoon's walk? Who made this thread? How long might she have taken?  
  *Child mode:* Write one sentence with your answer. Say where the walk would end.
  - Note: A silkworm makes the thread inside her body and pushes the thread out near her mouth. She takes a few days to spin the cocoon. The thread is one unbroken line, which is why it can be unwound and woven.
  - Think: A silkworm makes all of that thread inside her own body. What do you think that is like?

  *Parent says:* Which of your crosses will fill again first? Why do we need to do this every week?  
  *Child mode:* Put a note on the fridge: do the still-water hunt every week.

  - Think: The mosquito young are eaten by fish and dragonfly nymphs. Why might emptying the water still matter?

## Worksheets
### Insect or not, growing up and helping (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (count) A beetle, a spider and a bee sit on one leaf. How many legs are there altogether?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* A beetle has 6 legs, a spider has 8 and a bee has 6. 6 + 8 + 6 = 20. If you got 18, you gave the spider six legs.
2. (label) Label a worker bee: head, thorax, abdomen, antenna and one wing.  
   **Answer:** ['head: the front part, with the eyes and the mouth', 'thorax: the middle part, where the legs and wings join', 'abdomen: the back part', 'antenna: a feeler on the head', 'wing: joined to the thorax']. *Why:* An insect's body has three parts. The legs and wings join the middle part, and the feelers are on the head.
3. (sort) Sort each small animal. Is she an insect or not?  
   **Answer:** an ant → An insect; a spider → Not an insect; a mosquito → An insect; a centipede → Not an insect; a grasshopper → An insect; a woodlouse → Not an insect. *Why:* An insect has six legs and three body parts. A spider has eight legs, a centipede has many and a woodlouse has fourteen.
4. (truefalse) An insect wears her skeleton on the outside of her body.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Her skeleton is a hard cover on the outside, called an exoskeleton. Yours is inside you.
5. (fill) Shedding an old hard skin so that a bigger one can harden is called ________.  
   **Answer:** ['moulting']. *Why:* A growing insect cannot stretch her hard cover, so she sheds it. This is moulting.
6. (match) Match each young insect to the adult she becomes.  
   **Answer:** Silkworm → Silk moth; Wriggler → Mosquito; Maggot → Housefly; Nymph in a pond → Dragonfly. *Why:* Each young one lives a different life from her adult. The silkworm eats leaves and the moth does not eat at all.
7. (order) Put the stages of a silk moth in order.  
   **Answer:** ['egg', 'silkworm eating mulberry leaves', 'pupa in a cocoon', 'silk moth']. *Why:* The egg hatches into a silkworm. She spins a cocoon, becomes a pupa, and a moth comes out.
8. (mcq) Which of these crops usually gives more when insects visit the flowers?  
   Options: Wheat / Mustard / Maize  
   **Answer:** Mustard. *Why:* Mustard flowers are visited by bees and other insects, who carry pollen. Wheat and maize do not need insects to carry their pollen.
9. (truefalse) Every kind of bee lives in a hive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A carpenter bee nests alone in a hole in wood. Most kinds of bees do not live in hives.
10. (match) Match each insect to her work.  
   **Answer:** Ladybird → Eats aphids; Dung beetle → Buries dung; Tiny wasp → Lays eggs in mealybugs; Silkworm → Spins a long thread. *Why:* Each insect has her own way of living, and people benefit from some of them. A farmer who protects hunters needs less spray.
11. (write) Ants in the sugar tin are called pests. The same ants carrying a dead moth away are called helpers. What changed?  
   **Answer:** The ants did not change. What changed is the place and what they were doing. In the tin they take our sugar, and on the balcony they clear away a dead moth.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the name depends on the place and on what we want, and the insect is the same one. 'Pest' is a name for a meeting between ants and our plans.
12. (draw) Draw the three stages of a grasshopper: egg, nymph and adult. Show how the wings change.  
   **Answer:** An egg, then a nymph that looks like a small grasshopper with tiny wing buds, then an adult with long working wings.. *Why:* A grasshopper has no pupa. The nymph looks like a small adult, and her wings grow with each moult.
13. (perspective) A line of ants has found the sugar tin in a flat.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks one and gives a reason. The lid and the crumb ways keep the ants alive and leave no poison near food. The spray is quick, and it also leaves poison in a kitchen and kills ants who were only looking for food.. *Why:* A sealed tin solves the problem without harm. The crumb idea gives the ants somewhere else to go. A spray works fast but harms the ants and leaves a poison where food is kept.
14. (odd) Three of these are insects. Which is the odd one out? Ladybird, mosquito, scorpion, termite.  
   **Answer:** Scorpion. She has eight legs and two body parts, like a spider.. *Why:* A scorpion is an arthropod, like insects, but not an insect.
15. (truefalse) Scientists still disagree about whether insects feel.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Some scientists point to play, mood and learning as signs of feeling. Others say these are automatic responses. The question is still open.
### Why insects are built and grow this way (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A fly escapes before your hand lands. Which part of her body helps most?  
   Options: Her compound eyes, which catch movement fast / Her abdomen, which is large / Her sticky feet  
   **Answer:** Her compound eyes, which catch movement fast. *Why:* A compound eye is made of many tiny lenses, each looking in a slightly different direction. The picture is less sharp than ours and very good at catching movement.
2. (truefalse) A walking insect keeps three legs on the ground while the other three move.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The three on the ground always make a steady triangle. Then the two sets of legs swap.
3. (mcq) A caterpillar has a row of small dots along her side. What are they?  
   Options: Eyes / Ears / Breathing holes called spiracles  
   **Answer:** Breathing holes called spiracles. *Why:* Spiracles open into thin air tubes that carry air to every part of her body. An insect has no lungs.
4. (truefalse) A bee sees a red flower as bright red, just as you do.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bees do not see red well. They do see ultraviolet, which we cannot see.
5. (sort) Sort each insect by how she grows up.  
   **Answer:** grasshopper → Three stages, no pupa; dragonfly → Three stages, no pupa; ladybird → Four stages, with a pupa; mosquito → Four stages, with a pupa; cockroach → Three stages, no pupa; housefly → Four stages, with a pupa. *Why:* An insect with a nymph has three stages, and an insect with a larva and a pupa has four.
6. (mcq) Empty skins hang on the reeds beside a pond. Where did they come from?  
   Options: Dragonfly nymphs who moulted once more and flew off / Dead dragonflies / Snakes who shed their skins  
   **Answer:** Dragonfly nymphs who moulted once more and flew off. *Why:* A dragonfly nymph climbs a reed, moults one last time and flies away as an adult. The empty skin stays on the reed.
7. (truefalse) The banyan, like other wild fig trees, can make its seeds without any wasp at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The banyan's flowers are hidden inside the fig, and only its own kind of tiny wasp can carry pollen in. Each one needs the other.
8. (mcq) A farmer finds aphids on her chilli plants and a ladybird nearby. What is a bee-safe first step?  
   Options: Spray every leaf at noon / Wait a few days and watch the ladybird / Cut the plant down  
   **Answer:** Wait a few days and watch the ladybird. *Why:* A ladybird and her young eat dozens of aphids a day. Waiting gives the hunter time to work, and a spray would harm her and any bees.
9. (write) Young mosquitoes live in still water. Explain why tipping out the water every week stops them.  
   **Answer:** They need about 8 days or more in water to become adults. If the water is gone before that, they cannot finish growing.. *Why:* A good answer says that the young need still water for more than a week, so emptying it sooner breaks their life.
10. (write) A lone desert locust harms nobody. In a crowd she joins a swarm. What does this say about calling locusts 'pests'?  
   **Answer:** It is the crowd and the place, not the kind of insect, that makes the trouble. The same animal is quiet alone and a swarm in a crowd.. *Why:* A strong answer says that 'pest' describes the situation with people. The locust is the same animal.
11. (truefalse) Only a small share of insect kinds ever damage crops or carry illness.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A few thousand kinds do harm, out of about ten lakh named. That is well under one in a hundred.
12. (count) A locust swarm covers 2 square kilometres. Each square kilometre eats the daily food of 35,000 people. How many people's food?  
   **Answer:** 70,000. *Why:* 35,000 × 2 = 70,000. The same swarm eating for 3 days would eat 2,10,000 people's food.
13. (draw) Draw one plain yellow flower twice: once as you see the flower, and once as a bee sees the same flower.  
   **Answer:** The first flower is plain yellow. The second has dark lines pointing to the middle, which a bee sees in ultraviolet light.. *Why:* Many flowers have ultraviolet marks that point to the nectar. We cannot see them, and a bee can.
14. (perspective) Do insects feel? Two scientists read the same evidence in different ways.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks one reading and gives a reason. Then it names a test, such as watching whether a hurt insect avoids a place that hurt her. No one yet knows.. *Why:* Neither view can be proved wrong yet. Experiments keep adding evidence, and the question stays open.
15. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Grasshopper, cockroach, dragonfly, ladybird.  
   **Answer:** Ladybird. She grows up with a pupa, and the other three grow up in three stages.. *Why:* Grasshoppers, cockroaches and dragonflies all have nymphs. A ladybird has a larva and a pupa.
16. (mcq) If a spray is truly needed on a crop that bees visit, which timing harms bees least?  
   Options: At noon, when the flowers are wide open / Any time, because a mild spray never touches a bee / At dusk, after the bees have gone home  
   **Answer:** At dusk, after the bees have gone home. *Why:* Bees are out on open flowers at noon, and a spray would land on them. At dusk they are back in the hive. Even then, a mild spray is only for when it is truly needed, and never on open flowers.
### Faulty claims, fair tests and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Zoya sees a caterpillar eating her plant and says, 'Butterflies are pests.' What is wrong?  
   Options: Caterpillars are never pests / The caterpillar and the butterfly do different jobs / Butterflies eat leaves too  
   **Answer:** The caterpillar and the butterfly do different jobs. *Why:* A caterpillar eats leaves, and the butterfly she becomes carries pollen. One job does not give a name to the whole animal for her whole life.
2. (truefalse) A pupa does nothing, and nothing happens inside her.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Inside the pupa her body is taken apart and rebuilt, and an adult comes out.
3. (mcq) Imagine a beetle as big as a dog. Which problem would she meet first?  
   Options: Air would reach her muscles too slowly, down long tubes / Her compound eyes would stop working at that size / She could no longer eat leaves  
   **Answer:** Air would reach her muscles too slowly, down long tubes. *Why:* Insects breathe through air tubes. In a very large body the tubes must be very long, and air creeps slowly down them. Compound eyes and leaf-eating do not depend on size in this way.
4. (write) A friend says, 'An insect walking on a hurt leg feels no pain.' Give one reason this does not prove it.  
   **Answer:** Hunted animals often hide their weakness, so that a hunter does not see they are hurt. The same sign could have two causes.. *Why:* A strong answer says the sign could have another cause, and that we cannot see inside an insect. It does not say insects do feel, only that this sign cannot settle it.
5. (mcq) Dina counts 6 ants at sugar and 2 at salt, once. What should she do before saying, 'Ants love sugar'?  
   Options: Say it, because 6 is more than 2 / Add more salt to the salt / Repeat it, and swap where the foods sit  
   **Answer:** Repeat it, and swap where the foods sit. *Why:* One try can be a fluke, and ants may simply like the place nearest to where they arrive. Repeating and swapping the places shows whether the food is the reason.
6. (match) Match each young insect to where she lives.  
   **Answer:** Mosquito wriggler → Still water; Dragonfly nymph → A pond, hunting; Dung beetle grub → Inside a dung ball; Silkworm → On a mulberry leaf. *Why:* Young insects often live where their food is. A dung beetle grub lives in the dung she eats.
7. (sort) Sort each case by what people would call the insect there.  
   **Answer:** a termite eating a wooden door → Called a pest; a termite breaking down a fallen log in a forest → Called a helper; a ladybird eating aphids on a rose → Called a helper; a hadda beetle eating brinjal leaves → Called a pest; a lone locust resting in the grass → Neither; a fly visiting mango flowers → Called a helper. *Why:* Termites and ladybirds appear in more than one place. The names follow what the insect is doing and what we want.
8. (mcq) A farmer plants marigold and coriander among her vegetables. Which is the best reason?  
   Options: They poison the aphids / They feed hunters such as ladybirds / They keep the bees away  
   **Answer:** They feed hunters such as ladybirds. *Why:* Marigold and coriander flowers give hunting insects food and shelter. They do not poison anyone.
9. (write) Is 'friend' or 'foe' a fact about an insect, or about us? Use the caterpillar and the butterfly to explain.  
   **Answer:** It is about us and our plans. The caterpillar eats the leaf, and the butterfly she becomes carries pollen. It is one insect.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the label depends on where the insect is and what we want, and gives the caterpillar example. It may also say that some scientists see the insect's own life as outside friend or foe.
10. (truefalse) If every honeybee vanished, many crops would still give some food.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Wild bees, flies and other insects pollinate too, and rice, wheat and maize do not need insects. Some crops would give less, but many would still give food.
11. (order) Put these steps in order for finding out whether insects are falling in number.  
   **Answer:** ['Set the same traps in the same places', 'Repeat for many years', 'Compare the weights from year to year', 'Think about what else changed']. *Why:* A fair count uses the same method in the same places for many years. Only then can you compare, and then ask what else changed.
12. (write) Scientists disagree about whether insects feel. What would you test, and what result would change your mind?  
   **Answer:** Any test with a clear result, such as seeing whether a hurt insect avoids the place where she was hurt. It also needs a clear reason that the result would change the view.. *Why:* The answer should give a test, say what the result might be, and say what that would mean. There is no single right test.
13. (draw) Draw the whole growth of a mosquito. Show which stages live in water.  
   **Answer:** Four pictures: an egg on water, a wriggler hanging from the surface, a pupa swimming, then an adult flying away. The first three stages sit in water.. *Why:* A mosquito grows up in four stages, and three of them are in still water. That is why still water matters.
14. (perspective) Is it fair to end a silkworm's life to make a sari?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view and gives a reason. Then it names something still unknown, such as how the cost of ahimsa silk compares, or whether the moth is hurt either way.. *Why:* Each view is held by real people. Making silk without ending a life takes more time and costs more, and the question of whether the moth feels stays open.
15. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Ladybird, dragonfly, mantis, dung beetle.  
   **Answer:** Dung beetle. The other three hunt other insects, and she clears dung.. *Why:* A ladybird, a dragonfly and a mantis are predators. A dung beetle is a decomposer.
16. (fill) Protecting a crop by helping the insects who hunt the crop's eaters is called ________ control.  
   **Answer:** ['biological']. *Why:* Biological control uses living hunters, such as ladybirds and tiny wasps, instead of poison.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) You count eight legs and two body parts on a small creature in a corner. What is she?  
   Options: An insect with two legs hidden / A spider, not an insect / A young beetle  
   **Answer:** A spider, not an insect. *Why:* Eight legs and two body parts make a spider. An insect has six legs and three body parts. If you chose an insect, count the legs again and then the body parts.
2. (mcq) A caterpillar has many legs along her body. Is she an insect?  
   Options: No, she has more than six legs / No, only grown-up moths are insects / Yes, a young insect with six true legs  
   **Answer:** Yes, a young insect with six true legs. *Why:* Only the front six are true legs. The soft ones behind are grips, and a caterpillar is the young stage of an insect. If you said no, you counted the grips as legs.
3. (mcq) A male moth finds a female from far away, yet has no nose. What does he smell with?  
   Options: His antennae / His compound eyes / His spiracles  
   **Answer:** His antennae. *Why:* Antennae carry thousands of sensors for smell. Compound eyes see, and spiracles are breathing holes.
4. (mcq) A bee sees ultraviolet lines on a flower that looks plain yellow to you. What does this show?  
   Options: The flower changes colour for the bee / Bees see better than we do in every way / Bees see light that our eyes cannot  
   **Answer:** Bees see light that our eyes cannot. *Why:* Our eyes and a bee's eyes pick up different light, so the same flower looks different. A compound eye is also less sharp than ours, so bees do not see better in every way.
5. (mcq) An ant walks across a ceiling without tipping. Which helps most?  
   Options: Three legs always grip while three move / She is too light to fall / Her wings hold her up  
   **Answer:** Three legs always grip while three move. *Why:* Her six legs work as two tripods in turn, so three legs always make a steady triangle, with sticky pads and claws to grip. Wings are not used for walking.
6. (mcq) A grasshopper nymph has tiny wing buds. What happens to them at each moult?  
   Options: They fall off / They grow bigger until the wings work / They turn into legs  
   **Answer:** They grow bigger until the wings work. *Why:* A nymph looks like a small adult. Each moult makes her bigger, and her wings grow with her. After her last moult the wings work.
7. (mcq) Which of these grows up in three stages, with no pupa?  
   Options: A mosquito / A ladybird / A dragonfly  
   **Answer:** A dragonfly. *Why:* A dragonfly goes from egg to nymph to adult. A mosquito and a ladybird both go through a pupa, which is four stages.
8. (truefalse) True or false: a pupa is only resting, and nothing happens inside her.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Inside the pupa the body of the larva is taken apart and rebuilt as the adult's body. A pupa often looks still, and a great deal is going on.
9. (mcq) A farmer kills every fly in her mango orchard. Mango flowers are mostly pollinated by flies. What is likely?  
   Options: She will get more mangoes / She will get fewer mangoes / Nothing changes, because wind does the work  
   **Answer:** She will get fewer mangoes. *Why:* Flies carry the pollen from flower to flower, and without pollen a flower cannot make fruit. Wind and self-pollination are how rice and wheat manage, but mango needs its flies.
10. (mcq) A gardener watches a bee push into a mustard flower. What does each of them get from this visit?  
   Options: The bee gets nectar, and the flower's pollen is carried to another flower / The bee gets pollen to eat, and the flower gets nectar / Neither gets anything, because the bee is only resting  
   **Answer:** The bee gets nectar, and the flower's pollen is carried to another flower. *Why:* The bee drinks the sweet nectar, and the pollen on her body is carried to the next flower, so seeds can grow. Both gain. The bee does not give the flower nectar. The flower makes nectar to pay for the visit.
11. (mcq) Ladybirds, dragonflies and tiny wasps help a farmer in the same way. How?  
   Options: They carry pollen from flower to flower / They break down dung in the field / They hunt insects who eat the crop  
   **Answer:** They hunt insects who eat the crop. *Why:* All three are hunters, called predators. A farmer who protects them needs less spray. Pollen carriers and dung clearers do other jobs.
12. (mcq) A new country's fields fill up with cow pats that do not rot away. What is the likely reason?  
   Options: Cows make too much dung / No local beetles can handle that dung / Rain washes dung away slowly  
   **Answer:** No local beetles can handle that dung. *Why:* Australia found this out. Its dung beetles could not handle cow dung, and the pats covered the pastures until other dung beetles were brought in. Beetles do the clearing work.
13. (mcq) A lone locust harms nobody, but a crowd of them ruins crops. What does this show about 'pest'?  
   Options: The word names what insects do in a place / Only locusts can be pests / The lone locust is a different animal  
   **Answer:** The word names what insects do in a place. *Why:* The lone locust and the swarming locust are the same animal. Crowding changes her colour and behaviour. 'Pest' describes the situation with people, not a kind of insect.
14. (mcq) A caterpillar eats a leaf and later pollinates flowers as a butterfly. Which is the fairest thing to say?  
   Options: She is a pest and stays one / One insect, with two different jobs / She is a pest first and a friend later, so she changed  
   **Answer:** One insect, with two different jobs. *Why:* She is one being doing two jobs at two times in her life. If you chose the last one, notice that she did not change her mind. Only her job changed.
15. (mcq) Why can a poison spray leave more aphids on a field than before?  
   Options: Aphids grow bigger on poison / The spray feeds the aphids / It also kills the ladybirds and wasps who hunted them  
   **Answer:** It also kills the ladybirds and wasps who hunted them. *Why:* A spray reaches every insect that the poison lands on. Aphids breed fastest, so they come back first, with no hunters left to stop them.
16. (mcq) Caterpillars are eating young cabbages and bees visit nearby. Which choice is safest for bees?  
   Options: Cover the young plants with a fine net / Spray at noon, when bees are out / Spray every flower with poison  
   **Answer:** Cover the young plants with a fine net. *Why:* A net keeps the caterpillars off and does not touch the bees. Spraying at noon or on open flowers reaches the bees.
17. (mcq) Bumblebees rolled small wooden balls for no food, and young bees rolled more. What might this suggest?  
   Options: Bees need balls to build nests / Bees feel exactly as people do / It is hard to explain without something like play  
   **Answer:** It is hard to explain without something like play. *Why:* Young mammals play more than old ones too. Some scientists say this hints that bees may feel pleasure, and others say it may be an automatic response. It does not prove bees feel exactly as we do.
18. (mcq) A hurt insect keeps walking on a damaged leg. Does this prove she feels no pain?  
   Options: No, hunted animals often hide weakness / Yes, pain always stops walking / Yes, insects have no nerves  
   **Answer:** No, hunted animals often hide weakness. *Why:* Many hunted animals keep going so that a hunter does not see they are hurt. Insects do have nerve cells. This one sign cannot settle the question.

## Applications
- Engineers build six-legged robots to walk over rubble after an earthquake. They copy the insect's tripod walk, with three legs always on the ground. Search for a video of one climbing over bricks, and notice where its three steady legs are.
- In 2008 the papaya mealybug ruined fields in Tamil Nadu, and sprays failed. In 2010 scientists in Bengaluru released a tiny wasp who hunts the mealybug, and the fields recovered. Many farmers also hang small cards carrying the eggs of tiny wasps in sugarcane and other fields, as a way to protect crops without sprays.
- Silk saris from Kanchipuram, Mysuru and Assam begin as a caterpillar's thread. Find a silk sari at home and look at its label. Ask where the thread was spun, and by whom, and whether it was mulberry, tussar, eri or muga.
- Mosquito nets, a tight lid on the water pot and an empty tray every week all protect people without a spray. Guppy and gambusia fish are put into ponds and wells by many health departments to eat the young mosquitoes. Ask at your local health centre if they give them out.
- Beekeepers keep boxes of honeybees at the edge of mustard and sunflower fields, because crops that bees visit often give more seed. Look at a bottle of honey, and notice what is written on its label about where it came from.
- Indian farmers have long mixed dried neem leaves into stored grain to keep weevils away, and planted marigold at the edge of vegetable beds. These are bee-safe ways of protecting crops. Ask a grandparent or an older neighbour what they have used in the kitchen or garden.
- Gardeners and school gardens can plant flowers on purpose to feed bees and butterflies, such as marigold, tulsi and coriander left to flower. Walk through a park near you and count the flowers a bee could drink from. Notice how many months of the year the park has flowers.

## Spotted around us
- Look at a housefly on a window pane. Notice how her feet hold on to glass, and how she is gone before your hand lands. Her many small eyes catch movement very fast.
- After the first rains, look on a tree trunk or a wall for a brown, empty shell with a split down the back. It is the old skin of a cicada or a dragonfly, left behind when she grew. Notice the six legs on the shell.
- At the vegetable market, look for cabbage, brinjal or drumstick leaves with round holes in them. Notice that somebody has eaten them, and ask what the plant looked like before.
- On a tree or weed in flower, watch the flowers for five minutes. Notice who comes: a bee, a fly, a small beetle or a butterfly. Notice whether they touch the middle of the flower.
- After a monsoon shower, look at a puddle or a bucket that has stood for a few days. Look for small wrigglers hanging from the surface. Notice how they dive when your shadow falls.
- At a sari shop, look at the labels on silk saris: mulberry, tussar, eri or muga. Feel the cloth, and ask which place each one comes from. Notice that each name is a different insect or a different tree.
- Look under a street lamp after dark in the rains. Notice the moths circling it, and find the wings and the feathery antennae on a moth who has landed on a wall.
- On a village path or park after rain, look for a dung beetle rolling a ball. Notice how she keeps going in a straight line, even when the ball is bigger than she is.

## Open questions
- **Do insects have feelings?** Experiments show play in bumblebees and mood-like states in honeybees. Nobody has a way to measure experience directly, and scientists disagree on whether these behaviours need it.
- **How did insect wings begin?** No fossil shows the earliest stage of a wing. Gene studies suggest two older body parts joined to make it, but this is still being tested against fossils.
- **Are insects declining in India?** Answering needs the same places counted the same way for decades. India has almost no such records, so nobody can say how fast numbers are changing, or where.
- **How many kinds of insects are there?** About ten lakh are named. Estimates of the total run from about 20 lakh to more than 1 crore. Most insects live in tropical forests that have hardly been searched, and many kinds look alike.
- **What does a caterpillar's memory become inside the pupa?** A 2008 study by Douglas Blackiston and colleagues found that moths avoided a smell they had learnt to fear as caterpillars. How a memory survives while much of the brain is rebuilt is not understood.

## Connected maths
- A silkworm grows to about ten thousand times her first weight in four weeks. A baby weighs 3 kilograms at birth. How heavy would the baby be if she grew ten thousand times? **30,000 kilograms, which is 30 tonnes.**. 3 × 10,000 = 30,000. A baby growing that fast would weigh about as much as six elephants. A silkworm does it in a month because she is so tiny at the start. (queues `math.multiplication.scaling.L4`)
- A cockroach runs about 50 of her own body lengths every second. Her body is 4 centimetres long. How fast is she in centimetres per second? If a person who is 1.5 metres tall ran 50 body lengths a second, how many metres per second would that be? **200 centimetres per second, which is 2 metres per second. The person would run 75 metres per second.**. 50 × 4 = 200 centimetres. A person: 50 × 1.5 = 75 metres per second, which is much faster than the fastest sprinter at about 10 metres per second. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- For one kind of honeybee, a waggle lasting 1 second shows food about 1 kilometre away. A dancing bee waggles for 3 seconds, and another for 5 seconds. How far is each patch of flowers? How much farther is the second? **3 kilometres and 5 kilometres. The second is 2 kilometres farther.**. The waggle time is the distance in kilometres, so 3 seconds means 3 kilometres and 5 seconds means 5. 5 − 3 = 2. (queues `math.algebra.input-output.L4`)
- Mosquito young need about 8 days in water to become adults. A family empties the water tray every 7 days. Another empties it every 10 days. Which family stops the young becoming adults? Why? **The family who empties every 7 days. Eggs laid on day 1 have only 7 days before the water goes, which is less than 8.**. Compare 7 with 8 and 10. If the time between emptyings is shorter than the time the young need, none can finish growing. A gap of 10 days is longer than 8, so some can. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
- Scientists estimate about 25 lakh ants for every person on Earth. How many ants is that for a family of 4? Write it in lakhs and in crores. **1 crore ants, which is 100 lakh.**. 25 lakh × 4 = 100 lakh. 100 lakh is 1 crore. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- A desert locust swarm covering 1 square kilometre eats in a day what about 35,000 people eat. Suppose a swarm in India covers 5 square kilometres. How many people's food would it eat in one day? **1,75,000 people's food.**. 35,000 × 5 = 1,75,000. If the swarm stays 3 days, multiply by 3 again: 5,25,000. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)

## Warm-up
- How many legs does a grown-up insect have, and how many parts has her body? → Six legs, in three pairs, and three body parts: head, thorax and abdomen.
- A creature has eight legs and two body parts. Is she an insect? → No. She is a spider, who has eight legs and two body parts.
- What does an insect do when her skin becomes too small? → She sheds it and grows a bigger one. This is called moulting.
- Which grows up with a pupa: a silk moth or a grasshopper? → The silk moth. A grasshopper goes from egg to nymph to adult, with no pupa.
- What does a bee carry from flower to flower? → Pollen. This is called pollination, and it lets a flower make seeds and fruit.
- Name two insects who hunt other insects. → A ladybird, a dragonfly, a praying mantis, a lacewing or a tiny wasp, among others.
- Why do some mosquitoes bite? → Only females bite. They need the protein in blood to make their eggs.
- A caterpillar eats your plant. What does she become? → A moth or a butterfly. She may then carry pollen from flower to flower.
