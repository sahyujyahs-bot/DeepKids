# One cell, many jobs: how a baby's body gets built
`sci.body.how-babies-grow.L4`

**Summary.** Nearly every cell in your body holds the same full set of instructions. So why is one cell part of a fingernail and another part of your eye? In the womb, cells find their job from their place among their neighbours. A signal spreads from one spot and gets weaker with distance, and each cell reads how much has reached them. Your child tests this idea with strips of paper and dye. They also find out why the gaps between fingers are made by cells that die, how a baby breathes without air, why growth is fast and then slows, and how practice rewires a brain.

**Intro.** *Spread your fingers wide and look at the gaps between them. Before you were born, there were no gaps. Your hand was a flat paddle, like a tiny oar. What made the gaps?* Nothing was cut and nothing was glued. Cells between the fingers received a signal, and they died on purpose. That left five fingers. In this unit you find out how a body is built from one cell. You test how cells can learn where they are, from a signal that fades with distance. You time yourself writing with the wrong hand, to see how practice changes a brain. You also learn how a baby breathes and eats before birth, and why growth is fastest at the very start.

## Terms
- **DNA**: A very long molecule inside nearly every cell. It holds the instructions for building and running a body. *e.g.* If you stretched out the DNA from one of your cells, it would be about two metres long.
- **Gene**: One instruction on the DNA. Most genes tell a cell how to make one kind of protein, which is a building block or a worker in the body. *e.g.* One gene tells red blood cells how to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen.
- **Stem cell**: A cell that can divide to make more cells and that has not yet settled on a final job. Some stem cells can become many kinds of cell. *e.g.* Stem cells in your bone marrow make new blood cells every day.
- **Specialised cell**: A cell that has settled on one job and has a shape that suits that job. People have more than 200 kinds. *e.g.* A nerve cell is long and thin to carry signals. A red blood cell is a round disc to carry oxygen.
- **Signal**: A chemical message that one cell sends and other cells can sense. Cells use signals to tell neighbours what to do. *e.g.* A signal released in a growing embryo can tell nearby cells to become bone.
- **Gradient**: A change that goes smoothly from a lot to a little over a distance. The amount of a signal falling away from its source is a gradient. *e.g.* The smell of tadka is strong in the kitchen and weaker in the next room.
- **Embryo**: A baby in the first eight weeks of growing, while the main parts of the body are being laid down. *e.g.* At week four, an embryo is a small curved shape with a beating heart.
- **Foetus**: A baby from the ninth week of growing until birth. By then every main part has begun, and the baby mostly grows bigger. *e.g.* A foetus kicks, swallows and hears long before birth.
- **Programmed cell death**: Cells dying on purpose, when a signal tells them to, as part of building or caring for a body. The scientific name is apoptosis. *e.g.* The cells between your fingers died before you were born, and that left five separate fingers.
- **Organiser**: A small group of cells in an embryo that sends signals telling nearby cells what to become. Hilde Mangold found the first one, in work published in 1924. *e.g.* A piece of organiser tissue moved to the wrong place made a second body start to grow there.
- **Placenta**: A flat, soft organ that grows in the womb during pregnancy. It passes oxygen and food from the mother's blood to the baby, and waste back. *e.g.* The placenta is about the size of a dinner plate at birth.
- **Haemoglobin**: A red protein inside red blood cells. It holds oxygen in the lungs and lets it go in the body. *e.g.* A baby before birth makes a kind of haemoglobin that holds oxygen more tightly than adult haemoglobin does.
- **Cartilage**: A firm but bendy material that is not as hard as bone. Many of a baby's bones begin as cartilage and slowly harden. *e.g.* You can feel cartilage at the tip of your nose and in your ear.
- **Growth plate**: A layer of growing cartilage near each end of a long bone. It makes the bone longer, and turns to bone when growing stops. *e.g.* Your thigh bone gets longer at its growth plates, until you are in your late teens.
- **Fontanelle**: A soft spot in a baby's skull where the bones have not yet joined. It lets the head squeeze through birth and lets the brain grow. *e.g.* The big soft spot on top of a baby's head usually closes before the baby is two.
- **Synapse**: The tiny gap where one nerve cell passes a signal to the next. A brain has a huge number of them. *e.g.* Each time you practise a skill, the synapses you use get stronger.
- **Identical twins**: Two babies who began as one ball of cells that split in two early on. They have the same genes. *e.g.* Identical twins look alike, but they have different fingerprints.

## Explanations
### The same instructions in every cell · *defined*
**Definition:** Nearly every cell in your body holds a full copy of your DNA, and each kind of cell reads only some of the genes.

You began as one cell. That cell split in two, and each new cell got a full copy of the DNA. They split again, and again, and copied the DNA each time. Today you have about 30 trillion cells, and almost all hold the same set of about 20,000 genes. A gene is one instruction, usually for making one kind of protein. A skin cell uses the genes for skin proteins. A red blood cell uses the genes for haemoglobin. The other genes stay switched off, like pages in a cookbook that a cook does not open today. One surprising proof comes from frogs. In 1962 John Gurdon took the nucleus, which holds the DNA, from a cell in a tadpole's gut. He put it into a frog's egg cell whose own nucleus he had removed. A few of these eggs grew into healthy tadpoles, and later work made frogs. So the gut cell still held every instruction.
- e.g. A hair cell and a bone cell in the same person hold the same genes but use different ones.
- e.g. A cloned animal such as Dolly the sheep (1996) grew from DNA taken from a cell in an adult's udder.
- e.g. Red blood cells lose their DNA when they mature, so they are one of the few cells without it.
### How cells become different kinds · *defined*
**Definition:** Specialising is when a cell settles on one job, and uses only the genes for that job from then on.

In the first days, the cells in the growing ball are not yet different from each other. As the ball gets bigger, cells begin to choose different jobs. Scientists count more than 200 kinds of cell in a person. Each kind has its own shape that fits its work. Nerve cells are long and thin and carry signals. Muscle cells are long and can shorten. Red blood cells are small discs that carry oxygen. Some cells never fully settle. Stem cells stay ready to make new cells, for as long as you live. The stem cells in your bone marrow make about two million new red blood cells every second.
- e.g. Early cells in the ball can become any part of the body, and later cells can become only some parts.
- e.g. Stem cells in the lining of your gut replace it every few days.
- e.g. Skin cells are replaced as the top layer wears off.
### A signal that fades with distance tells cells where they are · *defined*
**Definition:** In a growing embryo, some cells send out a chemical signal that spreads and gets weaker with distance, and nearby cells take different jobs depending on how much of it reaches them.

A body needs the right cells in the right places. A cell cannot see or measure. But a cell can sense a chemical. In 1969 the British scientist Lewis Wolpert had an idea. Suppose a small group of cells sends out a signal. Cells close by get a lot of it, cells farther away get less, and cells far away get almost none. Cells could read the amount, and each would know their distance from the source. Many scientists have since found signals that work in this way. In 1988 Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard and her team found one in a fruit fly egg, which tells the front of the baby fly from the back. Signals like this have been found in mice, chicks, fish and people. In humans, a signal called Sonic hedgehog helps to set the order of the nerve cells in the spine. Most of the detailed work was done on flies, frogs, chicks and mice, because their embryos are easy to study.
- e.g. A paper strip dipped in dye at one end is dark near the dye and pale far from it, so the colour tells you the distance.
- e.g. In the spinal cord, cells close to the signal become one type of nerve cell and cells far away become another.
- e.g. Your five fingers get their different shapes partly from how much of a signal reaches each one.
### Early cells can change their minds: identical twins · *defined*
**Definition:** Early in a baby's growth, cells have not yet settled on their jobs, so a ball of cells can split and each half can make a whole baby.

In 1891 the German scientist Hans Driesch shook apart the two cells of a sea urchin egg that had just divided. Each cell grew into a whole tiny sea urchin. Cells from the early ball are not fixed on being a left half or a right half. They follow what the cells around them are doing. Identical twins are the same idea in people. Very early on, the ball of cells splits in two, and each half builds a whole baby. Twins from one ball have the same genes. Non-identical twins come from two separate egg cells. They are as alike as any brother and sister. Later, cells do settle on their jobs. Cells from an older embryo that are moved keep the job they had, and do not take on the job of their new neighbours.
- e.g. Identical twins have the same genes, but different fingerprints and different personalities.
- e.g. Non-identical twins can be a boy and a girl, because they came from two different cells.
- e.g. Moved cells from an early embryo follow their new neighbours, and moved cells from a later embryo keep their old job.
### Building by taking away: cells that die on purpose · *defined*
**Definition:** Programmed cell death is when cells receive a signal and take themselves apart, safely, as part of building a body.

Building a body is not only adding. Sometimes it is carving. Your hand began as a flat paddle. Around week six after you began, signals told the cells between the finger lines to die. By week eight your fingers were separate. The cells did not burst. They folded themselves up and were cleared away by neighbouring cells. A chick has separate toes, and the cells between the toes die. A duck has webbed feet, and in a duck most of those cells stay alive. A tadpole's tail also goes by cell death when the tadpole grows into a frog. Cell death does not stop after birth. Every day, your body removes tens of billions of old or worn cells, and stem cells make new ones to replace them. In 1972 John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie gave it the name apoptosis. In a few babies, some cells between fingers do not die. Then two fingers stay joined, and a small operation can separate them.
- e.g. Cells between your fingers and toes died before you were born.
- e.g. A tadpole's tail disappears as the frog grows.
- e.g. Every day your body clears away tens of billions of worn cells.
### The placenta: where two bloodstreams meet · *defined*
**Definition:** The placenta is an organ that grows in the womb. Blood from the mother and blood from the baby flow close together inside it, separated by a very thin wall.

A baby floating in water cannot breathe air or eat. The placenta does both jobs for them. The mother's blood flows through small pools inside the placenta. Inside the pools stand thousands of finger-like branches, which are full of the baby's blood. The cord carries blood between the baby and the placenta. The wall between the two bloodstreams is thinner than a hair, so oxygen and food pass through, and waste carbon dioxide passes back. Mother's blood and baby's blood do not mix. A baby can have a different blood group from their mother. The branches give the placenta a huge surface. Measurements differ, but one often-quoted estimate is about 12 square metres, about the floor of a small room, which is why exchange is fast. The baby's own cells build the placenta, from the outer layer of the early ball of cells. The placenta also makes hormones and passes antibodies from the mother. Some harmful things can cross too, such as alcohol and some germs. That is why doctors advise pregnant women about medicines and what to avoid.
- e.g. Oxygen flows from the mother's blood to the baby's blood, because the baby's blood holds oxygen more tightly.
- e.g. Sugar and other food molecules cross the thin wall into the baby's blood.
- e.g. After the baby is born, the placenta comes out of the womb a little later.
### The first breath: how the body switches over · *defined*
**Definition:** At birth, the lungs fill with air for the first time, blood begins to flow through them, and the small shortcuts in the baby's heart and blood vessels close.

Before birth, a baby's lungs are filled with fluid and do not breathe air. Most of the baby's blood skips the lungs. It crosses through two small shortcuts in the heart and a nearby vessel. At birth, the squeezing of the journey helps push fluid out of the lungs. The baby cries, and breathes in. Air opens the lungs, and blood starts to pour through them. The pressure changes shut the shortcuts within days. The cord is no longer needed. Over the first six months, the baby also swaps the haemoglobin they made before birth for adult haemoglobin. The belly button is where the cord was.
- e.g. A baby's first cry is also the first time their lungs fill with air.
- e.g. A premature baby may need extra oxygen, because the lungs are not yet ready for air.
- e.g. The belly button is the small scar left by the cord.
### Growth is not steady: fast, then slower, then a spurt · *defined*
**Definition:** A child grows quickly in the first two years, more slowly through middle childhood, and then quickly again in a growth spurt in the early teens.

A healthy baby grows about 25 centimetres in their first year, from about 50 to about 75 centimetres. In the second year they grow about 12 centimetres more. From age three until the teens, a child grows around five to six centimetres a year. Then comes a spurt, when the body can grow eight to ten centimetres in one year. Girls usually have the spurt earlier than boys. Long bones grow longer at their growth plates, the layers of cartilage near each end. Cartilage cells multiply and the new cartilage turns to bone. When growing ends, in the late teens, the growth plates close. In 2006 the World Health Organization published growth charts made from well-fed, well-cared-for children in six countries, Brazil, Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and the United States. The children grew in almost the same way. That shows that growth depends much more on food, care and health than on where a child lives.
- e.g. A baby's length grows about 11 centimetres in the first three months and about 4 centimetres in the three months after the ninth month.
- e.g. A child of seven grows slowly but steadily, a few centimetres a year.
- e.g. Many teenagers grow faster than at any time since they were babies.
### Soft bones, soft spots · *defined*
**Definition:** A newborn has about 300 bone pieces, many of them still partly cartilage. Over the years, pieces join and cartilage hardens until an adult has 206 bones.

A baby's skull is made of several plates that are not yet joined. The gaps between them are the fontanelles, or soft spots. During birth the plates can slide over each other, so the head can squeeze through the narrow passage. After birth the soft spots leave room for the fast-growing brain. The big one on top closes between about one and two years of age. The wrist begins as a handful of cartilage pieces that slowly harden into eight small bones. Doctors can read a child's age from an X-ray of the wrist, because the bones appear one by one. Many bones also join as you grow. The base of the spine, for example, begins as several pieces that fuse into one.
- e.g. A newborn's wrist is mostly cartilage, and an adult's wrist has eight hard bones.
- e.g. The soft spot on top of a baby's head is covered by tough skin that protects the brain underneath.
- e.g. A baby has about 300 bone pieces, and an adult has 206 bones.
### The brain: born with more connections than we keep · *defined*
**Definition:** A young child's brain makes many more connections between nerve cells than it keeps. Connections that are used get stronger, and connections that are not used fade.

A newborn already has most of the roughly 86 billion nerve cells they will ever have. The brain weighs about a quarter to a third of an adult brain. By age three it is about four-fifths of the adult size, and by age five about nine-tenths. In those years, the number of synapses shoots up. In 1979 the neurologist Peter Huttenlocher counted synapses in brains and found that a young child has far more than an adult. Then the brain prunes. Synapses that are used often are made stronger. Synapses that are not used are removed. This is how practice works. When you practise a skill, you make particular connections stronger, and the skill gets faster and easier. London taxi drivers, who must learn the whole city, were found in 2000 to have a larger back part of the hippocampus, a part used for finding the way, than other adults. A brain is shaped by what it does.
- e.g. A baby who hears two languages keeps the sounds of both.
- e.g. A skill such as riding a cycle feels hard at first, and then your connections strengthen and it feels easy.
- e.g. Learning to write with the wrong hand gets quicker with each try.
### What shapes a person: genes, surroundings, or both? · *debated*
Genes give the instructions, but a baby's surroundings change how the instructions are used. Scientists agree on this. They argue about how much each counts, and about how. In the winter of 1944 to 1945 the Netherlands had a famine called the Hunger Winter. Babies whose mothers were hungry in early pregnancy grew up with more health problems as adults, even though their genes were the same as their brothers' and sisters'. Scientists still debate exactly how the hunger left its mark.
- e.g. Identical twins have the same genes but are not the same.
- e.g. A well-fed, well-cared-for child grows taller than the same child would with too little food.
- e.g. A child's language comes from the people around them, not from their genes.
- **Genes matter most:** Studies of twins find that height and many other features are very similar in identical twins, even when they are raised apart. The genes set the main plan.
- **Surroundings matter most:** Food, health, care and learning change how a body grows. The Hunger Winter showed that what a mother eats can shape a baby's health for life.
- **Both together, in ways still being worked out:** Chemical tags on the DNA, which switch genes on and off, may carry the marks of early life. Scientists disagree on how large these marks are, and how long they last.

## Mostly agreed
- Nearly every cell in a body holds the full set of instructions, and each kind of cell uses only some of them. *How we know:* In 1962 John Gurdon put the nucleus from a tadpole's gut cell into an egg cell, and a few of these eggs grew into healthy tadpoles and frogs. In 1996 Dolly the sheep was cloned from a cell in an adult sheep's udder.
- Cells in an embryo find their jobs from signals sent by their neighbours. *How we know:* In work published in 1924, Hilde Mangold and Hans Spemann moved a small piece of newt embryo and the neighbouring cells began to build a second body. Since then, signals have been found in many animals.
- Cells die on purpose during growth, and this shapes the body. *How we know:* In chicks, cells between the toes die and the toes are separate. In ducks, most of those cells live and the feet stay webbed.
- The mother's blood and the baby's blood run close together in the placenta but do not mix. *How we know:* A baby can have a different blood group from their mother. This could not happen if the blood simply mixed.
- Healthy children grow in much the same way wherever they live, if they are fed and cared for. *How we know:* The World Health Organization measured about 8,000 children in Brazil, Ghana, India, Norway, Oman and the United States, and published the growth standards in 2006.
- Practice makes some connections in the brain stronger and unused ones fade. *How we know:* Studies of the brains of young children and other young animals show that connections are most numerous early in life and are then pruned by use.

## How we found out
**Hilde Mangold and Hans Spemann, building on Wilhelm Roux and Hans Driesch, 1888 (Roux), 1891 (Driesch) and 1924 (the year Mangold and Spemann's paper came out).** In 1888 the German scientist Wilhelm Roux killed one of the two cells of a frog egg with a hot needle. The other cell grew into half an embryo, so he said every cell is fixed from the start. In 1891 Hans Driesch separated sea urchin cells, and each grew into a whole small animal. So who was right? In Freiburg, Hilde Mangold, a young student of Hans Spemann, tried a new test. She chose a pale newt and a dark newt, so she could tell their cells apart. She took a small piece from the back of the pale embryo and placed it on the belly of the dark one. The dark host grew a second body, a twin joined to the first. Most of the new body was made of the host's own cells. The piece had not become the second body. It had told its neighbours what to become. The paper came out in 1924. That same year Hilde Mangold, still in her twenties, died after a fire accident at home. Spemann won the Nobel Prize in 1935 for the discovery. The name 'organiser' came from this work.  
*What people believed before:* Roux said cells are fixed on their jobs from the start, because half an embryo grew from one live cell. Driesch showed that cells from an early embryo can each make a whole animal. Mangold showed that a small group of cells tells their neighbours what to become.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Cells are born knowing what job they will do.** (Wilhelm Roux, a German scientist, 1888.; *rejected*)  
  For: Roux killed one of two frog cells with a hot needle. The other cell grew into only half an embryo. He said each cell was already fixed.  
  Against: In 1891 Hans Driesch separated sea urchin cells and each made a whole small sea urchin. Identical twins also grow from one ball that splits. Hilde Mangold's work, published in 1924, showed that neighbours change what cells become.
- **Genes decide everything about a person.** (Some people who read about DNA in the news.; *rejected*)  
  For: Identical twins share all their genes and are often very alike in height and looks.  
  Against: Identical twins have different fingerprints and sometimes different health. Children who are not fed well do not reach the height their genes allowed. Genes are instructions, and the surroundings change how they are used.
- **The placenta is only a pipe that passes the mother's food to the baby.** (A common simple picture.; *rejected*)  
  For: Food and oxygen do pass from the mother to the baby through the placenta.  
  Against: The placenta is also built by the baby's cells. It makes hormones, passes antibodies, removes the baby's waste, and keeps the two bloodstreams apart. It is an organ with several jobs, and not a pipe.
- **Cells dying inside a body always means damage or disease.** (A common idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: Cells do die when we are hurt or ill.  
  Against: Cells also die on purpose, on a signal, as part of building fingers, toes and many other parts. This is a normal and necessary part of growing.
- **We only use ten percent of our brain.** (A popular myth, often repeated in films.; *rejected*)  
  For: Brain scans show that a few parts light up for each task, which can look like only a small part is used.  
  Against: Scans show that over a day, nearly all parts of the brain are active. Damage to almost any part has a visible effect. Different tasks use different parts at different times.

## Roots
We have followed how one cell becomes a whole body, and how cells find their jobs. Now we ask what this says about who builds a body, and who we are.

**Deep truth.** Nearly every cell in your body holds the same instructions. Yet one becomes bone and another becomes skin, depending on where each one sits. A signal fades with distance, so cells can tell their place from how much has reached them. Cells get their jobs from their neighbours, not from a stamp inside.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a body as built from a blueprint, with every part given out in advance, and we picture cells as carrying their own labels, such as 'I am a nerve cell'. But in an early embryo, the job depends on the place and the neighbours.  
*Evidence:* In the Colour Map test, the same spot on a paper strip came out dark when the dye went in at one end, and pale when it went in at the other. In work published in 1924, Hilde Mangold moved a small piece of newt embryo and the cells around it started to build a second body. The paper is only a model, but the two results point the same way.

- *who am i:* You are a community of about 30 trillion cells that hold the same instructions, and each cell found their job by listening to their neighbours.
- *what is life:* A living body builds itself. Cells talk to cells, with no builder standing outside, and a hand, a heart and a brain appear.
- *what is this world:* A pattern can come from one smooth change, like a colour fading along a strip, and nobody has to draw each part.

One cell holds all the instructions. It splits, and the new cells hold them too. Then, with the help of signals, one set of instructions becomes more than 200 kinds of cell, in the right places. The One turns into the many without anything leaving the whole. Cells that began as one remain connected by the signals they pass. The fingers, the heart and the brain are all one growing thing, made of one family of cells.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** A father, Uddalaka, asks his son Shvetaketu to break open a fruit of the banyan tree, and then to break open one of its tiny seeds. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing, sir.' The father says that from this unseen essence, a great banyan tree grows. (A fertilised egg is too small to see, and no tree or body is visible inside it. Yet it holds the instructions for a whole person. Something unseen carries everything that will grow.)
- **Tao Te Ching 42:** 'The Tao gives birth to One. One gives birth to Two. Two gives birth to Three. Three gives birth to all things.' (One cell makes two, then four, then trillions. From a single start come hundreds of kinds of cell, and a whole body.)
- **Fazang's Treatise on the Golden Lion (about 700 CE), using the Hua-yen image of Indra's net:** Fazang, a Chinese Buddhist teacher, described a net of jewels in which every jewel reflects all the other jewels. Each one holds the whole net inside it. (In an embryo, every cell holds the whole set of instructions, and each cell's job depends on every cell around them.)
- Science (settled): Animals from flies to mice to people use signals that fade with distance to tell cells where they are, and the same families of signals are used again and again.
- Science (open): How the cells of a growing body know when to stop, so that each organ ends up the right size, is not fully understood.
- Other view, Many developmental biologists: A cell does not read, decide or know anything. A signal binds to a molecule on the cell's surface, and that sets off a chain of chemical changes that switch certain genes on. 'Reading a signal' is only a way of speaking about chemistry.
- Practice, Feel the warmth fade (3 min): Hold your palms facing each other, about one finger-width apart. Breathe slowly three times. Feel the warmth between your palms. Now slowly move them apart, about one finger-width at a time. Notice where the warmth gets weaker and where you stop feeling it. Then bring your hands back together, slowly, and notice the warmth return.
- Ask: If cells get their jobs from their neighbours, are they still the same cells they were before?
- Ask: Where in you does 'you' get decided: inside each cell, or between the cells?
- Ask: Two cells hold the same instructions, and become different. What made them different?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If every cell holds the same instructions, what makes a nerve cell different from a skin cell? (hint: Think about the instructions, and about who or what each cell sits next to.)
- *deep:* Are cells what they are because of what is inside them, or because of where they are? (hint: Remember what happened to piece 1 of the paper strip when the dye went in at the other end.)
- *big:* If cells die on purpose so that you can have fingers, is dying a part of living? (hint: Think about the tadpole's tail, a leaf falling, and the skin you lose every day.)
- *big:* Before birth, the mother and the baby are fed through one meeting place. Are they one body, or two? (hint: Their blood does not mix, but oxygen and food cross between them.)
- *deep:* A body is built with no builder standing outside it. Cells talk to cells, and a body appears. Who or what is making you? (hint: Think about the signals, the instructions, the mother's food, and the cells themselves.)
- *big:* Is a baby born ready, or made by what happens to them? (hint: Think about a newborn's brain, which has many more connections than an adult's.)
- *deep:* Why does growing slow down? What would a person be like if growth never stopped? (hint: Think about the growth plates, and about how tall a person could stand.)
- *big:* Early cells take on the job of their new neighbours. Later cells keep their old job. Do people change in a similar way as they grow up? (hint: Think about learning a language at three years old, and at thirty.)

## Story: The Smell Map
*A made-up story, set at a real kind of event, a big temple festival in Madurai. The science idea behind it is real. A smell, or any signal, gets weaker with distance from its source. Two such signals together can tell you exactly where you are. Cells in a growing embryo cannot smell, but they sense chemicals, and they use signals that fade with distance to find their place in the body.*

Thenmozhi was nine, and she had never seen so many people in one place. It was the night of the big temple festival in Madurai. The streets around the temple were packed from wall to wall.

'Hold the end of my sari,' said her aunt, Selvi Athai. 'And listen to this rule. If we lose each other, do not wander. Go to the jasmine sellers at the east gate, and wait there. Say it back to me.'

'East gate. Jasmine sellers. Wait,' said Thenmozhi.

They walked past stalls of glass bangles and tiny drums, and a woman crushing sugarcane in a steel machine. Thenmozhi stopped to watch a man pulling hot sugar into long golden threads. Then drummers came through, with a crowd of dancers behind them. The crowd swelled and pushed. When she looked down, the end of the sari was gone from her hand.

'Athai!' she called. She could not even hear her own voice. The drums were too loud. She jumped up and down, but she saw only shoulders, elbows and bright paper flags. Every lane looked the same under the strings of lights.

Her heart was thumping. She remembered the rule. East gate. But which way was east? The sun had set hours ago. She turned in a slow circle, and every direction looked like every other.

Her teacher, Rani Miss, always said that when you are frightened, you should stop and breathe slowly three times. Thenmozhi stood very still with her back against a pillar, and breathed in, and out, three times. On the third breath, she noticed her nose.

The air smelled strongly of hot oil and chillies. That was the vadai stall right beside her. Under it there was a very faint sweetness, cool and soft. It was jasmine.

She remembered coming in. At the east gate, the jasmine had been so thick that she had closed her eyes. As they had walked deeper into the crowd, the jasmine had faded, and the smell of frying had taken over. 'Strong jasmine means the gate,' she thought. 'Strong frying means the middle. And now there is strong frying and only a tiny bit of jasmine. So I am in the middle, a long way from the gate.'

But which way was the gate? She tried a few steps to her left. She sniffed. The frying smell was just as strong, but the jasmine was fainter. That was the wrong way. She turned around and tried the other side. The jasmine was a little stronger there, and the frying smell a little weaker.

'Yes,' she whispered. 'This way.'

She began to walk. At every few steps she asked herself the same two questions. Is the jasmine getting stronger? Is the frying getting weaker? When the answer was yes, she kept going. When the answer was no, she stopped and turned. A man tried to hurry her along, but she kept her place at the edge of the lane, close to the shop walls, where it was safe.

The frying smell faded. A cool smell of flowers took its place, and it grew stronger, step after step. Soon it was so strong that the whole air tasted sweet. She looked up. Ropes of white jasmine hung from a great stone arch, and beneath them sat a row of women with baskets of flowers.

Under the arch stood Selvi Athai. Her face was pale and her hair had come loose. She saw Thenmozhi and ran, and held her so tightly that Thenmozhi could hardly breathe.

'I looked in every lane!' said Athai. 'How did you find the gate? You could not see over the crowd.'

'I smelled it,' said Thenmozhi. 'The jasmine was strong at the gate and weak in the middle. The frying was strong in the middle and weak at the gate. So with both smells, I knew where I was.' She stopped. 'I did not know the way. But the smells did.'

The flower seller, Mallika Akka, laughed and gave her a small string of jasmine for her hair. 'Good nose,' she said. 'Our jasmine has led many lost people home.'

That night, Thenmozhi lay on the roof of her cousin's house, with the jasmine in her hair and the festival lights still glowing in the sky. She thought about the two smells, the strong and the weak. She had no map and no name board. She had only how much of each smell reached her nose. And that had been enough to tell her exactly where she stood.

**For the parent:** A smell gets weaker with distance from its source. Because of that, how strong it is tells you how far away you are, and two smells together tell you where you stand.

- Ask: A smell fades as you walk away. How can a fading smell tell you how far away its source is?
- Ask: Why did Thenmozhi need two smells and not just one?
- Ask: Cells in an embryo cannot see or hear. What could they use, instead of smell, to find out where they are?

## Experiments
### e1 · Colour Map: how can cells know where they are? (47 min)
- **question:** A strip of paper is dipped in dye at one end. Can you tell where you are on the strip just from the colour? And what happens to each piece's 'job' if the dye goes in at the other end?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After the dye has travelled along the strip, will the colour be the same all along, or different? What will happen if the dye goes in at the other end? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both strips are cut from the same paper towel. They have the same width and length. Each has the same dye, the same depth of dye in the same kind of katori, and the same time. Only the end that touches the dye is different. So any difference between the two strips comes from which end got the dye.
- **safety:** Food colour stains clothes, hands and table tops. Put the tray on newspaper. Do not drink or taste anything in the katoris, and keep the colour bottle away from small children. Use the scissors with a grown-up nearby. Wash your hands afterwards.
- **record:** Write the colour of each piece, dark, medium, pale or none, for strip A and for strip B. Then give each piece a made-up job: dark is bone, medium is muscle, pale is skin, and none is waiting.
- **results table:** Piece (counted from the left) | Strip A, dye at the left: colour | Strip A: job | Strip B, dye at the right: colour | Strip B: job
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the paper strips and the dye. Explain that they will make a model of cells in an embryo. Each piece of paper is a cell, and the dye is a signal. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Each piece of this strip is like a cell in a growing body. The dye is like a signal. Will the colour be the same along the whole strip? What if the dye goes in at the other end? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Each paper piece is like a cell. The dye is like a signal. Will the colour be the same all along? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the colour will look like that? (good: The dye gets weaker as it goes.; It will be dark where it starts.; It will spread evenly.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Tell them this is only a model, because real cells use chemical signals and not dye. One child with a parent: the child does everything and the parent times and writes the guess. Two to four children: pairs share one strip A and one strip B, so make one pair of strips and two katoris for each pair. In each pair one child watches the dye front and calls the lift, and the other child handles the strip. Then swap for the colour and jobs table. Compare all the pairs' results at the end.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut two strips, each 3 centimetres wide and 18 centimetres long. Draw five pencil lines across each strip, 3 centimetres apart, to make six pieces. Number the pieces 1 to 6 from the left, on both strips. Stir 5 drops of colour into each katori. Draw the pencil lines lightly.  
  *Parent says:* Cut two strips of paper towel. Draw lines to make six pieces on each. Number the pieces from the left, one to six. Stir the colour into the water.  
  *Child mode:* Cut two strips. Draw five lines to make six pieces. Number the pieces 1 to 6. Stir colour into the water.
  - Note: Food colours differ a lot in strength. If the dye is too strong, the whole strip will turn dark. Begin with 5 drops in 3 tablespoons of water. You can add drops or water later. Test one spare strip first, while the child writes the guess, so you know how long your paper takes.
  - Check: Why do we cut both strips from the same paper towel and make them the same size? → **So the only difference is which end touches the dye**
- **s3 (17 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Place a katori at each end of the tray. Fold the left end of strip A over the left katori so that 1 centimetre dips into the dye. Fold the right end of strip B over the right katori in the same way. Keep the two strips apart so they do not touch. Set a timer. Watch the dye climb. Lift strip A out when its front reaches piece 5. Lift strip B out when its front reaches piece 2, which is the same distance from its dye. Lay both on the tray.  
  *Parent says:* Strip A dips in at the left. Strip B dips in at the right. Start the timer and watch the dye climb. Lift strip A when the dye reaches piece five. Lift strip B when the dye reaches piece two. Then both have travelled the same distance.  
  *Child mode:* Dip strip A at its left end. Dip strip B at its right end. Watch the dye climb. Lift A at piece 5. Lift B at piece 2.
  - Ask: What do you see where the dye has not yet reached? (good: Still white.; The colour is fading.; The front is a pale line.)
  - Note: The dye front usually takes about 10 to 30 minutes to climb 12 to 15 centimetres, depending on the paper, and it is slower in cool or damp weather. A coffee filter is usually faster. Use the front as your clock, not the timer. Every 5 minutes, hold a ruler to the strip and write how far the front has climbed. It moves quickly at first and then slows, which is also a result. If the front stops short of piece 5, lift both strips when the slower one stops and compare pieces that are the same distance from the dye. If the whole strip is dark and the same, the dye is too strong. Add the same amount of water to the katori and try again with a new strip. That is a result too. If the whole strip is pale, add a few more drops. The colour will fade smoothly along each strip, but exact shades vary, so judge dark, medium and pale by comparing the pieces side by side.
  - Check: The dye is darkest near the katori and faint at the far end of the strip. What does this show? → **The amount of dye falls as you move away from where it went in**
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the two strips side by side on the tray, with piece 1 on the left on both. For each piece write its colour. Then give each piece its made-up job. Dark is bone, medium is muscle, pale is skin, and no colour is waiting. Cut each strip into its six pieces and lay them in rows.  
  *Parent says:* Look at each piece. Is it dark, medium, pale, or white? Write it down. Then give each piece a job. Dark is bone, medium is muscle, pale is skin, and white is waiting.  
  *Child mode:* Look at each piece. Write its colour. Dark is bone. Medium is muscle. Pale is skin. White is waiting.
  - Note: The jobs are made up, to show the idea. A real embryo uses chemical signals, and many more jobs. Look for a smooth change from dark to pale along each strip.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pick up piece 1 from strip A and piece 1 from strip B. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why. Then ask what would happen with a third strip, dipped in the middle.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Look at the two piece ones. They are the same paper. Why do they have different jobs?  
  *Child mode:* Hold piece 1 of each strip. Same paper, but different jobs. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What would happen to the pieces if the dye went into the middle of a strip? (good: Pieces near the middle are dark and the ends are pale.; Both ends would be pale.; It would be a mirror.)
  - Note: A strong answer says that piece 1 got the job its colour showed. It was dark when it was next to the dye, and pale or white when it was far from the dye. Nothing about the paper changed. Where it sat decided the job. After they have said what they saw, explain: a real embryo uses chemical signals that spread and fade in the same way. A cell reads how much has reached them, and so learns where they are. Cut a third strip and dip it in the middle if there is time. The pattern is a mirror. That is how symmetric parts of a body can be made from one signal.
  - Think: Real cells in an embryo cannot see colour. What could cells use, instead of colour, to find out where they are?
### e2 · Wrong-Hand Writing: how fast does practice work? (36 min)
- **question:** If you write the same short line with the hand you do not usually use, six times in a row, how much faster do you get? Does each try save the same amount of time?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many seconds will your first try take? Will your sixth try be faster, and by how much? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** You write the same line, on the same kind of paper, with the same pen, sitting the same way. You rest for one minute between tries. Write at the same neatness each time, so that someone else could read it. If you rush and scribble, the time is not comparable. Only the try number changes. So if the times get shorter, practice is the most likely reason.
- **record:** Write the time for each try in the results table. Work out the seconds saved since the last try. Then draw a line graph of time against try number.
- **results table:** Try | Time (seconds) | Seconds saved since the last try
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the line to write. The line is I am growing today. Ask them to guess the time of the first try, and of the sixth try. Ask for a reason, and write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* We will write the same line six times with your other hand. How long do you think the first try will take? And the sixth? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will write a line six times with your other hand. Guess your first time and your last time. Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the sixth try will be faster or slower? (good: I will get used to it.; My hand will learn.; It will be tired.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Use the same line each time, 'I am growing today', so the amount of writing does not change. One child with a parent: the parent times. Two to four children: all children write at the same time with their own paper, and the children take turns being timer for a partner. Each child then draws their own line graph, and you compare the shapes. Do not compare the seconds, because everyone starts from a different place.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to write the line once with their usual hand. Time it and write down the number. This is their best possible time, to compare with.  
  *Parent says:* First, write the line with your usual hand. Go. Stop. That time is your comparison.  
  *Child mode:* Write the line with your usual hand. Your helper times it.
- **s3 (16 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now they write the line with the other hand, six times, on a new line each time. Time each try. Write each time in the results table. Between tries, rest for one minute with the pen down.  
  *Parent says:* Now use your other hand. Ready, go. Stop. Write down the time. Now rest for one minute. Then try again.  
  *Child mode:* Use your other hand. Write the line. Your helper times it. Rest for a minute. Do this six times.
  - Ask: Which part of your hand or your head feels different on the sixth try? (good: My hand moves more smoothly.; I do not have to think about each letter.; It feels less odd.)
  - Note: Stay calm and do not hurry them. Do not let them look at earlier tries. If a time goes up, write it down. That is a result too. Tiredness can slow the later tries, and a child of 6 to 8 may take 40 to 90 seconds on the first try. If the line is too long for your child, shorten it to 'I am growing', and keep the same line for all tries. The sixth try may not be the fastest. If so, say so, and look at the overall trend.
  - Check: On the fourth try your child takes 3 seconds longer than on the third. What is the best way to treat this result? → **Write it down as it is and keep going**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out the seconds saved on each try. Then draw a line graph with the try number along the bottom and the time up the side. Join the points.  
  *Parent says:* For each try, take away this time from the last time. That gives the seconds saved. Now draw a line graph. Try number goes along the bottom. Time goes up the side.  
  *Child mode:* Work out the seconds saved on each try. Draw a line graph. Join the points.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why. Ask which try saved the most time.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think the times changed? Which try saved the most time?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why. Which try saved the most?
  - Note: Most people save the most time in the first two or three tries, and then the line flattens. After they have said what they saw, explain: each time you write, the connections between nerve cells that you use get stronger, so the movement needs less effort. The line flattens because the easy gains come first. If there is time, try again tomorrow. The first try tomorrow is often still faster than today's first try.
  - Think: If you practised for a week, what would the line look like at the end?

   Options: Nearly all of them hold the same DNA, but each kind uses different genes / Each kind of cell holds different DNA / Only brain cells hold DNA

2. (truefalse) A skin cell and a nerve cell in the same person hold different genes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They hold the same genes. The difference is that cells of each kind use only the genes for their own job.
3. (fill) A signal that gets ____ as you move away from its source can help cells find where they are.

4. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Gene → one instruction on the DNA; Stem cell → a cell that can make new cells and has not settled on a final job; Placenta → the organ that passes oxygen and food between the mother's blood and the baby's blood; Programmed cell death → cells dying on purpose when a signal tells them to. *Why:* These four words describe four parts of growing: instructions, cells that can still change, the meeting place for two bloodstreams, and the cells that leave on purpose.

3. (mcq) A scientist changes the dye strength and also the paper in the same test. What is the problem?  
   Options: She cannot tell which change caused the result / The test will take longer / There is no problem  
   **Answer:** She cannot tell which change caused the result. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing at a time. If two things change, any difference could come from either.
4. (mcq) How does oxygen get from the mother to the baby before birth?  
   Options: It crosses a thin wall in the placenta from the mother's blood to the baby's blood / The baby breathes it in from the water / It travels through the baby's mouth  
   **Answer:** It crosses a thin wall in the placenta from the mother's blood to the baby's blood. *Why:* The baby's lungs are full of fluid before birth. Oxygen crosses the thin wall in the placenta, and the cord carries the baby's blood back to the baby.
5. (mcq) What happens to a baby's blood at the first breath?  
   Options: It begins to flow through the lungs / It stops flowing / It mixes with the mother's blood  
   **Answer:** It begins to flow through the lungs. *Why:* Before birth most blood skips the lungs. At the first breath the lungs open and blood starts to pour through them. The shortcuts in the heart close within days.
6. (mcq) Which of these is made by cells dying on purpose?  
   Options: The gaps between the fingers / The nails on the fingers / The bones of the fingers  
   **Answer:** The gaps between the fingers. *Why:* A hand begins as a flat paddle. Cells between the finger lines die on a signal, and that leaves separate fingers. Nails and bones are made by growing cells.
7. (mcq) A line graph of a baby's length is steepest where?  
   Options: In the first months after birth / At two years old / It has the same slope everywhere  
   **Answer:** In the first months after birth. *Why:* A baby gains about 11 centimetres in the first three months and about 4 centimetres in the three months after the ninth month. The line is steepest early and flatter later.
8. (mcq) A child's growth line is below another child's, but they follow their own line steadily. What does this mean?  
   Options: They are probably growing well / They are growing badly / They have stopped growing  
   **Answer:** They are probably growing well. *Why:* Healthy children follow their own lines, and the lines are often different. What matters is a steady path, and not how close it is to someone else's.
9. (mcq) Which helps a skill become easier?  
   Options: Repeating it, so the connections you use get stronger / Waiting for a new brain part to grow / Doing it once, very hard  
   **Answer:** Repeating it, so the connections you use get stronger. *Why:* Repeating a skill strengthens the connections you use. This is why practice works. The line of improvement flattens as the easy gains are used up.
10. (mcq) Why might a scientist mark the cells of a moved piece with a different colour from the host?  
   Options: To tell which cells came from the piece and which belonged to the host / To make the picture prettier / To stop the cells dividing  
   **Answer:** To tell which cells came from the piece and which belonged to the host. *Why:* Marking lets the scientist see what the moved piece became and what the neighbours built. That shows whether the piece made the new body, or told its neighbours to build it.
11. (mcq) Identical twins begin as...  
   Options: One ball of cells that splits in two / Two separate egg cells / One baby that is cut in half  
   **Answer:** One ball of cells that splits in two. *Why:* Early cells are not yet fixed on their jobs, so each half can build a whole baby. Non-identical twins begin as two separate egg cells.
12. (mcq) A row of cells gets a signal from one end. Which cell gets the most signal?  
   Options: The one closest to the source / The one farthest from the source / They all get the same  
   **Answer:** The one closest to the source. *Why:* A signal fades with distance. The closest cell gets the most, and the farthest cell gets the least.

## Applications
- Doctors use growth charts to see whether a baby or child is growing steadily along their own line.
- Ultrasound scans let doctors watch a baby's heart and body parts form before birth.
- Doctors treat babies born with joined fingers, because they understand how fingers form.
- Stem cell treatments, such as bone marrow transplants, replace blood cells in people with some illnesses.
- Advice to pregnant women about food, medicines and alcohol is based on what can cross the placenta.
- Doctors use the X-ray of a wrist to estimate how old a child's bones are.
- Teachers use practice in short, regular bursts, because brains strengthen the connections they use.
- Developmental biologists study how signals set cell jobs, to learn how to prevent birth defects.
- Scientists grow small clumps of cells in a dish, called organoids, to study how organs form.
- Hospitals keep premature babies warm and give them extra oxygen while their lungs finish growing.

- A duck paddling in a pond, with their webbed feet spread wide.
- A tadpole in a pond or pot, with a tail that gets shorter each week.
- A baby's soft spot on top of their head, in a baby cousin, a neighbour's baby or a photograph.

- A belly button, on you and on everyone you know.

## Open questions
- **How does a growing body know when to stop, so that each organ ends up the right size?** Cells divide in response to signals, but scientists do not yet understand how the whole organ knows its final size.
- **How do cells decide to take a job, and not just follow a signal? What is the role of chance?** Signals set the main pattern, but individual cells also show random differences. Scientists are still working out how much chance matters.
- **Why do human babies grow so fast in the womb and the first two years, and then slow down?** Scientists have several ideas, such as the cost of energy and the risks of birth. They have not agreed on a single reason.
- **How does the early experience of a baby leave marks on the body that last for life?** The Dutch Hunger Winter showed that early hunger affects health later. How the marks are stored is still being studied.
- **Could we one day grow new organs from a person's own stem cells?** Scientists have grown small clumps of organ-like tissue. Growing a whole working organ is much harder, and nobody knows if it will work.
- **Why do some cells keep their ability to become many kinds, while most settle for life?** We know many of the switches involved, but we do not yet know the full story of why most cells never go back.

## Connected maths
- A baby's length is 50 cm at birth, 61 cm at 3 months and 67 cm at 6 months. How many more centimetres did the baby gain in the first three months than in the next three months? **5 cm.**. In the first three months the baby gained 61 minus 50, which is 11 cm. In the next three months the baby gained 67 minus 61, which is 6 cm. 11 minus 6 is 5. The baby gained 5 cm more in the first three months. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
- A ball of cells doubles in number each round: 1, 2, 4, 8, and so on. How many cells are there after 10 rounds? Then, since 10 rounds multiply the number by about 1,000, say roughly how many rounds make a million million. **1,024 cells after 10 rounds. About 40 rounds make a million million.**. The numbers go 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, and then 1,024 after 10 rounds. So every 10 rounds multiplies the number by about 1,000. Four lots of 10 rounds make 1,000 times 1,000 times 1,000 times 1,000, which is a million million. A body of about 30 trillion cells needs around 45 rounds of doubling, in a rough sum. In real bodies, not all cells divide, and many die. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- A paper strip is cut into 6 equal pieces. The 2 pieces next to the dye are dark, the next 2 are medium, and the last 2 are pale. What fraction of the strip is pale? Write it in its simplest form. **One third.**. 2 out of 6 pieces are pale. That is 2 over 6. Divide the top and the bottom by 2, to get 1 over 3. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A newborn's brain weighs about 350 grams. An adult's brain weighs about 1,400 grams. How many times heavier is an adult's brain? **4 times.**. 1,400 divided by 350 is 4. So the newborn's brain is about a quarter of the weight of an adult's. By age five, it is about nine-tenths of the adult weight. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- The heart starts to beat in week 3 of growing, and the baby is born in week 38. For how many weeks does the heart beat before birth? How many days is that? **35 weeks, which is 245 days.**. 38 minus 3 is 35 weeks. There are 7 days in a week, and 35 times 7 is 245. The heart beats for about 245 days before the baby is born. (queues `math.time.calendar.L4`)
- Your bone marrow makes about 2 million new red blood cells every second. How many is that in one minute? How many in ten minutes? **120 million in a minute, and 1,200 million in ten minutes.**. There are 60 seconds in a minute. 2 million times 60 is 120 million. For ten minutes, 120 million times 10 is 1,200 million, which is also called 1.2 billion. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- Divya, a girl in Class 4 in Kochi, times herself writing a line with her left hand. Her times are 38, 31, 27, 25, 24 and 24 seconds. How many seconds did she save between the first and the sixth try? Which try saved the most time? **14 seconds. The second try saved the most, with 7 seconds.**. 38 minus 24 is 14 seconds. From try 1 to try 2 she saved 38 minus 31, which is 7 seconds. The later tries saved 4, 2, 1 and 0 seconds. The savings get smaller each time. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In one often-quoted estimate, a placenta has a folded surface of about 12 square metres. A room is 4 metres long and 3 metres wide. What is the area of the room? Is it more or less than the placenta's surface? **12 square metres. It is about the same.**. The area of a room is length times width. 4 times 3 is 12 square metres. The folded surface of the placenta has about the same area as the floor of this room, though it fits into a flat organ the size of a dinner plate. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
