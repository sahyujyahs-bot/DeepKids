# Hygiene: how soap lifts germs off your hands
`sci.health.hygiene.L3`

**Summary.** Plain water runs off an oily hand and leaves the dirt behind. Germs, the few microbes that can make us ill, sit in that thin layer of skin oil. A soap molecule holds oil at one end and water at the other. So the rinse water carries the oil away, and the germs go with it. Most of those germs go down the drain still alive, far from your mouth. Washing with soap before food and after the toilet stops germs passing from hand to mouth, and from person to person.

**Intro.** *Rub a drop of cooking oil on your palm. Rinse it with plain water, then press your palm on a sheet of paper. Is there an oily mark? Now wash with soap, and press again.* Your hands touch hundreds of things every day, such as door handles, coins and your own face. Tiny living things called microbes ride along on every touch. Most are harmless. A few, called germs, can give you a tummy upset or a cold. They are far too small to see. So how can we know they are on our hands? And why does soap get them off when water alone does not?

## Terms
- **Hygiene**: The habits that keep our bodies, food, water and homes clean, so that germs do not make us ill. *e.g.* Washing your hands with soap before you eat is hygiene. So is covering a pot of water.
- **Microbe**: Any living thing too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria, many fungi and tiny algae are microbes. *e.g.* The microbes in curd turn milk sour and thick.
- **Germ**: A microbe, or a virus, that can make us ill. Only a small number of microbes are germs. *e.g.* The germ that causes cholera lives in dirty water.
- **Bacteria**: Living things made of just one tiny cell each. They live almost everywhere: in soil, water, air, and on and in you. One of them is called a bacterium. *e.g.* Bacteria in the soil turn fallen leaves back into soil.
- **Virus**: A tiny packet of genes inside a coat, far smaller than a bacterium. They can make copies of themselves only inside a living cell. Some viruses, such as flu, have an oily outer coat. *e.g.* The common cold and flu are caused by viruses.
- **Molecule**: A tiny group of atoms joined together. Everything is made of molecules: water, oil, soap and you. *e.g.* One drop of water holds more water molecules than there are stars in our whole galaxy.
- **Soap**: A cleaner made of molecules with two different ends. One end sticks to water. The other end sticks to oil and grease. *e.g.* A bar of soap, liquid handwash and dishwashing liquid all work this way.
- **Water-loving and oil-loving**: Water-loving molecules stick to water. Oil-loving molecules avoid water and stick to oil. Oil and water do not mix, because water molecules hold on to each other and leave the oil out. *e.g.* Oil floats in a layer on top of water in a glass.
- **Micelle**: A tiny ball made by many soap molecules. Their oily tails point inwards and hold oil and dirt. Their heads face out into the water. The water carries the balls away. *e.g.* When you rinse soapy hands, millions of micelles go down the drain with the grease inside.
- **Germ theory**: The idea, now proven, that many illnesses are caused by germs passing from one place or person to another. *e.g.* Germ theory is why doctors wash their hands and boil their tools.
- **Midwife**: A person, usually a woman, trained to help mothers give birth and to care for new babies. *e.g.* In many Indian villages, a trained midwife or nurse helps when a baby is born.
- **Microbiome**: All the microbes that live together in one place, such as your skin, your gut or a patch of soil. *e.g.* Your skin's microbiome is different on your elbow and on your forehead.
- **Resident and visitor microbes**: Resident microbes live on your skin all the time, deep in its folds. Visitor microbes are picked up from things you touch. A wash with soap lifts off mostly the visitors. *e.g.* After you pat a street dog, their microbes are visitors on your hand.
- **Saponin**: A natural soap-like molecule made by some plants. Like soap, it has a water-loving end and an oil-loving end. *e.g.* Reetha, the soapnut, makes foam in water because it is full of saponins.
- **Reetha and shikakai**: Reetha is the dried fruit of the soapnut tree. Shikakai is the pod of a climbing acacia plant. People in India have washed hair and clothes with them for centuries. *e.g.* Many grandmothers still soak reetha and shikakai to wash hair.
- **Disinfect**: To kill most of the germs on something, using heat or a strong chemical. *e.g.* Boiling drinking water for one minute kills almost all the germs in it.
- **Sanitation**: Keeping a place clean and healthy with toilets, drains, safe water and taking rubbish away. *e.g.* A clean public toilet and a covered drain are sanitation.
- **Mucous membranes**: The soft, wet linings of your eyes, nose and mouth. Germs get into the body most easily here. *e.g.* That is why we wash hands before rubbing our eyes or eating.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then you know what caused the difference. *e.g.* To test soap against water, use the same pretend germs, the same time and the same hands.

## Explanations
### How germs travel from one person to another · *defined*
**Definition:** Germs are the microbes and viruses that can make us ill. They travel from person to person on hands, in water and food, in the drops of a sneeze or cough, and on flies and mosquitoes.

Many bacteria can swim through water, but each second they cover less than the width of a hair. To get from one person to another, a germ needs a ride. Most often the ride is a hand. A hand touches a dirty surface, then touches the eyes, nose or mouth. Those soft, wet linings are the easiest way into the body. A sneeze sprays tiny drops across a room. Dirty water and uncovered food carry germs into the stomach. Flies walk on rubbish and then on food. Mosquitoes carry the germs of malaria and dengue from one person's blood to another's. Hygiene means breaking these paths at the right moments. The hand is the path that is easiest to break, with soap and water. How the body fights germs that do get in is in the unit on the immune system.
- e.g. A child plays with a ball outside, then eats a vada without washing. Germs ride from the ball, to the hand, to the mouth.
- e.g. A sneeze into the air can spray drops more than a metre. A sneeze into your elbow keeps most of them on your sleeve.
- e.g. A fly on an open rubbish heap can carry germs on their feet to uncovered food.
### Why water alone is not enough · *defined*
**Definition:** Skin is covered in a thin layer of natural oil. Many germs and much dirt stick in that oil. Water slides off oil, so water alone leaves most of them behind.

Your skin makes its own oil, called sebum. It keeps skin soft and waterproof. It also makes a sticky layer where dirt and germs hold on. Water molecules hold tightly on to each other. They do not hold on to oil. So when you rinse an oily hand, the water runs over the oil and away, leaving the oil and its germs behind. You saw this if you rubbed oil on your palm and rinsed it. Rubbing hard under water does help a little, because rubbing knocks some things loose. But to really lift oil, you need something that can hold oil and water at the same time.
- e.g. An oily steel plate rinsed with plain water still feels greasy.
- e.g. Drops of water bead up and roll off an oily hand instead of spreading.
### How soap works, down to the molecules · *defined*
**Definition:** A soap molecule has a head that sticks to water and a long tail that sticks to oil. The tails push into oily dirt and germs, and the heads face the water. Many soap molecules wrap each blob of oil in a tiny ball called a micelle, and the rinse water carries the balls away.

A soap molecule is long and thin. One end, its head, sticks to water. The other end, its long tail, avoids water and sticks to oil. When you rub soap on oily hands, millions of tails push into the oil. Rubbing breaks the oil into tiny blobs, and soap molecules cover each one, tails in and heads out. Now the outside of each blob sticks to water. So the rinse water carries the blobs off your skin and down the drain, with the dirt and germs inside them. This takes time and rubbing, which is why we rub for at least 20 seconds. Plain soap kills very few bacteria. It lifts them off, and the water carries them away alive.
- e.g. Dishwashing liquid lifts oil off a kadai the same way that handwash lifts it off your hands.
- e.g. In the jar test, shaking with soap turns the two clear layers into one cloudy mix. The cloudiness is millions of tiny oil drops, each covered in soap molecules.
### Why soap breaks some viruses but not your hands · *defined*
**Definition:** Flu viruses and coronaviruses have an oily outer coat. It is built from two-ended molecules like soap's, so soap can pull it apart. Your living cells are protected by the tough top layer of your skin.

Every cell in your body is wrapped in a thin skin called a membrane. It is made of two layers of molecules with water-loving heads and oil-loving tails, a little like soap. Some viruses take a piece of this membrane from the cell they grew in, and wear it as an outer coat. Soap molecules slip into that oily coat and break it apart. Without their coat, the virus cannot get into a new cell. A hand-wash does not reach your own living cells. The top layer of your skin is made of flat, dead cells packed tightly together, and soap cannot get through it. Soap does lift some of your skin's own oil, which is why washing very often can dry your hands.
- e.g. A soap bubble is a thin film of water held between two layers of soap molecules.
- e.g. Some viruses, such as the ones that cause many tummy upsets in children, have no oily coat. Soap cannot break them, but a good wash still lifts them off hands.
### What a wash does to the microbes on your skin · *defined*
**Definition:** A wash with soap lifts off mostly the visitor microbes, the ones picked up from things you touched. Most of them leave alive, in the rinse water. Most of the skin's own resident microbes stay where they live.

Only a small share of all microbes are germs. Most are harmless, and many are helpful, like the bacteria that turn milk into curd. Your skin is home to millions of microbes. Most live there all the time, deep in the folds and tiny openings of the skin. They are called residents. Some residents make substances that stop harmful bacteria from growing on you. Bacteria sense what is around them, such as food, warmth and other bacteria, and decide when to settle, swim or grow. Some scientists call these automatic responses. Visitor microbes are picked up from door handles, coins, animals and food. They sit on the surface, in the oil, so soap and water lift them off easily. Plain soap kills very few of them. It moves them, still alive, off your hands and down the drain. There they join the huge numbers of microbes that already live in drains, sewers and soil.
- e.g. In the bread test, slices touched by unwashed hands usually grow more mould than slices touched by soap-washed hands. The mould spores were washed off.
- e.g. Tests found that antibacterial soap, which kills bacteria, cleans hands at home no better than plain soap. Lifting germs off is what matters.
### When and how to wash your hands · *defined*
**Definition:** Wash hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, rubbing every part, at the moments germs are most likely to travel.

The most important moments are before you eat or touch food, after using the toilet, after blowing your nose or sneezing, after playing outside or with animals, and when you come home. Wet your hands. Add soap. Rub palms, then the backs of your hands, between your fingers, round each thumb, your fingertips and nails, and your wrists. Twenty seconds is about the time it takes to sing Happy Birthday twice. Rinse well. Then dry your hands on a clean towel or let them dry in the air. Wet hands pass on germs more easily than dry ones. Warm water and cold water work about the same. Plain soap works about as well as soap that says antibacterial, for everyday washing.
- e.g. In the turmeric test, the yellow stayed longest between fingers, round the thumbs and under nails.
- e.g. A study found that washing hands with soap can cut tummy illnesses like diarrhoea by about a third, or more.
### How clean is too clean? · *debated*
Hygiene saves millions of lives. But some scientists noticed something puzzling. Allergies, such as hay fever and some kinds of asthma, became more common as homes got cleaner. Could living with too few microbes be part of the reason? Scientists still argue about this.
- e.g. Children who grow up on farms with animals tend to have fewer allergies.
- e.g. No study has shown that washing hands before meals causes allergies.
- **David Strachan, a British doctor, in 1989:** He studied over 17,000 British children. Those with more older brothers and sisters had less hay fever. He suggested that catching more infections when very young might protect against allergies. This became known as the hygiene hypothesis.
- **Graham Rook, a British scientist, in 2003, and many scientists today:** It is not catching illnesses that helps. It is meeting the harmless 'old friends' microbes that people lived with for thousands of years, in soil, plants, animals and other people.
- **Sally Bloomfield, a British hygiene scientist, and others:** Calling it a hygiene problem is misleading, because it makes people stop washing hands. She argues for targeted hygiene: wash well at the key moments, such as before food and after the toilet, and otherwise let children play outside, in mud and with animals.
### Caring for the outside of your body · *defined*
**Definition:** Dirt and germs collect in different places on the body, and each part has its own way of being kept clean.

Bathe every day, or at least often, to wash away sweat, dead skin and visitor germs. Brush your teeth twice a day, morning and night. Bacteria in the mouth turn sugar left on teeth into acid, and the acid makes holes. Keep nails short and clean, because dirt and germs hide under them. Wash and comb hair, and check for lice, tiny insects that anyone can get. Never poke anything inside your ears. Your ears clean themselves, because earwax slowly moves out on its own. Do not rub your eyes with dirty hands. Blow your nose into a hanky or tissue, then wash your hands. Wear chappals outside, because some worm larvae in dirty soil can get in through bare feet. Your private parts, the parts covered by underwear, are kept clean and private. Your body belongs to you. If any touch feels wrong, say no, get away, and tell a grown-up you trust.
- e.g. A neem datun, a chewed twig, was India's toothbrush for centuries. Today dentists add fluoride toothpaste, which protects teeth from acid.
- e.g. Hookworm larvae in soil can enter through the skin of bare feet. Chappals stop them.
### Clean surroundings keep a whole street healthy · *defined*
**Definition:** Safe water, toilets, covered drains, covered food, rubbish taken away and no standing water stop germs from spreading to many people at once.

One family washing their hands helps that family. Clean water and toilets help a whole town. Germs from human waste can get into water and food, and make many people ill at once. That is why toilets and clean drains matter so much. Boiling or filtering drinking water kills or removes most germs. Covered food keeps flies off. Mosquitoes lay their eggs in still water, even a little water in a flowerpot tray or a coconut shell. In warm weather, the eggs become flying mosquitoes in about a week. So emptying and scrubbing every container of still water once a week stops them. The scrubbing matters, because some mosquito eggs stick to the sides and can stay alive for months without water. In 2014, India began the Swachh Bharat Mission to build toilets and clean streets. Sanitation workers, many of whom do hard and risky work for little thanks, keep towns healthy every day.
- e.g. A cooler or a tyre left with water in it can grow hundreds of mosquitoes in a week.
- e.g. ASHA workers, women health workers in Indian villages, visit families to talk about clean water, toilets and mosquitoes.
### Semmelweis, the midwives and the washing basin · *defined*
**Definition:** In 1847 in Vienna, Ignaz Semmelweis showed that mothers in the doctors' ward fell ill because doctors carried something from dead bodies on their hands. Washing hands in chlorine water made the illness drop sharply.

Vienna's big hospital had two wards for mothers having babies. In one, doctors and students helped the mothers. In the other, midwives did. Many more mothers caught a dangerous fever, called childbed fever, in the doctors' ward. The midwives' ward was much safer. Mothers knew this and begged to go there. Semmelweis wanted to know why. The doctors often came straight from cutting open dead bodies to learn anatomy. The midwives never did. Then his friend, Jakob Kolletschka, was cut by a student's knife during that work. He fell ill with the same fever, and died. Semmelweis guessed that something from the dead bodies was being carried on hands. Soap did not even remove the smell. So he made everyone wash in chlorine water, which did. In April 1847, about 18 out of every 100 mothers in the doctors' ward died. After washing began in May, it fell to about 2 out of every 100 by the summer.
- e.g. The midwives' ward was the clue. Without their safer ward to compare with, nobody would have known the doctors' ward could be safer.
- e.g. Many doctors were offended at the idea that their own hands caused illness. Semmelweis lost his job in Vienna and died in 1865, before most doctors accepted his idea.
### How we found out about germs · *defined*
**Definition:** Germ theory says that many illnesses are caused by tiny living things passed from one person or place to another. It took hundreds of years and many people to show this.

Long ago, many people thought illness came from bad smells in the air, or from bad luck. Some guessed the truth early. In 1546, an Italian doctor, Girolamo Fracastoro, wrote that diseases might spread by tiny invisible seeds. In the 1670s and 1680s, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a cloth-seller in the Netherlands, made his own tiny lenses. He saw 'little animals' swimming in water and in the white stuff scraped off his teeth. Nobody yet linked them to illness. In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis showed that doctors' unwashed hands were carrying illness to mothers. In 1854, Filippo Pacini in Italy saw the cholera germ, and John Snow in London showed that cholera spread through one water pump. In the 1860s, Louis Pasteur showed that microbes come only from other microbes. Joseph Lister began cleaning wounds and tools in surgery in 1867. In 1884, Robert Koch, working in Egypt and then in Kolkata, grew the cholera germ and convinced many doctors.
- e.g. Pacini described the cholera germ in 1854, thirty years before Koch. Hardly anyone noticed. Today the germ's full name includes his name: Vibrio cholerae Pacini 1854.
- e.g. Florence Nightingale and Mary Seacole cared for soldiers in the 1850s. Nightingale used careful counts to show that clean hospitals saved lives.
### Plant soaps and old cleaners · *defined*
**Definition:** Some plants make their own soap-like molecules, called saponins. Reetha and shikakai are full of them, which is why they foam and clean.

The soapnut tree's fruit, reetha, makes a rich foam when squeezed in water. Its saponins have a water-loving part and an oil-loving part, just like soap, so they lift oil off the same way. People in India have used reetha and shikakai to wash hair, silk and jewellery for centuries. Soap as we know it is made by mixing fat or oil with a strong alkali, a chemical that long ago came from wood ash. Some of the oldest known recipes for soap-like cleaners, mixing fat and ash, come from Mesopotamia, in today's Iraq, over 4,000 years ago. Plants probably make saponins to protect themselves from insects and fungi, not for us.
- e.g. Soak a few reetha shells in warm water and squeeze them. White foam appears.
- e.g. Ash was used to scrub steel and brass pots in many Indian homes, because it is alkaline and cuts grease.

## Mostly agreed
- Many illnesses are caused by germs that pass from person to person, or through water, food, air and insects. *How we know:* Scientists can grow germs, see them under microscopes, read their genes and follow how they spread. Stopping their path stops the illness, as John Snow showed with a water pump in 1854.
- Washing hands with soap and water removes most germs and dirt from hands. *How we know:* Many tests count germs on hands before and after washing. Studies in homes, schools and villages show fewer tummy illnesses and colds where people wash with soap.
- Plain soap cleans hands mainly by lifting germs off, not by killing them. *How we know:* Tests in homes found plain soap about as good as antibacterial soap that kills bacteria. In 2016 the United States stopped some germ-killing chemicals being sold in home soaps, because they worked no better.
- Soap works because its molecules hold both oil and water. *How we know:* Chemists know the shape of soap molecules. The oil, water and soap jar test shows soap mixing oil into water.
- Safe water and toilets are some of the best protections against illness ever invented. *How we know:* In many countries, illnesses like cholera fell sharply after clean water pipes and sewers were built, often before medicines for them existed.
- Most microbes are harmless or helpful, and our bodies carry many friendly ones. *How we know:* Scientists have read the genes of the microbes on skin and in the gut. They find thousands of kinds, most of them living peacefully with us.

## How we found out
**Ignaz Semmelweis, the midwives of Vienna's second maternity ward, and later Louis Pasteur, 1847, and 1879.** In the 1840s, most doctors in Europe thought childbed fever came from bad air, or from the weather, or from mothers' fear. Vienna's big hospital had two wards for mothers giving birth. In one, doctors and medical students helped the mothers. In the other, midwives did. More than twice as many mothers died in the doctors' ward. If bad air caused the fever, both wards should have suffered the same, because they were in the same hospital. A young doctor, Ignaz Semmelweis, looked for what was different. The doctors came to the mothers straight from cutting open dead bodies, and the midwives never did. In March 1847 his friend Jakob Kolletschka was cut by a student's knife during that work, and died of the same fever. So Semmelweis guessed that something from the dead bodies was carried on doctors' hands. From May 1847, everyone in his ward washed in chlorine water first. Deaths fell from about 18 in every 100 mothers in April to about 2 in June. Many doctors still refused to believe that their own hands carried illness. In 1879, Louis Pasteur found the microbes that cause childbed fever.  
*What people believed before:* Childbed fever came from bad air or the weather, and a doctor's hands could not carry illness. Comparing the two wards in one hospital showed the air was not the cause, and washing hands made the deaths fall.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Illness is caused by bad smells in the air, called miasma.** (Many doctors and thinkers for over 2,000 years, in Europe, India and China; *rejected*)  
  For: Dirty, smelly places really did have more illness. Cleaning them up helped.  
  Against: The smell was not the cause. The germs in the dirt and water were. John Snow showed that cholera followed one water pump, not the bad air. Germs were later seen and grown in laboratories.
- **Ignaz Semmelweis alone discovered why hand-washing saves lives.** (Many books and websites; *debated*)  
  For: He collected the numbers and made the doctors wash. That was brave and new.  
  Against: His clue came from comparing his ward with the midwives' ward, where far fewer mothers fell ill. The midwives' skill made that comparison possible. Others had suspected it too. In 1843, Oliver Wendell Holmes in America wrote that doctors could carry childbed fever. And in Scotland, in 1795, Alexander Gordon had said the same.
- **All bacteria are bad, and we should kill as many as we can.** (A common belief, helped by adverts for cleaning products; *rejected*)  
  For: Some bacteria cause serious illness, and killing them saves lives.  
  Against: Most bacteria are harmless or helpful. Our skin and gut need them. Killing them everywhere would harm us and the soil, sea and air. Soap mostly lifts germs off hands, which is what we need.
- **Antibacterial soap is much better than ordinary soap.** (Many adverts; *rejected*)  
  For: It contains chemicals that kill some bacteria.  
  Against: For everyday hand-washing at home, tests found it no better than plain soap and water. In 2016, the United States food and medicine authority stopped some of these chemicals being sold in home soaps for this reason. Some scientists also worry they may help germs become resistant.
- **Hot water kills germs on your hands, so it must be hot to work.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Very hot water, like boiling water, does kill germs.  
  Against: Water hot enough to kill germs would burn your skin. In a 2017 study in America, warm and cold water removed germs about equally well. The soap and the rubbing do the work.
- **Children today are ill with allergies because homes are too clean.** (A popular version of the hygiene hypothesis; *debated*)  
  For: Allergies rose as homes got cleaner. Farm children with animals have fewer allergies.  
  Against: Washing hands and cleaning kitchens has not been shown to cause allergies. Many scientists now think the real change is less time outdoors, fewer animals, and less contact with harmless soil microbes.

## Roots
We have been learning how soap lifts oil and germs off our hands. Now we ask what a wash really does to the living things on our hands, and what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the turmeric test, plain water ran over the oily yellow and left it behind. A soap molecule holds oil and water at once, so the rinse carried the yellow away. Most germs leave your hands the same way, still alive, in the rinse water. A good wash moves germs to a place where they cannot reach your mouth.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk about washing as a war that kills germs, because adverts do. A plain soap wash mostly moves microbes from one place to another, and that is enough to keep us well.  
*Evidence:* In the turmeric test, the yellow went down the drain with the soapy water. Plain soap kills very few bacteria, yet tests at home found it cleans hands as well as antibacterial soap that kills them.

- *who am i:* Your skin is home to millions of microbes. A good wash leaves most of them where they live.
- *what is life:* Bacteria sense what is around them and decide when to settle, swim or grow. On your hands, they live in the skin's oil.
- *what is this world:* In this world, oil and water stay apart by themselves. A soap molecule can hold on to both and join them.

Soap works because one molecule can hold two things that will not mix by themselves, oil and water. Your own cells are wrapped in the same kind of two-ended molecule, and so are the coats of some viruses. The water then carries the visitor microbes on, to the drain, the sewer and the soil, where many other microbes already live.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** Uddalaka asks his son to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the salt cannot be seen, but every sip tastes salty. What is unseen can still be everywhere. (Germs and soap molecules are far too small to see. Like the salt, we know they are there by what they do: mould on bread touched by unwashed hands, and the fever numbers in Semmelweis's ward.)
- **Acharanga Sutra, Book 1, Lecture 4, Lesson 1 (Jain):** No living being should be killed, harmed or hurt, however small. Jain monks sweep the ground gently and filter their water so as to harm as few tiny creatures as they can. (A plain soap wash mostly moves microbes off your hands instead of killing them. It keeps us well while harming very few of them.)
- **Patanjali, Yoga Sutras 2.32:** Among the five personal practices, the niyamas, the first is shaucha: cleanliness of the body and clarity of the mind. (Shaucha starts with keeping the body clean, and it needs care. In the turmeric test, the careful wash of every part of each hand was the one that worked.)
- Science (settled): Every cell is wrapped in a membrane of molecules with water-loving heads and oil-loving tails, built much like soap.
- Science (settled): Plain soap removes most microbes from hands by lifting them off in the rinse water, and kills few of them.
- Science (settled): Scientists estimate that about as many bacteria live on and in a person as there are human cells in their body.
- Other view, Many doctors and nurses: Whether a germ is killed or moved matters less than keeping them from the next person. In hospitals, alcohol hand rubs that kill germs are used, because they are fast and work very well. Killing germs on purpose, with medicines and boiling, has saved millions of lives.

Rub every part of each finger, counting to twenty very slowly. Rinse, and watch the foam slide away down the drain. Now hold your wet hands still in the air. Feel them cool as they dry. Where is that water going now? Who will it meet next?

- Ask: Is a microbe a germ wherever they are, or only when they reach the wrong place?

## Story: The Basin by the Door
*Real: the Vienna General Hospital, its two maternity wards (doctors and students in one, midwives in the other), the mothers begging to go to the midwives' ward, Jakob Kolletschka's death in March 1847, Semmelweis choosing chlorine water because ordinary soap did not remove the smell from his hands, the basin at the door from May 1847, and the fall in deaths. Imagined: Anna, her mother Liesl, the midwife Frau Weber, the laundry's chlorine water as the hint, and everything they say. Real too: Louis Pasteur described the microbes that cause childbed fever in 1879.*

My mother washed the sheets of the Vienna General Hospital, and I helped her. In the year 1847 I was nine years old. I carried the baskets. I knew every corridor by its smell. The kitchen smelled of cabbage. The chapel smelled of candles. And the long room where the student doctors cut open the bodies of the dead, to learn how people are made, smelled of something sweet and wrong that stayed in your nose all day.

There were two wards for mothers having babies. The First Ward belonged to the doctors and their students. The Second Ward belonged to the midwives. They were next to each other. They had the same beds, the same soup and the same grey windows. My mother washed their sheets in the same tubs, with the same soap.

But the mothers knew something. On the days the First Ward took new mothers, women cried at the gate. Some begged on their knees to be taken to the Second Ward instead. 'In the First Ward,' one whispered to me, 'the fever comes.' It was called childbed fever. In the First Ward, many mothers caught it after their babies were born, and some of them did not get better. In the midwives' ward, it came much less often.

'Why?' I asked my mother. 'It is the same building.'

'If I knew that,' said my mother, scrubbing hard, 'I would be a professor and not a washerwoman.'

I asked Frau Weber, the oldest midwife, who smelled of lavender and gave me plums. She was quiet for a while. 'We count everything here,' she said. 'Every mother, every baby, every fever. The numbers do not lie. Our ward is safer. Nobody upstairs has asked us why.'

Somebody upstairs did ask, in the end. His name was Doctor Semmelweis. He was young and round-faced and always in a hurry. He asked everyone questions. He asked the midwives how they held the babies. He asked the priest which way he walked through the wards. He even asked my mother whether the First Ward's sheets were dirtier. They were not. He wrote every answer in a little book.

In March, a kind professor, Doctor Kolletschka, died. A student's knife had slipped and nicked his finger while they worked on a body. Afterwards he fell ill with a fever, and it looked exactly like the mothers' fever. Doctor Semmelweis walked the corridors that week with a face like a closed door.

One morning he came down to the laundry. I was folding pillowcases. He stood at our big sink and washed his hands with our yellow soap, hard, for a long time. Then he held them out to me, wet and red.

'You have a good nose, child,' he said. 'Everybody says so. Smell.'

I smelled. Under the soap was the sweet, wrong smell of the cutting room. It had not gone.

'Yes,' he said quietly, as if I had told him something terrible and wonderful. 'Soap does not take it off. So something stays on our hands. Something we cannot see.'

I thought about the sheets. When a sheet came from the sick room smelling bad, ordinary soap did not fix it either. So my mother dipped it in the chlorine water, the sharp, stinging bath that bleached things white. Afterwards the sheet smelled of nothing at all.

I did not want to say it. I was nine, and he was a doctor. But I thought of Frau Weber saying that nobody upstairs ever asked. So I pointed to the chlorine tub.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he plunged his hands in, up to the wrists, and hissed at the sting. He pulled them out and held them to my face.

I smelled. Nothing. Only the chlorine, clean and sharp.

In May, a white basin of chlorine water appeared by the door of the First Ward. Every doctor and every student had to wash in it before they touched a mother. The students grumbled. Their hands cracked and stung. One of the older doctors said it was an insult. But Doctor Semmelweis stood by the basin like a soldier, and nobody got past.

In April, I had seen many coffins leave the First Ward. In June, I saw few. In July, fewer still. Frau Weber showed me the numbers in the big book, and ran her finger down them. 'Now,' she said, 'upstairs is as safe as downstairs.' She did not smile, but she gave me two plums instead of one.

Many doctors never believed him. They said a gentleman's hands could not carry illness. More than thirty years later, a scientist named Louis Pasteur found the tiny living things that cause childbed fever. Nobody wrote down my name, or my mother's, or Frau Weber's. But I still wash my hands slowly, every time, and I still smell them afterwards.

**For the parent:** For the parent: nobody in the story can see germs. The clue is a smell that soap cannot remove, so something invisible must still be on the doctors' hands. The midwives' careful counting gives the comparison, and the change of just one thing, washing in chlorine water at the door, changes the numbers. That is a fair test, and it is how the invisible was found. The story also asks quietly who gets listened to: mothers, midwives, washerwomen and a child all knew part of the answer.

- Ask: Why did the mothers beg to go to the midwives' ward?
- Ask: The doctor's hands still smelled after soap. What did that tell him?
- Ask: What changed in the First Ward in May, and what happened to the numbers?
- Ask: Frau Weber said nobody upstairs ever asked the midwives. Why do you think that was?
- Ask: Anna nearly did not speak. What helped her decide to point at the tub?

### g2 · Make a Micelle (15 min)
- **rules:** Each player is a soap molecule. Your left hand is your tail, which sticks to oil. Your right hand is your head, which sticks to water.; Put the SKIN sign at one side of the room and the DRAIN sign at the other. Scatter the 10 oil blobs on the SKIN side.; Oil blobs may only be touched by tails, your left hands. Heads, your right hands, must stay pointing outwards, into the 'water'.; Work in pairs or threes. Hold one blob between your left hands, heads facing out, and walk it to the DRAIN. That is a micelle.; If the blob drops, it sticks back on the SKIN. Take it back and try again.; Clear all 10 blobs before a grown-up counts slowly to 120. Then try with only 60.
- **Printable cards: Two big signs to put on the floor:** DRAIN; SKIN
- **Printable tokens: 10 'oil blobs': crumpled balls of yellow paper, each with a small germ drawn on it:** 🟡
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Crumple 10 sheets of scrap paper into balls, and draw a germ on each. These are the oil blobs. 2. Put the SKIN sign on one side of the room and the DRAIN on the other. 3. Scatter the blobs near SKIN. 4. Show everyone which hand is the tail and which is the head.  
  *Parent says:* You are soap molecules. Your left hand sticks to oil. Your right hand sticks to water. The yellow balls are blobs of oil, with germs inside.  
  *Child mode:* You are a soap molecule! Your left hand holds oil. Your right hand holds water.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Players pair up. 2. They pick up a blob between their left hands only, right hands pointing outwards. 3. They carry it to DRAIN without dropping it. 4. Count slowly to 120 while they clear all 10.  
  *Parent says:* Tails hold the oil. Heads face the water. Go!  
  *Child mode:* Hold a blob between your left hands. Keep right hands pointing out. Carry it to the drain!
  - Ask: Why must the right hands point outwards? (good: They are the water-loving heads.; The water grabs the heads and carries the ball away.)
  - Note: With two players, the parent and child are one micelle. With more players, a group of three or four around one blob looks most like a real micelle. Soft paper balls are safe to drop.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about how the game is like real soap, and how it is different.  
  *Parent says:* Real micelles are made of many soap molecules, and they are far too small to see. The rubbing of your hands breaks the oil into tiny blobs, so the soap can wrap them.  
  *Child mode:* Real micelles are made of many soap molecules. They are far too small to see.
  - Check: In a real micelle, where do the oil-loving tails of the soap molecules point? → **Inwards, into the oil**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Turmeric Germ Test: water or soap? (25 min)
- **question:** Does soap clean pretend germs off hands better than water? Does rubbing for longer help?
- **predict:** Before you test, guess. Rank the three ways from best to worst: water only for 20 seconds, soap for 5 seconds, soap for 20 seconds. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only the way of washing. Use the same amount of oil and turmeric each time, measured with the same spoon. Use the same tap, and count the seconds out loud. Wash off completely between rounds, then coat again.
- **safety:** Turmeric stains clothes yellow, so wear old clothes and roll up sleeves. Keep oily hands away from eyes. The floor near the tap can get slippery, so wipe it dry.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw your hands after each wash and colour where yellow stayed. Then draw a bar for each way of washing.
- **results table:** Way of washing | Yellow left, from 0 (none) to 5 (all) | Where the yellow stayed
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the oil, turmeric, soap and towel near the tap. 2. Ask your child to rank the three ways of washing, best first.  
  *Parent says:* Real germs are far too small to see. So today, turmeric in oil is our pretend germ. Germs on real hands sit in the skin's own oil, just like this. Which way of washing do you think will work best? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Turmeric in oil is your pretend germ. Real germs hide in your skin's oil. Guess which wash will work best.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put half a teaspoon of oil in your child's palms. 2. Add a pinch of turmeric. 3. Rub all over both hands, backs, between fingers and under nails, for 10 seconds. 4. Rinse under water only for 20 seconds, counting out loud, rubbing as normal. 5. Pat dry with the old towel.  
  *Parent says:* Round one: water only, for 20 seconds. Count with me. Now look. How much yellow is left, from 0 for none to 5 for all of it?  
  *Child mode:* Rub oil and turmeric on both hands. Rinse with water only. Count to 20. How much yellow is left? Score it, 0 to 5.
  - Ask: What do the water drops do on your oily hands? (good: They roll off.; They make little beads.; They don't spread out.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wash off fully with soap and dry. 2. Coat again with the same oil and turmeric, the same way. 3. This time use soap, but rub for only 5 seconds, then rinse. 4. Score the yellow.  
  *Parent says:* Round two: soap, but only 5 seconds. Same oil, same turmeric. Only one thing changes. Score it.  
  *Child mode:* Coat your hands again, the same way. Now soap for just 5 seconds. Rinse. Score the yellow, 0 to 5.
  - Note: Turmeric dyes skin a little, so a faint yellow tint may stay even after a good wash. Score the clumps and patches of powder, not the faint colour. A pale tint that will not wash off is the dye, not the pretend germs. The soap bar will turn a little yellow too. Rinse it at the end.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wash fully, dry, and coat again. 2. Now wash with soap for 20 seconds: palms, backs, between fingers, round each thumb, fingertips, under nails and wrists. Sing Happy Birthday twice. 3. Rinse well and score.  
  *Parent says:* Round three: soap for 20 seconds, every part. Sing Happy Birthday twice while you rub. Now score it. Where did the yellow go?  
  *Child mode:* Coat again. Soap for 20 seconds. Rub every part, even thumbs and nails. Sing Happy Birthday twice. Score it.
  - Ask: Where did the yellow go? (good: Down the drain with the water.; The soap took it off my hands and the water carried it away.)
  - Check: Which wash usually leaves the least yellow? → **Soap, 20 seconds**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart with one bar for each way of washing, as tall as its score.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn the scores into a picture. A shorter bar means less yellow left, so a cleaner hand.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each wash. Make it as tall as the score. Short bar means clean hands!
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: soap for 20 seconds cleans best. What I saw: water left a score of 4, and soap for 20 seconds left 0. The yellow went down the drain. Why: the turmeric was stuck in oil, water slides off oil, and soap holds both oil and water, so the rinse carried it away.
### e3 · Oil, Water and Soap in a Jar (15 min)
- **question:** Can soap make oil and water mix?
- **predict:** Guess first. After shaking, which jar will stay mixed longer: oil and water, or oil, water and soap? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two jars the same size. Put the same amount of water and oil in each. Add soap to only one jar. Shake both for the same time, 10 seconds, then put them down together and start the timer.
- **safety:** Close lids tightly. Use plastic bottles if a small child will shake them.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both jars straight after shaking, and after 2 minutes.
- **results table:** Jar | Seconds until a clear oil layer is back on top | What it looked like
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir a pinch of turmeric into the oil, so the oil turns yellow. 2. Put half a cup of water and 2 tablespoons of yellow oil in each jar. 3. Add half a teaspoon of soap to jar 2 only. 4. Close both lids tightly.  
  *Parent says:* Look: the yellow oil floats on top of the water in a layer. Oil and water don't mix. Jar 2 also has soap.  
  *Child mode:* Put water and yellow oil in two jars. Add soap to jar 2 only. See the oil float on top?
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shake both jars hard for 10 seconds, at the same time if you can. 2. Put them down side by side and start the timer. 3. Watch for a clear yellow layer coming back on top. Write down the seconds for each jar.  
  *Parent says:* Shake them both for 10 seconds. Now watch. Which jar separates first?  
  *Child mode:* Shake both jars for 10 seconds. Put them down. Start the timer. Which one separates first?
  - Note: Jar 1 usually shows a clear oil layer again within 1 to 3 minutes. Jar 2 stays cloudy and creamy for 10 minutes or more. If jar 2 is still cloudy at 10 minutes, stop the timer and write 'more than 600 seconds'. A creamy band may rise to the top of jar 2, but it stays cloudy, not clear. Some foam on top is normal. If jar 2 separates fast too, add a little more soap and shake again. Bar soap scraped into flakes works too, but slowly, so liquid soap is best.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain what is in the cloudy jar.  
  *Parent says:* The cloudy jar is full of millions of tiny oil drops, each one wrapped in soap molecules. Tails in the oil, heads in the water. That is exactly how soap carries oil off your hands.  
  *Child mode:* The cloudy jar is full of tiny oil drops. Each is wrapped in soap. That is how soap cleans hands!
  - Check: Why did the soapy jar stay cloudy? → **Soap molecules wrapped tiny drops of oil, so they stayed mixed in the water**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think, what did you see, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: soap helps oil mix with water. What I saw: the jar without soap separated in about a minute and a half, and the soapy one stayed cloudy for over 10 minutes. Why: soap holds oil with one end and water with the other.
### e4 · The Bread Test: what do hands leave behind? (30 min, 7 days)
- **question:** Do unwashed hands carry living things we can't see? Does washing help?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which slice will grow spots of mould first and most: not touched, dirty hands, water-only hands or soap-washed hands? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Keep everything the same except how the hands were cleaned. Use slices from the same loaf, the same bags, the same drops of water and the same cupboard. Rub each slice for the same 10 seconds.
- **safety:** Never open the bags once sealed. Mould can make people with asthma or allergies ill. At the end, throw the bags away still sealed and wash your hands.
- **record:** Fill in the table on days 3, 5 and 7. Draw each slice. On day 7, draw a bar chart of the spots on each slice.
- **results table:** Day | Not touched: spots | Dirty hands: spots | Water only: spots | Soap: spots
- **schedule:** day 1: Touch the slices in four ways, seal them in bags, and put them in a warm, dark cupboard. (15 min); day 3: Look through the bags without opening them. Count and draw any spots. (3 min); day 5: Look again. Count and draw the spots on each slice. (4 min); day 7: Look one last time. Fill in the table, draw a bar chart, and throw the sealed bags away. (8 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the four bags: Not touched, Dirty hands, Water only, Soap. 2. Use the spoon to put slice 1 into 'Not touched' without touching it. Seal it.  
  *Parent says:* Mould is a tiny fungus, a living thing. Their seeds, called spores, are too small to see. If they land on damp bread, they grow into spots we can see. Let's see what our hands carry.  
  *Child mode:* Label four bags. Use a spoon to put one slice in 'Not touched'. Do not touch it! Seal it.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Before washing, after play or after handling coins, your child rubs both hands on slice 2 for 10 seconds. Bag it. 2. Rinse hands under water only for 5 seconds, then rub slice 3. Bag it. 3. Wash with soap for 20 seconds, dry on a clean towel, and rub slice 4. Bag it. 4. Add 3 drops of water to each bag and seal tightly.  
  *Parent says:* Rub the dirty hands slice for 10 seconds. Now water only. Now soap for 20 seconds. Same 10-second rub each time.  
  *Child mode:* Rub one slice with dirty hands. Rinse with water. Rub the next. Wash with soap. Rub the last. Seal them.
  - Note: Bread with preservatives, which is most packet bread, can take 10 days or more to grow mould. Fresh bakery bread or pav works fastest, often in 4 to 6 days. Keep the bags in a warm, dark cupboard, not the fridge.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put all four bags together in a warm, dark cupboard.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wait. Living things need time to grow.  
  *Child mode:* Put all four bags in a warm, dark cupboard. Now wait. Living things need time.
  - Wait: Look again on day 3, day 5 and day 7, without opening the bags.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 3, 5 and 7, look through the bags without opening them. Count the spots of mould on each slice and write them in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Count the spots on each slice. Green, black, white or orange spots are all mould.  
  *Child mode:* Look through the bags. Do not open them! Count the spots on each slice. Write them down.
  - Note: Results are often messy. The 'dirty hands' slice usually grows most. The 'not touched' slice can still grow some, because spores float in the air everywhere. Water-only is usually in between. If nothing has grown by day 7, wait until day 10. If the soap slice grows a lot, ask: were the hands dried on a clean towel? That is a real result to talk about, not a failure.
  - Check: Why can the 'not touched' slice still grow a little mould? → **Mould spores float in the air everywhere, so some land on it anyway**
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 7, draw a bar chart of the spots on each slice. 2. Ask for three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why. 3. Throw the bags away still sealed, and wash hands.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each slice. Then say what you think, what you saw and why. Throw the bags away sealed.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: dirty hands carry living things we cannot see. What I saw: the dirty hands slice had 9 spots, the soap slice had 2. Why: soap washed most spores and microbes off my hands.

### a1 · How Often Do You Touch Your Face? (15 min)
- **record:** Make tally marks for each touch. Write which part was touched most: eyes, nose, mouth or other.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the task. Your child will count your face touches, and you will count theirs, without saying when.  
  *Parent says:* Germs get in most easily through the eyes, nose and mouth. Hands carry them there. So how often do our hands touch our faces? Let's spy on each other for 10 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Hands carry germs to your eyes, nose and mouth. How often do you touch your face? Let's count!

  *Child mode:* Count the marks. Multiply by 6 for one hour. Is it more than you guessed?

### a2 · Reetha Foam: a plant that makes soap (20 min)
- **safety:** Reetha water stings badly in the eyes. Keep it away from faces. A grown-up helps squeeze the shells. If it gets in an eye, rinse with plenty of clean water.
- **record:** Draw the foam. Write which cleaned the oily plate better: reetha water, soap or plain water.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Break the reetha shells into pieces and take out any hard black seeds. 2. Drop the shells into a cup of warm water. 3. Leave them to soak for at least 15 minutes. Start this an hour before, or in the morning, if you can.  
  *Parent says:* This dry fruit comes from the soapnut tree. People in India have washed hair and silk with it for hundreds of years. Let's find out why.  
  *Child mode:* Break the reetha shells. Take out the black seeds. Put the shells in warm water. Wait.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rub a few drops of oil on both steel plates. 2. Wipe one plate with plain water only, using your fingers. 3. Feel it. 4. Leave the other oily plate for the reetha.  
  *Parent says:* Feel this plate after plain water. Still greasy? Now let's see what reetha does.  
  *Child mode:* Rub oil on two plates. Wash one with plain water. Is it still greasy? Keep the other one.
- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Squeeze the soaked shells in the water with your fingers, again and again. 2. Watch foam appear. 3. Use the reetha water to wash the second oily plate. 4. Wipe both plates with a tissue and compare. 5. If you like, compare with a third plate washed with soap.

### p1 · Build a Tippy-Tap Hand-Washing Station (60 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** Only a grown-up heats the nail and makes the hole. Hold the hot nail with pliers or a thick cloth. Put the station where spilt water won't make a slippery floor.
- **record:** Draw your tippy-tap and label each part. Write how many washes one bottle gave, and one thing you changed.
- **schedule:** day 1: A grown-up makes the hole. Tie and hang the bottle. Add the foot loop and the soap on a string. Test it. (40 min); day 2: Use it all day. Count how many washes one full bottle gives. Improve the design. (20 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain what a tippy-tap is.  
  *Parent says:* A tippy-tap is a hand-washing station made from a bottle and string. You tip it with your foot, so your dirty hands never touch a tap. Families and schools in many countries use them where there is no running water.  
  *Child mode:* A tippy-tap is a tap made from a bottle. You tip it with your foot. Your hands touch nothing!
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up heats the tip of a nail, holding it with pliers. 2. They push it through the bottle's shoulder, about 3 cm below the cap, to make one small hole. 3. Fill the bottle about three-quarters full, so the water stays below the hole. Screw the cap on loosely, so a little air can get in.  
  *Parent says:* The hole is near the top. Standing up, no water comes out. When we tip it, the water pours from the hole.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes a small hole near the top. Fill the bottle three-quarters full. Cap on loosely.
  - Note: Keep the hole small, about the size of a pencil lead, so the water comes out in a thin stream and lasts longer. If the stream stops after a moment, loosen the cap a little more.
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie a string tightly round the middle of the bottle, and wrap tape over it so it cannot slip. 2. Hang the bottle by this string from a stick laid across two chairs, or from a railing. Hang it at about your child's waist, with the hole facing your child. 3. Tie a second string round the bottle's neck. Let it hang to the floor and tie a loop for a foot. 4. Hang the soap on its own string beside the bottle. 5. Put the bucket underneath.  
  *Parent says:* Press the loop with your foot. It pulls the neck down, so the bottle tips forward and water pours from the hole. Let go, and the heavy water at the bottom stands it up again.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the bottle by its middle. Tie a foot loop to its neck. Hang the soap beside it. Press the loop!
  - Note: The water sits low in the bottle, so it hangs upright by itself. If it tips over on its own, move the middle string a little higher. If it will not tip far enough to pour, make the foot string shorter.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test it. Your child washes their hands properly: wet, soap for 20 seconds, rinse. Check that hands touch only the soap.  
  *Parent says:* Wet your hands with your foot. Let go. Soap for 20 seconds. Rinse with your foot again. Did your hands touch anything else?  
  *Child mode:* Press the loop to wet your hands. Soap for 20 seconds. Press again to rinse. Your hands touched only soap!
  - Check: Why is the tippy-tap worked with a foot, not a hand? → **So clean hands do not touch a dirty tap and pick germs up again**
- **s5 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 2, everyone uses the tippy-tap all day. 2. Count every wash with a tally until the bottle is empty. 3. Talk about how to improve it: a hook for a towel, a cover against dust, a sign.  
  *Parent says:* How many washes did one bottle give? A running tap can pour a whole bottle in under a minute. What would you change?  
  *Child mode:* Count every wash until the bottle is empty. How many? What would make it better?
  - Note: Three-quarters of a 2-litre bottle is about 1,500 millilitres. It usually gives about 20 to 40 washes, depending on the hole size. That makes a good maths problem: divide the millilitres by the number of washes to find how much water one wash used.
  - Wait: Use it all of the next day, then count the washes.

### Germ Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A little boy touches a door handle that a child with a cold has just touched. Then he rubs his eye. At which moment do the germs get into his body?  
   Options: When he rubs his eye / When he touches the handle / When he looks at the handle / Never: germs cannot get in through the eye  
   **Answer:** When he rubs his eye. *Why:* Touching the handle only puts germs on the skin of his hand. Dry skin is a strong wall. The soft, wet lining of the eye lets germs in. Picking 'when he touches the handle' is a very common answer, but that is only where the germs got on, not where they got in.
2. (sort) Sort these habits. Does each one help germs travel, or stop them?  
   **Answer:** sneezing into your elbow → Stops germs; sharing one damp towel with the whole family → Helps germs travel; keeping a lid on the drinking water pot → Stops germs; leaving cut fruit uncovered where flies can sit → Helps germs travel; washing hands with soap before eating → Stops germs; drinking from a tap in the street without boiling the water → Helps germs travel. *Why:* Germs travel on hands, in water and food, in sneeze drops and on insects like flies. Each good habit blocks one of those roads.
3. (fill) In the turmeric test, the yellow turmeric on your hands stood for ____.  
   **Answer:** ['germs', 'pretend germs']. *Why:* Germs are too small to see, so the turmeric stood in for them. Like real germs, it sat in the oil on your skin. Where the yellow stayed after washing, germs would stay too.
4. (truefalse) A small cut or scrape on your skin can let germs into your body.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Dry, unbroken skin is a strong wall against germs. A cut is a gap in that wall. That is why we wash a scrape with clean water and cover it until it heals.
5. (mcq) In the turmeric test, where did the yellow stay longest after a quick wash?  
   Options: Between the fingers, round the thumbs and under the nails / On the middle of the palms / On the backs of the wrists only / Nowhere: it all came off  
   **Answer:** Between the fingers, round the thumbs and under the nails. *Why:* We rub the middle of the palms most, so they get cleanest. The gaps between fingers, the thumbs and under the nails are easy to miss. Picking 'palms' usually means thinking the part you look at most is the dirtiest.
6. (write) Your fingers are oily after eating a vada. You rinse them under the tap with water only. Predict how they will feel, and explain why.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer predicts that the fingers will still feel greasy. It explains that water slides off oil and cannot hold on to it, so the oil stays on the skin. It adds that soap would hold the oil and the water both, so the oil would wash away.. *Why:* Water and oil do not mix. That is why water alone leaves oily dirt, and the germs in it, behind.
7. (match) Match each body part to a way of caring for it.  
   **Answer:** Teeth → brush twice a day; Nails → keep them short and clean; Hair → wash it and comb it, and check for lice; Feet → wear chappals outside. *Why:* Brushing removes mouth bacteria that make acid. Short nails leave nowhere for germs to hide. Washing and combing keeps hair free of tangles and lice. Chappals stop worm larvae in dirty soil getting in through the feet.
8. (mcq) You need to sneeze, and there is no tissue. What is best?  
   Options: Sneeze into your elbow / Sneeze into your hand and carry on / Sneeze into the air / Hold the sneeze in for as long as you can  
   **Answer:** Sneeze into your elbow. *Why:* Your elbow catches most of the drops, and you don't touch things with your elbow. Picking 'into your hand' is what most people do. But then your hand spreads the germs to everything you touch, unless you wash straight away.
9. (write) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, says: 'I will just stop touching my face. Then I won't need to wash my hands before eating.' What would you tell her? Use what you found in the face-touch count.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that people touch their faces many times an hour without noticing, so Kavya cannot simply stop. It adds that eating puts her fingers in her mouth anyway, so germs on her hands would get in. Washing before food is the sure way.. *Why:* In a 2015 study, students touched their faces about 23 times an hour. Most touches happen without our noticing, so washing at the key moments works better than trying not to touch.
10. (mcq) After washing your hands, why should you dry them on a clean towel or let them dry in the air?  
   Options: Wet hands pick up and pass on germs more easily than dry hands / Water makes germs grow bigger / Drying makes the soap work / There is no reason  
   **Answer:** Wet hands pick up and pass on germs more easily than dry hands. *Why:* Germs move more easily onto and off wet skin. A shared damp towel can also hold germs. Picking 'water makes germs grow bigger' mixes up washing with leaving food damp for days, as in the bread test.
11. (draw) Draw a soap molecule. Label the head that sticks to water and the tail that sticks to oil.  
   **Answer:** A round head labelled 'sticks to water' and a long tail labelled 'sticks to oil'.. *Why:* Two different ends is the whole secret of soap. One end holds the water and the other holds the oil.
12. (write) Your private parts are the parts covered by your underwear. Write two things that are true about them.  
   **Answer:** They are kept clean, by washing every day. They are private, and my body belongs to me. If a touch ever feels wrong, I say no, get away and tell a grown-up I trust.. *Why:* Clean, private, and your own. A child is never in trouble for telling a trusted grown-up.
13. (mcq) A girl finds tiny wriggling creatures swimming in the water of a flowerpot tray on her balcony. What should she do?

   **Answer:** Tip out the water and scrub the tray. *Why:* The wrigglers are young mosquitoes. In about a week they will fly and bite, and some mosquitoes carry dengue or malaria. Tipping out the water stops them growing up. Picking 'perfume' comes from the old idea that bad smells cause illness.
14. (odd) Which one is the odd one out? Covering cut fruit, boiling drinking water, spraying room freshener, sneezing into your elbow. Say why.

In 1854, John Snow showed that cholera in London spread through water from one pump, not through smells.
16. (write) Choose one habit at home that stops germs, such as boiling water or covering food. Say which road for germs it blocks: hands, water, food, sneezes or insects.

### Soap Scientists (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 3, did the turmeric test. She scored the yellow left on her hands, from 0 (none) to 5 (all). Soap for 5 seconds still left a score of 2. What does that tell her?

   **Answer:** Soap needs time and rubbing to reach all the oil. *Why:* Soap for 20 seconds left 0, and soap for 5 seconds left 2. The soap was the same, so the extra rubbing time made the difference. Picking 'soap does not work' ignores the 20-second bar.

   Options: The soap tails push into the virus's oily coat and break the coat apart / Soap poisons the virus / Soap freezes the virus / Soap starves the virus  
   **Answer:** The soap tails push into the virus's oily coat and break the coat apart. *Why:* Flu viruses wear an oily coat made of molecules like soap's. Soap tails slip in and pull that coat apart. Picking 'poisons' is natural, because we think of cleaners as poisons. Soap pulls the coat apart instead.
6. (sort) Sort these microbes into the right group.  
   **Answer:** the microbes in curd → Helpful or harmless microbes; the cholera germ in dirty water → Germs that can make us ill; microbes that turn fallen leaves into soil → Helpful or harmless microbes; the virus that causes flu → Germs that can make us ill; most microbes living on your skin → Helpful or harmless microbes; the malaria germ carried by mosquitoes → Germs that can make us ill. *Why:* Most microbes are helpful or harmless.    Options: The visitors you picked up from things you touched / Every single microbe on your skin / Only the skin's own microbes that live there all the time / None at all  
   **Answer:** The visitors you picked up from things you touched. *Why:* Visitors sit on top of the skin and wash off. Most of the skin's own microbes live deeper in its folds and stay. Picking 'every single microbe' is common. Losing them all would actually be bad for skin.

   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives evidence, for example that soap lifts oil and germs off and the rinse carries them away, so killing is not needed.    **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In a 2017 study, cold and warm water removed germs about equally well. The soap and the rubbing do the work. Very hot water would burn your skin before it killed many germs.
11. (write) Explain to a younger cousin why soap cleans better than water. Use the words oil, head and tail.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that germs sit in the oil on our skin, and water slides off oil. A soap molecule has a head that sticks to water and a tail that sticks to oil. So the water can carry the oil and the germs away.. *Why:* Three steps make a full answer: germs are in oil, water cannot hold oil, and soap holds both.
12. (write) Reetha shells make foam when you squeeze them in water. What does that tell you about what is inside them?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that reetha has soap-like molecules, called saponins, with one end that sticks to water and one end that sticks to oil. That is why it foams and cleans oil off a plate.. *Why:* Foam is a sign of soap-like molecules holding air and water together.
13. (draw) Draw a micelle: a ring of soap molecules around a blob of oil. Show which way the heads and tails point.  
   **Answer:** A ring of soap molecules with their tails pointing into an oil blob in the middle, and their heads pointing out into the water.. *Why:* Tails in the oil, heads in the water. That is how the water can carry the oil away.
14. (mcq) After a wash with plain soap, where are most of the bacteria that were on your hands?  
   Options: Down the drain, still alive, in the rinse water / Killed on your hands by the soap / Still on your hands, hidden in the oil / Turned into soap  
   **Answer:** Down the drain, still alive, in the rinse water. *Why:* Plain soap kills very few bacteria. It wraps the oil they sit in, and the rinse water carries them away. Picking 'killed by the soap' is very common, because adverts talk about killing germs.
15. (write) In the bread test, the slice touched by unwashed hands grew the most mould. The slice touched after washing with soap grew the least. What does this tell you about hands that look clean?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that hands that look clean can still carry living things too small to see. The mould spots grew from them. Soap took most of them off, so that slice grew the least. It may add that the untouched slice is the comparison.. *Why:* The bread made invisible living things visible, by letting them grow into spots of mould.
16. (match) Match each person to what they did.  
   **Answer:** Antonie van Leeuwenhoek → saw 'little animals' through his home-made lenses in the 1670s; Ignaz Semmelweis → made doctors wash in chlorine water in 1847; John Snow → showed that cholera spread through one water pump in London in 1854; Filippo Pacini → first saw and described the cholera germ, in 1854. *Why:* Each person added one piece. Germ theory was built by many people over about 300 years.
### Hidden Worlds and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at the chart of Semmelweis's numbers. What happened after hand-washing began in May 1847?  
   Options: Deaths fell from about 18 out of 100 to about 2 out of 100 / Deaths stayed the same / Deaths went up / Nobody counted  
   **Answer:** Deaths fell from about 18 out of 100 to about 2 out of 100. *Why:* The numbers fell sharply after washing began, and stayed low. Picking 'nobody counted' misses how important counting was. The careful counts were the evidence.
2. (fill) In April 1847, about 18 out of 100 mothers in the doctors' ward died. By June, about 2 out of 100 did. That is ____ times fewer.  
   **Answer:** ['9', 'nine']. *Why:* 18 divided by 2 is 9. So about 9 times fewer mothers died after washing began.
3. (mcq) Ordinary soap did not remove the smell of the cutting room from Semmelweis's hands, but chlorine water did. What was he using the smell as?  
   Options: A sign that something invisible was still on his hands / A way to make his hands smell nice / A medicine / A test of the soap's colour  
   **Answer:** A sign that something invisible was still on his hands. *Why:* He could not see the 'particles' he suspected. The smell was a clue that something stayed. Scientists often use a clue they can sense to study things they cannot see.
4. (mcq) Many doctors in 1847 refused to wash. Which of their reasons was the LEAST scientific?  
   Options: A gentleman's hands could not possibly be dirty / Nobody had yet seen the germs / Semmelweis could not explain why washing worked / Chlorine water made their hands sore  
   **Answer:** A gentleman's hands could not possibly be dirty. *Why:* Saying a gentleman's hands cannot be dirty is pride, not evidence. The other reasons were real problems, even though the numbers already showed washing worked. Science should follow the evidence, even when it is uncomfortable.
5. (truefalse) The thin skin around each cell in your body, called its membrane, is built from molecules with water-loving heads and oil-loving tails, like soap.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Cell membranes are two layers of such molecules, heads out and tails in. The oily coat of a flu virus is built the same way, which is why soap breaks it.
6. (mcq) If soap can break a virus's oily coat, why doesn't soap break the cells of your hands?  
   Options: The top layer of your skin is tough, dead cells packed tightly, so soap cannot reach the living cells / Soap is too weak to touch any cell / Soap washes off before it reaches them / Soap does break them all  
   **Answer:** The top layer of your skin is tough, dead cells packed tightly, so soap cannot reach the living cells. *Why:* Your skin's top layer is a tough wall of dead cells. Soap can dry it by removing some oil, but cannot get through to living cells. Picking 'soap does break them all' is a sensible worry, but skin is built to stop that.
7. (write) Robert Koch is often given the credit for finding the cholera germ in 1884. Filippo Pacini saw and described the same germ in 1854, but few people noticed. Why do you think Pacini was forgotten? What does this tell you about who gets remembered?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that most scientists in 1854 believed illness came from bad air, so they did not listen to Pacini. Koch convinced the world thirty years later. It says that credit often goes to whoever convinces people, not always to who saw it first. Today the germ's scientific name includes Pacini's name and the year 1854.. *Why:* Science is done by people, and people do not always listen. Looking back at who was ignored is part of telling the history honestly.
8. (perspective) Scientists argue about why allergies, like hay fever, have become more common.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and gives evidence or a reason, for example that most microbes are harmless or helpful, which fits Rook. It admits a fair point from another side, for example that washing before food still saves lives. It does not conclude that washing hands is bad.. *Why:* This is still debated. No study shows that washing hands before meals causes allergies. Most scientists today agree that washing at the key moments still matters, because it stops real germs.
9. (write) Design a fair test to find out whether reetha water or soap cleans oil off a steel plate better. What will you change? What will you keep the same?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer changes only the cleaner: reetha water on one plate, soap on another. It keeps the same amount of oil, the same plates, the same amount of water and the same number of wipes. Then it compares how greasy each plate feels, or how a tissue looks after wiping.. *Why:* A good fair test changes one thing and names at least three things kept the same.
10. (write) In the story, Anna was nine. She nearly did not speak, because the man in front of her was a doctor. Why is it good for science when everyone's observations count, not only those of important people?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that anyone can notice a clue, and the person who notices may not be important. Anna knew that ordinary soap did not remove a bad smell, but chlorine water did. If the doctor had not listened, the clue would have been lost.. *Why:* Evidence matters, not who brings it. The midwives' careful counts and Anna's nose were both evidence.
11. (write) Microbes lived on Earth for billions of years before any animal. Do you think they matter only for what they do for people? Give a reason.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives a view with a reason. For example: no, they matter for themselves, because they are living beings with their own lives, and the whole Earth depends on them, not just people.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. A good answer gives a reason.
12. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how soap cleans an oily hand.  
   **Answer:** ['The soap tails push into the oil on your skin', 'Rubbing breaks the oil into tiny drops', 'Soap molecules wrap each drop, heads facing out to the water', 'The rinse water carries the wrapped drops, and the germs in them, down the drain']. *Why:* Each step needs the one before. That is why rubbing for 20 seconds matters: without it, the oil stays in big patches that the soap cannot wrap.
13. (write) Some people say dirt is just 'something in the wrong place'. Do you agree? Give one example for and one against.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives an example for each side. For example: for, soil is good in a flowerpot but dirty on a plate. Against, some things, like cholera germs in drinking water, are harmful almost anywhere we meet them.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. What counts is a reason for each side.
14. (mcq) Why might plants like reetha make soap-like molecules?  
   Options: To protect themselves from insects and fungi / So people can wash their hair / To make the fruit sweeter / By accident, for no reason  
   **Answer:** To protect themselves from insects and fungi. *Why:* Saponins taste bitter and can harm insects and fungi, which helps protect the plant. Picking 'so people can wash' is a very human-centred idea. Plants were making saponins long before people existed.
15. (mcq) Some tummy viruses have no oily coat, so soap cannot break them. Why does washing with soap still help against them?  
   Options: Soap and rubbing lift them off the hands, and the water carries them away / Soap makes them grow an oily coat / Washing does not help at all / The water is too cold for them  
   **Answer:** Soap and rubbing lift them off the hands, and the water carries them away. *Why:* Soap wraps the oil and dirt the viruses sit in, and the rinse carries them off. Picking 'washing does not help' means thinking soap must kill a germ to work. Mostly, soap works by moving germs off your hands.
16. (write) A friend says: 'Hand sanitiser kills germs, and soap only washes them off. So sanitiser is always better.' After eating a greasy vada, which would you use, and why? Then say when sanitiser is a good choice.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer chooses soap and water for greasy hands, because soap lifts the oil the germs sit in, and sanitiser does not remove oil or dirt. It adds that some tummy viruses are not killed well by sanitiser. It then gives the friend a fair point: sanitiser is useful when hands look clean and there is no soap or water, such as on a bus.. *Why:* Health experts say to wash with soap and water when you can, and use sanitiser when you cannot. Killing is not always better than removing.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a germ?  
   Options: The cholera bacteria in dirty water / The bacteria that turn milk into curd / A speck of dust / An ant  
   **Answer:** The cholera bacteria in dirty water. *Why:* A germ is a microbe or virus that can make us ill. Cholera bacteria do. Curd bacteria are microbes too, but they are helpful, not germs. Dust is not alive, and an ant is far bigger than a microbe.
2. (mcq) How do germs most often get into our bodies?  
   Options: Hands carry them to our eyes, nose and mouth / They jump through dry skin / They grow out of our hair  
   **Answer:** Hands carry them to our eyes, nose and mouth. *Why:* Hands carry germs to the soft, wet linings of the eyes, nose and mouth. Picking 'through dry skin' misses that skin is a strong wall, unless there is a cut.
3. (mcq) Why does water alone leave most germs on your hands?  
   Options: Germs stick in the skin's oil, and water slides off oil / Water is too cold / Water makes the germs grow  
   **Answer:** Germs stick in the skin's oil, and water slides off oil. *Why:* Water molecules hold on to each other, not to oil. So water runs over the oily layer where germs stick. Picking 'too cold' is common, but warm and cold water work about the same.
4. (mcq) A soap molecule has…  
   Options: A water-loving head and an oil-loving tail / Two water-loving ends / Two oil-loving ends  
   **Answer:** A water-loving head and an oil-loving tail. *Why:* Its two different ends let it hold oil and water both, so water can carry oil and germs away. Picking 'two oil-loving ends' misses that soap needs one end for each, so the water can carry it away.
5. (mcq) In the jar test, why did the soapy jar stay cloudy?  
   Options: Soap wrapped tiny oil drops so they stayed mixed in water / Soap made the oil disappear / The soapy jar was dirtier  
   **Answer:** Soap wrapped tiny oil drops so they stayed mixed in water. *Why:* Each oil drop is wrapped in soap molecules, tails in and heads out. Picking 'disappear' misses that the oil is still there, just in tiny wrapped drops.
6. (mcq) In the turmeric test, which wash left the least yellow?  
   Options: Water only for 20 seconds / Soap for 5 seconds / Soap for 20 seconds, rubbing every part  
   **Answer:** Soap for 20 seconds, rubbing every part. *Why:* Soap plus 20 seconds of rubbing reaches all the oil, even between fingers. Picking soap for 5 seconds usually means thinking soap works instantly.
7. (mcq) When is it MOST important to wash your hands with soap?  
   Options: Before eating and after the toilet / After reading a book / Before going to sleep only  
   **Answer:** Before eating and after the toilet. *Why:* Those are the moments germs most easily travel to your mouth, or from waste to other people. Picking 'after reading' is a kind thought, but books rarely carry germs that make us ill.
8. (mcq) A boy's ear feels itchy. Which is the safe thing to do?  
   Options: Wipe the outer ear with a cloth, and never poke anything inside / Clean inside with a hairpin / Pour water in every day  
   **Answer:** Wipe the outer ear with a cloth, and never poke anything inside. *Why:* Earwax moves out of the ear on its own and protects it. Poking can hurt the eardrum. Picking a hairpin is a common, risky habit.
9. (mcq) Your private parts are…  
   Options: Kept clean and private, and your body belongs to you / Something you can never talk about with anyone / Not really part of your body  
   **Answer:** Kept clean and private, and your body belongs to you. *Why:* They are part of you, kept clean and private. You can always talk to a trusted grown-up about them, especially if any touch feels wrong. Picking 'never talk about' is what makes children unsafe, so it is not right.
10. (mcq) Why do we empty pots, trays and coolers of still water once a week?  
   Options: Mosquito eggs in them become flying mosquitoes in about a week / Still water smells / To save water  
   **Answer:** Mosquito eggs in them become flying mosquitoes in about a week. *Why:* Emptying the water before the young mosquitoes grow up stops them. Picking 'smells' echoes the old bad-air idea. The problem is the mosquitoes, not the smell.
11. (mcq) Which of these protects a whole town from germs?  
   Options: Clean water, toilets and covered drains / A big hospital only / Lots of perfume  
   **Answer:** Clean water, toilets and covered drains. *Why:* Clean water and sanitation stop germs reaching many people at once. Picking 'hospital only' misses that hospitals mostly treat illness after it starts.
12. (mcq) What did Semmelweis do in 1847?  
   Options: Made doctors wash their hands in chlorine water before touching mothers / Invented the microscope / Discovered soap  
   **Answer:** Made doctors wash their hands in chlorine water before touching mothers. *Why:* The washing basin made deaths in his ward fall sharply. Picking 'microscope' is a mix-up: microscopes were around for 200 years before him.
13. (mcq) Semmelweis compared his ward with another, safer ward. Who ran it?  
   Options: Midwives / Soldiers / Priests  
   **Answer:** Midwives. *Why:* The midwives' ward had far fewer fevers. Their careful work gave Semmelweis his clue. Picking another answer usually means the midwives were left out of the story, as they often are.
14. (mcq) Most microbes are…  
   Options: Harmless or helpful / Germs that make us ill / Found only in dirty places  
   **Answer:** Harmless or helpful. *Why:* Only a small number of microbes are germs. Many help us, the soil and the sea. Picking 'germs' is a very common belief, often from adverts.
15. (mcq) What do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Whether too few harmless microbes in early life helps cause allergies / Whether germs exist / Whether soap holds oil  
   **Answer:** Whether too few harmless microbes in early life helps cause allergies. *Why:* The hygiene hypothesis and the 'old friends' idea are still debated. Picking the others misses that germs and soap are well understood.

## Applications
- Hand-washing in homes, schools and hospitals stops tummy bugs, colds and flu spreading.
- Doctors and nurses scrub before surgery, and boil or heat their tools to kill germs.
- Boiling, filtering and treating drinking water stops cholera, typhoid and other illnesses.
- Toilets, sewers and drains keep waste away from water and food.
- Covering food and water keeps flies and dust off.
- Emptying still water every week stops mosquitoes that carry dengue and malaria.
- Dishwashing liquid, shampoo and laundry soap all use two-ended molecules like soap.
- Reetha and shikakai are still used to wash hair, silk and jewellery in India.
- Food factories and restaurants follow hygiene rules to keep food safe.
- Global Handwashing Day, every 15 October, reminds the world that soap saves lives.

## Spotted around us
- Soap on a string, or a tippy-tap, by a tap at a school or roadside dhaba.

- A covered clay pot or steel water drum in the kitchen.

- ASHA workers visiting homes in villages to talk about health.
- Curd setting in a pot overnight, made by friendly microbes.

## Open questions
- **How much contact with harmless microbes do children need to stay healthy?** Studies of farm children suggest it matters, but nobody knows which microbes, how much, or when.
- **Why do our skins carry the particular microbes they do?** Each person's skin has a different mix, and it changes with age, place and season. Scientists are still mapping it.
- **Do cleaning chemicals that kill germs help germs become resistant?** Some laboratory studies suggest they might. How much it happens in real homes is still being studied.
- **Why do people touch their faces so often?** It may help us calm down or think, but scientists do not fully know.
- **What happens to the microbes we wash down the drain?** Drains, sewers and water treatment plants are full of microbes. Scientists are only starting to map which ones live there, and which survive the journey.

## Connected maths
- In the face-touch count, your grown-up touched their face 4 times in 10 minutes. There are 6 lots of 10 minutes in an hour. About how many face touches is that in one hour? **About 24 face touches in one hour.**. 4 touches every 10 minutes. An hour has 6 lots of 10 minutes. 4 × 6 = 24. So about 24 touches in an hour. That is close to what scientists found: about 23 times an hour. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Happy Birthday takes about 10 seconds to sing once. How many times must you sing it to wash your hands for 20 seconds? How many seconds would singing it 3 times take? **2 times for 20 seconds. Singing it 3 times takes 30 seconds.**. Each song is about 10 seconds. 10 + 10 = 20, so 2 songs make 20 seconds. 10 + 10 + 10 = 30, so 3 songs take 30 seconds. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Look at the chart of yellow left after washing. Water only scored 4. Soap for 20 seconds scored 0. Soap for 5 seconds scored 2. Put the three ways in order from cleanest to least clean. How many points cleaner was soap for 20 seconds than water only? **Cleanest to least clean: soap 20 seconds, soap 5 seconds, water only. Soap for 20 seconds was 4 points cleaner.**. A lower score means less yellow left. 0 is lowest, then 2, then 4. So the order is soap 20 seconds, soap 5 seconds, water only. 4 take away 0 is 4, so it was 4 points cleaner. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- You filled your tippy-tap bottle with 1,500 millilitres of water. It gave 30 hand-washes before it was empty. How much water did one wash use? **50 millilitres for each wash.**. Share 1,500 millilitres equally between 30 washes. 1,500 ÷ 30 is the same as 150 ÷ 3, which is 50. You can check: 30 × 50 = 1,500. 50 millilitres is about a quarter of a small steel tumbler. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the bread test, on day 7 you counted the mould spots. Not touched: 1. Dirty hands: 9. Water only: 5. Soap: 2. Make a tally chart, then answer: how many more spots did the dirty hands slice have than the soap slice? **7 more spots.**. Draw tally marks for each slice: 1, 9, 5 and 2. The dirty hands slice had 9 spots. The soap slice had 2. 9 take away 2 is 7. So the dirty hands slice had 7 more spots. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
