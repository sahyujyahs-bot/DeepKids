# Reading the night sky: why the stars turn, how the Pole Star shows where you are, and how astronomers measure how far away a star is
`sci.sky.stargazing.L4`

**Summary.** Watch the night sky for an hour and every star slides slowly across it, except one that hardly moves. That star is the Pole Star, called Dhruva in India, and it is how travellers have found north for thousands of years. It stands about 13 degrees above the northern horizon in Chennai, but about 29 degrees in Delhi, so its height tells you how far north you are. Your child works out why with a ball and a straw, measures how far the sky turns in one hour, and uses a thumb and a wall to measure distances the way astronomers measure the distance to stars. Along the way: why Orion is a winter star and Scorpius a summer one, why a red star is cooler than a blue one, and why planets do not twinkle but stars do.

**Intro.** *On a clear night, look north and find the Saptarishi, the seven stars shaped like a ladle. Two of them, Kratu and Pulaha, point at a lonely star called Dhruva, the Pole Star. Watch for an hour, and all the other stars swing slowly round it, like the spokes of a wheel round the hub. What is the sky turning around, and why does that one star stay still?* In this unit you find out. You build a model with a ball and a straw that shows why the Pole Star stands higher in the sky the further north you go. You measure with your fist how far the stars move in an hour. You use the jump of your thumb to measure distances, which is how astronomers first measured the distance to a star. You also learn why Orion and Scorpius never share a night sky, how a star's colour shows its temperature, and how sailors crossed oceans with nothing but the stars.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that the stars seem to turn once a day because the Earth spins, and that the sky turns about 15 degrees every hour.
1. I can find the Pole Star from the two pointer stars of the Saptarishi, and say why it stays almost still while other stars circle it.
2. I can explain why the height of the Pole Star above the horizon equals my latitude, and why some stars never set where I live.
3. I can explain why different stars are up at night in different seasons, using the Earth's journey round the Sun.
4. I can explain how parallax measures distance, and use the jump of my thumb to find how far away something is.
5. I can tell the difference between how bright a star looks and how bright it really is, and say what a star's colour shows about its temperature.
6. I can tell a planet from a star in the night sky, and explain why stars twinkle and planets mostly do not.

## Terms
- **Celestial pole**: The point in the sky straight above the Earth's North Pole, where the Earth's axis points. The whole sky seems to turn round this point. *e.g.* The north celestial pole is very close to the Pole Star.
- **Pole Star**: The bright star closest to the north celestial pole. In English it is called Polaris. In Sanskrit it is Dhruva, which means fixed or firm. It seems to stay still while all other stars circle it. *e.g.* You can find the Pole Star by following a line from Kratu through Pulaha, the two pointer stars of the Saptarishi.
- **Horizon and altitude**: The horizon is the line where the sky seems to meet the ground. A star's altitude is how high it is above the horizon, measured in degrees. The horizon is 0 degrees, and straight overhead is 90. *e.g.* A fist held out at arm's length covers about 10 degrees of sky, so a star two fists up has an altitude of about 20 degrees.
- **Latitude**: How far north or south of the equator a place is, measured in degrees. The equator is 0 degrees and the North Pole is 90 degrees north. *e.g.* Kanyakumari is at about 8 degrees north, Chennai about 13, Hyderabad about 17, Mumbai about 19, and Delhi about 29.
- **Circumpolar star**: A star that goes round the Pole Star in a circle that stays above the horizon, so it never sets. Which stars are circumpolar depends on your latitude. *e.g.* From Delhi, the stars close to the Pole Star never set. From the equator, no star is circumpolar.
- **Ecliptic**: The path the Sun seems to follow through the stars during a year. The Moon and the planets stay close to this path too. The constellations along it are the zodiac. *e.g.* In January the Sun lies in front of the stars of Sagittarius, so those stars are hidden by daylight.
- **Parallax**: The apparent shift of a near thing against a far background when you look from two different places. Your thumb seems to jump when you close one eye and open the other. The nearer the thing, the bigger the jump. *e.g.* Astronomers measure the parallax of a star by looking at it six months apart, from opposite sides of the Earth's orbit.
- **Baseline**: The distance between the two places you look from when you measure parallax. A longer baseline gives a bigger shift, which is easier to measure. *e.g.* For your thumb, the baseline is the gap between your eyes, about 6 centimetres. For a star, it is the width of the Earth's orbit, about 300 million kilometres.
- **Triangulation**: Finding the distance to something by drawing a triangle. You measure a baseline and the angle to the thing from each end. The shape of the triangle then gives the distance. *e.g.* Surveyors triangulated across India in the 1800s to map the country. They started near Chennai with a baseline of several kilometres.
- **Apparent brightness**: How bright a star looks from here. It depends on how much light the star gives out and also on how far away it is. *e.g.* The Sun looks by far the brightest star because it is so close. Canopus looks bright, but it is far away and truly very powerful.
- **True brightness**: How much light a star really gives out, whatever its distance. Astronomers call this luminosity and compare it with the Sun's. *e.g.* Sirius gives out about 25 times as much light as the Sun. Rigel gives out tens of thousands of times as much.
- **Magnitude**: A number for how bright a star looks. A smaller number means a brighter star. About 150 years before the Common Era, the Greek astronomer Hipparchus put stars into six classes, from 1 for the brightest to 6 for the faintest. *e.g.* The Pole Star has a magnitude of about 2. The faintest stars you can see in a dark place are about 6.
- **Star colour and temperature**: The colour of a star shows how hot its surface is. Cooler stars look red or orange, medium stars look yellow, and the hottest stars look blue-white. *e.g.* Antares, called Jyeshtha, is red and about 3,500 degrees Celsius. The Sun is yellow-white and about 5,500. Sirius is blue-white and about 10,000.
- **Twinkling**: The quick flicker in the brightness and colour of a star. It is made by the Earth's moving air, not by the star. Planets look like tiny discs and twinkle much less. *e.g.* A star near the horizon twinkles more than a star overhead, because its light crosses more air.
- **Planet (wanderer)**: The word planet comes from a Greek word for wanderer. From the Earth, the planets seem to wander through the star patterns over days and months, while the stars keep their places. *e.g.* Jupiter takes about a year to move across one constellation of the zodiac.
- **Precession**: The slow wobble of the Earth's axis, like the wobble of a spinning top. It makes the north celestial pole trace a circle in the sky once in about 26,000 years. *e.g.* About 4,700 years ago, a star called Thuban was nearest the north pole. In about 12,000 years it will be Vega.

## Explanations
### Why the whole sky seems to turn once a day · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth spins once on its axis about every 24 hours. So the stars seem to circle us, the way scenery seems to move past the window of a train. The sky turns about 15 degrees every hour.

If you take a photo of the night sky with a long exposure, each star leaves a curved trail. The trails are parts of circles with the Pole Star near the middle. In an hour, each star has moved through an arc of about 15 degrees, which is one-twenty-fourth of a full circle. The Sun and the Moon rise in the east and set in the west for the same reason. It is the Earth that turns, towards the east, and not the sky that turns towards the west. In 1851, in Paris, Leon Foucault hung a heavy ball on a very long wire and let it swing. Its line of swing slowly turned all through the day, which showed that the floor beneath it was turning. Many science museums have a Foucault pendulum.
- e.g. A long photo of the sky shows the stars as arcs. In a one-hour photo, every arc covers the same angle, about 15 degrees.
- e.g. A star that rises due east at 8 pm will be at its highest point, halfway across the sky, by 2 am, six hours later.
- e.g. Looking north, the stars circle the Pole Star anticlockwise. Looking south, they move across the sky from the left to the right.
### The Pole Star and the point the sky turns round · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's axis points almost exactly at the Pole Star. Because the axis points there, the Pole Star does not seem to move as the Earth spins, and all the other stars circle it.

Think of a spinning top. The point at the end of its axis stays still while everything else goes round. The Earth's axis points to a spot in the sky called the north celestial pole, and the Pole Star lies within a degree of it. That is why it is the one star that hardly moves. It is not a bright star, but it is easy to find from the Saptarishi. Take the two stars at the outer edge of the bowl, Kratu and Pulaha. Draw a line through them and carry on, about five times the gap between them. The line ends at the Pole Star. In the stories of India, Dhruva was a boy who was given a place in the sky that would never move. There is no bright star near the south celestial pole.
- e.g. The Pole Star is about as bright as the stars of the Saptarishi. It is not the brightest star in the sky, as many people think.
- e.g. Another way to find it is from Cassiopeia, a W-shaped group of stars on the other side of the Pole Star from the Saptarishi.
- e.g. Sailors and travellers found north by the Pole Star for thousands of years, long before the magnetic compass.
### The height of the Pole Star equals your latitude · *defined*
**Definition:** The altitude of the Pole Star above your northern horizon, in degrees, is about the same as your latitude. It is 0 degrees at the equator and 90 degrees at the North Pole.

The Pole Star is so far away that its light comes to every part of the Earth along the same direction. So the direction to the Pole Star is parallel to the Earth's axis wherever you stand. Imagine standing at the North Pole. The axis points straight up, so the Pole Star is overhead, at 90 degrees. On the equator, the axis lies flat along the ground, so the Pole Star sits on the northern horizon, at 0 degrees. In between, the star's height grows steadily with how far north you are. This is why a traveller walking north sees the Pole Star climb. A wanderer can find their latitude without a map. Polaris is not exactly on the pole, so the rule is right to within about one degree.
- e.g. In Kanyakumari, at 8 degrees north, the Pole Star is less than one fist above the northern horizon and is often hidden by haze.
- e.g. In Chennai it is at about 13 degrees, which is one fist and a bit.
- e.g. In Delhi it is at about 29 degrees, nearly three fists high.
### Stars that never set, and stars you never see · *defined*
**Definition:** A star is circumpolar if its circle round the Pole Star stays above your horizon. All stars within your latitude's number of degrees of the Pole Star are circumpolar for you. Stars close enough to the south pole never rise for you.

The Pole Star stands at your latitude in degrees above the horizon. A star that circles the Pole Star at a distance smaller than that never dips below the horizon. In Delhi at 29 degrees, stars within 29 degrees of the Pole Star never set. In Chennai at 13 degrees, only stars within 13 degrees never set. On the equator, nothing is circumpolar, and every star rises and sets. The same rule works in the south. In Chennai, stars within 13 degrees of the south pole never rise. So the stars you can see depend on where you live. From the southern tip of India, the Southern Cross can be seen low in the south on spring evenings, but it can never be seen from Kashmir.
- e.g. From Kashmir, at about 34 degrees north, Kratu, the star at the corner of the Saptarishi's bowl, never sets.
- e.g. From Chennai, the Saptarishi rides high on spring evenings and dips close to the northern horizon on autumn evenings.
- e.g. A traveller going from Delhi to Kanyakumari sees new stars appear low in the south that were never visible at home.
### Why the evening sky changes with the seasons · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth goes round the Sun once a year. At night we face away from the Sun, so each month we look out towards a different part of space. Stars rise about 2 hours earlier each month.

Each day, a star rises about 4 minutes earlier than the day before. In a month that adds up to about 2 hours. So after a few months, a star that rose at sunset has moved to rise in the afternoon, and then it can be seen only in the morning. After six months, it is up during the day and hidden by the Sun's glare. After a year, it is back where it began. The constellations opposite the Sun are up at midnight. In January, the Sun lies in front of Sagittarius, and Gemini is at its highest at midnight. The Sun's yearly path through the stars is the ecliptic, and the constellations along it are the zodiac. The Moon and the planets stay near this path.
- e.g. Orion, with its three belt stars and the red star Ardra, is a winter evening group in India. It is high at midnight in December.
- e.g. Scorpius, with the red star Jyeshtha, is highest at midnight around June and is a summer evening group.
- e.g. The full Moon is always opposite the Sun, so it sits in the group of stars that is highest at midnight that month.
### Parallax: measuring distance with two viewpoints · *defined*
**Definition:** Parallax is the shift in position of a near thing against a far background when you look from two places. The nearer the thing, the larger the shift. If you know the baseline and measure the shift, you can find the distance.

Hold a pencil at arm's length and close one eye, then the other. The pencil seems to jump against the wall. Now hold it closer to your nose and the jump is bigger. If you measure the jump, you can work out how far away the pencil is. Astronomers do the same with stars, but they need a baseline much wider than the gap between eyes. They look at a star in January and again in July, when the Earth is on the opposite side of its orbit, 300 million kilometres away. A near star shifts a tiny bit against the far stars behind it. The Gaia spacecraft has measured the parallax of well over a billion stars. The shift is so small that no one measured it until 1838. Even the nearest star's shift is only about the width of a coin seen from 6 or 7 kilometres away.
- e.g. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star after the Sun, is about 4.2 light-years away, and its parallax is about 0.77 arc-seconds, a tiny fraction of a degree.
- e.g. A surveyor measuring the width of a river without crossing it uses the same method, with a baseline along the bank.
- e.g. For more distant stars, astronomers use other methods, such as the pulsing of Cepheid stars, which were found to be a yardstick by Henrietta Leavitt.
### How bright a star looks and how bright it really is · *defined*
**Definition:** A star looks bright because it is near, because it is truly powerful, or both. Astronomers call the first apparent brightness and the second true brightness, or luminosity.

A candle two metres away can look brighter than a bright lamp across a road. Stars are the same. Sirius is the brightest star in our night sky, mostly because it is near, only about 8.6 light-years away. It gives out about 25 times as much light as the Sun. Canopus, called Agastya in India, is the second brightest and is about 310 light-years away. It must be truly powerful, about ten thousand times brighter than the Sun. Rigel in Orion is further still and gives out tens of thousands of times as much light as the Sun. To find true brightness, you need the distance first. In 1912, Henrietta Leavitt, an American astronomer working at Harvard, found that a type of pulsing star, the Cepheid, pulses more slowly if it is truly brighter. This allowed astronomers to find distances to stars too far for parallax. The Pole Star is a Cepheid.
- e.g. Alpha Centauri, the nearest bright star, is about 4.4 light-years away and is only a little more powerful than the Sun, yet it looks very bright because it is so close.
- e.g. If the Sun were as far away as Canopus, it would be far too faint to see without a telescope.
- e.g. Magnitudes are such that a difference of 5 steps means a star is 100 times as bright.
### A star's colour shows its temperature · *defined*
**Definition:** The hotter a star's surface, the bluer its light. A red star is cooler than a yellow one, and a blue-white star is hotter still.

A piece of iron in a fire first glows dull red, then orange, then yellow, and a white-blue colour if it gets very hot. A star glows in the same way. A red star such as Antares, which Indian astronomers call Jyeshtha, has a surface of about 3,500 degrees Celsius. The Sun is a yellow-white star at about 5,500. Sirius and Rigel are blue-white, at 10,000 degrees or more. Fire shows a similar thing: the blue part of a gas flame is hotter than the yellow flame of a diya. Around 1900, Annie Jump Cannon at Harvard sorted the light of hundreds of thousands of stars by colour into a sequence of types, from the hottest to the coolest. In 1925, Cecilia Payne showed from this work that stars are made mainly of hydrogen and helium.
- e.g. Betelgeuse, called Ardra, is a red star in Orion. Rigel, in the opposite corner of Orion, is blue-white. You can see the difference on a clear night.
- e.g. The thin wire in an old bulb glows yellow-white, and it is hotter than the coil of an electric heater, which glows red.
- e.g. Red stars are usually cooler than the Sun. A red star can still be huge, as Betelgeuse is.
### Planets and stars: who wanders, and who twinkles · *defined*
**Definition:** The planets are called wanderers because they move slowly against the stars. Stars twinkle because their light is bent by the moving air. Planets look like tiny discs, so they usually twinkle much less.

Starlight is a single tiny point when it reaches the top of our air. On the way down it passes through patches of air of slightly different temperature, which bend it one way and another. The point seems to flicker. A planet is a disc that is still very small to the eye, but its light comes from many points on the disc. Their flickers mostly average out, so the planet shines steadily. Over days and months, planets wander along the ecliptic against the stars. Venus is seen only near sunrise or sunset. Jupiter and Saturn move slowly. Mars is slightly red. A bright, steady dot is probably a planet. Stars near the horizon twinkle more than overhead stars, because their light crosses more air.
- e.g. Venus looks like a very bright, steady star in the west after sunset, or in the east before sunrise.
- e.g. A star low in the sky flashes red, green and blue as the air bends its colours differently.
- e.g. Mars is called Mangal in many Indian languages and Sevvai in Tamil, and its reddish colour is visible by eye.
### The Pole Star will not always be the Pole Star · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's axis slowly wobbles like a spinning top, tracing a circle in the sky once in about 26,000 years. So the star nearest the north pole changes over time. This wobble is called precession.

Because of precession, the north pole points to different stars at different times. About 4,700 years ago, a star called Thuban was the pole star, around the time the great pyramids of Egypt were built. In about 12,000 years, the bright star Vega will be near the pole. Precession also moves the Sun's place among the stars. The Sun enters the constellation of Capricorn, called Makara, later than it did long ago. In the Indian star-based calendar, the Sun enters Makara around 14 January, which is the festival of Makar Sankranti or Pongal. That date was close to 21 December about 1,600 years ago, and it keeps moving later by about one day every 70 years. The star signs of western astrology, based on the seasons, are now about 24 degrees, or about 24 days, ahead of the Indian star-based signs.
- e.g. Polaris will be at its closest to the pole around the year 2100, and then slowly drift away.
- e.g. In ancient Egypt, Thuban was the star around which the northern sky turned.
- e.g. Pongal on 14 January and the winter solstice on 21 December were once the same day and are now about 24 days apart.
### How did the people of the Pacific find tiny islands? · *debated*
Long before there were maps, compasses or ships with instruments, people crossed thousands of kilometres of open Pacific Ocean and settled on tiny islands. How they found those islands has been argued about for a hundred years. We know that traditional navigators carry a star compass in their heads: they remember the points on the horizon where dozens of stars rise and set. They also watch the swell of the sea, the flight of birds and the shape of clouds over an island. In 1976, a double-hulled canoe called Hokulea sailed from Hawaii to Tahiti, about 4,000 kilometres, guided by Mau Piailug, a navigator from the island of Satawal, without any instrument. But many scientists still argue about how settlers first crossed the ocean thousands of years ago.
- e.g. Stars that rise at a particular point on the horizon rise at the same point every night, so a navigator can steer by a star close to the horizon and then change to the next one as it climbs.
- e.g. A navigator can find latitude by noting which stars pass directly overhead. The star Arcturus, called Hokule'a by Hawaiians, passes over Hawaii.
- e.g. Birds that go out to sea to fish in the day and return at night point towards land.
- **Andrew Sharp, a New Zealand historian, in 1956:** Early voyages were mostly by accident. Canoes were blown off course by storms and wound up on islands by luck. Navigators could not have steered such long distances without instruments.
- **David Lewis, Ben Finney and the crew of Hokulea, from the 1960s and 1970s:** The Pacific peoples crossed on purpose. The 1976 voyage proved that a trained navigator can steer across thousands of kilometres using only stars, swells and birds, so it could have been done long ago.

## Mostly agreed
- The Earth spins once in about 24 hours, and that is why the stars, the Sun and the Moon seem to cross the sky each day. *How we know:* Long photographs of the night sky show every star as an arc of the same angle. A Foucault pendulum swings in a line that turns slowly through the day, which shows that the floor under it is turning.
- The Pole Star's height above the horizon equals the observer's latitude, to within about one degree. *How we know:* Sailors and surveyors have measured it for centuries, and it matches latitude from maps and from satellite positioning. It works because the star is so far away that its light reaches the whole Earth along the same direction.
- Different stars are up at night in different seasons because the Earth goes round the Sun. *How we know:* A given star rises about 4 minutes earlier every night, which adds up to a full circuit in a year. This is exactly what is expected if the Earth moves round the Sun once in a year.
- The distances to the nearest stars can be measured by parallax, and they are enormous. *How we know:* Friedrich Bessel first measured the parallax of a star in 1838. Today the Gaia spacecraft has measured the parallax of well over a billion stars, and its distances agree with older methods.
- A star's colour shows how hot its surface is: red stars are cooler and blue-white stars are hotter. *How we know:* In the early 1900s, Annie Jump Cannon at Harvard sorted the light of hundreds of thousands of stars into a sequence by colour. Physicists then showed that the sequence follows the temperature of the stars' surfaces.
- Henrietta Leavitt's discovery of the Cepheid stars gave astronomers a way to measure distances far beyond the reach of parallax. *How we know:* In 1912 she found that Cepheids which pulse more slowly are truly brighter. The rule has been checked on Cepheids whose distances are known from parallax.
- Stars twinkle because of the Earth's moving air, not because the stars themselves flicker. *How we know:* Stars photographed from space telescopes shine steadily. Stars seen low on the horizon, through more air, twinkle the most.

## How we found out
**Friedrich Bessel, a German astronomer, answering an argument made by Tycho Brahe, 1838.** For about 2,000 years, careful thinkers said that the Earth could not be moving. Their reason was a good one. If the Earth went round the Sun, they said, the nearer stars should seem to shift against the farther ones during the year, just as your thumb jumps when you swap eyes. Nobody could see any shift. In the 1500s the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe, who measured the stars more exactly than anyone before the telescope, used this as strong evidence that the Earth stands still. The followers of Copernicus replied that the stars must be too far away for the shift to show. It sounded like an excuse. Then in 1838 a German astronomer, Friedrich Bessel, watched a faint star called 61 Cygni for more than a year with a very precise instrument. He found a shift smaller than the width of a coin seen from about 16 kilometres away. It showed that the Earth moves, and that the star was about 11 light-years away.  
*What people believed before:* If the Earth moved, the nearer stars would seem to shift during the year. No shift had been seen, so the Earth must stand still. The stars are in fact far more distant than anyone had guessed, so the shift is too small to see by eye, and was found only in 1838.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The stars are fixed to a great sphere that turns round the Earth once a day.** (Many ancient Greek and Indian astronomers, and most people for about 2,000 years; *rejected*)  
  For: All the stars keep their places relative to each other as they cross the sky. A single turning sphere is a simple way to picture it.  
  Against: Parallax shows that the stars are at hugely different distances, so they are not on one shell. Spacecraft like Gaia have mapped the stars in three dimensions.
- **If the Earth went round the Sun, the stars would seem to shift during the year. Nobody sees a shift, so the Earth must be standing still.** (Tycho Brahe, a Danish astronomer of the 1500s, and many careful scientists before him; *rejected*)  
  For: The argument is sound. If the Earth did move, a near star would show parallax against the far ones, just as your thumb jumps when you swap eyes. Tycho measured stars more exactly than anyone in his time and saw no shift.  
  Against: The stars are much farther away than Tycho could imagine, so the shift is far smaller than his instruments could detect. In 1838 Friedrich Bessel measured it.
- **The Pole Star is the brightest star in the sky.** (A very common idea, even among adults; *rejected*)  
  For: It is famous, it is always in the same place, and it is used to find north, so it seems it must be special.  
  Against: The Pole Star is about as bright as the stars of the Saptarishi. Dozens of stars look brighter, including Sirius, which is about 20 times brighter to the eye.
- **The stars that look the brightest are the nearest ones.** (A natural guess, and one most children make; *rejected*)  
  For: A lamp looks brighter when it is closer. Some of the brightest stars, such as Sirius, are indeed among the nearest.  
  Against: Canopus looks bright but is about 310 light-years away, while many stars much nearer to us are too faint to see. How bright a star looks depends on both its distance and how much light it gives out.
- **The paintings in the cave of Lascaux, in France, about 17,000 years old, show the Pleiades and the stars of Taurus.** (Some archaeologists and astronomers, including Michael Rappenglueck, since the 1990s; *debated*)  
  For: A cluster of dots on the shoulder of a painted bull looks like the Pleiades in the right place. If true, people were mapping the sky a very long time ago.  
  Against: Dots appear all over the cave walls, and it is easy to see patterns in them. There is no way to check what the painters meant.

## Roots
We have measured how the sky turns, how high the Pole Star stands, and how the jump of a thumb tells distance. Now we ask what these tell us about seeing, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the thumb test, your two eyes saw the pencil in two places. The size of that gap told you how far away the pencil was. So a difference between two viewpoints is not an error to remove. It is how distance is measured, and astronomers use the Earth's yearly path as their pair of eyes.  
*Habit it breaks:* We often think that when two people see the same thing differently, one must be wrong, and that the true view is the one from nowhere. But the difference between views can carry information that no single view has.  
*Evidence:* In the thumb test, the jump was large when the pencil was near and small when it was far, and a hidden pencil's distance could be read from its jump. In 1838, Bessel measured a star's tiny shift between two places in the Earth's orbit, and found it was about 11 light-years away.

- *who am i:* You have two eyes a few centimetres apart. That small gap lets you tell how far away a cup is when you reach for it. You see depth because your two views are different.
- *what is life:* A cat measuring a jump to a wall, a hawk diving at a mouse, and a child catching a ball all use two slightly different views of the same thing. For many animals, living well depends on comparing viewpoints.
- *what is this world:* We can measure the distance to a star because the Earth is not still. The planet we stand on is a moving pair of eyes, and the whole sky has depth because of it.

Two people can look at the same pencil and see it in different places. Two eyes do the same thing all the time. Instead of being confused, your brain uses the gap to make a picture with depth. Astronomers do the same on a vast scale. They look at a star in January and again in July from opposite ends of the Earth's orbit, and the small shift tells them how far away the star is. If the Earth stood still, or if we saw from only one place, the sky would be flat. The universe has depth for us because we see it from more than one place. A view from one point is never the whole story, and several views together can show something that none shows alone.

- **Rig Veda 1.164.46:** Truth is one. The wise speak of it in many ways. (Two eyes, or two points in the Earth's orbit, see one star in slightly different places. Putting the views together gives the one distance.)
- **Udana 6.4 (the story of the blind men and the elephant):** Blind men each touched a different part of an elephant. One said it was like a pot, one like a fan, one like a plough. They began to quarrel, each sure that the others were wrong. (Each view was true of one part. The thumb test shows the gap between views is useful, and the whole picture is built from several views.)
- **Tirukkural 423:** Whatever the thing, whoever says it, to see the truth in it is wisdom. (In the thumb test, the second eye does not contradict the first. It adds what the first could not show.)
- Science (settled): Parallax gives the distance to nearby stars, and it has been measured for well over a billion stars by the Gaia spacecraft.
- Science (debated): Polynesian navigators could cross the open Pacific on purpose using stars, swells and birds. How the first settlers did it is argued about.
- Science (open): Whether the Lascaux paintings show the Pleiades is not known.
- Other view, Many astronomers: Parallax is simply geometry, and it contains no deeper lesson about viewpoints. A star has a distance whether anyone measures it or not. The difference between views is a tool we use because we are stuck in one place, and the best aim is a view from no particular place at all.
- Practice, One thumb, two eyes (3 min): Hold your thumb out at arm's length. Close your left eye and look at your thumb against something far away. Now close your right eye and open your left. Watch your thumb jump. Breathe in slowly three times. Notice that you see the world in depth because your two eyes disagree a little.
- Ask: When two people see the same thing differently, is one of them always wrong?
- Ask: If you could see the sky from a planet round another star, what would you learn that you cannot learn from here?
- Ask: Is a view from nowhere possible, or is every view from somewhere?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your two eyes see a pencil in two slightly different places, and the gap tells you how far away it is. Is a disagreement between two views a mistake, or is it a tool? (hint: Think of the thumb jumping. If both eyes saw exactly the same, could you tell what is near and what is far?)
- *deep:* You stand on a spinning planet but you feel perfectly still. How can you tell it is the Earth that turns, and not the sky? (hint: Think of a train at a station that seems to move when the train beside it starts. What would you look for to decide?)
- *big:* The Pole Star's height tells you how far north you are. What else around you tells you where you are, even without a map? (hint: Think of the Sun at noon, the length of a day, which plants grow nearby, or which way the rain comes from.)
- *big:* People crossed the Pacific, with no instruments, by watching stars, swells and birds. What does that tell us about how much a person can learn by watching carefully? (hint: A navigator can hold hundreds of star paths in memory. How might they have learned them, and how do you pass such knowledge on?)
- *deep:* A star looks bright. How can you tell whether it is bright because it is near or because it is truly powerful? (hint: You would need its distance first. How might you find that, and what does that tell you about how knowledge is built up?)
- *big:* The Pole Star will not stay the Pole Star. What other things that seem permanent are only changing too slowly for us to notice? (hint: Think of a mountain, a river's course, or the Moon's distance. How slowly can something change and still be real?)
- *deep:* The planets are called wanderers because they seem to wander among the stars. Which is really wandering, the planet or the Earth we watch from? (hint: Think about the train that seemed to move at the station. What does your own motion add to what you see?)
- *big:* Most children in big cities never see the Milky Way. What do you think is lost when the night sky is hidden by lights? (hint: Think about what the sky gave people: time, direction, stories, and the feeling of being very small. Can anything replace that?)

## Story: The Star That Climbed
*Meenakshi, Ammamma, Mr Das and Mrs Jayanthi, the train journey and the halt in the dark are made up. The science is real. The height of the Pole Star above the northern horizon equals the observer's latitude, and a fist at arm's length covers about 10 degrees. The kamal is a real navigating tool used by sailors in the Indian Ocean. Mrs Jayanthi's grandfather, who used one, is made up.*

Meenakshi was nine, and she had never been further north than Madurai. Now she and her grandmother, Ammamma, were going all the way to Delhi on the long train, for her cousin Leela's wedding. It would take two nights and almost three days.

They climbed aboard at Kanyakumari in the evening. On the platform, Ammamma pointed over the roofs to the north. 'There, very low, just above the trees. That is Dhruva, the Pole Star. In the old story, Dhruva was a boy who asked for a place in the sky that would never move.'

'It never moves,' agreed Meenakshi. 'Our teacher says all the other stars go round it.' 'Mm,' said Ammamma. 'But keep watching it on this journey. You may see it climb.' Meenakshi laughed. 'It cannot climb if it never moves!'

She had her notebook and her school atlas in her bag. In class she had learned that a fist held out at arm's length covers about ten degrees of sky. At a halt that night she stood at the door of the train and held her fist up against the star. It sat on top of her fist, no higher. She wrote: Kanyakumari, less than one fist.

The next day the train rolled north through Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh. Meenakshi played cards with the man in the seat opposite, Mr Das, a retired schoolteacher from Kolkata, who was going to Delhi for an important meeting. That evening, near Hyderabad, she checked the star again. Nearly two fists. She wrote it down.

On the second night, the train stopped in the dark. It did not stop at a station. There were no lights outside, only the sound of crickets and the long hum of the engine waiting. One hour passed, and then another. The lights in the compartment flickered and went out.

'Where are we?' said Mr Das, getting more and more worried. 'I have to reach Delhi by morning. Nobody says anything. We could be anywhere.' Ammamma shrugged. 'We are on a train, in India, in the dark. That is all I know.'

Meenakshi had an idea. She slid open the door a little and looked north. The Pole Star was clearly higher than it had been. She held her fist against it, then another fist on top, and then a little more. 'Two fists and a bit,' she whispered. 'About twenty-three degrees.'

She fetched her atlas and found the lines of latitude. The line for twenty-three degrees passed through the middle of India. 'Ammamma,' she said, 'I think we are near Bhopal, or just before it.' Mr Das laughed, not unkindly. 'A girl cannot find a train with a star.'

Just then, a torch beam came down the corridor. It was Mrs Jayanthi, the ticket examiner. 'Madam,' said Meenakshi, 'are we close to Itarsi?' Mrs Jayanthi stopped. 'Who told you that?' 'The Pole Star did,' said Meenakshi. 'It is two fists and a bit high. My atlas says that is the latitude of Itarsi or Bhopal.'

Mrs Jayanthi looked at her for a long moment. 'About twenty kilometres before Itarsi,' she said. 'We are waiting for a goods train to clear the line.' She smiled. 'My grandfather sailed dhows out of Mandvi in Gujarat. He used a kamal: a little wooden card on a knotted string. He held the string in his teeth, put the card at arm's length, and lined its edge up with the Pole Star. The knot told him the latitude. He never needed a map.'

Mr Das stared out of the door into the dark. 'So the star told you where we were,' he said slowly, 'and it never moved at all.' 'Dhruva did not climb,' said Meenakshi. 'We did. Each degree we go north, the star is one degree higher. That is the same number as the latitude in my atlas.'

The train gave a jolt and began to move. By the time they reached Delhi the next morning, Meenakshi had filled a page: less than one fist, nearly two, two and a bit. She drew a little arrow climbing up the page. It was still climbing.

That night on the terrace of Leela's building, Dhruva stood high above the water tanks. 'Look,' said Meenakshi. She held up her fist three times, one on top of the other, and it reached almost to Dhruva. 'In Kanyakumari it was hardly above my fist.' Leela, who was getting married the next morning and could not sleep, looked up. 'So did the star move?' she asked. 'No,' said Meenakshi. 'We did.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: the Pole Star's height above the northern horizon equals the observer's latitude. A traveller going north therefore sees it climb, though the star has not moved. Meenakshi measures the height with her fist, which covers about ten degrees at arm's length, and uses her atlas to turn it into a place. What changes is the viewpoint, not the star. This is also the idea behind parallax: a change of viewpoint changes what we see in a way we can measure.

- Ask: Why did the Pole Star look higher each night as the train went north?
- Ask: How did Meenakshi find out roughly where the train had stopped?
- Ask: When Meenakshi said 'We did', what did she mean?
- Ask: Why would the Pole Star look even lower from the southern tip of India than it does from Chennai?

### e1 · The Jump of Your Thumb: can a pencil's jump tell you how far away it is? (35 min)
- **question:** When you close one eye and then the other, a pencil seems to jump against a far wall. How does the size of the jump change with how far the pencil is from your nose? Can you use the jump to find a hidden pencil's distance?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the pencil jump more when it is close to your nose, or when it is far away? And if you bring it twice as close, will the jump be the same, twice as big, or four times as big? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Stand on the same spot, 3 metres from the wall, every time. Use the same pencil, held upright, with its tip lined up with the same mark on the wall strip. Use the same two eyes, one at a time. Only the distance from your nose to the pencil changes. Measure each distance with a string, and do each one twice.
- **safety:** Hold the pencil with the sharp tip pointing up and away from faces. Clear the floor so that nobody trips while stepping back from the wall.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then draw a line graph with the distance across the bottom and the jump up the side. Write down how close your guess for the hidden pencil came.
- **results table:** Distance from my nose to the pencil (cm) | Number on the wall strip with my right eye only | Number on the wall strip with my left eye only | Jump between the two numbers (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the pencil and the wall. Ask them to close one eye, then the other, with the pencil at arm's length. Then ask for the guess and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the pencil against the wall. Close one eye, then the other. It seems to jump. If I bring the pencil twice as close, will it jump the same, twice as much, or four times as much? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Swap eyes. Does the pencil jump? Guess what happens when it is closer. Why?

  *Child mode:* Half the distance. Look at the jump. What happens?

### c1 · Measure the Unreachable: how far is a tree you cannot walk to? (40 min)

## Worksheets
### The Turning Sky and the Pole Star (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Why do the stars seem to cross the sky during a night?  
   Options: The Earth spins, so we turn past the stars / The stars all circle the Earth once a day / The Sun pushes the stars across the sky / The stars are on a dome that the Moon turns  
   **Answer:** The Earth spins, so we turn past the stars. *Why:* The Earth spins once in about a day, and we spin with it. So the sky seems to turn the other way, like scenery seen from a train. Picking 'the stars circle the Earth' is the picture people held for thousands of years. The photos of star trails and the pendulum show that the Earth is what turns.
2. (fill) The sky turns about ____ degrees every hour.  
   **Answer:** ['15']. *Why:* A full turn is 360 degrees, and it takes about 24 hours. 360 divided by 24 is 15. So every hour the stars move through 15 degrees, about one and a half fists at arm's length.
3. (fill) The sky turns 15 degrees every hour. A half circle is 180 degrees. So the sky turns a half circle in ____ hours.  
   **Answer:** ['12']. *Why:* 180 divided by 15 is 12. So a star that rises in the east will be in the west about 12 hours later. A strong answer shows the division.
4. (truefalse) The Pole Star is the brightest star in the night sky.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Pole Star is only about as bright as the stars of the Saptarishi. Many stars look brighter, including Sirius. The Pole Star is special because of where it is, almost exactly in line with the Earth's axis, and not because of how bright it is.
5. (mcq) Which two stars of the Saptarishi point towards the Pole Star?  
   Options: The two stars at the outer edge of the bowl, Kratu and Pulaha / The two stars at the end of the handle / The two middle stars of the handle / The brightest and the faintest star  
   **Answer:** The two stars at the outer edge of the bowl, Kratu and Pulaha. *Why:* Draw a line through the two stars on the outer edge of the bowl, the ones furthest from the handle. ### Light, Colour and Distance (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Canopus gives out about 400 times as much light as Sirius, yet Sirius looks brighter in the sky. What does this tell you?  
   Options: Sirius must be much nearer to us than Canopus / Sirius must be hotter than Canopus / Canopus must be moving away / The sky is darker near Sirius  
   **Answer:** Sirius must be much nearer to us than Canopus. *Why:* How bright a star looks depends on how much light it gives out and how far away it is. Canopus is truly far more powerful, but it is about 310 light-years away. Sirius is only about 8.6 light-years away, so it looks brighter.
2. (fill) To work out how much light a star really gives out, you must first know its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['distance']. *Why:* A star looks fainter the farther away it is. So you cannot tell how powerful it is from how bright it looks, until you know how far away it is. This is why parallax matters.
3. (truefalse) Henrietta Leavitt's discovery about pulsing stars helped astronomers measure distances too great for parallax.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 1912 she found that Cepheid stars that pulse more slowly are truly brighter. If you know a Cepheid's true brightness and see how bright it looks, you can work out its distance. A strong answer says that this method reaches far beyond parallax.
4. (mcq) Three stars look red, yellow and blue-white. Which has the hottest surface?  
   Options: The blue-white star / The red star / The yellow star / They are all equally hot  
   **Answer:** The blue-white star. *Why:* The hotter a star's surface, the bluer its light. A red star is cooler, a yellow star is in the middle, and a blue-white star is hottest. A piece of iron glows red first, then yellow, then white, as it gets hotter.
5. (match) Match each star to its colour and its surface temperature.  
   **Answer:** Antares (Jyeshtha) → red, about 3,500 degrees Celsius; The Sun → yellow-white, about 5,500 degrees Celsius; Sirius → blue-white, about 10,000 degrees Celsius. *Why:* Colour is a thermometer for stars. A strong answer says why the order of the colours matches the order of the temperatures.
6. (write) Betelgeuse is red and huge. A friend says red stars must be the hottest, because red is the colour of fire. What would you say?  
   **Answer:** In a star, red means cooler. Blue-white stars like Rigel are much hotter. A glowing piece of iron is red at first and turns yellow and then white-blue as it gets hotter. Betelgeuse is cool for a star, but it is so big that it still gives out a great deal of light.. *Why:* A strong answer separates temperature from size. A cool star can be huge and bright. The colour only tells the temperature of the surface.
7. (mcq) Two bright dots are in the evening sky. One flickers and flashes different colours. The other shines steadily. Which is more likely to be a planet?  
   Options: The steady one / The flickering one / Both are planets / Neither can be a planet  
   **Answer:** The steady one. *Why:* A planet is a tiny disc, so the wobble of the air averages out and its light looks steady. A star is a point, so the air flickers all of it at once. If you picked the flickering one, think of the foil with the tiny hole.
8. (truefalse) A star low near the horizon twinkles more than the same star when it is overhead.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Low in the sky, a star's light crosses much more air before it reaches your eye, so there is more bending and flickering. Overhead, it passes through the thinnest layer of air.
9. (write) Why do planets twinkle much less than stars?  
   **Answer:** A star is a single point of light, so moving air bends all of it at once and it flickers. A planet is a tiny disc made of many points, and the flickers of the different points cancel out, so its light is steadier.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the difference is in the shape of the light, a point or a tiny disc, and not in the object being near or far.

In Delhi, stars near the Pole Star never set. Picking the Saptarishi forgets that the Pole Star is on the horizon.

   **Answer:** The Earth's axis slowly wobbles, like a spinning top, and traces a circle in the sky once in about 26,000 years. So the axis points at different stars over time. Thuban was near the pole then, Polaris is now, and in about 12,000 years it will be Vega.. *Why:* A strong answer names the wobble and the very long time it takes. The change is so slow that nobody sees it in a lifetime.
7. (mcq) In about 12,000 years, a different star will be near the north celestial pole. What is the best reason?

   **Answer:** The Earth's axis slowly wobbles and points at different stars. *Why:* The Earth's axis traces a slow circle in the sky because of a wobble called precession. It makes a complete circle in about 26,000 years. Picking 'the stars move towards the pole' is wrong, because stars move only very slowly.

## Applications
- Finding latitude from the height of the Pole Star, as sailors and travellers did for centuries before satellites.
- Measuring distances to stars by parallax, which is the first step in measuring the size of the universe.
- Measuring distances far beyond parallax using Cepheid stars, following the work of Henrietta Leavitt.
- Surveying a country or a field by triangles, as the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India began to do in 1802.
- Pointing a telescope at a star, which has to follow the sky's steady turn of 15 degrees an hour.
- Keeping calendars and festival dates, such as Makar Sankranti, in step with the Sun's place among the stars.
- Finding a ship's or a plane's place by satellites, which use the same geometry as triangles and parallax.
- Judging distance with two eyes, as a cat does before a jump or a cricketer does before catching a high ball.
- Designing dark-sky parks and shielded street lights, so people can still see the stars from a town.
- Sorting stars by colour into types to learn their temperature, a method started by Annie Jump Cannon and her colleagues.

## Spotted around us
- The Saptarishi in the northern sky and the Pole Star beyond it, from a terrace or a balcony on a clear evening.
- Venus or Jupiter low over the rooftops, a steady bright dot that does not flicker, next to a flickering star.
- Orion on a winter evening, with red Ardra at one shoulder and blue-white Rigel at one foot.
- A long-exposure night photo on a phone, where the stars become circles round one point in the north.
- From a moving bus or train, the near posts flash past while the faraway hills barely move. This is parallax.
- The shimmer in the air above a hot tawa, a hot road or a chimney, which makes distant things wobble just as the air makes stars twinkle.
- On the full Moon of Karthigai, the Moon is near Krittika, the Pleiades, in Taurus. On other full Moons, it is among other groups of stars.
- The two eyes of a hunting cat or a hawk, both facing forwards, which let them judge distance by parallax.

## Open questions
- **How far away is the Pole Star, exactly?** Different measurements have given between about 330 and 450 light-years. Polaris is a pulsing star, and is too bright for some instruments, so astronomers are still working on a good answer.
- **How did the first people find tiny islands across the open Pacific?** A modern voyage shows it can be done by stars, swells and birds. No one can prove how it was done thousands of years ago.
- **Did Ice Age people already map the stars?** Some scholars think the dots in the cave paintings of Lascaux show the Pleiades. Others see only random dots, and there is no way to check what the painters meant.
- **Are there faint stars very near the Sun that we have not found yet?** A very faint, cool star-like object was found only in 2013, about 7 light-years away. There may be more such dim neighbours that we cannot see.
- **What will our descendants call the stars of the Saptarishi in 100,000 years?** The stars slowly drift, and the ladle will bend out of shape. No one knows whether the names will last.
- **Why is it that nearly all the stars we see are in our own galaxy?** Almost every star you can see by eye is within a few thousand light-years. Why we live in a spot with so many bright stars nearby is not something astronomers have a settled answer for.

## Connected maths
- The sky turns 15 degrees every hour. How many degrees does it turn in 6 hours? What fraction of a full circle is that? **90 degrees, which is a quarter of a full circle.**. 15 times 6 is 90. A full circle is 360 degrees, and 90 is one quarter of 360, because 4 times 90 is 360. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- A fist covers about 10 degrees. In the Turning Sky test, a star went from 3 fists to 1 and a half fists above a roof edge in one hour. How many degrees did it move? How many hours would a full circle of 360 degrees take at that rate? **15 degrees. A full circle takes 24 hours.**. The star moved 3 take away 1.5, which is 1.5 fists. 1.5 times 10 is 15 degrees. 360 divided by 15 is 24. So the full turn takes 24 hours, which is one day. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- Kanyakumari is at 8 degrees north and Delhi is at 29 degrees north. By how many degrees does the Pole Star climb on a journey from one to the other? One degree of latitude is about 111 kilometres. About how far north is Delhi? **21 degrees. About 2,300 kilometres.**. 29 take away 8 is 21 degrees. 21 times 111 is 2,331, which is about 2,300 kilometres. So the Pole Star climbs one degree for about every 111 kilometres you go north. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In the Jump of Your Thumb test, a pencil at 25 centimetres jumped 60 centimetres, at 50 centimetres it jumped 28, and at 100 centimetres it jumped 11. Multiply the distance by the jump for each. What do you notice? **About 1,500, 1,400 and 1,100. The three answers are fairly close to each other.**. 25 times 60 is 1,500. 50 times 28 is 1,400. 100 times 11 is 1,100. They are not exactly equal, but they are of a similar size. If the jump halves each time the distance doubles, the answer stays about the same. It drops a little because the wall is only 3 metres away. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A star rises 4 minutes earlier each day. How much earlier does it rise after 30 days? Give your answer in minutes and in hours. **120 minutes, which is 2 hours.**. 4 times 30 is 120 minutes. 120 divided by 60 is 2 hours. So after a month, a star rises 2 hours earlier. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- Sirius is 8.6 light-years away. One light-year is about 9.5 trillion kilometres. About how many trillion kilometres away is Sirius? **About 82 trillion kilometres.**. 8.6 times 9.5 is about 82, because 8 times 9.5 is 76 and 0.6 times 9.5 is 5.7, which makes 81.7. So Sirius is about 82 trillion kilometres away. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- A star looks one quarter as bright when it is twice as far away. How bright does it look at 3 times the distance? At 10 times the distance? **One ninth as bright at 3 times the distance. One hundredth as bright at 10 times the distance.**. The light spreads out over an area that grows as the distance times itself. 3 times 3 is 9, so one ninth. 10 times 10 is 100, so one hundredth. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- The Earth's axis traces a full circle of 360 degrees in the sky in about 26,000 years. About how many years does it take to move 1 degree? **About 72 years.**. 26,000 divided by 360 is about 72, because 72 times 360 is 25,920. So the pole moves 1 degree in about 72 years. This is also why Makar Sankranti slips by about one day every 72 years. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
