# Why you pant when you run: how blood and breath deliver oxygen to every cell
`sci.body.heart-blood-breath.L4`

**Summary.** Run up a flight of stairs and two things happen that you did not ask for. You start to pant, and your heart starts to thump. Nobody gave either of them an order. In this unit you count your pulse and your breaths in five situations, and find that they rise and fall together. Then you follow the oxygen from your nose, through very thin walls in the lungs, into red blood cells, and out to every cell in your body.

**Intro.** *Sit still and count how many times you breathe in one minute. Then count how many times your heart beats. Which number is bigger, and by how much?* Most people find that their heart beats about four times for every breath. Now jump on the spot for one minute and count both again. Both numbers go up, and both go up together. Why would a heart and a pair of lungs, so far apart, speed up at the same moment?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can find my pulse, count my breaths, and say what is a usual rate for a child at rest.
1. I can plan a fair test of what changes pulse and breathing rate, and show the results on a line graph.
2. I can explain that blood carries oxygen from the lungs and food from the gut to every cell, and carries carbon dioxide back.
3. I can name what blood is made of, and say what red blood cells and haemoglobin do.
4. I can explain why the heart has two sides, one sending blood to the lungs and one sending it to the body.
5. I can explain why lungs and capillaries are built so thin and so wide, using the idea of surface area.
6. I can explain why pulse and breathing rate rise together, and what tells the body to do it.
7. I can say how blood moves to where it is needed most, and why cold fingers go pale.

## Terms
- **Pulse**: The small push you can feel in an artery each time the heart beats. You feel it best on the inside of your wrist. *e.g.* Two fingers on your wrist feel about 85 pushes in one minute when you sit still.
- **Heart rate**: How many times your heart beats in one minute. It is the same number as your pulse rate. *e.g.* A child sitting still has a heart rate of about 70 to 110 beats per minute.
- **Breathing rate**: How many breaths you take in one minute. One breath is one time in and one time out. *e.g.* A child sitting still takes about 18 to 25 breaths in one minute.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air. Every cell in your body uses oxygen to get energy out of food. Air is about one fifth oxygen. *e.g.* The oxygen in your last breath came from the air, and some of it is already in your blood.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas that cells make when they use food for energy. Blood carries it back to the lungs and you breathe it out. *e.g.* Your breath turns limewater milky because it carries carbon dioxide.
- **Red blood cell**: A tiny, round, flat cell in your blood that carries oxygen. It is shaped like a doughnut with a dent instead of a hole. *e.g.* A speck of blood the size of a pinhead holds about 5 million red blood cells.
- **Haemoglobin**: A red protein inside red blood cells. It holds oxygen, and it has iron in it. It is the reason blood is red. *e.g.* One red blood cell holds about 270 million haemoglobin molecules.
- **Plasma**: The pale yellow liquid part of blood. It carries blood cells, food, salts and waste. *e.g.* A little more than half of your blood is plasma, and most of it is water.
- **Platelet**: A tiny piece of a cell that helps blood to clot. When you get a cut, platelets stick together and plug the hole. *e.g.* A scab starts with platelets gathering at a cut.
- **Artery**: A blood vessel that carries blood away from the heart. It has a thick, stretchy wall, and you can feel your pulse in one. *e.g.* The pulse in your wrist is a push of blood going along an artery.
- **Vein**: A blood vessel that carries blood back to the heart. It has a thinner wall and small flaps inside that stop blood from slipping backwards. *e.g.* The bluish lines on the back of your hand are veins.
- **Capillary**: The thinnest blood vessel. Its wall is only one cell thick, so oxygen and food can pass through it to the cells outside. *e.g.* Red blood cells squeeze through a capillary in a single line.
- **Alveolus**: A tiny air bag at the end of the airways in your lungs. The plural is alveoli. Oxygen passes through its wall into the blood. *e.g.* An adult has several hundred million alveoli, packed like bunches of grapes.
- **Gas exchange**: The swap in the lungs. Oxygen goes from the air into the blood, and carbon dioxide goes from the blood into the air. *e.g.* Your breath out has less oxygen and more carbon dioxide than the air you breathed in.
- **Double circulation**: Blood goes through the heart twice on one full trip round the body. The right side sends it to the lungs, and the left side sends it to the body. *e.g.* A drop of blood goes to the lungs, comes back to the heart, and then goes out to your toes.
- **Anaemia**: Having too few red blood cells, or too little haemoglobin. The body gets less oxygen, so a person feels tired and breathless. *e.g.* Not eating enough iron-rich food, such as ragi, dal and green leaves, is one cause of anaemia.

## Explanations
### Pulse and breathing rate: two numbers you can count · *defined*
**Definition:** Your pulse rate is the number of heartbeats in one minute. Your breathing rate is the number of breaths in one minute. Both change all day.

Press two fingers, not your thumb, on the inside of your wrist and you feel a small push again and again. Each push is one heartbeat travelling along an artery. Count the pushes for 15 seconds and multiply by 4, and you have your pulse rate. To count breaths, watch a chest or a belly rise and fall for 15 seconds, and multiply by 4. A child of 6 to 10 sitting still has a pulse rate of about 70 to 110 beats per minute and a breathing rate of about 18 to 25 breaths per minute. A grown-up at rest has a slower pulse and slower breathing. A newborn baby has a much faster pulse and breathing. Heart rate and breathing rate are not fixed numbers. They are answers that your body gives, moment by moment, to the question: how much is being asked of me right now?
- e.g. A child lying down has a lower pulse than the same child standing up.
- e.g. After 30 step-ups, a child's pulse can double, and then falls back over a few minutes.
- e.g. A sleeping person has one of the slowest pulses of the day.
### Why cells need oxygen: getting energy out of food · *defined*
**Definition:** Every cell uses oxygen to get energy out of food. The cell makes carbon dioxide and water as waste.

Food holds energy, but a cell cannot use it straight away. Inside the cell, tiny parts called mitochondria take sugar from your food and oxygen from the air. They use the two together to release energy, and they leave carbon dioxide and water behind. This is called cellular respiration. It is slow burning without a flame. Your muscles, your brain and your heart are all busy cells, so all of them need oxygen all the time. The brain is only a small part of your weight, but it uses about one fifth of your oxygen. The food comes from the gut, and the oxygen comes from the lungs. The blood is the delivery system that brings both to every cell, and takes the carbon dioxide away.
- e.g. A cell in a leg muscle needs more oxygen when you run, because it is releasing energy faster.
- e.g. Brain cells keep using oxygen even when you sit and think, or when you sleep.
- e.g. The carbon dioxide in your breath is made from the carbon in the food you ate.
### Gas exchange: why lungs are so thin and so wide · *defined*
**Definition:** Gas exchange is the swap in the lungs. Oxygen goes from the air into the blood, and carbon dioxide goes the other way.

Air travels down your windpipe and splits into narrower and narrower tubes, like the branches of a tree. At the end of each twig is a tiny air bag called an alveolus. An adult has several hundred million of them. Each alveolus is wrapped in a net of capillaries, and the wall between air and blood is only about half a thousandth of a millimetre thick. Oxygen is more crowded in the air in the alveolus than in the blood that arrives, so oxygen drifts into the blood. Carbon dioxide is more crowded in the blood, so it drifts out into the air. This drifting needs no push. It is called diffusion, and it works well only when the wall is very thin and the surface is very large. If you could spread out all the alveoli flat, the surface would be somewhere between 50 and 100 square metres in an adult. Scientists give a range because it is hard to measure, and a child's lungs are smaller. Air you breathe out still holds about 16 out of every 100 parts oxygen, which is why mouth-to-mouth rescue breathing works.
- e.g. Air in is about 21 parts oxygen in 100. Air out is about 16 parts oxygen and 4 parts carbon dioxide.
- e.g. A bunch of grapes on a stalk is a good picture of alveoli on an airway.
- e.g. A crushed sugar lump dissolves faster than a whole one, because more of its surface touches the water.
### Blood: a liquid full of living cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Blood is a mix of plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. It carries oxygen, food, waste, heat and messages round the body.

If you let a tube of blood settle, it separates into layers. A little more than half is plasma, a pale yellow liquid that is mostly water. Nearly all the rest is red blood cells. A thin layer in between holds white blood cells, which fight germs, and platelets, which plug cuts. A speck of blood the size of a pinhead holds about 5 million red blood cells. A red blood cell has no centre part, so there is more room inside for haemoglobin. Haemoglobin is a red protein with iron in it, and it is what grabs oxygen in the lungs and lets go of it where it is needed. Blood that is full of oxygen is bright red. Blood that has given up much of its oxygen is dark red, not blue. Red blood cells live for about four months. The red bone marrow inside your bones makes about 2 million new ones every second in an adult.
- e.g. Plasma carries food and carbon dioxide, and the red cells carry most of the oxygen.
- e.g. Iron-rich food, such as ragi, dal, beetroot and green leaves, gives the body iron to build haemoglobin.
- e.g. Your bone marrow is making new blood cells right now.
### The heart's two sides: a double loop · *defined*
**Definition:** The heart is a muscle pump with four rooms. The right side sends blood to the lungs, and the left side sends blood to the rest of the body.

Your heart has two upper rooms called atria and two lower rooms called ventricles. The right side receives dark red blood that has given up its oxygen. It pumps this blood a short way to the lungs, where it picks up oxygen. The blood then comes back to the left side of the heart. The left side is the strong side, with a thicker wall, because it pushes blood all the way round the body, even to your toes. After the trip, the blood returns to the right side to start again. So the blood goes through the heart twice on one full round. Flaps called valves open and shut, and make the sound of a heartbeat, which is often written as lub-dub. One full round of the body takes about a minute at rest. The heart is about the size of your fist, and it sits in the middle of the chest, tilted a little to the left.
- e.g. Blood that is going to the lungs is low in oxygen. Blood that is coming back from the lungs is full of oxygen.
- e.g. A fish has a heart with only two rooms and one loop. A bird has four rooms and two loops, like us.
- e.g. The thick wall of the left side is why a heartbeat is felt most on the left of the chest.
### Arteries, capillaries and veins: three kinds of road · *defined*
**Definition:** Arteries carry blood away from the heart, capillaries are thin vessels where cells receive oxygen, and veins carry blood back to the heart.

Arteries have thick, stretchy walls, because blood arrives in strong pushes. That is why you can feel a pulse in an artery. The arteries divide again and again into smaller and smaller tubes. The smallest are capillaries, so thin that red blood cells squeeze through in single file. A capillary wall is only one cell thick, so oxygen and food pass through into the cells around it, and carbon dioxide passes in. Almost every cell in your body is close to a capillary. Then the capillaries join up into veins. Veins have thinner walls, and small flaps inside that act like one-way doors, so blood cannot slip backwards when you are standing. When you walk, the muscles in your legs squeeze the veins and help to push blood up. The total length of all your blood vessels is often said to be about 100,000 kilometres, but that figure is an estimate.
- e.g. A paper cut that bleeds a little has opened a few capillaries.
- e.g. The bluish lines on the back of your hand are veins just under the skin.
- e.g. Standing still for a long time can make some people feel dizzy, because the blood pools in the legs.
### How your body sets the pace: carbon dioxide is the signal · *defined*
**Definition:** Your breathing rate is set mostly by how much carbon dioxide is in your blood. Your heart rate follows nerve signals and chemical messengers.

You might think you breathe faster because you are running out of oxygen. In fact, the signal is mostly carbon dioxide. Busy muscles make more carbon dioxide, and it makes the blood slightly more acidic. Sensors in a part of the brain called the brainstem, and in large arteries in the neck, notice this change within seconds. They tell the breathing muscles to work harder. Your heart also gets the message. Nerve signals and a chemical messenger called adrenaline make the heart beat faster and push harder. So pulse and breathing rate rise together, because they answer the same call from the cells. When the cells quieten down, the carbon dioxide level falls, and both slow down again. This is why you cannot hold your breath for long. The urge to breathe grows as carbon dioxide builds up, long before oxygen runs low. Fit people have a lower resting pulse, because their heart pushes out more blood with each beat and does not need to beat as often.
- e.g. After a sprint, you keep panting for a while, because there is still extra carbon dioxide to clear.
- e.g. A person who has run for years often has a resting pulse below 60 beats per minute.
- e.g. Fear makes your heart race, because adrenaline reaches it even when you are not moving.
### Blood goes where it is needed: cold, warmth and posture · *defined*
**Definition:** The body sends more blood to the places that need it and less to the places that do not. It can narrow some blood vessels and widen others.

Tiny muscles in the walls of small arteries can squeeze them narrow or let them open wide. In the cold, the vessels near the skin narrow. Less blood reaches your fingers, so they turn pale and feel cold, and more blood stays near the heart and the other organs that must stay warm. In the heat, the vessels near the skin widen. More blood reaches the skin, your cheeks turn red, and heat escapes into the air. After a meal, more blood goes to the gut. During a run, more goes to the leg muscles. Gravity matters too. When you stand up, some blood sinks towards the legs, and the heart speeds up for a moment to keep enough blood going to the brain. This is why a pulse is a little higher standing than lying down. You can test how quickly blood returns to a fingertip. Press the nail until it turns white and let go. In a warm hand, the pink colour comes back in about one or two seconds.
- e.g. Pale, cold fingers on a winter morning in Shimla or Leh are the body protecting its middle.
- e.g. Red cheeks after a game of kabaddi are widened vessels letting heat out.
- e.g. A person who stands up too fast may see spots, because the blood has not caught up yet.
### Thin air, thick blood: living high up · *defined*
**Definition:** Higher up a mountain, each breath has less oxygen. Over weeks, the body makes more red blood cells and adjusts its breathing to cope.

At sea level, air pressure is highest, and each breath is packed with oxygen. In Leh, at about 3,500 metres, the air pressure is only about two thirds as high, so each breath holds about two thirds as much oxygen. A visitor from Chennai or Madurai breathes faster, has a faster pulse and feels tired. After a few days, the kidneys release a messenger called erythropoietin, and the bone marrow makes more red blood cells. This takes weeks to finish, so mountaineers go up slowly. People whose families have lived in the Himalayas for thousands of years have changes in their genes that help them cope with thin air. Scientists found that one such gene change in Tibetans looks like one passed down from an extinct group of humans, the Denisovans. Some visitors get altitude sickness, with headache and sickness, and the cure is to rest or go lower. Anyone who feels very ill at height needs a doctor.
- e.g. Mountaineers spend days at a camp partway up before they go higher.
- e.g. Some athletes train at high places to raise their red blood cell count.
- e.g. People with anaemia feel breathless sooner, because they have fewer red blood cells to carry oxygen.
### Other bodies, other ways of moving oxygen · *debated*
**Definition:** Different animals move oxygen in very different ways. Some have a heart and closed vessels like ours. Some have none.

An insect has no vessels that carry oxygen. Air goes directly into the body through tiny holes and small tubes, and the insect's blood only carries food and wastes. A fish has a heart with two rooms and sends blood in one loop through the gills. An octopus has three hearts and blood that is blue-green, because it carries oxygen with copper instead of iron. An earthworm has five pairs of muscular rings that squeeze blood along. A plant has no heart, but sap moves up from the roots and sugar moves down from the leaves. Whether any of these are better than ours is not a sensible question, because each fits the body that has it. A harder question is whether the number of heartbeats in a life is limited. Small animals such as mice beat their hearts about 600 times a minute and live a few years. Large animals such as elephants beat about 30 times a minute and live decades.
- e.g. A mouse heart beats about 600 times a minute, an elephant heart about 30.
- e.g. A blue whale's heart can slow to only a few beats per minute during a deep dive.
- e.g. A hummingbird's heart can beat well over 1,000 times a minute in flight.
- **Some biologists:** In many mammals, the total number of heartbeats in a life is roughly similar, at around a billion, because small animals beat fast and live short lives. This suggests that something limits how many beats a heart can make.
- **Other biologists:** Humans live far longer than the pattern predicts, with about 2 to 3 billion beats in a life, and many animals break the pattern. So the number of beats is probably a result of body size and energy use, not a fixed count.

## Mostly agreed
- Blood is pumped round the body in a closed loop, and goes through the heart twice on each full trip. *How we know:* Harvey showed in 1628 that blood circulates. Malpighi saw capillaries in 1661. Modern scans show blood moving through the four heart rooms in order.
- Every cell needs oxygen to release energy from food, and blood carries it there. *How we know:* Lavoisier measured oxygen used up by breathing animals in the 1780s. Modern instruments measure oxygen in blood, and cells without oxygen soon stop working.
- Haemoglobin in red blood cells carries most of the oxygen in blood. *How we know:* Max Perutz worked out the shape of haemoglobin with X-rays around 1960 and saw where oxygen is held. Blood without red blood cells carries far less oxygen.
- The air we breathe out has less oxygen and more carbon dioxide than the air we breathe in. *How we know:* Gas sensors measure about 21 parts oxygen in 100 in fresh air and about 16 in breathed-out air, with about 4 parts carbon dioxide.
- Breathing rate is controlled mainly by the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. *How we know:* People who breathe air with extra carbon dioxide breathe faster, while people who breathe air with less oxygen but normal carbon dioxide change much less at first.
- The body sends more blood to the parts that are busy and less to the parts that are not. *How we know:* Scans show blood flow rising in working muscles and in the gut after a meal, and falling in the skin of cold fingers.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, Joseph Priestley, Carl Scheele, Antoine Lavoisier, Pierre Laplace, Armand Seguin and Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, Aristotle about 350 BCE, the discovery of oxygen in the 1770s, the ice experiment about 1783, and the human breathing tests in 1789 and 1790.** For about two thousand years, many people followed Aristotle, who taught that the heart is the hottest part of the body and that we breathe to cool it, like fanning a hot pot. In the 1770s, Joseph Priestley and Carl Scheele each found a new air that made flames burn bright. Antoine Lavoisier, in France, named it oxygen. Around 1783, he and Pierre Laplace put a guinea pig in a chamber packed with ice. The animal's warmth melted the ice, and they measured the carbon dioxide it breathed out. The heat for each bit of carbon dioxide was close to that of burning charcoal. So breathing was not cooling. It was a slow, gentle burning of food. In 1789 and 1790, Lavoisier tested a young man, Armand Seguin, breathing through a mask at rest and while lifting weights. Seguin used far more oxygen when he worked. Lavoisier's wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, took notes and drew the apparatus.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle taught that breathing cools the heart, and later thinkers said that air only carried away hot vapours. Lavoisier's ice and oxygen measurements showed that breathing is slow burning of food, which makes heat and carbon dioxide, and that working harder uses more oxygen.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Blood in your veins is blue until it meets air.** (Many people, and some school books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Veins look blue under the skin, and diagrams colour them blue.  
  Against: Blood is always some shade of red. Dark blood with less oxygen is dark red. Veins look blue because of how skin and light work together. Blood drawn from a vein in a needle is clearly dark red.
- **We breathe out only carbon dioxide, and no oxygen.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen, so people often think we do the reverse, completely.  
  Against: Breathed-out air is about 16 parts oxygen and 4 parts carbon dioxide in 100. That is why a person can give rescue breaths to another person.
- **We breathe faster during exercise because we are running out of oxygen.** (Many people, and some older books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Oxygen is used faster during exercise, and tired people feel breathless.  
  Against: The main signal is the carbon dioxide level in the blood, and the pH change it causes. Breathing rate rises before oxygen has fallen much.
- **The pulse can tell a skilled reader about many diseases, as nadi pariksha says.** (Practitioners of Ayurveda, and similar traditions in other countries.; *debated*)  
  For: Doctors in many traditions have read the pulse for thousands of years. Modern doctors read its rate, rhythm and strength, and find real clues in them.  
  Against: Careful tests have not yet shown that pulse reading can tell the many kinds of illness that some traditions describe. Researchers continue to study it.
- **The heart is where we think and feel, and the brain only helps.** (Aristotle, and many cultures before and since.; *rejected*)  
  For: A frightened or happy person feels the heart race or swell. The heart also has its own network of nerve cells.  
  Against: Damage to the brain changes thinking, memory and feeling. A heart can be replaced by a transplant or a machine, and the person stays the same person.

## Roots
We have counted our pulse and breath, followed oxygen to the cells, and seen how blood goes where it is needed. Now we ask what this tells us about who is in charge of a body, and what we are made of.

**Deep truth.** Your heart is not the boss of your body. It speeds up because your cells ask for more oxygen, and your lungs speed up for the same reason. Pulse, breath and blood together make one answer to millions of small requests.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture the heart as an engine or a boss that sets the pace, and the lungs as a separate pair of bellows. This splits one job into separate machines. In the test, the pulse and the breathing rose and fell together, because both were answering the same call from the cells.  
*Evidence:* In the Pulse Detective test, the pulse and the breathing rate both rose after the step-ups and both came back down together over about four minutes. Muscle cells were using more oxygen and making more carbon dioxide, and carbon dioxide in the blood is the signal that speeds up both.

- *who am i:* You are not one thing in charge of the rest. You are trillions of cells, each asking for what it needs, with a heart and lungs that answer.
- *what is life:* Life is a constant exchange. Oxygen comes in, carbon dioxide goes out, and the flow does not stop for even a minute.
- *what is this world:* The oxygen in your blood was made by plants, algae and microbes. The iron in your blood was made inside stars, long before the Sun.

A few weeks after you began, a tiny tube of heart cells started to beat, long before there was a brain to give orders. A heart cell can even beat alone in a dish, and cells placed together soon beat in time. In your body, trillions of cells ask for oxygen and send out carbon dioxide, and one heartbeat answers all of them. The heart, the lungs and the blood are not three machines. They are one process that shows up as many parts, the way the One shows up as the many.

- **Prashna Upanishad, question 2, verses 2 to 4:** The powers of the body, such as speech, mind, sight and hearing, each said that it was the chief one. Prana, the breath of life, began to leave, and all the others began to leave with it, like bees following a queen. They saw that they all depended on the breath. (The Upanishad picks the breath as the one that holds the rest together. In our science, the dependence goes both ways, because no cell works without oxygen and the breath and the heartbeat answer to the cells.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 6 (about 300 BCE):** The true people of old breathed from their heels, and ordinary people breathe from their throats. (A slow, deep breath reaches further into the lungs than a quick, shallow one. At rest, the body asks for little, and the breath can be slow and deep.)
- **Kabir, 'Student, tell me, what is God?' (15th century, in Robert Bly's translation):** Kabir answers: he is the breath inside the breath. (Each breath brings in air, but the oxygen is what matters. It crosses a wall half a thousandth of a millimetre thick and rides inside a red blood cell. There is a breath inside the breath.)
- Science (settled): Almost all the oxygen in the air was made by living things. About 2.4 billion years ago, bacteria in the sea began to use sunlight to split water, and the oxygen they gave out slowly filled the air.
- Science (settled): The iron at the heart of haemoglobin was made inside stars and thrown out when they exploded. Every iron atom in your blood is older than the Sun.
- Other view, Many physiologists: Heart cells have a built-in pacemaker, and nerves and hormones only speed it up or slow it down. They describe the whole body as feedback loops of chemicals and signals, and do not say that cells ask for anything. Whether such a description is complete is a deep question.
- Practice, Listen to your own drum (3 min): Sit comfortably. Rest one hand flat on your chest and the other on your belly. Breathe slowly for one minute. Feel the beat under one hand. Feel the breath under the other. Then ask quietly, who is doing this?
- Ask: When you run, who asks your heart to speed up: you, your cells, your brain, or all of them?
- Ask: The oxygen in your blood was made by plants and microbes, and the iron by stars. Which part of you began with you?
- Ask: If one heart cell can beat alone in a dish, what makes many cells beat together as one?
- Ask: Is breathing something you do, something that happens to you, or both?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When you run, who decides to speed up your heart: you, your brain, or your muscles? (hint: Think about what happens when you try to slow your heart down on purpose, and what changes it when you are scared.)
- *big:* The iron in your blood was made inside a star. Does that make a red blood cell part of the sky? (hint: Think about where iron comes from, and how it got into the food you ate.)
- *big:* Plants give out the oxygen that your blood carries. Whose oxygen is it? (hint: Think about a leaf, the air in your lungs, and where the oxygen was an hour ago.)
- *deep:* Your heart started beating a few weeks after you began, long before you could think. Who or what was doing it? (hint: Think about heart cells that beat on their own in a dish, and about what 'you' means.)
- *deep:* A mouse and an elephant have hearts with the same plan, but beat at very different speeds. What does that say about how a body is made? (hint: Think about size, energy and how long it takes for blood to go round a small body or a big one.)
- *big:* A visitor to Leh gasps on the stairs, while a child there runs. Who is stronger, and what does 'strong' mean? (hint: Think about the air, the blood and how long each of them has lived there.)
- *deep:* Your pulse changes when you feel scared or excited. Does the body feel first, or does the mind? (hint: Scientists have argued about this since the 1880s. Think about what you notice when you are startled.)
- *big:* More than half of the women in India have anaemia. Why might it be so common, and who should fix it? (hint: Think about food, who eats last in many homes, and what blood needs to be made.)

## Story: Two Fingers on the Wrist
*A made-up story. The children and the places in it are imagined, though Leh is a real town at about 3,500 metres. The science is real: thin air, a faster pulse, cold fingers and the nail test.*

Arjun came to Leh from Madurai in the second week of May. The plane landed in a valley 3,500 metres above the sea. The sky was a darker blue than any sky he had ever seen. The second thing he noticed was his heart. It was knocking against his ribs as if he had run a race, and he had only walked to the car.

His cousin Dolma was ten, a year older than he was. She carried his bag up to the flat. There were twenty-two steps, and she did not even breathe hard. Arjun stopped at step twelve and held the railing, with his mouth open like a fish.

"Sit," said Dolma. "Your body does not know this air yet."

"What is wrong with the air? It looks the same."

"There is less in every breath," she said. She did not say less of what. She took his hand, and put two of his own fingers on the inside of his wrist. "Not the thumb. The thumb has a pulse of its own and it will fool you. Count for half a minute."

Arjun counted fifty-two. Dolma counted her own. She got forty. "Double them," she said. "A hundred and four for you, and eighty for me. Your heart is working harder than mine, and you are only sitting."

Dolma's grandmother, Ama-le, was drying apricots on a cloth by the window. She poured him salty butter tea and told him what to do. "Drink. Sleep. Go slowly for three days. Every breath up here carries a little less sky, and your body must learn how to find the rest." Then she took his hand and pressed her thumb on his fingernail until the pink went white. She let go and counted aloud. "One." The pink flooded back. "When the colour comes back fast, the blood is arriving. When it comes back slowly, the blood has gone somewhere else. Your fingers will tell you before your mouth does. And if hands ever go cold and white, never rub them. Warm them slowly, against your body."

Each morning for ten days, Arjun and Dolma sat on the roof and counted. The first morning it was a hundred and four. A week later it was ninety-two. By the third week it was eighty-eight, and he climbed all twenty-two steps and only laughed at the top.

In that third week, the channel stopped. The water that ran from the glacier to Ama-le's barley field came down a stone ditch, and something had blocked it high on the hill. The grown-ups were at the bazaar. The field would be dry by evening.

"I will go," said Dolma. "I know the path."

"I will come," said Arjun, and Dolma looked at him, and then she nodded. They climbed slowly. "Breathe in for two steps, out for two steps," she told him. He did it, and for the first time the hill did not beat him.

At the top, a slab of old ice and stones lay across the ditch. Dolma lay flat on the bank and pushed her arms into the stream up to the elbows. The water was so cold that it hurt, and then it stopped hurting. She heaved the slab aside. The water leapt past her, clear and loud, and ran down the hill towards the field.

She laughed, but the laugh was thin. "I am fine," she said. She dropped her woollen cap, and picked it up, and dropped it again.

Arjun looked at her hands. The tips of her fingers were the colour of candle wax. He took her first finger and pressed the nail until it turned white. He let go, and counted aloud as Ama-le had done. "One. Two. Three. Four." The nail stayed white.

"Ama-le said that slow means the blood has gone somewhere else," said Arjun. "It went to your middle, to keep your heart warm. Your body is saving the middle and letting go of the ends."

"I am not cold," said Dolma.

"Your fingers do not know that yet."

He did not rub her hands. Ama-le had said never to rub. He opened his jacket and tucked her hands under his arms, against his shirt, where it was warm. They sat on the bank in the loud sound of the water. Arjun felt her breaths slowing against his side. Slowly, the skin of her fingers lost its wax colour.

After a long time he tried the nail again. He pressed, and let go. "One," he said. The pink came back like a lamp being lit. "Two. There. It came."

They walked home slowly, breathing in for two steps and out for two. At the gate, Ama-le looked at Dolma's hands and then at Arjun. She said nothing. She pressed Dolma's nail, and nodded, and poured two cups of tea.

That night the water ran along the edge of the barley field, soaking the dry ground. Arjun lay in bed and put two fingers on his wrist. Eighty-eight. All through him, a red river that was never still was carrying the thin sky to the ends of him, and taking it back again. He had known the air for three weeks. He knew his cousin's hands because she had taught him how to find his own pulse first.

**For the parent:** When the body is cold, it sends blood away from the fingers to protect the middle. The slow return of colour in a pressed nail shows that the blood has gone elsewhere.

- Ask: Why did Arjun's pulse come down in the three weeks while he stayed in Leh?
- Ask: What did the white fingernail tell Arjun that Dolma could not feel?
- Ask: Why did Arjun warm Dolma's hands under his arms, and not by rubbing them?

### g1 · Faster, Same or Slower? A card game about pulse and breathing (20 min)
- **rules:** Faster, about the same and slower mean compared with your usual resting pulse and breathing, unless the card says otherwise.; One player is the reader. The reader takes the top card and reads the front out loud, but not the back.; Everyone else picks one token and puts it face down. When the reader says now, everyone turns it over.; The reader then reads the back. Each player with the right token scores one point.; After each card, the reader asks one player to say, in their own words, what the cells are asking for. Then the reader role passes to the left.; Playing alone, put your token down, read the back, and score yourself. Try to beat your last score out of 24.; The game ends when the cards run out. The player with the most points wins. Everyone shares the best reason they heard.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: a situation on the front, and the verdict and the reason on the back:** You are sound asleep in bed at night. What happens to your pulse and breathing, compared with sitting awake? (back: Slower. Your cells need less energy while you sleep, so the heart and lungs slow down below the usual resting rate.); You are sitting and reading a story book. What happens, compared with your resting rate? (back: About the same. Sitting and reading is what we call resting, so this is your usual resting rate.); You stand up quickly after lying on the floor. What happens to your pulse for a moment? (back: Faster. Gravity pulls blood down to the legs, so the heart beats faster for a few beats to keep blood reaching the brain.); You run to catch a bus. What happens to your pulse and breathing? (back: Faster. Your leg muscles use energy faster, so they need more oxygen and make more carbon dioxide. The heart and lungs speed up to match.); You walk for ten minutes in the strong midday sun. What happens to your pulse? (back: Faster. The heart pumps extra blood to the skin to let heat out, as well as to the working muscles.); You have a fever and your temperature is 39 degrees Celsius. What happens to your pulse? (back: Faster. A warm body has cells that work faster, and the heart speeds up too. The pulse rises by about 10 beats for each degree of fever.); You breathe in slowly for four counts and out slowly for six counts, for two minutes. What happens? (back: Slower. Slow breathing sends calm signals to the heart, and the pulse often slows a little.); You watch the last over of a close cricket match. What happens to your pulse? (back: Faster. Excitement releases adrenaline, which makes the heart beat faster and harder even when you sit still.); You climb three floors of stairs. What happens to your pulse and breathing? (back: Faster. Climbing uses your big leg muscles hard, so they ask for more oxygen.); A runner who trains every day sits next to you, both at rest. How does her pulse compare with yours? (back: Slower. A trained heart pushes out more blood with each beat, so it does not have to beat as often.); A loud bang makes you jump. What happens to your pulse? (back: Faster. A fright releases adrenaline in seconds, and the heart speeds up even though you have not moved.); You sit wrapped in a blanket in a cold room. What happens to your pulse, compared with your resting rate? (back: About the same. In the cold, the vessels near the skin narrow, but the pulse changes little for a body that is resting.); You hold your breath for 20 seconds and then breathe again. What happens to your breathing rate right after? (back: Faster. Carbon dioxide builds up while you hold your breath, and the body breathes faster to clear it.); You skip with a rope for two minutes. What happens to your pulse and breathing? (back: Faster. Skipping works your whole body, and your muscles ask for oxygen.); You hold your breath with your face in a bowl of cold water. What happens to your pulse? (back: Slower. People have a diving reflex. Cold water on the face and a held breath slow the heart a little, which saves oxygen.); You have a half-hour nap on a hot afternoon. What happens to your pulse and breathing? (back: Slower. As you drift into sleep, your body needs less, and the pulse and breathing fall below the resting rate.); You are standing in a long queue at a shop. What happens, compared with your resting rate? (back: About the same. Standing quietly asks for a little more than sitting, but your pulse is close to your resting rate.); A visitor from Chennai arrives in Leh, at 3,500 metres, and sits down. What happens to her pulse and breathing? (back: Faster. Each breath has about one third less oxygen, so she breathes faster and her heart beats faster, even at rest.); You dance for ten minutes at a wedding. What happens to your pulse and breathing? (back: Faster. Dancing is exercise. Your muscles work hard and ask for more oxygen.); A young baby lies in a cot. How does the baby's pulse compare with yours? (back: Faster. A baby's heart is tiny and beats about 100 to 160 times a minute, much more than a school child's.); You sit in a waiting room, worrying about an injection. What happens to your pulse? (back: Faster. Worry releases a little adrenaline, and the heart speeds up even though your body is still.); You sit quietly for five minutes after a run that stopped five minutes ago. What happens, compared with right after you stopped? (back: Slower. The extra carbon dioxide has cleared, and the pulse and breathing have come back down to near rest.); You drink a glass of water while sitting. What happens to your pulse and breathing? (back: About the same. A glass of water asks almost nothing of the heart and lungs.); You swim ten laps in a pool. What happens to your pulse and breathing? (back: Faster. Swimming works the arms, legs and the whole body, so the muscles ask for more oxygen.)
- **Printable tokens: 12 verdict tokens: four sets of three:** Faster; Faster; Faster; Faster; About the same; About the same; About the same; About the same; Slower; Slower; Slower; Slower
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the 12 tokens. Shuffle the cards and put them in a pile. Give each player one token of each kind.  
  *Parent says:* These cards show situations in daily life. For each one, you decide whether your pulse and breathing go faster, stay about the same, or go slower. Get your tokens ready.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Shuffle the cards. Take one of each token.
  - Note: The tokens are Faster, About the same and Slower. Four sets of three are enough for four players. Alone, use one set. The game needs only paper, scissors and a pen; draw the three words on 12 small squares if you cannot print. A younger child can play with a parent as the reader.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first eight cards. After each one, read the back aloud and ask what the cells are asking for.  
  *Parent says:* The reader reads the front. Everyone chooses a token and puts it face down. Turn them over together. Then read the back.  
  *Child mode:* The reader reads a card. Pick a token. Turn it over. Check the back.
  - Ask: What do your cells need more of when the pulse goes faster? (good: Oxygen.; Food and oxygen.; They need to get rid of carbon dioxide.)
  - Note: Do not tell the answers early. Let children argue. A child who guesses wrong and hears the reason is learning something.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the next eight cards in the same way. Keep score. Ask one child to explain a reason in their own words.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. After each card, one player explains why in their own words. Keep your score.  
  *Child mode:* Play the next eight cards. Keep score. Explain one reason in your own words.
  - Think: Which cards were hardest to guess, and what did you learn from them?
- **s4 (6 min)**

  *Parent says:* Last eight cards. Then we count the scores. Which cards surprised you?  
  *Child mode:* Play the last eight cards. Add up your score. Which cards surprised you?
  - Check: Many cards said faster when the muscles were busy. What do all those cards have in common? → **The cells were asking for more oxygen**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Pulse Detective: what changes your pulse and your breathing? (39 min)
- **question:** How do your pulse and your breathing change when you lie down, sit, stand and exercise, and how long do they take to settle afterwards?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. In which situation will your pulse be highest? Will your breathing change as much as your pulse? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same child is tested every time, with the same two fingers on the same wrist. Every count lasts 15 seconds, and the pulse is counted first and the breaths second. The same stopwatch is used, at the same time of day, and the child has not just eaten a big meal. Only the situation changes, so any difference comes from the situation.
- **safety:** Use a low, firm step with no wobble, and keep a grown-up beside the child. Stop the step-ups at once if the child feels dizzy, has pain in the chest or cannot catch their breath. Any child with a heart or breathing condition should ask the doctor before doing the step-ups.
- **record:** Fill the table as you go. Write the claim, the evidence and the reasoning at the end. Draw a line graph of the pulse per minute against the minutes after exercise.
- **results table:** Situation | Pulse in 15 seconds | Pulse per minute | Breaths in 15 seconds | Breaths per minute
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the child the results table. Ask for a guess and a reason for the pulse and for the breathing, before any counting. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will count your pulse and your breaths in different situations. In which situation do you think your pulse will be highest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will count your pulse and your breaths. Where will your pulse be highest? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Do you think your breathing will change as much as your pulse? Why? (good: Both will go up when I run, because I need more air.; Breathing will change more, because I can feel it.; The heart will change more, because it pumps blood everywhere.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write it down so the child can compare it with the result.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit together. Show the child how to rest two fingers, not the thumb, on the inside of the wrist. Press gently until a small push is felt. Practise one 15-second count.  
  *Parent says:* Rest two fingers on the inside of your wrist. Do not use your thumb. Press gently until you feel a small push. I will say start and stop.  
  *Child mode:* Two fingers on your wrist, not your thumb. Press gently. Feel the small push. Count when I say start.
  - Note: If the child cannot find it, slide the fingers a little towards the thumb side or press slightly harder. A heart is also easy to feel with a flat hand in the middle of the chest, a little to the left. With 2 to 4 children, work in pairs or threes: one is the subject, one counts the pulse, one holds the stopwatch and counts breaths, and everyone takes a turn at each job. Each child fills their own table, because pulses differ from child to child. One child with a parent: the child counts the pulse and the parent counts the breaths and keeps time.
  - Check: Why do we use two fingers and not the thumb to find a pulse? → **The thumb has a pulse of its own, which can fool you**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to lie on the mat and look at the picture book for two minutes, quietly. Then say start, and the child counts the pulse for 15 seconds. Straight after, count the child's breaths by watching the belly rise for 15 seconds, without saying so. Write both numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* Lie on the mat and look at the book. Stay quiet for two minutes. Then count your pulse when I say start. Afterwards, I will tell you what else I counted.  
  *Child mode:* Lie down and read the book. After two minutes, count your pulse for 15 seconds.
  - Note: Count breaths without telling the child during the count. People change how they breathe when they know it is being counted. Count one rise and fall as one breath. Tell the child afterwards, and talk about why we did it that way.
  - Think: Why might it be better that the child did not know you were counting their breaths?
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to sit up on the mat for one minute without moving much. Then count the pulse for 15 seconds, and the breaths for 15 seconds. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Sit up and rest for one minute. Then we count again. Pulse first, and then breaths.  
  *Child mode:* Sit up and rest for one minute. Count your pulse for 15 seconds.
  - Ask: Is your pulse the same as lying down? Why do you think that happened? (good: It is a bit higher, because I sat up.; It is the same, because I am still resting.; I am not sure.)
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to stand still for one minute, with the arms by the sides and no leaning. Then count the pulse for 15 seconds, and the breaths for 15 seconds. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Stand up straight and still for one minute. Now count your pulse again, and then I count your breaths.  
  *Child mode:* Stand still for one minute. Count your pulse for 15 seconds.
  - Note: Most children find the standing pulse is a few beats higher, sometimes as much as 10 to 15. This is normal. If the child feels dizzy, sit down at once.
  - Check: Standing gave a higher pulse than lying down. What is the best reason? → **Blood sinks towards the legs, so the heart speeds up to keep it reaching the brain**
- **s6 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to step up onto the step and down again, about one step-up every two seconds, for one minute. A grown-up stays beside the child. At once, count the pulse for 15 seconds and the breaths for 15 seconds. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Step up and down for one minute. I will count with you. When I say stop, count your pulse straight away.  
  *Child mode:* Step up and down for one minute. Stop. Count your pulse for 15 seconds.
  - Ask: What do you notice in your chest and your breathing? (good: My heart is thumping.; I am breathing fast and deep.; My legs feel warm.)
  - Note: Count straight away, because the pulse starts to fall within seconds. A child may lose count when the pulse is fast. Count together for the first try. Stop at once if the child feels dizzy or has any pain. Do this in a cool, shaded room and not in the afternoon heat, because a hot room raises the pulse by itself. Typical rested school-child pulses are about 70 to 100 a minute and rise to about 110 to 150 after a minute of stepping, but yours may differ.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to sit quietly. At 1, 2, 3 and 4 minutes after stopping, count the pulse for 15 seconds and then the breaths for 15 seconds. Write each pair down.  
  *Parent says:* Sit still. We will count again at one minute, two minutes, three minutes and four minutes after you stopped. Look at how the numbers change.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Count your pulse at 1, 2, 3 and 4 minutes.
  - Note: Use the stopwatch from the moment the step-ups stop. Most children are back close to their resting pulse by the fourth minute. A child who plays sport a lot often recovers a little faster. Start each 15-second count at the minute mark, so the pulse is counted from 1:00 to 1:15 and the breaths from 1:15 to 1:30. The pulse is a little lower in the second count of each pair, which is fine.
- **s8 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help the child turn each 15-second count into a count for one minute by multiplying by 4. Fill in the last two columns.  
  *Parent says:* Each count was for 15 seconds, and there are four of those in one minute. So multiply each number by 4. Write the answers in the table.  
  *Child mode:* Multiply each count by 4. That gives you beats and breaths in one minute.
  - Note: For a count of 22 beats in 15 seconds, 22 times 4 is 88. Breaths counted in 15 seconds are small numbers, so the answers are rough. That is fine. If two counts differ by only 1 beat, that is within the error of a 15-second count, so do not claim a change.
  - Check: You counted 30 beats in 15 seconds after exercise. What is the pulse per minute? → **120**
- **s9 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to say or write a claim, the evidence and the reasoning. Compare with the first guess. Do not give the explanation until the child has said what they think.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? Which number was highest, and when? What do you think made the heart and the breathing go up together?  
  *Child mode:* Tell me what you found. Which numbers went up? What do you think made them go up?
  - Ask: What do your cells need more of when you run? (good: Oxygen.; Air.; Energy from food and air.)
  - Note: Good evidence points to numbers in the table. A good claim might be: my pulse and my breathing went up together, then came down together. The explanation of why comes after this, in the cards.
  - Think: If both go up together, what might be telling them to go up?
### e2 · Cold Fingers: how fast does the pink come back? (31 min)
- **question:** How long does it take for the colour to return to a pressed fingernail when your hand is warm, at room temperature, or cold?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. In which hand will the pink come back fastest? Which will be slowest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same finger (the middle finger) of the same hand is pressed every time, for the same two seconds, with the same pressure. The same person counts the seconds, starting the moment the finger is let go. Each hand temperature is tested three times, and the hand is left to settle between tests. Only the temperature of the hand changes.
- **safety:** Keep the hand in the cold water for one minute only, and never more than two minutes. A grown-up watches the clock. Stop at once if the hand hurts or goes very white or numb. Do not do the cold part with a child who has poor circulation, and ask the doctor first if you are not sure. Never rub a very cold hand hard. Warm it gently.
- **record:** Write the three times for each hand and the colour of the fingertips. Then write which hand was fastest, which was slowest, and why you think so. Say which room you did it in and whether the weather was hot, cool or damp.
- **results table:** Hand | Try 1 (seconds) | Try 2 (seconds) | Try 3 (seconds) | Colour of the fingertips
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the child how a pressed nail goes white. Ask for a guess and a reason for the warm hand, the room-temperature hand and the cold hand. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will press a fingernail and time how fast the pink comes back. In which hand do you think it will come back fastest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will press a nail and time the pink coming back. Which hand will be fastest? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think happens to the blood in your fingers when they are cold? (good: It stops moving.; It goes slower.; It goes somewhere else.)
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. A common idea is that blood freezes or stops. Do not correct it. Let the test show. With 2 to 4 children, work in pairs: one child is the hand, one presses and times. Share the two bowls, but run two pairs at the same time only if you have a helper to watch each cold-water minute. Every child tests their own hand, because skin and nails differ.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to rest the left hand in warm water for two minutes, then dry it. Press the nail of the middle finger firmly for two seconds, until it goes white. Let go and count the seconds until the nail looks as pink as the finger beside it. Do this three times. Use the left hand and the same finger for every test.  
  *Parent says:* Rest your hand in warm water for two minutes. Dry it. I will press your nail for two seconds and let go. Watch the pink come back, and I will count.  
  *Child mode:* Put your hand in warm water for two minutes. Dry it. Ask a grown-up to press and time your nail.
  - Note: Count in half seconds if you can, saying one, and, two, and. Start counting the moment the finger is released. A warm hand often takes only about one second. Use the same finger, the same press and the same counter each time. Normal pink usually returns in about 1 to 2 seconds, so the differences are small and you must count carefully. A helper can say 'pink!' when the colour is back while the timer runs. If the nail colour is hard to see, press the soft pad of the fingertip instead and watch it go from pale to pink, and always use the same spot.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to rest the same left hand on the table for three minutes. This lets it settle back to normal. Press the same finger for two seconds and time the pink three times. Write the times down.  
  *Parent says:* Now rest the same hand on the table for three minutes. We press and time the nail in the same way.  
  *Child mode:* Rest the same hand on the table for three minutes. Press and time the nail again.
  - Note: A hand at room temperature is usually between the warm and the cold results. If the room is very cold or very hot, the result may be different. Write the room down in your notes.
- **s4 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up watches the clock. The child puts the same hand into the iced water for one minute. Take it out, dry it, and at once press the nail for two seconds and time the pink three times. Look at the colour of the fingertips.  
  *Parent says:* Put your hand in the cold water for one minute. I will watch the clock. Take it out and dry it. Then we press and time the nail.  
  *Child mode:* Put your hand in cold water for one minute. Dry it. Then press and time the nail.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the colour and the feel of your fingertips? (good: They look pale.; They feel numb.; The pink took longer to come back.)
  - Note: Do the three presses quickly, because the hand starts to warm up at once. If the times are all similar, note the colour of the fingertips as well. Pale, waxy fingertips are a clear sign. Stop the test if the hand hurts. A cold hand should be slower, but the size of the change differs from child to child, typically from about 2 seconds to 4 seconds or more. If it does not change at all, the water was not cold enough, or the hand warmed up while you dried it. Write that down honestly and try again with more ice.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Gently warm the cold hand under the other arm for two minutes. Press the nail again. Write down how long the pink takes now, and what the fingertips look like.  
  *Parent says:* Tuck your cold hand under your other arm, against your side. Wait two minutes. Then we press and time the nail once more.  
  *Child mode:* Warm your cold hand under your arm. Then test the nail again.
  - Note: Do not rub the hand hard. Warm it gently. The pink should return faster as the hand warms, and the fingertips should look pinker. This is a good place to talk about how cold fingers feel painful as they warm.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to write a claim, the evidence and the reasoning. Compare with the first guess. Do not explain until the child has said what they think.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? Which hand was fastest? Where do you think the blood went when your hand was cold?  
  *Child mode:* Tell me what you found. Which hand was fastest? Where do you think the blood went?
  - Check: The pink came back slowly in the cold hand. What does this show? → **Less blood reached the fingertips while the hand was cold**
### e3 · Big Surface, Fast Swap: why are lungs so wide? (30 min)
- **question:** Does the same amount of sugar dissolve faster as one lump, as crushed bits, or as a powder?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which glass will clear first, the lump, the crushed bits or the powder? Which will be last? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each glass holds 100 millilitres of the same warm tap water. Each glass gets one sugar lump's worth of the same sugar. No one stirs any glass. The glasses are started at the same moment and checked at the same times. Only the size of the pieces changes, so any difference comes from the size.
- **safety:** Use warm water that is not hot. A grown-up grinds the powder in a clean mortar or a mixer jar, because the blades are sharp. Do not drink the sugar water if the glasses were not food-clean, and keep the sugar away from ants.
- **record:** Write the time for each glass. Draw each glass at the end. Say how the size of the pieces changed the time. Say how long a glass had to be clear and what you saw at the bottom.
- **results table:** Glass | What is in it | Time until all the sugar has gone (minutes) | What you saw
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three kinds of sugar. Ask the child for a guess and a reason about which will clear first, and which last. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We have the same amount of sugar in three forms. Which glass do you think will clear first? Which will be last? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Same sugar, three forms. Which glass clears first? Which last? Why?
  - Ask: What is different between the three sugars? (good: One is a lump.; One is crushed.; One is powder, so it has many small pieces.)
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. Some children think that more pieces will take longer, because there is more to dissolve. The test will show. With 2 to 4 children: each child drops one sugar into one glass (A, B or C), and a fourth child, or the parent, runs the stopwatch and writes the times. Swap jobs and repeat if there is time. One child with a parent: the child drops all three and the parent keeps time.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Pour 100 millilitres of warm water into each glass. Mark the glasses A, B and C. Ask the child to wrap one cube in the cloth and crush it into bits. Keep every bit. A grown-up grinds one cube into a fine powder.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the same amount of warm water into each glass. Glass A gets a whole lump. Glass B gets a crushed lump. Glass C gets a powdered lump.  
  *Child mode:* Pour water into three glasses. A is the lump. B is crushed. C is powder.
  - Note: The water in each glass must be at the same warmth. Pour from one jug to be sure. Do not use hot water, because hot water makes everything dissolve fast and the difference is hard to see. Each glass gets the sugar of one whole cube (about one level teaspoon). Do not lose any bits or powder, because less sugar would clear faster and spoil the test.
  - Check: Why do we use the same amount of sugar in each glass? → **So that only the size of the pieces is different**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Drop the three sugars into the glasses at the same moment and start the stopwatch. Do not stir. Tip in the powder gently so it does not splash.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? Drop them in together, on three. One, two, three. Start the stopwatch. Do not stir, because stirring would hide the difference.  
  *Child mode:* Drop all three in together. Start the stopwatch. Do not stir.
  - Note: Stirring spreads the sugar and hides the effect of the size of the pieces, so keep the spoons away.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every minute, look at the glasses and write down which sugar is gone and which is not. Note the time when each glass is completely clear. Stop at 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the glasses every minute. Which one has cleared? Which one still has sugar? Write the time when each one is clear.  
  *Child mode:* Look every minute. Write down the time when each glass is clear.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the water around each lump? (good: The water near the lump looks wavy.; There are fewer bits in glass C.; The powder disappears quickly.)
  - Note: Call a glass 'clear' when you cannot see any grains at the bottom when you tilt it. The powder may be clear in about a minute, and may sit in a clump at first. The crushed bits take a few minutes. A plain sugar cube in warm water typically takes about 3 to 10 minutes without stirring, and mishri takes longer. If the lump is not gone after 15 minutes, write 'more than 15'. That is a good result. If all three finish at about the same time, the water was probably too hot: try again with cooler water.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to write a claim, the evidence and the reasoning. Then ask how this could link to lungs. Do not explain until the child has said what they think.

## Worksheets
### Pulse, Breath and Blood (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A child's pulse is counted for 15 seconds, and the count is 22 beats. What is the pulse per minute?  
   Options: 88 beats / 37 beats / 22 beats  
   **Answer:** 88 beats. *Why:* There are four 15-second spells in one minute. 22 times 4 is 88. If you chose 37, you added 15 to the count instead of multiplying.
2. (fill) One breath is one time in and one time ____.  
   **Answer:** ['out']. *Why:* A breath has two halves, in and out. When you count breaths, count each full in-and-out as one.
3. (truefalse) A child who is sitting still usually has a pulse of about 70 to 110 beats per minute.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Children aged 6 to 10 usually have a resting pulse in this range. Grown-ups are usually slower, and babies are faster.
4. (mcq) What do red blood cells carry from the lungs to the cells of the body?  
   Options: Oxygen / Only water / Carbon dioxide  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* Red blood cells pick up oxygen in the lungs and let go of it in the tissues. Carbon dioxide travels the other way, mostly dissolved in the plasma.
5. (mcq) Cells use food to get energy. What gas do they make as waste?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide / Oxygen / Nitrogen  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide. *Why:* Cells use oxygen to release energy from sugar, and they give out carbon dioxide and water. The carbon dioxide travels in the blood back to the lungs.
6. (match) Match each part of the blood to its job.  
   **Answer:** Red blood cell → carries oxygen; White blood cell → fights germs; Platelet → plugs a cut; Plasma → the pale liquid that carries cells and food; Haemoglobin → the red protein that holds oxygen. *Why:* Blood is a mix of liquid and different cells, and each one has its own job.
7. (fill) Red blood cells are red because they are full of a red protein with iron in it, called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['haemoglobin', 'hemoglobin']. *Why:* Haemoglobin holds oxygen. It has iron at its centre, which is why the body needs iron in food.
8. (mcq) Which blood vessels are so thin that red blood cells pass through them in single file?  
   Options: Capillaries / Arteries / Veins  
   **Answer:** Capillaries. *Why:* A capillary is only one cell thick, and it is narrow enough that red cells must go through one at a time. This lets oxygen cross easily into the cells around it.
9. (order) Put these in order, to follow one oxygen molecule from the air to a muscle cell.  
   **Answer:** ['It is breathed in through the nose', 'It travels down the windpipe to the lungs', 'It crosses the thin wall of an alveolus into a capillary', 'It is carried to the heart by a red blood cell', 'It is pumped out of the heart to the leg muscle']. *Why:* Oxygen goes from the nose to the lungs, across into the blood, along to the heart, and out to the muscle.
10. (truefalse) Blood in your veins is blue until it touches the air.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Blood is always red. It is bright red with oxygen and dark red without much. Veins look blue because of the way light goes through skin.
11. (mcq) Your pulse and your breathing both go up when you run. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Your muscle cells need more oxygen and make more carbon dioxide / Your heart and lungs are tired / Your brain wants to cool the blood  
   **Answer:** Your muscle cells need more oxygen and make more carbon dioxide. *Why:* Busy muscles use oxygen faster and make more carbon dioxide. The heart and lungs speed up to deliver the oxygen and clear the carbon dioxide.
12. (sort) Is each job done by the right side or the left side of the heart?  
   **Answer:** sends blood to the lungs → Right side; sends blood to the whole body → Left side; has the thickest wall → Left side; receives blood coming back from the body → Right side; receives blood coming back from the lungs → Left side. *Why:* The right side sends blood a short way to the lungs. The left side sends it all the way round the body, so it needs the thicker wall.
13. (label) Here is a picture of the heart cut open. Label the four rooms: top left, top right, bottom left and bottom right, as you look at the picture.  
   **Answer:** ['Top left of the picture: right atrium', 'Top right of the picture: left atrium', 'Bottom left of the picture: right ventricle', 'Bottom right of the picture: left ventricle']. *Why:* In pictures, the heart is drawn as if you face the person, so the person's right side is on the left of the picture.
14. (write) A friend says: 'You breathe faster when you run because you run out of oxygen.' What can you tell her, using what you found out?  
   **Answer:** Breathing speeds up mainly because carbon dioxide builds up in the blood when muscles work. Sensors notice the carbon dioxide and tell the lungs to breathe faster, and the heart speeds up to match. Oxygen does not run out.. *Why:* A strong answer names carbon dioxide as the main signal and says that the heart and lungs answer what the cells are doing.
15. (draw) Draw the heart as two pumps side by side. Show an arrow from the right pump to the lungs, and an arrow from the left pump to the body, and one arrow back to each.  
   **Answer:** Two pumps side by side. An arrow goes from the right one to the lungs and back to the left one. An arrow goes from the left one to the body and back to the right one.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows two loops that meet at the heart, so blood goes through the heart twice on one full trip.
16. (count) A child's heart beats 80 times a minute. How many times does it beat in 10 minutes?  
   **Answer:** 800. *Why:* 80 times 10 is 800. Multiplying by 10 puts a zero on the end.
17. (mcq) You press a fingernail until it goes white, and the pink comes back slowly. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Less blood is reaching the fingertip / The nail is growing / The finger has too much blood  
   **Answer:** Less blood is reaching the fingertip. *Why:* A white nail is a nail with the blood squeezed out. The pink returns when new blood arrives. If it comes back slowly, the blood is arriving slowly, as in cold fingers.
### Surface, Signals and Other Bodies (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Your lungs have hundreds of millions of tiny air bags and not one big bag. Why does this help?  
   Options: It gives a huge surface for oxygen to cross into the blood / It makes the lungs lighter / It keeps the air warm  
   **Answer:** It gives a huge surface for oxygen to cross into the blood. *Why:* Oxygen crosses the lung's thin wall, and the more wall there is, the more oxygen can cross each second. Many small bags have more surface than one big bag of the same volume.
2. (mcq) A crushed sugar lump dissolves faster than a whole one. What does this tell you about why lungs fold into so many bags?  
   Options: More surface means a faster swap / Small pieces are lighter / Warm water is better for powder  
   **Answer:** More surface means a faster swap. *Why:* A swap happens only where two things touch. Breaking something into many small pieces gives much more touching surface.
3. (truefalse) The air you breathe out has no oxygen left in it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Breathed-out air still holds about 16 parts oxygen in 100. The body takes only about a quarter of the oxygen in each breath.
4. (fill) The level of ____ in the blood is the main signal that tells your body to breathe faster.  
   **Answer:** ['carbon dioxide']. *Why:* Sensors in the brainstem and in the big arteries of the neck notice carbon dioxide rising, and tell the breathing muscles to work harder.
5. (mcq) Your pulse is a few beats higher when you stand than when you lie down. What is the likely reason?  
   Options: Gravity pulls blood to the legs, so the heart speeds up to keep it reaching the brain / Standing uses the arm muscles / The wrist moves nearer the heart  
   **Answer:** Gravity pulls blood to the legs, so the heart speeds up to keep it reaching the brain. *Why:* When you stand, blood tends to pool in the legs. The heart beats a little faster to keep blood moving to the head.
6. (order) Put these in order, from the slowest resting heart to the fastest.  
   **Answer:** ['an elephant', 'a person', 'a mouse']. *Why:* An elephant's heart beats about 30 times a minute, a person's about 70, and a mouse's about 600. Smaller bodies use energy faster for their size, so their hearts beat faster.
7. (label) A tube of blood has settled into three layers. Label them from the top to the bottom.  
   **Answer:** ['Top: plasma', 'Middle, a thin layer: white blood cells and platelets', 'Bottom: red blood cells']. *Why:* Plasma is the lightest part and floats on top. The red cells are the heaviest and sink to the bottom. The thin pale layer in between holds the white cells and the platelets.
8. (write) Why might a person with anaemia get out of breath sooner on the stairs than a person without it?  
   **Answer:** A person with anaemia has fewer red blood cells or less haemoglobin, so each beat of the heart carries less oxygen to the muscles. On the stairs, the muscles ask for more oxygen, so the body breathes faster and the heart beats faster sooner.. *Why:* A strong answer links fewer red cells to less oxygen delivered, and so to the body having to work harder to deliver enough.
9. (mcq) After a month in Leh, a visitor feels much better on the stairs. What has changed in her body?  
   Options: She has more red blood cells to carry oxygen / She has a bigger heart / She has stopped needing oxygen  
   **Answer:** She has more red blood cells to carry oxygen. *Why:* In thin air, the body makes more red blood cells over weeks, so each pass of the blood carries more oxygen.
10. (match) Match each living being to the way it moves oxygen round its body.  
   **Answer:** Mouse → a heart that beats about 600 times a minute; Octopus → three hearts and blood with copper; Fish → a heart with two rooms and one loop; Insect → air tubes that go straight into the body; Person → a heart with four rooms and two loops. *Why:* Moving oxygen is a problem every body must solve, and living things have solved it in different ways.
11. (perspective) Two children are arguing about what makes the heart speed up when you run.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer uses the test, for example that the pulse and breathing rose together and fell together, which suggests they answer the same call. It also admits that the heart has a built-in pacemaker.. *Why:* The heart does have its own rhythm, and signals from outside change it. Seeing both sides is better than picking only one.
12. (mcq) Why does the left side of the heart have a thicker wall than the right?  
   Options: It pushes blood all the way round the body / It is bigger than the right / It is older than the right  
   **Answer:** It pushes blood all the way round the body. *Why:* The right side sends blood a short way to the lungs. The left side must push blood through the whole body, so it needs stronger muscle.
13. (count) A child's heart pushes out 45 millilitres of blood with each beat, and beats 80 times in a minute. How many millilitres of blood is that in one minute?  
   **Answer:** 3600. *Why:* 45 times 80 is 3,600. That is 3.6 litres, more than the 2 to 3 litres of blood in the child's whole body. So the same blood must go round more than once each minute.
14. (truefalse) Veins have small flaps inside that stop blood from slipping backwards.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The flaps act as one-way doors. When leg muscles squeeze the veins, blood is pushed up towards the heart, and the flaps stop it from falling back.
15. (write) In the Cold Fingers test, the pink came back in 1 second in a warm hand and in 4 seconds in a cold hand. Write a claim, the evidence and the reasoning.  
   **Answer:** Claim: blood reaches the fingertips more slowly when they are cold. Evidence: the pink took 1 second in the warm hand and 4 seconds in the cold hand. Reasoning: in the cold, the small vessels near the skin narrow, so less blood arrives, which keeps the middle of the body warm.. *Why:* A strong answer has all three parts, uses the numbers as evidence, and links them to narrow vessels.
16. (sort) In each situation, do the small blood vessels near the skin get narrower or wider?  
   **Answer:** standing outside on a cold morning → Narrower; playing hard in the heat → Wider; dipping a hand in ice water → Narrower; red cheeks after a run → Wider; shivering in a cold room → Narrower. *Why:* In the cold, the body saves heat by narrowing the vessels near the skin. In the heat, it widens them to let heat out.
### Think Like a Heart Scientist (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A friend counts 14 beats in 10 seconds. What is her pulse per minute?  
   Options: 84 beats / 24 beats / 140 beats  
   **Answer:** 84 beats. *Why:* There are six 10-second spells in one minute. 14 times 6 is 84. If you chose 140, you multiplied by 10 instead of 6.
2. (count) After exercise, a child's pulse falls from 150 to 90 beats per minute in 3 minutes, falling by the same amount each minute. By how many beats per minute does it fall each minute?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* 150 take away 90 is 60. 60 divided by 3 is 20. So the pulse fell by 20 beats per minute, each minute.
3. (mcq) Some people breathe fast and deep many times and then hold their breath, and a few faint. What explains this?  
   Options: Fast breathing removes carbon dioxide, so the urge to breathe goes away while oxygen is still falling / Fast breathing adds too much oxygen / Holding the breath makes blood too thin  
   **Answer:** Fast breathing removes carbon dioxide, so the urge to breathe goes away while oxygen is still falling. *Why:* The urge to breathe is set by carbon dioxide, not by oxygen. If you wash out the carbon dioxide first, the warning comes late, and oxygen can fall too low. This is dangerous, so never do it, especially in water.
4. (odd) Three of these make the heart beat faster. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: A sudden fright / A fever / Climbing a hill / Falling into a deep sleep  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Fright, fever and climbing all raise the pulse. Deep sleep slows it.
5. (write) A mouse's heart beats about 600 times a minute and an elephant's about 30. Using the idea of cells that need oxygen, explain one reason for the difference.  
   **Answer:** A small body loses heat fast and each gram of a mouse uses oxygen much faster than a gram of an elephant. So a mouse's cells ask for oxygen at a faster rate, and the heart must deliver faster.. *Why:* A strong answer links the heart rate to how quickly the cells use oxygen, which depends on the size of the body.
6. (mcq) An octopus has blue-green blood. What is the reason?  
   Options: It carries oxygen with copper instead of iron / It has no oxygen in it / It is cold-blooded  
   **Answer:** It carries oxygen with copper instead of iron. *Why:* The octopus's oxygen carrier has copper, and copper makes the blood bluish. Ours has iron, and iron makes blood red.
7. (truefalse) In a tree, sugar and water are moved round by a heart.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A tree has no heart. Water is pulled up by the loss of water from the leaves, and sugar moves down tubes from the leaves to the rest of the tree.
8. (perspective) People have long said that feelings live in the heart.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer uses evidence, such as the heart-transplant fact or the racing heart in fear, and admits that each view has a point.. *Why:* Racing hearts are real, and brain damage changing feelings is real too. The question of where feelings live is still open.
9. (mcq) Why do climbers rest for days partway up a high mountain before going higher?  
   Options: The body needs time to make more red blood cells and adjust its breathing / The food is better at the lower camps / The air is warmer at the lower camps  
   **Answer:** The body needs time to make more red blood cells and adjust its breathing. *Why:* It takes days to weeks for the body to adjust to thin air. Going too fast can cause altitude sickness.
10. (match) Match each tool to what it tells a doctor.  
   **Answer:** Stethoscope → lets the doctor listen to the heart and lungs; Pulse oximeter → shows how much of the haemoglobin is carrying oxygen; Haemoglobin test → shows how much haemoglobin is in the blood; ECG → records the heart's electrical signals; Spirometer → measures how much air a person can breathe out. *Why:* Each tool lets us see a different part of what the heart, blood and lungs are doing.
11. (draw) A child's pulse after exercise was 138 at 0 minutes, 112 at 1 minute, 98 at 2 minutes, 90 at 3 minutes and 86 at 4 minutes. Draw a line graph of these.  
   **Answer:** A graph with minutes along the bottom from 0 to 4 and pulse up the side. The line starts high at 138 and drops steeply, then flattens out near 86.. *Why:* A line graph shows the fall in pulse and how it slows down. The steepest fall is in the first minute.
12. (mcq) A pulse oximeter clipped to a finger shows 98 percent. What does this mean?  
   Options: About 98 out of every 100 haemoglobin molecules are carrying oxygen / 98 percent of the air is oxygen / The pulse is 98 beats a minute  
   **Answer:** About 98 out of every 100 haemoglobin molecules are carrying oxygen. *Why:* A pulse oximeter shines light through the finger and measures how much of the haemoglobin is holding oxygen. It often shows the pulse in a separate number.
13. (write) Plan a fair test of this question: do children who play sport every day recover their pulse faster than children who do not? Say what you would keep the same.  
   **Answer:** Test several children from each group. Have each do the same one minute of step-ups on the same step, and count the pulse every minute for four minutes. Keep the exercise, the counting, the time of day and the way of counting the same. Compare the time to return close to resting.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing, the sport habit, and keeps the rest the same, and uses more than one child from each group.
14. (count) A red blood cell lives for about 120 days. About how many weeks is that, to the nearest whole week?  
   **Answer:** 17. *Why:* 120 divided by 7 is about 17.1. So a red blood cell lives about 17 weeks, a little over four months.
15. (mcq) At 3,500 metres, each breath holds about two thirds as much oxygen as at sea level. Which could help the body get the same oxygen?  
   Options: Breathing faster, pumping more blood and making more red blood cells / Breathing slower / Eating more salt  
   **Answer:** Breathing faster, pumping more blood and making more red blood cells. *Why:* All three of the first list help: more breaths bring in more air, more pumping moves it faster, and more red cells carry more per trip.
16. (order) Put these in order, to show how your body answers when you start to run.  
   **Answer:** ['Muscle cells use more oxygen and make more carbon dioxide', 'Sensors notice the carbon dioxide rising', 'The breathing and the pulse speed up', 'More oxygen reaches the muscle cells']. *Why:* The cells call first. The sensors notice. The lungs and heart speed up. More oxygen is delivered, which meets the cells' call.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) You count 25 beats in 15 seconds. What is the pulse per minute?  
   Options: 100 beats / 40 beats / 25 beats  
   **Answer:** 100 beats. *Why:* There are four 15-second spells in a minute. 25 times 4 is 100. If you chose 40, you added 15.
2. (mcq) Why is it better if the person does not know you are counting their breaths?  
   Options: People change how they breathe when they know it is being counted / The count is easier to do / Breaths are quieter then  
   **Answer:** People change how they breathe when they know it is being counted. *Why:* When we notice our breathing, we often change it. A fair count should be of normal breathing.
3. (mcq) What does the blood bring to a muscle cell?  
   Options: Food and oxygen / Only oxygen / Only carbon dioxide  
   **Answer:** Food and oxygen. *Why:* Blood brings both sugar from food and oxygen. The cell uses them together to release energy.
4. (mcq) What makes blood red?  
   Options: Haemoglobin, a red protein with iron in it / The salt in the blood / Air from the lungs  
   **Answer:** Haemoglobin, a red protein with iron in it. *Why:* Haemoglobin holds oxygen and is red. Blood is red whether or not the person has just breathed.
5. (mcq) Which part of the heart pushes blood to the whole body?  
   Options: The left side / The right side / Both sides do the same job  
   **Answer:** The left side. *Why:* The right side sends blood to the lungs and the left side sends it to the whole body.
6. (mcq) Why are the walls of the alveoli so thin?  
   Options: So oxygen can pass through quickly into the blood / So the lungs are light / So the air stays inside  
   **Answer:** So oxygen can pass through quickly into the blood. *Why:* A swap needs a thin wall. Oxygen crosses a wall that is only half a thousandth of a millimetre thick.
7. (mcq) Which gas level in the blood mostly tells your body to breathe faster?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide / Oxygen / Nitrogen  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide. *Why:* Rising carbon dioxide is the main signal. Falling oxygen matters later and only when it falls a long way.
8. (mcq) Hana's fingers are white and cold after a swim. When she presses a nail, the pink returns slowly. What is her body doing?  
   Options: Sending less blood to the fingers to keep the middle of the body warm / Making more blood in the fingers / Stopping her heart  
   **Answer:** Sending less blood to the fingers to keep the middle of the body warm. *Why:* In the cold, the small vessels near the skin narrow. The blood stays near the heart and the other organs that must stay warm.
9. (mcq) Which of these would give the lowest pulse?  
   Options: Sleeping soundly / Standing in a queue / Reading a book  
   **Answer:** Sleeping soundly. *Why:* During deep sleep the cells need less energy, so the pulse falls below the usual resting rate.
10. (mcq) A child counts her pulse three times and gets 80, 84 and 82. What does this show?  
   Options: The count is steady, so we can trust it / The count is wrong / Her pulse is changing quickly  
   **Answer:** The count is steady, so we can trust it. *Why:* Results that are close together show that the test gives about the same answer each time. A few beats of difference is normal.
11. (mcq) Why does a visitor to Leh breathe faster at first?  
   Options: Each breath of thin air holds less oxygen / Leh is hotter / The visitor is afraid  
   **Answer:** Each breath of thin air holds less oxygen. *Why:* At 3,500 metres the air has about two thirds of the oxygen per breath. Breathing faster brings in more air.
12. (mcq) A nurse takes blood from a vein in your arm. What colour is the blood in the syringe?  
   Options: Dark red / Blue / Bright yellow  
   **Answer:** Dark red. *Why:* Blood is always red. Blood from a vein has given up some oxygen, so it is dark red.

## Applications
- Doctors count the pulse and breathing rate of every patient, because the two numbers show quickly how the body is coping.
- A pulse oximeter clipped to a finger shows how much of the blood's haemoglobin is carrying oxygen.
- A stethoscope lets a doctor hear the valves of the heart close and the air moving in the lungs.
- Blood tests count red blood cells and haemoglobin, to find anaemia.
- Giving iron tablets and iron-rich food, such as ragi, jaggery, dal and green leaves, helps to treat anaemia.
- Athletes watch their resting pulse and their recovery pulse to see how fit they are.
- Mountaineers rest on the way up so their bodies can make more red blood cells.
- Heart-lung machines do the work of the heart and lungs for a few hours during heart surgery.
- A face mask with extra oxygen helps people who find it hard to breathe.
- Exercise, such as skipping, swimming and playing kabaddi, makes the heart stronger.

## Spotted around us
- Your own pulse, visible as a tiny flicker in the skin of the wrist when you are quiet and the light is good.
- A cat or a dog asleep, with a flank that rises and falls faster than yours.
- Players puffing after a game of kabaddi or football, with chests rising fast and cheeks pink.
- A doctor's clinic with a pulse oximeter, a stethoscope and a blood pressure cuff.
- Pale, cold fingers on a winter morning, or in an air-conditioned room.
- A packet of iron tablets or a poster about anaemia at a health centre or school.
- A runner on a morning walk with a smartwatch showing a heart rate.
- A fog of breath on a cold glass or a steel tumbler.

## Open questions
- **Why does the heart of each animal beat at the speed they do, and is there a limit to the number of beats in a life?** Small animals have fast hearts and short lives, but people live much longer than the pattern predicts, so no one has a firm rule.
- **How do heart cells that start beating on their own in a dish learn to beat together in time?** Scientists know that cells signal each other through tiny channels, but they are still working out the full detail.
- **Why do some people adapt well to life at high altitude and others do not?** Gene changes are known in Tibetans and Andeans, but many other factors are not understood.
- **How much does breathing slowly and deeply really change the heart, the brain and the mood?** Studies often find small benefits, but they are hard to run well, and the results are mixed.
- **How exactly do the body's sensors measure oxygen and carbon dioxide so quickly?** Cells in the neck's carotid bodies are known to be sensors, but the exact detail of how they sense oxygen is still being studied.
- **Why are so many women and children in India anaemic, even with food available?** Poor diet, infections, blood loss and long-standing patterns of who eats first all play a part, and researchers are still working out how much each one matters.

- In the Pulse Detective test, Kavya counted 21 beats in 15 seconds lying down and 33 beats in 15 seconds just after step-ups. What was her pulse per minute each time? (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
