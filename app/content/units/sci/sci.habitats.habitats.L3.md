# Habitats: the places that give living things what they need
`sci.habitats.habitats.L3`

**Summary.** Is there anywhere that nothing can live? In 1977, scientists in a small submarine went down to the dark floor of the Pacific Ocean. They expected warm springs and bare rock, and they found giant worms and clams. A habitat is the place where a living thing finds what they need: food, water, air, shelter, space and the right warmth. A pond, a forest, a desert and the sea each give some things easily and make other things hard. Even a bowl of warm milk can be a habitat, for the bacteria that turn milk into curd. Living things also change their habitats: trees make shade and damp air, and worms make soil. Your own body is a habitat for trillions of tiny living things. Nobody knows yet whether any other world has a habitat with life.

**Intro.** *Think of a place where you are sure nothing could live. The bottom of the deepest sea? A hot spring? The inside of a rock? Pick one, and say why nothing could live there.* In February 1977, a pilot and two scientists squeezed into a tiny submarine called Alvin. They sank about two and a half kilometres down into the dark Pacific Ocean, near the Galapagos Islands. No sunlight ever reaches that deep. They expected bare rock, cold and nearly empty. Instead, around cracks in the sea floor where warm water came seeping up, they found a crowd. There were giant worms in white tubes with red tips, some longer than your arm. There were clams as big as dinner plates, and pale crabs. How could so much life live where there is no sunlight? What was that place giving them? A place that gives a living thing what they need is called their habitat.

3. I can explain that the living and non-living parts of a habitat depend on each other, and that living things change their habitats.
4. I can say how people destroy habitats and how people make and protect them, with real examples from India.
5. I can say what scientists agree about habitats, and what they still do not know, such as where a habitat ends and whether life lives on other worlds.

## Terms
- **Habitat**: The place where a living thing lives and finds everything they need to stay alive. *e.g.* A pond is the habitat of a frog. They find water, insects to eat and mud to hide in.
- **Needs**: The things a living thing must have to stay alive and grow. Most need food, water, air, shelter, space and the right warmth. *e.g.* You need food, water and air every day. You need a home to keep you safe from rain and sun.
- **Shelter**: A safe place to rest, hide or sleep, away from hot sun, rain, cold and hungry animals. *e.g.* A tree hollow is a shelter for an owl. A burrow is a shelter for a desert rat.
- **Pond**: A small, still body of fresh water. Fresh water is water that is not salty. *e.g.* The pond in a village has frogs, fish, snails, dragonflies and lotus plants.
- **Forest**: A large place covered with many trees growing close together, with plants and animals living at every level. *e.g.* The Western Ghats have thick forests where hornbills, monkeys and frogs live.
- **Desert**: A place that gets very little rain all year. Deserts can be hot, like the Thar, or cold, like Ladakh. *e.g.* In the Thar Desert in Rajasthan, camels, desert foxes and khejri trees live with very little water.
- **Sea**: The huge body of salty water that covers most of the Earth. The biggest seas are called oceans. *e.g.* Fish, turtles, crabs, dolphins and corals live in the sea around India.
- **Microhabitat**: A very small habitat inside a bigger one. Micro means very small. *e.g.* The damp corner under a kitchen sink is a microhabitat. So is the space under a stone in a garden.
- **Living parts**: The parts of a habitat that are alive: plants, animals, fungi and tiny living things too small to see. *e.g.* In a forest, the trees, birds, mushrooms and ants are living parts.
- **Non-living parts**: The parts of a habitat that are not alive, but that living things need: sunlight, water, air, soil, rocks and warmth. *e.g.* In a desert, the sand, the hot sun and the rare rain are non-living parts.
- **Canopy**: The top layer of a forest, where the branches and leaves of the tall trees meet like a roof. *e.g.* Hornbills and many monkeys live high in the canopy.
- **Mangrove**: A kind of tree that grows in salty mud where rivers meet the sea. Their roots stand up out of the mud. *e.g.* The Sundarbans in West Bengal is the biggest mangrove forest in the world.
- **Coral reef**: A habitat in warm, shallow sea made by tiny animals called corals. They build hard homes that join into a huge reef. *e.g.* There are coral reefs around the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and in the Gulf of Mannar.
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold something is. We measure it in degrees Celsius with a thermometer. *e.g.* A balcony floor in the noon sun can be over 40 degrees Celsius. Inside a fridge it is about 4 degrees.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* To test whether warmth helps curd set, change only the warmth. Keep the milk, the amount of curd and the bowls the same.
- **Habitat loss**: When a habitat is destroyed or changed so much that its living things can no longer live there. *e.g.* When a pond is filled in to build houses, the frogs and fish lose their habitat.
- **Biodiversity**: How many different kinds of living things there are in a place. Bio means life. *e.g.* A forest in the Western Ghats has a huge biodiversity: thousands of kinds of plants and animals.
- **Hydrothermal vent**: A crack in the deep sea floor where water heated inside the rock comes out, full of minerals. Some vents give warm water, others very hot water. Strange animals live around them. *e.g.* Giant tube worms live around hydrothermal vents, in the dark, more than two kilometres down.
- **Microbe**: A living thing so small that you can only see them through a microscope. Bacteria are microbes. *e.g.* Microbes in milk turn it into curd. Microbes on your skin and in your tummy live with you all the time.

## Explanations
### What a habitat is · *defined*
**Definition:** A habitat is the place where a living thing lives and finds everything they need to stay alive: food, water, air, shelter, space and the right warmth.

A habitat is a place that gives a living thing what they need. A crow lives in a town because the town gives them scraps to eat, water in tanks and drains, trees and ledges to nest on, and room to fly. If one of these goes, the crow must move or they die. So when you want to know if a place is a habitat for an animal, ask six questions. Is there food? Is there water? Is there air? Is there shelter? Is there enough space? Is it the right warmth? Plants need things too: sunlight, water, air, soil and space for their roots.
- e.g. A fish's habitat is water. They get food, oxygen from the water and places to hide among water plants.
- e.g. A house sparrow's habitat is near people. They eat grain and insects, and nest in holes in walls and roofs.
- e.g. A cactus's habitat is the dry desert. They get plenty of sunlight, and they store the little rain that falls.
### Living and non-living parts work together · *defined*
**Definition:** A habitat has living parts, such as plants, animals and microbes, and non-living parts, such as sunlight, water, air, soil and rocks. Each kind changes the other.

Every habitat has living parts, like plants, animals and microbes. It also has non-living parts, like sunlight, water, air, rocks, soil and warmth. The two kinds are not separate. They change each other all the time. Rain fills a pond. The pond lets lotus plants grow. The plants make oxygen, which the fish breathe. Dead leaves fall and rot, and this makes the mud richer. So a habitat is not a box with animals inside it. It is a living system where everything affects everything else.
- e.g. In a forest, trees give shade. The shade keeps the soil damp, so worms and frogs can live there.
- e.g. Earthworms eat dead leaves and mix them into the soil. This makes the soil better for plants.
- e.g. In a desert, the rare rain makes seeds that have waited in the sand for months sprout in a few days.
### The pond: a home made of fresh water · *defined*
**Definition:** A pond is a small, still body of fresh water. It is a habitat for living things that need to stay wet or live in water.

A pond is a small, still pool of fresh water. Fresh water is water that is not salty. A pond gives water all the time, so it is a habitat for things that must stay wet, like fish, tadpoles and water snails. It gives food: tiny green algae, water plants, insects and worms. It gives shelter in the mud at the bottom and among the plants. Life in a pond lives at different levels. Some things float on top, like duckweed and water striders. Some swim in the middle, like fish. Some live in the mud, like worms and snails. What is hard about a pond? In summer, a small pond can dry up. Some frogs and fish then bury themselves in the mud and wait for rain.
- e.g. A lotus roots in the mud, but their leaves float on top to catch sunlight.
- e.g. A water strider is an insect that walks on top of the water without sinking.
- e.g. Some tiny pond animals lay eggs that can lie in dry mud for years, then hatch when the rain comes back.
### The forest: homes at every level · *defined*
**Definition:** A forest is a large area of trees growing close together, with different homes at each level, from the canopy at the top to the forest floor.

A forest is a place with many trees growing close together. Trees make a forest a habitat at many levels, like a tall building with many floors. At the very top is the canopy, where leaves catch most of the sunlight. Hornbills, monkeys and many insects live there. In the middle are smaller trees and climbers. On the dark, damp forest floor, fallen leaves rot into soil. Worms, beetles, frogs, fungi and snakes live there. A forest gives food all year, because different trees flower and fruit at different times. It gives shelter in hollows, under bark and under leaves. It also gives shade and damp air. What is hard about a forest? On the floor, there is very little sunlight, so small plants must find ways to reach the light.
- e.g. A tailorbird stitches leaves together with plant threads to make their nest.
- e.g. An owl rests by day in a tree hollow and hunts at night.
- e.g. The Western Ghats forests are home to thousands of kinds of plants and animals. Many live nowhere else on Earth.
### The desert: little water, lots of life · *defined*
**Definition:** A desert is a place that gets very little rain all year. It can be hot or cold.

A desert is a place that gets very little rain. People often think a desert is empty. It is not. The Thar Desert in Rajasthan is home to camels, desert foxes, chinkara gazelles, lizards, snakes, beetles and birds like the great Indian bustard. A desert gives plenty of sunlight and lots of open space. What is hard is water, and heat. Days can be burning hot and nights can be cold. So many desert animals stay hidden in burrows by day and come out at night or at dawn. Some get almost all their water from the food they eat. Desert plants have deep roots, small leaves or thick stems that store water. Not every desert is hot. Ladakh is a cold desert, high in the mountains, where it hardly rains and winters are freezing.
- e.g. The khejri tree's roots go very deep to reach water far under the sand.
- e.g. The desert fox shelters in a cool burrow by day and hunts at night.
- e.g. After a rare shower, the desert can turn green with new plants in a few days.
### The sea: the biggest habitat on Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** The sea is the huge body of salty water that covers most of the Earth. Its biggest parts are called oceans.

The sea is salty water that covers about seven parts out of every ten of the Earth's surface. It is so deep that most of the space where life can live on our planet is under the sea. Near the top, where sunlight reaches, tiny floating plant-like living things called phytoplankton make food from sunlight. Fish, turtles, dolphins and whales live here. Along the coast, there are rocky shores, sandy beaches, coral reefs and mangrove forests. Deeper down, it gets darker, colder and the water presses harder. At the bottom, in total darkness, live strange fish, worms and crabs. What is hard about the sea? Salt, waves, darkness in the deep and cold.
- e.g. Olive ridley turtles come in huge numbers to lay eggs on beaches in Odisha.
- e.g. Corals around the Andaman Islands build reefs that give homes to hundreds of kinds of fish.
- e.g. In the Sundarbans mangroves, tigers swim between muddy islands, and crabs climb the roots.
### Comparing the four habitats · *defined*
**Definition:** Every habitat gives some needs easily and makes others hard to meet. Living things live where they can use what the place gives.

Every habitat gives some things easily and makes other things hard. That is why different living things live in each one. Look at the table. A pond always has water but can dry up in summer. A forest has food and shelter but little light on the floor. A desert has lots of sun and space but little water. The sea has water everywhere, but it is salty, and deep down it is dark and cold. No habitat gives everything. Each living thing fits the place whose gifts they can use, and whose problems they can handle.
- e.g. A camel can go days without drinking, so the desert suits them. They would not manage in a pond.
- e.g. A frog's skin must stay wet, so they live near ponds, not in the open desert.
- e.g. A sea fish needs salty water. Most pond fish would die in the sea.
### Microhabitats: small worlds in small places · *defined*
**Definition:** A microhabitat is a very small habitat inside a bigger habitat, with its own conditions of light, warmth and dampness.

A microhabitat is a very small habitat inside a bigger one. The space under a stone is one. So is a crack in a wall, the underside of a leaf, a puddle or a rotting log. Each small place has its own weather. Under a stone it is darker, cooler and damper than on the open path next to it. That is why earthworms, centipedes and ants live there and not on the hot path. You do not need a forest, or even a garden, to study habitats. A flat has many: the damp corner under the kitchen sink, a dry bookshelf, the soil in a flowerpot and a bowl of warm milk with curd in it.
- e.g. A rotting log is a microhabitat for beetles, termites and fungi.
- e.g. A crack in a damp wall is a microhabitat for moss, spiders and geckos.
- e.g. Even one leaf can be a microhabitat for aphids and the ladybirds that eat them.
- e.g. Ants often visit the damp corner by the kitchen sink, where there are crumbs and water. They rarely visit a dry, clean shelf.
### Living things change their habitats · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things do not just live in their habitats. They change them, for example by making shade, soil, damp air or reefs.

We often say an animal lives in a habitat, as if the habitat were a box. But living things change their habitats all the time. Trees make shade and drop leaves that become soil. Their leaves give off water into the air, so a forest stays damp, and the forest helps make its own rain. Earthworms and ants dig and mix the soil. Corals build whole reefs that thousands of other creatures then live in. Beavers in some countries build dams that turn a stream into a pond. People change habitats more than any other living thing. So a habitat and its living things make each other.
- e.g. Over a large forest like the Amazon, a big share of the rain is water that the forest's own leaves gave off.
- e.g. Termites build tall mounds of mud that change the soil around them for years.
- e.g. Elephants knock over small trees and open up grassland, which helps deer and other grazers.
### People destroy habitats, and people make them · *defined*
**Definition:** Habitat loss is when a habitat is destroyed or changed so much that its living things can no longer live there. People can also protect and restore habitats.

Scientists agree that the biggest danger to wild plants and animals today is losing their habitats. People cut forests for farms, roads and towns. They fill in ponds and wetlands, and dirty rivers and seas. When the habitat goes, the living things go with it. The great Indian bustard, a tall bird of the Thar grasslands, is now very rare, partly because their open grasslands have been turned into farms and crossed by power lines. But people also protect and make habitats. In 1974, in the village of Reni in the Himalaya, Gaura Devi led the women of the village into the forest. They stood in front of the loggers and would not let them cut the trees. This was part of the Chipko movement. In Assam, Jadav Payeng planted a forest on a bare sandbar, starting in 1979. Elephants, deer and birds now live in it.
- e.g. People in Kerala, led by many groups and the poet Sugathakumari, protested to save the Silent Valley forest from being flooded by a dam. It became a national park in 1984.
- e.g. Many villages in India keep sacred groves: patches of forest where no tree is cut. They are some of the oldest forests left.
- e.g. A clay bowl of water on a balcony can make a small habitat for birds and insects in summer.
### Your body is a habitat · *defined*
**Definition:** Your body is a habitat for microbes: tiny living things, mostly bacteria, that live on your skin and inside you.

You live in habitats. But you are also a habitat. Tiny living things called microbes live on your skin, in your mouth and most of all in your tummy. Scientists have worked out roughly how many there are. A grown-up's body has about as many bacteria as human cells. Most of them are harmless or helpful. The ones in your tummy help you break down food and help protect you from germs. Your body gives them food, water, warmth and shelter, just like a pond gives a frog. Most grown-ups even have tiny mites living in the roots of their eyelashes and face hairs. They are too small to see and do no harm.
- e.g. Curd is made by bacteria. In the three bowls experiment, warm milk was a good habitat for them, and they turned it into curd. In the cold fridge, they slowed right down and waited.
- e.g. Your skin is drier on your arms and damper in your armpits. Different microbes live in each place, like different animals in a desert and a pond.
- e.g. When a baby is born, they pick up their first microbes from their mother and the world around them.
### Habitats at the edge: is there anywhere life cannot live? · *idea*
Scientists keep finding life in places that once seemed impossible. Around hydrothermal vents on the deep sea floor, there is no sunlight at all. There, bacteria make food from chemicals in the vent water, not from light. Worms, clams and crabs live off these bacteria. In 1969, Thomas Brock and his student Hudson Freeze described bacteria living in hot springs at about 70 degrees Celsius, in Yellowstone in the United States. Bacteria have been found living almost three kilometres down inside the rock, in a gold mine in South Africa. Tiny animals called tardigrades, or water bears, live in moss and can survive being dried out for years. So scientists now think life can live almost anywhere there is liquid water and some kind of energy. But nobody knows exactly where the limits are.
- e.g. Tube worms near deep sea vents have no mouth. Bacteria living inside their bodies make their food.
- e.g. Tardigrades live in the moss on walls and trees, maybe even near your home.
- e.g. Some microbes live inside the ice of Antarctica.
### Habitats beyond the Earth · *idea*
So far, Earth is the only place in the whole universe where we know for sure that life lives. Scientists are now asking: does any other world give what life needs? The big clue they look for is liquid water, plus energy and the right chemicals. Mars once had rivers and lakes, and still has ice under its ground. Europa, a moon of Jupiter, and Enceladus, a moon of Saturn, have oceans of salty water hidden under thick ice. In 2005, the Cassini spacecraft saw jets of water shooting out of Enceladus into space. Could there be living things there, like the life at deep sea vents? Nobody knows yet. This is one of the biggest questions in science.
- e.g. Rovers on Mars are searching rocks for signs of tiny life from long ago.
- e.g. Scientists call the region around a star where a planet could have liquid water the habitable zone.
- e.g. Thousands of planets have now been found circling other stars. Some are in their star's habitable zone.
### Where does a habitat end? · *debated*
On a map, we draw a neat line between a forest and a grassland, or between a pond and the land. In real life, it is usually not neat. Near the edge of a forest, trees slowly thin out. Near a pond, the ground gets wetter step by step. Scientists have argued for over a hundred years about whether habitats are real things with edges, or just names we give to parts of one big, changing world.
- e.g. The muddy edge of a pond is part pond and part land. Frogs live in both.
- e.g. Where a river meets the sea, the water is part fresh and part salty. Mangroves grow there.
- **Frederic Clements, an American plant scientist, in 1916:** The plants in a place grow together like one big living thing, almost like a body. So a forest or a grassland is a real unit with a clear place where it ends.
- **Henry Gleason, an American plant scientist, in 1926:** Each kind of plant grows wherever the conditions suit them, on their own. Plants do not grow in fixed groups. So habitats blend into each other with no real edges. Most scientists ignored him for about thirty years. In the 1950s, careful counts of plants along slopes showed he was largely right.
- **Scientists today:** Both ideas help. Each kind of living thing does spread out on their own, as Gleason said. But some places do have fairly sharp edges, like where a forest meets a lake, or where a river bank drops. Naming habitats is useful, as long as we remember that nature does not draw the lines.
### Should people live inside wild habitats? · *debated*
To protect a habitat, should we keep people out of it, or should we let the people who have always lived there look after it? This is a real argument, in India and around the world.
- e.g. The Bishnoi people of Rajasthan have protected khejri trees and blackbuck deer for hundreds of years.
- e.g. Some tiger reserves have moved villages out, so that tigers have more space.
- **Many wildlife scientists and park managers:** Some animals, like tigers and elephants, need very large areas with few people. Farms, roads and cattle inside a forest can slowly break it up. So some places need to be kept mostly free of people.
- **Many forest communities, and scientists like Madhav Gadgil, an Indian ecologist who studied sacred groves:** Local people often know a forest best and have cared for it for generations. Sacred groves and Bishnoi villages show this. Moving people out can be unfair to them, and can even leave a forest with no one to protect it.
- **Many people today:** It depends on the place and the animals. In many places, the best results come when local people share in decisions and in the benefits of protecting the habitat.

## Mostly agreed
- Every living thing needs a habitat that gives them food, water, air, shelter, space and the right warmth. *How we know:* When scientists keep animals or plants in a place without one of these, they do not survive. When a habitat loses one of them, its animals move away or die.
- Different habitats have different living things, because each habitat gives different things easily. *How we know:* Scientists have counted plants and animals in habitats all over the world for more than two hundred years. The same kinds of habitat, like deserts, have similar kinds of life, even on different continents.
- Even small places, like under a stone or under a kitchen sink, are habitats with their own conditions. *How we know:* Thermometers and moisture meters show that under a stone, it is cooler and damper than on the open ground just next to it. Small animals that dry out easily, like earthworms and centipedes, are found mostly in such places.
- Living things change their habitats, and the habitat changes them back. *How we know:* Measurements show that forests keep the air damp and help make rain. Charles Darwin measured how earthworms mix the soil, in his book of 1881.
- Habitat loss is the biggest danger to wild plants and animals today. *How we know:* A large study by hundreds of scientists for the United Nations, published in 2019, found that changing land and sea for human use is the main cause of the loss of wild species.

## How we found out
**Kathleen Crane; the pilot Jack Donnelly with Jack Corliss and Tjeerd van Andel in the submarine Alvin; Holger Jannasch; Colleen Cavanaugh, 1976 (Crane's warm-water survey); 1977 (the first dives at the Galapagos vents); 1981 (Cavanaugh's tube worm finding).** For a long time, scientists believed that every living thing on Earth depended on sunlight. Plants catch light and make food, and every animal eats plants, or eats animals that ate plants. So the deep sea floor, where no light ever reaches, was thought to have only a little life, living on scraps that sink from above. In 1976, Kathleen Crane, a young student scientist, towed an instrument she had helped build just above the sea floor near the Galapagos Islands. It found spots where the water was slightly warmer. In February 1977, she guided the next expedition back to those spots. The pilot Jack Donnelly and the scientists Jack Corliss and Tjeerd van Andel dived there in the small submarine Alvin. Around cracks seeping warm water, they found giant tube worms, big clams and crabs. Holger Jannasch and other scientists then showed that bacteria there make food from a chemical in the vent water, hydrogen sulphide, instead of from sunlight. In 1981, Colleen Cavanaugh, a student, showed that the tube worms carry these bacteria inside their bodies. A whole habitat was running on the Earth's own heat and chemicals.  
*What people believed before:* All life on Earth depends on sunlight, so the dark deep sea floor must have very little life. The vent crowds showed that a whole habitat can run on chemicals from inside the Earth instead.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Deserts and the deep sea are empty places with almost no life.** (A common belief. In the 1840s, the naturalist Edward Forbes thought nothing lived below about 550 metres in the sea. Even in the 1970s, many scientists thought the deep sea floor had only a little life, living on scraps that sink from above.; *rejected*)  
  For: In the day, a desert looks bare. The deep sea is dark and cold, with no plants.  
  Against: Desert animals hide by day and come out at night. In 1977, scientists in the submarine Alvin found crowds of worms, clams and crabs around warm vents more than two kilometres down. Empty-looking is not the same as empty.
- **A habitat is just a place, like a box that animals live in.** (A common way of talking, and how many textbooks draw habitats.; *debated*)  
  For: We can point to a pond on a map. It seems to be there before any frog moves in.  
  Against: Living things build and change their habitats. Corals build reefs. Trees make shade, soil and damp air. A sandbar became a forest because plants and animals changed it. So the place and its life make each other.
- **Zoos and aquariums can be real habitats for wild animals.** (Some zoo keepers, and many visitors; *debated*)  
  For: Good zoos give animals food, water, shelter and medical care. Some rare animals survive only because they are bred in zoos.  
  Against: A zoo cannot give the space many animals need. An elephant may walk many kilometres a day in the wild. Many scientists say the best way to save an animal is to save their wild habitat.
- **Life needs sunlight to survive.** (Most scientists, until the late 1970s; *rejected*)  
  For: Almost all food chains on Earth start with plants, which need sunlight to make food.  
  Against: At deep sea vents, and deep inside rocks, bacteria make food from chemicals, with no sunlight at all. Whole communities live on them.

## Roots
We found that warm milk can be a home for curd bacteria, and that a dark sea floor can be a home for giant worms. Now we ask what habitats tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Curd bacteria turned warm milk into curd by morning. In the fridge, the same milk and the same bacteria stayed milk. A place is a home only for living things whose needs it meets. So a place that seems empty to us may be full of life that needs other things.  
*Habit it breaks:* We learn habitats as a list of places on a map: pond, forest, desert, sea. So we think the place alone decides who lives there, and we judge every place by what we need. But the same bowl of milk was a home for bacteria in a warm corner and not in the fridge. And the dark sea floor that people called empty was crowded with life.  
*Evidence:* In the three bowls experiment, the same milk and the same curd set only where it was warm. In 1977, scientists found worms, clams and crabs living where no sunlight ever reaches, fed by bacteria that make food from chemicals.

- *who am i:* You are a good match for some places: ones with air, water, food and the right warmth. And your warm, damp body is a perfect match for trillions of bacteria.
- *what is life:* Living things sense their surroundings and look for places that suit them. Curd bacteria wait in the cold and get busy again when it is warm.
- *what is this world:* The world is not empty places with a few homes in between. Almost every place is a home for someone, even if it is not a home for us.

Habitats show how much variety life has. Each kind of living thing needs something different, so life has found a way into almost every corner of the Earth: ponds, deserts, warm milk, your tummy and the dark sea floor. No single kind of life could fill the world alone. Together, the many kinds fill nearly every place there is.

- **Isha Upanishad, verse 1:** Everything in this changing world is filled with the one Lord. So enjoy the world without grabbing it. Do not covet anyone's wealth. (A habitat is shared by everyone who lives in it. When one living thing takes too much, as people do when they cut a whole forest, everyone in that habitat loses.)
- **Acharanga Sutra (Jain), Book 1, Lecture 1:** The first lecture of this Jain text teaches that earth, water, fire, air and plants are living beings, as well as the creatures that move. It asks people to act with care, so as not to harm them. (Jain teachers saw life in places most people call empty. Their earth and water beings are not the same as bacteria. But much later, microscopes did show bacteria living in soil, water, air and milk.)
- **Aldo Leopold, A Sand County Almanac (1949), 'The Land Ethic':** We should see ourselves not as conquerors of the land, but as plain members and citizens of the community of soil, water, plants and animals. (People are members of habitats, not owners of them. That is why habitat loss harms us too.)
- Science (settled): At deep sea vents, bacteria make food from chemicals in the warm vent water instead of from sunlight. Tube worms, clams and crabs live off them.
- Science (settled): Bacteria send chemical signals to each other, and change what they do when enough of them are close together. Scientists call this quorum sensing. Some curd bacteria use such signals too.
- Other view, Many biologists: In their view, bacteria grow faster in warmth simply because the chemistry inside them runs faster, the way sugar dissolves faster in hot water. They say words like 'wait', 'sense' and 'choose' are only a way of speaking about bacteria. They also say a habitat is a real place that can be studied on its own, whoever lives there.
- Practice, Who is this place a home for? (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. Hold one hand near your tummy, without touching. Feel how warm you are. Your warm, damp body is a home for trillions of bacteria right now. Breathe in slowly. Breathe out. Now open your eyes and look around the room. Pick one spot, like a corner, a windowsill or a flowerpot. Ask quietly: who might this place be a home for?
- Ask: People once thought the dark deep sea floor had almost no life. Why do you think they were so sure?
- Ask: Is any place on Earth truly empty, or only empty of things like us?
- Ask: The fridge was a poor home for curd bacteria, but a good place to keep curd. Who decides whether a place is good?
- Ask: If trillions of bacteria live in you, are they part of you, or are you their home?

## Story: What the Sandbar Learned to Give
*Jadav Payeng is real. He began planting on a bare sandbar of the Brahmaputra in 1979, when he was about sixteen, after a flood. The dead snakes on the shadeless sandbar, the bamboo, the red ants he carried from his village, and the elephants, deer, rhinos and tigers that came later are all from his own accounts and from news reports. The forest is now called the Molai forest, after his nickname. The river's voice, the old man's words, what Jadav said and the small details of each day are imagined.*

I am the river Brahmaputra. I come down from the snow, wide and brown, and I cross Assam on my way to the sea. Every monsoon, the rain fills me and I grow strong. I carry sand and mud with me, and I drop them in heaps in the middle of my water. These heaps are islands of sand, called sandbars. People here call them chapori. I make them, and I take them away again. I have done this for longer than anyone can remember.

In 1979, after a great flood, I left one of my sandbars bare. It lay near Majuli, the big river island. When my water went down, the sun came out hard. There was no tree on that sandbar. There was no bush. There was no grass. There was only hot sand, shining white, and nowhere for anything to hide.

Snakes had come down with my flood, clinging to logs and roots. They crawled up onto the sandbar to dry. A snake cannot make their own cool. They need the world to give them shade when the sun is fierce. That day, the sandbar gave nothing. By afternoon, many of the snakes lay dead in the sand, dry and still.

A boy found them. His name was Jadav. He was about sixteen years old, and people called him Molai. He walked among the snakes for a long time. I have watched many people look at my floods and blame me. Jadav did not blame me. He crouched down and pressed his palm on the sand. Then he pulled it back fast.

"They didn't drown," he said, to nobody. "They cooked."

He went to the old people of his village. "What can I do?" he asked. "Can I carry the snakes somewhere cool?" An old man shook his head. "You cannot carry every snake," he said. "Tomorrow there will be more. Don't carry the animals to a home. Make a home, and the animals will come."

"How?"

"Grow trees," said the old man. "Start with bamboo. Bamboo is stubborn."

So Jadav came back to my sandbar with a bundle of bamboo plants. I laughed, the way a river laughs, with a small wave against the sand. Sandbars are mine. I make them and I unmake them. What were a few little plants against me?

But he came back the next day, and the next. The sand let water run straight through it, so the young plants were always thirsty. He carried my own water up from the edge in pots. He dug. He planted. Some plants died. He planted again. Other boys went to school, or to the fields, or into town. Jadav went to the sandbar.

The bamboo grew. Where there had been white sand, there was now a strip of green shade. In the next hot season, something small happened. A lizard lay in that shade at noon. A snake curled under the leaves. A spider strung a web between two stalks. Nobody had carried them there. The shade had called them.

Then Jadav did something the village laughed at. He went home and filled a bag with red ants from the trees near his house. He carried them all the way to the sandbar, while they bit his arms, and let them go. "This sand is dead," he told his family. "The ants will wake it up."

Ants dig. They carry bits of leaf underground. They mix the sand with dead things. Slowly, very slowly, my sand began to turn into soil. Now other trees could grow, trees that needed more than sand. Jadav planted those too, one after another, year after year.

Years went past. I flooded and I went down, and each time I came, I found the forest a little bigger. Fallen leaves made a soft, dark floor. Under the leaves lived worms and beetles and white threads of fungus. The roots held the soil, so I could not wash it all away. On the hottest days, it was cooler under the trees than out on the open sand. The leaves breathed water into the air, and the air in the forest stayed damp.

Birds came, and in their droppings came seeds of trees that Jadav had never planted. Deer came, because now there was grass and leaves to eat. And then, one winter, came the elephants. A whole herd waded across my water from the far bank. They walked into the forest as if it had always been there. They ate. They rested in the shade. They left their dung behind, full of more seeds. Later, a rhinoceros came, and even tigers.

For many years, almost nobody outside knew about this forest. Then forest officers, following a herd of elephants, walked into it. They could not believe one man had planted it.

People ask Jadav how he made a forest. They want a secret. I watched the whole thing, so I will tell you what I saw. He did not make the deer, or the birds, or the elephants. He could not have carried a single elephant. What he made was shade, and soil, and damp leaves, and food. He made a place that could give. All the rest walked, flew and crawled in by themselves. And each one that came gave something back: the ants made soil, the birds brought seeds, the elephants opened paths.

I still make my sandbars, and I still take them away. That is what I am. But now, when I pass that forest, I slow down a little. The trees drink from me. Their leaves breathe me back into the sky, and the sky rains me onto the hills, and the hills send me down again past the trees. I am no longer sure where the river ends and the forest begins.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the snakes did not die because of the flood. They died because the bare sandbar gave nothing a living thing needs: no shade, no shelter, no food, no damp soil. A habitat is not just a place. It is a place that gives. Jadav could not carry animals to a home. Instead he made the place give shade, soil, water and food, and the animals came by themselves. Each new living thing then changed the place too: ants made soil, birds brought seeds, trees made the air damp. Living things and their habitats make each other.

- Ask: Why did the snakes on the bare sandbar die?
- Ask: The old man said, 'Don't carry the animals to a home. Make a home.' What did he mean?
- Ask: What did the bamboo give that the bare sand did not? Who came because of it?
- Ask: Jadav carried red ants to the sandbar. How did the ants change the place?
- Ask: The village laughed at Jadav. Why do you think he kept going?
- Ask: At the end, the river says it is not sure where the river ends and the forest begins. What do you think it means?

### e4 · Three Bowls of Milk: Where Do Curd Bacteria Make a Home? (35 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Curd is made by bacteria, tiny living things too small to see. Which place is a good home for them: a warm wrapped corner, the kitchen counter or the fridge?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. By tomorrow morning, which bowl will have turned into curd: the warm one, the one on the counter, or the one in the fridge? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Only one thing changes: how warm each bowl is kept. Everything else stays the same: the same milk, the same amount in each bowl, one level teaspoon of the same curd, the same kind of bowl, a cover on each, and the same number of hours. If two things were different, we would not know which one made the change.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the milk and cools it. Use clean bowls and spoons, because other bacteria can get in from dirty ones. Eat the curd only if it smells fresh and sour, like normal curd. Throw away anything that smells bad or has fuzzy spots.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all three bowls. Draw each bowl tilted, the way it looked in the morning. Write one sentence for what you think, one for what you saw and one for why.
- **results table:** Bowl | Where it was kept | How warm the place felt (or the thermometer reading) | Tilt test next morning: runny, thick or set | Smell: milky or sour
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess and give a reason. Stir curd into warm milk in three bowls. Keep one warm, one on the counter and one in the fridge overnight. (18 min); day 2: Tilt and smell each bowl, fill in the table and say what you think, what you saw and why. Then put the fridge bowl somewhere warm and check it in the evening. (17 min)

  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child a spoon of curd and a cup of milk. 2. Explain the plan: three bowls, three places. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. 4. Write both at the top of the results table.  
  *Parent says:* Curd is made by bacteria. Bacteria are living things, far too small to see. This one spoon of curd holds millions of them. We will put some into three bowls of warm milk.   - Ask: Which bowl will turn into curd by morning? Why do you think so? (good: The warm one, because at home we keep the milk somewhere warm to set curd.; The fridge one, because curd is always kept in the fridge.; All three, because they all have curd in them.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is a good start. A common guess is the fridge, because that is where curd is kept. Do not correct it. The bowls will answer tomorrow.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up boils the milk and lets it cool. 2. Test a drop on the inside of the wrist: it should feel warm, not hot. 3.   *Parent says:* Every bowl gets the same milk and the same spoon of curd. Only the warmth will be different. Why does that matter? If two things were different, we would not know which one made the change.

  *Child mode:* Wrap the warm bowl in a towel. Put it in a closed cupboard or a covered pot. Put one bowl on the counter. Put one in the fridge. Which place feels warmest?

They grow fast when warm and slow right down in the cold. Only now, after your child has answered, share this.
  - Check: Suppose the warm bowl set into curd and the fridge bowl stayed runny. Both got the same curd. Why? → **The bacteria were in both bowls, but only the warm one was a good home for them**
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a guess and a reason first. 2. Wrap the fridge bowl in the towel and put it in the warm place. 3. Check it in the evening, after about 8 hours. 4. Tilt it and write what you see.  
  *Parent says:* The fridge bowl had bacteria too. Were they dead, or just waiting? Let's give them a warm home and see. What do you think will happen by evening? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put the fridge bowl somewhere warm. Will it turn into curd now? Why do you think so? Check it in the evening.
  - Note: It usually sets within 8 to 10 hours. If it is still runny in the evening, leave it until the next morning. This shows the cold did not kill the bacteria. They slowed down in the cold and got busy again once the place suited them. It is also why curd sets slowly in winter. At the end, all the bowls are good curd to eat, if they smell fresh and sour.
  - Think: The bacteria waited in the cold and got busy in the warm. How do you think they sensed that their place had changed?
  - Wait: Leave the fridge bowl in the warm place for about 8 hours, then tilt it.

  *Child mode:* Were you right? Say three things. What do you think now? What did the thermometer show? Why?
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: small places in one garden have very different weather. What I saw: the sunny path was 41 degrees, and under the leaves was 28 degrees, with 14 living things. Why: shade and leaves keep the sun off and hold water in.
  - Think: If one garden has five different worlds, how many worlds might there be in a whole forest?
- **s5 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put back every stone and leaf pile the way you found it.  
  *Parent says:* Let's put everything back. Each of these places is somebody's home.

### e3 · Does a Tree Give Water to the Air? (20 min)
- **question:** Do leaves give off water into the air? Is that one way a forest keeps itself damp?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. If you tie a clear plastic bag around a leafy branch in the sun, what will you see inside the bag after one hour? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, tie a second bag around a bare dry stick pushed into the same pot, in the same sunny place. Both bags stay on for the same time. Then only the leaves are different.
- **safety:** Choose a plant that is not thorny or poisonous, such as a hibiscus, guava or tulsi plant. Take the bag off after one hour. Leaves trapped in a bag in hot sun can get too hot and wilt.
- **record:** Draw both bags after one hour. Write how much water collected in each.
- **results table:** Bag | What I saw inside after 1 hour | Water collected (spoonfuls or drops)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the potted plant in full sun, on a balcony or by a sunny window. 2. Slip a clear plastic bag over a leafy stem, with plenty of leaves inside. 3. Tie the bag tightly around the stem. 4. Push a bare dry stick into the soil of the same pot and tie a second bag over it.  
  *Parent says:* We'll trap the air around these leaves. The second bag, over a bare stick, is our comparison. Only the leaves are different.  
  *Child mode:* Put the plant in the sun. Put a clear bag over some leaves. Tie it tight. Push a bare stick into the pot and put another bag over it.
- **s2 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave both bags in the sun for one hour. Set a timer.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wait an hour. What do you think will happen in each bag?  
  *Child mode:* Wait one hour. What will happen inside each bag? Guess.
  - Wait: Leave the bags for one hour, then come back.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at both bags before you touch them. 2. Take both bags off now, so the leaves do not get too hot. Hold the bottom corner of the leafy bag shut as you untie it. 3. Shake the drops into that corner. Open it over a spoon and let the water run in. 4. Do the same with the stick bag. 5. Write what you found.  
  *Parent says:* Look, the leafy bag is misty and has drops in it. Where did that water come from? We didn't pour any in. Let's take the bags off now, so the leaves do not get too hot.  
  *Child mode:* Look at both bags. Which one has water inside? Take them off the plant. Collect the drops in a spoon.
  - Note: On a warm, sunny day the leafy bag is usually misty within 15 to 30 minutes. After an hour there are usually many drops, sometimes a teaspoon or more. On a cool or cloudy day there may be only mist and a few drops: leave the bags on for two hours, or try again on a sunnier day. The bare-stick bag stays almost dry. This shows the water came out of the leaves. Plants pull water up from their roots and let it out through tiny holes in their leaves.
  - Check: In the tree water experiment, the bag on the leafy branch got misty with drops of water. Where did the water come from? → **From the leaves, which give off water into the air**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about what this means for a whole forest. Then ask for what I think, what I saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Our one branch gave all these drops in one hour. A big tree has thousands of branches. A forest has millions of trees. All that water goes up into the air. That is one reason a forest stays damp, and it helps make clouds and rain. Now tell me: what do you think, what did you see, and why?  
  *Child mode:* One branch gave all these drops. Think of a whole forest! Now say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: leaves give off water into the air. What I saw: the leafy bag had drops after an hour, the stick bag stayed dry. Why: plants pull water from the soil and let it out through their leaves. This is why a forest makes its habitat damp, and why cutting a forest can make a place drier.
  - Think: If a forest helps make its own rain, what might happen to the rain if the forest is cut down?

### c1 · Keep It Cool in the Desert (35 min)

## Worksheets
### Habitat Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Nila, a girl in Chennai, puts a bowl of water on her balcony. Next to it she hangs a tin packed with dry twigs. Which two needs of a habitat is she giving to small birds and insects?  
   Options: Water and shelter / Food and space / Sunlight and warmth / Air and food  
   **Answer:** Water and shelter. *Why:* The bowl gives water. The packed tin is a safe place to hide and rest, which is shelter. A strong answer also notices what is still missing: food, such as a plant with flowers. Picking 'food' forgets that water and twigs are not food.
2. (fill) A habitat gives a living thing food, water, air, space, the right warmth and a safe place to rest and hide. That safe place is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['shelter']. *Why:* Shelter keeps a living thing away from sun, rain and hungry animals. A tree hollow is shelter for an owl. A crack in a wall is shelter for a gecko.
3. (sort) Sort these into the living parts and the non-living parts of a pond.  
   **Answer:** a frog → Living; the mud at the bottom → Non-living; a lotus → Living; sunlight on the water → Non-living; a water snail → Living; the water itself → Non-living; green slime on the stones → Living. *Why:* Living parts grow, take in food and make more of their own kind. The green slime is tiny living algae, so it is living. Mud, water and sunlight are not alive, but every living part of the pond needs them.
4. (truefalse) At noon, the Thar Desert looks empty. So very few animals live there.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Many desert animals, like gerbils, foxes and beetles, hide in cool burrows in the heat of the day. They come out in the cool of the night. The desert only looks empty because we visit at the wrong time.
5. (write) Choose one animal you can see from your window or balcony, such as a crow, a pigeon or a gecko. Where do they find food, water and shelter? Which of these was hardest to spot?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names a real animal and a real place for each need, from watching, not guessing. For example: a pigeon eats grain that a neighbour throws down, drinks from a dripping AC pipe and sleeps on the ledge under the water tank. The hardest to spot was water, because I never saw them drinking until the second day.. *Why:* A habitat is often more than one spot. An animal may eat in one place, drink in another and sleep in a third.
6. (mcq) Olive ridley turtles spend most of their lives far out at sea. Yet each winter, thousands crawl onto beaches in Odisha at night. Why do they come ashore?  
   Options: To lay their eggs in warm, dry sand, which the sea cannot give / To find food on the beach / To rest, because swimming tires them / To get away from the cold sea  
   **Answer:** To lay their eggs in warm, dry sand, which the sea cannot give. *Why:* The sea gives a turtle food and space to swim. But turtle eggs must sit in sand to hatch, and only a beach gives that. So a turtle's habitat is two places: the open sea and one beach. A strong answer notices that one living thing can need different places at different times of life.
7. (match) Match each living thing to the part of their body that suits their habitat.  
   **Answer:** Camel → wide, flat feet that do not sink in sand; Lotus → big, waxy leaves that float and stay dry; Hornbill → a strong beak for picking fruit high in trees; Olive ridley turtle → flippers for swimming far in the sea. *Why:* Each body suits the place where they live. If you swapped the habitats, the same body parts would be little help: flippers are slow on sand, and wide camel feet cannot swim.
8. (write) A cactus lives well in a desert, but a cactus planted in a pond rots and dies. Explain why, using the word habitat.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that a cactus is built to store water and needs dry soil, so the desert is their habitat. In a pond, the roots sit in water all the time, cannot get air and rot. A pond is a good habitat for a lotus, but not for a cactus.. *Why:* A habitat is good only for the living things whose needs it meets. Too much of something, like water, can be as bad as too little.
9. (fill) The damp, dark corner under a kitchen sink is a small habitat inside a bigger one. A small habitat like this is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['microhabitat']. *Why:* Micro means very small. Under a sink, under a flowerpot, a crack in a wall and the soil of a potted plant are all microhabitats. Each one can be cooler, damper or darker than the room around it.
10. (odd) Which one is the odd one out? These are things that might help curd bacteria grow: milk, warmth, sunlight, time. Which do they not need, and why?  
   **Answer:** sunlight. *Why:* The warm bowl in the three bowls test was wrapped in a towel, in the dark, and it still set first. So curd bacteria need food, water and warmth, but not sunlight. Plants need sunlight. Many living things do not.
11. (mcq) A pond near Madurai dries up in April. What do some frogs do?  
   Options: Dig into the damp mud and wait there until the rain comes back / Hop to the sea / Turn into fish / Stay on the dry cracked mud in the sun  
   **Answer:** Dig into the damp mud and wait there until the rain comes back. *Why:* Deep down, the mud stays damp and cool, so it shelters them until the rains fill the pond again. Picking 'hop to the sea' forgets that sea water is salty, and most frogs cannot live in it.
12. (draw) Draw a pond cut in half, so we can see inside. Show one living thing at the top of the water, one in the middle and one in the mud at the bottom.  
   **Answer:** For example: a lotus leaf or a water strider at the top, a fish in the middle, a snail or a worm in the mud. A strong drawing also shows where the light is brightest.. *Why:* One pond holds several small habitats at different levels. The top gets the most light and air, and the mud is darkest.
13. (truefalse) A fish lives under water, so a fish does not need air.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Water holds a little air mixed into it, and fish take air in through their gills. That is why a fish tank has a pump that bubbles air into the water. Air is one of the needs of a habitat, even under water.
14. (write) House sparrows used to nest in holes in old walls and under tiled roofs. Many Indian cities now have new buildings with smooth walls and glass. In many of these cities, sparrows have become rare. Which need of a habitat could be missing? Explain.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names shelter: smooth new walls give no holes for a nest. It explains that a bird with food and water still cannot raise chicks without a safe nesting place. It may add one more possible cause, such as fewer insects to feed the chicks.. *Why:* Scientists think the loss of nesting places is one reason, along with fewer insects for chicks. A habitat must meet every need. If one is missing, the living thing cannot stay, however much of the others there is.
15. (mcq) A crow nests in a neem tree, eats scraps from the bins and drinks from the water tank on the roof. What is the crow's habitat?  
   Options: All three places together, and the space between them / Only the nest / Only the water tank / The crow has no habitat in a city  
   **Answer:** All three places together, and the space between them. *Why:* A habitat is everywhere a living thing finds their needs. For this crow, that is the tree, the bins and the tank. Picking 'only the nest' mixes up a habitat with a home to sleep in.
### Habitats Near and Far (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at the chart from the five worlds test. An earthworm needs cool, damp soil. Which place would suit them best?  
   Options: Under the leaves / The sunny path / The grass / Under a bush  
   **Answer:** Under the leaves. *Why:* Under the leaves was the coolest place, at 28 degrees. Fallen leaves also keep the soil damp and give food as they rot. The sunny path was hottest and driest, so an earthworm would dry out there. A strong answer uses the numbers in the chart.
2. (mcq) In the tree water test, why did we also tie a bag around a bare stick with no leaves?  
   Options: To check that the water came from the leaves, and not from the air or the plastic / To save plastic bags / Because the stick was thirsty / To make the test take longer  
   **Answer:** To check that the water came from the leaves, and not from the air or the plastic. *Why:* The leafy bag filled with drops and the bare-stick bag stayed dry. So the water came from the leaves. Without the bare stick, someone could say that damp air made the drops. A strong answer calls the bare stick the comparison.
3. (match) Match each habitat with what is hard about living there.  
   **Answer:** Desert → very little water; Forest floor → very little sunlight; Deep sea → darkness and cold; Small pond → it can dry up in summer. *Why:* No habitat gives everything easily. Each one has its own problem, and the living things there have their own ways to handle it.
4. (mcq) Ladakh gets very little rain and has freezing winters. What kind of habitat is it?  
   Options: A cold desert / A rainforest / A coral reef / It cannot be a desert, because it is cold  
   **Answer:** A cold desert. *Why:* A desert is a place with very little rain, hot or cold. Ladakh is a cold desert. Many people think every desert is hot and sandy, but the word is about rain, not heat.
5. (sort) Sort these. Did people destroy a habitat, or protect or make one?  
   **Answer:** filling a pond to build houses → Destroy; the women of Reni village guarding their trees in 1974 → Protect or make; cutting a forest for a road → Destroy; Jadav Payeng planting a forest on a sandbar → Protect or make; throwing plastic into the sea → Destroy; keeping a sacred grove uncut for hundreds of years → Protect or make. *Why:* People change habitats more than any other living thing. We can destroy them, but we can also protect them and even make new ones.
6. (mcq) A gecko waits on the wall next to a tube light every night. What does that spot give her?  
   Options: Food, because insects fly to the light and she catches them / Water from the light / Nothing: she is there by chance / Sunlight to grow  
   **Answer:** Food, because insects fly to the light and she catches them. *Why:* Many insects fly to lights at night, so the wall by the light is full of food for a gecko. That spot is part of her habitat. Picking 'by chance' does not fit, because she comes back to the same spot every night.
7. (write) Before Jadav Payeng planted trees, snakes washed onto the bare sandbar died in the hot sun. Explain how the trees changed the non-living parts of the sandbar, so that animals could live there.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names at least two non-living changes. The trees made shade, so the ground got cooler. Their fallen leaves rotted into soil. Their roots held the sand in floods, and the air under them stayed damper. Then animals found what they needed and came by themselves.. *Why:* Living things change their habitats. Here the trees changed heat, soil and dampness, which are all non-living parts.
8. (perspective) People disagree about whether a zoo can be a good habitat for an elephant.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses the needs of a habitat as evidence. For example: space is a need, and a zoo cannot give enough of it. It then gives a fair point from another side, for example that food, water and a vet are needs too. It may say the best answer is to protect wild forests.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. A zoo meets some needs very well and others poorly. The real argument is about which needs matter most, and what to do when wild habitats are lost.
9. (label) Label the layers of a forest: canopy, middle layer, forest floor.  
   **Answer:** ['canopy (top)', 'middle layer', 'forest floor (bottom)']. *Why:* Each layer is a different habitat. Hornbills live in the canopy, where there is the most light and fruit. Climbers and small trees live in the middle. Frogs, worms and fungi live on the dark, damp floor.
10. (mcq) In the mangroves of Pichavaram, in Tamil Nadu, the river meets the sea. At high tide the water is salty, and at low tide it is fresher. What is hard about living there?  
   Options: The saltiness and the water level keep changing every day / There is no water at all / It is always dark / Nothing is hard there  
   **Answer:** The saltiness and the water level keep changing every day. *Why:* Living things in mangroves must handle salt one hour and fresher water the next, and mud that floods and drains. Some mangrove trees push out extra salt through their leaves. Picking 'nothing is hard' forgets that every habitat has its own problem.
11. (truefalse) A habitat must be a big place, like a forest or a sea.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A habitat can be tiny: the bowl of warm milk was a habitat for curd bacteria, and the soil in a flowerpot is a habitat for ants and worms. What makes a place a habitat is that it meets the needs of the living things there.
12. (write) A girl keeps her tulsi plant on a sunny balcony. Her cousin keeps the same kind of plant in a dark bathroom with no window. Predict which plant will do better after a month, and explain using the needs of a habitat.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer predicts that the balcony plant will grow better. It says that plants use sunlight to make their own food, so the dark bathroom does not meet that need. It may add that the bathroom plant will grow pale, thin stems that lean towards any light.. *Why:* Light is part of a plant's habitat, because plants make their food from sunlight, air and water.
13. (mcq) On a summer afternoon, the top of the sand in the Thar Desert can be hotter than 50 degrees. A little way down, the sand is much cooler. What does a burrow give a desert animal?  
   Options: Shelter and the right warmth / Food and sunlight / More heat / Space to run fast  
   **Answer:** Shelter and the right warmth. *Why:* Sand is slow to let heat through, so a burrow stays cool in the day and warm at night. It is also a safe place to hide. Picking 'more heat' is the opposite of what the burrow does.
14. (order) A forest helps make its own rain. Put these steps in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Tree roots take up water from the soil', 'Leaves give off water into the air', 'The damp air rises and forms clouds', 'Rain falls on the forest again']. *Why:* The bag test showed the second step: leaves give off water. Over a big forest like the Amazon, much of the rain is water that the forest's own leaves gave off. So the living trees change the non-living rain.
15. (write) Some animals live in the forest canopy, and others on the forest floor below. Choose one need of a habitat other than light. Explain how the canopy and the floor give that need differently.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one need and compares both layers. For example, water: the canopy is hot, windy and dries fast after rain, so canopy animals drink from rain caught in leaves and tree holes. The floor stays damp for days, which suits frogs, worms and fungi that dry out easily.. *Why:* One forest holds several habitats on top of each other. The same need is met in different ways at each level.
### Habitat Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In the five worlds test, the sunny path was 41 degrees and under the stone was 29 degrees. Farhan, a boy in Class 4, says: 'An earthworm would be fine on the path. It is only 12 degrees more.' What is wrong with his thinking?  
   Options: Earthworms breathe through damp skin, so the heat and dryness of the path can kill them / Nothing: 12 degrees is a small difference / Earthworms like 41 degrees / Thermometers do not work outside  
   **Answer:** Earthworms breathe through damp skin, so the heat and dryness of the path can kill them. *Why:* For a small, damp-skinned animal, 12 degrees and dry ground are the difference between life and death. Remember the snakes on Jadav Payeng's bare sandbar. Farhan treats a small number as a small effect.
2. (perspective) Two plant scientists argued for years about where one habitat ends and the next begins.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses something real as evidence. For example: at the edge of a pond, the plants change slowly from water plants to grass, which fits Gleason. It also says what Clements gets right: from far away, a forest and a grassland look like two separate things with an edge.. *Why:* Most scientists now side mostly with Gleason, because careful counts of plants along slopes showed each kind spreading out on their own. But maps still draw edges, because they are useful. Scientists still argue about how much plants depend on each other.
3. (mcq) Tube worms live at deep sea vents. They have no mouth, and no sunlight reaches them. How do they get food?  
   Options: Bacteria living inside them make food from chemicals in the vent water / They eat sunlight / They eat sand / They do not need food  
   **Answer:** Bacteria living inside them make food from chemicals in the vent water. *Why:* The bacteria use a chemical in the vent water to make food, and share it with the worm. Picking 'do not need food' forgets that every living thing needs energy. Before 1977, scientists thought all food chains started with sunlight.
4. (write) Europa, a moon of Jupiter, has an ocean of salty water under thick ice. No sunlight reaches it. Which discovery in the deep sea gives scientists hope that something could live there? Explain.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names the deep sea vents found in 1977. It explains that life there gets energy from chemicals, not sunlight. So an ocean with no light might still be a habitat, if it has warmth and the right chemicals. It says honestly that nobody knows yet.. *Why:* Every living thing we know needs liquid water and some source of energy. The vents showed that the energy does not have to be sunlight.
5. (write) Design a fair test to find out whether milk sets into curd faster with one spoon of curd or with three spoons. Say what you would change, what you would keep the same, and what you would check.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer changes only the amount of curd: one bowl gets one spoon, the other gets three. It keeps the same milk, the same amount, the same kind of bowl and the same warm place. It checks both bowls by tilting them every 2 hours and writing down when each one sets.. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing, just as the three bowls test changed only the warmth.
6. (mcq) In the Square Metre Safari, a lawn had 6 kinds of living things and a square under a tree had 14. Kavya, a girl in Class 4, says: 'Under trees there are always more kinds.' Is that a fair conclusion from one test?  
   Options: No. One test in one garden is a start, but we need to test more places / Yes, one test is always enough / No, lawns always have more / Tests cannot tell us anything  
   **Answer:** No. One test in one garden is a start, but we need to test more places. *Why:* One result can be a fluke. Scientists repeat tests in many places before saying 'always'. Picking 'tests cannot tell us anything' goes too far. The test is good evidence for this garden.
7. (write) Some people say a city has no habitats, only buildings. Use three examples from your own home or street to argue against this.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives three real places and says who lives there and which need is met. For example: ants under the kitchen sink find water and crumbs, a gecko on the wall by the light finds insects, and pigeons on the window ledge find shelter from rain.. *Why:* Cities are full of microhabitats. Wherever a living thing finds their needs, there is a habitat.
8. (fill) In the Square Metre Safari, we counted how many different ____ of living things were in the square, not how many of each.  
   **Answer:** ['kinds', 'types', 'sorts']. *Why:* Counting kinds tells us how many different living things a place can support. Ten ants of one kind count as one kind. A place with more kinds usually meets more different needs.
9. (write) As curd bacteria grow in milk, they make the milk sour. That sourness stops many other germs from growing in it. Explain how the curd bacteria have changed their own habitat, and how that helps them.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the bacteria turned sweet milk into sour curd, so their habitat is now different from what they started in. Most other germs cannot grow in sour milk, so the curd bacteria have the food and space to themselves. It may add that this is why curd keeps longer than milk.. *Why:* Living things change their habitats, and sometimes the change helps them. Trees make shade for their own seedlings in the same way.
10. (sort) Sort these. Is a living thing changing their habitat, or is the habitat changing a living thing?  
   **Answer:** earthworms turn fallen leaves into soil → A living thing changes the habitat; a dry pond makes frogs dig into the mud → The habitat changes a living thing; trees make the ground below them shady and damp → A living thing changes the habitat; the cold of a fridge slows curd bacteria down → The habitat changes a living thing; corals build a reef that fish live in → A living thing changes the habitat; freezing winters make marmots in Ladakh sleep for months → The habitat changes a living thing. *Why:* It works both ways. A habitat shapes what its living things do, and living things change their habitat. The two depend on each other.
11. (mcq) A grandmother says: 'All bacteria are dirty. We should kill every one of them with strong soap.' A grown-up's body has about as many bacteria living in and on them as human cells. What is the best answer to her?  
   Options: Most of those bacteria are harmless or helpful, and they help keep harmful germs out / She is right: all bacteria make us ill / Bacteria only live on dirty skin / Soap kills every bacterium on the body  
   **Answer:** Most of those bacteria are harmless or helpful, and they help keep harmful germs out. *Why:* Your body is a habitat for trillions of microbes. Most are harmless or helpful: tummy bacteria help digest food and leave less room for harmful germs. It is still right to wash hands before food, because a few kinds are harmful. A strong answer is kind and gives the evidence.
12. (order) Put these habitats in order of size, from the smallest to the biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['a spoon of curd', 'the soil in a flowerpot', 'a village pond', 'the forests of the Western Ghats', 'the sea']. *Why:* Each one meets the needs of the living things in it, from curd bacteria to whales. Size is not what makes a place a habitat. The sea is the biggest of all, with life at every depth.
13. (write) Scientists look for liquid water when they search for life on other worlds. Could life somewhere else live without water? Say what you think, and what evidence would change your mind.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives a view with a reason. For example: every living thing found so far, from vents to hot springs, needs liquid water, so I think life elsewhere will need it too. It then names evidence that would change the view, such as finding something living in a place with no liquid water at all.. *Why:* This is an open question. Water is the best clue we have, but nobody knows yet whether life elsewhere must work like life on Earth.
14. (truefalse) When a forest is cut down, the animals can simply move to the next forest.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The next forest already has animals using its food, water and space, so there is not enough for newcomers. Many animals also cannot cross the farms and roads in between. This is why losing habitats is the biggest danger to wild living things.
15. (write) Gaura Devi and the women of Reni hugged their trees in 1974 to stop them being cut. Explain how saving the trees also saved homes for other living things.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that the trees are habitats for birds, insects, monkeys and fungi, and that the trees keep the soil and the air damp. So cutting the trees would take away food, shelter and water for many others. Saving the trees kept the whole habitat.. *Why:* When one big living thing goes, the habitat of many others goes with them. This is why losing habitats is the biggest danger to wild living things today.

## Applications
- Wildlife sanctuaries and national parks protect whole habitats, not just single animals.
- Farmers keep ponds, hedges and trees on their land so that frogs, birds and bees, which help crops, have homes.
- City planners leave lakes, wetlands and tree belts so the city stays cooler and has fewer floods.
- Gardeners grow host plants and nectar flowers to bring butterflies.
- Scientists count living things in squares, called quadrats, to check whether a habitat is healthy.
- Zoos and aquariums try to copy habitats, with water, shelter, warmth and space.
- People who plant mangroves along coasts protect villages from storms and give fish a nursery.
- Bird baths and water bowls on balconies help birds survive Indian summers.
- Engineers studied termite mounds to design buildings that stay cool without much electricity.
- Space scientists study life at deep sea vents and in very hot springs to know where to look for life on other worlds.
- Doctors study the microbes in our bodies, our inner habitat, to help with digestion and illness.
- Fisheries protect coral reefs and mangroves, because many fish grow up there.

## Spotted around us

## Open questions
- **Is there life anywhere other than Earth?** Mars, Europa and Enceladus may have liquid water, but nobody has yet found any living thing beyond Earth.
- **What are the limits of where life can live?** Scientists keep finding microbes in hotter, deeper, saltier and colder places than they thought possible. Nobody knows where the real limit is.
- **How many kinds of living things share Earth's habitats?** Scientists have named about two million kinds. Estimates of how many exist in total range from several million to far more, mostly tiny insects and microbes no one has named.
- **How much life is hidden deep inside the Earth's rocks?** Microbes have been found kilometres down in mines and boreholes. Some scientists think there may be a huge amount of life down there, but it is very hard to measure.
- **Do habitats have real edges, or are they just names we give?** Scientists have argued about this since Clements and Gleason, about a hundred years ago. Both ideas are partly right, and the answer may depend on the place.
- **How exactly do the microbes in our bodies affect our health and even our mood?** Scientists have found many links, but which microbes do what, and why, is still being worked out.
- **Can a destroyed habitat ever be fully brought back?** Some forests and wetlands have been restored, but scientists argue about whether they ever become exactly what they were, and how long it takes.

## Connected maths
- In a made-up five worlds test, the sunny path was 41 degrees Celsius. Under the stone, it was 29 degrees Celsius. How many degrees cooler was it under the stone? **12 degrees cooler.**. The path was 41 degrees. Under the stone was 29 degrees. 41 take away 29 is 12. You can check by counting on: 29 and 1 makes 30, and 11 more makes 41. 1 + 11 = 12. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
- In a made-up three bowls test, a family gently tilted the bowls to check them every 2 hours. The warm bowl had set at 6 hours. The counter bowl set at 10 hours. The fridge bowl was still runny at 24 hours. Where were the bacteria busiest? How many hours sooner did that bowl set than the counter bowl? **The warm bowl. It set 4 hours sooner than the counter bowl.**. The bowl that sets first is where the bacteria grew fastest. That is the warm bowl, at 6 hours. The counter bowl took 10 hours. 10 take away 6 is 4. So the warm bowl set 4 hours sooner. The fridge bowl had not set even after 24 hours, so the bacteria were slowest there. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In a made-up tree water test, one leafy branch gave 2 teaspoons of water in one hour. A small tree has 10 branches like this one. How much water might the whole tree give in one hour? **About 20 teaspoons.**. Each branch gave 2 teaspoons. There are 10 branches. 10 groups of 2 is 20. So the tree might give about 20 teaspoons in one hour. A big forest tree has many more branches, so they give much more. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In a made-up Square Metre Safari, the lawn square had these kinds of living things: 2 kinds of grass, 2 kinds of small plants, 1 kind of ant and 1 kind of spider. The square under a tree had 14 kinds. How many more kinds were under the tree? **8 more kinds.**. First add the lawn: 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 6 kinds. Then find the difference: 14 take away 6 is 8. So the square under the tree had 8 more kinds of living things. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- In the Habitat Corner diary, these visits were seen at the water bowl in two weeks: crows 6, bulbuls 4, sparrows 3, bees 8. Make a pictograph where one picture stands for 2 visits. How many pictures does each row need? **Crows 3 pictures, bulbuls 2, sparrows 1 and a half, bees 4.**. Each picture means 2 visits. Crows: 6 divided by 2 is 3. Bulbuls: 4 divided by 2 is 2. Sparrows: 3 is one 2 and one more, so 1 whole picture and a half picture. Bees: 8 divided by 2 is 4. (queues `math.data.pictographs.L3`)
