# Transparent, translucent and opaque: which things we can see through
`sci.light.transparent-opaque.L3`

**Summary.** A frosted bathroom window lets daylight in, yet nobody can see through it. Materials meet light in three ways. A transparent material, like window glass, lets light straight through, so you see a clear picture. A translucent material, like butter paper, lets light through but scatters it, so you see a glow but no picture. An opaque material, like a steel plate, lets no light through and makes a dark shadow. A thing can change group. A drop of oil makes paper clearer, and crushing turns clear ice white.

**Intro.** *A frosted bathroom window lets plenty of daylight into the bathroom. Yet you cannot see the tree outside through the glass. How can light get through when the picture does not?* Light gets through frosted glass, but the picture of the tree is lost on the way. In this unit you will test materials from your home with a torch and a card of big letters. You will sort them into three groups: transparent, translucent and opaque. By the end you will know where the picture goes, and why a few drops of oil can bring it back.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what transparent, translucent and opaque mean, and sort real things into the three groups by testing them with light.
1. I can run a fair test to compare how see-through materials are, measure with a ruler, and show my results in a table and a bar chart.
2. I can explain that translucent things scatter light at many tiny edges inside them, and that filling the gaps with oil or water makes them clearer.
3. I can choose the right material for a job, like windows, curtains, sunglasses, lanterns and food containers, and say why.
4. I can explain that being see-through is not fixed. It depends on the thickness, the kind of light and which side is brighter.
5. I can say what scientists agree about see-through materials, and what they still do not know, like what glass really is.
6. I can describe some surprising examples: my glowing hand, see-through glass frogs, light that takes thousands of years to leave the Sun, and the early universe that light could not cross.

## Terms
- **Light**: Light is what lets us see. It comes from things like the Sun, a bulb or a flame, and travels out from them. *e.g.* Sunlight comes in through the window in the morning.
- **Material**: A material is what a thing is made of. *e.g.* A window is made of glass. A curtain is made of cloth.
- **Transparent**: A transparent material lets light go straight through. You can see clearly through it. *e.g.* Window glass, clean water and cling film are transparent.
- **Scatter**: To scatter light is to send it off in many different directions. *e.g.* Frosted glass scatters light, so you see only a blur through it.
- **Translucent**: A translucent material lets light through but scatters it. You see a glow or blurry shapes, but not a clear picture. *e.g.* Butter paper, a frosted bathroom window and a thin white cotton saree are translucent.
- **Absorb**: To absorb light is to take it in. The light's energy stays in the material and warms it a little. *e.g.* A black umbrella absorbs sunlight and gets hot.
- **Opaque**: An opaque material lets no light through. It absorbs the light, sends it back, or both. You cannot see anything through it. *e.g.* A wooden door, a steel plate and a brick wall are opaque.
- **Shadow**: A shadow is the darker patch behind a thing that blocks light. *e.g.* An opaque steel plate makes a dark shadow. Butter paper makes only a pale shadow, because some light gets through it.
- **Frosted glass**: Frosted glass is glass with a rough surface. The roughness scatters light, so the glass is translucent. *e.g.* Many bathroom windows have frosted glass. Light comes in, but nobody can see in.
- **Tinted**: A tinted material is see-through but darkened, so less light gets through. *e.g.* Sunglasses and some car windows are tinted.
- **Ultraviolet light**: Ultraviolet light is light from the Sun that our eyes cannot see. Too much of it burns skin and can harm eyes. *e.g.* Good sunglasses are marked 'UV400'. That means they block ultraviolet light.
- **Glare**: Glare is light so bright that it is hard to see properly. *e.g.* Sunlight on a white wall or on water can cause glare.
- **Jaali**: A jaali is a screen of stone, wood or metal with holes cut in a pattern. The solid parts block light and the holes let it in. *e.g.* Old forts, mosques and tombs in India have carved stone jaalis.
- **Fair test**: A fair test changes only one thing at a time, and keeps everything else the same. *e.g.* In the gap test, we change only the material. The letters, the light and the ruler stay the same.
- **Molecule**: A molecule is a tiny group of atoms joined together. Atoms are the tiny pieces everything is made of. *e.g.* Water is made of water molecules, far too small to see.

## Explanations
### Three things light can do when it meets a material · *defined*
**Definition:** When light meets a material, it can go straight through, it can be scattered in all directions, or it can be stopped by being absorbed or sent back.

Light always does something when it reaches a material. If most of it goes straight through, the material is transparent. If it goes through but is scattered on the way, the material is translucent. If none of it gets through, the material is opaque. Most materials do a bit of each. We name them by what they mostly do.
- e.g. Window glass sends most light straight through.
- e.g. Butter paper lets light through, but scatters it.
- e.g. A steel plate lets none through. It sends some back and absorbs the rest.
### Transparent: light goes straight through · *defined*
**Definition:** A transparent material lets light pass straight through without scattering it, so you can see a clear picture through it.

Look out of a clean window. The light from the tree outside comes through the glass in straight lines, all in order. So the picture of the tree stays sharp. Transparent does not mean invisible. You can still see a glass of water on the table. That is because a little light bounces off its surfaces, and its edges bend the light a little.
- e.g. Window glass, spectacle lenses and a clear plastic bottle are transparent.
- e.g. Air is transparent. That is why you can see across a whole cricket ground.
- e.g. Clean water in a steel tumbler is transparent. You can see the bottom of the tumbler.
### Translucent: light gets through, but scattered · *defined*
**Definition:** A translucent material lets light through but scatters it on the way, so you see a glow or blurry shapes but no clear picture.

Inside butter paper there are millions of tiny paper threads with air between them. Each time light goes from air into a thread, or back out, some of it changes direction. After thousands of these little turns, the light comes out in every direction. The light still gets through, but the picture is lost. Here is a surprise. If the paper touches a page of writing, you can read it. Lift the paper a little and the words blur away. The further the light travels after leaving the paper, the more the light from each letter spreads out.
- e.g. A frosted bathroom window lets light in but hides you.
- e.g. A thin cotton saree lets light through, so a room behind it glows.
- e.g. A paper lantern glows all over, but you cannot see the bulb inside.
### Opaque: light is stopped · *defined*
**Definition:** An opaque material does not let light through. It absorbs the light, sends it back, or both.

A wooden door is opaque. Light that hits it is partly absorbed, and partly sent back into the room. None comes out of the other side. That is why an opaque thing makes a dark shadow behind it. Black things absorb most of the light that falls on them, so they warm up in the sun. Shiny metal sends most light back, which is why a steel plate gleams.
- e.g. A brick wall, a wooden almirah and a steel plate are opaque.
- e.g. A thick blackout curtain is opaque, so a room can be dark in the afternoon.
- e.g. A black umbrella is opaque and absorbs sunlight. Its cloth gets hot.
### Many tiny edges make things cloudy · *defined*
**Definition:** A material looks cloudy or white when light bounces off many tiny edges inside it. Fewer edges make it clearer.

One big clear crystal of sugar is quite see-through. A pile of sugar grains is white, because light bounces off every grain's edges. Stir the sugar into water and it vanishes. The water is clear again, because the sugar has broken into molecules far too small to bounce light. The same is true for ice and snow. A clear ice cube is transparent. Crushed ice and snow are white, because of all their edges. Bubbles trapped in ice make it cloudy too. Oil on paper fills the air gaps between the threads. Then light passes from thread to oil without bouncing much, so the paper turns see-through.
- e.g. Foam on top of water is white, though the water and the air are both clear.
- e.g. A spot of oil from a bajji makes the newspaper see-through.
- e.g. A wet white T-shirt is more see-through than a dry one.
### Why glass is see-through but metal is not · *defined*
**Definition:** Light comes in tiny packets of energy. Glass lets light through because its electrons cannot absorb the packets of light we see. Metal stops light because it has loose electrons that catch the light and send it back.

Every atom has electrons, tiny parts that move around its middle. An electron can absorb a packet of light only if the packet has just the right amount of energy for it. In glass, the electrons need a much bigger packet than the light we see carries. So they cannot absorb it, and the light passes on. Metals are different. Some of their electrons are loose and free to move. They catch the light easily and send it back. That is why metals are opaque and shiny. This is a simple telling of an idea from quantum physics, the science of very tiny things.
- e.g. Clear glass lets the light we see through, but stops much of the Sun's ultraviolet light, which has bigger packets.
- e.g. A steel plate is opaque and shiny because of its loose electrons.
- e.g. Gold beaten very thin, called gold leaf, lets a little greenish light through.
### See-through depends on the kind of light · *defined*
**Definition:** A material can be transparent to one kind of light and opaque to another.

Our eyes see only some kinds of light. There are other kinds we cannot see. Window glass lets the light we see through, but blocks much of the ultraviolet light that gives sunburn. Your hand stops most light, but lets red light through, which is why your fingers glow red over a torch. A brick wall is opaque to the light we see, yet radio waves and Wi-Fi signals, which are kinds of light we cannot see, go through it. So 'transparent' always means transparent to some kind of light.
- e.g. Your fingertips glow red over a torch, because red light gets through skin and blood best.
- e.g. A phone gets signals inside a brick house.
- e.g. Doctors use X-rays, another invisible light, to see bones through skin.
### Which side is brighter · *defined*
**Definition:** Through a thin curtain or net, you can see from the darker side into the brighter side, but not the other way.

A thin curtain lets a little light through each way. In the daytime the street is much brighter than your room. Lots of light comes in from the street, so you can see out. Only a little light goes out from your dim room, and it is swamped by the bright light bouncing off the curtain. So people outside see only a bright cloth. At night it flips. Your lamp makes the room the bright side. Now people outside can see in, and you see only the curtain. Many families add a thick, opaque curtain for the night.
- e.g. In the day, a thin net curtain hides you. At night with the lights on, it does not.
- e.g. A shadow-puppet show uses a translucent screen with a lamp behind it, so the audience sees the shapes.
- e.g. A car with tinted windows is easy to see into at night if its lights are on inside.
### Using the three kinds: windows, curtains and sunglasses · *defined*
**Definition:** People choose transparent, translucent or opaque materials depending on whether they want to see through, to let light in privately, or to block light.

Windows are transparent, so we can see out and let sunlight in. Bathroom windows are often translucent, so light comes in but nobody can see in. Thin curtains soften bright light. Thick curtains are opaque, so a baby can sleep in the afternoon. Sunglasses are tinted. They let only part of the light through, so bright sunshine is less dazzling. Good sunglasses also block ultraviolet light. Never look at the Sun, even with sunglasses. They do not make it safe.
- e.g. Milk often comes in opaque packets, and medicine in brown bottles,, because light can spoil them.
- e.g. A paper lantern, called a kandil, makes a bulb's light soft and spread out.
- e.g. A stone jaali lets light and air in but keeps a room private and cool.
### See-through animals · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals are almost transparent. This helps them hide, because predators look straight through them.

Being see-through is a clever way to hide. Many jellyfish are almost all water, so they are nearly invisible in the sea. Glass frogs in the rainforests of Central and South America have see-through skin on their bellies. But blood is red, and red is easy to spot. In 2022, scientists found that a sleeping glass frog hides almost all their red blood cells inside their liver. That makes them about two or three times more see-through. When they wake, the blood flows out again. Nobody yet knows how the frog stops this packed blood from clotting.
- e.g. A jellyfish can be very hard to see in clear water.
- e.g. A glass frog's heart can sometimes be seen beating through their skin.
- e.g. Some tiny shrimps and baby fish are almost transparent.
### Light that takes thousands of years to leave the Sun · *debated*
The inside of the Sun is not transparent. It is so packed with hot particles that light cannot travel straight. A packet of light made in the Sun's middle bumps into a particle, bounces off somewhere new, bumps again, and again, more times than anyone could count. So it takes a very long time to wander out to the surface. Once it is out, it reaches Earth in about eight minutes. Scientists agree on the wandering. They give different answers for how long it takes, because it is hard to work out.
- e.g. Some of the sunlight on your face today may have started its journey in the Sun before people built the first cities.
- e.g. A crowded railway platform is like the Sun's inside: you cannot walk straight across, you keep bumping and turning.
- **Some scientists' estimates:** About 10,000 years or a little more.
- **Other scientists' estimates:** As long as 170,000 years. NASA gives the whole range, from about 10,000 to 170,000 years, because the answer depends on how the Sun's insides are modelled.
### The universe was once a glowing fog · *defined*
**Definition:** For about the first 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe was opaque. Then it cooled and became transparent, and the light from that moment still reaches us today.

Long ago the whole universe was hot, crowded and full of loose tiny particles. Light kept bumping into them, like light in thick fog. Nothing could be seen across it. As the universe stretched and cooled, the particles joined into atoms. Suddenly light could travel freely. The universe became transparent. That very old light is still all around us. It has stretched into a kind of invisible light called the cosmic microwave background. Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson found it by accident in 1965. A little of the snow on an old TV screen came from it.
- e.g. Morning fog in winter over a field: you cannot see the trees until it clears.
- e.g. Satellites called COBE, WMAP and Planck have made maps of this oldest light.
### Matter that is perfectly see-through: dark matter · *debated*
Stars and galaxies move as if they are being pulled by much more matter than we can see. Vera Rubin, an American astronomer, measured this carefully in the 1970s by watching how fast stars go round in galaxies. Scientists call the missing matter dark matter. It does not give out light, and it does not block light. Light goes straight through it. So it is more see-through than the cleanest glass. Nobody knows what it is.
- e.g. Light from far galaxies is bent by the pull of dark matter, but never blocked by it.
- e.g. There seems to be about five times more dark matter than ordinary matter.
- **Most astronomers:** Dark matter is made of a kind of tiny particle we have not found yet. Big experiments deep underground are searching for it.
- **Some scientists, like Mordehai Milgrom, who proposed this in 1983:** There may be no dark matter. Maybe gravity works a little differently over huge distances than we think.
### Nothing is perfectly transparent · *idea*
Even the clearest glass is not perfect. At night, in a lit room, you can see a faint picture of the room in the window. That is because a little light bounces back at each surface of the glass, about 4 parts in every 100. Physicists found that light comes in tiny packets. Nobody can say in advance which packet will go through and which will bounce back. Only the share is fixed. Most physicists think this is pure chance. A few think some hidden detail, not yet found, sets the path of each packet.
- e.g. Fibres of very pure glass carry internet light under the oceans for many kilometres.
- e.g. Katharine Burr Blodgett, an American scientist, invented a thin coating in 1938 that made glass even clearer, by stopping most of that bounce.
- **Most physicists:** Which packet of light goes through is truly a matter of chance. Only the share can be predicted.
- **Some physicists:** There may be hidden details we cannot yet measure, which decide each packet's path.

## Mostly agreed
- Materials can be sorted by how much light they let through: transparent, translucent and opaque. *How we know:* Shine a torch through glass, butter paper and steel, and anyone can see the difference on the wall. Children all over the world do this test in class.
- Translucent and white materials scatter light at many tiny edges inside them. *How we know:* Fill the gaps with oil or water and many of them turn clearer. Grind clear glass into powder and it turns white.
- Whether a material is see-through depends on its thickness and on the kind of light. *How we know:* Thin gold leaf lets light through, but a gold coin does not. Glass passes the light we see but stops much ultraviolet light, which scientists measure with special meters.
- Ordinary glass is a solid. It does not slowly flow. *How we know:* Edgar Zanotto, a Brazilian scientist, worked out in 1998 that cathedral glass would take far longer than the age of the universe to sag noticeably. Old Roman glass has kept its shape for 2,000 years.
- The universe became transparent about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. *How we know:* The light from that moment, the cosmic microwave background, was found in 1965 and has been mapped very carefully by satellites.

## How we found out
**Isaac Newton, 1704.** It seems obvious that white paper hides what is behind it because paper is a blocking material. Isaac Newton, an English scientist, looked more carefully. In his book Opticks, published in 1704, he described simple observations. Clear glass, ground into a powder, turns white, and you cannot see through it. Foam is made of clear water and clear air, yet it is white. Paper soaked in water or oil becomes much more see-through. Newton argued that the tiny parts of almost every material let some light through. A thing looks white or opaque because light bounces off the many edges between its tiny parts and the gaps around them. Fill the gaps with water or oil, and there are fewer edges to bounce from, so more light goes straight through. So the old idea was wrong. What hides the view is often the edges inside a thing, not what it is made of. You can check this yourself with one drop of oil on a sheet of paper.  
*What people believed before:* White paper and other opaque things block light because their material blocks light. Newton showed that powdered clear glass turns white, and oiled paper turns see-through, so the many inside edges do the blocking.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Transparent things are invisible.** (A common idea, especially in children's stories about invisible things; *rejected*)  
  For: You look straight through a clean window and often do not notice the glass.  
  Against: You can still see a glass of water on the table. A little light bounces off its surfaces, and its edges bend light. Truly invisible things would have to bounce and bend no light at all. A very clean window comes close, which is why birds sometimes fly into one.
- **Glass is a liquid that flows very slowly. That is why old church windows are thicker at the bottom.** (A popular story told for over 100 years, even by some teachers; *rejected*)  
  For: Some very old windows in Europe really are thicker at the bottom.  
  Against: Old glass was made by spinning a blob into a disc, so it came out uneven. Builders usually put the thick edge at the bottom, but not always. Some old panes have their thick edge at the top. Measurements show glass would take far longer than the age of the universe to sag at room temperature.
- **Darker sunglasses always protect your eyes better.** (A common belief when buying sunglasses; *rejected*)  
  For: Dark lenses cut down a lot of the light we can see, so they feel more protective.  
  Against: Darkness and ultraviolet protection are different things. A dark lens with no ultraviolet filter lets the harmful light through. Eye doctors say to look for a 'UV400' or '100% UV protection' label. A light lens with that label protects better than a dark one without it.
- **Nothing is truly opaque. Make anything thin enough and some light gets through.** (Many physicists; *accepted*)  
  For: Gold leaf, a plant leaf, your fingertips and even a thin slice of rock let some light through. Geologists study rocks by slicing them so thin that light shines through.  
  Against: Some materials, like thick metal, stop practically all light. In daily life, calling them opaque is simply true.
- **Light that goes into a translucent material is lost.** (A natural guess, because the picture disappears; *rejected*)  
  For: Behind butter paper, the clear picture is gone.  
  Against: Most of the light is still there. It has only been mixed up in direction. A room behind a frosted window is still bright. Only the picture, the neat order of the light, is lost.
- **We already understand glass completely.** (A common assumption, since glass is so ordinary; *debated*)  
  For: People have made glass for over 4,000 years. We know exactly how to make every kind of glass we use.  
  Against: Scientists still argue about what glass really is. Its atoms are in no neat order, like a liquid's, yet it is stiff like a solid. In 1995 the Nobel Prize winner Philip Anderson called the nature of glass perhaps the deepest unsolved problem in the science of solids.

## Roots
We have been learning how light goes straight through some things, is scattered by others, and is stopped by the rest. Now we ask what this tells us about seeing, and about the world.

**Deep truth.** Butter paper lying on a word lets you read it. Lift it 2 cm, and the paper still glows, but the word is gone. The paper scatters the light from each letter in every direction, mixing it with light from the others. So a frosted window lets in light but shows no view: it mixes up the light from everything outside.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think we can see through something whenever light gets through it. A frosted bathroom window is bright, yet it shows no view, so light alone is not enough.  
*Evidence:* In the gap test, butter paper lying on the card let you read the word. Lifted 2 cm, it still glowed with light, but the word was gone.

- *who am i:* Your hand looks opaque, yet red light passes through your fingers. Even you are a little translucent.
- *what is life:* Some animals hide by being almost transparent. A sleeping glass frog even packs most of their red blood cells into their liver while they sleep.
- *what is this world:* The whole universe was once like a thick fog. Then it cleared, and light could travel. We still see that first clear light today.

Everything comes from one source and is connected. In this unit, the same torch light met glass, butter paper and a steel plate. Glass let it pass, butter paper scattered it, and steel sent it back or absorbed it. The light did not change, and neither did the materials. Transparent, translucent and opaque name what happens when the two meet.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** A father, Uddalaka, asks his son to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the son cannot see the salt. But every sip tastes salty. The father says: in the same way, what is finest is everywhere, unseen. 'You are that.' (Sugar stirred into water becomes clear and invisible, but it is still there in every sip. Not seeing something does not mean it is not there.)
- **Isha Upanishad 15:** The face of truth is covered by a golden lid. The seeker asks for the lid to be taken away, so the truth can be seen. (A golden lid is opaque. Gold sends light back, so it hides what is under it, however beautiful it looks. The verse asks what might be covering what is true.)
- **The Bible, 1 Corinthians 13:12:** 'For now we see through a glass, darkly.' The Greek word Paul used means a mirror. Mirrors then were polished metal, and showed only a dim, blurry picture. Paul says what we know now is like that picture, and one day we may see clearly. (In this unit, a blurry picture came from light being scattered on its way. An old metal mirror blurred for a similar reason: its surface was never perfectly smooth. Paul uses that blur to say that what we know now is not yet the clear view.)
- Science (settled): Whether a material is see-through depends on its thickness, its tiny edges and the kind of light. Glass passes the light we see but stops much ultraviolet light. A brick wall stops the light we see but lets radio waves through.
- Science (settled): For about 380,000 years, the young universe was opaque, like fog. Then it became transparent, and that first light still reaches us.
- Other view, The Charvaka thinkers of ancient India, and many scientists today: Transparency is just atoms and light, and nothing more. There is no one source behind it. Light passes through glass because of electrons and energy, and that is the whole story. This is an honest view, and many careful people hold it.
- Practice, Look at, then look through (3 min): Let's stand at a window together. First, look at the glass. Can you find a speck of dust, or a smudge? Now let your eyes go through the glass, to the farthest thing you can see. Breathe slowly three times. Did you stop noticing the glass? It was there the whole time. Now hold your fingers together up to the bright sky, never towards the Sun. Can you see a thin red glow between them?
- Ask: Butter paper let the light through, but not the word. Where did the picture of the word go?
- Ask: When you look through clean glass, are you seeing the glass, or only what is behind it?
- Ask: Is the sugar in the water still sugar, if you can't see it?
- Ask: What might be all around us, like dark matter, that we cannot see at all?

## Story: The Flowers That Bloomed at Night
*Lucknow's chikan embroidery on fine cotton muslin is real, and so is the way a thin curtain hides the dim side and shows the bright side. Meenu, her family and the lane are made up.*

Meenu lived above her father's cloth shop, in the old part of Lucknow. The lane outside was so narrow that two rickshaws could not pass. Her window looked down on it. Across the window hung a curtain her mother had made. It was white muslin, as thin as a breath, and Ammi had stitched it all over with white flowers. That kind of stitching is called chikan. It had taken her four months, sewing in the quiet hour after everyone else had gone to sleep.

In the daytime, Meenu loved that curtain. She could sit behind it and watch the whole lane. The woman frying jalebis in a black pan. The goat that ate posters. The postwoman who sang old film songs. And nobody ever looked up at her. Once she waved at Mrs. Rizvi from the sweet shop. She looked straight at the window and did not wave back. She saw nothing at all.

"It's a magic curtain," Meenu told her little sister Zoya. "I can see out, and nobody can see in."

Five days before Diwali, Meenu began her secret. Abba's old paper lantern, the kandil that hung over the shop door every year, had torn in last year's rain. "Never mind," he had said. "The shop can be dark this year." Meenu decided it would not be dark. She would make him a new one. She had red kite paper, thin sticks of bamboo, a pot of glue made of flour and water, and eleven rupees of her own, spent on gold paper for the edges.

After school there was homework, and then tuition. So she worked at night. She shut her door, lit the lamp on her desk, and cut and glued until her eyes stung. The curtain hung quietly beside her. The lane beyond it was dark.

On the third morning, Abba put down his tea and looked at her over his glasses. "Meenu-beti," he said, "who was the giant in your window last night? She had scissors as big as a door."

Zoya laughed so hard that milk came out of her nose. "I saw her too, from the lane," she said. "She was cutting out a big square. Then she put her finger in her mouth, because she cut it."

Meenu's face went hot. Abba only smiled and went down to open the shop. He did not ask what she was making. But Meenu sat very still. How had they seen? The curtain was magic. She had tested it.

That afternoon she tested it again. She went down into the lane, stood by the jalebi pan and looked up at her own window. She saw a square of white cloth, glowing a little in the sun. Nothing behind it. No desk, no bed, not even the dark shape of the cupboard. The magic was working.

That evening she tried it the other way round. She put Zoya in her room with the lamp lit, and told her to dance. Then she ran down the stairs, out into the lane, and looked up.

There was Zoya. Not her face, but all of her, a dark dancing shape, as clear as a puppet at a puppet show. She could see her sister's elbows. She could see the bright lamp on the desk, and the cupboard behind her. And across the whole curtain, Ammi's white flowers had turned dark. They stood out like a painted garden, every petal and every leaf, black against the glowing cloth.

Meenu stood in the lane for a long time. Ammi came out to buy coriander and found her there. "What are you staring at?"

"Your flowers," said Meenu. "They're dark. In the day they're white."

Ammi looked up. She had never seen her curtain from the lane at night. She tilted her head. "Where the cloth is thin, the light gets through," she said slowly. "Where I stitched, the thread is thick. The light can't get through the thick parts so easily."

"And the curtain isn't magic," said Meenu. She worked it out aloud. "In the day, the lane is bright and my room is dim. At night, my room is bright and the lane is dark. Whoever is on the bright side shows. The curtain only hides the dim side."

For the last two nights, Meenu took the heavy old razai out of the trunk. It was a thick cotton quilt that no light could get through. She hung it over the window, in front of the muslin. From the lane, Zoya reported, her window was now just a dark square, like every other window. She finished the lantern on the last night. When she held Abba's torch inside it, the red paper glowed all over. You could not see the torch at all, only a warm red light.

On Diwali evening, when the lane was full of lamps and noise, Meenu took the razai down. She hung the new kandil just inside her window, behind the muslin, with a small electric bulb inside it, the way Abba always hung his. She switched off her desk lamp. Then she ran down to the shop and pulled Abba out by the hand into the lane.

"Look up," she said.

Abba looked up. The window glowed red, like a coal. In the middle glowed the lantern itself, brightest of all, with its gold edges showing as dark lines. And all around it, dark against the red light, bloomed Ammi's flowers. There were hundreds of them, every one she had stitched in all those quiet hours. People in the lane stopped and looked up too. The jalebi woman said, "Wah." Ammi came out of the shop with a needle still in her hand. She stood beside them and said nothing at all for a long while.

Later, Abba carried the kandil down and hung it over the shop door, where it belonged. But every evening of that Diwali, Meenu switched on a bulb behind the curtain, so that the lane could see the garden that had been there all along.

**For the parent:** For the parent: thin muslin is translucent. Through it, you can see from the dim side into the bright side, but not the other way. By day the lane is bright, so Meenu sees out and is hidden. At night her lamp makes her room the bright side, so the lane sees her. The thick stitches block more light than the thin cloth. So they look white when light falls on them from the front, and dark when light comes from behind. The quilt is opaque and hides everything. Meenu solves her problem by understanding the light, not by finding a better curtain. The story also quietly asks whose patient work goes unseen until someone looks from the right side.

- Ask: In the daytime, why could Meenu see the lane, but Mrs. Rizvi could not see her?
- Ask: Why could Zoya see Meenu's shadow at night, through the same curtain?
- Ask: Why did Ammi's white flowers look dark from the lane at night?
- Ask: Why did hanging the razai over the window keep Meenu's secret?
- Ask: Ammi had never seen her own flowers from the lane at night. Why do you think she was quiet for so long?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Gap Test: how far until the words blur? (30 min)
- **question:** How see-through is each material? How far can we lift it off a page before we can no longer read the words?
- **predict:** Before you test, line up the materials. Put the one you think you can lift highest first, and the one that blurs the word soonest last. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only the material. Use the same card of letters, in the same light, and keep your eyes at the same height every time. Test each material 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** This test is safe. Do it in daylight by a window, or under a bright room light. Never use the Sun directly.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart, with one bar for each material. Make each bar as tall as its middle gap.
- **results table:** Material | Try 1: gap when the words blurred (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Middle value (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write LIGHT on a card in big letters, about 2 cm tall. 2. Cut postcard-sized pieces of the 5 materials. 3. Ask your child to put the materials in a line, from most see-through to least.  
  *Parent says:* Here are five materials. Which one do you think you can see through best? Put them in a line. Put the most see-through first.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the five materials. Which can you see through best? Put them in a line. Best first.
  - Ask: Why did you put that one first? (good: It is clear, like glass.; It is very thin.; I can see my hand through it.)
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the card on the table near a window or under a bright light. 2. Stand the ruler upright beside it, with 0 at the table. 3. Make a stack of books next to the card. 4. Your child rests their chin on the books, so their eyes stay at the same height.  
  *Parent says:* We'll keep your eyes at the same height every time, so the test is fair. Rest your chin on the books.  
  *Child mode:* Put your chin on the books. Keep it there the whole time. That keeps the test fair.
  - Note: The chin rest matters. If the eyes move closer or further, the results change. Keep the room light the same for every try.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the first material flat on the card. 2. Ask: can you read the word? 3. Hold the material by two edges, pulled gently tight, so it stays flat. 4. Lift it slowly, about 1 cm each second. 5. Your child says 'Stop!' the moment the word blurs so it cannot be read. 6. Hold still, read the gap on the ruler and write it down. 7. Do this 3 times for each material.  
  *Parent says:* Can you read the word now? Good. I'll lift it slowly. Shout 'stop' the moment you can't read it any more.  
  *Child mode:* Can you read the word? Now watch it as the material lifts. Shout 'Stop!' when you can't read it.
  - Note: Butter paper and tissue usually let you read the word when they touch the card, then blur within 1 or 2 cm. That surprises most children. If a material never blurs, like cling film, write the top of the ruler. To find the middle value, put the three numbers in order and take the middle one.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the middle values, with one bar for each material.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn the numbers into a picture. Each material gets a bar. The taller the bar, the higher we could lift it before the word blurred.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each material. Make it as tall as its middle number. Taller means more see-through.
  - Check: In the gap test, the plastic bag could be lifted 6 cm and the tissue only 1 cm. Which is more see-through? → **The plastic bag**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sort the five materials into transparent, translucent and opaque, using the results.  
  *Parent says:* Which material never blurred? That one is transparent. Which ones let light through but lost the word? Those are translucent. We tested nothing opaque. What could we add?  
  *Child mode:* Which one never blurred? That is transparent. Which let light through but lost the word? Those are translucent.
  - Think: Butter paper let you read the word when it touched the card. So is it transparent when it touches, and translucent when it is lifted?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now say three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: cling film is the most see-through. What I saw: the word never blurred with cling film, but with tissue it blurred at 1 cm. Why: the tissue has lots of tiny threads that scatter the light, and cling film is smooth all through.
### e2 · The Oil Spot Window (25 min)
- **question:** Can a drop of oil or water make paper more see-through? Does it last?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will a drop of water make paper more see-through, less see-through, or the same? What about a drop of oil? Which will last longer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same sheet of paper for all three spots, so the paper is the same. Use one drop of each, the same size. Score all the spots in the same light.
- **safety:** Use a little oil only, and wipe up any spills so nobody slips. Wash your hands after.
- **record:** Fill in the table with a score for each spot. 0 means you see nothing of the print. 1 means grey smudges where the print is. 2 means you see the shapes of words but cannot read them. 3 means you can read the words.
- **results table:** Spot | Score straight away (0 to 3) | Score after 1 hour (0 to 3)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw three circles on the paper, each as big as a coin. 2. Label them DRY, WATER and OIL. 3. Ask your child to guess what each drop will do.  
  *Parent says:* Paper is made of tiny threads with air between them. What do you think a drop of water will do to it? And a drop of oil?  
  *Child mode:* Draw three circles. Write DRY, WATER and OIL. Guess what each drop will do.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one drop of water in the WATER circle. 2. Put one drop of oil in the OIL circle. 3. Rub each gently with a clean fingertip so it spreads to fill the circle.  
  *Parent says:* One drop each. Rub it in gently, so it fills the circle.  
  *Child mode:* Put one drop of water in its circle. One drop of oil in its circle. Rub gently.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the paper over a newspaper page. 2. Score each circle from 0 to 3. 3. Then hold the paper up to a window and look at the light through each circle.  
  *Parent says:* Look through each circle at the print. Score it. Zero is nothing. One is grey smudges. Two is word shapes. Three is words you can read.  
  *Child mode:* Put the paper on a newspaper. Score each circle from 0 to 3. Three means you can read the words.
  - Check: Which spot let you see the newspaper words best? → **The oil spot**
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave the paper flat in a safe place for one hour.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wait one hour. What do you think will happen to each spot?  
  *Child mode:* Leave the paper for one hour. Guess what will change.
  - Wait: Leave it for 1 hour
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the paper over the newspaper again. 2. Score each circle again. 3. Compare with the first scores.  
  *Parent says:* Look again. Which spot changed? Why do you think the water spot went back to white?  
  *Child mode:* Score each circle again. Which one changed? Why?
  - Ask: Why did the water spot go back to white? (good: The water dried up.; Air came back into the gaps.)
  - Note: The water spot usually goes back to white as it dries, because air fills the gaps again. The oil spot stays see-through, because oil does not dry away. Long ago, before glass was cheap, many people used oiled paper in their windows. Butter paper is made from pulp beaten very fine and pressed hard, so its threads pack tightly with few air gaps. That is why it is more translucent than ordinary paper.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: oil makes paper more see-through, and it lasts. What I saw: the oil spot scored 3 and stayed 3. The water spot scored 2, then went back to 1. Why: oil and water fill the air gaps, so light scatters less. Water dries away, oil does not.
### e3 · Sunglasses Lab: when do they help? (25 min)
- **question:** Do sunglasses help us see better in bright light? Do they help indoors?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will you read smaller letters with sunglasses on, or without, in bright light outside? What about inside a dim room? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same letter chart, stand the same 3 big steps away, and change only the sunglasses and the place.
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun, with or without sunglasses. Sunglasses do not make it safe. A grown-up goes outside with you.
- **record:** Write the number of the smallest row you could read, each time.
- **results table:** Place | Smallest row I can read without sunglasses | Smallest row I can read with sunglasses
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On white paper, write six rows of letters with a dark pen. 2. Make row 1 about 2 cm tall, then 1 cm, 7 mm, 5 mm, 3 mm and 2 mm. Use a fine pen for the small rows. 3. Number the rows 1 to 6.  
  *Parent says:* We'll make an eye chart. Each row has smaller letters. We'll see which is the smallest row you can read.  
  *Child mode:* Write six rows of letters. Make each row smaller. Number them 1 to 6.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go to a bright balcony, terrace or courtyard in the late morning. 2. Hold the chart where the sunlight falls on it. 3. Your child stands 3 big steps away, with their back to the Sun. 4. They read the smallest row they can, without sunglasses, then with.  
  *Parent says:* Keep your back to the Sun. Never look at it. Read me the smallest row you can. Now put on the sunglasses and try again.  
  *Child mode:* Turn your back to the Sun. Never look at it. Read the smallest row. Now try with sunglasses.
  - Note: White paper in sunshine causes glare. Some children read one row smaller with sunglasses. Others see no difference outside. Both are honest results.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go into a dim room, with the curtains closed and one small light on. 2. Hold the chart 3 big steps away. 3. Your child reads it without sunglasses, then with.  
  *Parent says:* Now we're inside, where it's dim. Read the smallest row. Now with the sunglasses.  
  *Child mode:* Now read the chart in a dim room. Then with sunglasses. Is it harder?
  - Check: In the dim room, the sunglasses made the letters… → **Harder to read**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the label on the sunglasses together. Look for 'UV400' or '100% UV protection'.  
  *Parent says:* The darkness of the lenses is one thing. Blocking ultraviolet light, which we can't see, is another. The label tells us which.  
  *Child mode:* Find the label on the sunglasses. Does it say UV400? That means they block ultraviolet light.
  - Note: Dark lenses without ultraviolet protection can be worse than none. Behind dark lenses the pupils open wider, so more ultraviolet light gets in. Cheap toy sunglasses often have no ultraviolet filter.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* When do sunglasses help? When do they get in the way? What did you see, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: sunglasses help in bright sun, but not indoors. What I saw: outside I read row 5 with sunglasses and row 4 without. Inside I read row 4 without and only row 2 with them. Why: they let only part of the light through. That cuts glare in sun, but leaves too little light indoors.

### a3 · Cloudy Ice, Clear Ice (15 min, 2 days)

### From Glass to the Universe (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Why is glass see-through, but a steel plate is not?  
   Options: Glass has tiny holes in it / The electrons in glass cannot soak up the light we see, but metal has loose electrons that catch it / Steel is always thicker  
   **Answer:** The electrons in glass cannot soak up the light we see, but metal has loose electrons that catch it. *Why:* In glass, the light we see does not have the right energy for the electrons to soak it up, so it passes by. Metals have loose electrons that catch light and send it back. A common guess is holes, but glass has no holes you could see through.
2. (mcq) Meera sits by a closed glass window in the sun all afternoon. Why is she unlikely to get sunburnt?  
   Options: Window glass blocks much of the Sun's ultraviolet light / Glass makes sunlight cooler / Sunlight cannot pass through glass  
   **Answer:** Window glass blocks much of the Sun's ultraviolet light. *Why:* Sunburn is caused mainly by ultraviolet light, which we cannot see. Window glass lets the light we see through, but stops much of the ultraviolet. So glass is transparent to one kind of light and nearly opaque to another.
3. (truefalse) A brick wall is opaque, so no kind of light can pass through it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Brick blocks the light we see, but radio waves and Wi-Fi signals, which are kinds of light we cannot see, pass through it. That is why a phone works indoors. Opaque always means opaque to a certain kind of light.
4. (mcq) Glass frogs live in rainforests in Central and South America. When a glass frog sleeps, the frog becomes more see-through. How?  
   Options: They stop breathing / They pack most of their red blood cells into their liver / They drink a lot of water  
   **Answer:** They pack most of their red blood cells into their liver. *Why:* Scientists found this in 2022. Red blood is easy to spot, so while they sleep the frog hides most of their red blood cells in the liver. When they wake, the blood flows out again.
5. (mcq) For about the first 380,000 years after the Big Bang, light could not travel far through the universe. What was the universe like?  
   Options: Transparent, like clean glass / Opaque, like a thick fog / Completely dark and empty  
   **Answer:** Opaque, like a thick fog. *Why:* The early universe was hot and full of loose particles that light kept bumping into, like light in fog. When it cooled and atoms formed, it became transparent. 'Dark and empty' is a natural guess, but it was glowing hot.
6. (truefalse) Many people say old church windows are thicker at the bottom because glass slowly flows downwards over hundreds of years. Is this true?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Old glass was made unevenly, and builders often put the thick edge at the bottom. Some old panes have the thick edge at the top. Scientists worked out that glass at room temperature would take far longer than the age of the universe to sag enough to see.
7. (truefalse) Light made deep in the centre of the Sun reaches the Sun's surface within a few seconds.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The inside of the Sun is so crowded that light keeps bumping into particles and changing direction, like a person trying to cross a packed crowd. Estimates for how long it takes range from about 10,000 to 170,000 years.
8. (write) A gold coin is opaque. Gold beaten into gold leaf, thinner than paper, lets a little greenish light through. What does this tell you about whether 'opaque' is fixed for a material?  
   **Answer:** It is not fixed. The same gold is opaque when it is thick and lets light through when it is very thin. So being opaque depends on thickness, not only on what a thing is made of.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the gold example to make a general rule, and may add another example, like many sheets of butter paper.
9. (perspective) Scientists have argued about what glass really is.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and gives evidence, for example that window glass does not sag or flow, so it acts like a solid. It then states the other side fairly: inside, its atoms are jumbled like a liquid's, unlike the neat rows in salt or ice. The best answers say both facts are true, which is why the question is hard.. *Why:* This is a real open question. Glass acts like a solid but is arranged like a liquid, so each side points to true evidence.
10. (write) In the gap test, butter paper let you read the word when it touched the card, but not when you lifted it 2 centimetres. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Butter paper scatters light only a little. When it touches the card, the light from each letter has no room to spread before it comes through. When it is lifted, the scattered light spreads out over a wider patch, and the letters blur into each other.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the further scattered light travels, the more it spreads. That is why tracing paper works only when it lies flat on the picture.
11. (mcq) In 1938, Katharine Burr Blodgett invented a very thin coating for glass. With it, glass bounced back almost no light. Why did this make the glass clearer?  
   Options: More light went through, instead of bouncing off / The coating made the glass thinner / The coating made its own light  
   **Answer:** More light went through, instead of bouncing off. *Why:* Ordinary glass bounces back a little light at each surface, which makes a faint shine and reflections. Her coating stopped most of that bounce, so more light went through. Coatings like hers are used on camera lenses and spectacles today.
12. (sort) For each thing, do we want it transparent, translucent or opaque?  
   **Answer:** spectacle lenses → Transparent; the front of a fish tank → Transparent; a lampshade for a reading lamp → Translucent; a cover for a bird cage at night → Opaque; a blackout curtain for sleep → Opaque; a car windscreen → Transparent. *Why:* We choose a material by what the job needs: a clear view, soft light without glare, or darkness. A strong sorter can say the job behind each choice.
13. (mcq) Why do birds sometimes fly into a clean glass window?  
   Options: The glass is transparent, so the bird sees the trees through the glass and not the glass itself / Birds like the shine on glass / The glass is opaque to birds  
   **Answer:** The glass is transparent, so the bird sees the trees through the glass and not the glass itself. *Why:* Clean glass lets most light straight through, so the view behind the glass looks like open space. Sticking patterns or stickers on windows helps birds see that the glass is there.
14. (write) Invent a new use for a material that lets one kind of light through but blocks another kind.  
   **Answer:** Any idea with a clear reason. For example: a window that lets in the light we see but blocks heat from the Sun, so rooms stay cool in summer.. *Why:* Real engineers make glass like this. A strong answer names which kind of light passes, which is blocked, and why that helps.
15. (write) At night, a thin curtain shows you to the street when your lamp is on. Without buying a thicker curtain, give two ways to keep your room private in the evening. Explain each one.  
   **Answer:** For example: switch off the lamp near the window and use a small lamp further inside, so the room is less bright than the street. Or hang a thick opaque cloth, like a razai, over the window at night. Or keep a light on outside the window so the outside is the bright side.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the rule that the bright side is the one that shows, or uses an opaque material to block the light.
16. (match) Match each material to what it mostly does to light.  
   **Answer:** clear window glass → lets most light straight through; butter paper → scatters light in many directions; black cloth → absorbs light, soaking it up; a steel plate → bounces most light back. *Why:* Light that meets a material can pass straight through, be scattered, be absorbed or bounce back. Most materials do a mix. The biggest part decides how the material looks.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Farhan looks through a glass of water at a poster on the wall. He can read it clearly. Then he stirs in a spoon of milk. What happens?  
   Options: He can still read the poster clearly / The water turns translucent, and the poster goes blurry / The water turns completely opaque at once  
   **Answer:** The water turns translucent, and the poster goes blurry. *Why:* Milk has many tiny drops of fat floating in it. Each drop has an edge that scatters light. So light still gets through, but the picture is lost. A strong answer uses the word translucent.
2. (mcq) Lalitha holds a steel plate, a sheet of butter paper and a clear glass in front of a lamp, one at a time. Which one lets light through, but no clear picture?  
   Options: The steel plate / The butter paper / The clear glass  
   **Answer:** The butter paper. *Why:* Butter paper is translucent. Light gets through, but it is scattered in many directions, so no clear picture comes through. Steel blocks the light, and clear glass lets the picture through.
3. (mcq) Which of these makes the darkest shadow on the wall?  
   Options: A glass tumbler / A sheet of butter paper / A steel tumbler  
   **Answer:** A steel tumbler. *Why:* The steel tumbler is opaque and blocks all the light. Butter paper makes a pale shadow, and a glass tumbler hardly any.
4. (mcq) In the gap test, why do we try each material three times?  
   Options: Because one try can be a fluke / Because three is a lucky number / To use up the materials  
   **Answer:** Because one try can be a fluke. *Why:* A hand can wobble, or someone can say 'stop' too late. Three tries, and taking the middle result, give a fairer answer.
5. (mcq) In the gap test, the plastic bag could be lifted 6 cm before the word blurred, and the butter paper only 2 cm. How many times higher was the plastic bag?  
   Options: 3 times / 4 times / 8 times  
   **Answer:** 3 times. *Why:* 6 divided by 2 is 3. So the plastic bag could be lifted 3 times as high. Picking 4 usually means subtracting, 6 take away 2, instead of dividing.
6. (mcq) Water splashes on the rough side of a frosted bathroom window. What happens to the wet patch?  
   Options: It becomes clearer, because water fills the tiny bumps and light scatters less / It becomes darker, because water blocks light / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** It becomes clearer, because water fills the tiny bumps and light scatters less. *Why:* The rough surface has many tiny bumps with air between them, and each edge scatters light. Water fills the gaps, so light goes through more straightly and the patch looks clearer.
7. (mcq) A heap of salt looks white, but one big salt crystal is see-through. Why?  
   Options: The heap has many tiny edges that scatter light / The heap is made of a different salt / The big crystal is wet  
   **Answer:** The heap has many tiny edges that scatter light. *Why:* Each grain has edges where light bounces. Many grains scatter light in all directions, so the heap looks white. One big crystal has few edges.
8. (mcq) Kavya is packing Pongal sweets in boxes. She wants shoppers to see the sweets without opening the box. Which lid should she use?  
   Options: A clear plastic lid / A butter paper lid / A cardboard lid  
   **Answer:** A clear plastic lid. *Why:* To see the sweets, the lid must let a clear picture through, so it must be transparent.
9. (mcq) Why do sunglasses help on a bright, sunny day?  
   Options: They let only part of the light through, so there is less glare / They make the Sun safe to look at / They make the light warmer  
   **Answer:** They let only part of the light through, so there is less glare. *Why:* Sunglasses are tinted. They cut the bright light so your eyes are comfortable, and good ones also block ultraviolet light. They never make it safe to look at the Sun.
10. (mcq) At night, Meenu's room is lit and the street outside is dark. She looks at her closed glass window. What does she mostly see?  
   Options: The dark street, very clearly / Her own room reflected, like in a mirror / Nothing, the glass turns black  
   **Answer:** Her own room reflected, like in a mirror. *Why:* Glass lets most light through but bounces a little back. At night the room is the bright side, so the little light bounced back from the room is much more than the light coming in from the dark street.
11. (mcq) Divya holds one page of her notebook up to the window, and light glows through it. Then she holds up the whole closed notebook. What happens?  
   Options: Light glows through just the same / Almost no light gets through, so the notebook is nearly opaque / The notebook becomes transparent  
   **Answer:** Almost no light gets through, so the notebook is nearly opaque. *Why:* Each page scatters and soaks up some of the light. With many pages, almost none is left at the other side. So how see-through a thing is depends on how thick it is.
12. (mcq) Which question about glass do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Whether glass lets light through / What kind of material glass really is, deep down / Whether glass can break  
   **Answer:** What kind of material glass really is, deep down. *Why:* Everyone agrees glass is transparent and can break. But glass is stiff like a solid while its atoms are jumbled like a liquid's, and scientists still study what kind of material that makes it.
13. (mcq) When you press a torch under your fingers in the dark, which colour of light gets through your hand the most?  
   Options: Blue / Red / Green  
   **Answer:** Red. *Why:* Skin and blood soak up most colours, but red light gets through best. That is why your fingers glow red.

## Applications
- Windows of glass let in light and a view, while keeping out wind, rain and dust.
- Frosted and patterned glass in bathrooms and office doors lets light in but keeps privacy.
- Thin curtains soften bright sun. Thick blackout curtains make rooms dark for sleep.
- Sunglasses cut glare and block ultraviolet light. Welders wear very dark glass to protect their eyes from the blinding welding arc.
- Opaque and brown bottles protect medicines, milk and cooking oil from light that spoils them.
- Lampshades, kandils and paper lanterns use translucent materials to spread light softly.
- Greenhouses use transparent or translucent covers so plants get light and stay warm.
- Spectacles, camera lenses and phone screens need very transparent glass or plastic.
- Stone jaalis in Indian buildings let in light and air but keep rooms cool and private.
- Shadow-puppet artists, such as tholu bommalata puppeteers in Andhra Pradesh, use translucent painted leather so the audience sees coloured shadows.
- Tracing paper and butter paper let you copy a picture by laying the paper on top.
- Optical fibres of very clear glass carry internet signals as light under the oceans.
- Scientists slice rocks so thin that light passes through, to study what they are made of.
- Food packets for chips and biscuits are opaque, often lined with metal foil, to keep light and air out.

## Open questions
- **What exactly is glass?** Its atoms are in no neat order, like a liquid's, but it is stiff like a solid. How a liquid becomes glass as it cools is still one of the big puzzles of physics.
- **What is dark matter, the matter that light passes straight through?** It pulls on stars and galaxies, so we know something is there. But big experiments have not yet caught a single particle of it.
- **How does a glass frog keep their blood from clotting when they pack it into their liver?** In people, packed blood would clot dangerously. The frog does this every night and wakes up fine. Scientists are still studying how.
- **When a packet of light reaches a window, what decides whether it goes through or bounces back?** Only the share can be predicted. Most physicists say each packet is pure chance. Some think hidden details might decide. Experiments have not settled what this chance really is.
- **Exactly how long does light take to escape from the middle of the Sun?** Estimates range from about 10,000 to 170,000 years, because it depends on details of the Sun's inside that we cannot measure directly.
- **Could we make walls or cars that are see-through, but strong?** Scientists have made see-through wood and see-through ceramics. Making them cheap and strong enough for everyday use is still being worked on.

## Connected maths
- In the gap test, the plastic bag could be lifted 6 cm before the word blurred. The butter paper could be lifted only 2 cm. How many times higher was the plastic bag? **3 times.**. The plastic bag could be lifted to 6 cm. The butter paper only to 2 cm. 6 divided by 2 is 3. So the plastic bag went 3 times as high. You can check: 2 + 2 + 2 = 6. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the gap test, the handkerchief's three tries were 4 cm, 2 cm and 3 cm. What is the middle value? **3 cm.**. Put the three numbers in order: 2, 3, 4. The middle one is 3. So the middle value is 3 cm. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- In the oil spot experiment, each spot gets a score from 0 to 3. The dry spot scored 1, the water spot scored 2, and the oil spot scored 3. After one hour, the water spot scored 1. By how much did the water spot's score drop? **By 1.**. The water spot first scored 2. After an hour it scored 1. 2 take away 1 is 1. So it dropped by 1, back to the same as the dry spot. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- The Three-Light Lantern has 4 side windows. One is clear plastic, two are butter paper, and one is a card jaali. What fraction of the windows are butter paper? **Two quarters, which is the same as one half.**. There are 4 windows. 2 of them are butter paper. So 2 out of 4 are butter paper. That is two quarters. Two quarters is the same as one half. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- Dark sunglasses let through about 1 out of every 10 parts of the light that hits them. If 100 parts of light hit the lenses, about how many get through? **About 10.**. 1 out of every 10 parts gets through. 100 is 10 groups of 10. One from each group is 10. So about 10 parts of light get through, and about 90 are stopped. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
