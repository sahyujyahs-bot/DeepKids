# Science in the kitchen: how cooking changes rice, butter and milk
`sci.changes.kitchen-science.L2`

**Summary.** Ten grains of boiled rice make a much longer line than ten dry grains. Your child soaks some grains in cold water and boils others for the same time, and finds that heat lets the water go deep inside each grain. Then they melt a spoon of butter and watch it set again, while a grown-up heats another spoon until it becomes ghee, which never turns back into butter. They also find out how tiny living beings called bacteria turn milk into curd. Some kitchen changes can go back, and some stay changed for ever.

**Intro.** *Put one dry grain of rice next to one grain of cooked rice from lunch. Which one is bigger? Where did the extra come from?* Every day, cooking changes the food in your kitchen. Hard rice turns soft. Butter turns runny in a warm pan. Milk turns into thick curd overnight. In this unit you test what heat and water do to rice grains. You melt butter and watch a grown-up turn butter into ghee. You find out who really sets the curd. And you sort kitchen changes into ones that can go back and ones that cannot.

## Terms
- **Cooking**: Cooking is changing food, usually with heat, so that it is soft, safe and good to eat. *e.g.* Boiling rice, frying a dosa and roasting groundnuts are all cooking.
- **Absorb**: To absorb means to take something in, the way a sponge takes in water. *e.g.* Rice grains absorb water when they cook, so they get bigger and softer.
- **Starch**: Starch is the main part of a rice grain. It is a store of food that the rice plant made. *e.g.* Rice, ragi, wheat and potatoes are full of starch.
- **Melt**: To melt means to change from a solid into a liquid when something gets warm. *e.g.* Butter melts on hot dosa and runs down the sides.
- **Set**: To set means to go firm or solid. Butter sets when it gets cold. Curd sets when milk turns thick. *e.g.* Coconut oil sets white in the bottle on a cold morning.
- **Ghee**: Ghee is butter that has been heated until its water has bubbled away as steam. It is almost all fat. *e.g.* Many families make ghee at home from butter (vennai in Tamil, makkhan in Hindi).
- **Bacteria**: Bacteria are tiny living beings, far too small to see. Some of them live in curd. *e.g.* Bacteria in a spoon of curd turn a whole bowl of warm milk into curd by morning.
- **A change that can go back**: A change that can be undone, so the thing becomes what it was before. Scientists call it a reversible change. *e.g.* Melted butter goes back to solid butter in the fridge.

## Explanations
### Cooking changes food · *defined*
**Definition:** Cooking changes how food looks, feels, smells and tastes, usually by heating it.

Raw rice is hard and dry. After boiling, it is soft, white and bigger. A dosa starts as runny batter and ends as a crisp, brown circle. Heat does most of these changes, and cooking makes many foods easier to chew and digest.
- e.g. Raw dal is hard. Cooked dal is soft enough to mash with a spoon.
- e.g. Runny dosa batter on a hot tawa turns into a firm dosa in a few minutes.
- e.g. Hard maize kernels pop into fluffy popcorn in a hot pan.
### Rice absorbs water when it cooks · *defined*
**Definition:** When rice cooks in hot water, the starch inside each grain absorbs water and swells, so the grain gets longer, fatter and softer.

A dry rice grain is hard because its starch is packed tight. In cold water, only a little water gets in, so the grain turns a little whiter but stays hard. In boiling water, the heat loosens the packed starch. Now water can go deep inside, and the grain swells. One cup of rice with two cups of water fills about three cups when cooked.
- e.g. Ten boiled grains in a line are about one and a half times as long as ten dry grains. Long grains like basmati can grow to nearly twice as long.
- e.g. Soaked rice, used for idli and dosa batter, is white and a little bigger, but still hard.
- e.g. When rice cooks, the water in the pan goes down, because the grains drink it.
### Butter melts and sets again, but ghee stays ghee · *defined*
**Definition:** Butter melts when warm and sets again when cold. Heated for longer, its water boils away and it becomes ghee, which cannot turn back into butter.

Butter is mostly fat, with some water and a little milk mixed in. Warm it gently, and the fat melts. Cool it, and the fat sets again. Heat butter longer, and it fizzes. That sound is the water in the butter boiling away as steam. What is left is ghee, and the water cannot come back.
- e.g. Butter on hot rice melts, and sets again if the rice gets cold.
- e.g. Ghee is runny in a Chennai summer and goes solid and grainy on a cold Delhi morning. Both of those changes go back.
- e.g. The brown bits at the bottom of a ghee pan are the milk parts of the butter, cooked by the heat.
### Bacteria set the curd · *defined*
**Definition:** Tiny living beings called bacteria eat the sugar in milk and make an acid. The acid makes the milk thick and sour. That is curd.

Curd is made by bacteria, not by heat. A spoon of curd holds millions of them. In warm milk, they feed on the milk sugar and make an acid, which tastes sour. The acid makes the milk thick, so by morning the whole bowl has set. Cold slows the bacteria down, so milk in the fridge stays runny.
- e.g. Families all over India set curd at night, from a spoon of the day's curd, to eat with rice the next day.
- e.g. Curd rice in Tamil Nadu, mishti doi in Bengal and chaas in Gujarat all start with bacteria working on milk.
- e.g. Idli and dosa batter rise overnight because tiny living beings work in the batter too.
### Some changes go back, and some stay changed · *defined*
**Definition:** A change that can be undone is a reversible change. A change that cannot be undone is an irreversible change.

Melting and setting can go back and forth. Ice melts into water and freezes into ice again. Other changes make something new. Cooked rice will not turn back into raw rice, even when it is cold. Curd will not turn back into milk.
- e.g. Goes back: melted chocolate sets again in the fridge.
- e.g. Stays changed: a chapati cooked on a tawa cannot become dough again.
- e.g. Goes back: steam from a pressure cooker turns into water drops on a cold lid.

## Mostly agreed
- Heat makes the starch in rice absorb water and swell. This is why cooked rice is soft. *How we know:* Rice soaked in cold water stays hard. The same rice boiled for the same time goes soft. Under a microscope, starch grains in hot water swell and burst.
- Curd is made by bacteria, not by the milk alone. *How we know:* Warm milk with a spoon of curd sets by morning. The same milk with nothing added, or with boiled curd, does not set the same way. Under a microscope, curd is full of bacteria.
- Ghee is butter with its water boiled away. *How we know:* Butter in a hot pan fizzes and gives off steam until the bubbling stops. The ghee left over weighs less than the butter did, because the water has gone.

## How we found out
**Louis Pasteur, a French chemist, and Stamen Grigorov, a Bulgarian medical student. Justus von Liebig, a German chemist, held the wrong idea., Pasteur reported his work in 1857. Grigorov found the curd bacterium in 1905..** For thousands of years, families in India, Bulgaria, Turkey and many other places set curd from a spoon of old curd. Nobody knew what really changed the milk. In the 1800s, a famous German chemist, Justus von Liebig, said that souring was only a chemical change, a kind of slow breaking down. He said no living thing was needed. In 1857, Louis Pasteur in France studied this souring closely. Under his microscope he found tiny living beings, much smaller than yeast. When he added a little of them to a fresh sugary liquid, that liquid turned sour too, and the tiny beings multiplied. So the souring was done by living microbes. In 1905, Stamen Grigorov, a young medical student from Bulgaria working in Geneva, looked at yogurt from his home country. He found a rod-shaped bacterium that makes milk thick and sour. It was later named Lactobacillus bulgaricus, after Bulgaria.  
*What people believed before:* That milk and other foods turned sour by a chemical change alone, with no living thing involved. Pasteur showed the souring grew and spread only when the tiny living beings were there.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Cooking takes all the goodness out of food.** (Some people who eat only raw food; *rejected*)  
  For: Long boiling does lose some vitamin C from vegetables into the water.  
  Against: Cooking makes rice, dal and many other foods soft enough to digest, and boiling kills most germs. Raw rice and raw dal are very hard for our bodies to use. Steaming, or cooking vegetables for a short time, keeps most of their vitamins.
- **Soaking rice before cooking makes it cook faster.** (Many cooks; *accepted*)  
  For: Soaked grains already have some water inside, so the heat has less work to do.  
  Against: The time saved is a few minutes, and it depends on the kind of rice.
- **Learning to cook changed the bodies of our human ancestors.** (Richard Wrangham, a scientist who studies chimpanzees and human evolution; *debated*)  
  For: Cooked food is soft and easy to digest. Wrangham says this let our ancestors grow smaller teeth and guts, and bigger brains.  
  Against: Other scientists say the oldest signs of cooking fires are too few to be sure people cooked that long ago. They say eating meat, or cutting food with stone tools, may matter more.

## Roots
We have watched rice grains grow in boiling water, butter turn into ghee, and milk become curd. Now we ask what cooking tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In cold water, your rice grains stayed hard and grew only a little. In boiling water, the same grains grew long and soft. Heat let the water go deep inside each grain. So most of a plate of cooked rice is the water you cooked it in.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of rice as a dry food and water as a drink, two separate things on the table. But once rice is cooked, most of what we eat is water held inside the grains.  
*Evidence:* In the ten-grain test, boiled grains made a much longer line than soaked grains, though both sat in water for 12 minutes. In the two-pan test, the rice pan lost about 4 spoons more water than the water-only pan. One tumbler of dry rice makes about three of cooked rice.

- *who am i:* Your body is mostly water, like cooked rice. The water in your food becomes part of you.
- *what is life:* Curd bacteria change milk to feed themselves. Living beings are always changing their food, and being changed by it.
- *what is this world:* Some changes in the world go back, like melting. Many go only one way, like cooking.

Rice, water and heat sit on the stove as three separate things. In the pot, the water moves into the grain and the grain swells round it, until you cannot point to where the water is. From the same rice, water and heat, cooks make idli, dosa, pongal, payasam and pulao. Everything is conscious, down to the smallest particles, so the grain and the water take part in this change too. Many scientists see only particles moving and sticking.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.5.1:** Food, when it is eaten, is split three ways. The roughest part leaves the body, the middle part becomes flesh, and the finest part becomes mind. (Cooking is the first change food goes through. In the body it keeps changing, until it becomes part of you.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 15.14:** Krishna says he becomes the fire inside living bodies that digests their food. (Outside, the fire of the stove changes rice. Inside you, the body goes on changing it, and the Gita speaks of that as a fire too.)
- Science (settled): Heat makes the starch in rice absorb water and swell. Scientists call this gelatinisation.
- Science (debated): Some scientists think cooking changed our ancestors' bodies, giving them smaller teeth and guts. Others disagree.
- Other view, Many scientists: They say a grain taking in water is only particles moving and sticking together. They see no need to say that the grain or the water takes part in anything.
- Practice, Feel the water in the grain (3 min): Put one dry grain and one cooked grain on your palm. Close your eyes. Touch the dry grain with a fingertip. Now touch the cooked grain. Press it gently. Feel how soft and wet it is. That softness is the water from the pan. Now take one slow breath. Some of the water in your breath came from your food too.
- Ask: If most of cooked rice is water, where does the rice end and the water begin?
- Ask: Melted butter set again, but ghee did not turn back. What makes a change go only one way?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* Melted butter set again, but ghee never became butter again. When does a thing stop being itself and become something new? (hint: Think about butter, ghee, milk and curd. Which ones still have the same parts inside?)
- *big:* Bacteria set the curd while you slept. Who made the curd: you, or the bacteria? (hint: Think about who stirred in the curd, who did the changing, and who ate it.)
- *deep:* Could anything change and then go back to exactly how it was, with nothing at all different? (hint: Look closely at melted butter after it sets again. Is it exactly the same?)
- *big:* A spoon of curd can turn a whole bowl of milk into curd. How can something so small change something so big? (hint: Think about what the bacteria do while the milk is warm.)

## Story: Too Much Rice for the Pot
*Meenakshi, her father and her Paati are made up. Rice really does swell to about three times its dry size when it cooks, and boiling rice in a small, full pot really does froth over.*

Meenakshi was seven. She lived with her Appa and her Paati in a flat in Madurai, on the third floor. Paati was her grandmother. Paati cooked lunch every day, and Meenakshi liked to stand beside her and watch.

One Saturday, Paati went to Chennai for a wedding. 'Appa will cook lunch today,' she said on her way out. 'Help him, kanna.'

Appa had never cooked rice before. He found the rice tin. He found a small steel pot. He poured in rice until the pot was half full. Then he poured in water, right up to the top.

'That is a lot of rice,' said Meenakshi.

'We are hungry,' said Appa. 'And this pot is just the right size. Look, it all fits.'

Meenakshi looked at the pot. She remembered something Paati always did. Paati used a big pot for only a little rice. Meenakshi had once asked her why. Paati had said, 'Rice grows, kanna. Give it room.'

'Appa,' said Meenakshi. 'Paati says rice grows. She says to give it room.'

Appa laughed. 'Rice is not a plant in a pot of soil. It cannot grow in a kitchen.' He put the pot on the stove and lit the gas.

Meenakshi was not sure. So she did a small test of her own. She took two grains of rice from the tin. She laid them on a plate. Then she waited.

After a while, the pot began to bubble. White froth climbed up the sides. It rose to the rim, and then it spilled over. It hissed on the hot stove.

'Aiyo!' said Appa. He turned the gas down low. He lifted the lid. The rice had swollen right up to the top of the pot. There was hardly any water left. The rice was pushing at the lid.

'Where has all the water gone?' said Appa. 'I filled it right to the top.'

Meenakshi took a spoon. She scooped out two cooked grains and put them on the plate next to her two dry grains. The cooked grains were fat and white and soft. They were much longer than the dry ones. She pressed one. It squashed flat.

'Look, Appa,' she said. 'The rice drank the water. That is why the water is gone. And that is why the rice is bigger.'

Appa looked at the four grains for a long time. 'So Paati was right,' he said. 'Rice does grow. It grows by drinking.'

They moved half the rice into a big pot, with more water, and let it finish cooking. The pot did not spill again. When it was done, Meenakshi counted the cups of cooked rice. Appa had put in two cups of dry rice. Now there were six cups of cooked rice.

'Six!' said Appa. 'We will eat rice until Monday.'

That evening Paati phoned from Chennai. 'How was lunch?' she asked.

'Appa made enough rice for the whole building,' said Meenakshi. 'I told him rice grows. Rice drinks the water, Paati. Then it gets fat.'

Paati laughed so hard she had to sit down. 'Next time,' she said, 'you show Appa how much to use. One cup of rice for two people. And a big pot.'

The next day, they took the extra rice down to their neighbour, Farzana Aunty, who made lemon rice with it for the children. Meenakshi stood beside her and watched.

'Farzana Aunty,' she said, 'did you know rice drinks water?'

'I did,' said Farzana Aunty. 'Did you know it drinks much more when the water is hot?'

Meenakshi did not know that. She decided to test it next.

**For the parent:** Rice does not just sit in the water. It drinks the water, and that is why cooked rice is bigger, softer and heavier than dry rice.

- Ask: Why did the water in Appa's pot disappear?
- Ask: What was Meenakshi's small test, and what did it show?
- Ask: How could Meenakshi test Farzana Aunty's idea, that rice drinks more water when the water is hot?

## Experiments
### e1 · Ten Grains in a Line: what does cooking do to rice? (30 min)
- **question:** We keep ten rice grains dry, soak ten in cold water and boil some in hot water, for the same time. Which ten grains will make the longest line? Which will be soft?
- **predict:** Will the soaked grains and the boiled grains make longer lines than the dry grains, shorter lines or the same? Which will be soft? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the grains come from the same packet of rice. The soaked grains and the boiled grains each get one cup of water and stay in it for the same 12 minutes. We line up ten whole grains of each kind, end to end, in the same way. Only one thing changes: no water, cold water or boiling water. So any difference comes from the water and the heat.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water, adds the grains and pours them out. Let the boiled grains cool on the plate for two minutes before your child touches them.
- **record:** Draw or stick the three lines of ten grains on the paper strip. Write the length under each line, and the words hard or soft.
- **results table:** Grains | My guess | Length of the line of 10 grains | Hard or soft? | See-through or white?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a spoon of rice on a steel plate. 2. Your child picks out 40 whole grains, not broken ones. 3. Make heaps of 10, 10 and 20.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely at one grain. Is it hard or soft? Hold it up to the light. Can you see light through it?  
  *Child mode:* Pick out 40 whole grains. Make heaps of 10, 10 and 20. Feel one grain. Is it hard?
  - Note: We boil 20 grains, because a few may break. You need ten whole ones at the end.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the katori of cold water and the pan. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Ten grains stay dry. Ten go in cold water. Some go in boiling water. Which line of ten will be longest? Which grains will be soft? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Some grains stay dry. Some go in cold water. Some go in boiling water. Which will be longest? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Water makes things soft, so both wet ones.; Hot water cooks the rice, so the boiled ones.; Rice does not change size, so all the same.)
  - Note: Every guess is fine. Many children think the soaked grains and the boiled grains will be the same, because both were in water.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up puts one cup of water on to boil in a small pan. It takes about 4 minutes. 2. When it bubbles, your child puts 10 grains in a katori with one cup of cold water. 3. At the same moment, the grown-up drops in the 20 grains. 4. Turn the gas down so the water keeps bubbling gently, and start a 12-minute timer.  
  *Parent says:* Both lots of grains go in water at the same moment. That way they get the same time in the water.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up drops the grains in boiling water. You put yours in cold water. Start the timer.
  - Note: Keep the water bubbling gently. If it gets low, add a little more hot water. The grains must stay under water. Do the dry-grain line (next step) while the water heats and the timer runs.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the 10 dry grains on the paper strip, end to end, touching. 2. Mark the start and end with a pencil. 3. Write Dry beside the line. 4. Measure the line, or count finger-widths.  
  *Parent says:* Put the grains in a straight line, each one touching the next. How long is the line of ten?  
  *Child mode:* Put the dry grains in a line, touching. Mark both ends. How long is it?
  - Note: Lay the grains lengthwise, tip to tip, like a train. A line of ten dry sona masoori grains is about 5 cm. Basmati is longer.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. While the timer runs, your child draws one dry grain, large. 2. Next to it, they draw how they think a boiled grain will look.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a dry grain, big. Now draw what you think a boiled grain will look like. Will it be the same shape?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a dry grain. Then draw how you think a boiled one will look.
  - Think: Where might the water go?
- **s6 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When the timer rings, a grown-up pours the boiled grains through the tea strainer. 2. Spread them on the plate to cool. 3. Drain the soaked grains too.  
  *Parent says:* The boiled grains are hot. We wait two minutes before we touch them.  
  *Child mode:* Wait two minutes. The boiled grains are hot.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay 10 soaked grains under the dry line, end to end, and mark the ends. 2. Do the same with 10 whole boiled grains. 3. Measure each line. 4. Press one grain of each kind between finger and thumb.  
  *Parent says:* Which line is longest now? Press one grain of each kind. Which ones squash? Which ones are white?  
  *Child mode:* Make a line of soaked grains and a line of boiled grains. Which is longest? Squash one of each.
  - Note: Expect the boiled line to be clearly longer, often about one and a half times the dry line. The soaked grains may turn a little whiter and grow only a little. They stay hard. If a boiled grain is bent, straighten it gently. Some kinds of rice need a few minutes longer than 12 to go fully soft. If the middle of a boiled grain is still hard, say so honestly. The boiled line is still the longest.
- **s8 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw and why. Then ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think happened to the boiled grains? What did you see? Why did they grow more than the soaked ones?  
  *Child mode:* Say what happened. Say what you saw. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: Where did the extra length come from? (good: The grains drank the water.; Water went inside the grains.)
  - Ask: Both lots were in water for 12 minutes. Why did the boiled ones grow more? (good: The heat let more water in.; Hot water goes in better than cold water.)
  - Note: This is the heart of the unit. The only difference between the soaked and the boiled grains was heat. So heat is what let the water go deep inside.
  - Check: Which grains made the longest line? → **The boiled grains**
### e2 · How Much Water Does Rice Drink? (25 min)
- **question:** When rice cooks, the water in the pan goes down. How much does the rice drink, and how much goes off as steam?
- **predict:** Both pans start with 8 spoons of water. Which pan will have less water left? About how many spoons less? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both pans are the same size and get the same 8 tablespoons of water. Both are brought to a gentle boil, then kept on low heat with the lid on, for the same 12 minutes. Only one thing changes: one pan also has 2 tablespoons of rice. Steam leaves both pans. So the extra water missing from the rice pan is the water the rice drank.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the boiling and pours out the hot water. Let the pans cool for five minutes before the water is poured out.
- **record:** Draw the two pans. Under each one, draw a spoon for every spoonful of water left.
- **results table:** Pan | Spoons of water at the start | Spoons of water left | Spoons gone
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child counts 8 tablespoons of water into each pan. 2. They add 2 tablespoons of rice to one pan. 3. Label the pans Water and Rice.

### a1 · Melt It or Make Ghee? Two spoons of butter (22 min)

  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Melted things go solid again in the fridge.; Cooking changes things, so the stove one will be different.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the katori in the bowl of hot water. Do not let water get inside it. 2. Watch the butter melt. 3. Look for a clear yellow part and some white cloudy bits.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the edges first. What colour is the melted butter? Can you see some white bits?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the butter melt. What colours can you see?
  - Note: Butter is mostly fat, with some water and milk bits. When it melts, the white milk bits show up in the clear yellow fat.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the melted butter katori in the freezer, or in the fridge.  
  *Parent says:* Now we make it cold. Let's see if it sets again.  
  *Child mode:* Put the melted butter in the freezer.
  - Note: In the freezer it sets in about 10 minutes. The fridge takes about 20.

  *Child mode:* Listen to the butter fizz. Watch the bubbles. What smell comes now?
  - Ask: What is the fizzing? (good: Water boiling.; Steam coming out.; Something leaving the butter.)
  - Note: A spoon of butter takes about 3 to 5 minutes on low heat. The fizzing is the water in the butter boiling into steam. When the water is gone, it goes quiet. Turn off the gas at once, before the brown bits turn dark.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let the ghee cool for 2 minutes. 2. Put it in the freezer next to the butter. 3. While you wait, look at the brown bits at the bottom of the ghee and smell it.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the brown bits in the ghee. They are the milk parts of the butter. Smell it. Is it the same smell as the butter?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the ghee. What do the brown bits look like?
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take out both katoris. 2. Your child looks at, smells and gently presses each one. 3. Ask them to say what they think, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which one is butter again? What is different about the other one? Smell them both.  
  *Child mode:* Which one is butter again? Smell both. How are they different?
  - Note: The gently melted butter sets solid and pale yellow again. It may look a little different, with a thin watery layer underneath. The ghee may stay soft or set grainy, it is golden, has brown bits and smells nutty. Both are worth eating with rice afterwards.
  - Check: Why can ghee not turn back into butter? → **The water in the butter boiled away as steam**

## Challenges
### c1 · Set the Butter Fast, Without the Fridge (20 min)
- **problem:** A grown-up melts four spoons of butter: three for the three ways below and one for your own idea. Find the fastest way to make melted butter set solid again, without putting it in the fridge or freezer.
- **safety:** A grown-up melts the butter and carries the hot water. Keep the fourth katori in the hot water until you need it.
- **your turn:** Now try your own idea. Time how long your butter takes to set. Draw your best way and write the time.
- **constraints:** The butter itself does not go in the fridge or freezer.; Use only things from home. Ice cubes are allowed.; Start all your tries with the same amount of melted butter, one spoon each.
- **general:** Stand the katori in a bowl of cold water with ice cubes.; Spread the butter thin on a steel plate, so more of it touches the cold metal.; Wave a piece of cardboard over the katori.
- **creative:** Use a steel katori, not a plastic one. Steel takes heat away faster.; Add a pinch of salt to the ice water. Salty ice water gets colder than plain ice water.; Do two at once, so you can compare which way wins.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up melts the butter in katoris standing in hot water. 2. Your child chooses three ways to cool it.  
  *Parent says:* Today the butter cannot go in the fridge. How could we make it set fast? What takes heat away?  
  *Child mode:* How can you make the butter set fast, without the fridge?
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start all three at the same time. 2. Every two minutes, press each one gently with a clean spoon. 3. Write the time when each one is firm.  
  *Parent says:* Which one do you think will set first? Why? Press each one every two minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Start all three. Press each one every two minutes.
  - Note: On a hot day, plain tap water may be too warm to set butter, so use ice. A thin layer on a steel plate, or a steel katori in ice water, usually sets first. Waving cardboard alone is slow, and may not work at all in summer.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child says which way won and why. Then they try their own idea with the fourth spoon.  
  *Parent says:* Which way set first? Why do you think it worked best?  
  *Child mode:* Which way won? Why? Now try your own idea.
  - Ask: Why did that way win? (good: The cold water took the heat away.; Thin butter cools faster.; Steel gets cold fast.)

## Worksheets
### Rice, butter and curd (core, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Remember the ten-grain test? Which grains turned soft?  
   Options: The dry grains / The soaked grains / The boiled grains  
   **Answer:** The boiled grains. *Why:* Boiled grains absorbed lots of water and swelled. They squash flat between your fingers.
2. (truefalse) Rice soaked in cold water for 12 minutes goes as soft as boiled rice.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Soaked grains may turn a little whiter and grow a little, but they stay hard. Heat is needed to let the water go deep inside.
3. (match) Draw a line from each food to what it becomes.  
   **Answer:** Milk → Curd; Butter → Ghee; Dry rice → Soft rice; Dosa batter → Dosa. *Why:* Each food on the left changes into the one on the right when it is cooked or set.
4. (sort) Sort these changes: can they go back, or do they stay changed?  
   **Answer:** butter melting → Goes back; rice boiling → Stays changed; water freezing → Goes back; milk setting into curd → Stays changed; chocolate melting → Goes back; a chapati cooking → Stays changed. *Why:* Melting and freezing can go back. Cooking rice and chapati, and setting curd, make something new that cannot go back.
5. (fill) When butter gets warm, it ______. When it gets cold again, it ______.  
   **Answer:** ['melts', 'sets']. *Why:* Butter is mostly fat. Fat melts when warm and sets solid when cold.
6. (write) Say it out loud, and a grown-up writes it: where did the water in the rice pan go?  
   **Answer:** Most of it went inside the rice grains. Some went into the air as steam.. *Why:* A strong answer says the grains absorbed the water, and may add that some left as steam.
7. (odd) Three of these changes stay changed. Which one can go back?  
   Options: Rice boiling / Ice melting / A dosa cooking / Curd setting  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* Melted ice freezes back into ice. Rice, dosa and curd stay changed.
8. (label) Here is a pan of rice cooking. Label the steam, the water and the rice grains.  
   **Answer:** Steam above the pan, water in the pan, grains in the water.. *Why:* Steam is water that has turned into a gas and floats up. The grains sit in the water and drink it.
9. (perspective) Kavya, a girl of 8, and her brother Arjun, who is 6, melted some butter. Then they put it in the freezer, and it set again.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. For example: 'Arjun, because it is butter and it is solid again.' Then it is fair to Kavya: it may look a little different, because the water and milk bits settled.. *Why:* Both are partly right. The fat melted and set again, so that part went back. But the water and milk bits may have moved, so it is not exactly the same as before.
10. (mcq) A grown-up melts butter in a katori. What will make it set solid again?  
   Options: Heating it more / Making it cold / Stirring it with a spoon  
   **Answer:** Making it cold. *Why:* Butter is mostly fat. Fat sets solid when it gets cold.
### Heat, water and tiny living beings (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Why did the boiled grains grow more than the soaked grains?  
   Options: Heat let the water go deep inside / Boiled water is wetter / The soaked grains were smaller to start with  
   **Answer:** Heat let the water go deep inside. *Why:* Heat loosens the packed starch inside the grain. Then much more water can get in.
2. (order) Put these in order, to make ghee from butter.  
   **Answer:** ['Butter melts in the pan', 'It fizzes and bubbles', 'The fizzing goes quiet', 'Golden ghee with brown bits']. *Why:* First the fat melts. Then the water boils away with a fizz. When the water has gone, it goes quiet, and the milk bits turn brown.
3. (write) Anjali, a girl of 7 in Kochi, cooked rice. The pan had no water left. Her cousin said, 'All the water turned to steam.' What else happened to the water?  
   **Answer:** The rice grains drank most of it.. *Why:* A strong answer says that some water left as steam, but most of it went inside the grains. The two-pan test shows this.
4. (truefalse) You can turn curd back into milk by stirring it hard.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Stirring makes curd runny, but it is still sour curd. The bacteria have changed the milk for good.
5. (perspective) Divya, a girl of 8, and her friend Farhan, who is 8 too, are eating curd rice.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, for example: 'Farhan, because the rice drank two cups of water when it cooked.' Then it is fair to Divya: the water is held inside the grains, so you chew it as food.. *Why:* Both are right in a way. Cooked rice is more than half water, so it does give your body water. But the water is held inside the soft grains, so we eat it as a food.
6. (draw) Draw the katori of melted butter before the freezer, and after.  
   **Answer:** A runny yellow puddle, then a solid pale yellow layer.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows liquid butter with white bits first, then solid butter after, perhaps with a thin watery layer underneath.
7. (write) In the ten-grain test, we boiled 20 grains but only measured 10. Why did we start with extra?  
   **Answer:** Some grains may break in the water, and we need ten whole ones for a fair line.. *Why:* A strong answer says broken grains cannot go in the line, so extra grains make sure there are ten whole ones.
8. (mcq) A spoon of curd is stirred into warm milk. What does the spoon of curd bring to the milk?  
   Options: Bacteria / Heat / Sugar  
   **Answer:** Bacteria. *Why:* The curd holds millions of bacteria. They feed on the milk sugar and multiply.
9. (fill) A change that can go back is called a ______ change.  
   **Answer:** ['reversible']. *Why:* Reversible means it can be undone. Melted butter setting again is a reversible change.
10. (mcq) In the two-pan test, why did both pans get the same amount of water and the same time on the stove?  
   Options: So the steam lost from both pans would be about the same / So both pans would finish at the same time / Because the pans were the same colour  
   **Answer:** So the steam lost from both pans would be about the same. *Why:* If both pans lose the same to steam, the extra water missing from the rice pan is what the rice drank.
### Think like a kitchen scientist (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (write) Predict: if you boil rice grains for only 3 minutes, will the line of ten be as long as after 12 minutes? Why do you think so?  
   **Answer:** No, it will be shorter, because the grains will not have had time to absorb as much water.. *Why:* A strong answer says water needs time to soak deep into the grain, so a short boil gives a shorter line. It may suggest testing it.

8. (label) Here is a katori of homemade ghee. Label the golden ghee and the brown bits.  
   **Answer:** Golden ghee at the top, brown bits at the bottom.. *Why:* The brown bits are the milk parts of the butter, browned by the heat. The golden part is the fat.
9. (write) Does more water in the pan make rice grains swell more? Say how you would test it fairly. What would you keep the same?  
   **Answer:** Cook the same rice for the same time in different amounts of water, and keep the pan, the heat and the time the same.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the amount of water and keeps the rice, pan, heat and time the same.
10. (truefalse) Ice cream that melted and froze again is exactly the same as before.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It freezes again, but it turns hard and icy. A change can go back and still leave something a little different.

## Applications
- Cooks use the fact that rice grows about three times when they plan how much to cook for a wedding or a festival meal.
- Dairies in India, like the milk cooperatives in Gujarat, make curd and buttermilk by adding the right bacteria to warm milk.
- Ghee lasts for months without a fridge, because it has almost no water left in it for germs to grow in.
- Food scientists study how cooking changes food. Kamala Sohonie, one of the first Indian women to earn a science PhD, studied the food value of neera, a drink tapped from palm trees.

- Steam rising from a pot of rice, and water drops forming on the lid, in almost every Indian kitchen at lunchtime.

## Open questions
- **Did learning to cook change the bodies of our human ancestors?** Some scientists think cooked food let our ancestors grow smaller teeth and bigger brains. Others say the signs of very old cooking fires are too few to be sure.
- **How many kinds of bacteria live in homemade curd?** Curd from different homes and towns holds different mixes of bacteria. Scientists are still studying them.
- **When did people first cook rice?** Rice was farmed in China and India thousands of years ago. But cooked rice rots quickly, so very old meals are hard to find. Scientists look for burnt grains in old cooking pots.

## Connected maths
- In the ten-grain test, the line of 10 dry grains was 5 cm long. The line of 10 boiled grains was 8 cm long. How much longer was the boiled line? **3 cm longer.**. 8 take away 5 is 3. So the boiled line was 3 cm longer than the dry line. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L2`)
- One tumbler of dry rice makes about 3 tumblers of cooked rice. A cook is making rice for a family lunch with 3 tumblers of dry rice. About how many tumblers of cooked rice will there be? **About 9 tumblers.**. Each tumbler of dry rice becomes 3 tumblers. 3 and 3 and 3 makes 9. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- In the two-pan test, both pans started with 8 spoons of water. The water-only pan had 6 spoons left. The rice pan had 2 spoons left. How many spoons did the rice drink? **4 spoons.**. The water-only pan lost 2 spoons, as steam. The rice pan lost 6 spoons. Steam took 2 of those, the same as the other pan. 6 take away 2 is 4. So the rice drank 4 spoons. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Kavya, a girl of 8, watched a meal being cooked and found 7 changes. 2 could go back and 5 stayed changed. Make tally marks for each kind. Which kind did she find more of, and how many more? **Stays changed, by 3.**. Two tally marks for goes back, five for stays changed. 5 take away 2 is 3, so she found 3 more changes that stayed changed. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
