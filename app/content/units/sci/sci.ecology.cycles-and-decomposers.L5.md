# Carbon and nitrogen: how the same atoms go round from the air to living beings and back
`sci.ecology.cycles-and-decomposers.L5`

**Summary.** A paddy plant stands all day in air that is nearly four-fifths nitrogen. Yet the farmer buys bags of nitrogen fertiliser, because the plant has no way to use the nitrogen in the air. In nature, only certain bacteria can change that nitrogen into a form plants can take up. In this unit your child follows two kinds of atom, carbon and nitrogen, on their path from the air into living beings and back. They test three compost bottles to find out what decomposers need to eat. They also find out how burning coal and making fertiliser have changed both paths.

**Intro.** *Air is nearly four-fifths nitrogen, and every plant needs nitrogen to grow. A plant stands in air all day and all night. So why do farmers buy nitrogen in bags?* The nitrogen in the air is there, but plants have no way to take it in. In the air, nitrogen atoms hold on to each other in pairs, and a plant has no tool to pull a pair apart. A few kinds of bacteria have that tool. Almost all the nitrogen in your body passed through such bacteria, or through a factory, before it reached you. Carbon has its own path. A leaf takes carbon from the air, and your breath gives carbon back. In this unit you follow both atoms all the way round. You also make compost, and find out why a good compost bin needs two kinds of waste.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can explain that living beings do not make or destroy atoms. The same atoms move between the air, water, soil, rocks and living bodies.
1. By the end I can follow a carbon atom round the carbon cycle, and name the steps that take carbon out of the air and the steps that put carbon back.
2. By the end I can explain why plants cannot use the nitrogen in the air, and name who changes that nitrogen into a form plants can use.
3. By the end I can follow a nitrogen atom round the nitrogen cycle, from the air to a plant, to an animal, to the soil and back to the air.
4. By the end I can run a fair compost test, and explain why decomposers work best with both carbon-rich 'browns' and nitrogen-rich 'greens'.
5. By the end I can explain how burning coal and oil and making fertiliser have changed the two cycles, and what has followed.
6. By the end I can tell a store from a flow, and say what a drawing or a game of a cycle leaves out.

## Terms
- **Atom**: One of the tiny particles that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see. In living beings, atoms are never made and never destroyed. They only move and join up in new ways. *e.g.* A carbon atom in the sugar of a banana can leave your body in your breath an hour or two after you eat the banana.
- **Carbon**: One kind of atom. Carbon atoms join in chains and rings, and these make the frame of sugar, starch, fat, wood, protein and every living body. *e.g.* The black part of burnt toast is mostly carbon that was in the bread.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas with no colour and no smell. Each particle of this gas is one carbon atom joined to two oxygen atoms. This is the form in which carbon is found in the air. *e.g.* You breathe carbon dioxide out. The bubbles in soda water are carbon dioxide too.
- **Nitrogen**: Another kind of atom. Every protein and every set of genes has nitrogen atoms in it. About 78 of every 100 particles of air are nitrogen gas. *e.g.* Dal is rich in protein, so dal is rich in nitrogen.
- **Matter cycle**: The path that one kind of atom takes as it moves between the air, water, soil, rocks and living beings, and comes back to where it began. *e.g.* In the carbon cycle, a carbon atom can go from the air into a leaf, into a goat, and back into the air.
- **Store**: A place where many atoms of one kind stay for a time. The air, the sea, the soil, rocks and all living bodies are stores. *e.g.* A coal seam is a store of carbon. The carbon in coal has stayed there for hundreds of millions of years.
- **Flow**: The movement of atoms from one store to another. A cycle is made of stores joined by flows. *e.g.* When a leaf takes carbon dioxide from the air, carbon flows from the air into the plant.
- **Photosynthesis**: The way green plants and algae build sugar. They use carbon dioxide from the air, water, and the energy of light. They give off oxygen. *e.g.* A curry leaf plant on a balcony takes in carbon dioxide through tiny holes under each leaf.
- **Respiration**: The way living cells get energy from food. The cell takes sugar apart using oxygen. The carbon leaves as carbon dioxide. Plants, animals, fungi and most bacteria all respire. *e.g.* After you run, you breathe faster, because your muscle cells are respiring faster.
- **Fossil fuel**: Coal, oil or natural gas. These formed from living beings who were buried under mud and rock for millions of years. Burning a fossil fuel puts that old carbon into the air. *e.g.* The petrol in an auto and the coal burnt at a power station are fossil fuels.
- **Protein**: A kind of large particle that living bodies build and use for almost every job. Muscles, hair, enzymes and the red part of blood are proteins. Every protein contains nitrogen. *e.g.* Dal, milk, eggs, fish and groundnuts are foods rich in protein.
- **Nitrogen fixing**: Changing nitrogen gas from the air into a form that plants can take up. Certain bacteria do it. Lightning does a little, and fertiliser factories do a great deal. *e.g.* Bacteria living in the roots of a black gram plant fix nitrogen, and the plant uses it.
- **Legume**: A plant of the bean and pea family. The seeds grow in pods. Most legumes keep nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their roots. *e.g.* Black gram, green gram, toor dal, chana, groundnut, beans, peas and the touch-me-not plant are legumes.
- **Root nodule**: A small round lump on the root of a legume. The plant builds it as a home for nitrogen-fixing bacteria. A working nodule is pink or red inside. *e.g.* Dig up a groundnut plant gently, and you can see nodules like tiny beads on the roots.
- **Ammonia**: A gas with a sharp smell. Each particle is one nitrogen atom joined to three hydrogen atoms. In wet soil, ammonia turns into a form called ammonium, which stays in the soil. *e.g.* The sharp smell of a toilet that has not been cleaned is ammonia.
- **Nitrate**: A form of nitrogen joined to oxygen. Nitrate dissolves in water, and plant roots take it up easily. Rain also washes it away easily. *e.g.* Bacteria in the soil turn ammonium into nitrate, and a paddy plant takes up both.
- **Denitrifying bacteria**: Bacteria who live where there is little air, such as waterlogged soil. They use nitrate in place of oxygen, and give nitrogen gas back to the air. *e.g.* In a flooded paddy field, denitrifying bacteria send some of the fertiliser's nitrogen back into the air.
- **Compost**: Dark, crumbly matter that is left when decomposers have eaten plant and food waste. It holds nutrients and helps soil to hold water. *e.g.* Vegetable peels and dry leaves kept in a pot with air and a little water become compost in two to three months.
- **Browns and greens**: Two kinds of compost waste. Browns are dry and tough, with much carbon and little nitrogen. Greens are wet and soft, with more nitrogen. *e.g.* Dry leaves, torn newspaper and coconut fibre are browns. Vegetable peels, used tea leaves and old flowers are greens.
- **Fertiliser**: Something added to soil to give plants nutrients. Compost and dung are fertilisers made by living beings. Urea is a nitrogen fertiliser made in a factory. *e.g.* A farmer in Thanjavur scatters white grains of urea on her paddy three or four weeks after planting.

## Explanations
### Atoms are kept: living beings only move them and join them in new ways · *defined*
**Definition:** A matter cycle is the path that one kind of atom takes between the air, water, soil, rocks and living beings. No living being makes atoms or destroys them.

When a seed grows into a tree, the tree's body has to come from somewhere. When a log rots, what the log was made of has to go somewhere. Chemists have weighed what goes in and what comes out of burning, rotting, growing and breathing. The weights always match. So every atom in a living body was somewhere else before, and will be somewhere else later. Almost no matter reaches the Earth from space or leaves it. The Earth has been using the same atoms for about four and a half billion years. Energy is different. Energy arrives from the Sun every day and leaves again as heat. This unit follows two kinds of atom, carbon and nitrogen.
- e.g. The water you drink has been rain, river, sea and cloud many times before.
- e.g. A carbon atom in your breakfast idli was in the air above a paddy field a few months ago.
- e.g. In the project Bottle World, nothing goes into a sealed jar except light. The plant inside goes on living, because the atoms in the jar go round.
### The fast carbon cycle: from the air to a leaf, to an eater, and back to the air · *defined*
**Definition:** In the fast carbon cycle, photosynthesis takes carbon out of the air and builds it into living bodies. Respiration, rotting and burning put the carbon back into the air.

A leaf takes in carbon dioxide through tiny holes. Using the energy of light, the leaf joins the carbon with water to build sugar. The plant builds starch, wood, oil and protein from that sugar. So most of the dry weight of a plant came out of the air. This surprises most people, because a plant seems to grow out of the soil. Soil gives the plant water and small amounts of minerals. When an animal eats the plant, the carbon passes into the animal. Every living cell respires. That means the cell takes sugar apart to get energy, and the carbon leaves as carbon dioxide. Decomposers do the same with dead leaves and bodies. A carbon atom can go round this path in less than a year.
- e.g. About half of the dry weight of wood is carbon, and all of that carbon came from the air.
- e.g. About 18 of every 100 grams of your body is carbon. You ate all of it.
- e.g. When a person becomes thinner, most of the lost fat leaves the body as carbon dioxide in the breath.
### Stores and flows: where carbon stays, and for how long · *defined*
**Definition:** A store is a place where carbon stays for a time. A flow is the movement of carbon from one store to another. A cycle is a set of stores joined by flows.

The Earth's carbon is in five main stores. They are the air, living beings, the soil, the sea and rocks. The stores are very different in size. The air is one of the smaller stores, though it is the store that every plant feeds from. All the plants on land together hold about half as much carbon as the air. The soil holds more than the air and all the plants together, as dead matter that decomposers have not finished eating. The sea holds many times more than the air, dissolved in the water. Rocks hold far more than all the others together. A carbon atom stays in each store for a different time. It may stay a few years in the air, hundreds of years in the deep sea, and millions of years in a rock.
- e.g. In the game Carbon Journey, you leave the Air station on most throws, but you leave the Rock station only on a six.
- e.g. A fallen teak leaf in a Kerala forest rots within a year. The carbon in a teak door can stay there for two hundred years.
- e.g. The soil of a forest floor is dark because of the carbon stored in it.
### The slow carbon cycle: shells, limestone, coal and volcanoes · *defined*
**Definition:** In the slow carbon cycle, carbon is locked into rock and fossil fuel for millions of years, and comes back to the air through volcanoes and the slow wearing away of rock.

Carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in the sea. Many sea beings, such as snails, clams, corals and some tiny algae, take dissolved carbon and build shells from it. When they die, the shells sink and pile up on the sea floor. Over millions of years the piles are pressed into a rock called limestone. Some carbon is locked away in another way. In swamps and on sea floors, dead plants and algae are sometimes buried under mud before decomposers can eat them. Heat and pressure slowly turn them into coal, oil and gas. Left alone, this carbon comes back to the air very slowly. Rock is pushed down and melted deep in the Earth, and volcanoes let the carbon dioxide out. One full round can take more than a hundred million years.
- e.g. The coal of Singareni in Telangana and Jharia in Jharkhand formed from forests that grew more than 250 million years ago.
- e.g. A marble floor tile, a piece of limestone and an eggshell are all made of the same carbon-holding mineral as sea shells.
- e.g. In the experiment Sour Air, carbon dioxide went into water in a few minutes. The sea takes it in the same way.
### What burning coal, oil and gas does to the carbon cycle · *defined*
**Definition:** Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from rock to the air many times faster than volcanoes do. So the amount of carbon dioxide in the air is rising.

In 1958 an American scientist, Charles David Keeling, began to measure the carbon dioxide in clean air on Mauna Loa, a mountain in Hawaii. The measuring has gone on ever since. In 1960 there were about 317 particles of carbon dioxide in every million particles of air. In 2025 there were about 427. The line also goes down a little every northern summer and up every winter. In summer the forests of the northern lands are in leaf, and they take in more carbon than living beings give out. Each year people now add about 40 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide to the air. About half of it stays in the air. The sea takes up about a quarter, and plants and soil on land take up the rest. Carbon dioxide lets sunlight in and slows the escape of heat, so the Earth is slowly warming.
- e.g. In the activity Read the Keeling Curve, you plot the real numbers and see that the line climbs faster now than in the 1960s.
- e.g. In 1856 an American scientist, Eunice Foote, put glass tubes of different gases in sunlight. The tube of carbon dioxide grew warmest and stayed warm longest.
- e.g. The sea has taken up so much carbon dioxide that sea water is now slightly less alkaline than it was 200 years ago.
### Nitrogen: plenty in the air, but out of reach · *defined*
**Definition:** Nitrogen gas is made of pairs of nitrogen atoms. The two atoms in a pair hold each other so strongly that plants and animals cannot pull them apart.

Every living being needs nitrogen. Proteins are built from it, and so are genes. About 3 of every 100 grams of your body is nitrogen. About 78 of every 100 particles of air are nitrogen gas. So nitrogen seems to be the easiest thing in the world to get. But in the air, each nitrogen atom is joined to one other nitrogen atom by one of the strongest joins in nature. You breathe nitrogen in and breathe all of it out again, unchanged. A plant's leaves are bathed in nitrogen, and the plant can use none of it. A plant takes up nitrogen mainly when a nitrogen atom is joined to hydrogen, as ammonium, or to oxygen, as nitrate. That is why a crop can turn yellow for want of nitrogen while standing in air that is mostly nitrogen.
- e.g. A paddy plant short of nitrogen has pale yellow older leaves and stays small.
- e.g. A packet of chips is filled with nitrogen gas to keep the chips crisp. The gas is used because it hardly reacts with anything.
- e.g. In Browns, Greens or Both, the bottle of paper and dry leaves stood in air full of nitrogen and still rotted slowly.
### The nitrogen fixers: who can open the pair · *defined*
**Definition:** Nitrogen fixing means changing nitrogen gas into ammonia, a form that living beings can use. Certain bacteria do it. Lightning and fertiliser factories do it too.

Some kinds of bacteria make an enzyme that can pull a pair of nitrogen atoms apart and join each atom to hydrogen. An enzyme is a protein that does one job on one kind of particle. Some of these bacteria live free in soil and water. Some live inside the roots of legumes, such as black gram, groundnut and beans. The plant builds a small round home for them, called a root nodule, and feeds them sugar. The bacteria give the plant nitrogen. A working nodule is pink inside. The colour comes from a red protein, a relative of the one in our blood, which carries oxygen to the bacteria in small, safe amounts. Too much oxygen stops the enzyme. In flooded paddy fields, blue-green bacteria fix nitrogen in the water. Lightning is hot enough to join nitrogen to oxygen, but it fixes only a small share.
- e.g. In 1939 the Indian scientist P. K. De showed that blue-green bacteria fix nitrogen in the water of paddy fields. This helps to explain how the same fields could grow rice for centuries.
- e.g. Azolla is a tiny floating fern with nitrogen-fixing bacteria living inside the leaves. Farmers in Vietnam and China have grown azolla in paddy water for centuries, and some Indian farmers grow it today.
- e.g. In 2024 scientists reported a sea alga with a nitrogen-fixing part inside each cell. The part was once a free bacterium.
### Legumes and bacteria signal to each other and choose · *defined*
**Definition:** A legume and nitrogen-fixing bacteria find each other by sending chemical signals through the soil. Each answers only the right partner.

A legume root short of nitrogen lets certain chemicals out into the soil. Bacteria of the right kind sense them, swim towards the root and send a signal of their own back. When the root hair senses that reply, the root hair curls round the bacteria and lets them in. The plant then builds a nodule around them. The plant goes on checking. In a test with soya bean plants in 2003, scientists stopped the bacteria in some nodules from fixing nitrogen. The plants gave those nodules less oxygen, and the bacteria in them multiplied far less. So the plant senses which nodules are giving nitrogen, and decides which ones to keep feeding. Plants and bacteria sense, signal and decide. Some scientists prefer to call all of this automatic chemistry.
- e.g. A black gram plant growing in soil rich in nitrogen makes few nodules. The plant does not pay sugar for nitrogen they can get freely.
- e.g. Each kind of legume lets in only certain kinds of bacteria. The bacteria of a pea plant are turned away by a soya bean.
- e.g. In the story The Kiln Field, the plants in a new field had no nodules, because the right bacteria were not in that soil.
- **Toby Kiers, Ford Denison and their team, who did the soya bean test:** The plant acts like a careful trader, rewarding partners who give nitrogen and cutting off partners who do not.
- **Some plant scientists:** No choosing is needed to explain this. A nodule that makes less nitrogen sets off chemical changes that lower the oxygen reaching it.
### The nitrogen cycle, step by step · *defined*
**Definition:** In the nitrogen cycle, bacteria fix nitrogen from the air, plants and animals use it, decomposers free it from dead matter, and other bacteria return it to the air.

Step one is fixing. Bacteria change nitrogen gas into ammonia, which becomes ammonium in wet soil. Step two is uptake. Plant roots take up the ammonium and build proteins. Animals get their nitrogen by eating plants, or by eating animals who ate plants. Step three is the return to the soil. Animals pass out spare nitrogen in urine, and all living beings die. Decomposers eat the dead matter and the wastes, and set the nitrogen free as ammonium again. Step four is nitrifying. Certain soil bacteria get their energy by changing ammonium into nitrate. Roots take up nitrate even more easily. Step five closes the ring. In wet soil with little air, denitrifying bacteria use nitrate in place of oxygen and give off nitrogen gas. Every step except the eating is done by microbes, which means living beings too small to see. Most of them are bacteria.
- e.g. In the activity The Nitrogen Relay, a ball marked N passes round six stations. Three of the six are microbes.
- e.g. Cow urine and dung have been put on Indian fields for thousands of years. Decomposers free their nitrogen for the crop.
- e.g. Rain washes nitrate out of bare soil, because nitrate dissolves in water and does not stick to soil grains.
### Compost: giving decomposers a balanced meal · *defined*
**Definition:** Composting is keeping plant and food waste so that decomposers can eat it with enough air and water. Decomposers need carbon for energy and nitrogen to build their bodies.

Decomposers are living beings, and their bodies are built of protein like ours. So they need nitrogen as well as carbon. Dry leaves, paper and straw are mostly carbon. Gardeners call them browns. Vegetable peels, tea leaves and fresh grass hold more nitrogen and much more water. Gardeners call them greens. With only browns, the decomposers have plenty of energy food and too little nitrogen to multiply, so the heap rots very slowly. With only greens, the heap packs down wet and the air cannot get in. Bacteria who live without air take over and make bad smells. There is also more nitrogen than the decomposers can use, and the spare nitrogen leaves as ammonia gas. A mix of about two parts browns to one part greens gives them both foods, with air between the pieces. A large heap of such a mix grows hot, because so many decomposers are respiring.
- e.g. In Browns, Greens or Both, you test the three mixes side by side and measure how far each one sinks.
- e.g. From 1924 to 1931, at Indore, Albert Howard and the chemist Yeshwant Wad worked out a way of composting in layers. It is still called the Indore method, and it drew on the old practice of farmers in India and China. Gabrielle Howard, a plant scientist, had worked beside Albert on Indian crops for 25 years.
- e.g. The inside of a large compost heap can reach 55 to 65 degrees Celsius, too hot to keep your hand in.
### How people have changed the nitrogen cycle · *defined*
**Definition:** Factories now fix nitrogen from the air to make fertiliser. People fix about as much nitrogen each year as all natural fixing on land and in the sea together.

In 1908 the German chemist Fritz Haber patented a way to join nitrogen from the air with hydrogen, using great heat and pressure. Carl Bosch turned it into a factory process. The ammonia is made into fertilisers such as urea. Crops fed with it yield far more grain. One careful estimate says that food grown with this nitrogen feeds nearly half the people alive. But a crop takes up only part of the fertiliser. Rain washes some of the rest into streams and lakes. There it feeds algae, tiny green beings who live in water. They multiply until the water turns green. When they die, decomposers use up the oxygen in the water, and fish die. Soil bacteria also turn some of the spare nitrogen into nitrous oxide, a gas that warms the Earth. Farmers lose less nitrogen when they give smaller amounts at the right time.
- e.g. Since 2015, all urea made in India for farming is coated with neem oil. The neem slows the soil bacteria who change urea's nitrogen into nitrate, so less is washed away.
- e.g. Lakes in many Indian cities turn green with algae or fill with water hyacinth, because sewage and field water bring them nitrogen and other nutrients.
- e.g. In the challenge Keep It in the Field, you test ways to stop rain from carrying fertiliser off a slope of soil.
### Your body is part of both cycles · *defined*
**Definition:** The atoms of your body are not fixed in place. Carbon and nitrogen atoms enter with food, stay for a time, and leave in breath, urine and shed cells.

You look the same from month to month, but your body is being rebuilt all the time. Scientists found a clever way to measure this. In the 1950s and early 1960s, nuclear bombs were tested in the open air. The tests added a rare, heavier kind of carbon atom to the air, and plants took it in. Everyone alive then ate it. After most of these tests stopped in 1963, the amount in the air fell year by year. So the amount of this carbon in a cell shows the year when that cell was made. In 2005 Kirsty Spalding, Jonas Frisén and their team in Sweden used this to date human cells. Most cells turned out to be much younger than the person. About a tenth of your fat cells are replaced each year. The nerve cells in the outer layer of the brain are as old as you are.
- e.g. Spare nitrogen leaves your body as urea in urine. That is the same substance that farmers buy as fertiliser.
- e.g. The outer layer of your skin is shed and replaced all the time. The dust in a house contains shed skin.
- e.g. The carbon you breathe out today can be in a leaf of a street tree by next week.
### Do living beings keep the cycles steady? · *debated*
The amount of oxygen, carbon dioxide and nitrogen in the air has stayed within limits that suit life for hundreds of millions of years. Living beings make and use all three gases. In the 1970s James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis proposed that living beings, taken all together, keep the air and the climate suitable for life. They called this the Gaia idea, after a Greek name for the Earth. Other scientists objected strongly. Scientists still argue about how far the idea is true. Everyone agrees that living beings shape the air. They disagree about whether anything keeps it steady.
- e.g. Almost all the oxygen in the air was made by photosynthesis. Before there were living beings who could photosynthesise, the air had almost none.
- e.g. About 2.4 billion years ago, blue-green bacteria filled the air with oxygen. Oxygen was a poison to many of the beings alive then, and scientists think many kinds died out.
- e.g. Today one kind of living being, humans, is changing the amount of carbon dioxide in the air within a few lifetimes.
- **James Lovelock, a British chemist, and Lynn Margulis, an American biologist:** The living beings of the Earth and their surroundings act together as one system. When something shifts, living beings respond in ways that tend to bring it back.
- **Ford Doolittle, a Canadian biologist, writing in 1981:** No being works for the good of the planet. Each bacterium takes what feeds them now. The cycles are a side effect, and nothing promises that they will stay steady.
- **Many Earth scientists today:** Some feedbacks steady the Earth and some make changes bigger. Each one has to be measured. Life has both steadied the planet and upset it.

## Mostly agreed
- Living beings do not make or destroy atoms. Growing, eating, breathing, rotting and burning only move atoms and join them in new ways. *How we know:* Chemists have weighed everything that goes into these changes and everything that comes out, in closed containers. The total weight stays the same every time.
- Most of the dry weight of a plant is built from carbon dioxide and water, not from soil. *How we know:* Scientists gave plants carbon dioxide made with a rare, heavier kind of carbon atom. Soon afterwards they found that carbon in the plants' sugar.
- The amount of carbon dioxide in the air has risen by about a third since 1960, and the extra carbon comes mainly from fossil fuels. *How we know:* Measurements on Mauna Loa show about 317 parts in a million in 1960 and about 427 in 2025. Air trapped in old ice shows that the level was far lower before factories. The extra carbon also lacks the rare kind of carbon atom that slowly disappears with great age, as coal and oil do.
- Plants and animals cannot fix nitrogen by themselves. In nature, almost all nitrogen fixing is done by certain microbes. *How we know:* In the 1880s Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth grew peas in clean sand with no nitrogen. The peas grew well only when water from ordinary soil was added, and only then did nodules form on the roots.
- People now fix about as much nitrogen each year as all the natural fixing on land and in the sea together. *How we know:* A study of the whole nitrogen cycle in 2013 put the yearly total at about 413 million tonnes of nitrogen. About 210 million tonnes of that came from fertiliser factories, crops grown for their nitrogen-fixing bacteria, and engines.
- Decomposers need both carbon and nitrogen, and compost forms fastest when the waste has both, with air and water. *How we know:* Compost makers have tested many mixes. Heaps of dry leaves alone or wet scraps alone rot slowly or go sour. Your own test with three bottles shows the same.

## How we found out
**Jean-Baptiste Boussingault in France, then Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth in Germany, and Martinus Beijerinck in the Netherlands, Boussingault's farm tests began in 1836. Hellriegel first reported the pea tests in Berlin in September 1886, and the full account was printed in 1888..** From 1836, the French scientist Jean-Baptiste Boussingault ran tests on a farm at Pechelbronn. He measured the nitrogen that went into the soil and the nitrogen that came out. After a crop of clover or peas, the soil held more nitrogen than before. Many people supposed that such plants took nitrogen straight from the air, as all plants take carbon. Boussingault tested that too, and his answer was no. Plants cannot use the nitrogen gas of the air. So where did the peas get it? The puzzle stood for fifty years. In 1886 two Germans, Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth, grew peas in clean sand that held no nitrogen. The peas stayed small and died early. Then they added a little water that had run through ordinary field soil. Lumps formed on the roots, and the peas grew well. When they boiled the soil water first, no lumps formed and the peas failed. Something alive in the soil was living in the lumps and taking nitrogen from the air. Soon afterwards the Dutch scientist Martinus Beijerinck grew those bacteria by themselves.  
*What people believed before:* That plants of the bean family take nitrogen straight from the air by themselves. The pea tests showed that the plant alone cannot. Bacteria from the soil, living in the root nodules, do it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A plant's body is made from the soil the plant grows in.** (A very common belief, held by most children and many adults; *rejected*)  
  For: A plant grows out of the soil and dies without it. Plants in poor soil stay small. Farmers add things to the soil to make crops grow.  
  Against: A large tree weighs many tonnes, yet the soil level under the tree does not sink. Most of the dry weight of a plant is carbon and oxygen taken from the air as carbon dioxide, with hydrogen from water. Soil gives water, nitrogen and minerals. These are needed, but they are a small part of the weight.
- **When we breathe out, we add to the warming of the Earth.** (A worry that many children have once they learn that carbon dioxide warms the Earth; *rejected*)  
  For: Breath does contain carbon dioxide. Eight billion people and billions of other animals breathe out all day.  
  Against: The carbon in your breath came from your food. Your food took that carbon out of the air a few months earlier. So breathing puts back what plants took out, and the amount in the air stays the same. The carbon in coal and oil has been out of the air for millions of years. Burning them adds carbon that the air did not have.
- **Factory fertiliser harms the land, and compost is always better.** (Many organic farmers and gardeners; *debated*)  
  For: Compost adds carbon to the soil, which feeds soil life and helps the soil hold water. Compost lets its nitrogen out slowly, so less is washed away. Factory fertiliser used carelessly pollutes water and air.  
  Against: A root takes up the same ammonium and nitrate whether they came from compost or from a factory. There is not nearly enough compost and dung to grow food for eight billion people. Careful use of small amounts of factory fertiliser, together with compost, wastes the least.
- **Growing dal always leaves the soil richer in nitrogen.** (A common farming saying; *debated*)  
  For: Legumes with working nodules take nitrogen from the air. Crops grown after a legume often do better.  
  Against: Much of the nitrogen a dal plant fixes goes into the seeds, and the seeds are taken away and eaten. If the stems and roots are also removed, the soil may gain little. The soil gains most when the whole plant is ploughed back in. And a legume without the right bacteria fixes nothing.
- **Planting enough trees can cancel the carbon from burning coal and oil.** (Some companies and governments; *debated*)  
  For: A growing tree takes carbon out of the air and stores it in wood for many years. Forests also shelter many living beings.  
  Against: A tree holds carbon for decades or a few centuries, and gives it back when the tree burns or rots. Coal and oil held their carbon for millions of years. There is not enough spare land to grow trees for all the carbon that people burn. Most scientists say that trees help, and that burning less is still needed.
- **Spreading crushed rock on farm fields can take carbon dioxide out of the air.** (Some scientists and companies. One of them, working with small farmers in India, won a large world prize for carbon removal in 2025; *debated*)  
  For: Rain with carbon dioxide dissolved in it slowly wears rock away, and the carbon ends up locked in the sea. This is part of the slow carbon cycle. Crushing the rock gives the rain far more surface to work on, so it happens faster.  
  Against: It is hard to measure how much carbon a field has really taken up. Digging, crushing and carrying rock burns fuel. Nobody yet knows how well it works over many years and many kinds of soil.

## Roots
We have followed carbon and nitrogen atoms from the air into living beings and back. Now we ask what these two cycles tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In Browns, Greens or Both, the bottle of damp leaves and paper hardly rotted. It was full of carbon, and it stood in air that is four-fifths nitrogen. But most decomposers cannot take nitrogen from the air, and dry leaves hold very little. So a living being can go short of something while surrounded by it. What matters is the form a thing is in, and who is able to change that form.  
*Habit it breaks:* We count how much of a thing there is, and call it plentiful or scarce. That leaves out the form the thing is in. The nitrogen of the air is plentiful and almost useless to a plant until a bacterium has changed it. Having a great deal of something near you is different from being able to use it.  
*Evidence:* The bottle of only browns stood in air that is nearly four-fifths nitrogen, and still rotted the slowest. In 1886, peas in clean sand starved in the open air until bacteria from the soil were added. Today nearly half of all people eat food grown with nitrogen that factories have fixed from the air.

- *who am i:* The carbon in your body came out of the air through a leaf. The nitrogen in your muscles was taken from the air by a bacterium or a factory. You are built from air, by way of other living beings.
- *what is life:* No living being makes their own atoms. Each one takes atoms that others have finished with, uses them for a while and passes them on.
- *what is this world:* The world has one fixed set of atoms and uses them again and again. What changes is the form they are in, and who can use them in that form.

The same carbon atoms are found in the air, a leaf, an idli, your arm and a sea shell. The same nitrogen atoms are found in the air, a root nodule, a dal seed and your hair. So the many things we see are a few kinds of atom, joined in different ways and handed from one being to the next. Every being who takes part senses and decides. A root lets the right bacteria in, and a bacterium swims towards a root. The cycles are what living beings do together.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.10.6:** The text follows what leaves a body at death. It becomes mist, then cloud, then rain. It falls to the earth and is born as grain, herbs, trees, sesame and beans. Whoever eats that food carries it on. (The Upanishad is speaking of the journey of a soul, not of atoms. But the path it describes is the path of a matter cycle: air, rain, plant, eater. Its authors had watched closely where a body goes.)
- **Lucretius, On the Nature of Things 1.262 to 1.264:** Nothing that we see is wholly lost. Nature remakes one thing out of another, and lets nothing be born unless the death of something else has helped. (Lucretius wrote this in Rome about two thousand years ago, long before anyone had seen a bacterium. Decomposers are the ones who do the remaking he describes.)
- **Thirukkural 1038:** Manuring does a field more good than ploughing. And once the weeding is done, guarding the crop does more good than watering. (Thiruvalluvar wrote for Tamil farmers well over a thousand years ago. Nobody then knew of nitrogen. The farmers had learned from their fields that what a crop takes from the soil must be given back.)
- Science (settled): Living beings do not make or destroy atoms. The same carbon and nitrogen atoms are used again and again.
- Science (settled): In nature, only certain microbes can fix nitrogen from the air. Every plant and animal depends on them.
- Science (debated): Whether living beings, all together, keep the Earth's air steady is still argued about.
- Other view, Ford Doolittle, a Canadian biologist, writing in 1981, and many biologists since: There is no sharing and no plan in these cycles. Each bacterium takes what helps them to live now. What they leave behind happens to be useful to someone else. If the leftover were a poison, they would leave it just the same. Long ago, the oxygen made by blue-green bacteria poisoned many of the beings then alive.
- Practice, Follow one breath (3 min): Sit still and breathe in slowly. Most of this breath is nitrogen. It goes into you and comes out again unchanged. Now breathe out slowly. In this breath is carbon from the food you ate today. Think of a leaf near your window taking that carbon in. Now rest a hand on your arm. The nitrogen in this muscle was in the air once. A bacterium or a factory brought it in. Take three more slow breaths.
- Ask: A plant stands in nitrogen and still goes short of nitrogen. Is there something all around you that you cannot use yet? What would have to change?
- Ask: Every atom in you was somewhere else before, and will be somewhere else later. So what is it that stays 'you'?
- Ask: Bacteria fix nitrogen for every plant and animal. Are they helping the rest of us, or only living their own lives? Could both be true?
- Ask: People now fix as much nitrogen as the rest of nature. Does that make us part of the cycle, or a break in the cycle?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* A plant stands in air full of nitrogen and still goes short of it. What else do people have all around them and still cannot use? (hint: Think of sea water for a thirsty sailor, or a library for someone who has not learned to read.)
- *big:* Some carbon atoms in your body were once in a dinosaur, a fern or a rock. So in what way is your body yours? (hint: Your atoms change, but your shape and your memories stay. Which of these is 'you'?)
- *deep:* If the same atoms keep going round, why do we need the Sun every single day? (hint: Atoms can be used again. Can the energy that leaves your body as heat be used again?)
- *big:* Bacteria too small to see do most of the steps in the nitrogen cycle. Who matters more to a forest, the tiger or the bacteria? (hint: What would happen to the forest in ten years without each of them?)
- *deep:* Carbon from coal was out of the air for 250 million years. Why does putting it back in 200 years cause trouble? (hint: Think about flows. What happens to a bucket when water runs in faster than it runs out?)
- *big:* Factory fertiliser feeds nearly half the world, and it also pollutes lakes and air. How should people decide how much to use? (hint: Who gains and who loses when a farmer uses more? Who gains and who loses when she uses less?)

## Story: The Kiln Field
*The farming in this story is real. In the Cauvery delta of Tamil Nadu, farmers throw ulundu (black gram) seed into standing paddy 5 to 10 days before the harvest. The ulundu grows on the water left in the mud. Selling the top layer of a field to a brick kiln is also real. Working root nodules are pink inside. Farm scientists in Tamil Nadu advise coating the seed with a packet of the right bacteria, using cooled rice kanji to make it stick. Before such packets were sold, moving soil from an old legume field to a new one was a known way to carry the bacteria. Thenmozhi, her family, the officer and what happened in their two fields are made up.*

Thenmozhi's family had two fields that year. The old field had been her grandmother's, and her great-grandmother's before that. The new one her mother had taken on rent during the rains, cheaply, and everybody called it the kiln field. Its last owner had sold the top of its soil, a foot deep, to a brick kiln.

In January, a week before the paddy was cut, her uncle Selvam had walked through both fields throwing ulundu seed into the standing crop. Ulundu is black gram, the dal that goes into idli and vadai. It was the same seed from the same sack, thrown by the same hand on the same morning. The seeds sank into the wet mud between the paddy stalks. When the paddy was harvested, the ulundu was already up.

Now it was the last week of February. In the old field the ulundu stood as high as Thenmozhi's shin, and the leaves were a dark, even green. In the kiln field the plants were half that height, and yellow.

"Bad seed," said Selvam Mama.

"It is the same seed," said Thenmozhi's mother.

"Then the field wants urea. One bag."

"With what money? The ulundu is the money."

Paatti, her grandmother, said nothing. She was looking at the yellow field the way she looked at a child with a fever.

Thenmozhi sat on the bund between the two fields with her feet in the stubble. Same seed. Same sun. Same water too, because nobody watered ulundu. The plants lived on what the paddy had left behind in the mud. She was ten, and she liked a problem with only one loose end. Whatever was different, it was not above the ground. So it had to be below.

She borrowed Paatti's weeding blade. In the old field she dug in a circle round one plant and lifted the plant out with the soil still on, the way you lift a seedling you mean to keep. She did the same in the kiln field. Then she carried both plants to the water channel and let the water take the mud off the roots slowly.

The yellow plant's roots were thin white threads, and that was all. The green plant's roots had beads on them. The beads were round and pale, the size of sago pearls. There were a dozen or more, stuck along the threads.

"Mama! The good one is sick. Look, boils."

Selvam Mama came and looked and frowned. "Root worms," he said. "Then both fields are finished."

"Give me that," said Paatti. She took the green plant, pinched off one bead and split it with her thumbnail. Inside, the bead was pink, like the flesh of a guava. "This is no sickness. A plant with these is a plant who is eating well. When I was a girl we looked for them. Pink inside meant a good crop."

"Then why has the kiln field none?" asked Thenmozhi.

Paatti was quiet for a while. "When I came here as a bride, your great-grandmother opened new land beyond the canal. Before the first ulundu, she made me carry three baskets of earth from this field and scatter them over that one. I thought it was a custom, like breaking a coconut. I asked her why. She said, 'That field has not met ulundu yet. Somebody must introduce them.'"

Thenmozhi turned the pink bead over on her palm. There was a page in her science book that she had read without much interest. It said that plants of the bean family keep bacteria in small lumps on their roots. The bacteria take nitrogen from the air, which no plant can do alone, and give it to the plant. The plant gives them sugar.

So the beads were not exactly part of the plant. They were rooms, and somebody lived in them. And that somebody had to come from somewhere.

"They live in the soil," she said slowly. "In old ulundu soil. And the top of the kiln field..."

"Went to the kiln," said her mother.

"They baked them," said Thenmozhi. "They are inside somebody's wall."

It was late in the season, and Paatti said so. A plant four weeks old has already settled most of what they will become. But Thenmozhi wanted to know, and wanting to know costs less than urea. She filled a bucket with soil from the old field, taken from close around the roots. She stirred it into water until it was as thin as buttermilk. Then she poured it along two rows of the kiln field, right at the feet of the plants. She pushed a stick into the bund to mark the place. The rest of the field she left alone.

For ten days nothing happened, and Selvam Mama enjoyed saying so. In the third week he stopped saying it. The two rows by the stick were not dark green. But they were greener than their neighbours, the way one washed shirt shows on a line of unwashed ones. Thenmozhi dug up a plant from the two rows. She had to look hard. Near the top of the root there were five small beads. She split one. It was pink.

She did not say anything to Mama. She only held it where he could see.

The kiln field gave less than a third of what the old field gave that year. In April a woman from the research station at Aduthurai came through the village, and Thenmozhi's mother told her the story. The officer laughed, though kindly, and took a small packet from her bag. Inside was a black powder.

"Your bucket, in a packet," she said. "The same bacteria, grown and counted. Mix it with cooled rice kanji so that it sticks. Roll the seed in it, dry the seed in the shade, and sow the same day."

The next January, Thenmozhi rolled the seed herself on a palm-leaf mat, a sackful, until every seed was coated grey. She added one handful of earth from the old field. She told herself it was not for luck. Then Selvam Mama walked into the standing paddy of the kiln field and threw.

In the last week of February she went out alone with the weeding blade. The kiln field was green from bund to bund. She dug up one plant anyway, washed the roots in the channel and counted the beads. She did not need to split one. But she did.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn comes when Thenmozhi sees that the beads on the roots are homes for bacteria, and that the bacteria must already be in the soil. A black gram plant cannot take nitrogen from the air alone. Bacteria in the root nodules do it, and the plant feeds them sugar. The kiln field had lost its top soil, and the bacteria with it. So the same seed under the same sky went short of nitrogen. Her two marked rows were a fair test: the same field, with one thing changed.

- Ask: Both fields had the same seed, sun and water. Why did Thenmozhi decide to look under the ground?
- Ask: Why were the plants in the kiln field yellow, when the air above them was full of nitrogen?
- Ask: Why did she pour the muddy water on only two rows and leave the rest alone?
- Ask: What does the plant give the bacteria, and what do the bacteria give the plant?
- Ask: The officer's packet and Paatti's baskets of earth did the same job. What was the job?

### The Two Cycles (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Where does most of the dry weight of a tree come from?  
   Options: The soil / Water only / Carbon dioxide in the air / Sunlight  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide in the air. *Why:* A leaf takes carbon dioxide from the air and builds sugar, and the tree builds wood from sugar. Soil gives water and minerals, which are a small part of the weight. Sunlight gives energy, and energy has no weight to add.
2. (truefalse) True or false: when a log rots, the atoms of the log are destroyed.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Decomposers take the log apart. The atoms move into the air, the soil and the decomposers' own bodies. No atom is destroyed.
3. (fill) Green plants take carbon out of the air by ____.  
   **Answer:** ['photosynthesis']. *Why:* In photosynthesis a plant uses light to join carbon dioxide and water into sugar.
4. (sort) Does each of these take carbon out of the air, or put carbon into the air? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** A mango leaf in sunlight → Takes carbon out of the air; You, breathing out → Puts carbon into the air; A rotting banana peel → Puts carbon into the air; A burning candle → Puts carbon into the air; Seaweed in sunlight → Takes carbon out of the air; A petrol engine running → Puts carbon into the air; A paddy field growing → Takes carbon out of the air; A cow breathing → Puts carbon into the air. *Why:* Only photosynthesis takes carbon out of the air. Respiration, rotting and burning all put carbon into the air as carbon dioxide.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Store → a place where atoms stay for a time; Flow → the movement of atoms from one store to another; Nitrogen fixing → changing nitrogen gas into a form plants can use; Respiration → getting energy from food and giving off carbon dioxide; Legume → a plant of the bean family. *Why:* These five words are needed to talk about any matter cycle. A strong answer could give an example of each from a paddy field.
6. (order) Put the path of one nitrogen atom in order, starting from the air.  
   **Answer:** ['Nitrogen gas in the air', 'Bacteria in a root nodule', 'Protein in a dal seed', 'Protein in your muscle', 'Urea in your urine']. *Why:* Bacteria fix the nitrogen, and the plant builds it into protein. You eat the protein and build your own. Spare nitrogen leaves your body in urine.
7. (write) In Browns, Greens or Both, the bottle of only browns hardly sank. Explain why, using the word 'nitrogen'.  
   **Answer:** Dry leaves and paper have a lot of carbon and very little nitrogen. Decomposers need nitrogen to build their bodies. With so little nitrogen they could not multiply fast, so they ate the browns very slowly.. *Why:* A strong answer says that decomposers are living beings who need nitrogen as well as carbon. It may add that browns are also tough.
8. (draw) Draw the carbon cycle with four places: the air, a plant, an animal and the soil. Label each arrow.  
   **Answer:** Arrows go from air to plant (photosynthesis), plant to animal (eating), plant and animal to soil (dead leaves, dung, bodies), and from plant, animal and soil back to air (respiration).. *Why:* A strong drawing has only one arrow out of the air and at least three arrows back into it. Each arrow carries the name of what happens.
9. (odd) Three of these plants are legumes. Which one is not?  
   Options: Groundnut / Black gram / Rice / Beans  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Rice is a grass. Groundnut, black gram and beans are of the bean family, and keep nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules.
10. (mcq) Why can a paddy plant not use the nitrogen in the air?  
   Options: The air has too little nitrogen / Nitrogen atoms are held in tight pairs / Nitrogen gas poisons plants / Paddy leaves have no holes  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen atoms are held in tight pairs. *Why:* The air is mostly nitrogen, so there is plenty. But each nitrogen atom is joined very strongly to a partner, and a plant has no enzyme to split them.
11. (truefalse) True or false: about 78 of every 100 particles of air are nitrogen.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Most of the air is nitrogen gas. Oxygen is about 21 of every 100, and carbon dioxide is far less than 1.
12. (fill) A root nodule that is fixing nitrogen is ____ inside.  
   **Answer:** ['pink', 'red']. *Why:* The colour comes from a red protein that carries oxygen to the bacteria in small amounts. A white or green nodule is not working.
13. (label) Label each store of carbon 'fast' or 'slow': a leaf, limestone, a cow, coal.  
   **Answer:** ['A leaf: fast', 'Limestone: slow', 'A cow: fast', 'Coal: slow']. *Why:* Carbon passes through leaves and animals within months or years. Carbon in rock and coal stays for millions of years.
14. (perspective) A compost bin on a balcony smells bad. Two neighbours give different advice.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees with the second neighbour and uses the test: the bottle of only greens smelled, and the mix did not. It also grants that the first neighbour is right about one thing. A badly kept bin does smell, and neighbours should not have to put up with it.. *Why:* The smell is real, and so is the cure. A bin smells when it is too wet and has too little air, and browns fix both.
15. (mcq) Used tea leaves look brown. Why do they count as a 'green' in compost?  
   Options: They are wet and rich in nitrogen / They were green once / They smell pleasant / They are in small pieces  
   **Answer:** They are wet and rich in nitrogen. *Why:* The words brown and green are about carbon and nitrogen. Tea leaves are rich in nitrogen and hold water, so they act as greens whatever their colour.
### Following the Atoms (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (write) A plant lives for years in a sealed jar. Explain how the leaves keep getting carbon dioxide.  
   **Answer:** Decomposers in the soil eat dead leaves and roots and breathe out carbon dioxide. The plant's own cells respire too. The leaves take that carbon dioxide in again in the light. The same carbon atoms go round inside the jar.. *Why:* A strong answer names who makes the carbon dioxide and who uses it. It says that the atoms are used again, and only light comes in.
2. (mcq) Which step sends nitrogen from the soil back into the air?  
   Options: Nitrogen fixing / Photosynthesis / Denitrifying, by bacteria in wet soil / An animal eating a plant  
   **Answer:** Denitrifying, by bacteria in wet soil. *Why:* Denitrifying bacteria use nitrate in place of oxygen and give off nitrogen gas. Fixing goes the other way, from the air to the soil.
3. (order) Heavy rain falls on a field just after too much urea is spread. Put what follows in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Rain washes nitrate into a lake', 'Algae multiply and the water turns green', 'The algae die and decomposers eat them', 'The decomposers use up the oxygen in the water', 'Fish die']. *Why:* The spare nitrogen feeds algae first. The harm to fish comes later, when decomposers eating the dead algae use up the oxygen.
4. (truefalse) True or false: the carbon in your breath came from the food you ate.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Your cells take food apart for energy, and the carbon leaves as carbon dioxide. You do not get carbon from the air you breathe in.
5. (fill) Coal, oil and natural gas are called ____ fuels.  
   **Answer:** ['fossil']. *Why:* They formed from living beings who were buried for millions of years. A fossil is the remains of something that lived long ago.
6. (match) Match each living being to their part in the nitrogen cycle.  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen-fixing bacteria → change nitrogen gas into ammonia; Plants → build protein from ammonium and nitrate; Decomposers → free nitrogen from dung and dead bodies; Nitrifying bacteria → change ammonium into nitrate; Denitrifying bacteria → send nitrogen gas back to the air. *Why:* Each step is done by a different kind of living being. If one kind were missing, the nitrogen would pile up at that step.
7. (sort) Does each of these belong to the fast carbon cycle or the slow carbon cycle? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** A leaf rots within a year → Fast carbon cycle; Shells turn into limestone → Slow carbon cycle; A goat eats grass → Fast carbon cycle; Buried plants become coal → Slow carbon cycle; You breathe out → Fast carbon cycle; A volcano lets out carbon dioxide → Slow carbon cycle. *Why:* The fast cycle runs through living beings in months or years. The slow cycle runs through rock and takes millions of years.
8. (draw) Draw a legume root with nodules. Show what the plant gives the bacteria and what the bacteria give the plant.  
   **Answer:** A root with small round lumps on the sides. One arrow, labelled sugar, goes from the plant into a nodule. Another arrow, labelled nitrogen, goes from the nodule into the plant.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the trade in both directions. It may also show nitrogen gas coming from the air in the soil into the nodule.
9. (odd) Three of these put carbon into the air. Which one takes carbon out?  
   Options: Burning coal / A forest growing / A rotting log / A dog breathing  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* A growing forest takes in more carbon by photosynthesis than the forest gives out. Burning, rotting and breathing all give carbon dioxide to the air.
10. (mcq) Soda water turns purple cabbage water pinker. What does that show about carbon dioxide?  
   Options: It makes water more acid / It has a pink colour / It makes water saltier / It cannot dissolve in water  
   **Answer:** It makes water more acid. *Why:* Cabbage water turns pink with acid. Carbon dioxide joins with water to make a weak acid. The same thing happens, slowly, in the sea.
11. (perspective) Should a small farm use factory fertiliser, or only compost? Three people disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both the first two farmers are describing something real. Urea gives fast growth and big harvests, and it is easily lost to water and air. Compost builds the soil slowly, and there is rarely enough of it. Many will choose the scientist's plan and say why.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off between a quick supply of nitrogen and the long-term health of the soil and water.
12. (label) Label each kind of waste 'carbon-rich brown' or 'nitrogen-rich green': sawdust, grass cuttings, newspaper, banana peel.  
   **Answer:** ['Sawdust: carbon-rich brown', 'Grass cuttings: nitrogen-rich green', 'Newspaper: carbon-rich brown', 'Banana peel: nitrogen-rich green']. *Why:* Dry, woody waste is rich in carbon. Fresh, wet waste has more nitrogen. A bin needs about two parts of browns for one part of greens.
13. (write) Your breath has carbon dioxide in it. Explain why breathing does not make the air's carbon dioxide rise.  
   **Answer:** The carbon in my breath came from my food. The plants I ate took that carbon out of the air a few months earlier. So my breath only puts back what was taken out. Burning coal adds carbon that had been out of the air for millions of years.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the carbon back to a plant and to the air. It says the difference between a loop and a one-way flow.
14. (truefalse) True or false: lightning fixes most of the nitrogen that plants use.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Lightning fixes a small share. Bacteria fix most of the nitrogen in nature, and today factories fix about as much again.
15. (fill) The round lumps on bean roots where bacteria fix nitrogen are called root ____.  
   **Answer:** ['nodules']. *Why:* The plant builds each nodule as a home for the bacteria and feeds them sugar.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A mango leaf falls and rots away. What happens to the atoms of the leaf?  
   Options: They are destroyed / They move into air, soil and living beings / They turn into energy / They stay in the leaf for ever  
   **Answer:** They move into air, soil and living beings. *Why:* Decomposers take the leaf apart. The carbon goes mostly to the air as carbon dioxide, and the nitrogen goes to the soil. Atoms are not destroyed and do not turn into energy when something rots.
2. (mcq) Which of these takes carbon out of the air?  
   Options: A rotting log / A goat breathing / A neem tree in sunlight / A burning lamp  
   **Answer:** A neem tree in sunlight. *Why:* Only photosynthesis takes carbon dioxide out of the air. Rotting, breathing and burning all put carbon dioxide in.
3. (mcq) Coal is burnt at a power station. Where was its carbon before the coal formed?  
   Options: It was always underground / In ancient plants, and earlier in the air / Only in sea water / Inside volcanoes  
   **Answer:** In ancient plants, and earlier in the air. *Why:* Coal formed from plants buried hundreds of millions of years ago. Those plants took their carbon from the air by photosynthesis. Burning coal puts that old carbon back in the air.
4. (mcq) Air is mostly nitrogen. Why do crops still go short of nitrogen?  
   Options: Plants cannot split the paired nitrogen atoms / Nitrogen gas is too heavy to reach leaves / The air has nitrogen only at night / Crops do not need nitrogen  
   **Answer:** Plants cannot split the paired nitrogen atoms. *Why:* In the air, nitrogen atoms are joined in very strong pairs. Plants have no enzyme to split them, so they depend on bacteria, lightning or fertiliser for nitrogen in a usable form.
5. (mcq) Which of these can fix nitrogen from the air?  
   Options: Earthworms / Bacteria in the root nodules of legumes / Mango leaves / Mushrooms  
   **Answer:** Bacteria in the root nodules of legumes. *Why:* Certain bacteria have the enzyme that splits nitrogen pairs. Legumes keep such bacteria in their root nodules. Earthworms and mushrooms help to break down dead matter, and they do not fix nitrogen.
6. (mcq) A cow eats grass and passes urine onto the soil. Who frees that nitrogen for plants to use again?  
   Options: Decomposers and other soil bacteria / The Sun / The cow / The rain  
   **Answer:** Decomposers and other soil bacteria. *Why:* Soil microbes change the nitrogen in urine and dung into ammonium and nitrate, which roots can take up. Rain only moves the nitrogen. It does not change its form.
7. (mcq) In waterlogged soil, bacteria change nitrate into nitrogen gas. Where does that nitrogen go?  
   Options: Back into the air / Into rocks / Into plant roots / It is destroyed  
   **Answer:** Back into the air. *Why:* Denitrifying bacteria give off nitrogen gas, which goes back to the air. This closes the nitrogen cycle. Plant roots cannot take up nitrogen gas.
8. (mcq) Which mix makes compost quickly, without a bad smell?  
   Options: Only dry leaves / Only wet peels / About two parts browns to one part greens / Only soil  
   **Answer:** About two parts browns to one part greens. *Why:* Decomposers need carbon and nitrogen together, with air and water. Dry leaves alone have too little nitrogen. Wet peels alone pack down, lose their air and smell.
9. (mcq) A heap of only fresh grass smells sharp, like ammonia. What is leaving the heap?  
   Options: Carbon, as coal / Nitrogen, as a gas / Only water / Nothing is leaving  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen, as a gas. *Why:* Ammonia is a gas of nitrogen and hydrogen. Fresh grass has more nitrogen than the decomposers can use, and the spare nitrogen escapes. Adding browns keeps it in the heap.
10. (mcq) Carbon dioxide in the air has risen since 1960. What is the main cause?  
   Options: Burning coal, oil and gas / More people breathing out / More volcanoes / The Sun growing hotter  
   **Answer:** Burning coal, oil and gas. *Why:* Fossil fuels hold carbon that was out of the air for millions of years. Breath only returns carbon that plants took out a few months before, so it adds nothing new.
11. (mcq) Rain washes spare nitrogen fertiliser from fields into a lake. What can follow?  
   Options: Algae multiply, and later fish may die / The water becomes salty / The lake dries up / Nothing changes in the lake  
   **Answer:** Algae multiply, and later fish may die. *Why:* The nitrogen feeds algae, and the water turns green. When the algae die, decomposers eating them use up the oxygen in the water, and fish can die.
12. (mcq) Which of these is a flow, not a store?  
   Options: The carbon in a coal seam / The nitrogen in the air / A leaf taking in carbon dioxide / The carbon dissolved in the sea  
   **Answer:** A leaf taking in carbon dioxide. *Why:* A store is an amount that stays somewhere. A flow is a movement between stores. A leaf taking in carbon dioxide moves carbon from the air into the plant.

## Applications
- Farmers in the Cauvery delta sow black gram after paddy. Bacteria in the root nodules fix nitrogen from the air, so the crop needs little bought fertiliser.
- Before sowing a legume, farmers coat the seed with a packet of the right bacteria, so that nodules form even in a field new to that crop.
- Urea made in India is coated with neem oil, which slows the soil bacteria who would turn its nitrogen into easily lost nitrate.
- Homes, flats and whole towns compost their wet waste in pots, pipes and bins, by layering greens with browns and letting air in.
- A sewage treatment plant uses nitrifying and denitrifying bacteria, one after the other, to send the nitrogen in waste water back to the air.
- Scientists measure carbon dioxide on Mauna Loa and at other stations every day, so that every country can see how the air is changing.
- Doctors and scientists use the rare, heavier carbon left by bomb tests to find out how old the cells of a body are.

## Spotted around us

- A white bag of urea with the words 'neem coated' printed on it, at a farm supply shop.

- Dark, crumbly soil under an old banyan or tamarind tree, coloured by the carbon stored in it.
- A marble floor or a limestone step, made of the shells of sea beings who lived millions of years ago.

## Open questions
- **Will the land and the sea keep taking up half of the carbon dioxide that people add to the air?** Forests, soils and the sea take up about half today. Warmer water holds less gas, and warmer soil rots faster. Nobody knows how long the uptake will last.
- **Can crops such as rice and wheat be given nitrogen-fixing partners of their own?** A kind of maize from the hills of Oaxaca in Mexico feeds nitrogen-fixing bacteria in a slime on roots that grow above the ground. Scientists are trying to bring this to other crops, and nobody has done it yet.
- **How much nitrogen is fixed in the open sea, and by whom?** In 2024 scientists found a sea alga with a nitrogen-fixing part inside each cell, which nobody had known was possible. There may be more such fixers that have not been found.
- **Does spreading crushed rock on fields really remove carbon dioxide from the air, and how much?** The slow carbon cycle does this naturally over millions of years. Speeding it up is being tried on farms in India and elsewhere, and measuring the result is hard.
- **How much carbon will frozen northern soils let out as they thaw?** These soils hold dead plants that have been too cold to rot. As they thaw, decomposers begin to eat them. How fast, and how much gas they give off, is still being measured.
- **Do living beings, taken all together, keep the Earth's air steady?** Living beings make and use the gases of the air. Whether that steadies the air or only changes it has been argued about since the 1970s.

The browns sank 12 − 11 = 1 cm, which is 1/12. The greens sank 12 − 5 = 7 cm, which is 7/12, a little more than half. The mix sank 12 − 8 = 4 cm, which is 4/12 = 1/3. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- A good compost mix has 2 tumblers of browns for every 1 tumbler of greens. A family makes 3 tumblers of greens a day. How many tumblers of browns do they need for a week? **42 tumblers of browns.**. Each tumbler of greens needs 2 tumblers of browns. So 3 tumblers of greens need 3 × 2 = 6 tumblers of browns a day. In a week that is 6 × 7 = 42 tumblers. This is why people who compost collect dry leaves by the sackful. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- The label says 100 grams of toor dal has 22 grams of protein, and 100 grams of rice has 7 grams. About 16 of every 100 grams of protein is nitrogen. How much nitrogen is in 100 grams of each? **About 3.5 grams in the dal and about 1.1 grams in the rice.**. For the dal: 22 × 16 ÷ 100 = 3.52 grams. For the rice: 7 × 16 ÷ 100 = 1.12 grams. The dal holds about three times as much nitrogen as the rice, because 22 ÷ 7 is a little more than 3. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- The carbon dioxide in the air rose by 9 from 1960 to 1970, and by 24 from 2010 to 2020. About how many times faster was the second rise? **Between two and three times faster. It was about 2.7 times.**. Both rises took ten years, so you can compare them directly. 24 ÷ 9 = 2.67, which is about 2.7. So the amount of carbon dioxide in the air was rising more than two and a half times as fast as it did fifty years earlier. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In Carbon Journey, a coin leaves Coal and Oil only on a 6. You throw once each for 30 coins. About how many leave? Under the people's rule they leave on a 3, 4, 5 or 6. About how many leave now? **About 5 coins under the first rule, and about 20 under the people's rule.**. A die has 6 faces. Under the first rule 1 face in 6 lets a coin out, and 30 ÷ 6 = 5. Under the people's rule 4 faces in 6 let a coin out, and 30 × 4 ÷ 6 = 20. That is four times as many. Real throws will not give exactly these numbers, because a die is chance. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- People add about 40 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide to the air each year. About half stays in the air. If people added only 20 billion tonnes, and the sea and land still took up 20 billion, how much would stay? **None. The amount in the air would stop rising.**. Today 40 billion tonnes go in, and the sea and land take out about half, which is 20 billion. So 40 − 20 = 20 billion tonnes stay each year. If only 20 billion went in and 20 billion still came out, then 20 − 20 = 0. In real life the sea and land would slowly take up less, so this sum is only a first guess. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L5`)
