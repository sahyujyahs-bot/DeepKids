# Chemical reactions: how vinegar, baking soda and rusting iron make new substances
`sci.changes.chemical-reactions.L4`

**Summary.** Dry baking soda out of water and it comes back. Dry it out of vinegar and it does not. Why? In this unit you will try six mixes and look for signs of a reaction, test the powder left after the fizz, and build atom models of water and rust.

**Intro.** *A new iron tawa is grey and hard. Leave it wet for a week, and brown flakes cover it. Nobody painted it. So where did the brown flakes come from?* The iron joined with oxygen, a gas in the air, and with water. Together they made a new substance called rust. Rust is brown and crumbly, but iron is grey and hard. A change that makes a new substance is called a chemical reaction. In this unit you make vinegar and baking soda fizz. Then you test whether the baking soda is still there after the fizz. You also find out how much of the air a piece of rusting steel uses up.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a chemical reaction is, and how it differs from melting, mixing and dissolving.
1. I can name the reactants and the products when vinegar meets baking soda.
2. I can name five signs of a chemical reaction, and explain why a sign alone is not proof.
3. I can test whether a new substance has formed, by checking what the substance does.
4. I can explain what rust is, what iron needs in order to rust, and how people protect iron.
5. I can explain that a reaction rearranges atoms, and does not make or destroy them.
6. I can explain that some reactions give out heat and others take heat in.
7. I can give examples of chemical reactions in the kitchen, in my body and beyond the Earth.

## Terms
- **Substance**: One kind of matter, which is the same all the way through. *e.g.* Salt, sugar, water, iron and oxygen are five different substances.
- **Property**: Something a substance is like or does, which helps us tell it from other substances. *e.g.* Iron is grey and hard. Baking soda is white and fizzes in vinegar. These are properties.
- **Chemical reaction**: A change in which substances turn into new substances with different properties. *e.g.* Iron, oxygen and water react and make rust. Rust is a new substance.
- **Physical change**: A change in shape, size or state that makes no new substance. *e.g.* Ice melts into water. It is still the same substance, and it can freeze again.
- **Reactant**: A substance that goes into a chemical reaction and is used up. *e.g.* Vinegar and baking soda are the reactants when a fizz is made.
- **Product**: A new substance that a chemical reaction makes. *e.g.* Carbon dioxide gas is one product when vinegar meets baking soda.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas with no colour and no smell. It is heavier than air, and flames go out in it. *e.g.* The bubbles in a fizz of vinegar and baking soda are carbon dioxide.
- **Sign of a reaction**: Something you can notice that suggests a new substance may be forming. *e.g.* Bubbles, a new colour and a change in warmth are all signs.
- **Rust**: The brown, crumbly substance made when iron reacts with oxygen and water. *e.g.* An iron gate rusts where the paint has chipped and rain gets in.
- **Oxygen**: A gas that makes up about one fifth of the air. Animals breathe it, and iron and fuels react with it. *e.g.* Iron takes oxygen from the air when it rusts.
- **Atom**: One of the tiny particles that every substance is made of. There are about 90 kinds in nature. *e.g.* Iron is made of iron atoms. Water is made of hydrogen atoms and oxygen atoms.
- **Molecule**: A group of atoms joined together. *e.g.* A water molecule is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom.
- **Word equation**: A short way to write a reaction: the reactants, an arrow, then the products. *e.g.* Iron + oxygen + water → rust.
- **Galvanising**: Covering iron or steel with a thin coat of the metal zinc, so that the iron does not rust. *e.g.* A shiny grey steel bucket with a flowery pattern on its surface is galvanised.

## Explanations
### A chemical reaction makes new substances · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical reaction is a change in which substances turn into new substances. The new substances have different properties from the ones you started with.

The substances you start with are called reactants. The new substances are called products. A product is not a mixture of the reactants. It is a different substance, and it behaves differently. Iron is hard and grey. Oxygen is a gas you cannot see. When they react with water, the product is rust, which is brown and crumbles between your fingers. Chemists write a reaction as a word equation. The reactants go on the left of an arrow, and the products go on the right. Iron + oxygen + water → rust. The arrow means 'react and make'.
- e.g. Iron + oxygen + water → rust.
- e.g. Vinegar + baking soda → carbon dioxide + water + a salt.
- e.g. Candle wax + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
- e.g. Milk turns to curd when bacteria change the milk sugar into an acid.
### A physical change keeps the same substance · *defined*
**Definition:** A physical change is a change in shape, size or state. No new substance is made.

Melting, freezing, boiling, cutting, bending and dissolving are physical changes. Ice, water and steam are one substance in three states. Salt stirred into water seems to vanish, but the salt is still there. If the water dries up, the same salt is left behind, and it still tastes salty. In the After the Fizz experiment you dry two liquids. Baking soda that was only dissolved in water comes back as a powder that still fizzes in vinegar. Baking soda that reacted with vinegar does not come back. That is the difference between a physical change and a chemical reaction.
- e.g. Wax melts in a warm lamp and sets hard again. It is still wax.
- e.g. Sugar dissolves in tea. The tea is sweet, so the sugar is still there.
- e.g. A sheet of paper torn into pieces is still paper.
- e.g. Water boils into steam, and steam turns back into water on a cool lid.
### What the fizz of vinegar and baking soda makes · *defined*
**Definition:** Vinegar and baking soda react and make three products: carbon dioxide gas, water and a salt called sodium acetate.

Vinegar is water with a little acetic acid in it, about 4 or 5 parts in every 100. Baking soda is a white powder whose full name is sodium bicarbonate. When the two meet, the acetic acid and the baking soda are used up. The carbon dioxide leaves as bubbles, which is the fizz. The water joins the liquid. The sodium acetate stays dissolved, so you do not see it until the liquid is dried. In the After the Fizz experiment, a white crust is left in the pan. It looks a little like baking soda, but vinegar dropped on it makes no fizz. It is sodium acetate, one of the products. The fizz stops when one reactant runs out. If the baking soda runs out first, more vinegar makes no more bubbles.
- e.g. Lime juice and cooking soda fizz in the same way, because lime juice is also sour.
- e.g. Dhokla and bhatura batter rise because soda and sour curd make carbon dioxide inside them.
- e.g. Fruit salt fizzes in water because it holds baking soda and a dry acid together.
### Five signs of a reaction, and why a sign is only a clue · *defined*
**Definition:** A sign of a reaction is something you can notice that suggests a new substance may be forming.

There are five common signs. One, a gas appears, as bubbles or a fizz. Two, the colour changes. Three, a new solid appears in a liquid. Four, the mixture gets warmer or cooler without any stove or ice. Five, light or a new smell is given off. In the Six Mixes, Five Signs activity you look for these. But a sign can mislead. Water bubbles when it boils, and no new substance is made. Tea goes paler when you add plain water, and that is only mixing. Salt seems to disappear in water, and that is only dissolving. So a sign is a clue. To be sure, you test what the substance does afterwards. Does it still behave like the old substance, or does it behave differently?
- e.g. Gas: lime juice fizzes on baking soda.
- e.g. Colour: black tea turns much paler when lime juice goes in.
- e.g. New solid: warm milk makes white lumps when lime juice goes in.
- e.g. Heat and light: a sparkler at Deepavali glows and gets very hot.
### Rusting: iron reacts with oxygen and water · *defined*
**Definition:** Rusting is a slow chemical reaction. Iron, oxygen and water react and make rust.

Iron needs both oxygen and water to rust. Dry iron in dry air stays grey for years. Salt in the water makes rusting faster, so iron rusts quickly near the sea. Steel is mostly iron, so ordinary steel rusts too. Rust takes up more room than the iron it came from. So rust swells, cracks and flakes off, and fresh iron underneath is open to the air again. This is why a rusting gate can be eaten right through. In the Rust Uses Up Air experiment, wet steel wool rusts inside an upturned glass. The water climbs into the glass, because the rusting takes oxygen out of the air inside. People protect iron by keeping oxygen and water away from it. Paint and oil cover it. Galvanising covers it with zinc. Stainless steel has the metal chromium mixed in. The chromium reacts with oxygen and makes a very thin, clear skin, which keeps more oxygen out.
- e.g. A window grill near the sea at Kochi or Visakhapatnam rusts faster than one in dry Jaipur.
- e.g. An iron kadai is rubbed with oil after washing, to keep water and air off it.
- e.g. A galvanised bucket stays grey for years, even though it is wet every day.
- e.g. A stainless steel tumbler does not rust in the kitchen.
### A reaction rearranges atoms. It does not make or destroy them · *defined*
**Definition:** In a chemical reaction, the atoms of the reactants come apart and join up in new groups. Every atom that goes in comes out.

Everything is made of atoms, and there are about 90 kinds in nature. Each kind is called an element. Iron, oxygen, hydrogen and gold are elements. In a reaction the atoms change partners. Take two hydrogen molecules, which have two atoms each, and one oxygen molecule, which has two atoms. Together that is six atoms. They rearrange into two water molecules, which also have six atoms between them. You do this with coins and seeds in the Atom Swap activity. Because no atom is lost, the products weigh exactly as much as the reactants. A fizzing bowl gets lighter only because its gas floats away. If the gas is caught, nothing is lost. A reaction also cannot change one kind of atom into another. No reaction can turn iron atoms or lead atoms into gold atoms.
- e.g. Hydrogen + oxygen → water. Six atoms go in and the same six come out.
- e.g. A bowl of vinegar and baking soda weighs a little less after the fizz, because carbon dioxide has left.
- e.g. Rust weighs more than the iron did, because oxygen atoms from the air have joined it.
### A new substance behaves unlike the substances that made it · *defined*
**Definition:** The properties of a product are its own. They are not a blend of the properties of the reactants.

When you mix lime juice and sugar, the drink is sour and sweet. A mixture keeps the properties of its parts. A reaction is different. Sodium is a soft metal that catches fire in water. Chlorine is a green, poisonous gas. When they react, the product is the salt you sprinkle on your food. Hydrogen is a gas that burns, and oxygen is the gas that flames need. Joined together they make water, which puts fires out. The atoms are the same atoms as before. What changed is which atoms are joined to which. So what a substance does depends on how its atoms are joined. Chemists have recorded more than 200 million different substances, all made from the same few kinds of atoms.
- e.g. Sodium + chlorine → table salt.
- e.g. Hydrogen + oxygen → water.
- e.g. Hard grey iron + invisible oxygen + water → crumbly brown rust.
- e.g. Sour vinegar + baking soda → a gas, water and a salt that does not fizz.
### Reactions give out heat or take heat in · *defined*
**Definition:** Every chemical reaction either gives out energy or takes energy in. We usually notice this as heat.

Burning gives out a lot of heat and light, very fast. Rusting gives out heat too, but so slowly that you do not notice. Some hand-warmer packets hold iron powder, salt and water. When the packet is opened to the air, the iron rusts quickly and the packet stays warm for hours. Other reactions take heat in from whatever is around them. Vinegar and baking soda do this. In the Cold Fizz activity, one tumbler of vinegar fizzes with baking soda. It ends up a few degrees cooler than a tumbler of plain vinegar. A leaf making sugar takes in energy too, as light from the Sun.
- e.g. A burning candle gives out heat and light.
- e.g. Your body stays warm because reactions in your cells give out heat.
- e.g. A fizz of vinegar and baking soda takes in heat and feels cool.
- e.g. A leaf takes in sunlight to make sugar from carbon dioxide and water.
### Living bodies run on chemical reactions · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living body is kept going by chemical reactions, thousands of different kinds at the same time.

When you eat rice, reactions in your mouth and gut break the starch into sugar. In your cells, sugar reacts with the oxygen you breathe in. The products are carbon dioxide and water. The reaction gives out the energy you use to move, think and keep warm. You breathe the carbon dioxide out. A green leaf runs the opposite reaction. With energy from sunlight, the leaf makes sugar and oxygen from carbon dioxide and water. Bacteria run reactions too. Curd bacteria change milk sugar into an acid, and the acid makes the milk set. Humans and other animals, plants and bacteria all stay alive by keeping their reactions going.
- e.g. Sugar + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, in your cells.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide + water → sugar + oxygen, in a leaf in sunlight.
- e.g. Idli batter rises overnight because tiny living beings in the batter make carbon dioxide.
### Reactions on the Earth and far beyond it · *defined*
**Definition:** Chemical reactions happen wherever atoms can meet: in rocks, in the sea, in the air and in space.

Mars looks red from the Earth because the dust on Mars is full of iron that has joined with oxygen. In a way, Mars is a rusty planet. On the Earth, most iron is found in the ground as iron ore, which is iron already joined with oxygen. Steel plants at Bhilai, Visakhapatnam and Jamshedpur run a reaction that takes the oxygen away and leaves iron. Reactions also happen in the cold clouds of gas and dust between the stars. Astronomers have found more than 200 kinds of molecules there, including water. The fastest part of any reaction is the moment when atoms let go and join again. It takes less than a millionth of a millionth of a second. The Egyptian-born chemist Ahmed Zewail found a way to watch this moment with very short flashes of laser light. He won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1999.
- e.g. The red dust of Mars is iron joined with oxygen.
- e.g. Iron ore + carbon, heated very strongly → iron + carbon dioxide.
- e.g. Water molecules have been found in clouds of gas between the stars.

## Mostly agreed
- A chemical reaction makes new substances, with properties different from those of the reactants. *How we know:* Chemists test what substances do before and after a change. The products melt, dissolve, burn and react in different ways from the reactants.
- A reaction rearranges atoms and loses none of them, so the total weight stays the same. *How we know:* In the 1770s and 1780s, Antoine Lavoisier ran reactions in sealed glass vessels and weighed them before and after. The weight did not change.
- Rust is iron joined with oxygen and water, and iron needs both in order to rust. *How we know:* Iron kept in dry air, or under boiled water sealed with oil, stays grey. Iron in open water or damp air rusts.
- About one fifth of the air is oxygen. *How we know:* When iron rusts or a fuel burns in a closed space, about one fifth of the air is used up. Careful measurements give 21 parts of oxygen in every 100 parts of air.
- The gas made by vinegar and baking soda is carbon dioxide. *How we know:* The gas is heavier than air, puts out a flame and turns clear limewater milky. The Scottish chemist Joseph Black studied this gas in the 1750s.
- No chemical reaction can change one element into another. *How we know:* Alchemists tried for about two thousand years to make gold from other metals, and none did. We now know that a reaction only changes how atoms are joined.

## How we found out
**The alchemists, including Mary the Jewess. Then Robert Boyle, Antoine Lavoisier and Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, and John Dalton., Alchemy lasted from about 2,000 years ago until the 1700s. Boyle wrote in 1661, Lavoisier in 1789 and Dalton in 1808..** For about two thousand years, many people believed that the right reaction could turn lead into gold. They were called alchemists, and they worked in Egypt, China, India, the Arab world and Europe. One of the earliest we know of was a woman called Mary the Jewess. She worked in Egypt, perhaps 1,700 to 2,000 years ago. Her dates are not certain. She is said to have invented the gentle water bath that cooks still use. Alchemists found real things, such as strong acids and better ways to purify substances. But nobody ever made gold. In 1661 Robert Boyle suggested that an element is a substance that cannot be broken into simpler ones. In 1789 Antoine Lavoisier in France listed 33 substances that he thought were simple. Gold and lead were both on his list. The drawings in his book were made by his wife, the chemist Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier. In 1808 John Dalton, a teacher in Manchester, explained why gold could not be made. Each element has its own kind of atom. A reaction only moves atoms into new groups. So lead atoms stay lead atoms, whatever they are mixed with.  
*What people believed before:* That a chemical reaction can turn one metal into another, such as lead into gold. Two thousand years of failed attempts counted against it. Dalton then showed why. A reaction rearranges atoms, and it cannot change one kind of atom into another.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A chemical change can never be undone.** (Many school books say this.; *rejected*)  
  For: Burnt paper cannot be unburnt, and a boiled egg cannot be made raw again.  
  Against: Many reactions can be reversed by another reaction. Tea made pale by lime juice goes dark again with baking soda. Steel plants turn iron ore, which is like rust, back into iron. A phone battery runs its reactions backwards each time it is charged.
- **Bubbles always mean that a chemical reaction is happening.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Vinegar and baking soda bubble, and that is a reaction.  
  Against: Boiling water bubbles, and a bottle of soda bubbles when it is opened. In both cases no new substance is made. A sign is a clue, and only a test of the substance can settle it.
- **Dissolving salt in water is a chemical change, not only a physical one.** (Some chemists and chemistry teachers.; *debated*)  
  For: When salt dissolves, its crystals break up into tiny charged particles, and water molecules crowd round each one. The salty water carries electricity, which dry salt and pure water hardly do.  
  Against: Most school books call dissolving a physical change, because the same salt comes back when the water dries. Both sides agree on what happens. They disagree about which name fits best.
- **Rust weighs less than the iron it came from, because the iron is being eaten away.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: A rusty gate looks thinner and weaker, and bits fall off it.  
  Against: If all the rust is collected, it weighs more than the iron did. The extra weight is oxygen and water that have joined the iron.
- **The right chemical reaction could turn lead into gold.** (Alchemists in many countries, for about two thousand years.; *rejected*)  
  For: Reactions do change substances completely. A grey metal can become a red powder, so gold did not seem impossible.  
  Against: A reaction only changes how atoms are joined. It cannot change lead atoms into gold atoms. Around 1980, scientists did change a tiny amount of the metal bismuth into gold. They did it by smashing atoms in a giant machine, which is not a chemical reaction. It cost far more than the gold was worth.

## Roots
In this unit you tested the powder left after a fizz, and watched steel wool rust inside a glass. Here we ask where the properties of a new substance come from.

**Deep truth.** In After the Fizz, vinegar and baking soda left a new powder that no longer fizzed. In Rust Uses Up Air, hard grey steel and a gas from the air made brown crumbs. Each new substance behaved unlike both of the substances that made it. So to know what a new substance does, you have to test the substance itself.  
*Habit it breaks:* We expect a thing made from parts to be like its parts. A drink of lime juice and sugar is sour and sweet. In a chemical reaction this habit fails, because the product can behave unlike every reactant.  
*Evidence:* In After the Fizz, the crust left by vinegar and baking soda did not fizz in vinegar. Baking soda always does. In Rust Uses Up Air, steel and oxygen made rust, which is brown and crumbly. Sodium burns in water and chlorine is a poisonous gas, yet together they make table salt.

- *who am i:* Your body is made of food, water and air that have reacted together. You run, grow and mend your own cuts. A plate of rice and a glass of water do none of these.
- *what is life:* A living body is thousands of reactions kept going at once. In your cells, oxygen reacts with food, much as oxygen reacts with iron. Your body does it in small, careful steps.
- *what is this world:* Nature has only about 90 kinds of atoms. The sea, a mango, a steel bridge and you are these same atoms, joined in different ways.

A few kinds of atoms make millions of different substances, because joining gives properties that were not seen before. Hydrogen and oxygen are both gases, and joined together they are water. Many sages taught that all the different things in the world come from one source. Chemistry shows one small part of this that anyone can test. The same few kinds of atoms make all the substances around us. Everything is conscious, down to the atoms. How the awareness of atoms joins into the awareness of a whole body is a question nobody has answered.

- **Milindapanha (The Questions of King Milinda), Book 2, chapter 1: the chariot:** The monk Nagasena asks King Milinda where his chariot is. Is it the wheels, the axle, the pole or the seat? It is none of these. 'Chariot' is the name for all the parts when they are put together. (Nagasena was speaking about the self, and not about chemistry. Yet rust is like his chariot. Rust is not found in iron, in oxygen or in water. It exists only when they are joined.)
- **John Stuart Mill, A System of Logic (1843), Book 3, chapter 6:** Mill pointed out that water has none of the properties of hydrogen or of oxygen. He said that what chemical joining produces cannot be found by adding up what each part does alone. (This is what the After the Fizz experiment showed. The new crust did not do what baking soda does. It had to be tested as a substance in its own right.)
- Science (settled): Since the 1920s, physicists have been able to explain why atoms join, using quantum physics. For small molecules, computers can now calculate properties such as colour before anyone makes the substance.
- Science (debated): Whether everything about a whole can be worked out from its parts is still argued. In 1972 the physicist Philip Anderson wrote an essay called 'More Is Different'. He argued that each level of size needs laws of its own.
- Science (open): Philosophers who hold that awareness is in all matter face the 'combination problem'. Nobody has yet explained how many small awarenesses could join into one, as in a person.
- Other view, The Charvaka thinkers of ancient India, as later writers describe them: The Charvakas said that awareness is itself a new property of this kind. Betel leaf, areca nut and lime are not red, yet chewed together they turn red. In the same way, they said, awareness appears when the elements join in a body. It ends when the body ends. Most scientists who study the brain today hold a similar view.
- Practice, The warm reaction (3 min): Sit still and put one hand flat on your chest. Breathe in slowly. The oxygen in that breath is going to your cells. There it reacts with the food you ate today. Now feel the warmth under your hand. That warmth is given out by those reactions. Breathe out. The carbon dioxide leaving you is one of their products. Sit quietly for five more breaths.
- Ask: In After the Fizz, the new crust did not fizz. Where did the fizzing of the baking soda go?
- Ask: Rust is brown, and neither iron nor oxygen is brown. Was the brown hidden in them, or is it new?
- Ask: You are made of atoms that were once food, water and air. Is there anything in you that was not in them?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Baking soda fizzes away in vinegar. Every one of its atoms is still in the pan or the air. Is the baking soda still there? (hint: Think about the letters of a word. If you rearrange the same letters to spell a different word, is the first word still there?)
- *deep:* Bubbles, colour and warmth can all mislead. How can anyone be sure that a new substance has formed? (hint: In the After the Fizz experiment, you dropped vinegar on a white crust to see whether it fizzed.)
- *deep:* Hydrogen is not wet and oxygen is not wet. Where does the wetness of water come from? (hint: Is wetness inside the atoms, or does it belong to the way they are joined?)
- *big:* Iron is made from a rusty-looking rock called iron ore. When iron rusts, is something being spoiled or being returned? (hint: The owner of the gate would say the gate is being spoiled. Someone thinking about the ground the ore came from might say the iron is going back to what it was.)
- *big:* Chemists can make substances that never existed before, such as plastics. Who should decide which new substances are made? (hint: A new substance can be useful for fifty years and stay in the sea for five hundred.)
- *big:* Your body is made from food, water and air that have reacted. Is there anything in you that was not in them? (hint: Think about rust. It is brown, but iron is grey and oxygen has no colour. So where did the brown come from? Is there something like that in you?)

## Story: The Lamp Went Down First
*Devika, Ammamma, Sheeba chechi and their well are made up. These things are real. Carbon dioxide is about one and a half times as heavy as air. It collects in old wells, pits and tanks where leaves and other things rot. People have long lowered a flame to test for it. Stirring the air with leafy branches is an old method. Lifting the gas out with a bucket is Devika's idea in the story, and it works only slowly. The lamp test is not a safety rule. A well can hold other dangerous gases, or too little oxygen, even where a flame still burns. Trained workers today use gas meters and blowers. Children should never lean over a well or go into a well, pit or tank.*

The well behind Ammamma's house was older than the house. Its wall was built of red laterite stone, and small ferns grew out of the cracks.

For two years nobody had drawn water from it. Ammamma had been living with Devika's family in Kochi, and the house near Thrissur had stayed locked. Now it was the April holidays. They had come back, and the well had to be cleaned before anyone drank from it.

Sheeba chechi came at seven in the morning with a coir rope, two buckets and a bamboo ladder. She cleaned wells all over the village. She looked down at the dark water, and at the thick mat of jackfruit leaves floating on it.

"Two years of leaves," she said. "This will take until lunch."

Devika, who was nine, stood back from the wall, where Ammamma had told her to stand. From there she could still see down the well. The air inside it looked like any other air. She could see straight through it to the leaves.

Sheeba chechi tied the rope round her waist and swung one leg over the wall.

"Wait," said Ammamma. "The lamp goes down first."

She had brought a small clay lamp from the kitchen, with oil and a cotton wick. She lit it, stood it in a bucket and let the rope out slowly. Devika watched the flame go down, steady and yellow. Half way to the water it shrank to a blue bead. Then it went out.

"Wind," said Sheeba chechi.

"There is no wind in a well," said Ammamma. She pulled the bucket up, lit the lamp and lowered it again. At the same depth, the flame went out again.

Sheeba chechi took her leg back over the wall. Nobody spoke.

Devika stared into the well. Nothing had touched the flame, and she could see nothing in the air around it. Then she remembered the evening before.

The power had gone off at dusk. Ammamma had been stirring a pinch of cooking soda into lime juice, because her stomach felt heavy after the journey. The tumbler frothed and hissed beside the lamp on the table. Devika had picked the tumbler up to look, and tipped it a little towards the flame. Not one drop spilled. But the flame bent, shrank and went out.

"What did I do?" she had asked.

"You poured something on it," Ammamma had said, lighting the lamp again. "When soda meets something sour, the two of them make a new gas. You cannot see it. It is heavier than air, so it sits in the tumbler the way water does. And a flame goes out in it."

Now Devika looked from the well to her grandmother. "Ammamma, is it the same gas down there?"

"The same," said Ammamma. "Leaves do not just lie there. They rot, and rotting is a slow change. It makes that same heavy gas, a little every day. In an open field the wind carries it off. In a well it has nowhere to go. For two years it has been filling this well from the bottom."

"A person who climbs into it cannot tell," said Sheeba chechi quietly. "It has no colour and no smell. A man I know went down a well like this, in Palakkad. They pulled him out just in time."

Devika thought of the tumbler again. The gas had stayed in it like water. It had poured like water.

"If it sits there like water," she said slowly, "can we lift it out like water? With the bucket?"

Sheeba chechi laughed, and then she stopped laughing. "We usually stir it out," she said. "We tie leafy branches to a rope and pull them up and down, so that fresh air mixes in. But a bucket will carry some up as well. We will do both."

So for an hour they worked. Sheeba chechi lowered the empty bucket to the water and drew it up gently. Each time she carried it to the edge of the yard and tipped out what looked like nothing at all. Devika and Ammamma pulled a bundle of mango branches up and down on the second rope until their arms ached. A crow watched from the jackfruit tree while three people worked hard at emptying an empty well.

Then Ammamma lit the lamp once more and let it down. The flame passed the half-way mark. It went on down, small and yellow, until it stood just above the leaves. It stayed lit.

Sheeba chechi kept the lamp burning in a bucket beside her all the time she worked, and the rope stayed round her waist. Ammamma held the other end and watched the flame. By lunch the leaves lay in a wet heap in the yard, and the water at the bottom showed a round piece of sky.

That evening Devika squeezed half a lime into a tumbler and added a pinch of soda. She watched the froth rise and settle. Above the liquid, the tumbler looked empty. She knew now that it was not.

**For the parent:** The gas at the bottom of the well looked exactly like air. So did the gas above the fizzing lime juice. Devika could tell each one from air only by what it did to a flame. A reaction makes a new substance, and you know a new substance by how it behaves.

- Ask: Why could nobody see that the air in the well had changed?
- Ask: The leaves in the well were not burning or fizzing. How could they make a gas?
- Ask: How would you tell two clear liquids apart without tasting them?

### g1 · Reaction Families: two reactants and their product (20 min)
- **rules:** A family is three cards: two reactants and the product they make. The answer key shows all eight families.; Shuffle the cards. Deal five cards to each player. Put the rest face down in a pile, and turn the top card face up beside it.; On your turn, take one card. Take either the top card of the pile or the face-up card.; If you hold a whole family, lay it down and read it aloud as a word equation. For example, say 'iron plus oxygen and water make rust'.; The other players check the family against the answer key. A wrong family goes back into your hand.; End your turn by putting one card face up on the face-up card. Two cards that name the same substance can stand in for each other.; The game ends when the pile runs out. The player with the most families wins.; Playing alone? Spread all 24 cards face up and sort them into eight families. Time yourself, then check with the answer key. Play again to beat your time.
- **Printable cards: 24 reaction cards: 16 reactants and 8 products:** REACTANT. Iron. A hard grey metal.; REACTANT. Oxygen and water, from damp air.; PRODUCT. Rust. Brown and crumbly.; REACTANT. Vinegar. A sour liquid.; REACTANT. Baking soda. A white powder.; PRODUCT. Carbon dioxide gas, water and a salt.; REACTANT. Hydrogen. A gas that burns.; REACTANT. Oxygen. The gas that flames need.; PRODUCT. Water. A liquid that puts out fires.; REACTANT. Sodium. A soft metal that catches fire in water.; REACTANT. Chlorine. A green, poisonous gas.; PRODUCT. Table salt. White grains that you eat.; REACTANT. Quicklime. White lumps made by heating limestone.; REACTANT. Water. A clear liquid.; PRODUCT. Slaked lime, called chuna. The mixture gets very hot.; REACTANT. Carbon dioxide. A gas in the air.; REACTANT. Water, with sunlight, inside a green leaf.; PRODUCT. Sugar and oxygen.; REACTANT. Silver. A shiny white metal.; REACTANT. Sulfur. Tiny amounts float in the air.; PRODUCT. Black tarnish on anklets and lamps.; REACTANT. Sugar, inside the cells of your body.; REACTANT. Oxygen, which you breathe in.; PRODUCT. Carbon dioxide and water, with energy to move and keep warm.
- **Printable board: Answer key: the eight reactions:** Iron + oxygen and water → rust; Vinegar + baking soda → carbon dioxide gas, water and a salt; Hydrogen + oxygen → water; Sodium + chlorine → table salt; Quicklime + water → slaked lime (chuna), and heat; Carbon dioxide + water, with sunlight in a leaf → sugar and oxygen; Silver + sulfur → black tarnish; Sugar + oxygen, in your cells → carbon dioxide and water, with energy
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the answer key. Spread the cards face up. Read three or four of them aloud together.  
  *Parent says:* Each card names one substance. A reactant is a substance that goes into a reaction. A product is a new substance that comes out.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Spread them face up. Read a few aloud.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* With the cards still face up, your child tries to build all eight families before looking at the answer key. Then check each family against the key.  
  *Parent says:* Try to put each product with the two reactants that make it. Guess first. We will check with the answer key afterwards.  
  *Child mode:* Put each product with its two reactants. Then check the key.
  - Ask: Which product looks least like the reactants that made it? (good: Table salt, because sodium burns and chlorine is poisonous.; Water, because hydrogen and oxygen are both gases.; Rust, because iron is hard and rust crumbles.)
  - Note: This sorting round teaches the families. Do not worry about mistakes here. Two cards say Oxygen and two say Water, and either one may be used in either family.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Shuffle and deal five cards each. Play by the rules until the pile runs out. Each family that is laid down must be read aloud as a word equation.  
  *Parent says:* Now we play. Draw one card, lay down a family if you have one, and put one card back. Read every family aloud.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a card. Lay down a family if you can. Put one card back.
  - Note: A child playing alone sorts all 24 cards against a timer, and then tries to beat that time.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the families. Then look at the eight product cards together and ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the eight products. Does any product look like the reactants that made it?  
  *Child mode:* Count your families. Then look at the eight product cards.
  - Check: Sodium is a metal that reacts fiercely with water. Chlorine is a poisonous gas. What do they make together? → **Table salt, which is safe to eat**

## Experiments
### e1 · After the Fizz: is the baking soda still there? (35 min)
- **question:** Baking soda stirred into water dissolves. Baking soda stirred into vinegar fizzes. If both liquids are dried, does baking soda come back from each one?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. When the two liquids are dried, what will be left in each pan? Will each leftover still fizz when vinegar is dropped on it? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same amount of baking soda in both tumblers. Do this by making four equal heaps from one level teaspoon. Use the same amount of liquid in both: 2 tablespoons and 1 teaspoon. Dry both liquids in the same kind of pan, on the same low flame. Let both pans cool before the drop test, because a hot pan makes any liquid sizzle. Drop the same 5 drops of vinegar on each leftover. Only one thing differs: one tumbler has water and the other has vinegar.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the heating. Hot vinegar gives off a sharp smell, so open a window and keep faces away from the pan. Salt-like crusts can spit when they are nearly dry, so use a low flame and stand back. Let the pans cool before anyone touches them. Nobody tastes anything in this experiment.
- **record:** Fill in the results table with one row for the water pan and one row for the vinegar pan. Add a third row for fresh baking soda. Then write one sentence that begins 'After the fizz, the baking soda was…'.
- **results table:** Which liquid was dried | What was left in the pan | Did it fizz when 5 drops of vinegar were added?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the baking soda, the water and the vinegar. Explain what you will do. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will stir baking soda into water, and the same amount into vinegar. Then we will dry both liquids. What will be left in each pan? Will each leftover still fizz in vinegar? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what will be left in each pan after drying.
  - Ask: Why do you think that? (good: The powder is still in the water, so it will come back.; The fizz used the powder up, so nothing will be left.; Something will be left, but I do not know what.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect baking soda to come back from both, and some expect an empty pan after the fizz.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tip 1 level teaspoon of baking soda on to the dry plate. Divide it into 4 equal heaps with the knife. Put 2 tablespoons of water in tumbler W and 2 tablespoons of vinegar in tumbler V. Tip one heap into each tumbler and stir.  
  *Parent says:* Make four heaps of the same size. One heap goes into the water and one into the vinegar. Watch both tumblers. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Make four equal heaps. Tip one into water and one into vinegar. Watch.
  - Note: Keep the two spare heaps on the plate. One is for the drop test later. Do the stirring on a tray, because the vinegar tumbler froths up.
  - Check: Why must both tumblers get the same amount of baking soda? → **So only the liquid is different**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* When the fizz in tumbler V has stopped, add 1 more teaspoon of vinegar and watch. If it fizzes again, wait and add another teaspoon. Add 1 teaspoon of water to tumbler W for each teaspoon of vinegar.  
  *Parent says:* Has the fizz stopped? Add one more teaspoon of vinegar. If there are no new bubbles, the baking soda has all reacted.  
  *Child mode:* Add one more teaspoon of vinegar. Are there new bubbles?
  - Note: This step makes sure no baking soda is left over. With most vinegars, the first extra teaspoon gives no bubbles. Lime juice varies, so it may need two or three.
- **s4 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Open a window. Pour tumbler W into one pan and heat it on a low flame until the liquid has just gone. Take it off the stove. Do the same with tumbler V in the second pan. Stand both pans where they can cool.  
  *Parent says:* I will dry each liquid on the stove. Stand back and watch from there. Tell me what you see appear as the water goes.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up dries both liquids. Stand back. Watch what appears in each pan.
  - Note: Each pan takes about 3 to 4 minutes. Take the pan off while a small wet patch is left. The heat of the pan finishes the job. The vinegar pan smells sharp. If you prefer no stove, pour each liquid into a wide steel plate and leave it under a fan. It dries in about a day, or two days in the rains.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wait until both pans are cool enough to hold. Look closely at the two crusts together. Scrape a little of each with the knife.  
  *Parent says:* Both pans have something white in them. Do the two crusts look the same? Can you tell by looking which one is baking soda?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both white crusts. Do they look the same?
  - Note: The pans must be cool. A drop of any liquid sizzles on a hot pan, and that would look like a fizz. The crust from the vinegar pan is often a little glassy or flaky.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Drop 5 drops of vinegar on a spare heap of fresh baking soda. Then drop 5 drops on the crust in pan W, and 5 drops on the crust in pan V. Record each result.  
  *Parent says:* First, see what fresh baking soda does with five drops of vinegar. Now try the crust from the water pan. Now try the crust from the vinegar pan.  
  *Child mode:* Drop vinegar on fresh baking soda. Then on each crust. Which ones fizz?
  - Note: In most homes the fresh heap and the water crust fizz at once. The vinegar crust only gets wet and starts to dissolve. If the vinegar crust fizzes a little, some baking soda was left over. That means step 3 needed more vinegar.
  - Check: A white crust does not fizz when vinegar is dropped on it. What does that tell you? → **It is not baking soda**
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the results table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the table together. Where is the baking soda that went into the vinegar?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Baking soda came back from the water. Why did it not come back from the vinegar? (good: It reacted with the vinegar and became something new.; The fizz used it up.; It turned into the gas and the new crust.)
  - Ask: Both crusts are white. How did you tell them apart? (good: By what they did with vinegar.; One fizzed and one did not.)
  - Note: The reason is that in water, baking soda only dissolves, so the same substance is left when the water goes. In vinegar, baking soda reacts with the acid. The products are carbon dioxide gas, water and a salt called sodium acetate. The gas left as bubbles, and the sodium acetate is the crust. The dried water crust may be partly changed by the heat into washing soda, which also fizzes.
  - Think: The bubbles floated away. How could you find out what gas they were made of?
### e2 · Rust Uses Up Air: how far will the water climb? (30 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Wet steel wool is left to rust inside an upturned glass that stands in water. Does the water climb into the glass? If it does, how far does it climb before it stops?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the water inside the glass with steel wool go up, go down or stay where it is? How far will it move in three days? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two glass tumblers of the same size and shape. Push a wad of wet steel wool into the bottom of one glass. Push a wad of wet tissue paper of the same size into the other. Stand both upside down in the same depth of water, side by side, away from sunlight. Mark both water levels at the start, and measure both at the same time each day. Only one thing differs: one glass has iron in it.
- **record:** Fill in one row of the results table each day. On day 3, draw the glass with steel wool and mark the starting level and the last level, or take a photo of each glass.
- **results table:** Day | Glass with steel wool: how many centimetres the water has climbed | Glass with wet tissue: how many centimetres the water has climbed | What the steel wool looks like
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Set up the two upturned glasses and mark the water levels. (15 min); day 2: Measure how far the water has climbed in each glass. Look at the steel wool. (5 min); day 3: Measure again. Work out what share of the air was used. Explain the result. (10 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. Show your child the steel wool and the two glasses. Explain the set-up. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will leave wet steel wool inside an upturned glass that stands in water. Will the water inside the glass go up, go down or stay? How far will it move in three days? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Wet steel wool sits in an upturned glass. Will the water inside move? Why?
  - Ask: What is inside the glass, besides the steel wool? (good: Air.; Air, and a little water at the bottom.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Some children expect no change, because the glass 'has nothing in it'.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Soak the steel wool in the vinegar for 1 minute. Squeeze it out and pull it loose. Push it into the bottom of one glass so that it stays there when the glass is turned over. Wet the tissue with water and push it into the other glass.  
  *Parent says:* The vinegar cleans the thin coat off the steel, so that rusting can start. Push the wad right to the bottom. It must not fall out.  
  *Child mode:* Dip the steel wool in vinegar. Squeeze it. Push it into the glass.
  - Note: Pull the wad loose so that air can reach all of it. If steel wool pricks the fingers, use a spoon or wear a kitchen glove. Wash hands afterwards.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour water 2 centimetres deep into each plate. Put two coins in each plate. Stand each glass upside down with its rim on the coins. Mark the water level inside each glass with tape. Measure from the tape to the top of the air space and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* The coins let water pass under the rim. Mark where the water stands inside each glass now. How tall is the air space above the mark?  
  *Child mode:* Stand both glasses upside down in water. Mark the water level with tape.
  - Note: Keep the glasses out of direct sun, where they will not be knocked. Top up the plates if the water gets low. The rims must stay under water all the time.
  - Check: Why is there a second glass, with wet tissue and no steel wool? → **To show what happens without iron**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2. At about the same time of day, measure from the tape up to the new water level inside each glass. Record both. Look at the colour of the steel wool.  
  *Parent says:* Has the water moved in either glass? Measure from the tape to the water. What has happened to the steel wool?  
  *Child mode:* Measure from the tape to the water in each glass. Look at the steel wool.
  - Note: With steel wool cleaned in vinegar, the water has usually climbed 1 centimetre or more by now. If nothing has moved, check that the wool is the iron kind and that it is still damp.
  - Wait: Do this one day after setting up
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 3. Measure and record both levels again. Compare the climb with the height of the air space you wrote down on day 1.  
  *Parent says:* Measure again. How far has the water climbed in all? Is that more or less than half of the air space?  
  *Child mode:* Measure both glasses again. How far has the water climbed in all?
  - Note: The water usually stops after climbing about one fifth of the air space. In a glass with 10 centimetres of air that is about 2 centimetres. Anything from 1.5 to 2.5 centimetres is a good result. If the water is still climbing, measure once more tomorrow.
  - Wait: Do this two days after setting up
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the results table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the table together. Why did the water stop climbing, when there is still steel wool left?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: What took up the space that the water moved into? (good: Air that got used up.; The part of the air that joined the iron.; Oxygen.)
  - Ask: Why did the water in the tissue glass stay where it was? (good: There was no iron to rust.; Nothing used up the air in that glass.)
  - Note: The reason is that rusting is a reaction between iron, oxygen and water. The steel wool took oxygen out of the air in the glass, and the oxygen became part of the rust. Water moved up to fill the space. About one fifth of the air is oxygen. When the oxygen had gone, the rusting stopped, although iron was left. The rest of the air is mostly nitrogen, which does not react with iron.
  - Think: What would happen if you lifted the glass for a moment, let fresh air in and set it down again?

## Activities
### a1 · Six Mixes, Five Signs: which mixes make something new? (20 min)
- **record:** A table with six rows, one for each mix. Write what you mixed, which sign you saw, and your decision: 'new substance' or 'same substances'.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set out the six cups and the ingredients on a tray. Write the five signs at the top of the table. They are: a gas appears, the colour changes, a new solid appears, the mix warms or cools, light or a new smell.  
  *Parent says:* A sign is a clue that a new substance may be forming. There are five common signs. We will make six mixes and hunt for them. Nobody tastes anything.  
  *Child mode:* Set out six cups. Learn the five signs. Do not taste anything.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mix 1: 1 teaspoon of baking soda and 2 teaspoons of lime juice. Mix 2: 1 teaspoon of salt stirred into 4 tablespoons of water. Mix 3: 1 teaspoon of baking soda stirred into 4 tablespoons of water. Before each mix, ask for a guess. After each, record the sign.  
  *Parent says:* Before each mix, tell me what you think will happen. Then mix, watch for a minute, and tell me which sign you saw, if any.  
  *Child mode:* Make mixes 1, 2 and 3. Guess first. Which signs do you see?
  - Ask: The salt has disappeared into the water. Is that one of the five signs of a reaction? (good: No, disappearing is not on the list.; No. The salt is still there, because the water would taste salty.)
  - Note: Mix 1 fizzes at once. Mixes 2 and 3 show no sign: the powders only dissolve. A little baking soda may stay at the bottom of mix 3.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mix 4: 4 tablespoons of black tea and 2 teaspoons of lime juice. Mix 5: 4 tablespoons of black tea and 2 teaspoons of water. Mix 6: 4 tablespoons of warm milk and 2 teaspoons of lime juice. Hold cups 4 and 5 side by side against white paper.  
  *Parent says:* Guess first each time. Compare the two cups of tea. Did both change colour by the same amount? Now watch the milk for one minute.  
  *Child mode:* Make mixes 4, 5 and 6. Compare the two teas. Watch the milk.
  - Note: The tea with lime juice turns much paler, from dark brown to orange. The tea with water hardly changes. The warm milk makes small white lumps in a thin, watery liquid. Cold milk does this more slowly.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. For each mix, your child decides: new substance, or the same substances? Then ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Which mixes do you think made something new? Which ones only mixed or dissolved? How sure are you about each one?  
  *Child mode:* Decide for each mix. Is there a new substance, or the same ones?
  - Ask: Mix 5 went a tiny bit paler. Why is that not a reaction? (good: The water only spread the tea out.; It is the same tea, with more water in it.)
  - Ask: How could you check whether the baking soda in mix 3 is still baking soda? (good: Dry it and see if the powder still fizzes.; Add lime juice and see if it fizzes.)
  - Note: Mixes 1, 4 and 6 are reactions. Mixes 2, 3 and 5 are not. A sign is only a clue. In the After the Fizz experiment, you check by drying a liquid and testing the powder that is left.
  - Check: Which of these is a sign of a reaction? → **Bubbles appear when two things are mixed**
### a2 · Cold Fizz: can your hands feel a reaction? (15 min)
- **record:** Write four lines, one for each try. Put a tick if you picked the fizz tumbler as the cooler one, and a cross if you did not.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour 100 millilitres of vinegar into each tumbler, which is about half a cup. Stand both on the tray for 2 minutes. Your child holds one in each hand and says whether they feel the same.  
  *Parent says:* Both tumblers hold the same vinegar from the same bottle. Hold one in each hand. Do they feel equally cool? When one fizzes, will it get warmer, get cooler or stay the same? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Hold both tumblers. Do they feel the same? What will the fizz do?
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is fine. Many children expect a fizz to be warm, because it looks like boiling.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add 2 level teaspoons of baking soda to one tumbler, half a teaspoon at a time, and stir. Stir the other tumbler too. Wait 1 minute for the froth to settle.  
  *Parent says:* Add the baking soda slowly, so the froth stays in the tumbler. Stir the plain one as well, so both are treated alike.  
  *Child mode:* Add baking soda to one tumbler, slowly. Stir both. Wait one minute.
  - Note: The froth rises fast, so keep the tumblers on the tray. Wipe any spilled liquid off the outside of the tumblers before the next step.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover your child's eyes. Swap the tumblers round, or leave them, without saying which. Put one in each of their hands. They say which hand holds the cooler one. Do this 4 times and record each answer.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes covered. I am putting one tumbler in each hand. Which hand holds the cooler one? Take your time.  
  *Child mode:* Cover your eyes. Hold both tumblers. Which hand has the cooler one?
  - Note: The fizz tumbler is usually 3 to 5 degrees cooler, which most hands can tell. Do the four tries quickly, because the tumblers slowly warm up again. If you have a kitchen thermometer, measure both. Do not use a fever thermometer.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the ticks. Ask your child what they found, and where the coolness came from. Then explain, using the note below.  
  *Parent says:* How many times out of four did you pick the fizz tumbler? Nobody added ice. So why is it cooler?  
  *Child mode:* Count your ticks. Then tell your grown-up why you think the fizz tumbler was cooler.
  - Ask: Which of the five signs of a reaction did you just feel? (good: The mix got cooler by itself.; A change in warmth.)
  - Note: The reaction of vinegar and baking soda takes in heat. It takes the heat from the liquid and the tumbler, so they cool down. Burning does the opposite and gives heat out. If both tumblers felt the same every time, say so plainly. The difference is small and some hands miss it.
### a3 · Atom Swap: make water and rust without losing an atom (15 min)
- **record:** For each reaction, write the number of each kind of atom before and after. Take a photo of the groups you made, or draw them.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On the 'Before' side, make two pairs of pale seeds and one pair of rajma. Explain that each pair is a molecule. Your child counts the atoms of each kind and writes the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* A pale seed is a hydrogen atom. A rajma is an oxygen atom. Hydrogen gas is made of pairs, and so is oxygen gas. How many atoms of each kind are on the table?  
  *Child mode:* Make two pairs of pale seeds and one pair of rajma. Count them.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child moves the same seeds to the 'After' side and makes water molecules. Each water molecule is one rajma with two pale seeds. No seed may be added or left behind. Count again.  
  *Parent says:* A water molecule is one oxygen atom with two hydrogen atoms. Use only the seeds you have. How many water molecules can you make? Are any seeds left over?  
  *Child mode:* Move the seeds across. Make water: one rajma with two pale seeds.
  - Note: The seeds make exactly two water molecules, with nothing left over. Four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms go in, and the same six come out.
  - Check: Two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule react. How many atoms are there afterwards? → **Six, the same as before**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Clear the paper. On the 'Before' side put 4 coins, each on its own, and 3 pairs of rajma. Your child regroups them on the 'After' side into rust: each group has 2 coins and 3 rajma. Count before and after.  
  *Parent says:* A coin is an iron atom. Rust is made of groups of two iron atoms with three oxygen atoms. How many groups can you make? Is anything left over?  
  *Child mode:* Put down 4 coins and 6 rajma. Make groups of 2 coins and 3 rajma.
  - Note: This makes exactly two groups. It is a simple picture of rust. Real rust also holds some water.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 4 coins and only 1 pair of rajma on the 'Before' side. Ask how much rust can be made now. Then ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Now there are four iron atoms and only two oxygen atoms. Can you finish even one group of rust? What would you need more of?  
  *Child mode:* Try with 4 coins and only 2 rajma. Can you finish one group?
  - Ask: Could you make a gold atom by regrouping these coins and seeds? (good: No, there is no gold atom on the table.; No. Regrouping cannot make a new kind of atom.)
  - Ask: What happens to a reaction when one reactant runs out? (good: It stops.; The other reactant is left over.)
  - Note: With two oxygen atoms, no group of rust can be finished, because each group needs three. The reaction stops when a reactant runs out. In the Rust Uses Up Air experiment, the rusting stops when the oxygen in the glass is used up. Steel wool is still left.

## Projects
### p1 · The Rust Survey: ten iron things and how each one is protected (30 min, 2 days)
- **record:** A table with ten rows and four columns: the thing, where it is kept (dry, sometimes wet, or outdoors), how it is protected (paint, oil, zinc coat, stainless steel or nothing), and whether you can see rust.
- **schedule:** day 1: Find ten things made of iron or steel at home. Record where each one is, how it is protected and whether it has rust. (20 min); day 2: Sort the ten things. Find the pattern. Draw a rust map of your home. (10 min)
- **s1 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. Walk through your home together. Find ten things of iron or steel. Look at window grills, gates, a tawa, scissors, buckets, taps, a cycle, a cupboard, a lock and steel tumblers. For each one, fill in the four columns.  
  *Parent says:* For each thing, tell me three things. Is it kept dry, or does it get wet? What covers the metal? Can you see any rust? Before we start, where do you expect to find the most rust?  
  *Child mode:* Find ten iron or steel things. Is each one wet or dry? Covered? Rusty?
  - Note: Rust often shows where paint is chipped, at joints and screws, and low down where water collects. Do not touch flaking rust with a cut finger, and wash hands afterwards. A magnet does not stick to some stainless steel, so count kitchen tumblers anyway.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2. Sort the ten things into two lists: rust seen, and no rust seen. Look for what the rusty things share.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the rusty list. What do those things have in common? Now look at the list with no rust. What kept the oxygen and water away from them?  
  *Child mode:* Make two lists: rust and no rust. What do the rusty things share?
  - Ask: Why is there rust where the paint has chipped? (good: Air and water can reach the iron there.; The paint was keeping oxygen and water out.)
  - Wait: Do this the day after the survey
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a simple plan of your home. Mark an orange dot wherever you found rust. Talk about the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Where are most of the dots? Which rooms are the wettest? If you could protect one thing better, which would it be, and how?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a plan of your home, or just list the rooms. Mark the rust with orange dots, or count the rusty things in each room. Where is the most rust?
  - Note: Most homes find the dots at balconies, bathrooms and near the sink. In towns by the sea there are usually more, because salt speeds up rusting.

## Challenges
### c1 · The Self-Filling Bag: firm in 30 seconds, with no burst (25 min)
- **problem:** A car's airbag fills in less than a tenth of a second. A chemical reaction inside the airbag makes the gas. Your problem is a slower one. Make a sealed bag fill itself with gas from vinegar and baking soda. The bag must be firm within 30 seconds of mixing, and it must not pop open.
- **your turn:** Write your best recipe on a card, so clearly that someone else can follow it. Then take a bag of a different size. Work out the amounts before you test, and see how close you get.

## Worksheets
### New substance, or the same substance? (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a chemical reaction?  
   Options: A change that makes new substances / Any change that makes bubbles / A change of shape or size / Any change caused by heat  
   **Answer:** A change that makes new substances. *Why:* A chemical reaction turns substances into new substances with different properties. Bubbles and heat can also come with changes that make nothing new, such as boiling.
2. (truefalse) When ice melts, a new substance is made.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Ice and water are the same substance in two states. Melting is a physical change, and the water can freeze again.
3. (fill) The substances that go into a chemical reaction are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['reactants']. *Why:* Reactants go in and are used up. Vinegar and baking soda are the reactants in a fizz.
4. (fill) A new substance made by a chemical reaction is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['product']. *Why:* Products come out of a reaction. Rust is the product when iron, oxygen and water react.
5. (sort) Sort these changes. Does each one make a new substance, or keep the same substance?  
   **Answer:** an iron gate rusting → Makes a new substance; milk turning to curd → Makes a new substance; a candle burning → Makes a new substance; wax melting → Keeps the same substance; salt dissolving in water → Keeps the same substance; paper being torn → Keeps the same substance. *Why:* Rusting, curdling and burning are chemical reactions, and their products behave differently. Melting, dissolving and tearing change only the state, the mixing or the shape.
6. (match) Match each sign of a reaction to an example.  
   **Answer:** A gas appears → Lime juice fizzes on baking soda; The colour changes → Black tea turns pale with lime juice; A new solid appears → Warm milk makes white lumps with lime juice; It gets cooler by itself → Vinegar cools as baking soda fizzes in it; Light is given off → A sparkler glows. *Why:* These are the five common signs. Each one is a clue that a new substance may be forming.
7. (mcq) Which gas is in the bubbles when vinegar meets baking soda?  
   Options: Oxygen / Carbon dioxide / Steam / Hydrogen  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide. *Why:* The bubbles are carbon dioxide, one of the three products. If you chose steam, remember that nothing was heated.
8. (mcq) Which two things does iron need in order to rust?  
   Options: Heat and light / Salt and sand / Oxygen and water / Nitrogen and dust  
   **Answer:** Oxygen and water. *Why:* Rust forms when iron reacts with oxygen and water. Salt makes rusting faster, but iron rusts without it.
9. (truefalse) Boiling water bubbles, so boiling is a chemical reaction.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The bubbles in boiling water are water that has turned into a gas. No new substance is made. Bubbles are only a clue.
10. (order) Put the steps of the After the Fizz experiment in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Divide the baking soda into equal heaps', 'Stir one heap into water and one into vinegar', 'Wait for the fizz to end', 'A grown-up dries both liquids', 'Let both pans cool', 'Drop vinegar on each crust']. *Why:* Equal heaps make the test fair. The pans must cool before the drop test, because any liquid sizzles on a hot pan.
11. (write) In After the Fizz, the crust from the vinegar pan did not fizz in vinegar. Explain what that shows.  
   **Answer:** Baking soda always fizzes in vinegar. The crust did not fizz, so it is not baking soda. The baking soda reacted with the vinegar and became a new substance.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the test result as evidence, and says that a new substance was made.
12. (write) Explain why an iron kadai is rubbed with oil after it is washed.  
   **Answer:** Iron rusts when oxygen and water reach it. The oil covers the iron and keeps water and air off it. So the kadai does not rust.. *Why:* A strong answer names both oxygen and water, and says that the oil keeps them away from the iron.
13. (draw) Draw two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule turning into two water molecules. Count the atoms on each side.  
   **Answer:** On the left, two pairs of small circles and one pair of large circles. On the right, two groups, each with one large circle and two small circles. Both sides are labelled '6 atoms'.. *Why:* A strong drawing uses the same atoms on both sides. There are 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms before and after.
14. (mcq) A tumbler of vinegar gets cooler when baking soda fizzes in it. Why?  
   Options: Bubbles are always cold / Baking soda is kept in the fridge / The steel loses its shine / The reaction takes in heat  
   **Answer:** The reaction takes in heat. *Why:* This reaction takes heat in from the liquid and the tumbler, so they cool down. Burning does the opposite and gives heat out.
15. (fill) Complete the word equation: iron + oxygen + water → ____.  
   **Answer:** ['rust']. *Why:* Rust is the product. It is brown and crumbly, unlike the hard grey iron.
### Signs, tests and rust (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (odd) Three of these are chemical reactions. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A banana turning brown / A matchstick burning / Sugar dissolving in tea / An iron nail rusting  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Sugar in tea has only dissolved. The tea tastes sweet, so the sugar is still there. The other three make new substances.
2. (mcq) Salt seems to disappear in water. How could you show that the salt is still there?  
   Options: Let the water dry and look for salt / Stir for much longer / Add more salt / Shake the tumbler hard  
   **Answer:** Let the water dry and look for salt. *Why:* When the water dries, the same salt is left behind. That shows the salt only dissolved, and no new substance was made.
3. (truefalse) A product always looks and behaves like a blend of its reactants.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A product has its own properties. Sodium burns in water and chlorine is poisonous, yet together they make table salt.
4. (write) Tea goes a little paler when plain water is added. Explain why this is not a chemical reaction.  
   **Answer:** The water only spreads the tea out, so there is less tea in each spoonful. No new substance is made. If the extra water dried up, the tea would be as dark as before.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the same substances are still there, only more spread out.
5. (mcq) In the Rust Uses Up Air experiment, why did water climb into the glass with steel wool?  
   Options: The steel wool soaked up the water / Rusting used up oxygen in the glass / The glass got colder / The coins pushed the water up  
   **Answer:** Rusting used up oxygen in the glass. *Why:* The iron took oxygen out of the air in the glass to make rust. Water moved up into the space the oxygen had filled.
6. (count) A glass holds 15 centimetres of air. Rusting uses one fifth. How many centimetres does the water climb?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* One fifth of 15 is 15 divided by 5, which is 3. About one fifth of the air is oxygen, and rusting uses the oxygen.
7. (sort) Sort these. Does each one protect iron from rust, or help iron to rust?  
   **Answer:** a coat of paint → Protects iron from rust; a rub of oil → Protects iron from rust; a coat of zinc → Protects iron from rust; a chip in the paint → Helps iron to rust; water left on a tawa → Helps iron to rust; salty sea air → Helps iron to rust. *Why:* Paint, oil and zinc keep oxygen and water off the iron. A chip, a puddle and salty air let them reach it, or speed the reaction up.
8. (order) Put these in order, to show how an iron gate rusts under chipped paint.  
   **Answer:** ['A chip of paint falls off', 'Air and rain reach the iron', 'Iron, oxygen and water react', 'Rust forms and swells', 'The swelling rust lifts off more paint']. *Why:* Rust takes up more room than iron, so it pushes the paint off around the chip. That is why a small chip grows into a large rusty patch.
9. (write) Rust weighs more than the iron it came from. Explain where the extra weight comes from.  
   **Answer:** When iron rusts, oxygen atoms from the air and some water join the iron. These have weight. So the rust weighs as much as the iron plus the oxygen and water that joined it.. *Why:* A strong answer says that something was added to the iron, and names oxygen.
10. (mcq) Which pair of substances reacts to make table salt?  
   Options: Iron and oxygen / Hydrogen and oxygen / Sodium and chlorine / Vinegar and baking soda  
   **Answer:** Sodium and chlorine. *Why:* Sodium is a soft metal and chlorine is a green gas. Their product, table salt, is unlike both of them.
11. (truefalse) A chemical reaction can change iron atoms into gold atoms.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A reaction only changes how atoms are joined. Iron atoms stay iron atoms, whatever they join with.
12. (perspective) A town beside the sea must choose the railings for a new footbridge.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that salty air speeds up rusting, so paint by the sea fails sooner. Another is that paint works only while it has no chips.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses what iron needs in order to rust, and takes the cost and the work seriously.
13. (label) Label this picture from the Rust Uses Up Air experiment: the steel wool, the air space and the climbed water.  
   **Answer:** ['The steel wool: the orange-brown wad at the top of the upturned glass', 'The air space: between the steel wool and the water', 'The water that has climbed: between the tape mark and the new water level']. *Why:* The water climbed from the tape mark into the space where oxygen used to be. The rest of the air space is mostly nitrogen.
14. (write) A bowl of vinegar and baking soda weighs less after the fizz. Does this mean that atoms were destroyed? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No. The carbon dioxide gas floated out of the bowl and took its weight with it. If the gas were caught and weighed too, the total would be the same as before.. *Why:* A strong answer says where the missing weight went, and that no atom was lost.
15. (mcq) Which of these changes gives out heat?  
   Options: Vinegar fizzing with baking soda / A leaf making sugar in sunlight / Ice melting in a tumbler / A candle burning  
   **Answer:** A candle burning. *Why:* Burning gives out heat and light. The fizz takes heat in, and a leaf takes light in. Melting ice takes heat in too, and is not a reaction at all.
### Atoms, energy and evidence (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Four iron atoms and six oxygen atoms react to make rust. How many atoms are in the rust?  
   Options: Ten / Two / Six / Twenty-four  
   **Answer:** Ten. *Why:* 4 and 6 make 10. A reaction regroups atoms and loses none. If you chose two, you counted the groups of rust and not the atoms.
2. (count) A rust group has 2 iron and 3 oxygen atoms. How many groups do 10 iron and 12 oxygen make?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 12 oxygen atoms make 4 groups, because 12 divided by 3 is 4. Those groups use 8 iron atoms. The oxygen runs out first, and 2 iron atoms are left over.
3. (write) A reaction stops, although plenty of one reactant is left. Give a reason, with an example from this unit.  
   **Answer:** The other reactant has run out. In the Rust Uses Up Air experiment, the rusting stopped when the oxygen in the glass was used up. Steel wool was still left.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a reaction needs all of its reactants. It gives the steel wool or the leftover baking soda as an example.
4. (truefalse) Water is made from hydrogen and oxygen, so water burns as hydrogen does.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Water is a new substance with its own properties. It puts fires out, although hydrogen burns and oxygen helps things to burn.
5. (mcq) Two clear liquids are mixed, and nothing can be seen to change. What can you say?  
   Options: No reaction happened / A reaction may or may not have happened / A reaction certainly happened / The liquids have vanished  
   **Answer:** A reaction may or may not have happened. *Why:* Some reactions show no sign that eyes can see. To find out, you would test what the mixture does. A sign is only a clue, and not seeing a sign does not prove that nothing happened either.
6. (write) A white powder has no label. Without tasting, how could you test whether the powder is baking soda?  
   **Answer:** Put a little of the powder on a plate and drop vinegar or lime juice on it. Baking soda fizzes at once. To be sure, do the same with powder that is known to be baking soda and compare.. *Why:* A strong answer tests what the powder does, and compares it with known baking soda. Salt, sugar and flour do not fizz.
7. (fill) A reaction that takes in heat makes the things around it ____.  
   **Answer:** ['cooler']. *Why:* The heat comes out of the things nearby, so they cool down. In the Cold Fizz activity, the tumbler of fizzing vinegar cooled for this reason.
8. (match) Match each reaction to the place where it happens.  
   **Answer:** Sugar + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water → In the cells of your body; Carbon dioxide + water → sugar + oxygen → In a green leaf in sunlight; Iron + oxygen → red dust → On the surface of Mars; Iron ore + carbon → iron → In a steel plant. *Why:* The same kinds of reaction happen in living bodies, in factories and on other planets. A leaf runs the reverse of the reaction in your cells.
9. (perspective) Salt is stirred into water until it can no longer be seen. Chemists describe this change in different ways.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that the salt comes back unchanged. Another is that salty water does something that neither dry salt nor pure water does.. *Why:* Working chemists hold each of these views. A strong answer uses evidence, such as what comes back on drying, and is fair to another view.
10. (mcq) Why is no gold made when lead is heated with other substances?  
   Options: The fire is never hot enough / Gold is too heavy to make / Reactions cannot change one atom into another / Lead and gold never touch  
   **Answer:** Reactions cannot change one atom into another. *Why:* A reaction only regroups atoms. Lead atoms stay lead atoms. Alchemists tried for about two thousand years and never made gold.
11. (truefalse) Every chemical reaction either gives out energy or takes energy in.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Burning and rusting give out heat. The fizz of vinegar and baking soda takes heat in, and a leaf takes in light.
12. (draw) Draw the atoms before and after iron rusts. Show how iron atoms and oxygen atoms are joined afterwards.  
   **Answer:** Before: iron atoms packed together, and oxygen atoms in pairs nearby. After: groups in which iron atoms and oxygen atoms are joined to each other. The number of each kind of atom is the same in both pictures.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps every atom, and shows that the change is in which atoms are joined.
13. (write) In Rust Uses Up Air, the water climbed one fifth of the air space and stopped. What does this show?  
   **Answer:** Only about one fifth of the air can react with iron. That part is oxygen. The other four fifths must be something else, which does not join with iron.. *Why:* A strong answer says that air is a mixture, and that only its oxygen was used. The rest is mostly nitrogen.
14. (order) Put these changes in order, from the fastest to the slowest.  
   **Answer:** ["A car's airbag filling", 'Vinegar fizzing with baking soda', 'Milk turning to curd', 'An iron gate rusting through']. *Why:* An airbag fills in less than a second and a fizz lasts under a minute. Curd takes some hours, and a gate takes years to rust through.
15. (mcq) Tea made pale by lime juice goes dark again when baking soda is added. What does this show?  
   Options: The tea was never changed / The lime juice has dried up / Baking soda is a brown dye / Another reaction can reverse some chemical changes  
   **Answer:** Another reaction can reverse some chemical changes. *Why:* The lime juice changed the colouring substances in the tea, and the baking soda changed them back. Not every chemical change is permanent.

## Applications
- Cooks use the fizz. Soda and sour curd make carbon dioxide inside dhokla and bhatura batter, and the gas makes them rise.
- Steel plants at Bhilai, Visakhapatnam and Jamshedpur turn iron ore into iron. They run a reaction that takes the oxygen away from the iron.
- Buckets, roof sheets and electricity pylons are galvanised. A coat of zinc keeps oxygen and water off the steel.
- A car's airbag is filled by a chemical reaction. It makes a bagful of gas in less than a tenth of a second.
- Fertiliser factories react nitrogen from the air with hydrogen. Crops fed with this fertiliser grow food for about half the people on Earth.
- A phone battery gives electricity from a chemical reaction. Charging the phone runs the reaction backwards, ready for next time.
- Cement does not simply dry. It reacts with water and turns into a new, hard substance, which is why fresh concrete is kept wet.

## Spotted around us

- Mehndi that looks orange when the paste comes off, and turns deep brown over the next two days.

- The green coat on an old copper pot or a bronze temple bell.
- A sparkler at Deepavali, giving out light and heat as its metal powder burns.

## Open questions
- **How did the chemical reactions of life first get started on the young Earth?** Every living cell runs thousands of linked reactions. Nobody has yet shown how the first such set began from simple substances.
- **Did reactions in space supply some of the ingredients for life?** Rock brought back from the asteroids Ryugu and Bennu contains amino acids, which living bodies use. Scientists do not know how much of this material reached the early Earth.
- **Can chemists work out everything a new substance will do before anyone makes it?** For small molecules, computers can now calculate some properties. For large ones, such as a new medicine, the only sure way is to make the substance and test it.
- **Can carbon dioxide from the air be turned into fuel or stone cheaply enough to matter?** Plants do it with sunlight, and some factories do it on a small scale. So far the reactions people use need a lot of energy and money.
- **Can a coat be made that mends its own scratches, so that steel never rusts?** Chemists are testing paints that release a repairing substance when they crack. None has yet lasted for many years outdoors.

## Connected maths
- In the Rust Uses Up Air experiment, a glass starts with 10 centimetres of air. The water climbs 2 centimetres and stops. What fraction of the air was used up? **One fifth of the air.**. 2 centimetres out of 10 centimetres is 2 tenths. Divide the top and the bottom by 2, and 2 tenths becomes 1 fifth. So about one fifth of the air is oxygen. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A tall jar holds 25 centimetres of air above wet steel wool. Rusting uses one fifth of the air. How far should the water climb? After a week it has climbed only 3 centimetres. Give one possible reason. **It should climb 5 centimetres. One possible reason is that the steel wool dried out and stopped rusting early.**. One fifth of 25 is 25 divided by 5, which is 5 centimetres. A climb of 3 centimetres means some oxygen is still in the jar. The steel wool may have dried out, or it may be too tightly packed for air to reach it. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
- In the Atom Swap activity, one water molecule needs 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. You have 14 pale seeds for hydrogen and 9 rajma for oxygen. How many water molecules can you make, and what is left over? **7 water molecules, with 2 rajma left over.**. 14 divided by 2 is 7, so the pale seeds are enough for 7 molecules. Those 7 molecules use 7 rajma. 9 take away 7 is 2, so 2 oxygen atoms are left over. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- These are made-up numbers. A bowl of vinegar weighs 180 grams. 5 grams of baking soda are stirred in. After the fizz, the bowl weighs 183 grams. How many grams of gas left the bowl? What would a sealed bag of the same mixture weigh after the fizz? **2 grams of gas left the bowl. The sealed bag would weigh 185 grams.**. 180 and 5 make 185 grams at the start. 185 take away 183 is 2, so 2 grams of carbon dioxide floated away. In a sealed bag the gas cannot leave, so the weight stays at 185 grams. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- In The Self-Filling Bag challenge, half a teaspoon of baking soda and 3 tablespoons of vinegar fill a small bag. A bag four times as big needs four times as much of each. How much is that? **2 teaspoons of baking soda and 12 tablespoons of vinegar.**. Four halves make 2, so 2 teaspoons of baking soda are needed. 4 times 3 is 12, so 12 tablespoons of vinegar are needed. Both reactants must grow together, or one will run out. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- The table shows made-up results from the Rust Uses Up Air experiment. Between which two days did the water climb most? Why might it climb less each day, and then stop? **It climbed most between day 1 and day 2. Less oxygen was left each day, so the rusting slowed, and it stopped when the oxygen had gone.**. From day 1 to day 2 the water climbed 1.2 centimetres. From day 2 to day 3 it climbed 0.6, then 0.2, then nothing. At the start the glass held plenty of oxygen, so the iron rusted fast. As the oxygen was used, there was less to react. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a chemical reaction? → A change that turns substances into new substances with different properties.
- What are the substances that go into a reaction called? → Reactants.
- What gas makes the fizz when vinegar meets baking soda? → Carbon dioxide.
- What does iron need in order to rust? → Oxygen and water.
- Name two signs of a chemical reaction. → Any two of these: a gas, a new colour, a new solid, a change in warmth, light or a new smell.
- Does a reaction destroy atoms? → No. It only rearranges them.
- About how much of the air is oxygen? → About one fifth.
- Is melting a chemical reaction? → No. Melting changes the state, and no new substance is made.
