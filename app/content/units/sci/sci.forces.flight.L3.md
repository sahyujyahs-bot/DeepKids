# How things fly: leaning on the air
`sci.forces.flight.L3`

**Summary.** This unit is about how things fly. Air is real stuff, even though we can't see it. Everything that flies stays up by pushing air downwards. The air then pushes it up. That upward push is called lift. Kites lean on the wind and are held by a string. Paper planes and gliders slide slowly down the air. Birds flap to push air down and back, and some ride on rising warm air. Seeds float, glide and spin to travel far from their parent tree. Scientists agree on what lift does. But they still argue about the best simple way to explain it, and one famous explanation is wrong.

**Intro.** *Hold a sheet of paper flat by two corners and drop it. Now crumple an identical sheet into a ball and drop it. Now fold a third sheet into a paper plane and throw it gently. Same paper. Why do they come down so differently?* Look up on any sunny afternoon in an Indian city. You will probably see big brown birds circling high above, hardly flapping at all. They are black kites, called cheel in Hindi. They can hang in the sky for an hour. A crow beside them flaps and flaps. A paper kite on a string climbs only when the wind blows. A seed from a mahogany tree spins down like a tiny helicopter. All of them are doing the same thing in different ways. They are leaning on the air.

## Terms
- **Air**: The invisible stuff all around us. It is made of countless tiny bits that move very fast and bump into everything. *e.g.* You feel air when a fan blows on you, or when you wave a notebook near your face.
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces make things start moving, stop, speed up, slow down or turn. *e.g.* Wind pushes a kite. Gravity pulls a falling seed down.
- **Flight**: Moving through the air without touching the ground, for longer than a jump. *e.g.* A sparrow flying to its nest. A paper plane crossing the room.
- **Wing**: A wide, thin part that pushes air downwards as it moves through the air. Birds, bats, insects, planes and some seeds have wings. *e.g.* A bird's wing, a paper plane's wing and the papery wing on a drumstick seed.
- **Lift**: The upward push of the air on a wing or a kite. It comes from the wing pushing air downwards. *e.g.* Lift holds up a flying pigeon, a glider and a jumbo jet.
- **Weight**: The pull of gravity on a thing, down towards the Earth. Every flying thing has weight. *e.g.* A paper clip on a paper plane adds a little weight to its nose.
- **Drag**: The air pushing back against something moving through it. It slows things down. Drag is a kind of air resistance. *e.g.* Running with an open umbrella, you feel the air drag it back.
- **Thrust**: A forward push that keeps a flying thing moving. It can come from flapping wings, a propeller or a jet engine. *e.g.* Your arm gives a paper plane its thrust when you throw it.
- **Glide**: To fly without flapping or an engine, slowly sinking through the air while moving forward. *e.g.* A paper plane glides. So does a flying lizard jumping between trees.
- **Soar**: To stay up, or even climb, without flapping, by riding on rising air. *e.g.* Cheels and vultures soar in circles high over cities on hot afternoons.
- **Thermal**: A bubble or column of warm air rising from hot ground. Soaring birds circle inside it to climb. *e.g.* Over a hot road or a dry field at midday, thermals rise and black kites circle in them.
- **Tilt (angle of attack)**: How much the front edge of a wing or kite is raised into the oncoming air. A little tilt gives more lift. Too much tilt and the wing stops lifting well. *e.g.* Tilt your flat hand slightly in front of a fan and feel it pushed up.
- **Stall**: When a wing is tilted too steeply, the air can't follow its top surface. The wing loses most of its lift and drops. *e.g.* A paper plane that swoops up steeply, stops and then falls has stalled.
- **Balance point (centre of gravity)**: The point where a thing balances. Its weight acts as if it were all there. For a plane to fly steadily, this point must be a little forward of the middle. *e.g.* Balance a paper plane on your fingertip. Where it balances is its balance point.
- **Kite**: A light frame covered with paper or cloth that flies on the wind, held by a string. In India a paper kite is called a patang or guddi. *e.g.* On Makar Sankranti the sky over Ahmedabad fills with kites.
- **Air pressure**: How hard the air pushes on each bit of a surface. It comes from countless air bits bumping into the surface. *e.g.* Air pressure keeps a balloon blown up from the inside.
- **Molecule**: A tiny group of atoms joined together. Air is mostly molecules of nitrogen and oxygen, far too small to see. *e.g.* Each breath you take holds more air molecules than there are grains of sand on all the world's beaches.
- **Feather**: The light, strong covering of a bird. Wing feathers are stiff and shaped to push air. Fluffy feathers keep birds warm. *e.g.* A pigeon feather you find on a balcony.
- **Hollow bones**: Many flying birds have bones with air spaces inside, held up by thin struts. They are light but strong. *e.g.* A chicken bone, broken open, shows a spongy inside. A flying bird's bones have even more air.
- **Winged seed (samara)**: A seed with a thin, papery wing. When it falls, it spins or glides, so the wind can carry it further. *e.g.* Mahogany seeds spin down like helicopters. Drumstick seeds have three papery wings.
- **Seed parachute**: A tuft of fine hairs on some seeds. It catches the air, so the seed falls very slowly and drifts on the wind. *e.g.* Dandelion seeds, and the silky fluff from an aak plant or a semal tree.
- **Hover**: To stay in one place in the air. It takes a lot of work, because the wings must keep pushing air down all the time. *e.g.* A sunbird or a hoverfly hanging in front of a flower.

## Explanations
### Air is real stuff that pushes · *defined*
**Definition:** Air is a real material, made of countless tiny moving bits. It takes up space, it has weight, and it pushes on everything it touches.

We can't see air, so it is easy to think of it as nothing. But push a glass upside down into a bucket of water. A tissue stuck inside stays dry, because the air in the glass keeps the water out. Run with a big piece of cardboard held flat in front of you. You feel the air pushing back. Air even has weight. The air in a small bedroom weighs about 40 kilograms, as much as a ten-year-old child. Flying is only possible because air is real stuff. A wing has something to push on.
- e.g. A tissue stuck inside an upside-down glass stays dry under water.
- e.g. A ceiling fan pushes air down, and you feel the breeze.
- e.g. A blown-up balloon stays round because air inside pushes on its skin.
### Four forces on every flying thing · *defined*
**Definition:** A flying thing has four forces on it. Weight pulls it down. Lift pushes it up. Drag pushes it back. Thrust pushes it forward.

A force is a push or a pull. We draw each force as an arrow pointing the way it pushes. Our Pushes and Pulls unit shows how. Weight is gravity pulling the flying thing towards the Earth. Lift is the air pushing it up. Drag is the air pushing back as it moves. Thrust is whatever pushes it forward. That can be a flapping wing, a propeller or your throwing arm. A glider has no thrust of its own. It slowly trades height for speed, like a cycle rolling down a gentle slope. An English inventor, George Cayley, first drew these forces on a flying machine, around 1799.
- e.g. A pigeon flapping: its wings give both lift and thrust.
- e.g. A paper plane after it leaves your hand: no more thrust, so it glides slowly down.
- e.g. A kite: the wind gives the push, and the string holds it against the wind.
### How a wing makes lift: it pushes air down · *defined*
**Definition:** A wing makes lift by turning the air that flows past it downwards. When the wing pushes air down, the air pushes the wing up.

Every push has a push back. Isaac Newton wrote this down in 1687. When you swim, you push water backwards, and the water pushes you forwards. A wing works the same way with air. As it moves through the air, tilted slightly upwards at the front, it bends the air behind it downwards. The air pushes back, upwards, on the wing. That upward push is lift. Even a flat wing, like a paper plane's, can do this. A wing pushes lots of air down a little, all the time. A helicopter shows it plainly. Stand near one and you feel a gale blowing down.
- e.g. Under a hovering helicopter, a strong wind blows straight down.
- e.g. A flat paper plane wing still makes lift, because it is tilted a little.
- e.g. A crow's downstroke pushes air down and back. The air pushes the crow up and forward.
### Why the air speeds up over a wing: a famous puzzle · *debated*
Scientists measure lift very well. Engineers use their sums to build planes that carry hundreds of people safely. So nobody doubts what wings do. The argument is about the best simple way to explain it. One explanation, found in many old books, is simply wrong. Here are the main ideas.
- e.g. Smoke filmed in a wind tunnel shows air over the top of a wing reaching the back first, long before the air underneath.
- e.g. Paper planes have flat wings and fly well. Stunt planes can even fly upside down.
- **The 'equal time' story, in many old books and websites:** The top of a wing is curved, so air going over it has further to travel. The two streams must meet again at the back at the same time. So the air on top must go faster. This is wrong. Nothing makes the two streams meet again. In real tests, air over the top races ahead and arrives at the back much sooner.
- **Daniel Bernoulli, a Swiss scientist, in 1738:** Where air moves faster, it pushes less hard on the surfaces beside it. Over a wing, the air really does move faster, so it pushes less hard on top. The air underneath pushes harder. So the wing is pushed up. This is true. But on its own, it doesn't say why the air speeds up.
- **Followers of Isaac Newton's laws:** The wing turns air downwards, so the air pushes the wing upwards. This is also true. But on its own, it doesn't explain how a wing turns the air flowing over its top.
- **Scientists today, such as Holger Babinsky at Cambridge in 2003 and Doug McLean at Boeing in 2012:** Bernoulli's idea and Newton's idea are two views of one thing. A wing bends the air. Bending moving air needs a difference in push: less push above, more push below. That same difference speeds up the air on top. Some scientists still say there is no simple explanation that everyone accepts. In 2020, a magazine article called it a question no one can fully explain.
### How a kite flies · *defined*
**Definition:** A kite flies because the wind pushes on it while the string holds it back. The kite is tilted, so the wind is turned downwards and the kite is pushed upwards.

A kite has no engine and does not flap. Instead, the wind moves past it. The string pulls the kite back, so it can't simply be blown away. The tilted kite turns the wind downwards, and the wind pushes the kite up. If the string goes slack, the kite tumbles. If the wind stops, you can run, so the air still moves past the kite. A tail adds a little drag at the back. It keeps the kite's nose pointing into the wind, so it doesn't spin. Old stories from China say people made kites there more than 2,000 years ago. In India, kites fill the sky on Makar Sankranti in Gujarat and Rajasthan, and on Independence Day in Delhi.
- e.g. A patang climbs when the string is pulled tight and drops when it goes slack.
- e.g. On a still day you must run to fly a kite.
- e.g. A kite that spins round and round often needs a longer tail.
### How paper planes and gliders fly · *defined*
**Definition:** A glider has no engine. It moves forward by slowly sliding down through the air, while its wings make lift. To fly steadily, its balance point must be a little in front of the middle of its wings.

Your arm gives a paper plane its thrust, but only for a moment. After that, it glides. It sinks a little all the time, and that sinking keeps it moving forward. A good glider sinks very slowly and travels far. Where the plane balances matters a lot. If the nose is too light, the plane swoops up, stalls and drops. If the nose is too heavy, it dives. A paper clip on the nose moves the balance point forward. Bending up the back edges of the wings a little lifts the nose. Real gliders work in the same way. They can stay up for hours by finding rising air, just like soaring birds.
- e.g. A paper plane that swoops up and drops needs a paper clip on its nose.
- e.g. A paper plane that dives needs the back edges of its wings bent up a little.
- e.g. Otto Lilienthal, a German engineer, made about 2,000 glider flights in the 1890s.
### How birds fly: flapping, gliding and soaring · *defined*
**Definition:** Birds flap their wings to push air down and back, which gives them lift and thrust. Many birds also glide, and some soar on rising warm air without flapping at all.

On each downstroke, a bird sweeps its wings down and forward. The air is pushed down and back, so the bird is pushed up and forward. On the upstroke, the feathers twist and open to let air through. A bird's body is built for this. It has hollow bones with thin struts inside, and a big breastbone holding strong flying muscles. Its feathers are light but strong. Soaring birds like the black kite and the vulture hardly flap. On hot days, warm air rises from the ground in bubbles called thermals. The birds circle inside them and are carried up, like leaves in a rising breeze. Then they glide down to the next thermal.
- e.g. Cheels circle high over Indian cities at midday, rising on thermals.
- e.g. A sunbird hovers by a flower, flapping very fast.
- e.g. Common swifts can stay in the air for about 10 months without landing. A Swedish study tracked them in 2016.
### Seeds that fly · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants make seeds that fly, so the wind carries them far from the parent plant. Some float on parachutes of fine hairs. Some spin on one wing. Some glide on two wings.

A seed that lands under its parent has to share light and water with it. A seed that travels can find new ground. So plants have found many ways to fly. A dandelion seed hangs under a tuft of about a hundred fine bristles. In 2018, scientists in Edinburgh found that air flowing through the bristles makes a ring of swirling air above the seed. The ring holds the seed up, so it falls very slowly. A mahogany seed has one wing and spins as it falls. In 2009, scientists studying spinning seeds like this found something new. The spinning makes a little whirl of air over the wing, which gives extra lift. Some seeds glide. The Javan cucumber, a climbing plant in Asia, makes a paper-thin seed with wings about as wide as your hand. It glides like a tiny plane. In the early 1900s, an Austrian inventor, Igo Etrich, built gliders shaped like it.
- e.g. Silky aak and semal fluff floats away on the wind.
- e.g. Big-leaf mahogany seeds spin down like helicopters in many Indian cities.
- e.g. Drumstick seeds have three papery wings.
### Gliding animals of India · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals glide but can't flap. They spread out flaps of skin, or flatten their bodies, to glide from tree to tree.

Gliding saves time and energy. It is also safer than climbing down to the ground, where hunters wait. India's forests have many gliders. The flying lizard of the Western Ghats spreads skin flaps held out on its ribs. The Indian giant flying squirrel stretches skin between its arms and legs. It can glide across gaps longer than a cricket pitch. The ornate flying snake jumps from a branch and flattens its body into a wide ribbon. It wiggles through the air in an S shape. Flying fish in the Indian Ocean leap out of the water and glide on large fins. None of them can stay up for long, because they make lift without any thrust.
- e.g. A flying lizard spreading its bright skin flaps.
- e.g. A flying squirrel gliding at night between tall trees.
- e.g. A flying snake flattening its body into a ribbon.
### How did birds start to fly? · *debated*
Birds came from small feathered dinosaurs, more than 150 million years ago. Most scientists agree on that, because of fossils like Archaeopteryx, found in Germany in 1861. But how did flapping flight begin? Half a wing can't lift a body. So what good was half a wing? Scientists have argued about this for more than a hundred years.
- e.g. Archaeopteryx had feathered wings, but also teeth, claws and a long bony tail.
- e.g. Young chukar partridges flap their small wings to help them run up steep slopes.
- **The trees-down idea:** Small feathered dinosaurs climbed trees and glided down, like flying squirrels today. Over a very long time, gliding turned into flapping.
- **The ground-up idea:** Small feathered dinosaurs ran fast on the ground. Flapping feathered arms helped them leap, balance and catch insects. Later, the flapping lifted them right off the ground.
- **Kenneth Dial, an American scientist, in 2003:** Young partridges flap to run up tree trunks and steep rocks before they can fly. So even half a wing is useful. This may show how flight began. Other scientists are not sure, and the question is still open.
### Deep down: lift is air molecules bumping · *defined*
**Definition:** Air pressure is the push of countless air molecules bumping into a surface. A wing gets lift because the molecules underneath bump into it harder than the molecules on top.

Zoom into the air and keep zooming. You reach molecules, tiny groups of atoms. In air, they are mostly nitrogen and oxygen. Each one zooms about at roughly 500 metres every second. That is faster than a passenger jet. Every bit of your skin is being bumped by them, billions of times a second, from every side. We call that steady push air pressure. Usually the bumps are the same on all sides, so we don't notice them. A moving wing changes the pattern. Underneath, the bumps push a little harder. On top, they push a little less. The difference is lift. So a jumbo jet is held up by trillions of tiny bumps.
- e.g. A balloon stays round because molecules inside bump on its skin.
- e.g. High on a mountain, there are fewer molecules, so wings and lungs both have to work harder.
### Flying on other worlds · *defined*
**Definition:** Wings need air to push on. Where there is no air, as in space, wings do nothing. Where there is thin air, as on Mars, wings must move very fast.

In space there is no air at all, so a plane's wings would have nothing to push. Rockets work differently. They throw hot gas out backwards very fast, and the gas pushes the rocket forwards. Mars has air, but only about one hundredth as much as Earth. In 2021, NASA flew a small helicopter called Ingenuity on Mars. It was the first powered flight on another planet. Its blades spun about 2,400 times a minute, many times faster than a ceiling fan. The team was led by MiMi Aung, an engineer who grew up partly in Myanmar. Its chief engineer, Bob Balaram, studied at IIT Madras. Ingenuity was meant to fly 5 times. It flew 72 times. Saturn's moon Titan has thick air and weak gravity. NASA plans to send a flying drone there, called Dragonfly.
- e.g. Ingenuity's first flight, on 19 April 2021, lasted about 40 seconds.
- e.g. Astronauts on the Moon can't fly a kite, because the Moon has no air.
### How insects fly, and the bumblebee puzzle · *defined*
**Definition:** Insects fly by flapping their wings very fast and twisting them. Each stroke makes a small whirl of air over the wing, which gives much more lift than a still wing could.

A housefly beats its wings about 200 times every second. That makes the buzz you hear. Small wings flapping so fast work differently from big airplane wings. As each wing sweeps, a little whirlwind forms along its front edge and clings to it. That whirl gives extra lift. For a long time, nobody understood this. People joked that science said bumblebees should not be able to fly. In 1999, Michael Dickinson and his team in California built a giant robot fly wing, flapping in a tank of oil. They measured the forces and showed how insect wings make so much lift.
- e.g. A mosquito's whine is the sound of its wings beating hundreds of times a second.
- e.g. Dragonflies can fly backwards, and each of their four wings can move on its own.

## Mostly agreed
- Air is real stuff. It takes up space, has weight and pushes on things. *How we know:* A tissue stays dry in an upside-down glass pushed under water. A blown-up ball weighs a little more than a flat one, on a very fine scale.
- Everything that flies with wings stays up by pushing air downwards. *How we know:* Scientists measure the air flowing down behind wings, in wind tunnels and around real planes. Under a hovering helicopter, you can feel it.
- The 'equal time' explanation of lift is wrong. *How we know:* Wind tunnel films with pulses of smoke show air over the top of a wing reaching the back much sooner than the air underneath. Holger Babinsky at Cambridge made one in 2012.
- People first flew in a powered plane that they could steer in 1903. *How we know:* Orville and Wilbur Wright flew four times on 17 December 1903, at Kitty Hawk in the USA. Photographs and witnesses recorded it. The first flight lasted 12 seconds.
- Birds are living dinosaurs. *How we know:* Fossils show small dinosaurs with feathers, wishbones and hollow bones, just like birds. Archaeopteryx, found in 1861, has a mix of bird and dinosaur features.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Air over the top of a wing goes faster because it must meet the air from underneath at the back at the same time.** (Many older textbooks, websites and even some pilot training books; *rejected*)  
  For: Air over the top of a wing really does go faster. And the curved top looks like a longer path.  
  Against: Nothing makes the two streams of air meet again. Films of smoke show the air on top arriving much sooner. The sums don't work either. The 'equal time' speed is far too small to hold a plane up.
- **Wings only lift because their tops are curved.** (A common idea, from pictures of wing shapes; *rejected*)  
  For: Most bird and plane wings are curved on top, and curved wings do lift well.  
  Against: Paper planes have flat wings and still fly. Stunt planes fly upside down, with the curved side underneath. What matters most is that the wing turns the air downwards. Tilt matters as much as shape.
- **Science says a bumblebee should not be able to fly.** (A popular saying, from a quick sum repeated in a French book in 1934; *rejected*)  
  For: If you treat a bee's wing like a still airplane wing, the sum says it is too small to hold the bee up.  
  Against: Bee wings are not still. They flap and twist very fast, making whirls of air that give extra lift. In 1999, Michael Dickinson's robot fly wing measured this. Science never said bees can't fly. One rough sum did.
- **The Wright brothers were the first people to fly.** (Most history books, and the USA; *debated*)  
  For: In 1903 the Wrights made powered, controlled flights, with photographs and witnesses. They also worked out how to steer a plane in the air.  
  Against: People had flown long before in balloons, from 1783, and in gliders. In 1906, Alberto Santos-Dumont flew a plane in Paris in front of a big crowd. Many people in Brazil call him the first. Some say Gustave Whitehead flew in 1901, but there is little good evidence for that.
- **Shivkar Bapuji Talpade flew an unmanned plane over Mumbai's Chowpatty beach in 1895.** (Later books, newspaper stories and a 2015 Hindi film; *rejected*)  
  For: Talpade was a real Sanskrit scholar and teacher in Mumbai, who was interested in flight.  
  Against: No newspaper from 1895 reported such a flight. No photographs, drawings or witnesses' records have been found. Historians say the story first appeared many years later.
- **An old Sanskrit book, the Vaimanika Shastra, describes real flying machines from ancient India.** (Some books and websites; *rejected*)  
  For: Old Indian stories, like the Pushpaka Vimana in the Ramayana, describe flying chariots. People have always dreamed of flying.  
  Against: The book was written down in the early 1900s, not in ancient times. In 1974, engineers at the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru studied its designs. They found that none of them could fly. The flying chariots in old stories are wonderful stories, not records.
- **Things heavier than air can never fly. Only balloons can.** (Many people, and some well-known scientists, in the 1890s; *rejected*)  
  For: At that time, every attempt at a heavier-than-air flying machine with an engine had failed. Some inventors died trying.  
  Against: Birds are heavier than air, and they fly. In 1903 the Wrights flew. Today a big plane weighing as much as 80 elephants can fly. Wings, not lightness, hold it up.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If air is invisible, how do we know it is really there? (hint: Push an upside-down glass into water, with a tissue stuck inside. What keeps the tissue dry?)
- *deep:* Could you fly a kite on the Moon? (hint: What does a kite push on? Is there any of it on the Moon?)
- *deep:* Why can a heavy jumbo jet fly, but a light ball of paper just falls? (hint: Is it about how heavy it is, or about whether it pushes air down?)
- *big:* Plants can't walk, yet their seeds travel across rivers and fields. What else in nature travels without legs? (hint: Think of clouds, pollen, spider babies on silk threads and the smell of food.)
- *big:* People dreamed of flying for thousands of years before they could. Why do you think humans wanted to fly so much? (hint: Think of the Pushpaka Vimana, Icarus, and how the world looks from a rooftop.)
- *big:* A kite flies only because its string holds it back. Can being held back ever help us rise? (hint: Think of a rule, a teacher or a family that held you back, and what happened next.)
- *big:* Glass-coated kite string cuts thousands of birds every festival. Who should share the sky, and how? (hint: Whose sky is it: the people's, the birds', or everyone's?)
- *deep:* Ostriches, penguins and the dodo lost the power to fly. Why might a bird stop needing to fly? (hint: Flying uses lots of energy. What if there were no hunters to escape, or food was on the ground?)
- *big:* The 'equal time' explanation of wings was in books for decades, and it was wrong. How can we tell when an explanation is right? (hint: What would you need to see? Remember the smoke films in a wind tunnel.)
- *big:* Do birds enjoy flying? (hint: Crows have been seen sliding down snowy roofs again and again, as if for fun. How could we ever know what a bird feels?)
- *big:* Who should get the credit for a great invention: the first to try, the first to succeed, or the first to tell the world? (hint: Think of the Wright brothers, of Santos-Dumont, and of Charlie Taylor, the mechanic who built the Wrights' engine in six weeks.)

## Story: The Kite Who Watched the Kites
*Delhi really does fill with paper kites every Independence Day, and black kites really do soar over the old city. Glass-coated kite string really injures many birds, and the Charity Birds Hospital in Chandni Chowk, Delhi, really treats them. The narrator, Farah, Bilal and everything that happens to them are made up.*

I am a kite. Not the paper kind. I am the older kind, the kind with feathers. People in Delhi call me cheel. The English named their paper toys after my family, long ago, because the toys hang in the wind the way we do.

I live on the broken water tank above a sweet shop in the old city. From there I can see the whole jumble of rooftops, the domes of the mosque, and the river far away. On most days the sky belongs to my family and to the pigeons. But every year, on the fifteenth of August, the people climb onto their roofs, and the sky fills up with paper.

That year I watched a girl called Farah. She was nine, and she had one kite. It was pink, with a torn corner patched with tape. Her cousin Bilal had six kites and a big wooden spool of string that glittered in the sun. I knew that glitter. It was glass, ground fine and glued onto the thread, so that his string could cut other people's strings. It can cut feathers too.

Farah's kite would not fly. She held it above her head and ran across the roof, and it bumped along behind her like a tired dog. She threw it up into the air, and it turned over and fell between two water pots. Bilal laughed. 'Your kite is scared of the sky,' he said.

I did not laugh. I had been young once, and I had fallen too.

The sun was high. Over the hot roofs and the hot roads, warm air was beginning to rise in great invisible bubbles. I could feel one under the tank, like a breath. I stepped off, spread my wings, and did not flap. I tilted the front edges of my wings up, just a little, and leaned on the rising air. It pushed up under me. I turned in a slow circle, and the bubble carried me up, round and round, until Farah's roof was the size of a chapati.

From up there, I saw her stop running. She was looking at me. She shaded her eyes with one hand and watched me hang in the sky without moving a feather. I twisted my forked tail a little, to keep my nose pointing into the breeze. I could feel her wondering.

Then she did something clever. All morning she had run along with the wind, so the kite only drifted lazily after her. Now she stood still, with the wind blowing on her back. She called her little brother and put the kite in his hands. 'Carry it to the far edge,' she said. 'Hold it up high, with its face to the wind. Let go only when I say.' He walked away across the roof, and she let out string as he went, until it stretched between them, tight. The pink paper faced the breeze, the way I always face it.

'Now!'

He let go. She did not run. She pulled. The wind pushed the pink kite back, and the string held it, and the kite had nowhere to go but up. It shot up past the washing line, past the neighbour's satellite dish, wobbling and then steadying. Farah let out a little string, then pulled again, and it climbed again. Whenever the string went slack, the kite began to sag. Whenever she drew it tight, it rose. She was laughing out loud.

So that was the secret she found. A kite does not fly in spite of the string. It flies because of it. The wind pushes, the string holds, and the tilted paper turns the push into an upward push. Mine is the same trick, without the string. I lean on the moving air, and the air holds me.

For an hour the sky was perfect. Hundreds of paper kites hung over the old city, red and yellow and green, and my family wheeled among them. Then the fighting began. The boys crossed their strings and sawed, and kites came loose and drifted away over the rooftops. People cheered. Bilal cut three kites down, one after another.

I did not see the fourth string. It was loose, trailing from a kite that had already been cut. It drifted across the sky like a spider's thread, and the glass in it caught the light a moment too late. It wrapped once around my left wing. When I beat my wing to shake it off, it bit.

A wing is only a wing while it can push the air. Mine could not. The air slid past it, and I was falling. I tumbled through the paper kites and the shouting, and I hit the roof among Farah's water pots.

Everyone was looking up at the fight. Only Farah was looking down. She knelt beside me. I hissed at her and opened my beak. She did not run away, and she did not grab me. She took the dupatta from her shoulders and dropped it over me, gently, so that the world went dark and quiet. Through the cloth I felt her small hands hold my wings closed against my body.

'Bilal,' she said, in a voice I had not heard from her. 'Bring scissors. Not your string. Scissors.'

They cut the glass thread away. Then her father carried me through the crowded lanes, wrapped in pink cloth, to a building full of birds. It was a hospital just for birds, run by kind people in white. There were pigeons with bandaged feet, a parrot with a splint, and three other kites like me, all cut by string.

I stayed there for many weeks. My feathers grew back.

When they let me go, I flew straight to my water tank. It was a hot afternoon in October. Below me, on her roof, Farah was flying the pink kite on plain white cotton thread. It had no glass at all. Bilal stood beside her. He was holding the spool for her, and none of his string glittered.

I stepped off the tank and found a rising bubble of air. Farah looked up, and I am almost sure she knew me. For a long moment we hung there together over the old city. Her kite was held by a string, and I was held by nothing but the way I leaned. We were both doing the same thing. We were pushing the air down, and the air was pushing us up.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Farah's kite fails while she runs along with the wind and lets the string go slack. It flies the moment she stands still with the wind at her back, the kite's face turned into the wind, and pulls the string tight. The wind pushes the kite, the string holds it, and the tilted kite turns that push upwards. The cheel does the same thing without a string, leaning its tilted wings on rising warm air. When the glass string damages its wing, the wing can no longer push the air, and it falls. Every flyer in the story stays up the same way: by pushing air downwards. The story also quietly asks who the sky is for.

- Ask: In the story, Farah's kite would not fly at first. What did she change to make it climb?
- Ask: Why did the kite rise when Farah pulled the string tight, and sag when the string went slack?
- Ask: How did the cheel stay up in the sky without flapping its wings?
- Ask: Why did the cheel fall when the glass string cut its wing?
- Ask: At the end, Bilal holds a spool of plain cotton string. Why do you think he changed?

   **Answer:** Air in the glass keeps the water out. *Why:* The 'empty' glass is full of air, and the air takes up the space. Picking 'waterproof' usually means the child hasn't seen that the air is doing the work. Try a tissue in a glass held the right way up: it gets wet.

2. (mcq) Under a moving wing, molecules bump a little harder than on top. What does this make?  
   Options: Lift / Drag only / Weight / Nothing  
   **Answer:** Lift. *Why:* More push underneath than on top gives an upward push: lift. Picking 'weight' mixes up gravity, which pulls down, with the air's push, which pushes up.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They agree on what lift does, and they can calculate it very well. But they still argue about the best simple explanation.

   Options: Both. They are two views of the same thing / Only Bernoulli / Only Newton / Neither  
   **Answer:** Both. They are two views of the same thing. *Why:* Most scientists today say both are true. The wing bends the air down, and that bending makes less push above and more below. Picking only one is a common view in books and on the internet. Each one alone leaves part of the story out.

   **Answer:** Spin its blades very fast, about 2,400 times a minute. *Why:* With so little air to push, its blades had to move very fast to push enough air down. Picking 'no blades' means it is being mixed up with a rocket.

Flapping, twisting wings make much more lift. *Why:* Insect wings flap and twist very fast, making whirls of air that give extra lift. A robot fly wing measured it in 1999. Picking 'lighter than air' means bees are being mixed up with balloons.
10. (mcq) How did birds first start to fly?  
   Options: Scientists still argue: perhaps gliding down from trees, perhaps flapping while running up slopes / Birds were always able to fly / Dinosaurs jumped off cliffs / Scientists know exactly  
   **Answer:** Scientists still argue: perhaps gliding down from trees, perhaps flapping while running up slopes. *Why:* Scientists agree that birds came from small feathered dinosaurs. How flapping began is still debated. Picking 'know exactly' is common, because books often tell one story. The honest answer is that it is still open.
11. (mcq) A story says Shivkar Bapuji Talpade flew a plane over Mumbai in 1895. Why do historians doubt it?  
   Options: No newspaper, photograph or witness record from 1895 has been found / Mumbai had no beaches / People in 1895 had no paper / It is impossible to fly in India  
   **Answer:** No newspaper, photograph or witness record from 1895 has been found. *Why:* A claim needs evidence from the time. The story only appears many years later. Picking 'impossible in India' is wrong too. Planes fly in India every day. The problem is only the missing evidence.
12. (write) Who should get the credit for the first flight: the Wright brothers, Santos-Dumont, or the balloonists? Give a reason.  
   **Answer:** Any answer with a reason. For example: the Wright brothers, because they first flew a powered plane that could be steered, in 1903, and recorded it. Or: it depends on what you count as flying, because balloonists flew in 1783.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. It depends on what you count as 'flying'.
13. (write) A hot-air balloon and a glider both stay in the air. How are their tricks different?  
   **Answer:** A hot-air balloon floats because the hot air inside is lighter than the cold air around it, like a cork floating in water. A glider is heavier than air. Its wings push air down, so the air pushes it up.. *Why:* Balloons float. Wings lift. They are two different ways of staying up.
14. (write) Design a test to find out whether real mahogany seeds or paper spinners fall more slowly. Say what you will keep the same.  
   **Answer:** For example: drop 3 mahogany seeds and 3 paper spinners from the same height, 2 metres, in the same still room. Time each one. Keep the height and the room the same. Compare the middle times.. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing, here which seed, and keeps everything else the same.
15. (write) The kite in the story flies only because the string holds it back. Can being held back ever help a person rise? Give an example.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer with an example. For example: a cricket coach makes me practise the same shot again and again. It feels like being held back, but it makes me better.. *Why:* This links the science to life. There is no single right answer.
16. (draw) Draw a close-up of a wing with air molecules around it, as if you had a magic microscope. Show where the molecules push harder.  
   **Answer:** A wing with crowded molecules bumping underneath and fewer on top, with a big up arrow labelled lift.. *Why:* More bumping underneath than on top gives lift.
17. (truefalse) Common swifts can stay in the air for about 10 months without landing.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Scientists in Sweden fitted swifts with tiny trackers in 2016. Some stayed airborne for about 10 months.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is air?  
   Options: Real stuff made of tiny moving bits / Nothing at all / Only the wind / A kind of light  
   **Answer:** Real stuff made of tiny moving bits. *Why:* Air is real stuff. It fills space and pushes. Picking 'nothing' is very common, because we can't see it. An upside-down glass under water shows it is there.
2. (mcq) A tissue in an upside-down glass, pushed under water, stays dry. Why?  
   Options: Air in the glass keeps the water out / The glass is too small / Water can't touch tissue  
   **Answer:** Air in the glass keeps the water out. *Why:* The glass is full of air, which takes up the space. Picking 'too small' means the air isn't being counted. Hold the glass up the right way, and water floods in.
3. (mcq) Which force pulls a flying thing down towards the Earth?  
   Options: Lift / Weight / Thrust  
   **Answer:** Weight. *Why:* Weight is gravity pulling down. Picking lift mixes up the up push and the down pull.
4. (mcq) Which force pushes back against a plane moving through the air?  
   Options: Drag / Lift / Weight  
   **Answer:** Drag. *Why:* Drag is the air pushing back. Picking weight usually means every force is thought to point down.
5. (mcq) How does a wing make lift?  
   Options: It pushes air downwards, so the air pushes it up / It is lighter than air / It pulls itself up with gravity  
   **Answer:** It pushes air downwards, so the air pushes it up. *Why:* The wing turns air down, and the air pushes back up. Picking 'lighter than air' mixes wings up with balloons.
6. (mcq) What happens to a flying kite if the string breaks?  
   Options: It flies higher, because it is free / It is blown away and tumbles down / It stays exactly where it is  
   **Answer:** It is blown away and tumbles down. *Why:* The string holds the kite against the wind. Without it, the wind just blows it away and it falls. Picking 'flies higher' is a very common idea. It is the string's hold that lets the kite rise.
7. (mcq) In the Paper Clip Pilot test, what should change between throws?  
   Options: Only the number of clips / The clips and the thrower / Everything  
   **Answer:** Only the number of clips. *Why:* In a fair test, you change only one thing. Picking 'the clips and the thrower' means testing two things at once. Then you can't tell which one made the difference.
8. (mcq) In the Seed Spinners test, the long-wing spinner took 4 seconds to fall and the short-wing spinner took 2 seconds. Which fell more slowly?  
   Options: Long wings / Short wings / They fell the same  
   **Answer:** Long wings. *Why:* 4 seconds is longer than 2 seconds, so the long-wing spinner fell more slowly. It took twice as long. Picking 'short wings' usually means a bigger number was read as faster. Here, the number is the time taken.
9. (mcq) How do black kites stay up for an hour without flapping?  
   Options: They soar on rising warm air / They are lighter than air / They are held by strings  
   **Answer:** They soar on rising warm air. *Why:* Rising warm air, called a thermal, carries them up as they circle. Picking 'strings' mixes up the bird with the paper toy of the same name.
10. (mcq) Which seed spins like a helicopter as it falls?  
   Options: A mahogany seed / A mango seed / A coconut  
   **Answer:** A mahogany seed. *Why:* Mahogany seeds have one papery wing and spin down. Picking coconut is a good thought about travelling seeds. But coconuts travel by floating on the sea, not by flying.
11. (mcq) What do gliders, flying squirrels and paper planes all have in common?  
   Options: They have no thrust of their own, so they slowly sink as they fly / They all flap / They are lighter than air  
   **Answer:** They have no thrust of their own, so they slowly sink as they fly. *Why:* Gliders make lift but have no forward push, so they slowly sink. Picking 'they all flap' misses that gliding means holding still wings.
12. (mcq) Is this true? 'Air on top of a wing goes faster because it must meet the air from underneath at the back at the same time.'  
   Options: No. Tests show the top air arrives much sooner / Yes, that is exactly how wings work / Nobody has ever tested it  
   **Answer:** No. Tests show the top air arrives much sooner. *Why:* Wind tunnel films with smoke show that the air on top reaches the back first. Picking 'yes' means the famous old explanation has been met. It is still in many books, but it is wrong.
13. (mcq) Do scientists agree on the best simple explanation of lift?  
   Options: No, they still argue about the best simple way to explain it / Yes, completely / Nobody knows what lift does  
   **Answer:** No, they still argue about the best simple way to explain it. *Why:* They can measure and calculate lift very well. The argument is about how to explain it simply. Picking 'nobody knows what lift does' goes too far. Planes are built safely every day.
14. (mcq) Deep down, what holds up a flying plane?  
   Options: Air molecules bumping harder under the wings than on top / Gravity pushing up / Magnets in the wings  
   **Answer:** Air molecules bumping harder under the wings than on top. *Why:* Countless molecule bumps push harder underneath, and that difference is lift. Picking 'gravity pushing up' is a mix-up. Gravity only ever pulls down, towards the Earth.
15. (mcq) Why did the Ingenuity helicopter on Mars have to spin its blades so fast?  
   Options: Mars has very thin air, so there is little to push on / Mars is very hot / Mars has no gravity  
   **Answer:** Mars has very thin air, so there is little to push on. *Why:* Mars has only about 1 hundredth as much air as Earth, so the blades had to move fast to push enough air down. Picking 'no gravity' is a common idea. Mars does have gravity, a bit more than a third of Earth's.
16. (mcq) Who was the first Indian woman to fly a plane, in 1936?  
   Options: Sarla Thakral / Kalpana Chawla / Bessie Coleman  
   **Answer:** Sarla Thakral. *Why:* Sarla Thakral got her pilot's licence at 21 and flew a small plane in Lahore. Picking Kalpana Chawla is a good guess. She was an Indian-born astronaut, who flew in space much later, in 1997. Bessie Coleman was an American pilot.

## Applications
- Planes, gliders and helicopters carry people, post and medicines across India every day.
- Wind turbines have long blades shaped like wings. The wind lifts the blades around, and they make electricity.
- Ceiling fans and table fans have tilted blades that push air, just like wings.
- Racing cars have upside-down wings, which push them down onto the road so they grip better on corners.
- Drones deliver medicines and survey farms and forests.
- Kites lift cameras, weather instruments and even sails that pull big ships.
- Parachutes and paragliders let people float down safely or glide for hours.
- Engineers copy birds. The upturned tips on many airliner wings do a job a little like the spread feathers at a vulture's wingtips.
- Seed-shaped drones and sensors, copied from mahogany and dandelion seeds, are being tested to drift over forests and fields.
- Cricket and badminton: the shuttlecock's feathers add drag and keep its nose pointing forwards, like a kite's tail.
- Weather scientists study thermals, because the same rising air that birds use also builds clouds and storms.
- Ingenuity on Mars showed that helicopters could one day explore other planets.

## Open questions
- **What is the best simple explanation of lift?** Scientists can calculate lift very well. But they still argue about how to explain it in plain words, without missing part of the story.
- **How did birds first start to fly?** Fossils of the first flyers are rare. Some scientists think they glided down from trees. Others think they flapped while running and climbing.
- **How do birds find thermals they can't see?** They may watch other birds, feel the air, or notice the ground below. Scientists are still finding out.
- **How do migrating birds find their way across thousands of kilometres?** They seem to use the Sun, the stars, smells and the Earth's magnetism. Exactly how they sense the magnetism is still being studied.
- **How do swifts sleep while they fly for months without landing?** Scientists think they may nap for moments while gliding high up. Nobody has measured it in swifts yet.
- **Could people ever fly with strapped-on wings on Saturn's moon Titan?** Titan has thick air and weak gravity, so scientists think it might be possible. Nobody has been there to try.
- **Why do insects have so many different ways of flapping?** Each kind of insect twists and beats its wings a little differently. Scientists have measured only a few of them closely.

## Connected maths
- In the Paper Clip Pilot test, the plane with 1 clip flew 6 metres. The plane with no clip flew 4 metres. How much further did the 1-clip plane fly? **2 metres further.**. To find how much further, take the smaller distance away from the bigger one. 6 take away 4 is 2. So the 1-clip plane flew 2 metres further. You can check: 4 + 2 = 6. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In the Seed Spinners test, one spinner's three falls took 3 seconds, 5 seconds and 4 seconds. What is the middle value? **4 seconds.**. Put the three times in order, from smallest to biggest: 3, 4, 5. The middle one is 4. So the middle value is 4 seconds. We use the middle value because one odd drop can be a fluke. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In the Seed Spinners test, the short-wing spinner took 2 seconds to fall and the long-wing spinner took 4 seconds. Look at the chart. How many times as long did the long-wing spinner take? **2 times as long, which is twice as long.**. The long-wing spinner took 4 seconds. The short-wing spinner took 2 seconds. 4 divided by 2 is 2. So it took twice as long. You can check: 2 + 2 = 4. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the Bird Watch, you counted a pigeon's wingbeats. It flapped 3 times every second. If it kept flapping at that speed, how many times would it flap in 10 seconds? **30 times.**. Each second has 3 flaps. 10 seconds is 10 lots of 3. 3 × 10 = 30. So it would flap 30 times. You can check by skip counting in threes: 3, 6, 9, and on to 30. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Paper Clip Pilot test, four throws went 4 metres, 6 metres, 3 metres and 5 metres. Draw a bar chart of the throws. Which throw went furthest, and which was shortest? **The 6-metre throw went furthest, and the 3-metre throw was shortest. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each throw, as tall as its distance. Then look for the tallest and the shortest bar. The tallest bar is 6 metres. The shortest is 3 metres. The difference between them is 6 take away 3, which is 3 metres. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
