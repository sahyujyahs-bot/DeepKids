# Life beyond Earth: where living things could survive
`sci.sky.life-beyond-earth.L3`

**Summary.** Nobody has yet found life anywhere but on Earth. To guess where it might be, scientists first look at the hardest places on Earth where life already lives. Tiny living things called microbes live in hot springs close to boiling, in salt lakes, and in salty water trapped inside Antarctic ice. Every one of them needs liquid water. Salt keeps water liquid far below the cold that freezes plain water, so salty water under the ice of moons like Europa is one of the best places to look.

**Intro.** *Lay a thread across an ice cube. Lift the thread, and the ice stays behind. Now sprinkle a pinch of salt on the ice and wait one minute. Lift again, and the ice cube comes up hanging on the thread. What did the salt do to the ice?* Salt makes ice melt, even in the cold, and that small trick matters a great deal for life. Every living thing needs liquid water. In this unit you find out which tiny living things survive in boiling water, in ice and in salt. You test, in your own freezer, how cold salty water can get before it freezes. Then you use what you found to ask the big question: could there be life on other worlds, and where should we look?

## Terms
- **Microbe**: A living thing too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria are microbes. *e.g.* The bacteria that turn milk into curd are microbes. A spoon of curd holds many millions of them.
- **Liquid**: Something that flows and takes the shape of its container, like water or oil. Water can also be solid ice or a gas called water vapour. *e.g.* Water in a tumbler is a liquid. Put it in the freezer and it becomes solid ice.
- **Freezing point**: The temperature at which a liquid turns into a solid. Plain water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Ice cubes form in the freezer because it is colder than 0 degrees Celsius.
- **Boiling point**: The temperature at which a liquid bubbles and turns into gas all through. Plain water boils at about 100 degrees Celsius near the sea. *e.g.* Water for tea boils at about 100 degrees Celsius in Chennai, which is by the sea.
- **Below zero**: Colder than the temperature at which plain water freezes. Minus 10 degrees means 10 degrees colder than 0. *e.g.* A big home freezer is often about minus 18 degrees Celsius.
- **Brine**: Very salty water. *e.g.* Mango pickle sits in brine. Very salty water is also found in salt pans by the sea.
- **Extremophile**: A living thing that lives in a place most life could not survive, such as near-boiling water, ice, very salty water or strong acid. The word means 'lover of extremes'. *e.g.* Thermus aquaticus is a bacterium who lives in hot springs at about 70 degrees Celsius.
- **Hot spring**: A place where water heated deep underground comes up out of the ground. *e.g.* Yellowstone, a park in the United States, has hundreds of hot springs. India has hot springs too, such as at Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh.
- **Sea ice**: Ice that forms when sea water freezes. As it freezes, most of the salt is pushed out into tiny channels of brine inside the ice. *e.g.* The sea around Antarctica freezes into sea ice every winter.
- **Tardigrade**: A tiny animal, about half a millimetre long, with eight stubby legs. Tardigrades are also called water bears. They live in damp moss, soil and water. *e.g.* A tardigrade can dry out into a tiny ball and come back to life when water returns.
- **Planet and moon**: A planet is a big round world that goes round a star. A moon is a world that goes round a planet. *e.g.* Earth and Mars are planets that go round the Sun. Europa is a moon that goes round Jupiter.
- **Europa**: One of Jupiter's moons. Europa is covered in ice, and scientists have strong evidence of a deep salty ocean under the ice. *e.g.* NASA's Europa Clipper spacecraft was launched in 2024 to study Europa.
- **Enceladus**: A small icy moon of Saturn. Jets of water and ice spray out of cracks near Enceladus's south pole, from an ocean under the ice. *e.g.* The Cassini spacecraft flew through the jets and found salt in them.
- **Alien life**: Any life that began somewhere other than Earth. It could be as small as a microbe. *e.g.* If scientists found living microbes in Europa's ocean, that would be alien life.
- **Astrobiology**: The science that asks how life began, where else it could live, and how we could find it. *e.g.* An astrobiologist might study microbes in an Antarctic lake to learn where to look on Mars.

## Explanations
### What every living thing needs · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living thing found so far needs three things: liquid water, a supply of energy, and the chemicals their body is built from.

You need water, food and air. A tree needs water, sunlight and air. A microbe needs much less, but every living thing found so far needs liquid water. Inside every cell, the chemicals of life move about and join up in water. Ice is too stiff for that, and steam floats away. Living things also need energy. Plants take it from sunlight. Animals take it from food. Some microbes take it from chemicals in rocks, with no sunlight at all. Last, they need the chemicals to build their bodies, mostly carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. So when scientists hunt for life on other worlds, they look first for liquid water.
- e.g. A dry seed does almost nothing until you give them water. Then they swell and sprout.
- e.g. Curd bacteria work in warm milk, which is mostly water.
- e.g. Microbes deep in the sea, near hot vents, take their energy from chemicals in the vent water, not from sunlight.
### Salt keeps water liquid in deep cold · *defined*
**Definition:** Dissolving salt in water lowers its freezing point. The more salt, the colder it must get before ice forms. Very salty water stays liquid down to about minus 21 degrees Celsius.

When water freezes, its tiny particles line up and hold on to each other in a neat pattern. Salt particles mixed in get in the way. So salty water must get colder before the water particles can line up. Sea water, which is a little salty, freezes at about minus 2 degrees. Water with as much salt as it can hold stays liquid until about minus 21 degrees. A home freezer is usually between about minus 6 and minus 18 degrees, so very salty water in it stays liquid. This is why salt is spread on icy roads in cold countries, and why your salted ice cube melted.
- e.g. In cold countries, trucks spread salt on roads so ice melts and cars do not skid.
- e.g. Sea water around Antarctica stays liquid at about minus 2 degrees, colder than a plain ice cube.
- e.g. In the freezer test, the cup with six spoons of salt was still liquid after a whole night.
### Life in water close to boiling · *defined*
**Definition:** Some microbes live in hot springs and deep-sea vents at 70 degrees Celsius and more. A few live above 100 degrees, in deep sea water that cannot boil because of the great weight of water above it.

Heat is a problem for most living things. Heat breaks the working parts inside a cell, the way heat sets runny egg white into solid white. Microbes who live in hot springs have working parts that are built to hold their shape in heat. One of them, a bacterium called Thermus aquaticus, lives best at about 70 degrees. Their working parts are so heat-proof that one of them is now used in laboratories to copy DNA, the instructions inside cells. Other microbes live deep in the sea beside hot vents in the sea floor. The water there can be hotter than 100 degrees without boiling, because so much water presses down on it. Some of these microbes keep growing above 110 degrees.
- e.g. Thermus aquaticus was found in a hot spring in Yellowstone, in the United States, in 1966.
- e.g. The bright orange and yellow rings around some hot springs are thick mats of heat-loving microbes.
- e.g. Tests that look for the virus that causes COVID use a heat-proof chemical first found in Thermus aquaticus.
### Life in ice · *defined*
**Definition:** Microbes live in the thin channels of salty water inside sea ice, and in salty lakes sealed under ice in Antarctica, at temperatures well below zero.

Cold is a different problem. It slows everything down, and ice crystals can grow and burst a cell. But ice is not always solid all the way through. When sea water freezes, most of the salt is pushed out of the ice into thin channels of brine. The brine stays liquid, and bacteria and tiny algae live in those channels. Some of them make substances that stop ice crystals growing inside them. In Antarctica, Lake Vida lies under about 20 metres of ice. Its water is about six times saltier than the sea and about minus 13 degrees. In 2012, a team led by Alison Murray, an American scientist, reported bacteria living in it.
- e.g. Tiny algae in Antarctic sea ice can colour the bottom of the ice brown.
- e.g. Lake Vida in Antarctica has bacteria living in brine at about minus 13 degrees.
- e.g. In the sea ice test, the leftover water under the ice tasted saltier than the water you started with, because freezing pushed the salt out.
### Life in salt and in dry places · *defined*
**Definition:** Salt-loving microbes live in water so salty that it would pull the water out of most cells. Tardigrades survive drying out by curling into a dry ball and waiting for water.

Very salty water is a problem because salt pulls water out of living cells. Salted cucumber slices go limp for this reason. Salt-loving microbes fill their own cells with salts too, so the water stays inside them. Many of them are pink or red. In 2020, Lonar Lake in Maharashtra turned pink. The lake had become very salty in the hot, dry weather, and scientists in Pune found it was full of pink salt-loving microbes called haloarchaea. Drying out is another problem. A tardigrade lives in damp moss, which dries up in the hot months. So the tardigrade pulls in their head and legs and dries into a tiny ball. They can wait like this for years. When water comes back, they unroll and walk again.
- e.g. Lonar Lake in Maharashtra, which fills a crater made by a falling space rock, turned pink in 2020 because of salt-loving microbes.
- e.g. Salt pans by the sea can turn pink when salt-loving microbes grow in them.
- e.g. In 2007, dried tardigrades were sent into space on the outside of a spacecraft. Some survived and later laid eggs.
### Where in our solar system scientists look for life · *idea*
**Definition:** Scientists look where there is, or once was, liquid water: Mars, Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus.

Mars is cold and dry today, with thin air, and its water is mostly frozen. But dried-up river beds and lake beds show that Mars once had liquid water on the surface. NASA's Perseverance rover has been collecting rock samples from an old lake bed called Jezero crater, in case they hold signs of past microbes. Europa, a moon of Jupiter, is covered in ice. Strong evidence shows a deep salty ocean under that ice, with more water than all of Earth's oceans. Enceladus, a small moon of Saturn, sprays jets of water and ice into space from an ocean under its ice. The Cassini spacecraft flew through the jets and found salt and other chemicals that living things use. Nobody yet knows if anything lives in any of these places.
- e.g. Perseverance landed on Mars in 2021, in an old lake bed, and has been collecting rocks there.
- e.g. Europa Clipper, launched in 2024, is due to reach Jupiter in 2030.
- e.g. Cassini studied Saturn and its moons from 2004 to 2017.
### Could aliens exist? · *debated*
Nobody has found life beyond Earth, and nobody has shown there is none. The Milky Way, our galaxy, has more than 100 billion stars, and most stars probably have planets going round them. Some of those planets are at the right distance from their star for liquid water. That makes many scientists think life is likely somewhere. Others point out that life on Earth needed a long chain of lucky events, and complex life, like animals, took billions of years to appear. So microbes might be common, but animals and people rare. A third question is why, if life is common, nobody has heard from anyone. This is called the Fermi question, after Enrico Fermi, an Italian physicist who asked it in 1950.
- e.g. Since 1995, astronomers have found more than 5,000 planets going round other stars.
- e.g. In 2020, a team led by Jane Greaves, a British astronomer, reported a gas in the clouds of Venus that can come from microbes. Other scientists later questioned the result, and the argument goes on.
- e.g. Any alien life we find first will most likely be microbes, because microbes are the most common life on Earth.
- **Many astronomers:** There are so many stars and planets that life has very likely started on some of them. Earth's microbes show that life can survive in hard places.
- **Peter Ward and Donald Brownlee, American scientists, in their book Rare Earth (2000):** Microbes may be common, but complex life like animals needs many conditions at once, so it may be very rare in the galaxy.
- **Scientists who stress what we do not know:** We have only one example of life, Earth's, so we cannot yet say whether life is common or rare. We have to look.
### How scientists search for life · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists search for life in three ways: by sending spacecraft to look closely, by studying the air of faraway planets with telescopes, and by listening for signals.

Rovers on Mars drill into rocks and test them for the chemicals living things leave behind. Spacecraft like Europa Clipper fly past moons and measure what their ice and jets are made of. For planets round other stars, which are far too far to visit, telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope study the starlight that passes through a planet's air. Each gas takes away its own colours from the light, so scientists can tell which gases are there. Some gases, like oxygen on Earth, are made mostly by living things. Other scientists, such as the American astronomer Jill Tarter, have spent years listening with radio telescopes for signals sent on purpose by someone.
- e.g. Earth's air has lots of oxygen because plants and tiny algae keep making it.
- e.g. Spacecraft going to Mars are cleaned very carefully, so that they do not carry Earth microbes there by mistake.
- e.g. The Voyager spacecraft, launched in 1977, each carry a golden record of sounds and pictures from Earth.

## Mostly agreed
- Every living thing found so far needs liquid water. *How we know:* No living thing has been found growing without it. Microbes in ice live in the liquid brine between the crystals, not in the solid ice.
- Some microbes live in water hotter than 100 degrees Celsius, and some live in brine below minus 10 degrees. *How we know:* Scientists have grown microbes from deep-sea vents in the laboratory at over 110 degrees, and found active bacteria in Lake Vida's brine at about minus 13 degrees.
- Salt lowers the freezing point of water. *How we know:* Anyone can test it in a home freezer. Very salty water stays liquid at minus 18 degrees, while plain water freezes at 0.
- Mars once had liquid water on its surface. *How we know:* Spacecraft have photographed dried river valleys and lake beds on Mars, and rovers have found rocks that only form in water.
- No life has yet been found anywhere except Earth. *How we know:* Every claim so far has had another explanation that most scientists find more likely.

## How we found out
**Thomas Brock, an American microbiologist, and his student Hudson Freeze, 1965 to 1969, at Yellowstone National Park in the United States.** In the early 1960s, many biologists thought nothing could live in water much hotter than about 73 degrees Celsius. Thomas Brock, an American microbiologist, began studying the hot springs of Yellowstone National Park in 1965. In the water flowing out of one spring, Octopus Spring, he saw pink threads growing in water above 80 degrees. They were bacteria. In 1966, Brock and his student Hudson Freeze took water from another spring, Mushroom Spring, and grew a new bacterium from it in the laboratory. They named them Thermus aquaticus, and described them in 1969. Brock kept looking, and found bacteria in springs that were boiling, which at Yellowstone's height means about 93 degrees. The old limit had come from not looking in hot enough places. Later, scientists found microbes beside deep-sea vents growing above 110 degrees. A heat-proof chemical from Thermus aquaticus is now used in laboratories all over the world to copy DNA.  
*What people believed before:* Biologists thought no life could survive in water much hotter than about 73 degrees. Brock found bacteria growing in springs above 80 degrees, and even in boiling springs. Microbes have since been found growing above 110 degrees.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Mars is criss-crossed by canals built by Martians.** (Percival Lowell, an American astronomer, in books from 1895 to 1908; *rejected*)  
  For: Through small telescopes, some astronomers saw dark straight lines on Mars. An Italian astronomer, Giovanni Schiaparelli, had called them 'canali', which means channels.  
  Against: In 1965, the Mariner 4 spacecraft flew past Mars and sent back close pictures. They showed craters and dry ground, and no canals. The lines had been a trick of the eye at the edge of what the telescopes could show.
- **A meteorite from Mars, found in Antarctica, holds tiny fossils of Martian microbes.** (David McKay and his team at NASA, in 1996; *minority*)  
  For: The rock, called ALH84001, had tiny worm-like shapes and chemicals that microbes on Earth can make.  
  Against: Other scientists showed that the same shapes and chemicals can form without life. Most scientists today are not convinced.
- **A gas in the clouds of Venus could be a sign of living microbes.** (A team led by Jane Greaves, a British astronomer, in 2020; *debated*)  
  For: The team reported phosphine, a gas that on Earth is mostly made by living things, high in Venus's clouds, where it is not too hot.  
  Against: Other teams looked again and found much less phosphine, or none. Some think the signal came from a different gas, or from how the data was handled.
- **Life on Earth started from microbes that came here on space rocks.** (An old idea called panspermia, argued by Svante Arrhenius, a Swedish scientist, in 1903, and by others since; *minority*)  
  For: Tardigrades and some microbes can survive in space for a while. Rocks really do travel between Mars and Earth, knocked off by big impacts.  
  Against: There is no evidence that any Earth life came from space. And the idea only moves the question: where did that life begin?
- **Living things need sunlight.** (What most people, and most scientists, thought before 1977; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants need sunlight, and animals eat plants or eat animals that ate plants.  
  Against: In 1977, scientists in the submarine Alvin found crabs, clams and tube worms by hot vents on the dark sea floor. Their food chain starts with microbes who take energy from chemicals in the vent water.

## Roots
We have frozen salty water, tasted sea ice and met microbes in boiling springs and Antarctic brine. Now we ask what this tells us about life, and about our place among the worlds.

**Deep truth.** In the freezer, your plain water froze hard, but the very salty water stayed liquid. Salt keeps water liquid in cold that freezes plain water solid. Living things need liquid water, and microbes live in salty water trapped in Antarctic ice. So the salty oceans under the ice of moons like Europa could hold life too.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of icy places as dead, because cold feels deadly to us. But what life needs is liquid water, not warmth, and salt keeps water liquid far below zero.  
*Evidence:* In the freezer test, the six-spoon cup still poured after a night at freezer cold, while the plain cup froze hard. In Antarctica, bacteria live in Lake Vida's brine at about minus 13 degrees.

- *who am i:* You are mostly water, and your body keeps that water liquid and warm every moment you are alive.
- *what is life:* Life is found wherever there is liquid water and some energy, from springs near boiling to brine below zero.
- *what is this world:* Earth is one world with liquid water. Europa and Enceladus may be others, so Earth may not be the only home for life.

All life on Earth comes from one shared ancestor, and all of it needs the same thing, liquid water. From that one start came hot-spring bacteria, sea-ice algae, tardigrades, banyan trees and you. Each found their own way to live in their own place, hot or cold, salty or dry. One kind of chemistry, worked out in countless ways, has filled every corner of Earth that has liquid water.

## Story: Sea Ice in a Chennai Freezer
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: sea water freezes more slowly than tap water, freezing pushes salt out of the ice, microbes live in the brine inside sea ice, and Lake Vida and Europa are real.*

On Sunday evening, Kavya and her little brother Arjun came home from Marina Beach with sandy feet and a bottle of sea water. Kavya was nine. Arjun was six, and the bottle had been his idea.

"I am going to make sea ice," Arjun said. "Like at the South Pole. Then I will have penguin ice cubes."

Their mother laughed and found him an empty ice tray. Kavya filled the other half of the tray with tap water, because she wanted to see if sea ice looked any different. They pushed the tray into the freezer, next to a packet of frozen peas, and went to wash the sand off their feet.

After dinner, Arjun ran to check. He pulled out the tray and frowned. The tap-water cubes were hard and white. But the sea-water cubes were soft. When he pressed one with his finger, it squished like wet sugar, and salty water ran out of it.

"It's broken," he said. "The sea doesn't want to be ice."

Kavya pressed one too. There were little crystals of ice in it, but there was liquid all round them. "Maybe it just needs more time," she said. "The sea is salty. Maybe salt makes water slower to freeze."

"Ask Lakshmi Chithi," said their mother. "She will know."

Lakshmi Chithi was their mother's younger sister. She studied microbes, tiny living things too small to see, in a laboratory in Hyderabad. Kavya called her on the phone and turned on the video, and held the soft sea-ice tray up to the camera.

"You have made real sea ice," said Lakshmi Chithi, and she looked delighted. "Kavya, you are right about the salt. Plain water freezes at zero degrees. Salty water has to get colder before it freezes. The more salt there is, the colder it has to get."

"But why is it so wet inside?" asked Kavya.

"Because when sea water freezes, the ice pushes the salt out," said Lakshmi Chithi. "The ice crystals are made almost only of water. The salt gets squeezed into the water left between them. That leftover water gets saltier and saltier, so it stays liquid even when it is very cold. Arjun, lick one of the hard bits of ice, and then a drop of the runny water."

Arjun licked a crystal. Then he licked a drop of the liquid and screwed up his face. "The water is much saltier!" he said.

"Exactly," said Lakshmi Chithi. "The real sea ice around Antarctica is like that. It is full of thin channels of very salty water that never freeze. And here is my favourite part. Those channels are full of life. Bacteria and tiny algae live in them, in the dark and the cold, in water below zero."

Kavya stared at the tray. "Things live inside ice?"

"Inside the liquid in the ice," said Lakshmi Chithi. "Every living thing needs liquid water. Not ice, not steam. Liquid. Inside a cell, all the chemicals of life have to move about and meet each other, and they can only do that in liquid water. So wherever there is liquid water, scientists go looking for life. And they keep finding it. In Antarctica there is a lake called Lake Vida, sealed under ice as thick as a six-storey building is tall. The water in it is six times saltier than the sea, and colder than your freezer's ice tray. In 2012, a team led by a scientist called Alison Murray found bacteria living in that water."

Arjun was thinking hard. "Are there penguins in it?"

"No penguins," said Lakshmi Chithi, smiling. "Just microbes. But here is a bigger question. Far away, going round the planet Jupiter, there is a moon called Europa. Europa is covered in ice, kilometres thick. Under the ice, scientists are fairly sure there is a huge ocean of salty water. It is so far from the Sun that it should be frozen solid. But the salt, and heat from inside the moon, may keep it liquid."

"So there could be microbes in it," said Kavya slowly. "Like in Lake Vida."

"There could be," said Lakshmi Chithi. "Nobody knows yet. A spacecraft called Europa Clipper is on its way there now, to find out what the ice and the water are made of. Maybe when you are grown up, you will know the answer."

After the call, Kavya took a clean katori from the kitchen. She filled it with water, and stirred in salt, spoon after spoon, until no more would dissolve. She put it in the freezer, right at the back where it was coldest.

"What are you doing?" asked Arjun.

"Making a tiny Europa," said Kavya. "Let's see if it freezes by morning."

In the morning, the tap-water cubes were hard. The sea-water cubes had frozen at last. But Kavya's katori of very salty water was still liquid. When she tilted it, it moved like water, though the steel was so cold it stuck to her fingers for a moment.

Arjun put his face close to it. "Hello," he whispered to the water. "Is anybody in there?"

**For the parent:** For the parent: salt lowers the freezing point of water. Sea water freezes at about minus 2 degrees, so it froze more slowly than tap water. Very salty water stays liquid down to about minus 21 degrees, which is colder than home freezers, so Kavya's katori never froze. As sea water freezes, the ice crystals push the salt out into the leftover water, which is why the runny part tasted saltier. Microbes really do live in those brine channels in sea ice, and in Lake Vida's brine. Every living thing needs liquid water, so salty water under ice, on Earth or on Europa, is a place where life is possible.

## Experiments
### e1 · Salt in the Freezer: how cold can water get and stay liquid? (35 min)
- **question:** Does salt stop water freezing in a home freezer? Does more salt make a difference?
- **predict:** We will put three cups in the freezer: plain water, water with one spoon of salt, and water with six spoons of salt. Which will freeze first? Will any of them stay liquid all night? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use three cups that are the same, with the same amount of water, on the same freezer shelf, checked at the same times. Only the amount of salt changes. That way, if one freezes later, the salt is the reason.
- **safety:** Do not drink the salty water. It is far too salty to be good for you. When you finish, pour it down the sink with plenty of tap water. Very cold steel can stick to wet fingers for a moment, so hold the cups with dry hands or a cloth.
- **record:** Fill in the table at each check. Write one of these four words: liquid, thin ice on top, slushy, or frozen hard. Next morning, draw the three cups and colour the ice blue and the liquid water green.
- **results table:** Cup | Spoons of salt | My guess | After 1 hour | After 3 hours | Next morning
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the cups 0, 1 and 6. 2. Pour half a cup of tap water into each. 3. Add one level teaspoon of salt to cup 1. 4. Add six level teaspoons of salt to cup 6. 5. Stir each cup for one minute.  
  *Parent says:* We are making three kinds of water. Plain, a little salty, and very salty. Stir until the salt is mostly gone.  
  *Child mode:* Label three cups 0, 1 and 6. Add that many spoons of salt. Stir.
  - Note: The six-spoon cup holds almost as much salt as water can take, so a little may stay at the bottom. That is fine. Level spoons matter, so scrape each one flat with the back of a knife.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to guess what each cup will look like in 1 hour, in 3 hours and next morning. 2. Ask for a reason. 3. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which cup will freeze first? Will any of them stay liquid all night? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which cup will freeze first? Will any stay liquid? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the salty cups will be different? (good: The sea is salty and it does not freeze at home.; Salt melted the ice cube with the thread.; Salt gets in the way of the water turning to ice.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Any reason your child gives is useful, because it shows what they think now.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three cups side by side on the same shelf of the freezer. Note the time.  
  *Parent says:* Same shelf, side by side, so they all get the same cold. Now we wait one hour.  
  *Child mode:* Put all three cups on one shelf. Side by side. Wait one hour.
  - Note: If you have a digital thermometer, put its tip in the freezer too, and read the temperature later. A big double-door fridge is often about minus 18 degrees. The small freezer box of a single-door fridge is usually warmer, around minus 6 to minus 12 degrees.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the cups out one at a time. 2. Tap the top with the steel spoon. 3. Tilt the cup gently. 4. Write liquid, thin ice on top, slushy or frozen hard. 5. Put each cup straight back.  
  *Parent says:* Tap the top. Does the spoon go through? Tilt it gently. Does it move like water?  
  *Child mode:* Tap each cup with a spoon. Tilt it. Liquid, slushy, or hard?
  - Note: After an hour, the plain cup usually has ice on top and round the edges. The salty cups are usually still liquid. Work quickly so the cups do not warm up.
  - Wait: Check after 1 hour
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Check all three cups again in the same way and write what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Two more hours have passed. Which cups have changed?  
  *Child mode:* Check again. Which cups have changed?
  - Note: By now the plain cup is usually frozen hard. The one-spoon cup may be slushy, with ice crystals in liquid. The six-spoon cup should still be liquid.
  - Wait: Check after 3 hours
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check all three cups one last time. 2. Tilt the six-spoon cup over the sink. 3. Let your child hold it with a cloth and feel how cold it is.  
  *Parent says:* This cup has been in the freezer all night. Feel how cold the cup is. Is the water inside frozen?  
  *Child mode:* Check the cups. Tilt the very salty one. Is it still liquid? Feel how cold it is.
  - Note: The plain cup and the one-spoon cup will be frozen, though the one-spoon ice is often softer. The six-spoon cup will still pour, even though it is as cold as the freezer. A few salt crystals may sit at the bottom. If your freezer is unusually cold and the six-spoon cup has some ice crystals, it will still be much softer than the others, and that still answers the question.
  - Wait: Next morning
- **s7 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: salt stops water freezing, and more salt stops it more. What I saw: the plain water froze first, the one-spoon water froze later and softer, and the six-spoon water was still liquid next morning. Why: the salt gets in the way of the water turning into ice, so salty water has to get much colder before it freezes. Now tell them about Lake Vida, a salty lake under Antarctic ice at about minus 13 degrees, where bacteria live.
  - Check: The six-spoon cup stayed liquid all night in the freezer. What does this show? → **Very salty water stays liquid in cold that freezes plain water**
### e2 · Sea Ice in a Cup: does freezing push the salt out? (25 min)
- **question:** When salty water starts to freeze, is the ice as salty as the water, or does the salt stay behind in the liquid?
- **predict:** We will freeze salty water until it is half ice. Will the ice taste salty, or plain? Will the leftover water be more salty or less salty than at the start? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Taste the salty water before freezing, so you have something to compare. Rinse the ice quickly, so you taste the ice itself and not the salty water on its outside. Taste the ice and the leftover water one after the other, with a sip of plain water between.
- **safety:** This water is only as salty as the sea, so a small taste is safe. Use clean cups and drinking water, because you will taste it. Take only tiny sips.
- **record:** Write how salty each one tasted. If you used food colour, draw the cup and show where the colour was strongest.
- **results table:** What I tasted | My guess | Salty, a little salty, or plain?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir half a level teaspoon of salt into half a cup of drinking water. 2. Add two drops of food colour if you have it. 3. Take a tiny sip and remember how salty it is.  
  *Parent says:* This is about as salty as the sea at Marina Beach. Taste a tiny sip, and remember how salty it is.  
  *Child mode:* Stir in the salt. Taste a tiny sip. How salty is it?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Then put the cup in the freezer.  
  *Parent says:* When this is half frozen, will the ice taste as salty as this? Saltier? Or plainer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will the ice taste salty or plain? Why?
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Write down the guess and the reason.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check the cup. 2. When there is a layer of ice on top and liquid underneath, take it out.  
  *Parent says:* Look. Ice on top, water underneath. This is like the sea around Antarctica in winter.  
  *Child mode:* Is there ice on top and water below? Take it out.
  - Note: This often takes 1 to 2 hours, depending on your freezer. If it freezes solid, let it thaw on the counter until about half is liquid again. The first ice to melt is the saltiest part, so the test still works, but it is clearer when you catch it half frozen.
  - Wait: Check after about 1 hour, then every 20 minutes
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift out a piece of the ice with a spoon. 2. Rinse it under the tap for two seconds. 3. Let it melt in the clean katori, or lick it. 4. Sip plain water. 5. Taste a drop of the leftover liquid.  
  *Parent says:* First the ice. Then a sip of plain water. Then the leftover water. Which is saltier?  
  *Child mode:* Taste the ice. Sip plain water. Taste the leftover water. Which is saltier?
  - Note: The ice should taste much less salty than the water you started with. The leftover water should taste clearly saltier than at the start. If you used food colour, the ice is paler and the leftover water is darker. Home ice traps some salty water inside it, so the ice may not taste completely plain.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. Then show the picture of sea ice channels.  
  *Parent says:* So where did the salt go when the water froze? What does that mean for a microbe living in sea ice?  
  *Child mode:* Where did the salt go? Where could a microbe live in sea ice?
  - Note: A good answer: the ice was less salty, and the leftover water was saltier, so freezing pushed the salt out of the ice. In real sea ice, this pushed-out salty water collects in thin channels. Because it is so salty, it stays liquid far below zero. Bacteria and tiny algae live in those channels.
  - Check: In sea ice, where do bacteria and algae live? → **In thin channels of very salty liquid water between the ice crystals**

- **your turn:** Now plan your own clean way, try it, and check for yellow marks. Then try to do it faster without leaving any.
- **constraints:** The 'microbes' are a pinch of turmeric powder rubbed on your hands.; You must pick up the lander and put it in a clean plastic box.; At the end, there must be no yellow marks on the lander or inside the box.
- **general:** Real spacecraft are built in clean rooms, where the air is filtered and workers wear gowns, gloves and masks.; Parts are wiped with alcohol, and some are baked in ovens to kill microbes before launch.; The two Viking landers, which landed on Mars in 1976, were heated for many hours before launch to kill the microbes on them.
- **creative:** Put plastic bags over your hands like gloves.; Use tongs or two spoons so your hands never touch the lander.; Wash your hands well and dry them before you start.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rub a pinch of turmeric onto your child's hands. 2. Explain that the turmeric stands for microbes.  
  *Parent says:* Pretend this turmeric is Earth microbes. Real microbes are too small to see, so we use turmeric to see where they go.  
  *Child mode:* The turmeric is pretend microbes. Don't let it touch the lander!
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try once with bare yellow hands. Look for marks.  
  *Parent says:* First, just pick it up and put it in the box. Now look. Can you see yellow marks?  
  *Child mode:* Pick up the lander with yellow hands. Any marks?
  - Note: There will be yellow marks. This shows how easily microbes travel on hands. Wipe the lander and the box clean before the next try. Turmeric can stain cloth and plastic, so use an old cloth.
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Your child plans and tries a clean way, checking after each try.  
  *Parent says:* Now plan a clean way. You can use gloves, tongs or washing. Try it, then check for yellow.  
  *Child mode:* Plan a clean way. Try it. Check for yellow. Try again.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about why this matters.  
  *Parent says:* Why do space scientists care so much about keeping spacecraft clean?  
  *Child mode:* Why must spacecraft be so clean?
  - Check: Why are spacecraft to Mars cleaned so carefully? → **So Earth microbes do not travel to Mars, where we might mistake them for Martian life**

### Water, Ice and Life (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (write) Name the three things every living thing found so far needs.  
   **Answer:** Liquid water, a supply of energy (such as sunlight, food or chemicals), and the chemicals their body is built from.. *Why:* A strong answer names liquid water, not just water, because ice and steam are no use to a cell.
2. (mcq) In the freezer test, which cup froze first?  
   Options: The plain water / The one-spoon cup / The six-spoon cup  
   **Answer:** The plain water. *Why:* Plain water freezes at 0 degrees, so it freezes first. Salt lowers the freezing point, so the salty cups must get colder.
3. (write) In the freezer test, the six-spoon cup was still liquid next morning, but the plain cup was frozen hard. Both sat on the same shelf all night. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Salt lowers the freezing point of water. The salt particles get in the way of the water particles lining up into ice. Very salty water does not freeze until about minus 21 degrees, which is colder than a home freezer.. *Why:* A strong answer says the cups were equally cold, so the difference must come from the salt, and says what salt does.
4. (truefalse) The six-spoon cup stayed liquid because it was warmer than the plain cup.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both cups were in the same freezer all night, and the salty cup felt icy cold. It stayed liquid because salt lowers the freezing point, not because it was warm.
5. (sort) Sort these places. Has life been found living there, or not?  
   **Answer:** a hot spring at 70 degrees → Life found; Antarctic sea ice → Life found; Lake Vida's salty water at minus 13 degrees → Life found; a very salty lake → Life found; the surface of the Moon → No life found; the surface of Venus at 460 degrees → No life found; water boiled in a pressure cooker → No life found. *Why:* Every place with life has liquid water. The Moon has no liquid water, Venus is far too hot, and pressure-cooking kills nearly all living things.
6. (fill) A living thing who lives in a place most life could not survive, such as near-boiling water, is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['extremophile']. *Why:* Extremophile means 'lover of extremes'.
7. (match) Match each living thing to the hard place they live in.  
   **Answer:** Thermus aquaticus → a hot spring; Haloarchaea → a very salty lake; Bacteria in Lake Vida → salty water under Antarctic ice; Tardigrade → moss that dries out in summer; Vent microbes → hot water deep in the sea, with no sunlight. *Why:* Each of these lives where most life could not. Knowing where they live helps scientists choose where to look on other worlds.
8. (mcq) Shreya, a girl in Class 3, says, 'Heat breaks the working parts inside most cells.' How do hot-spring microbes survive?  
   Options: They stay cool by swimming fast / Their working parts are built to hold their shape in heat / They only come out at night  
   **Answer:** Their working parts are built to hold their shape in heat. *Why:* Their working parts are heat-proof, so they keep working in hot water. Swimming does not cool them, and the spring is hot day and night.
9. (draw) Draw a cup of sea water that is half frozen. Show where the ice is, and where the salt has gone.  
   **Answer:** Ice on top, almost without salt. Salty leftover water underneath, with the salt drawn as dots crowded in the liquid.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the salt pushed out of the ice and into the liquid, as the taste test showed.
10. (write) In the Sea Ice in a Cup test, the ice tasted less salty than the water you started with. Where did the salt go?  
   **Answer:** Into the leftover water under the ice. That water tasted saltier than at the start. Freezing pushes the salt out of the ice crystals.. *Why:* A strong answer uses both tastes as evidence: the ice was plainer and the leftover water saltier.
11. (mcq) Why do scientists look for life where there is liquid water?  
   Options: Water looks nice in photos / Every living thing found so far needs liquid water / Water is easy to find everywhere in space  
   **Answer:** Every living thing found so far needs liquid water. *Why:* Inside every cell, the chemicals of life move and join up in liquid water. No living thing has been found growing without it.
12. (truefalse) Microbes living in sea ice live inside the solid ice crystals.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They live in the thin channels of liquid brine between the crystals. Solid ice has no liquid water for a cell to use.
13. (write) Arjun, a boy of six, says, 'Nothing could live in Antarctica's ice. It is too cold.' What would you tell him, and what evidence would you use?  
   **Answer:** I would tell him that microbes live in the salty liquid water inside sea ice, and in Lake Vida under Antarctic ice at minus 13 degrees. My freezer test showed that salty water stays liquid far below zero, and living things need liquid water, not warmth.. *Why:* A strong answer is kind, uses a real example, and uses the freezer test as evidence.
14. (order) Put these in order from coldest to hottest.  
   **Answer:** ["Lake Vida's water", 'plain water freezing', 'your body', 'a hot spring with Thermus aquaticus', 'boiling water near the sea']. *Why:* Lake Vida is about minus 13, freezing is 0, your body is 37, the spring about 70, and boiling 100.

   **Answer:** The outer white layer is the ice shell. Under it is the salty ocean. In the middle is the rocky inside.. *Why:* Scientists think Europa's ocean sits between the ice shell and the rock, which is why it may hold life.
### Extreme Lives and Icy Moons (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meenakshi does the freezer test with a cup of sugar water as well. It freezes, but later than plain water. What does that tell her?  
   Options: Only salt can lower the freezing point / Other things dissolved in water lower the freezing point too / Sugar makes water colder  
   **Answer:** Other things dissolved in water lower the freezing point too. *Why:* Anything dissolved in water gets in the way of ice forming, so it lowers the freezing point. Salt does it more strongly for each spoon than sugar.
2. (write) Kavya's freezer is a small single-door fridge, at about minus 8 degrees. Her one-spoon cup stayed slushy all night. In her cousin's big fridge, at minus 18, the one-spoon cup froze. Explain both results.  
   **Answer:** One spoon of salt lowers the freezing point a few degrees below zero. At minus 8, the cup was only a little below its freezing point, so it froze slowly and stayed slushy. At minus 18, it was much colder than its freezing point, so it froze.. *Why:* A strong answer compares each freezer's temperature with the salty water's freezing point.
3. (truefalse) If a home freezer were colder than minus 21 degrees, even the six-spoon cup would freeze.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Water with as much salt as it can hold freezes at about minus 21 degrees. Below that, even very salty water freezes. Home freezers are not usually that cold.
4. (sort) Sort each problem with the way a living thing deals with it.  
   **Answer:** working parts built to hold their shape when hot → Heat; filling their cells with salts so water stays inside → Salt; curling into a dry ball and waiting for water → Drying out; taking energy from chemicals instead of sunlight → Darkness. *Why:* Each hard place gives a different problem, and each extremophile has a way to deal with theirs. The problem comes first, then the trick.
5. (write) Lonar Lake in Maharashtra turned pink in 2020, in very hot, dry weather. Explain how the dry weather could help pink salt-loving microbes.  
   **Answer:** In hot, dry weather, water evaporated from the lake, but the salt stayed behind. So the lake got saltier. Pink salt-loving microbes live well in very salty water, while many other microbes do not, so they grew and turned the lake pink.. *Why:* A strong answer links evaporation to more salt, and more salt to the pink microbes.
6. (mcq) A deep-sea vent can be hotter than 100 degrees, but the water does not boil. Why?  
   Options: The water is salty / The great weight of water above presses on it / It is dark down there  
   **Answer:** The great weight of water above presses on it. *Why:* Pressure from the water above stops it boiling. Salt raises the boiling point only a little, and darkness has nothing to do with boiling.
7. (perspective) Three people argue about where to send the next spacecraft to look for life.

It then admits a fair point, such as that Enceladus's jets make sampling easier.. *Why:* This is a real argument among space scientists. Each place has strengths, and the money for missions is limited.
8. (fill) Salty water inside sea ice is called ____.

9. (order) Put these steps of the freezer test in order.

10. (mcq) Why did the freezer test use three cups that were the same, on the same shelf?

11. (draw) Draw a tardigrade in moss in the rainy season, and the same tardigrade in summer when the moss is dry.

12. (write) Thermus aquaticus cannot grow at your body's warmth, 37 degrees, because it is too cool for them. So is 37 degrees 'extreme'? Explain your answer.

Each living thing has their own range.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that 'extreme' is measured from our own bodies, and other living things have different ranges.
13. (truefalse) Mars has liquid water flowing on its surface today, like rivers on Earth.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Mars's water today is mostly frozen, in its polar caps and under the ground. Dried river beds show water flowed long ago.

## Applications
- Salt is spread on icy roads in cold countries, so the ice melts and cars do not skid.
- Ice-cream makers once used ice mixed with salt, which is colder than ice alone, to freeze the cream.
- A heat-proof chemical from Thermus aquaticus is used in laboratories to copy DNA, in tests for diseases such as COVID and in solving crimes.
- Pickles and salted fish keep for months because most microbes cannot grow in that much salt.
- Spacecraft to Mars are built in clean rooms, so they do not carry Earth microbes to another world.
- Studying microbes in Antarctic ice helps scientists plan how to look for life on Europa and Mars.

## Spotted around us
- A jar of mango or lime pickle in brine, which keeps for months because few microbes can grow in so much salt.
- The frost on the inside of your freezer, which is water from the air turned straight to ice.
- Salt pans along the coast near Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu, where sea water dries in the sun and leaves salt.
- Steam and hot water at hot springs in India, such as Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh or Bakreshwar in West Bengal.
- Jupiter, bright in the night sky for part of each year, with Europa too small to see without a telescope.

## Open questions
- **Is there life anywhere beyond Earth?** Nobody has found any, and we have searched only a few places closely.
- **Is there life in Europa's ocean?** Europa very likely has a salty ocean, but nobody has looked inside it yet. Europa Clipper will study its ice and surface from above.
- **Did Mars ever have life?** Mars once had lakes and rivers. Perseverance has collected rock samples, but bringing them to Earth for testing is still being planned.
- **What is the hottest temperature any living thing can grow at?** The record keeps rising as new microbes are found. Scientists think the limit is somewhere around 120 to 150 degrees, but nobody knows exactly.
- **If life is common in the galaxy, why has nobody heard from anyone?** This is the Fermi question. There are many possible answers, such as great distances, short listening time, or that complex life is rare, and no way yet to choose between them.
- **Could life use a liquid other than water?** Saturn's moon Titan has lakes of liquid methane. Some scientists think life might be possible there, and others doubt it. NASA's Dragonfly mission is due to go to Titan.

## Connected maths
- Plain water freezes at 0 degrees. Water with as much salt as it can hold freezes at about minus 21 degrees. How many degrees colder must the very salty water get before it freezes? **21 degrees colder.**. From 0 down to minus 21 is 21 steps of one degree. On a thermometer, count down from 0 to minus 21. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
- Bacteria in Lake Vida live at about minus 13 degrees. Some microbes at deep-sea vents grow at about 113 degrees. How many degrees apart are these two temperatures? **126 degrees.**. From minus 13 up to 0 is 13 degrees. From 0 up to 113 is 113 degrees. 13 + 113 = 126. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- One level teaspoon of salt weighs about 6 grams. In the freezer test, how many grams of salt went into the six-spoon cup? How many grams did you use in all three cups? **36 grams in the six-spoon cup. 42 grams in all.**. 6 spoons times 6 grams is 36 grams. The one-spoon cup had 6 grams and the plain cup had none. 36 + 6 + 0 = 42 grams. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- Lakshmi asked 10 people what alien life would look like. 6 said a creature with a head and eyes, 2 said like an animal, 1 said like a plant, and 1 said a microbe. Draw a bar chart. What fraction of people said a microbe? **1 out of 10, which is one tenth.**. Only 1 of the 10 people said a microbe. 1 out of 10 is one tenth. Most scientists would say a microbe, so the chart shows how different films and science can be. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Europa Clipper was launched in October 2024. It is due to reach Jupiter in April 2030. About how many years and months is the journey? **About 5 years and 6 months.**. October 2024 to October 2029 is 5 years. October 2029 to April 2030 is 6 more months. So about 5 and a half years. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)

## Warm-up
- At what temperature does plain water freeze? → 0 degrees Celsius.
- What does salt do to the freezing point of water? → It lowers it, so salty water must get colder before it freezes.
- What does every living thing found so far need? → Liquid water, energy, and the chemicals their body is built from.
- What is an extremophile? → A living thing who lives where most life could not, such as near-boiling water, ice or very salty water.
- Name a moon with an ocean under its ice. → Europa, a moon of Jupiter, or Enceladus, a moon of Saturn.
- Where do microbes live in sea ice? → In thin channels of salty liquid water between the ice crystals.
- Who found bacteria living in Yellowstone's hot springs? → Thomas Brock, with his student Hudson Freeze.
- Has life been found beyond Earth? → Not yet.
