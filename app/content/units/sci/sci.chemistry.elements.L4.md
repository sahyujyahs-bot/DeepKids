# Elements: the 118 kinds of atom, metals and non-metals, and the elements in your body
`sci.chemistry.elements.L4`

**Summary.** Sugar and salt are both white grains, yet on a hot tawa sugar turns black and salt stays white. The black is carbon, an element that was inside the white sugar all the time. An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom, and 118 elements are known. Most of them are metals, but your body is built almost entirely from four non-metals: oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen. Your child heats eight kitchen things to find which ones hold carbon. They colour a hundred square of the elements in their own body. They also test whether pencil lead, which is carbon, passes the tests for a metal.

**Intro.** *Sugar is white. Salt is white. A grown-up heats a pinch of each on a tawa, and one of them turns black. Which one, and where did the black come from?* The black is an element called carbon. It was inside the sugar before the heating, joined with two other elements. There is carbon in rice, in cotton, in paper and in you. In this unit you find out which kitchen things hold carbon. You colour the elements of your own body on a hundred square. You number all 118 elements on a grid and see how many are metals. You also put pencil lead through three tests for a metal.

## Terms
- **Atom**: A tiny particle that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see. *e.g.* A grain of sugar holds more atoms than there are grains of sand on a beach.
- **Element**: A substance made of only one kind of atom. *e.g.* Pure gold has only gold atoms. Oxygen gas has only oxygen atoms.
- **Compound**: A substance made of two or more elements joined together. *e.g.* Water is hydrogen joined with oxygen. Sugar is carbon, hydrogen and oxygen joined together.
- **Atomic number**: The number that each element has, from 1 to 118. It counts the protons, which are particles in the centre of the atom. *e.g.* Hydrogen is number 1. Carbon is number 6. Gold is number 79.
- **Symbol**: The one or two letters that scientists everywhere use for an element. *e.g.* O is oxygen, C is carbon, and Na is sodium.
- **Metal**: An element that is usually shiny, bends without breaking, and carries heat and electricity well. *e.g.* Iron, copper, aluminium, gold and silver are metals.
- **Non-metal**: An element that lacks most of the properties of metals. Many non-metals are gases, and the solid ones are usually brittle. *e.g.* Oxygen, nitrogen, carbon and sulfur are non-metals.
- **Metalloid**: An element with some properties of a metal and some of a non-metal. *e.g.* Silicon is shiny like a metal but breaks like glass. It is used in the chips inside phones.
- **Lustre**: The shine of a clean metal surface. *e.g.* A steel plate shines again when you scrub it.
- **Malleable**: Able to be beaten or pressed into a new shape without breaking. *e.g.* Silver is beaten into the thin leaf, called varak, that covers some sweets.
- **Brittle**: Breaking or crumbling when it is hit or bent. *e.g.* Pencil lead and charcoal are brittle. They snap and do not bend.
- **Conductor**: A material that lets heat or electricity pass through it easily. *e.g.* Copper is a good conductor, so electric wires are made of copper.
- **Carbon**: A non-metal element, number 6. Every living thing is built around carbon atoms. *e.g.* Charcoal, pencil lead and diamond are carbon. The black on burnt toast is mostly carbon.
- **Trace element**: An element that the body needs in a very small amount. *e.g.* Your body holds only a few grams of iron, but your blood needs it to carry oxygen.

## Explanations
### An element is one kind of atom · *defined*
**Definition:** An element is a substance made of only one kind of atom. It cannot be broken down into anything simpler by heating or by mixing.

Everything is made of atoms, which are particles far too small to see. There are different kinds of atom. A substance with only one kind is called an element. Gold is an element, because every atom in pure gold is a gold atom. Water is a compound, because it is hydrogen atoms joined with oxygen atoms. Sugar is a compound of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. A compound can look nothing like the elements in it. Sugar is white and sweet. The carbon in it is black, and the hydrogen and oxygen are gases with no colour. Most things around you are compounds or mixtures. Only a few things at home are nearly pure elements.
- e.g. The aluminium in kitchen foil is an element.
- e.g. The copper inside an electric wire is an element.
- e.g. Salt is a compound of two elements, sodium and chlorine.
### There are 118 elements, numbered 1 to 118 · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists know 118 elements. Each has an atomic number from 1 to 118, which counts the protons in the centre of its atom.

The centre of an atom holds particles called protons. Hydrogen has 1 proton, so it is element number 1. Carbon has 6, and oxygen has 8. An atom with 79 protons is always gold. There is no element with 6 and a half protons, so there are no gaps in the list. About 90 of the 118 elements are found in nature on the Earth. People made the rest in laboratories, by smashing lighter atoms together. Many of those last less than one second before they break apart. The newest four were named in 2016. Number 118 is oganesson, and only a few atoms of it have ever been made. Nobody knows yet whether more elements can be made.
- e.g. Hydrogen is number 1, the lightest element.
- e.g. Uranium is number 92, the heaviest element found in large amounts in nature.
- e.g. Oganesson is number 118. Its atoms last less than a thousandth of a second.
### Every element has a symbol · *defined*
**Definition:** A symbol is one or two letters that stand for an element. The first letter is a capital and the second is small.

People speak thousands of languages, but all scientists use the same symbols. Many symbols are the first letters of the English name. H is hydrogen, C is carbon, and Ca is calcium. Some come from the old Latin name. Sodium is Na, from natrium. Potassium is K, from kalium. Iron is Fe, from ferrum. Gold is Au, from aurum, and silver is Ag, from argentum. The symbols let a chemist in Chennai read the work of a chemist in Japan. You can find symbols on labels at home. A packet of fertiliser marked NPK holds nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
- e.g. O is oxygen and N is nitrogen.
- e.g. Fe is iron, so iron tablets may be labelled 'ferrous'.
- e.g. Hg is mercury, from a Latin name that means liquid silver.
### What most metals share · *defined*
**Definition:** Most metals shine when clean, bend or flatten without breaking, ring when struck, and carry heat and electricity well.

Metals behave alike because of their atoms. In a metal, some electrons are free to move from atom to atom. An electron is a very light particle that carries electricity. These moving electrons carry electricity along a wire. They also pass heat on quickly, so a steel spoon in hot rasam soon feels hot. They throw light back, which gives a clean metal its shine, called lustre. The atoms of a metal can slide past each other and still hold together. So a metal can be beaten into a sheet, which is called being malleable. It can also be pulled into a wire. A struck metal shakes for a long time, so it rings. Not every metal has every property. Mercury is a liquid at room temperature. Sodium is so soft that a knife cuts it.
- e.g. One gram of gold can be beaten into a sheet nearly one square metre in size.
- e.g. Temple bells and ghungroos are made of metal because metal rings.
- e.g. Aluminium is rolled into foil thinner than a hair.
### Non-metals · *defined*
**Definition:** Non-metals are the elements that lack most of the properties of metals. Only 17 of the elements are non-metals.

Eleven non-metals are gases at room temperature. Oxygen and nitrogen make up almost all of the air. Helium, neon and argon are gases too. One non-metal, bromine, is a liquid. The rest, such as carbon, sulfur, phosphorus and iodine, are solids. A solid non-metal is usually brittle, which means it snaps or crumbles when you hit it. Most non-metals are poor conductors of heat and electricity. They do not ring. There are few non-metals, but they matter a great deal. The air, the water and almost all of every living body are made of non-metals.
- e.g. Air is about 78 parts nitrogen and 21 parts oxygen in every 100.
- e.g. The brown striking strip on the side of a matchbox holds phosphorus.
- e.g. The gas inside many light bulbs and tube lights is argon.
### Where is the line between metals and non-metals? · *debated*
Some elements do not sit clearly on one side. Six elements, such as silicon, are called metalloids. Silicon shines like a metal but shatters like glass, and it carries a little electricity. Carbon is a non-metal, yet one form of it behaves partly like a metal. Pencil lead is graphite, a form of carbon. Graphite is shiny and carries electricity, but it snaps when you bend it. Diamond is also pure carbon, and it carries no electricity at all. The atoms are the same, and only their arrangement differs. Hydrogen is a non-metal gas on the Earth. Deep inside the planet Jupiter it is squeezed so hard that it behaves as a liquid metal.
- e.g. Graphite carries electricity, and diamond does not. Both are carbon.
- e.g. Silicon is used in computer chips because it carries electricity only a little.
- e.g. Tin is a metal, but in great cold it slowly crumbles to a grey powder.
- **Most chemists:** Sort each element by how it behaves in an ordinary room. Then carbon is a non-metal and iron is a metal. A few elements in between are called metalloids.
- **Many physicists:** A substance is a metal whenever some of its electrons are free to move. That depends on how the atoms are arranged and how hard they are squeezed. So 'metal' describes a state that an element is in.
### The elements in your body, by weight · *defined*
**Definition:** In every 100 parts of a human body's weight, about 65 are oxygen, 18 carbon, 10 hydrogen and 3 nitrogen.

Six elements make up 99 parts in every 100 of your weight. They are oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium and phosphorus. Oxygen comes first because more than half of your body is water. Most of the weight of water is oxygen. Carbon is the frame of fat, protein and sugar. Nitrogen is in every protein. Calcium and phosphorus make your bones and teeth hard. The last 1 part holds about 20 more elements. Sodium and potassium help nerves carry signals. Iron in the blood carries oxygen. Iodine is needed by a gland in the neck, which is why salt is iodised. Almost none of these elements are loose in your body. They are joined into compounds such as water, protein and bone.
- e.g. A child of 30 kilograms holds about 19 and a half kilograms of oxygen.
- e.g. A grown-up holds about 1 kilogram of calcium, nearly all of it in bones and teeth.
- e.g. A grown-up holds only 3 to 4 grams of iron, about the weight of a small nail.
### Weighing atoms or counting atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** If you count the atoms in a body one by one, hydrogen comes first. About 62 of every 100 atoms in you are hydrogen.

Atoms of different elements have different weights. An oxygen atom weighs about 16 times as much as a hydrogen atom. A carbon atom weighs about 12 times as much. Water is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom. So two of every three atoms in water are hydrogen. But the one oxygen atom holds eight ninths of the weight. Your body is the same. Count the atoms, and about 62 in every 100 are hydrogen, 24 are oxygen and 12 are carbon. Weigh them, and oxygen is 65 parts in every 100. Both counts are correct. They answer two different questions: which atoms are most common, and which atoms carry most weight.
- e.g. In water, hydrogen is 2 of every 3 atoms but only 1 ninth of the weight.
- e.g. In your body, hydrogen is 62 of every 100 atoms but only 10 parts in 100 of the weight.
- e.g. A lorry and three cycles: the cycles are more in number, and the lorry is most of the weight.
### Carbon, the element living things are built around · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living thing is made of compounds built around chains and rings of carbon atoms.

A carbon atom can join to four other atoms at once, and carbon atoms join easily to each other. So carbon makes long chains and rings. Sugar, fat, protein, wood, cotton and paper are all carbon chains with hydrogen and oxygen attached. Strong heat breaks these compounds. The hydrogen and oxygen leave as water vapour and other gases. Much of the carbon stays behind as a black solid. That is why sugar, rice, bread and cotton all turn black on a hot tawa. Salt holds no carbon, so it stays white. A few things hold carbon that does not show as black. Chalk and cooking soda hold carbon joined tightly to oxygen, and they stay white when heated.
- e.g. A burnt dosa edge is black because the carbon of the batter is left behind.
- e.g. Charcoal is wood that was heated with very little air, so mostly carbon remains.
- e.g. Salt and a steel pin on the same hot tawa do not turn black.
### Where the elements were made · *defined*
**Definition:** Hydrogen was made soon after the universe began. Almost all other elements were made later inside stars or when stars exploded or collided.

The universe began about 13.8 billion years ago. In its first few minutes it made hydrogen, helium and a tiny amount of lithium. Stars formed later from this gas. Deep inside a star, hydrogen atoms are pressed together to make helium, and this gives out the star's light. Older stars press helium into carbon and oxygen. Very large stars build elements up to iron and then explode. The explosion scatters these elements into space. Elements heavier than iron, such as gold, are made in such explosions and when two dead stars collide. The Sun, the Earth and your body formed from this scattered matter. Margaret Burbidge and three other scientists worked this out in 1957. Today hydrogen and helium are still about 98 parts in every 100 of the ordinary matter in the universe.
- e.g. Every hydrogen atom in your body is about 13.8 billion years old.
- e.g. The calcium in your teeth was made inside large stars.
- e.g. In 2017 astronomers watched two dead stars collide and saw the light of freshly made heavy elements.

## Mostly agreed
- Everything we can touch is made from the same list of elements, and 118 are known. *How we know:* Chemists have taken apart thousands of substances from rocks, air, sea and living bodies. They always reach the same elements, each with its own atomic number.
- A compound can look and behave nothing like the elements in it. *How we know:* Sugar is white and sweet. Strong heat drives off water vapour and leaves black carbon, as in the Black Test.
- A living body is made of ordinary elements. No element is found only in living things. *How we know:* In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea, a compound found in urine. He made it from substances that had never been in a living body.
- Most elements are metals, but living bodies are almost all non-metal. *How we know:* Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen are non-metals. Together they make up 96 parts in every 100 of a human body by weight.
- The Sun and the stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium. *How we know:* In 1925 Cecilia Payne studied the colours missing from starlight. She showed that stars are mostly hydrogen and helium. Other astronomers doubted her at first, and later agreed.
- Elements heavier than hydrogen and helium were built inside stars. *How we know:* In 1957 Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle showed how stars build each element. The amounts they worked out match what astronomers measure.

## How we found out
**Hennig Brand in Hamburg. Later Robert Boyle in England, Johan Gahn and Carl Scheele in Sweden, and Antoine Lavoisier in France., Brand found phosphorus in 1669. Boyle made it in 1680. Gahn found it in bones in 1769, and Scheele made it from bone ash in 1771. Lavoisier's list was printed in 1789..** For hundreds of years, people called alchemists believed that ordinary matter could be turned into gold. Hennig Brand was a merchant in Hamburg in Germany. In 1669 he thought the secret might lie in urine, because urine is golden yellow. He collected many buckets of it. He let it stand for weeks, boiled it down to a paste, and heated the paste very strongly. No gold appeared. A white, waxy solid dripped out, and it glowed pale green in the dark. Brand had found phosphorus. He was the first person known by name to discover an element. He kept his method secret and sold it. Robert Boyle in England worked out the method again in 1680. About a hundred years later, in Sweden, Johan Gahn found phosphorus in bones. Carl Scheele then made it from bone ash. In 1789 Antoine Lavoisier put phosphorus on his list of simple substances that nobody could break down. So a search for gold showed something else. The human body holds an element that no one had known, and that element can be taken out and held.  
*What people believed before:* That ordinary matter can be turned into gold. Brand also took the golden colour of urine as a sign of gold in it. His work gave no gold. It gave a new element. Later chemists showed that heating or mixing cannot change one element into another.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The elements are earth, water, air, fire and space.** (Many old traditions. In India they are called the pancha bhuta. Greek thinkers listed four of them.; *rejected*)  
  For: It was the first clear idea that everything is made from a few basic kinds. The five also match solid, liquid, gas, energy and space quite well.  
  Against: Water can be split into hydrogen and oxygen, and air is a mixture of gases. So neither is an element in the chemist's meaning. The idea of a short list was right, and the list was wrong.
- **All metals are hard, heavy solids.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Iron, copper and steel are hard and heavy, and they are the metals we meet most.  
  Against: Mercury is a liquid. Sodium is soft enough to cut with a knife, and it is lighter than water. The metal gallium melts in a warm hand.
- **Only metals carry electricity.** (A common belief, and a useful first rule.; *rejected*)  
  For: Wires are metal, and most non-metals such as wood, rubber and sulfur carry almost none.  
  Against: Graphite is carbon, a non-metal, and it carries electricity. Salt water carries electricity too.
- **Living bodies are made of a special living matter that is different from the matter of rocks.** (Many scientists before the 1800s. The idea is called vitalism.; *rejected*)  
  For: Living things grow, heal and have young, and rocks do not seem to.  
  Against: Wöhler made urea in 1828 from non-living substances. Every element in a body is also found in rocks, air or sea.
- **Hydrogen can be a metal.** (Physicists, since Eugene Wigner and Hillard Huntington suggested it in 1935.; *debated*)  
  For: Measurements of Jupiter fit a deep layer of liquid hydrogen that carries electricity as a metal does.  
  Against: On the Earth, making solid metallic hydrogen needs enormous pressure. A claim to have made it in 2017 has not been repeated by others.
- **There can be no more than 118 elements.** (Some people, because the list has stopped at 118 since 2016.; *debated*)  
  For: Each new element has been harder to make and has lasted a shorter time.  
  Against: Laboratories in several countries are trying to make elements 119 and 120. Nothing known forbids them.

## Roots
In this unit you found the elements of your own body and counted them in two ways. Here we ask what those two counts say about you and about the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Body Hundred Square, you coloured 65 squares for oxygen when you counted by weight. When you counted atoms instead, 62 squares in 100 were hydrogen. Both counts are correct, because one oxygen atom weighs as much as 16 hydrogen atoms. So the question 'what are you mostly made of?' has two true answers, one for each way of counting.  
*Habit it breaks:* We expect a question such as 'what is this mostly made of?' to have one answer. The answer changes with whether you weigh the parts or count them. So the way of asking is part of the answer.  
*Evidence:* In the Body Hundred Square, the first box is mostly oxygen and the second is mostly hydrogen. Multiply each count of atoms by the weight of one atom, and the second box turns into the first.

- *who am i:* By weight you are mostly oxygen. By number of atoms you are mostly hydrogen, the simplest and oldest element.
- *what is life:* Every living thing in the Black Test left carbon behind. A rice plant, a cotton plant and you are built around the same element.
- *what is this world:* Stars, rocks, air and bodies are made from one list of 118 elements. Most of the list is metal, and most of you is non-metal.

Hydrogen is the simplest atom, with one proton. Stars pressed hydrogen together and built the heavier elements from it, step by step. Those elements joined into water, rock, rice and you. Many sages taught that the many things of the world come from one beginning. The science here says something close and plain. Every element in your body was built from the same simplest atom.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1:** From the Self came space. From space came air, from air fire, from fire water, and from water earth. From earth came plants, from plants food, and from food the person. (This is an old list of elements. It says the body is made of the same things as the world. Chemistry's list is different, with 118 elements. But it agrees that a body is built from food, air and water, and from nothing else.)
- **The Jain teaching of anekantavada (many-sidedness), with the story of the blind men and the elephant:** Each blind man touches one part of the elephant and describes the whole animal from that one part. Each is right about his part. The truth has many sides. (The weigher says you are mostly oxygen. The counter says you are mostly hydrogen. Each is right about what was measured, as in the Body Hundred Square.)
- **Carl Sagan, Cosmos (1980):** The nitrogen in our bodies, the calcium in our teeth and the iron in our blood were made inside stars. We are made of star-stuff. (This is the finding of Margaret Burbidge and her three colleagues in 1957, said in a few words. Sagan was a scientist, and here he states a result that has been measured.)
- Science (settled): Every hydrogen atom in your body was made in the first minutes of the universe. That was about 13.8 billion years ago.
- Science (settled): The carbon, oxygen and calcium in your body were made inside stars that died before the Sun formed.
- Science (open): Some philosophers, called panpsychists, think that even a single atom has a very simple kind of experience. Most scientists see no way yet to test this.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India: The Charvakas taught that a person is the elements and nothing more. Awareness arises when the elements come together in a body. They compared it to fermenting. Certain foods, mixed and left to stand, gain a new power to make people drunk. On this view, a list of your elements leaves nothing out. Many scientists today hold a view close to this one.
- Practice, A sip of the oldest element (3 min): Hold a tumbler of water in both hands. Two of every three atoms in it are hydrogen, and they are older than the Sun. The rest is oxygen, made inside a star. Take one slow sip. In a few minutes those atoms will be part of your blood. Sit quietly for five breaths and feel the water going down.
- Ask: In the Body Hundred Square, one box said oxygen and one said hydrogen. If someone asks what you are made of, what will you tell them?
- Ask: The hydrogen in you is 13.8 billion years old, and it was in other things before you. In what sense is it yours?
- Ask: In the Black Test, rice left the same black carbon as bread and cotton. What makes you different from a grain of rice?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* By weight you are mostly oxygen. By number of atoms you are mostly hydrogen. Is one of these the real answer? (hint: Think of a lorry and three cycles. Are there more cycles, or more lorry?)
- *deep:* Diamond and pencil lead are both pure carbon. So what makes a thing what it is: its atoms or their arrangement? (hint: The same bricks can build a wall or a well.)
- *big:* Every hydrogen atom in your body is about 13.8 billion years old. So how old are you? (hint: Is a new house old if its bricks are old?)
- *big:* Most elements are metals. Why might living bodies be built almost entirely from a few non-metals? (hint: Think about what carbon can do that iron cannot: join into long chains.)

## Story: The Black in the Lamp
*Nila, Appu, Ammamma and the baby are made up. These things are real. In Kerala, many families make kanmashi at home for a baby's twenty-eighth day. They burn a cotton wick in oil under a plate or pot and collect the soot. Soot is mostly carbon. Coconut shells are burnt to charcoal for heavy box irons. Doctors today advise families not to put kajal or kanmashi in a baby's eyes.*

On the day before the baby's twenty-eighth day, Ammamma lit the brass lamp in the middle of the afternoon, and that was how Nila knew it was a kanmashi day.

Kanmashi is the black that Ammamma made for every new baby in the family. She had made it for Nila nine years ago, and for Appu six years ago. Now there was a new cousin, asleep in the next room in a cloth cradle, and tomorrow she would get her name.

Nila and Appu sat on the cool red floor of the house in Thrissur and watched. Ammamma poured castor oil into the lamp. The oil was thick and pale yellow. She rolled a wick from clean white cotton and laid it in the oil. Then she stood three steel tumblers around the lamp and rested a small bronze plate on them, upside down, a hand's width above the wick.

"Now we wait," she said, and she lit the wick.

The flame was orange. The plate was the colour of dull gold. The oil was yellow and the wick was white. Appu counted the colours on his fingers. Then he lay on his stomach and looked up under the plate.

"Ammamma," he said, "something is making it black."

A soft black coat was growing on the bronze, exactly above the flame. It looked like velvet.

"That is the kanmashi," said Ammamma.

"But where is it coming from?" said Appu. "Nothing here is black."

Nila opened her mouth to answer and found that she did not know.

"I know," said Appu. "The fire is painting it. Fire paints things black. It painted my dosa this morning."

It was true that the edge of his dosa had burnt that morning. It was also true that he had refused to eat it.

"If fire paints things black," Nila said slowly, "then the lamp should be black."

They both looked at the lamp. It had stood in its own flame on every festival day for forty years. Around the wick it was brown with old oil, but under the oil it was brass, as yellow as ever.

"The lamp is strong," said Appu. "The fire cannot paint it."

"Salt is not strong," said Nila. She went to the kitchen and came back with a pinch of salt. She dropped a few grains on the hot rim of the lamp, beside the flame. They sat there. They did not turn brown. They did not turn black. They stayed as white as they had been in the jar.

Appu frowned. He did not like it when his ideas lost.

Nila was not looking at him. She was looking at the tip of the wick, where the white cotton went into the flame. The tip was black. She thought of the dosa edge. She thought of the coconut shells that the ironing woman at the corner burnt to charcoal for her heavy iron box. She thought of the time Amma forgot the payasam, and the sugar at the bottom of the pot turned into a black crust that took two days to scrub away.

"Dosa," she said. "Cotton. Coconut shell. Sugar. They all go black. Salt does not, and brass does not."

"So?" said Appu.

"So the fire is not putting the black in. The black must be inside them already, and the fire lets it out." She turned to her grandmother. "But the oil is yellow, Ammamma, and the wick is white. How can there be black inside?"

Ammamma did not answer at once. She turned the plate a little, so that a clean part of the bronze sat above the flame.

"Tell me where the cotton came from," she said.

"From a cotton plant."

"And the castor oil?"

"From castor seeds. From a plant."

"And the dosa, and the coconut shell, and the sugar?"

"Rice. A coconut tree. Sugarcane." Nila stopped. "They all grew. Every one of them grew."

"And the salt?"

"Salt comes from the sea. It never grew. And the lamp never grew."

Ammamma nodded. "Everything that grows is built out of the same black. A cotton plant builds white fluff from that black. A castor plant builds yellow oil. You cannot see the black, because it is joined up with other things. When the fire breaks them apart, the black is left over."

Appu had gone very quiet. He was looking through the doorway at the cradle, which was swinging a little.

"The baby is growing," he said.

"Yes," said Ammamma.

"So the black is in the baby." He looked at his own arm, and then at Nila. "And in me. And in her."

"In all three of you," said Ammamma. "And in me. A great deal of it."

"But I am brown," said Appu. "I am not black like the plate."

"Sugar is white," said Nila, "and you saw what was inside the sugar."

When the oil had burnt down, Ammamma let the plate cool. She scraped the soft black into a tiny silver box with the back of a spoon and worked one drop of ghee into it.

In the morning the house was full of aunts. The doctor had said nothing must go into the baby's eyes, so Ammamma put one small dot on the baby's cheek. Appu stood on a stool to see.

"Last year that dot was a cotton plant in a field," he told the nearest aunt. "It was inside the white, hiding."

The baby slept through everything, growing.

**For the parent:** Appu thought that fire paints things black. Nila saw that only things that once grew turn black, and salt and brass do not. The black is carbon. It is inside the white cotton and the yellow oil all the time, joined with other elements. Burning leaves it behind.

- Ask: Salt and brass sat next to the flame and did not turn black. What does that tell you about where the black comes from?
- Ask: White cotton and yellow oil both left black soot. How can a black element be inside a white thing?
- Ask: Name three things in your home that would leave black if they burnt, and one that would not.

## Games
### g1 · Three of a Kind: an element set game (20 min)

### e1 · The Black Test: which kitchen things hold carbon? (30 min)
- **question:** When eight kitchen things are heated on a tawa, which ones turn black? What do the ones that turn black have in common?
- **predict:** Before the heating, guess for each of the eight things: will it turn black, or will it stay as it is? Sugar and salt look alike. Will they do the same thing? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use a small amount of each thing, no bigger than a 1 rupee coin. Put all eight on one sheet of foil on one tawa. Then they all get the same heat for the same time. Heat for 10 minutes on a medium flame. Only one thing differs from heap to heap: what the heap is made of.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the heating. The child stays an arm's length from the stove. Open a window or switch on the kitchen fan, because the sugar smokes. Salt grains may crackle and jump. Let the foil cool for 10 minutes before anyone touches it.
- **record:** Draw a table with eight rows, one for each thing. Write your guess, what it looked like after 10 minutes, and whether it came from a living thing.
- **results table:** The thing | My guess | After 10 minutes | Did it come from a living thing?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the eight things on a plate. Ask for a guess and a reason for each. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up will heat these eight things for 10 minutes. Which will turn black, and which will stay the same? Sugar and salt look alike. Will they do the same thing? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the eight things. Which will turn black? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think sugar and salt will, or will not, behave the same? (good: They look the same, so they will do the same.; Sugar melts in a pan, so it may burn.; Salt is used in hot cooking and never burns.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children say sugar and salt will do the same thing.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover the tawa with foil. Mark eight places with a pencil, about 5 centimetres apart. Put a small amount of each thing in its place.  
  *Parent says:* Each heap must be small, no bigger than a 1 rupee coin. Why should all eight go on the same tawa?  
  *Child mode:* Put eight small heaps on the foil. Keep them well apart.
  - Note: The same tawa gives every heap the same heat for the same time. Put the sugar near the edge, as it spreads when it melts.
- **s3 (11 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Open a window. Heat the tawa on a medium flame for 10 minutes. Your child watches from an arm's length and says what changes. Then switch off the flame.  
  *Parent says:* Stay where you are and tell me what you see. Which heap changed first? What is the salt doing?  
  *Child mode:* Stand back and watch. Which heap changes first? What does the salt do?
  - Note: Sugar melts in a minute or two, bubbles, turns brown and then black. Bread, paper and thread darken next. Press the thread and paper flat with a spoon if they curl. Rice and dal are slowest and may only reach dark brown. If so, heat them for 5 minutes more. Salt may crackle.
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Wait 10 minutes for the foil to cool. Then your child fills in the table. They rub a black crumb of sugar or bread on white paper.  
  *Parent says:* Now it is cool. Write what each heap looks like. Rub a black crumb on the white paper. What does the mark remind you of?  
  *Child mode:* Write what each heap looks like. Rub a black crumb on paper.
  - Note: The black crumb leaves a grey-black mark, like a pencil or charcoal. All three are carbon.
  - Check: In the Black Test, which heap stayed white on the hot tawa? → **Salt**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. Read them the note below only after they answer.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Look at the last column. What do the black ones share?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: What do all the things that turned black have in common? (good: They all came from plants.; They all came from living things.; They all have carbon in them.)
  - Ask: Sugar is white. Where was the black before the heating? (good: Inside the sugar, joined to other elements.; It was hidden, and the heat left it behind.)
  - Note: The reason: everything that came from a living thing is made of compounds built around carbon. Heat breaks the compounds. Hydrogen and oxygen leave as water vapour and other gases, and black carbon stays. Salt and steel hold no carbon. Be honest about one limit. Chalk and cooking soda hold carbon joined tightly to oxygen, and they stay white. So black shows carbon, but white does not prove there is none.

### e2 · Shine, Bend, Ring: which metal tests does pencil lead pass? (25 min)
- **question:** Pencil lead is carbon, a non-metal. If it goes through three tests for a metal, how many will it pass? How many will a burnt matchstick pass, which is also carbon?
- **predict:** Before you test, guess. Will pencil lead pass none, one, two or all three of the metal tests? Will the burnt matchstick do the same as the pencil lead? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Do each test in the same way for every thing. Rub each thing five times with the same scrub pad. Bend each one with the same two fingers. Drop each one from the same height, one hand-width, onto the same upturned steel plate. Only the thing being tested changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up strips the wire, cuts the wood from the pencil lead, and burns and cools the matchstick. Wash your hands after handling the pencil lead and the burnt matchstick.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for each thing and a column for each test. Write yes or no for shine and ring. Write 'bends' or 'snaps' for the bend test.
- **results table:** The thing | Shines when rubbed? | Bends or snaps? | Rings when dropped?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the things on newspaper. Name the element in each: iron in steel, aluminium, copper, and carbon in the last two. Ask for the guess and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Pencil lead and this burnt matchstick are both carbon, a non-metal. We will give everything three metal tests. How many will the pencil lead pass? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Pencil lead is carbon. How many metal tests will it pass? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the pencil lead will pass that many tests? (good: It is a non-metal, so it should pass none.; It looks a bit shiny, so it may pass one.; It is hard like metal, so it may ring.)
  - Note: Steel is mostly iron. Pencil lead is graphite, a form of carbon, mixed with a little clay. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Shine test. Your child rubs each thing five times with the dry scrub pad, then holds it under the light. They write yes or no.  
  *Parent says:* Rub five times, then hold it under the light. Does the rubbed part throw the light back? Look closely at the pencil lead.  
  *Child mode:* Rub each thing five times. Hold it under the light. Does it shine?
  - Note: Metals show a bright fresh surface. Pencil lead shows a grey shine too. The burnt matchstick stays dull black.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Bend test. Your child bends each thing gently between two fingers, over the newspaper. They write 'bends' or 'snaps'.  
  *Parent says:* Bend each one gently. A metal bends and stays whole. What does the pencil lead do? What does the matchstick do?  
  *Child mode:* Bend each thing gently. Does it bend, or does it snap?
  - Note: The spoon and coin are too stiff to bend by hand. Write 'too stiff' for them. Nothing metal snaps.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ring test. Turn a steel plate upside down on the floor. Your child drops each thing onto it from one hand-width and listens. They write yes or no.  
  *Parent says:* Drop each one from the same height. Listen for a ring that lasts. Which ones only tick or thud?  
  *Child mode:* Drop each thing on the plate. Does it ring, or only tick?
  - Note: The foil pad hardly sounds, because it is thin, light and crumpled. Tap an aluminium vessel with a spoon to hear that aluminium does ring.
  - Check: In Shine, Bend, Ring, why is every thing dropped from the same height? → **So only the thing itself changes**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. Read them the note below only after they answer.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. How many tests did the pencil lead pass? Did the matchstick do the same?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Pencil lead and the burnt matchstick are both carbon. Why might they behave differently? (good: The atoms are arranged in different ways.; Pencil lead is in neat sheets and the burnt wood is jumbled.)
  - Ask: Is one test enough to decide that something is a metal? (good: No. Pencil lead shines but it is not a metal.; No. You need several tests that agree.)
  - Note: The reason: pencil lead passes the shine test and fails the other two. In graphite, carbon atoms sit in flat sheets. Some electrons are free to move along the sheets, as in a metal. So graphite shines and also carries electricity. The sheets slide apart easily, so it snaps and cannot ring. In burnt wood the carbon atoms are jumbled, so it is dull. Chemists call carbon a non-metal because of all its properties together.
  - Think: Diamond is also pure carbon. It is clear and very hard. What might be different about its atoms?

## Worksheets
### Elements, metals and your body (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is an element?  
   Options: A substance with one kind of atom / Any substance that is a solid / A mixture of many metals / The smallest living thing  
   **Answer:** A substance with one kind of atom. *Why:* An element has only one kind of atom. Gold has only gold atoms. A mixture or a compound has more than one kind.
2. (fill) Scientists know ____ elements.  
   **Answer:** ['118']. *Why:* The elements are numbered 1 to 118. About 90 are found in nature, and people made the rest in laboratories.
3. (truefalse) Sugar is an element.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Sugar is a compound of three elements: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Heat can break it down, and an element cannot be broken down.
4. (match) Match each symbol to its element.  
   **Answer:** O → Oxygen; C → Carbon; Fe → Iron; Na → Sodium. *Why:* O and C are the first letters of the English names. Fe comes from ferrum and Na from natrium, the Latin names.
5. (sort) Sort these elements: metal or non-metal?  
   **Answer:** iron → Metal; copper → Metal; aluminium → Metal; oxygen → Non-metal; carbon → Non-metal; nitrogen → Non-metal. *Why:* Iron, copper and aluminium shine, bend and carry electricity. Oxygen and nitrogen are gases, and carbon is a brittle solid.
6. (mcq) Which element makes up most of your body's weight?  
   Options: Oxygen / Carbon / Calcium / Iron  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* Oxygen is 65 parts in every 100 of your weight, because your body is mostly water. Calcium is about 2 parts, and iron is far less than 1.
7. (order) Put these body elements in order, from the most weight to the least.  
   **Answer:** ['Oxygen', 'Carbon', 'Hydrogen', 'Nitrogen']. *Why:* In every 100 parts of weight, oxygen is 65, carbon is 18, hydrogen is 10 and nitrogen is 3.
8. (write) Sugar turns black on a hot tawa. Salt stays white. Explain why in two sentences.  
   **Answer:** Sugar is a compound that holds carbon. Heat drives off the hydrogen and oxygen as water vapour, and black carbon is left. Salt holds no carbon, so nothing black is left.. *Why:* A strong answer says the carbon was inside the sugar before the heating. It also says that salt has no carbon.
9. (odd) Three of these are properties of most metals. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Shines when clean / Rings when struck / Bends without breaking / Crumbles when hit  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Metals shine, ring and bend. Crumbling when hit is being brittle, which is a property of solid non-metals such as charcoal.
10. (draw) Draw what a zoom into a grain of sugar might show. Use a different colour for each element.  
   **Answer:** Many small groups of joined balls in three colours, one each for carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. The groups all look alike.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows three kinds of atom joined together, and not three separate heaps. It has a key for the colours.
11. (perspective) What are you mostly made of?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. Oxygen is right by weight and hydrogen is right by number of atoms. The chemist is using a third meaning of 'mostly': what the body's compounds are built around.. *Why:* Each view is true for one meaning of 'mostly'. A strong answer says which meaning it is using, and uses a number.
12. (count) A body hundred square has 65 oxygen squares and 18 carbon squares. How many squares are left?  
   **Answer:** 17. *Why:* 65 and 18 make 83. 100 take away 83 leaves 17. Those 17 squares are hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium, phosphorus and the rest.
13. (truefalse) Every metal is a solid at room temperature.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Mercury is a metal, and it is a liquid at room temperature.
14. (label) Label the four largest bands on this body hundred square: oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen.  
   **Answer:** ['Oxygen: the band of 65 squares', 'Carbon: the band of 18 squares', 'Hydrogen: the band of 10 squares', 'Nitrogen: the band of 3 squares']. *Why:* The bands go in order of weight. Oxygen is by far the largest because the body is mostly water.
15. (fill) The black left when food burns is mostly the element ____.  
   **Answer:** ['carbon']. *Why:* Food is made of compounds built around carbon. Strong heat drives off the other elements and leaves black carbon.
### Testing, counting and sorting elements (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Pencil lead shines when rubbed but snaps when bent. What does this show?  
   Options: One test is not enough to decide / Pencil lead is a metal / The bend test was done wrongly / Shine is not a real property  
   **Answer:** One test is not enough to decide. *Why:* Pencil lead passes one metal test and fails others. Chemists sort an element by all its properties together.
2. (write) Chalk stays white on a hot tawa. Does that prove chalk holds no carbon? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No. The Black Test shows carbon only when heat leaves it behind as a black solid. Chalk holds carbon joined tightly to oxygen, and a tawa is not hot enough to break that.. *Why:* A strong answer sees the limit of the test. Black shows that carbon is there. White does not show that carbon is absent.
3. (sort) Sort these elements by their state at room temperature.  
   **Answer:** iron → Solid; carbon → Solid; mercury → Liquid; bromine → Liquid; oxygen → Gas; helium → Gas. *Why:* Most elements are solids. Only mercury and bromine are liquids. Eleven elements, such as oxygen and helium, are gases.
4. (match) Match each element to its job in your body.  
   **Answer:** Calcium → Makes bones hard; Iron → Carries oxygen in blood; Iodine → Needed by a gland in the neck; Sodium → Helps nerves carry signals. *Why:* Each of these is a small part of the body's weight, but the body cannot work without it.
5. (truefalse) By number of atoms, hydrogen is the most common element in your body.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. About 62 of every 100 atoms in you are hydrogen. Hydrogen atoms are so light that they are only 10 parts in 100 of your weight.
6. (count) One particle of water has 2 hydrogen atoms. How many hydrogen atoms are in 5 particles of water?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* 2 hydrogen atoms in each particle, times 5 particles, is 10 hydrogen atoms. There are also 5 oxygen atoms.
7. (fill) Fe is the symbol for ____.  
   **Answer:** ['iron']. *Why:* Fe comes from ferrum, the Latin name for iron.
8. (order) Put these elements in order of atomic number, smallest first.  
   **Answer:** ['Hydrogen', 'Carbon', 'Oxygen', 'Iron', 'Gold']. *Why:* Hydrogen is 1, carbon is 6, oxygen is 8, iron is 26 and gold is 79.
9. (perspective) Is pencil lead a metal?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. Chemists call carbon a non-metal because of all its properties together. The physicist's view explains why graphite still shines and carries electricity.. *Why:* A strong answer uses at least one test result. It also sees that the answer depends on what 'metal' is taken to mean.
10. (mcq) Salt holds the metal sodium. Why does salt not look like a metal?  
   Options: Its sodium is joined to chlorine / Its sodium has been used up / Sodium is not a real metal / Salt grains are too small  
   **Answer:** Its sodium is joined to chlorine. *Why:* In a compound the atoms are joined, and the compound has its own properties. The sodium is still there, and it can be taken out again.
11. (draw) Draw a strip of 10 squares to stand for the first 108 elements. Colour the share that are metals.  
   **Answer:** About 8 of the 10 squares are coloured. 85 of the first 108 elements are metals, which is close to 8 in every 10.. *Why:* A strong drawing colours 8 squares and says how the number was reached.
12. (odd) Three of these are gases at room temperature. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Nitrogen / Helium / Sulfur / Oxygen  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Nitrogen, helium and oxygen are gases. Sulfur is a yellow, brittle solid.
13. (write) Your blood holds iron. Explain why a magnet does not pull on your body.  
   **Answer:** A grown-up's body holds only 3 to 4 grams of iron, spread through all the blood. Each iron atom is joined into a compound. That compound is not magnetic the way a piece of iron is.. *Why:* A strong answer gives both reasons: the amount is tiny, and the iron is joined in a compound that behaves differently from iron metal.
14. (mcq) Where were the carbon atoms in your body first made?  
   Options: Inside stars / Inside the Earth / In green leaves / In the sea  
   **Answer:** Inside stars. *Why:* Stars build carbon from helium. Leaves take carbon from the air and join it into sugar, but they do not make the atoms.
15. (label) Label each thing with the element it is mostly made of: kitchen foil, electric wire, pencil lead, a floating balloon.  
   **Answer:** ['Kitchen foil: aluminium', 'Electric wire: copper', 'Pencil lead: carbon', 'A floating balloon: helium inside']. *Why:* Foil and wire are metals. Pencil lead is carbon, a non-metal solid. Helium is a non-metal gas lighter than air.
### Thinking like a chemist (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Design a test to find out whether a dry leaf holds carbon. Say what you would look for.  
   **Answer:** A grown-up heats a piece of the leaf on foil on a tawa. A pinch of salt goes next to it for comparison. If the leaf turns black and the salt stays white, the leaf holds carbon.. *Why:* A strong answer includes something to compare with, and says what result would mean yes.
2. (perspective) Were the old thinkers right that earth, water, air, fire and space are the elements?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. The chemist is right about what an element is today. The historian points out that the search for a short list began with the old thinkers.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the idea of a short list from the items on the list.
3. (mcq) An atom has 79 protons. What must it be?  
   Options: Gold / Any heavy metal / Silver or gold / It depends on its weight  
   **Answer:** Gold. *Why:* The number of protons decides the element. Every atom with 79 protons is gold, and no other element has 79.
4. (truefalse) A new element could be found with atomic number 6 and a half.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The atomic number counts protons, and protons come only in whole numbers. So there are no gaps between 6 and 7.
5. (count) One oxygen atom weighs as much as 16 hydrogen atoms. How many hydrogen atoms balance 3 oxygen atoms?  
   **Answer:** 48. *Why:* 16 hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom, times 3, is 48 hydrogen atoms.
6. (sort) Sort these substances: element or compound?  
   **Answer:** gold → Element; oxygen → Element; carbon → Element; water → Compound; salt → Compound; sugar → Compound. *Why:* Gold, oxygen and carbon each have one kind of atom. Water, salt and sugar are each two or three elements joined together.
7. (write) Most elements are metals, but your body is mostly non-metals. Suggest one reason why.  
   **Answer:** Carbon atoms can join into long chains and rings, and metals cannot. With hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, these chains make water, fat, protein and sugar.. *Why:* Nobody knows the full answer, so any reasoned idea counts. A strong answer names something non-metals can do that metals cannot.
8. (order) Put these in the order they were made, earliest first.  
   **Answer:** ['Hydrogen atoms', 'Carbon atoms, inside stars', 'The Earth', 'Your body']. *Why:* Hydrogen formed soon after the universe began. Stars made carbon later. The Earth formed from matter that old stars scattered, and your body is built from the Earth's matter.
9. (mcq) Deep inside Jupiter, hydrogen behaves as a metal. What is different there?  
   Options: How hard the atoms are squeezed / The kind of atom / The atomic number / The symbol  
   **Answer:** How hard the atoms are squeezed. *Why:* The atoms are still hydrogen, with 1 proton each. The enormous pressure frees their electrons to move, which is what makes a metal.
10. (match) Match each fact to the property of metals that it shows.  
   **Answer:** Electric wires are copper → Carries electricity; Silver is beaten into leaf for sweets → Malleable; Temple bells are metal → Rings when struck; A scrubbed steel plate gleams → Lustre. *Why:* People choose a metal for a job because of its properties. All four properties come from electrons that are free to move and atoms that can slide.
11. (fill) An element with some properties of a metal and some of a non-metal is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['metalloid']. *Why:* Silicon is a metalloid. It shines like a metal, shatters like glass and carries electricity a little.
12. (draw) Draw two bars for the hydrogen in your body: its share of the weight, and its share of the atoms.  
   **Answer:** The weight bar reaches 10 out of 100. The atoms bar reaches 62 out of 100.. *Why:* A strong drawing uses the same scale for both bars and labels each one.
13. (odd) Three of these turn black when heated on a tawa. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Rice / Cotton / Paper / Salt  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Rice, cotton and paper came from plants and hold carbon. Salt holds no carbon and stays white.
14. (write) Element 118 lasts less than a thousandth of a second. Should it count as an element? Give a reason.  
   **Answer:** Yes is the answer chemists give: its atoms have 118 protons, and that is what makes an element. A thoughtful no might say that nothing can be built from it or tested with it.. *Why:* A strong answer uses what an element is: one kind of atom, fixed by its number of protons.
15. (label) These five squares are numbered 1, 6, 8, 20 and 26. Write each element's symbol in its square.  
   **Answer:** ['1: H, hydrogen', '6: C, carbon', '8: O, oxygen', '20: Ca, calcium', '26: Fe, iron']. *Why:* All five are in your body. Fe is the only one of these whose symbol comes from a Latin name.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is an element?  
   Options: Gold / Water / Salt / Sugar  
   **Answer:** Gold. *Why:* Gold has only one kind of atom. Water, salt and sugar are compounds of two or three elements.
2. (mcq) How many elements are known today?  
   Options: 118 / 4 / 92 / 1,000  
   **Answer:** 118. *Why:* 118 elements are known. Four was the old list of earth, water, air and fire. 92 is the number of uranium, the heaviest common element in nature.
3. (mcq) What does the symbol K stand for?  
   Options: Potassium / Calcium / Carbon / Copper  
   **Answer:** Potassium. *Why:* K is potassium, from its Latin name kalium. Calcium is Ca, carbon is C and copper is Cu.
4. (mcq) Which property do most metals share?  
   Options: They carry electricity well / They are gases / They crumble when hit / They all stick to magnets  
   **Answer:** They carry electricity well. *Why:* Metals carry electricity because some of their electrons move freely. Only a few metals, such as iron, stick to magnets.
5. (mcq) Which of these is a non-metal?  
   Options: Nitrogen / Sodium / Calcium / Zinc  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen. *Why:* Nitrogen is a non-metal gas. Sodium and calcium are metals, even though we never see them as metal in food.
6. (mcq) Most of the 118 elements are which kind?  
   Options: Metals / Non-metals / Gases / Metalloids  
   **Answer:** Metals. *Why:* 85 of the first 108 elements are metals. Only 17 are non-metals, and 11 of those are gases.
7. (mcq) Graphite is carbon, a non-metal, yet it carries electricity. What does this show?  
   Options: Some elements do not fit one side neatly / Carbon is really a metal / Electricity is a useless test / Graphite is not an element  
   **Answer:** Some elements do not fit one side neatly. *Why:* Graphite has one property of metals and lacks the others. The two groups are useful, but a few elements sit between them.
8. (mcq) Which four elements make up 96 parts in every 100 of your weight?  
   Options: Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen / Calcium, iron, sodium, potassium / Carbon, calcium, iron, oxygen / Hydrogen, helium, oxygen, iron  
   **Answer:** Oxygen, carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen. *Why:* Oxygen is 65, carbon 18, hydrogen 10 and nitrogen 3. All four are non-metals. Calcium and iron matter, but they weigh little.
9. (mcq) Why is oxygen such a large part of your weight?  
   Options: Your body is mostly water / You breathe it in all day / Bones are made of oxygen / Oxygen atoms are the lightest  
   **Answer:** Your body is mostly water. *Why:* Water is most of your body, and oxygen is eight ninths of the weight of water. The oxygen you breathe in is a tiny amount at any moment.
10. (mcq) Which element has the most atoms in your body?  
   Options: Hydrogen / Oxygen / Carbon / Calcium  
   **Answer:** Hydrogen. *Why:* About 62 of every 100 atoms in you are hydrogen. Oxygen wins by weight, because each oxygen atom is 16 times as heavy.
11. (mcq) Bread turns black when it burns. What is the black?  
   Options: Carbon left behind from the bread / Paint from the flame / Dirt from the tawa / Iron from the bread  
   **Answer:** Carbon left behind from the bread. *Why:* Bread is made of carbon compounds. Heat drives off the other elements and leaves carbon. Salt on the same tawa stays white, so the flame adds no black.
12. (truefalse) The elements in a living body are different from the elements in rocks and air.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every element in a body is also found in rocks, air or sea. A body differs in how its elements are joined together.

## Applications
- Electric wires are made of copper or aluminium, because these metals carry electricity well and can be pulled into wire.
- A little iodine is added to salt, because the body needs iodine and many foods hold too little.
- Doctors measure the sodium, potassium, calcium and iron in a drop of blood to see how a body is working.
- Recycling centres lift iron and steel out of mixed waste with magnets, and push aluminium out with a spinning magnet.
- Computer chips are made of the metalloid silicon, because it carries electricity only a little and can be controlled.
- Astronomers find which elements a star holds by studying the colours missing from its light.

## Spotted around us
- The letters NPK on a bag of fertiliser at a plant nursery. They stand for nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
- The word 'iodised' on a packet of salt in the kitchen.
- Silver leaf, called varak, on a piece of kaju katli. Silver is so malleable that it can be beaten thinner than paper.
- Bright copper strands where an electrician has stripped a cable.
- The mark 916 inside a gold bangle. It means 916 parts in every 1,000 are gold, and the rest is other metals.
- Black charcoal glowing in a heavy box iron at an ironing stall, or in a tandoor. Charcoal is mostly carbon.

## Open questions
- **Is there a last element?** Laboratories are trying to make elements 119 and 120. Each new element is harder to make and breaks apart sooner. Nobody knows where the list must stop.
- **Could some very heavy elements last a long time?** Some physicists predict an 'island' of heavy atoms that last for minutes or years. Nobody has reached it yet, so the idea is untested.
- **Does life have to be built around carbon?** All life on the Earth uses carbon. Silicon can also join to four atoms, but its chains are weaker. With only one example of life, nobody can be sure.
- **Exactly which elements does a human body need?** About 25 are agreed. For a few, such as chromium and boron, studies disagree, because the amounts are too small to test easily.
- **Where was most of the universe's gold made?** Colliding dead stars make gold, as astronomers saw in 2017. Whether such collisions are common enough to explain all the gold is still argued.

## Connected maths
- In the Body Hundred Square, oxygen is 65 parts in every 100 of your weight and carbon is 18. A child weighs 30 kilograms. About how many kilograms of the child are oxygen, and how many are carbon? **About 19 and a half kilograms of oxygen and about 5 and a half kilograms of carbon.**. 65 parts in 100 of 30 kilograms is 30 times 65, divided by 100. That is 1,950 divided by 100, which is 19.5 kilograms. For carbon, 30 times 18 is 540, and 540 divided by 100 is 5.4 kilograms. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A particle of water has 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. A hydrogen atom weighs 1 unit and an oxygen atom weighs 16 units. What fraction of the atoms is hydrogen? What fraction of the weight is hydrogen? **Hydrogen is two thirds of the atoms, and one ninth of the weight.**. There are 3 atoms and 2 are hydrogen, so two thirds. The weights are 1 + 1 + 16 = 18 units. Hydrogen is 2 of the 18 units. Divide both by 2 to get 1 ninth. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In 100 atoms of your body, 62 are hydrogen, 24 are oxygen, 12 are carbon and 1 is nitrogen. They weigh 1, 16, 12 and 14 units each. Work out the weight of each group. Which group is heaviest, and is this what your first hundred square showed? **Hydrogen 62 units, oxygen 384, carbon 144, nitrogen 14. Oxygen is heaviest, as in the first square.**. 62 times 1 is 62. 24 times 16 is 384. 12 times 12 is 144. 1 times 14 is 14. The total is 604 units. Oxygen is 384 of 604, which is a little under two thirds. The first square gave oxygen 65 squares in 100, which is also about two thirds. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In the 118 Grid, 85 of the first 108 elements are metals. About how many in every 10 is that? If 20 elements were picked without looking, about how many would be metals? **About 8 in every 10. About 16 of the 20.**. 85 out of 108 is close to 80 out of 100, which is 8 out of 10. For 20 elements, double it: about 16. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In the Black Test, sugar, rice, dal, bread, cotton and paper turned black. Salt and a steel pin did not. Which of these three would you expect to turn black: a dry leaf, a glass bead, a groundnut? Give the rule you used. **The dry leaf and the groundnut. The glass bead would not.**. All six things that turned black came from living things, and both things that did not turn black never lived. The rule is: things made by living beings hold carbon that heat leaves behind. A leaf and a groundnut came from plants. Glass is made from sand. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In the Drying Potato, fresh slices balanced 20 coins and the dry slices balanced 4 coins. How many coins of weight were water? What fraction of the potato was water? **16 coins of water. Four fifths of the potato.**. 20 take away 4 is 16 coins. 16 out of 20 is the fraction. Divide both by 4 to get 4 out of 5. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
