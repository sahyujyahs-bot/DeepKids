# Seasons: why a leaning Earth has summer and winter at the same moment
`sci.day-night-seasons.seasons.L5`

**Summary.** The Earth is nearest to the Sun in early January, when Delhi is at its coldest. So what really makes the seasons? In this lesson you will test lean against distance with a torch, carry a leaning ball round a lamp for one whole year, and compare the day lengths of four Indian cities. You will also find out why Pongal comes three weeks after the shortest day, and why different peoples count two, four, six or twenty-four seasons.

**Intro.** *On about 3 January the Earth is 5 million kilometres nearer to the Sun than on about 4 July. Yet January is the coldest month in Delhi and Srinagar. And on that same January day, children in Sydney are swimming in the sea in full summer. How can one Earth, under one Sun, be in winter and in summer at the same moment?* Many grown-ups say that summer comes when the Earth is close to the Sun. It is a sensible guess, because a fire feels hotter when you step nearer. In this lesson you test that guess yourself. You measure how much a patch of torchlight changes when the torch moves away, and how much it changes when the ground leans. Then you carry a leaning ball round a lamp and count the hours of daylight. The answer you find explains the solstices, the monsoon, zero shadow day in Chennai and the date of Pongal.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can tell rotation from revolution, and say how long each takes and what each gives us.
1. By the end I can use my torch results to show that the slant of sunlight changes the heating far more than the Earth's distance from the Sun does.
2. By the end I can use a leaning ball to explain why the two halves of the Earth have opposite seasons, and why the axis must keep pointing the same way.
3. By the end I can name the two solstices and the two equinoxes, give their dates, and say where the noon Sun is overhead on each.
4. By the end I can explain why day length changes little in Kanyakumari, more in Delhi and most near the poles.
5. By the end I can explain how the seasons drive the monsoon winds, and why the hottest days come after the longest day.
6. By the end I can compare different ways of counting seasons, in India and elsewhere, and say what each count is watching.
7. By the end I can explain why Pongal and Makar Sankranti now come about three weeks after the December solstice.

## Terms
- **Rotation**: The spinning of the Earth round its own axis. One rotation takes about 24 hours and gives us day and night. *e.g.* A spinning pambaram, the wooden top, rotates round its nail.
- **Revolution**: The journey of the Earth round the Sun. One revolution takes one year, about 365 days and 6 hours. *e.g.* A child running once round a lamp post has made one revolution round it.
- **Orbit**: The path that one body follows round another. The Earth's orbit round the Sun is almost a circle. *e.g.* The Moon has an orbit round the Earth, and the Earth has an orbit round the Sun.
- **Axis**: The line through the North Pole and the South Pole that the Earth spins round. Nobody can see it. It is a line we imagine. *e.g.* The nail of a spinning top lies along the top's axis.
- **Tilt**: The lean of the Earth's axis. The axis leans about 23 and a half degrees away from standing straight up on the orbit. *e.g.* A ball leaning about a quarter of the way from standing up to lying flat has roughly the Earth's tilt.
- **Ellipse**: A stretched circle, like the top of a tumbler seen from the side. The Earth's orbit is an ellipse that is stretched very little. *e.g.* A round torch patch becomes an ellipse when the wall leans.
- **Perihelion**: The point on the orbit where the Earth is nearest to the Sun. The Earth is there in early January. *e.g.* At perihelion the Sun is about 147 million kilometres away.
- **Aphelion**: The point on the orbit where the Earth is farthest from the Sun. The Earth is there in early July. *e.g.* At aphelion the Sun is about 152 million kilometres away.
- **Hemisphere**: Half of the Earth. The equator divides the Earth into the Northern Hemisphere and the Southern Hemisphere. *e.g.* India is in the Northern Hemisphere. Australia is in the Southern Hemisphere.
- **Solstice**: One of the two days in the year when the noon Sun is as far north or as far south as it ever goes. One is about 21 June and the other about 21 December. *e.g.* About 21 June is the longest day of the year everywhere in India.
- **Equinox**: One of the two days in the year when the noon Sun is overhead at the equator. Day and night are then nearly equal everywhere. They fall about 20 March and 23 September. *e.g.* On the March equinox, Chennai and Srinagar both get about 12 hours of daylight.
- **Tropic of Cancer**: The line round the Earth, 23 and a half degrees north of the equator, where the noon Sun is overhead on the June solstice. *e.g.* The Tropic of Cancer crosses eight Indian states, from Gujarat to Mizoram.
- **Tropic of Capricorn**: The line round the Earth, 23 and a half degrees south of the equator, where the noon Sun is overhead on the December solstice. *e.g.* The Tropic of Capricorn crosses Australia, southern Africa and South America.
- **Arctic Circle**: The line round the Earth, about 66 and a half degrees north. North of it the Sun stays up all night on at least one day in June, and stays down all day on at least one day in December. *e.g.* In Tromso in Norway, the Sun does not set for about two months in summer.
- **Zero shadow day**: A day when the noon Sun is exactly overhead, so an upright pole has no shadow at noon. It happens twice a year, and only in places between the two tropics. *e.g.* Chennai and Bengaluru have zero shadow days about 24 April and 18 August.
- **Uttarayana and Dakshinayana**: The two halves of the Sun's yearly swing. In Uttarayana the noon Sun moves north, from the December solstice to the June solstice. In Dakshinayana it moves south again. *e.g.* During Uttarayana the days in India grow longer week by week.
- **Ritu**: A season in the old Indian count, which divides the year into six ritus of about two months each. *e.g.* Varsha ritu is the season of the rains.
- **Monsoon**: A wind that blows from one direction for part of the year and from the opposite direction for another part, bringing a wet season and a dry season. *e.g.* The south-west monsoon reaches Kerala about 1 June. The north-east monsoon brings Chennai's rain in October to December.
- **Precession**: The very slow wobble of the Earth's axis. The axis traces one full circle in about 26,000 years, the way a leaning top slowly circles. *e.g.* Because of precession, Makar Sankranti has slipped about three weeks away from the December solstice.
- **Leap year**: A year with one extra day, 29 February. It keeps the calendar in step with the seasons, because one revolution takes about a quarter of a day more than 365 days. *e.g.* 2024 and 2028 are leap years.

## Explanations
### Two motions at once: rotation and revolution · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth rotates on its axis once in about 24 hours, which gives day and night. At the same time it revolves round the Sun once in about 365 days and 6 hours, which gives the year.

The Earth does both things together, like a spinning top that is also sliding round a table. The spin carries you from day into night and back. The journey round the Sun is far longer. The orbit is about 940 million kilometres long, and the Earth covers about 30 kilometres of it every second. We feel nothing, because the ground, the air and our bodies all move together at a steady speed. The extra 6 hours each year add up to one whole day in four years. That is why a leap year gets a 29th of February.
- e.g. A day is one rotation. A year is one revolution.
- e.g. In the time you take to say 'Kanyakumari', the Earth has moved about 30 kilometres along its orbit.
- e.g. Your age in years is the number of times you have gone round the Sun.
- **Panchangam makers and ships' navigators:** They work as if the Earth stood still and the Sun travelled through the twelve rashis in a year. This picture matches what our eyes see, and it gives the right dates for sunrise, sankrantis and eclipses. For finding your way at sea it is the easier picture to use.
- **Aryabhata in 499 CE and Nicolaus Copernicus in 1543:** Aryabhata wrote that the Earth spins, and that the stars only seem to move, as the bank seems to move to a person in a boat. Copernicus added that the Earth also goes round the Sun. Later evidence supported this. In 1838 Friedrich Bessel measured a nearby star shifting back and forth each year, as it should if we are the ones moving.
- **Physicists since Isaac Newton and Albert Einstein:** The Earth and the Sun both go round their shared balance point, which lies deep inside the Sun because the Sun is so heavy. You may describe the motion from any place you like. The Sun-centred picture is chosen because it makes the rules of motion simplest.
### The orbit is almost a circle, and the Earth is nearest the Sun in January · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's orbit is an ellipse that is stretched so little that it looks like a circle. The Earth is nearest the Sun, at perihelion, in early January, and farthest, at aphelion, in early July.

In The True Shape of the Year you drew the orbit to scale. On a circle 20 centimetres wide, the Sun sits less than 2 millimetres from the centre. The nearest distance is about 147 million kilometres and the farthest about 152 million kilometres. That is a difference of about 3 parts in every 100. Books often draw the orbit as a long oval because they show it tilted, as if seen from the side. The drawing is honest about the view, but it leaves a wrong picture in the mind. The Earth also moves a little faster when it is nearer the Sun. So the half of the year from the September equinox to the March equinox is about 179 days long, and the other half is about 186 days.
- e.g. Early January: about 147 million kilometres from the Sun.
- e.g. Early July: about 152 million kilometres from the Sun.
- e.g. A steel plate seen from the side looks like a long oval, though it is round.
### Slant matters more than distance · *defined*
**Definition:** Sunlight that arrives at a slant is spread over more ground, so each patch of ground gets less of it. This slant changes through the year far more than the Earth's distance from the Sun does.

In Lean or Distance, moving the torch 1 centimetre further in 30 made the patch only a hair wider. Leaning the card as Delhi leans in December made the patch more than one and a half times as long. The same light was spread over much more card, so each square centimetre got much less. In Delhi the noon Sun is about 5 degrees from overhead in June and about 52 degrees from overhead in December. So each square metre of Delhi ground gets only about 6 parts in 10 of the noon sunlight in December that it gets in June. In Chennai the swing is smaller, from about 10 degrees to about 37 degrees, so Chennai's winter is mild. Summer also has longer days, so the ground is heated for more hours. The two effects, steeper light and longer days, work together.
- e.g. A dosa batter poured on a tilted tawa spreads into a long thin oval.
- e.g. Morning and evening sunlight is gentle because it comes at a low slant.
- e.g. Solar panels are tilted to face the Sun squarely.
- **Milutin Milankovitch, a Serbian scientist of the 1920s, and climate scientists after him:** Distance is small but not nothing. In early January the whole Earth gets about 7 parts in 100 more sunlight than in early July. This falls on the southern summer, where wide oceans soak it up. Over tens of thousands of years, slow changes in the orbit and the tilt help to start and end ice ages.
- **Scientists who study the air:** Slant does a second thing. Low sunlight passes through much more air before it reaches the ground. When the Sun is 30 degrees above the horizon, its light crosses twice as much air as when it is overhead. More of it is scattered and soaked up on the way.
### The axis leans, and keeps pointing the same way · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's axis leans about 23 and a half degrees from upright on its orbit. All year the axis keeps pointing to the same far place in space, close to the Pole Star.

In The Leaning Ball, the top dot of the ball pointed at the same wall at every place round the lamp. That is the key. In June the northern end of the axis leans towards the Sun. Six months later the Earth is on the other side of the Sun. The axis has not turned, so now the northern end leans away. A spinning body keeps its axis steady, as a fast top stays up and a slow top falls over. The northern end of the axis points almost at the Pole Star, called Dhruva in India. That is why Dhruva stays in one place in the sky while other stars circle round.
- e.g. A spinning top keeps its lean while it slides across the floor.
- e.g. The Pole Star is always in the north, at every hour and in every season.
- e.g. With the ball standing upright, every place on it got the same light all year.
- **Physicists who study spinning things:** A spinning body keeps the direction of its axis unless something twists it. Over one year the twist on the Earth is far too small to notice. So for a lifetime, the axis is as good as fixed.
- **Hipparchus, about 127 BCE, and astronomers of slow time:** The axis is not fixed for ever. The pull of the Moon and the Sun on the Earth's bulging middle makes the axis wobble in a slow circle, once in about 26,000 years. This is precession. In about 12,000 years the bright star Vega, called Abhijit, will be near the north point of the sky, and Dhruva will circle like any other star.
### Four marker days: two solstices and two equinoxes · *defined*
**Definition:** A solstice is a day when the noon Sun is as far north or south as it goes. An equinox is a day when the noon Sun is overhead at the equator. They fall about 21 June, 23 September, 21 December and 20 March.

About 21 June the noon Sun is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer, which crosses India from Gujarat to Mizoram. This is the longest day in India. About 21 December the noon Sun is overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, and India has its shortest day. On the two equinoxes the Sun is overhead at the equator, and the line between day and night passes through both poles. Between the tropics, the overhead Sun passes every place twice a year. Those are the zero shadow days. Chennai and Bengaluru have them about 24 April and 18 August, Mumbai about 15 May and 27 July, and Kanyakumari about 10 April and 1 September. Delhi, Lucknow and Srinagar lie north of the Tropic of Cancer, so the Sun is never overhead there. In June the noon Sun in Chennai is a little to the north, so noon shadows point south.
- e.g. 21 June: longest day in India, shortest day in Australia.
- e.g. The word solstice means 'the Sun stands still', because the noon Sun stops moving north or south for a few days.
- e.g. Nowruz, the new year of Iran and of the Parsis, is on the March equinox.
- **Astronomers:** Each season begins on a solstice or an equinox. By this count, northern summer starts about 21 June and winter about 21 December.
- **Weather offices in many countries:** Seasons should follow the weather and be easy to count. So summer is the three hottest whole months, and each season begins on the first day of a month.
- **The old Chinese and old Irish calendars:** The solstice is the middle of the season, not its start. The longest day is called midsummer. In the Chinese calendar summer begins about 5 May, when the light is growing strong, and ends in early August.
### Day length: a small change at Kanyakumari, a large one at Delhi, six months at the poles · *defined*
**Definition:** Because the axis leans, the share of each day that a place spends in sunlight changes through the year. The change is zero at the equator and grows towards the poles.

In Four Cities, Two Days, the day in Kanyakumari was less than one hour longer in June than in December. In Chennai the difference was about one and a half hours, in Delhi about three and a half, and in Srinagar about four and a half. In The Leaning Ball you saw why. A ring near the leaning pole has most of its circle in the light in summer. A ring near the equator is always cut nearly in half. North of the Arctic Circle the Sun does not set at all for part of the summer. At the North Pole there is one long day of about six months and one long night of about six months. At the equator, every day of the year is close to 12 hours long.
- e.g. Kanyakumari: about 12 hours 36 minutes on 21 June and 11 hours 39 minutes on 21 December.
- e.g. Delhi: about 13 hours 59 minutes on 21 June and 10 hours 20 minutes on 21 December.
- e.g. In Tromso in Norway the Sun stays up all night from about 20 May to 22 July.
- e.g. At the North Pole the Sun stays low in the sky, so its rays arrive at a slant. The ground stays cold, even on the long summer day.
- **A person who draws the Earth and the Sun with straight rays:** On an equinox, exactly half of every ring is lit. So day and night are exactly 12 hours each, everywhere.
- **A person who watches real sunrises:** The air bends sunlight, so we see the Sun a few minutes before it has truly cleared the horizon. The Sun is also a wide disc, not a point. So on the equinox the day is about 12 hours and 7 minutes long. Day and night are truly equal a few days before the March equinox.
### Opposite seasons in the two hemispheres, and a different year near the equator · *defined*
**Definition:** When the northern end of the axis leans towards the Sun, the Northern Hemisphere has summer and the Southern Hemisphere has winter. Six months later it is the other way round.

In The Leaning Ball the northern ring had the most lit dots at the very place where the southern ring had the fewest. So Christmas in Australia, Argentina and South Africa comes in high summer, and schools there take their long summer holiday in December and January. Near the equator the year has another shape. In Singapore, or at the southern tip of India, the Sun passes overhead twice a year and day length hardly changes. People there notice wet months and dry months more than hot and cold ones. Kerala and coastal Tamil Nadu stay warm all year, and their seasons are counted by the rains.
- e.g. When it is winter in Srinagar it is summer in Sydney.
- e.g. In June, penguins in Antarctica stand through weeks of night while Arctic terns in the far north have daylight all night.
- e.g. Kochi's hottest month and its coolest month differ by only about 3 degrees Celsius.
### The hottest days come after the longest day · *defined*
**Definition:** The ground, the sea and the air take weeks to warm up and weeks to cool down. So the hottest part of the year comes some weeks after the June solstice, and the coldest some weeks after the December solstice. This delay is called seasonal lag.

The same thing happens every day. The Sun is highest at noon, but the hottest hour is usually about 2 or 3 in the afternoon. The ground is still gaining more heat than it loses. Over the year, Delhi's shortest day is about 21 December, but its coldest weeks are in January. The sea warms and cools more slowly than the land, so places by the sea lag more. In much of the world the hottest month is July or August. India is different for a reason of its own. The monsoon clouds arrive in June and shade the land. So the hottest weeks in most of India are in May. In Tamil Nadu the hottest stretch of May is called Agni Nakshatram or Kathiri Veyil.
- e.g. A tawa stays hot for a while after the burner is turned off.
- e.g. The hottest hour of the day is in the afternoon, not at noon.
- e.g. Delhi is coldest in January, weeks after the shortest day.
### The seasons drive the monsoon · *debated*
**Definition:** A monsoon is a wind that changes direction with the seasons. In India the south-west monsoon blows from the sea from June to September, and the north-east monsoon blows from the land and the Bay of Bengal from October to December.

As the noon Sun moves north in April and May, the land of India heats strongly. Moist winds from the Indian Ocean then blow onto the land and bring rain. The south-west monsoon reaches Kerala about 1 June and covers the whole country by about 8 July. From mid-September it draws back. Then the winds turn. The north-east monsoon brings Tamil Nadu about half of its rain for the year. Everyone agrees that the tilt of the Earth sets this yearly rhythm. Scientists still argue about exactly how the heating makes the rain.
- e.g. Onam in Kerala comes in August or September, when the heaviest south-west rains are over and the first rice is harvested.
- e.g. Chennai gets most of its rain in October to December, when much of India is dry.
- e.g. The word monsoon comes from the Arabic word mausim, which means season.
- **Edmond Halley, an English astronomer, in 1686:** The monsoon is a giant sea breeze. Land heats faster than sea, so in summer the air over the hot land rises and moist sea air rushes in to take its place. In winter the land is cooler than the sea, and the wind turns round.
- **Sulochana Gadgil of the Indian Institute of Science, Bengaluru, with D. R. Sikka in 1980:** The monsoon is a belt of rain clouds round the Earth that follows the overhead Sun north in summer and south in winter. India's monsoon is that belt arriving over the land. This explains why the rain comes in bursts and breaks, and why the hottest land is often not the wettest.
- **William Boos and Zhiming Kuang in 2010:** The Himalaya matter most as a wall. The mountains stop cold, dry air from Central Asia mixing with the warm, moist air over India. In computer models the monsoon stays strong even when the high Tibetan Plateau is taken away, as long as the wall of mountains stays.
### How many seasons are there? · *debated*
**Definition:** A season is a part of the year that people mark off because the sky, the weather or living beings change. The Earth's orbit gives four marker days. How many seasons to count, and where each one starts, is a choice that different peoples have made in different ways.

The six ritus of the Sanskrit calendar are Vasanta, Grishma, Varsha, Sharad, Hemanta and Shishira. The old Tamil grammar, the Tolkappiyam, also counts six, with other names and starting points: Kaar, Koothir, Munpani, Pinpani, Ilavenil and Mudhuvenil. India's weather office counts four. Each count fits the land where it was made and the things its makers needed to know. None of them is the one true count.
- e.g. Varsha, the season of rains, has no partner in the four-season count of Europe.
- e.g. Munpani and Pinpani are the Tamil seasons of early dew and late dew.
- e.g. A child in Kochi and a child in Shimla may need different season calendars.
- **The Sanskrit and Tamil traditions of India:** Six seasons of two months each. The rains and the dew seasons get their own names, because farming depends on them. The Tamil count begins the cycle with Kaar, the rains.
- **The India Meteorological Department:** Four seasons, set by the winds and the rain: winter in January and February, the hot weather from March to May, the south-west monsoon from June to September, and the post-monsoon from October to December.
- **The Noongar people of south-western Australia:** Six seasons with no fixed dates: Birak, Bunuru, Djeran, Makuru, Djilba and Kambarang. A season begins when the land shows it, for example when certain flowers open or certain winds arrive.
- **The farmers of ancient Egypt:** Three seasons, set by the river Nile: Akhet when the river flooded, Peret when the crops grew, and Shemu when they were harvested.
- **The traditional Chinese calendar:** Twenty-four short seasons called solar terms, each about 15 days long, with names such as Awakening of Insects and Grain Rain. They are set by the Sun's place on its yearly path. Farmers used them to time each task.
### Festivals follow the Sun: Pongal, Sankranti, Vishu and Onam · *defined*
**Definition:** Many festivals mark a point in the Sun's year. Pongal and Makar Sankranti mark the Sun entering the star group Makara, about 14 January. Long ago this was also the day of the December solstice.

Pongal in Tamil Nadu, Makar Sankranti across India, Lohri in Punjab and Magh Bihu in Assam all come in mid-January. They thank the Sun as the days begin to lengthen, and they come when the winter harvest is in. But the days really begin to lengthen about 21 December. About 1,700 years ago the two dates matched. Since then the slow wobble of the axis, precession, has shifted the stars behind the Sun. Indian solar calendars follow the stars, so Sankranti moves about one day later every 70 years. Vishu and Puthandu, about 14 April, once marked the March equinox in the same way. Onam in Kerala follows the month of Chingam, in August or September, when the heavy rains ease and the first rice is cut.
- e.g. Pongal: about 14 January, 24 days after the December solstice.
- e.g. Vishu, Puthandu, Baisakhi and Poila Boishakh: about 14 or 15 April, 25 days after the March equinox.
- e.g. Dongzhi in China and Yalda in Iran are kept on the December solstice itself.
- **Most Indian panchangam makers:** The calendar should follow the stars, as it has for about 2,000 years. Makar Sankranti is the day the Sun enters Makara, wherever the solstice may be. The star-based year keeps festivals tied to the sky that our ancestors named.
- **Meghnad Saha and the Calendar Reform Committee, 1952 to 1955:** A calendar is for the seasons. The committee found that Indian solar calendars had slipped about 23 days from the seasons. It designed the Indian national calendar, used by the government since 22 March 1957. Its year begins just after the March equinox and stays with the seasons.
### Living beings read the year · *idea*
**Definition:** Plants, humans and other animals sense the changing length of the day and the changing weather, and many also carry an inner sense of the year. How these work together is still being studied.

Day length is the steadiest signal of the season, because it repeats exactly every year while heat and rain do not. Many old rice varieties of South India flower only when the days shorten after September, whatever date they were sown. Amur falcons gather in Nagaland in October and November. Then they fly across the Arabian Sea to southern Africa, where summer is beginning. Mango trees flower after the cool, dry weeks of winter. Humans change with the seasons too, in sleep, mood and the food our bodies ask for.
- e.g. Samba rice in Tamil Nadu is sown in August and flowers as the days shorten.
- e.g. Amur falcons spend the northern summer in China and Siberia and the southern summer in Africa, so they live in summer for most of the year.
- e.g. Bar-headed geese cross the Himalaya to winter in Indian lakes such as Koonthankulam and Chilika.
- **Eberhard Gwinner, a German scientist, from 1967:** Many birds carry an inner yearly clock. He kept warblers and stonechats for years in rooms where day length never changed. The birds still grew restless, put on fat and changed their feathers about once a year. Without the Sun their 'year' slowly drifted to about ten months.
- **Field scientists who study plants and insects:** Many beings follow the weather of the moment more than any calendar. Desert seeds wait for rain, however long it takes. Mango trees flower early after an early cool spell. For them a season is something that happens, and they answer when it comes.
### Other planets, other seasons · *defined*
**Definition:** Any planet with a leaning axis has seasons. The size of the lean and the shape of the orbit decide what the seasons are like.

Mars leans about 25 degrees, close to the Earth's lean. So Mars has four seasons like ours, each about twice as long, because a Mars year is 687 Earth days. Mars's orbit is far more stretched than the Earth's. At its nearest, Mars gets about 45 parts in 100 more sunlight than at its farthest. So on Mars distance really does matter, and southern summers are shorter and hotter than northern ones. Uranus lies almost on its side, leaning about 98 degrees. Each pole has about 42 years of sunlight and then about 42 years of darkness. Jupiter and Venus hardly lean at all, so they have almost no seasons.
- e.g. Mars: a lean of about 25 degrees and a year of 687 Earth days.
- e.g. Uranus: a lean of about 98 degrees and a year of 84 Earth years.
- e.g. Jupiter: a lean of about 3 degrees and almost no seasons.
- **A student of the Earth:** Distance from the Sun does not make the seasons. Lean does. This is true for the Earth, where the orbit is nearly round.
- **A scientist who studies Mars:** On a planet with a stretched orbit, both lean and distance make the seasons. Mars's great dust storms tend to begin when Mars is nearest the Sun. The rule 'distance does not matter' belongs to the Earth, not to every planet.
### Why does the Earth lean at all? · *idea*
**Definition:** Nobody saw how the Earth got its lean. The leading idea is that a body about the size of Mars struck the young Earth about 4.5 billion years ago, knocked it over and threw out the rock that became the Moon.

William Hartmann and Donald Davis proposed this giant impact in 1975. It explains why Moon rocks are so like the Earth's outer rock. The lean is not fixed. It rocks slowly between about 22 and 24 and a half degrees, once in about 41,000 years. Today it is about 23.4 degrees and getting slowly smaller. A larger lean gives stronger seasons. Scientists also ask what keeps the lean so steady, and here they disagree.
- e.g. Today's lean: about 23.4 degrees.
- e.g. The lean changes by less than 3 degrees over 41,000 years.
- e.g. Mars's lean has swung far more widely in the past than the Earth's.
- **Jacques Laskar and his team in Paris, 1993:** The Moon holds the Earth's lean steady. Their sums showed that without the Moon, the pulls of the other planets could swing the lean wildly, perhaps from 0 to 85 degrees. Seasons on such an Earth would sometimes be mild and sometimes violent.
- **Jack Lissauer, Jason Barnes and John Chambers, 2012:** The Moon helps less than was thought. They followed a moonless Earth in a computer for billions of years. Its lean mostly stayed within about 10 degrees of today's value. So a planet may not need a large moon to have steady seasons.

## Mostly agreed
- Radar and spacecraft give the Sun's distance as about 147.1 million kilometres in early January and about 152.1 million kilometres in early July. *How we know:* Since the 1960s, radar signals have been bounced off Venus and timed. Spacecraft tracking now fixes these distances to within a few metres.
- Satellites above the air measure about 7 parts in 100 more sunlight reaching the whole Earth in early January than in early July. *How we know:* Instruments on satellites have recorded the Sun's brightness every day since 1978. The yearly rise and fall matches the changing distance exactly.
- A nearby star shifts back and forth against far stars once a year. *How we know:* Friedrich Bessel first measured this shift in 1838, for the star 61 Cygni. The Gaia spacecraft has since measured it for more than a billion stars. It is direct proof that the Earth revolves.
- The year of the seasons is 365 days, 5 hours, 48 minutes and about 46 seconds long. *How we know:* Astronomers time the moment of the equinox year after year. Hundreds of years of records give the average.
- The Earth's lean is about 23.44 degrees today and is shrinking by about one hundredth of a degree each century. *How we know:* The lean is found from the height of the noon Sun at the two solstices. Records go back more than 2,000 years, to Greek, Chinese and Indian astronomers.

## How we found out
**Timocharis of Alexandria, Hipparchus of Nicaea, and Meghnad Saha with the Calendar Reform Committee of India, Timocharis measured Spica about 290 BCE and Hipparchus about 127 BCE. Saha's committee worked from 1952 to 1955, and the Indian national calendar began on 22 March 1957..** For thousands of years, people in India, Babylon, Egypt and Greece told the seasons by the stars. When a certain star rose before dawn, it was time to sow. They believed that the Sun came back to the same stars each time the same season came back. It fitted everything a person could see in one lifetime. About 127 BCE, the Greek astronomer Hipparchus compared his own measurement of the bright star Spica with one made about 160 years earlier by Timocharis. Spica had shifted by about 2 degrees from the place of the September equinox. The star year and the season year were not the same length. They differ by about 20 minutes. In one lifetime that is nothing. In 1,700 years it is more than three weeks. In 1952 the physicist Meghnad Saha led a committee to study India's many calendars. It found that Makar Sankranti had slipped about 23 days from the December solstice it once marked. The committee designed a national calendar that follows the seasons.  
*What people believed before:* That the Sun returns to the same stars each time the same season returns, so a calendar tied to the stars will stay with the seasons for ever. Hipparchus showed, from a star that had shifted in 160 years, that the two slowly drift apart.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The seasons come from the Earth's changing distance from the Sun.** (A very common belief. In a well-known 1987 film, A Private Universe, most of the new university graduates who were asked could not explain the seasons, and many gave this answer.; *rejected*)  
  For: A fire feels hotter when we step closer. Schoolbook pictures often show the orbit as a long oval. And the Earth's distance from the Sun really does change.  
  Against: The Earth is nearest the Sun in early January, in the northern winter. The two hemispheres have opposite seasons on the same day, though they are the same distance from the Sun. And in Lean or Distance, the true change of distance made almost no difference to the light.
- **Distance still matters a little, even on the Earth.** (Milutin Milankovitch in the 1920s, and most climate scientists today; *accepted*)  
  For: The Earth gets about 7 parts in 100 more sunlight in January than in July. Ice and mud from the deep past show ice ages coming and going in step with slow changes of the orbit and the lean.  
  Against: Within one year the effect is hard to find. Southern summers are not hotter than northern ones, because the southern half of the Earth is mostly ocean, which warms slowly.
- **Uttarayana begins on Makar Sankranti, about 14 January.** (Most Indian panchangams, which follow the stars; *debated*)  
  For: The Sun enters the star group Makara on that day, and the name Uttarayana has been tied to that event for about 2,000 years. A festival is a tradition, and traditions need not move.  
  Against: The noon Sun turns north about 21 December. Shadow measurements at any Jantar Mantar show it. Meghnad Saha's committee said the calendar should be set right. If nothing changes, Sankranti will keep moving later, into February and one day into summer.
- **The monsoon is a giant sea breeze.** (Edmond Halley in 1686, and many schoolbooks since; *debated*)  
  For: Land does heat faster than sea, and the winds do blow from sea to land in summer and turn round in winter.  
  Against: The land is hottest in May, before the rains, and cools once they start, yet the monsoon goes on for months. Sulochana Gadgil showed that the rain belt behaves like the cloud band that circles the Earth near the equator, moved north. Many scientists now use both ideas together.
- **Without the Moon, the Earth's lean would swing wildly and the seasons would be chaos.** (Jacques Laskar and his team, 1993; *debated*)  
  For: Their sums showed the Moon's pull damping the push of the other planets. Mars has only two tiny moons, and its lean has swung by tens of degrees.  
  Against: In 2012 Jack Lissauer's team ran a moonless Earth in a computer for billions of years and found its lean changed far less than feared. Nobody can test either result on a real planet.

## Roots
We have learned that a steady lean, carried round the Sun, gives summer and winter. Now we ask what this tells us about change, about opposites and about where we stand.

**Deep truth.** In The Leaning Ball, the lean of the ball never changed. Its top dot pointed at the same wall all the way round the lamp. Yet the northern ring went from nine lit dots to three, while the southern ring went from three to nine. So summer and winter are not two things that take turns. They are one steady lean, lit from one side, and both are always here.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think that a change in what we feel must come from a change in the thing that causes it. So we say that summer 'comes' and winter 'goes', as if heat were sent to us and taken away. And we treat summer and winter as opposites, when they are the two ends of one leaning ball on the same day.  
*Evidence:* In The Leaning Ball, the lamp and the lean stayed the same at all four places, and only our place on the circle changed. In Lean or Distance, moving the torch as the Earth really moves made almost no difference. And the Earth is nearest the Sun in January, when the north is cold and the south is hot.

- *who am i:* You are standing on a leaning, spinning ball, and what you call 'this season' is your place on it. A child in Sydney, on the same ball on the same day, is living your opposite.
- *what is life:* Living beings do not wait for the seasons to happen to them. Rice, falcons, mango trees and humans read the light and the rain and move with them. Some carry the year inside their own bodies.
- *what is this world:* The world is not hot in one season and cold in another. It is always both. The rains, the harvests and the festivals all come from one lean that has hardly changed since before there were humans.

The Sun gives the same light all year. The Earth keeps the same lean all year. Out of these two steady things come the monsoon, the mango season, the falcons' journey, Pongal, Onam and the six ritus. The many seasons of the many peoples are one lean, seen from different places on one ball.

- **Bhagavad Gita 2.14:** Krishna tells Arjuna that the touch of the world brings cold and heat, pleasure and pain. They come and they go and do not last. He asks Arjuna to bear them steadily. (The Gita names heat and cold as the first example of things that come and go. In The Leaning Ball you saw why they come and go, and what stays steady while they do.)
- **Heraclitus of Ephesus, fragment B67 in the Diels-Kranz numbering, about 500 BCE:** Heraclitus wrote that the divine is day and night, winter and summer, war and peace, plenty and hunger. It takes different forms, as a fire takes the scent of whatever spice is thrown on it. (He put winter and summer together as two faces of one thing. On the leaning ball they are exactly that: the two ends of one lean, at the same moment.)
- **Shatapatha Brahmana 2.1.3.1 to 2.1.3.3:** This old Indian text sorts the six seasons into two sets of three. It ties one set to the half of the year when the Sun moves north, and the other set to the half when the Sun moves south. (More than 2,500 years ago, people in India had already seen that the seasons follow the Sun's swing north and south. The leaning ball shows what makes the swing.)
- Science (settled): The Earth's seasons come from the lean of its axis, not from its distance from the Sun. The Earth is nearest the Sun in early January.
- Science (idea): The Earth's lean came from a giant impact about 4.5 billion years ago.
- Science (debated): The Moon is what keeps the Earth's lean, and so its seasons, steady.
- Science (open): How living beings keep an inner count of the year.
- Other view, A careful physicist: Something does change through the year: the direction from the Earth to the Sun. To say the cause is 'steady' is only to choose the Sun's point of view. And for a mango tree or a farmer, summer and winter are truly different times that follow each other. It does not help them to be told that both are always here somewhere else.
- Practice, Lean into the light (3 min): Sit sideways to a window or a lamp, an arm's length away. Close your eyes. Turn your face slowly towards the light as you breathe in. Feel it land full on your cheeks. Turn slowly away as you breathe out. Feel the light thin out on one cheek. The lamp has not changed. Only your lean has changed. Do this five times.
- Ask: In The Leaning Ball, the lamp and the lean stayed the same, yet you counted summer and winter. Where did the change come from?
- Ask: Is there anything in your own life that feels as if it comes and goes, when really you are the one who has moved?
- Ask: A child in Sydney is living the opposite of your season today. Are the two of you in two different worlds, or in one?

## Story: Pongal on the Beach
*A made-up story. Nila, Imran, Appa and Kavitha Chithi are imagined, and so is everything they say and do. The facts in it are real. The Earth is nearest the Sun in early January. Melbourne has summer in January, with daylight until about a quarter to nine at night. Clocks in Melbourne are five and a half hours ahead of clocks in India in January. Chithi is a Tamil word for a mother's younger sister.*

On Pongal morning the whole lane in Madurai smelled of jaggery and wood smoke. Nila, who was ten, had been up since five. She had drawn a kolam at the door with a Sun in the middle and sugarcane at the corners. The air was cool enough that she had kept her shawl on to draw it.

Now the pongal pot was on the stove in the yard. Appa watched the milk. Imran, who was seven and lived next door, watched Appa. Imran came every year, because Nila's father made the best sakkarai pongal in the lane and Imran's mother made the best biryani, and the two families had an agreement about this.

'Pongalo Pongal!' they all shouted when the milk rose and spilled over the rim.

Then Appa's phone rang. It was Kavitha Chithi, Appa's younger sister, who drove trams in Melbourne in Australia. Her face filled the screen. She wore a big straw hat and dark glasses, and behind her was a bright blue sea.

'Happy Pongal!' said Chithi. 'I am at the beach. It is 36 degrees here. I have eaten two ice creams already.'

'The beach?' said Imran. 'In January? It is cold in January.'

'Not here,' said Chithi. 'Here it is the middle of summer. The Sun will not set until a quarter to nine tonight.'

When the call was over, Imran sat frowning at the kolam. 'I know why,' he said at last. 'Australia is at the bottom of the world. So it must stick out towards the Sun. It is nearer, so it is hotter.'

Nila opened her mouth to agree. It sounded right. Then she stopped. 'Wait,' she said. 'Let us look.'

She took a sweet lime from the Pongal plate and held it near the small lamp that burned beside the pot. 'The lime is the Earth. Here is Madurai, near the top. Here is Melbourne, near the bottom.' She pressed two dots into the peel with her thumbnail. 'Now look from the lamp. Which dot is nearer?'

Imran looked hard. 'They are the same,' he said. 'Almost.'

'And the real Sun is very, very far,' said Nila. 'Miss told us. If the Earth were this lime, the Sun would be nearly a kilometre away, and as tall as a two-storey house. The top and the bottom of the lime are the same distance from something that far off.'

Appa looked up from the pot. 'There was something in the paper last week,' he said. 'It said the Earth was at its closest to the Sun for the whole year. On the third of January.'

'Closest? Now?' said Nila. 'But I wore a shawl this morning. And Delhi has fog, and the news showed snow in Kashmir.'

'That is what it said,' said Appa. 'I did not understand it either.'

So it could not be nearness. The whole Earth was near the Sun this month, and half of it was cold. Nila turned the lime in her fingers. There had to be something that was different for Madurai and Melbourne on the same day.

'It leans,' she said slowly. 'The globe at school leans. I thought it was only made like that so we could see India.'

She tilted the lime so that the stalk end leaned away from the lamp. 'Look now, Imran. Do not look at how near. Look at how the light lands.'

Imran bent close. On the Melbourne dot the lamplight fell straight and bright. On the Madurai dot it came in sideways and thin, stretched over the curve of the peel.

'Chithi is getting it full in the face,' said Imran. 'We are getting it from the side.'

'Now I walk to the other side of the lamp,' said Nila. 'That is six months. I must not turn the stalk. It keeps pointing at the gate.' She carried the lime round to the far side, with the stalk still leaning towards the gate. Now the stalk end leaned in towards the lamp. The light fell straight on the Madurai dot and thin on the Melbourne dot.

'May,' said Appa. 'When even the crows sit with their beaks open.'

'So Chithi will be cold in June,' said Imran, 'and she will ring us and we will be the ones eating ice cream.' He was very pleased with this.

They ate the pongal sitting on the step. The Sun was well up now, and the shawl lay folded beside Nila.

'Appa,' she said, 'Pongal is for thanking the Sun for coming back north. Paatti used to say so.'

'She did.'

'But the Sun did not go anywhere. We leaned.' She thought about it. 'And we did not even change our lean. We only went round to the other side.'

Imran licked his fingers. 'Then who do we thank?' he asked.

Nobody answered straight away. A crow landed on the wall and looked at the pot with one eye.

'One more thing,' said Imran. 'If Chithi has summer now and we have winter now, what does the Earth have?'

Nila looked at the sweet lime in her hand, half in the lamplight and half out of it. She did not know yet. She put it back on the plate, leaning.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn comes when Nila stops looking at how near each dot is to the lamp and looks at how the light lands on it. Both dots are the same distance away, and the whole Earth is nearest the Sun in January. What differs is the slant. The second turn is her own: the lean never changed, they only went round to the other side.

- Ask: Why did Nila ask Imran to look at how the light lands, and not at how near the dots were?
- Ask: Why did the stalk of the lime have to keep pointing at the gate?
- Ask: Imran asked: if it is summer and winter at the same time, what does the Earth have? What do you think?
- Ask: Nila said the Sun did not come back, and that they had only gone round to the other side. Then who, or what, would you thank at Pongal?

  *Parent says:* The Earth is about 3 parts in 100 further from the Sun in July than in January. Here that is 1 cm in 30. In Delhi the noon Sun is 5 degrees from overhead in June and 52 degrees in December. Which change will spread the light more? Why do you think so?

  - Ask: Which will change the patch more, and why do you think so? (good: Distance, because things are hotter when you are closer.; Lean, because the light will be stretched out.; Any guess with a reason. The test will decide.)
  - Note: Do not give the answer. Most children, and most grown-ups, guess distance. Write the guess down so you can come back to it.

  *Parent does:* 1. With the card upright, your child draws round the edge of the patch and measures it from top to bottom. Write it down as lean 0. 2. Lean the card back 10 degrees from upright.   *Parent says:* Ten degrees is the noon Sun in Chennai in June. Thirty-seven degrees is the noon Sun in Chennai in December. Watch the shape of the patch as the card leans.  
  *Child mode:* Measure the patch with the card upright. Lean the card back 10 degrees. Measure again. Then lean it 37 degrees. Measure again.
  - Note: With no protractor, use a card 20 cm tall and measure how far its top edge has moved back from upright: 3.5 cm for 10 degrees, 12 cm for 37 degrees, 1.7 cm for 5 degrees and 16 cm for 52 degrees. One person holds the card steady while the other draws. The width of the patch stays the same. Only its length grows. With a 6 cm patch, expect about 6.1 cm at 10 degrees and about 7.5 cm at 37 degrees.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lean the card back 5 degrees, slide it so the dot is 30 cm away, and measure the patch. 2. Do the same at 52 degrees. 3. Measure each a second time.  
  *Parent says:* Five degrees is the noon Sun in Delhi in June. Fifty-two degrees is the noon Sun in Delhi in December. Is Delhi's change bigger or smaller than Chennai's?  
  *Child mode:* Lean the card 5 degrees and measure. Lean it 52 degrees and measure. Is Delhi's change bigger than Chennai's?
  - Note: With a 6 cm patch, expect about 6 cm at 5 degrees and about 9.7 cm at 52 degrees. At 52 degrees the patch may run off a small card. Then move the card so the whole patch fits, keeping the dot at 30 cm.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the card upright again with the dot 30 cm from the tube. 2. Measure the width of the patch as carefully as you can, to the nearest millimetre. 3. Move the torch back exactly 1 cm, so the dot is 31 cm away. 4. Measure the width again. 5. Do both a second time.  
  *Parent says:* Now the distance test. Thirty centimetres is the Earth in January. Thirty-one is the Earth in July. Measure very carefully, because the change may be small.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the card upright. Measure the patch at 30 cm. Move the torch back 1 cm. Measure again, very carefully.
  - Note: Expect the patch to grow by about 2 mm, for example from 6.0 cm to 6.2 cm. Many families cannot see any change at all. That is a real result, so write it down as it is.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each setting, divide the upright length by the measured length. 2. Write the answer in the last column. It tells you what share of the straight-on light each square centimetre now gets. 3. Put the six settings in order, from the most light on each square to the least.  
  *Parent says:* The same light is now spread over a longer patch. If the patch is twice as long, each square gets half the light. Which setting gave each square the least light?  
  *Child mode:* Divide the upright length by each new length. A smaller answer means less light on each square. Which setting is lowest?
  - Ask: Which change spread the light more: 1 cm of distance, or Delhi's December lean? (good: The lean, by far. The patch was more than one and a half times as long.; The distance hardly changed the patch at all.)
  - Note: Worked example with a 6 cm patch: at 37 degrees, 6 divided by 7.5 is 0.8. At 52 degrees, 6 divided by 9.7 is about 0.6. For the distance test the patch grows in width and in height, so the share is about 0.93. Chennai in December still gets 8 parts in 10 of the straight-on light. Delhi gets only 6 parts in 10.
  - Check: In December each square of Delhi ground gets about 6 parts in 10 of June's noon light. Why? → **The same light is spread over a longer patch**
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child writes a claim, the evidence and the reasoning. 2. Go back to the guess from step 2 and compare. 3. Then tell your child when the Earth is nearest the Sun, and talk about what this test leaves out.  
  *Parent says:* Write what you think now, what you saw, and why. Then here is a fact. The Earth is nearest the Sun in early January. Does that fit your result? What does our torch test leave out?  
  *Child mode:* Write your claim, your evidence and your reason. The Earth is nearest the Sun in January. Does that fit what you found?
  - Note: The test leaves out three things. Real sunlight hardly spreads at all, so distance matters even less than the torch shows. Slanting sunlight also crosses more air. And summer days are longer, which adds to the heating. The Leaning Ball looks at the longer days.
  - Think: A torch beam spreads out, but sunlight arrives almost as straight, side-by-side rays. Does that make our distance test too strong or too weak?
### e2 · The Leaning Ball: would an upright Earth have seasons? (35 min)
- **question:** How many hours of daylight does a far northern place get at four points of the year, and a far southern place? What happens to the count if the Earth stands upright?
- **predict:** Each ring has 12 dots, and each dot stands for 2 hours of the day. Guess how many dots will be lit on the northern ring at each of the four places, with the ball leaning. Then guess for the ball standing upright. Give a reason for each guess.
- **fair test:** Use the same ball, the same lamp and the same four places, each 60 cm from the lamp. With the leaning ball, the top dot must lean towards the same wall at every place. In the second round only one thing changes: the ball stands upright. Count each ring twice, once from each side.
- **safety:** Use a lamp with an LED bulb, which stays cool. An old-style bulb gets hot enough to burn, so do not touch it.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the lamp and the four places seen from above, with the lean of the ball shown at each place. Then write your claim, your evidence and your reasoning.
- **results table:** Place round the lamp | Leaning: lit dots on the northern ring | Leaning: lit dots on the southern ring | Upright: lit dots on the northern ring | Upright: lit dots on the southern ring | Hours of daylight in the north (lit dots times 2)
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one rubber band round the middle of the ball. This is the equator. 2. Mark the top with a sticker. This is the North Pole. 3. Put a second band two-thirds of the way from the equator to the North Pole. Put a third band two-thirds of the way to the other end. 4. With the sketch pen, mark 12 dots along the northern band, evenly spaced. Mark the 4 quarter points first, then 2 more in each gap. 5. Do the same along the southern band.  
  *Parent says:* This ball is the Earth. The middle band is the equator. The upper band is a ring of places far in the north, like Norway. The lower band is far in the south. As the Earth spins, a place travels once round its ring in 24 hours. So each of the 12 dots is 2 hours.  
  *Child mode:* Put a band round the middle. Put a band near each end. Mark 12 dots along each end band. Mark the top with a sticker.
  - Note: Two-thirds of the way to the pole is 60 degrees north. The rings are far north and far south so that the change is big enough to count. India lies much nearer the equator, where the change is real but too small to count with 12 dots.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the lamp on an upturned tumbler in the middle of the floor. 2. Mark four places round it with paper and tape, each 60 cm from the lamp. Number them 1 to 4, going round anticlockwise as seen from above. 3. Choose the wall that place 1 faces across the lamp. Call it the lean wall. 4. Ask for the guesses and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* The ball will sit on a tumbler at each place. Its top will always lean towards the lean wall, about a quarter of the way from standing to lying. How many northern dots will be lit at each place? And if the ball stands upright? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Mark four places round the lamp. Pick one wall as the lean wall. Guess the lit dots at each place. Say why.
  - Ask: Will the count of lit dots be the same at all four places, and why do you think so? (good: No. When the top leans to the lamp, more of the top ring will be in the light.; Yes, because half the ball is always lit.; Any guess with a reason.)
  - Note: Place 1 is the place where the top dot leans towards the lamp. So the lean wall is the wall behind the lamp as seen from place 1. A quarter of the way from standing to lying is about 23 degrees. The lamp must be level with the middle of the ball.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the room dark. 2. Sit the ball on its tumbler at place 1, with the top leaning towards the lean wall. 3. Your child counts the dots in the light on the northern ring, then on the southern ring. A dot on the edge of the light counts as half. 4. Carry the ball to place 2. Do not turn it. The top must still lean towards the lean wall. Count again. 5. Do the same at places 3 and 4.  
  *Parent says:* Half of the ball is always lit. But is half of each ring lit? Count the dots in the light. Keep the top leaning to the same wall at every place.  
  *Child mode:* Put the leaning ball at place 1. Count the lit dots on each ring. Carry it to the next place. Keep the top leaning to the same wall.
  - Note: Expect about 9 and 3 at place 1, about 6 and 6 at places 2 and 4, and about 3 and 9 at place 3. One dot either way is fine. The usual slip is to turn the ball so that it keeps leaning towards the lamp. Check the top dot against the lean wall each time. With a phone torch, turn the torch to face the ball at each place.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now stand the ball upright on its tumbler, with the top dot pointing at the ceiling. 2. Count the lit dots on both rings at all four places.  
  *Parent says:* Now the Earth stands straight up. Nothing else has changed. Count again at each place.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the ball upright. Count the lit dots on each ring at all four places.
  - Note: Expect about 6 and 6 at every place. An upright Earth has 12 hours of day and 12 of night everywhere, all year.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Multiply each northern count by 2 to get hours of daylight. 2. Ask which place is the June solstice, which is the December solstice and which two are the equinoxes. 3. Look at the leaning counts for north and south side by side.  
  *Parent says:* Each dot is two hours. Which place gave the north its longest day? Which gave the south its longest day? What do you notice when you put the two columns side by side?  
  *Child mode:* Multiply each count by 2 to get hours. Which place is June? Which is December? Compare north and south.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the north and the south at place 1 and at place 3? (good: When the north has the most lit dots, the south has the fewest.; They swap. The seasons are opposite.)
  - Check: The ball leaned the same way at every place. So why did the count change? → **The light met the same lean from a different side**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child writes a claim, the evidence and the reasoning. 2. Compare with the guesses from step 2. 3. Talk about what the model leaves out.  
  *Parent says:* Would an upright Earth have seasons? Write what you think, what you counted, and why. Then tell me one way our ball is not like the real Earth.  
  *Child mode:* Would an upright Earth have seasons? Write your claim, your evidence and your reason.
  - Note: The model leaves out the air, the oceans and the clouds, which move heat about. The real Sun is also much further away for its size, so real sunlight lights almost exactly half the Earth. Our lamp lights a little less than half.
  - Think: What would you count if the ball lay flat on its side, like the planet Uranus?
### e3 · Four Cities, Two Days: where does the length of the day change most? (25 min)
- **question:** Is the difference between the longest day and the shortest day the same all over India? If not, where is it biggest, and how does it grow as you go north?
- **predict:** The four cities are Kanyakumari, Chennai, Delhi and Srinagar. Guess how much longer 21 June is than 21 December in each city, in hours and minutes. Do you expect the difference to grow evenly as you go north? Give your reason.
- **fair test:** Use the same two dates for every city: 21 June and 21 December. Use times from one source, all in Indian clock time. Work out every day length the same way: sunset time minus sunrise time.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar chart with two bars for each city, one for June and one for December. Then write your claim, your evidence and your reasoning.
- **results table:** City and how far north it is | My guess: difference | Day length on 21 June | Day length on 21 December | Difference
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find the four cities on a map. Kanyakumari is about 8 degrees north of the equator, Chennai 13, Delhi 29 and Srinagar 34. 2. Ask for a guess for each city and a reason. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* On 21 June the days are longest all over India. On 21 December they are shortest. How big is the difference in each city? Does it grow evenly as we go north? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Find the four cities on a map. Guess how much longer June's day is in each. Say why.
  - Ask: Where will the difference be biggest, and why do you think so? (good: In Srinagar, because it is furthest from the equator.; The same everywhere, because it is one country under one Sun.; Any guess with a reason.)
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Copy these times. Kanyakumari: 21 June, sunrise 6:03 and sunset 6:39. 21 December, sunrise 6:28 and sunset 6:07. 2. Chennai: 21 June, 5:43 and 6:37. 21 December, 6:26 and 5:47. 3. Delhi: 21 June, 5:23 and 7:22. 21 December, 7:09 and 5:29. 4. Srinagar: 21 June, 5:19 and 7:45. 21 December, 7:32 and 5:25. 5. Your child works out each day length. Count from sunrise up to 12 noon, then add the time from noon to sunset.  
  *Parent says:* All sunrises are in the morning and all sunsets are in the evening. Work out how long the Sun is up each time. Start with Chennai in June.  
  *Child mode:* Copy the times. For each one, count from sunrise to noon. Then add noon to sunset. That is the day length.
  - Note: Answers, for June and then December. Kanyakumari: 12 hours 36 minutes and 11 hours 39 minutes. Chennai: 12 hours 54 minutes and 11 hours 21 minutes. Delhi: 13 hours 59 minutes and 10 hours 20 minutes. Srinagar: 14 hours 26 minutes and 9 hours 53 minutes. The times are rounded to the minute and can differ by a minute or two from a newspaper.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take each December length away from the June length. 2. Draw a bar chart with two bars for each city. Let one square stand for one hour.  
  *Parent says:* Now find the difference for each city. Then draw the bars side by side, south to north. What shape do the pairs of bars make?  
  *Child mode:* Take December's length from June's length for each city. Draw two bars for each city.
  - Note: Differences: Kanyakumari 57 minutes, Chennai 1 hour 33 minutes, Delhi 3 hours 39 minutes, Srinagar 4 hours 33 minutes.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child writes a claim, the evidence and the reasoning. 2. Compare with the guesses. 3. Look at the sunrise times once more. In which month does Delhi see the Sun before Kanyakumari?  
  *Parent says:* What pattern do you see? Write your claim, your evidence and your reason. Then look again at the sunrise times. Something odd happens between June and December.  
  *Child mode:* Write what you found and why. Then look at the sunrise times in Delhi and Kanyakumari. What swaps?
  - Note: In June the Sun rises in Delhi 40 minutes before it rises in Kanyakumari. In December it rises in Kanyakumari 41 minutes before Delhi. The line between night and day leans across India one way in June and the other way in December. The differences do not grow evenly. They grow faster and faster towards the pole.
  - Check: A city is on the equator. How much longer is its day on 21 June than on 21 December? → **Almost no difference**
  - Think: At the equator the difference is almost nothing, and at the North Pole it is the whole 24 hours. What would you expect for a city halfway between Srinagar and the Pole?

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Mars has a much more stretched orbit than the Earth. It gets about 45 parts in 100 more sunlight at its nearest than at its farthest.
5. (fill) The slow wobble of the Earth's axis, once in about 26,000 years, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['precession']. *Why:* Precession slowly changes which stars lie behind the Sun on a given date. It is why star-based calendars slip against the seasons.
6. (count) A calendar year is 0.0078 of a day too long. How many whole days does it slip in 1,000 years?  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* 0.0078 times 1,000 is 7.8, which rounds to 8 days.
7. (perspective) Should Makar Sankranti be moved back to the December solstice?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. The first keeps the tie to the stars. The second keeps the tie to the solstice. The third keeps the tie to the crop.. *Why:* No view is marked right. This is a real argument in India, and each side protects something that the festival means.
8. (sort) Rotation, revolution or tilt? Sort what each one mainly gives us.  
   **Answer:** Sunrise and sunset → Rotation; The length of the year → Revolution; Leap years → Revolution; Longer days in summer → Tilt; Opposite seasons north and south → Tilt; The stars circling the Pole Star each night → Rotation. *Why:* The spin gives the day. The journey gives the year. The lean, carried along on the journey, gives the seasons.
9. (write) Design a test to show that an upright Earth has no seasons. What would you change and count?  
   **Answer:** Carry a dotted ball round a lamp, first leaning and then upright. Keep the lamp, the ball and the distance the same. Count the lit dots on a ring at four places. Leaning gives different counts. Upright gives the same count everywhere.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the lean, keeps everything else the same and names something to count.
10. (mcq) Sulochana Gadgil described the monsoon as what?  
   Options: A rain belt that follows the overhead Sun / A giant sea breeze / Wind made by the Earth's spin / Rain sent down by the Himalaya  
   **Answer:** A rain belt that follows the overhead Sun. *Why:* She showed that India's monsoon behaves like the band of rain clouds near the equator, moved north in summer. The sea breeze idea is Edmond Halley's, from 1686.
11. (order) Put these planets in order, from the smallest lean to the largest.  
   **Answer:** ['Jupiter', 'Earth', 'Mars', 'Uranus']. *Why:* Jupiter leans about 3 degrees, the Earth about 23 and a half, Mars about 25 and Uranus about 98.
12. (draw) Draw a pole's noon shadow in Chennai on 21 June and on 21 December. Mark north and south.  
   **Answer:** On 21 June the Sun is a little north of overhead, and a short shadow points south. On 21 December the Sun is well to the south, and a longer shadow points north.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows both the swap of direction and the difference in length.
13. (write) Your city needs its own season calendar. Name three seasons you would give it, and the sign that begins each.  
   **Answer:** Any three seasons that fit the child's place, each with a sign that can be noticed. For example: the mango heat, which begins when the first mangoes reach the market.. *Why:* There is no single answer. A strong answer uses signs from the child's own place and not from a book.
14. (truefalse) Day and night are exactly 12 hours each on the day of the equinox.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False, though it is close. The air bends sunlight and the Sun is a wide disc, so the day is about 12 hours and 7 minutes. Day and night are truly equal a few days before the March equinox.
15. (fill) The half of the year when the noon Sun moves north is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['Uttarayana']. *Why:* Uttarayana runs from the December solstice to the June solstice. Dakshinayana is the other half, when the noon Sun moves south.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which motion of the Earth gives us the year?  
   Options: Revolution round the Sun / Rotation on its axis / The wobble of the axis / The Moon's orbit  
   **Answer:** Revolution round the Sun. *Why:* One revolution round the Sun is one year. Rotation gives the day. If you chose rotation, think of a top that spins while it slides round a table.
2. (mcq) About how long does the Earth take to go once round the Sun?  
   Options: 365 days and 6 hours / 24 hours / 29 and a half days / 26,000 years  
   **Answer:** 365 days and 6 hours. *Why:* The extra 6 hours add up to one day in four years, which is why we have leap years.
3. (mcq) In which month is the Earth nearest to the Sun?  
   Options: April / January / June / September  
   **Answer:** January. *Why:* Early January. This is winter in the north, so nearness cannot be the cause of summer.
4. (mcq) Why does slanting sunlight warm the ground less than light from overhead?  
   Options: It is spread over more ground / It comes from a cooler part of the Sun / It travels more slowly / It is a different colour  
   **Answer:** It is spread over more ground. *Why:* The same light covers a larger patch, so each square metre gets less. It also crosses more air on the way.
5. (mcq) It is winter in Srinagar. What season is it in Sydney, Australia?  
   Options: Winter / Monsoon / Sydney has no seasons / Summer  
   **Answer:** Summer. *Why:* When the northern end of the axis leans away from the Sun, the southern end leans towards it. If you chose winter, picture the leaning ball at place 3.
6. (mcq) What would happen to the seasons if the Earth's axis stood upright?  
   Options: Almost no seasons / Hotter summers / Seasons would swap / Longer winters  
   **Answer:** Almost no seasons. *Why:* With no lean, every place would get the same slant of light and 12 hours of day all year.
7. (mcq) On which day is the noon Sun overhead on the Tropic of Cancer?  
   Options: 20 March / 21 June / 23 September / 21 December  
   **Answer:** 21 June. *Why:* On the June solstice the northern end of the axis leans most towards the Sun.
8. (mcq) What is true on the two equinoxes?  
   Options: The Earth is nearest the Sun / The Sun does not rise in India / Day and night are nearly equal everywhere / It is the hottest day  
   **Answer:** Day and night are nearly equal everywhere. *Why:* On an equinox the line between day and night passes through both poles, so every place gets about 12 hours of each.
9. (mcq) Which city has the biggest difference between its longest and shortest day?  
   Options: Kanyakumari / Chennai / Srinagar / Mumbai  
   **Answer:** Srinagar. *Why:* Srinagar is furthest from the equator. Its longest day is about 4 and a half hours longer than its shortest.
10. (mcq) The Sun stays up for about six months at the North Pole. Why is the pole still very cold?  
   Options: Its rays arrive at a slant and spread thin over the ground / The Sun never rises there / The pole is the farthest place from the Sun / The Sun's day is short there  
   **Answer:** Its rays arrive at a slant and spread thin over the ground. *Why:* The Sun does rise there in spring, and it stays up all summer. But it stays low in the sky, so its light spreads over a wide patch of ground. The pole is not the farthest place from the Sun, and the Sun's day is long there, not short.
11. (mcq) What is a monsoon?  
   Options: Any heavy rain / A storm from the sea / A wind that reverses with the seasons / The hottest month  
   **Answer:** A wind that reverses with the seasons. *Why:* The word means season. The wind blows from the south-west sea in summer and from the north-east in winter.
12. (mcq) The old Tamil calendar and the Sanskrit calendar both count how many seasons?  
   Options: Two / Four / Six / Twelve  
   **Answer:** Six. *Why:* Both count six, with different names and starting points. India's weather office counts four.
13. (mcq) Why does Pongal now come about three weeks after the December solstice?  
   Options: The axis slowly wobbles, shifting the stars / The Earth has slowed down / The calendar lost three weeks in 1957 / The Sun has moved  
   **Answer:** The axis slowly wobbles, shifting the stars. *Why:* Pongal follows the stars. Precession slowly shifts the stars behind the Sun, about one day in 70 years.
14. (mcq) A seasons journal has five themes: plants, animals, weather, water and people. Which theme does this entry fit? 'The tap ran dry in April and is running again in July.'  
   Options: Plants / Weather / People / Water  
   **Answer:** Water. *Why:* That note is about water in the tap. A note about heat or wind would go under weather, and a note about who came to the house would go under people.

## Applications
- Builders set the depth of a chajja, the ledge above a window, from the height of the noon Sun in summer and in winter.
- Solar panels in India are tilted towards the south by about as many degrees as the place is north of the equator, so that they face the Sun squarely over the year.
- Farmers choose sowing dates by the monsoon and by day length. Many rice varieties flower only when the days shorten.
- Calendar makers add leap days, or a whole extra month, to keep festivals and seasons together.
- The India Meteorological Department forecasts the date the monsoon will reach Kerala, and the whole country plans by it.
- Astronomers use the yearly shift of nearby stars, caused by the Earth's revolution, to measure how far away the stars are.
- Scientists who study planets round other stars ask about each planet's lean, to judge what its seasons may be like.

## Spotted around us
- The patch of sunlight from a window. It reaches far across the floor in December and stays close to the window in May.
- Zero shadow day in Chennai, Bengaluru, Kochi, Hyderabad or Mumbai, when poles and people have no shadow at noon.
- The place on the skyline where the Sun sets. It slides north from December to June and south again.
- The Samrat Yantra at Jantar Mantar in Jaipur or Delhi, a giant sundial whose shadow shows the Sun's swing north and south.
- The sunrise time in the newspaper, a few minutes later each week from July to January.
- The first mangoes, the first jamun, the first oranges. Each fruit reaches the market at its own point of the Earth's orbit.
- Kolams and the Pongal pot in January, pookalams at Onam, kites at Sankranti in Gujarat. Each marks a point in the Sun's year.
- The Pole Star, Dhruva, low in the northern sky. The lower it is, the nearer you are to the equator.

## Open questions
- **How will India's monsoon change as the Earth warms?** Warmer air holds more water, so most computer models expect heavier bursts of rain. But the models disagree about how the dates, the breaks and the total rain will change, and smoke and dust in the air push the other way.
- **Can the start of the monsoon, and the dry breaks inside it, be forecast a month ahead?** Forecasts a few days ahead are now good. Beyond about two weeks they are still weak, because small changes in the ocean and the air grow into large ones. Farmers need exactly this answer.
- **How does the inner yearly clock of a bird or a plant work?** The daily body clock is understood down to its genes. No one has yet found how cells count out most of a year. Eberhard Gwinner showed that the clock exists, but its parts are still unknown.
- **Did one giant impact really give the Earth its lean?** The impact fits the Moon rocks, but computer models of it give many different leans and spins. Some scientists now propose several smaller impacts. No direct record of the event is left.
- **Does a planet need seasons, or steady seasons, for life to spread across it?** We know only one planet with life. Telescopes are just beginning to measure the leans of planets round other stars, so there is nothing yet to compare the Earth with.

## Connected maths
- In Lean or Distance, the upright patch is 6 cm long. At 52 degrees it is 9.6 cm long. What fraction of the straight-on light does each square now get? **Five-eighths, or about 0.6.**. 6 divided by 9.6 is 0.625, which is five-eighths. The same light is spread over a longer patch. (queues `math.fractions.decimal-operations.L5`)
- The Earth is 147 million km from the Sun in January and 152 million km in July. About what percent further is it in July? **About 3 percent.**. The difference is 5 million km. 5 divided by 147 is about 0.034, which is about 3 percent. (queues `math.fractions.percentages.L5`)
- In Chennai on 21 June the Sun rises at 5:43 and sets at 6:37 in the evening. How long is the day? **12 hours 54 minutes.**. From 5:43 to noon is 6 hours 17 minutes. From noon to 6:37 is 6 hours 37 minutes. Together they make 12 hours 54 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- Delhi is 29 degrees north. On 21 June the noon Sun is overhead at 23 and a half degrees north. How many degrees from overhead is the noon Sun in Delhi? **5 and a half degrees.**. 29 minus 23 and a half is 5 and a half. In December you add the two numbers and get 52 and a half degrees. (queues `math.lines-angles.measuring-angles.L5`)
- A year of the seasons is 365.2422 days. How many extra days must a 365-day calendar add in 400 years, to the nearest whole day? **97 days.**. 0.2422 times 400 is 96.88, which rounds to 97. The Gregorian calendar adds exactly 97 leap days in 400 years. (queues `math.time.calendar.L5`)
- Makar Sankranti is 24 days after the December solstice. It slips about 1 day every 70 years. About how many years ago did the two match? **About 1,680 years ago.**. 24 times 70 is 1,680. So the two matched about 1,700 years ago. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In The Leaning Ball, the northern ring had 9, 6, 3 and 6 lit dots at the four places. What is the average number of lit dots over the year? **6 dots, which is 12 hours.**. 9 plus 6 plus 3 plus 6 is 24. 24 divided by 4 is 6. Over a whole year, every place gets about half its time in daylight. (queues `math.data.averages.L5`)
