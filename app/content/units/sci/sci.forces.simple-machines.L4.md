# Pulleys, gears and screws: how machines trade a small pull for a long way
`sci.forces.simple-machines.L4`

**Summary.** A child can lift a heavy bucket with a gentle pull, if the rope runs round the right wheels. Your child hangs a bottle from reels and a rubber band, and finds that two ropes halve the pull but double how far the hand must move. They then count the turns of a bicycle's gears, wind a paper ramp into a screw, and find where a machine's missing effort goes. They learn why pulleys, gears, screws and wedges are all the same trade, why no machine has ever given out more than it was given, and why a mathematician named Emmy Noether says that is a fact about time.

**Intro.** *Two broom handles. One rope, wound round them several times. A strong grown-up holds the handles apart. A child pulls the end of the rope, and the handles slide together, however hard the grown-up pushes. How can a child beat a grown-up?* The child did not become stronger. The rope did something clever. Each time it is wound round, one more length of rope pulls the handles together, so the child's pull is shared out and multiplied. In this unit you hang a bottle from two reels and count the ropes. You measure how much easier the pull gets, and how much further your hand must move. Then you follow the same idea into gears, screws and the tiny machines inside every living cell.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain how a fixed pulley only changes the direction of a pull, and how a moving pulley shares a load between ropes. I can count the ropes that hold a load and work out the pull.
1. I can explain that a machine trades force for distance, so that force times distance, which scientists call work, stays the same on both sides.
2. I can explain where the missing effort goes in a real machine: friction turns some of it into warmth, so a real machine always needs a little more than the ideal one.
3. I can explain how gears and belts trade turning strength for speed, and work out how many times a small gear turns from the number of teeth.
4. I can explain that a screw is a ramp wound round a rod and a wedge is two ramps back to back, so the six simple machines are really two ideas, the lever and the ramp.
5. I can run a fair test with repeats, show the results in a table and a bar chart, and say what I thought, what I saw and why.
6. I can name the machines inside a scissors, a bicycle or a pressure cooker, and say why the rule that machines never give out more than they take in has never been broken.

## Terms
- **Pulley**: A wheel with a groove round its edge, for a rope to run over. It can change the direction of a pull, and with more than one it can make the pull smaller. *e.g.* A well has a pulley over it, so you pull down to lift the bucket up.
- **Fixed pulley**: A pulley that stays hung in one place. It changes the direction of the pull, but not its size. *e.g.* A flagpole has a fixed pulley at the top, so you pull down to raise the flag.
- **Moving pulley**: A pulley that hangs on the rope and rises with the load. It makes two ropes hold the load, so each rope carries half. *e.g.* A bucket hung from a wheel that sits in a loop of rope.
- **Block and tackle**: Several pulleys joined with one long rope. The more ropes hold the load, the smaller the pull. *e.g.* A crane lifting a heavy beam at a building site.
- **Mechanical advantage**: How many times a machine multiplies your push or pull. If a load of 12 kilograms needs a pull of only 3, the advantage is 4. *e.g.* Four ropes holding a load give an advantage of about 4.
- **Work (in science)**: The push or pull on a thing, multiplied by the distance the thing moves in the direction of the push. It is a way to count how much energy was passed on. *e.g.* Lifting a bottle of 1 kilogram up by 10 centimetres is about 1 joule of work.
- **Joule**: The scientists' unit of work and energy. One joule is the work of pushing with 1 newton for 1 metre. *e.g.* Lifting a small apple up by one metre takes about 1 joule.
- **Newton**: The scientists' unit of force. A 1 kilogram bottle pulls down with about 10 newtons. *e.g.* A bottle of water of 1 litre weighs about 10 newtons.
- **Efficiency**: How much of the work you put in comes out as useful work. The rest turns into warmth through friction. *e.g.* A well-oiled pulley may give out 9 parts in every 10 that you put in.
- **Gear**: A wheel with teeth round its edge. Its teeth fit between the teeth of another gear, so turning one turns the other. *e.g.* The gears of a bicycle are joined by a chain.
- **Gear ratio**: How many times one gear turns when the other turns once. You find it by dividing the number of teeth on the big gear by the number on the small one. *e.g.* A gear of 40 teeth driving a gear of 10 teeth turns it 4 times.
- **Pitch of a screw**: The distance a screw moves forward in one full turn. It is the gap between one thread and the next. *e.g.* A jam jar lid rises by a few millimetres in each turn.
- **Thread**: The spiral ridge wound round a screw or a bolt. It is a ramp that goes round and round. *e.g.* The ridge inside a jar lid is a thread.
- **Compound machine**: A machine made of two or more simple machines working together. *e.g.* A pair of scissors is two levers with two wedges.
- **Belt drive**: A loop of belt or string round two wheels, so that turning one wheel turns the other. If the wheels are different sizes, one turns faster. *e.g.* A charkha's big wheel turns a thin spindle with a belt of thread.
- **Friction**: The grip between two things that rub. It slows moving things and turns some of their movement into warmth. *e.g.* A rope rubbing on a dry wooden pulley squeals and warms up.

## Explanations
### The fixed pulley: it only turns the pull round · *defined*
**Definition:** A fixed pulley is a wheel hung in one place with a rope over it. It changes the direction of a pull, but the pull stays the same size.

Think of a bucket in a well. Without a wheel, you would lean over the well and lift the bucket straight up with your arms. With a wheel over the well, you pull the rope down instead. The bucket still weighs the same, and you still pull with the same strength. Pulling down is easier because you can use your own body weight, and you can stand safely away from the edge. If the bucket needs 10 units of pull, a fixed pulley still needs 10. If you pull the rope 1 metre down, the bucket goes 1 metre up. A fixed pulley gives no advantage in strength. It gives a better direction.
- e.g. A flagpole has a fixed pulley at the top, so you pull down to send the flag up.
- e.g. A curtain cord turns the pull along the rail round to the side.
- e.g. A well with a wheel at the top lets you pull down to lift the bucket.
### The moving pulley and counting the ropes · *defined*
**Definition:** A moving pulley hangs on the rope and rises with the load. Count the ropes that hold the load. Each rope carries an equal share, so the pull is the load divided by the number of ropes.

In your test, the bottle hung from a reel that sat in a loop of string. The loop has two strands going up. Each strand holds half the bottle, so your hand only holds half. That is why the rubber band stretched about half as much. But there is a price. To lift the bottle by 10 centimetres, both strands must get 10 centimetres shorter. All that string has to come from the end you are pulling. So your hand moves 20 centimetres. With three ropes holding the load, the pull is a third and the hand moves three times as far. With four ropes, it is a quarter and four times. This is why cranes and boat sails use many pulleys and very long ropes. To count the ropes, count only the strands that touch the moving part and pull upwards on it. The strand that you hold in your hand does not count if it only passes over a fixed wheel.
- e.g. A load held by 2 ropes needs half the pull and twice the rope.
- e.g. A load held by 4 ropes needs a quarter of the pull but 4 times the rope.
- e.g. A ship's sail uses many pulleys, so a few sailors can raise a very heavy sail.
### Work: force times distance stays the same · *defined*
**Definition:** Work is the force on a thing multiplied by the distance it moves in the direction of the force. A machine can change the force and the distance, but the work that comes out cannot be more than the work that goes in.

Lifting a bottle of 1 kilogram by 10 centimetres needs a pull of about 10 newtons for 0.1 metres. Multiply them, and the work is 1 joule. With two ropes, the pull is 5 newtons and the hand moves 0.2 metres. Multiply them, and the work is again 1 joule. With four ropes, the pull is 2.5 newtons and the hand moves 0.4 metres, and the work is 1 joule once more. The numbers change but the product does not. That is the trade. A smaller force goes a longer way. We call the number of times a machine multiplies your force its mechanical advantage. A machine with an advantage of 2 needs half the force and twice the distance. No set-up of ropes, levers or ramps has ever given out more work than was put in.
- e.g. 1 kilogram lifted 10 centimetres: 10 newtons times 0.1 metres is 1 joule.
- e.g. The same lift with 2 ropes: 5 newtons times 0.2 metres is still 1 joule.
- e.g. A ramp 4 times as long as the height needs a quarter of the push.
### Where the missing effort goes: friction and efficiency · *defined*
**Definition:** In a real machine, some of the work you put in turns into warmth through friction. Efficiency is the part of the work that comes out as useful work.

In your test, two ropes should have halved the pull. But the rubber band probably stretched a little more than half. The reel rubbed on its pencil and the string rubbed on the reel. That rubbing turned some of your effort into warmth, which spread into the air. The work did not disappear. It went somewhere you did not want it. A pulley with an efficiency of 90 percent gives out 90 parts of useful work for every 100 you put in. The other 10 parts become warmth. Oil on an axle, a smooth wheel and a ball bearing all cut the rubbing. Adding more machines adds more rubbing, and their losses multiply. Two machines that are each 90 percent efficient together give 0.9 times 0.9, which is 81 percent. That is why more machines are not always better.
- e.g. A dry wooden pulley squeals and warms up, so it wastes more than an oiled one.
- e.g. A bicycle chain with oil on it wastes less of the rider's effort.
- e.g. Two machines of 90 percent each give 81 percent together.
### Gears: trading turning strength for speed · *defined*
**Definition:** A gear is a wheel with teeth. When two gears mesh, the smaller one turns more times than the bigger one. The ratio is the number of teeth on the big gear divided by the number on the small one.

A bicycle has a big gear at the pedals and a small gear at the back wheel. The big gear has about 44 teeth and the small one has about 18. For every turn of the pedals, the chain moves 44 teeth. That is enough to turn the small gear 44 divided by 18, which is about 2.4 times. So one turn of your feet turns the wheel 2.4 times, and the bicycle goes fast. But there is a price. The wheel turns with less strength than your feet put in. If you want strength, for example to climb a hill, you choose a bigger gear at the back. Then the wheel turns fewer times for each pedal turn, but it turns with more strength. This is the same trade as the ropes. Strength and speed can be swapped, but you cannot get both. A belt drive, like the string on a charkha, does the same, with two wheels and a belt instead of teeth.
- e.g. A gear of 40 teeth turning a gear of 10 teeth makes the small one spin 4 times for each turn.
- e.g. A cycle's low gear for a hill has a big gear at the back and turns the wheel fewer times.
- e.g. A charkha's big wheel turns a thin spindle many times, so the thread twists quickly.
### The screw: a ramp wound round a rod · *defined*
**Definition:** A screw is a ramp wound round and round a rod. The pitch is how far it moves forward in one full turn.

Imagine a ramp of 30 centimetres that climbs 1 centimetre. It is a very gentle slope, so a small push moves a load up it. Now wind that ramp round a pencil. It becomes a spiral, the thread of a screw. One turn of the screw takes you along one gap of the thread, which is the pitch. If the pitch is 2 millimetres, then 10 turns move the screw 20 millimetres. Each turn is a long, gentle walk up the ramp, so a small twist makes a strong push forward. That is why a screw can hold two pieces of wood together so tightly, and why a jar lid squeezes the jar shut. A screw jack lifts a car this way. The Archimedes screw, a spiral inside a tube, lifts water. It was used to water fields in ancient Egypt, and later in many other places.
- e.g. A jam jar lid goes down a few millimetres each full turn.
- e.g. A screw jack lifts a car with turns of a handle.
- e.g. An Archimedes screw lifts water up a tube when its spiral is turned.
### The wedge: two ramps that push things apart · *defined*
**Definition:** A wedge is two ramps back to back, with a thin edge at the front. Pushing it in pushes the material aside, with a force bigger than the push, over a smaller distance.

When you push a wedge into a log, the thin edge goes in only a short way, while the two sloping sides push the wood apart. The thinner the wedge, the longer and gentler its sloping sides, and the more it multiplies your push. A knife is a wedge. When it is sharpened, it gets thinner, so it cuts with less push. A blunt knife needs more push because it is a thick wedge. Your front teeth are wedges, for biting through an apple. A nail, a needle and the bow of a boat are wedges as well. A wedge works best along a line where the material wants to split. Wood splits along the grain far more easily than across it, and an axe or a knife slips in along the weak line.
- e.g. A knife gets easier to use when it is sharpened, because the wedge is thinner.
- e.g. A door stop is a wedge that holds a door by jamming under it.
- e.g. A chisel splits wood along the grain much more easily than across it.
### Six machines, two ideas · *defined*
**Definition:** The lever, the wheel and axle and the pulley are all kinds of lever. The ramp, the wedge and the screw are all kinds of ramp. Both ideas follow one rule: force times distance stays the same.

In Class 3 you met six simple machines. Look closely and they share their insides. A pulley is a lever with equal arms bent round into a wheel, and a moving pulley is a lever with arms in a ratio of 2 to 1. A wheel and axle is a lever that goes round and round, with the handle at the long end. A wedge is two ramps back to back, and a screw is a ramp wound round a rod. So six names, two ideas. A lever swaps force and distance by moving a fulcrum. A ramp swaps force and distance by changing the slope. Whichever you pick, the trade is the same. A smaller force goes a longer way. People sorted these machines into six kinds because they look different, and that sorting helps us remember them. But the sorting is ours. Nature has only one rule underneath.
- e.g. A moving pulley is a lever in which the load hangs from the middle and the effort is at the end.
- e.g. A screw is a ramp wound round a rod.
- e.g. A wheel and axle, such as a well winch, is a lever that goes round.
### Compound machines: machines made from machines · *defined*
**Definition:** A compound machine is made of two or more simple machines. Its total advantage is found by multiplying the advantages of its parts, but each part also adds friction.

A pair of scissors is two levers, joined at a screw or rivet, with a wedge on the end of each lever. A bicycle holds levers (the pedals and the brakes), wheels and axles, gears and screws. A wheelbarrow is a lever with a wheel at the end. A pressure cooker lid is a screw-like lock. A hand pump has a lever handle and a piston. In every one, ask the same two questions. What does each part trade, force for distance or the other way round? And where does the friction come from? If a lever with an advantage of 3 pushes a wedge with an advantage of 4, together they multiply to 12. But the friction in both parts takes a share of the work.
- e.g. Scissors: two levers and two wedges.
- e.g. A bicycle: levers, wheels and axles, gears and screws.
- e.g. A wheelbarrow: a lever with a wheel at the end.
### Machines smaller than a speck: motors inside living cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Living cells contain tiny machines made of protein. One of them is a rotary motor that turns like a wheel on an axle.

A bacterium swims by spinning a long thread called a flagellum. In 1973, Howard Berg and Robert Anderson suggested that it turns round like a propeller on a motor. In 1974, Michael Silverman and Melvin Simon proved it. They stuck the thread to a glass slide, and the whole cell spun round instead. The motor can turn hundreds of times a second. Another tiny motor, called ATP synthase, sits in the cells of plants, animals and bacteria. It turns like a wheel on an axle and makes the fuel molecule ATP. In 1997 Hiroyuki Noji and his team in Japan fixed part of one to glass, joined a tiny glowing filament to its axle, and watched it turn under a microscope. The rule of this unit still holds at this size. Each motor takes energy in and gives some out, and a little escapes as warmth.
- e.g. The bacterium's flagellar motor spins a thread to swim.
- e.g. ATP synthase turns like a wheel and makes the fuel ATP in your cells, and in every plant.
- e.g. Muscle proteins in your arm work like tiny levers.
### Why machines can never give out more: energy and time · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy is never made from nothing or destroyed, only moved or changed. In 1918 Emmy Noether showed that this follows from the laws of nature being the same on every day.

Everything in this unit has the same ending. However you arrange the ropes, the work that comes out is never more than the work that went in. For thousands of years inventors tried to build machines that gave out extra, and none worked. Energy is the thing that is passed on. It can change from a pull to height, or to warmth, but the total stays. In 1918, the German mathematician Emmy Noether showed something deeper. If the laws of nature do not change from one day to the next, then energy cannot be made or lost. The rule is not about ropes. It is about time. Noether did this at a university where, for years, she was not allowed to be a paid teacher, and her lectures were announced under the professor David Hilbert's name. Physicists say the rule gets tricky when the whole universe is stretching, and this is still being discussed.
- e.g. A pulley, a lever and a ramp all show the same total work in and out.
- e.g. A swing swings lower each time as friction turns its energy into warmth, but the total stays the same.
- e.g. A battery, a bulb and the warm wire together keep the same total energy.
### Who made the first wheels, pulleys and gears? · *idea*
**Definition:** Nobody knows who first made these machines. We have only the oldest things that survived, and some of them are very surprising.

The oldest wheel with an axle that we know of was found in a marsh near Ljubljana in Slovenia in 2002. Tests on the wood show that the wheel is about 5,200 years old. In the Indus cities, such as Harappa, people made little carts with wheels from clay and bronze about 4,500 years ago. The oldest pulleys that we know of come from Mesopotamia and Egypt, perhaps 3,500 years ago or more, but only a few survive, because rope and wood rot. The most amazing old machine is the Antikythera mechanism, pulled from a shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera in 1901. It is more than 2,000 years old and at least 30 of its bronze gears survive. It showed where the Sun and Moon would be and could predict eclipses. After that, no machine so complex is known for over a thousand years. Historians still argue about who made it and where.
- e.g. A wheel with an axle from a marsh in Slovenia, about 5,200 years old.
- e.g. Little wheeled carts from Harappan towns, about 4,500 years old.
- e.g. The Antikythera mechanism, a Greek gear machine more than 2,000 years old.

## Mostly agreed
- A fixed pulley changes the direction of a pull, and a moving pulley divides the pull between the ropes that hold the load. *How we know:* Spring balances and rubber bands show the pull in each rope, and it halves each time the number of supporting ropes doubles.
- A simple machine can make a force smaller, but only by making its distance longer. The work in and the work out are equal in an ideal machine. *How we know:* Careful measurements of levers, ramps, pulleys and gears have always shown force times distance staying equal.
- In real machines, friction turns some of the work into warmth, so the work out is always a little less than the work in. *How we know:* Pulleys, axles and gears feel warm after use, and oiling them reduces the loss.
- A gear that turns more slowly gives more turning strength, and a gear that turns faster gives less. *How we know:* Cyclists use low gears for hills and high gears on flat roads, and the tooth counts predict the turns exactly.
- A screw is a ramp wound round a rod, and a wedge is two ramps back to back. *How we know:* If you unwrap the thread of a screw, it makes a ramp of a fixed slope.
- Living cells contain tiny rotary motors that turn like wheels on axles. *How we know:* Silverman and Simon in 1974 and Noji and others in 1997 watched them turn under microscopes.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei, an Italian teacher and scientist (1564 to 1642), answering a Greek book of about 2,300 years before, Mechanical Problems, about 300 BCE. Le Meccaniche, in the 1590s..** About 2,300 years ago, a short Greek book called Mechanical Problems asked why a small push can lift a heavy stone with a lever. Its unknown author called this a wonder, a trick played on nature, and explained it by saying that the long end of a lever moves along a bigger circle. Some people after that hoped a clever machine could win something for nothing. In the 1590s, an Italian teacher named Galileo Galilei wrote a short work for his students called Le Meccaniche. He went through the lever, the pulley, the wheel and axle, the screw and the ramp. He showed that every one of them follows one rule. What a machine gains in force, it loses in distance. So there is no trick and no wonder, only a trade. The rule was later written in the language of energy.  
*What people believed before:* The Greek book called the lever a wonder that beats nature, and some people hoped a clever machine could win something for free. Galileo showed that every machine pays for a smaller force with a longer distance.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A pulley makes the load lighter.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels easier to pull down than to lift.  
  Against: A single pulley changes only the direction of the pull. The pull is the same as the weight of the load. Only more ropes holding the load make the pull smaller.
- **With enough clever wheels, a machine can give out more work than you put in.** (Inventors of 'perpetual motion' machines, for hundreds of years.; *rejected*)  
  For: Levers and pulleys seem to multiply your strength.  
  Against: They multiply force but shorten the distance, so the work is the same. Every attempt to beat the trade has failed.
- **The more machines you add, the easier the job becomes.** (Some students.; *rejected*)  
  For: Each machine can multiply the force.  
  Against: Each machine also adds friction and wastes a share of the work. Two machines of 90 percent efficiency give only 81 percent.
- **A bigger gear always makes things go faster.** (Some students.; *rejected*)  
  For: A big gear at the pedals turns a small gear many times.  
  Against: It depends on which gear drives which. A big gear driving a small one is faster. A small gear driving a big one is slower, but stronger.
- **Archimedes could have moved the Earth with a lever if he had a long enough one and a place to stand.** (Archimedes' saying, written down by later writers such as Pappus of Alexandria.; *debated*)  
  For: The law of the lever has no upper limit on the size of the load.  
  Against: The lever would have to be roughly as wide as our whole galaxy, and he would have to push his end for that whole length to lift the Earth by one centimetre. Nobody can build a fixed place to stand.

## Roots
We have counted ropes, turned gears and found that every machine trades a smaller pull for a longer way. Now we ask why that trade never changes, and what it tells us.

**Deep truth.** With every set of reels, the hand moved exactly as much further as the pull got smaller. In 1918 Emmy Noether showed why. If the laws of nature work the same on every day, energy cannot be made from nothing. So no machine can cheat, because cheating would need nature to change its rules from day to day.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat 'you cannot get something for nothing' as a fact about ropes and levers, a limit of the tools that we make. It is a wider rule, tied to how time works.  
*Evidence:* In the rope test, a fixed reel only turned the pull round, and a moving reel halved the pull and doubled the distance. Force times distance came out about the same each time, a little worse because of friction.

- *who am i:* You can lift more than your strength alone if you share the load between ropes. What you can do depends on how you arrange things.
- *what is life:* Inside every cell of your body, tiny motors turn like wheels on axles, and they follow the same trade as a pulley.
- *what is this world:* No machine anywhere, from a crane to a cell, has beaten the trade of force for distance. That is because the world's laws stay the same on every day.

Six machines turned out to be two ideas, and the two ideas turned out to be one trade. Behind the trade is a single rule about energy, which Emmy Noether linked to the way time behaves. Rope, wood, steel and protein all follow it. The many tools and cells are different shapes of the same rule. A rope pulled by a girl, a bullock or a motor passes on energy it received from somewhere, and that, in the end, came from the Sun.

- **Tao Te Ching 77:** The way of heaven is like bending a bow. What is high is brought down, and what is low is raised. It takes from what has too much and gives to what has too little. (A lever or pulley takes from the distance and gives to the force, so the two stay in balance.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 51:** People do not see how something that pulls against itself can still hold together, like the tension of a bow and a lyre. (A rope that is pulled at both ends holds a load. The pull in the rope and the weight of the load hold each other in balance.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 3:** Cook Ding used one knife for 19 years to cut up thousands of oxen, and it stayed as sharp as new. He did not cut through the bones, but slid his thin blade through the gaps between them. (A wedge works best along a line where a material already wants to split, so the thinnest edge does the most with the least push.)
- Science (settled): Emmy Noether's theorem of 1918: every steady symmetry of nature's laws has a conserved quantity. Laws that are the same on every day mean that energy is conserved.
- Science (debated): In the whole expanding universe, energy is harder to define, and physicists still argue about whether it is conserved overall.
- Other view, Henri Poincare, a French mathematician, in 1902: Poincare argued that the rule of energy may partly be our choice of how to keep books. When energy seemed to go missing in some atoms in the 1920s, Wolfgang Pauli suggested in 1930 a new invisible particle rather than give up the rule. The neutrino was found in 1956. Poincare would say we chose to keep the rule, and nature agreed. Others say nature chose the rule first.

- Ask: If nature has only one trade, why do we count six machines?
- Ask: Is the rule that machines never give more than they take a discovery about nature, or a choice that we made about how to keep count?
- Ask: If the laws of nature changed from day to day, what would a machine do?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A ramp has no moving parts. Is a ramp really a machine, and is a hill a machine too? (hint: Think about what makes something a machine: its parts, or what it does to a push.)
- *deep:* The six simple machines are two ideas. Who decided how to count them, nature or people? (hint: Think about what we notice first, and what the rule underneath says.)
- *deep:* No machine has ever given out more than it took in. Is that a fact about machines, or about the universe? (hint: Think about Emmy Noether's idea about the laws being the same on every day.)
- *deep:* A bicycle is slow up a hill in a high gear and fast on the flat in a low one. Why can't we have both at once? (hint: Think about the rope test, where you could swap pull for distance but not get both.)
- *big:* A bacterium has a tiny wheel on an axle, but no wheel ever evolved in the legs of a large animal. Why might that be? (hint: Think about blood, nerves and the way a wheel needs a clear path all around it.)
- *big:* A machine takes the same work from a strong person and a weak person. Is it fair that machines are mostly built for the people who already have power? (hint: Think about who owns a crane, a well pump or a mill, and who works it.)
- *big:* Some people ask whether an ant, a plant's root or a cell could be called a machine. What is a living body, if it is more than a machine? (hint: Think about the tiny motors in your cells, and whether a machine can decide anything.)
- *big:* When a bullock pulls a rope, the bullock is the engine and the pulley is the machine. Who is doing the work? (hint: Think about where the energy came from: the grass, and before the grass, the Sun.)

## Story: Twice the Rope
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Leather-bucket well lifts pulled by bullocks are real, and are called a mote in Karnataka and a charsa in the North. The names and what happened are imagined.*

On the farm near Gadag where Nagaveni lived, the well was older than anyone in the family. Her grandmother, Ajji, still watered the jowar every morning with the old well lift.

It was a leather bag as big as a barrel, hanging from a thick rope. The rope ran over a wooden wheel at the edge of the well and then along the ground to two bullocks, Bangara and Rama. When the bullocks walked down the sloping path, the rope pulled, and the bag came up full of water. At the top, it tipped into the channel. Then the bullocks walked back, and the bag went down again.

That summer the rain did not come. The water sank lower. Each morning, the bag had to climb a longer way, and each morning, it came up heavier with mud. On the fifth day, Bangara stopped halfway down the path. He leaned into the yoke and breathed hard. The rope stayed tight and the bag stayed in the dark of the well.

'They are tired,' said Ajji. 'They are too old for this well now.'

Their neighbour, Basavanna, was leaning on the wall. 'Buy a third bullock,' he said. 'Or wait for the rain. There is no other way.'

Nagaveni had come back from school that week with a lesson in her head. In class, her teacher had hung a bottle of water from two thread reels and a string. A rubber band at the end of the string showed how hard each pull was. With the right reels, the band stretched only half as far.

'Ajji,' she said, 'what if the bag hung from a second wheel?'

Basavanna laughed. 'A wheel cannot make water lighter, child.'

'No,' said Nagaveni. 'The water weighs the same. But look.' She took a stick and drew in the dust. 'Now there is one rope going up to the wheel at the top. If the bag hangs on a wheel of its own, in the loop of the rope, there are two ropes holding the bag. Each rope holds half. Bangara will feel half.'

'And where does the other half go?' asked Basavanna.

'Nowhere,' she said. 'It is paid in walking. To raise the bag by one step, both ropes must get one step shorter. So the bullocks must walk two steps. The bag will come up half as fast.'

Basavanna rubbed his chin. 'Then it is the same work. They walk twice as far.'

'The same work,' Nagaveni agreed. 'But Bangara can pull a certain weight, and he stops at a bit more than that. He is not too tired to walk. He is too weak for this pull. If the pull is half, he can walk all day.'

Ajji looked at the well, and then at the path. The path ran down the slope, past the cowshed and as far as the stone wall of the field. It was twelve metres long. The water was now eight metres down.

'Twice eight is sixteen,' said Nagaveni, who had counted. 'The path is only twelve metres, Ajji. We need four more.'

Everyone looked at Basavanna, because the wall beyond was his. He sighed. Then he walked to the wall and lifted down the top three stones. 'Let them turn along my field edge,' he said. 'Just this summer.'

They tied the end of the rope to the wooden frame. Nagaveni hung the bag from a spare wooden wheel from the carpenter, and ran the rope round it and back up. Then she tied her school rubber band into the rope, to see how hard the pull was. With one rope, the band had stretched long and thin. Now it stretched only a little more than half as far.

'A little more than half,' she said. 'The wheels are dry. They are rubbing.'

Ajji went to the kitchen and came back with a little coconut oil on a rag. She rubbed it into the axle of both wheels. The squeal went away, and the band shortened a little more.

Bangara and Rama leaned into the yoke. The bag rose slowly out of the dark with the water swinging in it. The bullocks walked down the path, and past the cowshed, and along the edge of Basavanna's field, a long, slow walk. Neither of them stopped.

When the bag tipped into the channel and the water ran down between the rows of jowar, Basavanna took off his cap.

'Twice the rope,' he said. 'Half the pull.'

'And twice the time,' said Nagaveni. 'There is always a price.'

Ajji watched the water run. 'It is a fair price,' she said. 'The bullocks pay it in walking, and they have legs for that.'

**For the parent:** Count the ropes that hold the load. Each rope carries an equal share. The pull is the load divided by the number of ropes, and the walk is that many times longer.

- Ask: Why did the bullocks have to walk twice as far when the bag hung from two ropes?
- Ask: What would happen to the pull and to the walk if the bag hung from four ropes?
- Ask: Why did the oil on the wheels make the pull a little smaller?

  - Think: Is there any card where you could get a smaller pull and a shorter distance at the same time?

## Experiments
### e1 · Count the Ropes: how much easier does a pulley make a lift, and what does it cost? (45 min)
- **question:** A bottle of water hangs from a rubber band. Does the band stretch less if the string goes over one fixed reel, or if a moving reel shares the load between two strings? And how far must the string move?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. In which of the three ways will the rubber band stretch least? Will the string move further in any of them? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same bottle, the same rubber band and the same 10 centimetre lift are used every time. Only the way the string runs changes. The straight lift is the control. Each way is done twice, so one odd try does not mislead you.
- **safety:** A grown-up must check that the beam is tied down and holds the bottle. A full bottle weighs about 1 kilogram, and the fixed reel carries about double that when you pull, so use heavy chairs with books on the seats, or a window grill. Keep feet away from under the bottle in case it falls. Keep your face away from the stretched band in case it snaps.
- **record:** For each set-up, write the stretch of the band on both tries, and how far the knot moved for a 10 centimetre lift. Measure the knot, not your hand, because the band stretches. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each set-up, using the bigger of the two stretches.
- **results table:** Set-up | Ropes holding the bottle | Band stretch in centimetres: try 1 | Band stretch in centimetres: try 2 | Distance the knot (where the string meets the band) moved for a 10 centimetre lift, in centimetres
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bottle, the reels, the band and the string. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write both at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will lift this bottle three ways. First straight up. Then over one reel. Then with a moving reel as well. In which way will the band stretch least? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three ways to lift a bottle. Which way stretches the band least? Why?
  - Ask: Do you think the string will move further in any of the three ways? (good: Yes, in the one with more string.; No, the bottle lifts the same height.; I am not sure.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say more reels always make it easier. Write down exactly what they say and why.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Thread string through the hole of each reel and knot it into a loop. Tie a loop round the bottle's neck. Tie the broom handle across two chair backs. Check it holds the bottle.  
  *Parent says:* Each reel hangs from its own string loop and can spin on it. Hang one reel from the beam. This is our fixed reel. Now measure the band with nothing on it, and write that length down.  
  *Child mode:* Make a string loop through each reel. Hang one from the beam. Measure the band with nothing on it.
  - Note: A grown-up should do the beam and check it. The thread reels from a tailor are free. If a reel will not spin easily, widen the loop. A bangle works too, but it rubs more, so your pull will be bigger. That is part of the lesson. With 2 to 4 children, give each a job and swap for every test: one pulls and holds the band, one reads the ruler, one marks the knot's height, one writes. One child with a parent: the child pulls, the parent reads and writes.
  - Check: Why do you measure the band with nothing hanging on it first? → **So you can work out how much it stretched**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test A. Tie the bottle's string to the band. Hold the band's top end. Lift the bottle until its base is level with the top of the 10 centimetre stack of books. Hold still and read the band. Do it twice. Then pour out half the water, hang the half-full bottle from the band, and write that stretch too. It shows what 'half the pull' looks like on your band.  
  *Parent says:* Lift slowly, then hold still, and read the band's length. The stretch is the length now minus the length with nothing on it. Write it down. Do it twice.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the bottle from the band. Lift it 10 centimetres. Hold still. Read the band.
  - Ask: Why do we hold still before we read the band? (good: Because a moving band is hard to read.; So we get the same answer each time.; So the pull is steady.)
  - Note: A good band stretches about 6 to 12 centimetres (typical). If it stretches less than 4, use a thinner band. If it nearly snaps, use two bands side by side. Measure the knot's height with the bottle resting and the string just tight, and again with the bottle 10 centimetres up: it moves about 10 centimetres. A rubber band slowly creeps longer, so read it after about 3 seconds of holding still, the same every time. The half-full bottle gives you your own 'half' to compare with Test C.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test B. Tie one end of the string to the bottle's loop. Run it up over the fixed reel and down to the band. Hold the band's other end and pull down until the bottle is 10 centimetres up. Hold still and read the band. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Now the string goes over the reel. You pull down. Lift the bottle until it reaches the same height. Hold still. What does the band say? How far did the knot move?  
  *Child mode:* String over the reel. Pull down. Lift the bottle 10 centimetres. Read the band.
  - Note: The stretch is usually the same, or a little bigger, because of rubbing. The knot moves 10 centimetres, down. Measure its height at the start and at the end if you want to be sure.
  - Check: In Test B the band stretches about the same as in Test A. What did the fixed reel do? → **It changed the direction of the pull**
- **s5 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test C. Tie one end of the string to the beam. Run it down under the second reel, which the bottle hangs from. Then take it up over the fixed reel and down to the band. Pull down until the bottle is 10 centimetres up. Hold still, read the band and measure the knot's height at the start and the end. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Now the bottle hangs from a moving reel. Count the strings that touch the moving reel. How many hold the bottle? Pull down and lift it 10 centimetres. What does the band say now? How far did the knot go?  
  *Child mode:* Hang the bottle from the moving reel. Count the strings holding it. Pull. Read the band.
  - Ask: How many strings hold up the bottle in this set-up? (good: Two.; Two, because the string goes under the reel and back up.; The one over the top does not hold it, it only turns the pull.)
  - Note: The stretch is usually a little more than half of Test A, because of rubbing, and a rubber band is not an even spring, so compare it with your half-full-bottle reading. The knot should move about 20 centimetres. If the stretch is exactly the same as A, check that the string runs under the moving reel. Count the strings that touch it. If nothing seems to change, the reels may be rubbing too hard: widen the loops or use smoother reels.
  - Check: In Test C the band stretches about half as much as in Test A, and the knot moves about twice as far. What does this show? → **Two strings share the load, so each carries half, but all the string must be pulled twice as far**
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar chart with one bar for each way, using the bigger of the two stretches. Then ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see in the table? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each way. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the fixed reel only changed direction, and that the moving reel halved the pull but doubled the distance. After they have said what they saw, explain it. A bottle of 1 kilogram needs about 10 newtons. Lifting it 0.1 metres is 1 joule of work. With two strings, the pull is about 5 newtons and the knot moves 0.2 metres, which is again 1 joule. The extra stretch in your results is friction.
  - Think: The stretch got smaller and the knot moved further. What stayed the same?
### e2 · Bicycle Gears: how many times does the wheel turn for one turn of the pedals? (30 min)
- **question:** When you turn the pedal of a bicycle once, how many times does the back wheel turn, and does the number of teeth on the gears tell you?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many times will the back wheel turn for one turn of the pedals? Will it be more than once or less? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same bicycle, the same gear and the same tape mark are used every time. You turn the pedals exactly one full turn, slowly, three times. Then you compare the counted turns with the number worked out from the teeth.
- **safety:** A grown-up must turn the bicycle upside down onto its seat and handlebars, or lift the back wheel on its stand. Keep fingers away from the chain, the teeth and the spokes, because they can pinch. Chain grease stains clothes, so wear old clothes. Only the grown-up turns the pedal.
- **record:** Write the number of teeth on each gear, the number of wheel turns you counted for each pedal turn, and the number you worked out by dividing the teeth.
- **results table:** Gear | Teeth on the pedal gear | Teeth on the back gear | Wheel turns counted for one pedal turn | Wheel turns worked out from the teeth
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bicycle. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* When I turn the pedals once, how many times will the back wheel turn? Less than once, once, or more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Pedal once. How many turns does the wheel make? Why?
  - Ask: Do you think the back wheel turns faster or slower than the pedals? (good: Faster, because the bicycle goes fast.; Faster, because the pedal gear is big.; The same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write what they say and why.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the bicycle over. Stick tape on the back tyre. Stick tape on one tooth of the pedal gear. Count the teeth on the pedal gear and on the small gear at the back wheel.  
  *Parent says:* Stick the tape on one tooth, so we know where to start. Count all the teeth on the big gear at the pedals. Then count the teeth on the small gear at the back wheel.  
  *Child mode:* Tape one tooth. Count the teeth on the big gear. Count the teeth on the small gear.
  - Note: Single-speed cycles often have about 30 to 48 teeth at the pedals and 14 to 20 at the back (typical; counts differ). If your cycle has many gears, use the gear that the chain is on. Counting the back teeth is easiest if you mark one with tape as well. With 2 to 4 children: one counts the pedal teeth, one counts the back teeth, and then they check each other's counts. A cycle with a carrier or a centre stand may be easier to lift on its stand than to turn over.
  - Check: Why do you stick tape on one tooth before counting? → **So you know where you started and do not count a tooth twice**
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the pedal slowly through one full turn, until the taped tooth is back where it began. Your child counts how many times the taped tyre goes round. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* I will turn the pedal once, slowly. You watch the tape on the tyre. How many times does it go round? Now we do it twice more. Do you get the same number each time?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the tape on the tyre. How many times does it go round for one pedal turn?
  - Ask: Why do we do it three times? (good: To check we get the same number.; One try could be a mistake.; So we can trust the answer.)
  - Note: A turn of 2 and a bit is normal. Count part-turns as halves or quarters. The wheel keeps turning after you stop pedalling, so stop the pedal exactly at the mark. If it is hard to judge, turn the pedal 5 full turns, count the wheel turns and divide by 5. Roles: one child watches the tyre mark, one watches the pedal tooth, one writes; swap each try.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Divide the teeth on the pedal gear by the teeth on the back gear. Write the answer next to your counted turns. If you have a gear change, move to another gear and try again.  
  *Parent says:* Divide the big number by the small number. Is the answer close to what you counted? If it is not, what could have gone wrong?  
  *Child mode:* Big gear teeth divided by small gear teeth. Is it near what you counted?
  - Check: A bicycle has 44 teeth at the pedals and 20 at the back. About how many turns does the back wheel make for one pedal turn? → **About 2 turns**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. If your cycle has gears, ask which gear they would use for a hill.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you count? Why do you think the wheel turned that many times?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says the wheel turns more than once, because the pedal gear has more teeth than the back gear. After they have said what they saw, add this: a bigger gear at the back gives fewer turns of the wheel for each pedal turn, but each turn is stronger. That is why people choose it for hills.
  - Think: Would you choose a bigger or a smaller gear at the back for a hill? Why?

### a1 · Paper Screw: wind a ramp round a pencil (25 min)

  *Child mode:* Count the turns to open the lid. Is the gap big or small?
  - Note: A bottle neck usually has a pitch of about 2 to 3 millimetres, and the cap needs about one to one and a half turns (typical). Some jam jar lids are the twist-off kind that need only a quarter turn, so use the bottle if so.
  - Think: Would a screw with a smaller pitch need more turns or fewer? Would it be easier or harder to turn?
### a2 · Clock Gears: why does the hour hand go 12 times slower? (15 min)
- **record:** How many times the minute hand went round while the hour hand went round once, and what that tells you about the gears inside.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set both hands of the clock to 12. Ask your child for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Both hands start at 12. The minute hand will go round and round. How many times must it go round before the hour hand goes round once? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Both hands at 12. How many minute turns for one hour-hand turn? Why?
  - Ask: How many times do you think the minute hand goes round for one turn of the hour hand? (good: 12, because there are 12 hours.; 24.; I am not sure.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is good.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the setting knob slowly, with a grown-up. Each time the minute hand reaches 12, write down which number the hour hand points at. Keep going until the hour hand is back at 12. Write down how many turns it took.  
  *Parent says:* Turn the knob slowly. Each time the long hand reaches 12, make a mark. How many marks before the short hand is back at 12?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the knob slowly. Count each time the long hand reaches 12.
  - Note: Only turn the knob in the direction that the clock allows, and never force it. Do not open the clock. On an alarm clock use the time knob, not the alarm knob.    **Answer:** He would have to push his end an unimaginably long distance for the Earth to move even a tiny bit. The lever would be as long as a galaxy.. *Why:* A strong answer says a lever trades force for distance, so a small push on an enormous load needs a huge distance.
14. (perspective) Farida and Ishaan are arguing about a ramp.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side, gives a reason, and says what evidence or definition would change their mind.. *Why:* It depends on what we mean by a machine. If we mean 'moving parts', a ramp is not one. If we mean 'something that trades force for distance', it is. The disagreement is about the word, not about the ramp.
15. (write) Physicists once saw energy go missing in some atoms. Wolfgang Pauli guessed an invisible particle instead of giving up the rule, and it was found 26 years later. Was that cheating or good science? Give one reason.  
   **Answer:** It was good science, because the guess could be tested and was found. The rule was kept because it had worked everywhere else, and the new particle was real.. *Why:* A strong answer says the guess made a prediction that could be checked, and that it was checked. A weaker answer says it is cheating without noticing that the particle was found.
16. (draw) Draw a block and tackle with 3 ropes holding a load of 30 newtons. Write the pull in each rope and how far your hand moves for a 1 metre lift.  
   **Answer:** A load hanging from a moving block with 3 ropes going up to a fixed block, each rope labelled 10 newtons, and a note that the hand moves 3 metres.. *Why:* Three ropes share 30 newtons, so each carries 10 newtons, and the pull is 10 newtons. The hand moves 3 times the lift, which is 3 metres.
17. (match) Match each tiny machine to its job.  
   **Answer:** The motor at the base of a bacterium's thread → spins the thread so the bacterium swims; ATP synthase → turns to make the cell's fuel molecule ATP; The head of a muscle protein → pulls like a tiny lever to shorten the muscle. *Why:* A bacterium swims by spinning a thread with a rotary motor. ATP synthase turns like a wheel on an axle to make ATP. A muscle protein pulls like a tiny lever to shorten the muscle.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A flagpole has one pulley at the top. What does it do for you?  
   Options: It lets you pull down to raise the flag / It makes the flag weigh less / It halves the pull  
   **Answer:** It lets you pull down to raise the flag. *Why:* A single fixed pulley changes the direction of the pull, so you pull down and the flag goes up. It does not change the size of the pull.
2. (mcq) A load is held by 4 ropes. You pull with the strength of 5 kilograms. What is the load, ignoring friction?  
   Options: 20 kilograms / 5 kilograms / 9 kilograms  
   **Answer:** 20 kilograms. *Why:* 4 ropes each carry 5 kilograms, so the load is 4 times 5, which is 20 kilograms. Picking 9 means the numbers were added.
3. (mcq) To lift a load by 2 metres with a tackle with 2 ropes holding it, how much rope must you pull?  
   Options: 4 metres / 2 metres / 1 metre  
   **Answer:** 4 metres. *Why:* Both ropes must get 2 metres shorter, so 4 metres pass through your hand.
4. (mcq) A machine halves the force you need. What must happen to the distance you move?  
   Options: It doubles / It halves / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It doubles. *Why:* Force times distance stays the same, so if the force halves, the distance doubles. No machine gives a smaller force over the same distance.
5. (mcq) Where does some of your effort go in a real pulley?  
   Options: Into warmth, from rubbing / Into making the rope heavier / Nowhere, it is lost  
   **Answer:** Into warmth, from rubbing. *Why:* Friction turns some of the work into warmth that spreads into the air. The energy is not destroyed. It goes somewhere you did not want it.
6. (mcq) Two machines are used together, and each wastes one tenth of the work. About how much of the work comes out?  
   Options: 81 parts in every 100 / 80 parts in every 100 / 90 parts in every 100  
   **Answer:** 81 parts in every 100. *Why:* 0.9 times 0.9 is 0.81. Losses multiply, so more machines can be worse.
7. (mcq) A cyclist moves the chain from a 16-tooth to a 24-tooth gear at the back. What happens to the wheel?  
   Options: It turns fewer times for each pedal turn, with more strength / It turns more times for each pedal turn / It turns the same number of times  
   **Answer:** It turns fewer times for each pedal turn, with more strength. *Why:* A bigger back gear means a smaller ratio, so fewer wheel turns for each pedal turn. The wheel gains turning strength, which helps uphill.
8. (mcq) What is a screw?  
   Options: A ramp wound round a rod / A wheel with teeth / A lever with a rope  
   **Answer:** A ramp wound round a rod. *Why:* The thread of a screw is a ramp that goes round and round. That is why a small twist gives a strong push forward.
9. (truefalse) A blunt knife needs more push than a sharp knife, because a blunt edge is a thicker wedge.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A thick wedge has steeper sides, so it multiplies the push less. Sharpening makes the wedge thinner.
10. (mcq) The six simple machines are really which two ideas?  
   Options: The lever and the ramp / The wheel and the rope / The push and the pull  
   **Answer:** The lever and the ramp. *Why:* The pulley and the wheel and axle are kinds of lever. The wedge and the screw are kinds of ramp. Both trade force for distance.
11. (mcq) Why is it a good idea to repeat each test in an experiment twice or more?  
   Options: To check that the result is steady and not a fluke / Because the band was tired / To make the bottle lighter  
   **Answer:** To check that the result is steady and not a fluke. *Why:* If two tries are close, we can trust the result. If they differ a lot, one try may have been a fluke, and we need to look for the cause.
12. (mcq) Why has nobody built a machine that gives out more work than it takes in?  
   Options: Energy cannot be made from nothing, so a machine only passes it on / Nobody has tried hard enough / Machines are too small  
   **Answer:** Energy cannot be made from nothing, so a machine only passes it on. *Why:* Every careful test has shown the same result. Physicists link it to the laws of nature being the same on every day.

## Open questions
- **Is energy conserved in the whole expanding universe?** Physicists agree that it is conserved in every small region we can measure, but in an expanding universe the definition becomes hard, and they are still discussing it.
- **How did the Antikythera mechanism's makers learn to cut such small gears so well?** No written plans survive, and no other machine as complex is known for over a thousand years.
- **Who first used pulleys?** Rope and wood rot, so very few pulleys survive, and the oldest ones we know are probably not the first.
- **How do the tiny motors in cells turn so efficiently?** Some measurements suggest that these motors waste very little energy, but scientists still argue about how little. Engineers are trying to copy them.
- **Could a machine ever be built with no friction at all?** Some magnetic and very cold systems come close, but nothing is exactly frictionless, and nobody knows how close we can get.
- **Why did no large animal evolve wheels?** A wheel needs a clear path round it, so blood and nerves cannot cross. Scientists still discuss other reasons, and whether some living things come close.

## Connected maths
- In a made-up bottle lift, the band stretched 9 centimetres in the straight lift and 5 centimetres with the moving reel. How many centimetres less was the stretch with the moving reel? **4 centimetres.**. 9 take away 5 is 4. The moving reel made the band stretch 4 centimetres less. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)
- Look at the bar chart of made-up results. Is the stretch for Test C a little more than half, or a little less than half, of the stretch for Test A? **A little more than half.**. Test A is 9 and half of 9 is 4 and a half. Test C is 5, which is a little more than 4 and a half. The extra comes from friction. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A tackle has 5 ropes and a load of 40 kilograms. Ignoring friction, what is the pull? In real life you pull with the strength of 10 kilograms. What percent of your effort is useful? **8 kilograms, and 80 percent.**. 40 divided by 5 is 8. The useful share is 8 divided by 10, which is 0.8, or 80 percent. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In the bottle lift, the knot moved 20 centimetres for a 10 centimetre lift with 2 ropes. How far would it move for a 30 centimetre lift with 4 ropes holding the load? **120 centimetres.**. With 4 ropes the knot moves 4 times the lift. 4 times 30 is 120 centimetres. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- A bicycle has 48 teeth on the pedal gear and 16 on the back gear. You turn the pedals 10 times. The wheel goes 2 metres in each turn. How far does the bicycle go? **60 metres.**. 48 divided by 16 is 3, so the wheel turns 3 times for each pedal turn. 10 times 3 is 30 wheel turns. 30 times 2 is 60 metres. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A girl lifts a 2 kilogram bag, which pulls down with about 20 newtons, up by 0.5 metres. How many joules of work is that? Then she lifts the same bag by the same height with a 4-rope tackle. What pull does she use, and how far does her hand move? **10 joules. A pull of 5 newtons and 2 metres.**. 20 times 0.5 is 10 joules. With 4 ropes, the pull is 20 divided by 4, which is 5 newtons. The hand moves 4 times 0.5, which is 2 metres. 5 times 2 is 10 joules again. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A screw has a pitch of 1.5 millimetres. How many full turns move it 12 millimetres? **8 turns.**. 12 divided by 1.5 is 8. Each turn moves it 1.5 millimetres, so 8 turns move it 12 millimetres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L4`)
- A lever has a push arm 1000 times as long as its load arm. What is the biggest load, in kilograms, that a 50 kilogram person could balance by pushing with all their weight? How far would their end move for the load to move 1 centimetre? **50,000 kilograms. 1000 centimetres, which is 10 metres.**. 50 times 1000 is 50,000 kilograms. Their end moves 1000 times as far as the load, so 1 centimetre of lift needs 1000 centimetres of push, which is 10 metres. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
