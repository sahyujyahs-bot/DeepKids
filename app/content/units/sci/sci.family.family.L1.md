# My family: who lives with us, and how we know each other
`sci.family.family.L1`

**Summary.** With your eyes shut, can you find Amma or Ammachi just by sniffing a shirt they wore? Most children can find at least one person. Every person has their own smell, and much of it is made by tiny living beings on their skin. People who live together pass these tiny beings to each other. Families come in many kinds: big and small, with one parent or two, with grandparents, by birth or by adoption. In this unit your child sniffs family cloths in a fair test, passes a smell down a line of hands, and meets families of many kinds, in people and other animals.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes. Someone in your family hugs you. Can you tell who it is without looking?* You know your family by their faces and their voices. But your nose knows them too. Every person has their own smell, a little different from everyone else's. A new baby learns the smell of the people who hold them. In this unit we find out if you can tell your family apart by smell alone. We also meet many kinds of families, in people and in other animals.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say who is in my family and who looks after me.
1. By the end I can name some different kinds of families, in people and in other animals.
2. By the end I can find some of my family by smell alone, in a fair test.
3. By the end I can say that tiny living beings called microbes live on our skin, and pass between people who live together.
4. By the end I can say why we cover our eyes and try twice to make a test fair.

## Terms
- **Family**: A family is a group of people who belong to each other and look after each other. *e.g.* Nila's family is her Amma, her Appa, her Ammachi and her little brother Arjun.
- **Relative**: A relative is someone in your family, near or far, like a grandmother, an aunt or a cousin. *e.g.* Revathi Chithi lives in Chennai, far from Nila. She is still Nila's relative.
- **Adopt**: To adopt a child is to take them into your family as your own son or daughter, for always. *e.g.* Revathi adopted baby Kayal. Kayal is Revathi's daughter now, for her whole life.
- **Smell**: Smell is the sense that tells you what is in the air, through your nose. *e.g.* You smell sambar before you see the pot, because a little of it floats into your nose.
- **Microbes**: Microbes are tiny living beings, far too small to see. Some of them live on your skin. *e.g.* Millions of microbes live on your hands right now. Most of them are friendly.

## Explanations
### What a family is · *debated*
**Definition:** A family is a group of people who belong to each other and look after each other.

Every child grows up with someone who feeds them, holds them and keeps them safe. Those people are the child's family. Some people join a family by being born into it. Some join by marriage, like Amma and Appa. Some join by adoption, like baby Kayal. People do not all agree on who counts as family. Here are the main ideas.
- e.g. Nila lives with her Amma, her Appa, her Ammachi and her brother Arjun in a flat in Kochi.
- e.g. Farzana lives with her Ammi and her Nani in Hyderabad. Her Abba works in Dubai and calls every night.
- e.g. Kayal was adopted by Revathi in Chennai when she was a baby. Revathi is her mother.
- **Some people:** Family means the people you are related to, by birth, by marriage or by adoption. Cousins in another country are family, even if you have never met them.
- **Other people:** Family means the people who live with you and look after you. A neighbour aunty who cares for you every day can feel like family.
### Families come in many kinds · *defined*
**Definition:** Families can be big or small, and can be made in different ways. Each kind is a real family.

Some families are small, with one parent and one child. Some are big joint families, with grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins under one roof. Some children are raised by a grandmother, an aunt or one parent. Some parents work in another city and come home when they can. Other animals have many kinds of families too. In an elephant family, mothers, daughters, aunts and young ones stay together, and an older female often leads. Grown-up males leave. An ant family has one mother, the queen, and thousands of daughters. Almost every ant you see is a sister of the ants beside her.
- e.g. Meenakshi's joint family in Madurai: Paati, Thatha, Amma, Appa, Periyamma, Periyappa, Meenakshi and two cousins, all in one house.
- e.g. Joyita in Kolkata lives with her Baba. It is just the two of them, and her Dida visits on Sundays.
- e.g. The ants on your kitchen floor are sisters. They all came from one mother queen in the nest.
### How we know our family by smell · *defined*
**Definition:** Every person has a body smell of their own. People can learn the smells of the people they live with, and know them again.

You may not notice it, but every person has their own smell. You notice it most when you hug someone, or hold something they wore. Scientists have tested this. Brothers, sisters and mothers can pick out a family member's worn T-shirt by smell alone. A new mother can learn her baby's smell in less than an hour. Other animals do this too. A mother sheep smells her new lamb and remembers that smell. Ants touch each other with their feelers, and know a sister by her smell.
- e.g. Many children sleep better with Amma's old saree or dupatta, because it smells of her.
- e.g. A dog greets each person at the door by sniffing them first.
- e.g. Two ants meet on the wall, touch feelers for a moment, and walk on together.
### The tiny living beings we share · *idea*
Your skin is home to millions of microbes, tiny living beings too small to see. When you sweat, these microbes feed on the sweat. As they feed, they make much of your smell. When people live together, they hug, hold hands and touch the same things. So they pass microbes to each other. In 2013, scientists in the United States found that people in one home share more skin microbes than people from different homes. A husband and wife, who are not related by birth, shared many microbes too. People who had a dog even shared microbes with their dog. Scientists do not yet know if the shared microbes stay for long, or come and go after each touch.
- e.g. Ammachi hugs Arjun every morning. Each hug passes a few microbes between them.
- e.g. Everyone at home drinks from the same steel tumblers and sits on the same sofa.
- e.g. A pet dog licks everyone's hands, and shares microbes with the whole family.
- **Scientists who found the sharing:** People who live together share more skin microbes than people who live apart, so living together shapes the tiny beings on us.
- **Scientists who are still checking:** Some shared microbes may only be passed on for a short time after a touch, and then go away. We need more tests to know which ones stay.

## Mostly agreed
- People can know their family members by smell alone. *How we know:* In 1981, Richard Porter and John Moore in the United States found that brothers, sisters and mothers could pick out a family member's worn T-shirt by smell. Many tests since then have found the same.
- Much of a person's body smell is made by microbes on the skin. *How we know:* Fresh sweat has very little smell. The smell comes when skin microbes feed on the sweat. Scientists have shown this by growing the microbes on sweat in a lab.
- Families come in many kinds, and every kind can give a child love and care. *How we know:* Children grow up well in big families, small families, with one parent, with grandparents and with adopted parents. What matters most is having grown-ups who care for them every day.

## How we found out
**Paul Broca, a French doctor (1879); Richard Porter and John Moore in the United States (1981); Marsha Kaitz and her team at the Hebrew University in Jerusalem (1987); John McGann, a brain scientist in the United States (2017), 1879 to 2017.** For a long time, many scientists thought people had a poor sense of smell. In 1879, Paul Broca, a French doctor who studied the brain, put humans in a group of 'non-smellers'. He said the smell part of the human brain was small next to the rest of the brain. Most people believed this for over 100 years. Then scientists began to test real noses. In 1981, Richard Porter and John Moore in the United States asked children to wear plain T-shirts. Then their brothers, sisters and mothers sniffed the shirts. They could pick out their own family member's shirt by smell alone. In 1987, Marsha Kaitz and her team in Jerusalem tested new mothers. After less than an hour with their babies, nine out of ten mothers could pick out their own baby's shirt by smell. In 2017, the brain scientist John McGann wrote that the weak human nose is a myth. The smell part of the human brain has about as many nerve cells as in many other mammals.  
*What people believed before:* People thought humans had a weak sense of smell, much weaker than other mammals. Tests showed that people can know their own family, and a mother her new baby, by smell alone.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Humans have a weak sense of smell. Only dogs and other animals really use their noses.** (A French doctor, Paul Broca, in 1879, and most people for over 100 years after him; *rejected*)  
  For: A dog's nose is much better than ours at many smells. The smell part of the human brain is small next to the rest of the brain.  
  Against: When scientists tested real people, they found humans can tell their own family by smell. The smell part of the human brain has about as many nerve cells as in many other mammals. In 2017, the brain scientist John McGann called the weak human nose a myth.
- **A child who was adopted is not a real part of the family.** (An old idea some people still hold; *rejected*)  
  For: An adopted child was not born to the parents.  
  Against: In India, the law says an adopted child is the son or daughter of the family in every way, for life. An adopted child is fed, held and loved like any other child. People who live together even share the same skin microbes.
- **Your body smell comes from the family you are born into.** (Some scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Twins who look the same smell more alike than other brothers and sisters. So genes play a part in body smell.  
  Against: Food, soap, sweat and skin microbes change body smell too. People who live together, related or not, share skin microbes. How much smell comes from genes and how much from living together is still being studied.

## Roots
We sniffed family shirts and passed a smell down a line of hands. Now we ask what this tells us about who we are, and what a family is.

**Deep truth.** With your eyes covered, your nose could find some of your family. Much of each person's smell is made by microbes living on their skin. People who live together pass these microbes to each other. So everyone in one home shares tiny living beings, whether born into the family or not.  
*Habit it breaks:* We often say family means 'the same blood', the people you are born from. The shirt test and the microbe study show that living close together also makes people alike, even people who were not born related.  
*Evidence:* In the shirt test, most children find at least one family member by smell alone. In 2013, scientists found that people in one home share more skin microbes, and a husband and wife share many, though they are not related by birth.

- *who am i:* You are a person, and also a home for millions of tiny living beings. Some of them came from the people who hug you.
- *what is life:* Life is shared. Living beings pass to each other through every hug, every handshake and every shared tumbler.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of families of many kinds: elephant families, ant sisters, joint families and families of two.

A family begins with one person caring for another. Then more join, by birth, by marriage or by adoption. Each person brings their own smell and their own tiny beings. Living close, they share them, until the home has a smell of its own. Many lives become one family, and the family is still many lives.

- **Maha Upanishad, chapter 6, verses 71 to 73:** Only a small mind asks, 'Is this person mine, or a stranger?' For a big-hearted person, the whole world is one family: vasudhaiva kutumbakam. (Families come in many kinds, and people join them in many ways. Even the tiny beings on our skin move from person to person. Where a family ends is not as fixed as it looks.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), on interbeing:** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the tree in it. Nothing exists all by itself. (Look deeply at your own skin, and you find tiny beings that came from the people you live with. Part of each of us came from the others.)
- Science (settled): People who live in one home share more skin microbes than people from different homes.
- Science (open): Whether shared microbes stay on us for long, or come and go after each touch, is not yet known.
- Other view, Many people, and some scientists: Family is about being born related. Genes from your parents shape your face, your body and part of your smell, and living together cannot change those genes.
- Practice, Breathe in the people you love (3 min): Sit close to someone you love. Close your eyes. Breathe in slowly through your nose, three times. Can you smell them? Now breathe out slowly. Each breath out carries a little of you into the room. Sit quietly together for one more breath.
- Ask: If some of your smell came from the people who hug you, is your smell only yours?
- Ask: Where does your family end? Does it end at your door, or does it go further?
- Ask: Baby Kayal was not born into her family. In what ways does she belong to it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If your smell is made partly by tiny living beings on your skin, is your smell really yours? (hint: Think about the microbes on you, and who passed them to you.)
- *big:* Who is in your family? Is it the people who live with you, or the people you are related to, or both? (hint: Think of someone who lives far away, and someone who is not related but looks after you.)
- *big:* An ant family has thousands of sisters. What would it be like to have a thousand sisters? (hint: Think about who would cook, who would play, and how you would know them all.)
- *deep:* A baby knows their family before they can see clearly or talk. How do you think a baby knows? (hint: Think about smell, touch, warmth and voices.)

## Story: Ammachi's Shawl
*Nila, Arjun, Amma, Ammachi, Revathi and Kayal are made up. People really can know their family by smell, and people who live together really do share skin microbes.*

Nila lived in a flat in Kochi, on the fourth floor. She lived with her Amma, her Appa, her little brother Arjun and her Ammachi, her grandmother. Nila was six. Arjun was four.

Every morning, Ammachi oiled Nila's hair with coconut oil and plaited it. Every night, Ammachi sat on the edge of the bed and told a story. Her old cotton shawl lay over her shoulders, soft and faded, with a green border.

One day, a phone call came from Chennai. It was Revathi Chithi, Amma's younger sister. 'She is here!' said Revathi Chithi. 'Our baby is home!' Revathi Chithi had adopted a baby girl. Her name was Kayal.

'A new baby needs many hands,' said Ammachi. 'I will go to Chennai for two weeks to help.' Nila's face fell. Two weeks felt like forever.

On the night before Ammachi left, she took off her old shawl and put it round Nila. 'Keep this for me,' she said. 'I will come back for it.'

That night, Nila lay in bed with the shawl. She pressed her face into it. It smelled of coconut oil, of the kitchen, and of something else. It smelled of Ammachi. Nila fell asleep at once.

Every night for two weeks, Nila slept with the shawl. Arjun wanted it too. 'Just one sniff,' he said. He put his nose in it. 'It smells like Ammachi!' he said, surprised. 'How does a shawl smell like a person?'

Nila did not know. So she asked Amma. Amma was folding clothes. 'Every person has their own smell,' said Amma. 'You know Ammachi's smell, because you have been close to her every day of your life. Your nose remembers it.'

'Do I have a smell too?' asked Arjun. Amma sniffed the top of his head. 'You smell like Arjun,' she said, and he giggled.

At last, Ammachi came home. She came through the door with a bag of banana chips and a phone full of photos of baby Kayal. Nila and Arjun ran to her and hugged her very hard.

Nila gave back the shawl. Ammachi held it to her nose, and laughed. 'Ayyo! This smells of you now, Nila, not of me!'

Nila sniffed it. Ammachi was right. The shawl smelled a little like Nila's own pillow.

'How did that happen?' asked Arjun.

Ammachi sat down and pulled them both close. 'Your skin is home to many tiny living beings,' she said. 'They are called microbes. They are far too small to see. They make much of our smell. When you hold something close, some of them move onto it. When people hug, some of them move across too.'

'So the shawl got my microbes,' said Nila slowly.

'Yes,' said Ammachi. 'And people who live together pass them to each other all the time. That is part of why a home has its own smell.'

Arjun thought about this. 'What about baby Kayal?' he asked. 'She was not born in Revathi Chithi's family.'

Ammachi showed them a photo. Revathi Chithi was holding Kayal close, cheek to cheek. 'Revathi holds her all day,' said Ammachi. 'They sleep side by side. Revathi already knows her baby's smell. And they are sharing their tiny beings, just like we do.'

Nila looked at the photo for a long time. Then she wrapped the shawl back round Ammachi's shoulders and hugged her, shawl and all. 'Now it will smell of both of us,' said Nila.

**For the parent:** A family's smell is made by living close together. Kayal shares it too, though she was adopted.

- Ask: Why did the shawl smell of Ammachi at first, and of Nila later?
- Ask: Baby Kayal was adopted. How does she come to share her family's smell?
- Ask: Is there something at home that smells of someone you love? Who is it?

### g1 · Family Line-Up: who comes first? (12 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 10 cards. Shuffle them and put them face down.; The youngest player is the first caller. The caller turns over a card and reads it, or a grown-up reads it out.; Everyone lines up in that order. Talk, check and swap places until everyone agrees.; The caller draws the line on the record sheet as stick people.; The person at the front of the line is the next caller.; After 5 cards, look at the record sheet. Is the line the same every time?
- **Printable cards: 10 line-up cards to cut out:** Line up from youngest to oldest. (back: Ask each person how old they are, if you do not know.); Line up from shortest to tallest. (back: Stand back to back to check.); Line up by who woke up first this morning. (back: The first one awake goes at the front.); Line up from the smallest hand to the biggest hand. (back: Put palms together to check.); Line up by who has the longest hair. (back: Shortest hair at the front, longest at the back.); Line up by who has lived in this home the longest. (back: The person who came first goes at the front.); Line up from the smallest foot to the biggest foot. (back: Stand side by side and look down.); Line up by who fell asleep first last night. (back: The first one asleep goes at the front.); Line up from the softest voice to the loudest voice. (back: Everyone says 'hello' one at a time.); Line up by who was born nearest to this city. (back: Ask everyone where they were born.)
- **Printable score sheet: Line-up record:** A row for each card: draw stick people in the order they stood.; Card 1: youngest to oldest; Card 2: shortest to tallest; Card 3: who woke first
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the cards. 2. Gather everyone at home in the biggest room. 3. Put the cards face down.  
  *Parent says:* Today the whole family plays a lining-up game. Do you think the youngest person will always be at the front? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Everyone lines up in a different order each time. Guess who will be first.
  - Ask: Will the oldest person always be the tallest? (good: Yes, grown-ups are tall.; No, Ammachi is shorter than Appa.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Write it down. Many children guess that oldest and tallest are the same.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child turns over the first card and you read it aloud. 2. Everyone lines up.  
  *Parent says:* Line up from youngest to oldest. Who goes at the front? Who goes at the back?  
  *Child mode:* Line up from youngest to oldest. Where do you stand?
  - Note: Let your child do the sorting. Help only with ages if needed.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play three more cards. 2. After each line, your child draws it as stick people on the record sheet.  
  *Parent says:* Now line up from shortest to tallest. Is the line the same as before, or did someone move?  
  *Child mode:* Now line up by height. Did someone move?
  - Ask: Who moved when we lined up by height instead of age? (good: Ammachi moved, because she is old but not tall.; Arjun stayed at the front both times.)
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one card that is not about bodies, like who woke first. 2. Draw that line too.  
  *Parent says:* Who woke up first this morning? Line up! Is this line like the others?  
  *Child mode:* Who woke up first today? Line up!
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the record sheet on the floor. 2. Look at all the lines together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all our lines. Is the same person always at the front? What does that tell us about our family?  
  *Child mode:* Look at all the lines. Is anyone always first?
  - Ask: Why are the lines different? (good: Each line sorts by a different thing.; The oldest is not always the tallest.)
  - Note: The point is that every person in a family is first at something. Sorting by different features gives different orders.
  - Check: Ammachi is the oldest. Is she always at the back of the line? → **No, it depends on what we line up by**

## Experiments
### e1 · Whose Cloth Is This? Can you find your family by smell alone? (22 min)
- **question:** Can I tell who wore a shirt just by smelling it, with my eyes covered?
- **predict:** Guess first. Whose shirt will you know most easily by smell? Whose will be hardest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every shirt goes in the same kind of steel tumbler, so the tumblers give no clue. Your eyes are covered, so you cannot see the shirt. Your hands stay in your lap, so your fingers give no clue. Nobody wears perfume the night before. We add one clean, washed shirt, to see what 'nobody' smells like. We do two rounds, because one right guess could be luck.
- **record:** Draw one tumbler for each shirt. Under each, draw the face of the person you guessed. Put a tick when you were right.
- **results table:** Whose shirt (written under the tumbler) | My guess, round 1 | My guess, round 2 | Right both times?
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the evening, tell everyone about the test. 2. Each person puts on a cotton T-shirt, vest or nightie for bed. 3. Ask everyone not to use perfume or scented powder tonight.  
  *Parent says:* Tomorrow we test your nose. Everyone sleeps in a cotton shirt tonight. Whose shirt do you think you will know best? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Everyone wears a cotton shirt to bed tonight. Tomorrow we sniff them. Whose shirt will you know best? Why?
  - Ask: Whose shirt will be easiest to know by smell? (good: Amma's, because I hug her the most.; Ammachi's, because she uses coconut oil.)
  - Note: Write down the guess and the reason. Say nothing yet. Children often guess the person they hug most.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the morning, while your child waits in another room, each person folds their shirt into a steel tumbler. 2. Fold one clean, washed shirt into a tumbler too. 3. Write each name on a chit and tuck it under that tumbler. 4. Mix up the tumblers on the counter.  
  *Parent says:* Wait here. We are hiding the shirts in the tumblers. No peeping!  
  *Child mode:* Wait in the other room. No peeping!
  - Note: All the tumblers should look the same. If some shirts are a different colour, push them right down so only the tumbler shows.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie the dupatta gently over your child's eyes. 2. Check that they cannot see. 3. Tell them they will not touch the shirts.  
  *Parent says:* I am covering your eyes, so your eyes cannot help. Keep your hands in your lap, so your fingers cannot help either. Only your nose can work now.  
  *Child mode:* Your eyes are covered. Keep your hands in your lap. Only your nose works now.
  - Ask: Why do we cover your eyes? (good: So I cannot see whose shirt it is.; So the test is fair.)
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold one tumbler under your child's nose for a few slow sniffs. 2. Ask whose shirt it is. 3. Write the guess next to that tumbler's place. 4. Do every tumbler. Between tumblers, let your child sniff the inside of their own elbow.  
  *Parent says:* Take three slow sniffs. Whose shirt is this? It is fine to say 'I don't know' or 'nobody'.  
  *Child mode:* Sniff slowly, three times. Whose shirt is it? You can say 'I don't know'.
  - Ask: What does this one smell like? (good: Like coconut oil and Ammachi.; Like nothing, maybe it is the clean one.)
  - Note: Sniffing their own elbow between tumblers clears the nose a little. Smells are faint, so keep the tumbler close and give your child time.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the dupatta off. 2. Mix the tumblers again while your child turns away. 3. Cover the eyes again and do round 2. 4. Write the second guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Now we do it all again in a new order. One right guess could be luck. Two right guesses is a real clue.  
  *Child mode:* Let's do it again in a new order. Two right guesses is not luck.
  - Note: Keep the chits under the tumblers as you mix them.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take off the dupatta. 2. Lift each tumbler and read the chit together. 3. Your child puts a tick on the results for each right guess.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look under the tumblers. Whose shirts did you know both times? Which ones were hard? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look under each tumbler. Which ones did you get right? Which were hard?
  - Ask: Why was one person's shirt easiest? (good: I hug Amma every day, so I know her smell.; Appa's shirt smelled of his hair oil.)
  - Note: Most children get at least one person right both times, usually the person they are closest to. If your child got none right, that is a real result too. Smells on a shirt are faint. Try again with shirts worn for two nights, or let a grown-up be the sniffer. Wash the shirts afterwards.
  - Check: What did you use to find whose shirt it was? → **My nose**
### e2 · Pass the Smell: how far does it go along a line of hands? (12 min)
- **question:** If one person has garlic on their hand, and we shake hands down a line, how far does the smell go?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the last person in the line smell of garlic? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Everyone washes and dries their hands first, so they start the same. Everyone shakes hands the same way, for the count of three. Everyone smells their own palm the same way.
- **safety:** Keep garlic away from eyes. Everyone washes their hands with soap at the end.
- **record:** Draw the line of people. Colour each hand: dark green for a strong smell, light green for a little, no colour for none.
- **results table:** Place in the line | Name | Garlic smell: strong, a little, or none?
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone washes hands with soap and dries them. 2. Stand in a line. 3. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* Ammachi will put garlic on her hand. Then we shake hands down the line. Will the last person smell of garlic? Why?  
  *Child mode:* One person gets garlic on their hand. We shake hands down the line. Will the last person smell of garlic? Why?
  - Ask: Will the last person smell of garlic? (good: Yes, it will pass along.; No, it will be used up by then.)
- **s2 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Crush the garlic clove with the back of the spoon. 2. The first person in the line rubs it on one palm for a few seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Smell the garlic. Now the first person rubs it on their palm.  
  *Child mode:* Smell the garlic. The first person rubs it on their palm.
  - Note: A grown-up crushes the garlic. Throw the clove in the cooking afterwards.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The first person shakes hands with the second, for a count of three. 2. The second shakes with the third. 3. Go to the end of the line.  
  *Parent says:* Shake hands, one, two, three. Now pass it on to the next person.  
  *Child mode:* Shake hands. Count one, two, three. Pass it on.
  - Note: Use the same hand each time.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone sniffs their own palm. 2. Each person says strong, a little, or none. 3. Your child records it.  
  *Parent says:* Sniff your palm. Is the garlic smell strong, a little, or none? Who has the most? Who has the least?  
  *Child mode:* Sniff your palm. Strong, a little, or none?
  - Ask: Why did the smell get weaker down the line? (good: Each hand got a little less than the hand before.; Some stayed on each hand.)
  - Note: Usually the first two people smell clearly of garlic and the end of the line smells faintly or not at all.
  - Check: Who smelled most of garlic? → **The first person**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone washes hands with soap. 2. Talk about microbes.  
  *Parent says:* Microbes on our skin are much too small to see or smell. But they pass from hand to hand, just like the garlic did. People who live together pass them to each other every day.  
  *Child mode:* Tiny microbes pass from hand to hand too, like the garlic. Now wash with soap!

### a1 · My Home Picture: who lives with me, and who lives far away (10 min)
- **record:** The folded drawing, with a name under each person.

## Applications
- Doctors and nurses put a newborn baby on the mother's chest soon after birth. Skin to skin, the baby is warm and close, and starts to know the mother's smell.
- Police dogs follow the smell of one person, because every person's smell is a little different.
- Scientists study skin microbes to understand some skin problems and how to treat them.
- Forest teams in India study elephant families to keep them safe and to know where they move.

## Spotted around us
- A mother cat sniffing her kittens one by one on a staircase or under a parked scooter.
- A line of ants on the kitchen floor, stopping for a moment to touch feelers as they pass.
- A big family at a wedding, Onam lunch, Bihu or Eid, with grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins together.
- A child at the airport or the bus stand, hugging a grandmother or an aunt who has come from far away.
- A dog greeting people at a gate by sniffing their hands first.

## Open questions
- **How much of our body smell comes from genes, and how much from food and skin microbes?** All three play a part. Scientists are still finding out how much each one matters.
- **Do the microbes we share with our family stay on us, or come and go after each touch?** The 2013 study found that people who live together share microbes. It could not tell how many stay for a long time.
- **How does a new baby learn their family's smell so quickly?** Babies seem to prefer familiar smells within days of birth, but tests do not always agree, and nobody knows exactly how this learning happens.

## Sources
- ideas/by_topic/family.md: the notebook lists family handprints, grandparents' games and handmade cards. The Class 3 unit already uses all three, so this unit builds on them in other ways: the memory book (p1) asks family members about the child's babyhood, the way the grandparents' games week asks elders about theirs.
- Porter, R. H. and Moore, J. D. (1981). Human kin recognition by olfactory cues. Physiology and Behavior 27: 493 to 495. Siblings and mothers identified a child's worn T-shirt by smell alone.
- Kaitz, M., Good, A., Rokem, A. M. and Eidelman, A. I. (1987). Mothers' recognition of their newborns by olfactory cues. Developmental Psychobiology 20: 587 to 591. About 90% of mothers recognised their newborn's smell after 10 minutes to 1 hour of contact.
- Song, S. J. and others (2013). Cohabiting family members share microbiota with one another and with their dogs. eLife 2: e00458. 159 people and 36 dogs in 60 households; household members, especially couples, shared more skin microbes.
- McGann, J. P. (2017). Poor human olfaction is a 19th-century myth. Science 356: eaam7263. Traces the idea to Paul Broca (1879).
- Body odour comes mainly from skin bacteria breaking down sweat: for example, the review by James, A. G. and others (2013), International Journal of Cosmetic Science.
- Asian elephant female social groups: work by T. N. C. Vidya's group at JNCASR, Bengaluru, on the Kabini population (Nagarahole and Bandipur), for example Nandini, Keerthipriya and Vidya (2017), Animal Behaviour.
- Hindu Adoptions and Maintenance Act, 1956, section 12: an adopted child is the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes.
