# My family: who lives with us, and how we know each other
`sci.family.family.L1`

**Summary.** With your eyes shut, can you find your mother or grandmother just by sniffing a shirt she wore? In this lesson you will try that in a fair test, pass a smell along a line of hands, and listen to stories of families of many kinds.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes. Someone in your family hugs you. Can you tell who it is without looking?* You know your family by their faces and their voices. Your nose knows them too. Every person has their own smell. In this lesson you find out if you can tell your family apart by smell alone. You also meet many kinds of families, in people and in other animals.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say who is in my family and who looks after me.
1. By the end I can name some different kinds of families, in people and in other animals.
2. By the end I can find some of my family by smell alone, in a fair test.
3. By the end I can say that tiny living beings called microbes live on our skin, and pass between people who live together.
4. By the end I can say why we cover our eyes and try twice to make a test fair.

## Terms
- **Family**: A family is a group of people who belong to each other and look after each other. *e.g.* Nila's family is her Amma, her Appa, her Ammachi and her little brother Arjun.
- **Relative**: A relative is someone in your family, near or far, like a grandmother, an aunt or a cousin. *e.g.* Revathi Kunjamma lives in Chennai, far from Nila. She is still Nila's relative.
- **Nuclear family and joint family**: A nuclear family is parents and their children living together. A joint family also has grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins in the same house. *e.g.* Joyita lives in a nuclear family in Kolkata. Meenakshi lives in a joint family in Madurai.
- **Family tree**: A family tree is a drawing of who is in a family. The oldest people are at the top and the youngest at the bottom. *e.g.* Meenakshi draws a family tree with Paati and Thatha at the top, and herself at the bottom.
- **Adopt**: To adopt a child is to take them into your family as your own son or daughter, for always. *e.g.* Revathi adopted baby Kayal. Kayal is Revathi's daughter now, for her whole life.
- **Smell**: Smell is the sense that tells you what is in the air, through your nose. *e.g.* You smell sambar before you see the pot, because a little of it floats into your nose.
- **Microbes**: Microbes are tiny living beings, far too small to see. Some of them live on your skin. *e.g.* Millions of microbes live on your hands right now. Most of them are friendly.

## Explanations
### What a family is · *debated*
**Definition:** A family is a group of people who belong to each other and look after each other.

Every child grows up with someone who feeds them, holds them and keeps them safe. Those people are the child's family. Some people join a family by birth, some by marriage, like a husband and wife, and some by adoption, like baby Kayal. People do not all agree on who counts as family.
- e.g. Nila lives with her Amma, her Appa, her Ammachi and her brother Arjun in a flat in Kochi.
- e.g. Farzana lives with her Ammi and her Nani in Hyderabad. Her Abba works in Dubai and calls every night.
- e.g. Kayal was adopted by Revathi in Chennai when she was a baby. Revathi is her mother.
- **Some people:** Family means the people you are related to, by birth, by marriage or by adoption. Cousins in another country are family, even if you have never met them.
- **Other people:** Family means the people who live with you and look after you. A neighbour aunty who cares for you every day can feel like family.
### Families come in many kinds · *defined*
**Definition:** Families can be big or small, and can be made in different ways. Each kind is a real family.

Some families are small, with one parent and one child. Some are big joint families, with grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins in one house. Some children are raised by a grandmother or an aunt. Other animals have families too. In an elephant family, mothers, daughters, aunts and young ones stay together, and an older female often leads. Grown-up males leave. In an ant nest, a queen lays the eggs, and the ants you see are her daughters. A family of parents and children living on their own is called a nuclear family. A family with grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins under one roof is a joint family. Families differ for many reasons. A job in a faraway city can take parents away from the village. A small flat has no room for many people. A family farm or shop can keep everyone together. A grandparent who needs care may come to live with the children. And no kind is better than another.
- e.g. Meenakshi lives in a joint family in Madurai. Nine people share one house: Paati, Thatha, Amma, Appa, Periyamma, Periyappa, Meenakshi and two cousins.
- e.g. Joyita in Kolkata lives with her Baba. It is just the two of them, and her Dida visits on Sundays.
- e.g. The ants on your kitchen floor are all girls. They are daughters of a queen who lives in the nest.
### Knowing my own details · *defined*
**Definition:** Personal details are facts about you, like your name, your address and your birthday.

Everyone has a name that the family chose. Your parents also have names, and so do your brothers and sisters. You live at an address, which tells where your home is. You have a birthday, the day each year when your family remembers the day you were born. Learn your parent's phone number by heart. If you are ever lost, you can tell a shopkeeper, a teacher or a police officer. Do not tell strangers where you live.
- e.g. Nila says: 'My name is Nila. My Amma is Lakshmi and my Appa is Suresh. I live in flat 4B in Kochi. My birthday is in August.'
- e.g. Ravi's school teacher asks every child to say a parent's phone number out loud, so each child can learn it.
- **Families with many names:** Many children have a pet name at home and another name at school. Both are yours. Your name is the way your family says it, and you can say both to a helper.
### A family tree · *defined*
**Definition:** A family tree is a drawing that shows who is in a family, with the oldest people at the top and the youngest at the bottom.

Start with grandparents, who are at the top. Draw a line down to their children, who are the parents. Draw a line from the parents to the children. That makes three generations. A generation is all the people of about the same age in a family. Write each person's name under their face. You can add the other grandparents too: your father's parents on one side, and your mother's parents on the other.
- e.g. Meenakshi draws Paati and Thatha at the top. Below them go Amma, Appa, Periyamma and Periyappa. At the bottom go Meenakshi and her cousins.
- e.g. Kayal draws Revathi, her mother, and Revathi's mother, her grandmother. She draws herself at the bottom.
- **Different families, different trees:** Every tree looks different. Some have two parents, some have one. A child who was adopted can draw the people who raised her. People do not all agree on who should be in a tree, so you decide with your family.
### How we know our family by smell · *defined*
**Definition:** Every person has a body smell of their own. People can learn the smells of the people they live with, and know them again.

Every person has their own smell. You notice it when you hug someone, or hold something they wore. Scientists have tested this. Brothers, sisters and mothers can pick out a family member's worn T-shirt by smell alone. A new mother can learn her baby's smell in less than an hour. Other animals do this too. A mother sheep smells her new lamb and remembers that smell.
- e.g. Some children feel calm with Amma's old saree or dupatta, because it smells of her.
- e.g. A dog greets each person at the door by sniffing them first.
- e.g. Two ants meet on the wall and touch feelers for a moment. They are smelling if they are from the same nest. Then they walk on.
### The tiny living beings we share · *idea*
**Definition:** Microbes on our skin pass from person to person when they live together.

Your skin is home to millions of microbes, tiny living beings too small to see. They feed on your sweat, and as they feed they make much of your smell. People who live together hug, hold hands and touch the same things, so they pass microbes to each other. In 2013, scientists in the United States found that people in one home share more skin microbes than people in different homes. Scientists do not yet know if shared microbes stay for long, or come and go after each touch.
- e.g. Nila's grandmother, Ammachi, hugs Nila's little brother Arjun every morning. Each hug passes a few microbes between them.
- e.g. Everyone at home drinks from the same steel tumblers and sits on the same sofa, so microbes pass between them.
- e.g. If the family has a pet dog, she licks everyone's hands and shares microbes with the whole family.
- **Scientists who found the sharing:** People who live together share more skin microbes than people who live apart, so living together shapes the tiny beings on us.
- **Scientists who are still checking:** Some shared microbes may only be passed on for a short time after a touch, and then go away. We need more tests to know which ones stay.

## Mostly agreed
- People can know their family members by smell alone. *How we know:* In 1981, Richard Porter and John Moore in the United States found that brothers, sisters and mothers could pick out a family member's worn T-shirt by smell. Many tests since then have found the same.
- Much of a person's body smell is made by microbes on the skin. *How we know:* Fresh sweat has very little smell. The smell comes when skin microbes feed on the sweat. Scientists have found that the smell begins when the microbes break the sweat down.
- Families come in many kinds, and every kind can give a child love and care. *How we know:* Children grow up well in big families, small families, with one parent, with grandparents and with adopted parents. What matters most is having grown-ups who care for them every day.

## How we found out
**Paul Broca, a French doctor (1879); Richard Porter and John Moore in the United States (1981); Marsha Kaitz and her team at the Hebrew University in Jerusalem (1987); John McGann, a brain scientist in the United States (2017), 1879 to 2017.** For a long time, many scientists thought people had a poor sense of smell. In 1879, Paul Broca, a French doctor who studied the brain, said that humans smell poorly. He said the smell part of the human brain was small next to the rest of the brain. Most people believed this for over 100 years. Then scientists began to test real noses. In 1981, Richard Porter and John Moore in the United States asked children to wear plain T-shirts. Then their brothers, sisters and mothers sniffed the shirts. They could pick out their own family member's shirt by smell alone. In 1987, Marsha Kaitz and her team in Jerusalem tested new mothers. After less than an hour with their babies, nine out of ten mothers could pick out their own baby's shirt by smell. In 2017, the brain scientist John McGann wrote that the weak human nose is a myth. The smell part of the human brain has about as many nerve cells as the smell part in many other mammals.  
*What people believed before:* People thought humans had a weak sense of smell, much weaker than other mammals. Tests showed that people can know their own family, and a mother her new baby, by smell alone.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Humans have a weak sense of smell. Only dogs and other animals really use their noses.** (A French doctor, Paul Broca, in 1879, and most people for over 100 years after him; *rejected*)  
  For: A dog's nose is much better than ours at many smells. The smell part of the human brain is small next to the rest of the brain.  
  Against: When scientists tested real people, they found humans can tell their own family by smell. Humans have about 400 kinds of smell sensors in the nose. In 2017, the brain scientist John McGann wrote that the weak human nose is a myth.
- **A child who was adopted is not a real part of the family.** (An old idea some people still hold; *rejected*)  
  For: An adopted child was not born to the parents.  
  Against: Adoption law in India says an adopted child is the child of the adoptive parents in every way. An adopted child is fed, held and loved like any other child. People who live together even share the same skin microbes.
- **Your body smell comes from the family you are born into.** (Some scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Identical twins smell more alike than other brothers and sisters. So genes play a part in body smell.  
  Against: Food, soap, sweat and skin microbes change body smell too. People who live together, related or not, share skin microbes. How much smell comes from genes and how much from living together is still being studied.

## Roots
We sniffed family shirts and passed a smell down a line of hands. Now we ask what this tells us about who we are, and what a family is.

**Deep truth.** In the Whose Cloth Is This? test, many children find at least one family member by smell alone, with their eyes covered. Much of each person's smell is made by microbes living on their skin. People who live together pass these microbes to each other. So everyone in one home shares tiny living beings, whether born into the family or not.  
*Habit it breaks:* We often say family means 'the same blood', the people you are born from. The shirt test and the 2013 study of skin microbes show that living close together also makes people alike, even people who were not born related.  
*Evidence:* In the shirt test, many children find at least one family member by smell alone. In 2013, scientists found that people in one home share more skin microbes, and a husband and wife share many, though they are not related by birth.

- *who am i:* You are a person, and also a home for millions of tiny living beings. Some of them came from the people who hug you.
- *what is life:* Living beings share with each other. Microbes pass from person to person with every hug, every handshake and every shared tumbler.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of families of many kinds: elephant families, ant sisters, joint families and families of two.

A family begins with one person caring for another. Then more people join, by birth, by marriage or by adoption. Each person brings their own smell and their own microbes. When they live close together, they share them. After a while the home has a smell of its own.

- **Maha Upanishad, chapter 6, verses 71 to 73:** “अयं बन्धुरयं नेति गणना लघुचेतसाम् / उदारचरितानां तु वसुधैव कुटुम्बकम्” Only a small mind asks, 'Is this person mine, or a stranger?' For a big-hearted person, the whole world is one family: vasudhaiva kutumbakam. (Families come in many kinds, and people join them in many ways. Even the microbes on our skin move from person to person. So it is hard to say exactly where a family ends.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), on interbeing:** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the tree in the paper. Nothing exists all by itself. (Look closely at your own skin and you find microbes that came from the people you live with. So part of each of us came from the others.)
- Science (settled): People who live in one home share more skin microbes than people from different homes.
- Science (open): Whether shared microbes stay on us for long, or come and go after each touch, is not yet known.
- Other view, Many people, and some scientists: Family is about being born related. Genes from your parents shape your face, your body and part of your smell, and living together cannot change those genes.
- Practice, Smell someone you love (3 min): Sit close to someone you love. Close your eyes. Breathe in slowly through your nose, three times. Can you smell them? Now breathe out slowly. Each breath out carries a little of you into the room. Sit quietly together for one more breath.
- Ask: If some of your smell came from the people who hug you, is your smell only yours?
- Ask: Where does your family end? Does it end at your door, or does it go further?
- Ask: Baby Kayal was not born into her family. In what ways does she belong to it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If your smell is made partly by tiny living beings on your skin, is your smell really yours? (hint: Think about the microbes on you, and who passed them to you.)
- *big:* Who is in your family? Is it the people who live with you, or the people you are related to, or both? (hint: Think of someone who lives far away, and someone who is not related but looks after you.)
- *big:* An ant nest can have thousands of ants, all daughters of a queen. What would it be like to have a thousand sisters? (hint: Think about who would cook, who would play, and how you would know them all.)
- *deep:* A baby knows their family before they can see clearly or talk. How do you think a baby knows? (hint: Think about smell, touch, warmth and voices.)

## Story: Ammachi's Shawl
*Nila, Arjun, Amma, Ammachi, Revathi and Kayal are made up. People really can know their family by smell, and people who live together really do share skin microbes.*

Nila lived in a flat in Kochi with her Amma, her Appa, her little brother Arjun and her Ammachi, her grandmother. Nila was six. Arjun was four.

Every morning, Ammachi oiled Nila's hair with coconut oil and plaited it. Every night, she told a story. Her old cotton shawl lay over her shoulders, soft and faded, with a green border.

One day a phone call came from Chennai. It was Revathi Kunjamma, Amma's younger sister. 'Our baby is home!' she said. Revathi Kunjamma had adopted a baby girl. Her name was Kayal.

'A new baby needs many hands,' said Ammachi. 'I will go to Chennai for two weeks to help.' Nila's face fell. Two weeks felt like forever.

On the night before she left, Ammachi put her shawl round Nila. 'Keep this for me,' she said. 'I will come back for it.'

Nila pressed her face into the shawl. It smelled of coconut oil, of the kitchen, and of Ammachi. She slept at once.

Every evening, Amma phoned Chennai so Nila could say hello to Ammachi. Baby Kayal cried loudly in the background, and Arjun giggled at the noise.

In the first few days the flat felt too quiet. Nila missed the coconut oil and the stories at night. Amma plaited her hair instead, but the plait came out lopsided. Nila laughed, and Amma laughed too. Still, every morning, Nila's first thought was of Ammachi.

Every night, Nila slept with the shawl. Arjun wanted a sniff too. 'It smells like Ammachi!' he said. 'How does a shawl smell like a person?'

Amma was folding clothes. 'Every person has their own smell,' she said. 'You know Ammachi's smell because you are close to her every day. Your nose remembers it.' She sniffed the top of Arjun's head. 'You smell like Arjun,' she said, and he giggled.

At last Ammachi came home, with banana chips and a phone full of photos of baby Kayal. Nila and Arjun hugged her very hard.

Nila gave back the shawl. Ammachi held it to her nose and laughed. 'Ayyo! This smells of you now, Nila, not of me!'

'How did that happen?' asked Arjun.

'Your skin is home to tiny living beings called microbes,' said Ammachi. 'They are far too small to see. They make much of our smell. When you hold something close, some of them move onto it. When people hug, some move across too. People who live together pass them to each other all the time.'

'What about baby Kayal?' asked Arjun. 'She was not born in Revathi Kunjamma's family.'

Ammachi showed them a photo. Revathi Kunjamma held Kayal close, cheek to cheek. 'Revathi holds her all day, and they sleep side by side. They are sharing microbes, just like we do. Kayal is her daughter.'

Nila wrapped the shawl round Ammachi's shoulders and hugged her, shawl and all. 'Now it will smell of both of us,' said Nila.

**For the parent:** Living close together gives a family a smell they share. Kayal shares it too, even though she was adopted.

- Ask: Why did the shawl smell of Ammachi at first, and of Nila later?
- Ask: Baby Kayal was adopted. How does she come to share her family's smell?
- Ask: Is there something at home that smells of someone you love? Who is it?

## Experiments
### e1 · Whose Cloth Is This? Can you find your family by smell alone? (22 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Can I tell who wore a shirt just by smelling it, with my eyes covered?
- **predict:** Guess first. Whose shirt will you know most easily by smell? Whose will be hardest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every shirt goes in the same kind of steel tumbler, so the tumblers give no clue. Your eyes are covered, so you cannot see the shirt. Your hands stay in your lap, so your fingers give no clue. Nobody wears perfume the night before. We add one clean, washed shirt, to see what 'nobody' smells like. We do two rounds, because one right guess could be luck.
- **record:** Fill in the results table: whose shirt it was, your two guesses, and a tick when you were right both times.
- **results table:** Whose shirt (written under the tumbler) | My guess, round 1 | My guess, round 2 | Right both times?
- **schedule:** day 1: In the evening: everyone puts on a cotton shirt or vest for bed. No perfume, no new soap. (2 min); day 2: In the morning: put each shirt in a tumbler. Cover your child's eyes. Your child sniffs each tumbler and guesses whose shirt it is. Do this twice and write down the guesses. (20 min)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the evening, tell everyone about the test. 2. Each person puts on a cotton T-shirt, vest or nightie for bed. 3. Ask everyone not to use perfume or scented powder tonight.  
  *Parent says:* Tomorrow we test your nose. Everyone sleeps in a cotton shirt tonight. Then you will try to tell whose shirt is whose.  
  *Child mode:* Everyone wears a cotton shirt to bed tonight. Tomorrow we sniff them.
  - Ask: Whose shirt will be easiest to know by smell? (good: The person I hug the most.; The person whose hair oil I know.)
  - Note: Write down the guess and the reason. Say nothing yet. Children often guess the person they hug most.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the morning, while your child waits in another room, each person folds their shirt into a steel tumbler. 2. Fold one clean, washed shirt into a tumbler too. 3. Write each name on a chit and tuck it under that tumbler. 4. Mix up the tumblers on the counter.  
  *Parent says:* Wait here. We are hiding the shirts in the tumblers. No peeping!  
  *Child mode:* Wait in the other room. No peeping!
  - Note: All the tumblers should look the same. If some shirts are a different colour, push them right down so only the tumbler shows. Do not say or show anything while your child sniffs, so you give no clue.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie the dupatta gently over your child's eyes. 2. Check that they cannot see. 3. Tell them they will not touch the shirts.  
  *Parent says:* I am covering your eyes, so your eyes cannot help. Keep your hands in your lap, so your fingers cannot help either. Only your nose can work now.  
  *Child mode:* Your eyes are covered. Keep your hands in your lap. Only your nose works now.
  - Ask: Why do we cover your eyes? (good: So I cannot see whose shirt it is.; So the test is fair.)
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold one tumbler under your child's nose for a few slow sniffs. 2. Ask whose shirt it is. 3. Write down the guess for that tumbler. 4. Do every tumbler. Between tumblers, let your child sniff the inside of their own elbow.  
  *Parent says:* Take three slow sniffs. Whose shirt is this? It is fine to say 'I don't know' or 'nobody'.  
  *Child mode:* Sniff slowly, three times. Whose shirt is it? You can say 'I don't know'.
  - Ask: What does this one smell like? (good: Like hair oil and someone I know.; Like nothing. Maybe it is the clean one.)
  - Note: Sniffing their own elbow between tumblers clears the nose a little. Smells are faint, so keep the tumbler close and give your child time.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the dupatta off. 2. Mix the tumblers again while your child turns away. 3. Cover the eyes again and do round 2. 4. Write the second guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Now we do it all again in a new order. One right guess could be luck. Two right guesses is a real clue.  
  *Child mode:* Sniff again, in a new order. Do you still think the same?
  - Note: Keep the chits under the tumblers as you mix them.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take off the dupatta. 2. Lift each tumbler and read the chit together. 3. Your child puts a tick in the results table for each right guess.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look under the tumblers. Whose shirts did you know both times? Which ones were hard? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look under each tumbler. Which ones did you get right?
  - Ask: Which shirt was hardest to know, and why? (good: The clean shirt, because it smelled of nothing.; A shirt from someone I do not sit close to.)
  - Note: Often a child gets at least one person right both times, usually someone close. If your child got none right, that is a real result too. Smells on a shirt are faint. Try again with shirts worn for two nights, or let a grown-up be the sniffer. Wash the shirts afterwards.
  - Check: What did you use to find whose shirt it was? → **My nose**
### e2 · Pass the Smell: how far does it go along a line of hands? (12 min)
- **problem:** You cannot see microbes, so we use a smell to show how tiny things pass from hand to hand. Garlic is not a microbe. But a garlic smell is also made of tiny bits that you cannot see. We will see how far the smell travels along a line of hands. Microbes also pass from person to person by touch.
- **question:** If one person has garlic on their hand, and we shake hands down a line, how far does the smell go?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the last person in the line smell of garlic? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Everyone washes and dries their hands first, so they start the same. Everyone shakes hands the same way, for the count of three. Everyone smells their own palm the same way.
- **safety:** Only a grown-up crushes the garlic and rubs it on a palm, for a few seconds. Keep garlic away from eyes. If the skin stings, wash at once. Everyone washes their hands with soap at the end.
- **record:** Fill in the results table: the place in the line, the name (or spoon, or cloth), and strong, a little or none.
- **results table:** Place in the line | Name (or spoon, or cloth) | Garlic smell: strong, a little, or none?
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone washes hands with soap and dries them. 2. Stand in a line. If you are only two, the line is: grown-up, child, steel spoon, cotton cloth. 3. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up will put garlic on one hand. Then we shake hands down the line, one after another. Guess what will happen at the end.  
  *Child mode:* One person gets garlic on their hand. Think about the last person in the line.
  - Ask: Will the last person smell of garlic? (good: Yes, it will pass along.; No, it will be used up by then.)
- **s2 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The grown-up stands first in the line. 2. The grown-up crushes the garlic clove with the back of the spoon. 3. The grown-up rubs it on one palm for a few seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Smell the garlic. Now the first person rubs it on their palm.  
  *Child mode:* Sniff the garlic first, so you know its smell.
  - Note: A grown-up crushes the garlic. Use the clove in cooking afterwards.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The first person shakes hands with the second, for a count of three. 2. The second shakes with the third. 3. Go to the end of the line. If you are only two: after the handshake, your child holds the steel spoon in that same hand for a count of three. Then press the spoon on the cotton cloth for a count of three.  
  *Parent says:* Shake hands, one, two, three. Now pass it on to the next person.  
  *Child mode:* Shake the next hand gently. Count one, two, three.
  - Note: Use the same hand each time. With only two people, the spoon and cloth are the end of the line. Their smell may be very faint, and that is a result too.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone sniffs their own palm, then the spoon and the cloth if you used them. 2. Each says strong, a little, or none. 3. Your child records it.  
  *Parent says:* Everyone sniffs their own palm. Is the garlic smell strong, a little, or none? Who has the most? Who has the least?  
  *Child mode:* Sniff your own palm. Take your time.
  - Ask: Why did the smell get weaker down the line? (good: Each hand got a little less than the hand before.; Each handshake passed on only some of the garlic.)
  - Note: Usually the first two in the line smell clearly of garlic and the end of the line smells faintly or not at all.
  - Check: Who smelled most strongly of garlic? → **The first person**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone washes hands with soap. 2. Talk about microbes.  
  *Parent says:* The garlic smell passed from hand to hand. Garlic is not a microbe. Still, its smell shows how tiny things you cannot see can pass along a line of hands. Microbes on our skin pass from hand to hand in the same way. People who live together pass them to each other every day.  
  *Child mode:* Wash your hands well with soap.

### a2 · Families of Many Kinds: listen and match (10 min)

  - Ask: Is Kayal's family a real family? (good: Yes, Revathi is her mother.; Yes, because they love each other.)

### a3 · Elephant Family Walk (8 min)
- **record:** Take a photo of your elephant family walk, or tell a grown-up who walked where, and why the young ones were in the middle.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the elephant family. 2. Choose the oldest grown-up at home to be the leader.  
  *Parent says:* Asian elephants live in families of mothers, daughters, aunts and young ones. Often an older female leads the way to water. In our game, the oldest grown-up is the leader. She or he leads!  
  *Child mode:* Who is the oldest grown-up at home? In our game, that person leads.
  - Note: This is true of Asian elephants in India. T. N. C. Vidya and her team in Bengaluru study elephant families in Nagarahole forest. Grown-up male elephants leave their family and mostly live alone or in small groups.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Everyone lines up behind the leader. 2. Each person holds the back of the shirt of the person in front, like a trunk holding a tail. 3. Walk slowly round the home.  
  *Parent says:* Hold on, like a baby elephant holding their mother's tail with their trunk. Walk slowly. Our leader is taking us to the river!  
  *Child mode:* Hold on to the person in front. Walk slowly behind the leader.
  - Note: Holding the tail is only for the game. Real baby elephants walk close beside their mothers.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The leader calls 'Tiger!' 2. The grown-ups stand close around the youngest child, arms out like a wall. If there is only one grown-up, stand close to your child, arms out. 3. Then walk on.  
  *Parent says:* Tiger! Quick, the young ones go in the middle. The grown-ups stand around them.  
  *Child mode:* Tiger! If you are small, go to the middle.
  - Ask: Why do the young ones go in the middle? (good: So the tiger cannot reach them.; The big elephants keep them safe.)
  - Note: Elephant families do gather around their calves when there is danger.

## Challenges
### c1 · Make My Family Picture Stand Up (15 min)
- **problem:** Make your family drawing stand up on a table by itself, without leaning on a wall, for as long as you count to 20. A picture that stands on a shelf lets you see your family every day, even the ones who live far away.
- **your turn:** Now make a stand for a picture of a family who lives far away, so they can be on the shelf too.
- **constraints:** Use only paper, old cardboard, a small box, a lump of atta dough, tape or glue.; The picture must face the front so everyone can see it.; It must not lean on the wall or on anything else.
- **general:** Fold a strip of cardboard into a triangle and tape it to the back of the picture, like a photo frame.; Glue the picture on thick cardboard. Fold the bottom of the cardboard forward in a wide strip, so it makes a flat foot.; Fold the paper into a tent shape, with the picture on one side.
- **creative:** Cut a slot in a small box and slide the picture into the slot.; Roll two paper tubes and tape them behind the picture like legs.; Stand the picture in a lump of atta dough on a small plate.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the drawing flat on the table. 2. Ask how to make it stand.  
  *Parent says:* Your family picture is lying down. Let's make it stand up by itself.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the picture lying flat. Think before you try anything.
  - Ask: How could you make it stand? (good: Put something behind it.; Fold the bottom.)
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child tries one idea with cardboard, paper and tape. 2. Stand it on the table. 3. Count to 20 together.  
  *Parent says:* Try your idea. Stand it up and let go. Did it stay up?  
  *Child mode:* Stand it up, let go, and count to 20 in your head.
  - Note: Help with cutting cardboard. If it falls, ask which way it fell, and what could stop it falling that way.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. If it fell, try a second idea. 2. Put the picture on a shelf where everyone can see it.  
  *Parent says:* Where shall we put our family, so everyone can see them?  
  *Child mode:* Put your family on the shelf.
  - Ask: What made the stand work? (good: The triangle at the back held it.; A wide foot stopped it falling.)

## Worksheets
### My Family and Its Smell (core) (core, 12 questions)
1. (draw) Draw three things in your home that you can know by their smell alone. Ask a grown-up to write what they are.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of three things with a smell, such as tea, soap or a flower.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows things the child really knows by smell. Smell tells us who and what is near.
2. (match) Draw a line from each family to who is in it.  
   **Answer:** An elephant family → Mothers, aunts and young ones, with an older female leading; An ant family → A queen and her many daughters; A girl who lives with only her father → A family with one parent. *Why:* Every one of these is a family, and each kind is different.
3. (mcq) In the shirt test, your eyes were covered. Circle what you used to guess whose shirt it was.  
   Options: My eyes / My ears / My nose  
   **Answer:** My nose. *Why:* Your eyes were covered, so only your nose could work.
4. (truefalse) A family must always have a mother, a father and children.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Some families have one parent, some have grandparents raising a child, and some have aunts. Any group with grown-ups who care for a child is a family.
5. (odd) Circle the one that is NOT a family.  
   Options: A grandmother and her two grandchildren / A mother and her son / A shopkeeper and the people buying rice / An aunt, an uncle and their three children  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A shopkeeper and customers meet to buy and sell. They do not belong to each other or look after each other. The others are families.
6. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down. Name one kind of family that is not like yours. Why is it a real family?  
   **Answer:** For example: A family with one parent is a real family, because the grown-up looks after the child.. *Why:* A strong answer names a kind of family from the unit and says it is real because the grown-ups belong to the child and look after the child.
7. (count) Meenakshi lives in a big joint family in Madurai. Count all the people in this picture. Write the number.  
   **Answer:** 9 people.. *Why:* There are Paati, Thatha, Amma, Appa, an aunt, an uncle, Meenakshi and two cousins: 9 people in all.
8. (perspective) Two sisters in Kochi are talking about families.  
   **Answer:** Any side, with a reason. Both can be right: many people are both related and live together.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason, for example 'Kunjamma lives far away but she is still my family'. It also sees what is good in the other view.
9. (sort) A girl knows her grandmother by clues. Colour the clues you see in blue. Colour the clues you smell in green.  
   **Answer:** her coconut oil → I smell it; her green shawl → I see it; her grey hair → I see it; her own body smell → I smell it. *Why:* We know our family with our eyes and with our noses. A smell clue works even in the dark.
10. (draw) Draw your own family tree. Put your grandparents at the top, your parents in the middle and you and your brothers and sisters at the bottom. Write each name.  
   **Answer:** A tree with three rows. For example: Paati and Thatha at the top, Amma and Appa in the middle, and the child at the bottom.. *Why:* A family tree shows three generations. The oldest people are at the top.
11. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: your name, your mother's name, your father's name, your brothers' and sisters' names, and your birthday.  
   **Answer:** Any true answer. For example: I am Nila. My Amma is Lakshmi and my Appa is Suresh. My brother is Arjun. My birthday is in August.. *Why:* These are your personal details. Knowing them helps you tell people who you are.
12. (label) Here is the shirt test. Draw a ring round the thing that stops the eyes from helping. Draw a cross on the thing that holds the shirt.  
   **Answer:** A ring round the dupatta over the eyes. A cross on the steel tumbler.. *Why:* A strong answer finds the dupatta, which makes the test fair, and the tumbler, which hides the shirt.
### How We Know Each Other (stretch) (stretch, 11 questions)
1. (mcq) Here are two shirts. A mother wore one all night. The other was just washed. Which one smells of a person?  
   Options: The one just washed / The one the mother wore / Both the same  
   **Answer:** The one the mother wore. *Why:* A worn shirt picks up the wearer's smell. Washing takes most of it away. That is why the Whose Cloth Is This? test has one washed shirt that nobody wore.
2. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down. In the Whose Cloth Is This? test, one shirt was clean and washed. Could you tell which one it was? What does that tell you about a shirt nobody has worn?  
   **Answer:** For example: The clean shirt smelled of nothing much, so a worn shirt smells of the person who wore it.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the clean shirt from the child's own test as evidence that the smell on a worn shirt comes from the person.
3. (draw) Microbes are too small to see. Draw what you think the microbes on your hand might look like, if you could zoom in.  
   **Answer:** Any drawing of many tiny beings on a hand.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows many tiny beings on the skin. Real skin microbes are round or rod-shaped, much too small to see.
4. (truefalse) A new mother can learn her baby's smell in less than one hour.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 1987, Marsha Kaitz and her team in Jerusalem found that about nine out of ten new mothers could pick out their baby's shirt by smell, after between 10 minutes and an hour together.
5. (order) Put the shirt test in order. Write 1, 2, 3, 4.  
   **Answer:** ['Sleep in a cotton shirt', 'Put each shirt in a tumbler', 'Cover the eyes with a dupatta', 'Sniff the tumbler and guess']. *Why:* First the shirt picks up the smell. Then it goes in the tumbler. Then the eyes are covered, and last comes the sniffing.
6. (match) Draw a line from each animal to how they know their family.  
   **Answer:** Two ants meeting → Touch feelers to smell if they are from the same nest; A mother sheep → Smells her new lamb and remembers the smell; A dog at the gate → Sniffs each person's hand first. *Why:* Humans and other animals use smell to know who is near. It is one of the most important ways they have.
7. (odd) Circle the one that is NOT a way to join a family.  
   Options: Being born into it / Being adopted / Getting married / Buying a bus ticket  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* People join a family by being born, by adoption or by marriage. A bus ticket only takes you somewhere.
8. (match) Draw a line from each family to its kind.  
   **Answer:** Parents and two children in a flat → A nuclear family; Grandparents, uncles, aunts, parents and cousins in one house → A joint family; A grandmother and her granddaughter → A family with grandparent and child. *Why:* A nuclear family has parents and children. A joint family has more relatives in one house. Each one is a real family.
9. (write) Say it to a grown-up. Someone says, 'People cannot smell each other. Only dogs use their noses.' What would you tell them from your shirt test?  
   **Answer:** I found some of my family by smell alone, with my eyes covered. So people do use their noses.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the child's own shirt test as evidence against the idea.
10. (label) Here is an elephant family walking. Draw a ring round the leader. Draw a star on the young one who is safest.  
   **Answer:** A ring round the older female at the front. A star on the calf in the middle.. *Why:* An older female often leads. The young ones walk in the middle, where the grown-ups can protect them.
11. (fill) People who live together pass tiny living beings to each other. These tiny beings are called ______.  
   **Answer:** microbes. *Why:* Microbes live on our skin, and pass between people who hug, hold hands and share a home.
### Families and Tiny Beings (challenge) (challenge, 11 questions)
1. (write) Say it to a grown-up. In the Pass the Smell test, the garlic smell got weaker down the line. Why?  
   **Answer:** Each hand kept some of the garlic and passed on only a little. So each hand had less than the one before.. *Why:* A strong answer says some of the garlic stayed on each hand, so less reached the end.

   Options: All her microbes will go away / Nothing will change at all / She will share more microbes with her daughter's family  
   **Answer:** She will share more microbes with her daughter's family. *Why:* People who live together share more skin microbes. So she will likely share more with her daughter's family. Scientists are still finding out how long shared microbes stay.

   **Answer:** Any side with a reason. All three are partly right: food, microbes and genes all play a part.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that scientists have found all three play a part, and are still finding out how much each one does.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The worker ants are all female. Each one is a daughter of a queen in the nest.

   **Answer:** For example: Yes, they live together and share the same soil and water. Or: Maybe, if they came from the same parent plant.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason, using what a family is: belonging together, sharing a home, or coming from the same parents.
10. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: why might one family live in a joint family and another in a nuclear family? Give two reasons.  
   **Answer:** A family farm or shop can keep many people together. A job in a faraway city can make a smaller family move to a small flat.. *Why:* A good answer gives two real reasons, such as work, the size of the house or caring for an old person, and does not say one kind is better.

   **Answer:** A drawing of an adult animal with her young.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows an adult animal close to the young ones, and says what she does for them, such as feeding or guarding.

3. (mcq) In a family tree, where do the grandparents go?  
   Options: At the bottom / At the top / In the middle, beside the children  
   **Answer:** At the top. *Why:* The oldest people go at the top, then their children, and the youngest at the bottom. That makes three generations.
4. (mcq) A girl lives with her mother and her grandmother. Is that a family?  
   Options: No, there is no father / Yes / No, it is too small  
   **Answer:** Yes. *Why:* Her mother and grandmother look after her. Families come in many kinds, and this is one.
5. (mcq) Rohan lives with his parents and his sister in a flat in Mumbai. What kind of family is this?  
   Options: A joint family / A nuclear family / Not a family  
   **Answer:** A nuclear family. *Why:* Parents and children living on their own are a nuclear family. A joint family also has grandparents, uncles and aunts in the same house. Both are real families.
6. (mcq) A family in a village moves to a small flat in a city for work. Why might they become a nuclear family?  
   Options: The flat is small and the job is far from the village / Joint families are not real families / Everyone stops loving their grandparents  
   **Answer:** The flat is small and the job is far from the village. *Why:* A job and a small flat can change how a family lives together. The love stays the same, and both kinds are real families.
7. (mcq) Who often leads an Asian elephant family?  
   Options: The youngest calf / An older female / A grown-up male  
   **Answer:** An older female. *Why:* Elephant families are mothers, daughters, aunts and young ones. An older female often leads. Grown-up males leave.
8. (mcq) In an ant nest, who lays the eggs?  
   Options: The biggest worker ant / The baby ants / The queen  
   **Answer:** The queen. *Why:* The queen lays the eggs. The worker ants are her daughters.
9. (mcq) Can people tell their family apart by smell alone?  
   Options: No, only dogs can / Yes, often they can / Only with their eyes open  
   **Answer:** Yes, often they can. *Why:* Scientists found that mothers, brothers and sisters can pick out a family member's worn shirt by smell. You may have done it too.
10. (mcq) You do the shirt test once and get one guess right. Why should you do it again?  
   Options: To make it take longer / Because the first time is always wrong / One right guess could be luck  
   **Answer:** One right guess could be luck. *Why:* A guess can be right by luck. If you are right two times, it is more likely your nose really knows.
11. (mcq) Why do we use the same kind of tumbler for every shirt?  
   Options: Because steel smells nice / So the shirts stay clean / So the tumblers give no clue  
   **Answer:** So the tumblers give no clue. *Why:* If one tumbler looked or felt different, it could give a clue. Same tumblers make a fair test.
12. (mcq) What makes much of a person's body smell?  
   Options: Tiny microbes on the skin / The colour of their clothes / How tall they are  
   **Answer:** Tiny microbes on the skin. *Why:* Microbes on the skin feed on sweat and make much of our smell. Food, soap and genes play a part too.
13. (mcq) A girl and her mother hug every day. What passes between them?  
   Options: Nothing at all / Tiny microbes from their skin / Their height  
   **Answer:** Tiny microbes from their skin. *Why:* When people touch, some skin microbes move across. People who live together share many microbes.
14. (mcq) Baby Kayal was adopted by Revathi. Will they share skin microbes?  
   Options: Yes, because they live close together / No, because Kayal was not born to Revathi / Only if they look alike  
   **Answer:** Yes, because they live close together. *Why:* Sharing microbes comes from living together and touching, not from being born into a family. A husband and wife share many too.

## Applications
- Doctors and nurses put a newborn baby on the mother's chest soon after birth. Skin to skin, the baby is warm and close, and starts to know the mother's smell.
- Police dogs follow the smell of one person, because every person's smell is a little different.
- Scientists study skin microbes to understand some skin problems and how to treat them.
- Forest teams in India study elephant families to keep them safe and to know where they move.

## Spotted around us
- A mother cat sniffing her kittens one by one on a staircase or under a parked scooter.
- A line of ants on the kitchen floor, stopping for a moment to touch feelers as they pass.
- A big family at a wedding, Onam lunch, Bihu or Eid, with grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins together.
- A child at the airport or the bus stand, hugging a grandmother or an aunt who has come from far away.
- A dog greeting people at a gate by sniffing their hands first.

## Open questions
- **How much of our body smell comes from genes, and how much from food and skin microbes?** All three play a part. Scientists are still finding out how much each one matters.
- **Do the microbes we share with our family stay on us, or come and go after each touch?** The 2013 study found that people who live together share microbes. It could not tell how many stay for a long time.
- **How does a new baby learn their family's smell so quickly?** Babies seem to prefer familiar smells within days of birth, but tests do not always agree, and nobody knows exactly how this learning happens.

## Connected maths

- Line up these family members from youngest to oldest: Appa 40, Arjun 4, Ammachi 70, Nila 6. **Arjun 4, Nila 6, Appa 40, Ammachi 70.**. 4 is the smallest number, then 6, then 40, then 70. So Arjun is first and Ammachi is last. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
