# Finding out: planning a test and watching slow changes
`sci.how-we-know.asking-questions.L3`

**Summary.** A cut apple slice turns brown, but nobody can see it happen. Look at it every ten minutes and draw it each time, and the change shows up clearly. To find something out, a scientist first makes a plan: the question, what to look at, what to compare, how often to look, and how to write it down. Some questions need one look. Others need many looks over minutes, days or years. In this unit you plan your own investigation, keep a record, and hear how Tycho Brahe's nightly measurements showed that even the sky can change.

**Intro.** *Cut a slice of apple and put it on a plate. Now stare at it for one whole minute without looking away. Did it change? Leave it on the plate, and look again in half an hour.* In half an hour the cut side has gone yellow or brown. Yet in the minute you stared, you saw nothing happen. The change was real, but too slow for one look. To catch a slow change, you need a plan: look again and again at set times, and write down what you see each time. That is how scientists watch things that change slowly, from a cut apple to a growing child to a star.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can turn something I wonder about into a question that looking or testing can answer.

4. I can explain why a cut apple turns brown, and why lemon juice and water slow it down.
5. I can tell how Tycho Brahe's measurements, night after night, showed that the sky can change.

## Terms
- **Question you can find out**: A question that looking, counting or testing can answer. 'Why is the sky?' is hard to answer by looking. 'Does a cut apple go brown faster in air or in water?' can be tested. *e.g.* 'Do more crows come to our window in the morning or in the afternoon?' You can find out by counting at both times for a week.
- **Observe**: To look at something carefully, and use your other senses too: listen, touch, smell, and sometimes taste, if it is safe. An observation is one thing you noticed. *e.g.* Kavya observes an apple slice: 'The cut side is cream, wet and shiny. It smells sweet.'
- **Record**: What you write, draw or photograph at the time you observe, so you don't have to remember it later. A record always says when each observation was made. *e.g.* '4:10 pm. Plain slice: light yellow, score 1.' That line is a record.
- **Prediction**: A guess about what will happen, made before you test, with a reason. 'I think the lemon slice will stay pale, because Amma puts lemon on cut apples' is a prediction with a reason. *e.g.* Before cutting the apple, Arjun predicts that the slice under water will stay pale longest.
- **Plan**: The steps you decide on before you start finding out: your question, your guess and why, what you will look at, what you will compare, how often and how long you will look, and how you will write it down. *e.g.* Lakshmi's plan: 'Three slices. Look every 10 minutes for an hour. Give each a colour score from 0 to 3.'
- **Compare**: To look at two or more things side by side to see how they are the same and how they differ. *e.g.* We compare a plain apple slice with a slice that has lemon juice on it.
- **Fair test**: A test where only one thing is different between the things you compare, and everything else is kept the same. Then any difference you see must come from that one thing. *e.g.* All three slices come from the same apple, are cut at the same time, and sit in the same room. Only plain, lemon or water is different.
- **Observing over time**: Looking at the same thing again and again, at set times, and recording each look. It shows changes that are too slow to see in one look. *e.g.* Photographing the same banana every morning for five days shows it going from yellow to spotted to brown.
- **Interval**: The time you wait between one look and the next. A fast change needs a short interval, and a slow change can have a long one. *e.g.* For an apple slice, an interval of 10 minutes works. For a banana, one day is enough.
- **Evidence**: What you actually saw, counted or measured, which shows whether an idea is right. Evidence comes from the record, not from what you hoped would happen. *e.g.* 'The plain slice reached score 2 after 30 minutes, and the lemon slice stayed at 0.' That is evidence.
- **Enzyme**: A tiny helper made by living cells that speeds up one particular change. An apple's cells make an enzyme that helps turn colourless substances brown when they meet oxygen. *e.g.* The enzyme in a cut apple works fast at room temperature and slowly in the fridge.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air all around us. We breathe it in. In a cut apple, oxygen from the air takes part in making the brown colour. *e.g.* A slice kept under water gets much less oxygen than a slice in the open air, and it browns more slowly.
- **Supernova**: A star that explodes. For weeks it can shine brighter than a whole galaxy of stars, and then it fades over months. *e.g.* The new star Tycho Brahe watched in 1572 was a supernova.

## Explanations
### Questions you can find out · *defined*
**Definition:** A question you can find out is one that looking, counting, measuring or testing can answer. It names what you will look at, and what might change.

Children wonder about everything, and every wondering is worth keeping. Some wonderings are big and hard, like 'Why do apples exist?' Others can be turned into questions you can answer at home. 'Why does my apple go brown?' becomes 'Does a slice go brown faster in the open air, or under water?' The second question tells you what to do: cut two slices, put one in water, and watch both. A good question to find out is small, clear and about something you can see, count or measure. Big questions still matter. Scientists often answer a big question one small question at a time.
- e.g. 'Are ants clever?' is big. 'Do the ants on our kitchen wall find a grain of sugar faster than a grain of salt?' can be tested.
- e.g. 'Why does the banana go bad?' becomes 'Does a banana get more brown spots each day?' You can count the spots every morning.
- e.g. 'Do crows like our balcony?' becomes 'How many times does a crow land on our railing between 7 and 8 in the morning?'
### Making a plan before you start · *defined*
**Definition:** A plan answers these questions before you begin: What is my question? What do I guess, and why? What will I look at? What will I compare, and what will I keep the same? How often will I look, and for how long? How will I write it down? What do I need, and what must be done safely?

A plan saves you from finding out too late that you forgot something. If you don't write the start time, you can't say how long the change took. If you don't keep everything else the same, you can't say what caused a difference. If you only look at the start and the end, you miss when the change happened. Writing your guess first matters too. Once you have seen the result, it is easy to think 'I knew that all along.' A written guess keeps you honest, and a wrong guess is often the most interesting part.
- e.g. Divya forgot to label her slices, and after an hour she could not tell which one had lemon on it. Now she writes labels before she cuts.
- e.g. A plan for a banana: 'Question: how fast do brown spots appear? Look each morning at 8. Same spot on the table, same light. Count spots and take a photo.'
- e.g. Before the apple test, Farhan writes, 'I think the water slice will brown fastest, because water makes things go soggy.' He turns out to be wrong, and that is useful.
### Observing over time · *defined*
**Definition:** Observing over time means looking at the same thing again and again, at chosen times, in the same way each time, and recording each look.

Many changes are too slow for our eyes. An apple slice browns, a banana ripens, a child grows, and the Moon changes shape. If you stare, you see nothing happen. If you look at set times and record each look, the records lay the change out in a row, like frames of a film. Each look should be done the same way: from the same place, in the same light, judged on the same scale. Otherwise a change in the light might look like a change in the apple. A phone photo from the same spot each time is a very good record.
- e.g. A height mark on the door frame every birthday shows how much a child grew, though nobody saw them grow.
- e.g. Maria Mitchell, an American astronomer, looked at the sky from her roof on clear nights. On 1 October 1847 she found a comet nobody had reported before. She knew it was new because she knew the sky so well.
- e.g. India's weather scientists have written down the day the monsoon reaches Kerala every year for more than a hundred years. That long record shows how much the date changes from year to year.
### How often to look · *defined*
**Definition:** The interval is the time between one look and the next. Choose it to match how fast the thing changes, so that each look shows a small, clear step.

If the interval is too long, you miss the change. Look at an apple slice only once after a day, and you know it went brown, but not how fast. If the interval is too short, you waste effort and see no difference between looks. Checking a banana every ten minutes would show nothing new for hours. A useful rule: guess how long the whole change takes, and plan to look about 5 to 10 times in that time. An apple slice changes over about an hour, so look every 10 minutes. A banana changes over about a week, so look once a day.
- e.g. Apple slice: every 10 minutes for an hour.
- e.g. Banana: every morning for five or six days.
- e.g. The Moon's shape: every evening for a month.
### Why we write it down at the time · *defined*
**Definition:** A record made at the time of looking is evidence. A memory, recalled later, can change without our noticing.

People often remember things wrongly, even things they saw clearly. Our memory fills gaps with what we expect, and our first guess can colour what we think we saw. Two people who watched the same thing often remember it differently. A record written at the moment you look does not change afterwards. It also lets someone else check your work, because they can read exactly what you saw and when. That is why every scientist keeps a notebook, and why Tycho Brahe wrote down every measurement he made.
- e.g. After an hour, ask someone what colour the plain slice was at the start. Then show them the first drawing. Many people remember it as darker than it was.
- e.g. Two cricket fans watching the same catch often argue about whether it touched the ground. The replay settles it, because it is a record.
- e.g. A doctor writes your temperature on a chart each time, rather than trusting how hot your forehead felt yesterday.
### Why a cut apple turns brown · *defined*
**Definition:** When an apple is cut, its cells break open. An enzyme from the cells helps colourless substances in the apple join with oxygen from the air. This makes new substances that are brown.

An apple is made of millions of tiny cells. Inside them are colourless substances, and in other parts of the cells there is an enzyme, a helper that speeds up one change. In a whole apple, these are kept apart, so the flesh stays cream. A knife breaks the cells, the two mix, and the oxygen in the air joins in. The brown appears first where the air touches the cut face. Under water, much less oxygen reaches the slice, so it browns slowly. Lemon juice is sour, or acidic, and this enzyme works slowly in acid. Lemon juice also has vitamin C, which undoes the first step of the browning. Cold slows the enzyme too, so a slice in the fridge browns more slowly. A brown apple slice is not rotten. It is safe to eat, though it may taste a little different.
- e.g. A cut potato, a cut brinjal and a peeled banana brown for the same reason.
- e.g. Many cooks drop cut brinjal or potato into a bowl of water until they are ready to cook it.
- e.g. A bitten apple browns around the tooth marks first, where the cells were broken.
### Why does an apple make brown at a cut? · *idea*
An apple tree's fruit faces real problems. Insects bite into it, birds peck it, and germs, such as fungi and bacteria, try to get in through any wound. Many scientists think the browning is part of the fruit's defence. The brown substances, and the ones made just before them, can harm germs and insects and seal the wound a little, rather like a scab. In one experiment, tomato plants made to have less of this enzyme were harmed more by disease. But this is still being studied, and the enzyme may have other jobs in the plant too.
- e.g. Scientists have made a kind of apple that hardly browns, by switching off the gene for this enzyme.
- e.g. Tea leaves and coffee beans turn brown by a similar change, and people use it on purpose to give them their flavour and colour.
- **Many plant scientists:** The browning helps defend a damaged fruit or leaf against insects and germs.
- **Other plant scientists:** Defence is one job, but the enzyme probably has other jobs inside the healthy plant that we don't fully understand yet.
### Tycho Brahe's plan: is the new star near or far? · *defined*
**Definition:** A near thing seems to shift against far things when you look at it from a different place. A very far thing does not seem to shift. Scientists call this shift parallax.

Hold a pencil at arm's length and look past it at a far wall. Close one eye, then the other. The pencil seems to jump, but the wall does not. The Moon is fairly near us, so as the Earth turns through the night, the Moon seems to shift a little against the far stars. In 1572 Tycho Brahe saw a bright new star. Many people said it must be something in the air, nearer than the Moon. Tycho planned a test: if the new light was near, it would shift against its neighbour stars during the night. He measured its position again and again with a large instrument he had made. It never shifted. So it was far beyond the Moon, out among the stars. The sky, which people had said could never change, had changed.
- e.g. Your thumb jumps against the window frame when you swap eyes, but the far building does not.
- e.g. From a moving train, nearby trees rush past, but a far hill seems to move slowly.

## Mostly agreed
- Scientists record observations at the time they make them, with the date and time, because memory can change. *How we know:* Many studies have shown that people misremember events they saw, often with great confidence. Written records and photos do not change afterwards.
- Looking again and again at set times shows changes that are too slow to see in one look. *How we know:* Time-lapse photos show flowers opening, plants turning and clouds forming. Long records of rainfall, temperature and plant flowering show changes over many years.
- A cut apple browns because an enzyme from its broken cells helps its colourless substances join with oxygen from the air. *How we know:* Slices kept away from air, kept cold, or coated in acid such as lemon juice brown much more slowly. Scientists have found the enzyme and studied it in the lab.
- The new star of 1572 was a supernova, an exploding star far beyond the Moon. *How we know:* Tycho Brahe found no shift against the other stars. Today, telescopes can see the cloud of gas left by that explosion, in the same place in the sky.

## How we found out
**Tycho Brahe, a Danish astronomer, testing the old idea of Aristotle, a Greek thinker, November 1572 (the new star appears) to early 1574 (it fades from sight), and 1573 (Tycho's book De nova stella, On the New Star).** For about two thousand years, many people in Europe followed the Greek thinker Aristotle. He taught that the sky beyond the Moon is perfect and never changes. So any new light in the sky had to be something in the air, nearer than the Moon. In November 1572, a bright new star appeared in the group of stars called Cassiopeia. A young Danish astronomer, Tycho Brahe, saw it on 11 November. He made a plan to find out how far away it was. Something near, like the Moon, seems to shift a little against the far stars as the Earth turns through the night. Night after night, Tycho measured the new star's position against its neighbours, with a large instrument he had built. It never shifted. So it was far beyond the Moon, out among the stars. He kept watching as it faded over about sixteen months. In 1573 he published a small book, On the New Star. The sky could change after all. Today we know he had seen a supernova, an exploding star.  
*What people believed before:* People believed the sky beyond the Moon was perfect and could never change, so a new light had to be in the air, nearer than the Moon. Tycho's measurements showed the new star did not shift like a near thing, so it was far away among the stars.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Scientists just look carefully, and the answer comes to them.** (A common idea about how science works; *rejected*)  
  For: Some discoveries did start with someone noticing something by chance.  
  Against: Noticing is only the start. To find out what is really going on, scientists plan what to look at, what to compare and when to look, and they write it all down. Even Tycho Brahe's first sight of the new star needed a planned test to show how far away it was.
- **If I watch carefully, I will remember what I saw. I don't need to write it down.** (Many children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: We do remember a lot of what we see, especially if it surprised us.  
  Against: Memory fills gaps with what we expect, and changes as time passes. Two careful watchers often remember the same thing differently. A record made at the time settles it.
- **A brown apple slice has gone bad and should be thrown away.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: Food that goes brown and soft is often rotten, so brown can look like a warning.  
  Against: Browning is a change in the apple's own substances, made with oxygen from the air. It happens within minutes, long before germs could spoil the fruit. A brown slice is safe to eat. Mould, a bad smell or slime are signs of rotting.
- **Lemon juice keeps an apple pale because it covers the slice and keeps the air out.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Lemon juice does coat the slice.  
  Against: Water coats a slice too, but in the air a wet slice still browns. Lemon works mainly because it is sour, or acidic, and the browning enzyme works slowly in acid. Its vitamin C also undoes the first step of browning.
- **The sky beyond the Moon is perfect and never changes.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 350 BCE, and many people in Europe for about 2,000 years after him; *rejected*)  
  For: The stars do keep the same patterns year after year, so the sky looks unchanging.  
  Against: In 1572 Tycho Brahe measured a new star and showed it was far beyond the Moon. Astronomers in China had recorded such 'guest stars' for more than a thousand years, and they recorded this one too. Today we know stars are born, change and die.

## Roots
We have planned a test and watched apple slices turn brown over an hour, and heard how Tycho Brahe watched a new star. Now we ask what slow changes tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Each time you glanced at the plain apple slice, it looked the same as a moment before. Yet your drawings from every ten minutes showed it turning brown. A change can be too slow for the eye, and still be real. So many things that look still are changing, only too slowly to see.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort the world into things that change and things that stay still. But 'still' often only means 'changing more slowly than we look'. A record kept over time shows the change our eyes miss.  
*Evidence:* In one minute of staring, nobody saw the plain slice change. Laid side by side, the drawings made every ten minutes went from cream to brown. Tycho's nightly measurements showed the 'unchanging' sky changing too.

- *who am i:* You are changing right now. Your hair grows about a centimetre a month, too slowly to see. Only a mark on the door frame shows how much you have grown.
- *what is life:* Every living being is always changing: growing, healing, ripening and ageing, mostly too slowly for one look.
- *what is this world:* Even stars change. Tycho Brahe watched a new star blaze up and fade away over about a year and a half.

Your apple slice was one thing, yet your drawings show it as many: cream, yellow, light brown, brown. Each drawing is the same slice at a different moment. Laid in a row, they show one change unfolding. The world is like that. One world shows itself as countless moments, and a record lets us see how they belong together.

- **Heraclitus, fragment B91 (Diels-Kranz numbering), about 500 BCE:** You cannot step into the same river twice. (A river looks the same from day to day, but the water in it is always new. The apple slice looked the same from minute to minute, but it was always changing.)
- **Dhammapada, verse 277:** All made things are impermanent. When one sees this with wisdom, one turns away from suffering. (The Buddha taught that nothing made stays the same. The apple, the banana and the new star of 1572 all show it, each at its own speed.)
- **Tirukkural 423:** Whatever is said, by whoever says it, wisdom is to find the truth in it. (Aristotle was famous, but Tycho checked the sky for himself. In the apple test, your own record, not anyone's say-so, tells you what happened.)
- Science (settled): Many changes are too slow for our eyes. Time-lapse photos and long records let scientists see them.
- Science (settled): Stars are born, change and die over millions or billions of years. A supernova is the sudden end of some stars.
- Other view, Parmenides, a thinker in ancient Greece: Parmenides argued the opposite of Heraclitus. He said that what truly is cannot change, and that change is something our senses only seem to show us.
- Practice, The still minute (3 min): Let's sit very still for one minute and look at the back of your hand. Does it look still? Now close your eyes and feel your breath going in and out. Feel your heart beating in your wrist. Listen to the sounds coming and going. Open your eyes. Your hand looked still, but inside it, blood was moving all the time. What else was changing that you didn't see?
- Ask: If everything is changing, what makes the apple slice still the same slice after an hour?
- Ask: Is there anything at all that never changes? How could you ever find out?
- Ask: You change every day, too slowly to see. What would a record of your whole life show?

## Story: The Pencil Line on the Wall
*The story is made up. What Dadi does is true: engineers check whether a crack is growing by marking its ends with a pencil line and the date, and by fixing a thin strip, called a tell-tale, across it. If the crack moves, the strip tears or shifts. Low light from the side makes cracks look deeper and darker.*

It had rained all afternoon in Hyderabad. Farzana was nine, and she was sitting on the balcony floor, drawing the rain. Her little brother Imran was lining up his toy autos along the railing.

Abba came out with his tea and stopped. He bent close to the wall beside the balcony door. A thin, dark crack ran down from the corner of the door frame, as long as a pencil.

"Look at this," Abba said. "This crack is getting bigger. It was tiny last year. The building is moving."

Ammi came to the door, drying her hands. "That crack has been there since we moved in," she said. "It was always that long."

"It was not," said Abba. "I remember it. It was half this size."

"I remember it too," said Ammi. "It was just the same."

Farzana looked from one to the other. They both sounded very sure. But they could not both be right.

Dadi was sitting in her chair by the window, reading the newspaper. For forty years she had worked as an engineer for the city, checking bridges and old buildings. She folded the paper and came over slowly.

"You both remember it differently," she said. "So which memory should we trust?"

Nobody answered.

"Neither," said Dadi. "Memory is a poor ruler. It stretches and shrinks without telling you. Let's ask the wall instead."

"How can you ask a wall?" said Imran.

Dadi smiled. "You make a plan, and you write down what it tells you. Farzana, bring your pencil and your drawing book."

Farzana fetched them. Dadi sat on the low stool by the crack.

"First, what exactly is our question?" Dadi asked.

Farzana thought. "Is the crack getting bigger?"

"Bigger how? Longer, or wider, or both?"

"Both," said Farzana. "So we have to watch the ends, to see if it gets longer. And the widest part, to see if it gets wider."

"Good. Draw a short pencil line across the crack, just past each end. Write today's date next to each line."

Farzana drew two small lines, one at the top and one at the bottom, and wrote the date in tiny numbers.

"If the crack grows past a line, we will know it got longer," said Farzana.

"Now the width." Dadi tore a thin strip of paper, about as long as a finger. She glued it across the widest part of the crack, one end on each side. "Engineers call this a tell-tale, because it tells tales. If the crack opens wider, it pulls the two sides apart, and the paper tears."

"How often do we look?" Farzana asked.

"Cracks change slowly, if they change at all. Looking every hour would show nothing. Once a week is enough. And after every heavy rain, because water can make walls swell and move."

Farzana made a table in her drawing book. Date. Top end past the line? Bottom end past the line? Paper strip torn? Notes. Then she took a photo of the crack with Ammi's phone, with a one-rupee coin stuck beside it with tape, so she could tell its size later.

"Always from the same spot," said Dadi. "And in the morning light, each time."

Every Sunday morning, Farzana checked the crack. The first Sunday, nothing had changed. The second Sunday, nothing had changed. The third Sunday, Imran said, "This is the most boring science in the world."

"Nothing changing is an answer too," said Dadi. "Write it down."

In the fourth week, a storm blew in from the east and the rain lashed the balcony for a whole night. The next morning Farzana ran out before breakfast. The paper strip had gone wrinkly from the damp. But it was not torn. The top end of the crack was still above the pencil line. The bottom end was still above the other one. She wrote it all down.

After six weeks, Farzana carried her drawing book to the dining table, where Abba was reading.

"Abba, look. Six Sundays and one storm. The crack did not get longer. It did not get wider. The paper never tore." She showed him the photos side by side. They looked exactly the same.

Abba studied the page for a long time. "Then why did it look so much bigger to me that evening?"

"What time was it?" asked Dadi.

"Evening. The sun was low, after the rain."

"Low light from the side fills every crack with shadow," said Dadi. "Cracks always look deeper and darker in evening light. That's one reason I check in the same light each time."

Abba laughed. "So my memory was wrong, and my eyes fooled me too. Your book is better than both of them."

"What if it does start to grow one day?" asked Farzana.

"Then your book will tell us early," said Dadi, "and we will call an engineer who still climbs ladders."

So Farzana kept checking, once a month now. Imran gave up on the crack. But he did draw a pencil line on the door frame at the top of his head, with the date beside it. "That's my tell-tale," he said. "Next year it will show how much I grew."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Abba and Ammi both trusted their memories, and their memories disagreed. Neither could be checked, so neither could settle the question. Farzana's plan turned the argument into something you could look at: pencil lines at the ends to show any lengthening, a paper strip across the crack to show any widening, the same time and light each week, and a record. The record showed no change, and 'no change' is a real result. It also explained why Abba was fooled: low evening light makes cracks look larger. Imran's height mark at the end is the same idea used for something that really does change.

- Ask: Abba and Ammi remembered the crack differently. Why couldn't they settle who was right?
- Ask: Why did Farzana draw lines past both ends of the crack, and not just one?
- Ask: What was the paper strip for? What would it have shown if the crack got wider?
- Ask: Why did Dadi say once a week was enough, and not every hour?
- Ask: Imran said nothing changing was boring. Dadi said it was an answer too. Who do you agree with, and why?
- Ask: Why did Dadi always check in the morning light?

  *Parent says:* This game tests two ways of remembering. First you'll use only your memory. Then you'll make a quick drawing. Which do you think will catch more changes?

- **s2 (5 min)**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Brown Apple Watch (35 min)
- **question:** Does a cut apple slice turn brown fastest in the open air, with lemon juice on it, or under water?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which slice will turn brown fastest, and which will stay pale longest? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason before you cut the apple.
- **fair test:** Cut all three slices from the same apple, at the same time, about as thick as a pencil. Keep them in the same room, out of direct sun. Only one thing is different: plain, lemon or water. Score every slice at the same times, using the same colour scale and the same light.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the apple. The slices are safe to eat afterwards, even the brown one.
- **record:** Fill in the table at every look. Draw each slice small, in a row, with the time under each drawing.
- **results table:** Time (minutes after cutting) | Plain slice: colour score (0 to 3) | Lemon slice: colour score | Water slice: colour score | What I noticed
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the colour scale card with your child. 2. Draw the results table in the notebook. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Before we cut anything, let's plan. Our question is which slice browns fastest. We'll look every 10 minutes for an hour. Which slice do you think will brown fastest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Make your colour card: 0, 1, 2, 3. Draw your table. Which slice will go brown first? Why? Write it down.
  - Ask: Which slice do you think will brown fastest, and why? (good: The plain one, because the air touches it.; The water one, because water makes things soggy.; The lemon one, because lemon is strong.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine, as long as it has a reason. Don't hint. A wrong guess written down is a very good start, because the test will surprise your child.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut three slices from the same apple, each about as thick as a pencil. 2. Put one on the plate by the label 'plain'. 3. Rub lemon juice over both sides of the second, and put it by the label 'lemon'. 4. Put the third in the bowl of water, with the small plate on top to keep it under. 5. Write the time.  
  *Parent says:* Now I'll cut three slices quickly, so they all start at the same time. Lemon juice goes on this one. This one goes under water. Let's write the start time.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts three slices. Put one by 'plain'. Rub lemon on one. Put one under water. Write the time now.
  - Note: Apple slices float, so the small plate or katori on top matters. Without it, the top of the slice sticks out into the air and browns. Cut fast, so all three start within a minute of each other.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at all three slices. 2. Hold each next to the colour card and give it a score. 3. Draw each one small in the notebook, with '0 minutes' under it. 4. Smell and touch the plain slice.  
  *Parent says:* This is our first look, at 0 minutes. Hold each slice next to the colour card. What score is it? How does it smell? How does it feel?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each slice next to your colour card. What score? Draw it. Smell it. Touch it. Write 0 minutes.
  - Note: Take the water slice out for a few seconds to score it, then put it straight back under the plate. All three should score 0 now.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to stare at the plain slice for one full minute, without looking away.  
  *Parent says:* Let's try something. Stare at the plain slice for one whole minute. Tell me the moment you see it change.  
  *Child mode:* Stare at the plain slice for one whole minute. Shout when you see it change!
  - Check: You stared at the plain slice for one minute. What did you see? → **It looked the same the whole minute**
- **s5 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every 10 minutes, for an hour, score and draw all three slices. 2. Write the time and the scores in the table each time. 3. Between looks, do something else, like reading the Tycho Brahe card.  
  *Parent says:* It's 10 minutes. Time to look again. Same light, same colour card. What scores now?  
  *Child mode:* Every 10 minutes, look again. Score each slice. Draw it. Write the time. Then go and do something else.
  - Note: Each look takes about 2 minutes, so six looks take about 12 minutes, spread over an hour. Look in the same place and light each time. Light from a window changes through the day, so a lamp you switch on for each look gives the fairest light. If after an hour the plain slice still looks pale, your apple is a slow-browning kind. Leave it two more hours and look again, or try the same test with a slice of raw potato, which browns faster.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the drawings side by side. 2. Ask your child to say what they think happened, what they saw, and why. 3. Read the explanation card on why a cut apple browns.  
  *Parent says:* Let's lay the drawings in a row. Which slice browned fastest? What did your record show that staring didn't? Now, why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your drawings in a row. Which slice went brown fastest? Was your guess right? Why do you think it happened?
  - Ask: What did you think, what did you see, and why do you think it happened? (good: I thought the water one would brown first. But the plain one did. The air touched it most.; The lemon slice stayed at 0. Lemon must stop the browning.)
  - Note: Expect the plain slice to brown most and the lemon slice least. The water slice is usually in between, browning slowly. Lemon juice is sour, and the enzyme that makes the brown works slowly in sour juice. Water keeps out most of the oxygen.
  - Check: The plain slice browned and the water slice stayed paler. What does this tell us? → **Something in the air helps the apple turn brown**
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let your child taste a small bite of each slice, if they want to.  
  *Parent says:* All three are safe to eat. Does the brown one taste different? Does the lemon one?  
  *Child mode:* You can taste each slice. They are all safe. Does the brown one taste different?
  - Think: If nobody had looked at the apple for an hour, would it still have gone brown?
### e2 · Plan Your Own Apple Test (30 min)
- **question:** Pick one: does a slice brown more slowly in the fridge? In salty water? Under an upside-down steel bowl? Your plan answers the one you pick.
- **predict:** Before you start, write what you think will happen to your two slices, and why you think so.
- **fair test:** Two slices from the same apple, cut at the same time, the same thickness. One is your 'normal' slice on a plate on the table. The other gets your one change. Everything else stays the same, and you score both at the same times with the same colour card.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the apple.
- **record:** Write your plan on one page and your results table on the next. Draw both slices at every look.
- **results table:** Time (minutes after cutting) | Normal slice: colour score (0 to 3) | Changed slice: colour score | What I noticed
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child choose one change and write a plan with these headings: My question. My guess and why. What I change. What I keep the same. How often I look, and for how long. How I record it.  
  *Parent says:* This time you're the scientist who makes the plan. Choose one change. Then write each part of your plan. I'll only check that nothing is missing.  
  *Child mode:* Choose one change: fridge, salty water, or a bowl on top. Write your question. Write your guess and why. Write what stays the same.
  - Ask: What will you keep the same for both slices? (good: The same apple.; Cut at the same time.; The same thickness.; The same colour card and the same light.)
  - Note: Let your child make the choices, even if you think a choice won't work well. If they forget to write how often they will look, ask 'When will you look?' rather than telling them. Every 10 minutes for an hour works. For the fridge test, take the slice out only for the few seconds it takes to score it, because warming it changes the test.
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Cut two slices at the same time. Put the normal slice on the plate on the table and give the other slice its change. Write the start time.  
  *Parent says:* Two slices, cut at the same moment. Which one gets the change? Write the time.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts two slices. Put one on the plate. Give the other your change. Write the time.
- **s3 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every 10 minutes for an hour, score and draw both slices, and write the time.  
  *Parent says:* Time for a look. Score both slices with the colour card. Draw them. Write the time.  
  *Child mode:* Every 10 minutes, score both slices. Draw them. Write the time.
  - Note: In the fridge test, the cold slice usually browns much more slowly. In salty water, the slice usually stays pale. Under an upside-down bowl, there is still air, so the slice usually browns almost as fast as the open one. Your child does not know this yet, so don't say it.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the two rows of drawings. Ask what your child found, and whether the plan worked.  
  *Parent says:* What did your test show? Did your plan work? Is there anything you would change if you did it again?  
  *Child mode:* What did your test show? Was your guess right? What would you change next time?
  - Check: Kavya tested a slice under an upside-down steel bowl. It browned almost as fast as her open slice. Why? → **There was still air with oxygen inside the bowl**

## Challenges
### c1 · Catch the Sunny Patch (30 min)
- **problem:** On a sunny day, the bright patch from a window creeps across the floor. It moves too slowly to see. How can you record where it goes over two hours, while still doing other things?
- **your turn:** Now design your own way to record the sunny patch. Draw your plan, try it for two hours, and show your record. What does it show that one look could not?
- **constraints:** Use only things from home.; You may not sit and watch the whole time.; Your record must show where the patch was and at what time.; Don't put tape where it may peel paint, and ask before marking the floor.
- **general:** Lay a big sheet of paper or newspaper on the floor where the patch is, and trace its edge every 15 minutes, writing the time on each outline.; Put a small piece of tape at one corner of the patch every 15 minutes, with the time written on it.; Take a photo from the same spot every 15 minutes.
- **creative:** Put a steel tumbler in the patch and draw the edge of its shadow every 15 minutes. The shadow moves as the patch does.; Set a phone to take a time-lapse video from a chair, and watch two hours in a few seconds.; Trace the patch in a different colour each time, so the outlines make a rainbow fan.

### c2 · The Plan Swap (30 min)
- **problem:** A scientist's plan must be so clear that another scientist, somewhere else, can repeat the test and check the result. Can you write an apple-slice plan that a grown-up can follow exactly, without asking you a single question?
- **your turn:** Now write your own plan for a different slow change, like a banana ripening, and swap it with someone. Could they follow it without asking you anything?
- **constraints:** Your plan must fit on one page.; The person following it may not ask you anything.; They must be able to say what to compare, what to keep the same, how often to look and how to record.
- **general:** Number every step.; Give amounts and times: 'three slices, each as thick as a pencil', 'every 10 minutes for 60 minutes'.; Draw the results table for them.
- **creative:** Add small pictures beside each step, like a recipe card.; Add a 'what to do if' line: what to do if the plain slice stays pale.; Ask two people to follow the same plan, and see if they get the same scores.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child writes a one-page plan for the brown apple watch, from memory and their own notes.  
  *Parent says:* Write a plan so clear that I could do the apple test without asking you anything. Every step, every amount, every time.  
  *Child mode:* Write the apple plan for a grown-up. Every step. Every amount. Every time. They can't ask you anything!
- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* The grown-up follows the plan exactly, doing only what is written. When something is missing, they stop and say what they don't know.  
  *Parent says:* I'll follow your plan exactly, word for word. If something is missing, I'll stop and tell you.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the grown-up follow your plan. Where do they get stuck?
  - Note: Be strict but kind. If the plan says 'cut slices' without saying how many, say 'How many?' and wait for your child to add it. The gaps are the lesson. You don't need to run the full hour: following the plan up to the first two looks is enough.

### Fair Tests and Good Records (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Arjun cut his plain slice from a red apple and his lemon slice from a green apple. The lemon slice stayed paler. What is wrong with his test?  
   Options: Nothing, it is a fair test / Two things were different, so he can't tell if the lemon or the apple made the difference / He should have used more lemon  
   **Answer:** Two things were different, so he can't tell if the lemon or the apple made the difference. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing differs. Arjun changed the apple and the lemon at once. Some kinds of apple brown more slowly than others, so the green apple alone might explain it. All slices should come from one apple.
2. (write) Lakshmi did the apple test but looked only once, after five hours. All three slices were brown. She says, 'Lemon doesn't work.' What went wrong with her plan, and how should she fix it?  
   **Answer:** She looked too late, so she missed the time when the lemon slice was still pale and the others were brown. Lemon slows browning, but after many hours even a lemon slice may brown. She should look every 10 minutes for the first hour.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that the interval and the length of the watch were wrong for the question, not the lemon. Slowing a change is not the same as stopping it.
3. (mcq) Here is part of Revathi's apple record. Plain slice: 1 at 20 minutes, 2 at 30 minutes. Water slice: 0 at 20 minutes, 0 at 30 minutes. Which slice changed between 20 and 30 minutes?  
   Options: The plain slice / The water slice / Both / Neither  
   **Answer:** The plain slice. *Why:* The plain slice went from score 1 to score 2. The water slice stayed at 0. Reading a record line by line shows exactly when each change happened.
4. (truefalse) Lemon juice keeps an apple slice pale only because it covers the slice and keeps the air out.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A slice wet with plain water still browns in the air. Lemon works mainly because it is sour, or acidic, and the browning enzyme works slowly in acid. Its vitamin C also undoes the first step of the browning.
5. (fill) The gas from the air that helps a cut apple turn brown is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['oxygen']. *Why:* Oxygen joins with the apple's colourless substances, helped by the enzyme, to make the brown ones.
6. (sort) Kavya tests whether a slice browns more slowly in the fridge. Sort these into what she changes and what she keeps the same.

7. (order) Remember Farzana in the story, who checked the crack in the wall. Put her plan in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Ask exactly what the question is: longer, wider or both?', 'Draw pencil lines past both ends and write the date', 'Glue a paper strip across the widest part', 'Check every Sunday and after heavy rain', 'Compare six weeks of records']. *Why:* A clear question comes first, because it decides what to mark. Then the marks, then regular looks, then comparing the record.
8. (perspective) Two friends in Mysuru are keeping records of a banana ripening. They argue about the best record.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that a photo is exact and quick but can miss what you didn't notice, while a drawing makes you look closely but can be inaccurate. Many scientists use both.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Each kind of record is good at something different. A strong answer uses an example from the child's own records.
9. (draw) Draw a plan card for the banana diary. Show your question, how often you will look, what you will count, and how you will record it.  
   **Answer:** A card with a question (for example 'How fast does the banana get brown spots?'), 'every morning at 8', 'count spots and score the colour', and a small table or photo plan.. *Why:* A strong plan card says what, how often, how long and how to record, so someone else could follow it.

   **Answer:** So a change in the light doesn't look like a change in the apple. *Why:* Window light changes through the day. A slice can look darker in dim light even if it hasn't changed. Keeping the light the same is part of keeping the test fair.
11. (write) The slice under water browned more slowly than the plain slice. Explain why, using the word oxygen.  
   **Answer:** The browning needs oxygen from the air. Water keeps most of the air away from the slice, so less oxygen reaches it, and it browns more slowly.. *Why:* A strong answer links the slow browning to less oxygen, not to the water itself doing something to the apple.
12. (match) Match each slow change to a good interval between looks.

   **Answer:** That the sky beyond the Moon never changes. *Why:* Many people in Europe followed Aristotle, who taught that the sky beyond the Moon was perfect and unchanging. A new star far beyond the Moon showed that the sky can change.

## Applications
- Weather scientists record rainfall and temperature every day, and long records show how the climate is changing.
- Doctors chart a patient's temperature every few hours to see whether a fever is going up or down.
- Engineers mark cracks in bridges and buildings and check them for months to see whether they are growing.
- Farmers note when crops flower and when pests appear, so they can plan the next season.
- Astronomers photograph the same patch of sky night after night to find new stars, comets and asteroids.
- Cooks keep cut potato and brinjal in water, and put lemon on cut fruit, to slow browning.
- Teachers and parents mark a child's height on a wall chart each year.
- Wildlife scientists count birds at the same place every year to see whether their numbers are rising or falling.
- Food scientists test new ways to keep cut fruit fresh, using the same kind of plan as the apple test.

## Spotted around us
- A height chart on a door frame or wall, with a pencil mark and a date for every birthday.
- Cut brinjal or potato kept in a bowl of water in the kitchen, waiting to be cooked.
- Lemon squeezed over cut fruit at a fruit stall, so the slices stay bright.
- A rain gauge or weather station on the roof of a school or office.
- A doctor's or nurse's chart at a hospital bed, with the time and temperature written in rows.
- A calendar where someone marks the days the milk, the newspaper or the water tanker came.
- The phone's photo gallery, where the same view photographed on different days shows changes.
- A bank of bananas at a fruit shop, from green to yellow to spotted, showing every stage at once.

## Open questions
- **Exactly what jobs does the browning enzyme do inside a healthy apple tree?** Many scientists think it helps defend damaged fruit and leaves against insects and germs. But the enzyme is also found in healthy parts of the plant. Its other jobs there are still being studied.
- **Why does memory change over time, and can we stop it?** Scientists know memories are rebuilt each time we recall them, and can pick up new details. How this happens in the brain is still being worked out.
- **Which stars in our part of the galaxy will explode next, and when?** Astronomers know some stars that will one day become supernovas, like the red giant Betelgeuse. But they can't yet say whether that will be in a hundred years or a hundred thousand.
- **How many slow changes around us has nobody ever recorded?** Many slow changes in soil, rocks, trees and animals have only been watched for a few years, or not at all. Long records, kept for decades, are rare and precious.
- **How small a change can the human eye notice?** It depends on the light, the colour and the person. Scientists have measured it in labs, but in everyday life it varies a lot, which is one more reason to use records.

## Connected maths
- In the brown apple watch, Revathi looks at her slices at 0 minutes, then every 10 minutes until 60 minutes. How many looks is that? **7 looks.**. She looks at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 minutes. That is 7 looks. Counting in tens from 10 to 60 gives 6, and the first look at 0 makes 7. It is easy to forget the start. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Divya cut her apple at 4:15 in the afternoon. Her plain slice reached score 2 at 4:45. How long did it take? **30 minutes.**. From 4:15 to 4:45, the minute hand moves from 15 to 45. 45 minus 15 is 30. So it took 30 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Tycho Brahe saw the new star in November 1572. It faded from sight in March 1574. About how many months could it be seen? **About 16 months.**. From November 1572 to November 1573 is 12 months. From November 1573 to March 1574 is 4 more months: December, January, February and March. 12 and 4 make 16. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- Sundari counted the brown spots on her banana each morning: 0, 2, 6, 15. How many new spots appeared each day? Do you think day 5 will have more or fewer than 25 spots? **2, then 4, then 9 new spots. Day 5 will probably have more than 25.**. From 0 to 2 is 2 new spots. From 2 to 6 is 4 new. From 6 to 15 is 9 new. The number of new spots grows each day, so on day 5 probably more than 9 new spots appear. 15 and 10 is 25, so day 5 probably has more than 25. The spots may soon join up and be too many to count. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In the Window Watch, Kavya counted crows for one minute at three times: 6 crows at 7 in the morning, 1 at 1 in the afternoon, 4 at 5 in the evening. How many more crows came at 7 than at 1? Can she say crows always come less at 1 o'clock? **5 more crows. She can't say 'always' from one day.**. 6 minus 1 is 5. But one day can be a fluke: maybe a loud lorry passed at 1 o'clock. To be sure of a pattern, she needs to count at the same times on many days. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- Arjun wants to check his banana every 10 minutes for one whole day of 12 hours. There are 6 lots of 10 minutes in an hour. How many looks would that be? Is it a good plan? **72 looks. It is not a good plan for a banana.**. 6 looks an hour for 12 hours is 6 times 12, which is 72. A banana changes so slowly that almost every look would show nothing new. One look a day is enough. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
