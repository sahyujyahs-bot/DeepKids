# Soil, water and crops: how soil holds water for roots, and why a field of beans feeds the next crop
`sci.planet-earth.soil-and-crops.L4`

**Summary.** Stand a cup of dry sand and a cup of fine dry soil in a tray of water. In an hour, the water has crept up both cups against gravity, but at very different speeds. Farmers use this every day when they cover soil with straw to keep the water in. They also know a second puzzle. A field tired by rice grows better rice after a crop of moong or groundnut, even though nobody added anything. Your child tests how water climbs in soil, how salt stops seeds, and why bean roots carry tiny helpers who pull nitrogen out of the air.

**Intro.** *Dip the corner of a dry cloth into a bowl of water and leave it. Slowly the water climbs the cloth, up and up, with nobody pushing it. Soil does the same thing. So why does the top of a bare pot dry out in a day, while the soil under a layer of straw stays damp?* Water can climb through soil because of the tiny gaps between the grains. How high it climbs depends on how small the grains are. In this unit you measure the climb in gravel, sand and fine soil. You find out what salt does to seeds, and how a cover of leaves saves water. Then you meet the bean plant and the bacteria in her roots, who together pull nitrogen out of the air and leave a field richer than before. You also learn why farmers across India follow rice with a pulse.

## Terms
- **Texture**: How coarse or fine a soil feels, decided by how much sand, silt and clay it has. *e.g.* A soil that feels gritty has a sandy texture. A soil that feels sticky when wet has a clayey texture.
- **Loam**: A soil with a good mix of sand, silt, clay and humus. It drains well and still holds water that roots can use. *e.g.* Many vegetable gardens and fruit orchards are planted in loam.
- **Pore**: A tiny gap between the grains in soil. Pores hold air and water. *e.g.* When you pour water on dry soil, bubbles pop out of the pores.
- **Drainage**: How fast extra water runs down and out of soil. *e.g.* Water runs out of a sandy pot in seconds, but a clayey pot stays wet for hours.
- **Capillary rise**: The climbing of water up through very narrow gaps, such as the gaps between soil grains or the threads of a cloth. *e.g.* Tea climbs up a sugar cube dipped in it, and oil climbs up the wick of a lamp.
- **Evaporation**: Water turning into an invisible gas and rising into the air. *e.g.* Wet clothes dry on a line because the water in them evaporates.
- **Mulch**: A cover of straw, dry leaves, husk or similar material laid on top of soil to slow down evaporation. *e.g.* Farmers lay paddy straw or sugarcane leaves between rows of vegetables.
- **Wilting**: When a plant droops because her cells have lost water. *e.g.* A tomato plant in a pot droops in the afternoon sun when the soil is dry.
- **Nitrogen**: A gas that makes up about four fifths of the air. It is also a nutrient that plants need to make leaves and grow green. *e.g.* A plant short of nitrogen has pale, yellowish leaves.
- **Fertiliser**: A material that farmers add to soil to give plants nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. *e.g.* Urea is a common fertiliser that holds a lot of nitrogen.
- **Legume**: A plant from the bean family, whose seeds grow in pods. Many legumes are pulses and can work with bacteria to take nitrogen from the air. *e.g.* Moong, urad, chana, groundnut and peas are legumes.
- **Root nodule**: A small round swelling on the root of a legume, where helper bacteria live. *e.g.* On a pulled-up groundnut plant, the nodules look like tiny beads on the roots.
- **Nitrogen fixation**: Changing nitrogen gas from the air into a form that plants can use. Some bacteria can do this work. People can do it in factories. *e.g.* Bacteria in a bean's root nodules fix nitrogen.
- **Crop rotation**: Growing different crops one after another in the same field, instead of the same crop every time. *e.g.* A farmer grows rice, then black gram, then rice again.
- **Fallow**: A field that is left without a crop for a season, to rest. *e.g.* In dry regions, farmers may leave a field fallow so its water can build up.
- **Salinity**: How much salt there is in soil or water. *e.g.* Seawater has high salinity. Rainwater has almost none.
- **Acidic and alkaline**: Words for two opposite kinds of liquid or soil. Vinegar is acidic. Baking soda in water is alkaline. A soil may be one or the other, or neither. *e.g.* Tea grows best in acidic soil, and many chalky soils are alkaline.
- **Intercropping**: Growing two or more crops together in the same field in the same season. *e.g.* Many farmers in Karnataka grow ragi with a row of red gram in between.

## Explanations
### Texture decides how soil behaves with water · *defined*
**Definition:** A soil's texture is how much sand, silt and clay it has. The size of the grains decides how much water the soil holds and how fast it lets go.

Water in soil does not sit in puddles between the grains. Most of it clings to the grains as thin films. The finer the grains, the more grain surface there is for water to cling to. A few grams of some clays have more grain surface than a classroom floor. So clay holds a lot of water, and holds it tightly. But its gaps are tiny, so water drains very slowly, and when the soil is soaked there is little air left for roots. Sand is the other way round. Its gaps are big, so water runs through quickly and air gets in, but very little water stays behind. Loam has some of each, so it drains well and still keeps a lot of water that roots can use.
- e.g. A sandy pot dries out in a day in summer and needs watering often.
- e.g. A field of heavy clay stays sticky and waterlogged after rain, and roots can struggle for air.
- e.g. Coconut palms on the sandy coast of Kerala and Tamil Nadu need plenty of water because sand holds so little.
### Water in soil: drains away, held for roots, held too tightly · *defined*
**Definition:** Water in soil comes in three kinds: water that drains away, water that roots can use, and water held so tightly by the finest grains that roots cannot pull it out.

After heavy rain, soil is full. The water in the biggest gaps runs down and drains away because of gravity. What is left is held on the grains as films. Roots can drink most of this water. But a small part sits so close to the finest grains that it is held very tightly, and roots cannot get it out. When the soil has dried down to that point, a plant droops, even though the soil still has a little water in it. This is called wilting. The best soil for a crop is one that, after the rain, keeps a lot of the second kind of water. Loam does that best. Sand has little of it, and clay holds a lot of the third kind.
- e.g. A sandy soil may need watering every day, because most of the water drains away and little is held for roots.
- e.g. A potted plant drooping at midday and standing up again in the evening has used up the easy water and is waiting for more.
- e.g. A farmer waters a field after the soil has begun to dry, not while it is still full, so the roots also get air.
### Capillary rise: how water climbs through soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Capillary rise is water climbing up through very narrow gaps against gravity. The narrower the gap, the higher the water climbs.

Water is sticky in two ways. Water particles stick to each other, and they stick to the surface of the grains. At the edge of a narrow gap, the water sticks to the wall and pulls the water behind it upwards. This goes on until the weight of the lifted water balances the pull. In a wide gap, the pull is small, so water climbs only a little. In a narrow gap, the pull is strong, so water climbs much higher. It also moves more slowly through narrow gaps, because the gap is tight. So water climbs only a centimetre or two in gravel, tens of centimetres in sand, and up to a metre or more in very fine soil in the ground. But the finer the soil, the slower the climb. A cup of sand fills quickly, and a cup of fine soil takes much longer to wet. This is why a wick lifts oil up a diya, and why a sugar cube turns brown when you touch it to tea.
- e.g. Water climbs up the threads of a dry towel hanging with one corner in a bucket.
- e.g. A mud wall of a house gets damp at the bottom after the rains, as water climbs up from the wet ground.
- e.g. A cotton wick lifts oil up from the bowl of a lamp to the flame.
### Why the top of a field dries, and what mulch does · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil loses most of its water to the air by evaporation from the top. Mulch, a cover of straw or leaves, slows this down.

Water climbs up through the soil by capillary rise until it reaches the surface. There the Sun and wind turn it into vapour and carry it away. On a hot day a bare field can lose a thick layer of water this way. A cover of straw, dry leaves or husk shades the soil and stops the wind from reaching it, so much less water escapes. Mulch also stops raindrops from beating the soil hard, and, as it rots, it turns into humus. Some farmers also loosen the top few centimetres of soil with a hoe. This is meant to break the narrow paths that the water climbs, so it is not drawn all the way up to the surface. Scientists find that this works well in some soils and little in others.
- e.g. Farmers lay paddy straw, coconut husk or sugarcane leaves around the plants in vegetable and banana fields.
- e.g. A gardener covers the soil of a pot with dry leaves to keep it damp.
- e.g. A black plastic sheet laid over soil, used on many farms, keeps the soil damp and the weeds down.
- **Many farmers and gardeners:** Hoeing the top of the soil in a dry spell saves water, because it breaks the paths that carry water to the surface.
- **Some soil scientists:** Loosening the soil helps in some soils and in some weather, but it can also dry out the top layer and bring more soil to the air. A cover of mulch works better in most tests.
### What plants take from the soil: nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants make their food from air, water and light. But they also need more than a dozen nutrients from the soil. The three needed in the largest amounts are nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.

A plant takes these nutrients through their roots, dissolved in water. Nitrogen goes into leaves and proteins, and gives leaves their green colour. A plant short of nitrogen has pale yellow leaves and grows slowly. Phosphorus helps roots grow, and helps flowers and seeds form. Potassium helps a plant manage her water and fight disease. Every harvest carries nutrients away from the field in the grain, the fruit and the stalks. So they must be put back. Farmers do this with compost, manure and fertilisers. A fertiliser bag shows three numbers for nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. Urea has 46 per cent nitrogen, so 100 kilograms of urea carries 46 kilograms of nitrogen. A plant cannot tell whether a nitrate ion came from manure or from a factory. The ion is the same.
- e.g. A pale yellow rice field often means the plants are short of nitrogen.
- e.g. The numbers on a bag of complex fertiliser, such as 19:19:19, are the shares of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
- e.g. The Soil Health Card tells a farmer whether the soil is low, medium or high in each of these.
### Nitrogen from the air: the bean and the bacteria · *defined*
**Definition:** Most plants cannot use the nitrogen gas in the air. Legumes can, because bacteria living in swellings on their roots change nitrogen gas into a form the plant can use.

About four of every five parts of air are nitrogen gas. Yet plants cannot use it, because the two nitrogen atoms in each molecule are held together very strongly. Some bacteria have a tool that can break that hold. Bean plants and their relatives have learned to house these bacteria. Rhizobium bacteria enter the root hairs of a young legume. The root grows a nodule around them. Inside, the bacteria change nitrogen gas into ammonia, which the plant turns into protein. The plant pays for this with sugar made in her leaves. Breaking the nitrogen bond takes a lot of energy. The tool the bacteria use is damaged by oxygen, so the nodule makes a red-pink substance that holds oxygen back. A working nodule is pink when you cut it open. Lightning also fixes a little nitrogen, and so do bacteria that live free in the soil. Rice farmers in India grow a floating fern called azolla in paddy fields, where blue-green bacteria in the fern leaves fix nitrogen.
- e.g. Moong, urad, groundnut, chana and peas all grow nodules on their roots.
- e.g. A working nodule is pink inside, and one that has stopped working is white or green.
- e.g. Azolla in a paddy field is a fern whose leaves hold nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
### Why crop rotation works · *defined*
**Definition:** Crop rotation is growing different crops in turn in the same field. It spreads out the work on the soil, and breaks up the life cycles of pests and diseases.

A crop of rice or wheat takes a lot of nitrogen out of the soil. A crop of moong or groundnut can fix its own nitrogen, so it takes less from the soil. The roots and leaves left behind in the field when a legume is harvested add nitrogen too, as they rot. This is why farmers have long followed a cereal with a pulse. But a pulse harvest also carries away nitrogen in its seeds, so the gain is smaller than many people think. Rotation also helps in other ways. A pest or disease that attacks one crop starves when the next crop is a different kind. Deep roots and shallow roots take water and nutrients from different layers. In the Kaveri delta, many farmers sow black gram or green gram on the damp soil left after the rice harvest. Some fields are also left fallow for a season, so the soil can rest and its water can build up.
- e.g. Rice then black gram, then rice again.
- e.g. Groundnut then ragi, a pattern followed in many places in Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. Cotton then a pulse, so the soil is not asked for the same thing twice in a row.
### Growing crops together: intercropping and mixed fields · *defined*
**Definition:** Intercropping is growing two or more crops together in the same field. Crops that need different things, or that help each other, can share one field.

A tall crop and a short crop use light at different heights. A deep-rooted crop and a shallow-rooted one use water from different layers. A pulse grown between rows of cereal adds nitrogen near its neighbours. If one crop fails, another may still give a harvest. Many Indian farmers grow ragi with red gram, or groundnut with red gram. In Uttarakhand, the baranaja system grows around a dozen grains and pulses together. In North America, the Haudenosaunee peoples grew maize, beans and squash together: the maize stalk held the bean, the bean added nitrogen, and the squash leaves shaded the ground. The gain depends on the crops, and some mixes do worse than separate fields.
- e.g. Ragi with rows of red gram in Karnataka.
- e.g. Coconut palms with banana, pepper and turmeric growing under them in Kerala.
- e.g. Maize, beans and squash planted together by the Haudenosaunee.
- **Many traditional farmers and some researchers:** A mixed field is steadier. If rain fails or a pest strikes one crop, the others still give a harvest.
- **Some modern agricultural economists:** One crop in a field is easier to plough, sow and harvest with machines, and gives a bigger harvest of that crop. Mixed fields can give a smaller total for the work, unless the farmer plans them well.
### Salt in the soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Salt builds up in soil when water that carries salt is used up by evaporation and plants, and the salt is left behind.

All water from rivers, canals and wells carries a little dissolved salt. When this water is spread on a field and evaporates, only the water goes into the air. The salt stays. Year after year, it builds up, and in dry places a white crust can appear on the top of the soil. Salt makes it harder for roots to take in water, and too much damages them. Seeds sprout badly in salty soil. Salt also arrives where seawater creeps into the ground along a coast. Farmers can clear it by letting extra water wash the salt down and out through drains. Some crops have learned to cope. In Kerala, the Pokkali rice grown in the backwaters of Alappuzha and Ernakulam can grow in salty water. Millions of hectares of farmland around the world are damaged by salt.
- e.g. A white crust on the edge of a dry irrigated field.
- e.g. A well along a coast turning brackish when too much groundwater is pumped out and seawater flows in.
- e.g. Pokkali rice growing in the salty backwaters of Kerala.
### Acid and alkaline soils · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil can be acidic, neutral or alkaline. Plants grow best in different kinds, and each kind changes which nutrients a plant can take up.

The pH scale runs from 0 to 14. A pH of 7 is neutral. Lower numbers are acidic, and higher numbers are alkaline. Vinegar is acidic. Baking soda in water is alkaline. Soils in rainy places, such as the laterite soils of Kerala, tend to be acidic, because rain washes away the minerals that balance acid. Soils in dry places, or made from chalky rock, tend to be alkaline. Tea bushes in Assam and the Nilgiris grow best in acid soil. In soil that is too acidic or too alkaline, some nutrients are locked up and a plant cannot take them in. A farmer can add lime to an acidic soil, and compost helps many soils. A Soil Health Card reports the pH of a field.
- e.g. Tea gardens in the Nilgiris and Assam grow on acid soils.
- e.g. Red cabbage water turns pink with vinegar and green-blue with baking soda, and can be used to compare soils.
- e.g. A farmer spreads lime on an acid field before sowing.
### Who is in charge of the trade between a bean and her bacteria? · *debated*
The bean gives sugar. The bacteria give nitrogen. But what happens when some bacteria give less than others? In 2003, researchers reported that soybean plants seemed to punish nodules that fixed little nitrogen, perhaps by cutting their oxygen supply, and those bacteria did less well. Many biologists read this as the plant sanctioning cheats. Others say there is no plan behind it. The plant simply gives more oxygen and sugar to the nodules that are working, as a result of how the nodule is built. Some scientists point out that the bacteria also change their behaviour, and that bacteria in the soil sense chemicals and choose where to swim. So the trade may be steered from both sides. Nobody has settled how much choice each side has.
- e.g. A soybean nodule that fixes less nitrogen gets less oxygen.
- e.g. Different kinds of Rhizobium bacteria can enter the same bean, and some fix more than others.
- e.g. Beans in rich soil with plenty of nitrogen grow fewer nodules.
- **Many biologists:** The plant rewards nodules that give her nitrogen and punishes those that do not, like a trader who stops buying from a poor supplier. Cheating bacteria do worse.
- **Other biologists:** Nothing is being decided. The nodule's oxygen and sugar supply simply follows how well the nodule is working, as the result of its build. Words like punish and reward are pictures.
- **Some plant scientists:** Both sides sense and respond. A root hair curls around the bacteria that the plant accepts, and bacteria swim towards the signals the root sends. The trade is a conversation, not an order.
### Nitrogen from factories, and what nobody knows yet · *idea*
**Definition:** In 1909 the German chemist Fritz Haber found a way to make ammonia from nitrogen gas and hydrogen, and Carl Bosch turned it into a factory process a few years later. Nitrogen fertiliser made this way is reported to feed about half the world's people today.

The Haber-Bosch process breaks the strong bond in nitrogen gas using high pressure and heat, and a metal catalyst. It uses a great deal of energy, which today mostly comes from natural gas and coal. The nitrogen that crops do not take up can wash into rivers and lakes. It feeds green algae, which then use up the oxygen in the water and kill fish. So scientists are asking new questions. Could cereals like rice and wheat be helped to grow their own nitrogen partners? A maize variety grown by the Mixe people in Oaxaca, Mexico, has been reported to grow root-like structures that drip a sugary jelly where nitrogen-fixing bacteria live. Can that be copied into other crops? Nobody knows yet whether it can be made to work at scale.
- e.g. A fertiliser factory turning nitrogen gas and hydrogen into ammonia.
- e.g. Green algae covering a lake after nitrogen washes in from fields.
- e.g. Researchers studying nitrogen-fixing bacteria on the aerial roots of the Oaxaca maize.

## Mostly agreed
- Water is held in soil as thin films on the grains and in the gaps, and finer soils hold more water. *How we know:* Soil scientists weigh soils at known wetness and measure how much water they hold. Clay soils hold more than sand, in every measurement made.
- Water climbs up through soil gaps against gravity, and finer gaps lift it higher but more slowly. *How we know:* The climb has been measured in glass tubes and in soils for over a hundred years, and the height follows the width of the gap.
- A cover of mulch cuts the water lost from the top of the soil. *How we know:* Field trials in many countries, including India, weigh the water lost from mulched and bare soil. Mulched soil loses less.
- Plants need nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium in large amounts, and more than a dozen other nutrients in small amounts. *How we know:* Plants grown in water with one nutrient left out show clear signs, such as pale leaves without nitrogen, and the signs go when the nutrient is added.
- Bacteria in the nodules of legumes change nitrogen gas into ammonia, and the plant pays them in sugar. *How we know:* Scientists give plants nitrogen gas with a marker form of nitrogen atom, and find the marker in the plant's proteins.
- Irrigation water leaves its dissolved salt behind when it evaporates, and the salt can build up in the soil. *How we know:* Salt crusts and measured salt levels are found in irrigated soils worldwide, and land managers have to wash the salt out.
- Growing different crops in turn lowers the build-up of pests and diseases and spreads the demand on the soil. *How we know:* Long-running field experiments, some over a hundred years old, compare fields with and without rotation.

## How we found out
**Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth, German scientists, and Martinus Beijerinck, a Dutch scientist, 1886 to 1888.** More than two thousand years ago, Roman farm writers told farmers to grow lupins and beans to enrich a field, but nobody knew why. In the 1800s many chemists believed that plants take all their nitrogen from the soil, and that beans were no different. Careful field trials at Rothamsted in England seemed to agree. Then in the 1880s two German scientists, Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth, grew peas and cereals in pots of pure sand with no nitrogen. The cereals starved. Peas starved too, unless the sand was watered with a little water from garden soil. Then the peas grew well and had nodules on their roots. When the soil water was heated first, the peas starved again. Something alive in the soil water was helping. In 1888 Martinus Beijerinck grew the bacteria from the nodules.  
*What people believed before:* Many chemists believed that plants take all their nitrogen from the soil, and that no plant could use nitrogen from the air. Hellriegel and Wilfarth showed that peas could grow on nitrogen from the air, but only when helped by living things in the soil.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Sand is bad soil and clay is good soil, or the other way round.** (A common idea among gardeners and farmers; *rejected*)  
  For: Sand dries out fast, and clay can be sticky and hard to dig. Clay holds a lot of water and nutrients.  
  Against: Each does some things well and some badly. Sand drains and has air, clay holds water and nutrients. Loam has some of each. What is good depends on the crop and the weather.
- **The more you water a plant, the better she grows.** (Many beginners; *rejected*)  
  For: A plant that wilts perks up when watered, so water seems to be the cure for everything.  
  Against: Roots need air as well as water. In soil that is always soaked, the gaps fill with water and roots can rot. Both too little and too much water harm a plant.
- **Fertiliser is plant food.** (Many people, and some bags and shops; *rejected*)  
  For: Farmers say they feed the field, and plants grow faster after fertiliser.  
  Against: Plants make their own food from air, water and light. Fertiliser gives the nutrients, such as nitrogen, that they need to build their bodies. It is more like the building material and vitamins than the meal.
- **Natural manure is always better for the soil than factory fertiliser.** (Many organic farmers and consumers; *debated*)  
  For: Manure and compost add humus, feed soil life and release nutrients slowly. Factory fertiliser used too much can wash into rivers.  
  Against: A plant takes up the same nitrate ions from either. Manure alone often cannot supply enough nitrogen for high yields. Many soil scientists recommend using both, in the right amounts.
- **A crop of beans always leaves the soil richer in nitrogen.** (Many farmers and textbooks; *accepted*)  
  For: Bean roots carry nitrogen-fixing bacteria, and fields after beans often grow better crops.  
  Against: A harvest of bean seeds carries nitrogen away. If the whole plant is taken off the field, the soil may gain little or even lose nitrogen. The gain is greatest when the roots and leaves are left to rot in the field.
- **A farmer who leaves a field fallow is wasting land.** (Some city people, and some farmers under pressure; *debated*)  
  For: A field with no crop gives no food that season.  
  Against: In dry regions, a fallow season lets rain water build up in the soil. It also lets weeds be ploughed in and pest cycles break. But a bare fallow is open to erosion, so it works best with a cover of plants or stubble. Where land is scarce, rotation with a pulse is often used in its place.

## Roots
We have watched water climb through soil, saved water with a cover of leaves, and met the bacteria in a bean's roots. Now we ask what a bean, a field and a bag of urea tell us about how life is fed.

**Deep truth.** Beans do not only take from a field. Tiny bacteria in swellings on their roots pull nitrogen out of the air, and the bean pays them in sugar. So a bean's food is made by two kinds of life working together. That is why a field can end a season richer than it began.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a plant as one being on her own, and of soil as a bank that she empties and a farmer refills. A nodule is part bean and part bacteria, and the giving goes both ways.  
*Evidence:* A working nodule is pink inside. Farmers in the Kaveri delta sow black gram after rice, and the next rice often grows greener. Scientists who feed beans nitrogen gas marked with a rare kind of atom find the marked atoms in the bean's protein.

- *who am i:* The protein in your dal was nitrogen gas in the air, until bacteria in a bean's roots turned it into something a plant can use. Your body then built it into you.
- *what is life:* A bean and her bacteria are two different kinds of life, and neither does well alone. A great deal of life is made of partnerships like this one.
- *what is this world:* Nitrogen passes from air to bacteria to bean to person to soil and back to the air. A field is a place where a stream flows through, not a store that empties.

The nitrogen in the air, the sugar in a bean leaf, the bacteria in a root and the dal on your plate look like four separate things. They are one stream. Sunlight makes the sugar, the sugar pays the bacteria, the bacteria turn air into protein, and a family eats the protein. Then bodies and roots and leaves return the nitrogen to the soil, and the stream begins again. No step in the chain can do it alone.

- **Bhagavad Gita 3.14:** From food, living beings come into being. From rain, food is born. (The verse follows the chain backwards from the body to the rain. This unit adds two more links in the chain: the soil that holds the rain, and the bacteria that put nitrogen into the food.)
- **Tao Te Ching 78:** Nothing in the world is softer or weaker than water, yet nothing is better at wearing down the hard and the strong. (Water is soft, yet it climbs through a cup of fine grains against gravity, and it dissolves the salt and the nutrients that roots need.)
- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** Every jewel in a great net reflects every other jewel, so each one holds all the others. (A nodule is a place where a plant, a bacterium, the air and the Sun all meet, and each is needed for the pink inside.)
- Science (settled): Bacteria in the root nodules of legumes change nitrogen gas into ammonia, and the plant pays them with sugar.
- Science (debated): Whether a bean plant chooses her bacteria, or the match simply follows from how the nodule is built, is argued about by biologists.
- Other view, Some plant biologists: A nodule is an organ built by the plant using her own genes, and the bacteria are workers inside the nodule. Calling the two a partnership of equals is a picture. The bean grows nodules when she needs nitrogen, and stops when she does not.
- Practice, Watch the cloth drink (3 min): Fold a strip of cloth or kitchen tissue and hang one end in a glass of water. Sit and watch the water climb. Breathe in slowly three times, and let the breath out gently. With each breath, notice the wet line moving a little higher. Nobody is pushing it. When you are ready, think of the same thing happening under every field.
- Ask: Is a bean plant one living being, or a bean plus her bacteria? Where would you draw the line, and why?
- Ask: Murugan Mama said soil is a bank. What does the bean show about that picture?
- Ask: Farmers knew that pulses help the next crop long before anyone saw a nodule. What else do you know without knowing why?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Water climbed up your cup of soil with nobody pushing it. What is doing the pulling? (hint: Think about how water sticks to the grains, and what water is made of.)
- *deep:* A bean plant gives sugar to bacteria in her roots, and they give nitrogen back. Where did the energy in the sugar come from, and where does it go? (hint: Follow the sugar back to the leaf, and the leaf back to the Sun.)
- *deep:* The air around you is about four fifths nitrogen, yet plants die of nitrogen hunger in a field. Why is plenty in the air not the same as plenty for the plant? (hint: Think about what has to be done to a nitrogen molecule before a plant can use it.)
- *big:* A bean and her bacteria cannot grow nodules alone. Is the nodule part of the bean, part of the bacteria, or something new? (hint: Think about where the plant ends and the bacteria begin.)
- *big:* Factories now make nitrogen fertiliser that helps feed about half the people on Earth. What would your town eat if the factories stopped? (hint: Think about how much food one bean field can produce, and how many people live in your city.)
- *big:* Farmers knew that pulses help the next crop for hundreds of years before anyone had seen a nodule. How can people know something without knowing why? (hint: Think about what a farmer sees, remembers and passes on.)
- *big:* A field is left fallow for a season. Who is resting, the soil, the farmer, or the living things in the soil? (hint: Think about what is still happening in the soil when no crop is growing.)
- *deep:* Sand drains fast and holds little. Clay holds a lot and drains slowly. Is there such a thing as a perfect soil, or only a soil that is perfect for one plant? (hint: Think about a coconut palm, a rice plant and a cactus.)

## Story: Pink Inside the Knot
*The story is made up. The farming in it is real. Black gram sown on the damp soil after a rice harvest is common in parts of the Kaveri delta. A working nodule is pink inside. A bean crop gives modest gains, and most farmers still use some fertiliser as well.*

Karthika was ten years old, and she lived in a village near Kumbakonam, in the Kaveri delta. Her mother, Kamala, grew rice on two acres of flat green land. Her grandmother, Paati, lived with them, and so did her small brother Selvam, who was six and asked a question about everything.

The harvest was over. The fields were golden stubble, and the soil was still damp and dark. Karthika walked along the bund with Selvam, kicking small stones into the channel.

"Look," said Selvam. "Our field is yellow, and Murugan Mama's field is green."

He was right. In the last two seasons, the rice on their field had come up thin and pale. The leaves were the colour of old lemons. Murugan Mama ran the fertiliser shop at the bus stand, and his own land was dark green from edge to edge.

That evening, Murugan Mama came to the house. He put a white bag on the step. "Urea," he said. "You need more of it, Kamala akka. Put it on the day you plant. Your soil is hungry."

"I know it is hungry," said Kamala. She rubbed her forehead. "But a bag of urea costs a lot of money."

Paati had been listening from her cot. "When your grandfather farmed this land," she said, "I scattered ulundu seeds on the wet soil, the week after the cutting. Black gram, you call it. We pulled the pods, and we gave the rest of the plants back to the soil. We never bought a bag of anything. Our rice was green."

"That was long ago, Amma," said Kamala. "There is no time for it now."

Murugan Mama laughed kindly. "A pulse is a waste of a season. The soil takes, you know. The soil is a bank. When the bank is empty, you must put money in. Urea is the money."

Karthika was thinking about her science lesson on yellow leaves. Her teacher had said that pale yellow leaves often mean a plant is short of nitrogen. But the air was full of nitrogen. Her teacher had said that too. About four of every five parts of air are nitrogen gas. So why did the rice go hungry with all that nitrogen around her?

She decided to look at the ulundu. Paati kept a tin of seeds under her bed. Karthika scattered a handful along the damp edge of the field, as Paati showed her, and they sprouted in four days. Three weeks later, the small plants were knee-high and covered in flowers.

She asked Paati to pull one up. Selvam shouted before anyone else did. "She has beads on her roots! Little white beads!"

Karthika washed the roots in the channel. Along them sat small round bumps, each about as big as a sesame seed. They were smooth and hard. "What are they?" she asked.

Paati shrugged. "Pearls, my mother used to say. The ulundu always has them."

Karthika took the plant to the kitchen. With her mother standing beside her, she cut one bump in half with the small vegetable knife. Selvam leaned in so close that his nose almost touched it.

"It is pink," he whispered. "Like the inside of a guava."

It was pink all through. Karthika remembered her teacher's words. A working nodule is pink inside. It holds bacteria that take nitrogen from the air and give it to the plant. She drew it in her notebook, and wrote under it: the bean does not only take. The bean gives.

"So," Kamala said slowly, "the ulundu has tiny helpers in her roots. They pull nitrogen from the air. When we plough the roots and leaves back in, the nitrogen stays in the field."

"And the rice gets it next season," said Karthika.

That season, Kamala sowed ulundu on half of her field and left the other half bare. The rest of the plants were ploughed back in after the pods were picked. In the next rice season, she bought a smaller bag of urea for the whole field, and put it on evenly.

Two months later, the rice on the ulundu half stood a little darker than the rice on the other half. It was not a huge difference, but anyone could see the line down the middle of the field. Murugan Mama came to look, with his hands behind his back.

"The beans have been paying rent in nitrogen all this time," he said at last. "And I told you the soil was a bank."

"It is a bank," said Karthika. "But something is also putting money in. The bacteria in the beans are the ones doing it."

Murugan Mama took a handful of ulundu seeds from Paati's tin. He said they were for his own field, and that he would try just a small corner. Then he looked at Selvam, who was staring at him with great interest.

"What?" said Murugan Mama.

"Can I cut one open?" said Selvam.

**For the parent:** For the parent: nitrogen is a gas that makes up about four fifths of air, but plants cannot use it as a gas. Bacteria in the nodules of legumes break the strong bond between the two nitrogen atoms and make ammonia, and the plant pays with sugar. Karthika's pink nodule was a working one. The bean crop did not make the field rich in one season, which is why the gain in the story is modest and the farmer still uses some urea. The key was seeing that a plant can give to the soil as well as take, with the help of another kind of living being.

- Ask: Why was the rice on the field pale yellow, and what did the ulundu plants do about it?
- Ask: What did Karthika see inside the cut nodule, and what did the colour tell her?
- Ask: Murugan Mama called the soil a bank. In what way is that a good picture, and in what way does the bean change it?
- Ask: Why did Kamala plough the roots and leaves back into the field after picking the pods?
- Ask: Paati and her mother knew that ulundu helped the rice, long before anyone had seen a nodule. How did they know?

### e1 · Water Climb: how fast does water rise in gravel, sand and fine soil? (40 min, 2 days)
- **question:** If three cups of dry gravel, sand and fine soil stand in a shallow tray of water, how high does the water climb in each one, and how fast?

In a very hot or dry room the tissue dries fast: cover each saucer loosely with an upturned plate and check each morning. In a cool room (below about 20 degrees) moong is slow, so count one day later. Moong in plain water should show a white root on most seeds by day 2 or 3. If fewer than 7 of 10 seeds in saucer A sprout, the seeds are old: start again with a fresh packet.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After one day, count the seeds that have sprouted in each saucer. Write the numbers in the table. Add one tablespoon of the right water to each saucer.  
  *Parent says:* Count the sprouted seeds in each saucer. Which saucer is ahead?  
  *Child mode:* Count the sprouted seeds. Write the numbers.
  - Ask: Which saucer is ahead, and which is behind? (good: The plain one is ahead.; The saltiest one has no sprouts.; The pinch one is almost as good as the plain one.)
  - Note: Often the plain saucer has most seeds sprouted by day 2 or 3. The pinch saucer may be a little behind. The half-spoon saucer is usually slow and may have few or none, and the one-spoon saucer none at all. The exact numbers depend on the seeds. If all four look the same, write that down, and try fresh seeds. That is a real result too.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 2 and 3, count the sprouted seeds again. Top up the right water each day. On day 4, take the seeds that have not sprouted out of the salty saucers. Rinse them in plain water. Put them on plain wet tissue.  
  *Parent says:* Now a second question. Salt held the seeds back. Did it kill them? Rinse them in plain water and wait two more days.  
  *Child mode:* Rinse the seeds that have not sprouted. Put them on plain wet tissue.
  - Note: Salt can hold seeds back or hurt them, depending on how salty it is. After a rinse, a few seeds from the middle saucers may sprout. Seeds from the saltiest saucer often do not, or do so only slowly. Count only roots that are at least 2 millimetres long. The salty seeds may smell or grow mould by day 4: throw them away if they do, and write that down. Write down whatever happens.
  - Wait: Wait two more days, then count the rinsed seeds.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 6, count the rinsed seeds that have sprouted. Fill in the last column. Then ask for the three things: what I think, what I saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. How much salt did it take to stop the seeds? What did you see? Why does salt do this?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: more salt means fewer sprouted seeds. In the saltiest water, almost no seeds sprouted. Why: salty water holds on to its water very tightly, so the seed finds it hard to take water in. Too much salt can also harm the seed. Real fields work the same way. A field with a white salt crust grows poor crops.
  - Check: A farmer's field has a white crust of salt on the top after many years of watering. Why are the seeds she sows there growing badly? → **The salt makes it hard for seeds and roots to take in water**
### e3 · Cover or Bare: does a cover of dry leaves keep the soil damp? (25 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Two cups of the same damp soil sit in the same sunny window. One is covered with dry leaves and one is bare. Which dries out deeper in three days?
- **predict:** Which cup will have a thicker dry layer at the top after three days? Will the cover of leaves make a difference? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both cups have the same soil, the same depth of soil, and the same amount of water poured in slowly. They sit side by side in the same sunny place. Only the leaf cover changes. We measure the pale dry layer at the same time each day.
- **safety:** Wash hands after touching soil and leaves. Keep the cups where small children or pets will not knock them over.
- **record:** Fill in the table each day. Draw the three cups on day 4 with the dark damp soil and the pale dry layer.
- **results table:** Day | Bare cup: thickness of the pale dry layer (mm) | Covered cup: thickness of the pale dry layer (mm) | Loosened cup: thickness of the pale dry layer (mm) | What I notice
- **schedule:** day 1: Fill the cups, wet them, cover one and loosen the top of another. Put them in the sun. (12 min); day 2: Lift the cover aside and measure the pale dry layer in each cup. (5 min); day 3: Measure again. (5 min); day 4: Measure again, then talk about the results. (5 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three cups, the soil and the leaves. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Two cups of the same soil, in the same Sun. One gets a cover of dry leaves. Which dries out more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Two cups, one covered. Which dries out more? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the leaves will make a difference? (good: The leaves keep the Sun off the soil.; The leaves keep the wind away.; The leaves will soak up the water.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Some children think the leaves will soak up the water and make the soil drier.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill each cup with soil to 6 centimetres. Pour 4 tablespoons of water slowly on each. Wait 10 minutes. Then lay 2 centimetres of dry leaves on one cup. Use the fork to loosen the top 2 centimetres of soil in another.  
  *Parent says:* Same soil, same water, same depth in each cup. One is bare, one is covered, and one has loose soil on top.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the cups. Add water. Cover one. Loosen one.
  - Note: Label the cups Bare, Covered and Loosened. The soil next to the clear wall will look dark when it is wet and pale when it is dry. If you have a kitchen scale, weigh each cup now and again each morning. The lighter the cup, the more water has gone. One child with a parent: one child sets all three cups. Two to four children: each child fills one cup, or each makes a full set of three and you compare sets. One child pours, one measures, one writes.
  - Check: Why do we pour the same amount of water on each cup? → **So that only the cover is different**
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three cups side by side in the same sunny place. Do not move them.  
  *Parent says:* Same Sun, same wind. Now we wait one day.  
  *Child mode:* Put the cups in the sun. Do not move them.
  - Wait: Leave the cups in the Sun until tomorrow.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each day, lift the leaves aside. Look at the soil next to the wall of each cup. Measure the thickness of the pale dry layer at the top with a ruler, in millimetres. Write it in the table. Put the leaves back.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the side of the cup. Dark soil is damp. Pale soil is dry. How thick is the pale layer at the top?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the side. Measure the pale layer. Write it down.
  - Ask: What is the difference between the bare cup and the covered cup? (good: The bare one has a thick dry layer.; The covered one is still dark.; The covered one dried less.)
  - Note: After a day or two in strong sun, the bare cup usually has a pale layer of a centimetre or more. The covered cup is usually still dark almost to the top. The loosened cup may be like the bare cup or may be a bit different, because the loose layer dries first. If the cups are alike, write that down: it is a result too. A damp, cloudy or monsoon week will make all the cups slow to dry: then measure for two more days, or stand the cups in a sunny, breezy place. If you have a scale, the weight lost each day is a second, clearer measurement.
  - Wait: Measure again tomorrow, and again on day 4.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 4, measure for the last time. Then ask your child for the three things: what I think, what I saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did the leaves do? What did you see? Why do you think the covered soil stayed damp?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: a cover of leaves keeps soil damp for longer. The bare cup had a thick dry layer and the covered cup had almost none. Why: the leaves shade the soil and stop the wind, so less water turns into vapour and leaves. Farmers lay straw between rows for the same reason.
  - Check: A gardener covers the soil of a pot with dry leaves in summer. What is she trying to do? → **Slow down evaporation from the soil**

### a3 · Nodule Hunt: find the pink pearls on a bean's roots (if you can get a whole plant) (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the nodules, because the knife is sharp. Groundnut is a common allergen: if anyone in the family is allergic to peanuts, use a moong or pea plant instead. Wash hands after touching the roots and the soil.
- **record:** Draw the roots with the nodules on them. Draw one cut nodule and colour the inside.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the plant over the bowl. Wash the soil off the roots very gently, by dipping them and swirling them in water. Do not pull.  
  *Parent says:* Wash the roots gently. Dip, swirl, and look. Do not pull, because the nodules can break off.  
  *Child mode:* Dip the roots in water. Wash gently. Do not pull.
  - Note: Soak the soil clump in water for a few minutes first, so the soil falls off by itself. Nodules rub off easily, so swirl, do not scrub. With two to four children, share one plant: each child counts the nodules on a different root and everyone adds up.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the roots on a white plate. Find the small round bumps stuck to the roots. Count how many you can find. Find a bump on a thin side root.  
  *Parent says:* Look for round bumps on the roots. They are the nodules. How many can you count? How big is the biggest?  
  *Child mode:* Look for round bumps on the roots. Count them.
  - Ask: What do the bumps look like? (good: Little round beads.; Small and round, like a sesame seed.; Smooth and stuck to the root.)
  - Note: Nodules on groundnut are small, round and a little pale or brown on the outside, from about 1 to 5 millimetres across. If you find none, the plant may have grown in soil without the right bacteria, or the roots may have been trimmed. That is real, and worth writing down. Nodules rub off with a finger; swollen lumps that are part of the root and will not rub off are not nodules (they may be galls made by tiny worms). A plant from a market after harvest is at the end of its life, so its nodules may be few and old.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts one big nodule in half with the knife. Look at the cut surface inside with the magnifying glass. What colour is it?  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up cuts a nodule in half. What colour is the inside? Pink means the nodule is working.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts a nodule open. What colour is the inside?
  - Note: A pink or reddish inside means the nodule is working and fixing nitrogen. A white or green inside means it has stopped. The pink comes from a substance that holds oxygen back from the nitrogen-fixing tool. Dried or old nodules look brown or green inside. On a plant sold after harvest this is common, so do not be surprised if none is pink: write down what you see, and try a younger plant (chana or moong at flowering time) if you can. Pink is clearest in nodules cut from a living, flowering plant.
  - Check: A cut nodule is bright pink inside. What does this tell you? → **The nodule is working and fixing nitrogen**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the root of a grass or a cereal, such as a weed from a pot. Does it have nodules?  
  *Parent says:* Compare with a root from a grass. Does it have the same round bumps?  
  *Child mode:* Look at a grass root. Does it have bumps?
  - Note: Grasses and cereals have no nodules. They take their nitrogen from the soil.

## Challenges
### c1 · Wick the Pot: water a plant for three days without pouring (40 min)
- **problem:** A family will be away for three days. Design a way to keep a potted plant's soil damp for three days with one 500 millilitre bottle of water, and without anyone pouring water on the soil.
- **your turn:** Now design your own way. Draw it first. Build it and fill the bottle. Check the soil with the pencil each day. Which design kept the soil damp for three days, and why?
- **constraints:** Use one bottle that holds 500 millilitres of water. Fill it once at the start.; Nobody may pour water on the soil for three days.; Use only things from home, such as cotton rope, strips of old cloth and a bucket.; Check the soil with a clean pencil pushed 5 centimetres into the soil. Wet, dark soil sticks to the pencil.; Use the same kind of pot, soil and plant for every design.
- **general:** Stand the bottle next to the pot. Run a cotton wick from the water to the soil. The water climbs the wick, just as it climbed the cup of sand.; Stand the pot on a plate of water, with a wick from the plate through the hole in the bottom of the pot.; Water the pot very well before you leave, then lay a thick cover of dry leaves on the soil.
- **creative:** Bury a small unglazed clay pot in the soil and fill it with water. The water slowly seeps through the clay into the soil. This is called pitcher irrigation, and farmers use it in dry places.; Use two wicks from two sides, to spread the water.; Hang the bottle upside down with a wick running out of the cap.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Look at the materials. Ask your child to draw two or three different designs.  
  *Parent says:* A plant must drink for three days, with no one pouring. What have we learned about how water moves? How can we use it?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two or three ways to water the pot without pouring.
  - Ask: What helped the water to climb in the sand cup? (good: The tiny gaps between the grains.; The gaps in the cloth.; Water sticking to the grains.)
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Build your first design. Fill the bottle. Water the soil well at the start. Push a pencil 5 centimetres into the soil and write down how it looks.  
  *Parent says:* Build your idea. Fill the bottle. Test the soil with the pencil. Write down what you see.  
  *Child mode:* Build your design. Test the soil with the pencil.
  - Note: Soak the wick before you put it in. A dry wick is slow to start. Stand the bottle higher than the soil, so the water has a short way to climb. Do not put the wick in with a tight loop, because a kink can block the water.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 2 and 3, push the pencil into the soil again and write down how it looks. Look at the level of water in the bottle.  
  *Parent says:* Check the soil each day. Is it still damp? How much water is left in the bottle?  
  *Child mode:* Check the soil each day. Is it still damp?
  - Note: A long wick is slow. A short, thick wick moves more water. Check the pencil, and the water level in the bottle.
  - Wait: Check again on day 2 and day 3.
- **s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose the design that kept the soil damp best. Ask why. Change one thing and test again, if you have time.  
  *Parent says:* Which design worked best? Why? What would you change to make it better?  
  *Child mode:* Which design worked best? Why? What would you change?
  - Check: Your wick pulled water from the bottle into the soil. What lifted the water? → **Water sticking to the threads of the cloth and pulling the water behind it**
  - Think: How could a wick tell when the plant has had enough?
### c2 · The Four-Year Plan: plan a field that never runs out of nitrogen (30 min)
- **problem:** A family has one small field and grows two crops a year, so eight seasons in four years. Plan the eight crops so that the nitrogen bank never runs empty, the family has rice at least four times, and there is a pulse in at least three seasons.
- **your turn:** Now plan your own four years. Write each season's crop and the nitrogen bank. Check it with your tokens. Swap your plan with someone else and see if it works for them. Which plan earned the most points?
- **constraints:** Use the crop cards from the game Soil Bank, or write your own, with the same costs and gains.; Start with a bank of 4 nitrogen tokens.; At the end of every season, add 1 token for rain, leaves and the bacteria in the soil.; The bank must never fall below 0, and you may not grow the same crop in two seasons in a row.; Write the bank at the end of each season in your plan.
- **general:** Rice, then black gram, then rice, then groundnut, and so on, like the rice and pulse pattern in the Kaveri delta.; Two cereals, then a pulse, then a rest, so the bank has time to fill.; Ragi in some seasons, because it asks little of the soil.
- **creative:** Grow ragi with a row of red gram between, and count the red gram as a pulse in the same season.; Grow azolla in the rice field to feed the rice with nitrogen, and count it as a gift of 1 token.; Leave one season fallow, and plant a cover crop of any plant so the soil is not bare.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem. Write down the numbers: the bank starts at 4, and every season ends with 1 token added.  
  *Parent says:* Our bank starts at 4. Every season adds 1 token at the end. Rice takes 3. How low will the bank be after two rice seasons?  
  *Child mode:* The bank starts at 4. Rice takes 3. Work out two rice seasons.
  - Ask: What happens to the bank if you grow rice two seasons in a row? (good: It falls to about 1 or 0.; The next rice fails.; You must put a pulse in.)
  - Note: Rice, then rice: 4 minus 3 is 1, plus 1 is 2. Then 2 is not enough for 3, so the crop fails. The rule says never to grow the same crop twice in a row, but the sums show why.
- **s2 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write your first plan of eight crops. Work out the bank after every season. Cross out any plan where the bank would go below 0.  
  *Parent says:* Plan the eight seasons. Work out the bank after each. If it goes below 0, change the plan.  
  *Child mode:* Plan eight seasons. Work out the bank each time.
  - Note: A good plan follows a heavy crop with a pulse. Pulses give 2 tokens, plus 1 at the end of the season, so they raise the bank by 3.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap plans with a second player. Check each other's sums. Find the plan with the most points. Talk about what each plan costs and gains.  
  *Parent says:* Check someone else's plan. Does the bank stay above 0? Which plan earns more?  
  *Child mode:* Swap plans. Check the sums. Which earns more?
  - Check: A plan grows rice, then moong, then rice, then moong. The bank starts at 4. What is the bank after four seasons? → **6**
  - Think: Why would a real farmer's plan be harder than this game?

## Worksheets
### Water, Gaps and Beans (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these soils holds the most water and drains the slowest?  
   Options: Sand / Clay / Gravel  
   **Answer:** Clay. *Why:* Clay has the finest grains, so it has the most grain surface for water to cling to and the tiniest gaps. Water drains from it very slowly. Sand and gravel have big gaps and let water run through.
2. (fill) Water climbing up through the narrow gaps in soil is called capillary ____.  
   **Answer:** ['rise']. *Why:* Capillary rise is the climb of water through very narrow gaps against gravity, as in a wick or a cloth.
3. (mcq) Water climbed about 1 centimetre in a cup of gravel and nearly to the top of a cup of sand. Why did gravel lift the water so little?  
   Options: Gravel is heavier than sand / The gaps in gravel are too wide to pull the water up far / Gravel is dry inside  
   **Answer:** The gaps in gravel are too wide to pull the water up far. *Why:* Water is lifted by its stickiness to the grains at the edge of a gap. In very wide gaps most of the water is far from any grain, so the pull is weak. Weight is not what matters.
4. (truefalse) A cover of dry leaves on top of a pot makes the soil under it lose water faster.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The leaves shade the soil and block the wind, so less water evaporates. That is why farmers spread straw as mulch.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Loam → a soil with some sand, silt, clay and humus; Mulch → a cover of straw or leaves laid on soil; Legume → a plant from the bean family; Fallow → a field left to rest for a season; Nodule → a swelling on a root where helper bacteria live. *Why:* These five words are used all through the unit.
6. (sort) Sort these crops by what they do to the nitrogen in a field.  
   **Answer:** rice → Takes a lot of nitrogen; groundnut → Brings nitrogen in; wheat → Takes a lot of nitrogen; moong → Brings nitrogen in; maize → Takes a lot of nitrogen; chana → Brings nitrogen in. *Why:* Rice, wheat and maize are cereals that need a lot of nitrogen. Groundnut, moong and chana are legumes with nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their roots.
7. (write) A pot of pure sand needs watering much more often than a pot of garden soil. Explain why, using what you know about grains and gaps.  
   **Answer:** Sand has big grains with big gaps, and the water runs straight through. Little clings to the grains. Garden soil has finer grains and humus, which hold water in thin films and in the gaps.. *Why:* A strong answer links the size of the grains and gaps to how much water is held and how fast it drains.
8. (mcq) A rice field has pale yellow leaves. Which nutrient is it most likely short of?  
   Options: Nitrogen / Salt / Sand  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen. *Why:* Nitrogen builds leaf proteins and chlorophyll, so a shortage gives pale yellow leaves. Salt is not a nutrient plants need in large amounts, and sand is not a nutrient.
9. (label) Label this drawing of a bean root: main root, nodule, soil, arrow of nitrogen from the air, arrow of sugar from the leaf.  
   **Answer:** The main root runs down through the soil. Round nodules sit on the side roots. An arrow of nitrogen runs from the air through the soil to the nodule. An arrow of sugar runs from the leaf down to the nodule.. *Why:* The nodule is where the two arrows meet. The bacteria receive sugar and give nitrogen.
10. (fill) Urea has 46 per cent nitrogen, so 100 kilograms of urea holds ____ kilograms of nitrogen.  
   **Answer:** ['46']. *Why:* 46 per cent means 46 parts in every 100. In 100 kilograms of urea, 46 kilograms is nitrogen.
11. (draw) Draw your Salty Seeds test on day 3. Show the four saucers and how many seeds sprouted in each.  
   **Answer:** Four saucers in a row. The plain saucer has the most sprouted seeds, the pinch of salt saucer has a few less, the half spoon saucer has very few and the one spoon saucer has none.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the number of sprouts falling as the salt rises.
12. (mcq) A white crust of salt forms on the top of a dry field that has been watered for many years. Where did the salt come from?  
   Options: It was in the irrigation water, and stayed behind when the water evaporated / It rose from the sky in the rain / It was made by the roots of the crop  
   **Answer:** It was in the irrigation water, and stayed behind when the water evaporated. *Why:* All irrigation water carries a little dissolved salt. When the water evaporates or is used by plants, the salt stays behind and builds up.
13. (truefalse) Moong, urad, chana and groundnut are all legumes.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. They all belong to the bean family and have seeds in pods. Their roots carry nodules with nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
14. (write) A gardener lays dry leaves on the soil of a pot and the soil stays damp for twice as long. Say why.  
   **Answer:** The leaves shade the soil and stop the wind from reaching it. So less water turns into vapour and leaves the soil. The water stays in the pot for longer.. *Why:* A strong answer says that mulch cuts the evaporation from the top.
15. (order) Put these steps in order, from the air to the bean.  
   **Answer:** ['Nitrogen gas is in the air', 'Bacteria in a nodule change the gas into ammonia', 'The bean plant turns the ammonia into protein', 'The bean seed is full of protein', 'A family eats the bean as dal']. *Why:* Nitrogen gas is turned into ammonia by the bacteria, the plant builds it into protein, and the protein ends in the seed. We eat the seed.
16. (mcq) What does a bean plant give the bacteria in her nodules?  
   Options: Sugar / Nitrogen gas / Salt  
   **Answer:** Sugar. *Why:* The bean makes sugar in her leaves with the help of sunlight, and gives some to the bacteria. The bacteria give nitrogen in return.
### Climbing Water and Helping Roots (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Two cups of soil are poured with 100 millilitres of water. The first holds back 80 millilitres. The second holds back 30. Which soil has more clay?  
   Options: The first soil / The second soil / They have the same  
   **Answer:** The first soil. *Why:* Clay holds a lot of water in its tiny gaps and drains slowly. The soil that holds back more water is likely to have more clay.
2. (write) Fine soil took longer than sand to wet, but in time it could lift water higher. Explain both parts.  
   **Answer:** Water is pulled up by its stickiness to the grains. In fine soil the gaps are narrower, so the pull is stronger and the water can rise higher in the end. But narrow gaps are harder for water to flow through, so the climb is slower.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason for the higher climb and a reason for the slower climb.
3. (mcq) Sand is wet to the top of a cup in 20 minutes, and fine soil is wet only 2 centimetres up. What will fine soil do if you wait a few more days?  
   Options: It will stay at 2 centimetres for ever / It will keep climbing, slowly, and may go much higher / It will drain away  
   **Answer:** It will keep climbing, slowly, and may go much higher. *Why:* Fine soil lifts water higher in the end than sand does, but takes much longer. So it keeps climbing slowly.
4. (perspective) A gardener and a farmer disagree about the dry season.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one and gives a reason from the unit, such as shading the soil or breaking the narrow paths, and admits a fair point from another view.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Scientists find that mulch works well in most tests and that hoeing helps in some soils, so a fair answer weighs both.
5. (truefalse) Fertiliser is food for plants.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Plants make their own food from air, water and light. Fertiliser gives the nutrients they need to build their bodies, like the bricks, and not the meal.
6. (fill) About four fifths of the air is ____ gas.  
   **Answer:** ['nitrogen']. *Why:* Air is about 78 parts nitrogen in every 100. Plants cannot use it as a gas.
7. (mcq) A nodule has a red-pink substance inside. What does it do?  
   Options: It holds back oxygen, which would damage the nitrogen-fixing tool / It makes the bean sweet / It keeps the nodule warm  
   **Answer:** It holds back oxygen, which would damage the nitrogen-fixing tool. *Why:* The tool the bacteria use to break the nitrogen bond is harmed by oxygen. The pink substance holds oxygen back so the tool keeps working.
8. (sort) Sort these actions by what they do to the water in the soil of a field.  
   **Answer:** spreading straw between the rows → Saves water; leaving bare soil in the hot Sun → Wastes water; watering the roots with a slow drip → Saves water; flooding the whole field when only the roots need it → Wastes water; covering the soil with a plastic sheet → Saves water. *Why:* Covers slow evaporation and shade the soil. Drip watering wets only the roots. Bare soil in the Sun and flooding let more water go to the air or drain away.
9. (write) A shopkeeper says that soil is like a bank. Crops empty it, and only fertiliser can fill it. Use what you know about beans to say what is missing from that picture.  
   **Answer:** A bean crop can put nitrogen into the soil, with bacteria in her roots. Rotting roots and leaves, manure and rain also add nutrients. So soil is not only emptied and filled by people. Living things fill it too.. *Why:* A strong answer names the bacteria and the roots, and says that living things add nitrogen.
10. (mcq) A farmer grows wheat in the same field year after year. What problem can build up?  
   Options: Pests and diseases that attack wheat can build up, and the soil's nitrogen can run low / The wheat grows into rice / The field turns into a pond  
   **Answer:** Pests and diseases that attack wheat can build up, and the soil's nitrogen can run low. *Why:* A pest that feeds on wheat finds food every year if wheat is always there. Wheat also takes a lot of nitrogen, with nothing putting it back. A rotation breaks both problems.
11. (draw) Draw a field with a white salt crust on top. Show where the water came from, where it went and where the salt stayed.  
   **Answer:** An irrigation channel on one side bringing water. Water vapour rising from the field into the air. A white crust of salt on the soil. A drain at the far end carrying away some salt.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the water leaves as vapour and the salt stays behind.
12. (match) Match each place or plant to what it shows about soil.  
   **Answer:** Pokkali rice in Kerala → a crop that copes with salty water; Tea in the Nilgiris → a crop that likes acid soil; Azolla in a paddy → a fern that carries nitrogen-fixing bacteria; Ragi with red gram → an example of growing crops together. *Why:* Each of these shows how a plant or a farmer works with the soil.
13. (mcq) Which field is most likely to get a white salt crust?  
   Options: A dry field watered from a canal for many years / A forest floor that gets heavy rain every year / A field that has just been ploughed  
   **Answer:** A dry field watered from a canal for many years. *Why:* Where rain is heavy it washes salt away. In a dry place, irrigation water evaporates and leaves its salt behind, year after year.
14. (order) Put these in order to show how salt builds up in a dry irrigated field.  
   **Answer:** ['Canal water carrying a little salt is spread on the field', 'The Sun and the crop use up the water', 'The salt stays in the soil', 'Year after year, the salt builds up', 'A white crust forms on the top of the soil']. *Why:* Only the water leaves. The salt stays and adds up over the years.
15. (write) A soil card says the nitrogen is low and the potassium is high. What would you tell the farmer?  
   **Answer:** Grow a pulse in the field, add compost or manure, and perhaps some urea. The potassium is high, so she does not need to add more.. *Why:* A strong answer matches the advice to what is low and leaves out what is high.
16. (truefalse) If the whole bean plant is taken from the field at harvest, the soil may gain little nitrogen.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A lot of the nitrogen goes into the seeds and the leaves. If these are all taken away, little returns to the soil. The gain is greatest when the roots and leaves are left to rot in the field.
17. (fill) On the pH scale, a pH of 7 means the soil is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['neutral']. *Why:* Seven is neutral. Lower numbers are acidic and higher numbers are alkaline.
### Nitrogen, Salt and the Future of Soil (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Water climbs 5 centimetres in one soil in 10 minutes, then stops. In a second soil it climbs 2 centimetres in 10 minutes, and keeps rising for hours. Which soil has the finer gaps?  
   Options: The first / The second / You cannot tell  
   **Answer:** The second. *Why:* Finer gaps pull harder, so water can keep rising, but they flow slowly. The soil that climbs slowly and keeps going has the finer gaps.
2. (write) A cotton wick from a bottle to a pot waters a plant with nothing pushing it. Explain why the water climbs, and why a thick wick moves more water than a thin one.  
   **Answer:** Water sticks to the fine threads and pulls the water behind it. A thick wick has more threads and more gaps, so more water moves at once.. *Why:* A strong answer names the stickiness between water and the threads, and says that more threads carry more water.
3. (fill) A 50 kilogram bag of urea has 46 per cent nitrogen. It holds ____ kilograms of nitrogen.  
   **Answer:** ['23']. *Why:* 46 per cent of 50 is half of 46, which is 23.
4. (perspective) A town is deciding how to feed its kitchen gardens.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view with a reason, such as soil life and humus, or the need for nitrogen, and says what a different view gets right.. *Why:* Organic and chemical farming is a real argument. Plants take up the same nitrate ions from both, but compost also builds the soil, so many scientists suggest using both.
5. (truefalse) Growing a pulse always leaves more nitrogen in the soil than there was before.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The gain depends on what happens to the plants. If the beans and the plants are taken away, the soil may gain little or lose nitrogen.
6. (label) Label the cut nodule: root, nodule wall, pink centre with bacteria, nitrogen arriving, sugar arriving.  
   **Answer:** The nodule sits on the root. Its pink centre is full of bacteria. Nitrogen gas arrives from the soil air and sugar arrives from the plant.. *Why:* The pink substance holds oxygen back so the nitrogen-fixing tool is safe.
7. (write) Pokkali rice can grow in salty water. Give one idea for how a plant might cope with salt, and say whether you are sure.  
   **Answer:** A plant might keep the salt out of her roots, or store it in old leaves, or make her cells hold water tightly. I am not sure which Pokkali does. Scientists are still studying it.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reasoned idea and says honestly what is not known.
8. (draw) Draw a hill farm that saves both soil and water. Show at least three things the farmer does.  
   **Answer:** Terraces or bunds across the slope, mulch between the rows, plants whose roots hold the soil, and a pond or tank at the bottom to catch the runoff.. *Why:* A strong drawing includes things that slow the water, cover the soil and keep the soil.
9. (sort) Sort these statements by how sure scientists are.  
   **Answer:** Mulch reduces evaporation from soil → Settled; Legumes carry bacteria that fix nitrogen → Settled; A bean plant punishes nodules that give her little nitrogen → Argued about; Loosening soil with a hoe always saves water → Argued about; Irrigation water leaves salt behind → Settled. *Why:* The first, second and last have been measured again and again. The other two depend on the soil, the crop and how the evidence is read.
10. (mcq) The air around a field is full of nitrogen. Why do plants still need fertiliser or bacteria to get nitrogen?  
   Options: The two atoms in each nitrogen molecule are held together very strongly, and plants cannot break the bond / Nitrogen is a liquid / Nitrogen is too heavy for roots  
   **Answer:** The two atoms in each nitrogen molecule are held together very strongly, and plants cannot break the bond. *Why:* Only bacteria with a special tool, or a factory using great heat and pressure, can break that bond.
11. (match) Match each person to what they found.  
   **Answer:** Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth → peas starved in sand without help from soil bacteria; Martinus Beijerinck → grew the bacteria from the root nodules; Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch → made ammonia from nitrogen gas in a factory. *Why:* These three steps moved the story from farmers' habit, to the bacteria, to the factory.
12. (write) Your dal is made of protein that holds nitrogen atoms. Write the journey of one nitrogen atom from the air to your body.  
   **Answer:** The atom was in nitrogen gas in the air. Bacteria in a nodule changed it into ammonia. The bean plant built it into protein in her seed. A family cooked the seed as dal, and the atom became part of a body.. *Why:* Any clear journey is good. A strong answer names the bacteria, the plant and the dal in the right order.
13. (mcq) A farmer wants to lower the amount of fertiliser she buys. Which change helps most?  
   Options: Grow a pulse in rotation and leave her roots and leaves in the field / Plant more rice / Leave the soil bare in the Sun  
   **Answer:** Grow a pulse in rotation and leave her roots and leaves in the field. *Why:* A pulse brings nitrogen into the soil through the bacteria in her roots. Leaving the roots and leaves in the field returns the nitrogen to the soil.
14. (fill) The factory process that makes ammonia from nitrogen gas and hydrogen is named after two German chemists: the ____ process.  
   **Answer:** ['Haber-Bosch', 'Haber Bosch']. *Why:* Fritz Haber found the method in 1909 and Carl Bosch made it work in a factory a few years later.
15. (write) Some people say that soil is a living thing as a whole. Others say that only the beings in it are alive. What would you want to find out before deciding?  
   **Answer:** I would find out whether the soil can grow, take in food, respond and change as a whole, or whether those things are only done by the creatures in it. I would also want to know how much the creatures depend on each other.. *Why:* There is no single answer. A strong answer says what evidence would help to decide.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which soil lets water run through fastest?  
   Options: Sand / Clay / Loam  
   **Answer:** Sand. *Why:* Sand has the biggest grains and the biggest gaps, so water runs straight through. Clay holds water in tiny gaps, and loam is in between.
2. (mcq) Why do many gardeners like loam?  
   Options: It drains well and still holds water that roots can use / It is the lightest soil / It has no humus  
   **Answer:** It drains well and still holds water that roots can use. *Why:* Loam is a mix of sand, silt, clay and humus. The sand lets extra water out, and the finer grains and humus hold water for roots.
3. (mcq) A cup of gravel stood in water. The wet line climbed only about 1 centimetre. What is the best reason?  
   Options: The gaps are too wide to lift the water far / Gravel is dry / Water cannot climb  
   **Answer:** The gaps are too wide to lift the water far. *Why:* Water is lifted by its stickiness to the edges of narrow gaps. Wide gaps give a weak pull.
4. (mcq) Water moves up through a wick with nobody pushing it. What does the lifting?  
   Options: Water sticking to the threads and to other water / A hidden pump / The Sun pushing down  
   **Answer:** Water sticking to the threads and to other water. *Why:* Water sticks to the surface of the threads, and pulls the water behind it. This is capillary rise.
5. (mcq) A bare cup of soil dries more than a cup covered in dry leaves. Why?  
   Options: The leaves shade the soil and block the wind, so less water evaporates / The leaves drink the water / The leaves are hot  
   **Answer:** The leaves shade the soil and block the wind, so less water evaporates. *Why:* Evaporation from the top of the soil is slowed by a cover of mulch.
6. (mcq) A plant with pale yellow leaves is most likely short of which nutrient?  
   Options: Nitrogen / Sugar / Sand  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen. *Why:* Nitrogen is needed for leaf proteins and chlorophyll. A plant without enough of it has pale leaves.
7. (mcq) The letters N, P and K on a fertiliser bag stand for which nutrients?  
   Options: Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium / Nuts, plants and kernels / Nitrate, pepper and kale  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. *Why:* The three numbers on the bag give the share of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
8. (mcq) Where does the nitrogen in a bean plant's protein first come from?  
   Options: The air / The Sun / The rock  
   **Answer:** The air. *Why:* Bacteria in the nodules take nitrogen gas from the air and change it into ammonia.
9. (mcq) What does a bean plant pay the bacteria in her root nodules?  
   Options: Sugar / Water only / Salt  
   **Answer:** Sugar. *Why:* The plant makes sugar in her leaves and gives some to the bacteria. The bacteria give nitrogen back.
10. (mcq) A nodule cut in half is bright pink inside. What does this tell you?  
   Options: The nodule is working / The nodule is rotten / The nodule is full of water  
   **Answer:** The nodule is working. *Why:* The pink substance holds oxygen back so the nitrogen-fixing tool can work. A white or green nodule has stopped.
11. (mcq) Why do farmers often follow a cereal with a pulse?  
   Options: The pulse puts nitrogen back and the next crop does better / The pulse is bigger / The cereal needs company  
   **Answer:** The pulse puts nitrogen back and the next crop does better. *Why:* A pulse works with bacteria that fix nitrogen, and the roots and leaves left behind add nitrogen. It also breaks the pest cycle of the cereal.
12. (mcq) In very salty water, moong seeds did not sprout. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Salty water holds on to its water so tightly that the seed cannot take it in / Salt is food for seeds / Salt makes the seed too heavy  
   **Answer:** Salty water holds on to its water so tightly that the seed cannot take it in. *Why:* Salty water holds its water tightly, so a seed or root finds it hard to drink. Too much salt also harms the seed.
13. (mcq) A dry field watered from a canal for years gets a white crust. What is it?  
   Options: Salt left behind when the water evaporated / Frost / Chalk from the crops  
   **Answer:** Salt left behind when the water evaporated. *Why:* Water carries a little dissolved salt. The water goes into the air and the salt stays.
14. (mcq) A soil card says nitrogen is low and humus is low. What is a good plan?  
   Options: Grow a pulse and add compost / Add more salt / Plough the field and leave it bare  
   **Answer:** Grow a pulse and add compost. *Why:* A pulse brings in nitrogen and compost adds humus, so the plan fixes both problems.

## Applications
- Farmers rotate pulses with cereals to cut the fertiliser they must buy.
- Gardeners cover pots and beds with mulch to save water in the dry months.
- Drip irrigation puts water on the roots only, so less of it evaporates.
- The Soil Health Card scheme tests a farmer's soil for nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, acidity and salt.
- Fertiliser factories make ammonia from nitrogen in the air, using the Haber-Bosch process.
- Pitcher irrigation buries unglazed clay pots that seep water to plant roots in dry places.
- Farmers wash salt out of salty fields with extra water and good drainage, and grow salt-tolerant crops such as Pokkali rice.
- Engineers use wicks and fabric to move water in self-watering planters.
- Builders put a damp-proof course in house walls to stop water climbing up from the ground.
- Green manure crops are grown to be ploughed in and feed the next crop.
- Azolla grown in paddy fields adds nitrogen for the rice.

## Open questions
- **Can rice and wheat be made to grow their own nitrogen-fixing partners?** Researchers are studying maize with sticky root jelly in Mexico and many ways to help cereals host bacteria. Nobody has made it work in a large field yet.
- **Does a bean plant choose her bacteria, or does the nodule simply follow how it is built?** Experiments on soybean show nodules that fix less nitrogen do worse, but scientists argue about whether the plant is choosing.
- **How much of the water in soil can a root use, and how do roots choose where to take it?** Roots are hidden in the soil and hard to watch. Scientists are still working out how they sense water in the soil and choose where to grow.
- **How many kinds of living things live in a handful of soil, and what do they all do?** Most soil bacteria and fungi have never been grown in a laboratory. DNA studies show that there are far more kinds than names.
- **How can we feed everyone while using less nitrogen fertiliser, which can wash into rivers?** Better crop rotations, new crops and smarter use of fertiliser all help. How far they can go without lowering harvests is still being tested.
- **Why do soil clays hold water so tightly, and could we change that for dry places?** Scientists understand the forces at the surface of clay flakes, but changing soils on a large scale is difficult and slow.

## Connected maths
- In the Water Climb test, the sand cup was wet to 9 centimetres in 30 minutes. If the water kept climbing at the same speed, how long would it take to reach 12 centimetres? Would it really? **40 minutes at the same speed, but in real life the climb slows down.**. 9 centimetres in 30 minutes is 3 centimetres in 10 minutes. 12 centimetres is 4 lots of 3, so it takes 4 lots of 10 minutes, which is 40 minutes. But the higher the water climbs, the more its own weight pulls it back and the more the gaps slow it, so it takes longer than that. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A bag of urea weighs 50 kilograms. Of every 100 parts, 46 are nitrogen. How many kilograms of nitrogen are in the bag? **23 kilograms.**. 46 per cent of 100 kilograms is 46 kilograms. 50 kilograms is half of 100, so the bag holds half of 46, which is 23 kilograms. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In the game Soil Bank, a field starts with 4 tokens. It grows rice (takes 3), then moong (gives 2), then rice again. At the end of each season it gains 1 token. How many tokens are in the bank after three seasons? **3 tokens.**. Start with 4. Rice takes 3, leaving 1. Add 1 for rain, making 2. Moong gives 2, making 4. Add 1, making 5. Rice takes 3, leaving 2. Add 1, making 3. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
- Plot a line graph for the fine soil in the Water Climb test: 1 centimetre at 15 minutes, 2 at 30, 4 at 60. Then plot the sand: 6 at 15, 9 at 30, then the top (10) at 60. Which line is steeper at the start? What does this say about speed? **The sand line. Sand climbs faster at the start.**. The sand rises 6 centimetres in the first 15 minutes, and the fine soil only 1. A steeper line means faster. Both lines flatten later because the water slows as it climbs. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- Saucer D in the Salty Seeds test had 1 teaspoon of salt, about 6 grams, in 100 millilitres of water. How many grams would that be in 1 litre? Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in a litre. How many times saltier than the sea was saucer D? **60 grams in a litre. About 1.7 times saltier than the sea.**. 1 litre is 10 lots of 100 millilitres, so 10 times 6 is 60 grams. 60 divided by 35 is about 1.7. So saucer D was nearly twice as salty as the sea. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L4`)
- In the plain saucer, 9 of 10 seeds sprouted. In the saucer with half a teaspoon of salt, 2 of 10 sprouted. How many fewer seeds sprouted in the salty saucer? Out of every 10, what fraction less? **7 fewer seeds. 7 out of 9, or about three quarters, fewer.**. 9 take away 2 is 7. 7 out of the 9 that sprouted in plain water is a bit more than three quarters. So salt cut sprouting by about three quarters. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In the Cover or Bare test, the pale dry layer in the bare cup grew 6 millimetres each day. How thick was it after 5 days if it kept growing at that speed? Why might it not? **30 millimetres, but in real life it may grow more slowly.**. 6 times 5 is 30. But once the top layer is dry, water has further to climb to reach the surface, so the drying slows down. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A farm follows this pattern: rice, black gram, ragi, rice, black gram, ragi, and so on. What will be grown in the 11th season? Explain how you worked it out. **Black gram.**. The pattern repeats every 3 seasons. 11 divided by 3 is 3 with 2 left over. The second crop in the pattern is black gram. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A classroom holds 200 cubic metres of air. About 78 of every 100 parts of air are nitrogen. About how many cubic metres of nitrogen are in the room? **About 156 cubic metres.**. 78 out of 100 is 78 per cent. Half of 200 is 100, and 78 per cent of 200 is 2 times 78, which is 156. So most of the air in the room is nitrogen, and plants cannot use it. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A groundnut plant has 36 nodules, and each is about 3 millimetres across. If you laid them in a row, how long would the row be in centimetres? **10.8 centimetres.**. 36 times 3 is 108 millimetres. 10 millimetres make 1 centimetre, so 108 millimetres is 10.8 centimetres. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)
- Fertiliser A costs 600 rupees for a 45 kilogram bag of urea with 46 per cent nitrogen. Fertiliser B costs 1,200 rupees for a 50 kilogram bag of a mix with 19 per cent nitrogen. Which gives more nitrogen for the money? **A gives more. About 20.7 kilograms for 600 rupees against 9.5 kilograms for 1,200 rupees.**. 46 per cent of 45 is about 20.7 kilograms. 19 per cent of 50 is 9.5 kilograms. A gives about twice as much nitrogen for half the money. B also has other nutrients, so it is not a simple comparison. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L4`)
