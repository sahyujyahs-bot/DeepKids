# The life cycle of a flowering plant: how fruit ripens, how plants time their lives, and how they end
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.plant-life-cycle.L4`

**Summary.** A hard green mango sits in a paper bag with a very ripe banana. The two never touch, yet the mango turns soft and yellow days before a mango left alone. Something travels through the air between them. Your child tests the ripening gas, watches leaves give up their green, and roots cuttings from nodes. Then the unit looks at the whole shape of a plant's life: plants that flower for one season or many, plants that save food for a second year, ferns that grow from dust, and trees that keep a diary in their rings.

**Intro.** *Take two hard green mangoes. Put one in a paper bag with a very ripe banana. Put the other in a paper bag alone. Wait four days and press both. The mango that sat with the banana is usually softer. The banana never touched it. So how could one fruit change the other?* Ripening looks like something each fruit does alone, slowly, as time goes by. But a ripe fruit also sends out a gas that changes the fruit next to them. In this unit you test that gas. You also find out why a leaf turns yellow, where a new money plant can grow roots, and how long different plants live. Some plants flower for a single season and some flower for hundreds of years. At the end you will see a plant's life as a whole, from the first leaf to the last.

## Learning outcomes

1. I can say what changes inside a fruit as it ripens, and why a fruit gets soft, sweet, coloured and scented.
2. I can explain what a leaf is doing when it turns yellow, and what ethylene and darkness do to leaves.
3. I can sort plants into annual, biennial and perennial, and into plants that flower in one season and plants that flower in many.
4. I can explain how plants tell the season, using day length and cold, and why coriander and palak go to seed in summer.

7. I can say what a tree ring records, and describe the different ways scientists think about how long a plant can live.

## Terms
- **Ripening**: The set of changes that turns a hard, sour, green fruit into a soft, sweet, coloured and scented one. *e.g.* A raw mango is hard and sour. A ripe mango is soft, sweet and yellow.
- **Ethylene**: A simple gas made by plants, with two carbon atoms and four hydrogen atoms. Ripe fruit give off a lot of it, and it tells nearby fruit to ripen. *e.g.* A ripe banana in a bag gives off ethylene, and a raw mango in the same bag ripens faster.
- **Senescence**: The planned ageing of a leaf, a fruit or a whole plant. The plant takes the leaf apart in an orderly way and moves useful materials to other parts. *e.g.* A yellowing leaf in autumn or dry season is in senescence.
- **Biennial**: A plant that takes two years to finish their life cycle. In the first year they grow leaves and store food. In the second year they flower, make seeds and die. *e.g.* Carrot, beetroot and onion are biennials.
- **Perennial**: A plant that lives for many years and flowers in many seasons. *e.g.* Mango, neem and coconut trees are perennials.
- **Photoperiod**: The length of the day and night. Many plants use it to tell the season. *e.g.* In summer the days are long and the nights are short.
- **Winter chill**: A spell of cold that some plants need before they will flower. Scientists call it vernalisation. *e.g.* Tulips and apples need a cold spell before they flower.
- **Spore**: A tiny single cell, much smaller than a seed, that can grow into a new plant. Ferns, mosses and mushrooms make spores. *e.g.* The brown dust under a fern leaf is full of spores.
- **Frond**: The leaf of a fern. *e.g.* A fern frond is cut into many small leaflets.
- **Prothallus**: The tiny, flat, heart-shaped plant that grows from a fern spore. The tiny plant makes eggs and sperm. *e.g.* A prothallus is only a few millimetres wide and grows on damp soil.
- **Storage root or stem**: A root or stem that swells up to store food for later, so that a plant can restart after a dry or cold season. *e.g.* Carrot, potato, ginger and onion are storage parts.
- **Rhizome**: A thick stem that grows sideways under or along the ground. New shoots and roots come from it. *e.g.* Ginger and turmeric are rhizomes.
- **Sucker**: A new shoot that grows from the base or roots of a plant. It becomes a new stem. *e.g.* A banana plant sends up suckers around their base.
- **Graft**: Joining a twig from one plant onto the roots and trunk of another, so that the two grow as one plant. *e.g.* Most Alphonso mango trees are grafts, not grown from a stone.
- **Clone**: A plant that is an exact copy of another, made without seeds. Cuttings, suckers and grafts make clones. *e.g.* A money plant cutting is a clone of the vine it was cut from.
- **Tree ring**: A ring of wood that a tree adds in a growing season. In places with a dry or cold season, one ring is usually one year. *e.g.* Count the rings on a cut branch to count the years.
- **Deciduous**: A tree that drops all their leaves in the dry or cold season, then grows new ones. *e.g.* A teak tree is deciduous. A mango tree is evergreen.
- **Lifespan**: How long a living thing lives. *e.g.* A moong plant has a lifespan of about three months.

## Explanations
### What ripening is, and why fruit do it · *defined*
**Definition:** Ripening is a set of changes that turns a hard, sour, green fruit into a soft, sweet, scented, coloured one. It happens when the seeds inside are ready to travel.

A raw mango is hard, green and sour. This helps the fruit, because animals leave the fruit alone until the seeds are ready. When the seeds are ready, the fruit changes. Enzymes cut the starch stored in the fruit into sugar, so the fruit tastes sweet. Other enzymes loosen the walls between the cells, so the fruit gets soft. The green chlorophyll is taken apart, and yellow and orange substances that were hidden underneath show, or new red ones are made, as in a tomato. Acids fall, so the sour taste fades. Tiny scent molecules are made, and these are what you smell from across a room. Each change is a signal to a bird, a bat, a monkey or a person that the fruit is ready to eat. The animal carries the seeds away. Ripening is a fruit asking to be eaten.
- e.g. A green banana is starchy and hard. A yellow banana is sugary and soft.
- e.g. A green tomato turns red as a red substance called lycopene is made.
- e.g. A raw mango is sour. A ripe mango is sweet, and you can smell it from across the room.
### Ethylene: the ripening gas · *defined*
**Definition:** Ethylene is a gas that plants make. It is a hormone that travels through the air, and it tells fruit to ripen. Ripe fruit make a lot of it.

Ethylene is one of the simplest chemicals a plant makes, with just two carbon atoms and four hydrogen atoms. A raw fruit makes a tiny amount. When the fruit begins to ripen, the fruit makes much more, and the gas drifts out into the air. A raw fruit nearby can sense the gas with receptors, which are proteins that catch it. In 1993, a team that included Caren Chang found the first of these receptors in a small plant. Sensing the gas makes the raw fruit make ethylene too, so the ripening spreads like a chain reaction. Fruit that ripen like this after they are picked, such as banana, mango, papaya, tomato, guava, apple and sapota, are called climacteric. Fruit such as grapes, oranges, pineapple, strawberries and watermelon do not ripen more after picking. Ethylene also yellows leaves and makes flowers and leaves drop. In sealed plant chambers, such as those used in space research, the gas has to be removed, or it builds up and harms the plants.
- e.g. A ripe banana in a closed bag speeds up a raw mango, a raw papaya or a hard green tomato in the same bag.
- e.g. A bunch of spinach next to a ripe banana yellows sooner, because the gas also pushes leaves to age.
- e.g. A grape picked green will stay sour. It will not ripen in a bag.
### Ripening at home, and what not to do · *defined*
**Definition:** You can ripen a hard fruit at home by keeping it warm and close to a ripe fruit in a loosely closed bag or box. Cold stops ripening, and a sealed space with no fresh air spoils the fruit.

Ripening works best at ordinary room warmth, about 20 to 30 degrees Celsius. In a refrigerator, ripening stops, and fruit from warm places such as mango, banana and tomato can be damaged by the cold, with dark skin and patchy colour. So ripen first, then cool a ripe fruit if you want to slow it. A paper bag or a loose cardboard box holds the gas close to the fruit, and a ripe banana or apple supplies it. Open the bag every day to let fresh air in, because fruit breathe, and in a sealed space the oxygen falls and the fruit turn sour and spoil. Families in many parts of India have long ripened mangoes in a heap of straw, rice or paddy husk, which holds the gas and the warmth, and they did not need the word ethylene. Some traders use a grey stone called calcium carbide to ripen fruit fast. Food safety rules in India do not allow this, because it can leave harmful dirt on the fruit. Ethylene gas itself, used in small controlled amounts, is allowed.
- e.g. A paper bag with a ripe banana and a green mango, opened each morning to let in air.
- e.g. Mangoes in a box of rice or straw, as many families do in the mango season.
- e.g. A green tomato on a kitchen shelf ripens in a few days, but in the fridge it stays green and pale.
### Do plants send messages through the air? · *debated*
Ethylene is one gas that plants send into the air, and many plants release other scent molecules when a caterpillar chews them. In 1983, two American scientists, Ian Baldwin and Jack Schultz, reported that poplar and sugar maple saplings with chewed leaves seemed to warn untouched saplings nearby. The untouched saplings made more bitter chemicals in their leaves. Later experiments on wild sagebrush by Rick Karban and others also found that plants near a damaged plant suffered less damage. Plants can sense and answer these gases. Scientists still argue about what to call it, and some of the early experiments were hard to repeat. The question is whether a damaged plant is warning others, or whether the neighbours are only overhearing a smell.
- e.g. A raw fruit senses ethylene from a ripe fruit next to them and starts to ripen.
- e.g. Cut grass gives out a fresh smell, which is a chemical alarm that other plants nearby can sense.
- e.g. Sagebrush near a damaged sagebrush was chewed less by insects.
- **Many plant ecologists:** Plants release chemicals when they are damaged, and neighbours sense them and prepare. This is a form of communication, even though no plant has a brain. The messages are real, and they change what the receiving plant does.
- **Some other scientists:** The damaged plant releases chemicals as a by-product of the damage, and the neighbours happen to overhear. Calling it a warning puts a purpose where there may be none, and some experiments have been hard to repeat.
### A leaf's last job: senescence · *defined*
**Definition:** Senescence is the planned ageing of a leaf. The plant takes the leaf apart in order and moves the useful materials to the stem, the roots or the seeds.

A leaf does not simply die. It is taken apart, like a shop being emptied before closing. First the green chlorophyll is broken down, and the nitrogen it contains is sent out through the leaf stalk. Yellow and orange substances were hidden in the leaf all along, and they show once the green goes. Proteins are cut up and moved, and sugars and minerals follow. At last the leaf stalk seals itself, and the empty leaf falls. In a crop such as wheat or rice, a large part of the nitrogen in the grains is moved there from older leaves, according to measurements. A leaf that is kept in the dark cannot make food, and it is taken apart much sooner than a leaf in the light. Ethylene speeds the process up. That is why a bunch of palak yellows faster in a closed dark cupboard next to ripe bananas.
- e.g. A moong plant's lower leaves turn yellow while the pods fill. Their materials are moving into the seeds.
- e.g. A covered patch of a leaf goes yellow while the uncovered part stays green.
- e.g. Coriander kept in the fridge next to ripe fruit goes yellow sooner.
### Why does an annual plant die after making seeds? · *idea*
Moong, rice, wheat and sunflower plants die soon after their seeds ripen. This is not simply a plant wearing out. In the 1950s, plant scientists, including A. Carl Leopold in the United States, reported that if you keep removing the flowers and young pods from a soybean plant, the plant stays green and alive for much longer. So the developing seeds seem to speed the end of the plant. Scientists have two main ideas about why. Some think the seeds pull so much food and minerals out of the plant that nothing is left to keep the leaves going. Others think the seeds send a chemical signal that switches on the ageing of the whole plant. Probably both happen, and the balance differs from plant to plant.
- e.g. A marigold that is deadheaded, with the old flowers pinched off, keeps flowering for longer than one left to make seeds.
- e.g. A soybean plant with the pods removed stays green long after untouched plants have died.
- e.g. A banana stem, after fruiting, dies back, while new shoots at the base carry on.
- **Scientists who favour the food-drain idea:** The seeds are a big sink for nitrogen and sugar. The plant pulls everything out of the leaves to fill them, and the leaves, left empty, yellow and drop. Plants that cannot get more food from the soil run out first.
- **Scientists who favour the signal idea:** Developing seeds make a chemical message that tells the leaves to start senescence, even if food is still available. Some plants die after seeding even when they are well fed, which fits a signal.
### Annual, biennial and perennial: three ways to spend a life · *defined*
**Definition:** An annual finishes its life cycle in one year or less. A biennial takes two years, with leaves and food storage in the first year and flowers in the second. A perennial lives for many years and flowers in many seasons.

Moong, rice, palak, coriander and marigold are annuals. Carrot, beetroot, cabbage and onion are biennials. In the first year a biennial makes leaves and stores food in a swollen root or bulb. In the second year they use that stored food to send up a flower stalk, set seed and die. We usually eat them in their first year, before they flower. Mango, neem, coconut and banyan are perennials. The groups are not fixed. A tomato plant is a perennial in the tropics but is grown as an annual because the plant gets diseased with age. A chilli plant can live for several years if looked after. A castor plant grows as a small annual in a field but can become a small tree in a warm place. So length of life depends on the place and the care as well as on the kind of plant.
- e.g. Annual: moong, rice, wheat, palak and marigold.
- e.g. Biennial: carrot, beetroot, cabbage and onion, which flower in their second year.
- e.g. Perennial: mango, neem, banyan, coconut, hibiscus and tulsi.
### One big flowering or many small ones · *defined*
**Definition:** Some plants flower only once in a lifetime and then die. Scientists call them monocarpic. Others flower in many seasons. Scientists call them polycarpic.

A moong plant flowers for a few weeks and dies, so moong is monocarpic. A mango tree flowers every year for decades, so mango is polycarpic. Some long-lived plants wait for years, flower once and then die. An agave, the century plant, grows a rosette of thick leaves for ten to thirty years, then sends up a tall flower stalk and dies. The Mizoram bamboo and the talipot palm do the same. Each plant makes a choice, in the way that every living thing has to, between spending their energy on growing, which keeps the plant alive, and on seeds, which make new plants. A plant that flowers many times spreads their effort over the years. A plant that flowers once puts everything into one big bet. Banana is a mix. Each banana stem flowers once and dies, but the clump sends up new stems through suckers and carries on. Other living things make the same choice. Pacific salmon swim upstream, spawn once and die.
- e.g. Monocarpic: moong, rice, sunflower, agave, talipot palm and the Mizoram bamboo.
- e.g. Polycarpic: mango, neem, coconut, banyan and peepal.
- e.g. A banana stem flowers once, but the clump lives on through suckers.
### Saving up for the second year · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants swell a root, stem or bulb to store food for a time when they cannot make it. The plant uses the stored food to restart after the dry or cold season. We eat many of these stores.

A carrot is a swollen root. Beetroot is another one. A potato is a swollen underground stem, an onion is a bulb made of thick leaf bases, and ginger and turmeric are rhizomes, which are thick underground stems that grow sideways. All of these are savings. The plant makes sugar in the leaves in the good season and packs it away as starch. When the dry or cold season ends, the plant spends the savings to grow shoots fast, before there are leaves to make new food. If you put the top of a carrot in a saucer of water, feathery leaves sprout from it, living on the food in the carrot. The leaves cannot make a new carrot, but it shows what the carrot is for. We eat the savings of the plant. Seeds are another kind of savings, packed for the plant's young.
- e.g. Put the cut top of a carrot in water and it grows green leaves using the stored food.
- e.g. A forgotten potato in a dark cupboard grows long pale shoots from the eyes.
- e.g. Ginger and turmeric pieces left in a damp place put out buds and roots.
### How plants tell the season: day length · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants measure the length of the night to tell the season. Short-day plants flower when nights are long. Long-day plants flower when nights are short. Day-neutral plants ignore day length.

Near the equator, the day length changes little through the year, but it changes enough for many plants to feel. In many parts of India, traditional kinds of rice are short-day plants. They flower when the nights become longer after the monsoon, whenever they were sown. Many modern kinds of rice are bred to be less sensitive, so that farmers can grow them in several seasons. Chrysanthemums are short-day plants, and flower growers cover the plants with dark cloth or add lamps at night to make them flower in time for a festival. Palak, coriander and lettuce are long-day plants. When the days grow long and hot, they send up a flower stalk, and the leaves turn bitter. Gardeners call this bolting. Tomato, cucumber and many beans are day-neutral. They flower when they are big enough. A plant measures the night with a clock inside the leaves. The leaves then send a message up the stem to the buds.
- e.g. Traditional rice flowers when the nights lengthen after the monsoon.
- e.g. Coriander and palak bolt in the long, hot days of summer.
- e.g. Flower growers cover chrysanthemums with dark cloth to bring on blooms for a festival.
### Remembering winter: flowering after a cold spell · *defined*
**Definition:** Some plants need a spell of cold before they will flower. The cold tells them that winter has passed. Scientists call this vernalisation. The plant remembers the cold for weeks.

In the 1910s, a German scientist, Gustav Gassner, showed that winter cereals, which are sown in autumn, flower only if the seed or seedling has been through cold. A seed that was kept warm made leaves but no flower stalk. If a plant flowered at the first warm day of autumn, the winter would kill the flowers. So the plant waits for a sign. What is remarkable is that the plant remembers the cold after it has gone. A small plant used in labs, a relative of mustard, holds the memory of winter in a gene that is switched off by chemical marks while the cold lasts. Caroline Dean, a British plant scientist, and her team have spent years finding how the plant's cells keep this memory. Apples, tulips and many hill flowers need winter chill. A mango tree in some places needs a cool, dry spell to flower.
- e.g. Tulip bulbs sold in warm countries are kept in cold storage first, so that they will flower.
- e.g. Apple trees in Kashmir and Himachal need a cold winter to flower well.
- e.g. In some places, mango trees flower after a cool dry spell.
### Ferns: a life cycle with two bodies · *defined*
**Definition:** A fern grows from a spore, not a seed. The spore grows into a tiny, flat, heart-shaped plant, which makes eggs and sperm. The sperm swim to the egg through a film of water, and the fertilised egg grows into a new fern.

Turn over a fern leaf and look for rows of brown dots. These are clusters of spore cases. When they are ripe, they fling out spores like dust. A spore that lands on damp soil grows into a tiny green heart, only a few millimetres wide, called a prothallus. This is a separate small plant. The tiny plant makes eggs and sperm. When the soil is wet, the sperm swim to the eggs, so ferns need damp places. The egg grows into a baby fern, and the heart-shaped plant withers away. So a fern's life cycle has two different bodies, the big fern that makes spores and the tiny heart that makes eggs and sperm. Flowering plants have the same two stages, but the second one is hidden. A grain of pollen is the tiny male stage, and the seed-start inside the flower holds the tiny female stage. Mosses also use spores.
- e.g. Brown dots on the underside of a fern frond are spore cases.
- e.g. A fern in a damp corner of a wall puts up fronds from a creeping rhizome.
- e.g. A moss capsule on a thin stalk releases spores.
### Copies and variety: cuttings, grafts and seedless fruit · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant grown from a cutting, a sucker or a graft is a clone, an exact copy of the parent. A plant grown from a seed gets instructions from two parents, so the new plant is a little different.

A farmer who wants every mango tree to bear the same sweet mangoes cannot plant stones, because each stone grows into a different tree. Instead the farmer grafts a twig from a good tree onto a young rooted plant. The twig grows into a branch that bears the same mangoes as its parent. Most Alphonso trees are grafts. Bananas we eat are seedless. They cannot make seeds, so every banana plant comes from a sucker or from a lab-grown cutting. Copies are quick and sure, and they keep the fruit the same. But copies have no variety. If a disease can harm one plant of that kind, it can harm all of them. Seeds, in contrast, mix instructions from two parents, and in a field of seedlings there are always some that cope with a new disease or a new climate. Variety is a plant's insurance.
- e.g. A money plant cutting rooted in water becomes a clone of the vine.
- e.g. A grafted mango tree bears fruit in three to five years, which is sooner than a tree grown from a stone.
- e.g. All the bananas of one kind in a plantation are clones from suckers.
### How old can a plant be? · *debated*
Some plants live a very long time. A bristlecone pine in California is reported to be about 4,800 years old. The Sri Maha Bodhi, a peepal tree in Anuradhapura in Sri Lanka, is said to have been planted in 288 BCE, which would make the tree more than 2,000 years old. A grove of quaking aspen trees in Utah called Pando is made of tens of thousands of trunks, all shoots of one root system, and the age of Pando is estimated at many thousands of years, though scientists disagree about how many. A clump of bamboo or banana can be older than any one stem in it. So the age of a plant depends on what you count: one trunk, or the whole living clone. Scientists also argue about whether trees age as animals do.
- e.g. A bristlecone pine in the dry mountains of California is reported to be about 4,800 years old.
- e.g. The Sri Maha Bodhi tree in Sri Lanka is said to be planted in 288 BCE.
- e.g. A banyan, such as the Great Banyan near Kolkata, spreads by roots hanging from the branches, and the original trunk can be replaced.
- **Some tree scientists:** Many old trees show very little weakening with age. They keep growing and making seeds, and they die from accidents such as storms, fire, fungus or drought. On this view some trees show almost no ageing.
- **Other tree scientists:** Big old trees do decline. They have more dead wood, rot in the centre, and carry a huge weight to feed, so they grow more slowly and are easier to kill. They simply decline very slowly.
### Tree rings: a diary in wood · *defined*
**Definition:** In places with a wet and a dry season, or a warm and a cold one, a tree grows fast in the good season and slowly in the poor one. This leaves a ring of wood for each year. Counting rings counts years, and the width of each ring records how good the year was.

Cut across a branch and you see a pattern of pale and dark circles. The pale wood grows in the good season, when there is rain, and the dark band grows in the dry or cold time. One pale and dark pair is one year. A wide ring means a good year with plenty of rain. A narrow ring means a hard year. Because the weather pattern is shared by all trees in a region, a dry year shows up as a narrow ring in every tree. Scientists match the patterns in living trees and old beams to read the weather of the past. Indian scientists have used the rings of teak trees to work out the monsoon rains of past centuries. Not every tree makes clear rings. Trees in places with no seasons may not.
- e.g. A log of teak or neem has clear rings from the dry and wet seasons.
- e.g. A tree with 30 rings is about 30 years old.
- e.g. A very narrow ring marks a drought year in the whole area.

## Mostly agreed
- Ripe fruit give off ethylene, a gas that speeds the ripening of fruit nearby. *How we know:* Fruit sealed in a bag with a ripe banana ripen sooner than fruit sealed alone. Scientists can measure the gas, and they have found the receptors that sense it.
- A fruit ripens by changes inside it: starch becomes sugar, the walls loosen, the green fades, acids fall and scents are made. *How we know:* These changes have been measured in many fruits, and the enzymes that cause them have been identified.
- A leaf turning yellow is being taken apart in order, with useful materials moved to other parts of the plant. *How we know:* Scientists have tracked nitrogen out of ageing leaves and into seeds and stems, and have found genes that switch the process on.
- Many plants measure the length of the night, or a spell of cold, to decide when to flower. *How we know:* Experiments in which a brief light is flashed in a long night, or a plant is chilled, change when the plant flowers.
- Ferns reproduce with spores, and the spore grows into a tiny separate plant that makes eggs and sperm. *How we know:* You can see this under a hand lens on damp soil a few weeks after sowing spores. Scientists described the whole cycle in the 1800s.
- Plants grown from cuttings or grafts are copies, and plants grown from seed vary. *How we know:* A row of grafted mango trees bear the same fruit, but a row of trees grown from stones bear different ones.

## How we found out
**F. E. Denny, an American plant scientist, and Richard Gane, an English plant scientist, The 1920s and 1934.** For years, citrus growers in the United States noticed that lemons turned yellow sooner in sheds heated with kerosene stoves. Everyone thought that the warmth of the stove did the work. In the 1920s, an American plant scientist, F. E. Denny, tested this. Other scientists had already found that a gas in the shed air, not the warmth, seemed to do the work. Denny reported that the active gas in the stove fumes was ethylene, and that even a tiny amount turned green lemons yellow. So the warmth was not the cause. A gas in the smoke was. In 1934, an English plant scientist, Richard Gane, showed that ripening fruit make ethylene themselves. A fruit does not need a stove at all. A ripe fruit makes the gas, and the gas ripens the fruit nearby.  
*What people believed before:* Growers believed that the heat of the stoves coloured the lemons. Tests that kept the temperature the same and changed only the gas showed that the gas alone made the difference.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Fruit ripen only because of the sun's heat.** (Many people, and many gardeners.; *rejected*)  
  For: Fruit on a tree ripen in warm weather, and ripening is slow in cold. Heat does speed up the process.  
  Against: A banana picked green ripens in a dark cupboard. A mango in a bag with a ripe banana ripens faster than one in the sun alone. In the 1920s, growers who heated lemons with kerosene stoves thought the heat coloured the lemons. The fumes of the stove were the cause, not the heat.
- **Every kind of fruit goes on ripening after it is picked.** (Many shoppers and some sellers.; *rejected*)  
  For: Mangoes, bananas, papayas and tomatoes do ripen after picking, so it seems true of fruit in general.  
  Against: Grapes, oranges, pineapples, strawberries and watermelons are picked ripe and do not get sweeter later. A green grape put in a bag with a banana stays sour.
- **An annual plant dies after seeding simply from being worn out.** (Many gardeners.; *debated*)  
  For: A moong plant looks tired at the end, with yellow leaves and dry stems. The plant has used up a lot of food.  
  Against: In soybean, taking off the flowers and young pods keeps the plant green and alive for much longer. So the seeds seem to take part in ending the plant. The end of the plant is partly set off by the seeds.
- **Ferns have seeds so tiny that nobody can see them, and people who carry fern seed become invisible.** (Many people in Europe for hundreds of years. Shakespeare has a character joke about it in a play.; *rejected*)  
  For: Ferns never show flowers or fruit, yet new ferns keep appearing, so people guessed there were hidden seeds.  
  Against: In the 1800s, scientists with microscopes saw that the brown dust under a fern leaf is spores, not seeds, and that spores grow into a tiny heart-shaped plant. The fern has no flowers or seeds at all.
- **A plant can pass on to their seeds what they learned in their own lifetime, so cold-treated wheat gives seeds that no longer need cold.** (Trofim Lysenko, a Soviet agronomist with political power in the 1930s and 1940s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Cold treatment of winter wheat really does change when the plant flowers, and some plants do pass on small chemical marks to the next generation.  
  Against: Careful experiments showed that the seeds of cold-treated plants still need the cold. The strong claim was false, and it did great harm. Lysenko's power was used against geneticists. Nikolai Vavilov, who built a great seed collection, died in prison in 1943.

## Roots
We tested how a ripe banana ripens a raw mango without touching. Now we ask what this message through the air tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the bag, a ripe banana and a raw mango never touched, yet the mango ripened sooner. A gas called ethylene passed between them through the air. So a ripe fruit sends a message, and a raw fruit nearby hears it and answers.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a fruit as a finished thing that sits alone and changes only with time. But a fruit senses, makes a gas, and answers the gas of the fruit next to them. Ripening is something fruit do together.  
*Evidence:* In the bag test, a mango sharing a bag with a ripe banana softened and yellowed days before a mango in a bag alone. A mango in the open was slowest. If your own result differed, write down what happened.

- *who am i:* A yawn can spread across a room, and a smell of rain can change your mood, without anyone touching you. You change people near you through the air, and they change you.
- *what is life:* A ripe fruit is alive and awake. They sense, make a gas, and call to their neighbours. Plants speak to each other with gases, as people speak with voices.
- *what is this world:* A bag of fruit, an orchard and a forest each carry news from plant to plant through the air. Nothing alive is truly alone.

One gas, made by one ripe fruit, spreads through a whole bunch of fruit, and the whole bunch ripens together. Each fruit is separate, yet the bunch acts like one body. The gas is a tiny molecule of just six atoms, and it joins them. So separateness and togetherness are both true here: there are many fruit, and there is one ripening.

- **Thich Nhat Hanh, on interbeing, in The Heart of Understanding:** If you are a poet, you will see clearly that there is a cloud floating in this sheet of paper. Without a cloud there is no rain, without rain the trees cannot grow, and without trees we cannot make paper. (A mango that ripens because of a banana has a banana in it, in a way. What a fruit becomes depends on the fruit around them.)
- **Purananuru 192, a Tamil poem by Kaniyan Pungundranar from the Sangam age:** Every town is our town. Everyone is our kin. (Fruit in one bag act like kin. What one fruit does for itself spreads to all the rest.)
- **Tao Te Ching 16:** All things grow and flower, and each returns to its root. Returning to the root is stillness. (A plant flowers, makes seeds, yellows and falls. What was in the leaves goes into the seeds and back to the soil. The ending carries on into the next beginning.)
- Science (settled): Ripe fruit give off ethylene, a gas that speeds the ripening of fruit nearby. Plants have receptors that catch the gas.
- Science (debated): Whether plants send each other warnings through scent molecules, and whether the neighbours are listening on purpose, is still argued about.
- Other view, Many plant scientists: The banana does not speak to the mango. A gas is released as part of the banana's own ripening, and the mango reacts to the chemical, as iron reacts to damp. Calling this a message or a conversation is a way of talking that adds an idea the chemistry does not need.
- Practice, Smell the message (3 min): Hold a ripe banana in one hand and a raw mango in the other. Close your eyes. Breathe in slowly through your nose. Notice the sweet smell of the banana. That smell is the fruit sending a message into the air. Breathe in slowly three times. What do you think the raw mango is doing right now?
- Ask: When a banana and a mango share a bag, are there two fruit, or one ripening?
- Ask: If a raw mango hears a banana, is the mango listening, or just reacting?
- Ask: What else in your day travels between you and others without a touch?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A ripe banana changes a raw mango without touching it. What else in the world changes something without touching it? (hint: Think about smell, sound, light and warmth.)
- *deep:* A moong plant dies as the seeds ripen. Is dying part of the plan, or a failure? (hint: What happens to the leaves' materials when a leaf yellows?)

## Story: Four Days to Sunday
*Anjana, her family and the mango cart are made up. The science is real. A ripe banana gives off ethylene, which speeds the ripening of raw mangoes nearby. Fruit also need fresh air, and a sealed box can spoil them. Ripening mangoes in rice or straw is a real traditional method. Calcium carbide is not allowed for ripening fruit in India. The times in the story are typical, but real ripening depends on the fruit and the weather.*

Anjana's uncle sold mangoes from a handcart near the temple in Thrissur. In the mango season, the cart was heaped high with small, sweet moovandan mangoes. But this year the mangoes ripened late. On Wednesday morning, every mango on the cart was still as hard and green as a stone.

That morning, a woman in a bright cotton sari stopped at the cart. 'My daughter's wedding is on Sunday,' she said. 'I need forty ripe mangoes for the pulissery. Can you have them ready by Saturday night?' Anjana's uncle, Vinod, looked at his green heap. He did not want to lose the order. 'Saturday night,' he said. 'Yes.' After the woman had gone, he rubbed his forehead. Nobody could ripen forty mangoes in three days.

A woman from the next stall had been listening. 'Easy,' she said. She held out a paper packet of small grey stones. 'Put these in the box with the mangoes. By morning, they will be yellow.' Anjana knew the grey stones. They were called carbide. Her teacher, Rekha Madam, had told the class that carbide is not allowed for ripening fruit, because it can leave harmful dirt on the skin. 'No,' said Anjana. 'Not on our cart.' Uncle Vinod looked at her, and then at the packet. 'No,' he said, a little slowly. 'Not on my cart.'

Anjana's grandmother, Ammamma, was sitting on a stool beside the cart, sorting mangoes. 'In my village,' she said, 'we buried raw mangoes in a basket of rice and put a cloth over the top. In four or five days, they came out yellow.' 'Why does the rice help?' asked Anjana. Ammamma laughed. 'The rice never told me.'

But Anjana had an idea, and it came from school. Last week, Rekha Madam had put a hard green mango in a paper bag with a very ripe banana. She had put another in a bag alone. After four days, the mango with the banana was soft and yellow, and the other was still firm. 'The two never touched,' Madam had said. 'A ripe fruit gives out a gas that you cannot see. It is called ethylene. Raw fruit nearby can sense it, and they start to ripen too. The bag keeps the gas close.'

'Bananas,' said Anjana. 'We need very ripe bananas.' By evening, Uncle Vinod had bought two dozen spotty bananas from the market, at half the usual price, because they were so ripe. They packed the forty mangoes into a big cardboard carton and tucked a banana in among them. Then Uncle Vinod sealed the whole carton with brown tape, all the way around, to keep the gas in. 'Tight as a drum,' he said proudly.

On Thursday evening, they peeped through a corner. Nothing had changed. The mangoes were still hard. On Friday morning, Anjana opened the carton, and a sour smell rolled out, like old curd. A few of the bananas had turned black and soft. Some of the mangoes had a dull yellow patch, and the rest were still green. 'It has gone wrong,' said Uncle Vinod. 'Maybe we should have used the stones.' Anjana did not answer. She was thinking.

She remembered the bags at school. Every morning, Rekha Madam had opened them for a minute and flapped them. 'Why?' someone had asked. 'Because fruit breathe,' said Madam. 'They need fresh air. Shut in a small space for too long, they use up the air, and they spoil instead of ripening.'

'Uncle,' said Anjana, 'we sealed them too tightly. They have no air.' 'But you said the bag keeps the gas in,' said her uncle. 'It does,' said Anjana. 'We need to keep the gas in and let the air in. Both.' Uncle Vinod scratched his head. 'How can you do both?'

Anjana thought of Ammamma's basket of rice. Rice is full of tiny gaps. Gas can stay among the grains, and air can seep in through them. 'We do not need to close it like a drum,' she said. 'We need to close it like a basket with a cloth.'

They peeled off the tape. They threw out the black bananas and put in fresh ripe ones. They set the mangoes in a single layer, with a banana close to every few. They laid a thick cloth loosely over the top. And every morning and every evening, they lifted the cloth for a minute and fanned fresh air in.

On Friday night, the first mangoes began to smell sweet. On Saturday morning, a third of them were yellow. By Saturday afternoon, Anjana pressed one with her thumb, and it gave softly. 'Warm and soft,' she said. They sorted them. Thirty-six mangoes were ready, soft, yellow and fragrant. Four were still hard.

The woman came on Saturday evening. She pressed a mango herself, and smelled it, and smiled. 'Thirty-six will do,' she said. 'The rest I can use for pickle.' She counted out the money. Uncle Vinod's hand shook a little as he took it.

Later, the woman from the next stall came by. She looked at the carton, at the cloth and at the heap of black bananas. 'How did you do it without stones?' she asked. 'Bananas,' said Anjana. 'And fresh air.' 'Bananas?' said the woman, and shook her head. 'Next time, bring your own bananas,' said Uncle Vinod, and for the first time that week, he laughed.

That night, Anjana sat with Ammamma on the doorstep. 'So the rice was doing the work all along,' she said. 'Rice, straw and bananas,' said Ammamma. 'People knew how before they knew why.' 'And now we know why,' said Anjana. 'One fruit tells the others. Without touching.' Ammamma looked at the carton in the dark. 'Then the question is not only how a fruit ripens,' she said. 'It is how a fruit speaks.'

**For the parent:** A ripe fruit sends out a gas that tells raw fruit nearby to ripen. The gas works best when kept close, but ripening fruit also need fresh air.

- Ask: Why did the mangoes in the tightly taped carton not ripen well?
- Ask: How does a ripe banana change a raw mango that it never touches?
- Ask: Ammamma's rice basket worked for generations. What do you think the rice did for the mangoes?
- Ask: Anjana taught her uncle something he did not know. Where did she learn it, and how could she check that it was true?
- Ask: Why do you think a trader might use grey stones instead, even when the stones are not allowed?

## Experiments
### e1 · Ripe in a Bag: does a ripe banana ripen a raw mango? (45 min, 6 days)
- **question:** Does a ripe banana make a hard green mango ripen sooner, even if the two never touch?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After four days, which mango will be softest: the one in a bag with a ripe banana, the one in a bag alone, or the one left in the open? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The three mangoes are the same kind, the same size and equally hard. They sit in the same warm room, out of the sun. Both bags are the same kind of paper bag, folded shut the same way. Compare A with B to see the banana. Compare B with C to see the bag. You check at the same time every day, and press with the same thumb.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts and checks the mangoes at the end. Wash the mangoes before eating. Mango sap near the stalk can sting the skin and lips, so wash your hands. Do not give mango to anyone who is allergic to it. Do not eat any fruit that smells sour or fizzy, or looks rotten. Keep the banana and mangoes out of reach of pets.
- **record:** Fill in the table each day. Use the softness scale: 1 is hard as a stone, 2 is firm, 3 gives a little, 4 is soft, 5 is very soft. ## Worksheets
### Ripening and Ages (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meena puts a hard green papaya in a paper bag with a very ripe banana. The papaya ripens sooner than a papaya left alone. What does the banana give off that helps the papaya ripen?  
   Options: Ethylene, a gas / Oxygen / Sugar / Heat  
   **Answer:** Ethylene, a gas. *Why:* A ripe banana gives off ethylene, a gas that raw fruit nearby can sense. It tells them to start ripening. Picking 'heat' is a common guess, but a banana gives off almost no heat. Picking 'sugar' mixes up what is inside the banana with what leaves it.
2. (truefalse) A sour green grape put in a bag with a ripe banana will slowly turn sweet.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Grapes are picked ripe, and they do not get sweeter after picking. Fruit such as bananas, mangoes and tomatoes keep ripening after picking, but grapes, oranges and pineapples do not.
3. (mcq) Why does a mango taste sweeter when it is ripe than when it is raw?  
   Options: Enzymes cut the stored starch into sugar / The mango takes in sugar from the air / The skin makes the inside sweeter / Water inside the mango turns into sugar  
   **Answer:** Enzymes cut the stored starch into sugar. *Why:* A raw mango stores starch. When it ripens, enzymes cut the starch into sugar, and the acids fall, so the fruit tastes sweeter. Picking 'from the air' is not right, because the fruit makes the change from its own stores.
4. (fill) As a fruit ripens, enzymes cut the stored ____ into sugar.  
   **Answer:** ['starch']. *Why:* Raw fruit, such as a green banana, are full of starch. Ripening turns the starch into sugar, which is why a yellow banana is sweet and a green one is not.
5. (write) Explain in two or three sentences why it helps a plant when their fruit turn soft, sweet and scented just when the seeds are ready.  
   **Answer:** A soft, sweet, scented fruit attracts animals such as birds, bats, monkeys and people. They eat the fruit and carry the seeds away, and drop them far from the parent plant. A hard sour green fruit stays untouched until the seeds are ready, so the plant keeps them safe.. *Why:* A strong answer links the changes to the seeds. Ripeness is a signal that says the seeds are ready to be carried.
6. (mcq) A shopkeeper packs ripening mangoes tightly in a sealed plastic box for several days. When he opens it, a sour smell rolls out and many mangoes are spoiled. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Ripening fruit breathe, and in a sealed space the air runs low, so they spoil / The mangoes were too sweet / Sealing fruit always makes them ripen faster / The box got too much light  
   **Answer:** Ripening fruit breathe, and in a sealed space the air runs low, so they spoil. *Why:* Fruit breathe oxygen even while they ripen. In a sealed box the oxygen falls, and the fruit spoil instead of ripening. Picking 'sealing always speeds ripening' mixes up holding in the gas with shutting out the air.
7. (truefalse) Putting a hard green tomato in the fridge is a good way to make it ripen faster.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Ripening is chemistry, and it runs slowly in the cold. A fruit from a warm place can also be damaged by a long spell in the cold. Ripen the fruit on a warm shelf, and cool it afterwards if it is ripe.
8. (sort) Sort these fruit. Do they keep ripening after they are picked, or not?  
   **Answer:** banana → Keeps ripening; mango → Keeps ripening; tomato → Keeps ripening; grapes → Does not keep ripening; orange → Does not keep ripening; pineapple → Does not keep ripening. *Why:* Banana, mango and tomato make a burst of ethylene as they ripen, and they go on ripening after picking. Grapes, oranges and pineapples are picked ripe and do not get sweeter later.
9. (mcq) A leaf on a plant turns yellow at the end of the growing season. What is the plant doing?  
   Options: Taking the leaf apart and moving useful materials into the stem, roots or seeds / Painting the leaf yellow / Giving up on the leaf because the leaf is dirty / Making the leaf grow bigger  
   **Answer:** Taking the leaf apart and moving useful materials into the stem, roots or seeds. *Why:* A leaf is taken apart in order. The plant breaks down the green chlorophyll and moves the nitrogen and other useful materials out. The yellow substances were in the leaf all along and show once the green goes. ## Applications
- Fruit traders ripen mangoes, bananas and papayas in warm rooms with small, controlled amounts of ethylene, so that the fruit is ripe on the day it is sold.
- Cold stores slow the ripening of apples and other fruit for months, and remove ethylene from the air to keep the fruit from ripening too soon.
- Bananas are picked green and shipped across the country, then ripened near the market, so that they arrive firm and undamaged.
- Flower growers and florists cover chrysanthemums with dark cloth, or add lamps at night, to make the flowers open for a festival.
- Gardeners pinch off old flowers from marigolds and zinnias, so that the plants keep flowering for longer.
- Plant breeders choose rice and wheat varieties for their day-length sensitivity and cold needs, so that the crop flowers at the right time in each region.
- Farmers graft mango and guava trees to get the same fruit as their best tree, sooner than from a stone.
- Tissue culture labs grow banana plants from tiny pieces of tissue, to supply clean, disease-free plants to farmers.
- Scientists read the rings of teak and other trees to work out the monsoon rains of past centuries.
- Archaeologists and furniture historians date old wooden beams by matching their ring patterns to trees of known age.
- Kitchens that store leafy greens away from ripe fruit, and put a ripe banana next to a hard avocado, use ethylene without knowing its name.
- Plant breeders are trying to develop perennial rice and wheat that regrow every year without replanting.

## Open questions
- **What switches on the burst of ethylene when a fruit begins to ripen?** Scientists know many of the genes and signals involved. Exactly what tips a fruit from waiting to ripening, and how the signals are timed, is still being worked out.
- **Do damaged plants warn their neighbours on purpose, or do the neighbours only overhear?** Many experiments show that plants respond to the scent of damaged neighbours. Whether the signal evolved as a warning, or the neighbours evolved to listen in, is still argued about.
- **How can a plant remember a winter for weeks, and how can a bamboo count decades?** Scientists have found the chemical marks on genes that keep the memory of a winter for weeks. Nobody has found a clock that could count a bamboo's 48 years.
- **Do trees grow old as animals do?** Some trees show very little decline for centuries, and others decline slowly. It is hard to follow one tree for thousands of years, so the question is still open.
- **Can we breed perennial grains, like perennial rice and wheat, that give as much grain as the annual crops?** Perennial grains would save ploughing and sowing each year. Plant breeders are trying, but so far the harvests are smaller than those of annual crops.
- **Why does an annual plant die after setting seed, when they could go on living?** Two main ideas, a food drain and a chemical signal from the seeds, both have support. Scientists do not agree on which matters most, and it may differ from plant to plant.

- A mango in a bag with a ripe banana is ripe after 3 days. A mango in the open takes 6 days. How many days sooner is the first mango? How many times as fast is it? **3 days sooner. It ripens 2 times as fast.**. 6 take away 3 is 3 days sooner. 6 divided by 3 is 2. So the mango with the banana ripened in half the time, which is twice as fast. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A teak log has 36 rings, and its outermost ring formed in 2019. In which year did the tree sprout? **1984.**. If the 36th ring is from 2019, the first ring is 35 years earlier. 2019 take away 35 is 1984. A check: from 1984 to 2019, counting both years, is 36 years. (queues `math.time.calendar.L4`)
- On the lifespan tape, one millimetre stands for one year. A moong plant lives about 3 months. How many moong lifetimes fit into one millimetre? How long is the tape for the Sri Maha Bodhi tree, which is about 2,300 years old, in metres? **4 moong lifetimes fit in a millimetre. The tape for the tree is 2.3 metres long.**. 3 months is a quarter of a year, so 4 moong lifetimes make 1 year, which is 1 millimetre. 2,300 millimetres is 2 metres and 300 millimetres, which is 2.3 metres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L4`)
- In the Pinch or Let Go project, the Pinch plants had 3, 8, 14, 20, 22 and 25 flowers at the six weekly counts, added over all three plants. The Let Go plants had 3, 6, 9, 8, 5 and 3. Which group had more flowers in total, and by how many? **The Pinch group had 92 flowers and the Let Go group 34. The Pinch group had 58 more.**. 3 + 8 + 14 + 20 + 22 + 25 is 92. 3 + 6 + 9 + 8 + 5 + 3 is 34. 92 take away 34 is 58. The numbers are made up, but real pinched marigolds often flower for longer. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- Mizoram bamboo flowered around 2006. The bamboo flowers about every 48 years. About what year will it flower next? A child who is 10 years old in 2026 will be how old then? **About 2054. The child will be 38.**. 2006 + 48 = 2054. A child who is 10 in 2026 was born in 2016. 2054 take away 2016 is 38. So the child will be about 38. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
- A farm has 5 kinds of banana in equal numbers. A disease harms only one kind. What fraction of the plants is harmed? What fraction would be harmed if the farm had only one kind? **One fifth. If there were only one kind, all of the plants, which is 5 out of 5.**. With 5 kinds in equal numbers, each kind is 1 out of 5 of the plants. The disease harms 1 of the 5 kinds, so 1 fifth of the plants. With one kind, the disease can harm every plant, which is 5 out of 5, or the whole farm. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Suppose, as a made-up rule, that one ripe banana can ripen about 4 raw mangoes in a loosely closed box. How many ripe bananas would you need for 40 mangoes? **10 ripe bananas.**. Each banana covers 4 mangoes. 40 divided by 4 is 10. So 10 ripe bananas would be needed, if the made-up rule held. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Marigold seedlings cost 5 rupees each. A pot costs 25 rupees. How much do 6 seedlings and 6 pots cost together? **180 rupees.**. 6 seedlings at 5 rupees is 30 rupees. 6 pots at 25 rupees is 150 rupees. 30 + 150 is 180 rupees. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What gas do ripe fruit give off that helps nearby fruit ripen? → Ethylene.
- Does a grape ripen after it is picked? → No. Grapes are picked ripe.
- Why does a fruit taste sweeter when it ripens? → Enzymes cut its starch into sugar.
- What is a biennial plant? → A plant that takes two years: leaves and stored food in the first year, flowers in the second.
- Where on a money plant stem do new roots grow? → At the nodes.
- How does a fern make new ferns? → With spores that grow into a tiny heart-shaped plant.
- What does a wide tree ring mean? → A good year with plenty of rain.
- Why do coriander and palak go to seed in summer? → Long, hot days tell them to flower.
- Why are plants grown from cuttings alike? → They are clones, copies of one parent.
