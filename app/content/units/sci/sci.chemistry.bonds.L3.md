# Chemical bonds: how atoms hold on to each other
`sci.chemistry.bonds.L3`

**Summary.** Boil water and the steam drips back off a lid as water. A small battery can do what boiling cannot: split water into two gases, hydrogen and oxygen. Water is made of atoms that hold on to each other very tightly. This holding is called a chemical bond, and it is made by the atoms' electrons. Every solid, liquid and gas around us, and every part of you, is atoms held by bonds.

**Intro.** *Next time someone cooks rice or boils milk, look under the lid. It is covered in drops. Touch one. It is plain water. The water went up as steam and came back as water.* So heating moves water around, but it does not change water into something else. Is water a single, simple stuff that can never be broken? People thought so for over two thousand years. In this unit you will split water yourself, with a pencil and a battery, and find out what it is made of.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Atom**: The smallest piece of an element. Everything around you is made of atoms, far too small to see. *e.g.* A single grain of salt has more atoms than there are grains of sand on a beach.
- **Element**: A substance made of only one kind of atom. There are 118 known elements. *e.g.* Oxygen, hydrogen, carbon, iron and gold are elements.
- **Electron**: A tiny particle with a negative electric charge, found on the outside of every atom. Electrons make the bonds between atoms. *e.g.* When two atoms share a pair of electrons, both atoms hold on to that pair.
- **Chemical bond**: The way two atoms hold on to each other. Their electrons are attracted by both atoms at once, so the atoms stay together. *e.g.* In water, each hydrogen atom has one bond with the oxygen atom.
- **Molecule**: A group of atoms held together by bonds. It is the smallest piece of a substance like water or sugar. *e.g.* A water molecule is one oxygen atom with two hydrogen atoms held on to it.
- **Compound**: A substance made of two or more kinds of atom held together by bonds. *e.g.* Water is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen. Sugar is a compound of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen.
- **Hydrogen**: The lightest element. A hydrogen atom usually makes one bond. Hydrogen gas burns easily. *e.g.* In your experiment, the bubbles at the pencil joined to the minus side of the battery were hydrogen.
- **Oxygen**: An element. The oxygen gas in air is what we breathe in. An oxygen atom usually makes two bonds. *e.g.* Air has oxygen molecules, each made of two oxygen atoms held together.
- **Carbon**: An element. A carbon atom usually makes four bonds. Diamond and pencil lead are both pure carbon. *e.g.* Carbon is in sugar, in wood, in rice and in every living thing.
- **Electrolysis**: Splitting a substance using electricity. Electro means electricity, and lysis means splitting. *e.g.* A 9-volt battery and two pencils split water into hydrogen and oxygen.
- **Chemical change**: A change where atoms let go of their partners and make new bonds, so a new substance is made. It is often hard to undo. *e.g.* Sugar heated on a spoon turns into brown caramel, which does not turn back into white sugar.
- **Physical change**: A change where molecules move apart or closer, but each molecule stays whole. No new substance is made. *e.g.* Ice melting into water, and water boiling into steam, are physical changes.
- **Graphite**: A soft, grey form of carbon. Its atoms are held in flat sheets that slide over each other. Pencil lead is graphite mixed with clay. *e.g.* When you write, sheets of graphite slide off the pencil and stay on the paper.

## Explanations
### What a chemical bond is · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical bond is the way two atoms hold on to each other. It is made by electrons, which are attracted by both atoms at once.

Every atom has a centre with a positive electric charge, and electrons around it with a negative charge. Opposite charges attract. When two atoms come close, some of their electrons are attracted by both centres at once. So the two atoms stay together. Nothing like glue or hooks is involved. The holding is the electric force.
- e.g. In a water molecule, the oxygen atom shares a pair of electrons with each hydrogen atom. Those two shared pairs are the two bonds.
- e.g. The oxygen you breathe is two oxygen atoms bonded together. They stay paired as they float in the air.
### Molecules: atoms held in a group · *defined*
**Definition:** A molecule is a group of atoms held together by bonds. It is the smallest piece of a substance like water.

Scientists write a molecule with letters and small numbers. H stands for hydrogen, O for oxygen, C for carbon and N for nitrogen. H2O means two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. So one drop of water is a huge crowd of H2O molecules. Each molecule is the same, wherever the water came from.
- e.g. Water: H2O, 3 atoms.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide, the gas you breathe out: CO2, 3 atoms.
- e.g. Table sugar: C12H22O11, 45 atoms in one molecule.
### Each kind of atom has a set number of bonds · *defined*
**Definition:** Each kind of atom usually makes the same number of bonds. Hydrogen makes 1, oxygen makes 2, nitrogen makes 3 and carbon makes 4.

Think of bonds as hands. Hydrogen has one hand, oxygen two, nitrogen three and carbon four. In a molecule, every hand is held by another atom's hand. Oxygen has two hands, so it holds two hydrogens. That makes H2O. Sometimes two atoms hold each other with two hands, or even three. This is called a double or triple bond.
- e.g. Methane, the main gas in biogas and in piped cooking gas: one carbon holding four hydrogens, CH4.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide: one carbon holds each oxygen with two hands, so all four of its hands are used.
- e.g. Nitrogen gas, most of the air: two nitrogen atoms holding each other with all three hands.
### Two kinds of holding: inside a molecule and between molecules · *defined*
**Definition:** Bonds hold the atoms inside a molecule together very strongly. Molecules also pull on each other, but much more weakly.

When ice melts, or water boils, the weak pulls between molecules give way. The molecules move further apart, but each one stays whole H2O. That is why steam drips back from a lid as water. Splitting the molecule itself means breaking the strong bonds inside it. It takes several times more energy than boiling the same water away. Your battery gave that energy, a little at a time.
- e.g. Ice, water and steam are all H2O molecules. Only the spacing and movement changes.
- e.g. Sugar dissolving in water: the sugar molecules move apart among the water molecules, but each sugar molecule stays whole.
### Breaking and making bonds makes new substances · *defined*
**Definition:** In a chemical change, atoms let go of some partners and make bonds with new ones. The atoms are all still there, but they now form new substances.

No atom is made or destroyed in a chemical change. The same atoms are just joined in a new way. Breaking bonds takes in energy. Making new bonds gives energy out. A gas flame is hot because the gas's atoms make new, strong bonds with oxygen from the air. The new molecules are carbon dioxide and water.
- e.g. Sugar heated on a spoon breaks down and forms brown caramel. Some of its hydrogen and oxygen atoms leave as steam.
- e.g. An iron gate rusts: iron atoms bond with oxygen, with help from water, and make brown rust.
- e.g. A dosa turns golden brown on the tava, as molecules in the batter break and join in new ways.
### Same atoms, different bonds: pencil lead and diamond · *defined*
**Definition:** Graphite and diamond are both made only of carbon atoms. They are different because their atoms are bonded in different patterns.

In diamond, every carbon atom holds four others in every direction, so the whole crystal is one strong frame. Diamond is the hardest natural material. In graphite, each carbon atom holds three others in a flat sheet. The sheets lie on top of each other and hold on only weakly. So they slide apart easily, and leave a grey mark on paper.
- e.g. A pencil leaves a mark because sheets of graphite slide off onto the paper.
- e.g. Diamond-tipped tools cut glass and stone, because no natural material is harder.
### Why some water carries electricity · *defined*
**Definition:** Water carries the battery's electricity well only when it has charged particles dissolved in it.

Cooking soda and salt are not made of molecules. They are made of particles with electric charges, held together by their opposite charges. In water, these charged particles move apart and can drift towards the battery's wires. That carries the electricity across. Sugar dissolves as whole molecules with no charge. So sugar water carries almost no electricity, and very little water splits.
- e.g. Salt water and soda water make many bubbles in the water-splitting test.
- e.g. Pure water, and sugar water, make very few bubbles with a 9-volt battery.
### What is a bond, really? · *debated*
We draw bonds as sticks. But no atom has a stick. Electrons spread round atoms as a cloud, not as little dots in a line. Since 2009, special microscopes have made pictures of single molecules where the bonds show up as lines. People still disagree about what those lines are.
- e.g. In 1955, the chemist Charles Coulson wrote that no one has ever seen a bond, and that it is an idea people made up.
- e.g. In 2009, scientists at IBM in Zurich made a picture of one pentacene molecule, and its bonds showed up as lines.
- **Many chemists:** A bond is real. You can measure its length and how much energy it takes to break. You can now see it in pictures.
- **Charles Coulson and some physicists:** Only atoms and electrons are real. A bond is a useful way of describing where the electrons gather. It is not a separate thing.

## Mostly agreed
- Water is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen, not an element. Each molecule is H2O. *How we know:* Water can be split by electricity into hydrogen and oxygen, with twice as much hydrogen as oxygen. Burning hydrogen in oxygen makes water again.
- Chemical bonds are made by electrons, held by the electric force. *How we know:* Bonds can be measured and calculated from the way electrons and atomic centres attract. The calculations match experiments.
- Melting, boiling and dissolving move molecules apart without breaking the bonds inside them. *How we know:* Steam turns back into water, and dissolved sugar can be dried back into sugar. The molecules were whole all along.
- In a chemical change, no atoms are made or lost. They only change partners. *How we know:* Antoine Lavoisier and later scientists weighed everything before and after reactions in closed containers. The total weight stays the same.
- Each kind of atom usually makes a set number of bonds: hydrogen 1, oxygen 2, nitrogen 3, carbon 4. *How we know:* Thousands of molecules have been measured, and almost all of them follow these numbers.

## How we found out
**Henry Cavendish, an English scientist; Antoine and Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier in Paris; and William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle in London, 1766 and 1781 (Cavendish), the 1780s (the Lavoisiers), and 1800 (Nicholson and Carlisle).** For over two thousand years, many thinkers in Greece and elsewhere said water was an element, one of the basic stuffs that could not be split. In 1766, the English scientist Henry Cavendish made a gas that burned easily, which he called inflammable air. In 1781 he burned it in air, and found that dew formed: the only thing made was water. In France, Antoine Lavoisier repeated this. His wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, kept the notes of their experiments and drew the equipment for his book. Lavoisier named the gas hydrogen, meaning 'water maker'. He said water was not an element, but hydrogen joined to oxygen. Some scientists still doubted it. Then in 1800, soon after Alessandro Volta described the first battery, William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle in London put two wires from a battery into water. Bubbles of gas rose from the wires, and the water was not boiling. Later tests showed one gas was hydrogen and the other oxygen, with twice as much hydrogen. Water had been split, so it could not be an element.  
*What people believed before:* People believed water was an element that could not be split into anything else. Cavendish and Lavoisier made water by burning hydrogen in air, and Nicholson and Carlisle split water into two gases with a battery.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Water is an element, one of the basic stuffs everything is made of.** (Greek and Indian thinkers for over 2,000 years, and most scientists until the 1780s; *rejected*)  
  For: Water never seems to change into anything else. Freeze it, boil it or mix it, and you get water back.  
  Against: In 1800, Nicholson and Carlisle split water into hydrogen and oxygen with a battery. You can do it at home.
- **Boiling water splits it into hydrogen and oxygen.** (A common guess, because both are gases; *rejected*)  
  For: Water turns into a gas when it boils.  
  Against: Steam caught on a cold lid turns back into water. If it were hydrogen and oxygen, it would not.
- **Atoms are held together by tiny hooks.** (Some ancient Greek and Roman thinkers, such as the poet Lucretius; *rejected*)  
  For: Things stick together, so something must catch hold.  
  Against: Atoms have no hooks. Bonds are made by electrons, held by the electric force.
- **A chemical bond is a real thing, as real as an atom.** (Many chemists, while some physicists and philosophers disagree; *debated*)  
  For: Bonds have lengths and strengths that can be measured. They show up as lines in pictures from special microscopes.  
  Against: What is really there is atoms and their electrons. A bond is our name for where the electrons gather.
- **When something burns, its atoms are destroyed.** (A common idea, because ash weighs less than the wood; *rejected*)  
  For: A log turns into a small heap of ash.  
  Against: Most of the wood's atoms go into the air as carbon dioxide and water. Catch the gases and weigh them, and nothing is lost.

## Roots
We have learned that atoms hold on to each other with bonds, and that a battery can split water. Now we ask what that tells us about the world and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Steam from hot water dripped back off your plate as water. Your battery split cool water into two gases instead. So the atoms inside water hold on far more tightly than water holds to water. That is why water stays water through ice, rain and steam.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of water as one simple stuff, and of heat as the strongest way to change things. People called water an element for over two thousand years, because heat never broke it.  
*Evidence:* In the water-splitting test, steam caught on a cool plate turned back into water. A 9-volt battery made hydrogen and oxygen bubbles in cool water, twice as much on the black wire side.

- *who am i:* More than half of your body's weight is water. Every one of those water molecules is three atoms holding on tight.
- *what is life:* Living things build their bodies by making and breaking bonds all day. A plant splits water with sunlight, and you put the atoms back together when you breathe.
- *what is this world:* Only 118 kinds of atom make up everything you see. The world's variety comes from how they hold on to each other.

Atoms of only a few kinds join, by their bonds, into countless different molecules. Every bond is made by the same electric force. The hydrogen in water and the hydrogen in sugar are the same kind of atom, held in different company. So the many substances around us are a few kinds of atom, held together by one force in different ways.

- **Prashastapada, Padarthadharmasangraha (about the 6th century CE), on the Vaisheshika teaching of Kanada:** Two paramanu, the smallest particles, join to make a pair. Three pairs join to make the smallest thing the eye can see, a speck floating in a sunbeam. (The Vaisheshika thinkers said visible things are built from tiny particles joining in small groups. That is close to what molecules are, though they did not know about electrons.)
- **Empedocles, fragment 17 (Diels and Kranz numbering):** Love brings the elements together into one, and Strife pulls them apart again, over and over. (Empedocles thought water was an element, which turned out to be wrong. But his picture of one force joining and another parting fits what you saw: bonds hold atoms together, and energy from a battery or a flame pulls them apart.)
- Science (settled): Every chemical bond is made by electrons attracted by two atoms' centres at once. This is the electric force, the same force that makes a balloon stick to a wall.
- Science (settled): Since 2009, special microscopes have made pictures of single molecules in which the bonds show up as lines.
- Other view, Charles Coulson, a British chemist, and many physicists: A bond is not a thing in itself. There are only atoms and electrons, and 'bond' is our word for where the electrons gather. Calling it holding hands is a helpful picture, not a fact.
- Practice, Hold on, let go (3 min): Let's sit in a circle and hold hands. Close your eyes. Feel the grip of the hands on each side of you. Now let go, and rest your hands on your knees. Breathe in slowly. Some oxygen atoms you breathe in will join with hydrogen inside you to make water. Breathe out. Now hold hands again.
- Ask: If a bond is made of electrons that both atoms share, which atom do those electrons belong to?
- Ask: Water is two gases holding on. Is water a new thing, or only hydrogen and oxygen together?
- Ask: When your body makes water from the oxygen you breathe, is that water part of you or part of the world?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Water's atoms hold on so tightly that boiling cannot pull them apart. What would the world be like if bonds were as weak as the pulls between molecules? (hint: Think of what would happen to your body, your food and the rocks on a hot day.)
- *deep:* Diamond and pencil lead are both pure carbon. Is a diamond a different thing from a pencil, or the same thing arranged differently? (hint: What would you need to change to turn one into the other?)
- *deep:* Nobody has ever seen a bond, only atoms close together. Is a bond a thing, or a name for how atoms behave? (hint: Is a handshake a thing, or two hands doing something?)
- *big:* The oxygen atoms you breathe out in water vapour were in the air a moment ago. Where does the air end and you begin? (hint: Your body uses oxygen from air to get energy from food, and makes water and carbon dioxide.)
- *big:* Every atom in you was once part of other things, held by other bonds. What does it mean for an atom to belong to you? (hint: Think of the rice you ate yesterday. Where are its atoms now?)
- *big:* Atoms of only about 25 elements make up a human body. How can so few kinds make so many different things? (hint: Think of how 26 letters make every word in English.)

## Story: Bubbles That Were Not Steam
*A made-up story. The water-splitting test it describes is the one in this unit, and it works as told.*

It was the morning of Pongal in Madurai. On the balcony of the flat, Paati had tied fresh turmeric leaves round the neck of the new pot. The milk in it was rising. Kavya, who was ten, held her little brother Arjun back from the stove.

The milk frothed over the side. 'Pongalo pongal!' everyone shouted. Paati added the rice and covered the pot with a steel lid.

Later, when Paati lifted the lid, Arjun pointed. 'Look, it is raining under the lid.' Big drops hung all over it. One dripped onto his hand. It was warm, and it was only water.

'The water in the pot went up as steam,' said Paati. 'The cold lid turned it back into water.'

Arjun thought about this while they ate. 'So when water boils, it is still water,' he said. 'And when it freezes, it is still water. Water is always water. You can never break it.'

'Are you sure?' said Kavya. She was smiling in a way that made Arjun suspicious.

'Sure,' he said. 'I have seen water go up, and come down, and turn into ice. It always comes back.'

After lunch, Kavya came out of her room with a small box. In it were two pencils, a nine-volt battery with a clip on two wires, and a strip of cardboard. Their aunt, Revathi Chithi, who mends phones and radios, had shown her this last summer.

Kavya sharpened both pencils at both ends. She pushed them through two holes in the cardboard and laid it across a glass of water. She stirred in a spoon of cooking soda. Then she twisted one wire round the top of each pencil.

'Now watch the bottom of the pencils,' she said.

At first, nothing. Then a thin line of bubbles began to rise from one pencil tip. A few seconds later, the other tip began to bubble too.

'It is boiling!' said Arjun.

'Touch the glass,' said Kavya.

He did. It was cool. The water was not even warm.

'Then what are the bubbles?' he asked.

'Gases that come out of the water,' said Kavya. 'Look closely. Which pencil makes more?'

Arjun put his nose almost on the glass. 'This one. The one with the black wire.'

Kavya swapped the wires round. Now the other pencil made more bubbles. 'So it is not the pencil,' Arjun said slowly. 'It is the black wire. The minus side.'

'Chithi told me the black side makes hydrogen,' said Kavya. 'The red side makes oxygen. Water is made of those two. Every tiny bit of water is one oxygen and two hydrogens, holding on tight.'

'Two hydrogens,' said Arjun. 'That is why there are more bubbles on that side!'

Kavya had not thought of that. She looked at the bubbles again. There were about twice as many on the black side. 'I think you are right,' she said.

Paati had come to the door. 'So the steam on my lid was not broken water?'

'No, Paati,' said Arjun, before Kavya could answer. 'Boiling only makes the water bits fly apart from each other. The bits stay whole, so they come back as water. The battery breaks the bits themselves.'

Paati laughed. 'Then all those years I called water the simplest thing in the kitchen, it was two gases holding hands.'

That evening, Arjun drew a picture for Revathi Chithi. It showed a glass, two pencils and many bubbles. Under it he wrote, in large letters: WATER CAN BE BROKEN. BUT NOT BY BOILING.

**For the parent:** For the parent: boiling only separates water molecules from each other, so steam turns back into water. The battery breaks the bonds inside each molecule, making two new gases, hydrogen and oxygen. There is twice as much hydrogen because each water molecule has two hydrogen atoms.

- Ask: Why did the drops under the lid turn out to be water, and not something new?
- Ask: How did Arjun know the bubbles were not from boiling?
- Ask: Why did Kavya swap the wires, and what did it show?
- Ask: Why were there more bubbles on one side?

## Experiments
### e1 · Split Water with a Pencil and a Battery (35 min)
- **question:** Can a battery split water into something that is not water? Which pencil makes more gas, and does it depend on the pencil or on the battery?
- **predict:** Guess first. When the battery is joined, what will you see at the pencil tips? Will both tips do the same thing? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Keep the same glass, water, soda and pencils all through. Only swap which wire goes to which pencil. If the busier side moves with the black wire, the battery decides it, not the pencil.
- **safety:** Use only a 9-volt battery, never a wall plug or a charger. Do not let the two clip wires touch each other, or the battery gets hot. Keep flames away, because a little hydrogen is made. Do not drink the water. Pour it down the sink afterwards. A grown-up handles the hot water.
- **record:** Fill in the table for both tests. Draw the glass, the pencils and the bubbles, and label which wire went where.
- **results table:** Test | Pencil A: few bubbles, some, or many | Pencil B: few bubbles, some, or many | Which wire was on the busier pencil?
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour hot water from a kettle or pan into a cup. 2. Hold a cool steel plate over it for a minute. 3. Let your child touch the drops underneath.  
  *Parent says:* First let's see what heat does to water. What are these drops made of?  
  *Child mode:* Look under the plate. Touch a drop. What is it?
  - Ask: Did boiling change the water into something new? (good: No, the steam became water again.; The drops are just water.)
  - Note: Hold the plate yourself, a little above the cup, so steam cannot scald anyone. The drops are water. Heat turned water to steam, and the cool plate turned it back.

  *Parent does:* 1. Sharpen both pencils at both ends. 2. Poke two holes in the cardboard strip, about 2 cm apart. 3. Push a pencil through each hole. 4. Fill the glass and stir in 1 teaspoon of cooking soda. 5. Lay the strip across the glass, so the tips are in the water.

### e2 · Wax or Sugar: which comes back the same after heating? (25 min)

- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw each spoon before heating and after cooling.

  *Child mode:* Look at the sugar and the wax. Feel them. Smell them. Guess what will happen, and say why.

  - Note: The wax melts into a clear liquid within a minute. Do not let it get hot enough to smoke. Put the spoon on the plate to cool.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**

  - Note: The wax is solid white wax again. The sugar is now a hard, brown, glassy lump that tastes slightly bitter. Soak the sugar spoon in hot water to clean it.
  - Check: The wax came back as wax, but the sugar became brown caramel. Why? → **Heat only moved the wax molecules apart, but it broke the sugar's bonds and made new ones**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: wax melted and came back as wax, so its molecules stayed whole. Sugar turned brown, smelled different, gave off steam and tasted different, so its atoms made new bonds and new substances.

## Worksheets
### Atoms Holding On (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Anjali, a girl in Class 3 in Hyderabad, builds a model of a water molecule. Which model is right?  
   Options: One oxygen and one hydrogen / One oxygen and two hydrogens / Two oxygens and one hydrogen / Three hydrogens  
   **Answer:** One oxygen and two hydrogens. *Why:* Water is H2O: one oxygen with two hydrogens held on to it. A strong answer explains that oxygen has two hands.
2. (fill) A group of atoms held together by bonds is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['molecule']. *Why:* A molecule is the smallest piece of a substance like water. Water, oxygen gas and sugar are all made of molecules.
3. (truefalse) When water boils, its molecules break into hydrogen and oxygen.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Boiling moves whole water molecules apart, as steam. That is why steam drips back from a lid as water. Only something like a battery breaks the bonds inside.
4. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Atom → the smallest piece of an element; Molecule → a group of atoms held together; Bond → the way two atoms hold on to each other; Electron → a tiny particle on the outside of an atom; Element → a substance with only one kind of atom. *Why:* These words come up all through the unit. A strong answer could add an example of each.
5. (sort) Sort each change. Does it make new stuff, or is it the same stuff moved around?  
   **Answer:** ice melting → Same stuff; an agarbatti burning → New stuff; clothes drying → Same stuff; a balcony grill rusting → New stuff; salt dissolving in water → Same stuff; milk setting into curd → New stuff; butter softening in the sun → Same stuff; onions browning in oil → New stuff. *Why:* Melting, boiling, drying and dissolving only move molecules. Burning, rusting, browning and setting curd make new bonds. A strong answer uses the test: can you get it back just by cooling or drying?
6. (write) When rice is cooked, the lid is covered in drops. Explain where the drops came from, and why they are still water.  
   **Answer:** Water in the pot boiled into steam. The steam touched the cool lid and turned back into drops. Boiling only moved the water molecules apart, so each one was still water.. *Why:* A strong answer says the molecules stayed whole, so the drops are water, not something new.
7. (draw) Draw a water molecule. Use a big circle for oxygen and small circles for hydrogen. Draw a line for each bond. Label each atom.  
   **Answer:** A big circle labelled O, with two small circles labelled H, each joined to the O by one line.. *Why:* Oxygen makes two bonds and each hydrogen makes one. A strong drawing has exactly two lines.
8. (mcq) How many bonds does a carbon atom usually make?  
   Options: 1 / 2 / 3 / 4  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* Carbon usually makes four bonds. That is why it can build so many different molecules, including the ones in food and in your body.
9. (odd) Three of these changes make new stuff. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Paper burning / An iron nail rusting / Ghee melting / A dosa browning  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Ghee melting is the odd one out. It only moves the ghee's molecules apart, and cooling makes it solid again. The other three make new substances.
10. (order) Put the steps of the water-splitting test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Sharpen two pencils at both ends', 'Stand them in soda water, not touching', "Join the battery's wires to the pencils", 'Watch which pencil makes more bubbles']. *Why:* First get the pencils ready, then put them in the water, then join the battery, then watch.
11. (label) Here is a picture of the water-splitting setup. Label the battery, the black wire, the pencil that makes hydrogen and the pencil that makes oxygen.  
   **Answer:** ['Battery: the small box', 'Black wire: joined to the busier pencil', 'Hydrogen: the pencil on the black wire', 'Oxygen: the pencil on the red wire']. *Why:* The minus, black side makes hydrogen and the red side makes oxygen. A strong answer also says which side had more bubbles.
12. (truefalse) The oxygen in the air is made of pairs of oxygen atoms, held together by bonds.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each oxygen molecule is two oxygen atoms holding each other with two bonds. It is written O2.
13. (write) In your water-splitting test, which pencil made more bubbles? What do you think those bubbles were?  
   **Answer:** The pencil on the black wire made more bubbles. They were hydrogen gas. The other pencil made oxygen.. *Why:* A strong answer uses what the child really saw, and links the extra bubbles to the two hydrogens in each water molecule.
14. (mcq) Which of these is the best picture of what holds atoms together?  
   Options: Tiny strings / Tiny hooks / Electrons that both atoms attract / Air pushing them together  
   **Answer:** Electrons that both atoms attract. *Why:* Bonds are made by electrons, which both atoms attract. There are no strings, hooks or glue. A strong answer names the electrons.
15. (fill) The tiny particles on the outside of an atom, which make bonds, are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['electrons']. *Why:* Electrons have a negative charge. They are attracted by the positive centres of atoms, and that holds atoms together.
16. (perspective) Two cousins in Kochi watch a grown-up melt wax and sugar on spoons.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the evidence: after cooling, the wax was wax again, but the sugar was brown, glassy and tasted different, and gave off steam. So the sugar's atoms made new bonds. It also gives Farhan his due: the atoms of the sugar are all still there, in the caramel and in the steam.. *Why:* Both see part of it. The substance sugar is gone, but its atoms are not. A strong answer says both.
### Bonds, Breaking and Making (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Why is water H2O, and not HO?  
   Options: Hydrogen is too small / Oxygen makes two bonds and hydrogen makes one / There is more hydrogen in the world / Water needs to be wet  
   **Answer:** Oxygen makes two bonds and hydrogen makes one. *Why:* Oxygen has two hands. With only one hydrogen, one of oxygen's hands would be empty. Two hydrogens fill both.
2. (write) Methane is the main gas in biogas. It has one carbon atom and some hydrogen atoms. Carbon has four hands and hydrogen has one. How many hydrogens does methane have? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Four. Each of carbon's four hands holds one hydrogen, so methane is CH4.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the hand numbers to reason, not just recalls the formula.
3. (draw) Draw carbon dioxide, CO2. Carbon is in the middle and an oxygen is on each side. Draw a line for every bond, so every atom uses all its hands.  
   **Answer:** O, C, O in a row, with two lines between the carbon and each oxygen.. *Why:* Carbon has four hands and each oxygen has two. So each oxygen holds the carbon with both its hands. A strong drawing shows two double bonds.
4. (truefalse) Diamond and pencil lead are both made only of carbon atoms.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. They differ because their carbon atoms are bonded in different patterns: a 3D frame in diamond, flat sheets in graphite.
5. (write) A pencil leaves a mark on paper, but a diamond can cut glass. Both are carbon. Explain why they behave so differently.  
   **Answer:** In pencil lead, carbon atoms are held in flat sheets that hold each other weakly, so the sheets slide off onto paper. In diamond every carbon holds four others in all directions, so it is very hard.. *Why:* A strong answer talks about the pattern of bonds, not about different atoms.
6. (mcq) Which takes more energy: boiling a glass of water away, or splitting the same water into hydrogen and oxygen?  
   Options: Boiling it / Splitting it / They take the same / Neither takes any energy  
   **Answer:** Splitting it. *Why:* Splitting it takes several times more energy, because it breaks the strong bonds inside each molecule. Boiling only separates whole molecules, which hold each other weakly.
7. (sort) Sort each change: does it give out heat, or does it need heat put in?  
   **Answer:** a candle burning → Gives out heat; rice cooking → Needs heat put in; a gas stove flame → Gives out heat; sugar turning to caramel → Needs heat put in; a wood fire → Gives out heat; ice melting → Needs heat put in. *Why:* Burning makes new strong bonds with oxygen, which gives out heat. Cooking and melting need heat put in, to break bonds or move molecules apart. A strong answer explains one of each.
8. (fill) In the water-splitting test, the gas that came off the pencil on the black wire was ____.  
   **Answer:** ['hydrogen']. *Why:* The black, minus side makes hydrogen. There was about twice as much of it, because each water molecule has two hydrogen atoms.
9. (match) Match each molecule to the atoms in it.  
   **Answer:** Water, H2O → 2 hydrogen, 1 oxygen; Oxygen gas, O2 → 2 oxygen; Carbon dioxide, CO2 → 1 carbon, 2 oxygen; Methane, CH4 → 1 carbon, 4 hydrogen; Ammonia, NH3 → 1 nitrogen, 3 hydrogen. *Why:* The letters say which atoms, and the small numbers say how many. A strong answer could check each one with the hand numbers.
10. (odd) Three of these are made of only one kind of atom. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: O2, oxygen gas / H2, hydrogen gas / N2, nitrogen gas / H2O, water  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Water has two kinds of atom, hydrogen and oxygen, so it is a compound. O2, H2 and N2 each have only one kind, so they are elements.
11. (perspective) Two friends in Mysuru look at an old rusty nail.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that the iron atoms are all still there, now bonded to oxygen, but the shiny metal iron is gone. So both are partly right, and the argument is about whether 'iron' means the atoms or the metal.. *Why:* This is a real question about what we mean by a substance. A strong answer separates atoms from the substance they make.
12. (truefalse) When atoms make new bonds in a chemical change, some atoms get used up and disappear.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. No atoms disappear. They only change partners. Weigh everything in a closed jar before and after, and the weight stays the same.
13. (mcq) When a grown-up heated sugar on a spoon, a little steam came off and brown caramel was left. Where did the sugar's atoms go?  
   Options: Some into the steam, the rest into the caramel / They were all burnt away / They all stayed as sugar / They went into the spoon  
   **Answer:** Some into the steam, the rest into the caramel. *Why:* Some hydrogen and oxygen atoms from the sugar left as water vapour, the steam. The rest are in the caramel. None were destroyed.
14. (write) In the water-splitting test, you swapped the wires. Why was that a good idea? What did it show?  
   **Answer:** It showed whether the pencil or the battery decided which side made more bubbles. The busier side moved with the black wire, so the battery's minus side decides.. *Why:* A strong answer explains that changing only one thing lets you find the cause.
15. (order) Put these in order from smallest to biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['an electron', 'an atom', 'a water molecule', 'a grain of sugar']. *Why:* An electron is part of an atom. Atoms make up a molecule. A grain of sugar holds a huge number of molecules.
16. (draw) Draw a box of ice and a box of steam. Show the water molecules in each. Do the molecules change, or only the spaces between them?  
   **Answer:** Ice: molecules close together in a neat pattern. Steam: the same H2O molecules far apart. Each molecule stays whole.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps every molecule as one O with two H's in both boxes.
### Puzzles About Bonds (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (perspective) In 1955, a British chemist called Charles Coulson wrote that nobody has ever seen a bond. In 2009, scientists made a picture of one molecule where the bonds showed up as lines.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer weighs both: bonds can be measured and now pictured, but what the pictures show is where electrons gather. It says what would change your mind.. *Why:* This is a real argument among chemists. Both sides have evidence.
2. (draw) Hydrogen peroxide, H2O2, is used to clean cuts. It has two hydrogens and two oxygens. Draw it so every atom uses all its hands, and no hydrogen holds two partners.  
   **Answer:** H, O, O, H in a chain: each hydrogen holds one oxygen, and the two oxygens hold each other.. *Why:* Each oxygen uses one hand for a hydrogen and one for the other oxygen. A strong drawing has three lines.
3. (write) Most of the air is nitrogen gas, N2. Nitrogen has three hands. How can two nitrogen atoms hold all their hands with only each other?  
   **Answer:** They hold each other with all three hands at once. This is a triple bond.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that two atoms can share more than one bond. This triple bond is very strong, which is why nitrogen gas changes so rarely.
4. (mcq) Vikram, a boy in Class 4, draws a molecule as H, H, O in a row: the middle hydrogen holds the other hydrogen and the oxygen. What is wrong with it?  
   Options: Nothing is wrong / The middle hydrogen has two partners, but hydrogen makes only one bond / Oxygen should be at the end / There are too many atoms  
   **Answer:** The middle hydrogen has two partners, but hydrogen makes only one bond. *Why:* The middle hydrogen is holding two partners, but hydrogen has only one hand. In real water, both hydrogens hold the oxygen.
5. (write) A sugar molecule has 12 carbon, 22 hydrogen and 11 oxygen atoms. When sugar was heated to caramel, steam came off. Where could that water have come from?  
   **Answer:** From the sugar itself. Some of its hydrogen and oxygen atoms broke away and joined as water molecules, which left as steam.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that water can be made from atoms that were part of another molecule.
6. (truefalse) A baked cake could be turned back into flour, sugar and eggs just by cooling it in the fridge.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Baking makes new bonds and new substances. Cooling only slows molecules down. It cannot undo a chemical change.
7. (sort) Sort these: an element (one kind of atom) or a compound (more than one kind)?  
   **Answer:** oxygen gas, O2 → Element; water, H2O → Compound; diamond → Element; carbon dioxide, CO2 → Compound; nitrogen gas, N2 → Element; methane, CH4 → Compound; hydrogen gas, H2 → Element; sugar → Compound. *Why:* Oxygen, hydrogen and nitrogen gas, and diamond, each have one kind of atom. Water, carbon dioxide, methane and sugar have two or more kinds.
8. (mcq) For 2,000 years people thought water was an element. Which finding showed that it is not?  
   Options: Water freezes into ice / Water boils into steam / A battery splits water into hydrogen and oxygen / Water dissolves salt  
   **Answer:** A battery splits water into hydrogen and oxygen. *Why:* A battery split water into two different gases. An element cannot be split into other substances. Freezing and boiling only change how water molecules move.
9. (write) Astronauts on the International Space Station get oxygen to breathe by splitting water with electricity. In your experiment, which pencil gave the gas they breathe? What else do they get?  
   **Answer:** The pencil on the red, plus wire gave oxygen. They also get hydrogen, twice as much, from the black wire side.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the result of the child's own test.
10. (fill) When two atoms share electrons in a bond, they are held together by the ____ force.  
   **Answer:** ['electric']. *Why:* Opposite electric charges attract. The positive centres of both atoms attract the shared electrons, and that holds the atoms together.
11. (odd) Three of these changes make new bonds. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A green banana ripening / An agarbatti burning / Ghee melting in a pan / Curd setting from milk  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Ghee melting is the odd one out. The others all make new substances.
12. (match) Match each change to what happens to the molecules.  
   **Answer:** Water boils → whole molecules move far apart; A battery splits water → bonds inside molecules break; Sugar dissolves → sugar molecules spread among water molecules; A gas flame burns → atoms make new bonds with oxygen. *Why:* A strong answer can say which changes break the bonds inside molecules and which do not.
13. (mcq) Graphite and diamond are both carbon. What makes them so different?  
   Options: The pattern of bonds between the carbon atoms / Diamond has more carbon / Graphite is mixed with water / Diamond is cold  
   **Answer:** The pattern of bonds between the carbon atoms. *Why:* The same atoms bonded in different patterns give different materials. A strong answer names the patterns: sheets and a 3D frame.
14. (write) Many people think heating always breaks things into smaller parts. Plan a test with things from your kitchen to check this idea.  
   **Answer:** Heat two things gently, such as ice and sugar, and cool them again. Ice comes back as water, nothing new. Sugar breaks, but it also joins up in new ways to make caramel. So heating can break bonds, but can also make new ones.. *Why:* A strong plan compares at least two things and says what result would support or go against the idea.
15. (truefalse) The oxygen atoms you breathe in can end up in water that you breathe out.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Your cells use oxygen to get energy from food. The oxygen atoms bond with hydrogen to make water, which leaves your body in your breath, sweat and urine.
16. (draw) Design a test to show that the bubbles in the water-splitting test are not steam. Draw what you would do and what you would expect to see.  
   **Answer:** For example, put a thermometer in the water or feel the glass: it stays cool. Or put an ice cube in the water: the bubbles keep coming.. *Why:* A strong design uses a way to check the temperature, since steam needs boiling water.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What holds two atoms together in a bond?  
   Options: Their electrons, attracted by both atoms at once / Tiny hooks on the atoms / Glue made inside the atom / Air pressing on them  
   **Answer:** Their electrons, attracted by both atoms at once. *Why:* A bond is made by electrons that are attracted by the centres of both atoms. That is the electric force. Hooks and glue are old pictures. Atoms have neither.
2. (mcq) A water molecule is written H2O. What does that mean?  
   Options: 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom / 2 oxygen atoms and 1 hydrogen atom / Hot water / 2 drops of water  
   **Answer:** 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. *Why:* H stands for hydrogen and O for oxygen. The small 2 says there are two hydrogen atoms. So one water molecule is two hydrogens and one oxygen.
3. (mcq) Nandini, a girl in Class 3 in Chennai, puts two pencils in soda water and joins a battery. Bubbles rise, but the water stays cool. What are the bubbles?  
   Options: Hydrogen and oxygen gas, split from water / Steam / Air from the pencils / Soda gas only  
   **Answer:** Hydrogen and oxygen gas, split from water. *Why:* The water is not hot, so it is not steam. The battery splits water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen. Air from the pencils would stop after a moment.
4. (mcq) In the water-splitting test, the pencil on the black wire makes about twice as many bubbles. Why?  
   Options: Each water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and only one oxygen / That pencil is longer / Black wires are stronger / Oxygen bubbles are invisible  
   **Answer:** Each water molecule has two hydrogen atoms and only one oxygen. *Why:* The black, minus side makes hydrogen. Each water molecule has two hydrogens for every oxygen. So there is about twice as much hydrogen gas.
5. (mcq) Steam from a pot drips back off the lid as water. What does this show?  
   Options: Boiling only moves water molecules apart. Each one stays whole. / Boiling splits water into hydrogen and oxygen / The lid makes water / Steam is a new substance  
   **Answer:** Boiling only moves water molecules apart. Each one stays whole.. *Why:* If boiling had split water, the gases would not drip back as water. The water molecules only moved apart, and came close again on the cool lid.
6. (mcq) Carbon usually makes 4 bonds, and hydrogen makes 1. How many hydrogens can one carbon hold?  
   Options: 4 / 1 / 2 / 8  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* Each of carbon's four hands holds one hydrogen. That gives CH4, methane, the main gas in biogas.
7. (mcq) Which of these is a chemical change, where new stuff is made?  
   Options: A dosa turning golden on the tava / Ghee melting in a pan / Ice melting in a glass / Sugar dissolving in milk  
   **Answer:** A dosa turning golden on the tava. *Why:* In the dosa, molecules break and join in new ways, giving new colour and taste. The others only move molecules apart, and could be undone.
8. (mcq) A grown-up melts candle wax and sugar on spoons. When they cool, which one is the same as before?  
   Options: The wax / The sugar / Both / Neither  
   **Answer:** The wax. *Why:* Wax melts and sets again as wax, because its molecules stayed whole. The sugar turned into brown caramel, a new substance.
9. (mcq) Diamond and pencil lead are both pure carbon. Why is pencil lead soft?  
   Options: Its carbon atoms are in flat sheets that slide over each other / It has less carbon / It is mixed with water / Its atoms are bigger  
   **Answer:** Its carbon atoms are in flat sheets that slide over each other. *Why:* Graphite's sheets hold each other only weakly, so they slide off onto paper. In diamond every carbon holds four others in all directions.
10. (mcq) Oxygen has two hands and hydrogen has one. Which molecule is not possible?  
   Options: One hydrogen holding two oxygens with its one hand / H2O / Two hydrogens holding each other, H2 / Two oxygens holding each other with two hands each  
   **Answer:** One hydrogen holding two oxygens with its one hand. *Why:* Hydrogen has only one hand, so it cannot hold two partners. The others use every hand exactly once.
11. (mcq) When something burns, where do most of its atoms go?  
   Options: Into the air, as new gases like carbon dioxide and water / They are destroyed / They all stay in the ash / They turn into heat  
   **Answer:** Into the air, as new gases like carbon dioxide and water. *Why:* Atoms are never destroyed in burning. They bond with oxygen and leave as gases. The ash is only a small part.
12. (mcq) Sneha, a girl in Class 4 in Pune, dissolves sugar in one glass and cooking soda in another. With the battery, the soda water bubbles a lot and the sugar water hardly at all. Why?  
   Options: Soda splits into charged particles that carry electricity, but sugar molecules have no charge / Sugar is heavier / Sugar stops the pencils working for ever / Soda water is warmer  
   **Answer:** Soda splits into charged particles that carry electricity, but sugar molecules have no charge. *Why:* Charged particles carry the electricity across the water. Sugar dissolves as whole molecules with no charge, so very little electricity flows and few bubbles form.

## Applications
- Making medicines: chemists design molecules atom by atom.
- Cooking: heat breaks and makes bonds, which gives food new tastes and colours.
- Splitting water with electricity makes hydrogen fuel, and oxygen for space stations.
- Cutting tools for stone and glass use diamond, the hardest natural material.
- Pencils use soft graphite, whose sheets slide off onto paper.
- Fertilisers are made from the nitrogen in air by breaking its strong triple bond.
- Biogas plants turn cow dung and food waste into methane for cooking.
- Rust-proof paint keeps oxygen from bonding with the iron in gates and grills.

## Spotted around us
- Drops of water under the lid of a pressure cooker or rice pot.
- Brown rust on a balcony grill or an old bicycle.
- A small diamond in a mookuthi, the nose ring many women wear.
- The grey lead of a pencil, leaving its mark on paper.
- The blue flame of a gas stove, where gas bonds with oxygen.
- A golden dosa on a tava, and the smell of onions browning.
- Curd setting overnight in a steel bowl.

## Open questions
- **What is a bond, really?** Bonds can be measured and pictured, but chemists still argue whether a bond is a separate thing or just a description of where electrons are.
- **How can we split water cheaply, using sunlight?** Plants split water every day with light. Scientists are still trying to build materials that do it as well and as cheaply, to make clean hydrogen fuel.
- **Can we predict exactly how any new molecule will behave before making it?** The rules of electrons are known, but the sums for big molecules are so huge that even fast computers must use shortcuts.
- **How did the first molecules of life join up on the early Earth?** Scientists have made some building blocks of life in the lab, but nobody knows how they first joined into living cells.
