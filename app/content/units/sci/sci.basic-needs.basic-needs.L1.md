# What we need to live: air, water, food, clothes and a home
`sci.basic-needs.basic-needs.L1`

**Summary.** You take a breath every few seconds, and you never stop. Every person needs air, water and food again and again, because the body keeps using them up. Clothes and a home keep us safe from sun, rain, cold and wind. These five are our basic needs. Your child builds a little wind shelter from a box and finds out which way its open side must face to keep a tissue still in the fan's wind.

**Intro.** *Think of a baby, a grandmother and a crow at the window. What does each one need every single day?* Say your guesses out loud first. Then count them on your fingers. In this unit we find the five things every person needs: air, water, food, clothes and a home. We find out why we need some of them every few seconds and some only a few times a day. And we build a little home from a box, put it in front of a fan, and test which way its door should face.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can name the five basic needs: air, water, food, clothes and a home.
1. By the end I can say that my body uses up air, water and food, so I need them again and again.
2. By the end I can say what clothes and a home keep me safe from: sun, rain, cold, wind and insects.
3. By the end I can show with my wind box that a wall facing the wind makes calm air behind it.
4. By the end I can say that crows, ants and plants have needs too, and that humans are animals with the same needs.
5. By the end I can tell how Florence Nightingale counted, and found that clean water and fresh air keep people alive.

## Terms
- **Basic needs**: Basic needs are the things every person must have to live and stay well: air, water, food, clothes and a home. *e.g.* A baby, a grandmother and a cricketer all have the same five basic needs.
- **Shelter**: A shelter is a place that keeps you safe from sun, rain, cold and wind. A home is a shelter. *e.g.* When it rains, a bus stop with a roof is a shelter.
- **Wind**: Wind is air that is moving. We cannot see air, but we can see what wind moves. *e.g.* A fan makes a wind. It flaps the pages of a book on the table.
- **Fresh air**: Fresh air is clean air coming in from outside. Used air goes out through windows and doors. *e.g.* Opening the window in the morning lets fresh air into the bedroom.

## Explanations
### The five basic needs · *defined*
**Definition:** Basic needs are the five things every person must have to live and stay well: air, water, food, clothes and a home.

Three of them we take inside our bodies: air, water and food. Two of them stay outside us and keep us safe: clothes and a home. Every person on Earth needs all five, wherever they live, rich or poor, young or old.
- e.g. A newborn baby drinks milk. Milk gives the baby both food and water.
- e.g. A fisher family at sea near Kochi takes drinking water on the boat. Sea water is too salty to drink.
- e.g. A woman soldier in the snow of Ladakh needs thick clothes and a warm tent.
### We need air, water and food again and again · *defined*
**Definition:** Our body keeps using up air, water and food, so we must take them in again and again.

Your body uses air all the time, so you breathe every few seconds, even in your sleep. Water leaves your body when you wee, sweat and breathe out, so you drink every day. Food gives you energy, so you eat a few times a day.
- e.g. After running, you breathe faster and feel thirsty.
- e.g. On a hot day in Chennai, you sweat and need more water.
- e.g. After a long game of kho-kho, you feel hungry.
### Clothes and a home keep us safe · *defined*
**Definition:** Clothes and a home protect our bodies from sun, rain, cold, wind, insects and dirt.

Your skin can burn in strong sun and get very cold in wind and rain. A cap and loose cotton clothes shade you from the sun, and a sweater keeps you warm. A home has a roof for rain and sun, walls for wind, and windows that let fresh air in.
- e.g. Farmers in the fields of Andhra Pradesh often tie a cotton towel on the head against the sun.
- e.g. In Shillong in winter, people wear woollen shawls and caps.
- e.g. Houses in rainy Kerala often have sloping roofs, so rain runs off.
### A wall makes calm air behind it · *defined*
**Definition:** When wind meets a wall, the moving air cannot go through, so it flows around and over it. Behind the wall, the air is calm.

In the wind box test, the closed wall faced the fan. The air hit that wall and went around the box. Inside, the tissue hung still.
- e.g. On a windy beach, people sit on the side of a boat away from the wind.
- e.g. Farmers plant rows of trees along the edge of a field, called windbreaks, to slow the wind.
- e.g. A pigeon sits on the side of a water tank away from the wind.
### Other living beings have needs too · *defined*
**Definition:** All animals need air, water and food, and many need a shelter. Plants need air, water and light. Humans are animals, so we share these needs.

A crow drinks from a bowl on the balcony on a hot day. A pigeon builds a nest on a ledge, out of the rain. A tulsi or money plant in a pot needs water and light. Crows, pigeons and plants are aware of the world around them, as we are. We are one animal among many, and our needs are much the same as theirs.
- e.g. A cat curls up in a warm, dry corner to sleep.
- e.g. A crow chooses a shady branch at noon in summer.
- e.g. Ants carry their young to a drier place when their nest gets wet.

## Mostly agreed
- Every person needs air, water and food to stay alive, and clothes and a home to stay safe and well. *How we know:* Doctors everywhere agree. People who go without air, water or food become ill quickly and can die.
- Air is needed soonest, then water, then food. *How we know:* A person can live only a few minutes without air, a few days without water, and some weeks without food.
- Wind flows around a wall and leaves calmer air behind it. *How we know:* You saw it in the wind box test. Farmers see it too: the plants just behind a row of trees are sheltered from strong wind.

## How we found out
**Florence Nightingale, a nurse from England, her nurses, and the Sanitary Commission sent from Britain, 1854 to 1855 at Scutari. Her diagrams were published in 1858..** In 1854, Britain was fighting a war near the Black Sea. Florence Nightingale, a nurse from England, went with 38 nurses to a big army hospital at Scutari, in Turkey. The hospital was crowded and dirty. The drains were blocked, the water was not clean, and the wards smelled bad. Many people thought war wounds were the main danger. Florence collected the numbers of who died and of what, and studied them. They showed something else. Far more soldiers were dying of illnesses than of wounds. In March 1855, a team came from Britain to clean the drains and the water supply. After that, far fewer soldiers died. Florence drew coloured pictures of her numbers, so that everyone could see. For the rest of her life she worked for clean water, fresh air and clean homes, in Britain and in India too.  
*What people believed before:* Many people thought war wounds were the main danger. The numbers showed that far more soldiers died of illness, which came with dirty water and blocked drains. Florence, like many people then, also thought illness spread through bad smells. Later scientists found that germs, for example in dirty water, spread it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A home must be shut on every side to keep out the wind.** (A common guess, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Shutting the doors and windows does stop the wind.  
  Against: In the wind box test, the box with its open side away from the fan kept the tissue still. A wall facing the wind is what matters most.
- **Food is the need we must have soonest.** (A common guess among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: We feel hungry many times a day, and food fills us up.  
  Against: A person can live some weeks without food, but only a few minutes without air. We need air soonest of all.

## Roots
We have found that a box kept a tissue flag still when its closed wall faced the fan. Now we ask what that tells us about homes, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** With its open side turned away from the fan, the box kept the tissue still. The moving air hit the back wall and flowed around the box. Behind that wall the air stayed calm. So a home can keep out the wind even with an open door, if a wall faces the wind.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a shelter keeps us safe only when it is shut on every side, like a box with a lid. In the test, one wall on the windy side made calm air behind it. Which way a wall faces matters as much as how many walls there are.  
*Evidence:* In the wind box test, the tissue flag flapped in a tunnel open at both ends, and flapped or wobbled with the box's door facing the fan. With the same box turned round, door away from the fan, the flag hung still.

- *who am i:* You are an animal who needs air, water and food every day, just like a crow or a cat.
- *what is life:* Being alive means taking in air, water and food again and again, and keeping safe while you do.
- *what is this world:* The same air flows over every home on Earth. Every shelter is a calm place the moving air goes around.

The air that moves as wind is the same air we breathe. A wall does not take that air away. It turns the air aside and makes a small calm place behind it. A pigeon finds that calm place behind a water tank, and a family makes it when they build a home.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** We cut out doors and windows to make a room. It is the empty space inside that makes the room useful. (In the wind box, the box gave the tissue flag a calm space. The walls and the door were useful because of the space they made inside.)
- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2:** Food is the source of life. From food beings are born, by food they live, and into food they go back. (We need food again and again, because our body keeps using it to live and grow.)
- **Thirukkural 11:** Rain keeps the world alive, so rain is called the nectar that gives life. (Every person, crow and plant needs water. The water in our taps and wells came first as rain.)
- Science (settled): Wind flows around a wall or a row of trees and leaves calmer air behind it. Farmers use windbreaks for this.
- Science (open): How a pigeon or an ant senses and chooses the safest, calmest spot is still being studied.
- Other view, Many scientists: Some scientists say the pigeon settles where the wind stops pushing on her feathers, the way a ball rolls into a dip, and that this is all it takes. Others say she notices the wind and picks the spot.
- Practice, Be a wall for your breath (2 min): Hold one hand up in front of your face, a little away. Blow gently on it. Can you feel your breath? Now hold your other hand in between, like a wall. Blow again. Can the back hand feel your breath now? Where did your breath go? Sit still and feel the calm air behind your wall.
- Ask: When the wind goes around your home, where does it go next?
- Ask: In the story, the pigeon sat behind the water tank. How do you think she knew where the calm air was?
- Ask: Is a home the walls, or the calm space the walls make?

## Story: The Tent on the Terrace
*Lasya, Kiran, Amma and Ammamma are made up. Visakhapatnam is a real port city on the coast of Andhra Pradesh. Wind really does flow around a wall and leave calmer air behind it.*

Lasya lived in a flat in Visakhapatnam, a big city by the sea. She was seven. Her little brother Kiran was five. On top of their building was a wide, flat roof called the terrace.

One warm evening in the holidays, Ammamma said, "Let us eat our dinner on the terrace tonight. We will make a little home up there."

Lasya and Kiran carried up two plastic chairs and an old bedsheet. Amma carried a steel bottle of water and a box of lemon rice. Ammamma brought her soft cotton shawl.

On the terrace, the wind was blowing hard from the sea. It flapped Lasya's frock and lifted Kiran's hair straight up.

Ammamma put the two chairs back to back, a little apart. She spread the bedsheet over them and tucked it in. Now it was a tent, with three sides closed and one side open for a door.

"Our house!" said Kiran. He crawled in with the box of lemon rice.

But the open door of the tent faced the sea. The wind rushed straight in. The bedsheet puffed up like a balloon. A paper plate flew out of the tent and rolled away across the terrace.

"My plate!" shouted Kiran. Lasya ran after the plate and caught it beside the big water tank.

Behind the water tank, the air was calm. Lasya's frock hung still. A grey pigeon sat there on a ledge, with her feathers smooth and tidy. Just one step away, the wind was still blowing hard.

Lasya stood still and thought. The wind was coming from the sea. The tank stood between her and the sea. So the wind hit the tank and went around the sides.

"Ammamma!" she called. "The pigeon has found a calm place. Look, behind the tank, on the side away from the sea."

Ammamma smiled. "Then what should we do with our tent?"

"Turn it round!" said Lasya. "Put the door away from the sea. Then the closed back will face the wind, like the tank."

Together they turned the chairs round. Now the closed back of the tent faced the sea, and the open door faced the town.

Lasya crawled in. The bedsheet was quiet. The paper plates lay still. Outside, the wind still whistled past, but inside the tent the air was calm.

Kiran looked at the open door. "But the door is open," he said. "Why doesn't the wind come in?"

"The wind hits the back wall," said Lasya. "Then it goes around the sides, past the door. The pigeon knew that before we did."

They sat inside and ate the lemon rice. They drank cool water from the steel bottle. When the sea wind turned cooler, Ammamma wrapped her cotton shawl around Kiran's shoulders.

Kiran counted on his fingers. "We have air to breathe. Water to drink. Rice to eat. A shawl to wear. And a home." He held up five fingers. "Five things!"

"Those are the five things every person needs," said Ammamma. "Air, water, food, clothes and a home. People everywhere need them, every day."

"Does the pigeon need them too?" asked Kiran.

"She needs air, water and food, just like us," said Lasya. "Her feathers are her clothes. And behind the tank is her shelter tonight."

Kiran thought about this. Then he filled the steel lid of the water bottle and set it down near the tank, for the pigeon.

Later, the stars came out over the sea. The pigeon was still on her calm ledge. And the whole family sat in their calm little tent, with the door wide open.

**For the parent:** Lasya finds calm air behind the water tank, on the side away from the sea wind, where a pigeon is sitting. She turns the tent so its closed back faces the wind, and the tent goes calm even with the door open.

- Ask: Why did the wind rush into the tent at first?
- Ask: How did the pigeon help Lasya?
- Ask: What five things did Kiran count on his fingers?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Wind Box: which way should the door face? (20 min)
- **question:** A box has a roof, walls and one open side, like a door. To keep a tissue flag still inside, should the open side face the fan or face away from it? And does a tunnel, open at both ends, keep the wind out?
- **predict:** Guess first. In which shelter will the tissue flag stay still: the tunnel, the box with its door to the fan, or the box with its door away from the fan? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The fan stays at the same speed and the same place, about two big steps from the box. The tissue flag stands in the middle of the box each time. Only the shelter changes.
- **safety:** Keep fingers, hair and the tissue away from the fan blades. A grown-up does the cutting and moves the fan.
- **record:** Draw the box and the fan for each test. Draw the tissue flying for 'flapped' and hanging straight down for 'still'.
- **results table:** Shelter | What the tissue flag did: flapped, wobbled, or hung still
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tear a strip of tissue, two fingers wide and as long as your hand. 2. Tape its top end to the top of the pencil. 3. Stand the pencil upright in the tumbler of rice.  
  *Parent says:* This is our wind flag. It will show us where the wind is. When the air moves, the tissue moves.  
  *Child mode:* Make a tissue flag. It shows you where the wind is.
  - Note: Tissue works best because it is so light. A strip from a thin plastic carry bag also works.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch the fan on to medium. 2. Put the flag on the floor or a low table, two big steps in front of the fan.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the flag. What is moving it? We cannot see the air, but we can see what it does.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the flag. What is moving it?
  - Ask: What is making the flag move? (good: The wind from the fan.; Moving air.)
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts the top and bottom flaps off the carton, so both ends are open. 2. Lay it on its side. 3. Show the tunnel, the cardboard and tape. 4. Ask for guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Soon this tunnel will become a box with one door. Which shelter will keep the flag still? Should the door face the fan, or face away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which shelter will keep the flag still? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Where should the door face? Why? (good: Away from the fan, so the wind can't get in.; Towards the fan, so I can see in.)
  - Note: Write down the guess and the reason. Any reason is a good start. Do not say which is right yet.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the tunnel over the flag, so the fan blows straight through one end and out of the other. 2. Watch the flag for a slow count of ten.  
  *Parent says:* The flag has a roof and two walls now. Watch it. Does it flap, wobble or hang still?  
  *Child mode:* Your flag has a roof now. Does it still flap?
  - Ask: Did the tunnel keep the wind out? (good: No, the wind blew straight through.; The flag still flapped.)
- **s5 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the carton off. 2. Tape the cardboard over one end, so the box has one open door. 3. Put it back over the flag with the door facing the fan. 4. Watch for a slow count of ten.  
  *Parent says:* Now the box has one door, and the door faces the fan. Watch the flag.  
  *Child mode:* Now the door faces the fan. Watch the flag.
  - Ask: What did the flag do? (good: It flapped.; It wobbled, because wind came in through the door.)
  - Note: The flag may flap hard or only wobble, depending on the size of the box. Either is fine. Compare it with the next test.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the box and turn it round, so the closed end faces the fan. 2. Put it back over the flag in the same place. 3. Watch for a slow count of ten.  
  *Parent says:* Now the closed wall faces the fan, and the door faces away. Watch the flag. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Now the wall faces the fan. What does the flag do?
  - Ask: What did the flag do now? (good: It hung still.; It hardly moved.)
  - Note: The flag should hang still or only shiver a little. If it still flaps, check that the fan blows at the closed end, and move the flag deeper inside the box.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you found out. Which way should a door face on a windy day? What did the flag show you? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Which way should the door face? What did your flag show you?
  - Note: A strong answer: I think the door should face away from the wind. The flag hung still when the wall faced the fan. The wind hit the wall and went around.
  - Check: In which test did the tissue flag hang still? → **The box, door facing away from the fan**

  *Parent does:* 1. Switch on the fan. 2. Put the tissue flag inside. 3. Turn the tent so the door faces the fan, then away from it.  
  *Parent says:* Let's test our tent with the flag. Which way keeps it calm inside?  
  *Child mode:* Put your flag inside. Turn the tent. When is it calm inside?

- **s3 (15 min)**

  *Parent says:* Now let's bring in all five needs. Count them on your fingers as we go.

  - Ask: Where is the air in our tent? (good: All around us.; Coming in through the door.)
  - Note: Air is the one children forget. Point out that the tent is full of it, and fresh air comes in through the open door.

### c1 · A Home for the Tissue Flag, Wind From Any Side (20 min)
- **problem:** A real home stands in one place, but the wind can come from any side. Build a shelter for your tissue flag that keeps it still when the fan blows from the front, then from the side.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own shelter. Draw it, build it, and test it with the fan from two sides. What would you change?
- **constraints:** Use only things from home: books, card, paper, a box or cloth.; The flag must hang still for a slow count of ten.; A grown-up moves the fan to a new side after the first test.
- **general:** Close all four sides and leave a small door you can shut.; Make the door face away from the wind, and turn the shelter when the wind changes.
- **creative:** Build a round wall that curls like a snail shell, so the door is tucked inside the curl.; Hang a cloth curtain over the door, so wind from any side cannot rush straight in.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the problem. Look at the flag in the open fan wind first.

  *Parent does:* Your child chooses an idea, or invents one, and builds the shelter.  
  *Parent says:* Which idea will you try? Where will the door go?  
  *Child mode:* Choose an idea. Build your shelter.
  - Note: Help with tape if needed, but let your child choose the shape.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**

  - Ask: What would you change? (good: Close the door when the wind changes.; Make the wall go all the way round.)
  - Note: Keep fingers and the tissue away from the fan blades.

### Our Five Needs (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (match) Draw a line from each need to the thing that gives it.  
   **Answer:** Air → An open window; Water → A clay matka; Food → A plate of dosa; Clothes → A raincoat; A home → A roof. *Why:* Each thing gives us one need. A window lets in fresh air, and a roof keeps us safe from rain.
2. (draw) Draw your hand. Draw one basic need on each finger.  
   **Answer:** Air, water, food, clothes and a home, one on each finger.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows all five needs. Any order is fine.
3. (sort) Circle the needs we take inside our body. Put a box round the needs that stay outside and keep us safe.  
   **Answer:** Air → Inside our body; Water → Inside our body; Food → Inside our body; Clothes → Outside, keeps us safe; A home → Outside, keeps us safe. *Why:* We breathe in air, drink water and eat food. Clothes and a home stay outside us and protect us.
4. (mcq) You hold a tissue flag in front of a fan and it flaps. What is moving it?  
   Options: Moving air / The floor / The light  
   **Answer:** Moving air. *Why:* Wind is moving air. We cannot see air, but we can see the flag it moves.
5. (draw) Draw the fan and the tissue flag. Draw lines to show the moving air.  
   **Answer:** The flag points away from the fan, with lines of air between them.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the flag blown away from the fan by the moving air.
6. (truefalse) We need air only when we are running.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. We breathe all the time, even when we sit still and when we sleep. We breathe faster when we run.
7. (odd) Which one does NOT keep the wind off us?  
   Options: A wall / A row of trees / A water tank / An empty open field  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Walls, trees and a tank make the wind go around them. An open field has nothing to stop it.
8. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: you ran fast in the sun. Which two needs does your body ask for after, and how can you tell?  
   **Answer:** Water, because I feel thirsty and sweaty. Air, because I breathe fast.. *Why:* A strong answer names water and air, and says what the body does to show it: thirst and fast breathing.
9. (label) Here is a house. Write R next to the part that keeps off the rain, W next to the part that stops the wind, and A next to the part that lets in fresh air.  
   **Answer:** R on the roof, W on a wall, A on a window.. *Why:* A roof keeps off rain and sun, walls stop the wind, and windows let fresh air in.
10. (mcq) A crow comes to a bowl on the balcony on a hot day. Which need is the crow meeting?  
   Options: Water / Clothes / A home  
   **Answer:** Water. *Why:* The crow is thirsty, like us on a hot day. Crows need water too.
### Needs, Wind and Shelter (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (perspective) Two friends in Kolkata, Rumi and Joyita, are building a tent on a windy terrace.  
   **Answer:** Joyita will have a calmer tent, because the closed back faces the wind. But Rumi is right that a home needs fresh air. A tent with its door away from the wind still gets fresh air through the door.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the wind box test and is fair to Rumi: fresh air is a need too.
2. (mcq) A farmer plants a row of trees to keep the wind off her crops. Which side of the field should the trees go?  
   Options: The side the wind comes from / The side away from the wind / The middle of the field  
   **Answer:** The side the wind comes from. *Why:* The trees are a wall. The wind goes around them, and the crops behind them get calm air.
3. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: why did the flag hang still when the box's closed wall faced the fan?  
   **Answer:** The wind hit the closed wall and went around the box. So the air inside stayed calm.. *Why:* A strong answer says the wind went around the wall, not through it.
4. (order) Put these in order, from the need we must have soonest to the one we can go without for longest.  
   **Answer:** ['Air', 'Water', 'Food']. *Why:* A person lives only a few minutes without air, a few days without water, and some weeks without food.
5. (mcq) Sunita lives in Shimla. In January it is very cold. Which clothes does she need?  
   Options: A woollen sweater, a cap and socks / A thin cotton frock only / A raincoat only  
   **Answer:** A woollen sweater, a cap and socks. *Why:* Wool keeps her warm. In Chennai in May she would need loose cotton instead. The need is the same, but the clothes depend on the place.
6. (draw) Draw a home for a pigeon. Show what keeps off the rain and the wind.  
   **Answer:** A nest on a ledge under a roof, or in a corner with a wall on the windy side.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a roof for rain and a wall against the wind. Pigeons choose such places in our buildings.
7. (truefalse) A wall stops the wind from moving at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The wind goes around the wall. That is why the air behind it is calm.
8. (fill) Fill the gap: Wind is air that is ____.  
   **Answer:** moving. *Why:* Wind is moving air. We cannot see it, but we see what it moves, like the tissue flag.
9. (label) Here is the wind box from above, with a fan on one side. Draw arrows to show where the wind goes. Put a C where the air is calm.  
   **Answer:** Arrows curve around both sides of the box. The C is inside the box, near the door.. *Why:* A strong answer shows the wind going around the closed wall, and the calm air inside.
10. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: how do clothes help you on a hot day, and on a cold night?  
   **Answer:** On a hot day, a cap and loose cotton clothes shade me and keep me cool. On a cold night, a sweater or shawl keeps me warm.. *Why:* A strong answer gives one kind of clothes for the heat and one for the cold, and says what each does.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is one of the five basic needs?  
   Options: Water / A toy car / A phone  
   **Answer:** Water. *Why:* Water is a basic need. A toy car and a phone are nice to have, but we can live without them.
2. (mcq) A newborn baby drinks only milk. What does the milk give the baby?  
   Options: Food and water / Clothes and a home / Only air  
   **Answer:** Food and water. *Why:* Milk is food for the baby, and the milk is water to drink too.
3. (mcq) Why do we drink water every day, and not just once?  
   Options: Water leaves our body when we wee, sweat and breathe out / Water makes us tall / Water is only for washing  
   **Answer:** Water leaves our body when we wee, sweat and breathe out. *Why:* Our body keeps losing water, so we must keep putting it back.
4. (mcq) What does a roof keep off us?  
   Options: Rain and hot sun / Air / Food  
   **Answer:** Rain and hot sun. *Why:* A roof keeps off the rain and the hot sun. Walls keep off the wind.
5. (mcq) Remember the wind box test? Where should the open door of the box face, to keep the tissue flag still?  
   Options: Towards the fan / Away from the fan / It does not matter  
   **Answer:** Away from the fan. *Why:* With the door away from the fan, the closed wall faced the wind. The air went around the box, so the flag hung still.
6. (mcq) In the wind box test, a tunnel was open at both ends. What did the tissue flag do inside it?  
   Options: It flapped, because the wind blew straight through / It hung still, because it had a roof / It fell over  
   **Answer:** It flapped, because the wind blew straight through. *Why:* A roof alone does not stop the wind. The wind blew in at one end and out of the other.
7. (mcq) Which of these is a shelter for a pigeon?  
   Options: A nest on a ledge under a roof / A puddle on the road / A plate of rice  
   **Answer:** A nest on a ledge under a roof. *Why:* A nest under a roof keeps a pigeon safe from rain and wind. It is the pigeon's home.
8. (mcq) Which is true?  
   Options: Humans are animals, and we need air, water and food like other animals / Only people need water / Plants need nothing  
   **Answer:** Humans are animals, and we need air, water and food like other animals. *Why:* Humans are animals. Crows, cats, ants and people all need air, water and food. Plants need air, water and light.
9. (mcq) What did Florence Nightingale find when she counted the soldiers in her hospital?  
   Options: Most were dying of illness from dirty water and drains, not of wounds / Most were dying because they had no toys / Nobody was ill  
   **Answer:** Most were dying of illness from dirty water and drains, not of wounds. *Why:* Her counts showed far more soldiers dying of illness than of wounds. When the drains and water were cleaned, far fewer died.
10. (mcq) Malini, a girl in Madurai, goes out in the hot sun in May. What should she wear?  
   Options: A cap and loose cotton clothes / A thick woollen sweater / Nothing on her head  
   **Answer:** A cap and loose cotton clothes. *Why:* A cap shades her head, and loose cotton lets sweat dry and keeps her cool.

## Applications
- Farmers plant rows of trees along the edge of a field, called windbreaks, to slow the wind and protect the crops.
- Campers put up a tent with its door facing away from the wind.
- On the coasts of Odisha and Andhra Pradesh, strong cyclone shelters are built so that families can stay safe in big storms.
- After a flood, relief teams bring the basic needs first: drinking water, food, clothes and tents.
- Homes, schools and hospitals are built with windows, so fresh air can come in.

## Spotted around us
- Pigeons sitting on a ledge under a building's roof, out of the rain.
- A crow drinking from a water bowl on a balcony in summer.

- Ants carrying crumbs into a crack in the kitchen wall, their home.

## Open questions
- **How exactly does a pigeon or an ant find the calmest, safest spot?** Animals sense wind, warmth and wet in many ways. Scientists are still finding out how each animal chooses a shelter.
- **How much water does each person really need in a day?** It depends on the heat, the person and the food they eat. Doctors still argue about exact amounts. The rule of eight glasses for everyone is not based on strong evidence.
- **Is sleep a basic need, like food and water?** Every animal studied so far sleeps or rests. Scientists still argue about all the things sleep does for the body.
