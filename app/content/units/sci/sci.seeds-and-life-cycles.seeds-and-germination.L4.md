# Waking seeds: why seeds from one packet do not wake together
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.seeds-and-germination.L4`

**Summary.** Thirty moong seeds from one packet lie on the same wet cloth, in the same warm room. Some have a white root by the next morning. Some wake a day later, and a few wait for days. Your child counts the wake-ups every few hours and draws the waking curve. Then your child tests how deep a tiny seed and a big seed can be sown, what happens to a shoot that never finds light, and why a hard seed coat is like a locked door.

**Intro.** *Put 30 moong seeds from one packet on a wet cloth. Leave them in a warm room. Look again after 12 hours. Some seeds already have a white root. Others have not moved at all. They had the same water, the same warmth and the same air. Why did they not wake together?* A packet of seeds looks like a crowd of identical things. But no two seeds are exactly alike, and every seed has their own way of waking. In this unit you count the wake-ups hour by hour. You also find out how deep a seed can be buried, why a seedling in the dark grows long and pale, and what a tamarind seed needs before water can get in. In the end you will see why a packet that wakes all at once might be a bad thing for the seeds.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can count how many seeds in a test sprouted, turn the count into a percentage, and say why a test uses many seeds.
1. I can explain that seeds from one packet wake at different times, and why that spread of wake-up times helps the seeds as a group.
2. I can explain dormancy, and name at least three different things that wake a dormant seed.
3. I can explain why a seed with a bigger food store can be sown deeper than a tiny seed.
4. I can describe two ways a seedling comes up, with the seed leaves lifted into the air or left under the soil.
5. I can explain what happens to a seedling that grows without light, and what light changes.
6. I can explain how dry and cold slow a seed's ageing, and why some seeds, such as mango and jackfruit, cannot be stored dry.

## Terms
- **Viable**: A seed is viable if the baby plant inside is alive. A viable seed can sprout when they get what they need. *e.g.* Fresh moong is viable. Boiled moong is not.
- **Germination percentage**: Out of every 100 seeds you test, the number that sprout. A packet that says 80 percent means that 80 of every 100 seeds sprouted in the seller's test. *e.g.* If 24 of 30 moong seeds sprout, that is 80 of every 100, or 80 percent.
- **Germination rate**: How fast seeds sprout. Seeds with a fast rate show roots early. Seeds with a slow rate take many days. *e.g.* Moong in a warm kitchen has a fast rate. Tamarind has a slow rate.
- **Dormancy**: When a seed is alive and has water, warmth and air, but still does not sprout. The seed is holding back until the right sign comes. *e.g.* Apple seeds are dormant until they have been through weeks of cold.
- **Scarification**: Rubbing, scratching or thinning a hard seed coat so that water can get in. The word comes from scar. *e.g.* Rubbing a tamarind seed on rough sandpaper lets water in.
- **Cold chill**: A spell of cold that some seeds need before they will wake. Scientists call it stratification. *e.g.* Apple seeds wake much better after weeks in a cold place such as a fridge.
- **Hormone**: A chemical messenger made by a living body. It travels from one part to another and tells cells what to do. Plants have hormones too. *e.g.* A hormone called gibberellin tells the food store of a seed to start working.
- **Radicle**: The first root of a baby plant, the part that comes out of the seed first. *e.g.* The white tail on a sprouted moong seed is a radicle.
- **Seed leaves above ground**: When the fat seed leaves are lifted into the air and turn green. Scientists call this epigeal germination. *e.g.* Moong and rajma lift their seed leaves above the soil.
- **Seed leaves below ground**: When the seed leaves stay under the soil and only the shoot comes up. Scientists call this hypogeal germination. *e.g.* Chana and peas leave their seed leaves under the soil.
- **Chlorophyll**: The green substance in leaves that catches sunlight so the plant can make food. *e.g.* A seedling grown in the dark has almost no chlorophyll, so they are pale.
- **Pale search seedling**: A seedling that grew in the dark. The seedling is long, thin, pale and floppy, because they spent their food stretching to find light. Scientists call this an etiolated seedling. *e.g.* The long white shoots of bean sprouts sold in markets are grown in the dark.
- **Sample**: A small part that stands for a bigger whole. A bigger sample gives a steadier answer. *e.g.* A seed lab tests 400 seeds to say how well a whole sack will sprout.
- **Moisture content**: How much of a seed's weight is water. A dry seed in a jar might be about one-tenth water. *e.g.* Chana kept in a damp monsoon kitchen slowly takes in water, and the moisture content of the chana rises.
- **Seed bank**: A safe store of many kinds of seeds, kept dry and cold so that they stay alive for many years. *e.g.* India has a national seed bank in New Delhi. Farmers like Rahibai Popere keep village seed banks of old local crops.
- **Dry-keeping seed**: A seed that stays alive when dried and kept cold. Scientists call this an orthodox seed. *e.g.* Moong, chana, wheat and rice can be dried and stored for years.
- **Wet-keeping seed**: A kind of seed that dies when the seed dries out, so the seed must be sown fresh. Scientists call this a recalcitrant seed. *e.g.* Jackfruit and cocoa seeds are wet-keeping seeds.

## Explanations
### A germination test: counting how many seeds wake · *defined*
**Definition:** A germination test counts how many seeds in a sample sprout. The count is turned into a germination percentage, which is how many seeds out of every 100 would sprout.

Seed sellers and seed labs test their seeds before they sell them. They put seeds on damp paper in warm conditions and count the ones with a root. If 24 of 30 seeds sprout, that is 24 out of 30. Share 30 into three lots of 10, and 24 becomes 8 out of every 10. That is 80 out of every 100, or 80 percent. A seed packet in India usually carries this number, so a farmer knows how many seeds to sow. If the packet says 80 percent and you want 40 plants, you sow 50 seeds. A real seed lab does not test just a few seeds. Many labs test four lots of 100 seeds, and compare the lots. A small test can mislead, because one or two odd seeds change a small count a lot.
- e.g. If 9 of 10 seeds sprout, that is 90 percent. If 18 of 20 sprout, that is also 90 percent.
- e.g. A packet says 80 percent. To get 40 plants you sow 50 seeds, because 8 out of every 10 sprout.
- e.g. A seed lab tests 400 seeds, in four lots of 100, so that one odd lot does not fool them.
### Seeds in one packet do not wake together · *defined*
**Definition:** Seeds from the same plant, in the same packet, wake at different times. A few sprout early, most sprout in the middle and a few sprout late. If you plot the running total against time, the line is shaped like a lazy letter S.

Every seed is a little different from every other seed. One was a bit bigger, one sat nearer the middle of the pod, one has a coat that is a little thicker. So water reaches the baby plant at different speeds, and the baby plants are ready at different speeds. You see this when you count a plate of moong every few hours. The running total starts slowly, speeds up in the middle and slows down at the end. Scientists call this a germination curve. The time when half the seeds have sprouted is a handy number. It tells you how fast the batch is. The gap between the first and last seed tells you how spread out the batch is. Old seeds are usually slower and more spread out than fresh seeds.
- e.g. On a plate of 30 moong, 4 might have roots at 12 hours, 15 at 24 hours, 24 at 36 hours and 28 at 48 hours.
- e.g. A packet that is two years old often starts later, finishes with fewer seeds, and has a longer gap between the first and last seed.
- e.g. Two plates of seeds from the same packet give similar curves, but never exactly the same numbers.
### Why it helps seeds to wake at different times · *idea*
Weather is hard to predict. A first rain may be followed by weeks of dry heat. If every seed woke at that first rain, a dry spell could kill every seedling and the plant would leave no young at all. If seeds wake over several days, or over several rains, some are sure to be in the ground at a good time. Biologists call this spreading the risk, or bet hedging. In 1966, an Israeli scientist, Dan Cohen, worked out with mathematics that in a place where rain is unpredictable, a plant does better when only some of their seeds wake in any one year. Many desert plants do exactly this. Part of the story is not settled. Some of the spread may be a plan the plant made over many generations. Some may simply be small differences that nobody needed to plan.
- e.g. Wildflowers in dry places keep a store of sleeping seeds in the soil, and wake only a part of them after each rain.
- e.g. A farmer sows bajra in many lots over the rainy season, so that a dry spell cannot spoil every lot. This is the same idea, used by a person.
- e.g. Weeds in a field come up all season long, even after the field was ploughed, because their seeds wake a few at a time.
- **Many ecologists:** The spread in wake-up times is a result of natural selection. Plants whose seeds all woke at once sometimes lost everything, so plants that spread the risk left more young over many years.
- **Some seed scientists:** A part of the spread is just the small, unplanned differences between seeds, and has no job to do. It is hard to tell which part is a plan and which part is chance.
### Dormancy: alive, wet, warm, and still asleep · *defined*
**Definition:** Dormancy is when a living seed does not sprout even though the seed has water, warmth and air. Dormancy stops a seed from waking at a bad time.

Moong has almost no dormancy, which is why moong sprouts so quickly. Many wild seeds are different. If a seed woke at the first warm day of autumn, the cold winter would kill the seedling. So the seed waits for a sign that winter has passed. Seed scientists describe two main kinds of dormancy. In one kind, the seed coat is the problem. It is so hard, or so waxy, that water cannot get in. In the other kind, the baby plant inside is held back by chemical signals until the right sign arrives. Dormancy is not damage and it is not death. A dormant seed is doing exactly the job that seeds are made for, which is to wait for the right time.
- e.g. A tamarind seed has a coat so hard that water hardly gets in, even after a night of soaking.
- e.g. An apple seed has water and air in the fridge, but wakes properly only after weeks of cold.
- e.g. Moong, chana and rajma have very little dormancy. They wake within a day or two of getting wet.
### The keys that unlock a sleeping seed · *defined*
**Definition:** Different seeds wait for different signs. The sign might be a thin spot in the coat, a spell of cold, a flash of light, smoke from a fire, or a big rain. A sign that wakes a seed is like a key.

A hard coat can be thinned by sandpaper, by slowly rotting in the soil, or by passing through the gut of a bird or a bat. A spell of cold tells apple seeds that winter is over. Light tells tiny seeds that they are near the surface, so their small food store will last until the shoot reaches the sun. Smoke tells some Australian and South African seeds that a fire has just cleared the land and given them room. A big rain tells some desert wildflower seeds that the soil is wet deep down. In the 1940s, a plant scientist called Frits Went suggested that seeds of some desert wildflowers have a chemical in the coat that only a large rain washes away. A small shower does not wash it away, so the seeds stay asleep. A small shower would wet the soil for only a day or two. The keys are all different. But each one is a sign that tells the seed that a good time has come.
- e.g. Tamarind: a rough rub thins the coat, so water can get in.
- e.g. Apple: weeks of cold tell the seed that winter has passed.
- e.g. Some desert wildflowers: a big rain washes away the sleep chemical.
### Two messengers inside a seed: sleep and wake · *idea*
Inside a seed, two plant hormones pull in opposite directions. Abscisic acid tells the seed to stay asleep. Gibberellin tells the seed to wake. A seed wakes when the balance tips from sleep to wake. In seeds that need a cold chill, the cold slowly lowers the sleep messenger and raises the wake messenger. In barley, the baby plant makes gibberellin, which travels to the food store and tells it to start making enzymes, the helpers that cut starch into sugar. Seed scientists have worked out many of the steps. How a seed adds up all the signs of water, warmth, light and cold, and tips the balance at just the right moment, is still being studied.
- e.g. A seed that makes more of the sleep messenger stays asleep longer.
- e.g. When a barley grain wakes, the gibberellin goes from the baby plant to the food store, and the food store starts to work.
- e.g. Gardeners sometimes soak stubborn seeds in a weak gibberellin solution, because it works as a wake message.
- **Most plant scientists:** The balance of the two messengers is the main switch. It is a chemical sum that the seed does by themself.
- **Some plant biologists:** The seed is deciding. The seed senses many signs, remembers the cold they felt, and acts at the right time. They think this counts as a simple kind of thinking without a brain.
### How deep can a seed be sown? The food store sets the limit · *defined*
**Definition:** A shoot has to push up through the soil before the shoot can see light. The shoot lives on the food in the seed all that time. So the bigger the seed's food store, the deeper the seed can be sown.

A tiny mustard seed has only a pinch of food. If the seed is buried 6 centimetres deep, the baby plant grows a pale, thin shoot and uses up the food before reaching the surface. The seedling dies in the dark, still under the soil. A big chana has a large food store, and the shoot can climb 6 centimetres or more and still have food to spare. Gardeners use a rule of thumb. Sow a seed about two or three times as deep as the seed is wide. A shoot grows in the dark by stretching, and stretching spends food fast. That is why a tiny seed that is buried deep fails. This is also why tiny seeds such as some lettuce wait for light before waking. The light tells them that they are near the surface.
- e.g. A mustard seed is about 1 millimetre wide, so the seed is sown about 1 centimetre deep.
- e.g. A chana is about 8 millimetres wide, so the chana can be sown 3 to 5 centimetres deep.
- e.g. A coconut, one of the biggest seeds, is often planted lying half-buried because the seed has so much food.
### Big food store or tiny food store: two ways to bet · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant can make a few big seeds with a lot of food each, or a huge number of tiny seeds with little food each. Both ways work, and each has a price.

A mango tree makes a few big stones with plenty of food, so each young tree has a good start. The price is that the tree can make only a few. A banyan or a peepal makes huge numbers of tiny seeds, each with very little food. The price is that nearly every seed fails. The orchid is the most extreme. An orchid seed is as fine as dust and has almost no food store at all. In the wild, an orchid seed can only grow when a tiny fungus in the soil feeds the seed. The largest seed in the world is the coco de mer of the Seychelles islands, which can weigh more than 10 kilograms. The seed grows very slowly, and the tree is slow to make seeds. Neither way is better. They are two different bets on a hard world.
- e.g. Mango and coconut: a few big seeds, each with a large store of food.
- e.g. Banyan and peepal: thousands of tiny seeds, each with a tiny store of food.
- e.g. Orchids: dust-like seeds with almost no food, fed by a fungus in the soil.
### Two ways a seedling comes up · *defined*
**Definition:** Some seedlings pull their seed leaves up into the air, where the leaves turn green. This is epigeal germination. Others leave the seed leaves under the soil and send up only the shoot. This is hypogeal germination.

In moong and rajma, the stem just below the seed leaves, called the hypocotyl, bends into a hook. The hook pulls the fat seed leaves up out of the soil. In the light, they turn green and work like small leaves for a while. In chana and peas, the part of the stem above the seed leaves does the growing. It pushes up through the soil, and the seed leaves stay under the ground, still feeding the seedling. Grasses such as rice, wheat and maize are different again. A sheath, called the coleoptile, covers the first leaf like a sharp tip and pushes through the soil. A seed leaf under the soil is protected from being eaten and from drying out. A seed leaf in the air gets light and can make some food.
- e.g. Moong and rajma: seed leaves lifted into the light and turned green.
- e.g. Chana and peas: seed leaves stay under the soil.
- e.g. Rice, wheat and maize: a pointed sheath pushes up, and the first leaf follows inside it.
### The pale search: a seedling that cannot find light · *defined*
**Definition:** A seedling grown in the dark stretches fast, stays pale and thin, and keeps the tiny leaves folded. The seedling is searching for light and spending their food to do it. Scientists call this an etiolated seedling.

In the dark, a seedling puts nearly everything into growing longer. Their stem grows long and thin, their leaves stay small and folded, and the hook at the top stays closed. They make almost no chlorophyll, the green substance that catches sunlight, so they are pale yellow or white. The long stem is the best use of their food. The sooner the top reaches the surface, the sooner they can make new food. Bean sprouts are sold long and white because they are grown in the dark on purpose. If the seedling reaches light, things change within hours. The stem slows down, the hook opens, the leaves unfold and chlorophyll starts to form, so they turn green. If they do not reach light before the food runs out, they cannot recover.
- e.g. Moong grown in a dark box for five days is long, thin and pale.
- e.g. The same moong in a bright window is shorter, thicker and green, with open leaves.
- e.g. A potato in a dark cupboard sends out long, pale shoots that search for light.
### Light flips the switch: how a seedling knows she has reached the sun · *idea*
A seedling has light sensors, which are proteins that change shape when light hits them. The first ones found were called phytochromes, which sense red and far-red light. Others sense blue light. When a sensor catches light, it sends a message that changes which genes are switched on. The stem slows down, the leaves open and the cells start to make chlorophyll. Scientists have found many of the proteins and genes in this chain. A seedling cannot see the way we do, and they have no eyes. Yet they tell the difference between dark, shade and bright sun, and tell the direction the light comes from. How the seedling's cells combine all these messages is still being worked out.
- e.g. A moong seedling moved from a dark box to a window starts to turn green the same day.
- e.g. A seedling under the shade of a bigger plant stretches taller, as if she senses the shade.
- e.g. A seedling in a window leans towards the glass.
- **Most plant biologists:** A seedling has sensors and chemical switches that respond to light. The pale search is a built-in programme, and light turns off the pale search programme and turns on the green programme.
- **Some plant scientists:** A seedling that senses shade, light and direction, and then changes how they grow, is making a choice. They say this is a simple kind of thinking, done by cells without a brain.
### How seeds keep for years: dry and cold slow the ageing · *defined*
**Definition:** Seeds slowly age and get damaged inside, even when they are asleep. Dry and cold slow the damage. A drier, colder seed lives longer.

A sleeping seed still uses a tiny bit of food and slowly wears out. Water is what drives the wearing out, so a very dry seed lasts longer. Warmth speeds it up, so a cold seed lasts longer too. An American seed scientist, James Harrington, wrote down two rough rules of thumb. For each 1 percent less water in a seed, the seed lives about twice as long. For each 5 degrees Celsius or so colder, the seed lives about twice as long. They only work within limits, but they show why seed banks dry their seeds and freeze them. India's national seed bank in New Delhi keeps crop seeds in very cold rooms at around minus 20 degrees Celsius. At home, a monsoon kitchen is bad for seeds, because the air is damp and warm. A tightly closed jar with a dry spoon of rice or a silica packet keeps the seeds much drier.
- e.g. A sealed jar of dry chana in a cool cupboard keeps the seeds sprouting for years.
- e.g. The same chana in an open bowl in a monsoon kitchen can mould, or sprout too early.
- e.g. Seed banks freeze seeds because every drop of 5 degrees Celsius roughly doubles the time that the seed stays alive.
### Seeds that cannot wait: mango, jackfruit and cocoa · *defined*
**Definition:** Some seeds die if they dry out. They must be sown fresh while still wet. They cannot be kept in a dry seed bank. Scientists call them wet-keeping, or recalcitrant, seeds.

Moong, chana and rice are dry-keeping seeds. They finish growing, dry out on purpose, and then wait. Mango, jackfruit, cocoa and many trees of wet forests are different. Their seeds never dry out inside the fruit and have no sleeping stage. They sprout soon after the fruit falls, if the ground is damp. Neem seeds are known to lose their power to sprout within a few weeks. Seed banks cannot store these seeds in the usual way. This is a real problem, because many trees of wet forests have wet-keeping seeds. Scientists are testing new ways to keep them, such as cutting out only the tiny baby plant and freezing the baby plant in very cold liquid nitrogen. Nobody yet has a way that works for every kind.
- e.g. A mango stone left in a dry drawer for a few months will not sprout. A fresh stone in damp soil sprouts in a few weeks.
- e.g. Jackfruit seeds sprout best when sown within days of eating the fruit.
- e.g. Cocoa seeds begin to sprout inside the pod if the pod is left too long.
### Do seeds remember? Seed priming and the mother's weather · *debated*
Seed scientists find that the behaviour of a seed depends on the past. Seeds that were soaked a little, then dried again, often wake faster when they are soaked the second time. Farmers and seed companies use this trick, which is called priming. Seeds from a plant that grew in a cold summer may be more dormant than seeds from the same kind of plant grown in a hot summer. So a seed carries something from their mother plant's weather. Scientists are studying how this happens. One idea is that chemical marks on the genes change how they switch on and off. But does carrying marks from the past count as remembering? The question depends on what we mean by the word.
- e.g. Farmers in some places soak seed overnight and dry the seed in the shade before sowing, so that the plants come up faster.
- e.g. Seeds from a plant that grew in a cool summer can stay dormant longer than seeds from a plant that grew in a hot summer.
- **Many seed scientists:** Priming and the mother's weather are chemical changes that last. They are a kind of memory in a loose sense, but they are not the same as a brain remembering.
- **Some plant scientists:** Any living thing that keeps a trace of the past and uses it later is remembering. A seed that is primed or marked by their mother plant's weather remembers in their own way.

## Mostly agreed
- Seeds from one batch wake at different times, even when they get exactly the same conditions. *How we know:* Seed labs and farmers have counted germination day by day for more than a century. The running total always rises in a lazy S shape, with some seeds early and some late.
- The germination percentage of a batch of seeds can be measured, and a bigger sample gives a steadier answer. *How we know:* Labs compare many lots of 100 seeds. Small tests with 10 seeds vary a lot from lot to lot, while tests of 100 or 400 seeds agree much better.
- A seed with a bigger food store can be sown deeper than a seed with a smaller store. *How we know:* Sowing depth tests with many crops show that large seeds come up from deeper soil than small seeds, and tiny seeds fail when they are sown too deep.
- Many seeds are dormant, and need a particular sign such as a thinned coat, a spell of cold, light, smoke or a big rain before they wake. *How we know:* Growers have used these keys for generations, and seed scientists have tested them in laboratories on thousands of kinds of seed.
- A seedling grown without light is long, thin and pale. Light changes their growth within hours. *How we know:* Anyone can see it by growing bean sprouts in the dark and in the light. Plant scientists have found the light sensors that cause the change.
- Seeds live longer when they are drier and colder, but some seeds, such as mango and jackfruit, die if they dry out. *How we know:* Seed banks measure how long seeds survive in storage. Seeds of wet forest trees are killed by the drying that keeps dry-keeping seeds alive.

## How we found out
**Charles Darwin, an English naturalist (1809 to 1882), Published in 1859.** Many people once thought that a seed which had not sprouted by the end of the season was dead, and that a field cleared of weeds was clean. Charles Darwin, an English naturalist, tested this in a simple way. He took three spoonfuls of mud from the edge of a pond and kept the mud covered in his study. He sowed nothing in it. Over six months, he pulled up each plant that grew from the mud and counted them. He counted 537 plants, of many kinds, from only three spoonfuls. The mud was full of seeds that had been lying there alive and waiting, some for a long time. He wrote this in his book On the Origin of Species, published in 1859. It showed that a seed which does not sprout is often not dead. The seed is waiting for their sign. Seed scientists now call this hidden store of seeds in the soil a soil seed bank.  
*What people believed before:* People thought that seeds which had not sprouted by the end of a season were dead. Darwin's covered mud showed that many waiting seeds were alive, and woke as the months went by.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A seed that has not sprouted when the others have is dead.** (Many gardeners, and many people checking a plate of seeds.; *rejected*)  
  For: Most live seeds sprout within a day or two on a plate. A seed with no root after a long time looks like a dud.  
  Against: Seeds from one batch wake over days and sometimes weeks. In 1859, Charles Darwin kept three spoonfuls of pond mud covered in his study. He counted 537 plants that rose from it over six months, and many seeds must have waited a long time. A late seed is often alive and only slow.
- **The seeds that wake first are the strongest, and the late ones are weak.** (Some farmers and some seed testers.; *debated*)  
  For: In tests of seed vigour, seeds that sprout fast often grow into bigger seedlings.  
  Against: In a dry year, the early seedlings may all die and the late ones may be the only survivors. Fast and slow seeds are each good in different weather. Strong or weak depends on the year.
- **The deeper you sow a seed, the safer they are.** (Many beginner gardeners.; *rejected*)  
  For: Deep soil is cooler and wetter, and birds and ants cannot find seeds easily.  
  Against: A small seed has only a small food store. If the seed is buried too deep, the shoot uses up the food before the shoot reaches the light. A seed should be sown about two or three times as deep as the seed is wide.
- **Soaking any seed in very hot water makes the seed sprout faster.** (Some gardeners.; *rejected*)  
  For: A brief dip in hot water helps some very hard seeds, such as some acacia seeds, by cracking the coat.  
  Against: Hot water cooks the baby plant inside most seeds, and a cooked seed never sprouts. Most seeds, including moong and chana, only need ordinary water. Hot water is risky for children as well.
- **Old local varieties of seeds are always better than new ones.** (Some farmers, seed savers and food writers.; *debated*)  
  For: Old local varieties have been grown in the same place for generations. They often cope well with local weather and pests, and they add variety that protects against disease.  
  Against: Some new varieties give much bigger harvests or resist particular diseases. Many farmers use both. Variety is the key point, and nobody knows in advance which kind will cope best with next year's weather.

## Roots
We counted how seeds wake, hour by hour, and found that seeds from one packet do not wake together. Now we ask what that spread in time tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** On one plate, thirty seeds from one packet woke over several days, not in one hour. The first and the last were far apart. If all had woken together, one dry spell could end the lot. So the spread in time is part of how a group of seeds survives.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a packet of seeds is 'the same', and we call the late ones slow, weak or dead. But the difference between seeds is not a fault. It is what keeps the group alive when the weather turns.  
*Evidence:* In the waking-curve test, seeds from one packet on one cloth sprouted over many hours, so the running total climbed slowly and not in one jump. In pond mud that Charles Darwin kept covered, 537 plants came up over six months. If your own plate looked different, write down what you saw.

- *who am i:* Children of the same age do not all learn to walk in the same week. Some walk at nine months and some at eighteen months, and both are healthy. Differences in timing are normal in living beings.
- *what is life:* A sleeping seed is alive. Living does not always look like growing. Sometimes living means waiting, and a late seed has not failed.
- *what is this world:* Nobody can predict the weather for a whole season. A group that spreads out in time lives through bad luck that would end a group that all did the same thing.

One plant made all thirty seeds and packed them in one pod. They share nearly the same instructions, and yet each seed wakes in their own time. So one source gives rise to many differences, and the group needs all of them. A field of bajra is one living thing made of many small wakings and waitings. When one part fails, another part is ready.

- **Tao Te Ching 64:** A tree too big to hug grows from a tiny shoot. A journey of a thousand miles begins where you stand. (A whole field of bajra begins as a few seeds meeting water in the sand. The seeds that start late are still beginnings.)
- **Qur'an 22:5:** You see the earth dry and lifeless. Then when rain falls on it, it stirs, swells and puts out every kind of beautiful growth. (Dry sand holds seeds that stir and swell when enough rain reaches them. This is what the seeds in the Thar do after the second rain.)
- **A doha of Kabir, a poet of North India in the 1400s:** Go slowly, my mind, for everything happens in its own time. The gardener may water with a hundred pots, but the fruit comes only when its season comes. (You cannot make a seed wake by pushing. Each seed has their own time, and watering more does not hurry the late ones.)
- Science (settled): In places where rain is unpredictable, plants that wake only part of their seeds each year leave more young over many years. Mathematics by Dan Cohen in 1966 showed why, and many desert plants behave this way.
- Science (open): How a seed adds up water, cold, light and chemical signs, and decides to wake, is still being worked out.
- Other view, Some evolutionary biologists: Part of the spread in wake-up times may be an accident. No two seeds can be built exactly alike, and not every difference has a purpose. Saying that seeds spread their risk puts a plan where there may only be chance, and it is hard to tell which is which.
- Practice, Wait like a seed (3 min): Hold one dry seed in the palm of your hand. Close your fingers gently. Breathe in slowly while you count to four. Breathe out slowly while you count to four. Do this ten times. Think of yourself as the seed, waiting for the sign. Do not hurry. Then open your hand and look at the seed. What sign would you wait for?
- Ask: Is a seed that has not woken yet late, or is she right on her own time?
- Ask: If a group needs its slow members as well as its quick ones, is 'late' a fair word?
- Ask: Are the thirty seeds on your plate thirty separate seeds, or one crop that wakes in thirty ways?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If every seed in a packet woke in the same hour, what could go wrong? And what does that tell you about why they do not? (hint: Think about a first rain that is followed by three weeks of heat.)
- *deep:* A tiny seed does not know how deep they are buried. So how can the seed wake only when they are near the top? (hint: Think about what the soil near the top has that the soil far down does not.)
- *deep:* A seed in a vault is alive but waiting for 100 years. Is waiting a way of being alive, or a way of being paused? (hint: Is a person asleep alive in the same way as a person running?)
- *deep:* Is the germination percentage on a packet a fact about the seeds, or a fact about the test? (hint: Would a different lot of seeds, or a different room, give the same number?)
- *big:* Farmers have saved and swapped seeds for about ten thousand years, and nobody can name the people who first grew rice or wheat. Who do seeds belong to? (hint: Who made the first rice? How many people helped, over how many years?)
- *big:* In some years, rain turns a dry desert into a carpet of flowers. The seeds waited for years for that rain. What does a seed's waiting say about time? (hint: How long can you wait for something you really want?)
- *big:* Seeds that wake late can save the whole group in a bad year. Should we call a slow one weak or smart? (hint: In which kind of year is the slow seed the lucky one?)
- *deep:* Plant scientists argue about whether a seed that adds up signs of cold, light and water is deciding. What would you need to see before you said a seed decides? (hint: What does it mean when you decide something? What do you do differently?)

## Story: The Second Rain
*Hema, her family and their village are made up. The science is real. Bajra is a main crop in the Thar desert, farmers there sow it with the first rain, and a dry spell after a light first rain can kill the seedlings. Seeds in dry sand stay asleep until enough rain reaches them. The numbers in Hema's count are made up, but the shape is typical.*

Hema lived in a small village in the Thar desert, not far from Jaisalmer. Her house was made of mud and stone, and the sand around it went on in waves as far as she could see. Rain was rare there. Dadi, her grandmother, said that rain was like a guest who comes when she likes, and never says when.

Every family kept its seed for the next year in a clay pot with a mud lid. Dadi's pot held bajra, the grain that grows best in the desert. All year, the pot stood in the coolest corner of the house, and Hema was not allowed to touch it.

At school, Sunita Madam gave the class some homework. 'Put thirty seeds on a wet cloth,' she said. 'Count how many have a root after twelve hours, after twenty-four hours, after thirty-six, and so on. Write the numbers down.' Hema asked Dadi for thirty seeds from the pot. Dadi gave her a long look, and then thirty seeds.

Hema counted. After twelve hours, six seeds had a tiny white tail. After twenty-four hours, seventeen. After thirty-six, twenty-five. After two days, twenty-seven. One more woke on the third day, and one more on the sixth. One seed never woke at all. 'They had the same cloth and the same water,' Hema told the cat. 'Why did they not wake together?' The cat did not know.

Then, one evening, the smell came. Wet sand smells like nothing else in the world. Clouds had rolled over the dunes, and rain fell for an hour. The whole village came out and stood in it with their faces up. In the morning, Hema's uncle, Bhanwar Chacha, took Dadi's pot to the field. He scattered the seeds along the rows and covered them with sand, the way he did every year. 'Sow at the first rain,' he said, 'and the crop gets the longest season.'

Four days later, the whole field was striped with thin green blades. The neighbours laughed for joy. Then the clouds went away, and the hot wind came back. One day passed. Then five. Then ten. The sand dried out to the depth of a hand. The green blades turned yellow, then brown, and then they fell over.

Bhanwar Chacha stood at the edge of the field with his turban in his hand. 'The seed was no good,' he said. 'Or the rain was a trick. We have lost the whole crop.' He began to talk about borrowing money to buy new seed and sow again.

Hema went into the field and knelt beside a row of dead blades. She thought of her cloth and her counts. Six seeds first. Twenty-five by the second day. And the last ones, long after the others. Not every seed in the pot had woken on the same day. So why should the seeds in the field be different? The rain had wetted only the top of the sand. Perhaps only some seeds had met the water.

She dug into the sand with her fingers, just below a dead blade. There, she found a plump, hard bajra seed. She pressed her nail into the seed. Inside, the baby plant was pale and firm. The seed was alive. She dug again, ten steps along, and found another. And another.

'Chacha,' she said, 'do not buy new seed. The field is not empty. Many of the seeds are still asleep.' He laughed, but not unkindly. 'A plate of seeds is not a field, beti.' 'It is the same pot,' said Hema. 'On my cloth, six seeds woke first and one woke last of all. Same seeds, same water, and no two woke together. They are just different from each other.'

Bhanwar Chacha was quiet for a long time. He looked at Hema, and then at Dadi. Dadi lifted one shoulder, the way she did when she was not going to say what she thought. 'It costs nothing to wait a week,' she said. Chacha put his turban back on.

Five days later, the second rain came. This one was a big one, and it fell all night. Hema lay awake and listened to it drum on the roof. In the morning, the sand was dark and wet down to a man's elbow.

Three days after that, new blades began to push out of the wet sand, between the dead brown ones. There were a few at first, then more, then more. 'Late seeds,' Hema told the cat. The cat looked unimpressed.

The field never looked as full as it would have if the first rain had been kind. But it carried a crop. In the cold months, there was bajra in Dadi's pot again, and bajra rotis on every plate.

After that year, Bhanwar Chacha always held back a third of the seed for a second sowing, in case the first rain was a trick. He asked Hema to count each new lot on a wet cloth. 'Tell me when the last one wakes,' he said. 'I want to know how long to wait.' Hema wrote the numbers in a notebook with a green cover. The first column and the last column were far apart. She had begun to think that this was not an accident.

**For the parent:** Seeds from one pot do not wake together. After a false start, the seeds that were still asleep can save the crop.

- Ask: Why did the seedlings that sprouted after the first rain die, while many seeds in the sand stayed alive?
- Ask: On Hema's cloth, the seeds woke at different times. What did that tell her about the seeds in the field?
- Ask: Why might a farmer hold back a third of the seed for a second sowing?
- Ask: Chacha said that a plate of seeds is not a field. How could you check whether a plate test tells you the truth about a field?
- Ask: Hema pressed a nail into a seed to see if the baby plant was alive. What else could you do to find out if a waiting seed is alive?

### e3 · The Pale Search: what happens to a seedling that never sees light? (36 min, 6 days)
- **question:** How does a moong seedling grow in the dark, compared with one by a window? What happens when the dark one gets light?

  *Child mode:* One cup in the dark, one by the window. Which grows taller? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the dark seedlings will be shorter, or taller? (good: They have no light, so they cannot grow.; They are searching for light, so they stretch.; Plants like the sun, so the window ones grow best.)

If the water turns cloudy or smells sour, pour it out and add fresh water. One child: look after both cups. Two to four children: each child sets up a pair of cups, dark and window, with 5 seeds each. Then compare the pairs. One child measures and one writes. Count only the seeds that grew. ### e4 · The Locked Seed: what does a tamarind seed need before water can get in? (35 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Does a tamarind seed with a hard coat swell in water? Does thinning the coat make a difference?

- **record:** Write the weights in the table at the start, after 24 hours and after 48 hours. Draw one seed from each group. Write whether each feels hard or soft.

  *Parent does:* Show your child the tamarind seeds and the sandpaper. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it in the table.

  *Child mode:* Rub six seeds on sandpaper until the shine goes dull. Leave six alone.

  *Parent says:* Weigh each group. Write the weight. Then cover them with water. Label the bowls so you know which is which.  
  *Child mode:* Weigh each group. Cover each with water. Label the bowls.
  - Note: Dry the seeds on a cloth before weighing. If your scale only shows whole grams, six seeds weigh about 3 grams. You can still see a doubling. Two to four children: one team for the rubbed seeds and one for the untouched seeds, with one child to press, one to weigh and one to write.

  *Parent says:* Weigh each group again. Press a seed from each bowl. Which is softer? Which is heavier?  
  *Child mode:* Weigh each group. Press one seed. Which feels softer?
  - Wait: Weigh after 24 hours
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 48 hours, weigh both groups again. Press and score each seed again. Write the weights and scores. Ask your child to say what they see.  
  *Parent says:* It has been two days. Which group has changed? How much heavier is it? Which is still hard?  
  *Child mode:* Weigh both groups again. Which changed? Which is still hard?
  - Note: Rubbed seeds usually swell within a day, and the weight typically goes up by half or more. Untouched seeds usually stay hard, though a few may swell slowly. If your results are different, write down what you saw, because that is a result too.
  - Wait: Weigh after 48 hours
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think a hard coat does? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a hard coat keeps water out until it is thinned. What I saw (example numbers): the rubbed seeds weighed 6 grams after 48 hours and the untouched seeds stayed at 3. Why: water could get through the dull patch, but not through the hard coat. In nature, the coat is thinned by time, soil, fire, or by the gut of an animal that eats the fruit.
  - Think: In the wild, no one rubs the seed on sandpaper. What might thin the coat there?

## Worksheets
### Waking Seeds (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meena tests 30 seeds from a packet on a wet cloth. After four days, 24 of them have a root. What is the germination percentage?  
   Options: 24 percent / 80 percent / 30 percent / 6 percent  
   **Answer:** 80 percent. *Why:* 24 out of 30 is the same as 8 out of 10, which is 80 out of every 100, or 80 percent. Picking 24 percent means using the count itself as the percentage, which only works when exactly 100 seeds are tested.
2. (fill) Kavya counts the seeds with a root on a plate. She finds 4 at 12 hours, 15 at 24 hours and 24 at 36 hours. How many new seeds woke between 24 hours and 36 hours? ____  
   **Answer:** ['9', 'nine']. *Why:* 24 take away 15 is 9. The counts are running totals, so the new seeds in each period are found by taking one count away from the next.
3. (truefalse) You test 30 seeds from one packet on two plates in the same room. The two plates give exactly the same count at every hour.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Even identical-looking plates differ a little, because each seed is a little different. A small difference between two plates is normal, and a good reason to test with more seeds.
4. (mcq) Why do the seeds in one packet not all wake at the same hour?  
   Options: Each seed is a little different, so water and warmth reach each baby plant at a different speed / The seeds argue about who goes first / Only the biggest seeds can wake / The packet was badly made  
   **Answer:** Each seed is a little different, so water and warmth reach each baby plant at a different speed. *Why:* No two seeds are exactly alike. One has a thinner coat, one is a little bigger, one sat in a different part of the pod. These small differences spread the wake-up times out. Picking 'only the biggest' is a common guess, but small seeds wake too.
5. (write) Describe in two or three sentences what the waking curve of a plate of seeds looks like, and what it shows about the seeds.  
   **Answer:** The line starts slowly, climbs steeply in the middle and goes flat at the end, like a lazy S. It shows that a few seeds wake early, most wake in the middle and a few wake late. Seeds from one packet do not wake together.. *Why:* A strong answer describes the shape and then says what the shape means about the seeds.
6. (match) Match each sleeping seed to the sign that helps the seed wake.  
   **Answer:** Tamarind seed → a rough rub that thins the coat; Apple seed → weeks of cold; Some desert wildflower seeds → a big rain; Some Australian bush seeds → smoke from a fire; Tiny lettuce seed → a flash of light. *Why:* Each seed waits for the sign that tells the seed that a good time has come. A hard coat needs thinning. An apple seed needs winter to pass. A desert seed needs a rain big enough to wet the soil deep down. A bush seed senses that a fire has cleared the land. A tiny seed senses that they are near the surface.
7. (mcq) A seed is alive, and has water, warmth and air, but still does not sprout. What is this called?  
   Options: Dormancy / Germination / Swelling / Respiration  
   **Answer:** Dormancy. *Why:* Dormancy is when a living seed holds back, even though conditions seem right, until the right sign comes. Germination is the opposite, when the seed starts to grow. Picking 'dead' would be wrong, because a dormant seed is alive.
8. (mcq) A tiny mustard seed is sown 8 centimetres deep. A big chana is sown 8 centimetres deep too. Which is more likely to reach the top, and why?  
   Options: The chana, because the bigger food store can feed a long climb in the dark / The mustard, because the seed is lighter / Both reach the top, because shoots always find the light / Neither, because seeds cannot grow in soil  
   **Answer:** The chana, because the bigger food store can feed a long climb in the dark. *Why:* A shoot grows in the dark using the food in the seed. The chana has a big food store, so the shoot can travel a long way. The mustard seed has only a pinch of food and runs out first. Picking 'lighter' mixes up weight with food store.
9. (write) Explain in your own words why a rule says to sow a seed about two or three times as deep as the seed is wide.  
   **Answer:** A seed has to push a shoot up through the soil using the food stored inside, because there is no light underground. A deeper sowing means a longer climb. A bigger seed has more food and can climb further. The rule matches the depth to the amount of food, so the shoot arrives at the surface before the food runs out.. *Why:* A strong answer links the depth to the food store and says that the shoot lives on that food until it reaches the light.
10. (odd) Which of these seeds is the odd one out? Moong, chana, rice, mango stone.  
   Options: Moong / Chana / Rice / Mango stone  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Moong, chana and rice are dry-keeping seeds. They finish growing, dry out and can wait for years. A mango stone is a wet-keeping seed. It dies if the seed dries out, so the seed must be sown fresh.
11. (order) Put these in order, from first to last, as a moong seedling comes up from the soil.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* First the seed drinks and swells. The root comes out first and anchors the seedling. Then the stem forms a hook and pulls the fat seed leaves up out of the soil. In the light, the hook straightens and the leaves open.
12. (sort) Sort these seedlings. Do they lift their seed leaves into the air, or leave them under the soil?  
   **Answer:** moong → Seed leaves lifted into the air; rajma → Seed leaves lifted into the air; chana → Seed leaves stay under the soil; peas → Seed leaves stay under the soil. *Why:* In moong and rajma, the stem hooks and pulls the fat seed leaves up into the light. In chana and peas, the stem above the seed leaves does the growing, and the seed leaves stay in the soil.
13. (mcq) Bean sprouts from the market are long and white. Why are they grown in the dark?  
   Options: In the dark the sprouts stretch long and stay pale, which is what people want to eat / The dark makes them sweet / Light would kill the seeds / They grow slower in the dark  
   **Answer:** In the dark the sprouts stretch long and stay pale, which is what people want to eat. *Why:* A seedling in the dark stretches fast and makes almost no chlorophyll, so they stay pale. Growers use this on purpose. Picking 'light would kill the seeds' is wrong, because the same seeds grow well in light as green, shorter seedlings.
14. (truefalse) A pale seedling that has grown in a dark box begins to turn green within a day or two of being moved to a window.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Light switches on the making of chlorophyll, the green substance that catches sunlight. The tips usually start to turn greener within a day, and the leaves begin to open.
15. (perspective) Hasan is counting seeds on a plate. After four days, three seeds still have no root. Three friends give him advice.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the idea that seeds in one batch wake at different times. Farhan's advice fits that idea best: seeds can be late. Lakshmi's test is a useful check that does not wait. Nisha is right that some seeds really are dead, but she cannot know that yet. A strong answer picks one and gives the others their due.. *Why:* Seeds that wake late are common, and a firm, plump seed may still be alive. But some seeds really are dead. The best plan combines waiting with a check.
16. (draw) Draw a waking curve for a plate of 30 seeds. Put the hours along the bottom and the number of seeds with a root up the side. Show a lazy S shape, and mark where about half the seeds had woken.  
   **Answer:** A line graph with hours along the bottom and seeds with a root up the side. The line starts low, climbs steeply in the middle and flattens near the top. A mark is on the line where it reaches about 15 seeds.. *Why:* The shape shows that seeds wake at different times, with a few early, most in the middle and a few late.
### Sleep, Keys and Light (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (fill) A packet says that 80 percent of its seeds sprout. You want 40 plants. How many seeds should you sow? ____  
   **Answer:** ['50', 'fifty']. *Why:* 80 percent means 8 of every 10 seeds sprout. 40 plants is 5 lots of 8, so you need 5 lots of 10 seeds, which is 50 seeds. Sowing only 40 would give about 32 plants.
2. (mcq) In a desert, the first rain of the year is often light, and a long dry spell may follow. Which sowing plan is the safest for a farmer?  
   Options: Sow all the seed at the first rain / Sow part of the seed at the first rain and keep part for a later rain / Sow nothing until the end of the rainy season / Sow the seed in the hottest week  
   **Answer:** Sow part of the seed at the first rain and keep part for a later rain. *Why:* A farmer who sows in two lots is spreading the risk. If the first lot is lost in a dry spell, the second lot may survive. Seeds that wake at different times work in the same way. Picking 'all the seed at the first rain' risks losing everything.
3. (write) A farmer says that the seeds that did not come up after the first rain are dead. Plan a fair test to find out. What will you do, and what will you look for?  
   **Answer:** Dig up several seeds that did not come up. Press them with a nail to see if they are firm and plump. Cut a few open to see if the baby plant is pale and firm. Put some on a wet cloth in a warm place and count how many sprout over several days. If some sprout, the seeds were alive and only waiting.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a way to check whether waiting seeds are alive, and says what the result would show. Putting them in good conditions is the fairest test.
4. (mcq) A tamarind seed soaked in water for a day does not swell. A tamarind seed with a rubbed patch on the coat swells in the same time. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: The hard coat keeps water out until it is thinned / The rubbed seed is hungrier / The seed has no baby plant inside / The rub warms the seed  
   **Answer:** The hard coat keeps water out until it is thinned. *Why:* A thick hard coat is like a locked door. Rubbing thins the coat in one spot and lets water in. Picking 'no baby plant' is a mistake, because the untouched seed is alive and only locked.
5. (truefalse) A dormant seed is a dead seed that has not rotted yet.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A dormant seed is alive and is holding back until the right sign comes. Boiled seeds are dead, and a dormant seed will sprout when the seed gets the right key.
6. (mcq) Apple seeds taken straight from an apple sprout badly. After several weeks in a damp, cold fridge, many more of them sprout. What is the best explanation?

## Applications
- Seed companies test every batch and print the germination percentage on the packet, so a buyer knows how many seeds to sow.
- Farmers sow extra seed to allow for the seeds that will not sprout, using the germination percentage to work out how much.
- Farmers soak and dry seed before sowing, a trick called priming, so that the plants come up faster and more evenly.
- Seed banks, such as India's national gene bank in New Delhi and the Svalbard vault, keep seeds dry and very cold so that they live for decades.
- Nurseries rub or nick seeds with hard coats, such as tamarind, or soak them overnight, so that water can get in.
- Forest restoration teams collect wild seeds and treat their dormancy, with cold, smoke or a rub, before sowing them to grow new forests.
- Growers of bean sprouts and sprouted pulses keep seeds in the dark on purpose, so that the shoots grow long and pale.
- Farmers in dry regions sow in two or three lots across the rainy season, so that one dry spell cannot ruin the whole crop.
- Weed scientists study the soil seed bank to plan weeding, because weed seeds wake a few at a time for years.
- Families and farmers dry grain well and keep it in airtight containers, so that the seed stays alive for the next sowing.
- Plant breeders test many seeds from many plants, because a bigger sample gives a steadier answer.

## Open questions
- **How does a seed add up water, warmth, light, cold and chemical signs, and decide when to wake?** Scientists have found the sleep and wake messengers and many of the sensors. How the seed combines all the signs into one decision at the right moment is still being worked out.
- **How can a seed sense the difference between a light shower and a big rain?** Some desert seeds seem to know how much rain has fallen, and wake only after enough. Scientists have ideas about the chemicals in the coat, but nobody has a full explanation.
- **How does a seed carry a memory of cold or of the mother plant's weather?** Seeds from a plant that grew in a cool summer can behave differently from seeds of a plant that grew in a hot summer. Researchers are studying chemical marks on genes, and they do not yet agree on how much these marks explain.
- **How much of the spread in wake-up times is a plan made over many generations, and how much is chance?** Mathematics shows that spreading wake-up times helps in an unpredictable climate. It is very hard to tell, in a real plant, which part of the spread was selected and which part is only unavoidable small differences.
- **Can we find a way to store seeds of mango, jackfruit and rainforest trees for decades?** These seeds die if they dry out, so ordinary seed banks cannot keep them. Scientists are trying ways such as freezing only the tiny baby plant, but no method works for every kind yet.
- **What happens inside a seed that is kept at about minus 20 degrees Celsius for a hundred years?** Seed banks began only about a century ago, so nobody has waited long enough to know. The first tests of old stored seeds will take many decades.

If you woke one seed on each of the 24 cards, about how many points would you win from those 24 seeds? What would 24 sleeping seeds be worth? Which is better? **26 points from waking, 24 points from sleeping. Waking is a little better on average, but with much more risk.**. 13 SAFE cards at 2 points is 26 points. The 11 DANGER cards give 0. 24 sleeping seeds at 1 point each is 24 points. 26 is a little more than 24, so on average waking is slightly better. But any single seed can lose, so players who wake everything at once can lose a lot on a bad day. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a germination percentage? → How many seeds out of every hundred sprouted in a test.
- Do all seeds in a packet wake at the same time? → No. A few wake early, most in the middle and a few late.
- What is dormancy? → A living seed holding back until the right sign comes.
- Name one key that wakes a sleeping seed. → A rub on a hard coat, a spell of cold, light, smoke or a big rain.
- Why can a chana be sown deeper than a mustard seed? → Chana has a much bigger food store.
- Why is a seedling grown in the dark long and pale? → They stretch to search for light and make almost no chlorophyll.
- What do moong seedlings do with their seed leaves? → They lift them above the soil into the light.
- What do dry and cold do to a seed in storage? → They slow the ageing, so the seed lives longer.
- Which seeds cannot be dried and stored? → Wet-keeping seeds, such as mango and jackfruit.
