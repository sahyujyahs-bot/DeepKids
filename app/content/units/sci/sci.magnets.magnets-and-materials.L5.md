# Magnetic metals: why a magnet attracts one coin and not another
`sci.magnets.magnets-and-materials.L5`

**Summary.** A magnet lifts a one-rupee coin and a two-rupee coin, but leaves a five-rupee coin and a ten-rupee coin on the table. All four are metal, so why? In this unit you will test every coin in the house, and check whether colour or shine can tell you which metals are magnetic.

**Intro.** *Put a one-rupee coin and a golden five-rupee coin on the table. Now think about a magnet. Which coin will the magnet lift: both, one, or neither? Say what you think, and why.* Many people expect a magnet to attract every metal. Others expect it to attract every coin, or none. In the first activity, Four Coins and a Magnet, you test four coins and see. Then you test every coin in the house. In this unit you find out which metals are magnetic, and how to test them fairly. You also find out why two things made mostly of iron can give a magnet two different answers.

## Terms
- **Magnet**: A magnet is a thing with a magnetic field of its own. It can attract magnetic materials, and attract or repel other magnets. *e.g.* A fridge magnet holds a paper to the steel door by itself.
- **Magnetic field**: A magnetic field is the space around a magnet where its force acts. We cannot see it. *e.g.* A coin jumps to a magnet before they touch, because the coin is already inside the field.
- **Magnetic material**: A magnetic material is a material that a magnet attracts. It has no magnetic field of its own. *e.g.* A steel nail is a magnetic material. Two nails do not attract each other.
- **Non-magnetic**: We call a material non-magnetic when an ordinary magnet does not attract it. *e.g.* Copper, aluminium, brass, gold and wood are non-magnetic.
- **Attract**: To attract is to pull something closer with a force. *e.g.* A magnet attracts a one-rupee coin.
- **Metal**: A metal is a material that is usually shiny, carries heat and electricity well, and can be beaten into shape. *e.g.* Iron, copper, aluminium, gold and nickel are metals.
- **Alloy**: An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, melted together. Most of the other elements are metals too. *e.g.* Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc. Steel is an alloy of iron and a little carbon.
- **Steel**: Steel is an alloy made mostly of iron, with a little carbon. The carbon makes it harder than plain iron. *e.g.* Nails, safety pins and paper clips are made of steel.
- **Stainless steel**: Stainless steel is steel with chromium added, so that it does not rust. Some kinds also hold nickel or manganese. *e.g.* A steel plate and a one-rupee coin are two different kinds of stainless steel.
- **Brass**: Brass is a yellow alloy of copper and zinc. A magnet does not attract it. *e.g.* Many lamps, bells and keys are made of brass.
- **Cupronickel**: Cupronickel is a silver-coloured alloy of copper and nickel. The centre of a ten-rupee coin is cupronickel. *e.g.* The centre of a ten-rupee coin is three parts copper and one part nickel.
- **Atom**: An atom is one of the tiny particles that everything is made of. It is far too small to see. *e.g.* A one-rupee coin holds billions of billions of iron atoms.
- **Ferromagnetic**: A material is ferromagnetic when the tiny magnets of its atoms line up with their neighbours. A magnet attracts it strongly. *e.g.* Iron, nickel and cobalt are ferromagnetic at room temperature.
- **Property**: A property is something you can find out about a material by looking or testing. *e.g.* Colour, hardness and being magnetic are three properties of a metal.
- **Fair test**: In a fair test you change one thing and keep everything else the same. *e.g.* To compare coins fairly, use the same magnet, held the same way, on every coin.
- **Prediction**: A prediction is a guess made before a test, with a reason for it. *e.g.* I predict the magnet will lift the nail, because nails are steel.
- **Conclusion**: A conclusion is what your results let you say at the end of a test. *e.g.* My conclusion is that colour does not tell me which coins are magnetic.

## Explanations
### A magnet and a magnetic material are different things · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnet has a magnetic field of its own. A magnetic material is attracted by a magnet, but has no field of its own.

A magnet attracts a steel nail, and it can attract or repel another magnet. A steel nail is only a magnetic material. Put two nails together and nothing happens, because neither has a field of its own. While a nail touches a magnet, the nail acts as a magnet for a short time and can lift a pin. Take the magnet away and the nail soon stops. So there are three kinds of thing: magnets, magnetic materials and non-magnetic materials. There is a test that needs only the two bars themselves. Touch the end of one bar to the middle of the other, like the letter T. A bar magnet is strong at its ends and weak in its middle. So the bar whose end holds on to the other's middle is the magnet.
- e.g. In Two Nails, Then the T Test, two nails lay side by side and did not attract each other.
- e.g. A fridge door is a magnetic material. It attracts no pins, but a magnet sticks to it.
- e.g. Two fridge magnets can push each other apart. A magnet and a nail never push apart.
### Only a few metals are magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** At room temperature, only three pure metals are strongly magnetic: iron, nickel and cobalt.

There are about ninety metals among the elements. A magnet strongly attracts only iron, nickel and cobalt. A fourth metal, gadolinium, joins them only when it is cooler than about 20 degrees Celsius. All the other pure metals, such as copper, aluminium, zinc, tin, lead, silver and gold, are not attracted by an ordinary magnet. So being magnetic is rare among metals. It only seems common because iron is common. About a third of the Earth's mass is iron, and steel is the most used metal of all.
- e.g. A magnet attracts an iron tawa and a steel nail. It does not attract copper wire or aluminium foil.
- e.g. Gold and silver jewellery is not attracted. A steel spring inside a clasp is.
- e.g. Gadolinium is magnetic on a cool morning and not on a hot afternoon.
### An alloy is a mixture of metals · *defined*
**Definition:** An alloy is a metal melted and mixed with one or more other elements. When it cools, the atoms of the different elements are mixed together in one solid.

Very few metal things at home are one pure metal. Steel is iron with a little carbon. Stainless steel is steel with chromium, which stops rust. Brass is copper with zinc, and bronze is copper with tin. People make alloys because an alloy can be harder, cheaper or longer lasting than a pure metal. Pure iron is quite soft and rusts fast, and steel is hard. Pure gold bends too easily for a bangle, so jewellers mix in a little copper and silver. When you test a thing made of an alloy, you test all its metals together.
- e.g. A brass lamp from Nachiarkoil in Tamil Nadu is copper and zinc.
- e.g. Gold stamped 916 is 91.6 parts gold in every 100. The rest is mostly copper and silver.
- e.g. The golden five-rupee coin is nickel-brass: copper, zinc and a little nickel.
### Indian coins are made of four different alloys · *defined*
**Definition:** The coins in use today are made of stainless steel, nickel-brass, aluminium-bronze and cupronickel. A magnet attracts only the stainless steel.

One-rupee coins have been made of a stainless steel since 1992. Two-rupee coins have been made of the same steel since about 2007. This coin steel is iron with about one sixth chromium, and a magnet attracts it. The golden five-rupee coin, made in recent years, is nickel-brass. A ten-rupee coin has two alloys. Its golden ring is aluminium-bronze, which is copper with a little aluminium. Its silver centre is cupronickel. A magnet attracts neither part. Older coins can differ. Old two-rupee coins with eleven sides are cupronickel. Thick old five-rupee coins are cupronickel too. For a short time in the late 2000s, some five-rupee coins are said to have been made of steel. Mints change the alloy when a metal becomes too costly. Steel costs less than copper and nickel.
- e.g. In the Coin Year Test, the magnet lifted the one-rupee and two-rupee coins.
- e.g. The magnet left the golden five-rupee coin and the ten-rupee coin on the table.
- e.g. An eleven-sided two-rupee coin from the 1990s is not attracted, though it is worth the same.
### The list of metals in an alloy does not tell you whether it is magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** An alloy can be non-magnetic though it holds a magnetic metal. An alloy can be magnetic though none of its metals is.

Nickel is a magnetic metal. The centre of a ten-rupee coin is one quarter nickel, and a magnet does not attract it. A steel plate is about seven tenths iron, and a magnet does not attract it either. Railway crossings are made of a steel with about 13 parts manganese in every 100. It is mostly iron, and a magnet hardly attracts it. The wire that glows in an electric heater is four fifths nickel, and it is not magnetic. The surprise also runs the other way. In 1903 Friedrich Heusler mixed copper, manganese and aluminium. None of the three is magnetic, yet a magnet attracted the alloy strongly. So you cannot add up the metals in the recipe. You have to test the alloy itself.
- e.g. The centre of a ten-rupee coin is cupronickel. It is about one quarter nickel, yet a magnet does not attract it.
- e.g. Kitchen stainless steel is mostly iron. Even so, a magnet does not attract it, or attracts it only faintly.
- e.g. Heusler's alloy is made only of copper, manganese and aluminium. None of them is magnetic, and a magnet still attracts the alloy strongly.
### Every iron atom is a tiny magnet · *defined*
**Definition:** Each iron atom has a very small magnetic field of its own, which comes from its electrons. So does each nickel atom and each cobalt atom.

An electron is one of the tiny particles in every atom, and every electron is a tiny magnet. In most atoms the electrons are in pairs that point opposite ways and cancel. In an atom of iron, nickel or cobalt, a few electrons are left unpaired, so the whole atom is a tiny magnet. One atom's field is far too weak to notice. A magnet attracts a nail strongly only because the tiny magnets of billions of iron atoms point the same way and their fields add up. If neighbouring atoms point in different ways, their fields cancel, and the magnet finds almost nothing to hold.
- e.g. A one-rupee coin holds more than ten thousand billion billion iron atoms.
- e.g. In a steel nail, groups of atoms called domains each point one way. A magnet nearby turns them to face it.
- e.g. Your blood holds iron, but the iron atoms are spread far apart. Because they are not packed together in a crowd, a magnet does not attract blood.
### Why a magnet attracts coin steel and not kitchen steel · *idea*
A one-rupee coin and a steel plate are both mostly iron, with chromium to stop rust. The plate also holds nickel or manganese. These extra metals make the iron atoms pack in a different pattern. In coin steel, each iron atom has 8 nearest neighbours, as in plain iron. Scientists call this steel ferritic. In kitchen steel, each atom has 12 nearest neighbours, packed more tightly. Scientists call this steel austenitic. In the first pattern, the tiny magnets of neighbouring atoms line up with each other. In the second pattern, they do not line up, so their fields cancel. This account is simplified. The full reason why one packing lines up and the other does not needs quantum physics, and it is still hard to calculate. What X-ray pictures show for certain is that the two steels are packed differently.
- e.g. In Same Coins, Two Patterns, the same coins touched 4 neighbours in one pattern and 6 in the other.
- e.g. Bend or press a kitchen steel vessel hard, and some of it changes to the other packing. That is why a magnet may cling faintly near a rim.
- e.g. Above 912 degrees Celsius, even pure iron changes to the tighter packing.
### Being magnetic is something many atoms do together · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is ferromagnetic when the tiny magnets of neighbouring atoms line up with each other by themselves. This lining up belongs to the whole group of atoms.

We say 'iron is magnetic', as if each iron atom carried the property inside. But the same iron atoms give three different answers. In a nail they line up, and a magnet attracts the nail strongly. In a steel plate they are packed differently, and the magnet does not attract the plate. In iron hotter than 770 degrees Celsius the atoms shake too hard to stay lined up, and the magnet lets go. In all three, each atom is still a tiny magnet. What changes is whether the neighbours line up. So 'magnetic' describes what a great many atoms do together.
- e.g. Pierre Curie showed in 1895 that iron stops being attracted above a fixed temperature, and is attracted again when it cools.
- e.g. Kitchen steel and coin steel hold the same kind of iron atoms, in different packings.
- e.g. The nickel atoms in cupronickel sit among copper atoms, and their tiny magnets do not line up.
### No clue you can see tells you whether a metal is magnetic · *defined*
**Definition:** Colour, shine and the name 'steel' do not tell you whether a magnet will attract a metal thing. Only a test with a magnet does.

Many people use a quick rule, such as 'silver-coloured metals are magnetic'. A rule like this can be tested. Look for one thing that breaks it. Aluminium foil is silver-coloured, and a magnet does not attract it. That one thing is enough to show the rule is wrong. 'Shiny metals are magnetic' is broken by a brass lamp. 'Dull metals are not magnetic' is broken by a black iron tawa. 'Everything called steel is magnetic' is broken by a steel plate. A rule that fits ten things can still be wrong. One thing that breaks it settles the matter. Fitting more things only makes a rule more likely to be right.
- e.g. In Can You Tell by Looking?, a one-rupee coin and a steel plate looked alike and gave different answers.
- e.g. A black tawa and a shiny safety pin look different and both are attracted.
- e.g. The cap of a glass soda bottle looks painted, but it is thin steel, and a magnet lifts it.
### A fair test of metals · *defined*
**Definition:** A fair test of metals uses the same magnet, held the same way, on one thing at a time, with a prediction first and every result written down.

The thing you change is the metal thing being tested. The thing you watch is whether the magnet lifts or holds it. Everything else stays the same: the magnet, the face of the magnet you use, and how you bring it near. Write your prediction before each test, so that you cannot change it afterwards. Write 'yes', 'no' or 'weak' for every thing, even the dull ones. A strong magnet gives more 'weak' answers than a weak magnet, so say which magnet you used. Test more than one spot on a big thing, because a handle, a rivet or a base may be a different metal.
- e.g. In the Coin Year Test, every coin lay flat on the table and met the same face of the same magnet.
- e.g. A pressure cooker can be aluminium with a steel plate in its base, so its side and its base answer differently.
- e.g. A result of 'weak' on a steel tumbler is still a result. Write it down.
### What a magnet test can and cannot tell you · *defined*
**Definition:** A 'yes' shows that something magnetic is within reach of the magnet. A 'no' shows that nothing magnetic enough is within reach. Neither tells you the whole recipe.

A magnet test answers one small question about one spot. It can be fooled in two ways. A thing can say yes because magnetic metal is hidden inside it, like a steel coin wrapped in foil, or steel under a thin coat of brass. A thing can say no because its magnetic part is too far inside, or because its alloy is one the magnet does not attract. So a yes on a 'gold' chain shows it has steel in it. A no does not show the chain is gold, because brass says no too. A magnet test is quick and useful. It is strongest when it is used with other tests, such as colour, mass and the stamp on the metal.
- e.g. In Fool the Magnet Test, a ball of foil with a coin inside clung to the magnet.
- e.g. A magnet sticks to a 'brass' sheet that is really steel with a thin brass coat.
- e.g. A magnet does not stick to a steel plate, and the plate is still mostly iron.
### Choosing an alloy for the job: bone pins and scanners · *defined*
**Definition:** Engineers choose an alloy partly by how it answers a magnet. Some jobs need a magnetic alloy, and some need one that a magnet does not attract.

A hospital MRI scanner takes pictures of the inside of a body with a very strong magnet. A common hospital scanner has a field about 30,000 times as strong as the Earth's magnetic field, and some are stronger. A loose iron thing near it would fly towards it. So the pins and plates that surgeons put in a broken bone are made of titanium alloy, or of a stainless steel that a magnet does not attract. A compass case is made of brass, so that it does not disturb the needle. Other jobs need the opposite. The base of a pan for an induction stove must be a magnetic steel. The core of a motor is a soft magnetic steel with a little silicon in it.
- e.g. Before an MRI scan, people are asked to take off keys, hairpins and belts.
- e.g. A ship built to find sea mines has a hull that magnets do not attract.
- e.g. A fridge door seal holds because the fridge door is a magnetic steel.
### The same carbon atoms give two answers to a magnet · *defined*
**Definition:** Graphite and diamond are both made only of carbon atoms. A strong magnet pushes graphite away far more than it pushes diamond.

A very strong magnet pushes weakly on materials we call non-magnetic. The push on graphite, the grey material in pencil lead, is one of the largest. In graphite the carbon atoms lie in flat sheets, and electrons can move around rings of atoms in each sheet. In diamond the same atoms are joined in a different pattern, and the push is much smaller. In the 1930s Kathleen Lonsdale and K. S. Krishnan measured how crystals answer a magnet in different directions. Their numbers showed that the pattern of the atoms sets the answer. The same thing is true of the two steels. Coin steel and kitchen steel hold the same iron atoms, and only the pattern is different.
- e.g. A thin pencil lead can be pushed aside by a very strong magnet.
- e.g. Lonsdale and Krishnan published their measurements together in 1936.
- e.g. Coin steel and kitchen steel show the same idea with iron. The atoms are the same, and the pattern decides how a magnet answers.
### Iron and nickel from space, and a magnet nobody could make · *idea*
Iron meteorites are pieces of metal that fall from space. They are an alloy of iron and nickel, from the middle of small worlds that broke apart long ago. A magnet attracts them. In some meteorites that cooled over millions of years, the iron and nickel atoms sorted themselves into neat layers, one layer of each. This layered alloy is called tetrataenite. It is a strong permanent magnet, though it holds only iron and nickel. For a long time nobody could make it, because the atoms sort themselves far too slowly. In 2022 a team in Cambridge reported that adding phosphorus lets the layers form in seconds. Other scientists are still checking how well this works. If it does, strong magnets could be made from two common metals.
- e.g. The Earth's core is also an iron and nickel alloy, but it is too hot to be a permanent magnet.
- e.g. Today's strongest magnets need neodymium, which is costly to mine and clean.
- e.g. The same two metals, mixed without layers, make a much weaker magnet.

## Mostly agreed
- A magnet strongly attracts iron, nickel and cobalt, and many alloys that hold them. It does not attract most other metals. *How we know:* Anyone can repeat the test at home, and laboratories measure the same thing with exact instruments.
- A magnetic material has no magnetic field of its own. Two pieces of plain steel do not attract each other. *How we know:* Two nails lie side by side without moving. Each one is attracted when a magnet is brought near.
- The metals in an alloy do not tell you whether the alloy is magnetic. *How we know:* Robert Hadfield's manganese steel of 1882 is mostly iron and hardly magnetic. Friedrich Heusler's alloy of 1903 has no magnetic metal in it and is strongly magnetic.
- Strong magnetism comes from the tiny magnets of many atoms lining up with each other. *How we know:* Iron stops being attracted above 770 degrees Celsius and is attracted again when it cools, as Pierre Curie measured in 1895. The atoms are the same throughout.
- The way atoms are packed in a metal changes how the metal answers a magnet. *How we know:* X-ray pictures show different packings in magnetic and non-magnetic stainless steels. Heusler's alloy is magnetic only when its atoms are in an ordered pattern.
- Indian coins of the same value have been made of different alloys in different years. *How we know:* The mints publish the metal of each coin. A magnet attracts a new two-rupee coin and not an old eleven-sided one.

## How we found out
**Friedrich Heusler in Germany, and A. J. Bradley and J. W. Rodgers in Britain, Heusler reported his alloy in 1903. Bradley and Rodgers studied its atoms with X-rays in 1934..** By 1900 many scientists treated it as a rule: a magnetic alloy must hold iron, nickel or cobalt. Then a German mining engineer and chemist, Friedrich Heusler, melted copper, manganese and aluminium together. None of the three is attracted by a magnet. Yet the new alloy was attracted strongly. He reported this in 1903. A magnetic alloy with no iron, nickel or cobalt broke the rule that people trusted. Later work showed that the alloy was magnetic only when its atoms sat in a neat, ordered pattern. In 1934 two British scientists, Bradley and Rodgers, used X-rays to show this pattern. Today scientists have made many 'Heusler alloys'. The old rule was true for every alloy tested before 1903, and it was still wrong.  
*What people believed before:* That a magnetic alloy must hold iron, nickel or cobalt. Heusler's alloy showed that three non-magnetic metals, mixed in the right pattern, can make a magnetic alloy.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If a metal thing has iron in it, a magnet will attract it.** (Many people, and some school books; *rejected*)  
  For: It is true for nails, pins, tawas and most things made of plain steel.  
  Against: A steel plate is about seven tenths iron and is not attracted. Railway crossing steel is mostly iron and is hardly attracted. What matters is how the iron atoms are packed.
- **Steel vessels that a magnet sticks to are poor quality.** (Some shopkeepers and shoppers in India; *rejected*)  
  For: The best-known kitchen steel, called 304, is hardly attracted. So a strong pull shows that a vessel is some other steel.  
  Against: A magnet tells you how the atoms are packed. It does not tell you how well a steel resists rust. Some cheaper steels are not attracted either. Some good steels, used for spoons and induction pans, are attracted.
- **A magnet can tell a real coin from a fake one.** (Makers of ticket and vending machines; *debated*)  
  For: Machines do check how a coin answers a magnet and how it carries electricity. A fake made of the wrong alloy usually fails.  
  Against: A magnet alone gives only yes or no. A fake made of any steel would pass as a one-rupee coin. Machines also measure size, mass and other properties.
- **A magnetic alloy must have iron, nickel or cobalt in it.** (Most scientists before 1903; *rejected*)  
  For: Every magnetic alloy known then held at least one of the three.  
  Against: Heusler's alloy of copper, manganese and aluminium is strongly magnetic. Later alloys without the three metals have been made too.
- **Everything a metal does can be worked out from its atoms alone.** (Many physicists; *debated*)  
  For: The laws that atoms and electrons follow are well known. Nothing in an alloy breaks those laws.  
  Against: In 1972 the physicist Philip Anderson wrote that large groups of atoms behave in ways that cannot in practice be worked out from one atom. Even today, nobody can calculate exactly the temperature at which iron stops being magnetic.
- **A magnet that does not stick proves that a chain is real gold.** (Some buyers of jewellery; *rejected*)  
  For: Gold is not attracted, so a chain that sticks to a magnet has steel in it and is not solid gold.  
  Against: Brass, copper and many other cheap alloys are not attracted either. The clasp of a real gold chain can stick, because it has a steel spring. A 'no' rules out steel and nothing more.

## Roots
In this unit you laid the same coins in two patterns. You found that a rule that works in one pattern can fail in the other. Here we ask what that says about the iron in a coin, and about you.

**Deep truth.** In Same Coins, Two Patterns, one rule worked for coins in rows and failed for coins in a triangle. In the same way, the iron atoms in a coin and in a plate are alike, yet only the coin answers a magnet. Each atom is a tiny magnet, but a coin's answer comes from how the whole crowd of atoms sits together. So that answer belongs to the group, and you cannot find it by looking at one atom.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think that a thing's properties are inside its smallest parts. So we say 'iron is magnetic', as if each atom carried it alone, and we expect a thing with iron to be attracted.  
*Evidence:* A one-rupee coin and a steel plate hold the same kind of iron atoms, and a magnet attracts only the coin. Iron hotter than 770 degrees Celsius is not attracted, and it is attracted again when it cools. The atoms never change. Only the way they sit together changes.

- *who am i:* You are a crowd of about thirty trillion cells. No single cell of you reads this page. What you do shows only in the whole crowd.
- *what is life:* A living body does what none of its parts does alone, as a crowd of iron atoms does what no single atom does. Life is not one thing in one place. It is how many things sit and act together.
- *what is this world:* Much of the world that we call by one name, such as magnetic or alive, belongs to a crowd. If you look only at the smallest piece, you can miss it.

A one-rupee coin and a steel plate hold iron atoms that are alike. In the coin the tiny magnets line up with their neighbours, and the magnet attracts it. In the plate the packing is different, and the magnet does not. Many teachers have said that the truth of a thing is found by looking at it as it is, and not by its name. A coin's name says one rupee. Only the test shows what the crowd inside does.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4, the teaching of Uddalaka to his son:** “यथा सोम्यैकेन मृत्पिण्डेन सर्वं मृन्मयं विज्ञातं स्यात् / वाचारम्भणं विकारो नामधेयं मृत्तिकेत्येव सत्यम्” By knowing one lump of clay, you know all things made of clay. The differences are only names for the same clay. (The teaching sees one stuff under many names. A coin and a plate are both mostly iron. This unit adds that the same stuff can answer differently when it sits in a different pattern. The Upanishad gives a way of seeing, which the science neither proves nor tests.)
- **Tirukkural 355, by Thiruvalluvar:** “எப்பொருள் எத்தன்மைத் தாயினும் அப்பொருள் / மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்பது அறிவு.” Whatever the thing, and whatever its kind, wisdom is to see the real truth in it. (The couplet asks us to look at what a thing is, and not at its kind or its name. A magnet test does this for a coin. It ignores the stamped value and asks what the metal does.)
- **Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65:** “Etha tumhe, kālāmā, mā anussavena, mā paramparāya, mā itikirāya, mā piṭakasampadānena, mā takkahetu, mā nayahetu, mā ākāraparivitakkena, mā diṭṭhinijjhānakkhantiyā, mā bhabbarūpatāya, mā samaṇo no garūti. / Yadā tumhe, kālāmā, attanāva jāneyyātha: / ime dhammā kusalā, ime dhammā anavajjā, ime dhammā viññuppasatthā, ime dhammā samattā samādinnā hitāya sukhāya saṁvattantī, atha tumhe, kālāmā, upasampajja vihareyyātha.” Do not believe a thing because it is written, or handed down, or said by a teacher. When you know for yourselves what is good, then follow it. (The Buddha invites a test, and not an inherited rule. Sravani changes her sign after one coin breaks it. A rule that nothing can break is a belief. A rule that is tested can grow.)
- Science (settled): Each iron, nickel and cobalt atom is a tiny magnet. A magnet attracts a piece of metal strongly only when the tiny magnets of many neighbours line up.
- Science (settled): Different packings of the same atoms, in coin steel and in kitchen steel, give different answers to a magnet. X-ray pictures show the two packings.
- Science (open): Why one packing lines up and another does not needs quantum physics. Scientists still cannot calculate it exactly for a real steel.
- Other view, Many physicists: Nothing in a crowd of atoms is beyond the laws that each atom follows. The crowd's answer can in principle be worked out from the atoms, with enough computing. Saying that a coin's answer belongs to the group only says that the sum is hard to do.
- Practice, Find the crowd (3 min): Hold a coin in your hand. Look at it for a minute. Think about the billions of billions of atoms inside it. No single atom is the coin. Now put your other hand on your chest. Feel your heart beat. No single cell of yours beats it alone. Notice the crowd.

- Ask: A one-rupee coin and a steel plate hold the same kind of iron atoms. Is the 'magnetic' in the atoms, or in how they sit together?
- Ask: You are a crowd of cells. What can you do that no single cell of yours does?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A coin and a steel plate hold the same iron atoms. So where is the 'magnetic': in the atoms, or in the way they are packed? (hint: Think about what changed between the coin and the plate, and what stayed the same.)
- *deep:* One thing that breaks a rule is enough to end it. How many things that fit a rule are enough to prove it? (hint: Before 1903, every magnetic alloy that anyone had tested held iron, nickel or cobalt.)
- *big:* A coin from 1998 and a coin from 2015 are different metals and buy the same things. What makes a coin worth two rupees? (hint: Think about what would happen if a shopkeeper refused the older coin.)

## Story: The Coin That Went the Wrong Way
*A made-up story. The science is real. Indian two-rupee coins made in the 1990s have eleven sides and are cupronickel, which a magnet does not attract. Two-rupee coins made since about 2007 are a stainless steel that a magnet attracts. Visakhapatnam really has a large steel plant. Sravani, Lakshmamma, the judge, the school and the machine are imagined.*

Every afternoon at half past one, Lakshmamma counted coins. She ran the canteen at the school in Visakhapatnam, and children paid for their idlis and lemon rice in coins. By the end of lunch the steel bowl beside her stove was full.

Sravani, who was ten, often stayed to help. She liked the sound the coins made. She did not like how long it took. There were ones in one heap, twos in another, and then fives and tens. Lakshmamma's fingers were quick, but the heap was always big.

Three weeks before Science Day, Sravani found a round black magnet in her uncle's box of broken things. It had come out of an old speaker. She took it to the canteen and dipped it into the bowl, just to see.

It came up bristling with coins. She looked at them. Every one was a one or a two. She dipped again. Ones and twos. Not a single golden five clung to it, and not a single ten.

'It likes the small money,' said Lakshmamma, and laughed.

That night Sravani drew a machine. It took her nine days and two shoe boxes to build it. A coin went in at the top and slid flat down a cardboard slope. Halfway down, under the cardboard, sat the speaker magnet. A one or a two stopped dead above the magnet, as if a hand had caught it. A five or a ten slid past and dropped into a cup at the bottom. A cardboard flap swept the caught coins sideways into a second cup.

She tested it with the whole canteen bowl, four hundred and twelve coins. It did not make one mistake. She painted a sign for the front: COIN SORTER. ONES AND TWOS TO THE LEFT. FIVES AND TENS TO THE RIGHT.

On Science Day the school hall was full of tables. There was a volcano, a water filter and three solar cookers. The judge was a tall man with a steel plant badge on his shirt. He stopped at each table and asked one question.

At Sravani's table he asked nothing. He read her sign, took a coin from his own pocket and dropped it in at the top.

It was a two-rupee coin. Sravani saw the big 2 on it as it went by. It slid down the slope, passed over the magnet without slowing at all, and fell into the cup for fives and tens.

Somebody behind the judge laughed. 'Your machine cannot count,' said a boy from Class 7.

Sravani's face went hot. Four hundred and twelve coins, and it had to go wrong now. She wanted to say that the slope was bumped, or that the coin was thrown too hard. But she had watched it. The coin had gone past the magnet as if the magnet were not there.

She picked the coin out of the cup and held it. It was a little heavier than the twos she knew. She turned it. It had corners. She counted them with her thumbnail: eleven sides. Under the lions was a year, 1998.

She took a canteen two from the other cup. It was round, and its year was 2014. She held the speaker magnet near it and the coin jumped on with a click. She held the magnet near the judge's coin. Nothing. She pressed them together and let go, and the old coin fell on the table and spun.

'The machine did not make a mistake,' she said slowly. She was working it out as she spoke. 'It cannot read the number. It never could. It asks each coin one question. Are you made of the metal a magnet attracts? This coin said no.'

'It is a two-rupee coin,' said the boy.

'It is worth two rupees,' said Sravani. 'It is not made of the same metal. Somebody changed the metal and kept the number.'

Lakshmamma had come over from the canteen with flour on her hands. 'Those old ones with the corners,' she said. 'I have not seen one in years. They always felt heavier in the palm. I thought the new ones were only thinner.'

'Does anybody have more old coins?' Sravani asked the crowd.

The watchman's mother, who sold guavas at the gate, untied a knot in the end of her sari. She had kept three coins there since her daughter's wedding. One was a thick five-rupee coin, silver-coloured and heavy. Two were twos with corners. Sravani sent all three down the slope. All three slid past the magnet and fell to the right.

The judge was smiling now. 'So what does your machine sort?' he asked. It was his one question.

Sravani looked at her sign. Then she turned it over and borrowed a marker. She wrote on the back in large letters: STEEL COINS TO THE LEFT. ALL OTHER COINS TO THE RIGHT. CHECK THE OLD ONES BY HAND.

'That is a more honest sign,' said the judge. 'In my work we test every batch of steel, even when the label says what it is. The label is what somebody wrote. The test is what the metal does.'

The next afternoon the machine stood beside the stove in the canteen. Lakshmamma poured in the bowl, a handful at a time, and the coins clicked left and right. Once in a while a coin with corners slid to the wrong side. Each time, she held it up to the light, read its year aloud and dropped it in a jar on the shelf.

By the end of the term there were seven coins in the jar. Sravani asked if she could keep them. She did not spend them. She taped them in a row in her science notebook, oldest first, and under each one she wrote its year and one more word: NO.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a magnet does not read the number on a coin. It responds only to the metal. Indian one-rupee and two-rupee coins made today are a stainless steel that a magnet attracts. Five-rupee and ten-rupee coins are copper alloys that a magnet does not attract. Older two-rupee coins with eleven sides are cupronickel, a copper and nickel alloy, so the magnet does not attract them, though they are worth the same. The machine sorted by material all along. The value stamped on a coin and the metal it is made of are two separate facts, and only a test finds the second.

- Ask: The machine sorted 412 coins without a mistake. Why did it send the judge's coin the other way?
- Ask: What question does the magnet ask each coin?
- Ask: Why did Sravani change her sign?
- Ask: The old coin and the new coin are both worth two rupees. What is different about them?
- Ask: The judge tests steel even when the label says what it is. Why?

  - Note: In some steels, such as 304, hard bending changes part of the iron to the packing that a magnet attracts. So a faint cling can appear at a bend, and the rest of the spoon stays the same.   - Check: After many bends, a steel spoon clings faintly to a magnet at the bend only. What is the best explanation? → **The bend changed how the iron atoms are packed there**

## Worksheets
### Magnets, metals and alloys (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is the difference between a magnet and a magnetic material?  
   Options: A magnet has its own field. A magnetic material is only attracted. / A magnetic material is stronger than a magnet. / A magnet is iron, and a magnetic material is not. / There is no difference.  
   **Answer:** A magnet has its own field. A magnetic material is only attracted.. *Why:* A magnet has a field of its own and can attract or repel. A magnetic material, such as a steel nail, is attracted but has no field of its own. Two nails do not pull each other.
2. (truefalse) Two steel nails lying side by side attract each other.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A nail is a magnetic material, not a magnet. In Two Nails, Then the T Test, the two nails did nothing until a magnet came near.
3. (mcq) Bar P's end holds firmly to bar Q's middle. Bar Q's end barely holds to P's middle. Which is the magnet?  
   Options: Bar P / Bar Q / Both / Neither  
   **Answer:** Bar P. *Why:* A bar magnet pulls hardest at its ends and least in its middle. So the bar whose end holds is the magnet.
4. (match) Match each metal to what an ordinary magnet does to it.  
   **Answer:** Iron → Attracts it strongly; Nickel → Attracts it strongly; Copper → Does not attract it; Aluminium → Does not attract it; Gold → Does not attract it. *Why:* Only iron, nickel and cobalt are strongly magnetic at room temperature. Most other metals are not attracted.
5. (sort) Sort each thing by what a magnet does to it.  
   **Answer:** steel nail → Attracted; brass key → Not attracted; aluminium foil → Not attracted; one-rupee coin → Attracted; golden five-rupee coin → Not attracted; iron tawa → Attracted. *Why:* Nails, one-rupee coins and tawas hold iron in a form a magnet attracts. Brass, foil and the five-rupee coin are copper or aluminium alloys.
6. (fill) Brass is an alloy of copper and ____.  
   **Answer:** ['zinc']. *Why:* Brass is copper with zinc. Neither metal is magnetic, and nor is brass.
7. (mcq) Which Indian coin made today does an ordinary magnet attract?  
   Options: The one-rupee coin / The golden five-rupee coin / The ten-rupee coin / All of them  
   **Answer:** The one-rupee coin. *Why:* The one-rupee coin is stainless steel. The golden five-rupee coin is nickel-brass, and the ten-rupee coin is two copper alloys.
8. (odd) Three of these are not attracted by a magnet. Which one is attracted?  
   Options: Golden five-rupee coin / Ten-rupee coin / Brass bell / One-rupee coin  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The one-rupee coin is steel. The five-rupee coin, the ten-rupee coin and the brass bell are copper alloys.
9. (count) A bowl holds 3 one-rupee, 2 two-rupee, 2 five-rupee and 1 ten-rupee coin. How many does a magnet lift?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* The 3 one-rupee coins and the 2 two-rupee coins are steel. The other 3 coins are copper alloys.
10. (truefalse) Every metal thing with iron inside it is attracted by a magnet.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A steel plate is mostly iron and a magnet does not attract it. How the iron atoms are packed matters.
11. (mcq) Which three pure metals are strongly magnetic at room temperature?  
   Options: Iron, nickel and cobalt / Copper, zinc and tin / Gold, silver and aluminium / Iron, copper and gold  
   **Answer:** Iron, nickel and cobalt. *Why:* Only iron, nickel and cobalt are strongly attracted. Gadolinium joins them only below about 20 degrees Celsius.
12. (fill) Steel is an alloy of iron and a little ____.  
   **Answer:** ['carbon']. *Why:* The carbon makes steel harder than plain iron.
13. (order) Put the steps of a fair test of metals in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Write the prediction', 'Test one thing with the same magnet', 'Write the result at once', 'Write a conclusion']. *Why:* The prediction comes first, so you cannot change it later. The result is written at once, and the conclusion comes last.
14. (perspective) Three people talk about how to tell steel from other metals.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses a result, such as the aluminium foil that is silver-coloured but not attracted. It chooses a view and gives a fair point from another.. *Why:* All three have a point. Looks are not reliable, a magnet is a good quick test, and a magnet answers only one small question.
15. (draw) Draw the T test with two bars. Mark which bar is the magnet and where the strong pull is.  
   **Answer:** One bar standing with its end touching the middle of a second bar, in the shape of a T. The standing bar is labelled magnet, with an arrow at the touching end.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the magnet's end touching the other bar's middle. The pull is at the touching end.
16. (write) In Four Coins and a Magnet, a magnet lifted two coins and left two. Explain why, using the word alloy.  
   **Answer:** Each coin is an alloy, a mixture of metals. The one-rupee and two-rupee coins are steel, which a magnet attracts. The five-rupee and ten-rupee coins are copper alloys, which it does not.. *Why:* A strong answer says the coins are made of different alloys. It does not say they are heavier or lighter.
### Why the same iron answers differently (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Kitchen steel holds extra nickel or manganese. What do these extra metals change about the iron atoms?  
   Options: How the atoms are packed / How many atoms there are / The size of each atom / The colour of each atom  
   **Answer:** How the atoms are packed. *Why:* The extra metals make the iron atoms pack in a different pattern. In that pattern, the tiny magnets of neighbours do not line up.
2. (truefalse) Each iron atom is a tiny magnet.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A few of its electrons are unpaired, so the whole atom has a small magnetic field. One atom's field is far too weak to notice.
3. (mcq) One iron atom is hardly attracted by a magnet, but a nail is attracted strongly. Why?  
   Options: The tiny magnets of billions of atoms line up and add together / A nail is heavier than an atom / The nail has more electrons than one atom has / A nail has been rubbed on a magnet  
   **Answer:** The tiny magnets of billions of atoms line up and add together. *Why:* One atom's field is very weak. In a nail, billions of tiny magnets point the same way and their fields add up.
4. (match) Match each alloy to the metals it is made of.  
   **Answer:** Brass → Copper and zinc; Bronze → Copper and tin; Steel → Iron and a little carbon; Cupronickel → Copper and nickel; Nickel-brass → Copper, zinc and a little nickel. *Why:* Every alloy is a recipe of metals, and each recipe answers a magnet in its own way.
5. (fill) Iron stops being attracted by a magnet when it is hotter than about ____ degrees Celsius.  
   **Answer:** ['770']. *Why:* Above 770 degrees Celsius the atoms shake too hard to stay lined up. Pierre Curie measured this in 1895.
6. (truefalse) Cupronickel holds nickel, which is magnetic, so a magnet attracts cupronickel.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The nickel atoms sit among copper atoms, and their tiny magnets do not line up. The alloy is not attracted.
7. (count) A made-up coin steel weighs 60 grams and is one sixth chromium. How many grams of chromium does it hold?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* One sixth of 60 is 10. So the coin holds 10 grams of chromium.
8. (sort) Do the tiny magnets of neighbouring atoms line up in each of these?  
   **Answer:** a steel nail → Line up; a steel plate → Do not line up; a one-rupee coin → Line up; iron at 800 degrees Celsius → Do not line up; the silver centre of a ten-rupee coin → Do not line up. *Why:* Coin steel and nails keep the packing of plain iron. Kitchen steel has a different packing. Red-hot iron shakes too much. In cupronickel the nickel is spread among copper.
9. (odd) Three of these alloys are attracted by a magnet. Which one is not?  
   Options: Alnico / Permalloy / Neodymium magnet alloy / Nichrome  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Nichrome is mostly nickel, yet the chromium stops the nickel atoms lining up. The other three are magnetic.
10. (mcq) In 1903 Friedrich Heusler mixed copper, manganese and aluminium. What surprised scientists?  
   Options: A magnet attracted the alloy, though none of its metals is magnetic / The alloy was lighter than water / The alloy melted in the cold / The alloy contained a lot of iron  
   **Answer:** A magnet attracted the alloy, though none of its metals is magnetic. *Why:* Before then, every magnetic alloy known held iron, nickel or cobalt. Heusler's alloy broke that rule.
11. (mcq) Why is a compass case made of brass and not of steel?  
   Options: Steel would pull the needle away from north / Brass is cheaper than steel / Steel is too soft / Brass is a magnet  
   **Answer:** Steel would pull the needle away from north. *Why:* The needle is a small magnet. A magnetic case would attract it. Brass is not attracted.
12. (truefalse) Graphite and diamond are both only carbon, so a strong magnet pushes them equally.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Graphite is pushed away far more than diamond. The pattern of the same atoms sets the answer.
13. (order) Put these in order, from the atom to the pull you feel.  
   **Answer:** ['Each iron atom is a tiny magnet', 'Neighbouring tiny magnets line up', 'The fields of billions of atoms add up', 'A magnet attracts the nail']. *Why:* Each step needs the one before it. If the neighbours do not line up, the fields cancel and the magnet finds almost nothing to hold.
14. (write) Explain with atoms why red-hot iron stops being attracted, and is attracted again when it cools.  
   **Answer:** Each iron atom is still a tiny magnet. When iron is hotter than 770 degrees Celsius, the atoms shake too hard for the tiny magnets to stay lined up. Their fields cancel. When it cools, they line up again.. *Why:* A strong answer says the atoms stay the same and only the lining up changes. It says that being magnetic belongs to the crowd of atoms.
15. (write) Why might a mint change the alloy of a coin and keep its value?  
   **Answer:** A metal can become too costly. Steel costs less than copper and nickel. The value is a decision stamped on the coin, and it does not depend on the metal.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the stamped value from the metal. It names a reason such as cost.
16. (perspective) Three people talk about a steel vessel that a magnet clings to.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the stamps, such as 304 giving no cling and 430 giving a cling. It sees that no cling is not the same as good, and that induction needs a cling.. *Why:* The seller treats a no as proof of quality. The scientist and the cook each name a true reason, for different jobs.
17. (draw) Draw two zoom circles of atoms with arrows. In one the arrows line up. In the other they do not.  
   **Answer:** Two circles, each with a grid of small balls. In the first all arrows point the same way, labelled coin steel. In the second the arrows point different ways, labelled kitchen steel.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the same atoms in both circles, and a different pattern of arrows.
### Testing ideas about metals (challenge, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Before 1903, every magnetic alloy that anyone had tested held iron, nickel or cobalt. What did that show?  
   Options: That it fitted every alloy tested so far, and that is all / That no other magnetic alloy can exist / That the rule was proved / That Heusler's alloy must hold iron  
   **Answer:** That it fitted every alloy tested so far, and that is all. *Why:* A rule that fits many things can still be wrong. One new thing, Heusler's alloy, broke it.
2. (mcq) In a made-up test, 6 of 6 one-rupee coins were lifted. A two-rupee coin from 1998 was not. What does it show?  
   Options: The value of a coin does not tell you the answer / The magnet had got weaker / Two-rupee coins are never lifted / The coin was lighter than the others  
   **Answer:** The value of a coin does not tell you the answer. *Why:* Two-rupee coins from 1998 are cupronickel, and newer ones are steel. One coin of a value that answers differently is enough to break the rule 'value tells the answer'.
3. (truefalse) A magnet that does not stick to a chain proves the chain is real gold.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Brass and copper do not stick either. A 'no' rules out steel and nothing more.
4. (mcq) In the Bent Spoon, why test a spot 5 centimetres away that is never bent?  
   Options: To compare, so that bending is the only change / To use up the spoon evenly / Because the magnet is weak at the bent spot / To check that the spoon is clean  
   **Answer:** To compare, so that bending is the only change. *Why:* Without the unbent spot, a cling could come from the spoon or the magnet. The comparison shows that bending caused the change.
5. (count) A coin sorter was run with 20 coins and sorted 17 correctly. What percentage was correct?  
   **Answer:** 85. *Why:* 17 out of 20 is 17 times 5 out of 100, which is 85 percent.
6. (mcq) You compare how two kinds of steel answer a magnet. What must stay the same?  
   Options: The magnet, its face and how you bring it near / The size of the steel pieces only / The room you sit in / Nothing, since the steels are different  
   **Answer:** The magnet, its face and how you bring it near. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing, here the steel. The magnet and the way you use it stay the same.
7. (fill) In a fair test you change ____ thing and keep everything else the same.  
   **Answer:** ['one']. *Why:* If you change two things, you cannot tell which one caused the result.
8. (sort) Can a magnet test alone settle each question?  
   **Answer:** Is this spoon attracted by the magnet? → Can settle; Does this foil ball hide something magnetic? → Can settle; Is this chain solid gold? → Cannot settle; Is this vessel good for health? → Cannot settle; Is this coin real? → Cannot settle. *Why:* A magnet test answers only whether something magnetic is within reach. A fake coin of the right steel would pass.
9. (order) Put these in order, from a rule to a better rule.  
   **Answer:** ['Write a rule', 'Test many things', 'Find a thing that breaks the rule', 'Change the rule']. *Why:* A rule is only a guess until it is tested. One thing that breaks it is enough to change it.
10. (odd) Three of these keep a test fair. Which one does not?  
   Options: Use the same magnet on every coin / Hold it the same way each time / Use a stronger magnet on a coin you expect to fail / Write your prediction first  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Changing the magnet for one coin means the coin is not the only thing that changed.
11. (truefalse) One thing that fits a rule proves the rule is true.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Many things that fit make a rule more likely, but only one thing that breaks it is needed to end it.
12. (mcq) Why are people asked to take off hairpins and keys before an MRI scan?  
   Options: The scanner's very strong magnet pulls on steel / Steel gives wrong pictures of the bones / The scanner heats all metal / Hairpins are made of iron only  
   **Answer:** The scanner's very strong magnet pulls on steel. *Why:* A common MRI magnet is about 30,000 times as strong as the Earth's field. A loose steel thing would fly towards it.
13. (write) A friend says: my magnet sticks to this steel vessel, so it must be poor steel. What would you ask or test?  
   **Answer:** I would ask which steel it is, and test other vessels with the same magnet. A cling shows how the atoms are packed. It does not show how well steel resists rust. Some good steels, used for induction pans, cling.. *Why:* A strong answer separates 'clings' from 'poor quality', and suggests a comparison.
14. (write) Design a fair test of three steel vessels. Say what you change, what stays the same and what you record.  
   **Answer:** Change the vessel. Keep the same magnet, the same face of it, and the same spots: base, side and rim. Write a prediction first. Record yes, weak or no for each spot in a table. Write a conclusion.. *Why:* A strong answer names one changed thing, the things kept the same, and a table.
15. (perspective) Three people talk about checking coins with a magnet.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the eleven-sided two-rupee coin, which is real and not attracted. It rejects the shopkeeper because a steel fake would pass. It says the maker's several checks are sensible.. *Why:* The shopkeeper's view fails both ways: a real coin can fail and a fake can pass. A magnet is one clue.
16. (draw) Draw the results table for a fair test of five metal things. Add columns for the thing, prediction and result.  
   **Answer:** A table with three columns headed thing, prediction and result, and five rows with one metal thing named in each row.. *Why:* A strong table has a prediction column filled in before the result column.
17. (mcq) Which of these can nobody yet calculate exactly from the atoms alone?  
   Options: The temperature where iron stops being magnetic / The number of coins in a bowl / The mass of a steel nail / The colour of brass  
   **Answer:** The temperature where iron stops being magnetic. *Why:* Large groups of atoms behave in ways that are very hard to work out from one atom. Iron's exact temperature is one of them.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a magnet, and not only a magnetic material?  
   Options: A fridge magnet / A steel nail / An iron tawa / A steel spoon  
   **Answer:** A fridge magnet. *Why:* A fridge magnet has a field of its own. A nail, a tawa and a spoon are attracted, and have no field of their own.
2. (mcq) Which of these metals is strongly attracted by a magnet?  
   Options: Nickel / Copper / Aluminium / Gold  
   **Answer:** Nickel. *Why:* Iron, nickel and cobalt are strongly magnetic. Copper, aluminium and gold are not attracted.
3. (mcq) A new two-rupee coin is attracted by a magnet. What is it made of?  
   Options: Stainless steel / Brass / Gold / Aluminium foil  
   **Answer:** Stainless steel. *Why:* Two-rupee coins made since about 2007 are a stainless steel that a magnet attracts. Eleven-sided coins from the 1990s are cupronickel.
4. (mcq) Why do you write your prediction before you test?  
   Options: So that you cannot change it afterwards / So the test takes less time / So the magnet is stronger / So that the answer is always right  
   **Answer:** So that you cannot change it afterwards. *Why:* A prediction written afterwards could be changed to fit. Written first, it shows what you really expected.
5. (mcq) The rule says: shiny metals are magnetic. Which thing breaks it?  
   Options: A shiny brass lamp / A black iron tawa / A steel nail / A one-rupee coin  
   **Answer:** A shiny brass lamp. *Why:* A brass lamp is shiny and not attracted. One thing like this is enough to show the rule is wrong.
6. (mcq) A one-rupee coin and a steel plate are both mostly iron. What differs?  
   Options: How the iron atoms are packed / How many iron atoms there are / The colour of the iron / Nothing  
   **Answer:** How the iron atoms are packed. *Why:* Extra nickel or manganese in the plate changes the packing. Then the tiny magnets of the atoms do not line up.
7. (mcq) Why does a magnet attract a steel nail strongly?  
   Options: Tiny magnets of billions of atoms line up / One iron atom is very strong / The nail is hot / The nail is a magnet already  
   **Answer:** Tiny magnets of billions of atoms line up. *Why:* Each atom is a very weak magnet. In a nail, billions of them point the same way, and their fields add up.
8. (mcq) A ball of foil clings to a magnet. What do you know?  
   Options: Something magnetic is inside it / The foil is steel / The ball is a magnet / The ball is full of iron  
   **Answer:** Something magnetic is inside it. *Why:* Foil is not attracted. So a cling shows that magnetic metal, such as steel, is hidden inside.
9. (mcq) A magnet does not stick to a gold-coloured chain. What can you say?  
   Options: It has no steel in it, but it may still be brass / It is real gold / It holds no metal / It is plastic  
   **Answer:** It has no steel in it, but it may still be brass. *Why:* A no rules out steel and nothing more. Brass, copper and gold all say no.
10. (mcq) What is the honest thing to say about magnetic alloys today?  
   Options: New magnetic alloys are still being found and explained / Everything about them is known / No new ones can exist / Only iron alloys matter  
   **Answer:** New magnetic alloys are still being found and explained. *Why:* Heusler alloys are still studied. Why atoms line up in one pattern and not in another is still hard to calculate.
11. (mcq) Which fact shows that a rule about magnetic alloys can be wrong?  
   Options: Heusler's alloy has no iron, nickel or cobalt, and is magnetic / A steel nail is attracted / Copper is not attracted / Brass is yellow  
   **Answer:** Heusler's alloy has no iron, nickel or cobalt, and is magnetic. *Why:* Before 1903 every magnetic alloy held iron, nickel or cobalt. Heusler's alloy broke the rule.
12. (mcq) In a coin sorter, why might an old coin go to the wrong cup?  
   Options: Its metal differs from a new coin of the same value / It is older, so it is heavier / The magnet knows the year / The slope remembers it  
   **Answer:** Its metal differs from a new coin of the same value. *Why:* The magnet answers to the metal, not the value or the year. An old cupronickel coin is not attracted, though it buys the same things.

## Applications
- Scrap dealers pull steel out of a mixed heap with a magnet. Copper, brass and aluminium stay behind and are sorted another way.
- A magnet test in a steel vessel shop tells a buyer which kind of stainless steel a vessel is. Only some vessels work on an induction stove.
- Vending machines, ticket machines and bus fare boxes check how a coin answers a magnet, along with its size and weight.
- A hospital MRI scanner uses a magnet at least 30,000 times as strong as the Earth's. Pins in bones are made of metals that a magnet does not attract.
- A compass case is brass, so that it does not turn the needle. A steel case would.
- The core of an electric motor or a transformer is a soft magnetic steel with a little silicon. It follows the field and lets go quickly.
- The mint chooses a coin's alloy. Steel is cheaper than copper and nickel, and a magnet tells the two kinds apart.

## Open questions
- **Why do the atoms of iron line up in one pattern and not in another?** The full reason needs quantum physics. Even the best computers give only rough answers for a real steel, so scientists still argue about the details.
- **Can nobody work out exactly the temperature where iron stops being magnetic?** Many atoms act together, and each one affects its neighbours. Calculating such a crowd exactly is one of the hardest problems in physics.
- **Can strong magnets be made from common metals instead of rare ones?** The strongest magnets today need neodymium, which is costly to mine. Work on iron and nickel layers, like those in meteorites, is still being checked by other scientists.
- **Are there magnetic alloys that no one has thought of?** Heusler's alloy of 1903 surprised everyone. Many similar alloys have been made since, and no rule yet tells scientists which new mixtures will be magnetic.
- **Why do very small pieces of a magnetic metal sometimes lose their magnetism?** Below a certain size, the tiny magnets in a piece of metal may keep changing direction by themselves. Scientists are still studying where this happens and why.

## Connected maths
- In a Coin Year Test, 13 of 20 coins were lifted by the magnet. What percentage was lifted? **65 percent.**. 13 out of 20 is 13 times 5 out of 100, which is 65 out of 100. So 65 percent were lifted. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A 25 gram bangle is 22-carat gold, which holds 91.6 grams of gold in every 100 grams. How many grams of gold does the bangle hold? **About 22.9 grams.**. 25 times 91.6 divided by 100 is 22.9. So the bangle holds about 22.9 grams of gold and about 2.1 grams of copper and silver. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- The centre of a ten-rupee coin is three parts copper and one part nickel. A made-up centre weighs 20 grams. How much copper and how much nickel does it hold? **15 grams of copper and 5 grams of nickel.**. Three parts and one part make 4 parts. 20 divided by 4 is 5 grams in each part. So the copper is 3 times 5, which is 15 grams, and the nickel is 5 grams. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A coin sorter made 6 mistakes with 20 coins on day 2, and 2 mistakes with the same 20 coins on day 3. By what fraction did the mistakes fall? **By two thirds.**. The mistakes fell from 6 to 2, which is 4 fewer. 4 out of 6 is two thirds. So the mistakes fell by two thirds. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- Iron stops being attracted above 770 degrees Celsius. A kitchen is at 30 degrees. How many degrees hotter must the iron become? **740 degrees.**. 770 minus 30 is 740. So the iron must become 740 degrees hotter, and a little more, before the magnet lets go. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L5`)
- A made-up coin steel weighs 6 grams and is one sixth chromium. How many grams of chromium does it hold, and how many grams of other metals? **1 gram of chromium and 5 grams of other metals.**. One sixth of 6 is 1. So there is 1 gram of chromium. The other 5 grams are mostly iron. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- A bowl holds 8 one-rupee, 6 two-rupee, 3 five-rupee and 2 ten-rupee coins. What fraction of the coins can the magnet rod catch? **14 nineteenths.**. The rod catches the 8 one-rupee and 6 two-rupee coins, which is 14 coins. The bowl holds 19 coins in all. So the fraction is 14 out of 19. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
