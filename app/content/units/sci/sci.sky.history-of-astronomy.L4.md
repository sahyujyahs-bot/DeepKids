# History of astronomy: how people changed their minds about who moves, the Earth or the sky
`sci.sky.history-of-astronomy.L4`

**Summary.** Every sky-watcher in history saw the same thing: the Sun rises, crosses the sky and sets, while the ground stays still. Yet in the year 499, Aryabhata wrote that the ground is what turns. How could anyone tell? Your child sits in a spinning chair with eyes shut and finds out how little a steady turn can be felt. She lights a ball with a lamp to repeat what Galileo saw when he pointed a telescope at Venus. She tests how finely she can measure an angle, as Tycho Brahe did, and meets the small gap of 8 arcminutes that made Kepler give up perfect circles. Along the way come the giant stone dials of Jaipur, the first Indian ideas about a Sun at the centre, and why a sundial and a phone never quite agree.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes in a smooth, steadily turning chair. Can you tell that you are turning? Open your eyes, and the room seems to spin round you. So who is really turning, you or the room? And what does that have to do with the Sun and the Earth?* For thousands of years, people looked up and saw the Sun and the stars move round them. A few said the Earth must be the one that turns. Nobody could feel it. In this unit you sit in a spinning chair and try to feel a steady turn. You make a lamp and a ball show the phases of Venus. You build a quadrant and find how exactly you can measure an angle. You draw an ellipse with string and pins. And you learn how an 8-arcminute gap in a table of numbers changed astronomy.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain why a steady turn or a steady movement cannot be felt, and why the Sun seems to go round us even though the Earth is what turns.
1. I can say why planets such as Mars sometimes seem to slide backwards among the stars, and how an Earth-centred and a Sun-centred model each explained it.
2. I can use a lamp and a ball to show that the phases of Venus cannot all be explained if Venus stays between the Earth and the Sun.
3. I can say what 'precision' means for a measurement, and show from my own readings that a steadier, longer instrument gives readings that agree more closely.
4. I can describe how Kepler used Tycho's careful readings to show that planets move on ellipses, and draw an ellipse with a loop of string.
5. I can explain why a sundial tilted at my city's latitude shows the Sun's time, and why it differs from my phone's clock.
6. I can name Aryabhata, Nilakantha, Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo and at least two women astronomers, and say one thing that each found out.

## Terms
- **Planet**: A big world that goes round a star. The word comes from the Greek for 'wanderer', because planets drift among the fixed stars from night to night. *e.g.* Mars, Jupiter and Venus are planets that can be seen without a telescope.
- **Retrograde motion**: The time when a planet seems to stop, slide backwards among the stars for a few weeks, and then go forward again. *e.g.* Mars seems to move backwards for about two months, about every 26 months.
- **Epicycle**: A small circle on a big circle. Ptolemy used epicycles to explain why planets seem to loop in the sky. *e.g.* In Ptolemy's model, Mars sat on a small circle, and the small circle itself went round the Earth.
- **Geocentric**: Having the Earth at the centre. A geocentric model says that the Sun, Moon and planets go round the Earth. *e.g.* Ptolemy's model of the sky was geocentric.
- **Heliocentric**: Having the Sun at the centre. A heliocentric model says that the planets, including the Earth, go round the Sun. *e.g.* Copernicus put the Sun at the centre in 1543.
- **Ellipse**: A shape like a stretched circle, an oval. It has two special points inside it called foci. *e.g.* Each planet goes round the Sun on an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus.
- **Arcminute**: A very small angle. There are 60 arcminutes in one degree. The Moon is about 30 arcminutes wide in the sky. *e.g.* Tycho Brahe measured the places of planets to about one arcminute without a telescope.
- **Quadrant**: A tool for measuring how high something is in the sky. It is a quarter of a circle with a scale of degrees and a way to sight along an edge. *e.g.* Tycho Brahe had a huge brass quadrant, with a radius of about two metres.
- **Astrolabe**: A round brass instrument, often hand-sized, for measuring the height of the Sun or a star and for working out the time and the direction. *e.g.* Mahendra Suri wrote the first Sanskrit book on the astrolabe in 1370.
- **Gnomon**: The part of a sundial that casts the shadow. It can be a stick, a wall or a stone stairway. *e.g.* The stairway of the great Samrat Yantra in Jaipur is a gnomon.
- **Latitude**: How far north or south a place is from the equator, measured in degrees. It is also the height of the Pole Star above the horizon. *e.g.* Chennai is at about 13 degrees north. Jaipur is at about 27 degrees north.
- **Local noon**: The moment when the Sun is highest in the sky at your place, and shadows point exactly north or south. *e.g.* Local noon in Kolkata comes about an hour before local noon in Jaipur.
- **Standard time**: One clock time used across a whole country. India's Standard Time is based on the Sun at 82.5 degrees east. *e.g.* Your phone shows Indian Standard Time, even in Jaipur, where the Sun reaches its highest point about 27 minutes later.
- **Precision**: How closely repeated readings of the same thing agree with each other. Small spread means high precision. *e.g.* Ten readings of an angle that all lie between 26 and 28 degrees are more precise than ten that lie between 20 and 34.
- **Phases of Venus**: Venus changes its lit shape like the Moon, from a thin crescent to nearly full, and it also changes its size as it gets closer or farther. *e.g.* Galileo saw this through his telescope in 1610.
- **Parallax**: The small shift in where a near thing seems to be, when you look at it from two places. *e.g.* In 1838, Friedrich Bessel measured the tiny parallax of a star as the Earth moved round the Sun.
- **Model**: A way of picturing how something works, which can be tested against what we see. Models can be drawn, built or written as numbers. *e.g.* Ptolemy's circles and Kepler's ellipses are two models of the planets.
- **Leap year**: A year with an extra day, 29 February, added to keep the calendar in step with the seasons. *e.g.* 2024 was a leap year. 1900 was not.

## Explanations
### The wandering stars and their backward loops · *defined*
**Definition:** Planets are the 'wandering stars'. Over weeks they move among the fixed stars, and every so often a planet seems to slow, stop, slide backwards and then go forward again.

Most stars keep their places from night to night. A few bright lights do not. The ancient Greeks called them planets, which means wanderers. Mars moves eastwards among the stars for many months. Then it slows, stops and slides backwards for about two months, before it turns and goes forward again. This happens about every 26 months. For people who thought the Earth stood still, this was a puzzle. A planet moving in a plain circle round us would never go backwards. About 150 CE, Ptolemy explained it with epicycles. Each planet sat on a small circle, and the small circle went round the Earth. The maths worked well enough to predict where planets would be for more than a thousand years.
- e.g. The loop of Mars happens every 26 months or so, and the Babylonians and Indians recorded it.
- e.g. Ptolemy's book, which the Arabs called the Almagest, gave tables for every planet.
- e.g. A planet is brightest in the middle of its loop, when it is closest to the Earth.
### Who moves? Aryabhata and the boat · *defined*
**Definition:** In 499, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote that the Earth spins once a day, and that the stars only seem to move westwards.

Aryabhata was twenty-three when he wrote his book, the Aryabhatiya. He wrote that a person in a boat moving forwards sees the still things on the bank moving backwards. In the same way, he said, people on the Earth see the still stars moving westwards, while it is the Earth that turns eastwards. He also wrote that eclipses are shadows. The idea was bold, because nobody could feel the Earth turn. Brahmagupta, another great astronomer, argued against it in 628. He asked why birds flying up from a tree can find the tree again, if the ground is racing away under them. Most Indian astronomers kept the old view for centuries. But the question stayed alive, and was asked again later in Kerala and elsewhere.
- e.g. When a smooth train pulls out of a station, the platform of the next train seems to slide backwards.
- e.g. In a boat on a still river, the trees on the bank seem to move.
- e.g. Al-Biruni, who studied Indian astronomy about the year 1000, wrote that the idea of a spinning Earth could not be disproved by the maths of the time.
### Why a steady turn cannot be felt · *defined*
**Definition:** You feel changes in your movement, such as starting, stopping and turning corners. You do not feel a steady movement at all.

In a smooth aeroplane, flying at 800 kilometres an hour, you can pour a cup of tea as if you were sitting at home. You feel the engines starting and the bumps, but not the speed. Galileo described this in 1632 with a ship: in a cabin below deck, with no window, nothing you do shows whether the ship is still or moving smoothly. The Earth turns once in 24 hours. At the equator, the ground moves at about 1,670 kilometres an hour. At Chennai it moves at about 1,630. But it never starts or stops, and it turns very gently, so we feel nothing. The Earth's own spin is the steadiest motion we know.
- e.g. A passenger on a smooth, steady train cannot tell with eyes shut if it is moving, until the train brakes or turns.
- e.g. The Earth also goes round the Sun at about 30 kilometres a second, and we feel none of it.
- e.g. A lift feels heavier or lighter only while it starts or stops.
### A Sun at the centre: Nilakantha and Copernicus · *defined*
**Definition:** A heliocentric model puts the Sun at the centre of the planets' paths. In Kerala about 1500, and in Poland in 1543, astronomers made models in which the planets go round the Sun.

Nilakantha Somayaji, an astronomer of Kerala, wrote a book called the Tantrasangraha in 1501. In his model, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn go round the Sun, and the Sun goes round the Earth. This explained the odd behaviour of the planets in a neat way. In 1543, the Polish astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus went further. In his book he put the Sun at the centre and made the Earth a planet that spins and goes round it. Copernicus still used circles, and still needed small extra circles to match the sky. At first his model was no more accurate than Ptolemy's. It won people over slowly, because it explained why planets loop. The loop is Earth overtaking a slower, outer planet, as a car overtakes a lorry. And the Sun-centred model made the planets' sizes and orders follow in a simple way.
- e.g. Tycho Brahe later made a middle model, in which the planets go round the Sun and the Sun goes round the Earth. Nilakantha's is similar.
- e.g. The loops of Mars seen from the faster Earth are like a lorry that seems to slide backwards while you overtake it.
- e.g. Copernicus kept his idea to himself for years, and his book appeared in print only in 1543, the year he died.
### Tycho, Kepler and the end of perfect circles · *defined*
**Definition:** Kepler used Tycho Brahe's careful readings of Mars to show that planets move on ellipses, not on circles.

Tycho Brahe, a Danish nobleman, built the finest instruments of his day and used them for 20 years. His readings of planets were accurate to about one arcminute, and no telescope had yet been invented. After he died in 1601, his assistant Johannes Kepler worked on the readings for Mars. Kepler tried many circles. At last he found one that matched Tycho's readings to within 8 arcminutes. But Tycho's readings were never wrong by that much. Kepler wrote that he could not ignore 8 arcminutes. He threw out the circle. In 1609 he published his discovery: Mars goes round the Sun on an ellipse, with the Sun at one focus. Later he found that a planet moves faster when it is closer to the Sun, and that planets farther out take longer for a lap.
- e.g. Eight arcminutes is about one quarter of the width of the full Moon.
- e.g. Mars's ellipse is so close to a circle that you can hardly see the difference. Only the Sun's off-centre place shows it.
- e.g. The Earth's own path is even closer to a circle, and it is a little nearer the Sun in early January than in July.
### Galileo's telescope · *defined*
**Definition:** In 1609 and 1610, Galileo used a telescope to show things in the sky that an Earth-centred sky could not easily explain: moons going round Jupiter, and the phases of Venus.

Galileo heard of the Dutch telescope in 1609, and made much better ones. In January 1610, he saw four small lights near Jupiter that changed places from night to night and went with the planet. They were moons going round Jupiter. So not everything went round the Earth. In late 1610 he watched Venus change from a small round disc to a big thin crescent over months. If Venus always stayed between the Earth and the Sun, it could only show crescents. Showing a nearly full disc meant it could also go behind the Sun. So Venus goes round the Sun. This did not prove that the Earth moves, because Tycho's middle model also fitted. But it killed Ptolemy's Venus. He also saw mountains and craters on the Moon, and far more stars in the Milky Way than anyone had known.
- e.g. Venus looks about six times wider when it is nearest to us than when it is farthest.
- e.g. The four large moons of Jupiter are still called the Galilean moons.
- e.g. Galileo's drawings of the Moon in 1610 show craters with shadows, the same way you see in your own eyes with a small telescope.
### Proofs that the Earth moves · *defined*
**Definition:** It took until the 1700s and 1800s to find direct evidence that the Earth goes round the Sun and spins.

In 1729, James Bradley found that every star seems to make a tiny yearly circle in the sky, because the Earth is moving through starlight, as rain seems to slant when you run. In 1838, Friedrich Bessel found the tiny parallax of a star called 61 Cygni. It seems to shift as the Earth goes round its orbit. The shift told him that the star is about 11 light-years away. In 1851, Léon Foucault hung a heavy pendulum on a long wire in Paris. Its swing slowly turned round the floor over the hours. The pendulum kept swinging in the same direction, while the Earth turned under it. Today, spacecraft see the Earth spin and go round the Sun.
- e.g. A Foucault pendulum in Paris turns its swing about 11 degrees every hour. Near the equator it would not turn at all.
- e.g. Bessel's star shifted by less than the width of a coin seen from 5 kilometres away.
- e.g. Each of these proofs was made possible by better and more precise instruments.
### Why bigger instruments were built · *defined*
**Definition:** Before telescopes, the finer an instrument's scale, the more exactly it could measure an angle. A bigger instrument can have finer divisions that are easier to read.

A quadrant that is a hand wide has a scale you can read to about one degree. A quadrant two metres wide has a scale where one arcminute is a few millimetres long. That is why Tycho built enormous brass instruments. From 1724 to about 1740, Raja Sawai Jai Singh II built five observatories in India, called the Jantar Mantar, at Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi and Mathura. The Samrat Yantra at Jaipur is a stairway about 27 metres high that works as a sundial, and its shadow edge creeps along the scale at about six centimetres a minute. Jai Singh built in stone because brass instruments bent and wore. Ulugh Beg built a huge observatory at Samarkand in the 1420s, with an arc made in a trench in the ground.
- e.g. A stone instrument cannot warp like a thin brass one, so it keeps its shape.
- e.g. The Jaipur Samrat Yantra is still used to tell the local time, and it is within a minute or two.
- e.g. Even a huge sundial has a blurry shadow edge, because the Sun is a disc and not a point.
### Astrolabes and observatories across the Islamic world and India · *defined*
**Definition:** From about 800 to 1500, astronomers in Baghdad, Cairo, Samarkand and India built observatories, improved the astrolabe and kept long records of the sky.

Astronomers wrote in Arabic, Persian and Sanskrit, and often learned from each other's books. Al-Biruni, who worked about the year 1000, measured the size of the Earth by climbing a mountain and measuring the dip of the horizon. He also wrote a book about India and its astronomy. A woman named Mariam al-Asturlabi made astrolabes in Aleppo about the year 950. In 1259, the Maragha observatory was built in what is now Iran, and its astronomers found new ways to use circles to match the planets. In 1370, Mahendra Suri wrote the Yantraraja, the first Sanskrit book on the astrolabe, at the court of Firuz Shah Tughlaq in Delhi. These astronomers made many of the exact tables that Europeans later used.
- e.g. An astrolabe could tell the time, the direction of Mecca and the height of a star, all on one brass disc.
- e.g. Ulugh Beg's catalogue of more than 1,000 stars, made in Samarkand about 1437, was the best for generations.
- e.g. Many star names in use today, such as Aldebaran, come from Arabic.
### What the stars are made of · *defined*
**Definition:** In 1925, Cecilia Payne showed that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium. This overturned the old belief that stars are made of the same stuff as the Earth.

Starlight can be split into a rainbow with dark lines. Each kind of atom leaves its own pattern of lines. In 1920, the Indian physicist Meghnad Saha worked out how the heat of a star changes the lines that atoms make. Using his equation, Cecilia Payne, a young astronomer at Harvard, studied the lines in the light of many stars in 1925. She found that stars are mostly hydrogen, with some helium, and only a small share of the heavier elements. The senior astronomers did not believe her at first. Her teacher said it was impossible, and she added a line saying so. Four years later, another astronomer confirmed her result, and she was proved right. She later became a professor at Harvard, the first woman to lead a department there.
- e.g. The Sun is about three quarters hydrogen by mass and one quarter helium.
- e.g. The same method of splitting light shows what is in galaxies we cannot visit.
- e.g. Meghnad Saha was born in what is now Bangladesh, and he later became a professor in Kolkata.
### Did old stone circles watch the sky? · *debated*
In many places, from India to Britain to Egypt, people raised stones and mounds in lines or circles. Some line up with the Sun at the solstice. Some historians think these sites were built to follow the sky. Others say the lines could be an accident, or that the sites had other uses. There is no written record for most of them.
- e.g. At Stonehenge in England, the Sun rises close to one of the stone lines on the longest day of the year.
- e.g. At Nabta Playa in southern Egypt, an old stone circle may point to the summer solstice sunrise.
- **Some archaeologists:** The sunrise lines at Stonehenge are too exact to be luck. The builders knew where the Sun rises at midsummer and midwinter.
- **Other archaeologists:** With so many stones, some are bound to line up with something. Without writing, we cannot be sure the builders cared about the Sun.
- **Many historians:** The sites probably did several jobs. They were places for meeting, burying and watching the sky.
### Did ideas from the Islamic world reach Copernicus? · *idea*
Some of the small circles that Copernicus used to match the planets look the same as ones invented by astronomers at Maragha and Damascus 200 years earlier, such as Nasir al-Din al-Tusi and Ibn al-Shatir, long before his time. Historians think it is likely that Copernicus or someone close to him learned of them, but nobody has found the book or the person that carried them. Many see this as one clear case of ideas travelling across lands.
- e.g. The 'Tusi couple' is a way of making straight-line motion from two circles, and it appears again in Copernicus.
- e.g. Greek books on astronomy were translated into Arabic in Baghdad, and later back into Latin in Spain.
- **Many historians of science:** The likeness is too close to be an accident. Some copy of the ideas must have travelled from the East to Europe.
- **Some other historians:** Different astronomers can find the same trick to solve the same problem. We should not claim a link until we find the road it took.

## Mostly agreed
- The Earth spins once every day, and it goes round the Sun once every year. *How we know:* Foucault's pendulum, the parallax of stars, the yearly shift of starlight, and photographs from spacecraft all show it.
- A steady movement cannot be felt. Only changes in movement, such as starting or stopping, are felt. *How we know:* Passengers in smooth trains and aeroplanes, and experiments in closed cabins, all find that nothing inside shows steady movement.
- Planets move on ellipses with the Sun at one focus, and they move fastest when closest to the Sun. *How we know:* Kepler found it from Tycho's readings, and every spacecraft sent to a planet has been aimed using it.
- Venus shows phases and changes size, because it goes round the Sun and not round the Earth. *How we know:* Anyone with a small telescope can watch Venus change shape over several months.
- Jupiter has moons that go round Jupiter, so not everything in the sky goes round the Earth. *How we know:* The four large moons change place from night to night and can be seen with a pair of binoculars.
- Stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium. *How we know:* The lines in starlight, studied since 1925 in thousands of stars, show the same result.
- A longer, steadier instrument gives readings that agree more closely. *How we know:* Astronomers from Tycho to Jai Singh built bigger fixed instruments for this reason, and your own quadrant readings show the same.

## How we found out
**Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer (1571 to 1630), using the readings of Tycho Brahe, a Danish astronomer (1546 to 1601), About 1600 to 1609.** For about 2,000 years, nearly every astronomer believed that the planets must move in perfect circles, because the circle seemed the perfect shape for the heavens. Ptolemy used circles, and so did Copernicus. Tycho Brahe, a Danish nobleman, spent twenty years measuring the places of planets with huge brass instruments, to about one arcminute, before any telescope existed. After Tycho died in 1601, Johannes Kepler used his readings of Mars. For years he tried one circle after another. At last he found one that matched Tycho's readings to within 8 arcminutes. That is about a quarter of the width of the Moon in the sky. Kepler knew that Tycho's readings were never wrong by that much. He wrote that he could not ignore those 8 arcminutes. He threw out his circle. Then he tried an oval, and it fitted. In 1609 he published his result: Mars goes round the Sun on an ellipse, with the Sun at one of its two foci.  
*What people believed before:* Astronomers believed for 2,000 years that planets must move on perfect circles. Kepler showed that a circle misses the real readings by 8 arcminutes, and that the path is an ellipse.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Sun goes round the Earth, because we can see it rise and set.** (Nearly everyone, for thousands of years; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun clearly moves across the sky, and the ground feels still.  
  Against: A steady turn cannot be felt, and from a turning Earth the Sun would look exactly the same. Foucault's pendulum, the parallax of stars and photographs from space show that the Earth turns.
- **Nobody before Copernicus thought that the Earth moved.** (A common idea in books; *rejected*)  
  For: Copernicus's book in 1543 is the one people remember.  
  Against: Aristarchus of Samos in Greece, about 250 BCE, said that the Earth goes round the Sun. Aryabhata in India wrote in 499 that the Earth spins. Nilakantha in Kerala put the planets in orbit round the Sun about 1500.
- **Copernicus's Sun-centred model was more accurate than Ptolemy's from the start.** (Many popular books; *rejected*)  
  For: It is simpler to say the planets go round the Sun.  
  Against: Copernicus still used circles and small extra circles. His tables were no more accurate than Ptolemy's. The Sun-centred picture won because Kepler, Galileo and Newton gave it better maths and better evidence.
- **Galileo's telescope proved that the Earth goes round the Sun.** (Many books and films; *rejected*)  
  For: Galileo's discoveries of Jupiter's moons and the phases of Venus fitted the Sun-centred view.  
  Against: Tycho's middle model also fitted the phases of Venus, with the Sun going round the Earth and the planets going round the Sun. The telescope showed that Ptolemy's model was wrong, but it did not prove that the Earth moves. That took the proofs of the 1700s and 1800s.
- **Summer is hot because the Earth is nearer to the Sun.** (Many people, even grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: A fire is hotter when you are closer to it.  
  Against: The Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the northern winter. Summer comes from the tilt of the Earth's axis, which makes the Sun climb higher and the days longer.
- **The Earth goes round the Sun on a very long, stretched oval.** (Many diagrams in books; *rejected*)  
  For: Kepler said that planets move on ellipses, and diagrams usually draw them stretched to show the idea.  
  Against: The Earth's path is very close to a circle. If you drew it 20 centimetres wide, it would differ from a circle by less than a millimetre. The drawings in books are stretched on purpose.

## Roots
We sat in a spinning chair, lit a ball like Venus, measured angles and drew planet paths. Now we ask what the long argument about the Sun and the Earth teaches about seeing and knowing.

**Deep truth.** With your eyes shut, a steady turn of the chair was hard to feel. With your eyes open, the room seemed to turn instead. A steady movement is seen only against something else. The Earth turns steadily, so the Sun seems to circle us while we sit still.  
*Habit it breaks:* We take what we see for what is happening. The Sun rises, so we say it moves. But a sight tells us how things move compared with us, and it cannot tell us which of the two is moving.  
*Evidence:* In The Spinning Chair, the turns that were steady were the hardest to feel, and starting and stopping were the easiest. In Galileo's Venus, the same lamp and ball gave very different views from different places. If your own results differ, write them down as they are.

- *who am i:* You are standing on a ball that spins at about 1,600 kilometres an hour and circles the Sun at about 30 kilometres a second. You feel none of it.
- *what is life:* Living bodies feel changes: a start, a stop, a bump. A body can get used to a steady movement so completely that it is no longer noticed.
- *what is this world:* Motion is always motion compared with something else. The Sun is still in the Earth's story and moving in the Milky Way's story.

The Sun going round us and the Earth turning under the Sun are the same sight from the ground. Aryabhata saw this in 499, with the picture of a boat. To tell the two stories apart, people needed more than looking. They needed a pendulum, a star's tiny shift and a telescope looking at Venus. Even then, the answer was only found by comparing: one view against another. What we know about the sky comes from looking from many places and seeing what stays the same.

- **Aryabhatiya, Golapada 9, by Aryabhata, 499:** Aryabhata wrote that a person in a boat moving forward sees still things on the bank moving backwards. In the same way, he wrote, people on the Earth see the still stars moving westwards. (The picture of the boat is the same as the spinning chair. A steady movement makes the world outside seem to move the other way.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 5:** The verse speaks of the One as moving and not moving, as far and as near, as inside everything and outside everything. (Whether the Sun moves or the Earth moves depends on where you stand. A thing can be said to move and not to move at the same time, depending on what you compare it with.)
- Science (settled): All steady motion is only motion compared with something else. No experiment inside a smoothly moving cabin can tell it from standing still.
- Science (debated): Whether this means that 'standing still' has no meaning at all is a question that scientists and philosophers still talk about.
- Other view, Many astronomers: Saying the Earth goes round the Sun or the Sun goes round the Earth are both fine ways to describe the same motion. The Sun-centred one is simply much easier to calculate with, which is why we use it to send spacecraft. It is a choice of the easiest description, and not a discovery about who is 'really' still.
- Practice, Watch the Earth turn (3 min): Find a patch of sunlight on the floor and mark its edge with a piece of tape. Sit still and breathe in slowly. Breathe out slowly. Watch the edge of the light for three minutes. It creeps along the floor. The Sun is not rushing. You are turning slowly under it, on the Earth. Take one more slow breath, and look at the tape again.
- Ask: If you could not feel the Earth turn and could not see the Sun, how would you know it was turning?
- Ask: Aryabhata's boat, Galileo's ship and your chair all say the same thing. Why might different people find the same idea in different places?
- Ask: When someone says they are sure, what could you do to check whether it is the world that moves or only their point of view?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the chair test, you could not tell by feel whether you or the room was turning. So how do we know it is the Earth that turns? (hint: Think about the pendulum, the stars and what a spacecraft sees.)
- *deep:* Tycho's readings were only 8 arcminutes away from a circle, and Kepler threw the circle away. When is a small difference worth believing? (hint: Think about how sure Kepler was of Tycho's readings, and why that mattered.)
- *deep:* Ptolemy's circles gave good tables for over 1,400 years, and the model was wrong. Does a model have to be true to be useful? (hint: Think about what the tables were used for, and what they could not explain.)
- *big:* Every place has its own noon, but a clock gives one time for the whole country. Which is the real time? (hint: Think about what each one is for, and who needs which.)
- *big:* Aryabhata wrote that the Earth spins in 499, but most people went on thinking the sky turns. Why do good ideas sometimes take hundreds of years to spread? (hint: Think about what a person would have needed to see to be sure, and who could check.)
- *big:* If you lived on Venus, what would you say moves, and what would you say stands still? (hint: Think about what you could see from there, and what you could not feel.)
- *big:* Cecilia Payne found that stars are mostly hydrogen, and was told she was wrong. Why might the same evidence be believed from one person and doubted from another? (hint: Think about who gets listened to, and what a careful scientist should do instead.)

## Story: The Blurred Edge of the Shadow
*Ishani, her grandmother, Dev, the guide and the visit are made up. The Jantar Mantar in Jaipur is real, and so is the great stone sundial, called the Samrat Yantra. India's clock time is set by the Sun at 82.5 degrees east. In mid-April the Sun reaches its highest point at Jaipur about 27 minutes after 12 o'clock Indian Standard Time.*

It was a hot April morning in Jaipur, and Ishani was nine years old and far from home. She had come from Madurai by train with her grandmother, Ammamma, and her cousin Dev, who was six. Ammamma had a friend in Jaipur and she wanted to see the old observatory.

"It looks like a place where giants left their stairs," said Dev.

He was nearly right. The Jantar Mantar was full of huge stone shapes. There were curved bowls, tall walls and flights of steps that went up and ended in the air. Ishani opened her notebook.

A guide in a green sari came up to them. "I am Sarika Madam," she said. "Raja Sawai Jai Singh built all this nearly 300 years ago. He wanted to measure the sky better than anyone had before."

She led them to the tallest thing in the garden, a stone stairway rising to a point as high as a house with eight floors. Its top edge was a sharp slope. On both sides, a curved stone track was marked in lines.

"This is the Samrat Yantra," said Sarika Madam. "It is a clock. The sloping edge makes a shadow, and the shadow falls on the curved scales."

"Why is it tilted?" asked Ishani. "Why not straight up?"

"Good question. The tilt is exactly the angle of Jaipur's latitude, about 27 degrees. That points the stairway's edge at the Pole Star. And then something nice happens: the shadow turns by exactly the same amount every hour, 15 degrees. The scale can be marked evenly."

Ishani looked at the scale. The shadow of the top edge lay across the stone, a dark band. She frowned and went closer. Then she crouched.

"Madam, the edge is not sharp," she said. "It is blurred. It is dark in the middle and then it fades. Someone has smudged it."

Dev peered at it. "Maybe a pigeon sat on the stone."

Sarika Madam laughed. "No pigeon. Everyone notices it, if they look carefully. Think. Is the Sun a tiny dot, or a round disc?"

"A disc," said Ishani slowly. "A big round ball. It is just far away."

"Every point on that disc makes its own shadow. They overlap in the middle and slide apart at the edges. That is why the edge fades. The dial is huge so that each minute is spread over a long distance, and you can read the middle of the blur very exactly. The shadow creeps about six centimetres a minute."

They watched the dark band crawl across the scale. Ammamma looked at her phone. It said 12:27.

At that moment, the middle of the shadow touched the line marked noon.

"Oh," said Ishani. "The dial says noon, and the phone says twenty-seven minutes past twelve. One of them is wrong."

"Which?" asked Sarika Madam, with a twinkle.

Ishani looked from one to the other. The phone was a famous machine. The stone had been here for nearly 300 years, and was as big as a house. She did not know which to trust.

"The phone shows Indian Standard Time," the guide said. "The whole country keeps one time, set by the Sun at a place far to the east, near Mirzapur. But the Sun does not reach every place at once. The Earth turns, and the Sun gets to the east first and the west later. In Jaipur, the Sun is at its highest about 27 minutes after it was highest at Mirzapur. This dial shows Jaipur's own noon. It is not wrong. It is telling you the time of this place."

Ammamma took out her phone again. "Let me ask my sister in Kolkata." She called, and put the call on loudspeaker.

"Noon?" said her sister. "We had it nearly an hour ago. The Sun is already well past the top."

Dev's mouth fell open. "But it is the same day! How can it be noon here and not there?"

Ishani had stopped writing. She looked up at the Sun, and then, very slowly, she looked at the stone. The stone had not moved. It had never moved. The shadow crept only because the Sun was moving, or because something else was.

"The Sun is not going round us," she said quietly. "We are going round under it. Kolkata is just further ahead on the same big turn."

"Now you are thinking like Jai Singh," said Sarika Madam.

That night, in the guest room, Ishani wrote in her notebook: The dial stays still. The Earth turns under the Sun. Every place has its own noon, and the clock is the one we agree to share.

She stopped. She added one more line. And the edge of a shadow is blurry, because the Sun is big.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the Earth turns steadily towards the east, so the Sun reaches the east of a country before the west. Each place has its own local noon. Indian Standard Time is one agreed clock for the whole country, set by the Sun at 82.5 degrees east. A dial tilted at the latitude of its city and aligned north shows local Sun time. In Jaipur, in mid-April, that is about 27 minutes after the phone's 12 o'clock. The shadow edge is blurred because the Sun is a disc, not a point.

- Ask: Why is the Samrat Yantra tilted at the angle of Jaipur's latitude?
- Ask: Why was the edge of the shadow blurred, and where do you read the time on a blurred edge?
- Ask: Why did the dial say noon when the phone said twenty-seven minutes past twelve?
- Ask: Why was it already past noon in Kolkata when it was noon in Jaipur?
- Ask: Ishani said that the stone had never moved. What did she mean the Sun and the Earth were doing?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Spinning Chair: can you feel a steady turn? (35 min)
- **question:** You sit in a swivel chair that a grown-up turns slowly and smoothly. With your eyes shut, can you tell whether the chair is turning or still? And with your eyes open, who seems to be turning, you or the room?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. With your eyes shut, will you be able to feel a slow, steady turn? Will you feel it start or stop? With your eyes open, who will seem to turn, you or the room? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same chair and the same turning speed every time, about one full turn in ten seconds. The grown-up decides in secret, by drawing slips, which rounds are turning and which are still. The child wears a scarf over the eyes and hums a tune, so that sounds give nothing away. Play six rounds. Only the secret order changes.
- **safety:** Turn the chair slowly, never faster than one turn in ten seconds. Stop at once if the child feels dizzy or sick, and let her rest for five minutes. Keep the floor around the chair clear of furniture and sharp corners. A grown-up holds the chair steady when the child gets on and off. Children who get dizzy easily, or have ear problems, should skip this test.
- **record:** Fill in the table and count the right answers. Under it, write one sentence about what you could feel and what you could not.
- **results table:** Round | What the grown-up really did (secret) | My answer: turning or still? | Right or wrong?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the chair, the scarf and the slips. Ask for a guess about the eyes-shut test and the eyes-open test, and a reason. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* You will sit in this chair. With your eyes shut, can you tell if it turns slowly and steadily? With your eyes open, who seems to be turning? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Eyes shut, can you feel a slow turn? Eyes open, who seems to turn?
  - Ask: Why do you think you will, or will not, feel it? (good: You can feel your body being pulled round.; The turn is too slow and smooth to feel.; It is hard to tell without seeing.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children are sure that they will feel the turn. Write down the guess.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* With the eyes open, turn the chair very slowly. Ask the child which sheet of paper is straight ahead as it passes. Then ask: is the room turning, or is the chair? Stop the chair gently.  
  *Parent says:* I will turn the chair very slowly. Call out the letter on the wall that is straight in front of you. Is the room turning round you, or are you turning round the room?  
  *Child mode:* Eyes open. Call out the letter in front of you. Who is turning?
  - Note: The child can tell by looking that the room seems to turn the other way. This is the picture in Aryabhata's boat: when you move, the world seems to move backwards. Keep the turning slow and smooth.
- **s3 (14 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Cover the eyes with the scarf. Draw a slip in secret. On a TURN slip, start the chair very gently and build up to one turn in ten seconds. On a STILL slip, touch the chair lightly and do nothing. After 20 seconds, ask: turning or still? Write down the answer. Do six rounds.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes shut and hum a tune. In a moment I will either turn you slowly or keep you still. After a little while, tell me: turning or still?  
  *Child mode:* Eyes shut. Hum a tune. After a while say: turning or still?
  - Note: Start each TURN round very gently, so that the push is hard to feel, and keep the turn steady. In a STILL round, make the same small touch and noises. If the child is right six times, ask which clue she used, such as a breeze, a creak or a touch. Chance alone gives about three right out of six. Your own results decide what you write.
  - Check: Why does the grown-up draw the slips in secret and make a small touch on every round? → **So that the child cannot guess from any clue except the turning itself**
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Count the right answers. Then do one last round: turn the chair steadily, with the eyes shut, and stop it suddenly. Ask what the child felt.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count how many you got right. Chance would give about three. Now one last try. I will turn you and then stop the chair quite suddenly. What do you feel?  
  *Child mode:* Count your right answers. Then feel what happens when the chair stops.
  - Note: Most children feel the sudden stop clearly, as a lean or a wobble inside the body. The turn itself is much harder to feel once it is steady. Stop the chair gently, with your hand on its back, so nobody tips.
  - Check: When the chair stopped suddenly with your eyes shut, what did you feel? → **A lean or a push, because stopping is a change of movement**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for what I think, what I saw and why. Then ask about the Earth.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you notice that shows it? And why? The Earth turns steadily once a day. Would you feel it?  
  *Child mode:* Say what I think, what I saw and why. Would you feel the Earth turn?
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: what I think: a slow steady turn is hard or impossible to feel. What I saw: I got only three or four of six right, which is about what chance gives, but I felt the sudden stop. Why: our bodies sense changes in movement, and a steady movement has no change to sense. The Earth turns steadily, and never starts or stops, so we feel nothing. With eyes open, the room seemed to turn the other way, as the bank does from a boat. That is why the Sun seems to move across the sky when it is we who are turning.
  - Check: The Earth turns once every 24 hours without stopping. Why can we not feel it? → **It turns steadily, and we feel only changes in movement**
### e2 · Galileo's Venus: can Venus look full if it stays between us and the Sun? (35 min)

- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the ball as you saw it at each stop, with the lit part shaded white.

  *Parent says:* In this model Venus goes right round the Sun. Walk the ball round and stop at five places. How much is lit? When does it look biggest? When does it look nearly full? How big does it look then?  
  *Child mode:* Walk the ball all the way round the lamp. Stop five times. What do you see?
  - Note: Expected: nearest the chair, the ball is mostly dark and looks big. At the sides, you see half. Behind the lamp, off to one side so the lamp does not block your view, you see nearly all of the ball lit, and it looks smaller because it is twice as far. This is what Galileo saw.
  - Check: When did the ball look nearly full in the Sun-centred model? → **When it was on the far side of the lamp from you**

  *Child mode:* Say what I think, what I saw and why. Which model fits Galileo?
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: what I think: Venus must go round the Sun, and not just stay in front of it. What I saw: in the Earth-centred circle the ball never looked more than a thin crescent. In the Sun-centred circle it looked nearly full when it was far side, and small. Why: a ball can look full only if it can go behind the lamp. Be fair to Tycho: his middle model, where the Sun goes round the Earth and Venus goes round the Sun, also fits. ### e3 · Tycho's Quadrant: how precisely can you measure an angle? (40 min)
- **question:** A sticker is stuck high up on a wall. If you measure its angle above the horizontal ten times, how much do your readings differ? Do a steadier instrument and a longer sighting tube make the readings agree more closely?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will your ten readings all be the same, nearly the same, or very different? Will resting the quadrant on a table help? Will a longer straw help? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same sticker, the same standing place and the same quadrant. Take ten readings for each setup. Only the setup changes: first the quadrant held in your hands with a short straw, then the quadrant resting on a table with the short straw, then resting on the table with a long straw. Look away between readings so that you do not remember the last number.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the straws if the child is younger than eight. Use a steady table and do not stand on a chair to reach the sticker.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For each setup, circle the smallest and the biggest reading. Change the spread from degrees to arcminutes by multiplying by 60.
- **results table:** Setup | My ten readings (degrees) | Smallest | Biggest | Spread (biggest take away smallest)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the protractor, the straws and the sticker. Ask for a guess about the readings, and a reason. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* We will make a quadrant to measure how high the sticker is. If we measure ten times, will we get the same number each time? Will a steadier setup help? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the angle ten times. Will you get the same number? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think your readings will, or will not, be the same? (good: My hand shakes, so they will differ.; If I am careful they will be the same.; A table will hold it still, so they will be closer.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Remind your child that the word precision means how closely repeated readings agree.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the protractor's flat edge to the card's edge, with the curved side away from the card. Tape the short straw along the flat edge. Tape the thread at the protractor's centre mark, and tie the weight to its other end so that it hangs freely.  
  *Parent says:* The straw is the sight. You look through it at the sticker. The thread hangs straight down, and it crosses the numbers on the scale. That number tells you the angle.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the straw to the flat edge. Tape a hanging thread at the centre mark.
  - Note: To read the angle: sight the sticker along the straw. Hold the thread against the card with a finger so that it stops swinging. Read the number on the scale. When the straw is level, the thread crosses 90. The angle of the sticker is the difference between the reading and 90, whichever way round. For example, a reading of 63 means 27 degrees.
  - Check: When the straw points level, the thread crosses the 90 mark. The thread crosses 63 when you sight the sticker. What is the sticker's angle above level? → **27 degrees**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand 4 metres from the wall. Hold the quadrant in your hands with the short straw. Sight the sticker, pinch the thread and read the number. Write it down. Look away, then do it nine more times.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the quadrant in your hands. Look through the straw at the sticker. Pinch the thread and read. Write it down, look away, and try again. Ten times.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the quadrant. Sight the sticker. Pinch the thread. Read. Ten times.
  - Note: Stand in the same place every time. Mark the spot with tape. Expect the readings to differ by a few degrees when the quadrant is held in the hands.
- **s4 (8 min)**

## Applications
- Phone maps use the same idea of two viewpoints as parallax and the same geometry as Kepler's ellipses, to place satellites and find your position.
- Spacecraft are aimed using Kepler's ellipses and Newton's gravity, as Mangalyaan was aimed at Mars.
- Standard time zones are set so that every place on Earth has a clock that is roughly in step with the Sun.
- Surveyors use the same angle-measuring idea as the quadrant, in an instrument called a theodolite, to map land and plan roads.
- Navigators on ships once used the astrolabe and later the sextant to find their latitude from the height of the Sun or the Pole Star.
- The leap-year rule keeps farmers, festivals and school terms in step with the seasons.
- A Foucault pendulum in a science museum shows visitors that the Earth turns.
- Astronomers still check old records, such as Chinese and Babylonian eclipse notes, to measure how the Earth's spin has slowed over thousands of years.
- Sundials and sun-tracking panels both use the idea of pointing at the Sun with a tilt that matches the latitude.
- Spectrum lines, first read in starlight, are used today to find the metals in an old pot, a gemstone or a river sample.

## Spotted around us
- A shadow on the floor from a window creeping across the tiles over an hour, which is the Earth turning.
- A sundial in a garden, a school or a temple courtyard, with a tilted blade casting a shadow on a plate.
- A bright planet in the evening sky, steady and not twinkling, while the stars around it flicker.
- A moving train next to yours that seems to slide backwards when you pull out of the station.
- A clock on a wall in a railway station showing the same time as every other station in India.
- The shadow of a flagpole or a lamp post, shortest around the middle of the day.
- A calendar on the wall with 29 February in a leap year.
- The Moon in the daytime sky, showing that sky objects keep moving whether or not it is dark.

## Open questions
- **Who first thought that the Earth might move?** Ideas about a moving Earth appear in Greece, India and the Islamic world, but old writings are lost or hard to date. Nobody knows who thought of it first.
- **How did astronomical ideas travel between India, the Islamic world and Europe?** Some books were translated and some tables were copied, but for many ideas nobody has found the exact road they took.
- **What were the old stone circles and mounds really for?** Many line up with the Sun or the Moon, but without written records we cannot be sure the builders meant them to.
- **How many women astronomers were forgotten?** Many women worked as assistants, calculators and instrument makers, and their names were not always written down. Historians are still finding more.
- **Why does a model that is wrong still sometimes give right answers?** Ptolemy's circles gave good tables for over a thousand years. Philosophers of science still discuss what it means for a model to be true or useful.
- **Is there any experiment that could tell steady motion from standing still?** Physicists have found none so far. Whether that means standing still has no meaning is still discussed.
- **How much did the sky-watchers of the past know that we have not yet found in their records?** Thousands of old astronomy books and tablets in India, the Middle East and elsewhere have not been read or translated.

## Connected maths
- Your best spread of quadrant readings is 2 degrees. Tycho's readings were good to 1 arcminute. There are 60 arcminutes in a degree. How many times more precise was Tycho than your quadrant? **120 times.**. 2 degrees is 2 times 60, which is 120 arcminutes. 120 arcminutes compared with 1 arcminute is 120 times. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- India's Standard Time is set by the Sun at 82.5 degrees east. Chennai is at about 80.3 degrees east. The Sun is 4 minutes later for every degree you go west. How many minutes later is the Sun's noon in Chennai than at the standard place? **About 9 minutes.**. 82.5 take away 80.3 is 2.2 degrees. 2.2 times 4 is 8.8 minutes, which is about 9 minutes. (queues `math.decimals.multiply-decimals.L4`)
- Jaipur is at about 75.8 degrees east. Standard time is set at 82.5 degrees east. The Sun is 4 minutes later for every degree you go west. By about how many minutes does the Sun reach its highest point at Jaipur after it did at the standard place? **About 27 minutes.**. 82.5 take away 75.8 is 6.7 degrees. 6.7 times 4 is 26.8, which is about 27 minutes. (queues `math.decimals.multiply-decimals.L4`)
- The Earth's circumference at the equator is about 40,000 kilometres, and it turns once in 24 hours. About how fast does the ground move at the equator? A very fast Indian train runs at about 160 kilometres an hour. How many times faster is the ground? **About 1,670 kilometres an hour, which is about 10 times as fast as the train.**. 40,000 divided by 24 is about 1,667. 1,667 divided by 160 is about 10.4. So the ground moves about 10 times faster than the train, and we do not feel it. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- With a loop of thread 24 centimetres round, the width of an ellipse is 24 take away the distance between the pins. How wide is the shape with the pins 6 centimetres apart? How wide with 12 centimetres apart? What is the pin distance for a width of 20 centimetres? **18 centimetres, 12 centimetres, and 4 centimetres.**. 24 take away 6 is 18. 24 take away 12 is 12. For a width of 20, the pins must be 24 take away 20, which is 4 centimetres apart. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.multi-digit.L4`)
- A calendar year with a leap year every fourth year is 11 minutes too long. How many minutes too long is it after 128 years? How many hours is that, and how close is it to one day? **1,408 minutes, about 23 and a half hours, which is almost one day.**. 11 times 128 is 1,408. 1,408 divided by 60 is about 23.5 hours. One day is 24 hours, so it is nearly a whole day. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- Under the Gregorian rule, 400 years have 97 leap days. How many days are in 400 years? What is the average length of a year? **146,097 days, and an average year of 365.2425 days.**. 400 times 365 is 146,000. Add 97 to get 146,097. 146,097 divided by 400 is 365.2425. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- In the spinning chair test, a child answered six rounds and got four right. If she had just guessed each round, she would expect to get three right. By how much did she beat chance? Is that enough to be sure she could feel the turn? **She beat chance by one round. That is not enough to be sure.**. 4 take away 3 is 1. With only six rounds, getting one more than chance can easily happen by luck. To be sure, you would need many more rounds. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- Venus is about 40 million kilometres from the Earth when it is nearest, and about 260 million kilometres when it is farthest. About how many times farther away is it when farthest? What does that suggest about how its size changes? **About 6 times farther, so it looks about 6 times wider when nearest.**. 260 divided by 40 is 6.5. A thing that is 6.5 times farther looks about 6.5 times smaller across. So Venus looks about 6 times wider when it is nearest. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- The Earth overtakes Mars about every 780 days. A month is about 30 days. About how many months is that? If Mars was last overtaken in March, in which season of the year will the next overtaking be? **About 26 months, so it will be about two years and two months later, in May.**. 780 divided by 30 is 26 months. 26 months is 2 years and 2 months. Two months after March is May. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- The Samrat Yantra at Jaipur is about 27 metres tall. You make a model of it that is 100 times smaller. How tall is the model in centimetres? **27 centimetres.**. 27 metres is 2,700 centimetres. 2,700 divided by 100 is 27 centimetres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale.L4`)
- At Paris, Foucault's pendulum turned its swing by about 11 degrees every hour. About how many hours would it take to turn all the way round, through 360 degrees? **About 33 hours.**. 360 divided by 11 is about 32.7. So it would take about 33 hours to turn all the way round. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
