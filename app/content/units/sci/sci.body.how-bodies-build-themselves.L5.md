# From one cell to a whole body: layers, folds, master switch genes and regrowing
`sci.body.how-bodies-build-themselves.L5`

**Summary.** You began as one cell with no head, no heart and no hands. Nobody put those parts in from outside. So how did they get made? In this lesson you will test how a flat strip of living cells can bend itself, with strips of spring onion in plain water and salt water. You will follow an embryo as they divide, form three layers and fold into tubes. You will also meet the Hox genes, which mark each place from head to tail, and the axolotl, who can regrow a whole leg.

**Intro.** *Run a finger down the groove between your nose and your upper lip. Why is there a groove there?* That groove is a join. When you had been growing in the womb for about six weeks, your face was still in separate pieces. Over the next week or two the pieces grew towards the middle and joined, and the groove marks the place. Your body has more joins like it. You were not carved out of one block, and you were not put together by a maker. Sheets of your own cells folded, met and joined. This lesson asks how cells can do that with nobody outside to help.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can describe, in order, how one cell becomes a ball of cells, then three layers, then a folded body with tubes.
1. I can name the three germ layers and give two body parts that come from each.
2. I can run a fair test with strips of spring onion, and use it to explain how a flat sheet of cells can bend.
3. I can explain how a chemical signal from one group of cells tells other cells where they are, using the hand as the example.
4. I can say what Hox genes do, and why a changed Hox gene gives a normal part in the wrong place.
5. I can explain why finding the same Hox genes in a fly and in a human surprised scientists.
6. I can describe how an axolotl regrows a leg, and what it means that her cells remember their place.
7. I can give two views on a question about building bodies that scientists have not settled.

## Terms
- **Embryo**: An embryo is a living being in the earliest stage of growing, before the main body parts are finished. In humans the word is used for the first eight weeks after fertilisation. *e.g.* At four weeks a human embryo is shorter than a grain of rice, and their heart is already beating.
- **Cleavage**: Cleavage is the first set of cell divisions after fertilisation. The one big cell divides into more and more cells, and each new cell is smaller. *e.g.* During cleavage the embryo goes from 1 cell to 2, 4, 8 and 16 cells without getting any bigger overall.
- **Morula**: A morula is the solid ball of about 16 cells that an embryo becomes after a few days of cleavage. The word is Latin for mulberry. *e.g.* A human embryo is a morula on about day 4, while still travelling down the egg tube.
- **Gastrulation**: Gastrulation is the stage when the cells of the early embryo move and rearrange into three layers, one over another. It is when the embryo first gets a head end, a tail end, a back and a belly. *e.g.* In a human embryo gastrulation happens in the third week, when the embryo is only about 1 to 2 millimetres long.
- **Germ layer**: A germ layer is one of the three sheets of cells that form at gastrulation. 'Germ' here means 'a beginning', as in 'germinate'. It has nothing to do with the germs that make us ill. *e.g.* Every organ in your body can be traced back to one or more of the three germ layers.
- **Ectoderm**: The ectoderm is the outer germ layer. It makes the outer skin, hair, nails, the brain, the spinal cord and the nerves. *e.g.* Your brain and the outer layer of your skin both came from the ectoderm.
- **Mesoderm**: The mesoderm is the middle germ layer. It makes muscles, bones, the heart, blood, blood vessels and the kidneys. *e.g.* The muscle that moves your arm and the bone inside your arm both came from the mesoderm.
- **Endoderm**: The endoderm is the inner germ layer. It makes the inner lining of the gut, and the liver, the pancreas and the lining of the lungs. *e.g.* The thin lining of your stomach, which touches your food, came from the endoderm.
- **Neural tube**: The neural tube is a hollow tube of cells along the back of the embryo. It forms when a flat strip of ectoderm rolls up and its two edges join. It becomes the brain and the spinal cord. *e.g.* In a human embryo the neural tube has closed by about day 28.
- **Signal**: In an embryo, a signal is a chemical that some cells release and other cells sense. Cells that sense it change what they do. *e.g.* A signal protein called Sonic hedgehog is made at the little-finger edge of a growing hand.
- **Gene**: A gene is a stretch of DNA that carries the instructions for making one protein. This is a simplified meaning, because some genes make other useful molecules. *e.g.* You have about 20,000 genes that carry instructions for proteins.
- **Master switch gene**: A master switch gene makes a protein that turns many other genes on or off. So one master switch gene can start the building of a whole part. *e.g.* The gene called Pax6 is a master switch gene for building an eye.
- **Hox genes**: Hox genes are a family of master switch genes. Different Hox genes are switched on at different places from the head end to the tail end, so they mark where each part of the body is. *e.g.* Humans have 39 Hox genes, and a fruit fly has 8.
- **Homeotic change**: A homeotic change is when one body part grows in the place of another because a master switch gene was changed. The part itself is well made. Only the place is wrong. *e.g.* A fruit fly with a homeotic change can have legs on their head where the antennae should be.
- **Regeneration**: Regeneration is growing back a lost body part, with the right tissues in the right places. *e.g.* An axolotl who loses a leg regenerates a new leg with bones, muscles, nerves and toes.
- **Blastema**: A blastema is the bud of dividing cells that forms on the stump after an animal such as an axolotl loses a limb. The new limb grows from it. *e.g.* About a week or two after an axolotl loses a leg, a cone-shaped blastema can be seen on the stump.
- **Positional memory**: Positional memory means that a cell in a grown body still keeps a record of where in the body they are. The record is kept as certain genes that stay switched on. *e.g.* Because of positional memory, an axolotl cut at the wrist regrows only a hand, and not a whole arm.

## Explanations
### One cell divides into a ball of cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Cleavage is the first set of cell divisions. The fertilised egg cell divides into 2, 4, 8 and 16 smaller cells and becomes a solid ball called a morula.

A fertilised human egg cell is about a tenth of a millimetre across, about as wide as a hair is thick. It is one of the biggest cells in the body. About a day after fertilisation it divides into two cells. These divide again, roughly once a day at first. The embryo does not eat during these days, so they cannot grow. Each division just shares out what was in the egg cell, and the cells get smaller each time. By about day 4 there are around 16 cells in a solid ball, the morula. By about day 5 the ball has become hollow, with a small clump of cells inside. That clump will become the whole body. The outer cells will help make the placenta. Why divide first, before building anything? Because building needs many small cells that can move, fold and take different jobs. One giant cell cannot fold.
- e.g. Cut a ball of chapati dough into 2, then 4, then 8 pieces. You have more pieces, but no more dough. Cleavage is like that.
- e.g. A frog's egg is big enough to see with your eyes. In a pond you can watch one cell become two in a few hours.
- e.g. The 8 cells of a human embryo on day 3 fit inside the same thin coat that held the one egg cell.
### Gastrulation: the embryo becomes three layers · *defined*
**Definition:** Gastrulation is the movement of cells in the early embryo that arranges them into three layers: an outer, a middle and an inner one.

In the second week the cells that will become the body lie in a flat, round disc, like a tiny dosa two layers thick. On about day 14 or 15 a groove appears along part of the top layer. It is called the primitive streak. Cells of the top layer crawl towards the groove, dive through it and spread out underneath. The first cells to dive in form the inner layer. Later ones settle between the top and the bottom and form the middle layer. The cells that stay on top are the outer layer. So after about a week of moving, the disc has three layers. The streak also shows the body's directions for the first time. The end where it starts is the tail end. The other end is the head end. Its line divides left from right. The scientist Lewis Wolpert said that the most important time in your life is not birth or marriage, but gastrulation. He meant that everything built later depends on these layers and directions.
- e.g. Before gastrulation, an embryo can still split and become identical twins. Once the streak has formed, there is one body plan, with one head end and one tail end.
- e.g. In a sea urchin or a frog, gastrulation looks different. A hollow ball of cells pushes inwards at one spot, like a finger pressed into a soft ball. The result is the same: layers inside layers.
- e.g. At the end of gastrulation a human embryo is about 2 millimetres long. Most mothers do not yet know that they are pregnant.
### The three germ layers and what each one makes · *defined*
**Definition:** The three germ layers are the ectoderm (outer), the mesoderm (middle) and the endoderm (inner). Each one makes a particular set of tissues.

The ectoderm makes the body's outer covering and its nervous system: the outer layer of the skin, hair, nails, the hard enamel of teeth, the brain, the spinal cord and the nerves. The mesoderm makes what holds the body up and moves things about: muscles, bones, the heart, blood, blood vessels, the kidneys and the deeper layer of the skin. The endoderm makes the lining of the long tube that food passes through, from the throat to the far end. It also makes the organs that bud off that tube: the liver, the pancreas and the lining of the lungs. Most organs are made by more than one layer working together. In your stomach, the lining is endoderm, the muscle wall is mesoderm and the nerves are ectoderm. Why does it seem odd that brain and skin are the same layer? Because we sort body parts by what they do. The embryo sorts them by where their cells started.
- e.g. Your fingernail and your brain both come from the ectoderm. Your thigh bone and your blood both come from the mesoderm.
- e.g. The lungs begin as a small bud that grows out of the food tube. That is why the windpipe and the food pipe meet in your throat.
- e.g. A group of ectoderm cells called the neural crest leaves the back of the embryo and travels far. These cells make much of the face, the colour cells of the skin and the nerves of the gut. Some scientists call them a fourth layer.
### Folding: how flat sheets become tubes · *defined*
**Definition:** A sheet of cells bends when the cells on one face of the sheet become narrower or wider than the cells on the other face. Folding turns the flat embryo into a body made of tubes.

After gastrulation the embryo is still flat. In the fourth week it folds. Along the back, a strip of ectoderm thickens. The cells along its middle line change shape. Each cell has a ring of protein threads near its top, made of the same two proteins that make muscles pull. The ring tightens like the drawstring of a cloth bag. The top of the cell becomes narrow while the bottom stays wide, so the cell becomes wedge-shaped. A row of wedges cannot lie flat. The sheet bends into a groove, the edges rise, meet and join, and a tube pinches off under the skin. This is the neural tube, the start of the brain and spinal cord. Pushing from the tissue on both sides helps the edges meet. At the same time the whole disc curls. Its sides fold down and meet along the belly, and the endoderm is rolled up inside as the gut tube. You are, roughly, a tube inside a tube. If the neural tube does not close fully, the baby is born with a gap in the spine or skull. Enough folate, a B vitamin found in green leaves and dals, lowers that risk. So doctors give folic acid tablets to women who may become pregnant.
- e.g. In the Curling Strip Test, strips of spring onion curl in water because the cells on one face swell more than the cells on the other face. The cause is different from the embryo's, but the geometry is the same.
- e.g. A papad held over a flame curls for a similar reason. The heated face changes size before the other face does.
- e.g. The neural tube closes like two zips, starting in the middle around day 22. The head end closes at about day 25 and the tail end by about day 28.
### Organs begin: the heart works while it is still being built · *defined*
**Definition:** Organs form from buds, folds and tubes of the germ layers, mostly between week 3 and week 8. The heart is the first organ to work.

A heart does not appear ready-made. Two thin tubes of mesoderm form on either side of the embryo. As the body folds, they are brought together in the middle and join into one tube. On about day 22 that tube starts to squeeze in a rhythm. It has no rooms and no valves yet, but it is already moving blood. Why so early? Until then, food and oxygen simply seep from cell to cell. Seeping works only over very short distances, and the embryo has grown too thick for it. Over the next weeks the beating tube grows longer than its space, so it bends into a loop. Walls grow inside and divide it into four chambers. It never stops beating while this happens. Other organs start as buds. The arms begin as two small bumps at about day 26, and the legs about two days later. The lungs begin as a bud from the food tube that branches again and again. By the end of week 8 the embryo is about 3 centimetres long, and every main organ has been started. From then until birth the baby is called a fetus, and the work is mostly growing and finishing.
- e.g. A cycle cannot be ridden until it is finished. An embryo's heart is used from the day it is a simple tube, and is rebuilt while in use.
- e.g. Your arm began as a bump about the size of a pinhead on the side of an embryo 4 millimetres long.
- e.g. A chick embryo's heart can be seen beating through a window cut in the eggshell after about two days of warming. This is how many of these facts were first found.
### How cells know where they are: signals from their neighbours · *defined*
**Definition:** Cells in an embryo release chemical signals, and other cells sense how much signal reaches them. From this a cell can tell where they are, and they switch on the genes that fit that place.

Nearly every cell in an embryo carries the same DNA. So a cell cannot find their job by reading something that only they have. They find it by sensing their surroundings. The hand is the best-studied example. A hand begins as a flat paddle. A small group of cells at the little-finger edge of the paddle makes a signal protein called Sonic hedgehog. The protein spreads across the paddle and thins out with distance. Cells that sense a lot of it, for a long time, build a little finger. Cells further away, which sense less, build the middle fingers. Cells that sense almost none build the thumb. In 1968 John Saunders and Mary Gasseling cut out those signalling cells from one chick embryo. They placed them on the thumb edge of another embryo's wing bud. That wing grew as a mirror image, with two sets of fingers back to back. In 1993 the team of Cliff Tabin found the signal itself. The fuller picture is that the amount is not the whole story. How long a cell is bathed in the signal, and which genes that cell already has switched on, matter too.
- e.g. Some babies are born with an extra finger or toe on the thumb side. In some families this comes from a small change in the DNA that makes Sonic hedgehog switch on at the thumb edge as well.
- e.g. The same Sonic hedgehog signal is used again in the neural tube, where it tells cells whether they are near the belly side or the back side.
- e.g. In the Mirror Wing activity you count how far each finger is from a coin, and see how a second coin makes a mirror hand.
- **Most developmental biologists:** The signal locks onto a receptor, a chain of chemical steps follows, and certain genes switch on. On this view, 'the cell works out where they are' is only a short way of describing that chain of chemistry.
### Hox genes mark each place from head to tail · *defined*
**Definition:** Hox genes are master switch genes. Different Hox genes are on at different places along the body, from behind the head to the tail, and the set that is on tells the cells which part to build there.

Signals tell a cell roughly where they are. The cell then needs to keep that information, even after the signal has gone. Hox genes do this. Each Hox gene makes a protein that grips DNA at certain spots and turns other genes on or off. Near the head end only the first few Hox genes are on. Further back, more of them are on. So each stretch of the body has its own set, like an address. In your backbone the address decides the kind of bone. Seven neck bones have no ribs. Twelve chest bones each carry a pair of ribs. Five lower back bones have none. One odd fact shows how address-like these genes are. On the chromosome, the Hox genes sit in a row, and their order in the row is the same as the order of the body parts they mark, from head end to tail end. A Hox gene does not hold the instructions for a rib or a leg. Those are in hundreds of other genes. The Hox gene only says which of them to use here. The very front of the head is marked by other switch genes, not by Hox genes.
- e.g. A mouse normally has 13 chest bones with ribs, then 6 lower back bones without. In 2003 Deneen Wellik and Mario Capecchi switched off the mouse's Hox10 genes. The lower back bones then grew ribs, as if their address said 'chest'.
- e.g. About 1 person in every 100 or 200 has a small extra rib on the lowest neck bone. Most never notice it.
- e.g. A giraffe has seven neck bones, the same number as you and as a mouse. Each bone is just very long.
### A changed Hox gene: the right part in the wrong place · *defined*
**Definition:** A homeotic change is a change in a master switch gene that makes one body part grow where another should be.

Fruit flies showed what Hox genes do. A fly's body is a row of segments. The middle part has three segments. The second carries the wings. The third carries two tiny knobs called halteres, which help the fly balance. Edward Lewis in California studied one Hox gene, Ultrabithorax, for more than thirty years. It is on in the third segment. When it is switched off there, the third segment builds what the second segment builds. The fly has four wings. In another fly, a Hox gene called Antennapedia is switched on in the head, where it should be off. The cells there read the address 'middle of the body' and build legs where the antennae should be. In both flies every part is properly made. A leg on the head has all its joints and bristles. This tells us something about how bodies are built. The instructions for a part work as one package. A master switch only has to say where to run the package.
- e.g. A fly with four wings has not gained a new gene for wings. They have lost the switch that said 'not here'.
- e.g. In the Address Game, a 'changed switch' card makes your creature build a good part in the wrong place.
- e.g. The English scientist William Bateson collected hundreds of such cases in animals and plants, and in 1894 he gave them the name 'homeosis'.
### The same switch genes build a fly and you · *defined*
**Definition:** Nearly all animals with a head end and a tail end use the same family of Hox genes to mark places along the body. The genes were inherited from an ancestor they all share.

A fly and a human look as different as two animals can. Until the 1980s most biologists were sure their bodies were built by quite different genes. Then, in 1984, two teams found that the fly's Hox genes all share one short stretch of DNA, 180 letters long. They named it the homeobox. When they searched other animals for the same stretch, they found it in beetles, worms, frogs, mice and humans. A fly has 8 Hox genes in one row. A human has 39, in four rows on four chromosomes. The four rows came about because the whole set was copied twice in our fish-like ancestors, long ago, and some copies were lost later. The order within each row still matches the order along the body. So the last ancestor that flies and humans share, probably a small worm-like animal that lived more than 500 million years ago, already marked their body from head to tail with these genes. The parts have changed beyond recognition. The way of marking where they go has been kept.
- e.g. A mouse Hox gene put into a fly embryo can do some of the work of the fly's own matching Hox gene.
- e.g. A centipede, a prawn and a snake all use Hox genes to tell the front of the body from the back.
- e.g. A jellyfish, who has no head end or tail end of this kind, has only a few Hox-like genes.
### Pax6: one switch that says 'build an eye here' · *defined*
**Definition:** Pax6 is a master switch gene that starts the building of an eye. The same gene does this job in flies, mice, squid and humans.

Flies with a broken copy of this gene have no eyes, so fly scientists named it 'eyeless'. In 1995 Georg Halder, Patrick Callaerts and Walter Gehring in Basel, Switzerland, did the opposite test. They switched the gene on in parts of a young fly where it is normally off. The flies grew extra eyes on their wings, legs and antennae. These were real fly eyes, with the full set of light-catching cells. Then the team used the matching gene from a mouse. The fly again grew extra eyes. They were fly eyes, not mouse eyes. This is the clearest test of what a master switch is. The mouse gene did not carry a plan for a mouse eye. It only said 'build an eye here', and the fly's cells built the kind of eye that flies build. The fuller picture is that Pax6 does not work alone. A handful of switch genes turn each other on, and the eye is built only when the whole group is running.
- e.g. People who inherit one broken copy of PAX6 are usually born with no iris, the coloured ring of the eye.
- e.g. A switch on a wall does not hold the light. The same switch could run a fan in another house.
- e.g. A fly's eye has hundreds of tiny lenses and a mouse's eye has one. The same switch gene starts both.
### Patterns that nobody draws: how five fingers may be spaced · *idea*
Signals that fade with distance can mark one edge from the other. But what makes a repeated pattern, such as finger, gap, finger, gap? In 1952 the English mathematician Alan Turing suggested an answer, using only pencil-and-paper maths. Imagine two chemicals that cells make. The first one makes cells produce more of both. The second one spreads faster and stops the first. Start with an even mix, and tiny chance differences grow. Peaks of the first chemical appear, each surrounded by a zone where the second chemical blocks any other peak. The peaks end up evenly spaced, as spots or as stripes. For decades nobody could find such chemicals in an animal. In 2014 the team of James Sharpe in Barcelona showed that three known signals in a mouse's growing paw behave this way, and their computer model drew stripes where the fingers form. This is now the leading idea for how fingers are spaced. It is not proved for humans, and other scientists think the pulling and squeezing of the cells themselves plays a part as well.
- e.g. When Sharpe's team weakened one of the signals in mouse paws, the fingers came out with different spacing, as the model predicted.
- e.g. The stripes of a zebrafish and the spots of a leopard may form in a related way, though the details differ.
- e.g. Ripples of sand on a beach are evenly spaced too, and nobody places them. Their cause is different, but they also show a pattern with no drawing behind it.
### Regrowing: how an axolotl replaces a lost leg · *defined*
**Definition:** Regeneration is growing back a lost part with the right tissues in the right places. An axolotl can regenerate legs, the tail, and parts of the spinal cord, heart and brain.

The axolotl is a salamander from the lakes and canals of Xochimilco, in Mexico City. She keeps her feathery gills and lives under water all her life. If she loses a leg, skin cells crawl over the wound within about a day. Under this cover, cells near the cut stop doing their grown-up jobs and start dividing. They form a bud called a blastema. Over some weeks the blastema grows and shapes itself into the missing part, with bones, muscles, nerves and toes. Two findings explain why the new leg comes out right. In 2009 Martin Kragl, Elly Tanaka and their team gave the cells of each tissue a glowing mark and followed them. Muscle cells made new muscle. Cartilage cells did not make muscle. So the cells do not become blank. They remember what tissue they were. In 2025 Leo Otsuki, Tanaka and their team found a memory of place as well. Cells on the little-finger side of the leg keep a gene called Hand2 switched on for life. After a cut, those cells switch Sonic hedgehog on again, the same signal that shaped the leg in the embryo. So regrowing is the embryo's method, run again by cells that kept their addresses.
- e.g. An axolotl cut at the wrist regrows a hand. Cut at the shoulder, she regrows the whole arm. The blastema builds only what is missing.
- e.g. A house gecko who drops their tail grows a new one. But the new tail has a plain rod of cartilage inside, where the first tail had a row of small bones.
- e.g. Wild axolotls are close to dying out, because their lakes have been drained and polluted. Many thousands live in laboratories and aquariums.
### Vitamin A and the legs that grew from a tail · *defined*
**Definition:** Retinoic acid, a chemical the body makes from vitamin A, is one of the signals that tells the cells of a limb how far they are from the shoulder. Too much of it resets their address.

In the 1970s, at the University of Rajasthan in Jaipur, Professor I. A. Niazi and his student Saroj Saxena cut the back legs of toad tadpoles and added extra vitamin A to their water. They wanted to see what the vitamin would do. In many tadpoles the stump grew far more than was missing, sometimes whole extra legs. Their papers of 1977 and 1978 were the first reports of this. In 1982 Malcolm Maden in London tested it carefully in axolotls. A leg cut at the wrist normally regrows a hand. With retinoic acid, the wrist stump grew a whole new arm, from shoulder to fingers, stuck onto the old one. The chemical had changed the cells' address from 'wrist' to 'shoulder', and they built everything from the shoulder outwards. In 1992 Priyambada Mohanty-Hejmadi and her team at Utkal University in Bhubaneswar went a step further. They cut the tails of tadpoles of the marbled balloon frog and gave them vitamin A. Some tadpoles grew legs where the tail had been. A tail address had been changed into a hip address.
- e.g. A tadpole with legs growing from the tail stump has no new genes. One chemical changed which address the cells read.
- e.g. This is why very high doses of vitamin A medicines are dangerous in pregnancy. They can upset the addresses in an embryo.
- e.g. The right amount of vitamin A, from foods such as carrot, papaya, drumstick leaves and milk, is needed for healthy eyes and for an embryo's normal growth.
### Why can an axolotl regrow a leg when a human cannot? · *debated*
Humans do regenerate some things. The outer skin, the lining of the gut and the blood are replaced all the time. A liver with a part cut away grows back to its full weight in weeks. A young child who loses the very tip of a finger can often grow it back if the base of the nail is still there. The English doctor Cynthia Illingworth reported this in 1974. In mice, Mayumi Ito's team showed in 2013 that the cells at the base of the nail send the signals that make the fingertip regrow. But a human who loses a hand heals with a scar. Scientists do not agree on why.
- e.g. Deer are mammals, and a stag regrows a full set of antlers, bone and all, every year.
- e.g. A zebrafish, a small striped fish first found in the rivers of Bengal and Bihar, can regrow fins and even part of the heart.
- e.g. In the Nail Clock experiment you measure how fast one part of you is still being built.
- **Scientists who study wound healing:** Mammals close wounds fast with a scar. A scar stops bleeding and infection quickly, which matters for a warm-blooded animal on land. But scar tissue blocks the signals a blastema needs.
- **Scientists who study cancer:** Regrowing needs grown cells to start dividing freely again. In a long-lived animal, cells that divide freely can turn into cancers. Our bodies may keep such cells under tight control, at the cost of regrowing.
- **Scientists who study axolotls, such as Elly Tanaka and Jessica Whited:** Humans have nearly all the genes that an axolotl uses, and a child's fingertip shows the method still works in us. The ability may be switched off rather than lost, and might be switched on again one day.
### Is the plan of the body written in the DNA? · *debated*
People often call DNA the blueprint of the body. A blueprint is a drawing in which each part of the drawing matches one part of the building. DNA is not like that. No stretch of DNA is a picture of a hand. DNA lists proteins and the conditions for making them. The shape appears as cells make those proteins, signal to their neighbours, move, pull and fold. So where is the shape stored? Scientists give different answers.
- e.g. A recipe for dosa batter does not contain a picture of a dosa. The round shape comes from pouring and spreading.
- e.g. In the Rule, Not the Drawing challenge you write rules for single coins, and a body pattern appears that no rule mentions.
- e.g. Flatworms that were made to grow two heads kept growing two heads after being cut again, though their DNA had not been changed.
- **Most geneticists:** Everything needed is in the fertilised egg cell: the DNA, and the molecules the mother packed into the egg. The shape is not drawn anywhere, but it follows from them step by step, as a cake follows from a recipe and an oven.
- **Michael Levin and his team at Tufts University:** Groups of cells also store a pattern in the electrical charges they hold and share, a little as a brain stores a memory. Changing that pattern can change the body's shape with no change in the DNA. Many biologists are not yet convinced this works in most animals.
- **Scientists who study the forces in tissues:** Much of the shape comes from plain mechanics. Sheets buckle, tubes loop and buds branch because of how they are squeezed and stretched. Genes set the conditions, and physics does the shaping.
### The building never stops · *defined*
**Definition:** The signals and switch genes that build an embryo keep working in a grown body, where they replace worn-out cells, heal wounds and keep each part the right size.

Being born is not the end of being built. Your skeleton is being taken apart and rebuilt a little every day. The outer layer of your skin is replaced about once a month. Your nails and hair grow from groups of dividing cells that never stop. When you cut your knee, cells at the edge of the wound crawl over it in a sheet, much as cells crawl during gastrulation. The same signals are used. This has a dangerous side. Many cancers start when one of the embryo's building signals, such as Sonic hedgehog, gets stuck in the 'on' position in a grown body. Cells then keep dividing as if they were still building. So some new cancer medicines work by blocking those signals. A body is something its cells keep doing for as long as it lives.
- e.g. A fingernail grows about three and a half millimetres in a month. In the Nail Clock experiment you measure your own.
- e.g. A broken arm bone knits itself together in about six weeks in a child. The cells first lay down soft cartilage and then turn it to bone, as they did before birth.
- e.g. The lining of your small intestine is replaced about every five days.

## Mostly agreed
- Every human body begins as one cell, and every part is built afterwards. No tiny ready-made body lies hidden in the egg cell. *How we know:* Embryos of chicks, frogs, mice and humans have been watched and photographed at every stage. The parts appear one after another, from cells that at first look alike.
- Early in growth, the cells of an animal embryo arrange themselves into layers, and each layer makes a particular set of tissues. *How we know:* Scientists have marked single cells in early embryos with dyes and glowing proteins, and followed where their daughter cells end up.
- Flat sheets of cells fold into tubes because the cells themselves change shape and move. *How we know:* Under the microscope, cells along the folding line of the neural tube can be seen narrowing at one end. Blocking the protein threads that tighten them stops the folding.
- Cells in an embryo find out where they are from chemical signals made by other cells. *How we know:* Moving the signalling cells of a chick's wing bud to the other edge gives a mirror-image wing (Saunders and Gasseling, 1968). The signal was later found to be the protein Sonic hedgehog (1993).
- Hox genes mark places along the body from head end to tail end, and animals as different as flies, mice and humans use the same family of genes for this. *How we know:* Changing one Hox gene makes a normal part grow in the wrong place, in flies and in mice. The same 180-letter stretch of DNA is found in the Hox genes of all these animals.
- When an axolotl regrows a leg, the cells of the stump keep a memory of what tissue they were and where they were. *How we know:* Cells marked with a glowing protein were followed through regrowth (Kragl and others, 2009). Cells on one side of the leg keep the gene Hand2 on for life (Otsuki and others, 2025).

## How we found out
**William Bateson, Calvin Bridges, Edward Lewis, Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard, Eric Wieschaus, Walter Gehring, William McGinnis, Matthew Scott and Amy Weiner, 1894 to 1995.** In 1894 the English scientist William Bateson published a book of oddities: animals with a normal part in the wrong place. He called this homeosis. In 1915 Calvin Bridges found a fruit fly whose balance knobs were partly wings. From the 1940s, Edward Lewis in California bred such flies for over thirty years. In 1978 he showed that a row of genes decides what each segment becomes. Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard and Eric Wieschaus then searched thousands of fly families in Heidelberg, Germany. In 1980 they reported 15 genes that lay out the segments. Still, almost everyone believed that these were fly genes only. The famous biologist Ernst Mayr had written in 1963 that looking for the same genes in distant animals was 'quite futile'. In 1984 the teams of Walter Gehring in Switzerland, and of Matthew Scott and Amy Weiner in the United States, found a short stretch of DNA shared by all the fly's Hox genes. Gehring's team searched other animals for it. It was in worms, frogs, mice and humans. Mayr had been wrong. Lewis, Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus shared the Nobel Prize in 1995.  
*What people believed before:* Until 1984 most biologists were sure that animals as different as a fly and a human must be built by quite different genes. Finding the same Hox genes in both showed this was wrong.

## Challenging perspectives
- **DNA is the blueprint of the body.** (Many school books and news reports; *debated*)  
  For: A change in the DNA can change the body, and identical twins, who share their DNA, look alike. So the information for building a body is passed on in the DNA.  
  Against: A blueprint is a drawing where each part matches a part of the building. No stretch of DNA matches a hand. DNA lists proteins and when to make them. The same mouse gene Pax6 gives a fly eye in a fly. A recipe, or a set of rules, is a closer comparison.
- **Each gene builds one part of the body.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Some genes are named after one part, such as 'eyeless'. Losing one gene can remove one part.  
  Against: The signal Sonic hedgehog helps shape the hand, the spinal cord, the face, the teeth and the hair. Most genes are used in many places and at many times. A gene's name usually records only the first thing scientists saw go wrong.
- **Humans have lost the ability to regenerate.** (Many older textbooks; *debated*)  
  For: A human who loses an arm or a leg heals with a scar and never regrows it.  
  Against: Young children can regrow a lost fingertip if the base of the nail remains. The liver regrows to its full weight. Humans carry nearly all the genes that an axolotl uses. The ability may be held back, not gone.
- **The body's shape is remembered partly in electrical signals between cells, and not only in genes.** (Michael Levin and his team at Tufts University, United States; *minority*)  
  For: The team briefly blocked the tiny channels that join the cells of cut flatworms. Some worms grew two heads. Cut again weeks later, in plain water, they grew two heads again. Their DNA had not been changed.  
  Against: The work has been done mostly in flatworms and frog tadpoles. Many biologists think the electrical changes are one more signal that switches genes, and not a separate store of the body's shape.
- **A snake has a long body with no legs because the Hox genes for 'chest' are switched on along almost the whole body.** (Martin Cohn and Cheryll Tickle, in a study of python embryos, 1999; *debated*)  
  For: In python embryos, Hox genes that mark the chest in other animals are on nearly all the way from the head to the hips. Nearly every bone of a python's back carries ribs.  
  Against: Later studies found that snakes still have small differences between regions of the backbone, and that their Hox genes are on in the usual order. Changes in how the body's cells answer the Hox genes seem to matter too.

## Roots
We have learned how one cell becomes a body by dividing, forming layers, folding, and using signals and switch genes. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Curling Strip Test, nobody bent the strips. Cells on one face swelled, and each strip curled. Your spinal cord rolled up from a flat sheet in a similar way, because its own cells changed shape. A cycle has a maker who stands outside it. In a body, the cells doing the building are the body being built.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a body is 'built' or 'made', the words we use for a house or a cycle. Those words bring a builder, a drawing and a pile of parts with them. An embryo has none of these. Nobody stands outside the embryo, no drawing of the body exists, and the embryo is alive and working at every stage.  
*Evidence:* In the Curling Strip Test, flat strips of spring onion curled in water with nothing bending them. An embryo's heart beats from about day 22 as a plain tube and keeps beating while it is rebuilt into four chambers. An axolotl regrows a leg from the cells of her own stump.

- *who am i:* You are not a thing that was finished and then switched on. You are cells that have been building, and being built, without a break since you were one cell.
- *what is life:* A living body makes itself. Each cell senses their neighbours and acts, and the whole shape comes from all of them acting together.
- *what is this world:* The world holds patterns that no one draws. A braid, a body and a row of fingers can all appear from small rules followed in many places at once.

You started as one cell. That one cell became many cells, and the many became different from each other: skin, bone, nerve and blood. Yet they did this by staying in touch, each sensing the others and answering. The cells never stopped being one body. A fly's body and yours are laid out by the same family of genes, handed down from one ancestor. So the many kinds of animal bodies are also one way of building, changed a little at a time.

- **Sushruta Samhita, Sharirasthana 3.32 (paraphrased):** The old physicians argue about which part of an unborn child forms first. One says the head, one the heart, one the navel, one the hands and feet, one the trunk. Dhanvantari answers that all the parts form together, and are at first too fine to be seen, like the parts of a bamboo shoot or of a young mango. (This is the same question this lesson asks, argued in India about two thousand years ago by people with no microscopes. Today's evidence says the parts do appear in an order, and the heart is the first to work. But Dhanvantari was right that the early stages were too small for anyone then to see.)
- **Tao Te Ching 64:** “合抱之木，生於毫末。 / 千里之行，始於足下。” A tree too big to put your arms around grows from a tiny shoot. A tower of nine storeys rises from a heap of earth. A journey of a thousand li begins under your feet. (Laozi says that the largest things come from small beginnings by small steps. A body of trillions of cells comes from one cell, by one division and one fold at a time. No single step is large.)
- **Aristotle, Generation of Animals, Book 2, chapter 1 (paraphrased):** Aristotle asks whether all the parts of an animal are there from the start, or come into being one after another, like the knitting of a net. He had watched chicks form inside hens' eggs, and he concludes that the parts form one after another. (Aristotle looked instead of only arguing, and the embryos you have read about agree with him. A net is also made by one simple knot repeated, with no knot holding the pattern of the whole net.)
- Science (settled): Every part of an animal's body is made after fertilisation, step by step, from cells that at first look alike. No ready-made body lies in the egg cell.
- Science (settled): Flies, worms, fish and humans mark their bodies from head to tail with the same family of Hox genes, inherited from one ancestor.
- Science (debated): Where the shape of a body is stored is not agreed. It may follow fully from genes and chemistry, or cells may also hold it in shared electrical patterns.
- Other view, Most developmental biologists: A cell does not sense and choose in any way that needs a mind. Signals lock onto receptors, chains of chemical reactions follow, and genes switch on or off. Words like 'decide' and 'remember' are a handy short way to describe chemistry, and the building of a body needs nothing more than that chemistry.

## Story: The Braid Nobody Knew
*A made-up story. Kayal, Muthu, Ponni akka and Selvi teacher are imagined. Pinnal kolattam is real. It is a dance from Tamil Nadu in which dancers hold ropes that hang from a pole, and weave the ropes into a braid by the way they move past each other. One wrong move can spoil the braid. The two rules in the story are a simple way such braids can be made. Real troupes use many different steps, and the patterns described here are imagined.*

Eight ropes hung from an iron ring at the top of the pole in the school yard. Four were red and four were white. For three weeks, eight girls had danced round that pole every evening, and every evening a braid had grown down it, red and white in neat checks, like the skin of a snake.

Ponni akka made it happen. She was sixteen, and she called out every beat in a voice that carried to the tank. Kayal, who was ten and held the third white rope, did what the voice said and tried not to think.

Three days before the festival, Ponni akka woke up with a fever and spots.

"Then there is no dance," said one of the older girls that evening. "Only Ponni knows the braid."

Nobody argued. Selvi teacher, who had taught them, was away in Thanjavur for her daughter's wedding. The eight ropes hung straight and still.

Kayal went home and tried to draw the braid. She drew the pole, and eight lines going round it, over and under. By the fourth crossing her page looked like a plate of idiyappam. She tore it out and tried again. It was worse.

In the morning she walked to Ponni akka's house and stood at the window, because nobody was allowed inside.

"Akka, tell me the whole braid. I will write it down. Every crossing."

Ponni akka laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough. "I don't know the whole braid."

"You do. You call it."

"I call the beat, kanna. Listen. Reds walk towards the tank. Whites walk towards the road. When someone comes at you, pass her on the inside. Pass the next one on the outside. Then inside again. That is all Selvi teacher ever told me. That is all there is."

"But the checks. The pattern."

"I have never once thought about the pattern," said Ponni akka. "I am busy not bumping into people."

Kayal walked back slowly. A rope's colour told a dancer which way to go. The girl coming towards her told her which side to pass. Nobody needed the page that looked like idiyappam. Nobody had ever had it.

That evening there were only seven girls, so Kayal brought her brother Muthu, who was seven and had only ever watched. She put the fourth red rope in his hand.

"I don't know the dance," he said.

"Nobody does. You are red, so you walk towards the tank. Pass the first person on the inside and the next on the outside. Keep doing that. Don't look at the pole."

She clapped the beat herself. Her voice did not carry to the tank, but it carried round the pole. Reds one way, whites the other, in and out. For a minute it was only people trying not to bump into each other. Then one of the girls looked up and gasped. A braid was coming down the pole in neat red and white checks.

At the height of Kayal's shoulder, it went wrong. A lump appeared, two reds lying side by side where a red and a white should cross.

Everybody stopped. Nobody had seen who did it. But the braid had kept the mistake, at the exact place where it happened, for all of them to see.

"I think I passed two people on the same side," said Muthu, in a very small voice.

One of the older girls took hold of the lump and pulled. It only tightened.

"Wait," said Kayal. "We made it by walking. We can unmake it by walking." She turned to face the other way. "Everyone go back the way you came. Outside where you went inside. Slowly."

They walked backwards through their own steps. The braid opened from the bottom, crossing by crossing, until the lump loosened and fell apart into two plain ropes. Above it the checks were as neat as before.

"Now forwards," said Kayal.

On the night of the festival, one of the white ropes had no dancer, because her family's bus was late. Seven dancers took their places round the pole under the lamps. Kayal's stomach hurt. She had no time to work out what seven would do.

She did not need to. Reds towards the tank, whites towards the road, inside, outside. The braid that came down the pole was not the braid of checks. The colours ran in slanting bands, a pattern none of them had seen before, and it was even all the way down.

Afterwards Muthu stood looking up at it with his rope still in his hand.

"Who made that one?" he asked. "Which of us?"

Kayal opened her mouth to answer. She looked at the seven dancers, and at the pole, and at the braid that none of them had ever seen until it was there.

"Hold your rope," she said at last. "We have to walk it back."

**For the parent:** Kayal thought someone had to know the whole braid. Nobody did. Each dancer knew only two small things: her rope's colour told her which way to walk, and the person coming towards her told her which side to pass. The braid appeared when everyone followed those rules. Cells build a body in a similar way. No cell holds a picture of a hand or a heart. Each cell senses where they are and what their neighbours are doing, and acts on that. The braid also kept Muthu's mistake at the exact place where he made it, as a body keeps a changed part at the place where a switch was wrong. And when one dancer was missing, the same rules still made a whole braid, as an embryo with fewer cells still makes a whole body.

- Ask: Why could Kayal not draw the braid, even though she had danced it for three weeks?
- Ask: Ponni akka said she had never thought about the pattern. So where was the pattern before the dance began?
- Ask: The lump showed where the mistake was made, but not who made it. What in an embryo is like that lump?
- Ask: With seven dancers the same rules made a different braid, and it was still even. What does that tell you about rules compared with drawings?
- Ask: Muthu asked which of them made the braid. What would you have answered?

  *Parent does:* 1. Fill both tumblers with cold water from one jug. 2. Stir 2 heaped teaspoons of salt into one tumbler until it has dissolved. 3. Ask your child for both guesses and the reasons. 4. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Three strips go in plain water, three in salt water and three stay dry. Which will bend most? Which face will be inside the bend? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which strips will bend most. Guess which face will be inside the bend. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Plain water, because the cells will soak up water and swell.; The soft inner face will swell more, so the tough skin ends up inside.; Any guess with a reason is a good guess.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children expect salt water and plain water to do the same thing.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay three strips on the dry plate. 2. Drop three strips into the plain water and three into the salt water. 3. Push them under with the spoon. 4. Write down the time.  
  *Parent says:* All nine strips start straight, so the gap between the two ends of each strip is 10 centimetres. Now we wait and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Put three strips on the plate. Put three in each tumbler. Push them under. Write the time.
- **s4 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 10 minutes, lift each strip out with the spoon and lay it on the plate without straightening it. 2. Measure the straight gap between its two ends. 3. Put it back. 4. Do the same after 20 minutes. 5. Work out the mean gap for each place.  
  *Parent says:* Do not pull the strips straight. Measure straight across from one end to the other. A small gap means a strong curl. Look at which face is on the inside of the curl.  
  *Child mode:* After 10 minutes, measure the gap between the ends. Put the strips back. Measure again after 20 minutes.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the strips in the three places? (good: The ones in plain water have curled, some into rings.; The ones in salt water are limp and nearly straight.; The shiny skin is on the inside of the curl.)
  - Note: While you wait, your child can draw the two faces of a strip. If a strip has curled into more than one full ring, write 0 for the gap and note the number of turns. Most families find the shiny skin on the inside of the curl. If yours curl the other way, write down what you see. Your strips are the evidence. If nothing has curled after 20 minutes, add ice and wait 15 minutes more. Thin strips curl faster than wide ones.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what will happen if the strips swap places. 2. Move the curled strips from the plain water into the salt water. 3. Rinse the salt-water strips and move them into fresh plain water. 4. Look again after 5 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Now the strips swap tumblers. What do you think will happen to the curled ones in salt water? What will happen to the limp ones in plain water? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what happens if the strips swap. Swap them. Look again after 5 minutes.
  - Note: The curled strips usually loosen in the salt water within a few minutes. The limp strips usually begin to curl in plain water, though some stay limp if the salt has harmed their cells. Either result is worth writing down.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for the three sentences. 2. Ask them to draw one curled strip and mark which face got bigger. 3. Only then read the explanation 'Folding: how flat sheets become tubes' together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Nobody bent these strips. So which face of the strip must have grown longer than the other?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed. Say what you saw. Say why it happened. Draw a curled strip. Mark the face that got longer.
  - Ask: On a curled strip, which face is longer: the one on the outside of the curl or the one on the inside? (good: The outside face, because it has further to go round.; The soft inner face of the leaf swelled, so it became the longer, outside face.)
  - Ask: Why did salt water not make the strips curl? (good: The cells could not take in water from salty water.; Salt water pulls water out of cells, so the soft face did not swell.)
  - Note: The soft cells on the inner face take in water and swell. The tough outer skin hardly stretches. One face is now longer than the other, so the strip must curl. In the embryo the cause is different: cells on one face tighten and become narrower. The result is the same kind of bend.
  - Check: In the Curling Strip Test, strips of spring onion curled in plain water. What made them curl? → **The cells on one face swelled more than those on the other**
### e2 · The Nail Clock: is your body still building, and how fast? (35 min, 29 days)
- **question:** How many millimetres does your thumbnail grow in four weeks? Does your big toenail grow as fast as your thumbnail?
- **predict:** Guess how many millimetres your thumbnail will grow in four weeks. Then guess whether the big toenail will grow as fast, faster or slower. Give your reason for each guess.
- **fair test:** Use the same person, and measure on the same day each week. Always measure from the edge of the skin at the base of the nail to the bottom edge of the mark, along the middle of the nail. Use the same ruler each time. Paint over the same mark if it fades, and never move it.
- **record:** Write the two gaps in the table each week. On day 29, draw a line graph with the days along the bottom and the gap up the side, one line for each nail. Then write what you guessed, what you measured, and what you think it shows.
- **results table:** Day | Thumbnail: gap from skin to mark (mm) | Big toenail: gap from skin to mark (mm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Paint a thin line across the thumbnail and the big toenail, touching the skin at the base. Write down your guesses. (10 min); day 8: Measure the gap from the skin to each line. Paint over the lines if they have faded. (5 min); day 15: Measure both gaps again. (5 min); day 22: Measure both gaps again. (5 min); day 29: Measure both gaps for the last time. Draw a line graph and work out the growth in one week. (10 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wash and dry one hand and one foot. 2. Dip the toothpick in the nail polish. 3. Paint a thin line across the thumbnail, touching the skin at the base. 4. Do the same on the big toenail. 5. Ask for the guesses and the reasons, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* This line sits where your nail comes out from under the skin. How far will it have moved in four weeks? Will the toenail's line move as far? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Paint a thin line at the base of your thumbnail. Paint one on your big toenail. Guess how far each will move. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: I cut my fingernails more often than my toenails, so fingernails must grow faster.; They are the same kind of nail, so they should grow the same.; Any guess with a reason is a good guess.)
  - Note: A line as thin as a pencil line is best. Let it dry fully. A child who bites their nails can still do this, because the mark starts at the base.
  - Wait: Leave the marks for 7 days
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 8, hold the ruler along the middle of the thumbnail. 2. Measure from the edge of the skin to the bottom edge of the line. 3. Do the same for the toenail. 4. Write both numbers down. 5. Paint over a faded line without moving it.  
  *Parent says:* Measure from where the skin ends to where the paint begins. Is there bare nail below the line now? Where did that new nail come from?  
  *Child mode:* Measure from the skin to the line on each nail. Write both numbers. Paint over the line if it has faded.
  - Note: After one week the gap on the thumbnail is about 1 millimetre, which is hard to read on a ruler. Estimate to the nearest half millimetre. The toenail's gap may be too small to measure yet.
  - Wait: Leave the marks for 7 days
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 15, measure both gaps in the same way. 2. Write them down. 3. Ask your child whether the two nails are keeping pace.  
  *Parent says:* Two weeks have passed. Measure both gaps again. Is one line moving faster than the other?  
  *Child mode:* Measure both gaps again. Write them down. Which line is moving faster?
  - Wait: Leave the marks for 7 days
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 22, measure both gaps and write them down. 2. Ask your child to guess the day 29 numbers from the pattern so far.  
  *Parent says:* Three weeks have passed. Look at your numbers so far. What do you expect next week, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Measure both gaps. Write them down. Guess next week's numbers from the pattern.
  - Wait: Leave the marks for 7 days
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 29, measure both gaps for the last time. 2. Draw the line graph with one line for each nail. 3. Divide each final gap by 4 to get the growth in one week. 4. Ask for the three sentences.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you guessed, what you measured, and what you think it shows. The nail you see is made of dead cells. So where is the living part that is doing the building?  
  *Child mode:* Measure both gaps. Draw a line for each nail. Divide each gap by 4. Say what you found.
  - Ask: The line moved, but the nail is hard and dead. What pushed it? (good: New cells made at the base, under the skin, pushed the older nail forward.; The living, dividing cells are at the root of the nail.)
  - Note: Most fingernails grow about 3 to 4 millimetres in four weeks, and toenails about half of that. Children's nails often grow a little faster than adults'. Why toenails are slower is not fully known. Ideas include a smaller blood supply to the toes.
  - Check: In the Nail Clock, the painted line moved away from the skin over four weeks. What does that show? → **New nail is made at the base and pushes the old nail forward**

### a4 · The Mirror Wing: how does a finger know which finger to be? (15 min)
- **record:** Draw the three hands you made: with one coin, with two coins, and with a coin in the middle. Write which fingers each hand has.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw five boxes in a row and number them 1 to 5. 2. Put one coin beside box 1. 3. Read the rule: a box 1 step from the coin grows a little finger. Then 2 steps gives a ring finger, 3 a middle finger, 4 an index finger and 5 a thumb. 4. Your child draws the five fingers.  
  *Parent says:* The coin is a group of cells sending out a signal. The signal gets weaker with every step. Each box senses how strong it is, and builds the finger that fits.  
  *Child mode:* Draw five boxes. Put a coin beside box 1. Count the steps from the coin. Draw the finger for each count.
  - Note: Box 1 counts as 1 step from the coin. The hand comes out as little, ring, middle, index, thumb.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw five new boxes. 2. Put one coin beside box 1 and a second coin beside box 5. 3. Ask your child to guess what hand will grow, and why. 4. They count from the nearer coin for each box and draw the fingers.  
  *Parent says:* Now there is a second group of signalling cells at the thumb edge. Each box senses the nearer coin. What hand will grow? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put a coin at both ends. Guess what hand grows. Say why. Count from the nearer coin. Draw the fingers.
  - Ask: What hand grew with two coins? (good: Little, ring, middle, ring, little.; A mirror hand, with no thumb at all.)
  - Note: This is what John Saunders and Mary Gasseling saw in chick wings in 1968. A real wing bud with a second signal also grows wider, so it has room for extra fingers.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw five more boxes. 2. Ask where the coin should go to make a hand with the little finger in the middle. 3. Test the idea by counting. 4. Ask what a hand with no coin at all would be.  
  *Parent says:* Where would you put one coin to get a little finger in the middle? And what if there were no coin at all?  
  *Child mode:* Where must the coin go to put the little finger in the middle? Test it. What if there is no coin?
  - Note: A coin at box 3 gives middle, ring, little, ring, middle. With no signal at all, real limb buds build very few fingers, and those are thumb-like.
  - Check: Cells at the thumb edge of a hand bud sense very little of the Sonic hedgehog signal. Why do they build a thumb? → **A weak signal tells them they are far from the source**

### c1 · The One-Sheet Body: make a tube inside a tube without cutting (25 min)

  *Parent does:* 1. Your child designs their own version and says what each fold is for. 2. They build it and test it with the pencil. 3. They find the join and compare it with their own body.

  *Child mode:* Design your own. Build it. Test it with a pencil. Find the join. Where is that join on you?
  - Note: On the paper body the join runs along the belly. On a real body the sides met along the front, and the navel is the last place that closed.
  - Check: The lining of your food tube is the inside of you. Where was that sheet of cells in the flat embryo? → **On the underside of the flat disc**
### c2 · The Rule, Not the Drawing: build a pattern that no rule describes (25 min)
- **problem:** Lay 12 coins in a row. Write rules so that the row ends up with a 'head' of 2 coins turned to heads at one end, a 'tail' of 2 coins standing on bottle caps at the other end, and a plain middle. No rule may name a coin's place in the row.
- **your turn:** Now write your own rules and hand them to your follower. Did the pattern appear? If one coin is taken out of the middle, do your rules still give a head, a middle and a tail?
- **constraints:** A rule may speak only about one coin and the coin on each side of it.; No rule may use a number for a place in the row, such as 'coin 3' or 'the last two'.; A grown-up or friend follows your rules exactly, coin by coin, and does nothing the rules do not say.
- **general:** Write 'make the first two coins heads'. This breaks the rule about naming places.; Tell the follower to copy a drawing of the finished row. This is a blueprint, which cells do not have.; Give each coin a different label before starting. Real cells all start with the same DNA.
- **creative:** Give one end coin a turmeric mark as a signal source. Rule: 'If the coin beside you has a mark, or is heads because of a mark, and you are tails, turn to heads and stop the message.' Count how far the message goes.; Let the message weaken. Give the marked coin 2 grains of rice. A coin that holds rice turns to heads, keeps one grain and passes the rest to the next coin.; Use two signals, one from each end, so that a coin that senses neither knows they are in the middle.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the 12 coins, tails up. 2. Read the problem and the three rules of the challenge. 3. Your child writes a first set of rules. 4. Follow them exactly, even if they go wrong.  
  *Parent says:* I will be the cells. I can only do what your rules say, and each coin can see only the coins beside it. Write your rules.  
  *Child mode:* Lay 12 coins tails up. Write rules for one coin and its neighbours. Your grown-up follows them exactly.
  - Note: Follow the rules as literally as you can. If a rule says 'the first coin', say that a coin cannot know it is first.
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Talk about what went wrong. 2. Try the signal idea: mark one end coin with turmeric as a source. 3. Give that coin 2 grains of rice. 4. A coin that holds rice turns to heads, keeps one grain and passes the rest on.  
  *Parent says:* A coin cannot know its place. But it can sense a signal from a neighbour. Let's give one end a signal that gets weaker at each coin.  
  *Child mode:* Mark one end coin. Give it 2 rice grains. A coin with rice turns to heads. It keeps one grain and passes the rest on.
  - Ask: How did two coins come to be the head, when no rule said 'two'? (good: The signal ran out after two coins.; How far the signal reaches sets the size of the head.)
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child writes final rules, with a second signal from the other end for the tail. 2. Follow them exactly. 3. Take one coin out of the middle, reset the row and run the rules again. 4. Ask whether the pattern still appears.  
  *Parent says:* Now make the whole body: head, middle and tail. Then we take one coin away and run it again. Will your rules still work? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Write rules for head and tail. Run them. Take a middle coin away. Run them again. Does the pattern come back?
  - Note: Rules based on signals still work with 11 coins or 15. A drawing of 12 coins would not fit. Embryos of different sizes still come out with the right parts for the same reason.
  - Check: A row of coins followed only neighbour rules, and a head, a middle and a tail appeared. Where was the pattern stored? → **In no single place: it came from the rules being followed**

### From one cell to a folded body (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) During cleavage the embryo's cells get more numerous. What happens to their size?  
   Options: Each cell gets bigger / Each cell gets smaller / They stay the same size / Only the outer cells shrink  
   **Answer:** Each cell gets smaller. *Why:* The embryo is not eating yet, so each division shares out what was in the egg cell. More cells from the same material means smaller cells.
2. (fill) A solid ball of about 16 cells is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['morula']. *Why:* Morula is Latin for mulberry, because the ball of cells looks like that fruit.
3. (order) Put these events of the first four weeks in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The egg cell is fertilised', 'The cell divides into a ball', 'Three layers form', 'The neural tube closes']. *Why:* Dividing comes first, because layers need many cells. The layers form in week 3, and the outer layer then rolls into the neural tube in week 4.
4. (match) Match each germ layer to its place in the flat embryo.  
   **Answer:** Ectoderm → the top sheet; Mesoderm → the middle sheet; Endoderm → the bottom sheet. *Why:* Ecto means outer, meso means middle and endo means inner. After folding, the top sheet covers the outside and the bottom sheet lines the gut.
5. (sort) Sort these body parts by the germ layer that made them.  
   **Answer:** brain → Ectoderm; thigh bone → Mesoderm; lining of the stomach → Endoderm; fingernail → Ectoderm; heart → Mesoderm; liver → Endoderm. *Why:* The outer layer makes the covering and the nervous system. The middle layer makes what supports and moves the body. The inner layer makes the food tube's lining and the organs that bud from it.
6. (truefalse) True or false: a germ layer is a layer of germs that can make you ill.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. 'Germ' here means a beginning, as in 'germinate'. A germ layer is a sheet of the embryo's own cells.
7. (mcq) In the Curling Strip Test, which strips of spring onion curled the most?  
   Options: The ones in salt water / The dry ones / All curled equally / The ones in plain water  
   **Answer:** The ones in plain water. *Why:* In plain water the soft cells on one face take in water and swell, so that face becomes longer and the strip curls. Check this against your own table.
8. (write) In the Curling Strip Test, why were three strips used in each place and not one?  
   **Answer:** One strip can behave oddly because of how it was cut or which part of the leaf it came from. Three strips, and their mean, show what usually happens.. *Why:* A strong answer says that one result can be a fluke, and that repeats let you see the usual result.
9. (draw) Draw a curled strip from the Curling Strip Test. Label the face that got longer.  
   **Answer:** A curved strip with the label 'longer face' on the outside of the curve, and 'shiny skin' on the inside.. *Why:* The face on the outside of a curve has further to go round, so it must be the longer one. That is the face whose cells swelled.
10. (mcq) How does a flat sheet of embryo cells bend into a groove?  
   Options: A bone pushes it from below / The mother's body folds it / The cells on both faces shrink equally / Cells on one face become narrower  
   **Answer:** Cells on one face become narrower. *Why:* A ring of protein threads tightens at the top of each cell, so the cells become wedge-shaped. A row of wedges cannot stay flat.
11. (fill) The neural tube becomes the brain and the ____ ____.  
   **Answer:** ['spinal cord']. *Why:* The front of the tube swells into the brain. The rest becomes the spinal cord, which runs down inside your backbone.
12. (truefalse) True or false: an embryo's heart waits until it has four chambers before it starts to beat.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The heart starts beating at about day 22, when it is a plain tube. It is rebuilt into four chambers while it works.
13. (label) Draw a cut-through of a folded embryo as two circles, one inside the other. Label the three layers.  
   **Answer:** ['Ectoderm on the outer circle', 'Endoderm on the inner circle', 'Mesoderm in the space between']. *Why:* After folding, the ectoderm covers the outside, the endoderm lines the gut tube, and the mesoderm fills the space between them.
14. (write) Why does an embryo need a working heart when it is only a few millimetres long?  
   **Answer:** Food and oxygen can seep from cell to cell only over a very short distance. Once the embryo is thicker than that, blood must carry them, so a pump is needed.. *Why:* A strong answer links the size of the embryo to the need to move food and oxygen.
15. (mcq) What do doctors give to women who may become pregnant, to help the neural tube close?  
   Options: Extra salt / Vitamin D only / Antibiotics / Folic acid  
   **Answer:** Folic acid. *Why:* Folate, a B vitamin, is needed in the first four weeks, when the neural tube closes. Tablets of folic acid lower the risk of a gap in the spine.
### Signals, switches and addresses (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Where in a growing hand is the signal Sonic hedgehog made?  
   Options: At the thumb edge / At the little-finger edge / In the middle finger / In the wrist only  
   **Answer:** At the little-finger edge. *Why:* A small group of cells at the little-finger edge makes the signal. It thins out towards the thumb edge.
2. (order) In the Mirror Wing, one coin sits beside box 1. Put the fingers in order from box 1 to box 5.  
   **Answer:** ['Little finger', 'Ring finger', 'Middle finger', 'Index finger', 'Thumb']. *Why:* The box nearest the coin senses the most signal and builds a little finger. Each step further away builds the next finger, ending with the thumb.
3. (draw) In the Mirror Wing, a coin sits at each end of five boxes. Draw the hand that grows.  
   **Answer:** Five fingers in the order little, ring, middle, ring, little. There is no thumb.. *Why:* Each box senses the nearer coin, so the counts are 1, 2, 3, 2, 1. The two halves mirror each other.
4. (truefalse) True or false: the cells that build your thumb have different DNA from the cells that build your little finger.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Nearly all your cells carry the same DNA. The cells differ in which signals reached them and which genes they switched on.
5. (fill) A gene that turns many other genes on or off is called a master ____ gene.  
   **Answer:** ['switch']. *Why:* A master switch gene makes a protein that grips DNA and turns other genes on or off, so it can start the building of a whole part.
6. (mcq) On a chromosome, how are the Hox genes arranged?  
   Options: In the same order as the body parts they mark / In order of size / Scattered at random / In alphabetical order  
   **Answer:** In the same order as the body parts they mark. *Why:* The Hox genes sit in a row, and the order in the row matches the order along the body, from the head end to the tail end.
7. (match) Match each changed animal to what happened.  
   **Answer:** Fly with four wings → a Hox gene was off in the third middle segment; Fly with legs on the head → a Hox gene was on in the head; Mouse with ribs on the lower back → the Hox10 genes were off; Fly with an eye on a wing → Pax6 was switched on in the wing. *Why:* In every case a master switch was on or off in the wrong place, and a well-made part grew where it should not.
8. (write) A fly with a changed Hox gene has well-made legs on the head. What does 'well-made' tell you?  
   **Answer:** The instructions for building a leg were not damaged. Only the instruction about where to build one was wrong. So the Hox gene carries the place, not the leg.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the plan for a part from the address for a part.
9. (sort) Sort each statement: is it about a signal or about a Hox gene?  
   **Answer:** It is released by some cells and sensed by others → Signal; It stays switched on inside a cell and marks a place → Hox gene; It thins out with distance → Signal; Changing it can put a rib on a neck bone → Hox gene; Sonic hedgehog is one → Signal; Ultrabithorax is one → Hox gene. *Why:* A signal travels between cells and tells them roughly where they are. A Hox gene works inside the cell and keeps a record of the place.
10. (count) A human has 39 Hox genes and a fruit fly has 8. How many more does the human have?  
   **Answer:** 31. *Why:* 39 minus 8 is 31. The human set is bigger because the whole row was copied twice in our fish-like ancestors, and some copies were lost.
11. (odd) Which animal is the odd one out for Hox genes that mark head end from tail end?  
   Options: Mouse / Fruit fly / Centipede / Jellyfish  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A mouse, a fly and a centipede each have a head end and a tail end marked by a full row of Hox genes. A jellyfish is built in a ring and has only a few Hox-like genes.
12. (mcq) A mouse's Pax6 gene is switched on in a fly's wing. What grows there?  
   Options: A fly's eye / A mouse's eye / A second wing / Nothing  
   **Answer:** A fly's eye. *Why:* Pax6 only says 'build an eye here'. The fly's own cells and genes do the building, so the eye is a fly's eye.
13. (write) Why were scientists surprised to find the same Hox genes in a fly and a human?  
   **Answer:** The two bodies look completely different, so most scientists expected different genes to build them. The same genes mean both inherited them from one ancestor that lived very long ago.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the old expectation and says what the shared genes show about ancestry.
14. (perspective) Two students disagree about what DNA is like.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary, but the evidence favours the recipe. No stretch of DNA matches a hand. The mouse Pax6 gene gives a fly's eye in a fly. A pattern can appear from rules alone, as in the Rule, Not the Drawing.. *Why:* A strong answer uses evidence about what genes actually do. Nila is right that the information is passed on in DNA. The disagreement is about what kind of information it is.
15. (truefalse) True or false: the same signal, Sonic hedgehog, is used in more than one part of an embryo.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It helps shape the hand, the neural tube, the face and more. Most signals and genes are used in many places.

## Applications
- Doctors give folic acid tablets to women who may become pregnant, because the neural tube closes in the first four weeks. In India, schools and health centres also give weekly iron and folic acid tablets to older girls and boys.
- Surgeons repair a cleft lip or a cleft palate, which happens when the pieces of the face or the roof of the mouth do not fully join before birth. About 1 baby in 700 is born with one.
- New medicines are tested for harm to embryos before pregnant women may take them. This rule came after thalidomide, a medicine of the late 1950s, stopped the arms and legs of thousands of babies from forming.
- An ultrasound scan at about 18 to 20 weeks of pregnancy lets a doctor check that the baby's heart, spine and other organs have formed well.
- Scientists grow tiny pieces of gut, kidney or brain tissue in dishes by giving stem cells the same signals, in the same order, that an embryo uses. They use them to test medicines.
- Some cancers of the skin are treated with medicines that block the Sonic hedgehog signal, because the cancer cells have that building signal stuck on.
- Doctors treat large burns with sheets of skin grown from a small piece of the person's own skin, using the way skin cells divide and spread to cover a wound.

## Spotted around us
- The groove between your nose and your upper lip. It marks where pieces of your face joined before you were born.
- A house gecko on the wall with a short, plain tail of a different colour. That tail has grown back after the first one was dropped.
- Mehndi on fingernails. As the weeks pass, a band of plain new nail appears at the base and the orange part moves towards the tip.
- Curls of spring onion on a plate of fried rice or noodles. Cooks make them by putting thin strips in cold water.
- A new banana leaf coming out of the plant as a tight roll, then opening flat. Plants also shape their parts by rolling and unrolling sheets of cells.
- Tadpoles in a rain puddle or a temple tank in the monsoon. Look for the ones whose back legs have just appeared as small buds.
- Two bananas or two brinjals grown joined together at the market. Parts that start close together in a bud can grow into one another.
- A scar on a knee or an elbow. It is smoother than the skin around it and has no hairs, because scar tissue is a quick patch, not a rebuild.

## Open questions
- **How does a growing arm know when to stop?** Your two arms grew to the same length without ever being compared. Scientists know some signals that speed up and slow down growth, but not how a part measures its own size.
- **Where is the shape of a body stored?** No stretch of DNA is a picture of a body. Most scientists think the shape follows from genes and chemistry. Some think cells also hold it in shared electrical patterns. The tests so far cover only a few kinds of animal.
- **Why can an axolotl regrow a leg when a human cannot?** Humans have nearly all the same genes. Scarring, the risk of cancer and the immune system may each play a part, and nobody has yet made a mammal regrow a whole limb.
- **What exactly happens in a human embryo between two and four weeks?** This is when the layers form and the body folds, but embryos of this age are almost never seen. Most of what we know comes from mice, chicks and a small number of old human samples.
- **Why is the order of Hox genes on the chromosome the same as their order along the body?** Part of the answer is that the DNA opens up for reading from one end of the row to the other, as the body grows from head to tail. Why this arrangement has been kept for over 500 million years is still argued about.

## Connected maths
- An embryo starts as 1 cell, and the number of cells doubles at each round of division. How many rounds does it take to pass 100 cells? **7 rounds, which gives 128 cells.**. Double each time: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. That is 7 doublings. After 6 there are only 64, which is fewer than 100. So it takes 7 rounds. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- On the Embryo Ruler, the embryo is 4 millimetres long on day 28 and 30 millimetres long on day 56. How many times as long has the embryo become in those four weeks? **7 and a half times as long.**. Divide the later length by the earlier one: 30 ÷ 4 = 7.5. So the embryo is 7 and a half times as long. If you grew that much in four weeks now, you would be about as tall as a three-storey building. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the Curling Strip Test, the gaps between the ends of three strips in plain water were 2, 4 and 3 centimetres after 20 minutes. What is the mean gap? **3 centimetres.**. Add the three gaps: 2 + 4 + 3 = 9. Divide by the number of strips: 9 ÷ 3 = 3. So the mean gap is 3 centimetres. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In the Nail Clock, a thumbnail grew 3.5 millimetres in 28 days. A whole thumbnail is about 14 millimetres long. About how long does it take to grow a whole new thumbnail? **About 112 days, which is 16 weeks.**. 14 millimetres is 4 times 3.5 millimetres, because 3.5 × 4 = 14. So it takes 4 times 28 days. 28 × 4 = 112 days, and 112 ÷ 7 = 16 weeks. So every bit of your thumbnail is less than about four months old. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the Mirror Wing, imagine seven boxes in a row with a coin at each end. Each box counts steps from the nearer coin. Write the seven counts. What do you notice? **1, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 1. The row is the same read from either end.**. Boxes 1, 2 and 3 are nearer the first coin and count 1, 2, 3. Box 4 is 4 steps from both. Boxes 5, 6 and 7 are nearer the second coin and count 3, 2, 1. The pattern has mirror symmetry about the middle box. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- Our fish-like ancestors had their row of 13 Hox genes copied until there were 4 rows. How many genes is that? Humans now have 39. What fraction was kept? **52 genes. Humans kept 39 of them, which is three quarters.**. 4 rows of 13 is 4 × 13 = 52. Humans have 39, so 52 − 39 = 13 were lost. The fraction kept is 39 out of 52. Divide both by 13 to get 3 out of 4. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
