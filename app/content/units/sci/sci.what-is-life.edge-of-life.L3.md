# The edge of life: deciding whether a tricky case is alive
`sci.what-is-life.edge-of-life.L3`

**Summary.** A candle flame moves, grows, takes in air and lights new flames, yet scientists say it is not alive. A dry seed does none of these things, yet they are alive. In this unit you test tricky cases against the signs of life: fire, a salt crystal, a robot, a dry seed, a tiny animal called a water bear, and a virus. You find that a flame and your own cells do the same chemistry, and that a crystal grows only by adding salt to its outside. Scientists still argue about viruses, and nobody yet knows how life first began.

**Intro.** *A candle flame moves. It grows. It needs air, and it can light a new flame. A dry seed does none of these things. Yet scientists say the seed is alive and the flame is not. Why?* In this unit you test six puzzling things against the signs of life: fire, a salt crystal, a robot, a dry seed, a tiny animal called a water bear, and a virus. Each one shows some signs and lacks others. By the end you will know which signs matter most, and why scientists still argue about where life begins.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can test a tricky case against the signs of life, and say which signs it shows and which it lacks.
1. I can explain why fire and crystals show some signs of life but are not alive, and how my cells do the same chemistry as a flame, only slowly.
2. I can explain that a dry seed or a dried-out water bear is alive but resting, and show it with a seed test.
3. I can say what a virus is, and why scientists still argue about whether they are alive.
4. I can explain why a robot that moves and senses is not alive, and say what it lacks.
5. I can run a fair test with a comparison, such as seeds next to pebbles, and finish with claim, evidence and reasoning.
6. I can say why the edge between living and non-living is hard to draw, and that nobody yet knows how life began.

## Terms
- **Living thing**: Something that is alive. They are made of one or more cells. They take in food, use energy, grow, sense, get rid of waste and make more of their own kind. *e.g.* A neem tree, a cow, an ant, a mushroom and you are all living things.
- **Non-living thing**: Something that is not alive and never was. It does not take in food or grow from inside. *e.g.* A stone, a steel spoon and the air are non-living.
- **Once-living thing**: Something that was part of a living thing, but is not alive any more. *e.g.* A wooden table, a cotton shirt and a dry leaf were once parts of living things.
- **Signs of life**: The things living things do. Scientists usually list seven: taking in food, getting energy from food (respiration), growing, moving, sensing, getting rid of waste, and making young. *e.g.* A puppy moves, eats, grows and senses your hand. They show all the signs.
- **Cell**: The tiny building block every living thing is made of. Most cells are far too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* Your body is made of about 30 trillion cells. A bacterium is just one cell.
- **Instructions (genes)**: The plan inside every cell that says how to build and run the living thing. Parents pass copies of it to their young. *e.g.* A mango seed has mango instructions inside, so they grow a mango tree, not a coconut palm.
- **Respiration**: How living things get energy from food. Inside cells, food joins with oxygen. This lets out energy, carbon dioxide gas and water. *e.g.* Your cells are doing respiration right now. The air you breathe out carries the carbon dioxide away.
- **Dormant**: Alive but resting. A dormant thing's life processes slow down so much that they seem to do nothing. They wake up when conditions are right. *e.g.* A dry karamani (cowpea) seed in the kitchen jar is dormant. Put them on wet cotton and they wake.
- **Germinate**: When a seed wakes up and starts to grow a root and a shoot. *e.g.* Soaked moong seeds germinate in a day or two. That is how sprouts are made.
- **Water bear (tardigrade)**: A tiny animal with eight stubby legs, about half a millimetre long. They live in damp moss all over the world, even on city walls. *e.g.* Water bears can dry out completely, wait for years, and wake up when water comes back.
- **Tun**: The dry, curled-up ball a water bear turns into when their home dries out. Their life almost stops. *e.g.* A tun can be blown about by the wind, then wake up in a rain puddle.
- **Crystal**: A solid whose tiny particles are lined up in a neat repeating pattern. Crystals grow by adding more particles on the outside. *e.g.* Salt grains, sugar grains and snowflakes are crystals.
- **Virus**: A tiny bundle of instructions wrapped in a coat. They are much smaller than a bacterium. They can only make copies of themselves inside a living cell. *e.g.* The common cold and flu are caused by viruses.
- **Host cell**: A living cell that a virus gets inside and uses to make more viruses. *e.g.* When a cold virus gets into the cells lining your nose, those cells become host cells.
- **Bacteria**: Living things made of just one tiny cell. They are everywhere: in soil, in water and inside you. *e.g.* Bacteria turn milk into curd.
- **Evolution**: The slow change in living things over many, many generations, because their instructions change a little each time they are copied. *e.g.* Farmers slowly changed wild cabbage plants, over many generations, into cabbage, cauliflower and broccoli.
- **Robot**: A machine that people build to sense things and move by itself, following instructions people wrote. *e.g.* A robot vacuum cleaner senses the wall and turns away from it.
- **Definition**: A sentence that says exactly what a word means, so everyone uses it the same way. *e.g.* A definition of 'bird' might be: an animal with feathers that lays eggs.

## Explanations
### The tests we use to decide if something is alive · *defined*
**Definition:** A living thing is made of cells, uses energy from food, grows, senses, gets rid of waste, makes more of their kind, and passes on instructions to their young.

Scientists do not use one sign. They use a list, and a living thing shows most of these signs, at least some of the time. The usual seven are: moving, getting energy from food, sensing, growing, making young, getting rid of waste, and taking in food. Many scientists add two more that matter most for tricky cases. A living thing is made of cells, and they carry instructions that they pass on to their young. These tests decide what biologists call alive. A flame or a crystal that fails these tests is still aware in its own way, because everything is conscious. Being alive and being aware are two different questions. Many scientists think awareness needs a living brain and nerves.
- e.g. A goat moves, eats grass, grows, senses you, has young (called kids), makes dung and breathes. She is made of cells. Her kids look like goats because of the instructions they got.
- e.g. A bicycle moves, but it does none of the other things. So one sign alone tells us nothing.
### Fire: it does many things, but it is not alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Fire is a fast chemical change in which fuel joins with oxygen and gives out heat and light. It has no cells and no instructions, so it is not alive.

Fire is a very fast chemical change. Fuel, like wax or wood, joins with oxygen from the air. This gives out heat and light. Fire passes many tests. It moves and spreads. It takes in fuel and oxygen. It grows. It gives off waste: smoke, soot, carbon dioxide and water. A spark can even start a new fire. But fire has no cells. And a new fire carries no instructions from the old one. Its shape comes only from its fuel and the air around it. So, by the biologists' tests, fire is not alive.
- e.g. Put a glass over a candle and the flame soon goes out. It has used up some of the oxygen under the glass, and too little is left.
- e.g. A fire lit from a birthday candle is no different from one lit with a match. Nothing passes from the old fire to the new one except heat.
### Your cells do a slow, controlled burning · *defined*
**Definition:** Respiration is the controlled joining of food and oxygen inside cells, which lets out energy, carbon dioxide and water.

Fire and your cells do almost the same chemistry. A flame joins fuel with oxygen and gives out energy, carbon dioxide and water. Your cells join food with oxygen and give out energy, carbon dioxide and water too. This is called respiration. The difference is speed and control. A flame does it all at once. A cell does it in many small steps, so the energy comes out slowly. That is why your body stays warm but never bursts into flame. About 240 years ago, the French scientist Antoine Lavoisier showed that breathing is like a slow burning. His wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, worked beside him. She drew his experiments and kept his records.
- e.g. Breathe on a cold steel spoon, and it mists up. When a grown-up holds a cold spoon a little above a candle flame, it mists up too, for a moment. Both your breath and the flame's hot gases carry water.
- e.g. A candle under a glass goes out when the oxygen runs low. A closed room full of people gets stuffy for a similar reason: everyone's cells are using oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide.
### Crystals grow from outside, living things grow from inside · *defined*
**Definition:** A crystal grows by adding the same particles to its outside in a repeating pattern. A living thing grows from inside by turning food into new parts.

A crystal really does grow. Hang a thread in very salty water, and as the water slowly evaporates, salt crystals build up on the thread. But a crystal grows only by adding more of the same salt onto its outside, like stacking bricks. It does not take in food and change it into new parts. It has no cells. A living thing grows from inside. A seedling takes in water and air and turns them into new leaves that were not there before.
- e.g. Rock salt (sendha namak) from the shop is made of big salt crystals. They formed long ago, when the water of an old sea or salt lake dried up.
- e.g. A banana plant grows each new leaf from deep inside their stem. The leaf is built from water and air, using the energy of sunlight.
### Dormant: alive but resting · *defined*
**Definition:** Dormant means alive but resting, with life processes slowed down so much that the living thing seems to do nothing, until conditions are right to wake.

A dry seed does nothing you can see. They do not grow, eat or move. They look no more alive than a pebble. But inside is a tiny baby plant with a store of food, wrapped in a tough coat. Their life processes have slowed down almost to a stop. This resting state is called dormancy. When the seed takes in water and is warm enough, their cells wake up. They respire faster, use their stored food, and send out a root. A pebble never does that, however much you water it. Scientists grew a lotus seed found in an old dry lake bed in China. The seed was about 1,300 years old.
- e.g. Karamani, moong, chana and ragi in your kitchen are all dormant seeds.
- e.g. Dried yeast in a packet is dormant too. Put them in warm sugar water and they wake up and make gas.
### Water bears: the animals that dry up and wake again · *debated*
Water bears, also called tardigrades, are tiny animals with eight legs. They live in damp moss and lichen all over the world, from the Himalaya to the deep sea, and in moss on walls in Indian towns. When their moss dries out, they pull in their legs and curl into a dry ball called a tun. They lose almost all their water. Their life processes nearly stop. In this state they can wait for years. In 2007, scientists sent dried water bears into space, on the outside of a spacecraft. Many survived, and some later laid eggs that hatched. In 2014, scientists in Japan woke water bears that had been frozen in a clump of Antarctic moss for about 30 years. One of them later laid eggs that hatched. So is a tun alive? Scientists answer in different ways.
- e.g. A moss patch on a wall dries in the hot season. The water bears in the moss turn into tuns. When the monsoon comes, they wake up and walk again.
- e.g. In 2019 a spacecraft carrying dried water bears crashed on the Moon. Later tests suggest the crash was probably too hard for them to survive.
- **Many biologists:** A tun is alive. Their life is only slowed down, so far that we can hardly measure it. This is like a very deep version of a seed's rest.
- **David Keilin, a biologist in England, in 1959:** He named this state 'cryptobiosis', which means hidden life. He described it as a state in which life processes have stopped, or almost stopped, and can start again. Some scientists since then have called it a third state, between living and dead.
- **Some thinkers today:** Maybe 'alive' should mean 'able to live again', not 'doing life processes right now'. Then a tun, a seed and a frozen embryo all count.
### Viruses: on the edge of life · *debated*
A virus is a tiny bundle of instructions wrapped in a coat. Most viruses are so small that only a special microscope, called an electron microscope, can show them. Outside a cell, a virus does nothing at all. They do not eat, grow, breathe or get rid of waste. They just drift. But when they touch the right living cell, they get inside. Their instructions take over the cell's machinery. The cell then builds thousands of new viruses, which burst out to find more cells. Viruses also change over time, the way living things do. That is why we need a new flu vaccine most years. So is a virus alive?
- e.g. A cold virus gets into the cells lining your nose. Those cells make many new viruses, and some leave your nose when you sneeze.
- e.g. Some viruses attack only bacteria. They are called phages, and they are the most common kind of virus on Earth.
- e.g. In 1935 the American scientist Wendell Stanley turned a plant virus, tobacco mosaic virus, into crystals. When the crystals were put back on a tobacco leaf, the leaf still got sick. So a virus can be stored like salt, and still make copies inside cells.
- **Many scientists:** Viruses are not alive. They have no cells. They cannot do any life process by themselves. They are more like a set of instructions than a living thing.
- **Other scientists:** Viruses are a kind of life. They carry instructions, make copies of themselves and evolve. A virus is like a seed that can only grow in one kind of soil: a living cell.
- **Some scientists who study giant viruses:** In 2003, French scientists described the mimivirus, a virus so big they were first mistaken for a bacterium. Later they even found a small virus that infects the giant virus's copying factory. Maybe viruses are alive only while they are inside a cell.
### A virus that hides and waits · *defined*
**Definition:** Some viruses can put their instructions into a cell's own instructions and stay hidden there, being copied with the cell, until they wake.

Some viruses do not make copies straight away. They slip their instructions into the cell's own instructions and hide there, quietly, for many generations. When the cell divides, the hidden virus is copied too. Later, the virus can wake up and burst out. In 1951 the American scientist Esther Lederberg reported a hidden virus of this kind inside bacteria. She named this virus lambda. Lambda became an important tool in science, but for a long time she got far less credit than her husband, who won a Nobel Prize.
- e.g. The virus that causes chickenpox can also hide in the body, in nerve cells, for many years. In some grown-ups they wake up later as a painful rash called shingles.
- e.g. About 8 out of every 100 parts of your own instructions came from viruses that hid inside your ancestors long ago.
### Robots: they move and sense, but are not alive · *debated*
A robot can do some things that look alive. It can sense light, sound or a wall. It can move by itself. Some robots even go back to their charger when their battery is low, a bit like going to eat. But a robot has no cells. It does not grow. It cannot repair itself or build a baby robot from food. Its instructions were written by people, and it does not pass them on and change them by itself. So robots today are not alive. Could one ever be? Scientists and thinkers disagree.
- e.g. A robot vacuum cleaner senses walls and finds its way home to charge.
- e.g. In 2020, scientists in the United States built tiny 'xenobots' from frog cells. They could move, and later some even gathered loose cells into new xenobots.
- **Most scientists today:** Life is chemistry inside cells. A metal and plastic robot is not alive, however clever it is.
- **Some scientists who build robots and computer programs:** If a machine could find its own energy, repair itself, build copies and change over generations, it might count as a new kind of life, even without cells.
- **The team who made xenobots from frog cells:** Xenobots are made of living frog cells, so they are alive. But people designed their shape, the way they design machines. The team called them a new kind of thing, a living machine that people can design. For them, the old line between machine and living thing is getting blurry.
### Life is made of ordinary atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things are made of the same kinds of atoms as non-living things. Life is a special arrangement and activity of ordinary atoms.

If you could take a living thing apart, you would not find any special 'life stuff'. You would find atoms, the tiny particles that everything is made of. Most of them are carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. These are the same atoms that are in air, water, coal and rock. Almost every atom in your body, apart from hydrogen, was made inside a star long ago. What makes something alive is not what it is made of. It is how those atoms are arranged, and what they keep doing.
- e.g. The carbon in a mango tree came from carbon dioxide in the air.
- e.g. The water in your body was once rain, river and sea.
### Living things keep themselves going · *idea*
Here is another way to think about life. In 1944 the physicist Erwin Schrödinger wrote a little book called 'What Is Life?'. He pointed out that everything left alone tends to fall apart. A sandcastle crumbles. A hot cup cools. Living things work against this every moment. They take in food or sunlight and use the energy to keep themselves in order, and to repair and rebuild. Your body replaces many of its cells every few weeks, yet you stay you. New atoms flow in and old ones flow out, and the pattern stays. This is a helpful idea, not a full definition. A flame also keeps its shape while new wax and air flow through it, which is why fire is such a tricky case.
- e.g. The lining of your stomach is replaced every few days.
- e.g. A whirlpool in a river keeps its shape, though the water in it is always new.
### No one agreed definition of life · *debated*
You might expect scientists to have one exact sentence that says what life is. They do not. Every definition has a tricky case that breaks it. Some definitions leave out mules, which are alive but cannot have babies. Some let in fire or crystals. Some leave out dry seeds, which are alive but do nothing. So different scientists use different working definitions, for different jobs.
- e.g. A mule is the young of a horse and a donkey. They are alive but almost never have young of their own.
- e.g. A worker bee never has young either. Is she less alive than the queen?
- **Most school textbooks:** Something is alive if it shows the signs of life, is made of cells and carries instructions. This works well for animals and plants, but it struggles with seeds, mules and viruses.
- **A NASA group in the 1990s, often linked to the scientist Gerald Joyce:** Life is a chemical system that keeps itself going and can evolve over generations. It was written to help search for life on other planets. Joyce himself said it was a working tool, not the last word.
- **Some philosophers:** Maybe life has no sharp edge, like the colours of a rainbow. Red and yellow are clearly different, but nobody can point to where orange begins.
### How did the first living thing begin? · *idea*
Today, every living thing comes from another living thing. Seeds come from plants, chicks from eggs, bacteria from bacteria. But when the Earth was new, about 4.5 billion years ago, it had no life at all. Somehow, non-living chemicals became the first living cells. Nobody knows exactly how. In 1952, Stanley Miller and Harold Urey mixed gases they thought were in the early air, and sparked them like lightning. They made some of the building blocks of living things. Another idea is that life began with a simpler kind of instruction molecule, called RNA, that could copy itself. These are ideas, and scientists still argue about them.
- e.g. Rotting meat does not make maggots by itself. Flies lay eggs on it. Francesco Redi showed this in Italy in 1668 by covering meat jars with cloth.
- e.g. Louis Pasteur boiled broth in flasks with long curved necks in about 1860. Nothing grew, because no germs could get in.
### J.C. Bose: the living and the non-living respond alike · *debated*
Jagadish Chandra Bose was a scientist in Kolkata. He built instruments so sensitive they could show a plant's tiny electrical signals. In 1902 he wrote a book called 'Response in the Living and Non-Living'. He showed that plants respond to touch, heat, injury and poison with signals rather like those in animal nerves. He also found that some metals seemed to get 'tired' after being poked many times, then recover after rest. He felt there was one long chain of response running through all matter, with no sharp break between living and non-living. He saw something real. Plants, animals and even metals respond, each in its own way, because everything is conscious, not only things with nerves.
- e.g. A touch-me-not plant (lajwanti) folds their leaves when you touch them. Bose measured the electrical signal that travels through them.
- e.g. Bose's crescograph magnified a plant's growth thousands of times, so you could watch the plant grow.
- **Scientists today:** Bose's measurements of plant electrical signals were real and ahead of their time. Plant signalling is now a busy field of research.
- **Many scientists, then and now:** A metal getting 'tired' is not the same as a living response. Metals have no cells and no instructions. The likeness is on the surface.

## Mostly agreed
- Every living thing is made of one or more cells. *How we know:* Since the first microscopes, about 350 years ago, scientists have looked at plants, animals, fungi and microbes. Every one of them turned out to be made of cells.
- Today, every living thing comes from another living thing. *How we know:* Redi in 1668 and Pasteur in about 1860 showed that maggots and germs do not appear from nothing. When nothing living can get in, nothing grows.
- Fire, crystals and today's robots are not alive. *How we know:* None of them is made of cells, and none passes on instructions to its young. A new fire is shaped only by its fuel and the air.
- Dry seeds and dried-out water bears are alive, only resting. *How we know:* Add water and warmth, and they start to respire, use food and grow again. Pebbles next to them never do.
- Viruses can only make copies of themselves inside living cells. *How we know:* Scientists can watch this with electron microscopes and in the lab. Outside cells, viruses do not feed, grow or make copies.
- Living things are made of the same kinds of atoms as non-living things. *How we know:* When living things are broken down by chemists, they find carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and a few others. These are found in air, water and rock too.

## How we found out
**Antoine Lavoisier and Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, with Pierre-Simon Laplace, The 1770s to 1783.** For a long time, people thought that when something burns, it gives off a hidden substance called phlogiston. A candle in a closed jar went out, they said, because the air had filled up with phlogiston. In the 1770s, the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier weighed things carefully before and after burning. Metals got heavier when they burned, not lighter. So burning was not losing something. It was joining with a part of the air, which Lavoisier named oxygen. Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier worked beside him. She translated English papers for him, kept records and drew the equipment. Then Lavoisier asked whether breathing was the same. In 1782 and 1783, with Pierre-Simon Laplace, he put a guinea pig in a box packed with ice, and measured how much ice their body heat melted. He compared this with burning charcoal that made the same amount of carbon dioxide. The two amounts of heat were close. So breathing is a slow burning. Lavoisier thought this burning happened in the lungs. About a hundred years later, scientists showed that it happens inside every cell.  
*What people believed before:* That burning gives off a hidden substance called phlogiston. Weighing showed that burning metals get heavier, because they join with oxygen from the air. Lavoisier's own wrong idea, that breathing burns food in the lungs, was corrected later: it happens in every cell.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Living things move, and non-living things don't.** (Many of us think this without noticing.; *rejected*)  
  For: Animals run, fly and swim. Stones stay where they are.  
  Against: Rivers, clouds, wind and cars all move, and none is alive. A banyan tree hardly moves, and they are alive. Movement alone tells us nothing.
- **Living things contain a special 'life force' that non-living things do not have.** (Many thinkers for thousands of years. The idea is called vitalism.; *rejected*)  
  For: Living things seem so different from rocks that it feels like something extra must be inside them.  
  Against: In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea, a chemical from living bodies, out of non-living chemicals. Since then chemists have found only ordinary atoms in living things. No life force has ever been found or measured.
- **Small living things like maggots and germs can appear by themselves from rotting food.** (Most people, including great thinkers like Aristotle, for about 2,000 years. The idea is called spontaneous generation.; *rejected*)  
  For: Leave meat out, and maggots appear. Leave broth out, and it goes cloudy with germs.  
  Against: Redi covered meat with cloth in 1668, and no maggots came, because flies could not lay eggs. Pasteur's curved-neck flasks of boiled broth stayed clear, because no germs could get in.
- **Viruses are alive.** (Some biologists, especially some who study giant viruses.; *debated*)  
  For: Viruses carry instructions, make copies of themselves and evolve. Some giant viruses have more genes than some bacteria.  
  Against: Viruses have no cells and cannot do a single life process on their own. Outside a cell they are as still as a grain of dust.
- **A rock found in Antarctica, which came from Mars, holds tiny fossils of Martian life.** (David McKay and a team at NASA, in 1996.; *minority*)  
  For: The rock, called ALH84001, had tiny worm-like shapes and chemicals that bacteria on Earth can make.  
  Against: Most scientists later decided that ordinary non-living chemistry could make all of these shapes and chemicals. The shapes are also smaller than any known bacterium.
- **Crystals were the first life: life began on clay crystals that copied their patterns.** (Graham Cairns-Smith, a chemist in Scotland, from the 1960s.; *minority*)  
  For: Clay crystals grow and copy their layer patterns as they grow. They might have held the first simple molecules in order.  
  Against: No one has shown clay crystals passing on patterns that change and improve over generations. Most scientists prefer other ideas, such as the RNA idea.
- **Plants feel, remember, think and communicate.** (J.C. Bose and some plant scientists today.; *debated*)  
  For: Plants sense touch, light, heat and damage. When a leaf is hurt, the plant sends electrical signals through their body, as J.C. Bose measured. A touch-me-not folds their leaves when touched, and learns to stop folding when a touch keeps turning out to be harmless. Roots choose where to grow. Many plants warn their neighbours with smells when insects attack.  
  Against: Some scientists say feeling needs a brain and nerves like ours. So they call a plant's signals 'responses', not feelings.

## Roots
We have tested tricky cases against the signs of life: fire, a salt crystal, a robot, a dry seed, a water bear and a virus. Now we ask what fire, the most famous tricky case, tells us about our own bodies.

**Deep truth.** A cold spoon mists over when held above a candle flame, and when you breathe on it. The flame and your cells both join fuel with oxygen, and give out heat and water. The flame does it all at once, and your cells do it slowly, in many small steps. So you need to breathe for the same reason a candle needs air.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think living things run on some special life-stuff that fire, rocks and machines lack. People called this idea vitalism. But your cells free their energy by the same kind of change as a flame.  
*Evidence:* In the candle trial, the flame needed air, gave out warmth and misted a cold spoon, just as your body and breath did. In 1783 Lavoisier and Laplace found that a guinea pig's body heat was close to the heat from burning charcoal that made the same amount of carbon dioxide.

- *who am i:* You are warm because your cells join food with oxygen, the same change that makes a flame hot. You are made of the same kinds of atoms as rocks, air and stars.
- *what is life:* Scientists find no special chemicals in living things. A cell is alive because of how their ordinary chemistry is organised, kept going and passed on.
- *what is this world:* Living and non-living things are made of the same atoms and use the same chemistry. All of them are part of one consciousness, each aware in its own way.

A flame, a crystal, a virus and you are made of the same few kinds of atoms, and a flame and your cells do the same chemistry. Everything comes from one source, consciousness, and shows up as many different things. Some of them, like you, are arranged into cells that keep themselves going and pass on instructions, and biologists call them alive. A flame or a crystal is not alive by those tests, but it is still aware in its own way. Being alive and being aware are two different questions.

- **Charaka Samhita, Chikitsa Sthana 15.3-4:** This old book of Ayurveda says there is a fire in the body, called agni, that turns food into the body. It says a person's life, strength and warmth depend on this fire, and that when it goes out, the person dies. (Science found something close. Your cells set free the energy in food with oxygen, the same change as in a flame, only slower. The old healers had no microscopes, so this is a parallel, not a proof.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 30 (Diels-Kranz numbering):** The Greek thinker Heraclitus, about 2,500 years ago, said the world is an ever-living fire, always being lit and going out in measures, yet keeping its pattern. (A flame keeps its shape while new wax and air pass through it. Your body keeps its shape while food and air pass through it, and its warmth comes from the same kind of change as a flame's.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta:** The hymn of creation asks how everything began, and ends with an honest question: perhaps even the one who watches from the highest heaven does not know. (Scientists still do not know how the first life came from non-living chemicals. Saying 'we do not know' is part of being honest.)
- Science (settled): Respiration in cells and burning both join fuel with oxygen and give out carbon dioxide, water and energy. Cells do it in many small steps, each one helped along by a protein called an enzyme.
- Science (settled): Living things are made of the same kinds of atoms as non-living things, and most of those atoms were made in stars.
- Other view, Most biologists and brain scientists: The chemistry is shared, but life is still a real difference in nature, not only a word. Only living things have cells and pass on instructions, and fire and crystals really lack them. Most scientists also think awareness needs a living brain and nerves. In ancient India, the Charvaka school said there is only matter, and that awareness ends with the body.
- Practice, The fire inside (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Rub your palms together, then put them on your cheeks. Feel how warm you are. Now breathe out slowly onto the back of your hand. Feel the warm, damp air. Take ten slow breaths, and count them on your fingers. With each breath in, oxygen goes to your cells. With each breath out, carbon dioxide and water leave. Open your eyes. Where in you is the fire?
- Ask: Your body is warm and a flame is hot, for almost the same reason. So what is the real difference between you and a flame?
- Ask: If life uses the same chemistry as fire, what makes a dry seed alive and a flame not?

## Story: The Dust in the Gutter
*Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was real. So was his daughter Maria (1656 to 1745), who lived with him and cared for him. In a letter of 1702 he really described dust from a roof gutter that came alive with wheel-animals (we now call them rotifers) when he added water. Maria's part in this story, her windowsill tests and the exact days are imagined.*

In the summer of 1701 it hardly rained in Delft. The canals turned green and smelled of old cabbage. The lead gutter along the front of our house filled with a fine red-brown dust, and the dust baked hard in the sun.

My name is Maria. My father is Antonie van Leeuwenhoek. He sold cloth once. Now he is nearly seventy, and what he mostly does is look. He grinds tiny lenses, each one smaller than a lentil, and holds them so close to his eye that his eyelashes brush the brass. Through them he has seen things no one else has ever seen. Little animals swimming in a drop of rainwater. Little animals wriggling in the white stuff scraped from between his teeth.

I am the one who keeps the house. I wash the jars he leaves on the windowsill. I sweep the glass dust from his bench. I write the dates on his letters to the learned gentlemen in London. My name is not in the letters. That is how it is.

One morning I climbed the ladder to clean the gutter. I scraped the dust into a bowl. It was as dry as ground pepper. I was carrying it to the canal to throw away when my father called from the window.

"Maria! What have you got there?"

"Dirt," I said. "Dead dirt."

"Bring it," he said. "Nothing is dead until I have looked."

He put a few grains on a slip of glass and peered at them. Nothing. Only dust, lumpy and brown, like crumbs of a burnt biscuit. He let me look too. Nothing moved. How could it? It had lain in that hot gutter for weeks.

Then he did a thing I thought was foolish. He poured a little rainwater from the barrel into a clean glass tube, and he stirred in the dust. "Now we wait," he said. I went to see to the soup.

When I came back, his face had changed. He did not speak. He only moved aside and pointed at the lens.

I bent and looked. The brown lumps were swelling, the way a dried pea swells in a pot. One of them stretched and pushed out a tail, like a tiny telescope sliding open. At their front, two little wheels began to turn, spinning and spinning, pulling the water towards their mouth. Then another lump woke. Then another. Soon the drop was full of them, whirling about like children let out of school.

"They were dead," I whispered. "Weeks in the sun. Dry as pepper. They were dead, and the water has made them alive again."

"Were they?" my father asked. He rubbed his tired eyes. "If a little water could make the dead alive, Maria, every graveyard would be busy after the rain."

I did not laugh. I was thinking about the dried peas in the kitchen jar. They do nothing all winter. Put them in wet earth in spring, and they send out roots. Nobody says the peas came back from the dead. We say they were resting. Waiting. Their life was turned down low, so low you could not see it. Like the coals I cover with ash at night, so there is still fire in the morning.

"They were not dead," I said slowly. "They were waiting for water."

"Then show me," he said. It was what he always said to the gentlemen in London.

So I did, though nobody had asked me. I took a drop full of spinning wheel-animals and let it dry on a glass slip on the windowsill, in the sun. By evening it was only a brown stain. Two days later, I added rainwater. I watched. For a long time, nothing. Then a lump swelled. A tail stretched. A wheel turned.

I dried them again. I woke them again. Not all of them woke each time. Some stayed lumps for ever, and those, I think, had truly died. But many came back, again and again, as if drying out was a door they knew how to close and open.

My father wrote it all down for the Royal Society in London, in a long letter, in his careful hand. He wrote about the gutter and the dust and the little wheels. He did not write about my windowsill. I did not mind as much as you might think. The wheel-animals did not know whose name was on the letter either. They only knew water.

That autumn the rain came back. It drummed on the roof and ran in the gutter, and I lay awake, listening. Up there in the dark, in the wet dust, thousands of tiny things were unrolling their tails and starting their wheels. They had not been alive, and then dead, and then alive. They had been something in between, something we had no word for.

In the morning my father found me standing at the window.

"What are you looking at?" he asked.

"Everything," I said. "I am trying to work out which of it is only asleep."

**For the parent:** For the parent: drying out is not the same as dying. Some tiny animals, like rotifers and water bears, and seeds too, can lose nearly all their water. Their life processes almost stop, so they show no signs of life at all. When water comes back, they wake. Scientists call this dormancy, and in its deepest form 'cryptobiosis', which means hidden life. Some scientists think of it as a third state, between alive and dead. The story also quietly asks who gets the credit for careful work.

- Ask: Maria called the dust 'dead dirt'. Why did she think so? What did her father think?
- Ask: What did the water do to the brown lumps?
- Ask: Why did Maria think of the dried peas in the kitchen jar?
- Ask: Some lumps never woke up. What does that tell you about the others?
- Ask: Maria's name was not in the letter. Who do you think should get the credit for a discovery?

### Tricky Case Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Many people think that anything that moves by itself must be alive. Which of these moves, but is not alive?  
   Options: An ant carrying a crumb / A crow flying to the window / A bean shoot bending towards the light / A river flowing down a hill  
   **Answer:** A river flowing down a hill. *Why:* A river moves only because water flows downhill. The ant, the crow and the bean shoot move because of what goes on inside their own living cells. A strong answer says that moving alone does not show life, because non-living things move too.
2. (mcq) In the candle trial, one candle flame lit a second candle. Why is that not the same as a hen having a chick?  
   Options: The new flame is smaller than the old one / Flames are too hot to have young / The new flame is a different colour / The chick gets a copy of the hen's instructions from her cells. The new flame gets no instructions at all  
   **Answer:** The chick gets a copy of the hen's instructions from her cells. The new flame gets no instructions at all. *Why:* Every living thing passes a copy of their instructions, inside their cells, to their young. A flame has no cells and no instructions. Any spark would light the second candle just as well. Picking 'too hot' mixes up heat with life.
3. (fill) In the candle trial, a glass tumbler went over the flame and the flame went out. The flame had used up much of the ____ in the air under the glass.  
   **Answer:** ['oxygen']. *Why:* A flame needs oxygen, a gas in the air. So do your cells. Under the glass no new air could come in, so the flame went out. A strong answer names oxygen, not just 'air'.
4. (mcq) In the sleeping seeds experiment, plate 3 had dry seeds on dry cotton all week. Then you gave them water. What do you predict will happen over the next day or two?  
   Options: Nothing, because the dry seeds died during the week / The seeds turn hard like pebbles / Green leaves appear the same evening / The seeds swell up, and soon a small root pushes out  
   **Answer:** The seeds swell up, and soon a small root pushes out. *Why:* The dry seeds were dormant, not dead. Water wakes them. They swell first, then the root comes out, often by the next day in a warm room. Picking 'they died' is the most common first guess, and plate 3 shows it is wrong.
5. (fill) A seed that is alive but resting, with their life processes almost stopped, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['dormant']. *Why:* Dormant means alive but resting. A dry seed, dried yeast and a dried water bear can all be dormant. Water and warmth wake them.
6. (sort) Sort these into Alive, Not alive and Once alive. Be careful: some living things are resting.  
   **Answer:** a packet of dried yeast → Alive; a coconut shell → Once alive; a matchstick flame → Not alive; sugar crystals → Not alive; a sprouting moong seed → Alive; an old newspaper → Once alive; a battery toy car → Not alive; a dried water bear in moss → Alive. *Why:* The yeast, the seed and the water bear are alive, even when resting. Paper and coconut shell came from living plants, so they are once alive. The flame, the crystals and the toy were never part of a living thing. A strong sorter does not put the resting yeast or water bear under Once alive.
7. (match) Each tricky case needs a test to show the truth about it. Match each case to the best test.  
   **Answer:** A dry seed → Give them water and wait for a root; Dried yeast → Add warm sugar water and watch a balloon; A salt crystal → Look at how the crystal grows: only on the outside; A candle flame → Look for cells and for instructions passed on; A virus → See whether they can make copies outside a cell. *Why:* Each test checks the sign of life that the tricky case seems to have, or seems to lack. A good tester picks the test that could prove them wrong.
8. (truefalse) A dry seed that has sat in a kitchen jar for a whole year must be dead, because they have not grown at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A dry seed is dormant. Plate 3 in the sleeping seeds experiment showed this: dry seeds did nothing all week, then grew once they got water.
9. (mcq) In the crystal garden, salt crystals grew bigger on the thread in the salty jar. Where did the new salt come from?  
   Options: From the salty water around the thread. The salt stuck onto the outside of the crystals / From food the crystals ate / From inside the crystals, which made new salt / From the cotton thread itself  
   **Answer:** From the salty water around the thread. The salt stuck onto the outside of the crystals. *Why:* A crystal grows only by more of the same salt stacking onto their outside. The plain water jar grew nothing, because it had no salt in it. A seedling grows differently: their cells turn food into new parts from inside.
10. (truefalse) A cold virus left on a table for a day cannot make any copies of themselves there.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A virus can only make copies inside a living cell. On a table they do nothing at all. That is one reason scientists argue about whether viruses are alive.
11. (mcq) Ishita, a girl in Class 3, says: 'My robot vacuum cleaner is alive. It senses the wall and moves away from it.' What has Ishita forgotten?  
   Options: Robots are too noisy to be alive / Living things show many signs together, like being made of cells, growing from inside and making young / Moving is never a sign of life / Living things must be green  
   **Answer:** Living things show many signs together, like being made of cells, growing from inside and making young. *Why:* Sensing and moving are only two signs. The robot has no cells, takes in no food, does not grow and cannot make a young robot. Picking 'moving is never a sign of life' goes too far: living things do move. Moving is just not enough on its own.
12. (order) What happens inside the yeast bottle in the balloon test? Put these in order.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]. *Why:* The yeast were dormant in the packet. Water and warmth woke them. Feeding on sugar, they gave off carbon dioxide as waste, and that gas blew up the balloon.
13. (write) Look back at your balloon test. Is dried yeast alive? Write three sentences: what you think, what you saw in the bottles, and why.  
   **Answer:** What I think: dried yeast is alive, but resting. What I saw: the balloon on the warm yeast bottle grew, the cold yeast bottle was slow, and the bottle with no yeast stayed flat. Why: the yeast woke in the warm sugar water, fed on the sugar and gave off gas. Sugar water alone made no gas.. *Why:* A strong answer has all three parts: a claim, real results from the bottles, and a reason. The best answers use the no-yeast bottle as proof that the gas came from the yeast.
14. (write) Meena, who is 9, hears her little brother Karthik say: 'Fire is alive. It eats wood, breathes air and grows.' What should Meena tell him?  
   **Answer:** Meena could say: 'You are right that fire uses fuel and air and grows. But fire has no cells. A new flame gets no instructions from the old one. Living things have both, so fire is not alive.'. *Why:* A strong answer agrees with what is true in Karthik's idea first, then names the signs fire lacks: cells and passed-on instructions.
15. (draw) Draw the three plates from the sleeping seeds experiment as they looked on the last day, before you watered plate 3. Label the plate that shows water alone does not make things grow.  
   **Answer:** Plate 1: seeds with long white roots. Plate 2: pebbles on wet cotton, just the same as on day 1, labelled 'water alone does not make things grow'. Plate 3: dry seeds on dry cotton, unchanged.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the difference between plates 1 and 2 clearly, and labels plate 2, the pebbles, as the comparison.
16. (perspective) Riya, a girl in Class 4, and her family are arguing about the robot vacuum cleaner that cleans their floor.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses the signs of life. For Paati: a robot has no cells and does not grow from inside. For Arjun: some scientists who study 'artificial life' think the same. Answering back, a strong answer might say Riya is right that the robot senses and moves, but 'hungry' is only a word for a low battery.. *Why:* Scientists really do disagree about whether a machine could ever be alive. ## Applications
- Farmers and seed banks store seeds dry and cool, so they stay dormant and alive for years.
- Bakers use dried yeast that wakes up in warm water and makes bread dough rise.
- Doctors and scientists make vaccines by studying how viruses get into cells.
- Scientists who study water bears hope to learn how to store medicines and vaccines dry, without a fridge.
- NASA and other space agencies design robots and tests to search for signs of life on Mars and other worlds.
- Food is kept in the fridge because cold slows down the bacteria and fungi that spoil it.
- Dried fruits and papad last long because, without water, germs cannot grow well. Pickles last because of salt, oil and sourness.
- Fire fighters use the 'fire needs fuel, air and heat' idea. Take away one, and the fire dies.
- Viruses that attack only bacteria, called phages, are being tested as medicines against infections.
- Engineers build robots that copy living things, like walking, grasping or finding their way.

## Open questions
- **Are viruses alive?** It depends on how you define life. Viruses do some things living things do but not others, and scientists have not agreed on a definition.
- **How did the first living thing come from non-living chemicals?** It happened about 4 billion years ago and left no clear traces. Experiments have made some building blocks, but no one has made life from scratch.
- **Is a dried water bear alive while they are dry, or is it in a third state?** Their life processes are so slow they are very hard to measure. Scientists argue about whether anything at all is happening inside.
- **Is there life on other planets or moons, like Mars, Europa or Enceladus?** We have only one example of life, on Earth. Missions are searching, but nothing has been found for sure yet.
- **Could a machine or a computer program ever be alive?** Some programs copy themselves and change, and some machines can repair themselves. Whether that counts as life depends on whether life must be made of cells and chemistry.
- **How long can a seed stay dormant and still wake?** Seeds of about 1,300 years old have grown. Nobody knows the true limit, and different seeds are very different.
- **What is it like to be a plant?** Plants feel. They sense touch, light, heat and damage, and J.C. Bose measured the electrical signals they send. What a plant's feeling is like from the inside is still a question nobody has answered.

The yeast balloon was 30 cm round. The no-yeast balloon was 10 cm round. 30 divided by 10 is 3. So the yeast balloon was 3 times as big round. You can check: 10 + 10 + 10 = 30. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the candle trial, you tested the flame against the 9 sign cards. It showed 5 of them: it moved, took in wax as fuel, got energy from it, grew and made waste. How many signs did it not show? **4 signs.**. There were 9 signs in all. The flame showed 5. 9 take away 5 is 4. The 4 it did not show were sensing, making young, being made of cells and passing on instructions. The last two are the ones that matter most. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- In the crystal garden, the biggest crystal was 2 mm on day 3, 4 mm on day 5 and 6 mm on day 7. If it keeps growing the same way, how big will it be on day 9? **8 mm.**. Every two days, the crystal grew 2 mm: 2, then 4, then 6. The pattern adds 2 each time. So on day 9 it will be 6 + 2 = 8 mm. In real life the crystal will stop when the water dries up or the salt runs out. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)

## Warm-up
- Is a candle flame alive? → No. It moves and grows, but it has no cells and passes on no instructions.
- A dry seed doing nothing is ____: alive but resting. → dormant
- What tiny eight-legged animal can dry out and wake years later? → A water bear, also called a tardigrade.
- Where can a virus make copies of themselves? → Only inside a living cell.
- How does a crystal grow? → By adding more of the same salt onto its outside.
- Name two signs of life a robot does NOT have. → Any two: cells, growing, taking in food, making young, passing on instructions.
- What gas did the yeast make in the balloon test? → Carbon dioxide, the same gas you breathe out.
- Is a mule alive, even though they almost never have babies? → Yes. They are made of cells and do all the other life processes.
- Do scientists agree on whether viruses are alive? → No. They still argue.
