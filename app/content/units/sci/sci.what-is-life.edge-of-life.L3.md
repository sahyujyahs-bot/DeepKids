# The edge of life: fire, crystals, viruses and sleeping seeds
`sci.what-is-life.edge-of-life.L3`

**Summary.** This unit puts tricky cases on trial. Is fire alive? It moves, grows, needs air and makes more fire, but it is not alive. A crystal grows too, but only by adding on from outside. A dry seed and a dried-up water bear show no signs of life at all, yet they are alive, only resting. A virus does nothing on its own, but inside a living cell it makes thousands of copies of itself. Scientists still argue about viruses. So the line between alive and not alive is not as sharp as it looks, and nobody yet knows exactly how life first began.

**Intro.** *Hold a dry moong seed in one hand and a small pebble in the other. Both are hard. Both are still. Both do nothing at all. One of them is alive. Which one? And how could you prove it?* A candle flame moves. It grows. It needs air. It can even start new flames. A dry seed does none of these things. Yet scientists say the seed is alive and the flame is not. In this unit you will put six puzzling things on trial: fire, crystals, a robot, a dry seed, a tiny animal called a water bear, and a virus. You will find that the line between living and non-living is harder to draw than anyone thought.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can test a tricky case against the signs of life, and say which signs it shows and which it lacks.
1. I can explain why fire and crystals show some signs of life but are not alive.
2. I can explain that a dry seed or a dried-out water bear is alive but resting, and show it with a seed test.
3. I can say what a virus is, and why scientists still argue about whether it is alive.
4. I can explain why a robot that moves and senses is not alive, and say what it lacks.
5. I can run a fair test with a comparison, such as seeds next to pebbles, and finish with claim, evidence and reasoning.
6. I can say that people draw the line between living and non-living, and that nobody yet knows how life began.

## Terms
- **Living thing**: Something that is alive. It is made of one or more cells. It takes in food, uses energy, grows, senses, gets rid of waste and makes more of its own kind. *e.g.* A neem tree, a cow, an ant, a mushroom and you are all living things.
- **Non-living thing**: Something that is not alive and never was. It does not take in food or grow from inside. *e.g.* A stone, a steel spoon and the air are non-living.
- **Once-living thing**: Something that was part of a living thing, but is not alive any more. *e.g.* A wooden table, a cotton shirt and a dry leaf were once parts of living things.
- **Signs of life**: The things all living things do. Scientists often list seven: moving, breathing out energy from food, sensing, growing, making young, getting rid of waste and taking in food. *e.g.* A puppy moves, eats, grows and senses your hand. It shows all the signs.
- **Cell**: The tiny building block every living thing is made of. Most cells are far too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* Your body is made of about 30 trillion cells. A bacterium is just one cell.
- **Instructions (genes)**: The plan inside every cell that says how to build and run the living thing. Parents pass copies of it to their young. *e.g.* A mango seed has mango instructions inside, so it grows a mango tree, not a coconut palm.
- **Respiration**: How living things get energy from food. Inside cells, food joins with oxygen. This lets out energy, carbon dioxide gas and water. *e.g.* Your cells are doing respiration right now. The air you breathe out carries the carbon dioxide away.
- **Dormant**: Alive but resting. A dormant thing's life processes slow down so much that it seems to do nothing. It wakes up when conditions are right. *e.g.* A dry rajma seed in the kitchen jar is dormant. Put it on wet cotton and it wakes.
- **Germinate**: When a seed wakes up and starts to grow a root and a shoot. *e.g.* Soaked moong seeds germinate in a day or two. That is how sprouts are made.
- **Water bear (tardigrade)**: A tiny animal with eight stubby legs, about as long as a grain of salt is wide. It lives in damp moss everywhere, even on city walls. *e.g.* Water bears can dry out completely, wait for years, and wake up when water comes back.
- **Tun**: The dry, curled-up ball a water bear turns into when its home dries out. Its life almost stops. *e.g.* A tun can blow about in dust, then wake in a rain puddle.
- **Crystal**: A solid whose tiny particles are lined up in a neat repeating pattern. Crystals grow by adding more particles on the outside. *e.g.* Salt grains, sugar grains and snowflakes are crystals.
- **Virus**: A tiny bundle of instructions wrapped in a coat. It is much smaller than a bacterium. It can only make copies of itself inside a living cell. *e.g.* The common cold and flu are caused by viruses.
- **Host cell**: A living cell that a virus gets inside and uses to make more viruses. *e.g.* When a cold virus gets into the cells lining your nose, those cells become host cells.
- **Bacteria**: Living things made of just one tiny cell. They are everywhere: in soil, in water and inside you. *e.g.* Bacteria turn milk into curd.
- **Evolution**: The slow change in living things over many, many generations, because their instructions change a little each time they are copied. *e.g.* Farmers slowly changed wild cabbage plants, over many generations, into cabbage, cauliflower and broccoli.
- **Robot**: A machine that people build to sense things and move by itself, following instructions people wrote. *e.g.* A robot vacuum cleaner senses the wall and turns away from it.
- **Definition**: A sentence that says exactly what a word means, so everyone uses it the same way. *e.g.* A definition of 'bird' might be: an animal with feathers that lays eggs.

## Explanations
### The tests we use to decide if something is alive · *defined*
**Definition:** A living thing is made of cells, uses energy from food, grows, senses, gets rid of waste, makes more of its kind, and passes on instructions to its young.

Scientists do not use one sign. They use a list, and a living thing must pass all of it, at least some of the time. The usual seven are: moving, getting energy from food, sensing, growing, making young, getting rid of waste, and taking in food. Many scientists add two more that matter most for tricky cases. A living thing is made of cells. And it carries instructions that it passes on to its young.
- e.g. A goat moves, eats grass, grows, senses you, has kids, makes dung and breathes. It is made of cells. Its kids look like goats because of the instructions they got.
- e.g. A bicycle moves, but it does none of the other things. So one sign alone tells us nothing.
### Fire: it does many things, but it is not alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Fire is a fast chemical change in which fuel joins with oxygen and gives out heat and light. It has no cells and no instructions, so it is not alive.

Fire is a very fast chemical change. Fuel, like wax or wood, joins with oxygen from the air. This lets out heat and light. Fire passes many tests. It moves and spreads. It 'eats' fuel and 'breathes' oxygen. It grows. It gives off waste: smoke, ash, carbon dioxide and water. A spark can even start a new fire. But fire has no cells. And a new fire does not carry instructions from the old one. Its shape comes only from the fuel and the air around it. So fire is not alive.
- e.g. Put a glass over a candle and the flame goes out. It has used up the oxygen it needs.
- e.g. A forest fire spreads like a hungry animal. But a fire started from a birthday candle is no different from one started from a match.
### Your body does a slow, gentle burning · *defined*
**Definition:** Respiration is the controlled joining of food and oxygen inside cells, which lets out energy, carbon dioxide and water.

Here is a surprise. Fire and your cells do almost the same chemistry. A flame joins fuel with oxygen and lets out energy, carbon dioxide and water. Your cells join food with oxygen and let out energy, carbon dioxide and water too. That is called respiration. The difference is speed and control. A fire does it all at once, wildly. A cell does it step by step, so slowly that it stays warm but never burns. About 240 years ago, the French scientist Antoine Lavoisier showed that breathing is like a slow burning. His wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, worked beside him. She drew his experiments and wrote up the results.
- e.g. Breathe on a cold steel plate. The mist is water from your respiration.
- e.g. A candle and a mouse in a closed jar both use up oxygen. Lavoisier and others did experiments like this long ago. We never do this to an animal.
### Crystals grow from outside, living things grow from inside · *defined*
**Definition:** A crystal grows by adding the same particles to its outside in a repeating pattern. A living thing grows from inside by turning food into new parts.

A crystal really does grow. Hang a thread in very salty water, and salt crystals build up on it day by day. But a crystal grows only by adding more of the same stuff onto its outside, like stacking bricks. It does not take in food and change it into new parts. It has no cells. It does not use energy to keep itself going. A living thing grows from inside. A seedling takes in water and air and turns them into new leaves that were not there before.
- e.g. Rock salt from the shop is a big crystal. It grew slowly in salty water, adding on layer by layer.
- e.g. A banana plant grows a new leaf from deep inside its stem. The leaf is made from water, air and sunlight.
### Dormant: alive but resting · *defined*
**Definition:** Dormant means alive but resting, with life processes slowed down so much that the living thing seems to do nothing, until conditions are right to wake.

A dry seed does nothing. It does not grow, eat or move. It looks no more alive than a pebble. But inside it is a tiny baby plant with a packed lunch of stored food, wrapped in a tough coat. Its life processes have slowed down almost to a stop. This resting state is called dormancy. When the seed takes in water and is warm enough, its cells wake up. It starts to breathe faster, uses its stored food, and sends out a root. A pebble can never do that, however much you water it. In China, scientists woke up a lotus seed found in an old dry lake bed. It was about 1,300 years old, and it grew.
- e.g. Rajma, moong, chana and wheat in your kitchen are all dormant seeds.
- e.g. Dried yeast in a packet is dormant too. Put it in warm sugar water and it wakes up and makes gas.
### Water bears: the animals that dry up and wake again · *debated*
Water bears, also called tardigrades, are tiny animals with eight legs. They live in damp moss and lichen all over the world, from the Himalaya to the deep sea, and on old walls in Indian towns. When their moss dries out, they pull in their legs and curl into a dry ball called a tun. They lose almost all their water. Their life processes nearly stop. In this state they can stay for years. In 2007, scientists sent dried water bears into space on the outside of a spacecraft. Many survived and later had babies. In Japan, water bears frozen in moss for about 30 years were woken up in 2014, and one laid eggs. So is a tun alive? Scientists answer in different ways.
- e.g. A moss patch on a wall dries in the hot season. Its water bears turn into tuns. When the monsoon comes, they wake up and walk again.
- e.g. In 2019 a spacecraft carrying dried water bears crashed on the Moon. Later tests suggest the crash was probably too hard for them to survive.
- **Many biologists:** A tun is alive. Its life is only slowed down, so far that we can hardly measure it. It is like a very deep version of a seed's rest.
- **David Keilin, a biologist in England, in 1959:** He called this state 'cryptobiosis', which means hidden life. He suggested it is a third state, neither fully living nor dead, because for a while nothing at all may be happening inside.
- **Some thinkers today:** Maybe 'alive' should mean 'able to live again', not 'doing life processes right now'. Then a tun, a seed and a frozen embryo all count.
### Viruses: on the edge of life · *debated*
A virus is a tiny bundle of instructions wrapped in a coat. Most viruses are so small that only a special microscope, called an electron microscope, can show them. Outside a cell, a virus does nothing at all. It does not eat, grow, breathe or get rid of waste. It just drifts. But when it touches the right living cell, it gets inside. Its instructions take over the cell's machinery. The cell then builds thousands of new viruses, which burst out to find more cells. Viruses also change over time, the way living things do. That is why we need a new flu vaccine most years. So is a virus alive?
- e.g. A cold virus gets into the cells lining your nose. Those cells make so many new viruses that you sneeze them out.
- e.g. Some viruses attack only bacteria. They are called phages, and they are the most common kind of virus on Earth.
- **Many scientists:** Viruses are not alive. They have no cells. They cannot do any life process by themselves. They are more like a set of instructions than a living thing.
- **Other scientists:** Viruses are a kind of life. They carry instructions, make copies of themselves and evolve. A virus is like a seed that can only grow in one kind of soil: a living cell.
- **Some scientists who study giant viruses:** In 2003, French scientists described the mimivirus, a virus so big it was first mistaken for a bacterium. Later they even found a small virus that infects the giant virus's copying factory. Maybe viruses are alive only while they are inside a cell.
### A virus that hides and waits · *defined*
**Definition:** Some viruses can put their instructions into a cell's own instructions and stay hidden there, being copied with the cell, until they wake.

Some viruses do not make copies straight away. They slip their instructions into the cell's own instructions and hide there, quietly, for many generations. When the cell divides, it copies the hidden virus too. Later, the virus can wake up and burst out. In 1951 the American scientist Esther Lederberg discovered a virus doing exactly this inside bacteria. She named it lambda. Her work became an important tool in science, but for a long time she got far less credit than her husband, who won a Nobel Prize.
- e.g. The virus that causes chickenpox can hide in the body for many years. In some grown-ups it wakes up later as a painful rash called shingles.
- e.g. About 8 out of every 100 parts of your own instructions came from viruses that hid inside your ancestors long ago.
### Robots: they move and sense, but are not alive · *debated*
A robot can do some things that look alive. It can sense light, sound or a wall. It can move by itself. Some robots even go back to their charger when their battery is low, a bit like going to eat. But a robot has no cells. It does not grow. It cannot repair itself or build a baby robot from food. Its instructions were written by people, and it does not pass them on and change them by itself. So robots today are not alive. Could one ever be? Scientists and thinkers disagree.
- e.g. A robot vacuum cleaner senses walls and finds its way home to charge.
- e.g. In 2020, scientists in the United States built tiny 'xenobots' from frog cells. They could move, and later some even gathered loose cells into new xenobots.
- **Most scientists today:** Life is chemistry inside cells. A metal and plastic robot is not alive, however clever it is.
- **Some scientists who build robots and computer programs:** If a machine could find its own energy, repair itself, build copies and change over generations, it might count as a new kind of life, even without cells.
- **The team who made xenobots from frog cells:** Our xenobots are living, because they are made of living cells. But they are also designed, like machines. Maybe the old line between machine and living thing is getting blurry.
### Life is made of ordinary atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things are made of the same kinds of atoms as non-living things. Life is a special arrangement and activity of ordinary atoms.

If you could take a living thing apart, you would not find any special 'life stuff'. You would find atoms, the tiny bits that everything is made of. Mostly carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. These are the same atoms that are in air, water, coal and rock. Almost every atom in your body, apart from hydrogen, was made inside a star long ago. What makes something alive is not what it is made of. It is how those atoms are arranged and kept busy.
- e.g. The carbon in a mango tree came from carbon dioxide in the air.
- e.g. The water in your body was once rain, river and sea.
### Living things keep themselves going · *idea*
Here is another way to think about life. In 1944 the physicist Erwin Schrödinger wrote a little book called 'What Is Life?'. He pointed out that everything left alone tends to fall apart. A sandcastle crumbles. A hot cup cools. Living things fight this every moment. They take in food or sunlight and use the energy to keep themselves in order, and to repair and rebuild. Your body replaces many of its cells every few weeks, yet you stay you. You are less like a statue and more like a flame or a river: the stuff flows through, and the pattern stays. This is a helpful idea, not a full definition. A flame also keeps its shape this way, which is why fire is such a tricky case.
- e.g. The lining of your stomach is replaced every few days.
- e.g. A whirlpool in a river keeps its shape, though the water in it is always new.
### No one agreed definition of life · *debated*
You might expect scientists to have one exact sentence that says what life is. They do not. Every definition has a tricky case that breaks it. Some definitions leave out mules, which are alive but cannot have babies. Some let in fire or crystals. Some leave out dry seeds, which are alive but do nothing. So different scientists use different working definitions, for different jobs.
- e.g. A mule is the young of a horse and a donkey. It is alive but almost never has young of its own.
- e.g. A worker bee never has young either. Is it less alive than the queen?
- **The checklist:** Something is alive if it passes the signs of life, made of cells and carrying instructions. This works well for animals and plants, but struggles with seeds, mules and viruses.
- **A NASA group in the 1990s, often linked to the scientist Gerald Joyce:** Life is a chemical system that keeps itself going and can evolve over generations. It was written to help search for life on other planets. Joyce himself said it was a working tool, not the last word.
- **Some philosophers:** Maybe life has no sharp edge, like the colours of a rainbow. Red and yellow are clearly different, but nobody can point to where orange begins.
### How did the first living thing begin? · *idea*
Today, every living thing comes from another living thing. Seeds come from plants, chicks from eggs, bacteria from bacteria. But long ago, about 4 billion years ago, the Earth had no life at all. Somehow, non-living chemicals became the first living cells. Nobody knows exactly how. In 1952, Stanley Miller and Harold Urey mixed gases they thought were in the early air, and sparked them like lightning. They made some of the building blocks of living things. Another idea is that life began with a simpler kind of instruction molecule, called RNA, that could copy itself. These are ideas, and scientists still argue about them.
- e.g. Rotting meat does not make maggots by itself. Flies lay eggs on it. Francesco Redi showed this in Italy in 1668 by covering meat jars with cloth.
- e.g. Louis Pasteur boiled broth in flasks with long curved necks in about 1860. Nothing grew, because no germs could get in.
### J.C. Bose: the living and the non-living respond alike · *debated*
Jagadish Chandra Bose was a scientist in Kolkata. He built instruments so sensitive they could show a plant's tiny electrical signals. In 1902 he wrote a book called 'Response in the Living and Non-Living'. He showed that plants respond to touch, heat, injury and poison with signals rather like those in animal nerves. He also found that some metals seemed to get 'tired' after being poked many times, then recover after rest. He felt there was one long chain of response running through all matter, with no sharp break between living and non-living.
- e.g. A touch-me-not plant (lajwanti) folds its leaves when you touch it. Bose measured the electrical signal that travels through it.
- e.g. Bose's crescograph magnified a plant's growth thousands of times, so you could watch it grow.
- **Scientists today:** Bose's measurements of plant electrical signals were real and ahead of their time. Plant signalling is now a busy field of research.
- **Many scientists, then and now:** A metal getting 'tired' is not the same as a living response. Metals have no cells and no instructions. The likeness is on the surface.
- **Bose himself:** He saw a deep sameness running through matter and life. This was his view, and most scientists do not share it.

## Mostly agreed
- Every living thing is made of one or more cells. *How we know:* Since the first microscopes, about 350 years ago, scientists have looked at plants, animals, fungi and microbes. Every one of them turned out to be made of cells.
- Today, every living thing comes from another living thing. *How we know:* Redi in 1668 and Pasteur in about 1860 showed that maggots and germs do not appear from nothing. When nothing living can get in, nothing grows.
- Fire, crystals and today's robots are not alive. *How we know:* None of them is made of cells, and none passes on instructions to its young. A new fire is shaped only by its fuel and the air.
- Dry seeds and dried-out water bears are alive, only resting. *How we know:* Add water and warmth, and they start to breathe, use food and grow again. Pebbles next to them never do.
- Viruses can only make copies of themselves inside living cells. *How we know:* Scientists can watch this with electron microscopes and in the lab. Outside cells, viruses do nothing.
- Living things are made of the same kinds of atoms as non-living things. *How we know:* When living things are broken down by chemists, they find carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and a few others. These are found in air, water and rock too.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Living things move, and non-living things don't.** (Many of us think this without noticing.; *rejected*)  
  For: Animals run, fly and swim. Stones stay where they are.  
  Against: Rivers, clouds, wind and cars all move, and none is alive. A banyan tree hardly moves, and it is alive. Movement alone tells us nothing.
- **Living things contain a special 'life force' that non-living things do not have.** (Many thinkers for thousands of years. The idea is called vitalism.; *rejected*)  
  For: Living things seem so different from rocks that it feels like something extra must be inside them.  
  Against: In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler made urea, a chemical from living bodies, out of non-living chemicals. Since then chemists have found only ordinary atoms in living things. No life force has ever been found or measured.
- **Small living things like maggots and germs can appear by themselves from rotting food.** (Most people, including great thinkers like Aristotle, for about 2,000 years. The idea is called spontaneous generation.; *rejected*)  
  For: Leave meat out, and maggots appear. Leave broth out, and it goes cloudy with germs.  
  Against: Redi covered meat with cloth in 1668, and no maggots came, because flies could not lay eggs. Pasteur's curved-neck flasks of boiled broth stayed clear, because no germs could get in.
- **Viruses are alive.** (Some biologists, especially some who study giant viruses.; *debated*)  
  For: Viruses carry instructions, make copies of themselves and evolve. Some giant viruses have more genes than some bacteria.  
  Against: Viruses have no cells and cannot do a single life process on their own. Outside a cell they are as still as a grain of dust.
- **A rock found in Antarctica, which came from Mars, holds tiny fossils of Martian life.** (David McKay and a team at NASA, in 1996.; *minority*)  
  For: The rock, called ALH84001, had tiny worm-like shapes and chemicals that bacteria on Earth can make.  
  Against: Most scientists later decided that ordinary non-living chemistry could make all of these shapes and chemicals. The shapes are also smaller than any known bacterium.
- **Crystals were the first life: life began on clay crystals that copied their patterns.** (Graham Cairns-Smith, a chemist in Scotland, from the 1960s.; *minority*)  
  For: Clay crystals grow and copy their layer patterns as they grow. They might have held the first simple molecules in order.  
  Against: No one has shown clay crystals passing on patterns that change and improve over generations. Most scientists prefer other ideas, such as the RNA idea.
- **Plants feel pain and emotions, the way animals do.** (Some popular books, going further than J.C. Bose's own measurements.; *debated*)  
  For: Plants send electrical signals when they are hurt, and they respond to touch, light and chemicals.  
  Against: Plants have no brains or nerves like ours. Many scientists say a signal is not the same as a feeling. Nobody has a test yet for what a plant feels.

## Roots
We have been putting tricky cases on trial: fire, crystals, robots, sleeping seeds, water bears and viruses. Now we ask what these puzzles tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Life does not begin where we draw the line. The edge between alive and not alive is drawn by us, not by nature.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort the world into two boxes, alive and not alive, as if nature made the boxes. But a dry seed, a tun and a virus sit between them. The boxes are our words, and the world is smoother than our words.  
*Evidence:* A dry seed shows no signs of life and is alive. A flame shows many signs and is not. A virus is lifeless outside a cell and busy inside one.

- *who am i:* You are made of the same atoms as rocks, air and stars. About 8 out of every 100 parts of your instructions came from viruses long ago.
- *what is life:* Life is not a special stuff. It is ordinary atoms, arranged and kept busy, like a flame that knows how to keep itself going.
- *what is this world:* The world is not split cleanly into living and non-living. There are sleepers, waiters and in-betweens everywhere.

Everything in a seed, a flame and you is made of the same few kinds of atoms. DeepKids believes this is the One showing up as many things. Some of those things are arranged to stay busy, repair themselves and pass on instructions, and we call them alive. Others rest, like a seed, and some only borrow life, like a virus. The many look sharply different, but underneath they shade into each other.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to break open a tiny banyan seed. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing at all.' The father says: from this nothing that you cannot see, the whole great banyan tree grows. (A dry seed looks like nothing is happening inside it. Yet a whole living tree waits there, resting.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 30 (Diels-Kranz numbering):** The Greek thinker Heraclitus said the world is like an ever-living fire, always burning and always changing, yet keeping its pattern. (A flame and a living body both keep their shape while new stuff flows through them. That is why fire is such a tricky case, and why life is so hard to define.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta:** The hymn of creation asks how everything began, and ends with an honest question: perhaps even the one who watches from the highest heaven does not know. (Scientists still do not know how the first life came from non-living things. Saying 'we do not know' is part of being honest.)
- Science (settled): Living things are made of the same kinds of atoms as non-living things, and most of those atoms were made in stars.
- Science (open): How the very first life came from non-living chemicals is not known. There are ideas, like the RNA idea, and experiments, but no answer yet.
- Other view, Many biologists: The line is not just our words. Cells and inherited instructions are a real difference in nature. Fire and crystals really lack them. A few in-between cases do not mean there is no line, just as dusk does not mean day and night are the same.
- Practice, The sleeping seed (3 min): Let's be a seed. Curl up small on the floor, and hold one dry seed in your hand. Breathe in slowly. Breathe out slowly. Make your breath so small and quiet that you almost cannot feel it, like a resting seed. Stay very still while I count to twenty. Now imagine warm rain falling on you. Slowly, slowly, uncurl. Stretch one arm up like a shoot. Open your eyes. Were you alive the whole time you were still?
- Ask: When you are fast asleep, how do you know you are still alive?
- Ask: Is there anything in the world that is completely not alive? How could you be sure?
- Ask: If the line between alive and not alive is drawn by people, who should get to draw it?
- Ask: Part of you came from viruses long ago. Where do you end and the rest of the world begin?

- *deep:* Your cells and a candle both join fuel with oxygen. So what makes you alive and the candle not? (hint: Think about cells, instructions, and who controls the burning.)

## Story: The Dust in the Gutter
*Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was real. So was his daughter Maria (1656 to 1745), who lived with him and cared for him. In a letter of 1702 he really described dust from a roof gutter that came alive with wheel-animals (we now call them rotifers) when he added water. Maria's part in this story, her windowsill tests and the exact days are imagined.*

In the summer of 1701 it hardly rained in Delft. The canals turned green and smelled of old cabbage. The lead gutter along the front of our house filled with a fine red-brown dust, and the dust baked hard in the sun.

My name is Maria. My father is Antonie van Leeuwenhoek. He sold cloth once. Now he is nearly seventy, and what he mostly does is look. He grinds tiny lenses, each one smaller than a lentil, and holds them so close to his eye that his eyelashes brush the brass. Through them he has seen things no one else has ever seen. Little animals swimming in a drop of rainwater. Little animals wriggling in the white stuff scraped from between his teeth.

I am the one who keeps the house. I wash the jars he leaves on the windowsill. I sweep the glass dust from his bench. I write the dates on his letters to the learned gentlemen in London. My name is not in the letters. That is how it is.

One morning I climbed the ladder to clean the gutter. I scraped the dust into a bowl. It was as dry as ground pepper. I was carrying it to the canal to throw away when my father called from the window.

"Maria! What have you got there?"

"Dirt," I said. "Dead dirt."

"Bring it," he said. "Nothing is dead until I have looked."

He put a few grains on a slip of glass and peered at them. Nothing. Only dust, lumpy and brown, like crumbs of a burnt biscuit. He let me look too. Nothing moved. How could it? It had lain in that hot gutter for weeks.

Then he did a thing I thought was foolish. He poured a little rainwater from the barrel into a clean glass tube, and he stirred in the dust. "Now we wait," he said. I went to see to the soup.

When I came back, his face had changed. He did not speak. He only moved aside and pointed at the lens.

I bent and looked. The brown lumps were swelling, the way a dried pea swells in a pot. One of them stretched. It pushed out a tail, like a tiny telescope sliding open. At its front, two little wheels began to turn, spinning and spinning, pulling the water towards its mouth. Then another lump woke. Then another. Soon the drop was full of them, whirling about like boys let out of school.

"They were dead," I whispered. "Weeks in the sun. Dry as pepper. They were dead, and the water has made them alive again."

"Were they?" my father asked. He rubbed his tired eyes. "If a little water could make the dead alive, Maria, every graveyard would be busy after the rain."

I did not laugh. I was thinking about the dried peas in the kitchen jar. They do nothing all winter. Put them in wet earth in spring, and they send out roots. Nobody says the peas came back from the dead. We say they were resting. Waiting. Their life was turned down low, so low you could not see it. Like the coals I cover with ash at night, so there is still fire in the morning.

"They were not dead," I said slowly. "They were waiting for water."

"Then show me," he said. It was what he always said to the gentlemen in London.

So I did, though nobody had asked me. I took a drop full of spinning wheel-animals and let it dry on a glass slip on the windowsill, in the sun. By evening it was only a brown stain. Two days later, I added rainwater. I watched. For a long time, nothing. Then a lump swelled. A tail stretched. A wheel turned.

I dried them again. I woke them again. Not all of them woke each time. Some stayed lumps for ever, and those, I think, had truly died. But many came back, again and again, as if drying out was a door they knew how to close and open.

My father wrote it all down for the Royal Society in London, in a long letter, in his careful hand. He wrote about the gutter and the dust and the little wheels. He did not write about my windowsill. I did not mind as much as you might think. The wheel-animals did not know whose name was on the letter either. They only knew water.

That autumn the rain came back. It drummed on the roof and ran in the gutter, and I lay awake, listening. Up there in the dark, in the wet dust, thousands of tiny things were unrolling their tails and starting their wheels. They had not been alive, and then dead, and then alive. They had been something in between, something we had no word for.

In the morning my father found me standing at the window.

"What are you looking at?" he asked.

"Everything," I said. "I am trying to work out which of it is only asleep."

**For the parent:** For the parent: drying out is not the same as dying. Some tiny animals, like rotifers and water bears, and seeds too, can lose nearly all their water. Their life processes almost stop, so they show no signs of life at all. When water comes back, they wake. Scientists call this dormancy, and in its deepest form 'cryptobiosis', which means hidden life. So 'alive' and 'dead' are not the only two boxes. There is a third: waiting. The story also quietly asks who gets the credit for careful work.

- Ask: Maria called the dust 'dead dirt'. Why did she think so? What did her father think?
- Ask: What did the water do to the brown lumps?
- Ask: Why did Maria think of the dried peas in the kitchen jar?
- Ask: Some lumps never woke up. What does that tell you about the others?
- Ask: Maria's name was not in the letter. Who do you think should get the credit for a discovery?

## Experiments
### e1 · Sleeping Seeds: is a dry seed alive? (30 min, 5 days)
- **question:** Is a dry seed alive, even though it does nothing? What does it need to wake up?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. What will happen to seeds on wet cotton? To pebbles on wet cotton? To seeds on dry cotton? Draw your guess for each plate.
- **fair test:** To make it fair, keep everything the same except one thing. Use the same plates, the same cotton and the same place. The wet seeds and the wet pebbles test whether water alone makes things grow. The wet seeds and the dry seeds test whether seeds need water. Use 6 seeds, because one seed can be a dud.
- **safety:** Do not eat raw seeds from the experiment. Wash hands after handling them. Keep pebbles away from very small children.
- **record:** Fill in the table each day. Draw the three plates side by side each day. On the last day, draw a line chart of the longest root on plate 1, one point for each day.
- **results table:** Day | Plate 1: seeds on wet cotton | Plate 2: pebbles on wet cotton | Plate 3: seeds on dry cotton | Longest root on plate 1 (mm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess what will happen. Set up the three plates. Draw them. (15 min); day 2: Check all three plates. Draw what you see. Add a few drops of water to plates 1 and 2. (4 min); day 3: Check, draw and measure the longest root. Water plates 1 and 2. (4 min); day 4: Check, draw and measure. Water plates 1 and 2. (3 min); day 5: Last check. Finish the table. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (4 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a dry moong seed and a pebble side by side on your child's palm.  
  *Parent says:* Look at these two. Both are hard, both are still, both do nothing. One of them is alive. Which one, do you think? How could we test it?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the seed and the pebble. Both do nothing. Which one is alive? How could you test it?
  - Ask: How could we find out which one is alive? (good: Give them both water and see which one grows.; Watch them for a few days.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw three plates and their guesses for each.  
  *Parent says:* Here is our plan. Plate 1: seeds on wet cotton. Plate 2: pebbles on wet cotton. Plate 3: seeds on dry cotton. What will happen on each plate? Draw your guess.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three plates. Seeds on wet cotton. Pebbles on wet cotton. Seeds on dry cotton. Draw what you think will happen.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a layer of cotton on each plate. 2. Wet the cotton on plates 1 and 2 until damp but not swimming. 3. Leave plate 3 dry. 4. Put 6 seeds on plate 1, 6 pebbles on plate 2 and 6 seeds on plate 3. 5. Put the plates side by side, away from direct sun.  
  *Parent says:* We use two plates of seeds, so the only difference is the water. And we put pebbles on wet cotton, to check that water alone does not make things grow.  
  *Child mode:* Put cotton on three plates. Wet plates 1 and 2. Keep plate 3 dry. Add seeds, pebbles and seeds. Put them side by side.
  - Note: Cotton should be damp, like a squeezed sponge. Too much water can make seeds rot.
  - Check: Why do we put pebbles on wet cotton too? → **To check that water alone does not make things grow**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each day for four days, check all three plates. Draw them. Add a few drops of water to plates 1 and 2. Measure the longest root on plate 1.  
  *Parent says:* Let's check our plates. What has changed? Which plate looks different? Let's measure the longest root.  
  *Child mode:* Check your plates. What has changed? Draw them. Measure the longest root.
  - Note: Expect seeds on plate 1 to swell within a day. A white root appears on day 2 or 3, then a shoot. Pebbles and dry seeds stay the same. Use the hand lens to look at the root tip.
  - Wait: Come back each day for four days.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On the last day, add water to plate 3, the dry seeds. Talk about what will happen next.  
  *Parent says:* Plate 3 has been dry all this time. Were those seeds dead, or still waiting? Let's give them water and see.  
  *Child mode:* Plate 3 was dry all week. Are its seeds dead or waiting? Give them water. Check tomorrow.
  - Check: The dry seeds did nothing all week. Then you gave them water, and next day they swelled up. What does this show? → **They were alive all along, resting until water came**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like scientists. What do you think now about dry seeds? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a dry seed is alive, only resting. What I saw: the seeds on wet cotton grew roots 40 mm long by day 5, but the pebbles on wet cotton did not change. Why: the seed has a baby plant inside that wakes up when it gets water.
### e2 · The Balloon Test: is the sand in the yeast packet alive? (35 min)
- **question:** Dried yeast looks like sand. Is it alive?
- **predict:** Guess which bottle will blow up its balloon: yeast with warm sugar water, yeast with cold sugar water, or warm sugar water with no yeast.
- **fair test:** Keep everything the same except one thing at a time. Each bottle gets the same amount of water and sugar. Bottles 1 and 2 test whether warmth matters. Bottles 1 and 3 test whether the yeast is what makes the gas.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the warm water. It must feel like bath water, not hot. Do not drink the mixture.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the three bottles and their balloons after 30 minutes.
- **results table:** Bottle | What is inside | Balloon after 10 minutes | Balloon after 30 minutes | Distance round the balloon after 30 minutes (cm)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour a little dried yeast into your child's palm. Let them look at it with the hand lens.  
  *Parent says:* This is dried yeast, from the kitchen shop. It looks like sand. Bakers say it is alive. What do you think? How could we test it?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the dried yeast with the lens. It looks like sand. Is it alive? How could you find out?
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 1 teaspoon of sugar in each bottle. 2. A grown-up pours half a cup of warm water into bottles 1 and 3, and half a cup of cold water into bottle 2. 3. Add 1 teaspoon of yeast to bottles 1 and 2 only. 4. Swirl each bottle. 5. Stretch a balloon over each neck.  
  *Parent says:* Bottle 1 has yeast and warm water. Bottle 2 has yeast and cold water. Bottle 3 has warm water and no yeast. Which balloon do you think will blow up?  
  *Child mode:* Put sugar in all three bottles. A grown-up adds water. Add yeast to bottles 1 and 2. Swirl. Put on the balloons.
  - Note: Warm means bath-warm, about as warm as your wrist can comfortably hold. Water that is too hot can kill the yeast.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Watch the bottles for 30 minutes. Check at 10 minutes and at 30 minutes. Look for bubbles and froth.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look at the bottles. Do you see bubbles? Froth? Is any balloon standing up? Living things take in food and give off waste. What could the gas be?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the bottles. Do you see bubbles? Is a balloon filling up? Check again in a while.
  - Note: Bottle 1 should froth and its balloon should slowly stand up and fill. Bottle 2 will be slow. Bottle 3 will not change. The gas is carbon dioxide, the same gas you breathe out.
  - Wait: Check again after 30 minutes.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wrap a string around the middle of each balloon. Measure the string against the ruler.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure how big each balloon got. Wrap the string around its fattest part, then hold it on the ruler.  
  *Child mode:* Wrap the string round each balloon. Measure the string with the ruler. Write it down.
  - Check: Only the yeast bottles made gas. What does this tell us about dried yeast? → **It is alive: it was resting, and in warm sugar water it ate sugar and gave off gas**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like scientists. What do you think now about the yeast? What did you see? And why do you think it happened? Why was bottle 2 so slow?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why. Why was the cold bottle slow?
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: dried yeast is alive but resting. What I saw: the warm yeast balloon grew to 30 cm round, but the no-yeast balloon stayed at 10 cm. Why: the yeast woke up, ate the sugar and gave off gas. Cold slows living things down.

- **schedule:** day 1: Make the salty water and the plain water. Hang the threads. Guess what will happen. (15 min); day 3: Look at both threads with the hand lens. Draw them. (5 min); day 5: Look again. Measure the biggest crystal. Draw. (5 min); day 7: Last look. Compare with the sleeping seeds. Put the crystal on trial with the signs of life. (5 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child some salt grains through the hand lens.  
  *Parent says:* Look at these salt grains. What shape are they? These are crystals. We are going to grow bigger ones. If they grow, are they alive?  
  *Child mode:* Look at salt grains with the lens. What shape are they? We will grow bigger ones. If they grow, are they alive?
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up pours hot water into a jar. 2. Stir in salt, one spoon at a time, until a little stays at the bottom. 3. Fill the second jar with plain hot water. 4. Tie a thread to each pencil. 5. Lay a pencil across each jar so the thread hangs in the water. 6. Put both jars somewhere still.  
  *Parent says:* The hot water can hold lots of salt. As it cools and slowly dries up, the salt has to come out again. Let's see where it goes.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes the salty water. Hang a thread in it. Hang another thread in plain water. Leave both jars very still.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 3, 5 and 7, look at both threads with the hand lens. Draw them. Measure the biggest crystal.  
  *Parent says:* What is on the threads? How are the crystals growing? From inside, or by adding on at the outside?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both threads with the lens. What do you see? Draw it. Measure the biggest crystal.
  - Note: Crystals usually appear in 2 to 4 days and keep growing as the water dries up. The plain water thread stays bare. Notice the flat faces and square corners of salt crystals.
  - Wait: Look again on days 3, 5 and 7.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the crystal thread next to the moong seedlings from the sleeping seeds experiment, if you did it. Go through the signs of life one by one.  
  *Parent says:* Let's put our crystal on trial. Does it move? Eat? Get energy from food? Sense? Make young? Get rid of waste? Is it made of cells? Now the seedling. What is the difference in how they grew?  
  *Child mode:* Put your crystal on trial. Go through each sign. Then try the seedling. How did each one grow?
  - Check: The crystal and the seedling both grew. What is the big difference? → **The crystal grew by adding salt on the outside. The seedling grew from inside, by turning water and air into new parts**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now: is the crystal alive? What did you see? Why do you think so?

  - Note: A good answer: What I think: the crystal is not alive. What I saw: it grew to 5 mm, but only salt built up on the outside, and it did nothing else. The plain water thread stayed bare. Why: the salt came out of the water as it dried. The crystal has no cells and takes in no food.

## Worksheets
### Tricky Case Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Scientists use many signs to decide if something is alive. Which of these is NOT enough on its own?  
   Options: Being made of cells / Moving / Passing on instructions to its young / Getting energy from food  
   **Answer:** Moving. *Why:* Moving alone tells us nothing. Rivers, clouds and cars move, and they are not alive. Being made of cells and passing on instructions are the two signs non-living things find hardest to fake.
2. (mcq) A candle flame moves, grows, uses wax and air, and gives off smoke. Why is it not alive?  
   Options: It is too hot / It has no cells, and a new flame carries no instructions from the old one / It is too small / It does not move  
   **Answer:** It has no cells, and a new flame carries no instructions from the old one. *Why:* Fire does many things living things do. But it has no cells and passes on no instructions. Picking 'too hot' mixes up temperature with life. Some living things live in hot springs.
3. (fill) When the grown-up covered the candle with a glass, the flame went out. It had used up the ____ in the glass.  
   **Answer:** ['oxygen']. *Why:* A flame needs oxygen, a gas in the air. So do we. Under the glass, the oxygen ran out.
4. (mcq) Remember the sleeping seeds experiment, with seeds and pebbles on wet cotton? What happened to the pebbles?  
   Options: They grew roots / Nothing. They stayed the same / They melted / They became seeds  
   **Answer:** Nothing. They stayed the same. *Why:* The pebbles stayed the same, because they are not alive. Only the seeds grew. The pebbles show that water alone does not make things grow.
5. (mcq) A dry moong seed in the kitchen jar does nothing for a year. What is it?  
   Options: Dead / Alive but resting, which is called dormant / A kind of pebble / Once-living  
   **Answer:** Alive but resting, which is called dormant. *Why:* A dry seed is dormant. Its life processes have slowed nearly to a stop. Give it water and warmth, and it wakes. Picking 'once-living' mixes it up with a dry leaf or wood, which can never grow again.
6. (fill) Alive but resting, with life almost stopped, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['dormant']. *Why:* Dormant means alive but resting. Seeds, dried yeast and dried water bears can all be dormant.
7. (mcq) What is a water bear?  
   Options: A small brown bear that lives near water / A tiny eight-legged animal that lives in damp moss / A kind of fish / A virus  
   **Answer:** A tiny eight-legged animal that lives in damp moss. *Why:* A water bear, or tardigrade, is a tiny animal about half a millimetre long. It has eight stubby legs and lives in damp moss. Its name comes from the way it waddles like a bear.
8. (truefalse) A dried-up water bear, curled into a tun, can wake up again when it gets water.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A tun is a water bear's resting form. When water comes back, it uncurls and walks again. Some have woken after about 30 years frozen.
9. (mcq) How does a salt crystal grow?  
   Options: From inside, using food / By adding more salt onto its outside / By having baby crystals / By breathing  
   **Answer:** By adding more salt onto its outside. *Why:* A crystal grows only by stacking more of the same particles onto its outside. Living things grow from inside, by turning food into new parts.
10. (mcq) Where can a virus make copies of itself?  
   Options: Anywhere, even on a table / Only inside a living cell / Only in water / Only in the air  
   **Answer:** Only inside a living cell. *Why:* A virus can only make copies inside a living cell. On a table or in the air it does nothing at all. That is why scientists argue about whether it is alive.
11. (truefalse) Scientists all agree that viruses are alive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Scientists still argue. Some say viruses are not alive, because they do nothing alone. Others say they are a kind of life, because they carry instructions, make copies and evolve.
12. (mcq) A robot vacuum cleaner senses walls and moves by itself. What is it?  
   Options: Alive, because it senses and moves / Not alive, because it has no cells, does not grow and cannot make young / Once alive / Dormant  
   **Answer:** Not alive, because it has no cells, does not grow and cannot make young. *Why:* A robot follows instructions people wrote. It has no cells, does not take in food and cannot build a baby robot. Picking 'alive' means counting two signs and forgetting all the others.
13. (sort) Sort these into Alive, Not alive and Once alive.  
   **Answer:** a dry rajma seed → Alive; a candle flame → Not alive; a wooden spoon → Once alive; a salt crystal → Not alive; dried yeast → Alive; a cotton shirt → Once alive; a toy robot → Not alive; a mushroom → Alive. *Why:* The seed, yeast and mushroom are alive, even if resting. Fire, crystals and robots are not alive. Wood and cotton came from plants that were alive, so they are once alive.
14. (match) Match each tricky case to the reason it is tricky.  
   **Answer:** Fire → It grows and makes new fires, but has no cells; A crystal → It grows, but only by adding on outside; A dry seed → It does nothing, but it is alive; A virus → It makes copies, but only inside a cell; A robot → It moves and senses, but has no cells. *Why:* Each tricky case passes some tests of life and fails others. That is what makes it tricky.
15. (write) In the balloon test, only the bottles with yeast made gas. Write three sentences: what you think about dried yeast, what you saw, and why.  
   **Answer:** What I think: dried yeast is alive but resting. What I saw: the balloon on the warm yeast bottle grew, but the bottle without yeast made no gas. Why: the yeast woke up in warm sugar water, ate the sugar and gave off carbon dioxide.. *Why:* This is claim, evidence and reasoning. Any answer with all three parts, using what was seen, is good.
16. (write) Your friend says: 'Fire is alive, because it eats, breathes and grows.' What would you tell your friend?  
   **Answer:** Fire does do some of those things. But it has no cells, and a new fire gets no instructions from the old one. So scientists say it is not alive.. *Why:* A good answer agrees with what is true in the friend's idea, then names the missing signs.
### Tricky Case Detective (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Fire and your cells both join fuel with oxygen. What is the big difference?  
   Options: Cells do it slowly, step by step, and under control. Fire does it wildly, all at once / Fire uses no oxygen / Cells make light / There is no difference  
   **Answer:** Cells do it slowly, step by step, and under control. Fire does it wildly, all at once. *Why:* Both give off energy, carbon dioxide and water. A cell does it so slowly and carefully that it stays warm but never burns. Picking 'no difference' forgets that cells have instructions controlling every step.
2. (mcq) Lavoisier, a French scientist about 240 years ago, compared breathing to what?  
   Options: A slow burning / A river / A crystal / A song  
   **Answer:** A slow burning. *Why:* Lavoisier showed that breathing is like a slow, gentle burning. His wife, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, drew his experiments and wrote up his results.
3. (mcq) A mule eats, grows and breathes, but almost never has babies. Is it alive?  
   Options: No, because living things must make young / Yes. It is made of cells and does all the other life processes / Only half alive / It is dormant  
   **Answer:** Yes. It is made of cells and does all the other life processes. *Why:* Making young is a sign for a kind of living thing, not for every single one. Mules, worker bees and many people never have young, and they are fully alive.
4. (mcq) In the 1990s a NASA group suggested a working definition of life. What did it say?  
   Options: Life is anything that moves / Life is a chemical system that keeps itself going and can evolve / Life is anything green / Life is anything with a brain  
   **Answer:** Life is a chemical system that keeps itself going and can evolve. *Why:* The NASA working definition says life keeps itself going and can evolve over generations. It was written to help search for life on other planets. It is a tool, not a final answer.
5. (fill) A water bear's dry, curled-up resting form is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['tun']. *Why:* A tun is a water bear with its legs pulled in and almost all its water gone. Its life nearly stops until water returns.
6. (mcq) In 2007 scientists sent dried water bears into space, outside a spacecraft. What happened?  
   Options: They all exploded / Many survived, and later had babies / They turned into crystals / They grew huge  
   **Answer:** Many survived, and later had babies. *Why:* Many dried water bears survived the vacuum of space and some survived strong sunlight too. Back on Earth, they woke and later laid eggs. This shows how deeply they can rest.
7. (mcq) Why do we get a new flu vaccine most years?  
   Options: Because the old vaccine goes bad / Because flu viruses change over time, so the vaccine must change too / Because viruses are animals / Because vaccines are made of crystals  
   **Answer:** Because flu viruses change over time, so the vaccine must change too. *Why:* Flu viruses change a little every time they are copied. Over a year, they change enough that last year's vaccine may not match. This changing is evolution, one reason some scientists call viruses a kind of life.
8. (truefalse) Esther Lederberg discovered a virus that can hide inside bacteria for many generations.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 1951 she discovered the virus lambda, which slips its instructions into a bacterium's own instructions and hides there. She got far less credit than her husband, who won a Nobel Prize.
9. (mcq) Your hair grows every week, but cutting it does not hurt. Why?  
   Options: Hair is alive and very brave / The part of the hair you cut is made of dead cells. It is pushed out by living cells at the root / Hair is a crystal / Hair is a virus  
   **Answer:** The part of the hair you cut is made of dead cells. It is pushed out by living cells at the root. *Why:* The hair you see is made of dead cells. Living cells under your skin, at the root, push it out. So hair 'grows' by being pushed from below, a little like a crystal growing from one side.
10. (mcq) In 1668, Francesco Redi covered jars of meat with cloth. No maggots appeared. What did this show?  
   Options: Maggots grow from cloth / Maggots come from eggs laid by flies, not from the meat itself / Meat is alive / Cloth is alive  
   **Answer:** Maggots come from eggs laid by flies, not from the meat itself. *Why:* When flies could not reach the meat, no maggots came. So maggots come from flies' eggs. Living things only come from other living things.
11. (mcq) Which of these is the best evidence that a dry seed is alive?  
   Options: It is hard / It is brown / Given water and warmth, it sends out a root, but a pebble never does / It is small  
   **Answer:** Given water and warmth, it sends out a root, but a pebble never does. *Why:* The comparison with the pebble is what makes the evidence strong. Both get water. Only the seed grows. That is why the sleeping seeds experiment uses a pebble plate.
12. (order) Put the steps of a virus making copies in order.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]. *Why:* A virus does nothing until it reaches a cell. Then the cell does all the work of making copies.
13. (sort) Which signs of life does a candle flame show? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** moves → The flame shows this; uses fuel → The flame shows this; gives off waste → The flame shows this; grows → The flame shows this; made of cells → The flame does not show this; passes on instructions → The flame does not show this. *Why:* Fire shows many signs, but never cells or instructions. That is why it is not alive.
14. (write) Why do we put a pebble plate next to the seeds in the sleeping seeds experiment? Write two sentences.  
   **Answer:** The pebbles get water too, so we can check that water alone does not make things grow. When the seeds grow and the pebbles don't, we know something inside the seed made the difference.. *Why:* A comparison like this is called a control. It is what makes a test fair.
15. (write) Write one way a dry seed and a dried water bear are the same, and one way they are different.  
   **Answer:** Same: both are alive but resting, and both wake up with water. Different: the seed is a plant and grows roots and leaves, but the water bear is an animal and walks.. *Why:* Any true likeness and difference is good. Dormancy is the big likeness.

   **Answer:** A candle flame, which keeps its shape while wax and air flow through it. *Why:* A flame keeps its shape while new fuel and air flow through it all the time, a bit like a living thing. This is exactly why fire is such a tricky case. A stone does nothing to keep itself in order.
5. (mcq) A lotus seed found in a dry lake bed in China was about 1,300 years old. What happened when scientists gave it water?  
   Options: Nothing / It turned to dust / It sprouted and grew / It became a crystal  
   **Answer:** It sprouted and grew. *Why:* The seed had been dormant for about 1,300 years, and it still grew. Its cells had stayed alive, only resting, all that time.
6. (truefalse) In 1828, Friedrich Wöhler made urea, a chemical from living bodies, out of non-living chemicals. This helped show there is no special 'life force' in the chemicals of living things.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Wöhler's work helped show that the chemicals in living things are ordinary chemicals, made of ordinary atoms.
7. (mcq) In 2020 scientists built 'xenobots' from frog cells. Why are they a puzzle?  
   Options: They are made of living cells, but they were designed by people, like machines / They are made of metal / They are giant frogs / They are crystals  
   **Answer:** They are made of living cells, but they were designed by people, like machines. *Why:* Xenobots are living cells arranged by people into a new shape. They move, and some even gathered loose cells into new xenobots. So are they living things or machines? They blur the line.
8. (mcq) A rock from Mars, found in Antarctica, was once said to hold tiny fossils of Martian life. What do most scientists think now?  
   Options: It definitely holds Martian life / Ordinary non-living chemistry could have made the shapes, so it is not good evidence / It is a dinosaur bone / It came from the Moon  
   **Answer:** Ordinary non-living chemistry could have made the shapes, so it is not good evidence. *Why:* The tiny shapes in the rock, called ALH84001, could be made without life. Most scientists were not convinced. Big claims need strong evidence.
9. (mcq) Which is the best reason the line between alive and not alive is fuzzy?  
   Options: Scientists are not clever enough / Tricky cases like viruses and dormant seeds have some signs of life but not others, and every definition has a case that breaks it / Everything is alive / Nothing is alive  
   **Answer:** Tricky cases like viruses and dormant seeds have some signs of life but not others, and every definition has a case that breaks it. *Why:* Every definition so far has a tricky case. That does not mean anything goes. Fire, rocks and robots are clearly not alive, and trees clearly are. The fuzziness is at the edge.
10. (fill) J.C. Bose, a scientist in Kolkata, wrote a book in 1902 called 'Response in the Living and ____'.  
   **Answer:** ['Non-Living']. *Why:* Bose measured responses in plants and even in metals, and he felt there was no sharp break between living and non-living. Most scientists today agree with his plant measurements, but not with all his ideas.
11. (order) Put the sleeping seed's waking-up in order.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]. *Why:* Water is the signal. The seed swells, its cells wake up and use stored food, then the root comes first and the shoot after.
12. (match) Match each scientist to what they found.  
   **Answer:** Esther Lederberg → A virus that hides inside bacteria; Francesco Redi → Maggots come from flies' eggs; J.C. Bose → Plants send electrical signals; Antonie van Leeuwenhoek → Gutter dust held tiny animals that woke in water; Antoine Lavoisier → Breathing is like a slow burning. *Why:* Each of these people moved the line between living and non-living, or showed where it really is.
13. (write) Is a virus alive? Write what you think, and give one reason for and one reason against.  
   **Answer:** Any view is fine with good reasons. For: a virus carries instructions, makes copies and evolves. Against: it has no cells and can do nothing outside a living cell.. *Why:* Scientists still argue about this. A strong answer gives both sides before choosing.
14. (write) Design a test to find out whether something in a spoon of Mars soil is alive. Say how your test avoids being fooled by ordinary chemistry.  
   **Answer:** Add warm sugar water and cover with a balloon to catch any gas. Do the same with plain sand as a comparison. Then heat some soil strongly and test again. If gas only comes from the unheated soil, and not from the sand, something living may be there.. *Why:* A good test has a comparison, like the pebble plate or the no-yeast bottle.
15. (write) Some sages say the same life runs through everything. Most scientists say life is a special arrangement of ordinary atoms. Write what you think, and why.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer is good. For example: 'I think living things are made of the same atoms as everything else, so maybe we are not so separate. But only some things are arranged to be alive.'. *Why:* This is an open question. There is no single right answer, only well-argued ones.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which sign alone tells us that something is alive?  
   Options: Moving / Growing / None of them alone. We need to look at many signs together / Being warm  
   **Answer:** None of them alone. We need to look at many signs together. *Why:* No single sign is enough. Fire moves and grows. Crystals grow. A dry seed does neither, yet is alive. Picking 'moving' or 'growing' is the most common trap.
2. (mcq) Why is fire not alive?  
   Options: It is too hot / It has no cells, and passes on no instructions / It does not move / It does not need air  
   **Answer:** It has no cells, and passes on no instructions. *Why:* Fire moves, grows, uses fuel and air, and makes waste. But it has no cells and passes on no instructions.
3. (mcq) How is a growing crystal different from a growing seedling?  
   Options: The crystal adds on outside. The seedling grows from inside using food / The crystal grows faster / There is no difference / The seedling is made of salt  
   **Answer:** The crystal adds on outside. The seedling grows from inside using food. *Why:* A crystal stacks more particles on its outside. A seedling builds new parts from inside its cells. Picking 'no difference' treats any growing as a sign of life.
4. (mcq) What is a dormant seed?  
   Options: A dead seed / A seed that is alive but resting / A seed made of plastic / A seed that has already grown  
   **Answer:** A seed that is alive but resting. *Why:* Dormant means alive but resting. Picking 'dead' is the most common first answer. But a dead seed never wakes, and a dormant one does.
5. (mcq) What does a dried-up water bear need to wake up?  
   Options: Food / Water / Sunlight / A loud noise  
   **Answer:** Water. *Why:* Water wakes a tun. The water bear soaks it up, uncurls and walks again.
6. (mcq) In the sleeping seeds experiment, seeds and pebbles both sat on wet cotton. Only the seeds grew. What does this show?  
   Options: Water makes anything grow / Something inside the seed, not just the water, made it grow / Pebbles are dormant / Cotton is alive  
   **Answer:** Something inside the seed, not just the water, made it grow. *Why:* The pebbles are the comparison. They got the same water and did not change. So the seed's own life made the difference. Picking 'water makes anything grow' ignores the pebble plate.
7. (mcq) What does a virus need to make copies of itself?  
   Options: Sunlight / A living cell / A crystal / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** A living cell. *Why:* A virus can only make copies inside a living cell, using the cell's parts and energy. Alone, it does nothing.
8. (mcq) Do all scientists agree about whether viruses are alive?  
   Options: Yes, they are alive / Yes, they are not alive / No, scientists still argue / Nobody has ever asked  
   **Answer:** No, scientists still argue. *Why:* Scientists still disagree. Viruses make copies and evolve, but do nothing outside a cell. Both 'yes' answers pretend the question is settled.
9. (mcq) A robot senses a wall and turns away. Why is it still not alive?  
   Options: It has no cells, does not grow or take in food, and cannot make young / It is not green / It is too slow / It has wheels  
   **Answer:** It has no cells, does not grow or take in food, and cannot make young. *Why:* A robot follows instructions people wrote. Sensing and moving are only two of the signs. Picking 'not green' confuses being a plant with being alive.
10. (mcq) In the balloon test, why did one bottle have warm sugar water but no yeast?  
   Options: To make it fair, as a comparison, to show sugar water alone makes no gas / Because we ran out of yeast / To grow crystals / To feed the balloon  
   **Answer:** To make it fair, as a comparison, to show sugar water alone makes no gas. *Why:* The no-yeast bottle is the comparison. It shows that the gas came from the yeast, not from the sugar water.
11. (mcq) Who decides exactly where the line between alive and not alive goes?  
   Options: Nature has one sharp line that everyone can see / People do, with definitions, and they still argue about the edge / Only robots / Nobody, because nothing is alive  
   **Answer:** People do, with definitions, and they still argue about the edge. *Why:* Nature gives us real differences, like cells and instructions. But the exact edge, where viruses and dormant things sit, is drawn by people, and scientists still argue about it.
12. (mcq) Do scientists know exactly how the first living thing began?  
   Options: Yes, it is fully known / No. They have ideas and experiments, but nobody knows for sure / Life has always been here / It came from a crystal, everyone agrees  
   **Answer:** No. They have ideas and experiments, but nobody knows for sure. *Why:* Nobody knows yet. Miller and Urey made some building blocks of life from gases in 1952, and there are ideas like the RNA idea, but the question is still open.

## Applications
- Farmers and seed banks store seeds dry and cool, so they stay dormant and alive for years.
- Bakers use dried yeast that wakes up in warm water and makes bread dough rise.
- Doctors and scientists make vaccines by studying how viruses get into cells.
- Scientists who study water bears hope to learn how to store medicines and vaccines dry, without a fridge.
- NASA and ISRO design robots and tests to search for life on Mars and other worlds.
- Food is kept in the fridge because cold slows down the bacteria and fungi that spoil it.
- Dried fruits, papad and pickles last long because, without water, germs cannot grow well.
- Fire fighters use the 'fire needs fuel, air and heat' idea. Take away one, and the fire dies.
- Viruses that attack only bacteria, called phages, are being tested as medicines against infections.
- Engineers build robots that copy living things, like walking, grasping or finding their way.

## Spotted around us
- A jar of rajma, chana or moong in your kitchen: hundreds of dormant baby plants.
- Moong sprouts at a sabzi stall: seeds that woke up.
- Green moss on an old wall after the monsoon. Water bears may be living in it.

- A packet of dried yeast or a bakery's rising dough.
- A sneeze: millions of cold viruses made by your own nose cells, flying out.
- A robot vacuum cleaner or a talking toy: moving and sensing, but not alive.

## Open questions
- **Are viruses alive?** It depends on how you define life. Viruses do some things living things do but not others, and scientists have not agreed on a definition.
- **How did the first living thing come from non-living chemicals?** It happened about 4 billion years ago and left no clear traces. Experiments have made some building blocks, but no one has made life from scratch.
- **Is a dried water bear alive while it is dry, or is it in a third state?** Its life processes are so slow they are very hard to measure. Scientists argue about whether anything at all is happening inside.
- **Is there life on other planets or moons, like Mars, Europa or Enceladus?** We have only one example of life, on Earth. Missions are searching, but nothing has been found for sure yet.
- **Could a machine or a computer program ever be alive?** Some programs copy themselves and change, and some machines can repair themselves. Whether that counts as life depends on whether life must be made of cells and chemistry.
- **How long can a seed stay dormant and still wake?** Seeds of about 1,300 years old have grown. Nobody knows the true limit, and different seeds are very different.
- **Do plants feel anything?** Plants respond and send electrical signals, as J.C. Bose showed. But nobody yet knows what plants feel, or how to test it.

## Connected maths
- In the sleeping seeds experiment, 6 moong seeds went on wet cotton. By day 5, 5 of them had grown a root. What fraction of the seeds woke up? **5 out of 6, which is five sixths.**. There were 6 seeds in all. 5 of them grew a root. So 5 out of 6 woke up. We write this as the fraction five sixths. Only 1 seed out of 6 did not wake. That one may have died, or may need more time. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- Look at the chart of the longest moong root. On day 3 it was 12 mm long. On day 5 it was 40 mm long. How much did it grow in those two days? **28 mm.**. On day 5 the root was 40 mm. On day 3 it was 12 mm. 40 take away 12 is 28. So the root grew 28 mm in two days. That is about 14 mm each day. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In the balloon test, the string round the warm yeast balloon measured 30 cm. The string round the no-yeast balloon measured 10 cm. How many times bigger round was the yeast balloon? **3 times.**. The yeast balloon was 30 cm round. The no-yeast balloon was 10 cm round. 30 divided by 10 is 3. So the yeast balloon was 3 times as big round. You can check: 10 + 10 + 10 = 30. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the candle trial, you tested the flame against 9 signs of life. It showed 5 of them: moving, using fuel, using air, growing and making waste. How many signs did it not show? **4 signs.**. There were 9 signs in all. The flame showed 5. 9 take away 5 is 4. The 4 it did not show included being made of cells and passing on instructions. Those are the ones that matter most. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- In the crystal garden, the biggest crystal was 2 mm on day 3, 4 mm on day 5 and 6 mm on day 7. If it keeps growing the same way, how big will it be on day 9? **8 mm.**. Every two days, the crystal grew 2 mm: 2, then 4, then 6. The pattern adds 2 each time. So on day 9 it will be 6 + 2 = 8 mm. In real life the crystal will stop when the water dries up or the salt runs out. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
