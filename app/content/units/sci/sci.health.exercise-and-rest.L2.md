# Exercise and rest: why moving makes you breathe hard, and why your body needs sleep
`sci.health.exercise-and-rest.L2`

**Summary.** Dance fast for one minute, and you breathe much faster than when you sit still. In this unit you count your breaths before and after dancing, and you time how long your breathing takes to slow down again. Working muscles use up oxygen from the air, so your body breathes in more. Exercise makes your muscles, heart and bones stronger. Rest and sleep let your body recover and grow, and a child of 7 needs about 9 to 12 hours of sleep. Good food and keeping clean are the other two ways to stay healthy.

**Intro.** *Sit very still and put one hand on your tummy. Feel it go out and in. That is you breathing. What do you think will happen to your breathing if you dance as fast as you can for one minute?* You breathe all day and all night, even when you are not thinking about it. Each breath brings air into your lungs, and part of that air is oxygen. Your muscles use oxygen when they work. In this unit you will count your breaths to find out what dancing does to them.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Exercise**: Moving your body so that your muscles work hard. Running, skipping, dancing, swimming and climbing stairs are all exercise. *e.g.* Skipping with a rope on the terrace is exercise. Watching a cartoon is not.
- **Muscle**: A part of your body that can get shorter and pull. Muscles move your bones. Your heart is a muscle too. *e.g.* Bend your arm and feel the top of it go hard. That is a muscle pulling.
- **Breathe**: To take air into your body and let it out again. You breathe in through your nose or mouth, and the air goes down into your lungs. *e.g.* When you blow out a candle, you are breathing out hard.
- **Lungs**: Two soft parts inside your chest that fill with air when you breathe in. They pass oxygen from the air into your blood. *e.g.* Put your hands on your ribs and breathe in deeply. Your chest gets bigger as your lungs fill.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air that you cannot see. Every part of your body needs oxygen to work. Plants put oxygen into the air. *e.g.* A potted tulsi plant on the window sill gives out oxygen in the daytime.
- **Heartbeat**: One squeeze of your heart. Each squeeze pushes blood around your body. Your heart beats faster when you exercise. *e.g.* After running up the stairs, you can feel your heart thumping in your chest.
- **Rest**: Stopping hard work so that your body can recover. Sitting quietly, lying down and sleeping are all kinds of rest. *e.g.* After a game of kabaddi, the players sit down and drink water.
- **Sleep**: The long rest your body takes every day, usually at night. Your eyes close and you stop noticing most things around you. Your body and brain are still busy. *e.g.* A child of 7 who goes to bed at 9 at night and wakes at 7 in the morning sleeps for 10 hours.
- **Recover**: To get back to how you were before. After exercise, your breathing and heartbeat recover and slow down again. *e.g.* After the fast dance, your breathing took a few minutes to recover.

## Explanations
### Why you breathe faster when you move · *defined*
**Definition:** When your muscles work hard, they use more oxygen. So your body breathes faster and deeper to bring in more air.

Every muscle needs oxygen to work. When you sit still, your muscles do little work, so slow, small breaths bring in enough air. When you dance or run, your leg and arm muscles work hard. They use oxygen much faster. Your body notices this, and you breathe faster and take bigger breaths. Your heart beats faster too, so your blood carries the oxygen to the muscles more quickly. When you stop, your muscles need less oxygen, and your breathing slowly goes back to normal.
- e.g. After running up three floors of stairs, you puff and pant.
- e.g. Sitting and reading a book, you hardly notice your breathing.
- e.g. At the end of a race, a runner breathes hard for a while.
- e.g. Fast dancing makes you breathe harder than slow stretching.
### Getting back to normal after exercise · *defined*
**Definition:** Recovery is the time your body takes to get back to its resting breathing and heartbeat after exercise.

Your breathing does not slow down the moment you stop. It comes down a little each minute. For a few minutes after hard exercise, your body keeps taking in extra oxygen. It uses this oxygen to put the muscles back how they were, so they are ready to work again. A person who exercises often usually recovers faster, because their heart and lungs get used to the work.
- e.g. After a fast dance, many children need a few minutes to get back to their resting breath count.
- e.g. A grown-up who walks every day may recover faster after climbing stairs than one who never walks.
- e.g. After a long race, a runner keeps breathing hard for several minutes.
### What exercise does to your body · *defined*
**Definition:** Exercise is moving your body so that your muscles work hard. Regular exercise makes muscles, the heart and bones stronger.

When you use a muscle again and again, over weeks it gets stronger. Your heart is a muscle, so running and dancing make it stronger too. Jumping, skipping and climbing make your bones stronger. Exercise also helps you sleep well and feel cheerful. Doctors say children should be active for about an hour every day, all added up. The World Health Organization gives this advice. Play counts: chasing games, hopscotch, dancing and cycling are all exercise.
- e.g. Hopscotch drawn with chalk on the floor of a flat's parking area.
- e.g. Climbing the stairs instead of taking the lift.
- e.g. Dancing to a song for ten minutes before dinner.
- e.g. Swimming in a pool or a tank, with a grown-up.
### Where muscles get their energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Muscles get their energy from food. They use oxygen to release the energy stored in food.

The rice, dal, ragi, fruit and vegetables you eat are broken down in your body. Your blood carries the food and the oxygen to every muscle. Inside the muscle, the oxygen helps to release the energy in the food. Some of it is kept in a quick store, ready to use. In the 1920s, a scientist from Andhra Pradesh, Yellapragada Subbarow, worked with Cyrus Fiske in America. They found one of the substances a muscle uses as a quick store of energy. For a long time, Subbarow was not given the credit he earned.
- e.g. A child who skips breakfast may feel tired and slow in a morning race.
- e.g. Idli and sambar, or poha with peanuts, give energy for a busy morning.
- e.g. Your body also turns some of the food's energy into heat, which is why you get warm when you run.
### What your body does while you sleep · *defined*
**Definition:** Sleep is a long daily rest. While you sleep, your body grows and repairs itself, and your brain sorts out what you learned.

When you fall asleep, your muscles relax and your breathing slows down. But your body is busy. In deep sleep, your body sends out most of the growth signal that helps children grow. It mends small injuries. Your brain goes over what you did and learned in the day, and keeps the important parts. You also dream. A child of 6 to 12 needs about 9 to 12 hours of sleep every night. Without enough sleep, it is harder to learn, to stay cheerful and to play well.
- e.g. After a late night at a wedding, a child is grumpy and sleepy the next day.
- e.g. A new baby sleeps for about 14 to 17 hours a day.
- e.g. Many grown-ups need about 7 to 9 hours.
### Do all animals sleep? · *debated*
Every mammal and bird that scientists have studied sleeps. But they sleep in very different ways. In a 2017 study, Nadine Gravett and her team in South Africa tracked two wild elephant matriarchs in Botswana. The elephants slept only about 2 hours a day, mostly standing up. A little brown bat sleeps about 20 hours a day. A dolphin sleeps with half of the brain at a time, so she can keep swimming and come up to breathe. In 2017, scientists in America found that upside-down jellyfish have a sleep-like rest at night. These jellyfish have no brain at all.
- e.g. A house cat sleeps about 12 to 15 hours a day.
- e.g. A crow sleeps at night in a tree with many other crows.
- e.g. Rajma plants lower their leaves at night and lift them in the morning.
- **Many sleep scientists:** Sleep is found in every animal that has been carefully studied, even ones with no brain. So sleep is very old, and may be needed by every body made of cells.
- **Other scientists:** Real sleep is something brains do. A jellyfish or a plant has a daily rhythm of rest, but we should not call it sleep until we know more.
- **Scientists who study plants:** Plants like the rajma bean have a daily rhythm of moving their leaves. Some call these 'sleep movements'. Whether plants sleep the way we do is still an open question.

## Mostly agreed
- You breathe faster and deeper during hard exercise, because working muscles use more oxygen. *How we know:* People of every age, in every country, show this. Scientists measure it with masks that collect the breath during exercise.
- Regular exercise makes muscles, the heart and bones stronger. *How we know:* Many long studies of children and grown-ups compare people who are active with people who are not. Active people have stronger hearts, muscles and bones.
- Children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night. *How we know:* Sleep experts in many countries studied how much children sleep, and how they learn and feel. Children who slept less than this did worse.
- The body grows and repairs itself during sleep. *How we know:* Blood tests during the night show that most of the growth signal comes out in deep sleep.

## How we found out
**Mary Carskadon, a sleep scientist, working with William Dement at Stanford University, The Stanford summer sleep camp ran from 1976 to 1985. The work on teenagers' body clocks followed in the 1990s..** For a long time, many people thought that children need less sleep as they grow older. Babies sleep most of the day, and grown-ups sleep less. So a teenager who slept late was often called lazy. In 1976, a young scientist called Mary Carskadon helped start a summer sleep camp at Stanford University in America, with the sleep scientist William Dement. Children aged 10 to 12 came to the camp each summer, and came back year after year as they grew up. They played games in the day. At night they slept with small wires taped to their heads, to record their sleep. Every two hours in the day, they lay down to see how fast they fell asleep. Carskadon expected the older children to need less sleep. They did not. The teenagers needed as much sleep as they had at age 10, and they got sleepier in the day. Later, her studies showed that a teenager's body clock shifts later, so they feel sleepy later at night.  
*What people believed before:* That older children need less sleep, so teenagers who sleep late are lazy. The camp showed teenagers need as much sleep as younger children, and their bodies want it later at night.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Exercise is bad for you when you are tired, so you should never move after school.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Very hard exercise when you are worn out or ill can be too much. Rest is needed when you are ill.  
  Against: Gentle play after a long day of sitting often makes children feel more awake and sleep better at night.
- **Sitting still for a long time is as harmful as eating badly.** (Some newspaper headlines; *debated*)  
  For: Studies find that people who sit for many hours a day, and move little, are more often ill.  
  Against: Scientists still argue about how harmful sitting is by itself. Getting up and moving a little every hour seems to help a lot.

## Roots
We have counted our breaths before and after dancing, and learned why the body needs rest and sleep. Now we ask what the puff count tells us about our bodies and the air around us.

**Deep truth.** Sitting still, you took about 10 breaths in half a minute. Right after dancing, you took many more, and bigger ones. Working muscles use oxygen fast, so your body pulled in more air. So the harder you move, the more of the room's air passes through you.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of exercise as something the legs and arms do, and of our body as ending at the skin. The puff count shows that moving the legs changes the breathing, so the air of the room joins in every move.  
*Evidence:* In the puff count test, the count right after a fast dance was much higher than sitting still, often about twice as high. In the three moves test, star jumps raised it far more than slow stretching. Then the count fell back, minute by minute, as the muscles rested.

- *who am i:* You are a body that is always breathing in and out. Part of the air of your room is inside you right now.
- *what is life:* Living things move, rest and sleep in a rhythm. A crow, a cat, a bean plant and you all have busy times and quiet times.
- *what is this world:* The oxygen you breathe was given out by plants. The air you breathe out is used by plants. Plants and animals, including us, share the same air.

Every breath you take in has oxygen that a plant or a tiny sea creature gave out. Your muscles use it to release the energy in your food, and that food was made by plants from sunlight. You breathe out carbon dioxide, and plants take it in to make more food and oxygen. So a fast dance in a small flat moves air between you, a plant on the sill and the trees outside. Your body senses what your muscles need and changes your breathing to match. Some scientists call this an automatic reflex.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.1:** The parts of a person had an argument about who was the most important. Speech left the body for a year, then the eyes, the ears and the mind. Each time, the person went on living. Then breath began to leave. All the others felt themselves being pulled out too. They agreed that breath, prana, was the most important of all. (In the puff count test, your breathing changed by itself to keep your dancing muscles going. Every part of the body depends on breath.)
- **Anguttara Nikaya 6.55, the Sona Sutta:** A monk named Sona tried so hard that his feet bled from walking. The Buddha asked him, 'You used to play the vina. When the strings were too tight, did it sound good?' 'No.' 'And when they were too loose?' 'No.' 'When they were neither too tight nor too loose?' 'Then it sounded good.' The Buddha said effort is the same. (A body needs both: hard work to grow strong, and rest and sleep to recover. Too much of either is like a string tuned badly.)
- Science (settled): During exercise, working muscles use more oxygen and make more carbon dioxide. The body senses the change and makes breathing faster and deeper.
- Science (open): Every animal that scientists have carefully studied rests or sleeps, even upside-down jellyfish, who have no brain. Why sleep is needed is still not fully known.
- Other view, Many physiologists, the scientists who study how bodies work: They say the faster breathing is a reflex. Sensors in the blood vessels and the brain detect more carbon dioxide and send signals to the breathing muscles. They describe it as a chain of signals, that you do not have to decide on.
- Practice, Watch your breath slow down (3 min): Stand up and jump on the spot ten times. Now sit down and close your eyes. Put one hand on your tummy. Feel it going fast. Don't try to slow it. Just watch. Can you feel it getting slower, all by itself? Now think of a plant by the window, or a tree outside. The air you are breathing out is going to the plant.
- Ask: Your jumps made your breathing change. What else in your body changes when you move?
- Ask: If plants give you oxygen and you give them carbon dioxide, who is looking after whom?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When you dance, your body breathes faster without you deciding to. Who, or what, made that choice? (hint: Think about what your body notices when your muscles work. Your body sensed the change and acted on it.)
- *deep:* Why does sleep feel like doing nothing, when your body is so busy? (hint: Think of what grows and gets mended at night.)
- *big:* The oxygen you breathe in was given out by plants. The air you breathe out is used by plants. Where does your body end? (hint: Follow one breath from a plant, into you, and back out again.)
- *big:* An elephant sleeps 2 hours and a bat sleeps 20. Is there a right amount of sleep, or does each body find their own? (hint: Think about how each animal lives: what they eat, and who might hunt them.)

## Story: The Rainy Week in Kochi
*Anju, Tomin and Ammachi are made up. Ammachi is a Malayalam word many children in Kerala use for their grandmother.*

Anju was seven. She lived on the sixth floor of a block of flats in Kochi, with her parents, her little cousin Tomin, and her grandmother, Ammachi.

It was the monsoon. For four days the rain had poured down. The play area at the bottom of the building was a brown pond. Nobody could go down to play.

By Thursday, Anju and Tomin were grumpy. They had watched cartoons. They had coloured. They had argued about the colours. Tomin was lying on the sofa, kicking a cushion.

"My legs feel funny," he said. "Like they want to run away without me."

Ammachi put down her newspaper. "Your legs are asking for work," she said. "Muscles like to be used. Push the chairs back. We are going to dance."

She found a fast old film song on her phone. She was seventy-two, but she clapped and stamped like a girl. Anju and Tomin jumped and spun and swung their arms. Anju's plait flew round her head. Tomin fell over twice and laughed both times.

When the song ended, they flopped onto the floor. Anju's chest was going up and down very fast. Her cheeks were hot.

"Why am I puffing like a train?" Tomin asked.

"Put your hand on your tummy," said Ammachi. "Feel how fast it goes. Your leg muscles were working hard. Working muscles use up oxygen from the air, so your body breathes in more."

"I didn't tell it to," said Anju. "My breathing just did it."

"Your body noticed by itself," said Ammachi. "Now sit still, and let's count." She held up the phone timer. Anju counted Tomin's breaths, and Tomin counted Anju's. Each minute the numbers were smaller. After about four minutes they were back to where they had started.

"It went back down," said Tomin, surprised. "Without me doing anything."

"Resting is not nothing," said Ammachi. "While you sat, your body was getting your muscles ready to work again."

They drank a tumbler of water each. Then Anju had an idea. "Let's make the dance every day, until the rain stops," she said. Ammachi agreed, as long as everyone took a bath after, because they were sweaty.

That night, Tomin fell asleep before his story was finished. Anju lay awake a little longer, listening to the rain on the window. She felt heavy and warm and sleepy.

"Ammachi," she whispered. "Why am I so sleepy tonight?"

"Because you used your body today," said Ammachi softly. "Now it will take its long rest. While you sleep, your body grows a tiny bit, and mends any little bumps. Your brain sorts out what you learned. Tomorrow you will wake up fresh."

"So my body works even when I'm asleep?"

"All night long," said Ammachi. "Your heart keeps beating. Your breathing keeps going, slow and steady. And you need about ten hours of it, because you are still growing."

Anju counted the hours on the clock in her head. Nine o'clock to seven o'clock. Ten hours. She was asleep before she reached the end.

In the morning, the rain was still falling. But Anju jumped out of bed. She felt fresh, and her legs wanted to dance again.

"First breakfast," said Ammachi, putting out puttu and kadala curry. "Muscles need food for energy, too. Then we dance."

By the end of the rainy week, Anju noticed something. On Thursday, the dance had made her puff for four minutes. By the next Wednesday, she was back to normal in three. Ammachi said that was her heart and lungs getting stronger.

On the last day, the sun came out at last. The play area dried. Anju and Tomin raced down six floors of stairs to play. At the bottom, they stopped, panting, and grinned at each other.

"Count!" said Tomin, putting his hand on his tummy.

And they both started to laugh too much to count anything.

**For the parent:** When I danced, my body breathed faster all by itself, to bring in more air for my muscles. When I rested and slept, it got my body ready to move again. Moving and resting work together.

- Ask: Why was Tomin's breathing so fast after the dance?
- Ask: What happened to the breath counts when Anju and Tomin sat still?
- Ask: What did Ammachi say the body does during sleep?
- Ask: Why did Anju recover faster at the end of the rainy week?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Puff Count: how fast do you breathe after dancing, and how long until you calm down? (20 min)
- **question:** How many breaths do you take in half a minute sitting still, and how many after a fast dance? How many minutes does your breathing take to get back to normal?
- **predict:** Sit still and feel your breathing. After one minute of fast dancing, will you take more breaths, fewer, or the same? How many minutes will it take to calm down? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every count lasts exactly half a minute, timed on a phone or a clock with a seconds hand. The same person counts each time, in the same way. You sit in the same place, in the same way, for every count. The first count, sitting still, shows your normal breathing, so you can compare.
- **safety:** Dance with clear space around you, away from table corners. Stop if you feel dizzy or have pain in your chest. A child with asthma should keep their inhaler nearby and dance at an easier pace.
- **record:** Write your guesses at the top. Fill in the table: sitting still, right after dancing, and after 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 minutes of rest. Draw a tall or short tower of blocks for each count.
- **results table:** When I counted | My guess | Breaths in half a minute
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to sit still and put one hand on their tummy. 2. Ask the two questions below. 3. Write down both guesses and the reasons.  
  *Parent says:* Feel your tummy go out and in. That is one breath. After a fast dance, will you take more breaths, fewer, or the same? How long will it take to calm down? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put your hand on your tummy. Feel it go out and in. After a fast dance, will you breathe more, or less? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that? (good: More, because when I run I puff.; The same, because breathing just happens.; It will calm down in one minute, because I get better fast.)
  - Note: Don't say if the guess is right. Many children say 'more'. Fewer children have a guess about how long the calm-down takes, so help them pick a number of minutes.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child sit quietly for one minute first. 2. Start the timer for half a minute. 3. Count each time the tummy goes out. 4. Write the number in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Sit still and breathe normally. Don't try to breathe slowly or fast. I will count each time your tummy goes out.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Breathe the way you always do. A grown-up counts your breaths.
  - Note: Count one breath each time the tummy or chest goes out. Many children of 7 take about 9 to 13 breaths in half a minute when sitting still. If your child changes their breathing because they know you are watching, chat to them for a minute and count again. A child can also count their own breaths with a hand on the tummy, while you watch the timer.
- **s3 (2 min) · grown-up needed**

### e2 · Three Moves: which kind of exercise makes you breathe hardest? (20 min)
- **question:** Which makes you breathe faster: slow stretching, walking on the spot, or star jumps?
- **predict:** Look at the three moves. Which one will make you breathe the fastest, and which the slowest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Each move lasts exactly one minute. You count for half a minute straight after each move, sitting in the same place. You rest between moves until your breathing is back to your sitting count, so each move starts from the same calm breathing.
- **safety:** Make sure there is clear space around you for star jumps. Stop if you feel dizzy or have pain in your chest. A child with asthma should keep their inhaler nearby and go at an easier pace.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw three towers of blocks, one for each move, and colour the tallest one.
- **results table:** Move | My guess: fast, middle or slow breathing? | Breaths in half a minute after the move
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the three moves. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Count their breaths sitting still for half a minute, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Which move will make you breathe the fastest? Which the slowest? Why do you think so? Now sit still, and I will count your normal breathing.  
  *Child mode:* Which move will make you puff most? Why? Now sit still while a grown-up counts.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Star jumps, because I move my whole body fast.; Stretching, because it pulls my muscles hard.)
  - Note: Some children guess that stretching will be hardest, because it can feel hard. That is a good guess to test.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start the timer. 2. Your child reaches up high, then bends to touch their knees, slowly, again and again for one minute. 3. They sit, and you count for half a minute. 4. Write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Reach up high, slowly. Now bend down to your knees, slowly. Keep going until I say stop.  
  *Child mode:* Reach up high, slowly. Bend down to your knees. Keep going for one minute.
  - Note: The count after slow stretching is usually close to the sitting count.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count half a minute of sitting breaths. If the number is still higher than the first sitting count, rest one more minute and count again. 2. Your child walks on the spot, lifting the knees, for one minute. 3. They sit, you count for half a minute, and write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Walk on the spot and lift your knees. Not running, just walking.  
  *Child mode:* Walk on the spot for one minute. Lift your knees.
  - Note: The count is usually a little higher than after stretching.
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count half a minute of sitting breaths again. Wait until it is back near the first sitting count. 2. Your child does star jumps for one minute, as fast as they can. 3. They sit, you count for half a minute, and write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Jump and spread your arms and legs out wide. Then jump them back in. As fast as you can!  
  *Child mode:* Do star jumps for one minute. Arms and legs out, then in!
  - Note: If neighbours live below, do the jumps on a folded blanket or on your toes. The count is usually the highest after star jumps.
  - Check: After which move did you breathe the fastest? → **Star jumps**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Help your child draw three towers. 2. Ask the three questions below.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think star jumps and stretching were so different?  
  *Child mode:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think it happened? (good: Star jumps make my legs and arms work really fast, so they need more air.; Stretching is slow, so my muscles don't need as much oxygen.)
  - Think: Which games you play make you breathe like star jumps? Which are more like stretching?

### Move and Rest (core) (core, 10 questions)

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What happens to your breathing when you dance fast?  
   Options: It gets slower / It gets faster and deeper / It stops for a moment  
   **Answer:** It gets faster and deeper. *Why:* Dancing makes your muscles work hard, so you breathe faster and take bigger breaths.
2. (mcq) Why do you breathe faster when you exercise?  
   Options: Your muscles need more oxygen / Your nose gets hot / The air in the room gets thinner  
   **Answer:** Your muscles need more oxygen. *Why:* Working muscles use up oxygen quickly, so your body brings in more air. The air in the room stays the same.
3. (mcq) After a fast dance, how long does breathing usually take to get back to normal?  
   Options: No time at all / A few minutes / A whole day  
   **Answer:** A few minutes. *Why:* Breathing slows down a little each minute. For many children it is back to normal within a few minutes.
4. (mcq) Which of these is exercise?  
   Options: Reading a comic / Skipping with a rope / Sleeping  
   **Answer:** Skipping with a rope. *Why:* Skipping makes your muscles work hard. Reading and sleeping are rest.
5. (mcq) What does regular exercise make stronger?  
   Options: Only your legs / Your muscles, heart and bones / Only your hair  
   **Answer:** Your muscles, heart and bones. *Why:* Exercise strengthens all the muscles you use, your heart, which is a muscle, and your bones.
6. (mcq) How much sleep does a child of 7 need each night?  
   Options: About 5 hours / About 9 to 12 hours / About 20 hours  
   **Answer:** About 9 to 12 hours. *Why:* Children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours. 20 hours is how long a little brown bat sleeps.
7. (mcq) What does your body do while you sleep?  
   Options: Nothing at all / It grows and mends itself / It stops breathing  
   **Answer:** It grows and mends itself. *Why:* Your body grows and repairs itself in sleep, and your brain sorts what you learned. Breathing goes on all night.
8. (mcq) Which is NOT one of the four ways to keep healthy?  
   Options: Keeping clean / Eating good food / Staying up very late  
   **Answer:** Staying up very late. *Why:* The four ways are exercise, rest and sleep, good food, and keeping clean. Staying up late takes away sleep.
9. (mcq) Why do we wash after a sweaty game?  
   Options: To wash off sweat, dirt and germs / To make our muscles bigger / To make us breathe faster  
   **Answer:** To wash off sweat, dirt and germs. *Why:* Washing takes away sweat, dirt and germs, so the skin stays healthy.
10. (mcq) In the puff count test, why did you count your breaths sitting still first?  
   Options: To know your normal breathing, so you could compare / To get tired before dancing / Because it was the easiest count  
   **Answer:** To know your normal breathing, so you could compare. *Why:* The first count shows your normal breathing. Without it, you could not tell how much the dance changed it.
11. (mcq) Where do your muscles get their energy from?  
   Options: From the food you eat / From your hair / From the TV  
   **Answer:** From the food you eat. *Why:* The energy is in food. Oxygen helps your muscles release it.

## Applications
- Doctors count a child's breaths in one minute to check if they are ill. Fast breathing at rest can be a sign of a chest infection.
- Sports coaches time how fast a player's breathing and heartbeat recover, to see if they are getting fitter.
- Schools plan games periods and a lunch break, so children move and rest in the same day.
- Hospitals keep wards dim and quiet at night, so patients can sleep and heal.

## Spotted around us
- Children puffing and laughing after a game of kho-kho or lagori in a school ground.
- A street dog panting in the shade on a hot afternoon. Dogs pant to cool down, and also after running.
- Morning walkers at a beach or a park, like the Marina in Chennai, walking fast and swinging their arms.
- A whole family asleep on mats on a terrace on a hot summer night, in many towns.

## Open questions
- **Why exactly do humans and other animals need to sleep?** Scientists know many things that happen during sleep: growth, repair, sorting memories, cleaning the brain. But nobody knows for sure which one, or which mix, is the main reason we must sleep.
- **Why do we dream?** Some scientists think dreams help the brain sort memories or practise for the day. Others think dreams are a side effect of the brain being busy. Nobody knows yet.
- **Do plants sleep?** Many plants move their leaves on a daily rhythm, and some rest their growth at night. Whether this is like sleep in animals is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- Fathima, a girl in Class 2, took 11 breaths in half a minute sitting still. After dancing, she took 22. Is that more or less than twice as many? **Exactly twice as many.**. Double 11 is 11 and 11, which is 22. So after dancing Fathima took exactly twice as many breaths. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Here are one child's counts in the puff count test: 11 sitting still, 22 after dancing, then 17, 14, 12 and 11 after each minute of rest. After how many minutes was the count back to 11? **After 4 minutes.**. Count along the rest counts: 17 after 1 minute, 14 after 2, 12 after 3, and 11 after 4. 11 is the sitting-still count. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In the three moves test, Sneha, a girl in Class 2, took 12 breaths after stretching, 15 after walking and 24 after star jumps. That is ten 2s. So the bat sleeps 10 times as long as the elephant. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)

How many more minutes does she need to reach 60? **15 more minutes.**. 15 and 20 make 35. 35 and 10 make 45. From 45 to 60 is 15 more. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
