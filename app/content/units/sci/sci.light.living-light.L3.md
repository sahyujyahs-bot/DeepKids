# Living light: how fireflies and other living things make their own light
`sci.light.living-light.L3`

**Summary.** A firefly makes their own light with chemistry inside their body, to find a partner in the dark. Light made by a living thing is called bioluminescence. Some mushrooms in Meghalaya and the Western Ghats glow too. In the deep sea, where sunlight never reaches, most animals can make light. Living light makes almost no heat.

**Intro.** *Switch off every light in the room tonight. Wait a little. Now snap a glow stick. It shines, but it has no battery, no wire and no flame. Where is its light coming from?* On summer nights before the monsoon, the trees near Bhandardara in Maharashtra begin to sparkle. Thousands of tiny lights blink on and off. They are not lamps. They are fireflies, small beetles that make light inside their own bodies. Far away, deep in the sea, an anglerfish hangs a glowing lure in front of their mouth, in total darkness. How can a living thing make light? And what does the light do for them?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what living light is: light that a living thing makes themself, using chemistry inside their body.
1. I can name living things that glow, like fireflies, glowing mushrooms, deep-sea fish, jellyfish and sea sparkle, and say where they live.
2. I can explain that a firefly mixes a chemical called luciferin with oxygen to make light, and that this light is cold.
3. I can give four reasons living things glow: to talk, to catch food, to hide and to scare off an attacker.
4. I can run a fair test with glow sticks to find out how warmth changes a glow, and say what I think, what I saw and why.
5. I can tell made light from stored light: a firefly makes their light, but a glow-in-the-dark star only gives back light it soaked up.
6. I can say what scientists agree on, what they still argue about, and how bright lights at night harm fireflies.

## Terms
- **Light source**: Something that makes its own light. *e.g.* The Sun, a candle flame, a bulb and a firefly are all light sources.
- **Living light (bioluminescence)**: Light made by a living thing, using chemistry inside their body. Scientists call it bioluminescence. Say it like this: bye-oh-loo-mi-NESS-ence. *e.g.* A firefly's flash is living light.
- **Chemical reaction**: When two or more substances meet and change into new substances. Some reactions give out heat, and some give out light. *e.g.* Burning a matchstick is a chemical reaction. So is the glow inside a glow stick.
- **Luciferin**: The chemical that reacts with oxygen to give out light inside a glowing living thing. Its name comes from a Latin word meaning light-bringer. Say it like this: loo-SIF-er-in. *e.g.* Inside a firefly's tail, luciferin meets oxygen and gives out light.
- **Luciferase**: A helper chemical made by the glowing living thing. It makes luciferin and oxygen react quickly. Say it like this: loo-SIF-er-ase. *e.g.* A firefly makes luciferase in their tail, so their flash comes on fast.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air. We breathe it in. Living light needs it too. *e.g.* A firefly switches on their light by letting oxygen reach their lantern.
- **Energy**: What makes things happen: moving, warming, growing, shining. Energy is never lost. It only changes from one kind to another. *e.g.* A firefly's food gives them energy. Some of that energy comes out as light.
- **Cold light**: Light that comes with almost no heat. *e.g.* A glow stick and a firefly both shine but stay cool. An old bulb with a wire inside gets hot.
- **Firefly**: A small beetle that makes light in the end of their body. In Tamil they are called minmini poochi, and in Hindi jugnu. Fireflies are not really flies. *e.g.* On summer nights, fireflies blink over fields and trees.
- **Glow-worm**: The young form of a firefly, or a wingless female firefly. Many glow-worms glow steadily. *e.g.* Firefly young live in damp soil and leaf litter, and they glow too.
- **Fungus**: A living thing that is not a plant or an animal. Mushrooms are part of a fungus. Most of the fungus is a web of thin threads hidden in soil or wood. *e.g.* The mushrooms on a rotting log, and the threads inside the log, are one fungus.
- **Glowing mushroom**: A mushroom, or a fungus on rotting wood, that makes their own soft green light all night. *e.g.* A glowing mushroom was found on dead bamboo in Meghalaya in 2018.
- **Deep sea**: The part of the ocean far below the surface. Below about 1,000 metres, no sunlight reaches at all. It is dark all day and all night. *e.g.* Anglerfish and many jellyfish live in the deep sea.
- **Lure**: Something that tempts an animal to come close, so they can be caught. *e.g.* An anglerfish has a glowing lure on a stalk on their head. Small fish swim up to it.
- **Belly lights (counter-illumination)**: Lights on the underside of a sea animal. They match the faint light from above, so the animal's shadow-shape disappears. *e.g.* A hatchetfish glows on their belly, so hunters below cannot see their dark outline.
- **Bacteria**: Very tiny living things, each made of just one cell. Some kinds glow. *e.g.* The lamp on an anglerfish's head is full of glowing bacteria.
- **Plankton**: Tiny living things that drift in the sea. Most are too small to see one by one. *e.g.* Sea sparkle is a kind of plankton that glows when the water moves.
- **Sea sparkle**: A tiny glowing plankton, called Noctiluca by scientists. When waves or feet stir them, they flash blue. *e.g.* On some nights, beaches near Mumbai, Goa and Chennai glow blue at the edge of the waves.
- **Glow stick**: A plastic tube with two liquids inside. When you bend it, they mix and react, and give out light. *e.g.* Glow sticks shine at wedding processions, with no battery.
- **Glow-in-the-dark (stored light)**: A material that soaks up light, then gives it back slowly in the dark. It does not make new light of its own. Scientists call it phosphorescence. *e.g.* Glow-in-the-dark stars on a ceiling fade after a while, unless a light charges them again.
- **Nocturnal**: Awake and busy at night. *e.g.* Fireflies, owls and many moths are nocturnal.
- **Light pollution**: Too much artificial light at night, where it is not needed. *e.g.* A bright street lamp all night can stop fireflies from seeing each other's flashes.
- **Photon**: The tiniest bit of light there can be. Light comes in these tiny packets. *e.g.* Each firefly flash is made of a huge number of photons.

## Explanations
### What living light is · *defined*
**Definition:** Living light, or bioluminescence, is light that a living thing makes themself, using a chemical reaction inside their body.

Most things we see do not make light. The Moon, a leaf and your hand only send back light from the Sun or a lamp. Things that make their own light are called light sources, like the Sun, a flame and a bulb. A few living things are light sources too. They make light with chemistry, the way a glow stick does, with no fire, wire or battery.
- e.g. A firefly makes yellow-green flashes in the end of their body.
- e.g. Some mushrooms glow green all night on rotting wood.
- e.g. Many deep-sea fish, squid and jellyfish make blue light.
### How a firefly makes their light · *defined*
**Definition:** A firefly makes light when a chemical called luciferin reacts with oxygen from the air. A helper chemical called luciferase makes the reaction happen fast.

The light comes from a part at the end of the firefly's body, called the lantern. The lantern's cells hold luciferin and luciferase, ready to use. They also use energy from the firefly's food. Tiny air tubes bring oxygen into the lantern. When the firefly lets oxygen reach these cells, light comes out. When they hold the oxygen back, the light stops. This is how a firefly switches their light on and off.
- e.g. In the 1880s, the French scientist Raphael Dubois made two liquids from a glowing click beetle. Both were dark. When he mixed them, they glowed. He named the two chemicals luciferin and luciferase.
- e.g. Different groups of glowing living things use different luciferins. A firefly's is different from a jellyfish's.
- e.g. Scientists still argue about exactly how a firefly lets oxygen in and holds it back so quickly.
### Living light is cold light · *defined*
**Definition:** Cold light is light made with almost no heat. Living light is cold light.

Most lights we know get hot. A candle flame burns your finger. An old bulb with a thin wire inside turns most of its energy into heat, and only a little into light. A firefly's lantern is part of their own body. If their light came with that much heat, the firefly would burn themself. Their reaction sends a large share of the energy out as light, and very little as heat. So a glowing firefly feels no warmer than the air around them.
- e.g. A glow stick stays cool in your hand, even while it shines.
- e.g. A candle flame gives light, but you feel its heat from above.
- e.g. LED bulbs also make light with much less heat than old bulbs.
### Fireflies talk in flashes · *defined*
**Definition:** Fireflies use flashes as signals, to find a partner of their own kind.

A firefly has a problem: they must find a partner of their own kind, at night. There are about 2,000 kinds of fireflies in the world, and several kinds can live in one field. In the dark, a firefly cannot see who is who. So each kind has its own flash code. In many kinds, a male flies about, flashing his pattern. A female waits on a leaf. If the code is her own kind's, she answers after a set wait. He flies down to her. Some fireflies in Asia and America flash all together, so a whole tree blinks on and off at once.
- e.g. In one American firefly, the male flashes once every few seconds. The female answers about two seconds later.
- e.g. In the mangroves of Malaysia and Thailand, whole trees of fireflies flash together.
- e.g. In 1965, an American scientist, James Lloyd, found a trick. Some female fireflies copy the answer of another kind. When a male of that kind flies down, she eats him!
### Why did fireflies start glowing? · *debated*
All firefly young glow, even before they have wings or a partner to find. So why did glowing begin? Scientists have more than one answer.
- e.g. Firefly young taste bad to many hunters. A glow may say: do not eat me.
- e.g. Some baby fireflies glow when touched, like a sudden warning.
- **Many firefly scientists, after studying firefly family trees:** Glowing began as a warning. Firefly young carry bitter chemicals. Their glow tells toads, mice and birds: I taste terrible. Much later, adult fireflies began to use the same light to talk.
- **Some other scientists:** The light chemistry may first have helped fireflies deal with oxygen inside their bodies, which can harm cells. The glow came along by chance, and was only used for signals later.
- **Scientists today:** The warning idea has the most evidence so far. But nobody was there to watch, and the full story is not known.
### Glowing mushrooms · *defined*
**Definition:** Glowing mushrooms are fungi that make their own soft green light, using luciferin and oxygen, all through the night.

More than 100 kinds of fungi glow. Often it is not just the mushroom but the thin threads of the fungus inside rotting wood. People long ago called glowing wood foxfire. A fungus does not flash like a firefly. They glow steadily, like a night-light, for as long as they are alive and damp. In 2018, scientists found a new glowing mushroom on dead bamboo near Mawlynnong in Meghalaya. Their stem glows. People there had known about them for a long time.
- e.g. More than 2,300 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle wrote that some rotting wood shines in the dark.
- e.g. In 1775, an American submarine called the Turtle used glowing foxfire to light the dials inside, so no flame was needed under water.
- e.g. Glowing mushrooms have also been photographed in the forests of Goa and Kerala, in the monsoon.
### Why do mushrooms glow? · *debated*
A firefly flashes to find a partner. But a mushroom has no eyes and no partner to call. So why do they glow?
- e.g. In a Brazilian forest, scientists put fake glowing mushrooms next to fake dark ones. More insects landed on the glowing ones.
- e.g. Some glowing fungi glow brightest at night and dimmer in the day. Their glow follows a daily clock.
- **Anderson Oliveira, Cassius Stevani and others, studying a Brazilian mushroom in 2015:** The glow brings insects. The insects walk over the mushroom and carry away their spores, the tiny specks that grow into new fungi. So the light helps the fungus spread.
- **Other scientists:** For many fungi, the glow may be a side effect. It might be left over from the way they break down wood, or deal with oxygen. The light may have no job at all.
- **Scientists today:** Both may be true for different fungi. Each kind may glow for their own reason.
### The deep sea: the biggest home of living light · *defined*
**Definition:** In the deep sea, most animals can make their own light.

Sunlight only goes down a few hundred metres into the sea. Below about 1,000 metres, it is completely dark. There is no day and no night there. Yet many animals live there. In 2017, scientists counted animals filmed by underwater robots in the sea off California. About three out of every four could make light. In the deep sea, almost all the light there is comes from living things.
- e.g. A lanternfish has rows of tiny lights along their body, like buttons.
- e.g. Some deep-sea shrimp spit out a cloud of glowing liquid.
- e.g. Many jellyfish glow when something bumps them.
### Four jobs for light in the dark sea · *defined*
**Definition:** Deep-sea animals use light for four main jobs: to catch food, to hide, to scare off attackers, and to talk.

Deep in the sea it is always dark, food is hard to find, and hunters are everywhere. Light helps with each of these problems. Catch: an anglerfish cannot chase food in the dark. They hang a glowing lure in front of their mouth, and small fish swim up to it and are swallowed. Hide: hunters below look up for dark shapes against the faint light from above. A hatchetfish's belly lights match that light, so their shape vanishes. Scare: some jellyfish flash in circles when grabbed. The flashes may call a bigger hunter to eat the attacker. Talk: in the dark, an animal must find their own kind. Some fish and squid have light patterns that only their own kind uses.
- e.g. The Atolla jellyfish spins a ring of blue flashes when they are caught. Scientists call it a burglar alarm.
- e.g. The vampire squid squirts glowing slime and swims off in the dark.
- e.g. In 2012, the scientist Edith Widder built a fake glowing jellyfish called the e-jelly. Its flashes helped film a live giant squid in their deep-sea home, near Japan, for the first time.
### Borrowed light: glowing bacteria · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals do not make their own light. They keep glowing bacteria inside a special pocket in their bodies.

Some bacteria can glow. But one glowing bacterium is far too faint to see. So these bacteria only switch on their light when there are lots of them together. They tell each other they are there by giving off tiny amounts of a chemical. When enough of the chemical builds up, they all glow at once. An anglerfish's lure is a pocket full of such bacteria. The fish gives them a home and food. The bacteria give the fish their lamp.
- e.g. The Hawaiian bobtail squid keeps glowing bacteria in a pocket under their body. Their light hides their shadow on moonlit nights. The scientist Margaret McFall-Ngai has studied this squid and their bacteria for many years.
- e.g. Bonnie Bassler, an American scientist, studies how bacteria count each other with chemicals.
- e.g. Old fish in a market sometimes glow faintly in the dark, because of glowing bacteria.
### Sea sparkle on Indian beaches · *defined*
**Definition:** Sea sparkle is a tiny glowing plankton. They flash blue when the water around them is stirred.

Each sea sparkle is a single cell, just big enough to see as a dot. Small sea animals try to eat them. When something stirs the water around them, they flash blue. Scientists think the sudden flash startles the animals that try to eat them. On most nights there are too few to notice. But sometimes they grow in huge numbers near the coast. Then every breaking wave glows blue, and footprints in wet sand sparkle. Huge numbers of sea sparkle can be a sign that the sea is changing, for example from too much sewage and farm waste washing in.
- e.g. Beaches near Mumbai, Goa, Mangaluru, Kerala and Chennai have glowed blue on some nights.
- e.g. On the islands of Lakshadweep and the Andamans, swimmers have seen their arms glow in the water.
- e.g. Sea sparkle flashes only when disturbed, so the brightest glow is where waves crash.
### Living light began many times · *idea*
Fireflies, mushrooms, jellyfish, fish and bacteria are not close relatives, and they use different kinds of luciferin. So scientists think living light began separately, again and again. In 2021, two scientists, Emily Lau and Todd Oakley, counted at least 94 separate starts. One study in 2024 suggested that soft corals may have glowed more than 500 million years ago. These are careful best guesses, made by comparing family trees of living things. They may change as we learn more.
- e.g. Fireflies and click beetles are cousins, and both glow. Even so, a study in 2018 found that they probably began glowing separately.
- e.g. A glowing mushroom and a glowing fish use completely different chemicals.
- e.g. No glowing flowering plant has ever been found in nature.
### Down to the atoms: chemistry becomes light · *defined*
**Definition:** When luciferin reacts with oxygen, a new molecule is made with extra energy. It lets that energy go as a tiny packet of light, a photon.

Everything is made of atoms. Atoms join into groups called molecules. Luciferin is a molecule. When it meets oxygen, its atoms rearrange. For a moment, the new molecule holds extra energy. Scientists say it is 'excited'. It settles down by giving out a photon, the tiniest bit of light. A firefly flash is made of a huge number of photons. So energy from a firefly's food comes out as the energy of light. Energy is never made or destroyed. It only changes form.
- e.g. A glow stick works the same way, with different molecules.
- e.g. Fireworks also make light when atoms get extra energy and let it go, but with fire and great heat.
- e.g. The energy in a firefly's food first came from sunlight, caught by plants.
### Living light helps doctors and scientists · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists use the chemicals of living light as tools, to see what is happening inside cells.

In 1961, a Japanese scientist, Osamu Shimomura, studied a glowing jellyfish. He collected thousands of them by hand. From them he found a glowing protein, and another that glows green under blue light. That green protein is now used in labs all over the world. Scientists attach it to parts of a cell, and those parts light up. Shimomura shared a Nobel Prize for this in 2008. Firefly chemicals are also used in hospitals, to check if a surface still has germs on it.
- e.g. The green jellyfish protein lets scientists watch nerve cells grow in a living worm.
- e.g. A swab test with firefly chemicals glows if living cells, like germs, are left on a kitchen counter.
- e.g. In 2020, scientists put genes from a glowing mushroom into a tobacco plant. The plant glowed faintly all their life.
### Do people glow? · *idea*
Your body makes a very, very faint light. It is about a thousand times too weak for our eyes to see. In 2009, Japanese scientists used a super-sensitive camera in total darkness. They found that faces glowed least in the morning and most in the late afternoon. This light comes from tiny chemical reactions with oxygen in our skin. It is real, but it is not used for anything, as far as we know. Scientists are still finding out what it can tell us.
- e.g. Most living things give out this ultra-weak light.
- e.g. Nobody can see it. Only special cameras can catch it.

## Mostly agreed
- Many living things make their own light, using a chemical reaction. The light comes when a luciferin reacts with oxygen. *How we know:* In the 1880s, Raphael Dubois made a glowing beetle's light in a test tube by mixing two juices from them. Scientists have since found the luciferin chemicals of fireflies, jellyfish, mushrooms and bacteria.
- Living light is cold light. It makes almost no heat. *How we know:* A glowing glow stick pressed on your wrist feels no warmer than a whole one. Measurements show that a large share of the reaction's energy leaves as light, not heat.
- Fireflies flash to find partners of their own kind. *How we know:* Scientists flash small lights at fireflies in fields. When the light copies a female's answer, males fly down to it.
- In the deep sea, most animals can make light. *How we know:* Underwater robots have filmed hundreds of thousands of deep-sea animals. In one count off California, about three in four could glow.
- Bright lights at night harm fireflies. *How we know:* In experiments, fireflies near bright lights flash less and find fewer partners. In 2020, firefly experts around the world named light pollution as one of the biggest threats to fireflies, after the loss of their homes.

## How we found out
**Robert Boyle, then Raphael Dubois, the 1660s and the 1880s.** For thousands of years, people saw rotting wood and old fish glowing in the dark. Many thought the glow was a slow, cool kind of fire, or sunlight the wood had stored. In the 1660s, the English scientist Robert Boyle tested it. He put glowing wood inside a glass jar and pumped the air out. The glow faded away. When he let the air back in, the glow came back. So the light needed air, as a flame does. But the wood stayed cold, and nothing was burning. Later, scientists found that the glow came from fungi living in the wood. In the 1880s, the French scientist Raphael Dubois took the glowing parts of a click beetle. He soaked some in cold water. That liquid glowed for a while, then went dark. He soaked others in very hot water, which stopped the glow at once. Then he mixed the two dark liquids, and the light came back. The heat had destroyed a helper chemical but left the fuel unharmed. He named the fuel luciferin and the helper luciferase. Living light was chemistry.  
*What people believed before:* Glowing wood was burning with a slow fire, or giving back stored sunlight. Boyle showed the glow needed air but gave no heat, and Dubois showed it came from two chemicals that make light together with oxygen.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only the Sun, fire and electric lamps can make light.** (A common way of thinking, especially in cities where few people see fireflies.; *rejected*)  
  For: Nearly all the light we use every day comes from the Sun, flames or bulbs.  
  Against: Fireflies, mushrooms, jellyfish, fish and even bacteria make their own light with chemistry. In the deep sea, living light is the main kind of light there is.
- **Glowing rotten wood and glowing seas are caused by spirits or by stored sunlight.** (Many people in many lands, for thousands of years. Some scientists in the 1600s thought the glow was a kind of slow fire, or stored light.; *rejected*)  
  For: The glow appears in damp, dark places with no flame and no Sun. It is mysterious.  
  Against: The glow comes from living fungi and plankton, using luciferin and oxygen. Take away the oxygen, or kill the fungus, and the glow stops.
- **Mushrooms glow to bring insects, which spread their spores.** (Anderson Oliveira, Cassius Stevani and others, after tests with a Brazilian mushroom in 2015.; *debated*)  
  For: More insects landed on fake mushrooms with green lights than on dark ones.  
  Against: This was tested on one kind. Many other glowing fungi glow inside wood, where spores are not made. For them, the glow may be a side effect.
- **Fireflies are disappearing everywhere.** (Many people who remember more fireflies in their childhood, and some news reports.; *debated*)  
  For: Fireflies lose their homes when wetlands and fields are built over. Bright lights and pesticides harm them too. Many grandparents say they saw more.  
  Against: For most places, including India, there are very few long, careful counts. Some kinds are surely in trouble. For others, we do not know.
- **Living light is a rare oddity in nature.** (Scientists long ago, who studied mostly animals on land.; *rejected*)  
  For: On land, only a few animals glow, like fireflies and some worms.  
  Against: Scientists now think living light began separately at least 94 times. In the open sea and deep sea, most animals can glow. Some scientists, such as Edith Widder, think light may be the most common way animals signal to each other on Earth.

## Roots
We have been learning about living light: fireflies, mushrooms and sea animals that make their own glow. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The glow stick in warm water shone brightest. A stick kept cold overnight usually still glows next morning. All of them began with the same chemicals, and warmth only made them react faster. So how bright a light is shows how fast it is using up its store of energy.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a brighter light is a stronger light, with more inside it. The glow stick test shows that brightness is about speed: the same store, spent quickly or slowly.  
*Evidence:* In the glow stick test, the warm stick shone brightest. The stick kept cold in the fridge shone dimmer, but usually glowed longest. Fireflies also flash more slowly on cool nights.

- *who am i:* Your body runs on chemistry too. It even gives out a faint light, far too weak for your eyes to see.
- *what is life:* A firefly turns energy from their food into flashes, and uses them to find a partner in the dark.
- *what is this world:* Sunlight never reaches the deep sea. Almost all the light down there is made by living things.

Plants catch sunlight and store its energy as food. Firefly young eat snails and worms, which fed on plants. Some of that stored energy later leaves the firefly as a flash. The glow stick test showed that a store of energy can come out fast and bright, or slow and dim. At every step the energy changes form, but it is never destroyed.

- **Katha Upanishad 2.2.15 (also Mundaka Upanishad 2.2.10):** There the Sun does not shine, nor the Moon and stars, nor lightning, let alone this fire. When That shines, everything shines after it. By its light, all this is lit. (The verse says every light we see shines by a light behind it. In this unit, a firefly's flash is energy that came from the Sun, through plants and food. Science can follow that chain back to the Sun. The verse asks what lights even the Sun.)
- **The Buddha, speaking to Ananda near the end of his life, in the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (Digha Nikaya 16):** Be a lamp for yourselves. Be a refuge for yourselves. The Pali word 'dipa' can mean both lamp and island, and scholars translate it both ways. (A firefly does not borrow their light from the Sun or the Moon. They make it in their own body. The Buddha asked people to find their way by their own understanding, not by leaning on others.)
- Science (settled): Living light comes from a chemical reaction of luciferin with oxygen. The energy in the food that powers it first came from sunlight caught by plants.
- Science (idea): Living light began separately at least 94 times in the history of life. Scientists worked this out by comparing family trees of living things.
- Other view, Many biologists, and the old Indian Charvaka school, which taught that only what we can sense is real: A firefly's glow is chemistry that helped fireflies survive and have young. It needs no deeper meaning. There is no One behind it, only molecules and evolution. That is an honest and careful view.
- Practice, Sit with a small light (3 min): Let's sit in the dark with one glow stick between us. Breathe in slowly while I count to four. Breathe out while I count to four. Look softly at the glow. Now close your eyes. Can you still see a little light behind your eyelids? Sit quietly for a few breaths. Open your eyes. Is the glow stick as bright as when we started?
- Ask: The energy in your breakfast came from the Sun. Is some of the Sun inside you now?
- Ask: A firefly flashes for a moment, then rests. A mushroom glows dimly all night. Which way of shining would you choose, and why?
- Ask: The mushrooms glowed all along, but nobody saw them in daylight. What might be shining around us that we do not notice?

## Story: The Lamps That Stay
*Glowing bamboo mushrooms really grow near Mawlynnong in Meghalaya. Scientists found them in 2018, and people there had long known them. Iba, Phrang, their grandmother and this night are made up.*

The rain stopped just as the sun went down, and the whole forest dripped. Iba was eleven. Her brother Phrang was six. They had spent the afternoon at their aunt's house in the next village, eating red rice and smoky dal, and listening to their cousins argue. Now they were walking home along the bamboo path, the way they had walked a hundred times in daylight.

"Stay by the stream," their mother had said that morning. "The stream knows the way home, even when you don't."

Iba carried the torch. It was an old one, with a crack in the glass. Its beam was already yellow and tired. Halfway home, under the tallest bamboo, it flickered twice and went out.

The dark came down all at once. It was not like the dark of their room at night, with a little grey at the window. Clouds covered the sky. There was no Moon and not a single star. Iba held up her hand in front of her face. She could not see it at all.

Phrang found her sleeve and held on with both fists. "Iba," he whispered. "I can't see anything."

"Neither can I," said Iba, because she did not believe in lying to him, even now.

They stood still. The stream chattered somewhere to their left. Water dripped from leaves. A frog said one word and stopped.

Then, ahead of them, a tiny light blinked on. It blinked off. Another blinked on, higher up, and then another, low among the ferns. Soon there were dozens, golden-green, flashing and gone, flashing and gone.

"Jugnu!" said Phrang. He was not whispering any more. "Iba, look! They came to show us the way!"

He let go of her sleeve and ran towards them.

"Phrang, wait!" Iba ran after him. She caught his shirt and held it. They had left the path. She could feel it. The ground under her feet was soft and full of roots. The fireflies were all around them now, flashing everywhere and nowhere. When Iba stepped towards one, they went dark, and another lit up behind her. She turned, and turned again. She could no longer hear the stream.

Her heart was banging. She made herself breathe slowly, the way her grandmother breathed before she began a long story.

Her grandmother liked fireflies. She said they were the most talkative creatures in the hills. "All night they say, here I am, here I am, where are you?" she would say. "But they are not talking to us, child. They are talking to each other. A firefly goes wherever they like."

Iba looked at the flashing lights again. They were not leading anywhere. They were calling to each other, in a language that had nothing to do with two children lost in a wet forest. Following them was like following someone else's conversation.

"They're not for us," she said quietly. "Phrang, sit down with me for a moment. Close your eyes and count to twenty. Then open them, and look low. Look at the ground, not the air."

Phrang counted, very fast. Iba counted slowly. When she opened her eyes, the forest was just as black. But now she saw something she had missed. Down near the ground, a little way off, there was a faint smudge of green. It did not blink. It did not move. It just glowed, soft as a breath on cold glass.

She crept towards it on her hands and knees, with Phrang holding her ankle. It was a fallen bamboo, old and rotting, half sunk in wet leaves. Along its side grew tiny mushrooms, no bigger than her fingernail. Their thin stems were shining green. She held her hand close. She felt no warmth at all. The light was cool, like water.

"Who lit them?" whispered Phrang.

"Nobody," said Iba. "There's no Moon to shine on them. There's no torch. They're making the light themselves." Saying it out loud made the hairs on her arms stand up.

And then she understood something else. Bamboo grows thickest by water. When old bamboo falls, it falls near where it grew. And the glowing mushrooms grow only on fallen bamboo that is old and damp. They do not fly about. They stay where they are, all night long.

She looked past the first glow. There, a few steps further on, was another faint green smudge. And past that, another, and another, like a dotted line drawn in the dark.

"Hold my hand," she said. "We'll walk from one green light to the next. Only the ones that stay still."

They went slowly. The fireflies flashed all around their heads, busy with their own lives. Iba ignored them. She kept her eyes low, on the steady glows. At the fourth one, she heard it: the stream, chattering on her left again. At the seventh, her feet found the hard, smooth path.

Far ahead, a lantern was swinging. It was their mother, calling their names.

Phrang ran to her. Iba stopped and looked back once. Behind her, the line of small green lamps lay along the stream, quiet and patient. The fireflies were still flashing above them, still saying here I am, where are you, to someone who was not her.

The next morning, she walked back alone in the sunshine to find the mushrooms. She found the bamboo easily. The mushrooms were still there. But in daylight they were just tiny brown things, so plain that nobody would look twice. Their light had not gone anywhere. It was only that the Sun was so much brighter.

Iba crouched beside them for a long time. Then she said thank you, softly, which felt silly and right at the same time.

**For the parent:** For the parent: there are two kinds of living light in this story, and they do different jobs. The fireflies flash to talk to each other. Their lights move, switch on and off, and lead nowhere for a human. The mushrooms glow steadily all night, and they stay where they grow, on old bamboo by the stream. Iba gets home by telling the two apart. She also realises the light cannot be borrowed, because there is no Moon or torch. The mushrooms make it themselves, and it is cold. In the morning she sees their light was there all along, just hidden by brighter sunlight.

- Ask: Why did Iba stop following the fireflies? What were the fireflies really doing with their flashes?
- Ask: How was the mushrooms' light different from the fireflies' light?
- Ask: How did Iba know the mushrooms were making their own light, and not just shining back the light of something else?
- Ask: Why did the line of glowing mushrooms lead back to the stream?
- Ask: In the morning the mushrooms looked plain and brown. Where had their light gone? Can you think of other things we only notice in the dark?

## Experiments
### e1 · Warm Glow, Cold Glow: does warmth change a glow stick? (23 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Does a glow stick glow brighter in cold water or in warm water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the glow stick in warm water be brighter, dimmer or the same as the one in cold water? Say why.
- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only one thing: how warm the water is. Use three glow sticks of the same colour and size, from the same packet. Snap them at the same moment. Use the same amount of water in the same kind of glass.
- **safety:** A grown-up handles the warm water. It should be warm like a bath, never boiling. Never cut or bite a glow stick. If one leaks, wash hands and do not touch eyes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Rank the brightness 1, 2 and 3. Next morning, write whether each one still glows.
- **results table:** Glass | How the water feels | Brightness rank (1 is brightest) | Still glowing next morning? (yes, faint or no)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Set up three glasses. Snap the glow sticks and drop them in. Compare the glow in the dark. Put the icy glass in the fridge. (16 min); day 2: Next morning, take the icy stick out and compare all three in the dark. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (7 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a glow stick. Do not snap it yet. Ask what they notice.  
  *Parent says:* This is a glow stick. Inside is a thin glass tube with one liquid, floating in another liquid. When I bend it, the tube cracks, the liquids mix, and they react. That makes light. A firefly makes light by mixing chemicals too. Do you think warm or cold will make the glow brighter?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the glow stick. Inside are two liquids, kept apart. Bend it and they mix. Then it glows! Guess: brighter in warm water, or in cold?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Warm things go faster.; Cold makes things slow.; Hot things glow more.)
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put ice and cold water in the first glass. 2. Room-temperature water in the second. 3. Warm water in the third. 4. Fill all three to the same height.  
  *Parent says:* Let's fill three glasses to the same height. One icy, one normal, one warm, like a bath. Feel each one with a finger.  
  *Child mode:* Help fill three glasses to the same height. One icy. One normal. One warm, for a grown-up to pour. Feel each with one finger.
  - Note: Warm enough that you can keep a finger in the water comfortably. Never use boiling water.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Snap all three glow sticks at the same moment. Shake them. Drop one into each glass. Wait 2 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* On three, we all bend our sticks till they click. One, two, three! Shake them, and drop one in each glass. Now we wait two minutes, so the water can warm or cool them.  
  *Child mode:* Bend all three sticks at once till they click. Shake them. Drop one in each glass. Wait two minutes.
  - Note: Snapping them together matters. If one started earlier, the test would not be fair.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Switch off the light. Look at the three glasses. Rank them 1, 2 and 3 for brightness. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Lights off! Which is brightest? Which is dimmest? Let's give them a number. 1 is the brightest.  
  *Child mode:* Lights off! Look at the three glasses. Which is brightest? Give it a 1. Write it down.
  - Check: In the glow stick test, which glass glowed brightest? → **The warm water**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the icy glass, with its stick, in the fridge. Leave the other two on a shelf, out of reach of small children. Next morning, take the icy stick out and look at all three in a dark bathroom straight away.  
  *Parent says:* The icy one goes in the fridge, so it stays cold all night. The other two stay out. Tomorrow we will see which one still glows. What do you guess, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Put the icy one in the fridge. Leave the others out. Tomorrow, check all three in the dark.
  - Note: Party glow sticks usually last several hours at room temperature, so by morning the two on the shelf are often faint or dark. The fridge stick usually glows more clearly, because the cold slowed its reaction all night. This does not always work: if all three are dark or all three still glow, write that down. It is still a result.
  - Check: Next morning, which glow stick was most likely still glowing? → **The one kept cold in the fridge**
  - Wait: Look again next morning
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Let's put it together. First, what do you think now? Second, what did you see that shows it? Third, why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: warmth makes a glow brighter, and cold makes it last longer. What I saw: the warm stick was brightest, and next morning the fridge one glowed more than the others. Why: warmth makes the chemicals react faster, so they get used up sooner. Fireflies also flash more slowly on cool nights.
### e2 · Cold Light Test: is a glow hot? (15 min)
- **question:** Does a glowing glow stick give out heat?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the glowing stick feel warmer than the one that is not glowing? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use two glow sticks from the same packet and let them lie side by side on the same cloth. Press each one on the same place on the inside of your wrist, for the same time, a count of twenty. Then test them again in the other order.
- **safety:** Never cut or bite a glow stick. If one leaks, wash hands and keep it away from eyes. In the extra step, a grown-up lights and watches the diya all the time.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the two glow sticks and write which one felt warmer, or the same.
- **results table:** Stick | Glowing? | On my wrist: warmer, cooler or the same as the room? | Thermometer touching it after 2 minutes (degrees), if you have one
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay two glow sticks side by side on a cloth. Snap one, shake it, and leave the other whole.  
  *Parent says:* One of these makes light and one does not. Both lie on the same cloth in the same room. Do you think the glowing one will feel warmer? A firefly makes light with chemistry too. Does that light bring heat?  
  *Child mode:* One glow stick glows. One does not. Guess: will the glowing one feel warmer?
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child presses the glowing stick on the inside of a wrist for a count of twenty. Then the whole stick, for a count of twenty. Then do both again in the other order.  
  *Parent says:* Press the glowing one on your wrist. Count to twenty. Now the whole one. Which is warmer? Now try again, the other way round.  
  *Child mode:* Press the glowing stick on your wrist and count to twenty. Now the whole stick. Which felt warmer?
  - Check: Which stick felt warmer on your wrist? → **About the same**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If you have a thermometer, lay it touching each stick for 2 minutes and read it.  
  *Parent says:* Feelings can fool us. A thermometer cannot be fooled. Let's measure.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the thermometer touching each stick. Wait two minutes. Read the number.
  - Note: Both readings should be close to the room temperature. If you have no thermometer, skip this step.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you feel or measure? Why do you think a firefly needs cold light?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I felt. Why a firefly needs cold light.
  - Note: A good answer: The glow stick makes cold light. The glowing stick felt the same as the whole one. A firefly needs cold light because their light is inside their own body, and a hot light would harm it. Extra, with a grown-up: light a diya on a steel plate and hold a hand one hand-width beside the flame, never above it, for a count of five. A flame gives out a lot of heat as well as light.
### e3 · Made or Stored? Glow-in-the-dark stars (22 min)
- **question:** If we shine a torch on a glow-in-the-dark star for longer, does it glow for longer in the dark?
- **predict:** Guess. Will a star charged for 60 seconds glow longer than one charged for 10 seconds? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use two stars from the same packet, side by side. Shine the same torch from the same distance. Change only how long you shine it. Finish charging both at the same moment, and watch both at the same time.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw two bars, one for each star.
- **results table:** Star | Torch time (seconds) | Brighter at 1 minute? (yes or no) | Minutes until you can no longer see it (stop at 10)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep both stars in a dark drawer for an hour before you start, so they are empty of light.  
  *Parent says:* These stars glow in the dark. A firefly makes their own light from food. Do you think these stars make their own light too? Let's find out.  
  *Child mode:* Do glow-in-the-dark stars make their own light, like a firefly? Let's test them.
  - Ask: What would happen if we never shone any light on them? (good: They wouldn't glow.; Maybe they would still glow a little.)
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the two stars side by side. Cover star A with a steel bowl. Shine the torch on star B for 50 seconds. Then lift the bowl and shine on both stars for 10 more seconds. Keep the torch the same distance away.  
  *Parent says:* Star A hides under the bowl first. Star B gets fifty seconds of torchlight. Now I lift the bowl, and both get ten more seconds. So star A had ten seconds and star B had sixty, and both finished at the same moment.  
  *Child mode:* Cover star A with a bowl. Shine the torch on star B for 50 seconds. Lift the bowl. Shine on both for 10 more seconds.
  - Note: Finishing together matters. If star A were charged first, it would already be fading while star B was still being charged.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn off the lights and start the stopwatch. At 1 minute, say which star is brighter. Then note the minute when each star fades so you cannot see it. Stop at 10 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Lights off. Which star is brighter now? Keep watching. Tell me the moment each one fades away.  
  *Child mode:* Lights off! Which star is brighter? Shout when each one fades away.
  - Note: Some stars fade in a few minutes. Others glow faintly for much longer. If a star still glows at 10 minutes, write 'more than 10'. Some stars fill up with light very fast, so both may glow the same. If so, write that down. It is a real result: 10 seconds was already enough.
  - Check: Star A and star B glow about the same at 1 minute. Did the test fail? → **No. The stars may fill up with light quickly, and that is a real result**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare with a firefly. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* A firefly glows in the darkest night, with no torch at all. A star only gives back light it soaked up. What do you think now? What did you see? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A firefly needs no torch. A star does. Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: The stars do not make their own light. They glowed only after the torch had shone on them, so they give back light they soaked up. A firefly is different, because they make new light from food and oxygen.

### Living Light Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, sees a firefly blink on a dark night. Next morning she sees a green leaf shining in the sunshine. Which one is making their own light?  
   Options: Only the firefly / Only the leaf / Both of them  
   **Answer:** Only the firefly. *Why:* The firefly makes new light with chemistry inside their body. That is living light, or bioluminescence. The leaf only sends back sunlight that lands on it. Picking 'both' mixes up making light and sending back light. In a dark room, the leaf would show nothing.
2. (sort) Sort these. Does each one make their own light, or not?  
   **Answer:** a firefly → Makes their own light; the Moon → Does not make their own light; a glowing mushroom → Makes their own light; a mirror → Does not make their own light; the glowing bacteria in an anglerfish's lure → Makes their own light; a white cat in torchlight → Does not make their own light. *Why:* Living light is made by the living being themself. The Moon, a mirror and a cat in torchlight only send back light from somewhere else. A strong answer puts the white cat in 'does not', even though the cat is alive: being alive is not the same as glowing.
3. (fill) A firefly makes light when a chemical called luciferin meets the gas ____ from the air.  
   **Answer:** ['oxygen']. *Why:* Oxygen from the air reacts with luciferin, and light comes out. The firefly switches their light on by letting air into their lantern.
4. (truefalse) Anything that gives out light must also be hot.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False, though most people think so, because bulbs and flames are hot. A firefly and a glow stick give out light with almost no heat. This is called cold light.
5. (write) Remember the cold light test? You pressed a glowing glow stick and a whole one on your wrist. What did your wrist feel each time? What does this tell you about a firefly's light?  
   **Answer:** Both sticks felt the same on my wrist, and neither was warm. So a glow can be cold. A firefly's light is cold light too, so the lantern does not harm their body.. *Why:* A strong answer says what was felt each time, then uses it to explain why a firefly's lantern can be part of their body.
6. (mcq) Why do most fireflies flash?  
   Options: To find a partner of their own kind / To see where they are flying / To keep warm  
   **Answer:** To find a partner of their own kind. *Why:* Each kind of firefly has their own flash code, and they flash to find a partner. Picking 'to see where they are flying' treats the flash as a torch, but their flashes are messages. Picking 'keep warm' misses that firefly light is cold.
7. (match) Match each glowing living being to where they live.  
   **Answer:** Firefly → fields and gardens on warm nights; Glowing mushroom → rotting wood in damp forests; Anglerfish → the deep, dark sea; Sea sparkle → waves near the beach. *Why:* Living light is found on land, in forests and all through the sea.
8. (write) In the glow stick test, the stick in warm water glowed brightest, and the one in icy water glowed dimmest. Explain why. Use the word 'reaction'.  
   **Answer:** The light comes from a chemical reaction inside the stick. Warmth makes the reaction go faster, so more light comes out every second. Cold slows the reaction, so less light comes out.. *Why:* A strong answer links brightness to how fast the reaction runs, not to the water giving the stick light or heat to shine with.
9. (mcq) You shine a torch on a glow-in-the-dark star, then switch off the light. The star glows. Is it making its own light, like a firefly?  
   Options: No, it is giving back light it soaked up from the torch / Yes, just like a firefly / Yes, it makes light from the air  
   **Answer:** No, it is giving back light it soaked up from the torch. *Why:* The star stores light, then lets it out slowly. A firefly makes new light with a chemical reaction. Picking 'just like a firefly' mixes up glowing and making light. A star kept in a dark drawer all day does not glow at all.
10. (mcq) Deep in the sea, below about 1,000 metres, how much sunlight is there?  
   Options: None at all / A little, at midday / Lots, because water makes light brighter  
   **Answer:** None at all. *Why:* Sunlight fades as it goes down through water. Below about 1,000 metres it is completely dark, day and night. Picking 'a little at midday' is right for a few hundred metres down, but not for the deep sea.
11. (truefalse) Most animals in the deep sea can make their own light.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In one count of deep-sea animals off California, about three out of every four could glow. In the dark, light is the best way to signal, trick or hide.
12. (mcq) An anglerfish has a glowing ball dangling in front of their mouth. What is it for?  
   Options: To bring small fish close, so the anglerfish can eat them / To see in the dark / To hide from hunters  
   **Answer:** To bring small fish close, so the anglerfish can eat them. *Why:* The glowing lure is bait. Small fish swim up to the light and are swallowed. Picking 'to see in the dark' treats the lure as a headlamp. Picking 'to hide' mixes it up with belly lights.
13. (draw) Draw a firefly at night. Show where their light comes from. Draw an arrow for the air going in.  
   **Answer:** A beetle with their glowing lantern at the end of their body, and an arrow of air going into the lantern.. *Why:* The light comes from the lantern, where luciferin meets oxygen from the air. A strong drawing shows the light at the back end, not the head.
14. (perspective) A park in Chennai has fireflies. The town wants to put up bright lights along the path. Three people disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a plan and explains it with firefly science: fireflies flash to find partners, and bright light drowns out their flashes. It then gives the other side a fair point, for example that people really do need to see the path. It may mix ideas, like shaded lamps that light only the ground.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. It settles down by giving out a photon, the tiniest bit of light. Picking 'catches sunlight' mixes it up with glow-in-the-dark stars. Picking 'catches fire' misses that there is no flame or heat.
3. (truefalse) A glowing mushroom and a firefly use exactly the same chemicals to make their light.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both use a luciferin and oxygen, but the luciferins are different chemicals. Fungi, fireflies, jellyfish and bacteria each found their own way to make light.
4. (write) Living light began separately at least 94 times, in very different living beings. What does that suggest about making light in the dark?  
   **Answer:** It suggests that making light is very useful in the dark. Again and again, different living beings found their own way to do it, because it helped them find food, hide, warn or find a partner.. *Why:* A strong answer links 'many separate beginnings' to 'very useful'. When the same ability appears many times, it usually helps living beings survive.
5. (mcq) In Brazil, scientists put fake glowing mushrooms next to fake dark ones in a forest. More insects landed on the glowing ones. What does this suggest?  
   Options: The glow may bring insects that spread the mushroom's spores / Insects are scared of the dark / It proves every mushroom glows for this reason  
   **Answer:** The glow may bring insects that spread the mushroom's spores. *Why:* This test, in 2015, suggests one reason for one kind of mushroom. Picking 'it proves every mushroom' goes too far. One test on one kind cannot tell us about all fungi.
6. (write) A friend's grandmother says there were many more fireflies when she was a child. Is that good evidence that fireflies are disappearing? How could you find out for sure?  
   **Answer:** It is a useful clue, but memories can be wrong or patchy. To find out for sure, many people would need to count fireflies in the same places, at the same time, every year, and compare the counts.. *Why:* A strong answer takes the memory seriously but asks for counts that can be compared. Scientists say there are very few long counts of fireflies in India.
7. (write) Here are made-up results from a firefly count, three minutes each night. Night 2 was cool and windy. Give two possible reasons why the count was low that night. How could you check which reason is right?  
   **Answer:** Fireflies may flash less when it is cool, because the cold slows the chemistry in their bodies. Or the wind may keep them from flying. To check, count on many nights and write down the warmth and the wind each time. Then see which one goes with low counts.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two different reasons, links one of them to the glow stick test, and plans more counts to compare. One low night is not enough to decide.
8. (match) Scientists use the chemicals of living light as tools. Match each one to what it is used for.  
   **Answer:** A green glowing protein from a jellyfish → makes parts of cells light up under a microscope; Firefly chemicals on a swab → glow if germs are left on a kitchen counter; Glowing bacteria in a water sample → dim if the water has poison in it. *Why:* Osamu Shimomura found the green protein in a jellyfish and shared a Nobel Prize for it in 2008. Each tool works because living light shows where living cells are, or whether they are healthy.
9. (truefalse) Your body gives out a very faint light, about a thousand times too weak for your eyes to see.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 2009, scientists in Japan photographed it with a super-sensitive camera in total darkness. What it can tell us is still being studied.
10. (mcq) Most deep-sea animals cannot see red light. A black dragonfish shines a red light from under their eyes, and they can see red. How might that help the dragonfish?  
   Options: They can light up small animals to eat, without being seen / Red light keeps them warm in the cold sea / Red light tells other animals to stay away  
   **Answer:** They can light up small animals to eat, without being seen. *Why:* The red light works like a secret torch. The dragonfish sees the small animals in their red light, but those animals cannot see the light, so they do not swim away. Picking 'stay away' would only work if the others could see red. Picking 'keeps them warm' misses that living light is cold.
11. (perspective) Glowing bamboo mushrooms grow near Mawlynnong in Meghalaya. Scientists found them in 2018 and gave them a name a few years later. Who discovered them?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, for example that people who knew the mushrooms first discovered them. It then says what the other side adds, for example that naming and studying let the whole world learn about them. It may say both should be credited.. *Why:* This is a real question about fairness and about different ways of knowing. Local knowledge came first. Science shared it widely. Both matter.
12. (mcq) A cookiecutter shark is a small shark that takes round bites out of bigger fish. Their belly glows all over, except for a dark collar. From below, the collar may look like a small fish. Why might that help the shark?  
   Options: Bigger fish swim up to catch the 'small fish', and the shark bites them instead / It helps the shark sleep / It keeps the shark warm  
   **Answer:** Bigger fish swim up to catch the 'small fish', and the shark bites them instead. *Why:* The dark patch may work as a lure, like the anglerfish's glow. Light and dark can both be used to trick. Picking 'keeps warm' misses that living light is cold.
13. (draw) Draw a deep-sea scene with three animals. Give each one a light with a different job: catch food, hide, scare an attacker or talk. Label the jobs.  
   **Answer:** For example, an anglerfish with a lure (catch food), a hatchetfish with belly lights (hide), and a jellyfish flashing a ring (scare).. *Why:* A strong drawing puts each light on the part of the body where real animals have it, and matches it to a job that light really does in the dark sea.
14. (mcq) Why do glowing mushrooms look plain and brown in the daytime?  
   Options: Their faint glow is still there, but sunlight is far brighter / They stop being alive in the day / They are different mushrooms  
   **Answer:** Their faint glow is still there, but sunlight is far brighter. *Why:* A faint light is hard to see next to a bright one. In the dark, the same faint glow stands out. Picking 'stop being alive' misses that the mushroom lives day and night. Some fungi do glow a little dimmer by day, but mostly the sunlight hides the glow.
15. (write) In the glow-in-the-dark star test, star A got 10 seconds of torchlight and glowed for 3 minutes. Star B got 60 seconds and glowed for 7 minutes. Priya, a girl in Class 4, says, 'So the stars make light, like fireflies.' What is wrong with her thinking?  
   **Answer:** The results show the opposite. The more light the star soaked up, the longer it glowed. So it is giving back stored light, not making new light. A firefly makes new light from chemicals, even in a dark room.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the numbers as evidence: more light in gave more glow out. It contrasts this with a firefly, who needs no light to glow.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is living light?  
   Options: A firefly's flash / A glow stick at a wedding / A glow-in-the-dark star  
   **Answer:** A firefly's flash. *Why:* Living light is made by a living being. A glow stick uses the same kind of chemistry, but nobody alive is making it. A glow-in-the-dark star only gives back stored light.
2. (mcq) A jellyfish glows in a fish tank in a room with every light switched off. Is that living light?  
   Options: Yes, because there is no other light for the jellyfish to send back / No, the jellyfish is only sending back light / No, the water in the tank is glowing  
   **Answer:** Yes, because there is no other light for the jellyfish to send back. *Why:* In a fully dark room there is no light to bounce back. So any glow must be made by the jellyfish themself, with chemistry. That is living light. Picking 'sending back light' forgets that the room is dark.
3. (mcq) Where do glowing mushrooms usually grow?  
   Options: On rotting wood in damp forests / In deserts / In the deep sea  
   **Answer:** On rotting wood in damp forests. *Why:* Glowing fungi grow on damp, rotting wood, like the fallen bamboo near Mawlynnong in Meghalaya. Picking 'deep sea' mixes them up with glowing fish.
4. (mcq) Where on Earth do the most glowing animals live?  
   Options: In the sea, especially the deep sea / In deserts / In cities  
   **Answer:** In the sea, especially the deep sea. *Why:* In the deep sea, about three out of four animals can make light. Picking 'cities' may come from all the lamps there, but those are not living lights.
5. (mcq) On some nights, the waves on beaches near Chennai and Mangaluru glow blue. What makes the glow?  
   Options: Tiny living beings in the water called sea sparkle / Moonlight on the waves / Oil from boats  
   **Answer:** Tiny living beings in the water called sea sparkle. *Why:* Sea sparkle are tiny one-celled living beings. They flash blue when the water is stirred. Moonlight on waves is silver and does not flash.
6. (mcq) A scientist mixes luciferin and luciferase, the two firefly chemicals, in a sealed tube with no air in it. What happens?  
   Options: No light, because there is no oxygen / A bright flash, because both chemicals are there / A slow glow that gets hot  
   **Answer:** No light, because there is no oxygen. *Why:* Luciferin needs oxygen to react. The luciferase only speeds the reaction up. With no oxygen, nothing reacts, so no light comes out. Let air in, and the tube glows. Picking 'a bright flash' forgets the oxygen.
7. (mcq) A firefly's light goes off. What has the firefly stopped doing?  
   Options: Letting air into their lantern / Eating / Flying  
   **Answer:** Letting air into their lantern. *Why:* Without fresh oxygen from the air, the reaction stops and so does the light. A firefly can sit still and still flash.
8. (mcq) A firefly flashes a code to find a partner. Which job of living light is that?  
   Options: Talking / Hiding / Catching food  
   **Answer:** Talking. *Why:* Living light has four main jobs: talking, catching food, hiding and scaring off an attacker. A flash code is a message, so it is talking.
9. (mcq) A small squid in Hawaii keeps glowing bacteria under their body. On moonlit nights the glow hides their shadow from hunters below. Which job is that?  
   Options: Hiding / Talking / Catching food  
   **Answer:** Hiding. *Why:* The glow fills in the dark shadow, so hunters below cannot see the squid. That is hiding.
10. (mcq) To make the glow stick test fair, what must be different between the glasses?  
   Options: Only how warm the water is / The colour of each glow stick / Everything  
   **Answer:** Only how warm the water is. *Why:* A fair test changes just one thing. If the colours were different too, we would not know what caused the change. Picking 'everything' is a common first idea, but then we could never tell which change mattered.
11. (mcq) A glow-in-the-dark star stops glowing after a while. Why?  
   Options: It has given back all the light it soaked up / It got too cold / It ran out of oxygen  
   **Answer:** It has given back all the light it soaked up. *Why:* Glow-in-the-dark stars only store light. When the stored light is used up, they go dark until a light charges them again. Picking 'ran out of oxygen' mixes it up with living light, which needs oxygen.
12. (mcq) A glow-in-the-dark star and a living firefly both spend a whole day in a dark cupboard. Then you open the door at night. Which one can still glow?  
   Options: Only the firefly / Only the star / Both of them  
   **Answer:** Only the firefly. *Why:* The star can only give back light it soaked up, and it got none in the cupboard. The firefly makes new light with chemistry, so they can flash after a day in the dark. Picking 'only the star' gets the two the wrong way round.
13. (mcq) What do bright lights at night do to fireflies?  
   Options: Hide their flashes, so they find fewer partners / Help them see their partners / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** Hide their flashes, so they find fewer partners. *Why:* Bright lights drown out faint firefly flashes. Picking 'help them see' may come from moths, which fly to lights. Fireflies are different.
14. (mcq) Why do mushrooms glow?  
   Options: Scientists are still not sure. One idea is that the glow brings insects that spread spores / To find partners, like fireflies / To keep warm at night  
   **Answer:** Scientists are still not sure. One idea is that the glow brings insects that spread spores. *Why:* For one Brazilian mushroom, tests show the glow brings insects. For most other fungi, nobody knows yet. Picking 'to find partners' fits fireflies, who signal to their own kind, not fungi.

## Applications
- Glow sticks, used for safety at night, in camping, at sea and at celebrations, work by the same kind of chemistry as fireflies.
- Hospitals and food factories use firefly chemicals in swab tests. The swab glows if germs or food bits are left on a surface.
- Scientists attach the green glowing protein from a jellyfish to parts of cells, to watch them grow and move.
- Doctors and scientists use glowing chemicals to test new medicines on cells in the lab.
- Engineers copied the shape of tiny scales on a firefly's lantern, to help more light escape from LED lights.
- Scientists have put mushroom glow genes into plants, which glow faintly. One day, plants might light paths.
- Glowing bacteria are used to test whether water has harmful chemicals in it. If the bacteria dim, something is poisoning them.
- Deep-sea scientists use fake glowing lures, like Edith Widder's e-jelly, to bring shy animals to their cameras.
- Sailors have long known that glowing plankton can show the trail of a ship or a submarine at night.
- Firefly festivals in Maharashtra bring visitors and money to villages that protect their forests.
- Lighting designers make park and beach lights that point down and stay dim, to protect fireflies and turtles.
- Glow-in-the-dark exit signs and paint help people find their way out in a power cut.

## Spotted around us
- Fireflies, called jugnu, blinking over fields, gardens and trees on warm nights from late May to July, before and during the first rains.
- Firefly festivals near Bhandardara and Purushwadi in Maharashtra, where whole trees blink in the pre-monsoon weeks.
- Glowing mushrooms on fallen bamboo near Mawlynnong in Meghalaya, in the monsoon.
- Glowing fungi on rotting logs in the Western Ghats forests of Goa, Karnataka and Kerala, in the rainy season.
- Blue glowing waves of sea sparkle on some nights at beaches near Mumbai, Goa, Mangaluru, Kerala and Chennai.
- Glow sticks at wedding processions, Ganesh festivals and New Year parties.
- Glow-in-the-dark stars on bedroom ceilings, and glowing hands on old clocks and switches.
- Glow-worms, the young of fireflies, glowing steadily on wet leaves and grass at the edge of fields after rain.

## Open questions
- **How does a firefly switch their light on and off so fast?** Scientists know oxygen is let in and shut off. Exactly how the firefly controls this in a fraction of a second is still argued about.
- **How do thousands of fireflies flash at exactly the same moment?** Each firefly seems to watch their neighbours and adjust. Scientists build computer models, but how real fireflies do it is not fully known.
- **Why do most glowing mushrooms glow?** For one Brazilian mushroom, the glow brings insects. For many others, nobody has tested it.
- **How did living light first begin in each group of living things?** It began separately many times. Nobody was there to see, and old glowing animals rarely leave fossils that show their light.
- **How many kinds of glowing animals live in the deep sea?** Most of the deep sea has never been visited. New glowing animals are found on almost every deep trip.
- **Are India's fireflies disappearing, and how fast?** There are very few long, careful counts of fireflies in India. Scientists need many more people counting, in the same places, year after year.
- **Does the faint light given off by our bodies tell us anything useful about health?** It has been measured, but it is so faint that scientists are only beginning to study it.

## Connected maths
- In the made-up firefly count, these were the flashes counted in 3 minutes: Night 1, 24. Night 2, 9. Night 3, 31. Look at the chart. Put the nights in order, from most flashes to fewest. **Night 3 (31), then Night 1 (24), then Night 2 (9).**. Compare the numbers. 31 is bigger than 24, and 24 is bigger than 9. So Night 3 had the most, then Night 1, then Night 2. On the chart, the tallest bar is Night 3 and the shortest is Night 2. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- On the night walk, you counted 12 firefly flashes in 1 minute in the dark spot, and 3 flashes in 1 minute next to a bright lamp. How many times more flashes did you see in the dark? **4 times more.**. In the dark spot there were 12 flashes. Near the lamp there were 3. 12 divided by 3 is 4. You can check: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12. So there were 4 times as many flashes in the dark. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- A firefly flashes once every 5 seconds. How many times do they flash in 1 minute? **12 times.**. One minute is 60 seconds. The firefly flashes once in every group of 5 seconds. 60 divided into groups of 5 makes 12 groups. Or count in fives: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60. That is 12 fives. So they flash 12 times. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the made-up star test results, star A glowed for 3 minutes after 10 seconds of torchlight. Star B glowed for 7 minutes after 60 seconds of torchlight. Look at the chart. How many minutes longer did star B glow? How many seconds is that? **4 minutes longer, which is 240 seconds.**. Star B glowed for 7 minutes. Star A glowed for 3 minutes. 7 take away 3 is 4. One minute is 60 seconds, and 60 + 60 + 60 + 60 = 240. So star B glowed 4 minutes, or 240 seconds, longer. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Sunlight fades as it goes down into the sea. At 200 metres it is dim. Below 1,000 metres it is completely dark. An anglerfish lives at 2,000 metres. How many metres below the start of the total darkness are they? **1,000 metres below.**. Total darkness starts at about 1,000 metres. The anglerfish lives at 2,000 metres. 2,000 take away 1,000 is 1,000. So they live 1,000 metres below where the last light ends. That is about as deep as ten football fields laid end to end. (queues `math.number-sense.place-value.L3`)
