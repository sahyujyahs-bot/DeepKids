# A balanced diet: different plates for different people, and what a missing nutrient does
`sci.food.balanced-diet.L5`

**Summary.** A child can eat until she is full every day and still fall ill, because one nutrient is missing. How can a tiny amount matter so much? In this unit you will time how long your eyes take to see in a dim room, and boil cabbage to see where the vitamin C goes.

**Intro.** *The iodine your body needs in a whole day is a tiny amount. It weighs about the same as a speck of chalk dust the size of a full stop. Why would a body fall ill for want of something so small?* Most people think a good diet means enough food. Enough food stops hunger, but it does not always stop illness. Sailors with full stomachs lost their teeth, because their food had no vitamin C. Families who ate plenty of white rice lost the strength in their legs. The polished rice had lost its vitamin B1. These are called deficiency diseases. In this unit you weigh out one day's needs, starting with two spoons of protein, which is a lot, and ending with a tiny amount of iodine. To see how tiny, you will put a speck of chalk dust beside it, because the two weigh about the same. You time your own eyes in a dark room. You test what boiling does to the vitamin C in cabbage. You also find out why your plate should not look like your grandmother's plate.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a balanced diet is: enough of every nutrient, with water and fibre, for one person's body.
1. I can match each deficiency disease to its missing nutrient, and name a cheap food that prevents it.
2. I can explain why nutrients needed in tiny amounts still matter, and what hidden hunger is.
3. I can explain, with numbers, why a baby, a pregnant woman, a worker and an old person need different diets.
4. I can give four ways to make cheap food more nourishing, and say why each one works.
5. I can explain how fortified food, sunlight and tablets prevent deficiency, and why too much can harm.
6. I can run a fair test with food or with my own eyes, and say what the result shows.
7. I can tell how doctors found the cause of goitre, and say what is still unsure about kwashiorkor.

## Terms
- **Nutrient**: A substance in food that the body needs to work, grow and stay healthy. Carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins and minerals are nutrients. *e.g.* Dal gives the nutrient protein. A guava gives the nutrient vitamin C.
- **Balanced diet**: Food and drink that give one person enough of every nutrient, water and fibre, and not too much of any. *e.g.* Ragi dosa with sambar, a greens poriyal, curd and a guava make a balanced meal for a child.
- **Deficiency disease**: An illness caused by too little of one nutrient over a long time. Deficiency means not having enough. *e.g.* Scurvy is a deficiency disease. It comes from too little vitamin C.
- **Vitamin**: A substance that the body needs in very small amounts and cannot make enough of. It must come from food. Vitamin D is the exception, because skin makes it in sunlight. *e.g.* Humans cannot make vitamin C, so we must eat it. A goat makes her own.
- **Mineral**: A nutrient that first came from rocks, soil or sea water. Plants take minerals up through their roots. *e.g.* Iron, iodine and calcium are minerals.
- **Micronutrient**: A nutrient needed in tiny amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms. Vitamins and minerals are micronutrients. *e.g.* A child needs about 30 grams of protein a day, but only about 100 micrograms of iodine. That is a very small amount.
- **Microgram**: One millionth of a gram. One thousand micrograms make one milligram, and one thousand milligrams make one gram. *e.g.* A speck of chalk dust the size of a full stop weighs very roughly 100 micrograms. Specks differ in size, so this is only a rough figure. We use a speck here only because it is small and easy to picture.
- **Hidden hunger**: Being short of a vitamin or a mineral while eating enough food to feel full. *e.g.* A child who eats mostly white rice and biscuits may feel full and still be short of iron.
- **Malnutrition**: Poor health from the wrong amount of food or of a nutrient for a long time. It can be too little or too much. *e.g.* Too little iron is malnutrition. So is far too much sugar and fat.
- **Anaemia**: Having too little haemoglobin in the blood, so the body gets less oxygen. The person feels tired and breathless and looks pale. Too little iron is the most common cause. *e.g.* A girl with anaemia may be out of breath after one flight of stairs.
- **Haemoglobin**: The red substance inside red blood cells that carries oxygen. Each haemoglobin molecule holds four atoms of iron. *e.g.* A health worker pricks a finger and measures the haemoglobin in one drop of blood.
- **Night blindness**: Not being able to see in dim light, although sight is normal in bright light. It is the first sign of too little vitamin A. *e.g.* A child with night blindness trips over the doorstep at dusk.
- **Carotene**: An orange substance that plants make. The human body turns carotene into vitamin A. *e.g.* Carrot, papaya, pumpkin and drumstick leaves are rich in carotene.
- **Rickets**: A disease in which a child's bones stay soft and the legs bend. It comes from too little vitamin D, and often too little calcium. *e.g.* A toddler with rickets may have legs that bow outwards.
- **Goitre**: A swelling at the front of the neck. The thyroid gland grows bigger there, usually because the food has too little iodine. *e.g.* Goitre was once common in the Himalayas, where the soil has little iodine.
- **Scurvy**: A disease in which the gums bleed, teeth loosen and wounds do not heal. It comes from too little vitamin C. *e.g.* Sailors on long voyages got scurvy when they had no fresh fruit or vegetables.
- **Beriberi**: A disease in which the legs become weak and numb and the heart grows weak. It comes from too little vitamin B1. *e.g.* Beriberi spread when people began to eat rice polished white by machines.
- **Kwashiorkor**: A serious kind of malnutrition in young children. The feet, legs and face swell with water, and the skin and hair change. *e.g.* Kwashiorkor often begins after a child stops breastfeeding and eats mostly thin, starchy porridge.
- **Marasmus**: A serious kind of malnutrition from too little food of every kind. The child becomes very thin, with no swelling. *e.g.* In a famine, when there is little of any food, many children get marasmus.
- **Fortified food**: Food with a nutrient added on purpose, so that everyone who eats it gets enough. *e.g.* Iodised salt is salt fortified with iodine.
- **Fibre**: The part of plant food that human enzymes cannot digest. It holds water and keeps food moving through the gut. *e.g.* Whole wheat atta, ragi, rajma, guava and greens are rich in fibre.

## Explanations
### What 'balanced' means · *defined*
**Definition:** A balanced diet gives one person enough of every nutrient, water and fibre, and not too much of any.

The body needs seven things from food and drink. Carbohydrates and fats give energy. Proteins build and repair the body. Vitamins and minerals keep its chemistry working. Water carries everything, and makes up more than half of your weight. Fibre keeps food moving through the gut. No single food holds all seven in the right amounts, so a balanced diet needs variety. Balance has two sides. A diet can be unbalanced because something is missing. It can also be unbalanced because there is too much sugar, salt or fat. The words 'for one person' matter too. A plate that suits a woman who works at a desk is too small for a fast-growing girl.
- e.g. Rice alone gives energy, but little protein and almost no vitamin A or vitamin C.
- e.g. Rice with sambar, a greens poriyal and curd covers energy, protein, iron, calcium and several vitamins.
- e.g. The same full plate every day, with two packets of chips added, is unbalanced the other way.
### Grams, milligrams and micrograms: why tiny amounts matter · *defined*
**Definition:** Macronutrients are needed in grams each day. Micronutrients are needed in milligrams or micrograms. A lack of either causes illness.

A child of ten needs about 30 grams of protein a day. That is about two heaped tablespoons. The same child needs about 50 milligrams of vitamin C. One moong seed weighs about 50 milligrams, so that is the weight of vitamin C a child needs in a day. The child also needs about 100 micrograms of iodine. A speck of chalk dust the size of a full stop weighs very roughly 100 micrograms, so that is about the weight of iodine a child needs in a day. The seed and the chalk dust do not contain these nutrients here. We use them only to show how little each nutrient weighs. The amounts are very different because the nutrients do different jobs. Protein is building material, so the body needs a lot of it. Iodine is a tiny part of a messenger substance called thyroid hormone. Each messenger needs only a few atoms of iodine. But the body cannot make the messenger without those atoms. A person can eat enough to feel full and still be short of a micronutrient. This is called hidden hunger. It is hidden because fullness tells the body about energy and bulk. No feeling tells you that iodine or vitamin A is running low.
- e.g. One grain of table salt weighs roughly 60 micrograms, and grains differ in size.
- e.g. A child needs only about 2 micrograms of vitamin B12 in a day. That weighs about one thirtieth of a single grain of table salt. We compare it to salt only to show how tiny the amount is.
- e.g. India's health survey of 2019 to 2021 found anaemia in more than half of the women tested.
### Vitamins: what goes wrong when one is missing · *defined*
**Definition:** A vitamin is a substance that the body needs in small amounts and cannot make enough of. Each vitamin does its own job, so each lack causes its own disease.

Vitamin A builds the substance in the eye that catches dim light. Without it, a person cannot see at dusk. This is night blindness. If the lack goes on, the surface of the eye dries, and the child can go blind. Vitamin B1 helps cells get energy out of starch and sugar. Without it, nerves and the heart weaken. This is beriberi. Vitamin C helps build collagen, the strong threads in skin, gums and blood vessels. Without it, gums bleed and wounds stay open. This is scurvy. Vitamin D helps the gut absorb calcium. Without it, a child's bones stay soft and bend. This is rickets. Most animals make their own vitamin C. Humans, other apes and monkeys, guinea pigs and fruit bats cannot, because one gene needed for it no longer works. Vitamin B12 is made only by bacteria. Plants hold almost none, so people who eat no meat or fish get theirs from milk, curd and eggs.
- e.g. A child short of vitamin A sees well at noon but stumbles in a dim room.
- e.g. In 1747 the ship's doctor James Lind gave two sailors with scurvy oranges and lemons. They recovered fastest. One was fit for duty again in six days.
- e.g. A baby kept indoors all winter can get rickets, even with enough milk.
### Minerals: iron, iodine and calcium · *defined*
**Definition:** Minerals are nutrients that first came from rock, soil and sea. Iron carries oxygen in the blood, iodine makes thyroid hormone, and calcium hardens bones and teeth.

Iron sits inside haemoglobin, the red substance that carries oxygen in the blood. With too little iron, the body makes too little haemoglobin. Less oxygen reaches the muscles and the brain. The person is tired, breathless and pale. This is anaemia, and it is the most common deficiency in India. The body absorbs iron from meat and fish easily. It absorbs iron from plants less easily. Vitamin C in the same meal helps, and tea or coffee with the meal hinders. Iodine is needed by the thyroid, a gland at the front of the neck. The thyroid makes a hormone that sets how fast every cell uses energy. With too little iodine, the gland grows larger to catch more, and the neck swells. This is goitre. If a pregnant woman is short of iodine, her baby's brain may not grow fully. Calcium makes bones and teeth hard, and nerves and muscles use it too. A growing child lays down new bone every day.
- e.g. Ragi has about three times as much calcium as the same weight of milk.
- e.g. A squeeze of lemon on kala chana helps the gut absorb more of the iron.
- e.g. Before iodised salt, goitre was common in the hills from Kashmir to Nagaland.
### Protein, energy and the most serious malnutrition · *defined*
**Definition:** A young child may get far too little food, or food that is almost all starch. Then growth stops, and the body breaks down its own muscle. The two severe forms are marasmus and kwashiorkor.

In marasmus the child gets too little of everything. The body burns its fat and then its muscle for energy. The child becomes very thin, and the skin hangs loose. In kwashiorkor the child may get some energy, often from thin maize or rice porridge. The feet, legs and face swell with water. The skin peels and the hair turns thin and pale. The swelling can hide how thin the child is. Both happen mostly between six months and five years of age. At that age a child grows fast, has a small stomach and catches many infections. Health workers find children at risk with a simple tape around the upper arm. Treatment is slow, careful feeding with special foods at a health centre. Prevention starts with breast milk alone for the first six months. After that come soft family foods with dal, egg, milk, oil and vegetables, several times a day.
- e.g. Anganwadi workers weigh young children every month and mark the weight on a growth chart.
- e.g. A tape that shows red around a small child's upper arm means the child needs help at once.
- e.g. After a long bout of loose motions, a small child needs extra food for two weeks to catch up.
### Why your eyes need vitamin A · *defined*
**Definition:** The cells that see in dim light hold a light-catching substance called rhodopsin. The eye builds rhodopsin from vitamin A and a protein.

At the back of the eye is a thin layer called the retina. It holds two kinds of light-catching cells. Cones work in bright light and see colour. Rods work in dim light and see only light and dark. Each rod is packed with rhodopsin. When light strikes a rhodopsin molecule, the molecule changes shape and comes apart. That change starts the signal to the brain. Bright light breaks rhodopsin faster than the eye can rebuild it. So when you walk from sunlight into a dark room, your rods have almost none, and you see nothing. Over the next 20 to 30 minutes the eye rebuilds its rhodopsin, and dim things appear one by one. This is called dark adaptation. The eye needs a steady supply of vitamin A for the rebuilding. A person short of vitamin A rebuilds too little. Their eyes stay nearly blind in dim light, however long they wait.
- e.g. In the Dark Room Test, a white towel shows up within a minute, but a dark book takes several minutes.
- e.g. A torch shone in your eyes at night leaves you blind to the dark for minutes afterwards.
- e.g. Vitamin A drops are given to young children in India twice a year to protect their eyes.
### Different people need different plates · *defined*
**Definition:** How much of each nutrient a person needs depends on four things. They are body size, growth, how hard the muscles work, and whether the person is feeding another body.

Growth needs building material. A baby doubles their birth weight in about five months. So for each kilogram they need far more food than a grown-up. A child of two needs about 85 kilocalories for each kilogram of body weight. A woman who works at a desk needs about 30. A kilocalorie is the unit for the energy in food. Girls and boys from 10 to 18 grow fast. They need more protein, calcium and iron than at any time since babyhood. Girls need extra iron, because they will soon lose some blood every month. A pregnant woman builds a whole new body, so she needs more energy, protein, iron, folate and iodine. A breastfeeding mother makes about 750 millilitres of milk a day, which costs her about 600 kilocalories. A person who does heavy work with their muscles needs much more energy and water. An old person needs less energy, but just as much protein and more calcium. Their food should be soft to chew. A sick person needs fluids first, and extra food while they recover.
- e.g. A woman who carries loads all day needs about 1,000 kilocalories more than a woman at a desk.
- e.g. A two-year-old has a small stomach, so they need five or six small meals a day.
- e.g. Pregnant women in India are given iron and folic acid tablets at the health centre.
### Cheap ways to make food more nourishing · *defined*
**Definition:** Some of the most nourishing foods are among the cheapest. The way food is paired, prepared and cooked also changes how much nourishment the body gets.

Pair a cereal with a pulse. Proteins are built from 20 kinds of units called amino acids. The body cannot make nine of them. Rice and wheat are low in one, called lysine. Dals are low in another, called methionine. Eaten together, each fills the other's gap. Sprout whole pulses. A dry moong seed has almost no vitamin C, and a sprouted one makes their own. Ferment batters. The bacteria and yeasts in idli batter make some B vitamins as they grow. Choose millets and greens. Ragi is rich in calcium, bajra in iron, and drumstick leaves in carotene, iron and calcium. Cook to keep vitamins. Vitamin C and the B vitamins dissolve in water. So wash vegetables before cutting, cook them in little water with a lid, and use the cooking water. Help the iron along. Add lemon or tomato to iron foods, and keep tea away from meals. Food cooked in an iron kadai also picks up some iron.
- e.g. Idli, pongal, khichdi, rajma with rice and dal with roti all pair a cereal with a pulse.
- e.g. In the Cooking Water Test, the water from boiled cabbage cleared the blue liquid in fewer spoons than plain water.
- e.g. A handful of roasted chana and groundnuts costs less than a packet of chips and gives far more protein.
### Preventing deficiency: food, fortified food, sunlight and tablets · *defined*
**Definition:** Deficiency is prevented in four ways. They are a varied diet, nutrients added to common foods, sunlight on skin, and tablets or drops from health workers.

Varied food comes first, because whole foods carry many nutrients together. But some soils hold little iodine, so no local food can supply it. So iodine is added to salt, which nearly everyone eats in nearly the same small amount. This is called fortification. In India some oil and milk are fortified with vitamins A and D, and some atta and rice with iron. Fortified packets carry a small +F sign. Vitamin D is different. Skin makes it when strong sunlight falls on it. The Sun must be high in the sky, and window glass blocks the rays that do this. Some people need more than food can easily give. Young children in India get vitamin A drops twice a year. Schoolchildren and pregnant women get iron and folic acid tablets. These are given in measured doses, because more is not better. Too much vitamin A or iron is a poison. So vitamin and iron tablets are taken only on the advice of a doctor or a health worker.
- e.g. Iodised salt in India must hold at least 15 micrograms of iodine in each gram when you buy it.
- e.g. In the Short Shadow Test, the pencil's shadow was shorter than the pencil only around the middle of the day.
- e.g. Polar explorers have been poisoned by the vitamin A in a polar bear's liver.
### Fibre, water and too much of a good thing · *defined*
**Definition:** Fibre and water are not burned for energy, yet the body cannot stay well without them. Sugar, salt and fat are needed, yet too much of them causes illness.

Fibre is the part of plants that human enzymes cannot digest. It holds water, so faeces stay soft and pass easily. The bacteria in the large intestine feed on fibre. Whole grains, pulses, vegetables and fruit hold fibre. Maida, white sugar and oil hold almost none. Water is lost all day in urine, sweat and breath, so it must be replaced all day. Pale yellow urine is a sign of enough water. A diet can also be unbalanced by too much. Extra sugar and fat are stored as body fat. Sweet drinks, fried snacks and packet foods give a lot of energy and salt, with few vitamins or minerals. A person can therefore be overweight and anaemic at once. India now has both problems, sometimes in the same family.
- e.g. Whole wheat atta leaves brown flakes of bran in a fine sieve. Maida leaves none.
- e.g. A 250 millilitre bottle of a sweet fizzy drink holds about 6 teaspoons of sugar.
- e.g. A child who drinks too little water on a hot day gets a headache and dark yellow urine.
### What causes kwashiorkor? · *debated*
**Definition:** Doctors agree that kwashiorkor comes from a poor diet in early childhood. They do not agree on exactly what in that diet causes the swelling.

Two children can eat the same poor diet. One gets the swelling of kwashiorkor, and the other becomes thin with marasmus. Too little protein does not fully explain this. Scientists are still testing other causes.
- e.g. Cicely Williams described kwashiorkor in the Gold Coast, now Ghana, in 1933.
- e.g. In Malawi, scientists studied twins of whom only one had kwashiorkor.
- **Dr Cicely Williams, a doctor in the Gold Coast, in 1933 and 1935:** She saw the illness in children who had been weaned on to maize porridge when a new baby came. She said some needed part of the diet was missing, most likely protein. Milk cured many of the children.
- **Dr C. Gopalan in Hyderabad in 1968, and Dr Michael Golden in the 1980s:** Gopalan compared the diets of children who got kwashiorkor and children who got marasmus. He found no clear difference. Golden proposed that harmful molecules damage the body when protective nutrients are missing.
- **Michelle Smith, Jeffrey Gordon and their team, in 2013:** They studied 317 pairs of twins in Malawi. In some pairs only one twin got kwashiorkor on the same food. The ill twin's gut microbes were different. Mice given those microbes and the same poor diet lost weight. So gut microbes may be one of the causes.

## Mostly agreed
- A person can eat enough food and still fall ill from the lack of one nutrient. *How we know:* In 1747 James Lind treated twelve sailors with scurvy on one ship. All ate the same meals. Only the two who were also given oranges and lemons recovered quickly.
- Too little iodine causes goitre, and iodised salt prevents it. *How we know:* From 1956, Dr V. Ramalingaswami's team compared villages in the Kangra valley. After about five or six years, goitre had fallen by about half or more where the salt was iodised, and not where it was plain.
- The eye needs vitamin A to see in dim light. *How we know:* In 1933 George Wald found vitamin A in the retina. Later work showed that the light-catching substance of the rods is built from it.
- Vitamin C helps the gut absorb iron from plant foods. Tea with a meal lowers the amount absorbed. *How we know:* In the 1970s, scientists gave volunteers meals with iron atoms that could be traced. Less iron was absorbed with tea, and more with vitamin C.
- A cereal and a pulse eaten together give better protein than either alone. *How we know:* Chemists measured the amino acids in grains and pulses. Cereals are low in lysine and pulses are low in methionine, and each has plenty of the other.
- Growing children need more food for each kilogram of body weight than adults do. *How we know:* Nutrition scientists have measured the energy that people of each age use in a day. India's National Institute of Nutrition in Hyderabad publishes the figures.

## How we found out
**Adolphe Chatin in France, Robert McCarrison in Gilgit, David Marine and O. P. Kimball in the United States, and V. Ramalingaswami and S. S. Sooch in the Kangra valley, Chatin published his iodine measurements from 1850. McCarrison worked in Gilgit from 1902. Marine and Kimball's test began about 1917. The Kangra valley study began in 1956, and India's salt programme in 1962..** For centuries, many people in the Himalayas lived with goitre, a swelling at the front of the neck. From 1902 a British army doctor, Robert McCarrison, studied goitre in Gilgit. Villages lower down a water channel had more goitre than villages higher up. So he decided that dirty water carried something, perhaps a germ, that caused goitre. Half a century earlier, a French pharmacist and botanist named Adolphe Chatin had measured iodine in water and plants. He found less iodine in places where goitre was common. Many doctors doubted him. The test began about 1917 in Akron, a town in the United States. David Marine and O. P. Kimball gave about two thousand schoolgirls small doses of iodine. Only five of them developed goitre. Among the girls who took no iodine, nearly five hundred did. In 1956 Dr V. Ramalingaswami and his team began a larger test in the Kangra valley, now part of Himachal Pradesh. Some villages got salt with iodine added, and others got plain salt. After about five or six years, goitre had fallen by about half or more where the salt was iodised. It had not fallen where the salt was plain. In 1962 India began a national programme to iodise salt.  
*What people believed before:* That goitre is caused by a germ in dirty water. Marine and Kimball showed that a small dose of iodine prevented goitre in schoolgirls. The Kangra valley study showed that iodine in salt did the same for whole villages.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If you eat until you are full, you get everything your body needs.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Hunger is the body's signal for food, and it stops when the stomach is full.  
  Against: Fullness reports energy and bulk. No feeling reports vitamins or minerals. Sailors with scurvy and rice eaters with beriberi had full stomachs.
- **Kwashiorkor is caused by too little protein, and nothing else.** (Most textbooks since the 1950s.; *debated*)  
  For: It appears in children weaned on to starchy porridge, and milk often cures it.  
  Against: Children on the same diet do not all get it. Gut microbes, infections and missing protective nutrients may each play a part.
- **Vitamin tablets are as good as food, and more vitamins are always better.** (Many advertisements.; *rejected*)  
  For: Tablets do cure deficiency diseases, and health workers give them for that.  
  Against: Too much vitamin A or iron is a poison. Large studies find little benefit from extra vitamin tablets for people who eat a varied diet. Whole foods also carry fibre and many other substances.
- **Big doses of vitamin C stop you catching colds.** (Linus Pauling, a chemist who won two Nobel Prizes, in a book in 1970.; *rejected*)  
  For: Vitamin C is needed by the cells that fight infection, and Pauling was a great chemist.  
  Against: Many careful trials have tested it since. For most people, daily vitamin C does not prevent colds. It may shorten a cold slightly.
- **Spinach is the best food for iron.** (A common belief, helped by cartoons.; *rejected*)  
  For: Spinach, called palak in Hindi, does hold iron.  
  Against: Spinach also holds substances that grip the iron, so the gut absorbs little of it. Bajra, kala chana, rajma, eggs and meat give iron that is absorbed better.
- **India's Green Revolution ended hunger in India.** (Many accounts of India's history.; *debated*)  
  For: From the 1960s, new wheat and rice gave far bigger harvests, and India stopped depending on grain from abroad.  
  Against: Fields of pulses and millets gave way to wheat and rice. Energy became cheap, but iron, protein and vitamins did not. M. S. Swaminathan, who helped lead the work, later called for farming that also ends hidden hunger.

## Roots
In this unit you timed your eyes in a dark room. You learned that vitamin A lets them see in dim light. Here we ask where that vitamin A came from, and what that says about you.

**Deep truth.** In the Dark Room Test, the dark book showed up only after several minutes. In that time, your eyes rebuilt a light-catching substance from vitamin A. All vitamin A begins as carotene, an orange colour that plants and algae make. So your eyes see in dim light with a colour that a plant made.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of seeing as something our eyes do by themselves. We think of food as fuel that is burned and gone. Some food is never burned. Its molecules become working parts of the body, and the body has no other way to get them.  
*Evidence:* In the Dark Room Test, sight in dim light comes back slowly, because the eye must rebuild rhodopsin. People short of vitamin A stay blind in dim light. Animals cannot make vitamin A from nothing. They make it from the carotene of plants and algae, or get it from another animal who did.

- *who am i:* You are built partly from things that other living beings made for their own lives. The substance that lets you see at dusk began as a colour in a leaf or a fruit.
- *what is life:* No living being makes everything they need. Plants make carotene and vitamin C, bacteria make vitamin B12, and your skin makes vitamin D with the Sun. Every body depends on what other bodies make.
- *what is this world:* Iodine comes originally from the sea, and iodised salt carries a little of it to your food. Your thyroid needs only a few atoms of iodine to work. So very small amounts of something can do very large jobs inside you.

A drumstick leaf makes carotene, which helps the leaf catch sunlight and protects the leaf from too much of it. You eat the leaf. Your gut wall splits each carotene molecule into vitamin A. Your eye joins the vitamin A to a protein, and the new substance catches light again, this time for you. Many sages taught that all beings live by one another. The science here shows one exact case of it. A substance that worked with light in a plant goes on working with light in your eye.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.2:** “अन्नाद्वै प्रजाः प्रजायन्ते / याः काश्च पृथिवीँ श्रिताः / अथो अन्नेनैव जीवन्ति / अथैनदपि यन्त्यन्ततः” From food all beings are born. By food they live, and into food they return at the end. (The verse says that every living body is made from other living bodies. Vitamin A is a small, exact example. Animals do not make vitamin A from nothing, so the vitamin passes from plant to animal to animal. The Upanishad says this as an insight about all life, which science does not test.)
- **Tirukkural 942 and 943, by Thiruvalluvar:** “மருந்தென வேண்டாவாம் யாக்கைக்கு அருந்தியது / அற்றது போற்றி உணின். / அற்றால் அளவறிந்து உண்க அஃதுடம்பு / பெற்றான் நெடிதுய்க்கும் ஆறு.” A body needs no medicine if a person eats only after the last meal is digested. Eat in the measure that suits your body. (Thiruvalluvar ties health to a measure of food that fits one body. This unit shows that the measure differs: a girl of ten, a pregnant woman and a grandmother each need a different plate. The couplets give advice. They do not name nutrients.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, 'interbeing', in The Heart of Understanding (1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the forest there. Nothing exists by itself alone. (Look at your own eye in the same way. The substance that catches dim light in it began in a leaf or a fruit. This is a way of looking, and the facts about vitamin A stand without it.)
- Science (settled): Animals cannot make vitamin A from nothing. It starts as carotene made by plants, algae and some microbes, and passes along food chains.
- Science (settled): Humans, other apes and monkeys, guinea pigs and some bats cannot make vitamin C, because one gene no longer works. Most other animals make their own.
- Science (open): The microbes in a person's gut may change how much of some nutrients that person needs. Scientists are still measuring how much.
- Other view, Many biochemists: Carotene is a molecule and nothing more. Once your gut wall has split it and your eye has built it into rhodopsin, it is fully yours. Nothing of the plant remains. Saying that you see with a plant's colour only describes where some atoms were last week.
- Practice, Watching the room come back (3 min): Switch off the light at night and sit still. At first the room is black. Breathe slowly and keep your eyes open. Notice the first thing that appears, and then the next. Your eyes are rebuilding their light-catching substance now. Think of the last orange or green food you ate. Some of that food is at work in your eyes.
- Ask: In the Dark Room Test, your eyes rebuilt their light-catching substance from vitamin A. That vitamin A began in a plant. Is the substance yours or the plant's?
- Ask: A goat makes her own vitamin C, and you must get yours from fruit and leaves. What does your body gain or lose by depending on plants?
- Ask: The iodine you need in a whole day weighs about the same as a speck of chalk dust the size of a full stop. That is a very small amount, yet your body falls ill without it. What else that is very small might be doing large jobs inside you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The vitamin A in your eye began as carotene in a plant. Is the substance that catches dim light yours, or the plant's? (hint: Your gut wall split the carotene and your eye built the substance. The plant made the starting material.)
- *deep:* Your body cannot make vitamin C, but a goat's body can. Why might a body lose the power to make something it needs? (hint: Our ancestors ate fruit and leaves every day. What happens to a tool that is never needed?)
- *deep:* You feel hunger and thirst. Why is there no feeling for 'short of iodine'? (hint: Think about how long each shortage takes to harm you, and how our ancestors could have answered it.)
- *big:* India grows enough grain for everyone, yet more than half of its women are anaemic. Is that a problem of growing, of sharing or of knowing? (hint: Think about what is grown, what it costs, who eats last in a family, and what people know.)
- *big:* Is giving everyone the same amount of food fair? (hint: Compare what a child of two, a girl of fourteen and a grandmother each need.)
- *big:* Cooks paired rice with dal long before anyone had heard of amino acids. How can people know that something works before they know why? (hint: Think about how many meals and how many generations a kitchen has had to try things.)

## Story: The Equal Ladle
*Ammu, Saramma, Jancy, Minnu, Rosamma, Sneha and the others are made up. These facts are real: in August 2018 floods covered much of Kerala, and Chengannur on the Pamba river was among the worst-hit towns. More than ten lakh people lived for days in relief camps, many of them in schools. Kanji, which is rice boiled soft in plenty of water, and payar, which is green gram, were common camp meals. A breastfeeding mother needs about 600 kilocalories more each day. A two-year-old has a small stomach and needs small meals often.*

In August 2018 the Pamba river came into Ammu's house, and her family went to live in a school.

The school stood on high ground in Chengannur. Four hundred people slept in its classrooms. In the yard, under a blue plastic sheet, Saramma chechi cooked for all of them in two pots as wide as cartwheels. One pot held kanji, which is rice boiled soft in plenty of water. The other held payar, green gram cooked with salt and coconut.

Ammu was eleven, and she wanted a job. Saramma gave her the payar ladle.

On the first evening a man in the line pointed at the plate ahead of his. "She got more than I did," he said. Ammu looked. Perhaps the woman had. Ammu's ears went hot.

That night she made a rule. Every person in the line would get one ladle, filled level. She told Saramma. "Then nobody can say it is unfair."

Saramma was scraping the kanji pot. "Try it," she said.

The next day Ammu served four hundred level ladles. She scraped the top of each one flat with a spoon. Nobody in the line complained.

But afterwards she saw four things.

Jancy came back to the pot with an empty plate and her baby, Appu, on her shoulder. "Is there any left?" she asked. "One ladle each, chechi," said Ammu. "That is the rule." Jancy nodded and went away.

Minnu, who was two, got a level ladle like everyone else. She ate five spoonfuls and pushed the plate away. Two hours later she was crying for food, and the pots were empty.

Kuttan appachan, who was eighty, ate slowly from the edge of his plate. He left half his payar. "My teeth," he said, and he smiled as if it were his fault.

At dusk the cleaning party came back. All day they had shovelled river mud out of houses. Rosamma had carried the mud away in buckets, two at a time. She ate her share in a minute. Then she sat on the steps with her head on her knees.

Ammu washed the plates that night. The slop bucket beside her was heavy with payar that people had left. She had to drag it to the pit with both hands. At the top of the steps, Rosamma was still sitting with her head down.

Ammu stood between the bucket and the steps. Food had been thrown away, and people were still hungry. Both things had come out of the same pot, by the same ladle.

She fetched her school notebook and drew a line down a page. On the left she wrote the names of those who had left food. Minnu. Appachan. Two of the old women from Room 3. On the right she wrote the names of those who were still hungry. Jancy. Rosamma. The others who had shovelled. Her cousin Sneha, who was fourteen and had grown out of two skirts that year.

She looked at the lists for a long time. The left side was easy. They were small, or they were old. The right side took longer. Rosamma worked with her whole body all day. Sneha was growing. But Jancy was not growing, and she did no shovelling.

Then Appu cried somewhere in the dark. The crying stopped, and Ammu knew he was feeding. Everything Appu was made of came to him through Jancy. Jancy was eating for two bodies, and one of them was growing faster than anyone else in the camp.

In the morning Ammu took the notebook to Saramma. "The ladles were equal," she said. "The people were not."

Saramma read both lists. "So what will you do? It is the same pot."

Ammu had thought about it half the night. "Minnu gets half a ladle at noon and half at four o'clock. Her stomach is as small as her fist. Appachan gets half, mashed, with more kanji water. The ones who shovel get two. Sneha gets one and a half."

"And Jancy?"

"Two. One of them is Appu's."

Saramma counted on her fingers. What the small and the old would leave came to nearly what the others needed. "The pot will do," she said.

That evening the same man stood in the line. He watched Ammu give Jancy a second ladle. "She got two," he said.

"She is feeding two," said Ammu. "Appu takes his share through her."

The man looked at the baby. He looked at Rosamma behind him, with mud up to her elbows. Then he held his own plate back a little. "Half is enough for me," he said. "I sat in the classroom all day."

That night the slop bucket was so light that Ammu carried it with one hand. Minnu had eaten at four, and she was asleep. Rosamma sat on the steps again. This time she was telling a story, and the people around her were laughing.

Ammu rinsed the ladle and hung it on its nail. It was the same ladle, and tomorrow there would be the same two pots. But she would not fill it level for everyone again.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Ammu thought that a fair share was the same amount for everyone. Her two lists showed her that bodies need different amounts. A body needs more when it is growing, when it does heavy work, and when it is feeding a baby. A small child needs little at a time, but often. An old person needs less, and softer food. A balanced diet is measured against one person's body.

- Ask: Ammu gave everyone the same ladle. Why were some people still hungry while others left food?
- Ask: Jancy was not growing and did no heavy work. Why did her body need more food?
- Ask: Minnu was hungry again two hours after the meal. What does that tell you about feeding a small child?
- Ask: Who in your home needs the most food for their size? What makes you think so?

### e1 · The Dark Room Test: how long do your eyes take to see in dim light? (35 min)
- **question:** After bright light, you sit in a nearly dark room. How many seconds do your eyes take to see a white towel, a steel tumbler and a dark book? Does a short flash of light undo the change in both eyes, or only in the eye that saw it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will you see all three things at once, or one after another? How long will the dark book take? If one eye stays covered during a flash of light, will the two eyes see the same afterwards? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Sit in the same bright place for 2 minutes before each try. Use the same room, the same three things and the same places for them. Keep the door closed in the same way each time, so the room is equally dark. The person with the clock stands outside the door, so that no phone screen lights the room. Only one thing changes: who is being tested.
- **safety:** Clear the floor of the dark room, and sit down as soon as you are inside. Do not walk about in the dark. Never look straight at the Sun or at a bright bulb. A grown-up stays just outside the door the whole time.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows: white towel, steel tumbler and dark book. Write the seconds for the child and for the grown-up. Under the table, write what each eye saw after the flash.
- **results table:** The thing | Seconds until the child sees it | Seconds until the grown-up sees it
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the room nearly dark, with only a thin line of light under the door. Put the towel, the tumbler and the book on the floor, about a metre from the stool. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* You will go from bright light into a dark room where I have put three things. Will you see them all at once? How long will the darkest one take? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three things wait in a dark room. Will you see them at once? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why might a dark book take longer to see than a white towel? (good: White things send back more light.; My eyes need time to get used to the dark.; The book is nearly the same shade as the dark room.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Check the room first with your own eyes: after five minutes inside you should just be able to make out the dark book. If you cannot, open the door by a finger's width.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child sits for 2 minutes near a sunny window or under a bright light, looking around the room. They must not look at the Sun or the bulb.  
  *Parent says:* Sit here in the bright light for two minutes. Look around the room, and never straight at the Sun or the bulb.  
  *Child mode:* Sit in bright light for two minutes. Do not look at the Sun.
  - Note: Bright light breaks down the dim-light substance in the eyes. Do not explain this yet. Two minutes is enough.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Lead your child into the dark room and seat them on the stool. Close the door and start the clock. Stand outside. Each time they call out a thing they can see, write the seconds. Stop after 10 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Sit still and keep your eyes open. Call out the moment you can see something, and tell me what it is. I am right outside the door.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still in the dark. Call out when you see each thing. Say what it is.
  - Note: Most children see the white towel within a minute and the tumbler in one to four minutes. The dark book takes several minutes. If everything shows in the first 20 seconds, the room is too bright. Block the gap under the door with a cloth and start again from the bright light.
  - Check: In the Dark Room Test, why does the person with the phone stand outside the door? → **So no phone light reaches the child's eyes**
- **s4 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Stay in the dark room together. Your child closes one eye and presses a palm over it. Switch the light on for 10 seconds, then off. Your child now looks at the book with each eye in turn.  
  *Parent says:* Close your left eye and cover it with your palm. I will switch the light on for ten seconds. Now it is off. Look at the book with your right eye only. Now with your left eye only.  
  *Child mode:* Cover one eye with your palm. The light goes on, then off. Look with each eye.
  - Note: The covered eye still sees the book. The eye that saw the light sees almost nothing for some minutes. Let your child describe it before you say anything.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now your child times you. Sit in the bright light for 2 minutes, then in the dark room. Your child stands outside with the clock and writes your seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Now you time me in the same way. Do you think my eyes will be faster or slower than yours? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Now time your grown-up. Will their eyes be faster or slower? Why?
  - Note: To save time, stop when you see the dark book. Eyes usually get slower at this with age, so a grown-up often takes longer than a child.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your numbers. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Why did only the uncovered eye lose its sight in the dark?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: The covered eye could still see the book, and the other eye could not. What does that tell you? (good: The light changed something inside the eye that saw it.; The change happens in each eye by itself.; The dark did not change. My eye changed.)
  - Ask: A person short of vitamin A waits in the dark, and the book never appears. Why? (good: Their eyes cannot rebuild enough of the substance that catches dim light.; The substance is made from vitamin A.)
  - Note: The reason: the cells that see in dim light are packed with a substance called rhodopsin. Bright light breaks it down. In the dark the eye rebuilds it over 20 to 30 minutes, so dim things appear one by one. The flash broke it down again, but only in the eye that saw the flash. The eye builds rhodopsin from vitamin A. A person short of vitamin A cannot rebuild enough, and stays nearly blind in dim light. That is night blindness. A slow time in this test does not mean your child lacks vitamin A. The time depends mostly on how dark the room is.
  - Think: Drivers at night are told not to look at the headlights of cars coming towards them. Why?
### e2 · The Cooking Water Test: where does the vitamin C go when cabbage is boiled? (40 min)
- **question:** When cabbage is boiled for 10 minutes, does vitamin C move out of the cabbage into the water? Does the same thing happen when raw cabbage only sits in cold water for 10 minutes?

After boiling, add plain water to bring the cooking water back up to 1 cup, because some has boiled away. Cool it to room temperature, because the blue colour fades in hot liquid.   *Child mode:* Strain both waters. Top up the boiled one to one cup. Cool it.
  - Check: In the Cooking Water Test, why is the boiled water topped up to 1 cup again? → **So both waters are the same amount**

### e3 · The Short Shadow Test: when is the Sun high enough for skin to make vitamin D? (25 min)
- **question:** At which times of day is the shadow of an upright pencil shorter than the pencil? For how many hours of the day does that last?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. You will measure at 8 in the morning, 10, 12 noon and 4 in the afternoon. At each time, will the shadow be longer or shorter than the pencil? At which of these times will it be shortest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same pencil each time and stand it straight up, not leaning. Use the same flat place each time. Measure the shadow from the foot of the pencil to the tip of the shadow. Only one thing changes: the time of day.
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun. Each measurement takes two minutes, so there is no need to stay out long. On a terrace, a grown-up comes along.
- **record:** Write the height of the pencil at the top of the page. Draw a table with four rows, one for each time. Write the length of the shadow, and 'shorter' or 'longer'.
- **results table:** Time | Length of the shadow in centimetres | Shorter or longer than the pencil?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At about 8 in the morning, stand the pencil upright in the dough on the flat, sunny place. Measure how tall it stands above the floor. Ask for the guess and the reason. Then measure the first shadow.  
  *Parent says:* We will measure this pencil's shadow four times today. At which times will the shadow be shorter than the pencil? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Stand the pencil up. When will the shadow be shorter than the pencil? Why?
  - Ask: What makes a shadow long or short? (good: How high the Sun is in the sky.; A low Sun makes a long shadow.; The time of day.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. If the place is in shade at 8, measure as soon as the Sun reaches it. Write that time.
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure the shadow again at about 10, at 12 noon and at 4 in the afternoon. Each time, check that the pencil is upright. Write the length, and 'shorter' or 'longer'.  
  *Parent says:* Measure from the foot of the pencil to the tip of the shadow. Is the shadow shorter or longer than the pencil now?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the shadow at 10, 12 and 4. Write shorter or longer each time.
  - Note: If the Sun leaves your spot in the afternoon, use a second spot for the last measurement and note it. On a cloudy day with no clear shadow, wait for a sunny day.
  - Wait: Come back at about 10, 12 noon and 4 in the afternoon
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the four rows together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they found, and why they think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your four shadows. Say what you thought would happen, what you found, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you found. Say why it happened.
  - Check: A pencil's shadow is longer than the pencil. Where is the Sun? → **Low in the sky**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After your child has answered, read them the explanation in the note below. Then ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Skin makes vitamin D only in strong sunlight. Some doctors use the short shadow as a rough sign of that. At which hours could your skin make vitamin D today?  
  *Child mode:* Skin makes vitamin D when shadows are short. When was that today?
  - Ask: A baby is kept indoors behind closed glass windows all winter. Why might the baby get rickets? (good: Glass blocks the rays that make vitamin D.; The baby's skin gets no direct strong sunlight.; Without vitamin D the gut absorbs too little calcium.)
  - Note: The rays that make vitamin D are a kind of ultraviolet light. When the Sun is low, the air absorbs most of them. A shadow shorter than the thing that casts it means the Sun is high enough. This is a rule of thumb, not an exact line. In north India in December, the shadow may stay longer than the pencil all day. That is a real result. People there make little vitamin D in winter. Window glass blocks these rays too. Ten to fifteen minutes of sun on bare arms and legs, a few times a week, is the usual advice. Darker skin needs longer. Scientists still argue about the exact minutes.
  - Think: India is a sunny country, yet many people here are short of vitamin D. What might keep the sunlight off their skin?

1. (mcq) What is a balanced diet?  
   Options: Enough of every nutrient for one person's body / As much food as a person can eat / Only fruit and vegetables / The same plate for every person  
   **Answer:** Enough of every nutrient for one person's body. *Why:* A balanced diet gives one person enough of every nutrient, with water and fibre, and not too much of any. 'As much as you can eat' and 'the same for everyone' both ignore what one body needs.
2. (match) Match each deficiency disease to the nutrient that is missing.  
   **Answer:** Night blindness → Vitamin A; Scurvy → Vitamin C; Rickets → Vitamin D; Anaemia → Iron; Goitre → Iodine; Beriberi → Vitamin B1. *Why:* Each nutrient does its own job, so each lack shows in its own way: in the eyes, the gums, the bones, the blood, the neck or the nerves.
3. (fill) An illness caused by too little of one nutrient for a long time is called a ____ disease.  
   **Answer:** ['deficiency']. *Why:* Deficiency means not having enough. Scurvy, rickets and goitre are deficiency diseases.
4. (truefalse) A person who always eats until they are full cannot have a deficiency disease.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Feeling full tells the body about energy and bulk. A person can be full of rice and still short of iron, iodine or vitamin A. This is hidden hunger.
5. (mcq) Which pair of foods gives better protein together than either gives alone?  
   Options: Rice and dal / Rice and sugar / Chapati and ghee / Biscuits and tea  
   **Answer:** Rice and dal. *Why:* Rice is short of the building unit lysine, and dal is short of methionine. Each fills the other's gap. Sugar, ghee and tea add no protein.
6. (sort) Sort these nutrients. Is each one needed in grams, or in milligrams or less, each day?  
   **Answer:** protein → Grams; carbohydrate → Grams; fat → Grams; iron → Milligrams or less; iodine → Milligrams or less; vitamin C → Milligrams or less. *Why:* Carbohydrates, fats and proteins are fuel and building material, so the body needs grams of them. Vitamins and minerals do special jobs, and tiny amounts are enough.
7. (truefalse) Sunlight that comes through closed window glass makes vitamin D in the skin.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Glass blocks the rays that make vitamin D. The skin needs direct sunlight, with the Sun high in the sky.
8. (mcq) Why is iodine added to salt, and not to sweets?  
   Options: Nearly everyone eats a little salt daily / Salt tastes better with iodine / Sweets already hold iodine / Iodine makes salt whiter  
   **Answer:** Nearly everyone eats a little salt daily. *Why:* Rich and poor eat salt every day in nearly the same small amount. So everyone gets enough iodine and nobody gets far too much.
9. (fill) The red substance in blood that carries oxygen and holds iron is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['haemoglobin']. *Why:* Each haemoglobin molecule holds four atoms of iron. With too little iron the body makes too little haemoglobin, and less oxygen is carried.
10. (write) In the Dark Room Test, the dark book appeared only after several minutes. Explain why eyes need this time.  
   **Answer:** Bright light breaks down rhodopsin, the substance in the eye that catches dim light. In the dark the eye rebuilds it slowly from vitamin A. Dim things appear as more of it is rebuilt.. *Why:* A strong answer says that something in the eye is used up by light and rebuilt in the dark. It names vitamin A as the material.
11. (write) A child of 2 and a grandmother of 70 share one family pot. Write one change for each, with a reason.  
   **Answer:** For the child of 2: mash the food and leave out the chilli. Give small amounts five or six times a day, because the stomach is small. For the grandmother: softer food and a smaller helping, with curd, ragi or sesame, because she needs less energy and more calcium.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a change and ties it to that person's body: a small stomach and fast growth, or weaker teeth and bones.
12. (draw) Draw a balanced plate for yourself for one meal. Label the food that gives energy, protein, iron, calcium and vitamin C.  
   **Answer:** A cereal or millet for energy. A pulse, egg, fish or curd for protein. Greens or a pulse for iron. Curd, milk or ragi for calcium. A fruit, tomato or lemon for vitamin C. One food may carry two labels.. *Why:* A strong drawing uses foods the child really eats, and shows that one food can do more than one job.
13. (perspective) At a family meal, should every person get the same helping?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason: a child of 2 needs about 85 kilocalories for each kilogram and a woman at a desk about 30. Another: equal helpings are simple and stop quarrels, but they leave growing children and hard workers short.. *Why:* A strong answer uses a fact about needs, such as growth, work, pregnancy or age. It treats the other view fairly.
14. (order) Put these in order, from the largest amount a child needs each day to the smallest.  
   **Answer:** ['Protein', 'Vitamin C', 'Iron', 'Iodine']. *Why:* A child needs about 30 grams of protein and 50 milligrams of vitamin C. They need about 16 to 28 milligrams of iron and 100 micrograms of iodine. A milligram is a thousandth of a gram, and a microgram is a thousandth of a milligram.
15. (mcq) A child sees well at noon but stumbles in a dim room. Which nutrient may be short?  
   Options: Vitamin A / Vitamin C / Calcium / Iodine  
   **Answer:** Vitamin A. *Why:* The eye builds its dim-light substance from vitamin A. Trouble seeing at dusk, called night blindness, is the first sign of too little.
### Why each habit works (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does a squeeze of lemon on kala chana help the body?  
   Options: Vitamin C helps the gut absorb iron / Lemon adds a lot of protein / Lemon removes the fibre / Sour food kills all microbes  
   **Answer:** Vitamin C helps the gut absorb iron. *Why:* The iron in plant foods is hard to absorb. Vitamin C in the same meal changes the iron into a form that crosses the gut wall more easily.
2. (mcq) Boiled cabbage water cleared the blue liquid in 2 teaspoons. Cold cabbage water took 7. What follows?  
   Options: More vitamin C moved into the boiled water / Boiling made new vitamin C / Cold water destroys vitamin C / The cabbage held no vitamin C  
   **Answer:** More vitamin C moved into the boiled water. *Why:* Fewer teaspoons means more vitamin C in each teaspoon. Heat softened the cells of the leaf, so more of the vitamin leaked into the water.
3. (mcq) A girl of 36 kilograms needs 2,060 kilocalories. A woman of 55 kilograms needs 1,660. Who needs more for each kilogram?  
   Options: The girl: about 57 against about 30 / The woman: about 57 against about 30 / They need the same / It cannot be worked out  
   **Answer:** The girl: about 57 against about 30. *Why:* 2,060 divided by 36 is about 57. 1,660 divided by 55 is about 30. The girl is growing, so each kilogram of her body needs more.
4. (truefalse) Polishing rice until it is white removes most of its vitamin B1.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Vitamin B1 sits in the outer layers and the germ of the grain. Polishing takes these off as bran. This is why beriberi spread with machine-polished rice.
5. (truefalse) Taking extra vitamin A tablets every day makes healthy eyes see better and better.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Once the body has enough vitamin A, more does not help. Far too much is a poison that harms the liver and bones.
6. (odd) Three of these help the body get more iron from a meal. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Lemon juice on the food / Tomato in the dal / Cooking in an iron kadai / Tea with the meal  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Vitamin C from lemon and tomato helps iron cross the gut wall, and an iron kadai adds a little iron. Substances in tea hold on to iron, so less is absorbed.
7. (count) Salt holds 15 micrograms of iodine in each gram. How many micrograms of iodine are in 6 grams of salt?  
   **Answer:** 90. *Why:* 6 grams hold 6 times 15, which is 90 micrograms.
8. (sort) Sort these signs. Does each belong to kwashiorkor or to marasmus?  
   **Answer:** swollen feet and face → Kwashiorkor; peeling skin → Kwashiorkor; very thin, with loose skin → Marasmus; no swelling → Marasmus. *Why:* In kwashiorkor water gathers in the feet, legs and face, and the skin and hair change. In marasmus the child gets too little of everything and becomes very thin, with no swelling.
9. (match) Match each kitchen habit to the reason it works.  
   **Answer:** Sprouting moong → The growing seed makes vitamin C; Fermenting idli batter → Microbes add B vitamins; Cooking greens in little water with a lid → Less vitamin is lost to water; Eating dal with rice → Each fills the other's protein gap; Using iodised salt → The thyroid gets its iodine. *Why:* None of these habits costs much. Each one raises the nourishment the body gets from the same cheap food.
10. (write) Explain how a person can be overweight and anaemic at the same time.  
   **Answer:** Fried snacks, sweets and sweet drinks give a lot of energy but very little iron. The extra energy is stored as body fat. The body still cannot make enough haemoglobin without iron.. *Why:* A strong answer separates energy from micronutrients. Too much of one does not make up for too little of the other.
11. (write) After the flash in the Dark Room Test, only the covered eye still saw the book. What does this show?  
   **Answer:** The light changed something inside the eye that it entered. The covered eye kept its dim-light substance, and the open eye lost it. So the change happens in each eye, and the room itself did not change.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the two eyes as a comparison: same room, same moment, and only the light differed.
12. (write) The sea holds iodine, and hill soils hold little. Explain why goitre was common in the Himalayas, and what changed that.  
   **Answer:** Rain washes iodine out of hill soils, so crops grown there hold little, and sea fish are far away. People's thyroid glands swelled as they tried to catch more iodine. Adding iodine to salt gave everyone enough.. *Why:* A strong answer links the soil to the food and the food to the thyroid. It links the cure to a food that everyone eats.
13. (perspective) Tablets or food: what is the best way to end anaemia in India?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For tablets: a pregnant woman needs more iron than food easily gives. For food: it carries many nutrients together, and too much iron in tablets is harmful. A strong answer may say both are needed for different people.. *Why:* This is a real question that health planners argue about. A strong answer says who needs what, and why.
14. (label) On an outline of a body, label where each of these shows: night blindness, goitre, rickets, scurvy.  
   **Answer:** ['Night blindness: the eyes', 'Goitre: the front of the neck, where the thyroid gland sits', 'Rickets: the leg bones', 'Scurvy: the gums']. *Why:* Each deficiency shows in the part of the body that depends most on the missing nutrient.
15. (fill) The substance in the eye that catches dim light, and is built from vitamin A, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['rhodopsin']. *Why:* Rhodopsin fills the rod cells of the retina. Bright light breaks it down, and the eye rebuilds it in the dark.
### Testing ideas about food (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A ship's doctor gave sick sailors the same meals, with a different addition for each pair. Why the same meals?  
   Options: So only the addition could explain a recovery / So the cook had less work / So the sailors would not quarrel / So the food lasted longer  
   **Answer:** So only the addition could explain a recovery. *Why:* This was James Lind's test in 1747. Everything else was the same. So the quick recovery of the pair given oranges and lemons could only be due to the fruit.
2. (mcq) In a test of iodised salt, some villages were given plain salt. Why?  
   Options: To compare with villages given iodised salt / Because iodine had run out / Because plain salt is cheaper / To punish those villages  
   **Answer:** To compare with villages given iodised salt. *Why:* Goitre might have fallen for some other reason, such as better roads or more food. Only a comparison group shows what the iodine itself did.
3. (mcq) Which is the best evidence that tea with a meal reduces the iron absorbed?  
   Options: Less traced iron reached the blood with tea / Tea is dark in colour / Tea drinkers say they feel tired / Tea plants grow in iron-poor soil  
   **Answer:** Less traced iron reached the blood with tea. *Why:* Scientists gave meals holding iron atoms they could trace, and measured how much reached the blood. A measurement beats a guess from colour or from feelings.
4. (truefalse) A slow time in the Dark Room Test proves that a child is short of vitamin A.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The time depends mostly on how dark the room is and how bright the light was before. Age matters too. Only a doctor can tell whether vitamin A is short.
5. (truefalse) In north India in December, a pencil's shadow can stay longer than the pencil all day.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In winter the Sun stays low in the northern sky all day. Shadows stay long, and skin makes little vitamin D.
6. (count) RICE holds 2 rajma and 6 chana. DAL holds 6 and 2. How many bricks from 3 RICE and 1 DAL?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* Rajma: 3 times 2, plus 6, is 12. Chana: 3 times 6, plus 2, is 20. A brick needs one of each, so the 12 rajma set the limit. Eight chana are left over.
7. (order) Put these steps in order, from a leaf to seeing in dim light.  
   **Answer:** ['A plant makes carotene', 'A person eats the leaf', 'The gut wall splits carotene into vitamin A', 'Blood carries vitamin A to the eye', 'The eye builds rhodopsin from vitamin A', 'Rhodopsin catches dim light']. *Why:* The carotene is made by the plant. The body changes it in two places: the gut wall makes vitamin A, and the eye builds rhodopsin.
8. (fill) Adding a nutrient to a common food, so that everyone who eats it gets enough, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['fortification']. *Why:* Iodised salt is the best-known fortified food. Some oil and milk are fortified with vitamins A and D.
9. (write) Many people believe spinach is the best food for iron. What would you measure to check this?  
   **Answer:** Measure how much iron from the food reaches the blood. Compare it with other foods such as bajra, chana or egg. The iron a food holds and the iron the body absorbs are different amounts.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that 'holds iron' and 'gives the body iron' are two claims. The second needs a measurement in a person.
10. (write) Design a fair test of this question: does a lid on the vessel save vitamin C when cabbage is boiled?  
   **Answer:** Boil two equal cups of cabbage in equal water for the same time, one with a lid and one without. Top up both waters to the same amount and cool them. Test each with the blue liquid and count the teaspoons. Repeat once.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the lid and keeps the cabbage, water and time the same. It says what will be counted.
11. (write) Villages lower down a stream had more goitre. What test could tell a germ in the water from a missing nutrient?  
   **Answer:** Give some villages iodine and leave their water unchanged. If goitre falls there and not in the other villages, the cause was the missing iodine. Boiling the water in some villages would test the germ idea.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing at a time and keeps a comparison group. The same pattern can have two explanations, and only a test separates them.
12. (write) India grows enough grain for everyone, yet many people are anaemic. Give two reasons from this unit.  
   **Answer:** Grain gives energy but little iron that the body can absorb. Pulses, greens and millets cost more or are grown less than before. Tea with meals reduces the iron absorbed. In many families women and girls eat last and least.. *Why:* A strong answer separates enough energy from enough micronutrients, and gives reasons about food, cost or habits.
13. (perspective) From the 1960s India grew far more wheat and rice. Was that a full success?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. The farmer is right that famine ended. The scientist is right that more than half of India's women are still anaemic. The millet farmer is right that ragi has about three times the calcium of milk.. *Why:* All three speakers describe something true. A strong answer weighs ending famine against ending hidden hunger.
14. (draw) Draw one rod cell of the eye twice: after bright light, and after 20 minutes of dark. Show the rhodopsin.  
   **Answer:** After bright light: a long thin cell with almost no dots of rhodopsin. After 20 minutes of dark: the same cell packed with dots. An arrow shows vitamin A arriving in the blood to build them.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the same cell in two states, and shows where the material for rebuilding comes from.
15. (match) Match each observation to what it shows.  
   **Answer:** Only sailors given citrus fruit recovered → A missing food substance can cause disease; A covered eye still sees after a flash → Light changes something inside the eye; One twin of a pair gets kwashiorkor → Diet alone does not explain kwashiorkor; A pencil's shadow is shortest at noon → The Sun is highest at noon; Two rice slips build only 4 bricks → The scarcest unit sets the limit. *Why:* Each observation is evidence, and each conclusion says only what that evidence supports.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which meal is the most balanced for a child of ten?  
   Options: Ragi dosa, sambar, greens, curd and a guava / White rice with ghee and sugar / Chips, biscuits and a sweet drink / Three kinds of fruit and nothing else  
   **Answer:** Ragi dosa, sambar, greens, curd and a guava. *Why:* The first meal has a millet, a pulse, a green vegetable, curd and a vitamin C fruit. The others each leave out protein, iron or energy.
2. (mcq) A person's gums bleed and small wounds do not heal. Which nutrient is most likely missing?  
   Options: Vitamin C / Iodine / Vitamin D / Calcium  
   **Answer:** Vitamin C. *Why:* Vitamin C helps build collagen, the strong threads in gums, skin and blood vessels. Without it they weaken. The disease is scurvy.
3. (mcq) Which cheap food helps most to prevent night blindness?  
   Options: Drumstick leaves / Polished white rice / Sugar / Tea  
   **Answer:** Drumstick leaves. *Why:* Drumstick leaves are rich in carotene, which the body turns into vitamin A. Rice, sugar and tea hold almost none.
4. (mcq) What is hidden hunger?  
   Options: Lacking a vitamin or mineral while feeling full / Feeling hungry between meals / Hiding food to eat later / Eating too fast to taste the food  
   **Answer:** Lacking a vitamin or mineral while feeling full. *Why:* Fullness reports energy and bulk. A person can feel full and still be short of iron, iodine or vitamin A.
5. (mcq) Who needs the most energy for each kilogram of body weight?  
   Options: A child of 2 years / A woman who works at a desk / A grandmother of 70 years / A man who works at a desk  
   **Answer:** A child of 2 years. *Why:* A child of 2 needs about 85 kilocalories for each kilogram, because they are growing fast. A grown-up at a desk needs about 30.
6. (mcq) Why does a breastfeeding mother need extra food?  
   Options: She makes milk that feeds a growing baby / Her stomach has grown bigger / She sleeps less at night / Milk needs extra salt  
   **Answer:** She makes milk that feeds a growing baby. *Why:* She makes about 750 millilitres of milk a day. Everything in it comes from her own food and body, and it costs her about 600 kilocalories.
7. (mcq) Why should the water from boiled vegetables go into the dish?  
   Options: Vitamins have dissolved into it / It holds most of the protein / It holds all the fibre / It makes the dish cook faster  
   **Answer:** Vitamins have dissolved into it. *Why:* Vitamin C and the B vitamins dissolve in water. Pouring the water away throws them out. Rasam, sambar and kootu keep it.
8. (mcq) Why do rice and dal together give better protein than rice alone?  
   Options: Each supplies a building unit the other lacks / Dal makes the rice softer / Rice has no protein at all / Together they hold more water  
   **Answer:** Each supplies a building unit the other lacks. *Why:* Rice is short of lysine and dal is short of methionine. The body builds with complete sets, so together they build more.
9. (mcq) Why are iron tablets taken only on the advice of a health worker?  
   Options: Too much iron is poisonous / Iron tablets taste bitter / Iron works only at night / Tablets hold no real iron  
   **Answer:** Too much iron is poisonous. *Why:* The body needs a measured amount. A large dose of iron can poison a child. Health workers give the dose that suits the person.
10. (mcq) In a fair test of two ways of cooking cabbage, what must stay the same?  
   Options: The cabbage, the water and the time / Only the colour of the vessel / Nothing needs to stay the same / Only the person who cooks  
   **Answer:** The cabbage, the water and the time. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing. If the amount of cabbage, the water and the time are equal, any difference comes from the cooking method.
11. (mcq) Goitre fell only in villages that were given iodised salt. What did that show?  
   Options: Too little iodine was the main cause / Goitre spreads from person to person / Plain salt causes goitre / Hill air causes goitre  
   **Answer:** Too little iodine was the main cause. *Why:* The villages differed in one thing, the iodine in their salt. So the fall in goitre was due to iodine. Plain salt did not cause goitre. It only failed to prevent it.

## Applications
- Iodised salt carries iodine to nearly every home in India. Since it spread, goitre has become rare in places where it was once common.
- Health workers put vitamin A drops in the mouths of young children twice a year. The drops protect their eyes and help them fight infections.
- Pregnant women and schoolchildren are given iron and folic acid tablets at health centres and schools, to prevent anaemia.
- Anganwadi workers weigh small children every month and mark a growth chart. A line that stops rising is a warning before any illness shows.
- School lunches in India are planned by nutrition rules: a cereal, a pulse, vegetables and oil. Tamil Nadu has served free school lunches for decades, and many schools add eggs.
- Plant breeders have bred bajra with more iron and wheat with more zinc. Farmers grow them like any other variety.
- Hospitals plan a separate diet for each patient: soft food after an operation, extra protein for healing, and less salt for a weak heart.

## Spotted around us
- The word 'iodised' on the salt packet in your kitchen.
- A small +F sign on a packet of oil, milk, atta or rice. It means a vitamin or a mineral has been added.
- A drumstick tree in a yard or beside a road. The leaves are one of the cheapest sources of carotene, iron and calcium.
- Sprouted moong or chana sold in small heaps at a vegetable market.
- A black iron kadai or dosa tawa in an older kitchen. Food cooked in iron picks up a little iron.
- A baby being given an oil massage in the morning sun on a verandah or terrace.
- The moment your eyes go blind when the lights go off in a cinema, and then slowly see the seats.
- A health worker at a school handing out a red or blue tablet once a week.

## Open questions
- **What exactly causes kwashiorkor?** Too little protein does not explain why one child gets it and another on the same food does not. Gut microbes, infections and missing protective nutrients are all being studied.
- **Why are so many people in sunny India short of vitamin D?** Indoor life, covered skin, air pollution and darker skin may each play a part. Nobody knows how much each matters, and experts disagree about what level counts as too low.
- **How much of each nutrient does one particular person need?** The numbers in tables are averages with a safety margin. Bodies, genes and gut microbes differ, and scientists are only beginning to measure by how much.
- **Do vitamin tablets help people who already eat a varied diet?** Large studies have mostly found little benefit for well-fed adults. Scientists still argue about which groups gain from them.
- **Can crops bred or changed to hold more vitamins and minerals end hidden hunger?** Such crops help in trials. Whether enough farmers grow them and enough families eat them is still being tested. People also disagree about some of the methods.

## Connected maths
- In the Dark Room Test, the white towel appeared after 40 seconds and the dark book after 380 seconds. How many times as long did the dark book take? **9 and a half times as long.**. 380 divided by 40 is 9.5. So the dark book took nine and a half times as long to appear as the white towel. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A child of 2 weighs 13 kilograms and needs 1,110 kilocalories a day. A woman weighs 55 kilograms and needs 1,660. Who needs more for each kilogram, and about how many times as much? **The child: about 85 against about 30, which is nearly 3 times as much.**. 1,110 divided by 13 is about 85. 1,660 divided by 55 is about 30. 85 divided by 30 is about 2.8. So for each kilogram, the child needs nearly 3 times as much. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- A salt packet holds 30 micrograms of iodine in each gram. A child needs 100 micrograms a day. How many grams of this salt hold a day's iodine? **About 3 grams, which is a little more than half a level teaspoon.**. 100 divided by 30 is about 3.3. So about 3 grams of salt hold 100 micrograms of iodine. A level teaspoon of salt is about 5 to 6 grams. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L5`)
- Groundnuts cost 14 rupees for 100 grams and hold 24 grams of protein. Paneer costs 40 rupees for 100 grams and holds 19 grams. How much protein does 10 rupees buy in each? **About 17 grams in groundnuts, and about 5 grams in paneer.**. Groundnuts: 24 divided by 14 is about 1.7 grams for each rupee, so 10 rupees buy about 17 grams. Paneer: 19 divided by 40 is about 0.5 grams for each rupee, so 10 rupees buy about 5 grams. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L5`)
- A RICE slip holds 2 rajma and 6 chana. A DAL slip holds 6 rajma and 2 chana. A brick needs one of each. Which builds more bricks: 4 RICE slips, or 3 RICE slips and 1 DAL slip? **3 RICE and 1 DAL: 12 bricks against 8.**. 4 RICE slips hold 8 rajma and 24 chana, so they build 8 bricks. 3 RICE and 1 DAL hold 12 rajma and 20 chana, so they build 12 bricks. - In the Cooking Water Test, boiled cabbage water cleared the blue liquid in 2 teaspoons. Cold cabbage water took 7. Plain water did not clear it in 10. Which liquid held the most vitamin C in each teaspoon? Explain why plain water was tested at all. **The boiled cabbage water held the most. Plain water was the comparison.**. Fewer teaspoons means more vitamin C in each one, so 2 teaspoons beats 7. Plain water shows that simply adding liquid does not clear the blue. Without it, nobody could be sure the cabbage did anything. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In the Short Shadow Test, a pencil stood 18 centimetres tall. The shadows were 40, 14, 4 and 30 centimetres long at 8, 10, 12 and 4 o'clock. At which times was the shadow shorter than the pencil? What fraction of the pencil's height was the noon shadow? **At 10 and at 12. The noon shadow was two ninths of the pencil's height.**. 14 and 4 are less than 18, and 40 and 30 are more. The noon shadow is 4 out of 18. Divide both by 2 to get 2 out of 9. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is a deficiency disease? → An illness caused by too little of one nutrient for a long time.
- Which vitamin do the eyes need to see in dim light? → Vitamin A.
- Which mineral does haemoglobin need? → Iron.
- What is goitre, and what prevents it? → A swelling of the thyroid in the neck. Iodised salt prevents it.
- Why do rice and dal go well together? → Each supplies a protein building unit that the other lacks.
- Who needs more food for each kilogram: a child of 2 or a grown-up? → The child of 2, because they are growing fast.
- What is hidden hunger? → Being short of a vitamin or mineral while feeling full.
- Where does vitamin C go when vegetables are boiled? → Much of it moves into the cooking water.
