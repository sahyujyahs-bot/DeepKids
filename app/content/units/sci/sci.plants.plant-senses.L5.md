# How plants send and receive signals, and whether plants feel, learn and remember
`sci.plants.plant-senses.L5`

**Summary.** When a caterpillar chews a maize leaf, tiny wasps arrive and lay eggs in it, though nobody can see it. How do the wasps find it? In this unit you will sniff for a torn leaf, stroke moong seedlings every day to see if they grow differently, and trace how plant signals travel.

**Intro.** *A caterpillar bites one leaf of a plant. Two minutes later, leaves on the far side of the plant begin to switch on their defences. No caterpillar has touched those leaves. Nerves are one kind of wiring, and a plant has her own kind. So how did the far leaves get the news?* Scientists filmed the answer in 2018, and you will read it in this unit. The news travelled inside the plant as a wave of electric charge and chemicals. A plant also sends news out of their body. Some goes into the air as smells, and some goes into the soil from the roots. Other living beings receive that news: neighbouring plants, insects, fungi and you. You start with your own nose. In the first activity, the Torn Leaf Test, you find out whether a leaf gives off something new when it is damaged.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what a signal is, and name the sender, the carrier and the receiver in a plant example.
1. By the end I can describe three paths for plant signals: inside the plant, through the air, and from the roots into the soil.
2. By the end I can explain what a mycorrhiza is, and what the plant and the fungus each give.
3. By the end I can run a fair touch test with a control cup, ten seedlings in each cup, a mean and a chart.
4. By the end I can explain how a short touch can change a plant's growth for days.
5. By the end I can give evidence that plants keep information about their past, and say why scientists argue about the words learn, remember and feel.
6. By the end I can say what an experiment must rule out before we accept its claim, and why other scientists must repeat it.
7. By the end I can say what is still unknown about fungal networks and about what plants feel.

## Terms
- **Stimulus**: A stimulus is anything a living being can sense: a touch, light, a chemical, warmth or damage. For more than one we say stimuli. *e.g.* A caterpillar's bite is a stimulus for a maize plant.
- **Response**: A response is what a living being does because of a stimulus. *e.g.* A touch-me-not folding their leaflets after a touch is a response.
- **Signal**: A signal is something that carries news from one place to another and changes what the receiver does. Every signal has a sender, a carrier and a receiver. *e.g.* The smell of a chewed maize leaf is a signal. The leaf is the sender, the air is the carrier and a wasp is one receiver.
- **Receptor**: A receptor is a tiny part of a cell that fits one kind of stimulus, as a lock fits one key. When the right thing arrives, the receptor starts a change inside the cell. *e.g.* Your nose has receptors for smell chemicals. A plant's cells have receptors for light, touch and many chemicals.
- **Hormone**: A hormone is a chemical that one part of a living body makes and another part responds to. It is a signal that stays inside the body. *e.g.* Jasmonic acid is a plant hormone. Damaged leaves make it, and it switches on the making of defence chemicals.
- **Electrical signal**: An electrical signal is a wave of electric charge that passes from cell to cell. Charged particles called ions move in and out of each cell in turn. *e.g.* A touch-me-not leaf folds because an electrical signal runs along it at 2 to 3 centimetres a second.
- **Volatile**: A volatile is a chemical that turns into a gas easily and drifts through the air. Every smell is made of volatiles. *e.g.* The smell of freshly cut grass is a mix of volatiles that grass leaves make when they are cut.
- **Root exudate**: A root exudate is any chemical that a living root lets out into the soil. Roots let out sugars, acids and signal chemicals. *e.g.* Root exudates from a jowar plant tell nearby fungi that a root is close.
- **Hypha**: A hypha is one thread of a fungus. It is a tube far thinner than a hair. For more than one we say hyphae (say 'high-fee'). *e.g.* The white fluff on old bread is a tangle of hyphae.
- **Mycelium**: A mycelium is the whole body of a fungus, made of all their hyphae together. It lives hidden in soil, wood or food. *e.g.* A mushroom is only the fruit of a fungus. The mycelium below the ground is the main body.
- **Mycorrhiza**: A mycorrhiza is a partnership between the roots of a plant and a fungus. The word means 'fungus-root'. Say 'my-ko-rye-za'. *e.g.* A mango tree's roots are joined to fungi. The fungi bring water and minerals, and the tree gives them sugar.
- **Common mycorrhizal network**: A common mycorrhizal network is a web of fungal threads that is joined to the roots of two or more plants at once. *e.g.* In a pot, two tomato plants can be joined underground by the threads of one fungus.
- **Thigmomorphogenesis**: Thigmomorphogenesis is the change in a plant's shape caused by touch or shaking. Touched plants grow shorter and thicker. Say 'thig-mo-mor-fo-jen-e-sis'. *e.g.* A tree on a windy hilltop is shorter and thicker than the same kind of tree in a sheltered valley.
- **Habituation**: Habituation means responding less and less to a stimulus that keeps coming and does no harm. It is the simplest kind of learning. *e.g.* You stop noticing the sound of the ceiling fan after a few minutes.
- **Priming**: Priming means that a plant who has met a danger once responds faster and more strongly the next time. *e.g.* A maize seedling that has smelled a damaged neighbour makes defence chemicals faster when a caterpillar arrives.
- **Control**: A control is the part of an experiment where you do not make the change you are testing. Everything else is kept the same, so you have something to compare with. *e.g.* In the Touch Test, the cup of seedlings that nobody strokes is the control.
- **Replicate**: To replicate an experiment is to do it again, often in another laboratory, to see whether the same result comes out. *e.g.* In 2020 a scientist tried to replicate a test of learning in pea plants, and did not get the same result.

## Explanations
### What a signal is · *defined*
**Definition:** A signal is something that carries news from a sender to a receiver and changes what the receiver does. The thing that carries it is called the carrier.

A plant senses many kinds of stimulus: light, gravity, touch, water, warmth, chemicals and damage. To sense a stimulus, a cell needs a receptor that fits it. But sensing in one place is not enough. A root that finds dry soil is far from the leaves that lose the water. So the news must travel, and whatever carries it is a signal. To understand any signal, ask three questions. Who sent it? What carried it? Who received it, and what did they do then? The third question matters most. A smell that changes nobody's behaviour is not acting as a signal. The same smell becomes a signal when someone has a receptor for it and responds.
- e.g. A caterpillar bites a leaf. The bitten leaf is the sender, a wave of electric charge is the carrier, and far leaves are the receivers. They respond by getting their defences ready.
- e.g. A ripe banana gives off the gas ethylene. The air carries it, and a raw mango nearby responds by ripening sooner.
- e.g. In the Torn Leaf Test, the torn leaf is the sender, the air is the carrier and your nose is the receiver.
### Signals inside a plant · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside a plant, news travels as electrical signals that pass from cell to cell, and as hormones that move in the plant's sap.

Every living cell keeps more of some charged particles inside than outside. Charged particles are called ions. Because of this, there is a small electric voltage across the skin of every cell. In a plant cell the voltage is between one and two tenths of a volt. When a caterpillar bites a leaf, the broken cells spill a chemical called glutamate. The next cells have receptors for glutamate. Those cells open tiny gates, and calcium ions rush in. That change makes the cells after them do the same, so a wave travels along the veins. In 2018 Masatsugu Toyota and Simon Gilroy filmed this wave in thale cress, a small relative of mustard. They used plants that glow wherever calcium rises. The wave moved at about 1 millimetre a second and reached far leaves within one or two minutes. There the leaves began to make the hormone jasmonic acid, which switches on the making of defence chemicals. This is much slower than your nerves, which carry signals at up to 100 metres a second. But a caterpillar takes many minutes to eat one leaf, so the news arrives in time.
- e.g. A touch-me-not folds because an electrical signal runs along the leaf at 2 to 3 centimetres a second. J.C. Bose measured such signals in Kolkata more than a hundred years ago.
- e.g. A Venus flytrap's trap snaps shut when an electrical signal crosses it. You can read about this under 'Plants keep information about their past'.
- e.g. When roots meet dry soil, they send a hormone up to the leaves. The leaves respond by closing their tiny air holes, which saves water.
### Signals through the air · *defined*
**Definition:** A damaged plant gives off volatiles, which are chemicals that drift through the air as smells. Other living beings that have receptors for those volatiles respond to them.

Some leaf smells are stored in advance. Curry leaves, tulsi and mint keep scented oils in tiny pockets, and tearing the leaf bursts the pockets. Other smells are made fresh. Within seconds of being cut, the cells of a grass blade or a palak leaf start making 'green leaf volatiles', the smell of cut grass. A cabbage or radish leaf makes sharp mustard oils only when their cells are broken. Hours after a caterpillar starts feeding, a plant makes a further mix of volatiles. In 1990 Ted Turlings and his team showed that maize plants do this when caterpillar spit touches the wound. A plain cut gives a much weaker smell. Tiny wasps follow that smell and lay their eggs inside the caterpillars. The young wasps then eat the caterpillars. Other plants receive these volatiles too. In 2000 Richard Karban found that wild tobacco plants growing near clipped sagebrush bushes were eaten less by insects. Later tests showed why. Plants that receive damage volatiles get their own defences ready, which is called priming.
- e.g. The smell of a freshly mown cricket ground is the grass leaves' damage volatiles.
- e.g. Dodder is a yellow thread-like plant that lives on other plants. In 2006 Consuelo De Moraes and her team showed that dodder seedlings grow towards the smell of a tomato plant.
- e.g. A lima bean plant uses their own damage volatiles as an inside signal too. The smell travels through the air from a damaged leaf to other leaves of the same plant.
### Mycorrhiza: plants and fungi live joined together · *defined*
**Definition:** A mycorrhiza is a partnership between a plant's roots and a fungus. The fungus brings water and minerals to the plant, and the plant gives sugars and fats to the fungus.

The body of a fungus is a mass of fine threads called hyphae. Each thread is many times thinner than a hair, so it can enter gaps in the soil that are too narrow for any root. In a mycorrhiza the threads grow into or around the plant's roots and far out into the soil. Why do the two live together? A fungus has no chlorophyll, so they cannot make food from light. A plant can. But a plant's roots reach only a small part of the soil. Phosphorus is a mineral every cell needs, and it hardly moves in soil. The fungal threads reach it and carry it back. In return the plant gives the fungus food, often about a tenth of all the food the plant makes. More than eight of every ten kinds of land plant live this way, including mango, coconut, banana, ragi, pine and sal. A few, such as mustard, cabbage and palak, do without fungi. Fossils about 400 million years old show fungal threads inside some of the first land plants. Many scientists think that fungi helped plants to live on land before plants had proper roots.
- e.g. Rugra is a round mushroom sold in the markets of Ranchi in the monsoon. It is the fruit of a fungus that lives joined to the roots of sal trees. People gather it from sal forests.
- e.g. An orchid seed is as fine as dust and carries almost no food. In the wild, an orchid seed can grow only after a fungus enters and feeds them.
- e.g. In 1885 the German scientist Albert Bernhard Frank gave mycorrhiza its name. Many scientists then thought fungi on roots were a disease. Frank said the fungi were feeding the trees, and later experiments showed he was right.
### Signals from roots into the soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Roots let out chemicals called root exudates into the soil. Fungi, bacteria and other plants that have receptors for those chemicals respond to them.

A plant spends real food on root exudates, so these chemicals must matter to the plant. Some of them call partners. A root that is short of phosphorus lets out chemicals called strigolactones. The threads of mycorrhizal fungi sense them and branch towards the root. Kohki Akiyama and his team showed this in 2005. The roots of beans and other dals let out different chemicals that call the bacteria who live in their root swellings. But any signal put into the soil can be received by others too. Witchweed is a small plant that lives by tapping the roots of jowar, bajra and sugarcane. A witchweed seed can lie in the soil for years. They sprout only when they sense strigolactones, because that means a root is close enough to reach. So the plant's call to a helpful fungus is also the wake-up call for a plant that will steal from them. Roots also pass on news of trouble. In 2011 Omer Falik and his team in Israel kept one pea plant short of water. Neighbours who shared soil with that plant's roots closed their leaf holes within an hour, though they had plenty of water themselves.
- e.g. Farmers sometimes sow cotton in a field full of witchweed seeds. Cotton roots make the seeds sprout, but witchweed cannot live on cotton, so the seedlings die.
- e.g. A sandalwood tree's roots join on to the roots of neighbouring plants and take water and minerals from them. So growers plant a sandalwood seedling beside a host plant such as tur.
- e.g. A sea rocket is a small beach plant. In one study from 2007, sea rockets grew more roots beside strangers than beside sisters and brothers. Other scientists are still testing whether plants can tell family from strangers.
### Do food and warnings pass between plants through fungal threads? · *debated*
**Definition:** A common mycorrhizal network is a web of fungal threads joined to two or more plants. Scientists agree that such networks exist. They disagree about how much food and news passes through them from plant to plant in a real forest.

Some facts are settled. Ghost plants are white, with no chlorophyll at all, so they cannot make food. They get all their sugar from fungi that are joined to nearby trees. So sugar that a tree made can reach another plant through a fungus. In 1997 Suzanne Simard fed marked carbon dioxide to birch trees in a Canadian forest. Some of the marked carbon turned up in fir trees nearby, and shaded firs received more. The science journal Nature called this the 'wood-wide web'. In pots, a warning seems to pass as well. In 2010 Yuan Yuan Song and her team joined tomato plants by fungal threads and infected one plant with a leaf disease. The healthy partners switched on their defence genes. In 2013 Zdenka Babikova and her team did a similar test with bean plants and aphids. When a fine mesh stopped the threads from joining the plants, no warning arrived. The argument is about real forests. Does enough food move to matter to a seedling? Does the news go through the threads, or through the soil water beside them? Does a tree 'send' anything, or does the fungus move food to suit themself? Nobody has shown the answers yet.
- e.g. A ghost plant under Himalayan forest trees is white from top to bottom. All their food comes through fungal threads from the trees around them.
- e.g. In the game Under the Forest, you play the idea that big trees feed shaded seedlings through the threads. Then you play the critics' idea and compare.
- e.g. To test the path, scientists bury plants in bags of mesh. One mesh lets fungal threads through but stops roots. A finer mesh stops the threads but lets water through.
- **Suzanne Simard, a forest scientist in Canada:** Old trees are joined by fungi to many younger ones. Food and warnings move through the threads, and seedlings in deep shade do better when they are joined to the network. Foresters should leave the big old trees standing.
- **Justine Karst, Melanie Jones and Jason Hoeksema, who reviewed the forest studies in 2023:** The networks are real, but the popular story has run ahead of the evidence. Very few forest studies have mapped a network. The amounts of food found moving were small. In many tests, seedlings joined to a network grew no better, and some grew worse.
- **Toby Kiers and other scientists who study the fungi:** Ask what the fungus gains. A fungus is a living being with their own needs, not a pipe. In laboratory tests, fungi send more minerals to the roots that pay them more sugar. Food may move because it suits the fungus.
### How a touch changes the way a plant grows · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant that is touched, bent or shaken again and again grows shorter and thicker. This change is called thigmomorphogenesis.

In the Touch Test, each stroke lasts about a second, yet the seedlings change over a week. Here is what happens in between. Plant cells have touch receptors in their skins. When a stem bends, these receptors open and calcium ions flow into the cells. Within about half an hour, that switches on a group of genes called touch genes. A gene is an instruction for building one of the cell's working parts. Janet Braam found the touch genes in 1990, by accident. She sprayed plants with a hormone, and found that the plants sprayed with plain water changed as well. The spray itself was a touch. The touched plant then makes less gibberellin, the hormone that makes stems grow long. The plant also builds thicker cell walls. Why would this help a plant? A stem that keeps being bent is probably growing in a windy place. A short, thick stem is much harder to snap than a tall, thin one. So the plant changes their growth to suit the place they are in. The scientist Mark Jaffe gave the change its long name in 1973.
- e.g. Trees on a windy sea coast or hilltop grow shorter and thicker than the same kind of tree in a sheltered place.
- e.g. In Japan, farmers have long walked over young wheat and barley plants in winter. The trodden plants grow sturdier. The custom is called mugifumi.
- e.g. A young tree tied tightly to a stake cannot sway. The trunk stays thin, and the tree may fall when the stake is taken away.
### Plants keep information about their past · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant's response today can depend on what happened to the plant minutes, weeks or months ago. So the plant's body holds information about the past.

Here are three cases that scientists agree on. The first lasts seconds. A Venus flytrap is a plant that catches insects in a trap made of two leaf halves. One touch on a trap hair does not shut the trap. A second touch within about 30 seconds does. The first touch raises the calcium inside the trap cells, and the calcium fades slowly. A second touch that comes in time adds enough to pass the level that shuts the trap. So the trap counts to two, and avoids closing on a raindrop. The second case lasts months. Cabbage and carrot plants flower only after they have been through weeks of cold. The cold slowly sticks chemical tags on to one gene, the gene that blocks flowering, and the tags switch that gene off. The tags stay in place when warm days return, so the plant flowers in spring. That is why seed for these vegetables is grown in the cool hills. The third case is priming. A plant that has been attacked once responds faster the next time. In each case something in the plant's body changed and stayed changed. Whether to call this 'remembering' is argued over, and the next card gives the views.
- e.g. A flytrap closes when a fly touches the trap hairs twice within about half a minute. Five or more touches start the digestive juices flowing.
- e.g. Most kinds of cabbage grown all year in a hot place make leaves but no flowers, because the plants have had no winter.
- e.g. A bean plant in a dark cupboard still lifts and lowers their leaves at about the right times for a few days. Earlier days set the plant's inside clock.
### Can a plant learn? · *debated*
**Definition:** To learn is to change what you do because of what has happened to you, in a way that lasts. Scientists are still arguing about whether experiments have shown this in plants.

Plants learn. In 2014 Monica Gagliano dropped potted touch-me-nots 15 centimetres on to foam, again and again. At first the plants folded their leaves. After many drops, they stopped folding. Were the plants just worn out? She tested that by shaking the same plants sideways, and they folded at once. So they still had the energy to fold. They had stopped only for the harmless drop. Many plants still did not fold for a drop 28 days later. This fits the definition of habituation, the simplest kind of learning. In 2016 Gagliano reported a harder kind of learning. Pea seedlings were given a breeze from a fan and then light from the same side, many times. Afterwards, she reported, the seedlings grew towards the fan even with no light. In 2020 Kasey Markel repeated that test with more plants and did not get the same result. So the first kind of learning has good evidence, and the second kind is in doubt until someone replicates it.
- e.g. You stop noticing a ticking clock. That is habituation, and it shows that even the simplest learning is useful: it saves effort.
- e.g. In the Drop Test in this unit, you can repeat Monica Gagliano's first experiment if you have a touch-me-not.
- e.g. In the activity Learned or Tired?, you work out which results fit each explanation.
- **Monica Gagliano, who did the experiments:** The plants changed what they did because of experience, and the change lasted for weeks. In any animal we would call that learning and memory, so we should use the same words for a plant.
- **Critics such as Lincoln Taiz and his co-authors:** The touch-me-not result may be a change in the leaf's folding parts, with nothing like a memory behind it. The word 'learning' should be kept for animals with nerve cells until much stronger evidence comes.
- **Kasey Markel, who repeated the pea experiment:** The question is a fair one for science, and the way to answer it is to repeat the tests carefully. The pea result did not come out again, so for now it should not be counted as shown.
### Do plants feel? · *debated*
**Definition:** To feel is to have an experience, such as pain or comfort, and not only to respond. Nobody has found a way to measure feeling directly in any being.

Plants feel, remember and decide. Here is what can be measured. A plant has receptors for touch, light, gravity, warmth, many chemicals and damage. The plant sends electrical signals, as your nerves do, and uses some of the same signal chemicals, such as glutamate. The gases that put people to sleep for an operation also work on plants. A touch-me-not kept in ether fumes stops folding, and so does a Venus flytrap. When the fumes clear, the plant responds again. What nobody can measure is the feeling itself. This is true for people as well. You cannot measure what a friend feels. You judge it from what she does and says, and from the fact that her body is like yours. A plant's body is very unlike yours, so that second clue is missing, and scientists read the evidence in different ways.
- e.g. J.C. Bose showed in the early 1900s that a touch-me-not given chloroform stops responding, and recovers in fresh air, as an animal does.
- e.g. A plant responds differently to a caterpillar's bite and to a clean cut, so plants tell kinds of damage apart.
- e.g. Roots growing through soil turn away from salt and towards water, and they weigh the two against each other.
- **J.C. Bose, and today Paco Calvo, Monica Gagliano and Stefano Mancuso:** Plants sense, signal, choose and learn with their whole bodies. Feeling does not need a brain, just as breathing does not need lungs. A plant takes in air through tiny holes instead.
- **Lincoln Taiz and seven other scientists, writing in 2019:** In animals, feeling goes with brains of a certain size and kind. Plants have nothing like that, and their responses can be explained without feeling. To them, calling a plant conscious adds a guess and explains nothing new.
- **Philosophers who study this question:** Nobody yet knows how any body gives rise to feeling, not even a human brain. Until we know that, we have no test that can settle the question for plants, and both sides should say so.
### Why plants and animals signal in similar ways · *idea*
**Definition:** Plants and animals both signal with moving ions and with chemicals such as glutamate. The best explanation is that both inherited these tools from a shared ancestor.

Follow a signal down to the smallest scale and it is made of atoms carrying electric charge. A calcium ion is a calcium atom that has lost two electrons. A cell uses energy from food to pump ions out, as you might pump water up to a tank. When a gate opens, the ions rush back in, and that rush is the signal. Bacteria, fungi, plants and animals all do this. Plants and animals last shared an ancestor more than a billion years ago. That ancestor was a single cell. Plants have glutamate receptors built on the same plan as the ones in your brain. So scientists think this kind of signalling is older than both plants and animals. Nerves and brains came later, as one way of doing it fast. This last step is an idea drawn from comparing living things today. Nobody can test the signals of a being who lived a billion years ago.
- e.g. Glutamate carries signals between the nerve cells in your brain. In a plant, glutamate from broken cells starts the warning wave.
- e.g. Fungal threads carry electrical pulses too. Scientists do not yet know what the pulses do.
- e.g. Even a bacterium senses chemicals with receptors and swims towards food.

## Mostly agreed
- News of damage travels through a plant's body within minutes, as electrical and chemical signals. *How we know:* In 2018 Toyota and Gilroy filmed waves of calcium crossing glowing plants after a caterpillar's bite. J.C. Bose recorded electrical signals in plants from 1901 onwards.
- Damaged plants give off volatiles, and insects and other plants respond to them. *How we know:* Turlings and his team showed in 1990 that wasps follow the smell of caterpillar-chewed maize. Field and laboratory tests since 2000 show that neighbouring plants prime their defences.
- Most land plants live joined to fungi, and both partners gain. *How we know:* The fungal threads can be seen in roots under a microscope. When phosphorus or carbon is marked, it can be followed from fungus to plant and from plant to fungus.
- Sugar made by one plant can reach another plant through fungal threads. *How we know:* Ghost plants have no chlorophyll and live only on sugar from fungi joined to trees. Marked carbon fed to one tree has been found in a neighbour.
- Repeated touch makes plants grow shorter and thicker. *How we know:* This has been measured in dozens of kinds of plant since Mark Jaffe's tests in 1973. Plant nurseries brush seedlings to keep them sturdy.
- A plant's response can depend on events long past, such as a winter. *How we know:* Plants that need cold before flowering stay leafy for years without it. The chemical tags that the cold leaves on one gene have been found and measured.

## How we found out
**David Rhoades, Ian Baldwin and Jack Schultz, who first reported it. Ted Turlings, Richard Karban, Martin Heil and many others, who tested it properly., 1983 (the first reports), 1990 (wasps and maize), 2000 (sagebrush and wild tobacco) and 2007 (the plastic bag test).** Until the 1980s, most scientists believed that a plant could respond only to what touched that plant directly. In 1983 two studies challenged this. David Rhoades found that willow trees near caterpillar-eaten willows became worse food for caterpillars. Ian Baldwin and Jack Schultz tore the leaves of young poplars and maples in closed boxes. Undamaged plants that shared only the air made more defence chemicals. Newspapers wrote about 'talking trees', and many scientists laughed. They also pointed out real problems. The tests had few plants, and a disease could have spread between Rhoades's trees. For years the idea was dropped. Then came better tests. In 1990 Ted Turlings showed that wasps follow the smell of chewed maize. In 2000 Richard Karban clipped sagebrush bushes in open country, and the wild tobacco plants beside them were eaten less. In 2007 Martin Heil tied plastic bags round bean leaves. With the bags on, no warning passed. That showed the news was travelling through the air.  
*What people believed before:* That a plant can respond only to what touches that plant directly, so nothing can pass from one plant to another through the air. Tests with marked plants in open country, and with plastic bags round leaves, showed that damage smells do pass and that neighbours respond.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Plants grow better when people play them music or speak kindly to them.** (A popular belief, spread by the 1973 book The Secret Life of Plants; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants do sense vibrations. In 2014 Heidi Appel and Rex Cocroft played thale cress plants a recording of a caterpillar chewing. Those plants later made more defence chemicals than plants kept in silence.  
  Against: The music tests from the 1970s were not fair tests. The plants differed in light and warmth, and nobody measured without knowing which plant was which. Careful repeats found no effect of music or kind words.
- **Big old trees feed their own seedlings through fungal threads.** (Suzanne Simard and the 'mother tree' idea; *debated*)  
  For: Marked carbon has moved between trees in a forest. In some tests, seedlings joined to a network survived better.  
  Against: A 2023 review found very few forest studies that support it. The amounts of carbon were small, and several tests found no gain for seedlings.
- **A damaged plant sends out smells in order to warn neighbouring plants.** (The usual way the finding is told; *debated*)  
  For: Neighbours do respond to the smells, and they are eaten less afterwards.  
  Against: Helping a neighbour who competes for the same light and water gains the sender nothing. Many scientists think the smells are first a signal to the plant's own far leaves and to helpful wasps. Neighbours pick the news up, as you might overhear a call.
- **Pea seedlings can learn to link a breeze with light.** (Monica Gagliano and her team, 2016; *minority*)  
  For: In her test, most trained seedlings grew towards the fan when no light was given.  
  Against: In 2020 Kasey Markel repeated the test with more seedlings and measured without knowing which group each seedling was in. The effect did not appear.
- **Only a being with a brain can feel.** (Many scientists who study brains and plants; *debated*)  
  For: In animals, feeling changes when the brain is hurt or put to sleep. No part of a plant looks like a brain.  
  Against: Plants sense, signal, choose and keep information with chemical signals, not a brain, and the gases that put animals to sleep work on plants as well. Nobody has shown that a brain is the only kind of body that can feel.

## Roots
We have learned that plants send signals through their bodies, through the air and through the soil, and that many other beings receive them. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Torn Leaf Test, torn leaves gave off a smell into the open air, and your nose found them. A nearby plant treats that smell as a warning, a hunting wasp as a guide, and you as a sign of dinner. The leaves sent one smell, and each receiver made a different use of it. So what a signal means depends on who receives it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a message as having one meaning, fixed by the sender, like a letter in an envelope. A plant's smell goes to everyone downwind. Whether it is a warning, a guide or nothing at all depends on the receptors of whoever meets it.  
*Evidence:* In the Torn Leaf Test you picked out the torn leaves by smell alone. Wasps follow the smell of caterpillar-chewed maize, and neighbouring plants prime their defences when they receive damage smells. Underground, the root chemical that calls a helpful fungus also wakes the seeds of witchweed.

- *who am i:* You are a receiver too. Your nose and tongue use receptors, as a plant's cells do. You notice only the news you have receptors for.
- *what is life:* Every living cell senses and responds. No living being has been found who does not. Brains and nerves are one way of doing it, and a fast one.
- *what is this world:* The air and the soil are full of chemical news. Most of it passes you by, because other beings have the receptors for it and you do not.

One smell leaves a torn leaf. In a neighbouring plant it becomes a warning, in a wasp a direction to fly, and in you the thought of sambar. The same happens underground, where one root chemical feeds a partnership with a fungus and wakes a witchweed seed. Plants, fungi, insects and people can all read parts of the same chemical world. All of us come down from the same early cells, who already sensed chemicals with receptors. So the many meanings come from one smell, and the many readers come from one family.

- **Jakob von Uexküll, A Foray into the Worlds of Animals and Humans (1934):** Every creature lives in a world made of the few things their body can sense. He called this the creature's 'Umwelt', the world around them. A tick's world holds only a smell, a warmth and a touch. (A wasp's world holds the smell of chewed maize, and yours does not. The same field is a different world to each of you.)
- **Udana 6.4, the Buddhist story of the blind men and the elephant, which Jain teachers also tell to explain anekantavada, many-sidedness:** “Imesu kira sajjanti, / eke samaṇabrāhmaṇā; / Viggayha naṁ vivadanti, / janā ekaṅgadassino” Several blind men each touch one part of an elephant. One says the elephant is like a pillar and another says like a fan. Each is right about his part and wrong to think it is the whole. (A plant, a wasp and a person each receive one smell and take a different thing from it. No one of them holds the whole of what that smell is.)
- **Mahabharata, Shanti Parva, section 184 (the sage Bhrigu answers Bharadvaja):** Bharadvaja asks whether trees are alive and aware. Bhrigu answers that trees feel heat and touch, drink through their roots, and know pleasure and pain. He gives what he has seen as his reasons: leaves wither in heat, and a creeper finds a way round a tree. (Bhrigu argued from what he could see, and had no instruments. The experiments in this unit test the same question with rulers, controls and repeats.)
- Science (settled): Insects and neighbouring plants respond to the smells of damaged plants. This has been shown in fields and laboratories since 1990.
- Science (debated): Biologists separate a 'signal', which the sender is built to send, from a 'cue', which a receiver simply picks up. Which of these a plant's damage smell is, for neighbouring plants, is not agreed.
- Science (open): Whether there is something it is like to be a plant, and how any body gives rise to feeling, is not known.
- Other view, Many biologists who study signals: A signal does have one true meaning: the job it was shaped for over many generations. Maize plants whose smell brought wasps left more seeds, so the smell is 'for' the wasps and for the plant's own leaves. Other receivers are only eavesdroppers. What they make of the smell is their business, and is not part of what the smell means.
- Practice, One smell, many receivers (3 min): Take one fresh leaf and tear it under your nose. Close your eyes and breathe in slowly, three times. Notice what the smell makes you think of. Now open your eyes and look around the room. That smell is spreading to every corner. Who else here could receive it? Think of an ant on the floor, a plant on the windowsill and a person in the next room. What might it be to each of them?
- Ask: A torn leaf's smell reached a wasp, a neighbouring plant and you. Was it a message to all three, to one of you, or to nobody?
- Ask: A seedling grows differently for a week after a one-second touch. Is that remembering? What would you need to see to decide?
- Ask: You judge what a friend feels from what she does. Should the same rule be used for a plant? Why, or why not?
- Ask: What news might be passing through this room right now that you have no receptors for?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A wasp, a neighbouring plant and you all receive the smell of a damaged leaf. Whom is that smell for? (hint: Think about what the sender gains from each receiver.)
- *deep:* A seedling changes their growth for a week after a touch that lasted one second. What more would they have to do before you would say they remember? (hint: Write your own test for remembering. Then check whether you pass it yourself when you ride a cycle.)
- *big:* You cannot measure what your best friend feels. So how do you know she feels anything, and does your method work for a plant? (hint: List the clues you use for your friend. Which of them does a plant give?)
- *big:* A tree and a fungus are joined so closely that each would do badly alone. Is that two living beings or one? (hint: Think about the bacteria in your own gut, which help you digest your food.)
- *big:* A news story says 'trees talk to each other'. A scientist's paper says 'carbon moved between two trees'. Which one tells you more, and which would you rather read? (hint: One is exciting and easy to remember. The other says exactly what was measured.)
- *deep:* If one careful scientist gets a result and another careful scientist does not, what should the rest of us believe in the meantime? (hint: Think about the pea seedlings and the fan. Is 'we do not know yet' a proper answer?)

## Story: The Wasps in the Maize
*A made-up story. Gowri, her mother, Basavaraj Mama and Dr Nirmala are not real people. The science is real. The fall armyworm is a caterpillar that reached Karnataka in 2018 and feeds deep inside the rolled young leaves of maize. Maize chewed by caterpillars gives off a smell that a plain torn leaf does not, and small wasps follow that smell and lay their eggs in the caterpillars. Ted Turlings and his team showed this in 1990. A Krishi Vigyan Kendra is a government farm science centre. In real fields the wasps do not always win, and farmers often need other methods as well.*

That July the maize stood as high as Gowri's waist. Her mother's two acres lay outside a village near Davanagere, where almost every field grew maize in the rains.

On Monday morning Amma came back from the field with a rolled-up leaf in her hand. She opened it on the veranda. The leaf was full of ragged holes. In its folds lay wet brown crumbs, like sawdust.

"The worm," said Amma.

Everyone in the village called them that. They were striped caterpillars, and when they were fully grown they were as long as Gowri's little finger. They lived deep inside the rolled young leaves at the top of each plant, where no bird could see them.

Basavaraj Mama from the next field was leaning on the gate. "Spray tomorrow, akka," he said. "I will bring my sprayer. One tank of the strong medicine, and every creeping thing in your field will be dead by evening."

Amma looked at the leaf for a long time. The spray cost money, and it made her cough for two days.

"First I want to know how bad it is," she said. "Gowri, walk the rows after school. Look at every tenth plant. Count how many have holes."

So that afternoon Gowri walked the rows with her maths notebook. She made a mark for each plant with holes and a dot for each plant without. By the fourth row she had noticed something that had nothing to do with counting.

Small black wasps were flying over some of the plants. They were no longer than a grain of rice. They hovered, landed and walked down into the rolled leaves. Over other plants there were none at all.

Gowri went back along the row and checked her marks. Every plant that had wasps overhead was a plant with holes.

Perhaps the wasps liked damaged leaves. Gowri knew where to find plenty of those. At the far corner, three days before, Shanthakka's goats had pushed through the thorn fence. They had torn up a whole patch before anyone chased them out. The leaves there hung in strips.

She stood in the torn patch and watched. There was not one wasp. She looked into the tops of the plants. There were no brown crumbs and no caterpillars.

Maybe the tears were too old. So Gowri chose a healthy plant, with no holes anywhere. She tore three of the leaves with her own fingers. Then she sat down on the bund to wait.

The shadows of the maize grew long. No wasp came to her plant. Two rows away, three wasps were circling a plant with fresh brown crumbs in the top.

Gowri thought about it all the way home. The caterpillars were hidden. She could find one only by unrolling a leaf. Yet the wasps found them from the air. A torn plant brought no wasps. A chewed plant brought them in.

Before school the next morning she unrolled the top leaf of one chewed plant. The caterpillar inside was limp and yellow, and hardly moved. Beside the caterpillar, stuck to the leaf, was a little heap of white cocoons, like grains of puffed rice.

When she came back to the house, Basavaraj Mama was at the gate with the sprayer on his back. A woman in a grey sari stood beside Amma, holding a file. Gowri had seen her before. She was Dr Nirmala from the Krishi Vigyan Kendra, and she had come to the village to talk about the worm.

"Mama, wait," said Gowri. "Come and see something first."

She led all three of them down the rows. She showed them the wasps over the chewed plants. She showed them the goat patch with no wasps, and her own torn plant with no wasps. Last she showed them the white cocoons.

"The wasps are hunting the worms," she said. "But they cannot see them, because the worms are inside. And it is not the holes they look for, or they would come to the goat patch. So something tells them which plants have worms. The only one who knows is the plant."

Basavaraj Mama laughed. "Now the maize is making phone calls."

"In a way, yes," said Dr Nirmala. She bent over the cocoons. "When a caterpillar chews, their spit gets into the wound. The maize senses the spit. Some hours later the plant gives off a smell that a torn leaf does not give. These wasps follow that smell. Each one lays her eggs inside a caterpillar. The young wasps eat the caterpillar and then spin these cocoons."

She looked at Gowri. "Scientists showed that only in 1990. You worked out half of it with three torn leaves."

"And if I spray?" asked Amma.

"The wasps fly in the open, so the spray reaches them first," said Dr Nirmala. "The caterpillar sits deep in the rolled leaf, where the spray hardly goes."

Nobody spoke for a moment. A wasp landed on the plant beside them and walked down out of sight.

"I cannot promise you the wasps will win," said Dr Nirmala. "Sometimes they do not. Count for four more days. Count the plants with fresh crumbs, and count the plants with cocoons. Then you will know which number is growing."

"Four days," said Amma to Basavaraj Mama. "Then I will decide."

Every evening that week Gowri walked the rows. Her notebook had two columns now. On Friday the cocoon column was longer than it had been on Tuesday, and the crumb column was shorter.

The wind came across the field. Gowri breathed in. To her it smelled only of green leaves. But the wasps were still arriving.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a maize plant can tell a caterpillar's bite from a plain tear, because the caterpillar's spit gets into the wound. Hours later the chewed plant gives off a mix of smell chemicals that a torn plant does not. Small wasps have receptors for that smell and follow it to the hidden caterpillar. So one living being's response to damage works as a signal for another. Gowri's test is a real control: she tore leaves herself, to check whether damage alone would bring the wasps.

- Ask: The caterpillars were hidden inside rolled leaves. What did Gowri notice that made her think the wasps were not using their eyes?
- Ask: Why did Gowri tear three leaves on a healthy plant? What would it have meant if wasps had come to that plant?
- Ask: The maize gave off the smell. The wasps followed it. Who gained from this, and how?
- Ask: Dr Nirmala did not tell Amma what to do. She told her to count two things for four days. Why two things?
- Ask: Gowri could not smell any difference between the plants. What does that tell you about the smells in the air around you now?

- **creative:** Use two kinds of mesh. One has holes about half as wide as a hair, which lets fungal threads through and stops roots. The other is far finer, and stops the threads but lets water through.; Grow each plant inside a mesh tube, and give some tubes a small turn every day. The turn snaps the fungal threads and leaves the soil water as it was.; Fill some pots with soil that was heated first, so that the soil holds no living fungus.

### Plant signals: paths, partners and the Touch Test (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What must a smell do before we call it a signal?  
   Options: Be strong enough for people to smell / Change what a receiver does / Come from a flower / Travel a long way  
   **Answer:** Change what a receiver does. *Why:* A signal carries news that changes what a receiver does. Many signals are far too faint for a person's nose. Choosing 'strong enough for people' puts people at the centre, but most plant signals are not received by us.
2. (label) Draw a chewed maize plant, a smell trail and a wasp. Label the sender, the carrier and the receiver.  
   **Answer:** ['Sender: the chewed maize plant', 'Carrier: the air, which carries the smell', 'Receiver: the wasp']. *Why:* The plant gives off the smell, the air carries it and the wasp responds to it. A strong answer may add that the plant's own far leaves and neighbouring plants are receivers too.
3. (match) Match each path to an example of news that travels that way.  
   **Answer:** Inside the plant → a bitten leaf warns a far leaf of the same plant; Through the air → a ripe banana makes a raw mango ripen; Through the soil from roots → a witchweed seed senses a jowar root; Along fungal threads → a ghost plant gets sugar that a tree made. *Why:* Each path has a different carrier: the plant's own veins, the air, the soil water or a fungus.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a plant needs nerves to send news from one leaf to another.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Nerves are one kind of wiring, and plants use another. News travels as waves of electric charge from cell to cell, and as hormones in the sap.
5. (fill) A partnership between a plant's roots and a fungus is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['mycorrhiza']. *Why:* Mycorrhiza means 'fungus-root'. More than eight of every ten kinds of land plant have one.
6. (sort) In a mycorrhiza, sort these by who gives them.  
   **Answer:** sugars → The plant gives; water → The fungus gives; phosphorus → The fungus gives; fats → The plant gives; other minerals → The fungus gives. *Why:* The plant makes food from light, so the plant gives sugars and fats. The fungus's fine threads reach far into the soil, so the fungus brings water and minerals.
7. (mcq) Why can fungal threads collect minerals that roots miss?  
   Options: They are stronger than roots / They make minerals from light / They are thin enough for tiny soil gaps / They are green  
   **Answer:** They are thin enough for tiny soil gaps. *Why:* A fungal thread is many times thinner than a hair, so it enters gaps that no root can. The threads also spread far beyond the roots. No living thing makes minerals from light.
8. (write) In the Touch Test, both cups stood at one window and swapped places daily. Why?  
   **Answer:** So that both cups got the same light. Then the strokes were the only difference between them.. *Why:* A strong answer names light as the thing kept the same, and says that only one thing may differ in a fair test.
9. (order) Put the steps of the Touch Test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Plant the same number of seeds in two cups', 'Guess, and measure every shoot', 'Stroke one cup twice a day for six days', 'Measure again and compare the means']. *Why:* Set up two equal cups, record the start, make one change, then measure the result.
10. (mcq) A tree on a windy hilltop is shorter and thicker than the same kind in a valley. Why?  
   Options: The wind blew the top off / Being bent often changed how the tree grew / Hilltop soil has no water / The hilltop tree is older  
   **Answer:** Being bent often changed how the tree grew. *Why:* A plant that is bent again and again makes less of the hormone that lengthens stems, and builds thicker walls. Choosing 'blew the top off' treats the shape as damage, but the tree grew that way.
11. (fill) A touched plant makes less of the ____ that makes stems grow long.  
   **Answer:** ['hormone', 'gibberellin']. *Why:* The hormone is called gibberellin. With less of it, the stem stays shorter.
12. (truefalse) True or false: a Venus flytrap closes at the first touch on a trap hair.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The trap closes only when a second touch comes within about 30 seconds. So the trap holds on to the first touch for a short time.
13. (draw) Draw a fungus living in soil. Show the mushroom and the main body, and label both.  
   **Answer:** A mushroom above the ground, and a wide web of fine threads below it, labelled 'mycelium' or 'hyphae'.. *Why:* A strong drawing makes the web of threads much bigger than the mushroom. A drawing with only a mushroom shows the common idea that the mushroom is the whole fungus.
14. (write) Cabbage plants flower only after weeks of cold. What does that tell you about plants and the past?  
   **Answer:** The plant's body keeps a record of the cold. Something changed during the winter and stayed changed, so the plant flowers months later.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the response today depends on something that happened long before.
15. (odd) Three of these are real paths for plant signals. Which one is not?  
   Options: Through the air as a smell / Inside the plant as an electrical signal / Through the soil from the roots / Along the plant's nerves  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Nerves are animal wiring. Plants send news through the air, inside their bodies as electrical and chemical signals, and from their roots into the soil.
### Evidence and controls in plant experiments (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Wasps come to maize chewed by caterpillars, but not to maize torn by hand. What does that show?  
   Options: Wasps look for holes in leaves / Torn leaves have no smell / The plant tells a bite from a tear / Caterpillars call the wasps  
   **Answer:** The plant tells a bite from a tear. *Why:* Both plants are damaged, but only the chewed one brings wasps. So the plant's response differs. Caterpillar spit in the wound makes the plant give off a different smell. If wasps looked for holes, torn leaves would bring them too.
2. (write) A witchweed seed sprouts only when a jowar root is near. How does the seed know?  
   **Answer:** The root lets out a chemical into the soil. The seed has receptors for that chemical, and sprouts only when it arrives.. *Why:* A strong answer names a chemical from the root as the carrier. It may add that the root makes this chemical to call helpful fungi.
3. (count) The heights of ten stroked seedlings add up to 110 centimetres. What is their mean height, in centimetres?  
   **Answer:** 11. *Why:* The mean is the total divided by the number of seedlings. 110 ÷ 10 = 11 centimetres.
4. (mcq) Why should the person measuring the seedlings not know which cup was stroked?  
   Options: They might stroke the wrong cup / Hopes can shift a measurement without anyone meaning to / Seedlings grow faster when watched / It makes the test quicker  
   **Answer:** Hopes can shift a measurement without anyone meaning to. *Why:* A person who expects the stroked cup to be shorter may read the ruler a little low for that cup. Measuring without knowing removes that risk.
5. (truefalse) True or false: when one scientist reports a result, the question is settled.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Other scientists must replicate the test. A result that comes out again in other laboratories can be trusted more.
6. (sort) Sort these claims: agreed by most scientists, or still debated.  
   **Answer:** Plants send electrical signals → Agreed; Most plants live joined to fungi → Agreed; Touch makes plants grow shorter and thicker → Agreed; Old trees feed seedlings through fungi in forests → Still debated; Pea seedlings can link a breeze with light → Still debated; Feeling needs a brain → Still debated. *Why:* The first three have been measured many times. The other three rest on a few studies, or on a question that nobody can yet test.
7. (write) A trained touch-me-not folds for a shake but not for a drop. Why does this rule out tiredness?  
   **Answer:** A tired plant could not fold at all. This plant folded for the shake, so the plant had the energy to fold and had stopped only for the drop.. *Why:* A strong answer says what tiredness would predict, and that the opposite happened.
8. (perspective) Two forest scientists disagree about big trees and fungal threads.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one view and gives its evidence: marked carbon did move between trees, or very few forest studies found a gain for seedlings. It then says what the other side has right, and names a measurement that would help, such as comparing seedlings with and without a link to the network.. *Why:* This is a real and current disagreement. Both scientists accept that the networks exist. They differ on how much they matter.
9. (fill) Responding less and less to a harmless thing that keeps happening is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['habituation']. *Why:* Habituation is the simplest kind of learning. It saves the energy of responding to something that does not matter.
10. (mcq) A plant's warning wave moves 1 millimetre a second. How long does it take to reach a leaf 6 centimetres away?  
   Options: 6 seconds / 10 seconds / 60 seconds / 600 seconds  
   **Answer:** 60 seconds. *Why:* 6 centimetres is 60 millimetres. At 1 millimetre a second, that takes 60 seconds. Choosing 6 seconds forgets to change centimetres into millimetres.
11. (draw) Draw two plants in one pot, joined by fungal threads. Mark where a mesh would block the threads.  
   **Answer:** Two plants with roots, fine threads running between the roots, and a mesh drawn as a wall in the soil between the two plants.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the mesh right across the soil between the plants. The mesh reaches from the top of the soil to the bottom of the pot, so no thread can go round.
12. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Priming → responding faster after meeting a danger once; Control → the part of a test where the change is not made; Replicate → to do an experiment again to check the result; Volatile → a chemical that drifts through the air as a smell; Receptor → a part of a cell that fits one kind of stimulus. *Why:* These are the words scientists use when they test whether plants learn and signal.
13. (order) Put these in order, from the fastest to the slowest.  
   **Answer:** ['A nerve signal in a person', 'The signal that folds a touch-me-not leaf', 'The warning wave in a small plant', 'A stem growing thicker after touch']. *Why:* A nerve signal travels up to 100 metres a second. The touch-me-not signal travels 2 to 3 centimetres a second. The warning wave travels about 1 millimetre a second. A change in growth takes days.
14. (truefalse) True or false: a ghost plant shows that sugar can pass from a tree to another plant through a fungus.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A ghost plant has no chlorophyll and makes no food. All their sugar was made by trees and arrives through a fungus joined to both.
15. (write) A friend says plants do not respond, because they do not move. Give two pieces of evidence against this.  
   **Answer:** Any two of these. Stroked seedlings grow shorter and thicker. A bitten leaf warns far leaves within two minutes. A chewed plant gives off a smell that brings wasps. A touch-me-not folds when touched.. *Why:* A strong answer gives evidence, and may add that most plant responses are changes in growth or chemistry, which are slow or cannot be seen.
### Reasoning about plant signals, learning and feeling (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Plan a fair test of this claim: 'Seedlings grow taller if you talk to them.' Name the control.  
   **Answer:** Grow two equal cups of seedlings with the same soil, water and light. Talk to one cup for the same time each day. The other cup is the control: it spends the same time in the same kind of place with no talking. Measure all the seedlings without knowing which cup is which, and compare the means.. *Why:* A strong answer keeps everything the same except the talking. It may also notice that a talking person breathes out carbon dioxide and moves the air, so the control needs a person sitting silently nearby.
2. (perspective) Is a damaged plant's smell a warning to neighbouring plants, or do the neighbours overhear it?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one view and uses evidence: neighbours do respond and are eaten less, but the sender competes with them for light and water. It then says what the other view has right. It may say that the smell is first a signal to the plant's own leaves and to wasps.. *Why:* Scientists really do disagree here. Everyone accepts that the smell is given off and that neighbours respond. The argument is about whom the smell is for.
3. (mcq) In a pot, a warning passes between plants. A mesh that blocks fungal threads but not water stops it. What follows?

   **Answer:** The warning needed the fungal threads. *Why:* Water still crossed the mesh, and the air was not blocked, yet the warning stopped. The only thing that changed was the threads. So in that pot the threads were needed.

6. (truefalse) True or false: a result that fits two explanations equally well helps you choose between them.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. To choose, you need a result that one explanation predicts and the other does not. That is why the shake test was needed after the drops.

   **Answer:** Plants and animals probably inherited this way of signalling from a shared ancestor, a single cell that lived more than a billion years ago. So signalling between cells is older than brains.. *Why:* A strong answer links the shared chemical to a shared ancestor, and says that this is an idea drawn from comparing living things.

   **Answer:** The fungus acts for their own gain. *Why:* The fungus rewards the root that pays best, which helps the fungus. So the fungus is a partner with their own needs, not a pipe that the tree controls.

   **Answer:** More scientists should repeat the test, with many seedlings, and measure without knowing which seedlings were trained. Until then the honest answer is 'not shown yet'.. *Why:* A strong answer does not simply pick the scientist it likes. It asks for more tests and accepts 'we do not know yet' for now.

## Applications
- Push-pull farming. In East Africa, farmers plant desmodium between rows of maize. The smell of desmodium drives stem-borer moths away, and chemicals from desmodium roots stop witchweed. Zeyaur Khan and his colleagues at icipe in Kenya developed this method.
- Farmers and nurseries add mycorrhizal fungi to the soil when they plant seedlings. The fungi help young plants take up phosphorus and water.
- Plant nurseries brush trays of tomato and other seedlings with a bar or a sheet each day. The touched seedlings grow shorter and sturdier, and survive planting out better.
- Gardeners tie a young tree loosely to a stake, so that the trunk can sway. A trunk that sways grows thicker.
- Farmers sow a 'trap crop' such as cotton in a field full of witchweed seeds. The cotton roots make the seeds sprout, and the seedlings die without a host.
- Maize roots attacked by beetle grubs give off a smell that calls tiny worms, and the worms kill the grubs. Some modern kinds of maize had lost this smell, and scientists have bred it back in.
- Engineers are building electronic 'noses' that sense the damage smells of crops. Such a sensor could warn a farmer of pests before any damage can be seen.

## Open questions
- **In a real forest, how much food and how many warnings pass from plant to plant through fungal threads?** Mapping the threads between real trees is very hard. Only a few forests have been studied, and the results do not agree.
- **When food moves through a fungal network, who decides where it goes: the tree, the fungus, or nobody?** In laboratory dishes, fungi send more minerals to the roots that pay more sugar. Nobody has been able to follow such choices in a forest.
- **Can plants learn to link two things, as a dog links a bell with food?** One study of pea seedlings said yes. A larger repeat of the study did not find it. More tests are needed.
- **Where in a plant's body is a habit kept, when a touch-me-not goes on staying open for a month?** Nobody has found what changes inside the plant. It may be calcium, chemical tags on genes, or something not yet thought of.
- **What, if anything, is it like to be a plant?** Nobody knows how any body gives rise to feeling, not even a human brain. So there is no test yet that could settle it for a plant.

## Connected maths
- In the Touch Test, the ten untouched seedlings measured 14, 15, 13, 16, 14, 12, 15, 14, 13 and 14 centimetres. What is their mean height? **14 centimetres.**. Add the ten heights: 14 + 15 + 13 + 16 + 14 + 12 + 15 + 14 + 13 + 14 = 140. Divide by the number of seedlings: 140 ÷ 10 = 14. So the mean is 14 centimetres. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- The untouched cup has a mean height of 14 centimetres. The stroked cup has a mean of 10.5 centimetres. What percentage shorter is the stroked cup? **25 per cent shorter.**. The difference is 14 − 10.5 = 3.5 centimetres. Compare it with the untouched height: 3.5 ÷ 14 = one quarter. One quarter is 25 per cent. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Torn Leaf Test there are two katoris. If you only guess, the chance of being right once is one half. What is the chance of guessing right five times in a row? **1 chance in 32.**. Each guess halves the chance. One half of one half is one quarter. Go on halving five times: 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32. So five lucky guesses in a row would happen about once in 32 tries. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- In a Touch Test, the stroked seedlings were between 8 and 13 centimetres tall, with a mean of 11. The untouched ones were between 10 and 16, with a mean of 14. A friend says: 'One stroked seedling is taller than one untouched seedling, so stroking does nothing.' Is she right? **No. The two groups overlap, but the stroked group is 3 centimetres shorter on average.**. Seedlings always vary, so the shortest untouched one can be shorter than the tallest stroked one. The question is about the groups, not about single seedlings. The means are 11 and 14, which differ by 3 centimetres. To be surer, she could repeat the test with more seedlings. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- A plant's warning wave travels 1 millimetre each second. A caterpillar bites a leaf, and the farthest leaf is 9 centimetres away. The caterpillar needs 20 minutes to finish the first leaf. How long does the far leaf have to get ready before the caterpillar moves on? **18 and a half minutes.**. 9 centimetres is 90 millimetres, so the wave takes 90 seconds, which is 1 and a half minutes. 20 minutes take away 1 and a half minutes leaves 18 and a half minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- In Under the Forest, every move of beans along a thread costs 1 bean for the fungus. A big tree wants a seedling to receive 6 beans. How many beans must leave the big tree if they go in 1 move? In 2 moves? In 6 moves? What is the rule? **7 beans, 8 beans and 12 beans. The rule is: beans sent = 6 + the number of moves.**. Each move loses 1 bean. With 1 move, 7 leave and 6 arrive. With 2 moves, 8 leave and 6 arrive. With 6 moves of 2 beans each, 12 leave and 6 arrive. So fewer, bigger moves waste less. (queues `math.algebra.input-output.L5`)
