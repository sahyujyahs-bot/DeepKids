# Heat and temperature: why the same heat warms oil faster than water
`sci.energy.heat.L4`

**Summary.** Stand a tumbler of cooking oil and a tumbler of water in the same bath of hot water, and the oil warms up about twice as fast. Pour hot water at 60 degrees over ice in a small katori, and it melts less ice than a big bowl of water at only 40 degrees. Your child measures both with a thermometer. They find that temperature says how hot a thing is, while the heat it can give depends on how much of it there is and what it is made of. Then they follow heat into melting ice, a balloon on a bottle, a wet cloth and a burning groundnut.

**Intro.** *Two steel tumblers stand in the same pan of hot water. One holds a katori of cooking oil and the other a katori of water. Both started at room temperature. After ten minutes, one of them has warmed far more than the other. Which one, do you think? And why?* The pan gives the same heat to both tumblers, yet they do not end up the same. In this unit you test this with two thermometers. You find out why a small cup of hot water can melt less ice than a big bowl of warm water. You watch a thermometer stay still while ice melts, though heat keeps going in. You blow up a balloon with warm air, cool a thermometer with a wet cloth, and measure the heat in a groundnut.

## Terms
- **Thermal energy**: The total movement energy of all the tiny particles in a thing. A bigger thing at the same temperature has more thermal energy. *e.g.* A bucket of water at 40 degrees has far more thermal energy than a katori of water at 40 degrees.
- **Heat**: Thermal energy that moves from a warmer thing to a cooler thing. *e.g.* When you hold a hot tumbler, heat moves from the tumbler into your hands.
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold a thing is. It tells how fast the particles jiggle, on average. *e.g.* A thermometer in a cup of tea reads 65 degrees Celsius.
- **Calorie**: A unit of heat. One calorie is the heat that warms 1 gram of water by 1 degree Celsius. It is about 4.2 joules. *e.g.* To warm 100 grams of water by 10 degrees you need 1,000 calories.
- **Kilocalorie**: 1,000 calories. It is the unit on food packets. People often write it as Calorie with a capital C, or as kcal. *e.g.* A medium chapati has about 100 kilocalories, enough to warm a litre of water from 28 degrees to boiling.
- **Joule**: The scientist's unit for energy, and so for heat too. One calorie is about 4.2 joules. *e.g.* Heating a cup of tea (150 grams) from 28 to 70 degrees takes about 26,000 joules.
- **Specific heat**: The heat needed to warm 1 gram of a material by 1 degree Celsius. Each material has its own number. *e.g.* Water needs 1 calorie. Cooking oil needs about half a calorie. Steel needs about a tenth of a calorie.
- **Heat store**: A thing that holds a lot of heat, so it warms and cools slowly. *e.g.* A hot water bottle is a heat store. It keeps a bed warm for hours.
- **Melting point**: The temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. For ice it is 0 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Ice in a glass of water stays at 0 degrees until the last piece has melted.
- **Boiling point**: The temperature at which a liquid boils. For water at sea level it is 100 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Water in an open pot stays at 100 degrees however high the flame is.
- **Latent heat**: The heat that goes into melting, boiling or evaporating something. The temperature stays still while it goes in. Latent means hidden. *e.g.* Melting 1 gram of ice takes 80 calories, with no change in temperature.
- **Evaporation**: A liquid slowly turning into a gas from its surface, at any temperature. The fastest particles leave first. *e.g.* Wet clothes dry on a line because their water evaporates.
- **Contract**: To take up less space. Most things contract when they cool down. *e.g.* A balloon shrinks when you put it in the fridge, because the air inside contracts.
- **Thermal equilibrium**: When two things that touch are at the same temperature, so no heat moves between them. *e.g.* A thermometer in a tumbler of water stops rising when it reaches the water's temperature.
- **Climate**: The usual weather of a place over many years. *e.g.* Chennai has a warm, humid climate. Jaisalmer has a hot, dry one.
- **Absolute zero**: The coldest temperature that is possible, about minus 273 degrees Celsius. Particles move as little as the rules of nature allow. *e.g.* Scientists in laboratories have cooled things to a tiny fraction of a degree above absolute zero.
- **Sweat**: Salty water that skin lets out when the body is too warm. When it evaporates, it takes heat from the skin. *e.g.* After a long run, your skin is wet with sweat, and the breeze feels cool.
- **Insulate**: To slow down the movement of heat, for example with a cloth, a lid or a layer of air. *e.g.* A lid on a pot of dal keeps the dal hot for longer.

## Explanations
### Temperature and heat are different things · *defined*
**Definition:** Temperature tells how fast the particles of a thing jiggle, on average. Heat is the thermal energy that moves from a warmer thing to a cooler thing.

A katori of tea and a bucket of tea can have the same temperature. Their particles jiggle equally fast on average. But the bucket has far more particles, so it holds far more thermal energy, and it can give away far more heat. A thermometer cannot tell you this. It only tells you how hot a thing is, and so which way heat will go. To know how much heat a thing can give, you must also know how much of it there is, and what it is made of.
- e.g. A spark from a sparkler is hotter than boiling water, but it hardly warms your hand, because it holds almost no heat.
- e.g. A bucket of bath water at 40 degrees can warm you more than a cup of tea at 70 degrees, because it holds far more heat.
- e.g. A big pot of rasam stays hot for much longer than a small bowl of it at the same temperature.
### Measuring heat: the calorie and the joule · *defined*
**Definition:** A calorie is the heat that warms 1 gram of water by 1 degree Celsius. It is about 4.2 joules. A kilocalorie is 1,000 calories.

Heat is energy, so we measure it in the same units as energy. Scientists use the joule. An older unit, the calorie, is easier to picture, because it is defined with water. To warm 100 grams of water by 10 degrees, you need 100 times 10, which is 1,000 calories. The Calories on a food packet are really kilocalories. One kilocalorie warms a litre of water by 1 degree. A medium chapati has about 100 kilocalories. In theory, that is enough heat to take a litre of water from 28 degrees to boiling.
- e.g. Warming a cup of tea (150 grams) from 28 to 70 degrees takes 150 times 42, which is 6,300 calories, or about 6 kilocalories.
- e.g. An adult eats about 2,000 kilocalories a day, as much heat as it takes to warm 2,000 litres of water by 1 degree.
- e.g. One calorie is about 4.2 joules, so the cup of tea needs about 26,000 joules.
### How much heat it takes: amount, material and change · *defined*
**Definition:** The heat needed to warm a thing depends on three things: how much of it there is, what it is made of, and how many degrees you want to warm it.

In calories, heat needed is the mass in grams, times the specific heat of the material, times the number of degrees. Water has a specific heat of 1. Cooking oil has about one half. Dry sand and aluminium have about one fifth. Steel has about one tenth. So to warm 200 grams of water by 30 degrees you need 200 times 1 times 30, which is 6,000 calories. For 200 grams of oil you need about half as much, 3,000 calories. The same heat will warm the oil by about twice as many degrees. This is what the oil and water test shows.
- e.g. A steel spoon heats up very fast in a hot pan, because steel needs only about a tenth of a calorie for each gram and each degree.
- e.g. A brick wall takes a long time to warm in the sun and to cool at night, because a brick wall has a lot of mass.
- e.g. A gold bangle needs very little heat to warm up, because gold needs only about 0.03 of a calorie for each gram and each degree.
### Why water holds so much heat · *idea*
**Definition:** Water needs more heat for each degree than almost any common material. The reason is partly how its particles are built and joined.

Heat does not only make particles move faster. In water, a good part of the heat goes into making the particles spin, wobble and stretch the links that hold them together. That heat does not show up as speed, so the thermometer rises less. Scientists understand this in general. Working out the exact number for water from first principles is still hard. Water is odd in dozens of ways, and scientists are still working out the reasons for some of them. We call this an idea, not a finished answer.
- e.g. Water takes about twice as much heat as oil to warm by the same amount.
- e.g. Water takes more than eight times as much heat as steel, gram for gram.
- e.g. Ice takes only about half as much heat as liquid water to warm by one degree.
- **Many chemists:** The links between water particles, called hydrogen bonds, take in a lot of heat as they stretch and bend. This is a big part of why water holds so much heat.
- **Careful physicists:** The links are only part of the story. Water's particles can also store heat as spin and wobble, and quantum rules decide how much each way takes. The full numbers are still being worked out.
### Sea, sand and sky: why places warm differently · *defined*
**Definition:** Land warms and cools faster than the sea. This is because water needs more heat for each degree, and because sunlight and waves spread heat through deep water.

In the sun, dry sand needs only about a fifth of the heat that water needs for each degree. Sunlight stays at the sand surface, so the top layer gets very hot. In the sea, sunlight goes down several metres, and waves and currents mix the heat through a deep layer. So the sea warms slowly by day and cools slowly by night. Places by the sea, such as Chennai, Kochi and Mumbai, usually have only about 7 to 10 degrees between their day and night. In a desert, such as Jaisalmer, the day and night can differ by 15 degrees or more. The difference in warming between land and sea also helps to drive the monsoon winds.
- e.g. The sand at Marina beach in Chennai burns your feet at noon, while the sea beside it feels cool.
- e.g. Desert nights are cold because dry sand and thin dry air hold little heat and give it up quickly.
- e.g. The sea along India's coast keeps the weather milder than far inland, all year round.
- e.g. In 1856, Eunice Foote, an American scientist, is reported to have put thermometers in glass tubes of different gases in the sunshine. The tubes warmed by different amounts, so different materials take up the sun's heat differently.
### Sharing heat: where a mixture ends up · *defined*
**Definition:** When you mix two amounts of the same liquid at different temperatures, the mix ends between them, nearer the temperature of the bigger amount.

A mix of 1 litre at 80 degrees and 3 litres at 20 degrees: multiply each amount by its temperature, add, and divide by the total. 1 times 80 is 80. 3 times 20 is 60. 80 plus 60 is 140. Divide by 4 litres and you get 35 degrees. The 3 litres are three times as much, so the mix is three times closer to 20 than to 80. For different materials you must also include their specific heats. A hot steel spoon dropped into a cup of cool water warms the water by only a degree or two, because the spoon holds very little heat for each degree.
- e.g. A little hot milk in a big glass of cold milk warms it only a little.
- e.g. A bucket of bath water is mixed from a kettle of hot water and a lot of cold water.
- e.g. A hot idli steamer plate dropped into a basin of water warms the water by a few degrees.
### Heat in, but no change in temperature: melting and boiling · *defined*
**Definition:** While ice melts or water boils, heat keeps going in but the temperature stays still. The heat is used to pull the particles apart.

Melting 1 gram of ice takes about 80 calories, as much heat as it takes to warm 1 gram of water by 80 degrees. Boiling 1 gram of water away takes about 540 calories. All that time, the thermometer sits at 0 degrees for melting ice and at 100 degrees for boiling water at sea level. The heat is not lost. It breaks the links between the particles. Scientists call it latent heat, which means hidden heat. This is why ice cools a drink so well, and why steam burns so badly.
- e.g. A glass of lassi with ice stays near 0 degrees at the ice, until all the ice has melted.
- e.g. A pot of water on a high flame boils faster, but its temperature stays at about 100 degrees.
- e.g. Steam at 100 degrees burns your skin worse than water at 100 degrees, because the steam gives out all its hidden heat as it turns back to water.
### Making heat from movement · *defined*
**Definition:** Rubbing, bending, hammering and squashing all make heat, because movement energy turns into the jiggling of particles.

Rub your palms and they get warm. Bend a wire back and forth and the bend gets warm. Squash the air in a bicycle pump and the pump gets warm. In each case, a push moved something, and the movement energy ended up as heat. In the 1840s, James Joule showed that the same amount of movement always makes the same amount of heat. He let falling weights turn a paddle in water and measured how much the water warmed. His numbers gave about 4.2 joules for each calorie, which is the number we still use. This is also why friction makes things warm.
- e.g. Matchsticks light when you rub the head against the box.
- e.g. The brake blocks of a bicycle get warm when you brake hard.
- e.g. A meteor glows as it falls into the air, because the air in front of it is squashed so hard that it becomes very hot.
### Things grow bigger when they are warm · *defined*
**Definition:** Most things expand when they are warmed and contract when they are cooled. Gases expand the most, liquids less, solids the least.

Warm air in a closed bottle pushes out more strongly. Air grows by about 1 part in 270 for each degree. Over 30 degrees that is about 11 parts in 100. Liquids grow far less. Solids grow least of all. A 100-metre steel bridge grows about 4 centimetres when it warms by 30 degrees. That is why bridges and railway tracks have small gaps. In India, blacksmiths fitted iron tyres on wooden cart wheels by heating the tyre until it grew a little, slipping it on, and cooling it with water. As it shrank, it gripped the wheel tight. Water is odd. It shrinks as it cools down to about 4 degrees, then expands again as it freezes.
- e.g. A stuck metal jar lid often comes loose in hot water, because the metal lid grows more than the glass jar.
- e.g. Overhead power lines hang lower on a hot day, because the wire has grown longer.
- e.g. A thick glass of cold water can crack if you pour boiling water in, because the inside grows before the outside.
### Cooling by evaporation · *defined*
**Definition:** When a liquid evaporates, the fastest particles leave the surface. The particles left behind move more slowly on average, so the liquid cools.

Water particles at the surface move at very different speeds. The fastest ones have enough energy to escape into the air. They carry their energy away with them. What stays behind is cooler. Evaporating 1 gram of water takes more than 500 calories from whatever it leaves. That is why sweat cools you, why a wet cloth feels cold, and why water in a clay matka stays cooler than the air. On a humid day, such as in Chennai or Kolkata in the rainy season, the air is already full of water, so sweat evaporates slowly and you feel hotter.
- e.g. Coming out of a bath, you feel cold until you dry, because the water on your skin is evaporating.
- e.g. Water in a clay matka stays cooler than the air, because some water seeps through the clay and evaporates from the outside.
- e.g. Dogs pant, and elephants flap their wet ears, to lose heat by evaporation.
### A body that stays at 37 degrees · *defined*
**Definition:** Humans and other mammals and birds keep their insides at a nearly steady temperature by making heat and getting rid of it at just the right rate.

Your body makes heat all the time from food. When you are too warm, your skin widens its blood vessels, so more heat leaves, and you sweat. When you are too cold, you shiver, because shivering muscles make extra heat. A fever is the body setting a higher target for a while. Your body is about 60 to 70 parts in 100 water, and water holds so much heat that your temperature changes slowly. A lizard has no such inside heater. She warms on a sunny rock, and cools in the shade. Most living beings are somewhere between the two.
- e.g. A crowded room warms up, because every person gives out heat.
- e.g. Children shiver when they come out of the sea, because their muscles make heat to keep the body warm.
- e.g. A hen's body is about 41 degrees, warmer than yours.
### The coldest and the hottest · *debated*
**Definition:** Absolute zero, about minus 273 degrees Celsius, is the coldest temperature possible. Nobody knows if there is a hottest temperature.

As things cool, their particles move less. At absolute zero they would move as little as the rules of nature allow. They do not stop completely, because quantum physics says some small movement always remains. No one can reach absolute zero exactly, but laboratories have come within a billionth of a degree. At the other end, the centre of the Sun is about 15 million degrees. In particle accelerators, scientists have made a tiny blob of matter that was reported to reach several trillion degrees for a moment. Is there a top limit? That is still an open question.
- e.g. The Boomerang Nebula, a cloud of gas in space, is reported to be one of the coldest natural places known, at about minus 272 degrees Celsius.
- e.g. The centre of the Sun is about 15 million degrees Celsius.
- e.g. Scientists have cooled atoms to less than a billionth of a degree above absolute zero in some laboratories.
- **Many physicists:** There is probably no hottest temperature in principle, but near a value called the Planck temperature our known laws of physics stop working. So we cannot say what happens there.
- **Some physicists:** Hot and cold are not mirror images. Cold has a floor, but heat may have a ceiling set by a deeper theory of gravity and quantum physics that nobody has yet.

## Mostly agreed
- The heat needed to warm a thing equals its mass, times its specific heat, times the change in temperature. *How we know:* Heat has been measured in careful experiments since the 1760s, by warming known masses of many materials. The numbers agree every time. Our oil and water test is a small version.
- Water takes more heat than almost any common material to warm by one degree. *How we know:* Tables of specific heats, measured in laboratories around the world, list water near the top. This is why seas warm and cool slowly and steady the weather of coasts.
- While a solid melts or a liquid boils, heat goes in but the temperature stays still. *How we know:* A thermometer in melting ice stays at 0 degrees until the last ice has gone. A thermometer in an open pot of boiling water stays at about 100 degrees at sea level.
- Movement energy can turn into heat, and the exchange rate is fixed: about 4.2 joules for each calorie. *How we know:* James Joule measured it in the 1840s, with paddles driven by falling weights. Many careful measurements since then have found the same number.
- Most solids, liquids and gases expand when they are warmed and contract when they are cooled. *How we know:* This is how liquid thermometers work. It is also why bridges and railways are built with small gaps.
- When a liquid evaporates, the liquid left behind is cooler. *How we know:* A thermometer wrapped in wet cloth reads lower than a dry one in the same air. Sweat cools us in the same way.

## How we found out
**Joseph Black, a Scottish chemist and doctor (1728 to 1799), About 1760 to 1765.** For a long time, people thought that a thermometer told them how much heat a thing held. A hotter thing had more heat, and two things at the same temperature had the same amount. Early in the 1700s, the instrument-maker Daniel Fahrenheit mixed water and mercury at different temperatures. The mix did not land halfway, and the Dutch doctor Herman Boerhaave reported this puzzle. In the early 1760s, Joseph Black, a Scottish chemist and doctor at the University of Glasgow, thought hard about it. He argued that different materials need different amounts of heat to warm by the same number of degrees. He also saw that melting ice takes in a lot of heat while a thermometer in it stays still. So the amount of heat and the temperature are not the same thing. He gave them separate names and measured them separately. In the 1840s, James Joule showed that heat is energy, and that movement can turn into it.  
*What people believed before:* A thermometer shows how much heat a thing holds, so things at the same temperature hold the same heat. Black showed that different materials, and different amounts, need different heat to change by the same number of degrees.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A thermometer reading tells you how much heat a thing holds.** (Many people, and scientists before the 1760s.; *rejected*)  
  For: A hotter thing usually feels as though it has more heat, and a thermometer is how we measure it.  
  Against: A bucket of bath water at 40 degrees holds far more heat than a cup of tea at 70 degrees. Joseph Black showed in the 1760s that temperature and the amount of heat are different things.
- **If you turn up the flame under a pot of boiling water, the water gets hotter.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: A bigger flame puts in more heat, and more heat usually means a higher temperature.  
  Against: In an open pot, boiling water stays at about 100 degrees whatever the flame. The extra heat makes the water boil away faster. To make it hotter, you must stop it boiling, as a pressure cooker does.
- **Sweat cools us because sweat is cold.** (A common idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: Sweat feels cold on the skin, and a cold hand cools a warm cheek.  
  Against: Sweat starts at the temperature of the body. It cools you by evaporating, which takes heat from your skin. On a humid day, sweat evaporates slowly, so it cools you less.
- **Land and sea warm differently because the sun shines harder on the land.** (A common idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: Sand feels far hotter than the sea at noon.  
  Against: The sun shines about equally on both. The sea warms less because water needs more heat for each degree and because sunlight and waves spread the heat through deep water.
- **A hot thing weighs more because heat is a substance with weight.** (Many scientists in the 1700s, who thought of heat as a fluid.; *rejected*)  
  For: Heat seemed to flow from one thing into another, like water from a jug.  
  Against: Careful weighing found no gain in weight. Today we know that energy has a tiny mass, but it is far too little to weigh. A litre of water heated by 100 degrees gains only a few trillionths of a kilogram.
- **At absolute zero, nothing moves at all.** (Many people, and some older textbooks.; *rejected*)  
  For: As particles cool they move less and less, so it seems they would stop completely.  
  Against: Quantum physics says particles keep a small amount of movement even at absolute zero. Scientists call it zero-point motion, and it has effects that can be measured.

## Roots
We have measured how fast oil and water warm, and watched ice melt without a change in temperature. Now we ask what this tells us about thermometers, about our own bodies and about the world.

**Deep truth.** The oil and the water sat in the same bath, and the oil ended up much hotter. A thermometer does not count the heat that went in. It shows how that one material answered the heat. So a temperature tells you about the stuff as well as the heat.  
*Habit it breaks:* We read a temperature as if it were an amount of heat, as in 'it is hotter, so it has more heat'. But the same heat gives a different temperature in different stuff, and the same temperature can hide very different amounts of heat.  
*Evidence:* In Oil and Water in a Warm Bath, both tumblers stood in the same bath. In a typical test the oil warmed by about twice as many degrees as the water. In the ice test, a small katori of water at 60 degrees melted less ice than a big bowl at 40.

- *who am i:* You are about two thirds water, so your body warms and cools slowly. That helps to keep your inside near 37 degrees on a hot day and on a cool night.
- *what is life:* A lizard takes in the sun's heat on a warm rock, and a hen makes her own. Water inside every living cell helps all of them keep their heat steady.
- *what is this world:* The seas hold so much heat that they steady the weather for whole countries. The Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea keep India's coasts milder than the plains far from them.

Heat is one thing, energy, but every kind of stuff takes it in in its own way. Oil takes it in mostly as faster movement, so it warms quickly. Water also spreads it into spinning, wobbling and stretching, so it warms slowly. The same heat meets many materials and shows up as many different temperatures. A warm sea, a hot sand dune and a 37-degree body are all the same energy, meeting different stuff.

- **Tao Te Ching 78:** Nothing in the world is softer or more yielding than water. Yet for wearing down what is hard and strong, nothing does better. (Water gives way easily and warms gently, yet it holds more heat than almost anything and steadies the hottest day and the coldest night.)
- **Thales of Miletus, as reported by Aristotle in Metaphysics, book 1, chapter 3:** Thales, a Greek thinker of about 600 BCE, is reported to have taught that water is the stuff all things come from. (Water covers most of the Earth and fills every body. Its way of holding heat shapes the climate and keeps our bodies steady.)
- Science (settled): Water has one of the highest specific heats of any common liquid. This is why seas steady the weather of coasts.
- Science (open): Exactly why water holds so much heat, and why it has dozens of other unusual properties, is still being worked out.
- Other view, Many physicists: Temperature is a simple thing: the average energy of motion of the particles. It belongs to the heat alone. Oil and water follow the same rule. The water simply has more ways to store energy, so it needs more heat. Saying that temperature is partly about the stuff is a confusing way to put it.
- Practice, Where does your warmth go? (3 min): Sit still. Hold your two palms a finger-width apart, without touching, and breathe slowly three times. Feel the warmth between them. Now wrap both hands round a full steel tumbler of tap water, and hold it for one minute. Notice that your hands cool, but the water hardly warms. Where did your warmth go? Why did the water not get hot?
- Ask: If a thermometer shows temperature and not heat, how could you find out how much heat is in a cup of tea?
- Ask: Is a temperature something a thing has by itself, or something that happens between a thing and the heat that meets it?
- Ask: Your hands gave heat to the water and the water hardly warmed. Where is your heat now?

## Story: The Drums on the Cold Night
*A made-up story. Meera, her family and their shed are imagined. The science is true: water holds much more heat for each degree than air, so drums of water soak up the sun's heat by day and give it back slowly at night. On cold January nights, frost can settle in the high valleys near Munnar. In real life, drums of water can keep a small closed shed a few degrees warmer, but they could not protect an open field.*

Meera lived on a hillside above Munnar, in Kerala. Tea bushes covered the slopes like a green carpet. Every morning the mist was so thick that the tea pluckers walked into it and disappeared.

Her family did not work on a tea estate. They had a small plot behind the house, with a shed roofed in clear plastic. Inside the shed, her mother Sreedevi grew tomatoes and beans for the Sunday market. The shed was her pride.

One January morning, the radio said that a cold wave was coming. Tonight would be the coldest night in years. Meera knew what that meant. On such nights, frost settled on the grass in the high valleys. One touch of frost could kill every tomato flower in the shed.

Her uncle Gopan had just come home after working for years in the Gulf. He was proud of how much he knew. At noon he marched into the shed with a bucket of glowing charcoal. 'We will warm the air,' he said. 'Charcoal is hot.'

Ammamma, Meera's grandmother, caught his sleeve. 'Burning charcoal in a closed shed?' she said. 'The smoke will put every one of us to sleep. Take it out.' Gopan set the bucket down outside and rubbed the back of his neck.

'Then what do we do?' he asked. 'There is no power for a heater, and the cold is coming.' Nobody answered. Sreedevi looked at her tomato plants and said nothing.

Meera was thinking about last week at school. Her teacher, Lathika Teacher, had given the class a puzzle. Why does oil in the kadai get hot almost at once, while the water for tea takes so long?

The class had tested it. A tumbler of oil and a tumbler of water stood in the same bath of hot water. The oil warmed up twice as fast. 'Water needs a lot of heat to warm up,' Lathika Teacher had said. 'So when water cools down, it gives a lot of heat back.' Meera had copied this into her notebook in her best handwriting.

In the corner of the shed stood three old blue drums, where Amma kept rainwater. Meera counted them. 'Uncle,' she said, 'please fill the drums with water.'

Gopan laughed. 'Water? Child, water is the coldest thing in this shed.'

'Water is not cold,' said Meera. 'Water just takes a lot of heat to change. The sun has been on this shed all day. If we fill the drums, they will soak up the sun's heat. Tonight, when the air gets cold, the drums will give it back slowly.'

'Where did you learn this?' asked Gopan. 'From a tumbler of oil,' said Meera.

Gopan did not believe her. But the sky was turning grey, and nobody else had an idea. So he picked up the buckets, and they filled the three drums from the rain tank, one bucket after another.

By six in the evening, the shed was warm and damp. Meera hung her school thermometer from a beam inside. Gopan hung a second one outside, on a nail. Inside it read 24. Outside it read 9.

They sat up with a lantern and a flask of tea. At nine o'clock the shed read 16. At midnight it read 11. Outside, the nail thermometer showed 4, and then 2. 'It is falling,' said Gopan. 'Of course it is,' said Meera. 'The shed is losing heat to the sky. But look how slowly.'

At three in the morning, the thermometer outside read 1. The grass was white, and the air stung their noses. Inside the shed it read 8. Gopan put his hand on the nearest drum. 'It is warm,' he whispered. 'How can it still be warm?'

'It has a lot to give,' said Meera. 'One drum of water holds as much heat for every degree as the air of fifteen sheds. It cannot give it all away in one night.' She had worked out the sum on the back of her notebook.

In the morning, the whole valley was white. Rajan Chettan next door had left his beans in the open. Their leaves hung black and soft, like wet cloth. In Sreedevi's shed, the tomato flowers were yellow and fresh. A few leaf tips were nipped, and that was all.

Gopan walked round the shed twice. Then he sat on an upturned bucket. 'Meera,' he said, 'explain it to me again. Slowly this time. I want to tell the men at the tea shop.'

Meera opened her notebook. She drew the sun, the drums and the cold night. Under the picture she wrote: Water is slow to warm and slow to cool. The drums store the sun's heat.

Sreedevi, listening from the door, added a warning. 'Three drums can keep a small closed shed above freezing. They could not save a whole field under the open sky. The sky takes heat away too fast.'

'A field would need far more water,' said Meera, 'and a roof.' That evening Ammamma tucked a hot steel bottle into Meera's blanket. 'Your great-grandmother knew this too,' she said. 'She never needed a thermometer.'

**For the parent:** Water takes a lot of heat to warm up, so it also gives out a lot of heat as it cools. Drums of water soak up the day's warmth and give it back slowly through the night.

- Ask: Why did Meera fill the drums with water, when water feels cold?
- Ask: Why would three drums of oil have kept the shed less warm than three drums of water?
- Ask: Why can drums of water help a small closed shed, but not a whole open field?
- Ask: Where did the heat in the drums come from in the afternoon, and where had it gone by morning?

  *Child mode:* Which things are big? Which hold a lot of heat for their size?
  - Think: A child's body needs about three times as much heat per degree as the air in a small room. What does that tell you about why your body stays warm?

## Experiments
### e1 · Oil and Water in a Warm Bath: which warms faster? (37 min)
- **question:** If a tumbler of cooking oil and a tumbler of water stand in the same hot bath, which one warms up more in 12 minutes, and by how much?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which will warm up more, the oil or the water? Will the difference be small or big? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two tumblers of the same size and metal. Put 100 millilitres of oil in one and 100 millilitres of water in the other. Both must start at room temperature. Stand them side by side in the same bath. Stir gently before every reading, and read both at the same times. Only the liquid changes, so any difference comes from the liquid.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water, mixes the bath and sets the tumblers in it. The oil in this test is never put on a flame and never gets hotter than about 70 degrees. Work on a steady table with a tray under the pan. Glass thermometers break easily, so never stir with them, and lay them flat on a cloth. Wipe up any spilt oil at once, because it is slippery. The steel tumblers get hot in the bath, so lift them out with a cloth or tongs.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw two lines on one graph, one for the oil and one for the water, with time along the bottom. Write how many degrees each one rose in 12 minutes.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Oil (°C) | Water (°C) | Bath (°C)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the two tumblers, the oil, the water and the thermometers. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write both down at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will put one tumbler of oil and one of water in the same hot bath. After 12 minutes, which will have warmed up more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Oil and water in the same hot bath. Which warms more? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will warm up more? (good: Oil is thinner, so heat gets in quickly.; Water soaks up heat.; They will warm the same, because the bath is the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect water to warm faster, because water feels hot so quickly. Others expect no difference. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure 100 millilitres of oil into one tumbler and 100 millilitres of water into the other. Stand a thermometer in each. Wait until both lines stop. Write the two starting numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Here are 100 millilitres of oil and 100 millilitres of water. Both have stood in the room for a while. Let's see if they start at the same temperature.  
  *Child mode:* Pour 100 millilitres of oil in one tumbler. Pour 100 millilitres of water in the other. Read both.
  - Note: Both tumblers should read within a degree of each other, near the room temperature. If the oil came from a cold shelf, wait until it matches. Equal volumes of oil and water do not have exactly equal masses, because oil is a little lighter. That does not change the result, which is much bigger.
  - Check: Why must both tumblers start at the same temperature? → **So that only the liquid changes, and the difference comes from the liquid**
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up mixes boiled water and tap water in a wide pan, to about 70 degrees. The bath should be about 3 centimetres deep. Check it with a thermometer. Stand both tumblers in the pan side by side, and start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* I am making the bath now, because it is hot. The water in the pan should come up to about the same height as the liquid inside each tumbler. Keep your hands away from the pan.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes the hot bath. Do not touch it. Watch both tumblers.
  - Note: Aim for a bath of 65 to 70 degrees. A wide pan holds a lot of water, so it cools slowly. If a tumbler floats or tips, pour a little water out of the pan. Never put the oil on a flame in this test.
- **s4 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Every 3 minutes, for 12 minutes, stir each tumbler gently with its own spoon. Read the oil, then the water. Wipe the thermometers after each reading. Write both numbers. Read the bath at 6 and 12 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Stir gently, then read the oil, then the water. Write both numbers. What do you notice about the two numbers as time goes on?  
  *Child mode:* Stir. Read the oil. Read the water. Write both numbers. Again in 3 minutes.
  - Note: A typical result in a 29 degree room, with a bath starting at 70: the oil reads 29, 41, 50, 56, 60. The water reads 29, 35, 40, 44, 47. The oil warms by about 31 degrees and the water by about 18, which is not quite twice as much but near it. Your numbers will differ. The bath cools by a few degrees. A thermometer in oil may need an extra wipe.
  - Check: Why do we stir each liquid gently before every reading? → **So the whole liquid is at one temperature, and the reading is right**
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a line graph with two lines. Then ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw two lines on one graph. Then say what I think, what I saw, and why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: oil warms about twice as fast as water in the same bath. What I saw: the oil rose from 29 to 60, and the water from 29 to 47. Why: water needs about twice as much heat as oil for each degree, so the same heat warms the oil by more degrees.
  - Check: In the same bath the oil warmed by about 30 degrees and the water by about 15. About how much more heat does water need for each degree? → **About twice as much**
### e2 · The Katori and the Bowl: which melts more ice? (25 min)
- **question:** A small katori holds hot water at 60 degrees. A big bowl holds warmer-than-room water at 40 degrees. If you put the same amount of ice in each, which melts more ice?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which will melt more ice, the hotter katori or the cooler bowl? Or will they melt the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Take the ice cubes from the same tray, and put the same number in each container at the same time. Stir both gently in the same way. Count the cubes left at the same times. Only the amount and the temperature of the water change, so the difference comes from them.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water, mixes it and pours it. The child does not touch the hot water. Wipe up any spills, because wet floors are slippery. Glass thermometers break easily, so never stir with them.
- **record:** Write the temperature of each container at the start and at the end. Write how many cubes were left at 5 and 10 minutes. Draw each container with the cubes left.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Cubes left: katori | Cubes left: bowl | Temperature at the end (°C)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the katori, the bowl and the ice. Explain that the katori will hold hotter water and the bowl will hold cooler water. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* A small katori will hold hot water. A big bowl will hold warm water, but cooler than the katori. We will drop the same ice in both. Which will melt more? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A small katori of very hot water. A big bowl of warm water. Which melts more ice? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will melt more ice? (good: The hotter water is stronger.; The bowl has more water, so more heat.; They will melt the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children say the hotter katori, because hotter seems stronger. That is the idea this test checks.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up mixes half boiled and half tap water to about 60 degrees. Pour 100 millilitres into the katori. Fill the jug with 600 millilitres of tap water, add 400 millilitres of the hot mix, and pour it into the bowl. Read both temperatures and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* I am mixing the hot water now. The katori gets 100 millilitres at about 60 degrees. The bowl gets a whole litre, at about 40 degrees. Let's read both.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes the hot katori and the warm bowl. Read both. Write them down.
  - Note: The katori should read about 60 and the bowl about 40. The bowl is cooler than the katori, but it holds ten times as much water. If your bowl reads 35 to 45, that is fine.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take 12 ice cubes from the same tray. Let them stand on a plate for 2 minutes, so the surface starts to melt. Then put 6 in the katori and 6 in the bowl at the same moment. Start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* Six cubes in each, at the same moment. Why do we use the same number of cubes, from the same tray?  
  *Child mode:* Put six ice cubes in each. Start the timer.
  - Note: Standing the cubes for 2 minutes lets them reach about 0 degrees, rather than the freezer's minus 18. If the cubes are tiny and all melt in both containers, repeat with 12 cubes in each.
  - Check: Why do we use the same number of ice cubes, from the same tray, in both? → **So the only differences are the amount and the temperature of the water**
- **s4 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stir both gently with a spoon. At 5 minutes and at 10 minutes, count the ice cubes still floating, big or small, and write the numbers. At 10 minutes, read both temperatures.  
  *Parent says:* Stir gently. How many cubes are left now? How many are left in the bowl? What is happening to each temperature?  
  *Child mode:* Stir. Count the cubes left. Read the temperatures. Write them down.
  - Note: A typical result: after 10 minutes the katori still has 1 or 2 small pieces, and reads about 0 to 2 degrees. The bowl has no ice, and reads about 25 degrees. The katori has too little heat to melt all its ice, even though it started hotter. If the katori melts all the ice too, add 4 more cubes to each and repeat.
  - Check: The bowl started cooler than the katori, but melted more ice. Why? → **The bowl held far more water, so it held far more heat to give**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what I think, what I saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: how much heat a thing can give depends on how much of it there is, as well as on its temperature. What I saw: the katori at 60 melted only part of its ice, but the bowl at 40 melted all of its ice. Why: the bowl had ten times as much water, so ten times as much heat to give away for each degree it cooled.
  - Check: If the bowl held only 100 millilitres of water at 40 degrees, like the katori, which would melt more ice? → **The katori at 60 degrees, because it is hotter and holds the same amount**
### e3 · Stuck at Zero: where does the heat go while ice melts? (35 min)
- **question:** If you stand a tumbler of crushed ice in a bath of warm water, how does the thermometer in the ice change as the ice melts?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Heat will flow into the ice from the warm bath. Will the thermometer rise steadily, stay still for a while, or jump? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Read the thermometer at the same times, every 2 minutes. Stir the ice gently with a spoon, never with the thermometer. Keep the same bath for the whole test. This test has one change, which is time, so we watch how the reading changes as heat keeps going in.
- **safety:** A grown-up cracks the ice and mixes the warm water. Keep the bath no hotter than a pleasant bath. Wipe up any water, because wet floors are slippery. Glass thermometers break easily, so never stir with them and lay them flat on a cloth.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a line graph with time along the bottom and temperature up the side. Circle the flat part, and write when the last ice melted.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Reading in the ice (°C) | Is there ice left?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the ice, the thermometer and the bath. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will stand a tumbler of ice in warm water. Heat will keep flowing into the ice. What will the thermometer do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Warm water heats the ice. What will the thermometer do? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the thermometer will do that? (good: Heat is going in, so it will rise.; It will stay at zero until the ice is gone.; It will jump up quickly.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children expect a steady rise. The flat part is the surprise of this test.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up wraps 10 ice cubes in a cloth and cracks them with a rolling pin. Put the pieces in the steel tumbler, with 50 millilitres of cold tap water. Stir with a spoon. Stand the thermometer in it and wait until the reading stops. Write the start.  
  *Parent says:* I am cracking the ice into small pieces, so it can melt faster. The thermometer should read about zero. Is that what it reads?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cracks the ice. Put it in the tumbler with a little cold water. Read the thermometer.
  - Note: Ice that has just come out of a freezer can read 1 to 3 degrees below zero at first. After a minute of stirring it should settle at about 0. The thermometer is not exact, so a reading from minus 1 to 1 counts as zero.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up fills the basin with 2 litres of warm tap water, about as warm as a pleasant bath. Stand the tumbler in the basin, so the water outside comes up the tumbler's side by about 4 centimetres. Start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the warm bath. Heat can only flow one way, from the warm bath into the cold ice. Let's start the timer.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up fills the basin with warm water. Stand the tumbler in it. Start the timer.
  - Note: If the bath is cooler than about 30 degrees, the ice melts slowly and the test takes much longer. Ask a grown-up to add a little warm water. The bath is large so that it stays warm all through the test.
- **s4 (16 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every 2 minutes, stir the ice gently with the spoon. Read the thermometer. Write the number and whether there is ice left. Keep going until all the ice is gone and the reading has risen at least 5 degrees.  
  *Parent says:* Stir gently, then read. What number is it now? Is there ice left? What is the thermometer doing?  
  *Child mode:* Stir. Read. Write the number. Is there ice left? Again in 2 minutes.
  - Note: A typical result: 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 5, 10, 15, 20, 24 at the 2-minute marks. The reading stays at zero for 10 to 14 minutes, while there is ice, and then climbs fast once the last ice has gone. Your times will differ, but the flat part should appear. If the reading never stays flat, the bath may be too hot or the ice too small. Try again with fewer, bigger pieces.
  - Check: For ten minutes the thermometer stayed at 0, though the bath kept giving heat. Where was the heat going? → **Into melting the ice, by pulling its particles apart**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a line graph. Circle the flat part. Then ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the graph. Circle the flat part. Say what I think, what I saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: while ice melts, heat goes in but the temperature stays at zero. What I saw: the reading stayed at 0 for about 12 minutes, and then rose quickly after the last ice melted. Why: the heat was used to melt the ice, and only when the ice was gone could the heat make the water warmer.
  - Check: At the moment the last ice melted, what did the thermometer start to do? → **Rise quickly, because the heat now went into warming the water**

## Activities
### a1 · The Balloon on the Bottle: does warm air take up more room? (30 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water. It must not be boiling, because a thin plastic bottle goes soft in very hot water. Hold the bottle by its neck. Do not blow up the balloon while it sits on the bottle.
- **record:** Draw the bottle and balloon three times: at the start, in hot water and in ice water. Write the measurement round the balloon under each drawing.
- **a1s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bottle, the balloon and the two basins. Ask for a guess about the hot water and the ice water, with a reason. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* The balloon sits on the bottle. We stand the bottle in hot water. What will the balloon do? Then we stand it in ice water. What will it do? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A balloon on a bottle. Hot water. Ice water. What happens to the balloon? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the balloon will do that? (good: The hot water goes in the bottle and pushes the balloon.; The air inside gets bigger when warm.; Nothing will happen.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children think the hot water or steam gets in. The bottle stays closed, so only the air already inside is involved.
- **a1s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fit the balloon over the mouth of the empty bottle. Wrap a string round the widest part of the balloon, and measure the string with a ruler. Write it down as the start.  
  *Parent says:* The bottle looks empty, but it is full of air. The balloon seals the air inside. Measure round the balloon. What does the string measure?  
  *Child mode:* Fit the balloon on the bottle. Measure round it. Write the number.
  - Note: The balloon will be flat, so the string measure is small, perhaps 4 to 6 centimetres. The balloon must fit tightly. If you can hear air hissing out, press the neck of the balloon down or use another balloon.
- **a1s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up pours hot water, about 5 centimetres deep, into a basin. Stand the bottle in the water and hold it by its neck. Watch the balloon for 3 to 4 minutes. Then measure round the balloon again.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the balloon. What is it doing? Is the bottle leaking? Where is the air coming from?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours the hot water. Stand the bottle in it. Watch the balloon.
  - Note: Expect the balloon to swell slowly to the size of a lime or even an orange, and the string measure to climb to 15 to 25 centimetres. The balloon has to stretch before it starts, so the first minute can look slow. If nothing happens, the balloon may be too stiff or leaking.
  - Check: The balloon swelled up in the hot water, but the bottle was closed. Where did the extra air come from? → **Nowhere. The air already inside took up more room**
- **a1s4 (8 min)**

  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: warm air takes up more room than cool air. What I saw: the balloon swelled in hot water and shrank in ice water. Why: heat made the air particles move faster and push further apart.

  - Note: A groundnut often burns for 30 seconds to a minute. If it will not catch fire, hold the match to it for a few seconds, or use dry coconut. Keep the katori steady and close. The water usually rises 5 to 15 degrees. Keep the pin's tip away from fingers. Put the burnt nut in the bowl of water.

  - Ask: Where did the rest of the heat go? (good: Into the air.; Into the katori and the thermometer.; Into the smoke and the hot gases.)
  - Note: Most of the heat warms the air around the flame, the katori and the smoke. A good home result catches a quarter to a third of the heat. This is why scientists in a proper laboratory put the flame inside a closed container. Good measuring includes saying how much you may have lost.

## Worksheets
### Heat and Temperature (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (fill) One calorie is the heat that warms 1 gram of ____ by 1 degree Celsius.  
   **Answer:** ['water']. *Why:* The calorie was defined using water, because water is easy to find and easy to measure. Water needs 1 calorie for each gram and each degree.
2. (mcq) A small katori of water is at 70 °C. A big bucket of water is at 40 °C. Which statement is true?  
   Options: The katori is hotter, but the bucket can give away more heat / The katori is hotter, and it can also give away more heat / The bucket is hotter, because it is bigger  
   **Answer:** The katori is hotter, but the bucket can give away more heat. *Why:* Temperature tells how hot a thing is, and the katori is hotter. But the bucket has far more water, so it holds far more heat to give. Picking the second answer mixes up hot with how much heat.
3. (truefalse) Two things at the same temperature always hold the same amount of heat.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A bucket and a katori of water can be at the same temperature, but the bucket holds far more heat because it has far more water.
4. (mcq) Kavya heats 100 grams of oil and 100 grams of water on the same stove for the same time. What happens?  
   Options: The oil warms by more degrees / The water warms by more degrees / Both warm by the same number of degrees  
   **Answer:** The oil warms by more degrees. *Why:* Oil needs only about half as much heat as water for each degree. So the same heat warms the oil by about twice as many degrees. Picking the same number of degrees is common, because the heat is the same. But the two liquids answer it differently.
5. (sort) Sort these. In the same sun for the same time, does each one warm up slowly or quickly?  
   **Answer:** 1 kilogram of water in a steel vessel → Warms slowly; 1 kilogram of dry sand → Warms quickly; 1 kilogram of coconut water → Warms slowly; 1 kilogram iron tawa → Warms quickly; 1 kilogram of milk → Warms slowly; 1 kilogram of bricks → Warms quickly. *Why:* Water, coconut water and milk are mostly water, so they need a lot of heat for each degree and warm slowly. Sand, iron and brick need much less heat for each degree, so they warm quickly.
6. (match) Match each thing to what happens to its temperature.  
   **Answer:** Ice melting in a glass → Stays at 0 °C while any ice is left; Water boiling in an open pot at sea level → Stays at about 100 °C; Water in a kettle that is warming up → Rises; A cup of tea left on the table → Falls towards room temperature. *Why:* While a solid melts or a liquid boils, heat goes into changing the state and the temperature holds still. In the other two cases, the heat changes the temperature.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the one that needs the least heat to warm by one degree (same mass), to the one that needs the most.  
   **Answer:** ['Gold', 'Steel', 'Dry sand', 'Cooking oil', 'Water']. *Why:* Gold needs about 0.03 calories for each gram and each degree. Steel needs about 0.1, dry sand about 0.2, oil about 0.5 and water 1. Water needs the most.
8. (mcq) A chapati has about 100 kilocalories. How many calories is that?  
   Options: 100,000 calories / 100 calories / 1,000 calories  
   **Answer:** 100,000 calories. *Why:* A kilocalorie is 1,000 calories. 100 times 1,000 is 100,000 calories. Picking 100 means forgetting that the 'kilo' means a thousand.
9. (truefalse) Sweat cools you because sweat is colder than your skin.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Sweat starts at about body temperature. It cools you by evaporating, which takes heat from your skin.
10. (fill) While ice is melting in a glass, a thermometer in the ice stays at ____ degrees Celsius.  
   **Answer:** ['0']. *Why:* The heat that goes in is used to melt the ice, so the temperature stays at 0 until the last ice has melted.
11. (write) A steel spoon in a hot pan gets hot much faster than a cup of water in the same pan. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Steel needs only about a tenth as much heat as water for each gram and each degree, and a spoon has little steel. So a small amount of heat warms it by a lot. The cup holds far more water, and water needs a lot of heat for each degree, so it warms slowly.. *Why:* A strong answer says that different materials need different amounts of heat for each degree, and that a small spoon has little material to warm.
12. (write) You rub your hands together hard for 20 seconds, and they get warm. Where did the heat come from?  
   **Answer:** From the movement of rubbing. The movement energy of the hands turned into heat, by making the tiny particles in the skin jiggle faster. It did not come from a store of heat inside the hands.. *Why:* A strong answer says that movement turned into heat, and that you can keep rubbing for as long as you like, so the heat is not a store that runs out.
13. (draw) Draw two thermometers. Put one in melting ice and one in water boiling in an open pot at sea level. Write what each one reads.  
   **Answer:** The first thermometer shows 0 °C, in a glass with ice floating in water. The second shows about 100 °C, in a pot with bubbles and steam.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows 0 and 100, and remembers that both stay at these numbers while the ice melts and while the water boils.
14. (label) Label this drawing of a balloon on a bottle standing in hot water: balloon, bottle, warm air, hot water.  
   **Answer:** The balloon is on top of the bottle and swollen. The bottle is the clear plastic body below the balloon. The warm air is the air inside the bottle and balloon. The hot water is in the basin round the bottle.. *Why:* The air inside is sealed in, and when it warms it expands and fills the balloon. Nothing comes in from outside.
15. (mcq) Why do bridges and railway tracks have small gaps in them?  
   Options: Steel grows longer when it warms, and needs room to expand / Steel shrinks in the sun, so the gaps fill up / The gaps let rainwater run through  
   **Answer:** Steel grows longer when it warms, and needs room to expand. *Why:* Most solids expand when they warm. A long steel bridge can grow several centimetres on a hot day, and the gaps give it room. Picking the second answer has it backwards.
16. (mcq) On a very humid day, the air is already full of water. How does this change the way sweat cools you?  
   Options: Sweat evaporates slowly, so you feel hotter than on a dry day at the same temperature / Sweat evaporates faster, so you feel cooler / Sweat does not matter, only the air temperature does  
   **Answer:** Sweat evaporates slowly, so you feel hotter than on a dry day at the same temperature. *Why:* Sweat cools you only as fast as it evaporates. In humid air it evaporates slowly, so less heat leaves, and you feel hotter. This is why Chennai in the rains feels so sticky.
### Sharing and Hiding Heat (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)

   **Answer:** They are at the same temperature, but the bucket holds far more heat. *Why:* A thermometer shows how hot something is, not how much heat it holds. The bucket has far more water, so it holds far more heat. Picking the second answer is the most common slip.

   Options: Kilocalories / Degrees Celsius / Newtons  
   **Answer:** Kilocalories. *Why:* A kilocalorie is 1,000 calories, and a calorie is a unit of heat. Degrees Celsius measure temperature, and newtons measure force.

   Options: 100 grams of water / 100 grams of cooking oil / 100 grams of steel  
   **Answer:** 100 grams of water. *Why:* Water needs about 1 calorie for each gram and each degree. Oil needs about half of that, and steel about a tenth. So water takes the most.

   **Answer:** The oil is hotter than the water. *Why:* Oil needs less heat for each degree, so the heat from the bath warms it by more degrees. Picking exactly the same is common, because the bath is the same. But the liquids answer the bath differently.

   **Answer:** It stays at about 100 °C, and the water boils away faster. *Why:* The extra heat goes into turning more water to steam. The water cannot go much above 100 °C while it boils in an open pot.

## Applications
- Hot water bottles and water-filled heat packs keep a bed warm for hours, because water holds so much heat.
- Passive solar buildings and greenhouses use drums or tanks of water, or thick stone walls, to soak up the day's sun and give it back at night.
- Cars have radiators full of water and coolant to carry heat away from the engine, because water takes up a lot of heat.
- Cooks choose pans by how they hold heat: a thick iron tawa stays hot for dosas, and a thin aluminium pan heats quickly.
- Calorie labels on food packets tell us the heat energy in food, found by burning food in a sealed device, as in the groundnut test.
- Weather forecasters use the sea's slow warming to explain why coastal towns have milder temperatures than inland ones, and why the monsoon starts.
- Railways and bridges are built with expansion gaps, so that hot days do not bend the steel.
- Steam engines and the steam in a pressure cooker work because boiling water stores a lot of hidden heat that steam gives back.
- A clay matka or a wet cloth cools water by evaporation, without a fridge or any electricity.
- Doctors use the fact that sweat cools us when they check for fever and heat stroke, and tell people to drink water in hot weather.

## Open questions
- **Exactly why does water hold so much heat?** Scientists know that links between water particles take in heat, but working out the exact number from first principles is still hard.
- **Is there a hottest possible temperature?** Coldest is clear: absolute zero. Nobody knows if hot has a limit, because at extreme temperatures the known laws of physics stop working.
- **Why does water shrink as it cools to 4 °C and then expand again?** Scientists have good ideas about how the water particles arrange themselves, but the full reason is still being debated.
- **How do living cells sense heat and cold?** Scientists found special gates in cells that open at certain temperatures, work that won a Nobel Prize in 2021. How the whole body turns them into feelings of warm and cold is not fully understood.
- **Does a black hole have a temperature?** Stephen Hawking calculated in 1974 that a black hole should glow very faintly, like a warm object. Nobody has measured it, and nobody is sure how it works.
- **How hot was the very early universe, and what was it made of?** Scientists think the universe began extremely hot, and that matter was a soup of tiny particles for a moment. They study it by smashing nuclei together, but many questions remain.

## Warm-up
- What does one calorie measure? → Heat: the heat that warms 1 gram of water by 1 degree Celsius.
- Which needs more heat to warm by one degree, 100 grams of water or 100 grams of oil? → The water.
- A katori of tea and a bucket of tea are at the same temperature. Which holds more heat? → The bucket.
- What does a thermometer in melting ice read? → About 0 degrees Celsius, until the ice is gone.
- Can boiling water in an open pot go above 100 degrees at sea level? → No. More heat only makes it boil away faster.
- What turns into heat when you rub your hands? → Movement energy.
- Why does sweat cool you? → It takes heat away from your skin as it evaporates.
- Why does the sea warm more slowly than the sand? → Water needs a lot of heat for each degree, and mixes the heat through a deep layer.
- What happens to most things when they warm up? → They expand, and take up more room.
