# Animals around us: coverings, legs, wings and fins
`sci.animals.animals-around-us.L2`

**Summary.** A crow stands on two legs, a cat on four and an ant on six. Animals are covered in hair or fur, feathers, scales, a shell or bare skin. They move with legs, wings, fins, or their whole body. You count an ant's legs, look closely at your own hair and nails, and build a dough animal to test how many legs a body needs to stand without help. You also sort the animals you see into pets, farm animals and wild animals, and find that many wild animals live right next to us.

**Intro.** *A crow on the window ledge has two legs. The cat downstairs has four. The ant on the kitchen floor has six. Why do animals come with so many different numbers of legs?* Animals around us look very different. A crow is covered in feathers, a cat in fur, a gecko in tiny scales and you in skin and hair. A fish swims with fins, a crow flies with wings, and a snake slides along with no legs at all. Scientists compare animals by looking at their bodies, part by part. In this unit you count legs, compare coverings and test how many legs a body needs to stand without help.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can sort animals by their body covering: hair or fur, feathers, scales, a shell, or bare skin.

2. By the end I can say which animals move with legs, wings, fins or their whole body, and that some use more than one.
3. By the end I can explain why a body with three or four legs stands still easily, and how two-legged animals keep their balance.
4. By the end I can tell a pet, a farm animal and a wild animal apart, and say that wild animals live near us too.
5. By the end I can say that humans are animals too: mammals with hair, two legs and no wings.

## Terms
- **Body covering**: A body covering is what covers the outside of an animal's body. *e.g.* A crow's covering is feathers. A cat's covering is fur. Your covering is skin and hair.
- **Fur and hair**: Fur is thick, soft hair that covers an animal's body. Hair grows out of the skin. *e.g.* A dog, a goat and a squirrel have fur. You have hair on your head and tiny hairs on your arms.
- **Feathers**: Feathers are the light covering of a bird. Every bird has feathers, and no other animal alive today has them. *e.g.* A pigeon, a peacock and a hen all have feathers.
- **Scales**: Scales are small, hard, flat plates that cover the skin of some animals. *e.g.* A fish, a snake and a gecko have scales.
- **Shell**: A shell is a hard case that covers and protects a soft body. *e.g.* A snail and a tortoise each carry a shell.
- **Wings**: Wings are the body parts an animal flaps to fly. *e.g.* A crow, a bat and a butterfly have wings.
- **Fins**: Fins are flat body parts that a fish uses to swim, steer and stay upright in water. *e.g.* A fish has a tail fin, a fin on the back and fins on the sides.
- **Mammal**: A mammal is an animal with hair or fur, whose mother feeds her babies on milk. *e.g.* A cow, a dog, a bat, an elephant and you are all mammals.
- **Balance**: Balance means keeping your body steady so that you do not fall over. *e.g.* When you stand on one foot, your foot and ankle keep moving a little to keep your balance.
- **Pet**: A pet is an animal that a family keeps at home and looks after. *e.g.* A cat that sleeps at home and is fed every day is a pet.
- **Farm animal**: A farm animal is an animal that people keep for milk, eggs, wool or work. *e.g.* A cow, a goat, a hen and a buffalo are farm animals.
- **Wild animal**: A wild animal lives on their own, and finds their own food and home. *e.g.* A crow, a gecko on the wall and a tiger in the forest are wild animals.

## Explanations
### Body coverings · *defined*
**Definition:** A body covering is the outside layer of an animal's body: hair or fur, feathers, scales, a shell, or bare skin.

Most mammals are covered in hair or fur. Birds are covered in feathers. Fish, snakes and lizards have scales. Snails and tortoises carry a shell. Frogs and earthworms have bare, damp skin. Your hair and nails, a crow's feathers, a cow's horns and a gecko's scales are all made of the same tough material. It is called keratin. Fish scales are different: they are made of a bony material.
- e.g. A goat is covered in fur.
- e.g. A peacock is covered in feathers.
- e.g. A gecko is covered in tiny bumpy scales.
- e.g. A frog has bare, damp skin.
### Counting legs · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals can have no legs, two, four, six, eight or many legs.

Snakes, fish and earthworms have no legs. Birds and humans walk on two legs. Dogs, cows, frogs and geckos have four. Every adult insect, such as an ant, a fly or a butterfly, has six legs. Spiders have eight legs, so a spider is not an insect. Centipedes and millipedes have many legs. One millipede in Western Australia has 1,306 legs!
- e.g. A sparrow has 2 legs.
- e.g. A buffalo has 4 legs.
- e.g. A mosquito has 6 legs.
- e.g. A spider has 8 legs.
### Wings and fins · *defined*
**Definition:** Wings are body parts that an animal flaps to fly. Fins are flat body parts that a fish uses to swim and steer.

Birds, bats and most insects have wings. A bat is not a bird: bats have fur and feed their babies milk, so bats are mammals. Some birds have wings but do not fly, such as the penguin and the ostrich. A fish has a tail fin that pushes them forward. Fins on the back and belly stop the fish from rolling over. Fins on the sides help them steer and stop. Dolphins and whales have fins too, but they breathe air and feed their babies milk. They are mammals, not fish.
- e.g. A crow has wings and two legs.
- e.g. A butterfly has wings and six legs.
- e.g. A fish has fins and no legs.
- e.g. A dolphin has fins and is a mammal.
### Standing and balance · *defined*
**Definition:** A body stands still by itself when its weight is above the space between its feet. Three feet, spread out, make that space a triangle.

One leg or two legs give a body only a small place to stand, so the body tips over. Three legs, spread out, make a triangle, so a stool with three legs stands. Four or six legs stand easily too. Humans and birds stand on two legs, and keep their balance by moving all the time. Your feet feel the floor, your ears feel you tilt and your eyes see you lean. Your muscles then tighten and loosen to catch you. With your eyes shut, you sway more, so you feel this more.
- e.g. A camera stand has three legs.
- e.g. A cow standing in the road has four legs on the ground.
- e.g. A crow standing on one leg keeps adjusting their foot on the wire.
### Pets, farm animals and wild animals · *defined*
**Definition:** A pet lives with a family who look after them. A farm animal is kept for milk, eggs, wool or work. A wild animal lives on their own and finds their own food.

Wild animals do not only live in forests. Crows, pigeons, squirrels, geckos and ants are wild animals that live in cities. In Mumbai, leopards live in a forest park inside the city. Being a pet depends on how an animal lives, not on what kind of animal they are. A parrot in the forest is wild. In India, the Wildlife Protection Act of 1972 makes it against the law to catch or keep wild Indian birds, such as parakeets and munias. Some animals, like street dogs and temple cows, are fed by many people but belong to no one family. People argue about what to call them.
- e.g. A cat at home is a pet.
- e.g. A buffalo kept for milk is a farm animal.
- e.g. A crow on the water tank is a wild animal.
- e.g. A street dog fed by the whole lane is a community dog.
### Sorting by more than one part · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists sort animals by looking at many body parts together, not just one.

Wings alone do not make a bird: butterflies and bats have wings too. Fins alone do not make a fish: dolphins have fins. Scales alone do not make a reptile: the Indian pangolin is a mammal covered in scales, and the mother feeds her baby on milk. So scientists look at the covering, the legs, how the animal breathes and how the babies are fed, all together. Humans are mammals: you have hair, you breathed air from your first minute, and babies drink milk.
- e.g. A bat has wings and fur, so a bat is a mammal.
- e.g. A pangolin has scales and drinks milk as a baby, so a pangolin is a mammal.
- e.g. A penguin has wings and feathers but cannot fly, and a penguin is a bird.
### Why did feathers first grow? · *debated*
Fossils show that some dinosaurs had feathers, millions of years before birds flew. So feathers did not first grow for flying. Scientists have different ideas about what the first feathers did.
- e.g. Fossils of small feathered dinosaurs have been found in China since 1996.
- e.g. Baby chicks have soft fluffy feathers that keep them warm.
- **Many scientists:** The first feathers kept small dinosaurs warm, like the fluff on a chick.
- **Other scientists:** The first feathers were for showing off, to attract a mate or warn a rival, like a peacock's tail.
- **Some scientists:** Both were true, and feathers were used for flying only much later.

## Mostly agreed
- Every bird has feathers, and no other animal alive today has feathers. *How we know:* Scientists have studied about 11,000 kinds of birds. Every one has feathers. No mammal, fish, reptile or insect alive today has them.
- Every adult insect has six legs. *How we know:* Ants, flies, bees, beetles and butterflies all have six legs as adults. Spiders have eight legs, which is one reason spiders are not insects.
- Humans are mammals. *How we know:* Humans have hair, breathe air with lungs and feed babies on milk, like cows, dogs and bats.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, Carl Linnaeus, and a jury in New York, About 350 BCE, 1758 and 1818.** For a very long time, many people thought a whale was a fish. Whales live in the sea, and they swim with fins and a tail. But about 2,300 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle looked closely at dolphins and whales. He wrote that they breathe air, give birth to live babies and feed them milk, like a cow. Fish breathe with gills and do not feed their babies milk. Still, for many hundreds of years after him, most books called whales fish. In 1758 the Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus put whales into his new group, the mammals, with cows, bats and humans. Even then, many people did not agree. In 1818, a court in New York had to decide if whale oil was fish oil. The jury said that a whale is a fish! Today scientists agree that whales are mammals. Here is one more clue you can see in any picture: a whale's tail moves up and down, but a fish's tail moves from side to side. To sort an animal, look at the whole body and how the animal lives, not only at where they live.  
*What people believed before:* A whale is a fish, because whales live in the sea and swim with fins.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Anything with fins that lives in water is a fish.** (A very common idea, held for hundreds of years; *rejected*)  
  For: Most animals with fins that live in water are fish, like rohu, sardines and goldfish.  
  Against: Dolphins and whales have fins, but they breathe air and feed their babies milk. The river dolphin of the Ganga and the Brahmaputra is a mammal.
- **Wild animals live only in forests.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Tigers, elephants and many other wild animals do live in forests.  
  Against: Crows, pigeons, squirrels, geckos and ants are wild, and live in cities. In Mumbai, leopards live in Sanjay Gandhi National Park, inside the city.
- **Bats are a kind of bird, because bats have wings.** (An old idea, found in many old books; *rejected*)  
  For: Bats have wings and fly, like birds.  
  Against: Bats have fur, not feathers. Bat babies drink their mother's milk. So bats are mammals.
- **Dogs became pets because people caught wolf pups and tamed them.** (One idea among scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Wolf pups raised by people can become friendly with people.  
  Against: Other scientists think the friendliest wolves came close to people's camps by themselves, to eat scraps. Over many generations, these wolves became dogs. Nobody knows for sure which story is right.

## Roots
We have compared animals by their coverings, legs, wings and fins, and tested how many legs a body needs to stand. Now we ask what standing on two legs tells us about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Your dough animal stood up without help on three or four legs, but fell over on two. Standing on two legs with your eyes shut, you felt your feet and ankles keep shifting. Each shift catches your body before it tips, so you do not fall. So standing on two legs is work your body keeps doing, every second you stand.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think standing still means doing nothing. But a body on two legs is acting all the time to stay up, and the body decides each tiny shift without you having to think about it.  
*Evidence:* In the Dough Legs test, the dough animal fell over on two legs every time, and stood on three or four. Standing with eyes shut, children feel their toes press and their ankles tighten and loosen to keep them up.

- *who am i:* You are an animal with hair, skin and two legs. Your body keeps you standing, second by second.
- *what is life:* A living body keeps acting to stay as it is. Standing up on two legs is one of the things your body does without stopping.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of bodies built in different ways: two legs or eight, feathers or fins. Each body suits the life that animal lives.

Animal bodies come in many forms: two legs or eight, feathers or fur, fins or wings. Underneath, many of these different coverings are one material: your hair, a crow's feathers and a gecko's scales are all keratin. And every body keeps itself going in their own way. A stool stands by its shape, but a crow and you stand by sensing and correcting all the time. Your feet and ankles feel the floor, and your muscles answer each shift.

- **Yoga Sutras of Patanjali 2.46 and 2.47:** A posture should be steady and comfortable. Patanjali says it becomes steady when effort is relaxed and the mind rests on the endless. (In the eyes-shut stand, you were steady only because your feet and ankles kept working. Your effort could relax, because your body kept correcting below what you noticed.)
- **Udana 6.4, the Tittha Sutta (Buddhist Pali Canon):** Some men who could not see each felt one part of an elephant. One felt the leg and said an elephant is like a pillar. One felt the trunk and said an elephant is like a snake. Each was partly right, and they quarrelled. (In this unit, one body part alone often fooled us. Wings did not make the bat a bird, and scales did not make the pangolin a reptile. We knew each animal only by putting all the parts together.)
- Science (settled): When a person stands still, the body sways a little all the time, and ankle muscles correct the sway. With the eyes shut, the sway gets bigger.
- Science (open): Many of these corrections are run by the spinal cord and brain without our attention. Whether the body feels or knows each correction in its own way is an open question.
- Other view, Many neuroscientists: These quick corrections are reflexes: fixed, automatic loops of nerves, with no awareness needed. In this view, awareness belongs to the brain, and only some of what the body does reaches it.
- Practice, Let the Floor Hold You (3 min): Stand up tall and shut your eyes. Feel your feet working to keep you up. Now lie down flat on the floor. Let your arms and legs go loose. Feel how your feet can rest now. The floor is holding you. Breathe in and out slowly, three times.
- Ask: When you stand still, who is doing the work of keeping you up?
- Ask: A stool stands by its shape. You stand by moving. Which way of standing is more like being alive?
- Ask: A crow sleeps on a branch without falling. What do you think the crow's feet feel?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When you stand still, which parts of you are working? Who decides each tiny shift? (hint: Remember standing with your eyes shut. Did you decide to move your ankle, or did your ankle move by itself?)
- *big:* Is a pet a kind of animal, or a kind of friendship between an animal and a family? (hint: A parrot can be wild in the forest. A street dog can be fed by a whole lane. What makes an animal a pet?)

- *big:* Does the gecko on your wall think of your home as their home too? (hint: The gecko was there before you noticed. The gecko finds food, hides and sleeps there.)

## Story: The Clicking in the Hall
*A made-up story. House geckos really do make clicking calls, eat moths near lights, grip with tiny hairs on their toes and can drop their tails.*

Every night that week, something in Nila's flat went tchk, tchk, tchk.

Nila was seven. She lived on the fourth floor of a building in Bengaluru, with Amma, her grandmother Ajji and her little brother Kabir, who was five.

'There is a mouse in the shoe rack,' said Kabir. 'A big one.'

'Maybe,' said Amma, tying her bank badge on. 'Or maybe not. You two can find out.'

Nila decided to find out the way a scientist would. She took a notebook and wrote CLUES at the top.

Clue one: the sound came at night, from near the tube light in the hall. Clue two: on the window ledge, Nila found three tiny droppings. Each one was dark, with a little white tip at one end.

That evening Hema aunty came over from the flat opposite. Hema aunty helped at an animal rescue centre on weekends, and she knew a lot about animals.

Nila showed her the notebook. Hema aunty read it carefully.

'Before you guess,' said Hema aunty, 'think about the bodies of the animals that might live here. What covers them? How many legs do they have? What do they leave behind?'

Nila drew three boxes. In the first she drew a mouse. 'A mouse has fur and four legs,' she said. 'A mouse squeaks.'

In the second she drew a cockroach. 'A cockroach has six legs and a hard, shiny case, and wings,' said Nila. 'I have never heard a cockroach go tchk.'

'Now look at the droppings,' said Hema aunty. 'Mouse droppings are dark all over. Birds and lizards are different. They do not pass wee as water. Their wee comes out as a white paste, with the dropping. That is the white tip.'

'A bird can't get into the shoe rack,' said Kabir. 'The door is shut.'

'So,' said Nila slowly, 'our visitor is a lizard.'

That night, all four of them sat in the dark hall. Ajji held the torch. They waited and waited. Kabir nearly fell asleep.

Then Ajji switched the tube light on.

On the wall beside the light was a small, pale animal with four legs and a long tail. A moth fluttered near the light. The animal ran up the wall in a flash, and the moth was gone.

'Halli!' whispered Kabir. That is the Kannada word for a house gecko.

The gecko ran across the ceiling, upside down, and stopped. The gecko did not fall.

'How do her feet stick?' asked Kabir.

'Under her toes are millions of tiny hairs,' said Hema aunty. 'They are so small and so many that they hold on to the ceiling.'

Nila looked through Ajji's reading glasses. The gecko's skin was covered in tiny bumps. 'Scales,' said Nila. 'Like a snake. But with four legs.'

Just then the gecko went tchk, tchk, tchk.

Everyone laughed. The mystery was solved.

'Can she be our pet?' asked Kabir. 'We can call her Chikki.'

Hema aunty smiled. 'Chikki lives in your home, but she is a wild animal. Nobody feeds her. She catches her own moths and mosquitoes. She comes and goes as she likes. My cat Pepper is a pet. I give Pepper food and water every day, and take her to the vet.'

'So Chikki is our wild neighbour,' said Nila.

'Will she bite me?' asked Kabir.

'House geckos are harmless,' said Hema aunty. 'But do not try to catch her. If a gecko is grabbed, her tail can come off, so that she can run away. A new tail grows back, but it is never quite the same.'

The next morning, Nila made a new page in her notebook. She drew the mouse, the cockroach and the gecko. She drew the crow on the window ledge, with feathers, two legs and wings. Then she drew herself, with hair, skin, two legs and no wings.

'Why are you on the page?' asked Kabir.

'Because I am an animal too,' said Nila. 'A mammal, like the mouse. Hema aunty said so.'

That night the tube light went on, and the moths came, and so did Chikki.

'Good night, wild neighbour,' whispered Nila.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Nila tells animals apart by their bodies: what covers them, how many legs they have and what they leave behind. She also learns that an animal can live in your home and still be wild.

- Ask: Which clues helped Nila find out that the animal was a gecko and not a mouse?
- Ask: Why is Chikki a wild animal, even though she lives in Nila's home?
- Ask: Why did Nila draw herself on the same page as the animals?

## Experiments
### e1 · Dough Legs: how many legs does a body need to stand? (25 min)
- **question:** How many legs does a body need to stand still without help? And how do we stand on only two?
- **predict:** Guess first. How many legs will the dough animal need to stand without help without falling over? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same dough body and legs of the same length every time. Only the number of legs changes. We let go and count to five each time, and try each number twice, because one try can be luck.
- **safety:** Toothpicks have sharp points. A grown-up cuts any sticks, and nobody waves the legs near eyes. Stand near a wall or a grown-up when you shut your eyes.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw the dough animals that stood and the ones that fell.
- **results table:** Number of legs | Did it stand without help? | Did it wobble?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Roll the dough into a ball, then press it into a short, fat egg shape. 2. Push one leg into the middle of the bottom.  
  *Parent says:* This is our dough animal's body. Now the body has one leg.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the dough into a fat egg. Push in one leg.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess and to say why. Write the guess on the paper.  
  *Parent says:* How many legs will our animal need to stand without help? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. How many legs does it need to stand? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Two, because I stand on two legs.; Four, because a cow and a dog stand on four.)
  - Note: Many children guess two, because they stand on two legs themselves. Do not correct the guess. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the body on the board on one leg and let go. 2. Count to five. 3. Write down what happened. 4. Add a second leg and test again. 5. Then test three legs, four legs and six legs. 6. Try each number twice.  
  *Parent says:* Let go gently and count to five. Did the animal stand, wobble or fall?  
  *Child mode:* Let go. Count to five. Did it stand or fall?
  - Ask: What happened with two legs? (good: The dough animal fell over, forward or backward.; It fell every time unless I held it.)
  - Note: Push the legs in so they spread out a little, like the legs of a stool. With one or two legs, the body tips over every time. With three legs spread out, it stands. Four and six stand easily. If three legs fall, spread them wider and keep the body in the middle.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take out all but two legs. 2. Press a flat dough foot, about as wide as a coin, onto the end of each leg. 3. Test again.  
  *Parent says:* Birds stand on two legs with wide, spread toes. Will big feet help our animal stand on two legs?  
  *Child mode:* Give the two legs big flat feet. Does it stand now?

### e2 · Roll Stopper: what does a fin do? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a fin stop a floating bottle from rolling over?
- **predict:** Guess first. When we add a fin, will the bottle roll more, less or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same bottle, the same water and the same flick each time. The only change is the fin. We try three flicks each way, because one flick can be harder than another.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the plastic. Wipe up spilled water so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the bottle with and without the fin.
- **results table:** Bottle | Flick 1: turns | Flick 2: turns | Flick 3: turns
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour water into the bottle until it is about a quarter full. 2. Close the cap tight. 3. Stick a bindi on the side. 4. Lay the bottle on its side in the bucket.  
  *Parent says:* This bottle is our fish body. The bindi helps us count each time it turns over.  
  *Child mode:* Put a little water in the bottle. Stick on a bindi. Float it.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Brush a finger quickly across the top of the bottle, from one side to the other. Count how many times the bindi goes round. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Flick the top of the bottle. Count how many times the bindi comes round.  
  *Child mode:* Flick the bottle. Count the turns.
  - Note: Without a fin, the bottle usually rolls round one to three times with each flick.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess and to say why.  
  *Parent says:* Now we will tape a fin along the bottom. Will the bottle roll more, less or the same? Why?  
  *Child mode:* We will add a fin. Will it roll more or less? Why?
- **s4 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dry the bottle. 2. Tape the plastic strip along the length of the bottle, standing out like a fin. 3. Float the bottle with the fin pointing down into the water.  
  *Parent says:* A fish has a fin on the back and one underneath. Our fin goes underneath, in the water.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the fin on. Float it with the fin down.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Flick the bottle the same way, three times. Count the turns.  
  *Parent says:* Flick it just like before. What happens now?  
  *Child mode:* Flick it the same way. Count the turns.
  - Ask: What happened with the fin? (good: The bottle rocked and stopped.; It did not roll over at all.)
  - Note: With the fin, the bottle rocks a little and stops. The fin has to push a lot of water out of the way to turn, so it hardly turns.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* So what does a fin on a fish's back or belly do?  
  *Child mode:* What does a fin do for a fish?
  - Ask: Why did the fin stop the rolling? (good: Because the fin pushes against the water.; Because the water holds the fin back.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses the counts: no fin, the bottle rolled. With a fin, it stopped. Fins on a fish's back and belly do the same job. They push against the water and keep the fish upright.
  - Check: With a fin, the bottle stopped rolling. What does the fin on a fish's back do? → **Keeps the fish from rolling over**

   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 6 take away 3 is 3. The three legs on the ground make a triangle, so the ant does not tip over while walking.
9. (match) Match each body part to the right animal.  
   **Answer:** Sticky toes with tiny hairs → Gecko; Toes spread, three forward and one back → Crow; A tail fin → Fish; Hooves → Cow. *Why:* Geckos grip walls with tiny hairs on their toes. Crows grip branches with spread toes. Fish push with a tail fin. Cows walk on hard hooves.
10. (order) Put these animals in order, from most legs to fewest legs.  
   **Answer:** ['a millipede', 'a spider', 'an ant', 'a goat', 'a sparrow', 'a snake']. *Why:* A millipede has many legs, a spider 8, an ant 6, a goat 4, a sparrow 2 and a snake none.
11. (label) Write the job of each fin: tail fin, back fin, side fins.  
   **Answer:** ['tail fin: pushes the fish forward', 'back fin: stops the fish rolling over', 'side fins: steer and stop']. *Why:* Each fin does a different job. The Roll Stopper test showed what a back or belly fin does.
12. (fill) Your hair, your nails and a crow's feathers are all made of the same material, called ________.  
   **Answer:** keratin. *Why:* Hair, nails, feathers, horns and the scales of snakes and lizards are all keratin, shaped in different ways.
13. (sort) Sort these animals: insect, or not an insect?  
   **Answer:** an ant → Insect (6 legs); a spider → Not an insect; a butterfly → Insect (6 legs); a centipede → Not an insect; a mosquito → Insect (6 legs); an earthworm → Not an insect. *Why:* Ants, butterflies and mosquitoes have six legs. Spiders have eight, centipedes have many, and earthworms have none.
14. (mcq) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 2, says: 'All animals with wings are birds.' Which animal shows that she is wrong?  
   Options: A crow / A butterfly / A peacock  
   **Answer:** A butterfly. *Why:* A butterfly has wings but is an insect, with six legs and no feathers. Crows and peacocks are birds, so they do not show she is wrong.
15. (write) Two cats look the same. One lives in a flat and is fed by a family. The other lives in the lane and catches rats. Is one of them a different kind of animal? Which one is a pet? Give a reason.  
   **Answer:** No, they are the same kind of animal. The cat in the flat is a pet, because a family feeds and looks after her. The lane cat finds her own food.. *Why:* A strong answer says that being a pet depends on how an animal lives, not on what kind of animal she is.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which animal is covered in feathers?  
   Options: A cat / A pigeon / A frog  
   **Answer:** A pigeon. *Why:* A pigeon is a bird, and every bird has feathers. A cat has fur, and a frog has bare skin.
2. (mcq) How many legs does a spider have?  
   Options: 6 / 8 / 4  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* A spider has eight legs. Insects, like ants, have six. If you said 6, you were thinking of insects.
3. (mcq) Which animal swims with fins?  
   Options: A rohu fish / A goat / A sparrow  
   **Answer:** A rohu fish. *Why:* A rohu is a fish, and fish swim with fins. Goats walk on four legs, and sparrows fly with wings.
4. (mcq) In the Dough Legs test, which dough animal stood without help?  
   Options: The one with three legs spread out / The one with one leg / The one with two thin legs  
   **Answer:** The one with three legs spread out. *Why:* Three legs spread out stood, and one leg or two thin legs fell over. Three feet make a triangle to stand on.
5. (mcq) A crow lives on the roof of a building in Hyderabad. What kind of animal is the crow?  
   Options: A pet / A wild animal / A farm animal  
   **Answer:** A wild animal. *Why:* The crow finds their own food and home, and nobody owns them. Wild animals live in cities too.
6. (mcq) Which group are humans in?  
   Options: Birds / Mammals / Insects  
   **Answer:** Mammals. *Why:* Humans have hair, and our babies drink milk, so we are mammals, like cows and dogs.
7. (mcq) A butterfly has wings. How many legs does a butterfly have?  
   Options: 2 / 6 / 8  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* A butterfly is an insect, so a butterfly has six legs. Wings do not make an animal a bird.
8. (mcq) What does the fin on a fish's back do?  
   Options: Pushes the fish forward / Keeps the fish from rolling over / Helps the fish breathe  
   **Answer:** Keeps the fish from rolling over. *Why:* The back fin pushes against the water when the fish starts to roll, like the fin on the floating bottle. The tail fin pushes the fish forward.
9. (mcq) When you stood still with your eyes shut, what did your feet and ankles do?  
   Options: Nothing at all / Kept making tiny moves to keep you up / Went to sleep  
   **Answer:** Kept making tiny moves to keep you up. *Why:* Your feet and ankles keep making tiny moves to catch you before you tip. With your eyes shut, you sway more, so you feel them more.
10. (mcq) A green parakeet lives in a forest in Kerala. What kind of animal is the parakeet?  
   Options: A pet / A wild animal / A farm animal  
   **Answer:** A wild animal. *Why:* The parakeet lives on their own and finds their own food, so the parakeet is wild. In India the law protects wild birds like parakeets.
11. (mcq) What is your own body covered in?  
   Options: Feathers / Skin and hair / Scales  
   **Answer:** Skin and hair. *Why:* Your body is covered in skin, with hair growing out of it. Like other mammals, you have hair.

## Applications
- Camera stands and old milking stools have three legs, because three legs never rock.
- Engineers build six-legged walking robots that move like insects, keeping three legs on the ground at a time.
- Scientists have made sticky tapes that copy the tiny hairs on a gecko's toes.
- Sailing boats have a keel, a fin under the boat, that helps keep the boat upright, like a fish's fins.
- Vets compare the bodies of animals to know how to care for each one.

## Open questions
- **When and where did dogs first come to live with people?** Different studies point to different places and times, more than 15,000 years ago. Scientists are still arguing.
- **Why did the first feathers grow?** Some dinosaurs had feathers long before birds flew. Scientists do not agree if the first feathers were for warmth, for showing off, or both.
- **How many kinds of animals are there on Earth?** Scientists have named over a million kinds of animals. Many more have never been found or named, and nobody knows how many.

## Connected maths
- Two crows and one dog are standing on the street. How many legs are there in all? **8 legs.**. Each crow has 2 legs, so two crows have 2 and 2, which is 4. The dog has 4 legs. 4 and 4 make 8. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Three ants walk across the kitchen floor. How many legs is that? **18 legs.**. Each ant has 6 legs. 6 and 6 is 12, and 6 more is 18. That is three groups of 6. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- A spider has 8 legs and an ant has 6 legs. How many more legs does the spider have? **2 more legs.**. Count up from 6 to 8: 7, 8. That is 2 more. So the spider has 2 more legs than the ant. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In the Dough Legs test, the dough animal fell over on 1 leg and on 2 legs. It stood on 3, 4 and 6 legs. What is the smallest number of legs that let it stand? What do you think would happen with 5 legs? **3 legs. With 5 legs, it should stand too, but we would need to test it.**. 1 and 2 fell. 3 is the smallest number that stood. Every number bigger than 3 also stood, so 5 should stand as well. A test would tell us for sure. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L2`)
