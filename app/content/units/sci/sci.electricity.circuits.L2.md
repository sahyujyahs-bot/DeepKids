# Making a circuit: how a cell, two wires and a bulb make light
`sci.electricity.circuits.L2`

**Summary.** A small bulb lights when a cell, two wires and the bulb are joined in a ring. Electricity flows out of one end of the cell, through the bulb, and back into the other end. This ring is called a circuit. Your child builds a circuit, then tests it with the wires coiled, crossed and knotted. The bulb still lights, but it goes dark when both wires touch the same end of the cell.

**Intro.** *Here is a cell, a small bulb and two wires. Can you make the bulb shine using only these?* Most children try one wire first, and the bulb stays dark. With two wires joined the right way, it shines. Then a new question comes up. Does it matter how the wires lie? In this unit you build your first circuit. You twist, coil and knot the wires, and find out what the bulb really needs. You look inside a bulb at the thin wire that glows. And you find out why a cell, a bulb and a plug each need two metal parts.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can join a cell, two wires and a bulb so that the bulb lights.
1. By the end I can trace the path of electricity round my circuit with a finger.
2. By the end I can say why a bulb stays dark when there is a gap in the circuit.
3. By the end I can say that both ends of the cell must join the bulb.
4. By the end I can say that the shape of the wires does not change whether the bulb lights.
5. By the end I can name the parts of a torch bulb and say which part glows.

## Terms
- **Circuit**: A circuit is a complete ring that electricity flows round. It goes out of the cell, through the bulb and back to the cell. *e.g.* A torch has a circuit inside. When you switch it on, the ring is complete and the bulb shines.
- **Cell**: A cell is a small store of electricity. Many people call it a battery. It has two ends, one marked + and one marked −. *e.g.* An AA cell from the TV remote has a bump at the + end and a flat − end.
- **Bulb**: A bulb makes light when electricity flows through it. A torch bulb is small, with a glass top and a metal bottom. *e.g.* An old torch has a small bulb behind its glass.
- **Filament**: The filament is the very thin wire inside a bulb. Electricity makes it so hot that it glows. *e.g.* Look at a torch bulb closely, and you can see the filament as a tiny coil.
- **Wire**: A wire is a thin piece of metal that electricity flows along. It is covered in plastic, with bare metal at the ends. *e.g.* The charger for a phone has wires inside its plastic cover.
- **Connection**: A connection is a place where two metal parts touch, so electricity can pass from one to the other. *e.g.* A crocodile clip biting the end of a cell makes a connection.
- **Gap**: A gap is a break in the circuit, where metal does not touch metal. Electricity cannot cross it, so the bulb goes dark. *e.g.* Unclip one wire, and there is a gap. The bulb goes out at once.

## Explanations
### A circuit is a complete ring · *defined*
**Definition:** A circuit is a complete path that electricity can flow round, from one end of the cell, through the bulb, and back to the other end.

In your first circuit, one wire went from the cell to the bulb. The other wire went from the bulb back to the cell. When you traced it with a finger, your finger came back to where it started. That ring is the circuit. When you unclipped one wire, the ring had a gap. The bulb went dark at once, even though everything else was still there.
- e.g. A torch is a circuit you can hold. The switch opens and closes a gap in the ring.
- e.g. A doorbell rings only while the button is pressed. Pressing it closes the gap.
- e.g. A toy car runs only when its cells are in and its switch is on.
### Both ends of the cell must be joined · *defined*
**Definition:** Electricity flows out of one end of a cell and comes back in at the other end. So each end of the cell must be joined to a different metal part of the bulb.

A cell has two ends, + and −. A bulb has two metal places: the tip at the bottom and the metal side. In the Shape Test, the bulb stayed dark when both wires were on the flat end of the cell. The wires still made a ring, but it started and ended at the same end. Electricity needs a path from one end of the cell to the other.
- e.g. Every AA cell has a + end with a bump and a flat − end.
- e.g. A torch bulb touches the circuit at its tip and at its metal side.
- e.g. In a remote, one spring touches the flat end of each cell, and a flat plate touches the bump.
### The shape of the wires does not matter · *defined*
**Definition:** A bulb lights when there is an unbroken metal path from one end of the cell, through the bulb, to the other end. Bending, coiling or knotting a covered wire does not break that path.

In the Shape Test, the bulb lit with straight wires. It also lit with a coiled wire, crossed wires and a knotted wire. The brightness looked the same each time. Inside the plastic, the metal was still one unbroken piece. A loose bulb, or a wire on the wrong end, makes the bulb dark, however neat the wires look.
- e.g. The wires inside a wall go round corners, and the light still works.
- e.g. A phone charger's wire can be coiled up, and the phone still charges.
- e.g. Electricians bend wires to fit inside a switchboard.
### Why the bulb glows · *defined*
**Definition:** When electricity flows through the very thin filament, the filament gets so hot that it glows and gives out light.

Electricity flows easily through the thick wires. The filament is far thinner than a hair, and it is hard for electricity to get through it. That makes it very hot, and hot metal glows, like an iron rod in a blacksmith's fire. The glass keeps air away from the filament, so it does not burn up. That is why a bulb that has been on for a while feels warm.
- e.g. A torch bulb feels warm after it has been on for a minute.
- e.g. The coil in an electric heater glows orange when it is hot.
- e.g. A blacksmith's iron rod glows red in the fire.
### What moves in the wire · *defined*
**Definition:** A metal wire is full of tiny particles called electrons. A cell pushes them round the circuit, all at once, all the way round.

You cannot see electricity in a wire. Scientists have found that metal is full of tiny particles called electrons, far too small to see. When the circuit is complete, the cell pushes these electrons along the whole ring at once. That is why the bulb lights the moment the last clip goes on. You will learn more about them in later years.
- e.g. The bulb lights the moment you close the last gap, not a little later.
- e.g. Copper wires carry electricity in homes, because their electrons move easily.
- e.g. Lightning is a huge number of electrons moving through the air at once.

## Mostly agreed
- A bulb lights only when there is a complete circuit from one end of the cell, through the bulb, to the other end. *How we know:* Unclip any wire, unscrew the bulb, or put both wires on one end of the cell, and the bulb goes dark at once. Children and scientists everywhere get the same result.
- A bulb glows because its thin filament gets very hot. *How we know:* A bulb that has been on for a while feels warm. Through a magnifying glass you can see that only the thin filament glows.
- A small AA cell is safe to play with in circuits, but the sockets in the wall are not. *How we know:* An AA cell gives 1.5 volts, which you cannot feel through dry skin. Indian homes get mains electricity at about 230 volts, which can kill. That is why we never use wall sockets for experiments.

## How we found out
**Joseph Swan, a chemist in Newcastle in England, and Lewis Latimer, a Black American engineer who taught himself technical drawing, 1879 (Swan's carbon bulb) and 1882 (Latimer's better carbon threads).** In the 1800s, inventors found that a thin thread of carbon glows when electricity flows through it. But their bulbs burned out within minutes. Joseph Swan, a chemist in Newcastle in England, worked on this for years. He worked out that the trouble was the air left inside the glass. Hot carbon burns when air touches it. In 1865, Hermann Sprengel had invented a much better pump for taking air out of glass. Using a pump like this, Swan took almost all the air out of his bulbs. Now the carbon thread glowed without burning up. Early in 1879, he showed his bulb to a hall full of people in Newcastle. Thomas Edison's team in America made long-lasting bulbs at about the same time. Then the carbon threads were hard to make, and many broke. Lewis Latimer, a Black American engineer who had taught himself technical drawing, found a better way to make them. He was given a patent for it in 1882. The wrong idea was that a glowing thread would always burn up quickly. Taking the air away showed that it need not.  
*What people believed before:* Many early bulbs burned out in minutes, and a long-lasting electric bulb seemed out of reach. Swan showed that air inside the glass was burning the hot carbon. With the air taken out, the thread glowed for hours.

## Challenging perspectives
- **One wire from the cell to the bulb should be enough, because the electricity just goes into the bulb.** (A common idea among children and many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: It looks as if the cell fills the bulb with electricity, like water filling a cup.  
  Against: With one wire, the bulb stays dark. It lights only when a second wire goes back to the other end of the cell. The electricity has to go round and come back.
- **A knot or a coil in the wire will block the electricity.** (A common guess before the Shape Test; *rejected*)  
  For: A knot in a water pipe blocks the water. Electricity seems to flow like water.  
  Against: In the Shape Test, the bulb lit just as brightly with a knotted wire and a coiled wire. The metal inside the plastic was still one unbroken piece.
- **The + end of the cell must go to the tip of the bulb, or the bulb will not light.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Cells in a remote only work one way round, so it seems the bulb should care too.  
  Against: In the Which Way Round test, the bulb lit just the same with the cell turned round. A plain bulb lights either way. A remote has parts inside that need the electricity to flow one way.
- **Thomas Edison invented the light bulb on his own.** (Many school books; *rejected*)  
  For: Edison's team made bulbs that lasted a long time, and his name is the one most people know.  
  Against: Joseph Swan in England showed a working carbon bulb early in 1879. Many people had tried before them. Lewis Latimer, a Black American engineer, found a better way to make the glowing carbon threads in 1882.
- **Only animals with a brain can count or decide.** (Many people; *debated*)  
  For: Counting and deciding seem to need a brain like ours.  
  Against: A Venus flytrap plant counts. She shuts her trap only after an insect touches her trigger hairs twice. Each touch sends an electric signal through her leaf. Some scientists call this a reflex, not counting.

## Roots
We have built a circuit, coiled and knotted its wires, and looked inside a bulb. Now we ask what the Shape Test tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your bulb lit with the wires straight, coiled, crossed or knotted. It stayed dark when both wires touched the same end of the cell. So whether a bulb lights depends on what is joined to what. The shape of the wires does not change it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We judge things by how they look: a neat ring looks as if it should work, and a tangle looks as if it should not. The Shape Test shows that the joins decide, and the shape does not.  
*Evidence:* In the Shape Test, four very different shapes all lit the bulb equally. A tidy-looking circuit with both wires on the flat end stayed dark.

- *who am i:* Your body has paths for electric signals too. Your nerves carry tiny signals from your fingertips to your brain, round every bend in your arm.
- *what is life:* Thinking is not only for brains. A Venus flytrap counts touches with electric signals in her leaf, and decides when to shut. Some scientists call this a reflex.
- *what is this world:* Everything is conscious, down to the tiny electrons in a wire. Most physicists today disagree. They say an electron only follows fixed laws.

A straight wire and a knotted wire look different. To the circuit they are the same path, because the metal is unbroken. A Chennai metro map works the same way. It does not show the real bends of the streets, only which stations join, and that is enough to travel. What matters is how things are joined.

- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18:** Nagarjuna, a teacher in South India about 1,800 years ago, taught that nothing has its nature all by itself. Each thing is what it is because of what it depends on. (A bulb on its own does nothing. It glows only because of what it is joined to: the wires, and the two ends of the cell.)
- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the net of Indra:** Buddhist teachers describe a great net with a jewel at every knot. Each jewel reflects every other jewel. (In a net, what matters is which knots are joined, not how the net is folded. A circuit is a small net of joins too.)
- Science (settled): A bulb lights only when there is a complete metal path from one end of the cell, through the bulb, to the other end.
- Science (settled): Whether a circuit is complete depends only on its joins, not on the shape of its wires. Mathematicians call properties like this topological.
- Other view, Most physicists: An electron in a wire is a tiny particle that follows fixed laws of nature. They say it has no inner life at all, and that awareness needs a brain, or at least a living body.
- Practice, Feel the join (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Touch the tip of your first finger to the tip of your thumb. Your hand makes a ring. Feel the place where they meet. Now move them apart, just a hair's width. Can you still feel each other? Touch them again. Breathe slowly three times. Where does your finger end and your thumb begin?
- Ask: Your knotted wire and your straight wire did the same job. In what way were they the same?
- Ask: A bulb glows only because of what it is joined to. What are you joined to?
- Ask: If everything is conscious, what might the electrons in a wire be aware of?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your knotted wire still lit the bulb. What would you have to do to the wire to make the bulb go dark? (hint: Think about what is inside the plastic, and what has to stay unbroken.)
- *deep:* The bulb goes dark the moment you unclip one wire. How does the bulb know the gap is there, far away at the cell? (hint: Think about the electrons in the wire. They are pushed round the whole ring at once.)
- *big:* A metro map shows which stations join, but not the real shape of the streets. Is a drawing of a circuit the same kind of map? (hint: Draw your circuit two ways, neat and messy. Would both drawings help someone build it?)
- *big:* Your nerves carry tiny electric signals from your fingers to your brain. Is your body full of circuits too? (hint: Think about what happens when you touch something hot, and how fast you pull your hand away.)

## Story: The Light in the Golu Temple
*Thenmozhi, Arun, Selvi chithi and Paati are made up. Chithi means mother's younger sister in Tamil, and Paati means grandmother. Golu is a display of dolls on steps, set up for Navaratri in many homes in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh.*

Thenmozhi was seven. She lived in a flat in Madurai with her Amma, her Appa and her little cousin Arun, who was five.

It was nine days before Navaratri. In Thenmozhi's home, that meant one thing. It was time to set up the golu.

The golu was a set of wooden steps covered in a white cloth. On the steps sat rows of clay dolls. There were kings and queens, a wedding party, cows, a temple and a tiny vegetable seller with a basket.

This year, Thenmozhi had one wish. 'I want a light inside the little temple,' she said. 'So it glows at night.'

'Ask Selvi chithi,' said Amma. 'She will know.'

Selvi chithi was Amma's younger sister. She was an electrician. She fixed wiring in shops and houses all over the city. She came that evening with her tool bag.

'First, a rule,' said Selvi chithi. 'The sockets in the wall are never for experiments. Their electricity is strong enough to kill. We will use this.' She held up a small AA cell, the kind that goes in the TV remote.

'That is too small to hurt you,' she said. 'But it can light a bulb.'

From her bag she took a tiny torch bulb in a holder, and two wires. One was red and one was black. Each wire had a metal clip at both ends, with teeth like a crocodile.

'Make it shine,' said Selvi chithi. Then she sat back and drank her coffee.

Thenmozhi clipped the red wire to the bump on top of the cell. She clipped the other end to the bulb holder. Nothing happened.

'It needs more electricity,' said Arun.

'No,' said Thenmozhi slowly. 'I think it needs the other wire.' She clipped the black wire to the other side of the bulb holder. Then she held its far end in the air. Where should it go?

She looked at the cell. It had a bump at the top and a flat end at the bottom. She clipped the black wire to the flat end.

The bulb shone. It was a small, warm, yellow light.

Arun clapped. Selvi chithi smiled. 'Now follow the path with your finger,' she said.

Thenmozhi put her finger on the bump of the cell. She slid it along the red wire to the bulb. Then out of the bulb, along the black wire, and into the flat end of the cell. Her finger was back where it started.

'It goes round,' she said. 'Out of one end and back into the other.'

'That ring has a name,' said Selvi chithi. 'It is called a circuit.'

Now came the hard part. The temple was on the top step, and the cell had to hide behind the dolls. The wires had to go round the dancing girls and under the wedding party. Arun helped. He wound the red wire round a pencil to make it fit. He pushed the black wire in a loop behind the cows.

'You will spoil it,' said Thenmozhi. 'All those twists will block the electricity.'

Arun let go of the wire, and they both looked at the temple. The bulb was still shining, just as brightly.

'The wire is still one piece inside,' said Selvi chithi. 'You can bend it and coil it. The metal inside does not break.'

Then Arun had another idea. He wanted the cell to lie flat in a little box, so he unclipped the red wire and clipped it next to the black one, on the flat end. The temple went dark.

'You broke it!' said Thenmozhi.

'I did not cut anything,' said Arun. 'Both wires are still on the cell.'

Thenmozhi looked at the cell for a long time. Both wires were there. But both were on the same end.

'The electricity has to come out of one end and go back into the other,' she said. 'If both wires are on the same end, it has nowhere to go.' She moved the red wire back to the bump. The temple glowed again.

Selvi chithi nodded. 'An electrician looks at what joins to what,' she said. 'Not at how pretty the wires look.'

On the first night of Navaratri, the neighbours came to see the golu. Paati from upstairs bent down and peered at the little temple. 'There is a light inside!' she said. 'How did you do that?'

Thenmozhi grinned. 'It is a circuit,' she said. 'Out of one end of the cell, through the bulb, and back into the other end. The wires can go any way you like.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: a bulb lights when there is an unbroken metal path from one end of the cell, through the bulb, to the other end. Coiling or looping a covered wire does not break the metal inside, so the bulb stays lit. Putting both wires on the same end of the cell leaves no path from one end to the other, so the bulb goes dark. Thenmozhi found that what joins to what matters, and the shape of the wires does not.

- Ask: Why did the bulb stay dark with only one wire?
- Ask: Arun coiled the wire round a pencil. Why did the bulb still shine?
- Ask: Both wires were still on the cell. Why did the temple go dark?

### g1 · Find the Fault: a circuit detective game (15 min)
- **rules:** Build a working circuit with a cell, a rubber band, two wires and a bulb.; The finder turns away. The fixer takes a fault card, reads the back, and does what it says.; The finder turns back and looks. They must say what is wrong before they touch anything.; Then the finder fixes it. If the bulb lights, the finder gets 2 points.; Swap jobs each round. After 8 rounds, the player with the most points wins.
- **Printable cards: 8 fault cards:** LOOSE BULB (back: Unscrew the bulb one turn, so it still looks in place.); CLIP ON THE PLASTIC (back: Clip one wire onto its own plastic cover, not the metal.); SAME END (back: Move one wire so both wires are on the flat end of the cell.); WIRE JUST OFF (back: Take one wire end out from under the band, but leave it lying close to the cell.); TIRED CELL (back: Swap in a used-up cell from the drawer.); PAPER UNDER THE BAND (back: Slip a small piece of paper between one wire end and the cell.); TURN THE CELL (back: Turn the cell round. ## Experiments
### e1 · The Shape Test: does it matter how the wires lie? (25 min)
- **question:** Does the bulb still light if the wires are coiled, crossed or knotted? What does the bulb really need to light?
- **predict:** Before each test, guess: will the bulb light or stay dark? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same cell, the same two wires and the same bulb for every test. We change only one thing each time. After each test we put the circuit back to straight wires and check that the bulb still lights, so we know nothing else has gone wrong.
- **safety:** Use only small AA cells. Never use a wall socket, a plug or a phone charger. If a wire or the cell gets warm, unclip it at once. It means the two ends of the cell are joined without the bulb.
- **record:** Fill in the table with the six tests: 1 straight wires, 2 coiled wire, 3 crossed wires, 4 knotted wire, 5 both wires on the flat end, 6 bulb unscrewed. Draw a sun for light and a dark circle for dark. Then count the suns.
- **results table:** Test | My guess: light or dark? | What happened: light or dark?
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Child mode:* Put one wire on the bump of the cell. Put the other on the flat end. Join both to the bulb.
  - Note: Press the bare metal ends firmly under the band, so metal touches metal. If the bulb only glows faintly, that is fine in a dim room. A bulb marked 2.5 V glows dimmer on one cell than one marked 1.5 V. If it does not light at all, try a fresh cell and check the bulb is screwed in.

### e2 · Which Way Round? Turning the cell and the bulb (15 min)
- **question:** Does the bulb still light if we turn the cell round, so the electricity goes the other way?
- **predict:** Guess first. If we turn the cell round, will the bulb light, stay dark or get dimmer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same cell, wires and bulb each time. We only turn the cell round, or swap the wires at the bulb. We look at the bulb in the same spot in the room, so the brightness is easy to compare.
- **safety:** A grown-up takes the cells out of the remote and puts them back. Keep any button cells far away from small children.
- **record:** Draw the circuit three times: as it was, with the cell turned round, and with the wires swapped at the bulb. Draw rays round each bulb that lit.
- **results table:** Test | My guess | What happened
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Build the circuit with a rubber band on the cell. 2. Check that the bulb lights. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* The bump end is called plus, and the flat end is minus. If I turn the cell round, what will happen? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. If you turn the cell round, will the bulb light?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: It will go dark, because the cell is the wrong way.; It will light, because both ends are still joined.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the wire ends out from under the band. 2. Turn the cell round. 3. Tuck each wire back under the band at the end now beside it. 4. Look at the bulb. 5. Then swap the two clips at the bulb holder and look again.  
  *Parent says:* Now the electricity has to go round the other way. Look at the bulb. Is it lit? Is it as bright?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the cell round. Is the bulb lit? Now swap the clips at the bulb.
  - Note: The bulb lights just the same both ways. A plain bulb does not care which way the electricity flows through it.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the remote's cell cover. 2. Show the + and − marks inside. 3. Turn one cell round and press a button. 4. Put it back the right way.  
  *Parent says:* Now look at the remote. Inside it says which way the cells go. Let's turn one round and press a button.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the + and − marks in the remote. Turn one cell round. Does it work?
  - Note: With one cell turned round, the remote usually stops working. Its tiny parts inside need the electricity to flow one way. A plain bulb does not. Put the cell back the right way afterwards.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what your child found, and why. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* What happened when we turned the cell round in our circuit? What happened in the remote? Why are they different?  
  *Child mode:* What did the bulb do? What did the remote do?
  - Ask: Why did the bulb light both ways? (good: Both ends of the cell were still joined to the bulb.; The bulb does not mind which way the electricity goes.)
  - Check: Murali turns the cell round in his bulb circuit. What happens? → **The bulb lights just the same**

## Applications
- Every torch, doorbell, toy car and TV remote has a circuit inside, with metal paths in place of loose wires.
- Electricians follow the path of a circuit to find faults in homes and shops, just like the Find the Fault game.
- Phones and computers have thousands of tiny circuits printed as thin metal lines on a board.
- Hertha Ayrton, a scientist in London, studied the bright electric arc lamps that once lit streets. In 1899 she became the first woman member of the Institution of Electrical Engineers in Britain.

## Spotted around us
- In Madurai and Chennai, homes light up their golu dolls with strings of tiny bulbs for Navaratri. Each string is one long circuit.
- In Kolkata during Durga Puja, pandals glow with thousands of small lights, joined by wires that run behind the cloth.
- Many fruit sellers' handcarts at night markets, as in Hyderabad, have a small light run from a battery.
- A cycle rickshaw in Guwahati or Patna may have a small light at the front, joined by wires to a battery.
- The lighthouse at Mamallapuram, near Chennai, shines its light far out over the sea at night.

## Open questions
- **What is an electron, really?** Scientists can measure how electrons move and push on each other very exactly. But what an electron is in itself, and whether it has any parts, is still not known.
- **How does a Venus flytrap keep count of the touches?** Scientists can measure the electric signals in her leaf, and know she shuts after two touches. Exactly how her cells hold the count for a short time is still being worked out.
- **Can we make lights that waste no energy as heat?** An old bulb turns most of its energy into heat, which is why it gets warm. LED lights waste much less, but every light still makes some heat. Scientists are still trying to waste less.

- In the Shape Test, Kavitha's bulb lit in 4 tests and stayed dark in 2. How many tests did she do? **6 tests.**. 4 lit and 2 stayed dark. 4 add 2 is 6. So she did 6 tests in all. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Every circuit needs 2 wires. Count in twos: how many wires do 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 circuits need? What pattern do you see? **2, 4, 6, 8, 10. Each time it goes up by 2.**. One circuit needs 2 wires. Each new circuit adds 2 more. So the numbers go 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. They are all even numbers. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L2`)
- In Count the Twos, Farida found a cell with 2 ends, a bulb with 2 metal places, a charger with 2 pins and a mixie plug with 3 pins. Make a tally chart. Which number came up most? **2 came up 3 times. 3 came up once.**. Make one tally mark for each thing. Under 2 there are 3 marks: the cell, the bulb and the charger. Under 3 there is 1 mark: the mixie plug. Its third pin is the earth pin, for safety. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- In the Find the Fault game, Ammu found the fault in 5 rounds out of 8. Her brother found it in 3 rounds out of 8. Each find gets 2 points. Who won, and by how many points? **Ammu won, by 4 points.**. Ammu: 5 finds, 2 points each, is 10 points. Her brother: 3 finds is 6 points. 10 take away 6 is 4. So Ammu won by 4 points. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)

## Warm-up
- How many wires does a bulb circuit with one cell need? → Two. One out to the bulb and one back to the cell.
- What is a circuit? → A complete ring that electricity flows round.
- Which part of a bulb glows? → The thin wire inside, called the filament.
- Does a knot in a covered wire stop the bulb lighting? → No. The metal inside is still one piece.
- Both wires on the flat end of the cell: light or dark? → Dark. There is no path to the other end.
- Is a wall socket safe for experiments? → Never. Only small cells are safe.
