# Animals with a backbone: five groups and how to tell them apart
`sci.animals.vertebrate-groups.L2`

**Summary.** A fish, a frog, a lizard, a crow and you all have a backbone, a chain of small bones down the middle of the back. Animals with a backbone are called vertebrates. Scientists sort them into five groups: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. You tell the groups apart by checking more than one clue. The clues are what covers the body, how they breathe, and how their babies start life. Your child feels their own backbone, then makes a straw backbone to find out how a chain of hard bones can bend.

**Intro.** *Ask a grown-up to bend forward. Run your fingers down the middle of their back. You feel a row of hard bumps. Is that one long bone, or many?* The bumps you feel are bones. Together they make the backbone. A fish has a backbone too, and so do a frog, a lizard, a crow and a cow. An ant, a prawn and an earthworm do not have one. In this unit you meet the five groups of animals with a backbone. You learn the clues that tell them apart. And you build a backbone from a straw, to find out how a row of hard bones can bend.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can find my own backbone and say that it is a chain of many small bones called vertebrae.
1. By the end I can say that animals with a backbone are vertebrates, and animals without one, like ants and prawns, are invertebrates.
2. By the end I can name the five groups of vertebrates, fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals, and give an animal from each.
3. By the end I can tell the groups apart with clues: what covers the body, how they breathe, how the babies start, and whether mothers make milk.
4. By the end I can sort a tricky animal, like a bat, a turtle or a pangolin, by checking more than one clue.
5. By the end I can make a guess with a reason, test a straw backbone fairly, count what I see and say what I found.
6. By the end I can say that humans are mammals, and that we are animals with a backbone like the rest.

## Terms
- **Backbone**: The backbone is the row of small bones down the middle of your back, from your neck to your bottom. Its other name is the spine. *e.g.* When you bend forward, you can feel the bumps of your backbone under the skin.
- **Vertebra (many are vertebrae)**: A vertebra is one of the small bones that make up the backbone. Many of them together are called vertebrae (say VER-tuh-bray). *e.g.* You have 33 vertebrae. A snake has a few hundred.
- **Vertebrate**: A vertebrate is an animal with a backbone. *e.g.* A crow, a frog, a goldfish and you are all vertebrates.
- **Invertebrate**: An invertebrate is an animal with no backbone. *e.g.* Ants, prawns, earthworms, snails and spiders are invertebrates.
- **Fish**: Fish are vertebrates that live in water, breathe with gills and swim with fins. Most have scales. *e.g.* A sardine (mathi), a rohu and a seahorse are fish.
- **Gills**: Gills are the parts behind a fish's head that take in oxygen from the water. Oxygen is a gas in air and water that every animal needs. *e.g.* Lift the flap behind a fish's eye, and the red gills are underneath.
- **Amphibian**: An amphibian is a vertebrate with smooth, damp skin and no scales. Most start life in water as tadpoles and later live on land too. *e.g.* Frogs and toads are amphibians. The word means 'two lives', in water and on land.
- **Tadpole**: A tadpole is a baby frog or toad. A tadpole lives in water, has a tail, and breathes with gills. *e.g.* In the monsoon, puddles and ponds can fill with black tadpoles.
- **Reptile**: A reptile is a vertebrate with dry skin covered in scales. Reptiles breathe with lungs, and most lay eggs on land. *e.g.* The house gecko, the cobra, the turtle and the crocodile are reptiles.
- **Scales**: Scales are small, hard plates that cover the skin of fish and reptiles. *e.g.* You can see the shiny scales on a fish at the market, and dry scales on a snake's skin.
- **Bird**: A bird is a vertebrate with feathers, a beak, two legs and two wings. Every bird hatches from an egg with a hard shell. *e.g.* Crows, sparrows, hens and peacocks are birds.
- **Mammal**: A mammal is a vertebrate with hair or fur, whose mother feeds the babies milk. *e.g.* Cows, dogs, bats, elephants and humans are mammals.

## Explanations
### A backbone is a chain of small bones · *defined*
**Definition:** A backbone is a row of small bones, called vertebrae, joined one after another along the back.

Your backbone is not one long bone. It is a chain of 33 small bones called vertebrae, with soft pads between them. Each joint tilts only a little, but many small tilts add up, so your back can bend a lot. Every vertebra has a hole in the middle, and the spinal cord runs through the holes. The spinal cord is a thick bundle of nerves that carries messages between your brain and your body. So the backbone holds you up, lets you bend, and keeps your spinal cord safe.
- e.g. You have 33 vertebrae.
- e.g. A snake has a few hundred, so a snake can curl up into rings.
- e.g. A frog has only about nine, so a frog's back is short and stiff.
- e.g. A giraffe and you both have 7 bones in the neck. The giraffe's neck bones are just much longer.
### Vertebrates and invertebrates · *defined*
**Definition:** Vertebrates are animals with a backbone. Invertebrates are animals without one.

A crow, a goat, a frog and a fish all have a backbone, so they are vertebrates. An ant, a prawn, a spider and an earthworm have no backbone, so they are invertebrates. A prawn has a hard covering, but it is on the outside of the body, not a row of bones inside. Most kinds of animals in the world are invertebrates. Only about 1 in every 20 known kinds of animal has a backbone.
- e.g. Vertebrates: a crow, a cat, a frog, a cobra, a sardine, you.
- e.g. Invertebrates: an ant, a prawn, a crab, a snail, an earthworm, a butterfly.
### Fish · *defined*
**Definition:** Fish live in water, breathe with gills and swim with fins. Most fish are covered in scales.

A fish takes water in through the mouth and pushes it out over the gills. The gills take oxygen from the water. Fins help a fish steer and stay upright, and the tail pushes the fish along. Most fish lay many tiny eggs in the water. About half of all the kinds of vertebrates in the world are fish.
- e.g. A sardine (mathi) from the sea off Kerala.
- e.g. A rohu from a pond in Bengal.
- e.g. A seahorse, who swims upright.
- e.g. A shark, who is a fish too.
### Amphibians · *defined*
**Definition:** Amphibians have smooth, damp skin with no scales. Most begin life in water as tadpoles, then live on land and in water.

A frog lays soft eggs in jelly, in water. A tadpole hatches, with a tail and gills, like a small fish. Slowly the tadpole grows legs, loses the tail and becomes a frog. A grown frog breathes with lungs, and also through the damp skin. That is why frogs stay in damp places. Amphibians include frogs, toads, and caecilians, who have no legs and live in wet soil in the Western Ghats and the Northeast.
- e.g. The common Indian toad, seen on wet roads in the monsoon.
- e.g. A tree frog with sticky toe pads.
- e.g. The purple frog of the Western Ghats, who lives underground most of the year. The Indian scientist S. D. Biju described her in 2003.
- e.g. A caecilian, who looks like a big earthworm but has a backbone.
### Reptiles · *defined*
**Definition:** Reptiles have dry skin covered in scales. They breathe with lungs, and most lay eggs on land.

A reptile's scales are dry, not slimy. A snake's skin feels dry and smooth, like a leather bag. Reptiles breathe air with lungs, even turtles and crocodiles who spend a lot of time in water. Most reptiles lay eggs with a tough shell, in sand, soil or a nest of leaves. A few snakes, like the Russell's viper, give birth to babies instead.
- e.g. The house gecko near the tube light.
- e.g. The spectacled cobra.
- e.g. The olive ridley sea turtles who lay eggs on Odisha's beaches.
- e.g. The mugger crocodile in rivers and lakes.
### Birds · *defined*
**Definition:** Birds have feathers, a beak, two legs and two wings. Every bird hatches from an egg with a hard shell.

Feathers are the clue that only birds have. A bird breathes with lungs, and has no teeth, only a beak. Every bird lays eggs with a hard shell, and the parents usually keep the eggs warm. Most birds can fly, but not all. A hen can only flutter up a little way, and an ostrich cannot fly at all.
- e.g. A crow on the window ledge.
- e.g. A sparrow in a hole in a wall.
- e.g. A hen with her eggs.
- e.g. A peacock, India's national bird.
### Mammals · *defined*
**Definition:** Mammals have hair or fur, and the mothers feed their babies milk.

Mammals breathe with lungs. Most mammals give birth to babies, not eggs. Every mammal mother makes milk for her young. Mammals come in many shapes: a bat flies, a river dolphin swims, and an elephant walks. Humans are mammals too. You have hair, you breathe with lungs, and you drank milk as a baby. So you are an animal with a backbone, one mammal among many.
- e.g. A cow and her calf.
- e.g. An Indian flying fox, a big bat, hanging in a tree.
- e.g. The Ganga river dolphin, India's national aquatic animal.
- e.g. You.
### Tricky animals: check more than one clue · *defined*
**Definition:** To sort an animal into a group, check several clues, not just one: the covering, the breathing, the babies and milk.

Some animals fool us if we look at only one clue. A bat flies, but has fur, and the mother feeds her baby milk, so a bat is a mammal. A river dolphin swims with fins, but breathes air and drinks milk as a baby, so she is a mammal. A turtle swims in the sea, but has dry scales and lungs, so she is a reptile. A pangolin is covered in scales, but has some hair and feeds her baby milk, so she is a mammal. A caecilian has no legs, like a snake, but has smooth damp skin, so they are an amphibian.
- e.g. Bat: flies, but a mammal.
- e.g. River dolphin: swims, but a mammal.
- e.g. Pangolin: scales, but a mammal.
- e.g. Turtle: swims in the sea, but a reptile.
- e.g. Flying lizard of the Western Ghats: glides on skin flaps, but a reptile.
### Are there really five groups? · *debated*
Most school books use five groups, and the five are easy to tell apart. But some scientists sort animals by their family tree: who is whose closest relative. Seen that way, the groups look different.
- e.g. Sharks and sardines are both called fish, but they are only distant cousins.
- e.g. A crocodile's closest living relatives are birds, not lizards.
- **Many teachers and scientists:** Five groups are useful. Each one has clues you can see, like feathers or damp skin. So anyone can sort a new animal quickly.
- **Scientists who draw family trees of animals:** Groups should hold relatives. Birds and crocodiles came from the same ancestors, so birds belong inside the reptile family. And 'fish' is really several groups.

## Mostly agreed
- Animals with a backbone are vertebrates, and every vertebrate has a backbone made of many small vertebrae. *How we know:* Scientists have looked at the skeletons of thousands of kinds of fish, frogs, reptiles, birds and mammals. Every one has a backbone made of separate vertebrae.
- Vertebrates can be sorted into fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals, by clues such as covering, breathing, eggs and milk. *How we know:* These clues hold for almost every animal. All birds have feathers, and all mammal mothers make milk. Scientists still argue about how the groups fit on the family tree.
- Humans are mammals, so humans are animals too. *How we know:* Humans have hair, breathe with lungs, have a backbone of vertebrae, and are fed milk as babies, just like other mammals.
- All vertebrates come from the same ancestors, small animals like fish who lived in the sea more than 500 million years ago. *How we know:* The oldest fossils of animals with a backbone are more than 500 million years old, and all are from the sea. The genes of all vertebrates show they are related.

## How we found out
**Carl Linnaeus, a Swedish scientist, and Alexandre Brongniart, a French scientist, Linnaeus sorted frogs and reptiles together in 1758. Brongniart split them apart in 1800..** About 270 years ago, people did not sort frogs apart from lizards. In 1758, the Swedish scientist Carl Linnaeus wrote a famous book that named and sorted animals. He put frogs, toads, lizards, snakes, turtles and crocodiles together in one group. He called the group Amphibia. Many people agreed, and frogs and lizards stayed in one group for many years. Then, in 1800, a young French scientist called Alexandre Brongniart looked again. He saw that frogs and toads have bare, damp skin with no scales. He saw that their young are tadpoles, who live in water and slowly change their shape to become frogs. Lizards, snakes, turtles and crocodiles have dry, scaly skin, and their young hatch looking like small adults. Brongniart put frogs and toads in a group of their own. Later scientists agreed, and made the frogs' group a whole class, with the name amphibians. The lizards, snakes, turtles and crocodiles became the reptiles. Brongniart looked at more than one clue, and that is how the two groups were told apart.  
*What people believed before:* That frogs, toads, lizards, snakes, turtles and crocodiles all belong in one group. Looking closely at skin and at how the young grow up showed that frogs and toads are a different group.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A river dolphin is a fish, because she lives in water and has fins.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Dolphins live in water all their lives, swim with fins and a tail, and have a smooth body like many fish.  
  Against: A dolphin breathes air through a hole on the top of her head, and has no gills. Her baby drinks her milk. So she is a mammal.
- **Snakes are slimy.** (A very common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Snakes look shiny, and many people think shiny means wet.  
  Against: Snake skin is dry. The shine comes from smooth scales. Frogs and toads are the ones with damp skin.
- **Fish do not feel pain.** (An old idea, still held by some scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Fish brains are built differently from ours. Fish do not cry out or make faces when hurt.  
  Against: Fish feel pain and fear. In 2003 the British scientist Lynne Sneddon found nerves on the faces of trout that sense harm. Trout given a sting rocked from side to side, rubbed their lips on the gravel, and were slow to start eating again. Some scientists still argue that fish only react, without the feeling.
- **Birds should be counted as reptiles.** (Many scientists who study family trees; *debated*)  
  For: Birds come from dinosaurs, who were reptiles. A bird's closest living relative is the crocodile. Birds have scales on their legs and lay eggs with shells, like reptiles.  
  Against: Birds have feathers, and they make their own body heat, which most reptiles do not. Many scientists find it more useful to keep birds as a group of their own.

## Roots
We have learnt that a backbone is a chain of small bones, and that every fish, amphibian, reptile, bird and mammal has one. Now we ask what the Bendy Backbone test tells us about bodies, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** You felt many separate bumps down your back. Your straw backbone hugged the tumbler only when cut into many short pieces. Each piece tilts a little, and together the tilts make a big curve. A backbone bends because it is a chain of many hard bones.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'backbone', one word, as if it were one stiff bone. In fact it is a chain of many small vertebrae, and the bending happens at the joints between them.  
*Evidence:* In the Bendy Backbone test, the whole straw stayed straight and touched the tumbler at one point, while 8 pieces wrapped round it. When a grown-up bends forward, the bumps of the vertebrae spread apart and stand out. A cooked fish's backbone lifts out as a row of tiny bones that can bend.

- *who am i:* You are a mammal, an animal with a backbone of 33 small bones. When you bend, they all tilt together.
- *what is life:* Every fish, frog, lizard, bird and mammal is built round the same kind of chain of bones.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of backbones of every length: a frog's short one, a snake's long one and yours.

More than 500 million years ago, the first animals with a backbone swam in the sea. Every fish, frog, lizard, bird and mammal alive today comes from them, humans too. The same plan, a chain of small bones, has grown into a snake's coil, a frog's short, stiff back, a giraffe's tall neck and your own bending spine.

- **Samyutta Nikaya 5.10, the Vajira Sutta:** Vajira, a Buddhist nun, taught that the word 'chariot' is a name we give to many parts put together. In the same way, she said, 'a being' is a name for many parts. (In this unit, 'backbone' is one word for a chain of many vertebrae. The bending you felt happens at the joints between the parts.)
- **Tirukkural 322:** Thiruvalluvar, the Tamil poet, wrote that sharing your food and caring for the many kinds of living beings is the greatest of all teachings. (Fish, frogs, lizards, birds and mammals are the many kinds of beings with a backbone, and humans are one of them. Knowing how each group lives, wet skin or dry, water or air, is a first step in caring for them.)
- Science (settled): All animals with a backbone come from the same ancestors, small fish-like animals who lived in the sea more than 500 million years ago.
- Science (debated): Almost every mammal, from a mouse to a giraffe, has 7 neck vertebrae. Why mammals keep to 7, when birds and reptiles have many different numbers, is still argued about.
- Other view, The Nyaya thinkers of ancient India: The Nyaya school argued about this with Buddhist thinkers. They said a whole, like a cloth, is a real thing of its own, not only its threads. In this view, your backbone really is one thing, made of many bones.
- Practice, Bend Like a Chain (3 min): Kneel on your hands and knees, like a cat. Breathe in slowly and let your tummy sink down, with your head up. Breathe out slowly and round your back up high, with your head down. Do it three times, very slowly. Feel each part of your back move, one after the other. Now sit up tall and still. Your whole chain of bones is holding you up.
- Ask: Your backbone is many bones. Is it one thing, or many things?
- Ask: A fish, a frog and you each have a chain of bones. What does that tell you about how you are related?
- Ask: If a pangolin has scales like a lizard, who decides that she is a mammal: nature or people?

## Story: The Chain in the Fish
*A made-up story. A cooked fish's backbone really does lift out as a chain of small bones. Prawns have no backbone. Bats are mammals: they have fur and feed their babies milk.*

Meenakshi was seven. She lived in a flat in Mangaluru with Amma, her grandmother Ajji and her little brother Kiran, who was five.

One Sunday, Ajji fried bangude for lunch. Bangude is the Tulu and Kannada name for mackerel, a fish from the sea. There was also a bowl of prawns in red masala.

'Watch,' said Ajji. She pressed her fingers along the top of the fish. Then she held the tail and lifted. A long white bone came out in one piece, with thin side bones sticking out like the teeth of a comb.

Meenakshi put the bone on her plate. She looked closely. It was not one long stick. It was a row of tiny bones, joined end to end, like beads on a thread. She tried to count them, but there were so many that she lost count.

She held the bone at both ends and bent it very gently. The row curved like a bow.

'That chain is the fish's backbone,' said Ajji. 'Each little bone in it is called a vertebra. Many of them are called vertebrae.'

'Ver-te-bray,' said Kiran, and laughed at the word.

Then Kiran picked up a prawn. He pulled off the hard pink shell. Inside was soft white meat. 'Where is the prawn's backbone?' he asked.

Meenakshi looked. There were no bones at all. 'The prawn has no backbone,' she said. 'The hard part is the shell, on the outside.'

'Yes,' said Ajji. 'Animals with a backbone are called vertebrates. Animals without one, like prawns and ants, are invertebrates.'

'Do I have a backbone?' asked Kiran.

'Bend over and touch your toes,' said Meenakshi. Kiran bent over. Meenakshi ran her finger down the middle of his back. Under his vest she felt a row of hard bumps, from his neck down to his shorts.

'Kiran is a chain too!' she said.

Kiran stood up and wriggled. 'I am a bendy chain,' he said. 'Like the fish.'

After lunch, Meenakshi sat at the window with her notebook. She wrote two lists: 'Backbone' and 'No backbone'. A crow landed on the ledge. 'A crow has a backbone,' said Ajji. 'So does the gecko behind the tube light.' The ants on the ledge went in the other list.

'So all the backbone animals are one big family?' asked Meenakshi.

'They are all related,' said Ajji. 'Scientists sort them into five groups.' She held up one hand and spread her fingers. 'Fish. Amphibians, like frogs. Reptiles, like lizards and snakes. Birds. And mammals.'

'How do you know which group an animal is in?' asked Meenakshi.

'You look for clues,' said Ajji. She touched each finger. 'Fish have fins and gills. Amphibians have smooth, wet skin. Reptiles have dry scales. Birds have feathers. Mammals have hair, and their mothers feed them milk.'

That evening the sky turned orange. Big bats flew over the coconut trees, flapping slowly.

'Birds!' said Kiran.

Meenakshi thought about the clues. 'Bats fly, like birds,' she said. 'But do they have feathers?'

'No,' said Ajji. 'A bat's body is covered in fur. And a bat mother feeds her baby milk.'

'Fur and milk,' said Meenakshi. 'Then a bat is a mammal, not a bird.'

'Flying is only one clue,' said Ajji. 'Always check more than one.'

Kiran was quiet for a moment. Then he asked, 'What group am I in?'

Meenakshi counted on her fingers. 'You have a backbone. You have hair. You breathe air. And Amma fed you milk when you were a baby.' She smiled. 'You are a mammal.'

'So I am an animal?' asked Kiran.

'Yes,' said Ajji. 'Humans are mammals, and mammals are animals. So are you, and so am I.'

That night Meenakshi drew two pictures side by side. One was the fish's backbone, a long row of tiny bones. The other was Kiran bending over, with a row of bumps down his back. Under them she wrote: 'Fish, frog, lizard, crow, bat, Kiran and me. We each have a chain of bones.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: Meenakshi sees that a backbone is a chain of small bones, in a fish and in her brother. She learns the five groups, and finds out with the bats that one clue, like flying, can fool you. Kiran finds out that humans are mammals, and so animals too.

- Ask: What did Meenakshi see when she looked closely at the fish's backbone?
- Ask: Why did the prawn have no backbone to find?
- Ask: Kiran thought the bats were birds. Which two clues showed they are mammals?

## Experiments
### e1 · Bendy Backbone: how many pieces does a backbone need to bend? (25 min)
- **question:** Does a backbone bend better when it is one long piece, or many short pieces?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which will wrap round the tumbler best: 1 long piece, 2 pieces, 4 pieces or 8 short pieces? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same straw every time, so the total length stays the same. We use the same wool, the same tumbler and the same gentle pull. Only the number of pieces changes. That way, any difference comes from the number of pieces.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw the shape each chain made round the tumbler.
- **results table:** Number of pieces | How many pieces touch the tumbler | Biggest gap: how many fingers fit? | Draw the shape
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wrap tape round one end of the wool to make a stiff tip. 2. Push the tip through the straw. 3. Tie a big knot at the other end.  
  *Parent says:* This straw is our pretend backbone. The wool is like the spinal cord that runs through the middle of your own backbone.  
  *Child mode:* Push the wool through the straw. This is your pretend backbone.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the four chains you will test: 1, 2, 4 and 8 pieces. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will cut the straw into more and more pieces. Which one will wrap round the tumbler best? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which will wrap round the tumbler best? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Eight pieces, because it can bend in more places.; One piece, because it is strong.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine, as long as there is a reason. Do not tell your child what will happen.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the tumbler still. 2. Wrap the straw round it. 3. Pull both ends of the wool gently. 4. Count the pieces that touch the tumbler. 5. Try to fit fingers in the biggest gap.  
  *Parent says:* Wrap it round the tumbler and pull the wool gently. How many pieces touch the tumbler? How many fingers fit in the gap?  
  *Child mode:* Wrap it round the tumbler. Count the pieces that touch. Fit your fingers in the gap.
  - Note: A whole straw stays nearly straight. It touches the tumbler at one point, and three or four fingers fit in the gap at each end. A thin plastic straw may kink if you pull hard. A kink is a fold, not a joint, so pull gently, or use the rolled newspaper tube.
- **s4 (11 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pull the straw off the wool. 2. Cut it in half. 3. Thread both pieces and test again. 4. Cut each piece in half again for 4 pieces, then again for 8. Test each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now cut it in half and test again. Then cut again. What changes each time?  
  *Child mode:* Cut the straw in half. Test again. Then cut again and test.
  - Ask: What happens as you add more pieces? (good: It bends round more.; The gaps get smaller.; More pieces touch the tumbler.)
  - Note: Usually 2 pieces make a bent V and touch at 2 places. 4 pieces make a square-ish shape. 8 short pieces hug the tumbler almost all the way round, with gaps too small for a finger. Each joint only tilts a little, but more joints add up to a bigger curve. Keep the pull on the wool gentle, so it does not snap. Cutting and threading three times is fiddly, so the grown-up helps with the scissors and the wool.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the results table together. 2. Ask what your child thinks, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which chain bent best? Was your guess right? Why can a backbone bend?  
  *Child mode:* Which chain bent best? Why?
  - Ask: Why can your back bend, if every bone in it is hard? (good: Because it is many small bones, and each one tilts a little.; Like the 8-piece straw, it bends at lots of joints.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses the evidence: 'The 8-piece chain hugged the tumbler, but the whole straw stayed straight. A backbone has many bones, so it bends at many joints.' Now read the card on what a backbone is.
  - Check: A snake can curl up into rings. What does the Bendy Backbone test tell you about a snake's backbone? → **It has a great many small vertebrae**
### e2 · The Clue Test: which clue sorts animals into their groups? (25 min)
- **question:** Which clue sorts animals into their true groups best: where they live, how they move, or what covers their body?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which clue will put the animals with their own group: where they live, how they move, or what covers their body? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same 18 cards every time. Only the clue changes. We count the colours on each mat the same way every time. Then we add them up. Five colours in all would mean every group stayed together.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Circle the clue with the fewest colours in all.
- **results table:** Clue | Colours on each mat | Colours in all | Which animals were mixed up?
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the 18 names from the list in the grown-up note below, one on each card. 2. Your child adds a tiny picture to each. 3. Out of sight, put a coloured dot on the back of each card.  
  *Parent says:* Add a tiny picture to each card. I will put a secret colour on the back. It shows the animal's true group.  
  *Child mode:* Add a tiny picture to each card. Do not look at the backs!
  - Note: The 18 animals: sardine, shark, seahorse, frog, toad, tree frog, house gecko, cobra, crocodile, sea turtle, crow, duck, peacock, bat, river dolphin, cow, pangolin, you. Dots: blue for sardine, shark, seahorse. Green for frog, toad, tree frog. Brown for gecko, cobra, crocodile, turtle. Red for crow, duck, peacock. Yellow for bat, river dolphin, cow, pangolin, you.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* We will sort by three clues. Which clue will keep each group together best? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which clue will keep the groups together? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Where they live, because fish all live in water.; The covering, because fish have scales and birds have feathers.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay 3 mats: Water, Land, Air. 2. Your child puts each card where that animal spends most time. 3. Turn the cards over and count the colours on each mat. 4. Add them up.  
  *Parent says:* First clue: where do they live? Put each animal on Water, Land or Air. Now turn them over. How many colours on each mat?  
  *Child mode:* Sort by where they live. Turn the cards over. Count the colours.
  - Note: There is no wrong place if your child has a reason. Usually the Water mat gets 4 or 5 colours, because the dolphin, turtle, crocodile and duck join the fish. The total is usually about 10 or 11.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay 3 mats: Swims, Walks or crawls, Flies. 2. Sort again. 3. Turn over, count and add up.  
  *Parent says:* Second clue: how do they move? Does this clue mix the groups more, or less?  
  *Child mode:* Sort by how they move. Count the colours again.
  - Note: The bat goes with the birds, and the dolphin and turtle swim with the fish. The total is usually about 9 or 10.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay 5 mats: Fins and wet scales, Smooth damp skin, Dry scales or a shell, Feathers, Hair or fur. 2. Sort again. 3. Turn over, count and add up.  
  *Parent says:* Third clue: what covers the body? Look at each mat. Which animals are still mixed up?  
  *Child mode:* Sort by what covers the body. Count the colours again.
  - Ask: Which animals are still in the wrong place? (good: The pangolin is with the reptiles.; The dolphin is with the frogs.)
  - Note: Usually only two cards are mixed up. The pangolin goes on dry scales, and the dolphin on smooth skin. The total is usually about 7.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add one more clue: does the mother feed the babies milk? 2. Move every milk animal to the Hair or fur mat. 3. Count again. 4. Ask what your child thinks, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now add one more clue. Which animals drink milk as babies? Move them. How many colours now? What did you find?  
  *Child mode:* Move the milk animals to Hair or fur. Count again. What did you find?
  - Note: Now there should be 5 colours in all, one on each mat. A strong answer says: 'Where they live gave 11 colours in all. Covering gave 7. Covering and milk together got all 5 groups right.'
  - Check: In the Clue Test, a pangolin went on the Dry scales mat with the reptiles. Which clue moved her to the mammals? → **Her mother fed her milk**

## Challenges
### c1 · A Backbone That Bends and Stands Up (20 min)
- **problem:** Can you make a straw backbone that wraps round a tumbler, and also stands up straight holding a one-rupee coin on top for 10 seconds?
- **your turn:** Make your own backbone. Test it twice: does it bend round the tumbler, and does it stand up with the coin on top for 10 seconds? Change one thing and test again.
- **constraints:** Use the 8 straw pieces from the test, or pieces like them.; You may use wool, rubber bands, paper, tape and small pieces of eraser or cotton.; The backbone must still bend round the tumbler afterwards.
- **general:** Pull the wool tight and tie it, so the pieces press together. Real backbones are held tight by muscles and strong bands called ligaments.; Stand the chain on a flat base, like a bottle cap or a lump of dough.
- **creative:** Put a small soft pad, like a bit of eraser or folded paper, between the pieces. Your backbone has soft pads called discs between the vertebrae.; Stretch two rubber bands along the sides, like the muscles on each side of your backbone.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the loose 8-piece chain from the test up on the table and let go. Watch it flop.  
  *Parent says:* Our chain bent beautifully, but look. It cannot stand up. Your back can bend and stand. How?  
  *Child mode:* Your chain flops. Can you make it bend and stand up?
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child tries their own ideas with the materials.  
  *Parent says:* Try one idea. Does it stand with the coin? Does it still bend?  
  *Child mode:* Try an idea. Test it. Change one thing and test again.
  - Note: Pulling the wool tight is usually the first thing that works. Too tight, and the chain stops bending. Let your child find the balance. It is hard to get both. A chain that stands but bends only a little, or bends well but needs a hand to stand, is still a good result. Your real backbone solves this with discs, ligaments and muscles all working together.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the best design twice: standing with the coin for 10 seconds, then wrapping round the tumbler.  
  *Parent says:* Let's test it both ways. What made it work?  
  *Child mode:* Test it both ways. What made it work?
  - Ask: What made your backbone stand and still bend? (good: Pulling the wool tight held the pieces together.; The rubber bands worked like muscles.)

## Worksheets
### A Backbone and Five Groups (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (match) Draw a line from each animal to their group.  
   **Answer:** Crow → Bird; Frog → Amphibian; Sardine → Fish; Cow → Mammal; House gecko → Reptile. *Why:* A crow has feathers, a frog has damp skin, a sardine has gills and fins, a cow feeds her calf milk, and a gecko has dry scales.
2. (sort) Sort the animals: backbone, or no backbone?  
   **Answer:** a dog → Backbone; an ant → No backbone; a crow → Backbone; a prawn → No backbone; a frog → Backbone; an earthworm → No backbone. *Why:* Dogs, crows and frogs are vertebrates. Ants, prawns and earthworms are invertebrates. A prawn's hard part is a shell on the outside, not bones inside.
3. (draw) Remember the Bendy Backbone test. Draw the whole straw and the 8-piece chain wrapped round the tumbler.  
   **Answer:** A straight straw touching the tumbler at one point, and a chain of 8 pieces curving close round the tumbler.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows big gaps beside the straight straw, and small gaps between the 8-piece chain and the tumbler.
4. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: why did the 8-piece chain wrap round the tumbler better than the whole straw?  
   **Answer:** Because it could bend at many joints. Each piece tilted a little, and the tilts added up.. *Why:* A strong answer says the chain bent at the joints between the pieces, and that more joints made a rounder curve.
5. (mcq) Which clue tells you for sure that an animal is a bird?  
   Options: Feathers / Four legs / Lives in a tree  
   **Answer:** Feathers. *Why:* Only birds have feathers. Monkeys and squirrels live in trees too, and birds have two legs, not four.
6. (truefalse) A snake's skin is slimy and wet.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Snake skin is dry. Snakes are reptiles, with dry scales. Frogs and toads are the ones with damp skin.
7. (label) Label the drawing of a child's back: neck, backbone, one vertebra, hips.  
   **Answer:** ['neck: at the top, under the head', 'backbone: the row of bones down the middle', 'one vertebra: one small bone in the row', 'hips: at the bottom of the backbone']. *Why:* The backbone runs from the neck to the hips. Each small bone in it is a vertebra.
8. (fill) Animals with a backbone are called ________.  
   **Answer:** vertebrates. *Why:* Vertebrates are animals with a backbone. Animals without one are invertebrates.
9. (perspective) Lakshmi sells fish at the market in Kochi. Dr Asha is a scientist who studies animals. They sort sea animals in different ways.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both ways are useful for different jobs. Lakshmi's way helps shoppers and cooks. Dr Asha's way shows which animals have the same kind of body and are related.. *Why:* There is more than one good way to sort. The scientist's five groups are best for finding relatives. The seller's way is best for cooking and selling.
10. (count) Count the legs: a crow, a cow and a cobra. How many legs in all?  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* A crow has 2 legs, a cow has 4 and a cobra has none. 2 and 4 and 0 make 6. Legs alone do not tell you the group.
### Telling the Groups Apart (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) A tadpole lives in a pond. What does a tadpole breathe with?  
   Options: Gills / Lungs / Feathers  
   **Answer:** Gills. *Why:* A tadpole breathes with gills, like a fish. When the tadpole becomes a frog, the frog breathes with lungs and through the damp skin.
2. (write) An olive ridley turtle swims in the sea all her life. Why is she not a fish? Give two clues. A grown-up can write down what you say.  
   **Answer:** She breathes air with lungs, not gills. She has dry scales and a shell. She lays her eggs on a beach.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two clues that show she is a reptile: lungs, dry scales, eggs laid on land.
3. (sort) Sort by skin: dry scales, or smooth damp skin?  
   **Answer:** house gecko → Dry scales; toad → Smooth damp skin; cobra → Dry scales; frog → Smooth damp skin; crocodile → Dry scales; tree frog → Smooth damp skin. *Why:* Geckos, cobras and crocodiles are reptiles, with dry scales. Toads, frogs and tree frogs are amphibians, with skin that has no scales. A toad's skin is bumpy, but still has no scales.
4. (order) Put a frog's life in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Eggs in jelly in the water', 'A tadpole with a tail and gills', 'A tadpole with back legs', 'A young frog with a short tail', 'A grown frog']. *Why:* Eggs, then a tadpole, then legs grow, then the tail shrinks, then a grown frog. Amphibians begin life in water.
5. (draw) Make up a new reptile and draw them. Show two clues that tell people your animal is a reptile.  
   **Answer:** A made-up animal with dry scales, and eggs with shells laid on land, or lungs.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows reptile clues, like dry scales and shelled eggs, and leaves out feathers, fur and gills.
6. (perspective) After the Clue Test, Ammu and her friend Divya, both aged 7, argue about the best clue.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the test results. Covering sorted most animals well, so Ammu is partly right. But milk only tells you about mammals, and does nothing for a frog or a lizard. Using both clues together got every animal right.. *Why:* Each clue does part of the job. Checking more than one clue works best.
7. (label) Label the fish: gills, fin, scales, tail.  
   **Answer:** ['gills: under the flap behind the eye', 'fin: on the back and belly', 'scales: covering the body', 'tail: at the back end']. *Why:* Gills take in oxygen from water. Fins steer, the tail pushes, and scales cover and protect the body.
8. (truefalse) All birds can fly.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every bird has feathers, but not every bird flies. An ostrich cannot fly, and a hen only flutters a little way.
9. (fill) Baby frogs and toads are called ________.  
   **Answer:** tadpoles. *Why:* Frogs and toads start life as tadpoles in water, with tails and gills.
10. (count) In the Clue Test, when you sorted by where animals live, the mats had 5, 4 and 2 colours. How many colours was that in all?  
   **Answer:** 11. *Why:* 5 and 4 make 9. 9 and 2 make 11. With only 5 groups, 11 colours means the groups were very mixed up.
### Tricky Animals (challenge) (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (write) A pangolin is covered in scales, like a lizard. Which clues show that she is a mammal? A grown-up can write down what you say.  
   **Answer:** She has some hair on her face and belly, and the mother feeds her baby milk.. *Why:* A strong answer names milk, and hair if known. Scales alone do not decide the group, because fish, reptiles and pangolins all have them.
2. (perspective) Two scientists argue about where birds belong.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, and sees that both are right about something. Feathers make birds easy to tell apart. But birds and crocodiles really are close relatives.. *Why:* Scientists really do argue about this. It depends whether groups are for telling animals apart, or for showing who is related to whom.
3. (mcq) A caecilian lives in wet soil in the Western Ghats. They have no legs, smooth damp skin and no scales. Which group are they in?  
   Options: Reptiles, like snakes / Amphibians, like frogs / Invertebrates, like earthworms  
   **Answer:** Amphibians, like frogs. *Why:* Smooth, damp skin with no scales is the amphibian clue. A caecilian has a backbone, so is not an invertebrate. No legs is not enough to make an animal a snake.
4. (draw) In the Bendy Backbone test you tried 1, 2, 4 and 8 pieces. Predict and draw what a chain of 16 pieces would look like round the tumbler.  
   **Answer:** An almost perfect circle round the tumbler, with almost no gaps.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that 16 pieces curve even more closely than 8, because there are even more joints, each tilting a little.
5. (write) A snake has a few hundred vertebrae. You have 33. How do so many vertebrae help a snake? A grown-up can write down what you say.  
   **Answer:** With so many joints, a snake can bend into tight curves, coil up and wriggle along the ground without legs.. *Why:* A strong answer links many vertebrae to many joints, and many joints to a bigger, rounder bend, as in the Bendy Backbone test.
6. (truefalse) Every animal that lives in water is a fish.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A river dolphin is a mammal and a sea turtle is a reptile, and both live in water. Tadpoles are amphibians. Where an animal lives is only one clue.
7. (sort) Sort the animals: lays eggs, or gives birth to babies?  
   **Answer:** hen → Lays eggs; cow → Gives birth to babies; cobra → Lays eggs; crocodile → Lays eggs; bat → Gives birth to babies; frog → Lays eggs. *Why:* Birds, most reptiles and amphibians lay eggs. Cows and bats are mammals, and most mammals give birth to babies.
8. (label) Label the bird: beak, feathers, wing, legs.  
   **Answer:** ['beak: the hard mouth at the front', 'feathers: covering the body', 'wing: on each side', 'legs: two, underneath']. *Why:* A beak, feathers, two wings and two legs are the bird clues. Feathers belong to birds alone.
9. (order) Put these animals in order, from the fewest vertebrae to the most.  
   **Answer:** ['a frog', 'you', 'a snake']. *Why:* A frog has about nine vertebrae, you have 33, and a snake has a few hundred.
10. (mcq) Which of these animals is an invertebrate?  
   Options: A crab / A shark / A toad  
   **Answer:** A crab. *Why:* A crab has a hard shell outside and no backbone. A shark is a fish and a toad is an amphibian, so both have a backbone.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is your backbone made of?  
   Options: One long, stiff bone / A chain of many small bones / Soft muscle with no bones  
   **Answer:** A chain of many small bones. *Why:* Your backbone is a chain of 33 small bones called vertebrae. You can feel them as bumps when you bend.
2. (mcq) In the Bendy Backbone test, which chain wrapped round the tumbler best?  
   Options: The whole straw / The straw in 2 pieces / The straw in 8 pieces  
   **Answer:** The straw in 8 pieces. *Why:* More pieces meant more joints. Each joint tilted a little, and together they curved round the tumbler.
3. (mcq) Which of these is a vertebrate?  
   Options: An ant / A crow / A prawn  
   **Answer:** A crow. *Why:* A crow has a backbone, so is a vertebrate. Ants and prawns have no backbone.
4. (mcq) Which group has smooth, damp skin and starts life as tadpoles?  
   Options: Reptiles / Amphibians / Birds  
   **Answer:** Amphibians. *Why:* Frogs and toads are amphibians. They have smooth, damp skin and begin life in water as tadpoles.
5. (mcq) How does a fish take in oxygen?  
   Options: With gills / With lungs / Through feathers  
   **Answer:** With gills. *Why:* A fish takes water in through the mouth and over the gills. The gills take oxygen from the water.
6. (mcq) What is true of every mammal?  
   Options: They can fly / The mothers feed their babies milk / They lay eggs with hard shells  
   **Answer:** The mothers feed their babies milk. *Why:* Every mammal mother makes milk for her young. Only a few mammals fly, and birds are the ones that lay hard-shelled eggs.
7. (mcq) A bat flies with wings. Which group is a bat in?  
   Options: Birds, because bats fly / Mammals, because bats have fur and drink milk / Reptiles, because bats like the dark  
   **Answer:** Mammals, because bats have fur and drink milk. *Why:* Flying is only one clue. A bat has fur, and the mother feeds her baby milk, so a bat is a mammal.
8. (mcq) A house gecko lives near the tube light. What covers a gecko's skin?  
   Options: Dry scales / Feathers / Fur  
   **Answer:** Dry scales. *Why:* A gecko is a reptile, with skin covered in tiny dry scales.
9. (mcq) Which group are humans in?  
   Options: Mammals / Reptiles / We are not in any animal group  
   **Answer:** Mammals. *Why:* Humans have hair, breathe air and drink milk as babies. So we are mammals, and mammals are animals.
10. (mcq) In the Clue Test, which clue mixed up the groups the most?  
   Options: What covers the body / Where the animals live / Whether the mother makes milk  
   **Answer:** Where the animals live. *Why:* Sorting by where they live put the dolphin, turtle, crocodile and duck with the fish. Body covering kept most groups together.
11. (truefalse) A river dolphin is a fish because she lives in water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A river dolphin breathes air and her baby drinks her milk. She is a mammal, even though she lives in water.
12. (mcq) How many bones are in a giraffe's neck?  
   Options: 3 / 7 / 20  
   **Answer:** 7. *Why:* A giraffe has 7 neck bones, the same number as you. Each of the giraffe's bones is just much longer.

## Applications
- Doctors and physiotherapists check how a person's backbone bends, to help people with back pain move well again.
- Engineers have built snake robots with many small joints in a row, like a backbone, to crawl into pipes and broken buildings.
- Wildlife rescuers must know an animal's group to care for them. A turtle needs air and a dry place to lay eggs, but a fish needs water over the gills.
- Scientists count frogs and toads to check that ponds and streams are clean, because amphibians take in water through their damp skin.
- Fish sellers and cooks cut along the backbone to clean a fish, because the backbone runs down the middle of the body.

## Open questions
- **Why did the first animals grow a backbone?** The first animals with a backbone lived in the sea more than 500 million years ago. Scientists think a backbone may have helped them swim, or protected the nerve cord, but the fossils do not tell us for sure.
- **How many kinds of frogs live in India?** Scientists keep finding new ones. In 2017 the Indian scientist Sonali Garg and her team named seven new night frogs from the Western Ghats. Some are tiny frogs. Many more may still be waiting to be found.
- **What is it like to be a fish?** Fish feel pain and fear, and they learn and remember. But fish sense the world through water, with gills, a sense of touch along the side of the body and eyes made for water. What that feels like from the inside, nobody knows.

## Connected maths
- In the Bendy Backbone test, 1 piece touched the tumbler at 1 place, and 2 pieces at 2 places. 4 pieces touched at 4 places. How many places for 8 pieces? **8 places.**. Each piece touched the tumbler once. So the number of places is the same as the number of pieces: 1, 2, 4, 8. Each time we cut every piece in half, the number doubles. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L2`)
- In the Clue Test, sorting by where animals live gave 11 colours in all. Sorting by body covering gave 7. Which clue mixed up the groups less, and by how many colours? **Body covering, by 4 colours.**. Count on from 7 to 11: 8, 9, 10, 11. That is 4. Fewer colours means fewer groups mixed up, so body covering was the better clue. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Draw two overlapping circles. One is 'Lives in water'. The other is 'Has a backbone'. Where do these go: a sardine, a prawn, a crow, a frog, a crab? **Sardine and frog in the middle, where the circles overlap. Prawn and crab in 'Lives in water' only. Crow in 'Has a backbone' only.**. A sardine lives in water and has a backbone. A frog lives in and near water and has a backbone. Prawns and crabs live in water but have no backbone. A crow has a backbone but does not live in water. (queues `math.logic.sets-and-venn.L2`)
- In the story, Meenakshi, her little brother Kiran, her mother Amma and her grandmother Ajji each have 7 bones in the neck. How many neck bones do these four people have in all? **28 neck bones.**. There are 4 people with 7 each. 7 and 7 make 14. 14 and 14 make 28. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
