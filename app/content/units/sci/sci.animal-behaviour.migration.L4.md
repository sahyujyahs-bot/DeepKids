# Migration: how a body is built for a journey across the world
`sci.animal-behaviour.migration.L4`

**Summary.** A bar-tailed godwit weighs less than a one-kilogram bag of sugar, and in 2022 a young one flew about 13,500 kilometres from Alaska to Tasmania in 11 days, without stopping once. How can a body that small carry enough fuel? Before leaving, she eats until nearly half her weight is fat, and her gut shrinks to make room for flight muscle. Her fuel comes from insects, worms and shellfish eaten in a faraway marsh or mudflat, which grew in the long days of a northern summer. This unit follows migration from the sunlight in the far north to the fat in a bird's body, the wetlands along the way, and the tags that tell us where she went.

**Intro.** *In winter, ducks, waders and swallows appear in our lakes, canals and wires. In summer, they are gone. Where were they, and what did they do to get here?* Many of the birds you see in India from October to March spent the summer thousands of kilometres away, in Siberia, Mongolia or the Himalaya. A small bird cannot carry a packed lunch. So how does her body pay for a trip like that?

## Terms
- **Migration**: A regular, long journey that an animal makes each year, going out and coming back. *e.g.* A bar-headed goose flies over the Himalaya to India each autumn, and back each spring.
- **Resident**: An animal that lives in one place all year and does not make a long journey. *e.g.* The house crow outside most Indian windows is a resident.
- **Winter visitor**: An animal that spends the cold months of the north in a warmer place, then returns north to raise young. *e.g.* The rosy starling arrives in India around August or September and leaves again in spring.
- **Summer visitor**: An animal that comes to a place for the warm months to raise young, then leaves. *e.g.* Some cuckoos come to south India to breed and leave again afterwards.
- **Passage migrant**: An animal that stops in a place for only a few days or weeks on the way to somewhere else. *e.g.* Amur falcons pass through Nagaland on their way from Siberia to Africa.
- **Flyway**: The whole route that a group of migrating birds follows, with all the places where they rest and feed. *e.g.* The Central Asian Flyway runs from the Arctic to the Indian Ocean and crosses India.
- **Stopover**: A place on a journey where a migrating animal rests and feeds before flying on. *e.g.* Chilika Lake in Odisha is a stopover and winter home for many water birds.
- **Fat reserve**: Fat that the body stores to use as fuel later. *e.g.* A migrating godwit can have nearly half her weight as fat before a long flight.
- **Fuel**: Something that stores energy a body can use. A body burns food or fat as fuel. *e.g.* Fat is the fuel a bird burns as she flies over the sea.
- **Day length**: The number of hours between sunrise and sunset in one day. *e.g.* In June, day length is about 13 hours in Chennai and 24 hours north of the Arctic Circle.
- **Wetland**: A place that is wet for much or all of the year, such as a marsh, a mudflat or the edge of a lake. *e.g.* Marshes, ponds, paddy fields and mudflats are wetlands.
- **Ringing**: Putting a light numbered ring on a bird's leg, so that anyone who finds the bird can report where. *e.g.* A ringed bird caught again in another country shows where she travelled.
- **Satellite tag**: A tiny device on a bird's back that sends her position to a satellite, so scientists can follow her journey. *e.g.* A tag the weight of a few paper clips has shown a godwit flying 11 days without stopping.
- **Geolocator**: A very light tag that records the time of sunrise and sunset, so scientists can work out where a bird has been after she is caught again. *e.g.* A geolocator weighs about one gram, so it can be carried by a small songbird.

## Explanations
### Why animals migrate: the trip costs less than staying · *defined*
**Definition:** An animal migrates when the gain from moving to a second place is bigger than the cost of the journey. The gain is usually food, space or safety for raising young.

Most people say birds fly away because winter is cold. But many birds stay through freezing winters, and some birds in warm places migrate too. What matters most is food. In the Arctic summer, insects appear in huge numbers for a few weeks. A bird that goes there can feed her chicks well, with few other birds to share the food. But the insects vanish in autumn, so she must leave. The journey is dangerous and uses up a great deal of energy. So a bird migrates only when the trip pays off: the extra food and space must be worth the risk. Different animals strike this bargain in different ways. A hilsa fish swims from the sea up a river to lay eggs. A Himalayan bird moves down the mountain in winter and back up in summer.
- e.g. Siberian waders breed on the Arctic tundra, where midges and other insects are plentiful in summer, and spend winter on Indian mudflats.
- e.g. Crows and sparrows stay through the winter in the same city, because they can find food all year.
- e.g. Hilsa fish live in the sea and swim up large rivers such as the Ganga to lay their eggs.
### Day length: why the far north is a summer nursery · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's axis is tilted. So for part of the year the far north leans towards the Sun and has very long days, and for part of the year it leans away and has very short days.

Near the equator, the day is about 12 hours long all year, in every season. The farther north you go, the more the day length changes. North of the Arctic Circle, the Sun does not set for weeks in midsummer, and does not rise for weeks in midwinter. In the Arctic summer, plants grow and insects hatch in the long light. Birds there can feed almost around the clock, and a chick can be fed through the whole bright night. That is why so many birds travel so far north to raise their young. When the light fades in autumn, the insects die and the ground freezes. Then the birds must leave. The tilt of the Earth sets the whole calendar of migration.
- e.g. At Chennai, the longest day in June is about 13 hours and the shortest in December is about 11 hours.
- e.g. North of the Arctic Circle, the Sun stays above the horizon for the whole day around 21 June.
- e.g. Waders breeding on the Arctic tundra feed their chicks during the bright nights.
### Fuel: fat, and a body rebuilt for the journey · *defined*
**Definition:** Migrating birds store energy as fat before a journey, because fat holds more energy for its weight than anything else in the body. Their bodies change shape to carry it and burn it.

A gram of fat holds more than twice the energy of a gram of sugar or starch. So a bird that must carry her fuel in the air stores it as fat. In the weeks before leaving, she eats much more than usual, and she can nearly double her weight. In some waders, up to about half of the weight at take-off is fat. Her body is also rebuilt. Scientists weighed the organs of godwits and knots before and after fattening. Before a long flight, the gut and liver shrink, and the flight muscles and heart grow. A gut full of food is heavy and useless in the air, so she makes it smaller and the muscles bigger. During the flight she burns the fat, and she arrives much thinner. After she lands, the gut grows back.
- e.g. Bar-tailed godwits can fly more than 11,000 kilometres without eating, drinking or landing, fuelled by their fat.
- e.g. Ruby-throated hummingbirds, which are far smaller, double their weight before crossing the Gulf of Mexico.
- e.g. Before the journey, a bird eats far more than usual. Scientists call this extra eating hyperphagia.
### Stopovers: a flyway is a chain · *defined*
**Definition:** A flyway is a chain of places. A bird needs every link in the chain: where she breeds, where she winters, and the stopovers where she refuels in between.

Many migrants do not fly the whole way in one go. They fly for a few days, land at a good place, and feed for a week or two to build their fat again. This is a stopover. A stopover can be small, such as a lake or a stretch of mud, but thousands or millions of birds may depend on it. If a stopover is drained or built over, the birds that used it arrive hungry at the next place, or fall into the sea on the way. The flyway is only as strong as its weakest link, and the birds can do nothing about the loss. A country that guards its own wetlands is looking after birds that belong to other countries too, and other countries are looking after theirs.
- e.g. Chilika Lake in Odisha is a wintering ground and a stopover for many thousands of water birds.
- e.g. The tidal mudflats of the Yellow Sea are a key stopover for waders on the way between Australia and the Arctic.
- e.g. Keoladeo National Park near Bharatpur in Rajasthan is a wetland where many winter visitors arrive after crossing the Himalaya.
### Other travellers: fish, turtles and mountain animals · *defined*
**Definition:** Migration is not only for birds. Fish, turtles, insects, whales and mammals all make regular journeys, and some are short. Moving up and down a mountain with the seasons is also migration.

Olive ridley turtles live in the sea, and every year thousands of mothers swim to the same beaches in Odisha, such as Gahirmatha and Rushikulya, to lay eggs. Many come together within a few days. This is called an arribada, from the Spanish word for arrival. The hatchlings go to sea alone. Hilsa fish swim up rivers to spawn. In the Himalaya, many birds spend summer on the high slopes and move down to the foothills when the snow comes. This is altitudinal migration, and it covers only a few hundred metres to a few thousand. Not every animal moves every year. Some move only in some years, when food runs short. These are called irruptions.
- e.g. Olive ridley turtles gather to lay eggs on a few beaches in Odisha.
- e.g. The hilsa swims from the sea up the river to spawn.
- e.g. Many Himalayan birds move to lower slopes in winter.
### How does a bird know when to leave? · *debated*
Nobody taught the bird the date. Scientists agree that day length matters, because the days get shorter in autumn. But a bird kept in a cage with constant light still gets restless at the usual time of year. This restlessness has been seen in many caged birds, and is called migratory restlessness (Zugunruhe, a German word). So the bird seems to have a yearly clock inside the body as well. Weather also matters: a bird often waits for a good wind. Scientists still argue about how the clock inside works and how the clock and the daylight work together.
- e.g. Caged warblers hop and flutter towards the south at night in autumn, even when they cannot see the sky.
- e.g. Birds often leave on the first night with a good tailwind.
- e.g. Some individuals of a species migrate and others do not, even in the same place.
- **Scientists who stress day length:** The shortening days of autumn switch on the urge to fatten and fly. Changing the light in a cage can change the birds' timing.
- **Scientists who stress an inside clock:** Birds in constant light and constant warmth still go through their migration season at about the right time of year. A yearly clock in the body seems to keep the time, and the light only adjusts it.
### Thin air and strong winds: the bar-headed goose · *defined*
**Definition:** A migrating bird is carried or held back by the air she flies in. Wind, temperature and air thickness change how much energy a journey costs.

Bar-headed geese breed on high lakes in Central Asia, including Ladakh, Tibet and Mongolia, and spend winter in India. To get there they cross the Himalaya, where the air is thin and cold and holds much less oxygen. Satellite tags show them flying over 6,000 metres high. They do not always fly as high as they can. Many follow the mountain slopes up and down, like a roller coaster, and save energy. Geese also fly at night and in the morning, when the air is calmer. A tailwind can carry a bird hundreds of kilometres for no extra energy, so birds often wait on the ground for a favourable wind before leaving.
- e.g. Bar-headed geese have been tracked flying above 6,000 metres over the Himalaya.
- e.g. Many birds wait at a stopover until the wind is behind them.
- e.g. Cranes and eagles use rising warm air to climb without flapping.
### How we know: rings, tags and radar · *defined*
**Definition:** We follow migrants by marking them (rings), by tags that record or send their position (geolocators and satellite tags), by radar that sees flocks in the sky, and by counts that many people share.

A ring is the cheapest and oldest way. A bird is caught, ringed and let go. If she is found or caught again, the number tells us where she has been. Rings tell us about two points on a journey but not what happened in between. A satellite tag sends her position for months. Tags are light, and a rule of thumb is that a tag should weigh no more than a few parts in a hundred of a bird's weight, so a heavy tag only suits a big bird. A geolocator is lighter, but the bird must be caught again to get the data. Weather radar can show the flocks of birds that fly at night. Millions of people also add their sightings to databases such as eBird, which show the arrival of birds week by week.
- e.g. Salim Ali and the Bombay Natural History Society ringed birds in India for many years.
- e.g. A satellite tag showed a young godwit flying non-stop for 11 days.
- e.g. eBird maps show when a winter visitor reaches your city.
### When the journey goes wrong: lost links and wrong timing · *idea*
Migrants face two kinds of trouble. One is a lost stopover. Scientists found that about two thirds of the tidal flats of the Yellow Sea were lost in 50 years, and the waders that used them have dropped in number. The other is a mismatch of timing. If spring comes earlier at a breeding place, the insects may peak before the chicks hatch. A study of pied flycatchers in the Netherlands found that where caterpillars peaked early, the flycatchers fell in number. Many scientists think climate change is changing the timing of both. Others say the picture is not clear yet, since some species are adjusting their dates.
- e.g. Siberian cranes once wintered at Keoladeo in Rajasthan. They have not been seen there for many years.
- e.g. Pied flycatchers in the Netherlands fell where the caterpillar peak came too early.
- e.g. The Yellow Sea mudflats lost about two thirds of their area over about 50 years.

## Mostly agreed
- Animals migrate when the food, space or safety they need is in different places at different times of the year. *How we know:* The timing of journeys matches the peaks of food, such as the summer insects of the Arctic, and tracked animals do the same journey in the same season, year after year.
- Before a long flight, migrating birds store fat and rebuild their bodies, with a smaller gut and larger flight muscles. *How we know:* Birds caught and weighed before and after fattening, and measured with scans, show the weight and organ changes (Piersma and Gill, 1998).
- A flyway is a chain: a loss of one important stopover can hurt a whole population. *How we know:* Counts of waders have fallen where tidal flats on the Yellow Sea were lost (Murray and others, 2014).
- A bird can migrate without being taught the date, because a yearly clock inside her body helps to set the time. *How we know:* Caged birds kept under a steady light still show migratory restlessness at about the right time of the year.
- Rings, tags and radar give accurate evidence of where migrants go. *How we know:* Hundreds of thousands of ring recoveries and tracks show the same flyways, and different methods agree.

## How we found out
**A stork with an African spear (1822) and Hans Christian Cornelius Mortensen, a Danish teacher who began ringing birds in 1899., 1822 and 1899.** For centuries, people in Europe did not believe that small birds could fly to another continent. A book from 1555 even showed fishermen pulling swallows out of a frozen lake in a net, because many people thought swallows slept under the water in winter. An English writer in 1703 suggested that birds flew to the Moon. Then, in 1822, a white stork landed in a village in Germany with a long wooden spear stuck through her neck. The spear was not German. Experts said it came from central Africa. The stork had flown north from Africa with it, still alive. In 1899, a Danish teacher named Hans Mortensen began putting numbered metal rings on starlings. When a ringed bird was found far away, the ring showed where she had been. Thousands of rings now showed that birds really did cross continents, and not one was found under a lake.  
*What people believed before:* Many people believed that swallows slept at the bottom of ponds in winter, and that small birds were too weak to cross seas. Spear-carrying storks and thousands of ring recoveries showed they fly to other continents.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Birds migrate because it gets too cold.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many birds leave the cold north in autumn.  
  Against: Crows, sparrows and some ducks stay through hard winters. Some tropical birds also migrate, from Africa to India for the monsoon. What changes is food, and cold is only one reason food runs short.
- **Swallows spend the winter asleep in the mud at the bottom of ponds.** (Ideas held in Europe for many centuries, and written about by Olaus Magnus in 1555. Aristotle also said some birds hibernate.; *rejected*)  
  For: Swallows disappear in autumn, and people had seen birds in ponds and marshes in cold weather.  
  Against: Ringed swallows were later found in southern Africa in winter. Nobody has ever found a swallow breathing under water.
- **Birds fly to the Moon for the winter.** (Charles Morton, an English writer, in 1703.; *rejected*)  
  For: Nobody knew where birds went, and he thought the Moon was the only place far enough away.  
  Against: There is no air to breathe on the Moon, and rings and tags later showed the real destinations on Earth.
- **Tagging birds is wrong because tags harm them.** (Some people who study and watch birds.; *debated*)  
  For: A tag adds weight, and a few studies found that tagged birds sometimes breed less well.  
  Against: Most studies of light tags find little harm, and tracking has found the stopovers that now get protection. Scientists keep tags very light and stop using any that harm.

## Roots
We have followed a migrating bird from the long light of the Arctic to the fat in her body and the wetlands on her way. Now we ask what that journey says about what an animal is, and where she begins and ends.

**Deep truth.** The long days of an Arctic summer grow a flood of insects, worms and shellfish. A bird eats them and stores the energy as fat. Weeks later she burns that fat over another country. So a bird carries the sunlight of one place to another, and her body is a bridge between them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a place's wildlife is what lives inside its borders, and that a bird belongs to one country. A migrant's fuel is collected in one place and spent in another, so her body belongs to several places at once.  
*Evidence:* In the spinning ball, the dot near the pole stayed in the light all through a northern summer. Under that light, insects hatch, mudflat worms and shellfish thrive, and waders feed. A godwit then flies 11 days on fat alone, and the gut that held the food has shrunk.

- *who am i:* Part of what you were made from last year came from a field, a sea or a river somewhere else. A godwit shows it more clearly: her fat comes from a marsh in one country and burns in the sky over another.
- *what is life:* A bird in flight is burning something that grew in the sunlight of another place a few weeks ago. Life moves energy from place to place, and migrants carry it farthest.
- *what is this world:* The tilt of the Earth sets the length of the days, the days set the insects, the insects set the fat, and the fat sets the journey. One tilt shapes the whole yearly movement of birds.

Everything a godwit burns began as sunlight. Plants and algae caught it, small animals ate them, and the bird ate the small animals. The same energy then lifts her across the sea. Nothing in this chain was made by the bird. She is a place where energy passes through on its way from the Sun to the sky. Zero becomes One becomes the many, and here the many are the marsh, the midge, the godwit and the wind, all made of one flow. When you look closely, the line between the bird and her places is hard to find.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2:** The Upanishad says food is the source of all beings. From food they are born, by food they live, and into food they go back. (A godwit is made of the insects she ate, and she is later eaten or returns to the soil. Her body is food passing through a shape for a while.)
- **Zhuangzi (Chuang Tzu), chapter 1, Free and Easy Wandering:** The Chinese teacher Zhuangzi tells of a huge bird, the Peng, who flies 90,000 li south on a great wind. A small dove and a cicada laugh at her: why go so far when a short flight to the next bush is enough? (A wader, a sparrow and a crow all see the same sky. The journey that looks pointless to one is the whole life of another.)
- Science (settled): Migrating birds store fat and reshape their organs before a long flight, then burn the fat in the air. We know this from weighing and measuring birds before and after.
- Science (open): Nobody yet knows how a young bird, who has never made the journey, knows when to leave and when to stop. A yearly clock inside her body and the length of the day both play a part.
- Other view, Some ecologists: Most of the energy in a marsh comes from the marsh's own plants and sunlight. A visiting flock may add or remove only a small share. They agree the link between places is real, and ask how large it is in each case. Others answer that even small flows can matter at certain times, such as the weeks before a chick hatches.

- Ask: Does a bird feel a journey as one long trip, or as many different places?

## Story: The Weight of the Falcon
*The girl, her grandmother and her uncle are imagined, and so is the village meeting. The science in the story is real. Amur falcons do pass through Nagaland each October to November, and they do eat flying termites before crossing to Africa. In real life, many falcons were killed around the Doyang reservoir. After a campaign by local people and conservationists, including Bano Haralu, the villages there stopped, and the birds are now welcomed. In 2013 three falcons were fitted with satellite tags and flew from Nagaland to Somalia.*

The rain stopped on a Thursday in October, and by evening the termites came out of the ground.

Sentila stood on the roof of her grandmother's house and watched them rise. Thousands and thousands of them, with thin wings the colour of glass, were lifting off the wet earth in the last of the light. They looked like smoke going the wrong way, up instead of down.

And the falcons came for them.

Sentila had seen the falcons every October of her life. They came to the village by the big lake in their thousands, small grey birds with red legs, and sat in rows along the wires until the wires sagged. In the evening they flew up into the termites and snatched them, one after another, and turned in the air, and came back for more. The sky was full of cutting and turning.

'Where do they come from?' she asked her grandmother, who was shelling beans beside her.

'From very far,' said her grandmother. 'Somewhere cold. They arrive thin and they leave fat. I do not know where they go after. Nobody here ever asked.'

'They are only here for a few weeks,' Sentila said.

'Yes. And in those few weeks they are the easiest food in the valley.'

Sentila knew what her grandmother meant. Every year, some men from the village hung nets between the trees by the lake at night. In the morning the nets were full of birds, and the birds were sold in the market for meat. It had been done for as long as the oldest people remembered. Nobody thought of it as cruel. The birds came, and the birds were food, the way fish come up the river in the season.

That year her uncle Wati brought a falcon home, tangled in the net, still alive. He gave it to Sentila to hold while he fetched a knife.

She held it carefully in both hands, and she was surprised. It was not light. A bird the size of her fist should have felt like a handful of leaves. This one felt like a stone wrapped in feathers. Under her thumb, the breast was thick and soft, not hard bone. She pressed gently, and the skin was oily and smooth with fat.

She thought of her science book, and a page about a godwit. The page said that birds do not fly across oceans on an empty stomach. Before a long journey, a migrating bird eats and eats, until much of her body is fat. Fat is a fuel. A bird carries her tank as fat, and that is the only way a small bird can cross a sea.

Sentila looked at the bird in her hands. She looked at the termites rising from the wet earth, and at the falcons feeding in the dusk. And she understood. The falcons had not come to the village to stay. They had come to fill a tank.

'Uncle,' she said, 'wait.'

'Why?'

'Look how heavy she is. She is not heavy because she lives here. She is heavy because she is getting ready to leave.' Sentila held the bird up. 'This is petrol, Uncle. The fat is petrol for the sea. If she is ready, then she is going. And if we stop her now, she will not cross anything.'

Wati stared at his niece. He was not an unkind man. 'Where is she going?' he said at last.

'I do not know,' Sentila said. 'Somewhere very far. But she is a traveller, not our hen.'

That night the village council met in the long house, and for the first time a ten-year-old girl was given a place by the fire. She told them about the termites, and about the weight of the bird in her hands. She told them about the book. The elders were quiet for a long time. One of them, an old man who had never said a word about the nets, said, 'I have wondered every year where they go. I never wondered what they were doing here.'

Not everyone agreed that night. There were arguments, and some people were angry, because the birds had always brought a little money in October. But the next year, the lake had no nets. A few of the men guided visitors to see the falcons at dawn instead. And in the third year, when the rain stopped in October and the termites rose, the sky over the lake was so full of falcons that people drove up from the town just to stand and look.

On the last morning of that October, Sentila woke before light and went to the roof. The wires were empty. In the night, every falcon in the valley had gone, all together, out over the plains and towards the sea that Sentila had never seen.

She stood there for a long time in the cold, looking at the empty wires, and she thought about all those little tanks of fat going away into the dark, burning slowly, one wingbeat after another, over a sea that had no place to land.

**For the parent:** A fat bird is a bird about to leave. The fat is her fuel for the sea crossing, and it was collected from the termites of one place to be spent over another.

- Ask: What do you think the falcon would do with her fat if she were stopped?

  *Child mode:* Fill the tray with rice. Hide ten lentils near the top and ten deep. Cover it.
  - Note: Keep a note of which lentils are shallow and which are deep. You can use two colours of lentil.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take off the cloth, for low tide. Start the timer for 20 seconds. Use the spoon to scoop only the top 1 centimetre. Count the worms you find. Then cover the tray again.  
  *Parent says:* The tide is out. You have 20 seconds. Use the spoon, and dig only the top. Count your worms.  
  *Child mode:* Take off the cloth. Hunt with the spoon for 20 seconds. Count the worms.
  - Note: Expected: the spoon finds many shallow worms and almost no deep ones.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Reset the tray with the same number of worms. Take off the cloth and start the timer for 20 seconds. Use the chopsticks to probe down and pick up worms. Count them.  
  *Parent says:* Reset the tray. The tide is out again. This time use the long bill. You have 20 seconds. Count your worms.  
  *Child mode:* Reset the tray. Hunt with the chopsticks for 20 seconds. Count the worms.
  - Note: Expected: the chopsticks find fewer worms in total, but they find the deep ones. Results vary, so try each bill twice and add them up.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the counts. Ask: which bill found more shallow worms, and which found the deep ones? Why might many kinds of wader share one mudflat?  
  *Parent says:* Look at the counts. Which bill found the worms near the top? Which found the deep ones? Why can many kinds of waders share one mudflat?

## Applications
- Airports use radar and bird counts to warn pilots when large flocks are crossing, so planes avoid collisions.
- Protecting wetlands as nature reserves and Ramsar sites, such as Chilika and Keoladeo, keeps stopovers open.
- Health teams watch migrating waterbirds for diseases such as bird flu, because flocks can carry them between countries.
- Planners place wind turbines and power lines away from busy flyways and narrow bottlenecks.
- Citizen science projects such as eBird and Bird Count India use many people's sightings to follow migrants.
- Doctors and engineers study how birds store and burn fat, and how they save energy in flight, for ideas in medicine and aircraft design.

## Spotted around us
- Barn swallows sitting in rows on wires or sheltering under a bridge in winter, from October to March in much of India.
- Gulls on the coast or a harbour in winter. Many are winter visitors that leave in spring.
- V-shaped lines of cranes or geese at dusk, in north India and Gujarat in winter.
- Small waders such as common sandpipers bobbing along the edge of a pond, a canal or a paddy field in winter.
- Flamingos in shallow water along the coast, for example near Mumbai, Chennai or Pulicat, in winter.
- An empty wire where the swallows sat in winter. In April, notice that they have gone.

## Open questions
- **How does a young bird, who has never made the journey, know when to leave and when to stop?** Day length and an inside clock both help. Scientists do not know how the clock works, or how it joins the light.
- **How does a godwit sleep, or rest, during 11 days of non-stop flight?** Some birds can rest one half of the brain at a time. Nobody has measured it for a godwit on a long flight.
- **Why do some birds of one kind migrate and others of the same kind stay?** In some species, part of the flock migrates and part stays. Genes, age, food and weather all seem to play a part, but nobody knows the full story.
- **Why did the Siberian crane stop coming to Keoladeo?** Scientists point to the loss of wetlands and to hunting along the route, but the full reasons are still debated.
- **How will climate change affect the timing of migration?** Some species are adjusting their dates and some are not. We do not yet know how many will keep up.
- **Do birds feel a journey as one long trip, or as many separate days?** We can measure what birds do. Nobody can yet say what a flight feels like to the bird.

## Connected maths
- In the Midnight Sun Spin, one dot was lit for 8 of the 12 counts. What fraction of the turn was it lit, in its simplest form? **2/3**. 8 out of 12 is 8 over 12. Divide the top and bottom by 4. That gives 2 over 3. The dot was lit for two thirds of the turn. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A godwit weighs 700 grams before she leaves, and about half of that is fat. How many grams of fat is that? **350 grams**. Half of 700 is 700 divided by 2. That is 350. So about 350 grams of her weight is fat. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A godwit flew about 11,000 kilometres in 8 days. About how many kilometres did she fly each day? **1,375 kilometres**. 11,000 divided by 8 is 1,375. She flew about 1,375 kilometres each day, day and night. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A tag should weigh no more than 5 out of every 100 parts of a bird's weight. A bird weighs 300 grams. What is the heaviest tag she should carry? **15 grams**. 300 divided by 100 is 3. Then 3 times 5 is 15. So the tag should weigh at most 15 grams. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In the Stepping-Stone Flyway, 10 of 12 birds arrived with all four wetlands, and 6 of 12 arrived with one drained. How many more birds arrived in the first round, and what fraction of the birds fell in the second round? **4 more birds. Half fell, or 6/12 = 1/2.**. 10 take away 6 is 4. In round 2, 12 take away 6 is 6 birds that fell. 6 out of 12 is one half. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- Tara the goose, an imagined bird, moved from 48 degrees north to 27 degrees north. One degree of latitude is about 111 kilometres. About how far did she travel from north to south? **About 2,331 kilometres**. 48 take away 27 is 21 degrees. Then 21 times 111 is 2,331. So she travelled about 2,331 kilometres from north to south. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A godwit flies for 11 days, rests for 2 weeks, and flies again for 5 days. How many days is that from start to finish? **30 days**. 2 weeks is 14 days. Then 11 plus 14 is 25, and 25 plus 5 is 30. So the journey takes 30 days. (queues `math.time.calendar.L4`)
- Suppose fat gives 9 units of energy in each gram and sugar gives 4. A bird needs 180 units for one day of flying. How many grams of fat does she need, and how many grams of sugar would she need instead? **20 grams of fat, or 45 grams of sugar**. 180 divided by 9 is 20 grams of fat. 180 divided by 4 is 45 grams of sugar. Fat is much lighter to carry, since she needs only 20 grams, not 45. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Tara rested for 4 weeks at the middle stopover. She flew for 1 week before it and 1 week after it. What fraction of those 6 weeks was spent resting? Give it in its simplest form. **2/3**. 4 weeks out of 6 is 4 over 6. Divide the top and bottom by 2. That gives 2 over 3. She spent two thirds of the time resting. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What does migration mean? → A regular journey that an animal makes each year, going out and coming back.
- Why does the far north have such long summer days? → The Earth is tilted, and in summer the north leans towards the Sun.
- Why do migrating birds store fat? → A gram of fat holds more than twice the energy of a gram of sugar, so she carries less weight.
- What is a stopover? → A place where a migrating bird rests and feeds before flying on.
- What is a winter visitor? → A bird that spends the northern winter in a warmer place and goes back to breed.
- What does a flat part on a tracking graph show? → The bird stayed in about the same place.
- What happens to a godwit's gut before a long flight? → It shrinks, and the flight muscles grow.
- Name two ways of following a migrating bird. → A ring on her leg, and a satellite tag.
