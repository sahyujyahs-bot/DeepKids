# Where we use electricity: from the wall and from cells
`sci.electricity.uses-of-electricity.L2`

**Summary.** In a power cut, the fan, the lights and the fridge stop at once, but the torch and the wall clock keep going. The things that stop get mains electricity, which comes along wires from a power station to the socket in the wall. The things that keep going carry their own cells, small stores of electricity. Your child listens to the jobs electricity does at home, then tests old cells that the family thought were dead. Many cells that cannot light a torch still make a clock tick.

**Intro.** *Imagine the power goes off on a hot evening. What stops working? What keeps going?* Most children name the fan, the lights and the TV. Fewer remember the fridge, the lift or the water pump. But the torch still shines, and the wall clock still ticks. Why do some things stop and others keep going? The things that stop all get electricity from the wall. The things that keep going carry their own small store of electricity, called a cell. In this unit you find out where electricity comes from, and you test how long a cell lasts.

## Learning outcomes

1. By the end I can say that mains electricity comes along wires from a power station to the sockets in the wall.
2. By the end I can tell which things use mains electricity and which use cells, and say what keeps working in a power cut.

5. By the end I can give the safety rules for mains electricity and for cells, with a reason for each.

## Terms
- **Electricity**: Electricity is a kind of energy that flows along wires and makes things work. *e.g.* Electricity flowing into a fan makes the fan's blades turn.
- **Appliance**: An appliance is a machine at home that uses electricity to do a job. *e.g.* A fan, a fridge, a mixie, a TV and a torch are all appliances.
- **Mains electricity**: Mains electricity is the strong electricity that comes along wires from a power station to the sockets in the wall. It is strong enough to kill a person. *e.g.* The fridge, the TV and the geyser use mains electricity through a plug in the wall.
- **Socket and plug**: A socket is the place in the wall where mains electricity comes out. A plug on the end of an appliance's wire fits into it. *e.g.* A grown-up pushes the mixie's plug into the socket in the kitchen.
- **Power station**: A power station is a big building where mains electricity is made. *e.g.* The power station at the Idukki dam in Kerala makes electricity from falling water.
- **Cell**: A cell is a small store of electricity that you can carry. Many people call it a battery. *e.g.* A torch, a TV remote and a wall clock run on cells.
- **Rechargeable battery**: A rechargeable battery is a store of electricity that can be filled again from the wall. Filling it is called charging. *e.g.* A phone has a rechargeable battery inside. We charge it from a socket.
- **Power cut**: A power cut is a time when mains electricity stops coming to a home or a street. *e.g.* In a power cut, the fans stop, but a torch still works.

## Explanations
### Electricity does many jobs at home · *defined*
**Definition:** Electricity is a kind of energy. Appliances change it into light, sound, heat, cold or movement.

In the listening activity, you heard the fan whirr and the fridge hum. When the switch went off, the sound stopped. Electricity was doing a job in each one. A bulb changes electricity into light. A doorbell changes it into sound. An iron and a geyser change it into heat. A fridge uses it to make cold. A fan and a mixie use it to make movement. Some appliances do two jobs at once. A TV makes light and sound.
- e.g. A tube light changes electricity into light.
- e.g. A mixie changes electricity into movement, so the blade spins and grinds the chutney.
- e.g. A fridge uses electricity to keep curd and milk cold.
### Mains electricity comes from a power station along wires · *defined*
**Definition:** Mains electricity is made in power stations. It travels along wires, on poles or under the ground, to every building and then to the sockets in the walls.

Every appliance with a plug gets its electricity from the wall. The wall does not make it. A wire inside the wall brings it from the meter box of your building. Bigger wires bring it from the street. Even bigger wires on tall towers bring it from a power station, often very far away. Power stations make electricity from burning coal, from falling water at a dam, from the wind and from sunlight. In India, most electricity comes from burning coal. More and more comes from the Sun and the wind. If a wire on the way is broken, everyone after it has a power cut.
- e.g. The Idukki dam in Kerala uses falling water to make electricity.
- e.g. Near Kanyakumari in Tamil Nadu, hundreds of tall windmills make electricity from the wind.
- e.g. At Pavagada in Karnataka, a huge field of solar panels makes electricity from sunlight.
### A cell is a store of electricity that runs down slowly · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell holds chemicals that make electricity flow when the cell is in a torch, a clock or a toy. As the cell is used, the chemicals change, and the cell gives less and less.

A cell is a small store of electricity that you can carry. That is why a torch works in a power cut. Inside a cell are chemicals. When the cell is used, they slowly change and make electricity flow. A cell does not go from full to empty in one moment. It runs down little by little. In the Tired Cell Test, some cells could not light the torch but still made the clock tick. A torch needs a lot of electricity every second. A clock needs only a tiny amount. So the torch stops long before the clock does. A phone's battery is a store you can fill again from the wall.
- e.g. In the Tired Cell Test, an old cell that could not light the torch often still ran the wall clock.
- e.g. A wall clock can run for many months on one cell.
- e.g. A phone battery runs down faster when the torch and the screen are both on.
### Living bodies use electricity too · *debated*
**Definition:** Nerves in animals, including us, send messages as tiny electric signals. Plants also send electric signals through their bodies.

Your body has no plug and no cell, but it uses electricity all the time. Tiny electric signals run along your nerves when you touch something. A tiny electric signal starts every beat of your heart. The energy for these signals comes from the food you eat. Plants use electric signals too. Touch a touch-me-not plant, and a signal runs along her stalk. Her leaves fold within seconds. Plants sense, decide and act with these signals. Scientists still argue about what this means.
- e.g. A doctor's ECG machine shows the electric signals of a beating heart as a wavy line.
- e.g. A touch-me-not plant folds her leaves after a touch, and an electric signal runs along her stalk first.
- e.g. Jagadish Chandra Bose, a scientist in Kolkata, measured electric signals in plants more than a hundred years ago.
- **A plain fact, from the work of J. C. Bose and many plant scientists since:** A plant senses a touch, sends the news along her body as an electric signal, and acts on it. That is sensing and deciding, done without a brain.
- **Some other scientists:** These signals are automatic responses, like a reflex. They say a plant's signals are reflexes, not feelings.

## Mostly agreed
- Mains electricity is made in power stations and reaches homes along wires. *How we know:* When a wire on the street breaks or a power station stops, every home after it has a power cut until the fault is fixed.
- A cell stores electricity using chemicals, and runs down as it is used. *How we know:* A torch left on gets dimmer or stops, and a new cell makes it work again. Alessandro Volta made the first battery in Italy in 1800.
- Mains electricity can kill a person, but the small cells in a torch are not strong enough to give a shock. *How we know:* Indian homes get mains electricity at about 230 volts. An AA cell gives only 1.5 volts, which you cannot feel through dry skin.

## How we found out
**Michael Faraday, a scientist in London who began as a bookbinder's apprentice, and Joseph Henry, a scientist in the United States, 1820 (a wire moves a compass) and 1831 (a moving magnet makes electricity).** In 1820, Hans Christian Oersted, in Denmark, saw a compass needle swing when electricity flowed in a wire near it. So electricity can act like a magnet. Many scientists then asked the opposite question. Can a magnet make electricity? They put strong magnets beside coils of wire and waited. Nothing flowed. Michael Faraday tried too, on and off, for years. He had left school young and taught himself science while working for a bookbinder in London. In 1831, he found the answer. Electricity flowed in his coil only while a magnet was moving in or out of it. When the magnet stopped, the flow stopped. Joseph Henry found the same thing in the United States at about the same time. The wrong idea was that a magnet sitting still next to a wire could make electricity. The magnet has to move. Today, most power stations use this discovery. Steam, falling water or wind spins huge magnets near coils of wire, and the electricity travels along wires to your home.  
*What people believed before:* People expected a strong magnet sitting still beside a coil of wire to make electricity flow. Faraday showed that electricity flows only while the magnet moves.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The electricity is made in the socket, or in the switch.** (A common idea among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: When you press the switch, the light comes on at once. It looks as if the switch makes it.  
  Against: In a power cut, pressing the switch does nothing. The switch only opens and closes the path. The electricity comes along wires from a power station.
- **A cell is either new or dead. A dead cell has nothing left.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: A torch with old cells goes dark, so the cells seem empty.  
  Against: In the Tired Cell Test, many old cells that could not light the torch still made the clock tick. They still held some electricity, too little for the torch.
- **Cells are small and safe, so they are fine for little children to play with.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: An AA cell is too weak to give you a shock.  
  Against: A swallowed button cell can badly burn a small child's insides within two hours. Old cells can leak a chemical that hurts skin and eyes. Cells are safe to use, but not to play with.
- **Only animals with a brain use electric signals.** (Many people; *debated*)  
  For: We know about nerves and brains, and they work with electric signals.  
  Against: Plants send electric signals too. A touch-me-not folds her leaves after a signal runs along her stalk. Some scientists call this a reflex, not a feeling.

## Roots
We have listened to the jobs electricity does, followed the wires back to the power station and tested old cells. Now we ask what the Tired Cell Test tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The cells that could not light the torch still made the clock tick. They still held some electricity. A torch needs a lot of electricity each second, and a clock needs very little. So a cell runs down little by little, and stops a torch long before a clock.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort cells into two kinds, new or dead, as if a cell were full one day and empty the next. The test shows that a cell runs down slowly, and what it can still do depends on how much the job needs.  
*Evidence:* In the Tired Cell Test, many old cells that gave no light in the torch still moved the clock's second hand.

- *who am i:* You run on electricity too. Tiny electric signals carry messages along your nerves and start every beat of your heart, using energy from your food.
- *what is life:* Every living being uses tiny electric signals. A touch-me-not plant sends one along her stalk when you touch her, and her leaves fold.
- *what is this world:* The light in your room began far away: in a river behind a dam, in coal under the ground, in the wind or in sunshine on a solar panel.

A bulb's light, a fan's breeze and a fridge's cold seem like very different things. In your home, all of them are made from the same electricity, coming along the same wires. That electricity was made from falling water, coal, wind or sunlight. Water is lifted into clouds by the Sun's heat, coal is made from plants that grew in sunlight long ago, and the wind blows because the Sun warms the Earth unevenly. So most of the jobs electricity does in your home began as sunlight.

- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper, and you can see a cloud in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, without rain no tree, and without the tree no paper. (Look deeply at the light in your room, and you can see the river at the dam, the coal from the mines and the sunshine on the solar panels. You can also see the people who keep the wires working.)
- **Dhammapada 277:** The Buddha taught that everything that is made keeps changing. Nothing that is made stays the same. (A cell is made in a factory, and its chemicals change a little every minute the clock ticks. It does not stop all at once. It changes slowly, even when we cannot see it.)
- Science (settled): Energy is never made or lost, only changed. A cell's chemical energy becomes light and heat in a torch, and movement in a clock.
- Science (open): How to store enough of the day's sunlight to run whole cities at night, cheaply, is still being worked out.
- Other view, Many scientists: Electricity is only tiny particles moving through a wire. A plant's electric signal is an automatic response, like a doorbell ringing when it is pressed. It has nothing to do with feeling.
- Practice, Feel your own electricity (3 min): Sit still and shut your eyes. Put your hand flat on the left side of your chest. Wait until you feel your heart beating. Every beat starts with a tiny electric signal inside your heart. Your heart has no plug and no cell. Count ten beats quietly. Now think about this morning's breakfast. That food is giving your heart its energy right now.
- Ask: The clock ran on a cell the torch could not use. Was that cell used up or not?
- Ask: Your heart has no plug and no cell. Where does its electricity come from?
- Ask: When the light in your room is on, where in the world did it begin?

## Story: The Night the Lift Stopped
*Anju, Rahul, Ammamma, Amma and Thresiamma are made up. Ammamma means grandmother in Malayalam. Many homes in Kerala villages had no electricity in the 1960s, and grinding stones were used by hand before mixies.*

Anju was seven. She lived on the sixth floor of a tall building in Kochi, in Kerala.

One hot evening in April, she was doing her homework under the ceiling fan. Her cousin Rahul, who was five, was watching cartoons. Ammamma, her grandmother, was grinding coconut in the mixie for chutney.

Then, all at once, everything stopped.

The fan slowed down and went still. The TV went black. The mixie gave one last whirr. The lights went out, and the flat was dark.

'Power cut!' shouted Rahul.

From the corridor came voices. 'The lift has stopped!' someone called. 'Is anyone inside?' It was Thresiamma aunty from next door. Luckily, the lift was empty.

Ammamma felt her way to the kitchen drawer and found the torch. She clicked it on. A round circle of light jumped onto the wall.

'Why does the torch still work?' asked Rahul. 'Everything else stopped.'

Anju thought about it. She shone the torch on the mixie. Its wire went to a plug in the wall. She shone it on the TV. Its wire went to the wall too. Then she looked at the torch in her hand. It had no wire at all.

'I think I know,' she said. 'The TV and the mixie get their electricity from the wall. When the wall stops, they stop.'

'And the torch?' asked Rahul.

Anju twisted open the back of the torch. Inside were two cells, like short, fat pencils with metal ends. 'The torch carries its own electricity,' she said. 'It keeps it in these cells.'

Tick, tock, went the clock on the wall. Rahul looked up. 'The clock didn't stop either!'

Ammamma lifted the clock down. On the back was one small cell.

Then Rahul heard music. Ammamma's old radio on the shelf was still playing film songs, very softly. It had cells too.

Amma's phone buzzed on the table. 'A phone has no cells,' said Rahul. 'But it still works.'

'A phone has a battery inside it,' said Ammamma. 'We fill it from the wall when we charge it. Then it carries the electricity with it, like a water bottle you fill at the tap.'

By torchlight, Anju made a list on the back of her homework. On one side she wrote STOPPED: fan, TV, mixie, lights, fridge, lift. On the other side she wrote KEPT GOING: torch, clock, radio, phone.

'Everything that stopped has a wire to the wall,' she said. 'Everything that kept going carries its own store.' Then she frowned. 'But where does the wall get its electricity?'

Ammamma took them out to the balcony. The whole street was dark. Not one window was lit.

'Look at those wires on the poles,' Ammamma said. 'They carry electricity to every building, from a power station far away. Tonight something is wrong on the way. So the whole street has a power cut.'

'Even the ice cream shop downstairs?' asked Rahul.

'Even the ice cream shop,' said Ammamma.

Rahul groaned. He was getting hot. Ammamma picked up a palm-leaf fan and waved it over him.

'When I was your age, in our village near Thrissur, there was no electricity at all,' she said. 'Every night we used a fan like this one. We had a kerosene lamp for light. My mother ground the rice for dosa on a big stone, by hand.'

'No TV?' asked Rahul.

'No TV,' said Ammamma, and she laughed.

Anju looked at her list again. 'Ammamma, your mixie needs the wall. But your mother's grinding stone needed only her arms.'

'And her arms ran on breakfast,' said Ammamma.

Just then, with a click, the fan began to turn. The lights flashed on. From every flat in the building came a cheer.

Anju drew a little plug at the top of her first list, and a little cell at the top of the second. Then she showed Rahul.

'Now you know what to do in a power cut,' she said. 'Look for the things that carry their own cells.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: in the power cut, Anju sorts the flat into things that stopped and things that kept going. Everything that stopped had a wire to the wall, which brings mains electricity from a power station. Everything that kept going carried its own cells, or a battery charged earlier from the wall.

- Ask: In the power cut, what stopped working in Anju's flat? What kept going?
- Ask: How did Anju work out why the torch still worked?
- Ask: Ammamma said a phone battery is like a water bottle. What did she mean?
- Ask: Why did the whole street have a power cut, and not only Anju's flat?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Tired Cell Test: can a cell that fails the torch still run a clock? (25 min)
- **question:** Old cells cannot light a torch any more. Do they still hold any electricity? Can they still run a wall clock?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will an old cell that cannot light the torch make the clock tick? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same torch and the same clock for every cell. We watch the clock's second hand for the same time, 10 seconds, for every cell. We also try one new cell in both, so we know the torch and the clock work.
- **safety:** If a cell has a white or green crust, or feels wet, do not touch it with bare hands. Put it in a paper bag for e-waste, and wash your hands. Never open a cell or put it in your mouth. Keep button cells away from small children.
- **record:** Fill in the table with a tick or a cross for the torch and the clock. Then colour each cell's sticker: green if it ran both, yellow if it ran only the clock, red if it ran nothing.
- **results table:** Cell number | Did the torch light? | Did the clock tick?
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Collect the old cells together. 2. Stick a numbered sticker on each one. 3. Check each cell for crust or leaks.  
  *Parent says:* These cells were taken out because they stopped working. Let's give each one a number.  
  *Child mode:* Help collect old cells. Stick a number on each.
  - Note: Look at every cell before your child holds it. Put aside any cell with white or green crust, or a wet look, in a paper bag. Most homes find old cells in remotes, toys, a drawer or a stopped clock.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the new cell in the torch and the clock to show both work. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* The new cell lights the torch and runs the clock. Now, will an old cell run the clock? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Will an old cell run the clock? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: No, because it is dead.; Maybe, because the clock is small.; Some might, because they are not all the same age.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Write it down and do not say what will happen. If your torch takes two cells, keep one new cell in it, and test each old cell alongside it.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put an old cell in the torch. 2. Point it at a wall and switch it on. 3. Mark a tick if it lights, a cross if not. 4. Do the same for every cell.  
  *Parent says:* Put cell number 1 in the torch. Switch it on. Does it light the wall? Tick or cross?  
  *Child mode:* Put a cell in the torch. Switch it on. Does it light?
  - Ask: What did you see? (good: Nothing.; A very dim light.; It lit up bright.)
  - Note: A dim, yellow glow counts as a tick, but say it was dim. Many torches today have bright white lights, called LEDs, that go dark suddenly when cells are weak.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put an old cell in the clock. 2. Count to ten while you watch the second hand. 3. Mark a tick if it moved, a cross if not. 4. Do the same for every cell.  
  *Parent says:* Now put cell number 1 in the clock. Watch the second hand while we count to ten. Did it move?  
  *Child mode:* Put the cell in the clock. Watch the thin hand. Did it move?
  - Note: Put the cell in the right way round. Match the little plus sign on the cell with the plus sign in the clock. A clock that runs, even slowly, counts as a tick.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the ticks in each column. 2. Sort the cells into three groups. 3. Ask what your child found, and why. 4. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* How many cells lit the torch? How many ran the clock? What did you find out? Why do you think that happened?  
  *Child mode:* Count your ticks. Which ran more cells, the torch or the clock? Why?
  - Ask: Why did some cells run the clock but not the torch? (good: They had a little electricity left.; The torch needs a lot and the clock needs a little.)
  - Note: Wait for your child's own claim and evidence. Then say it plainly: the old cells still held some electricity. The torch needs a lot every second, so it stopped. The clock needs very little, so it kept going. If every cell ran both, your cells were not so old. If none ran the clock, they were truly used up. Both are real results. Put the clock-only cells in a clock or a remote, and take the red ones to an e-waste box.
  - Check: A cell cannot light the torch, but it makes the clock tick. What does that show? → **The cell still has a little electricity**
### e2 · The Phone Battery Race: which job uses up the battery faster? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a phone's battery go down faster when the phone is resting, or when it plays a video with its torch on?
- **predict:** Guess first. In which 8 minutes will the battery number drop more? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same phone, not charging, for both tests. Each test lasts exactly 8 minutes on a timer. We read the battery number at the start and at the end of each test.
- **record:** Write the battery number before and after each test. Draw a bar for how much it dropped in each test.
- **results table:** Test | Battery at the start | Battery at the end | How much it dropped
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find the battery number on the phone. 2. Show it to your child. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This number shows how full the phone's battery is. A hundred means full. Which test will make it drop more? Why?  
  *Child mode:* This number shows how full the battery is. Which test will empty it faster?
  - Note: Take the phone off the charger first. On many phones you see the number by pulling down from the top of the screen or in the battery settings.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the battery number. 2. Lock the phone and put it face down. 3. Set the timer for 8 minutes. 4. Write the number again.  
  *Parent says:* The phone is resting now. While we wait, let's draw what the phone is doing. Now read the number again.

## Applications
- Inverters in many Indian homes store mains electricity in a big battery and run fans and lights during power cuts.
- Solar lamps fill their battery from sunlight during the day, and light homes and shops at night where power cuts are common.
- Doctors use machines that run on batteries, such as a pacemaker inside the chest that keeps a heart beating steadily. Esther Takeuchi, an American engineer, invented batteries used in devices that are put inside the body to help the heart.
- A. Lalitha, from Chennai, became India's first woman electrical engineer in 1943. Engineers like her design the machines and wires that bring mains electricity to homes.

## Spotted around us
- Near Kanyakumari in Tamil Nadu, hundreds of tall windmills turn in the wind and make electricity.
- At the Idukki dam in Kerala, water falling from the reservoir makes electricity for homes across the state.
- At the Barefoot College in Tilonia, Rajasthan, grandmothers from villages around the world learn to build and fix solar lamps for their own villages.
- On a city street in Hyderabad or Pune, thick black wires run from poles into every building.
- On rooftops in many cities and villages, families put up blue solar panels to make their own electricity.

## Open questions
- **How can we store enough of the day's sunlight to run whole cities at night?** Batteries for this are still very costly and use metals that are hard to dig up. Scientists in India and around the world are trying new kinds, such as batteries made with salt.
- **What does a touch-me-not plant feel when her leaves fold?** Scientists can measure the electric signal that runs along her stalk. What the plant feels, and how that compares with our own sense of touch, is still argued about.
- **Can we make cells that never leak and can always be used again?** Rechargeable batteries help, but they still wear out after many charges. Engineers are still looking for batteries that last much longer.

## Warm-up
- Name one appliance that makes light. → A bulb, a tube light, a torch or a TV.
- Where is mains electricity made? → In a power station.
- What does a torch carry its electricity in? → Cells, which many people call batteries.
- Does a fridge work in a power cut? → No. It uses mains electricity from the wall.
- Can a cell too weak for a torch still run a clock? → Often yes. A clock needs much less electricity.
- Should you touch a socket with wet hands? → Never. Water helps mains electricity give a shock.
