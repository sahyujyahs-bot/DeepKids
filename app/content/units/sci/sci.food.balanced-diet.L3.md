# A balanced diet: why your body needs many kinds of food
`sci.food.balanced-diet.L3`

**Summary.** A plate of rice can fill your stomach and still leave your body short of protein and vitamins. Why? In this lesson you will build a balanced thali, test which fruit has the most vitamin C, and follow a chapati to your cells with paper clips.

**Intro.** *Breathe out slowly onto your hand. Feel how warm it is. Part of that breath is the chapati or rice you ate yesterday. How did your food get into your breath?* Almost all our food starts as plants growing in sunlight. In this lesson we follow food after you swallow it. A spoon of dal does not stay dal for long. It is broken into pieces far too small to see. Then it travels in your blood to every part of you. Some of it becomes your muscles and your hair. Your cells use some of it as fuel. What is left over leaves your body as a gas when you breathe out. Some of it feeds trillions of tiny living things in your gut. So why do we need so many kinds of food? Why not just eat rice, or just eat sweets?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a balanced diet is: many kinds of food, in about the right amounts, over a day or a week.
1. I can sort foods into energy foods, body-building foods and protecting foods, and know that many foods do more than one job.
2. I can make a balanced plate or thali from the food in my own kitchen.
3. I can explain that food is broken into tiny building blocks, carried by the blood to my cells, and used for energy and for building.
4. I can explain why my body needs each kind of food: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre and water.
5. I can run a fair test, count or measure the results, and say what I think, what I saw and why.
6. I can explain that trillions of microbes live in my gut and eat the fibre I cannot digest.
7. I can say what scientists agree on about diets, and what they still argue about, without calling any food or person good or bad.

## Terms
- **Diet**: All the food and drink a person usually has. It does not mean eating less. *e.g.* A family in Kerala and a family in Punjab may have very different diets. Both can be healthy.
- **Balanced diet**: Eating many kinds of food in about the right amounts, over a day or a week, so the body gets everything it needs. *e.g.* Idli and sambar for breakfast, rice, dal and poriyal (cooked vegetables) for lunch, a guava after school and chapati with egg curry or paneer at night.
- **Nutrient**: A part of food that the body uses to live, grow and stay well. *e.g.* Protein in dal, calcium in milk and vitamin C in amla are all nutrients.
- **Energy foods (go foods)**: Foods that give the body most of its energy. Rice, chapati, millets, potatoes, bananas, sugar and fats are energy foods. *e.g.* A plate of upma gives you energy to run to the park.
- **Body-building foods (grow foods)**: Foods with lots of protein. The body uses protein to build and mend itself. *e.g.* Dal, rajma, chana, sprouts, milk, curd, paneer, eggs, fish, meat and peanuts.
- **Protecting foods (glow foods)**: Vegetables and fruits. They give vitamins, minerals and fibre, which help every part of the body work and stay well. *e.g.* Palak, carrots, tomatoes, guava, amla and papaya.
- **Carbohydrate**: A nutrient made of sugars. Sugar, starch and fibre are all carbohydrates. Sugar and starch give the body most of its energy. *e.g.* Rice and chapati are mostly a carbohydrate called starch.
- **Starch**: A long chain of sugar pieces joined together. Plants store their energy as starch. *e.g.* When you chew a piece of chapati for a long time, it starts to taste a little sweet. Your spit is cutting the starch into sugar.
- **Glucose**: The simple sugar that your cells use for energy. Starch is broken into glucose inside you. *e.g.* Your blood carries glucose to your brain all day and all night.
- **Protein**: A long chain made of building blocks called amino acids. Your muscles, hair, skin and blood are built with proteins. *e.g.* Your hair and nails are made mostly of a protein called keratin.
- **Amino acids**: The building blocks of proteins. There are about 20 kinds. Your body joins them in new orders to make its own proteins. *e.g.* The amino acids in dal are joined again inside you to make your own muscle.
- **Fat**: An oily nutrient. It carries a lot of energy. It also builds the thin skin around every cell, called the cell membrane, and helps the body take in some vitamins. *e.g.* Ghee, oil, butter, coconut and peanuts all have fat.
- **Vitamin**: A nutrient the body needs in very small amounts to work properly. Each vitamin has its own jobs. *e.g.* Vitamin C in amla and guava helps your body mend cuts.
- **Mineral**: A nutrient that comes from rocks and soil, through plants and animals into us. Calcium and iron are minerals. *e.g.* Calcium from milk, ragi and til builds your bones.
- **Iron**: A mineral that the blood uses to carry oxygen around the body. *e.g.* Dal, palak, rajma, bajra, eggs and meat all have iron.
- **Calcium**: A mineral that makes bones and teeth hard. It also helps muscles and nerves work. *e.g.* Milk, curd, paneer, ragi, til and green leaves have calcium.
- **Fibre**: A kind of carbohydrate in plant foods that your body cannot break down. It keeps food moving through your gut and feeds your gut microbes. *e.g.* The skin of an apple, the green skin on whole moong and the strings in beans and bhindi are fibre.
- **Digestion**: Breaking food into pieces small enough to pass into the blood. *e.g.* Digestion starts in your mouth when you chew, and goes on in your stomach and intestines.
- **Enzyme**: A kind of protein that cuts big pieces of food into smaller ones, very fast. It works like tiny scissors. *e.g.* An enzyme in your spit cuts starch into sugar.
- **Cell**: The tiny living unit that every living thing is made of. Your body has about 30 trillion cells. *e.g.* Skin cells, muscle cells, blood cells and brain cells are all cells, each with their own job.
- **Mitochondria**: Tiny parts inside your cells that burn food with oxygen to release energy. *e.g.* Your muscle cells are full of mitochondria. That is why they can work so hard.
- **Gut microbes**: The trillions of tiny living things, mostly bacteria, that live in your gut, most of all in the large intestine. *e.g.* Gut microbes eat the fibre from your dal and poriyal, which you cannot digest yourself.
- **Kilocalorie (kcal)**: A unit for measuring the energy in food. One kilocalorie is the energy needed to warm 1 litre of water by 1 degree Celsius. Food labels write it as kcal. People often just say 'calories'. *e.g.* A banana gives roughly 100 kcal. A child of eight uses very roughly 1,500 to 2,000 kcal in a day.
- **Deficiency**: When the body is not getting enough of one nutrient for a long time. It can make people ill. *e.g.* Too little vitamin C for many weeks causes an illness called scurvy.

## Explanations
### What a balanced diet is · *defined*
**Definition:** A balanced diet is eating many kinds of food, in about the right amounts, over a day or a week. It gives the body all the nutrients it needs, plus enough water.

Your body needs many different things from food. No single food has all of them. Rice gives lots of energy but little calcium. Milk gives calcium but almost no vitamin C. Amla is full of vitamin C but gives little energy. So we eat many kinds. A balanced diet does not mean every meal must be perfect. A festival plate of sweets is fine. What matters is the mix over many days. Balanced also does not mean eating less. Growing children need plenty of food.
- e.g. A day of idli and sambar, rice with dal and poriyal, a guava, and chapati with egg curry is balanced. It has energy foods, body-building foods and protecting foods.
- e.g. Eating only rice and pickle for weeks is not balanced. The body would miss protein, vitamins and minerals, even though the stomach feels full.
- e.g. One plate of jalebis at a wedding does not make a diet unbalanced. A diet is what you usually eat.
### Go, grow and glow: three jobs food does · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy foods (go foods) give the body fuel. Body-building foods (grow foods) give protein to build and mend. Protecting foods (glow foods) give vitamins, minerals and fibre that keep the body working well.

An easy way to plan a meal is to think of three jobs. Go foods are rice, chapati, millets like ragi and bajra, potatoes and bananas. Grow foods are dal, chana, rajma, sprouts, milk, curd, paneer, eggs, fish, meat and nuts. Glow foods are vegetables and fruits. Water and a little fat are needed too. Many foods do more than one job. Dal gives protein and also fibre and iron. Peanuts give protein, fat and energy. Milk gives protein and calcium. So the groups are a helpful map, not strict boxes. Different countries draw them differently. Some use five groups, some use a plate and some have used a pyramid.
- e.g. A lunch of rice (go), dal (grow) and bhindi poriyal (glow) does all three jobs.
- e.g. Egg bhurji with chapati and tomato does all three jobs too.
- e.g. A plate of rice and potato curry is mostly go. Adding a bowl of curd and a cucumber adds grow and glow.
### The balanced plate: how much of each · *defined*
**Definition:** About half of a balanced plate is vegetables and fruits. About a quarter is grains or millets. The last quarter is dal, milk foods, eggs, fish, meat or nuts. Water is the drink.

Nutrition scientists in many countries draw a healthy meal as a plate. India's National Institute of Nutrition in Hyderabad uses one called 'My Plate for the Day' in its 2024 food guide. Like many others, it gives about half the plate to vegetables and fruits. Grains and millets take about a quarter. Pulses, milk foods, eggs, fish, meat and nuts take the rest. Fats and oils come in small amounts. Many Indian homes already eat this way. A thali has small bowls of dal, poriyal, curd and chutney around rice or chapati. A banana-leaf meal in the south puts many small heaps of vegetables around the rice. One-pot foods count too. Khichdi with vegetables, or a biryani with raita and salad, can be balanced.
- e.g. A thali with a big helping of poriyal and salad, one chapati and some rice, a bowl of dal and a bowl of curd.
- e.g. A Kerala sadya on a banana leaf has many vegetable dishes like avial, thoran and olan around the rice.
- e.g. Vegetable khichdi with a spoon of ghee and a bowl of curd is a balanced one-pot meal.
### What happens to food inside you: digestion · *defined*
**Definition:** Digestion is breaking food into pieces small enough to pass through the wall of the gut into the blood.

Food goes on a long trip through a tube called the gut. In your mouth, teeth crush it and spit wets it. An enzyme in your spit starts cutting starch into sugar. In your stomach, strong acid and more enzymes break proteins apart. The stomach churns the food into a thick soup. In the small intestine, juices from the pancreas take over. They cut starch into glucose, proteins into amino acids, and fats into smaller pieces. Bile from the liver breaks fat into tiny drops so the enzymes can reach it. Then those tiny pieces pass through the intestine wall into the blood. Whatever is left, mostly fibre and water, moves on to the large intestine. There the gut microbes get to work.
- e.g. A chapati becomes glucose. A spoon of dal becomes amino acids. A drop of ghee becomes fatty acids.
- e.g. Vitamins and minerals do not need to be cut up. They are already small enough to pass into the blood.
- e.g. The small intestine of a grown-up is several metres long. Laid out straight, it would stretch past two grown-ups lying head to toe.
### Your food travels to 30 trillion cells · *defined*
**Definition:** After digestion, the blood carries the tiny food pieces to every cell in the body. The cells each take what they need.

Your body is made of about 30 trillion cells. That is 30 followed by twelve zeros. Each one is alive and needs food. Your heart pumps blood past almost every cell, all the time. The blood carries glucose, amino acids, fats, vitamins, minerals and water. A muscle cell takes amino acids to build more muscle. A bone cell takes calcium. A cell in the bone marrow takes iron and folate to make new red blood cells. A brain cell takes glucose. Your body is always rebuilding itself like this. The lining of your gut is replaced every few days. So part of you next month will be built from this month's food.
- e.g. Your red blood cells each live about four months. New ones are made all the time from the iron, protein and vitamins in your food.
- e.g. When you grow taller, your bone cells are using calcium and protein from your food.
- e.g. Even while you sleep, your brain uses glucose from your dinner.
### Inside the cell: food plus oxygen gives energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside each cell, tiny parts called mitochondria join glucose or fat with oxygen. This releases the energy the cell uses. Carbon dioxide and water are left over.

Your cells need energy for everything: moving, growing, thinking and keeping warm. They get it by slowly burning food with oxygen. It is a bit like a fire. But it happens gently, in tiny steps, so it never gets hot enough to hurt. This is why you breathe. You breathe in oxygen for your cells. You breathe out the carbon dioxide they made from your food. When the body burns its stored fat, most of that fat leaves through the breath, as carbon dioxide. Two scientists in Australia worked this out in 2014. The brain uses a lot of energy. In a child of about five, the brain uses nearly half of all the energy the body burns.
- e.g. Run fast and you breathe harder. Your muscle cells are burning more food and need more oxygen.
- e.g. Your body stays at about 37 degrees Celsius. That warmth comes from your cells burning food.
- e.g. Breathe out onto a cold mirror. The mist is partly water made from your food.
### Why the body needs carbohydrates · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbohydrates, like starch and sugar, are the body's main fuel. They are broken into glucose, which every cell can burn for energy.

Rice, chapati, ragi, jowar, bajra, poha, potatoes and bananas are mostly starch. Starch is a long chain of glucose pieces. Your body cuts the chain and sends the glucose into the blood. Whole grains take longer to break down than white rice or maida. This is especially so when you eat them with dal and vegetables. So their glucose enters your blood slowly and steadily. Sugar and sweets hardly need breaking down at all, so their glucose enters your blood very fast. Your body can handle that sometimes. But lots of added sugar every day feeds the mouth bacteria that make holes in teeth. Over many years, eating a lot of added sugar is also linked to illnesses such as diabetes. Any extra glucose that is not needed is stored, first in the liver and muscles, then as fat.
- e.g. Chapati made from whole atta, eaten with dal and poriyal, gives your body steady energy through the afternoon.
- e.g. A glass of sugary sharbat gives your body energy very quickly.
- e.g. Chew a piece of chapati thirty times. It starts to taste sweet as your spit cuts starch into sugar.
### Why the body needs protein, and why dal and rice go together · *defined*
**Definition:** Proteins are long chains of about 20 kinds of building blocks called amino acids. The body breaks food proteins into amino acids, then joins them in new orders to build its own proteins.

Your muscles, skin, hair, nails, blood and enzymes are made of proteins. They wear out and are rebuilt all the time, and children are also growing. So you need protein every day. Your body can make some amino acids itself. But there are nine kinds it cannot make. They must come from food. Eggs, milk, fish and meat have all nine in good amounts. Grains like rice and wheat are a little low in one, called lysine. Dals and beans are a little low in another, called methionine. Eaten over the same day, they fill each other's gaps. That is one reason dal-chawal, rajma-chawal, idli-sambar and chapati-chana are such good pairs.
- e.g. Idli is made from rice and urad dal ground together. So every idli has grain protein and dal protein, and they fill each other's gaps.
- e.g. A vegetarian family can get all the protein a child needs from dals, beans, milk, curd, paneer, nuts and grains.
- e.g. Your hair is mostly a protein called keratin. When you grow it longer, you are using protein from your food.
### Why the body needs fat · *defined*
**Definition:** Fat carries the most energy of any nutrient. It also builds the thin skin around every cell, called the cell membrane. And it helps the body take in vitamins A, D, E and K.

Fat is often talked about as if it were only a problem. But the body cannot live without it. Every one of your 30 trillion cells is wrapped in a thin skin made largely of fats. A large part of your brain is made of fat. Carrots and palak have beta-carotene, which your body turns into vitamin A. Beta-carotene and vitamin A dissolve in fat. That is why a little oil or ghee in the cooking helps you take in more of them. Fat in the body also stores energy and keeps you warm. Scientists do argue about which fats are best, and how much of each. You will meet that argument on another card.
- e.g. Peanuts, til, coconut, ghee and mustard oil all carry fat.
- e.g. Carrots cooked with a little oil give your body more beta-carotene, and so more vitamin A, than raw carrots with no fat at all.
- e.g. Fish from the sea, walnuts and flax seeds have a kind of fat called omega-3 that the brain uses.
### Vitamins and minerals: tiny amounts, big jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Vitamins and minerals are nutrients the body needs in very small amounts. Without them, many parts of the body slowly stop working well.

You need only a pinch of each vitamin and mineral, but you need them every week. Vitamin C, in amla, guava, lemon and oranges, helps make skin, gums and blood vessels strong. It also helps the body take in iron from plant foods. Iron, in dal, palak, bajra, rajma, eggs and meat, goes into the blood to carry oxygen. Calcium, in milk, curd, ragi, til and green leaves, makes bones hard. Iodine, in iodised salt and sea fish, helps the body grow and the brain develop. Vitamin D is made in your own skin when sunlight falls on it. Folate, in green leaves, dals and yeast, helps make new blood cells. Many of these vitamins and minerals were discovered when people fell ill without them.
- e.g. Squeeze lemon on your dal. The vitamin C helps you take in the dal's iron.
- e.g. Sailors on long voyages used to fall ill with scurvy. In 1747 a ship's doctor, James Lind, showed that oranges and lemons cured it. We now know the cure is vitamin C.
- e.g. Since 1962, India has worked to add iodine to salt. This has prevented a swelling of the neck called goitre in millions of people.
### Fibre and the trillions of microbes in your gut · *defined*
**Definition:** Fibre is the part of plant food that your body cannot digest. In the large intestine, trillions of gut microbes eat it. They make substances that help feed the cells of your gut.

Your large intestine is home to roughly as many bacteria as there are cells in your whole body. That is tens of trillions. There are hundreds of different kinds. They are living things with their own lives, not just helpers. They live in a warm, dark tube with food arriving several times a day. Their favourite food is the fibre you cannot break down, from dal husks, whole grains, vegetables and fruit. As they eat it, they make gas, which is why rajma and chana can make you fart. They also make small acids. One, called butyrate, is the main food of the cells lining your large intestine. So you feed the microbes with fibre. In return, they make acids that feed some of your own gut cells. People who eat many kinds of plants tend to have more kinds of gut microbes. Scientists are still finding out what that means for health.
- e.g. Rajma and chana can make a lot of gas. Your gut microbes make that gas as they eat the fibre.
- e.g. The Hadza people of Tanzania hunt and gather their food. Their gut microbes change with the seasons, as their food changes.
- e.g. Curd contains living microbes. Most of them pass through, but some may help while they are inside you.
### Water: the nutrient we forget · *defined*
**Definition:** Water is a nutrient. It makes up more than half of your body weight. Blood, spit, digestive juices and the inside of every cell are mostly water.

You lose water all day, in your breath, sweat and urine. So you need to drink it back, as plain water, milk, buttermilk, dal and the water in fruit and vegetables. When you do not drink enough, you feel tired and can get headaches. Children playing in Indian summers can lose a lot in sweat. A simple check is the colour of your urine. Pale yellow usually means you are drinking enough. Dark yellow means drink more. A person can live only a few days without water, but weeks without food.
- e.g. A glass of buttermilk on a hot day gives water, protein and calcium together.
- e.g. A watermelon is about nine-tenths water.
- e.g. After a cricket match in May, your shirt is wet with sweat. That water must be drunk back.
### A healthy diet and a less healthy diet · *debated*
Scientists agree on a lot. Diets with plenty of vegetables, fruits, pulses, whole grains and nuts are linked to good health in many countries. Some diets have lots of added sugar, salt and very processed packet foods, and few vegetables. Those diets are linked to more tooth decay, and over many years to heart illness. But scientists do not agree on everything. They argue about which fats are best, about eggs, and about why packet foods seem to make people eat more. And very different traditional diets have kept people healthy. No single food makes a diet healthy or unhealthy. What matters is the pattern over years.
- e.g. Eating sweets after every meal, every day, gives mouth bacteria a steady supply of sugar. They make acid that eats holes in teeth.
- e.g. A diet of the same few foods, day after day, can miss a vitamin even when the stomach is always full.
- e.g. A family that eats home-cooked dal, rice, poriyal and curd most days, with sweets on festivals, has a healthy pattern.
- **Most nutrition scientists, and the World Health Organization:** Eat mostly vegetables, fruits, whole grains, pulses and nuts. Keep added sugar under a tenth of your food energy, and less is better. Use less salt. This advice is based on studies of millions of people.
- **Carlos Monteiro and colleagues in Brazil, from 2009:** The problem is not one nutrient but how food is made. 'Ultra-processed' packet foods, made in factories from many added ingredients, may cause people to eat more than they need. In a careful 2019 test in the United States, people ate about 500 kcal more each day on such foods. Others say the label is too broad, because it puts very different foods together.
- **People studying traditional diets:** Healthy diets come in very different shapes. Some traditional diets have kept people well with lots of fish and fat, others with lots of rice and vegetables, and others with lots of milk. So we should be careful about one rule for everyone.
### Different bodies, different plates · *debated*
A balanced diet is not one fixed menu. A baby, a growing child, a pregnant mother, a farmer and an old grandparent need different amounts. Some bodies also digest foods differently. Most people in the world digest milk sugar less well once they grow up. Some get tummy aches from milk. In India, this is more common in the south and east than in the north-west. Some people cannot eat wheat, or peanuts. Families also choose foods because of their beliefs. Some eat no meat, Jain families eat no root vegetables, and some eat no beef or pork. All of these people can still eat a balanced diet. Scientists are now asking a newer question. Why does the same food raise one person's blood sugar a lot, and another's only a little?
- e.g. A Jain family eats no root vegetables like onion and potato. They can still balance their plate with dals, milk foods, grains, leafy greens and fruit.
- e.g. Someone who gets tummy aches from milk can have curd, which has less milk sugar, or ragi and til for calcium.
- e.g. A grandmother and a growing ten-year-old may eat the same dal, but need different amounts.
- **Most nutrition guidelines:** General rules work well for most people: a mix of food groups, enough protein for growth, lots of vegetables, and not too much added sugar or salt.
- **Eran Segal, Eran Elinav and colleagues, Israel, 2015:** They measured the blood sugar of 800 people after meals. The same food raised it very differently in different people. Their gut microbes seemed to be part of the reason. They think diets may one day be planned for each person. Other scientists say this is promising but not yet proven.

## Mostly agreed
- The body needs many different nutrients, and no single food has all of them in the right amounts. *How we know:* Scientists have measured the nutrients in thousands of foods. In India, C. Gopalan and his colleagues at the National Institute of Nutrition published these measurements for Indian foods from 1971, in a book called 'Nutritive Value of Indian Foods'.
- Food is broken into small building blocks, which pass into the blood and are carried to the cells. *How we know:* Doctors can measure glucose and amino acids rising in the blood after a meal. A simple finger-prick test shows blood sugar going up after eating rice.
- Missing one vitamin or mineral for a long time causes a particular illness, and eating it again cures it. *How we know:* Scurvy is cured by vitamin C, beriberi by vitamin B1, and one kind of anaemia by folate. These cures have been tested in careful trials since James Lind's test on sailors in 1747.
- Sugar eaten often helps mouth bacteria make acid that causes holes in teeth. *How we know:* Studies across many countries and many years show that people who eat sugar more often get more tooth decay. Bacteria in the mouth can be watched turning sugar into acid in the laboratory.
- Tens of trillions of microbes live in the human gut, mostly in the large intestine. They digest fibre that we cannot. *How we know:* Scientists count them by studying samples of poo and reading the microbes' genes. In 2016, Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo estimated about 38 trillion bacteria, roughly as many as the body's own cells.

## How we found out
**Christiaan Eijkman, Gerrit Grijns, Barend Jansen and Willem Donath, 1886 to 1926.** In the 1880s, many soldiers and prisoners in Java, then ruled by the Netherlands, fell ill with beriberi. Their legs grew weak and numb, and some died. Doctors were sure a germ caused the illness. In 1886 a Dutch doctor, Christiaan Eijkman, went to Java to find that germ. By luck, the chickens at his laboratory fell ill with the same kind of weakness. They were being fed leftover white rice from the hospital, with its outer layers polished off. Then a new manager stopped giving the hospital's rice to the chickens. They got cheaper unpolished rice instead, and they got better. Eijkman thought white rice carried a poison, and that the outer layer of the grain cured it. In 1901 his colleague Gerrit Grijns said the opposite. The outer layer held something the body needs, and white rice simply lacked it. Grijns was right. In 1926 two scientists in Java, Barend Jansen and Willem Donath, took the missing nutrient out of rice bran. Today we call it vitamin B1, or thiamine.  
*What people believed before:* Beriberi was caused by a germ, or by a poison in white rice. In fact it was caused by something missing from white rice: vitamin B1.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Some foods are 'good' and some foods are 'bad'.** (A very common way of talking about food, in homes, schools and adverts; *rejected*)  
  For: Some foods, like vegetables and dals, give the body a lot that it needs. Others, like sweets and fried snacks, mostly give quick energy, fat or salt.  
  Against: Nutrition scientists look at the whole diet, not one food. A laddoo at Deepavali does no harm in a balanced diet. And even 'good' foods can be too much: a diet of only spinach would miss protein and energy. Calling foods bad can also make children feel ashamed of what they eat, or of what their family can afford.
- **Fat in food is the main cause of heart disease, so eat as little fat as possible.** (The American scientist Ancel Keys, from the 1950s, and many food guides in the 1980s and 1990s; *debated*)  
  For: Some studies linked diets high in certain fats, like those in butter and meat, with more heart disease.  
  Against: The British scientist John Yudkin argued that sugar was a bigger problem, in his 1972 book 'Pure, White and Deadly'. He was mocked at the time. In 2016, researchers found papers showing that a sugar industry group had paid Harvard scientists in the 1960s to play down sugar's role. Today most scientists think the kind of fat matters more than the amount, and that added sugar also matters.
- **Vegetarians must eat dal and rice in the same meal to get complete protein.** (Frances Moore Lappé, in her famous 1971 book 'Diet for a Small Planet'; *rejected*)  
  For: Grains and pulses each lack enough of one amino acid. Together they make up the full set.  
  Against: Lappé herself said in the 1981 edition that strict combining at every meal was not needed. The body keeps a pool of amino acids, so eating grains and pulses over the same day is enough. She changed her mind when the evidence changed, which is what careful thinkers do.
- **Eating curd, or taking 'good bacteria' pills, makes you healthy and helps you live longer.** (The Russian scientist Ilya Mechnikov, in his 1907 book 'The Prolongation of Life', and many adverts today; *debated*)  
  For: Mechnikov noticed that some villagers in Bulgaria who drank soured milk lived long lives. Some trials show certain microbes can help with some kinds of diarrhoea.  
  Against: Most microbes in curd or pills pass through the gut and do not stay. The long-lived villagers may have been healthy for many other reasons. Many claims on packets go far beyond the evidence. Curd is a good food, but not a magic one.
- **The vitamins were discovered by a few famous men.** (Many history books, and the 1929 Nobel Prize, which went to Christiaan Eijkman and Frederick Gowland Hopkins; *rejected*)  
  For: Eijkman found that chickens fed white polished rice got a nerve illness that unpolished rice cured. Hopkins showed that rats needed tiny 'extra' food factors to grow.  
  Against: Gerrit Grijns, who worked with Eijkman, was the first to say clearly that a missing nutrient caused the illness, but he got no prize. Lucy Wills, working in Bombay from 1928, found a cure for an anaemia of pregnant women that led to the discovery of folate. Elsie Widdowson measured the nutrients in British foods from the 1930s. Kamala Sohonie, the first Indian woman to earn a PhD in science, later studied the food value of neera, a palm drink, for children. Discoveries are usually made by many people, and not all of them are remembered.
- **Breakfast is the most important meal of the day.** (A common saying, used a lot in cereal adverts since the early 1900s; *debated*)  
  For: Many children who eat breakfast pay attention better in the morning. School breakfast and lunch schemes help children who would otherwise be hungry.  
  Against: In studies of adults, skipping breakfast has not clearly made people less healthy. What matters most is the whole day's food. The saying was spread partly by companies selling breakfast cereal. For growing children, eating regular meals still matters.

## Roots
We have been learning what happens to food inside us, and why the body needs so many kinds. Now we ask what a balanced diet tells us about food, about ourselves and about the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Paper Window Test, the oil spot stayed on the paper while the water spot dried. In the tumbler of water, the drops of oil floated and would not mix. Because fat does not mix with water, your body uses it to build the thin skin around every cell. So some of the fat you eat becomes the skin of your cells.

## Story: The Brown Jar
*Lucy Wills (1888 to 1964) was a real British doctor and scientist. From 1928 she worked in Bombay, studying a dangerous anaemia in pregnant women who worked in the textile mills. She noticed that richer women rarely got it, so she suspected their food. She tested diets on animals in her laboratory, then found that Marmite, a cheap spread made from yeast left over from brewing, cured the women. She published this in the British Medical Journal in 1931. The missing nutrient, first called the 'Wills factor', was later named folic acid, or folate, in 1941, after it was found in spinach leaves. Kamal, her mother, the nurse, how Dr Wills looks, the conversations, the microscope scene and the methi tin are made up.*

In the winter of 1929, in the city of Bombay, my mother began to turn the colour of the moon.

My name is Kamal. I was ten. We lived in one room of a chawl in Parel, on the third floor, where the mill whistle woke us before the crows did. My mother, Sakhu, whom I called Aai, worked at a spinning machine in the cotton mill, twelve hours a day. There was a baby growing inside her. And every week she grew paler.

First it was her lips. Then it was the inside of her eyelids, which should be red like a guava and were white like a radish. On the stairs she stopped at every landing, holding the rail, breathing as if she had run all the way from the sea. 'It is nothing,' she said. 'It is the cotton dust.' Our neighbour said it was the heat. Another said it was the evil eye, and tied a black thread on Aai's wrist.

I carried her tiffin to the mill gate at noon. It was always the same. Rice, a little thin dal water and a slice of onion. On good days, a green chilli. She always made me eat first. 'I am not hungry,' she said, and she meant it. Her stomach was full. It was the rest of her that was empty, but none of us knew that yet.

Many women in our chawl were pale like her. Some of the pregnant ones went to hospital and did not come back. People said that is how it is for mill women. Nobody asked why.

Then one day a nurse came up our stairs. She said a lady doctor at the hospital was studying the pale sickness, and wanted to see Aai. My mother did not want to go. I made her go. I held her elbow all the way.

The doctor was called Dr Wills. She was tall and thin, with short grey hair and a plain dress, and she did not talk about the evil eye at all. She talked about food. Through the nurse, she asked my mother what she had eaten yesterday, and the day before, and the day before that. She wrote every answer in a black notebook. Rice. Dal water. Onion. Rice. Tea with sugar. Rice.

'Why does she care what we eat?' I whispered to the nurse. 'My mother is not hungry. She eats every day.'

The nurse asked the doctor. The doctor looked at me for a long moment, as if I had asked a good question.

'Enough food,' she said, and the nurse turned her words into Marathi, 'is not the same as the right food. The rich ladies on Malabar Hill hardly ever get this sickness. They eat rice too. But they also eat milk, vegetables, fruit, eggs and meat. Something is in their plates that is missing from your mother's. Something very small. I am trying to find out what.'

She told us she had fed animals in her laboratory the same food the mill women ate. They became pale too, with the same strange blood. Then she had tried adding different foods, one at a time, to see which one brought them back.

A week later, the nurse came up our stairs again. She was carrying a small brown jar.

Inside was a thick, dark, sticky paste that smelled like salt and burnt bread. It was called Marmite. It was made in England, the nurse said, from the yeast left at the bottom of the big vats where beer is brewed. 'A spoon a day,' she said, 'stirred into warm water or onto rice. The doctor says so.'

The women in our chawl laughed. 'Medicine from beer dregs!' said one. 'English chutney!' said another. Aai made a face like a cat that has tasted lemon. But I had seen the doctor's notebook, and the way she looked at me when I asked my question. So every morning I stirred the brown spoon into warm water, and I stood there until my mother drank it all.

After a week, she could climb two landings without stopping. After two weeks, the inside of her eyelids turned pink. After three, she was laughing at the neighbour's jokes again, and her lips were the colour of lips.

When we went back to the hospital, Dr Wills pricked Aai's finger and put a drop of her blood on a piece of glass. She looked at it through a brass microscope. Then, to my great surprise, she lifted me onto a stool and let me look too.

I saw a crowd of tiny pink circles, packed close like coins in a bag. 'Before,' the nurse explained, 'there were far fewer, and many were too big and strange. Her body could not make good new ones. Now it can.'

'What is in the jar?' I asked. 'What is the medicine?'

The doctor smiled, and the nurse said, 'She says she does not know yet. Something the body needs only a pinch of, but cannot do without. It is in yeast. She thinks it is in other foods too. Leaves, perhaps. Dals. Liver. She will keep looking.'

I thought about our village in the Konkan, before we came to the city. My grandmother used to send me to pick wild leaves after the first rain. Taakla, she called them. We ate them with bhakri. In Bombay, leaves cost money, and there was never enough money for leaves.

My sister was born in March. She was small and loud and perfectly pink, and Aai held her and did not turn the colour of the moon again.

That summer I found a broken kerosene tin and filled it with earth from the railway side. I begged a pinch of methi seeds from the vegetable woman and pushed them in with my finger. I watered them every morning before the mill whistle.

When the first leaves came, small and round and green, I picked a handful. I washed them at the tap, chopped them as fine as I could, and stirred them into my mother's dal.


## Another story: Sundari and the Sweet Rice Balls
**

My name is Sundari. I am an elephant, and I live at a big temple in Kumbakonam, a town of many temples near the Kaveri river.

Every morning, before the sun is hot, my keeper Selvi scrubs my back with a coconut husk and paints a white and red mark on my forehead. Then I walk to the temple gate and stand on the warm stone, and the people come.

They bring me gifts. Bananas, a stick of sugarcane, and best of all, sweet rice balls with jaggery. I reach out with the tip of my trunk, take each gift and pop it into my mouth. Then I touch each child's head gently with my trunk. The children squeal and laugh, and their mothers fold their hands.

I loved the sweet rice balls most. They were soft and warm, and the jaggery stuck to my tongue. One, then ten, then fifty. By evening I was so full that I did not want the grass Selvi brought me. I pushed the bundle away with my foot.

Months went by like that. I grew heavy. My feet ached on the hot stone. At night my tummy rumbled and grumbled, and I could not sleep. I was never hungry, but I was not happy either. Selvi stroked my ear and said nothing, but I could tell she was worried.

One day a young woman came to see me. She was Dr. Revathi, an animal doctor. She did not bring me a sweet. She sat on the temple steps with a notebook and watched me for the whole day. She counted the rice balls. She looked at my feet. She even looked at my dung, which I thought was rude.

In the evening she said to Selvi, 'Sundari is eating festival food every day. An elephant's body is made for plants: grass, leaves, branches and bark. In the forest, elephants walk and eat plants for most of the day and part of the night.'

'But she loves the sweets,' said Selvi.

'Everyone loves sweets,' said Dr. Revathi. 'That is why it is hard to stop.'

So one cool season, they took me on a long, bumpy ride in a truck to a camp in the forest by a river. I smelled the other elephants before I saw them. There were dozens of us, all temple elephants, all a little too heavy.

In the forest there were no rice balls. There were no bananas on plates. There was tall grass, and bamboo, and leafy branches piled high.

I sulked. I stood under a tree and did not eat. Selvi sat beside me and did not make me.

An old elephant named Kamakshi walked past. She wrapped her trunk around a clump of grass and pulled it up, roots and all. She knocked the soil off against her knee, thump, thump, and put the grass in her mouth. She chewed slowly, looking at nothing, and then she pulled up another clump.

By evening I was hungry. Not a little bit hungry, the way I felt at the temple when a child came late with a rice ball. This hunger was deep and loud. My tummy roared.

So I copied Kamakshi. I pulled up a clump of grass. I knocked off the soil, thump, thump. It was rough and it took a long time to chew. It was not sweet at all. I pulled up another clump, and another. I stripped the leaves off a branch with my trunk. I walked to the river and drank, and splashed water on my back, and came back for more.

That night I slept the whole night through.

The days went on like this. Walk, pull, thump, chew. Bathe. Walk, pull, thump, chew. I was eating almost all day, but I never felt stuffed. The ground was soft under my feet, and slowly the ache in them went away. My tummy was quiet. My body felt like my own again.

One afternoon Dr. Revathi came to the camp and found me by the river. She watched me for a while, the way she had on the temple steps. Then she smiled. 'Well, Sundari,' she said. 'Is grass as good as rice balls?'

I did not have words to answer her. I pulled up another clump, knocked it on my knee, the way Kamakshi does, and ate.

After many weeks I went home to the temple. Now, every morning, Selvi brings me a great heap of grass and palm leaves, and I eat it slowly while the bells ring. People still come to the gate.

Yesterday a small girl named Kavya came with her mother. She held up one banana in both hands, her arms stretched as high as they would go. I took it with the tip of my trunk, very gently, and put it in my mouth.

It was the sweetest thing I had tasted in weeks. It tasted like a festival.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Sundari's own problem comes first. An elephant's body needs a lot of plants, eaten slowly through the day, but the sweet rice balls filled her so fast that she never ate the grass her body needed. The turn comes when she feels real hunger in the forest and copies Kamakshi: she eats plain food all day and her body feels well again. At the end, one banana tastes like a festival, because a treat is special when it comes sometimes, not all day.

- Ask: Why did Sundari push away the grass at the temple?
- Ask: How was the hunger in the forest different from how Sundari felt at the temple?
- Ask: Why did Sundari feel better in the forest, even though she ate almost all day?
- Ask: Why did one banana taste like a festival at the end?
- Ask: What food is your 'festival food', the kind you have only sometimes?

### e1 · The Paper Window Test: which foods carry fat? (28 min)
- **problem:** Fat leaves a see-through spot on paper, like a little window. Water makes a see-through spot too, but the water dries away and the spot disappears. So a see-through spot that is still there after 15 minutes means that the food carries fat. In this test you rub foods on paper to find which ones carry fat.
- **question:** Which of our everyday foods carry fat, and which carry a lot?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which foods will leave a see-through spot on the paper? Which will leave nothing? Write your guess next to each food, and say why you think so.
- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only the food. Use the same paper, cut into squares of the same size. Use a piece of each food about the size of your thumbnail. Rub each one the same number of times, 10 rubs. Oil and water are tested too, so you can see what a fat spot and a water spot look like.
- **safety:** Skip the peanut if anyone in your family has a nut allergy. Wash your hands afterwards, and do not eat the pieces rubbed on paper.

  - Ask: Why did you guess that one? (good: Peanuts feel oily.; Chips are fried in oil.; Apple is juicy, not oily.)

### e2 · The Vanishing Blue: which fruit has the most vitamin C? (35 min)
- **problem:** Iodine turns starch dark blue. Vitamin C takes that blue colour away again. Add a juice to a dark blue liquid, one drop at a time. The number of drops needed to clear the blue shows how much vitamin C the juice has. The fewer drops it needs, the more vitamin C it has. In this test you compare five juices and plain water.
- **question:** Which juice has the most vitamin C: amla, guava, lemon, mosambi or a packet fruit drink?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Put the juices in order, from the most vitamin C to the least. Write your order down, and say why you think so.
- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only the juice. Each bowl gets the same amount of blue liquid, 2 tablespoons. Use the same dropper or straw for every juice, rinsed in between. Swirl the same way after each drop, and stop at the same point: when the blue has gone. Water is tested too, to show that just adding liquid does not clear the blue.
- **safety:** A grown-up handles the iodine. It stains skin and clothes, and it must never be swallowed. Nobody drinks anything from the test bowls. Pour them down the sink afterwards and wash the bowls.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then show each juice as a bar, as tall as the number of drops. Draw the bars, or line up one button for each drop. The shortest bar has the most vitamin C.
- **results table:** Juice | My guess (1 = most vitamin C) | Drops needed to clear the blue
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the fruits and the packet drink. 2. Ask your child to guess the order, from most vitamin C to least. 3. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Vitamin C helps mend cuts, keeps gums strong and helps your body take in iron. Which of these juices do you think has the most?  
  *Child mode:* Vitamin C helps your body mend cuts and take in iron. Which juice has the most? Guess the order.
  - Ask: Why did you guess that order? (good: Lemons are sour, so they must have a lot.; The packet says it has vitamins.; Amla is very sour.)
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In a jug, mix 1 cup of water with 2 tablespoons of rice water. 2. Add iodine drop by drop and stir. About 5 to 10 drops makes it the dark blue of fountain-pen ink. 3. Pour 2 tablespoons into each of the 6 bowls. 4. Put the bowls on a white paper.  
  *Parent says:* Iodine turns starch dark blue. Vitamin C undoes that and makes the blue disappear. The more vitamin C a juice has, the fewer drops it needs.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up mixes rice water and iodine. It turns dark blue. Pour the same amount into six bowls.
  - Note: The starch in rice water turns iodine blue-black. If the liquid is almost black, add more water until you can see through it a little. If it stays brown, add a little more rice water. A very dark start makes every juice need many more drops.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add amla juice to the first bowl, one drop at a time. 2. Swirl after every drop and count. 3. Stop when the blue has gone, and write the number. 4. Rinse the dropper. 5. Do the same for each juice, and for water.  
  *Parent says:* Add one drop of amla juice to the first bowl and swirl it. Keep adding drops, one at a time. Stop the moment the blue is gone.  
  *Child mode:* Count the drops out loud. Stop when the blue is gone. Then say the number.
  - Note: Amla usually clears the blue in just a few drops. Guava is usually next. Lemon and mosambi need many more drops, often about the same number. Packet drinks often need many drops. If vitamin C has been added to the drink, it will need fewer, and that is a real result too. Read the packet to check. Water never clears the blue. Stop any juice at 40 drops and write '40 or more'. Orange-coloured juices tint the liquid, so look for the blue going, not for the liquid turning clear. Amla also has other substances, called tannins, that clear the blue too. So the test measures vitamin C together with some similar substances, not vitamin C alone.
  - Check: Amla cleared the blue in 3 drops. Lemon needed 18 drops. Which has more vitamin C? → **Amla**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think amla cleared the blue so fast?  
  *Child mode:* Now say three things: what you think, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: amla has the most vitamin C, and the packet drink had very little. What I saw: amla cleared the blue in 3 drops, lemon in 18, and the packet drink was still blue at 40. Why: vitamin C reacts with the iodine and takes away its blue. So the fewer drops, the more vitamin C. Extra: vitamin C is also lost when food is boiled a long time or cut and left open. If you are curious, test lemon juice that was squeezed and left out overnight. The change may be small.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about how vitamin C helps the body take in iron. Squeeze lemon on a bowl of dal at the next meal, or eat a guava with your lunch.  
  *Parent says:* Vitamin C has another job. It helps your body take in the iron from dal, palak and bajra. So the old habit of squeezing lemon on dal also helps your body.  
  *Child mode:* Vitamin C helps your body take iron from dal. Squeeze lemon on your dal at dinner.
  - Think: Old recipes put lemon on dal and amla in chutney, long before anyone knew about vitamins. Why do you think these habits lasted?
### e3 · Balloon Bellies: what do microbes like to eat? (61 min)
- **problem:** Yeast is a tiny living fungus. When yeast eats, they give off a gas, and the gas fills the balloon. Your gut microbes also eat and make gas, so yeast in a bottle is a small model of them. In this test you feed yeast sugar, atta or nothing. You measure the balloons to see what the yeast likes to eat.
- **question:** Does yeast, a tiny living fungus, make more gas when fed sugar, atta or nothing at all?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which balloon will grow biggest: the sugar bottle, the atta bottle or the plain water bottle? Will any stay flat? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only the food. Every bottle gets the same amount of yeast (2 teaspoons) and the same amount of warm water (150 millilitres). All the bottles sit in the same place for the same time. The plain water bottle has no food, so you can see what happens without any.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then show each bottle as a bar, as tall as the distance round its balloon after 45 minutes. Draw the bars, or lay the three strings side by side.
- **results table:** Bottle | Distance round the balloon after 15 minutes (cm) | After 30 minutes (cm) | After 45 minutes (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the yeast. 2. Explain that each grain holds a great many tiny living fungi, resting until they get water, warmth and food. 3. Ask them to guess which food the yeast will like best.  
  *Parent says:* Yeast is a living thing, a tiny fungus. Each grain holds a great many of them, resting. When they eat, they make a gas. Your gut microbes do the same thing with the fibre you cannot digest. Which food will the yeast like best?  
  *Child mode:* Yeast is alive. Each grain holds a great many tiny fungi, resting. When they eat, they make gas. Which food will they like best?
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the bottles Sugar, Atta and Water. 2. Using a funnel, put 2 teaspoons of yeast in each. 3. Add 1 tablespoon of sugar to the first and 1 tablespoon of atta to the second. Add nothing to the third. 4. Pour 150 millilitres of warm water into each. 5. Swirl gently. 6. Stretch a balloon over each mouth.  
  *Parent says:* Same yeast, same warm water, same bottles. Only the food is different. That is what makes it a fair test.  
  *Child mode:* Put yeast in all three bottles. Add sugar to one and atta to one. Add warm water. Put on the balloons.
  - Note: Test the water on the inside of your wrist. It should feel warm but not hot. Hot water kills yeast, and cold water keeps them resting. Stretch each balloon a few times first so it fills more easily.
- **s3 (45 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the bottles in a warm spot, away from the fan. 2. Every 15 minutes, wrap a string round the fattest part of each balloon, then measure the string on a ruler. 3. Write the numbers. 4. In between, play Cell Delivery or read the story.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure each balloon with the string. Which one is growing fastest?  
  *Child mode:* Every 15 minutes, measure each balloon with a string. Write down the numbers.
  - Note: The sugar balloon usually starts to stand up within 15 to 20 minutes, and fills more by 45. The atta balloon grows slowly, because enzymes in the flour must first cut its starch into sugar for the yeast. The water balloon stays almost flat. If the balloons are still small at 45 minutes, keep going to 60 minutes. If nothing at all has happened after 20 minutes, the water was too hot or too cold, or the yeast was old. Test the yeast: a spoonful in warm sugar water should froth within 10 minutes. In cold months, stand the bottles in a bowl of warm water.
  - Check: The sugar balloon grew big. The water balloon stayed flat. What does that show? → **Yeast cells make gas only when they have food to eat**
  - Wait: Check every 15 minutes, three times.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why. Then talk about how the yeast is like, and unlike, gut microbes.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why? Now, your gut microbes are not yeast. They are mostly bacteria. But they also eat and make gas. What do they eat?  
  *Child mode:* Now say three things: what you think, what you saw, and why. Then say what your gut microbes eat.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: yeast makes gas when they eat, and they like sugar best. What I saw: the sugar balloon was 28 centimetres round after 45 minutes, atta 13 and water about 1. Why: the yeast ate the food and gave off carbon dioxide gas. Our gut microbes eat fibre from dal, poriyal and fruit. They also make gas, and acids that feed our gut cells. A model is never exactly the real thing. Yeast is a fungus, and most gut microbes are bacteria.

### My Balanced Plate (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A woman eats rice and thin dal every day and always feels full. Yet she grows pale and ill. Why?  
   Options: Enough food is not the same as the right food. Her meals are missing something her body needs. / She ate too much rice / Feeling full always means the body is well fed  
   **Answer:** Enough food is not the same as the right food. Her meals are missing something her body needs.. *Why:* Her stomach is full, but her meals have almost no vegetables, fruit, milk or eggs. So her body is short of a vitamin it needs only a pinch of. A full stomach and a balanced diet are not the same thing.
2. (write) Many people use the word 'diet' to mean eating less. What does a balanced diet really mean? Give an example from your own home.  
   **Answer:** A balanced diet means eating many kinds of food, in about the right amounts, over a day or a week. It does not mean eating less. For example, in my home we eat idli with sambar in the morning, rice, dal and poriyal at lunch, and fruit in the evening.. *Why:* A strong answer says 'many kinds' and 'over a day or a week', and gives a real example from the child's own meals.
3. (sort) Sort these foods by their main job in your body.  
   **Answer:** ragi mudde → Go (energy); idli → Go (energy); moong dal → Grow (body-building); boiled egg → Grow (body-building); keerai → Glow (protecting); papaya → Glow (protecting). *Why:* Ragi and idli are mostly starch, which gives energy. Dal and egg give protein to build the body. Keerai, which is spinach and other greens, and papaya give vitamins, minerals and fibre. Many foods do more than one job, so this is each food's main job.
4. (mcq) Here is an idea: "Potato is a vegetable. So a plate of rice and potato curry is half vegetables." What is the mistake in this thinking?  
   Options: Potato is mostly starch, so in the body it acts more like rice than like greens / Potato is not a plant / There is no mistake  
   **Answer:** Potato is mostly starch, so in the body it acts more like rice than like greens. *Why:* Potato grows in the soil like a vegetable, but it is mostly starch, which gives energy. So that plate is nearly all energy food. Greens, beans, carrots or gourds would fill the vegetable half.
5. (fill) Food is broken into tiny pieces that are small enough to pass into the ____, which carries them to every cell.  
   **Answer:** ['blood', 'bloodstream']. *Why:* Digestion breaks food into tiny building blocks. These pass through the wall of the gut into the blood. The blood carries them to all your cells.
6. (truefalse) Eating one plate of jalebis at a wedding makes your whole diet unhealthy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A diet is what you usually eat over many days. Sweets on special days are part of a balanced diet. What matters is the everyday pattern.
7. (mcq) Why is it better to eat vegetables of many colours, not the same one every day?  
   Options: Different vegetables carry different vitamins and minerals, so a mix gives the body more of what it needs / Bright colours make food taste better / One vegetable has every vitamin, so the colour does not matter  
   **Answer:** Different vegetables carry different vitamins and minerals, so a mix gives the body more of what it needs. *Why:* Orange carrots and pumpkin give beta-carotene, which the body turns into vitamin A. Dark green keerai gives iron and folate. Tomatoes and amla give vitamin C. No one vegetable has everything, so a mix fills more gaps.
8. (mcq) In the Paper Window Test, which food left a see-through spot that stayed?  
   Options: Peanut / Apple / Water  
   **Answer:** Peanut. *Why:* Peanuts carry fat, which leaves a see-through spot that does not dry. Apple has almost no fat, and water dries away. If you picked water, you may have looked at the paper before the water had time to dry.
9. (write) In the Paper Window Test, two friends rub plain chapati on paper. One friend's chapati leaves a see-through spot that stays. The other friend's leaves none. What could explain the difference?  
   **Answer:** The first chapati may have had ghee or oil on it. Fat leaves a see-through spot that stays, so the spot shows fat. The second chapati was plain.. *Why:* A strong answer says the first chapati carried some fat, such as ghee, and connects a spot that stays with fat.
10. (match) Match each nutrient to one of its jobs.  
   **Answer:** Protein → builds muscles, hair and skin; Calcium → makes bones and teeth hard; Iron → helps the blood carry oxygen; Vitamin C → helps mend cuts and take in iron. *Why:* Each nutrient has its own jobs. Protein builds the body. Calcium hardens bones. Iron carries oxygen in the blood. Vitamin C helps mend skin and take in iron from plant foods.
11. (order) Put the journey of a spoon of dal in order.  
   **Answer:** ['mouth', 'stomach', 'small intestine', 'blood', 'muscle cell']. *Why:* The dal is chewed in the mouth. Acid and enzymes break it down in the stomach. In the small intestine it is cut into tiny pieces called amino acids. These pass into the blood. A muscle cell joins them to make new muscle protein.
12. (write) Suppose you ate only dosa, every meal, every day. Dosa gives energy and some protein. What would your body be short of, and why?  
   **Answer:** My body would be short of vitamins like vitamin C, minerals like calcium and iron, and fibre, because dosa has very little of these. No single food has every nutrient. So I would need vegetables, fruit, curd or milk too.. *Why:* A strong answer names at least two missing nutrients and gives the main idea: no single food has everything.
13. (draw) Draw a balanced thali. Show the half for vegetables and fruit, and the two quarters.  
   **Answer:** A plate with half vegetables and fruit, a quarter grains, a quarter protein foods like dal or curd, and a glass of water.. *Why:* Drawing the plate in parts helps you remember the amounts: half vegetables and fruit, a quarter each for grains and protein.
14. (write) Write a balanced dinner using food your family often cooks. Label each food go, grow or glow.  
   **Answer:** Any meal with all three kinds. For example: rice (go), sambar with toor dal (grow), beans poriyal (glow) and a glass of water.. *Why:* A balanced meal has at least one food from each group, with water. Using your own family's food shows you can do it at home.
15. (truefalse) Water has no energy in it, so it is not a nutrient.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A nutrient is anything the body needs from food and drink, not only things with energy. Water makes up more than half of your body. Blood, spit and the inside of every cell are mostly water.

1. (mcq) What is a balanced diet?  
   Options: Many kinds of food, in about the right amounts, over a day or week / Eating less / Only eating vegetables  
   **Answer:** Many kinds of food, in about the right amounts, over a day or week. *Why:* A balanced diet mixes many kinds of food so the body gets all it needs. It does not mean eating less or only one kind. Picking 'eating less' mixes up 'diet' with going on a diet.
2. (mcq) Which group is dal, egg and paneer in?  
   Options: Go (energy) / Grow (body-building) / Glow (protecting)  
   **Answer:** Grow (body-building). *Why:* Dal, eggs and paneer are full of protein, which builds and mends the body. Picking glow might mean thinking all healthy foods are protecting foods.
3. (mcq) Moong dal gives protein, iron and fibre. What does this show?  
   Options: Many foods do more than one job / Dal is a glow food / Iron is a kind of protein  
   **Answer:** Many foods do more than one job. *Why:* Most foods give several nutrients. The food groups are a helpful map, not strict boxes. Picking 'iron is a kind of protein' mixes up a mineral and a nutrient that builds the body.
4. (mcq) On a balanced plate, about how much is vegetables and fruit?  
   Options: A quarter / Half / None  
   **Answer:** Half. *Why:* About half. Vegetables and fruit give vitamins, minerals and fibre, but little energy, so we need a lot of them. Picking a quarter is close to what many plates have, but the advice is half.
5. (mcq) Why must food be digested before the body can use it?  
   Options: Only tiny pieces can pass through the gut wall into the blood / Food is dirty / The stomach likes acid  
   **Answer:** Only tiny pieces can pass through the gut wall into the blood. *Why:* Big chains like starch and protein are cut into glucose and amino acids. Only these tiny pieces pass into the blood. Picking 'food is dirty' confuses digestion with cleaning food.
6. (mcq) What do your cells do with glucose?  
   Options: Burn it with oxygen for energy / Store it in the bones / Turn it into water only  
   **Answer:** Burn it with oxygen for energy. *Why:* Mitochondria join glucose with oxygen and release energy. Carbon dioxide and water are left over. Picking 'store it in the bones' mixes glucose up with calcium.
7. (mcq) What does iron do in the body?  
   Options: Helps the blood carry oxygen / Makes bones hard / Makes food taste sweet  
   **Answer:** Helps the blood carry oxygen. *Why:* Iron is part of the red part of blood that carries oxygen. Calcium makes bones hard. Picking 'makes bones hard' is a common mix-up between the two minerals.
8. (mcq) Why does the body need some fat?  
   Options: It builds the thin skin around every cell, and helps us take in some vitamins / It is never needed / It makes bones grow  
   **Answer:** It builds the thin skin around every cell, and helps us take in some vitamins. *Why:* Every cell is wrapped in a thin skin, the cell membrane, made largely of fats. Fat also carries vitamins A, D, E and K. Picking 'it is never needed' is a common idea, but the body cannot live without some fat.
9. (mcq) In the Vanishing Blue test, which result means the most vitamin C?  
   Options: The juice needing the fewest drops / The juice needing the most drops / The juice with the brightest colour  
   **Answer:** The juice needing the fewest drops. *Why:* Vitamin C takes away the blue. If a few drops are enough, each drop holds a lot of vitamin C. Picking 'most drops' reads the test backwards.
10. (mcq) In Balloon Bellies, every bottle had the same yeast and warm water. Only the food was different. Why?  
   Options: So we know the food caused the difference / To use up the yeast / Because balloons need water  
   **Answer:** So we know the food caused the difference. *Why:* In a fair test, you change only one thing. Then any difference must come from that one thing. Picking 'to use up the yeast' misses the reason for keeping things the same.
11. (mcq) What do gut microbes mostly eat?  
   Options: Fibre that we cannot digest / Only sugar / Our blood  
   **Answer:** Fibre that we cannot digest. *Why:* Fibre passes through to the large intestine undigested, and gut microbes feast on it. They make acids that feed our gut cells in return. Picking 'only sugar' fits the yeast in a bottle. But our own body takes up most sugar before it reaches the gut microbes.
12. (mcq) Suppose you eat white bread and no vegetables or fruit for a whole week. What happens to your gut microbes?  
   Options: Many of them go hungry, because they get very little fibre / They grow more, because bread has sugar / Nothing. Gut microbes do not need food  
   **Answer:** Many of them go hungry, because they get very little fibre. *Why:* Gut microbes live mostly on fibre, which comes from vegetables, fruit, dals and whole grains. White bread has little fibre, so many microbes go hungry. Our own body takes up the sugar from bread before it reaches them.
13. (mcq) Your friend says 'sweets are bad food'. What does science say?  
   Options: No single food is good or bad. The whole diet over many days matters. / Sweets are poison / Sweets are the best food  
   **Answer:** No single food is good or bad. The whole diet over many days matters.. *Why:* Scientists look at the overall pattern. Sweets give quick energy and are fine sometimes. Lots of added sugar every day is linked to tooth decay and other problems. Picking 'sweets are poison' goes far beyond the evidence.

## Applications
- School midday meals (PM POSHAN), planned to give children energy, protein and vitamins every school day.
- Anganwadi centres, which give hot meals and take-home rations to young children and pregnant mothers.
- Iodised salt, which has prevented goitre in millions of Indians.
- Fortified foods, such as rice or flour with added iron, folic acid and vitamin B12, and milk or oil with added vitamins A and D.
- Folic acid tablets given to pregnant mothers, which come from Lucy Wills's discovery in Bombay.
- Hospital diets planned by dietitians for people who are ill, recovering or have diabetes.
- Food for astronauts, soldiers at high altitude and mountaineers, planned to give a lot of energy in a light pack.
- Food labels with a Nutrition Information table, which most food packets in India must carry.
- Oral rehydration solution (ORS), a simple mix of water, salt and sugar that saves many children with diarrhoea.
- Plans for feeding cattle, poultry and pets, which use the same ideas of balanced nutrients.
- Sports nutrition for athletes, who need more energy and protein.
- Studying gut microbes to help treat some illnesses of the gut.

## Open questions
- **Is there one best diet for everyone?** Very different traditional diets have kept people healthy. Scientists still argue about whether general rules or personal plans are best.
- **How much do gut microbes affect our mood, thinking and illnesses?** Mice with no gut microbes behave differently, and some human studies show links. But in people, cause and effect are hard to untangle, and many claims go beyond the evidence.
- **Which fats are best for the heart, and how much of them?** Studies disagree, partly because people eat many foods together and it is hard to test one fat for many years.
- **Why do 'ultra-processed' foods seem to make people eat more?** One careful 2019 test showed people ate more on such foods, but nobody yet knows exactly why: speed of eating, softness, taste or something else.
- **Are there nutrients in food that we have not discovered yet?** The last vitamin was found in 1948. But plants contain thousands of other substances, and scientists are still finding out what some of them do in the body.
- **How does the body know when it has had enough of a particular nutrient?** Animals seem to seek out protein and salt when they are low. In people, how hunger for particular foods works is only partly understood.

## Connected maths
- A juice box says 'Sugars: 12 grams per 100 millilitres'. The box holds 200 millilitres. A level teaspoon of sugar weighs about 4 grams. How many teaspoons of sugar are in the whole box? **6 teaspoons.**. The box is 200 millilitres, which is 2 lots of 100. So the sugar is 12 + 12 = 24 grams. Each teaspoon is 4 grams. 24 divided by 4 is 6. So there are 6 teaspoons of sugar in the box. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Your thali has 8 equal spaces. About half the plate should be vegetables and fruit. A quarter should be grains. A quarter should be dal, curd or eggs. How many spaces does each get? **Vegetables and fruit 4 spaces, grains 2 spaces, dal, curd or eggs 2 spaces.**. Half of 8 is 8 divided by 2, which is 4. A quarter of 8 is 8 divided by 4, which is 2. Check: 4 + 2 + 2 = 8. All the spaces are used. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- In the 30-Plant Week, one leaf stands for 2 new plants. On Monday you found 10 new plants, on Tuesday 6, on Wednesday 5 and on Thursday 3. How many leaves do you draw for each day? How many plants is that so far? **Monday 5 leaves, Tuesday 3 leaves, Wednesday 2 and a half leaves, Thursday 1 and a half leaves. That is 24 plants so far.**. Divide each day's plants by 2. 10 divided by 2 is 5. 6 divided by 2 is 3. 5 divided by 2 is 2 with 1 left over, so 2 and a half leaves. 3 divided by 2 is 1 with 1 left over, so 1 and a half leaves. Total plants: 10 + 6 + 5 + 3 = 24. (queues `math.data.pictographs.L3`)
- In the Vanishing Blue test, amla cleared the blue in 3 drops. Lemon needed 18 drops. About how many times as much vitamin C is in a drop of amla as in a drop of lemon? **About 6 times as much.**. The same amount of blue needs the same amount of vitamin C. Amla did it in 3 drops and lemon in 18. 18 divided by 3 is 6. So one drop of amla carries about as much vitamin C as 6 drops of lemon. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
- In Balloon Bellies, the sugar balloon was 8 cm round at 15 minutes, 17 cm at 30 minutes and 28 cm at 45 minutes. How much did it grow in each 15 minutes? Was it growing faster at the start or at the end? **It grew 8 cm, then 9 cm, then 11 cm. It grew fastest at the end.**. From 0 to 8 is 8 cm. From 8 to 17 is 17 − 8 = 9 cm. From 17 to 28 is 28 − 17 = 11 cm. The growth got bigger each time, so it was fastest at the end. Dry yeast takes a while to wake up in warm water. As more of the yeast starts eating, the gas comes faster. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)

## Warm-up
- Name one go food, one grow food and one glow food. → For example: rice (go), dal (grow), palak (glow).
- On a balanced plate, how much is vegetables and fruit? → About half.
- What is starch broken into inside you? → Glucose, a simple sugar.
- What are proteins broken into? → Amino acids.
- Which mineral helps the blood carry oxygen? → Iron.
- In the Vanishing Blue test, which fruit juice cleared the blue in the fewest drops? → Usually amla.
- What do your gut microbes eat? → Fibre, which you cannot digest yourself.
- Why squeeze lemon on dal? → Its vitamin C helps your body take in the dal's iron.
- When your body burns food for energy, where does most of that food go? → Out in your breath, as carbon dioxide.
- Is any single food good or bad on its own? → No. What matters is the whole diet over many days.
