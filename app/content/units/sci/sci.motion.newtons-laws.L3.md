# Inertia: why moving things keep moving and still things stay still
`sci.motion.newtons-laws.L3`

**Summary.** Walk across a room and drop a pair of rolled socks over a plate. They land past the plate, not on it. The socks were moving forward with you, and they kept moving forward as they fell. Every thing keeps doing what it is doing, staying still or moving in a straight line at the same speed, until a force changes it. Scientists call this inertia, and Isaac Newton made it his first law of motion in 1687. On Earth, friction and the air slowly stop moving things, so a ball on a rough floor soon stops. On smooth ice it slides much further, and in empty space, with nothing to slow it, it would never stop. That is why you need a seatbelt, why a coin can drop into a tumbler when a card is flicked away, and why you never feel the Earth carrying you round at great speed.

**Intro.** *Try this. Put a card on top of a steel tumbler and a coin on the card. Pull the card away slowly. Now set it up again and flick the card away hard and fast with your finger. Where did the coin go each time? Nothing pushed the coin down into the tumbler. So why did it end up inside?* The coin was sitting still, and it went on sitting still while the card shot out from under it. Then gravity pulled it straight down into the tumbler. This is inertia: things keep doing what they are already doing until a force changes it. In this unit you test it with your own body, with socks, eggs, water and marbles. You find out why a rolling ball on Earth always stops, why a ball on ice slides so far, and why a spacecraft launched in 1977 is still travelling today.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. A force can start a thing moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down or turn it. *e.g.* Your kick is a force that starts a football moving.
- **Motion**: Moving from one place to another. A thing in motion is moving. *e.g.* An auto driving down the road is in motion.
- **At rest**: Not moving at all, compared with the things around it. *e.g.* A tumbler standing on the table is at rest.
- **Inertia**: The way every thing keeps doing what it is doing. A still thing stays still, and a moving thing keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line, until a force changes it. *e.g.* When a bus brakes suddenly, the passengers lurch forward, because their bodies keep moving.
- **Newton's first law of motion**: Isaac Newton's rule, from 1687: a thing stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at the same speed, unless a force acts on it. *e.g.* A puck on an air hockey table glides a long way after one tap, because almost nothing slows it.
- **Law of science**: A short rule that says what always happens, tested many times by many people. A law says what happens, not always why. *e.g.* Newton's first law says what moving things do. It does not say why they do it.
- **Friction**: The grip between two surfaces that touch. It works against sliding and rolling, and slows moving things down. *e.g.* A marble rolling on a towel stops quickly, because the towel gives a lot of friction.
- **Air resistance**: The push of the air on a thing moving through it. It slows the thing down. *e.g.* A flat sheet of paper drifts down slowly, because air resistance pushes up on it.
- **Mass**: How much matter a thing has. More mass means more inertia: the thing is harder to start, stop or turn. *e.g.* A full gas cylinder is much harder to push into motion than an empty one.
- **Speed**: How far a thing moves in a certain time. *e.g.* A cycle at 10 kilometres an hour goes 10 kilometres in one hour.
- **Direction**: The way a thing is moving, such as forward, left or up. *e.g.* A cricket ball hit straight changes direction when a fielder's hands stop it.
- **Seatbelt**: A strong strap that holds a passenger in the seat. It gives the force that stops the passenger when the car stops suddenly. *e.g.* Everyone in a car, front and back, should wear a seatbelt.

## Explanations
### Inertia: things keep doing what they are doing · *defined*
**Definition:** Every thing keeps doing what it is already doing. If it is still, it stays still. If it is moving, it keeps moving at the same speed in the same straight line. Only a force can change this. This is called inertia.

Isaac Newton wrote this down in 1687 as his first law of motion. It sounds simple, but it surprises most people. We see moving things stop all the time, so we think stopping happens by itself. It does not. Something always stops them: friction, the air, a wall or a hand. In the walking drop, the socks kept moving forward after you let go, because nothing pushed them back. They landed past the plate.
- e.g. A coin on a card stays still when the card is flicked away fast, and drops into the tumbler.
- e.g. Rolled socks dropped while you walk land ahead of where you let go.
- e.g. When an auto brakes, the passengers lurch forward.
### A still thing stays still · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing at rest stays at rest until a force moves it. A force also needs time to get it moving.

When you pull the card slowly, friction between the card and the coin acts for a long time, and the coin moves with the card. When you flick the card fast, friction acts for only a tiny moment. That is far too short to get the coin moving. So the coin stays where it was, with nothing under it, and gravity pulls it down into the tumbler. A heavier coin, with more mass, stays behind even better. The same idea explains the carrom tower: hit the bottom coin hard and fast, and only that coin shoots out.
- e.g. Flick the card, and the coin drops into the tumbler.
- e.g. Strike the bottom coin of a tower of carrom coins, and the rest drop straight down.
- e.g. Pull a newspaper fast from under a plastic tumbler, and the tumbler hardly moves.
### A moving thing keeps moving · *defined*
**Definition:** A moving thing keeps moving at the same speed, in a straight line, until a force speeds it up, slows it down or turns it.

Nothing has to keep pushing a moving thing to keep it going. A push is needed only to change its motion. The Voyager 1 spacecraft was launched in 1977. Its rockets stopped firing long ago, yet it is still moving away from the Sun at about 17 kilometres every second. There is almost nothing in space to slow it down. Inside a raw egg, the runny inside keeps turning after you stop the shell, and it sets the shell turning again.
- e.g. Voyager 1 is still travelling out of the solar system, almost 50 years after launch, with no engine pushing it forward.
- e.g. A raw egg starts spinning again after you stop it for a moment.
- e.g. Water stirred in a steel bowl keeps turning after you take the spoon out.
### Why things on Earth stop: friction and air resistance · *defined*
**Definition:** On Earth, moving things slow down and stop because forces act on them: friction from the surface they touch, and air resistance from the air.

Roll a marble from the same ramp onto a towel, a doormat and a bare floor. On the towel it stops after a short way, because the towel gives a lot of friction. On the smooth floor it rolls much further. Ice is smoother and more slippery still, so a ball or a stone slides a very long way on ice. The less friction and air resistance, the further a thing goes. With none at all, it would never stop. This is the 'ball on ice' that the first law is often explained with.
- e.g. A marble stops quickly on a towel but rolls across a tiled floor.
- e.g. A stone slid across a frozen lake glides a long way.
- e.g. An air hockey puck floats on a thin layer of air, so it glides far after one tap.
### Turning needs a force too · *defined*
**Definition:** A moving thing goes in a straight line unless a force turns it. Changing direction is a change of motion, so it needs a force.

Swing a rolled sock on a string in a circle, and the string pulls the sock towards your hand all the time. That pull keeps turning it. Let go of the string, and the sock flies off in a straight line, the way it was going at that moment. It does not keep curving. When a bus turns left, your body keeps going straight, so you lean against the side of the bus. The bus turns, and you are carried round only when the seat or the bar pushes on you.
- e.g. When a bus turns a corner, passengers lean towards the outside of the turn.
- e.g. A sock swung on a string flies off in a straight line when you let go.
- e.g. Water flies off a wet umbrella in straight lines when you spin it.
### More mass, more inertia · *defined*
**Definition:** The more mass a thing has, the more inertia it has. It needs a bigger force to start it, stop it or turn it.

A loaded lorry needs a much longer distance to stop than a cycle going at the same speed. Its brakes must take away the motion of all that mass. A heavy coin or a stack of coins stays behind better in the card trick than a light one. In the raw egg, the runny inside has mass, so it keeps turning when the shell stops. Mass is the amount of matter in a thing, and inertia grows with it.
- e.g. An empty steel almirah is easier to push into motion than a full one.
- e.g. A loaded lorry needs a longer road to stop than a car.
- e.g. Two stacked ten-rupee coins drop into the tumbler more surely than one small light coin.
### A smoothly moving room feels still · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside a train, a ship or a lift that moves smoothly in a straight line at a steady speed, everything behaves just as it does at rest. A ball tossed straight up falls back into your hand.

The ball is moving forward with the train before you toss it. While it is in the air, it keeps that forward motion, so it keeps up with you and your hand. You can feel the train only when it speeds up, slows down or turns. The Earth carries you too. In Chennai, the spinning Earth carries you east at about 1,600 kilometres an hour, and the Earth carries all of us round the Sun at about 30 kilometres every second. You do not feel either, because the motion is smooth and everything around you moves with you. Around 499 CE, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote that the stars seem to move west only because the Earth turns. He compared it to a person in a moving boat, who sees the trees on the bank moving backwards.
- e.g. In a smoothly moving train, a ball tossed straight up lands back in your hand.
- e.g. In a lift moving at a steady speed, you feel nothing. You feel a push only when it starts and stops.
- e.g. You cannot feel the Earth spin, though everywhere in India it carries you east at more than 1,300 kilometres an hour.
### Why passengers need seatbelts · *defined*
**Definition:** When a car, bus or auto stops suddenly, the passengers keep moving forward at the old speed until a force stops them. A seatbelt gives that force safely.

Put a small atta-dough person on a toy car and roll it into a book. The car stops at the book, but the dough person keeps going and flies off the front. With a rubber band across the dough person, they stay on. A real seatbelt works the same way. It holds you to the seat, so you stop with the car, gently, instead of hitting the seat in front or the windscreen. A two-wheeler rider has no seatbelt, so a helmet protects the head if they are thrown off.
- e.g. A dough person flies off a toy car that hits a book, unless they are tied on.
- e.g. A school bag on the back seat slides forward when the car brakes hard.
- e.g. Water in a tumbler slops forward when a bus brakes.
### Newton's three laws of motion · *defined*
**Definition:** Newton wrote three laws of motion in his book Principia in 1687. The first is inertia. The second says a bigger force changes motion more, and more mass needs more force. The third says forces come in pairs: when you push something, it pushes back on you just as hard.

This unit is mostly about the first law, because the other two build on it. You can feel the second law when you push an empty trolley and a full one with the same push: the empty one speeds up much more. You can feel the third law when you push a wall: the wall pushes you back. Newton built on the work of many others, including Galileo Galilei in Italy and Pierre Gassendi and René Descartes in France.
- e.g. First law: a coin stays still when a card is flicked from under it.
- e.g. Second law: the same push speeds up an empty trolley more than a full one.
- e.g. Third law: a balloon pushes air out of the back, and the air pushes the balloon forward.
### Why do things have inertia at all? · *debated*
Newton's law says what things do, but not why. Nobody knows for sure why mass resists being started, stopped or turned. Scientists have measured inertia very exactly for hundreds of years, and the law has passed every test on Earth and in space. But the reason underneath is still argued about.
- e.g. Mass and inertia always go together: a thing with twice the mass has twice the inertia.
- e.g. Spacecraft are steered using Newton's laws, and they arrive where expected.
- **Isaac Newton:** Newton thought there is one fixed, unmoving space, and inertia is how matter resists changing its motion through that space.
- **Ernst Mach, an Austrian scientist, in the 1880s:** Mach suggested that a thing's inertia might come from all the other matter in the universe, the far-away stars and galaxies. Nobody has shown whether this is true.
- **Many physicists today:** Most of the mass of the things around you comes from the energy holding the tiny parts inside atoms together. So inertia may be a property of energy itself. This explains where mass comes from, but the deepest why is still open.

## Mostly agreed
- A thing stays at rest, or keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line, unless a force acts on it. This is Newton's first law. *How we know:* It has been tested for over 300 years, on Earth and in space. Spacecraft such as Voyager 1 travel exactly as the law predicts.
- Moving things on Earth stop because of forces such as friction and air resistance, not by themselves. *How we know:* The smoother the surface, the further a ball rolls from the same ramp. On ice, or on an air hockey table, things glide much further.
- The more mass a thing has, the more force is needed to change its motion. *How we know:* Pushing an empty and a full trolley shows it. Lorries need longer to stop than cars at the same speed.
- Inside a room that moves smoothly at a steady speed, nothing inside behaves differently from a room at rest. *How we know:* Gassendi's stone, dropped from the mast of a moving ship in 1640, landed at the foot of the mast. A ball tossed up in a smooth train lands back in your hand.

## How we found out
**Aryabhata, an Indian astronomer, Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, and Pierre Gassendi, a French priest and scientist, Around 499 CE (Aryabhata), 1632 (Galileo) and 1640 (Gassendi).** Around 499 CE, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote that the Earth turns. He said the stars only seem to move west, the way trees on a river bank seem to move backwards to someone in a moving boat. For more than a thousand years, most people elsewhere disagreed. In Europe, they had a strong argument. If the Earth were moving, a stone dropped from a tall tower would be left behind, and land far from the tower's foot. It lands right at the foot. So, they said, the Earth must be still. They added that a stone dropped from the top of a moving ship's mast lands behind the mast. In 1632, Galileo Galilei answered that the stone moves forward with the ship, and keeps moving forward as it falls. But had anyone really tried it? In 1640, the French priest and scientist Pierre Gassendi did. On a galley, a ship rowed by many oars, moving fast in the sea off Marseille, stones were dropped from the top of the mast. They landed at the foot of the mast, not behind it. A moving stone keeps its motion, so the old argument against a moving Earth was wrong.  
*What people believed before:* People argued that if the Earth were moving, a stone dropped from a tower would be left behind and land far from its foot. In the same way, they said a stone dropped from the mast of a moving ship would land behind the mast.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A moving thing needs a push all the time to keep moving.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 2,300 years ago, and the everyday feeling of most people; *rejected*)  
  For: Stop pedalling a cycle and it slows down. Stop pushing a trolley and it stops.  
  Against: The cycle and the trolley are slowed by friction and air resistance. Take those away, as on ice or in space, and moving things keep going.
- **When you drop something while walking, it falls straight down to the spot where you let go.** (A very common guess, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: To the person who drops it, it seems to fall straight down beside their legs.  
  Against: In the walking drop, the socks land past the target, because they keep their forward motion while they fall. Seen from the side, they fall in a curve.
- **If the Earth were really moving, a stone dropped from a tall tower would land far behind it.** (People who argued against a moving Earth, in Europe before about 1640; *rejected*)  
  For: It seems the ground should move away under a falling stone.  
  Against: The stone is moving with the Earth before it is dropped, and it keeps that motion. Gassendi showed the same on a moving ship.
- **Inertia comes from all the other matter in the universe.** (Ernst Mach, in the 1880s, and some physicists since; *debated*)  
  For: It is hard to say what 'moving' means except compared with other things, such as the far-away stars.  
  Against: Nobody has found a test that shows this. Most physicists treat it as an interesting idea, not a settled one.
- **Heavy things keep moving because they are heavy, and light things stop because they are light.** (A common idea among children; *rejected*)  
  For: A light paper ball thrown hard soon stops, while a cricket ball goes far.  
  Against: The paper ball is slowed more by air resistance, because it is light for its size. In a place with no air, both would keep moving. Mass makes things harder to stop, but light things have inertia too.

## Roots
We have learned that things keep doing what they are doing until a force changes it. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You let go of the socks right above the plate, yet they landed past it. While you carried them, they moved forward with you. After you let go, nothing pushed them forward, but they kept going. A moving thing carries its motion with it until a force takes it away.  
*Habit it breaks:* We split things into still and moving, and think still is natural while moving needs a cause. But the socks needed no push to keep moving. Only a change in motion needs a cause, a force.  
*Evidence:* In the walking drop, the socks landed about 35 cm past the plate at a slow walk and about 70 cm past at a fast walk. Standing still, they landed on it. Nothing pushed them forward after they left the hand.

- *who am i:* Your body has inertia too. When a bus brakes, you keep moving forward until something stops you.
- *what is life:* A cheetah or a running child must push on the ground to start and to stop, because their body keeps doing what it is doing.
- *what is this world:* The Earth carries you, your home and the sea round the Sun very fast and very smoothly. You move together, so you feel still.

Before you let go, you and the socks moved as one. After you let go, the socks went on alone, still carrying the motion you had shared. The Earth does this for all of us. Every person, tree, river and building on the Earth shares the same motion round the Sun. Our separate journeys across a room or a city all happen inside that one shared movement.

- **Aryabhata, Aryabhatiya, Golapada section, about 499 CE:** A person in a moving boat sees the still trees on the bank moving backwards. In the same way, people on the turning Earth see the still stars moving west. (Aryabhata saw that a smooth motion you share cannot be felt, only seen in what is outside you. That is exactly the smooth train and the walking drop.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 3.5:** No one can stay even for a moment without acting. (Science finds the same for the body: even sitting still on the sofa, you are moving with the Earth all the time. Nothing on Earth is ever truly at rest.)
- **Samyutta Nikaya 12.21, a Buddhist text:** When this is, that is. From the arising of this, that arises. (Newton's first law says the same about motion: no change happens without a cause. If a moving thing slows, turns or stops, a force made it happen.)
- Science (settled): Newton's first law: things stay at rest, or keep moving straight at a steady speed, unless a force acts. It has passed every test for over 300 years.
- Science (settled): No experiment done inside a smoothly moving room can show whether the room is moving or still.
- Science (debated): Why things have inertia at all has no agreed answer. Mach's idea, that it comes from the rest of the universe, is still argued about.
- Other view, Aristotle, a Greek thinker, and the everyday feeling of most people: Rest is the natural state of things on Earth. A stone, a cart or a ball moves only while something moves it, and when that stops, it comes to rest. This fits almost everything we see around us every day.

Now sit down, close your eyes, and take three more slow breaths. Which did you feel, the smooth moving or the stopping?

- Ask: If you cannot feel the Earth moving, what else might be moving that we cannot feel?
- Ask: Is anything in the world ever truly still?

## Story: The Ball on the Madurai Train
*Kavya, Arun, their grandfather and this journey are made up. Trains really run between Chennai and Madurai through Tiruchirappalli. Aryabhata's boat comparison is real: it is in his book, the Aryabhatiya, written around 499 CE.*

The train left Chennai Egmore at six in the morning, while the sky was still pink. Kavya had the window seat. She was nine. Her little brother Arun, who was six, sat in the middle, and Thatha, their grandfather, sat by the aisle with a steel flask of coffee on his knees. They were going to Madurai for their cousin's wedding.

By seven o'clock the train was going very fast. Coconut palms, paddy fields and a white temple tower slid past the window. Arun had a small red rubber ball. He held it tight in both hands.

'Throw it to me,' said Kavya.

Arun shook his head. 'If I throw it up, the train will go on without it. It will fly to the back of the train. It might go out of the door.'

Thatha nodded slowly. 'He may be right, Kavya. This train is going faster than a car on the highway. A ball in the air has nothing holding it. The train will move away under it.'

Kavya thought about this. It did sound sensible. Then she remembered last week, at home in the hall. She had walked past a steel plate on the floor and dropped a pair of rolled socks right above it. Everyone had guessed the socks would land on the plate. They had landed past it, every time. The faster she walked, the further past they went.

'I don't think it will fly to the back,' she said. 'I think it will land right back in Arun's hand.'

'Why?' asked Thatha.

'Because the ball is already going as fast as the train,' said Kavya. 'It's going as fast as us. When Arun throws it up, nothing pushes it backwards. So it keeps going forward, with us.'

Thatha raised his eyebrows. 'Hmm. Let's test it, then. But only a little way up, Arun. Not to the fan.'

Arun's hands were shaking a bit. He tossed the ball up, just above his head. It went up. It came down. It landed, plop, in his open hands.

He stared at it. 'It stayed with me!'

'Do it again,' said Kavya. He did it ten times. Every time, the ball came straight back down into his hands, as if the train were standing still in a station.

Thatha was quiet for a while. Then he laughed. 'Seventy years I have been riding trains,' he said, 'and I never once tried that.'

Near Tiruchirappalli, Thatha poured coffee from his flask into a paper cup. Just then the train began to brake for the station. Arun was holding the ball up again, ready to toss it. He let go just as the brakes squealed. This time the ball did not land in his hands. It landed on Kavya's knee, a little in front of him. And Thatha's coffee slopped over the front edge of the paper cup, onto his white veshti.

'Aiyo!' said Thatha, dabbing at it with his towel.

'Look,' said Kavya. 'The coffee went forward, and so did the ball. The train slowed down, but the coffee didn't. It kept going at the old speed, so it slopped forward. The ball too.'

'So the ball only stays with us,' said Arun slowly, 'when the train goes smooth?'

'Yes,' said Kavya. 'When the train goes smooth and straight, everything inside goes with it, and you can't even feel that you're moving. You only feel it when the train speeds up, slows down or turns.'

Arun closed his eyes. 'I can't feel anything now,' he said, because the train had pulled away from the station and was running smoothly again. 'If I didn't look out of the window, I wouldn't know we were moving.'

Kavya looked out of the window at the fields sliding backwards. 'Thatha, did you know that Aryabhata wrote about this, about fifteen hundred years ago?' she said. 'Amma read it to me. He said that when you sit in a moving boat, the trees on the bank seem to move backwards. And he said the stars seem to move across the sky for the same reason. They're not really moving round us. The Earth is turning, and we're turning with it.'

'So we're moving now,' said Arun, 'even when the train is stopped?'

'All the time,' said Kavya. 'Even when you're asleep. The Earth carries us round, smooth as anything, so we never feel it.'

Arun held the ball very still in his lap, as if it might fly away. Then he grinned, and tossed it up again.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the ball was moving forward at the train's speed before Arun tossed it. Once in the air, nothing pushed it backwards, so it kept that forward speed, and stayed above his hands. This is inertia, Newton's first law. When the train braked, the train and Arun's hands slowed down, but the ball and the coffee kept their speed for a moment, so they moved forward. Inside anything moving smoothly in a straight line at a steady speed, everything behaves as if it were still. That is why we cannot feel the Earth turning, which Aryabhata understood around 499 CE.

- Ask: Why did Arun think the ball would fly to the back of the train?
- Ask: What had Kavya seen at home that made her think differently?
- Ask: Why did the coffee slop forward when the train braked?
- Ask: Thatha had ridden trains for seventy years but never tested the ball. Why do you think people often don't test the things they are sure about?
- Ask: If you can't feel the Earth moving, how do you think people found out that it does?

### e1 · The Walking Drop: where will it land? (30 min)
- **question:** If I let go of a pair of rolled socks right above a plate while I am walking, where will they land? Does it change if I walk faster?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the socks land on the plate, before it, or past it? Will walking faster change where they land? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same socks, the same plate and the same height every time: hold the socks at chest height, arm out to the side. Only the walking speed changes: standing still, slow walk, fast walk. Do 3 tries at each speed, because one try can be a fluke. The helper always says 'now' when your hand is right above the plate.
- **safety:** Walk, never run. Clear toys and bags off the floor first, and don't do it on a wet floor.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw two pictures: the path of the socks as you saw them, and as your helper saw them from the side.
- **results table:** How I moved | Try 1: cm past the plate | Try 2 | Try 3 | Most common result
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the plate in the middle of a clear floor. 2. Mark a starting line about 2 metres before the plate with a slipper. 3. Show your child how to hold the rolled socks: arm out to the side, at chest height.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to drop these socks right above the plate. First, you guess. If I let go right above the plate while I walk, where will the socks land? On it, before it, or past it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the plate on the floor. You will walk past it and let go of the socks. Where will they land? On the plate, before it, or past it? Why?
  - Ask: Where will the socks land, and why do you think so? (good: Any guess with a reason is good. Many children say 'on the plate, because things fall straight down'. That is a fine guess to test.)
  - Note: Write down the guess and the reason before you start. Don't explain anything yet. Most children, and many grown-ups, guess the socks will land on the plate or just behind it.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands still with their hand right above the plate. 2. They let go. 3. Mark where the socks land with a coin, and measure from the edge of the plate nearest to the start.  
  *Parent says:* First, the easy one. Stand still, hold your hand above the plate, and let go. Where did they land?  
  *Child mode:* Stand still. Hold the socks above the plate. Let go. Did they land on it?
  - Note: Standing still, the socks should land on the plate. Write 0 cm. This is the result to compare with.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child walks slowly from the starting line, the way they walk round the flat. 2. You watch from the side and say 'now' when their hand is right above the plate. 3. They let go at 'now', and keep walking. 4. Mark and measure where the socks land. 5. Do it 3 times.  
  *Parent says:* Now walk slowly past the plate. I'll say 'now' when your hand is right above it. Let go at 'now', and keep walking. Don't throw them, just open your hand.  
  *Child mode:* Walk slowly past the plate. Let go when your helper says now. Just open your hand. Keep walking. Where did they land?
  - Note: Expect the socks to land past the plate, often about 20 to 50 cm past, in the direction your child was walking. Measure from the plate to the middle of the socks. If they land on the plate, check that your child is not stopping or pulling the hand back as they let go.
  - Check: Riya, a girl in Class 3, walks past a plate and lets go of rolled socks right above it. Where do the socks land? → **Past the plate, in the direction she was walking**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now your child walks fast, but does not run. 2. Say 'now' above the plate, as before. 3. Mark and measure. 4. Do it 3 times. 5. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now walk fast, but don't run. Let go at 'now' again. Will the socks land in the same place, nearer the plate, or further away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Walk fast this time. Don't run! Let go when your helper says now. Do the socks land further away? Measure.
  - Note: Walking fast, the socks usually land further past the plate, often 50 to 90 cm. Results vary by 10 or 20 cm between tries, because nobody lets go at exactly the same moment. That is why we do 3 tries.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap jobs: you walk and drop, and your child watches from the side, crouching low. 2. Ask them to watch the path of the socks in the air. 3. Then ask what the dropper saw.  
  *Parent says:* Now you watch from the side, and I'll walk. Watch the socks in the air very carefully. Do they fall straight down, or in a curve?  
  *Child mode:* Now you watch from the side. Your helper walks and drops. Watch the socks in the air. Straight down, or a curve?
  - Ask: What path did the socks take, seen from the side? (good: A curve, going forward and down.; Like a ball thrown gently forward.)
  - Ask: When you were the one walking, what did the socks seem to do? (good: They seemed to fall straight down beside my leg.; They stayed next to me as they fell.)
  - Note: Seen from the side, the socks fall in a forward curve. The person walking sees them fall almost straight down beside their leg, because the socks keep up with them. Both are true views of the same fall.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Challenge your child to make the socks land on the plate while walking slowly. Give 3 tries.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a challenge. Can you walk slowly and make the socks land on the plate? When should you let go?  
  *Child mode:* Can you make the socks land on the plate while you walk? When will you let go?
  - Note: The trick is to let go before your hand reaches the plate, about one big step early at a slow walk. Children who work this out on their own have understood the idea. Aid workers dropping food parcels from planes must let go long before the plane is over the target, for the same reason.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: when you let go of something while you walk, it keeps moving forward as it falls. What I saw: standing still, the socks landed on the plate. Walking slowly, they landed about 35 cm past. Walking fast, about 70 cm past. Why: the socks were moving forward with me, and nothing pushed them back after I let go, so they kept going forward until they hit the floor.
### e2 · The Egg That Keeps Turning (25 min)
- **question:** Can we tell a raw egg from a boiled egg by spinning it, stopping it and letting go?
- **predict:** Guess first. When you stop a spinning egg with one finger and let go, will it stay still or start turning again? Will a raw egg and a boiled egg do the same thing? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use eggs of about the same size, spun on the same plate. Stop each one with one fingertip for about one second, then lift your finger. Test each egg 3 times. You don't know which egg is which until the end, so you can't fool yourself.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the eggs and cools them in cold water before the child touches them. Wash hands with soap after touching raw eggs. If your family does not use eggs, do the Water That Keeps Turning activity instead (a2). It shows the same idea.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the inside of a raw egg and a boiled egg, and show with arrows what keeps turning.
- **results table:** Egg | Easy or hard to spin fast? | Starts turning again after I stop it? Try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3 | My guess: raw or boiled
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Before your child comes in, hard-boil 2 eggs for about 10 minutes and cool them in cold water. 2. Mix them with 2 raw eggs of the same size. 3. Write A, B, C and D on them in pencil. 4. Note secretly which are raw.  
  *Parent says:* I have 4 eggs. Two are boiled and two are raw, and only I know which. Can you find out without cracking them?  
  *Child mode:* Here are 4 eggs, A, B, C and D. Two are raw. Two are boiled. Can you find out which, without cracking them?
  - Note: Boil the eggs well ahead, so they are fully cooled. Warm eggs would give the answer away. Keep the note of which is which out of sight.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay egg A on its side on the plate. 2. Show your child how to spin it with a quick twist of the fingers. 3. Spin each egg once. Is it easy or hard to spin fast?  
  *Parent says:* Spin each egg on its side. Do they all spin the same way? Is any of them harder to spin fast?  
  *Child mode:* Spin each egg on its side, on the plate. Does one wobble? Is one harder to spin? Write it down.
  - Note: A boiled egg spins fast and smoothly. A raw egg is harder to spin fast and often wobbles, because its runny inside does not turn at once with the shell. Keep spins gentle, so no egg flies off the plate.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spin egg A. 2. Touch the top of it with one fingertip, just long enough to stop it, about one second. 3. Lift your finger at once. 4. Watch for 3 seconds. Does it stay still or start turning again? 5. Do 3 tries for each egg and fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now the real test. Spin the egg, stop it with one finger, and let go straight away. Watch it closely. What do you think a raw egg will do? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Spin the egg. Stop it with one finger. Let go at once. Does it stay still? Or does it start turning again? Try each egg 3 times.
  - Note: A boiled egg stays still once stopped. A raw egg slowly starts turning again, for half a turn or more. If nothing happens with any egg, the stop was too long: touch for only about one second, then lift your finger right away.
  - Check: Meera, a girl in Chennai, stops a spinning raw egg with her finger and lets go. The egg starts turning again. Why? → **The runny inside kept turning after the shell stopped, and it turned the shell again**
- **s4 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child says which eggs they think are raw and why. 2. Show your secret note. 3. Crack one egg your child chose as raw into a bowl, to check.  
  *Parent says:* Which two do you think are raw? What did they do that tells you? Let's check one by cracking it.  
  *Child mode:* Which two eggs are raw? Say why. Now crack one into a bowl. Were you right?
  - Note: Use the raw egg for cooking afterwards, so it isn't wasted. Wash hands with soap after touching raw eggs.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the runny inside of a raw egg keeps turning when the shell is stopped. What I saw: eggs B and D started turning again after I stopped them, 3 times out of 3. Eggs A and C stayed still. B was raw when we cracked it. Why: my finger stopped only the shell, and the inside kept moving because of inertia, so it turned the shell again.
### e3 · How Far Will It Roll? On the way to ice (25 min)
- **question:** A marble rolls down the same ramp onto a towel, a doormat and a bare floor. On which will it roll furthest, and why?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put the three surfaces in order, from shortest roll to longest roll. Then guess: on ice, would it roll even further? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same marble, the same ramp at the same height, and let go from the same mark every time. Don't push the marble, just let go. Only the surface changes. Roll 3 times on each, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Pick up the marble after each roll so nobody slips on it. Keep marbles away from children under 3.
- **record:** Fill in the table and draw a bar chart with one bar for each surface.
- **results table:** Surface | Try 1 (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Middle result (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rest one end of the ruler on the book. 2. Lay the towel flat so the low end of the ruler touches it. 3. Mark a starting line near the top of the ruler with a pencil.  
  *Parent says:* We'll roll this marble down the same ramp onto three floors: a towel, a doormat and the bare floor. Which will it roll furthest on? Which shortest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Make a ramp with the ruler and a book. Which floor will the marble roll furthest on? The towel, the mat or the bare floor? Why?
  - Ask: Put the three surfaces in order, and say why. (good: Towel shortest, then the mat, then the floor, because the towel is rough and the floor is smooth.; Any order with a reason is a good guess.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the marble at the starting line and let go, without pushing. 2. Mark where it stops with a coin. 3. Measure from the end of the ruler to the coin. 4. Do 3 rolls on each surface. 5. Write each one in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let go of the marble at the line. Don't push it. Where did it stop? Let's measure, then do it again.  
  *Child mode:* Let go of the marble at the line. No pushing! Put a coin where it stops. Measure. Do 3 rolls on each floor.
  - Note: On a towel, the marble may stop within 10 to 30 cm. On a mat, a little further. On smooth tiles it can roll 2 metres or more, sometimes all the way to the wall. If it reaches the wall, write 'reached the wall' and lower the ramp to half a book, then do all three surfaces again at that height.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find the middle result for each surface: the one between the smallest and the biggest. 2. Compare them.  
  *Parent says:* Which surface slowed the marble most? Which slowed it least? What do you think slowed it down?  
  *Child mode:* Which floor stopped the marble fastest? What slowed it down?
  - Check: Arjun's marble rolled 20 cm on a towel and 250 cm on tiles, from the same ramp. Why did it go so much further on the tiles? → **The tiles gave less friction, so less force slowed it down**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to imagine smoother and smoother floors. First ice, then something even more slippery. Last, a floor with no friction and no air.  
  *Parent says:* On ice, the marble would slide much further than on tiles. What if the floor were even more slippery than ice? And what if there were no friction and no air at all?  
  *Child mode:* Imagine a floor of ice. Then one even more slippery. Then one with no friction and no air. How far would the marble roll?
  - Ask: With no friction and no air at all, how far would the marble roll? (good: For ever.; It would never stop, because nothing would slow it.)
  - Note: This is the step that matters most. The pattern in your table points to it: less friction, longer roll. With nothing at all to slow it, the marble would keep rolling for ever. That is Newton's first law, worked out from your own results.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: friction is what slows a rolling marble down, and with no friction it would never stop. What I saw: from the same ramp, the marble rolled about 20 cm on the towel, 60 cm on the mat and 250 cm on the tiles. Why: the rougher the surface, the more friction, and the faster it stopped. Less friction means a longer roll, so with none it would keep going.

   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The dough passenger was moving and kept moving. The other three were still and stayed still while something around them moved.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which sentence says Newton's first law?  
   Options: A moving thing always slows down by itself / A thing stays still, or keeps moving straight at the same speed, until a force changes it / Heavy things fall faster  
   **Answer:** A thing stays still, or keeps moving straight at the same speed, until a force changes it. *Why:* This is the first law. The first option is the old, wrong idea. Moving things slow down only when a force, such as friction, acts on them.
2. (mcq) A coin sits on a card on a tumbler. You flick the card away fast. What does the coin do?  
   Options: Flies away with the card / Drops into the tumbler / Stays floating in the air  
   **Answer:** Drops into the tumbler. *Why:* The coin stays still because of inertia, and then gravity pulls it down into the tumbler. It does not fly with the card, because the flick rubs it for too short a time.
3. (mcq) Nisha walks past a plate and lets go of rolled socks right above it. Where do they land?  
   Options: On the plate / Behind the plate / Past the plate  
   **Answer:** Past the plate. *Why:* The socks keep moving forward as they fall, so they land past the plate. Picking 'on the plate' means thinking they lose their forward motion when they leave her hand.
4. (mcq) In the walking drop, what happens when you walk faster?  
   Options: The socks land further past the plate / The socks land on the plate / The socks land behind you  
   **Answer:** The socks land further past the plate. *Why:* Walking faster gives the socks more forward speed, so they travel further forward while they fall.
5. (mcq) Why does a rolling marble stop sooner on a towel than on tiles?  
   Options: The towel gives more friction / The towel is heavier / The tiles push the marble  
   **Answer:** The towel gives more friction. *Why:* The towel's rough fibres give more friction, which slows the marble. On smooth tiles there is less friction, so it rolls further.
6. (mcq) A stone is slid across smooth ice. Why does it go so far?  
   Options: Ice gives very little friction to slow it / Ice pushes it forward / Cold makes stones faster  
   **Answer:** Ice gives very little friction to slow it. *Why:* Ice is very slippery, so there is very little friction. With little to slow it, the stone keeps moving a long way.
7. (mcq) Why should everyone in a car wear a seatbelt?  
   Options: When the car stops suddenly, their body keeps moving forward, and the belt stops them safely / It keeps the car from moving / It makes the car go faster  
   **Answer:** When the car stops suddenly, their body keeps moving forward, and the belt stops them safely. *Why:* In a sudden stop, a passenger's body keeps its speed. The seatbelt gives the force that stops them along with the car, instead of the seat in front or the windscreen.
8. (mcq) A bus brakes suddenly. Which way does water in a tumbler on the seat slop?  
   Options: Forward / Backward / It does not move  
   **Answer:** Forward. *Why:* The water keeps moving forward while the tumbler slows with the bus, so it slops forward.
9. (mcq) Why can't we feel the Earth moving?  
   Options: It moves smoothly and steadily, and we move with it / It is not moving / It moves too slowly  
   **Answer:** It moves smoothly and steadily, and we move with it. *Why:* The Earth moves very fast, but smoothly. Like a ball in a smooth train, everything moves with us, so we feel nothing.
10. (mcq) Aryabhata said the stars seem to move west because the Earth turns. What did he compare this to?  
   Options: A person in a moving boat, who sees the trees on the bank move backwards / A spinning top / A falling stone  
   **Answer:** A person in a moving boat, who sees the trees on the bank move backwards. *Why:* Aryabhata wrote this comparison around 499 CE, in his book the Aryabhatiya.
11. (mcq) Pierre Gassendi dropped stones from the top of a moving ship's mast in 1640. What did he find?  
   Options: They landed at the foot of the mast / They landed behind the ship / They floated  
   **Answer:** They landed at the foot of the mast. *Why:* The stones kept the ship's forward motion while they fell, so they landed at the foot of the mast. This showed a moving Earth would not leave falling things behind.
12. (mcq) You stop a spinning raw egg for a second and let go. What happens?  
   Options: It stays still / It starts turning again / It breaks  
   **Answer:** It starts turning again. *Why:* The runny inside keeps turning when the shell is stopped, because of inertia. When you let go, it turns the shell again.
13. (mcq) What do scientists still not know about inertia?  
   Options: Whether it exists / Why things have inertia at all / How to measure mass  
   **Answer:** Why things have inertia at all. *Why:* Inertia has been measured very exactly, and the law always works. But why mass resists changes in motion is still argued about.

## Applications
- Seatbelts and child car seats hold passengers when a car stops suddenly.
- Helmets protect riders who are thrown forward off a two-wheeler.
- Lorry and bus drivers keep a long gap on the highway, because a heavy vehicle needs a long distance to stop.
- Spacecraft such as Voyager 1 and India's Mangalyaan coast through space for months with their engines off.
- Planes dropping food parcels in floods let go well before they are above the target.
- Shopkeepers tie down loads on bullock carts, tempos and cycles so they don't slide off when the vehicle stops.
- Cricket fielders pull their hands back as they catch, so the ball is stopped more gently.
- Washing machines spin water out of clothes: the drum turns, but the water flies off straight through the holes.

## Spotted around us
- Passengers lurching forward when a city bus or an auto brakes.
- Water slopping out of a bucket when someone carrying it stops suddenly.
- Shaking water off a wet umbrella: the drops fly off in straight lines.
- Banging the bottom of a ketchup bottle, or tapping a dabba of rice flour: the bottle stops, and what is inside keeps moving.
- Tapping the end of a steel tumbler of rice on the counter to settle the grains.
- A carrom striker gliding a long way across a powdered board.

## Open questions
- **Why do things have inertia at all?** The law has passed every test, but nobody can say for certain why mass resists changes in motion. Mach's idea, that it comes from the rest of the universe, has not been shown to be true or false.
- **Is there anywhere in the universe that is truly still?** The Earth moves round the Sun, the Sun moves round the middle of our galaxy, and the galaxy moves too. Physics says there is no one place that counts as 'really still', but people still argue about what that means.
- **Will Voyager 1 keep moving for ever?** Space is almost empty, so very little slows it. In about 40,000 years it will pass a star called Gliese 445, still far from it. Nobody knows what it might meet in millions of years.
- **Exactly where does the mass of an atom come from?** Scientists know most of it comes from energy deep inside the atom's centre, and they can calculate much of it. Some details are still being worked out.

## Connected maths
- In the walking drop, Kavya's socks landed 30 cm, 40 cm and 35 cm past the plate when she walked slowly. Put the results in order. Which is the middle result? **30 cm, 35 cm, 40 cm. The middle result is 35 cm.**. In order from smallest to biggest: 30, 35, 40. The middle one is 35 cm. The middle result is a fair single number for the three tries, because it is not the biggest or the smallest. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- Walking slowly, the socks landed about 35 cm past the plate. Walking fast, about 70 cm past. How many times as far is that? What does the pattern tell you? **2 times as far. Walking about twice as fast sends the socks about twice as far.**. 70 divided by 35 is 2. Check: 35 + 35 = 70. The faster you move, the further the socks go while they fall. That fits the idea that they keep the speed you gave them. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- From the same ramp, the marble rolled 20 cm on a towel, 60 cm on a doormat and 240 cm on tiles. Draw a bar chart. How much further did it roll on tiles than on the towel? **220 cm further.**. 240 minus 20 is 220. So it rolled 220 cm further on the tiles. On the bar chart, the tiles bar is 12 times as tall as the towel bar, because 20 times 12 is 240. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- The marble rolled 240 cm on tiles. A friend says, 'On ice, with even less friction, it might roll 10 times as far.' How far would that be, in metres? **2,400 cm, which is 24 metres.**. 240 times 10 is 2,400 cm. There are 100 cm in a metre, so 2,400 cm is 24 metres. That is longer than most flats! It shows why things slide so far on ice. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- In the raw egg test, egg B started turning again 3 times out of 3, egg D 3 times out of 3, and eggs A and C 0 times out of 3. Make a tally chart. Which eggs were raw? **B and D were raw. A and C were boiled.**. A tally chart shows 3 marks for B, 3 for D, and none for A and C. A raw egg starts turning again because its runny inside keeps moving. So B and D were raw. Testing 3 times makes sure one lucky try did not fool us. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
