# Air, sea water and soil: what each is made of, and how smoke turns rain into acid rain
`sci.chemistry.chemistry-of-earth-and-space.L4`

**Summary.** River water is fresh, and every river runs into the sea. Yet the sea is salty. Your child finds out why with a plate, a pinch of salt and five days of sunshine. Air, sea water and soil are each a mixture, and each has a recipe. Clean rain picks up a gas from the air and becomes a weak acid. Over millions of years that weak acid has broken rock into soil and carried salts to the sea. Smoke from coal and fuel makes rain a much stronger acid, called acid rain. Your child burns match heads under a jar to see how, and tests which stones a sour drop can mark.

**Intro.** *River water is fresh. Every river runs into the sea, all day and all night. So why is the sea salty?* People asked this question for a very long time. The answer starts in the air, passes through a raindrop and ends in the sea. In this unit you mix a bottle of sea water by its recipe. You count out the recipe of air in rice and dal. You dry fresh-tasting water on a plate for five days, and then taste the plate. You also find out what smoke does to rain, and what that rain does to marble.

## Terms
- **Mixture**: Two or more substances together that have not joined into one new substance. Each one can be separated out again. *e.g.* Sea water is a mixture of water and salts. Dry the water away and the salts are left.
- **Nitrogen**: The gas that makes up 78 parts in every 100 parts of air. It has no colour or smell, and it reacts with very few things. *e.g.* A packet of chips is puffed up with nitrogen, so the chips stay crisp.
- **Oxygen**: The gas that makes up 21 parts in every 100 parts of air. Humans and other animals need it, and so does a flame. *e.g.* A candle under an upturned tumbler goes out when the oxygen runs low.
- **Argon**: A gas that makes up nearly 1 part in every 100 parts of air. It reacts with nothing at all. *e.g.* Welders blow argon over hot metal to keep the oxygen of the air away from it.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas made when things burn and when living beings use food. Only about 4 parts in every 10,000 parts of air are carbon dioxide. *e.g.* The bubbles in soda water are carbon dioxide.
- **Dissolve**: To spread through a liquid as particles too small to see. A gas can dissolve in water, just as salt can. *e.g.* Carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in a falling raindrop.
- **Salinity**: How much salt is dissolved in water. Sea water holds about 35 grams of salts in every litre. *e.g.* The Dead Sea has a salinity almost ten times that of the open sea.
- **Evaporate**: To change from a liquid into a gas and go into the air. Water evaporates, but the salt dissolved in it stays behind. *e.g.* In a salt pan the sun evaporates the water, and workers rake up the salt.
- **Mineral**: A solid substance that rocks are made of. Soil holds tiny grains of minerals, and water holds a little of them dissolved. *e.g.* Quartz is the mineral in most sand. Calcite is the mineral in marble and limestone.
- **Humus**: The dark, soft part of soil made from dead plants and animals that microbes have broken down. *e.g.* The dark soil under a heap of old leaves is rich in humus.
- **Weathering**: The slow breaking of rock by rain, air, heat, cold and living beings. Rain water breaks rock by dissolving part of it. *e.g.* The letters on a very old limestone gravestone grow faint, because rain has dissolved the surface.
- **pH**: A number that says how acid or how basic a liquid is. Pure water is 7, acids are below 7 and bases are above 7. Each step down is ten times more acid. *e.g.* Lemon juice has a pH of about 2. Clean rain is about 5.6. Sea water is about 8.
- **Indicator**: A dye that changes colour in acids and in bases. It lets you see how acid a liquid is. *e.g.* Red cabbage water is purple in plain water, pink in lemon juice and green in soapy water.
- **Sulfur dioxide**: A sharp-smelling gas made when anything that holds sulfur burns. Coal, oil and match heads all hold sulfur. *e.g.* The sharp smell just after a match is struck is mostly sulfur dioxide.
- **Acid rain**: Rain made much more acid than clean rain by sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning coal and fuel. Its pH is often between 4 and 5. *e.g.* Acid rain took the fish from thousands of lakes in Sweden and Norway.
- **Neutralise**: To use up an acid by reacting it with a base or with limestone. The liquid is then no longer acid. *e.g.* Powdered limestone spread on a sour lake neutralises the acid in the water.

## Explanations
### Air is a mixture of gases with a fixed recipe · *defined*
**Definition:** Dry air is 78 parts nitrogen, 21 parts oxygen and nearly 1 part argon in every 100 parts. All the other gases together make up far less than 1 part.

Air looks like nothing, but it is a mixture of gases. The recipe is almost the same everywhere on the Earth, because winds keep stirring it. Nitrogen is the largest part. Your body breathes nitrogen in and breathes it out again unchanged. Oxygen is the part that humans and other animals use, and that a flame uses. Argon is a gas that reacts with nothing. The air you breathe out has a different recipe. It still holds about 78 parts nitrogen, but only about 16 parts oxygen. It holds about 4 parts carbon dioxide, which is 100 times as much as the air you breathed in. Nitrogen is the largest part of air for a simple reason. It reacts with very few things, so once it is in the air it stays for millions of years.
- e.g. In One Hundred Grains of Air, 78 grains of rice stand for nitrogen. The 21 grains of dal stand for oxygen.
- e.g. On a high mountain the air is thinner, but its recipe is the same.
- e.g. Factories get oxygen for hospitals by cooling air until it turns to liquid, and then separating it.
### The small parts of air that matter a great deal · *defined*
**Definition:** Air also holds water vapour, carbon dioxide, ozone and floating dust. Their shares are small, but weather, plants and rain depend on them.

Water vapour is water as a gas. Its share changes from place to place. Desert air may hold almost none, and the air of a Kerala monsoon day may hold 4 parts in 100. Clouds and rain come from it. Carbon dioxide is only about 4 parts in 10,000. Yet green plants build their bodies from this gas. Carbon dioxide also dissolves in rain, and this makes rain a weak acid. Ozone is a form of oxygen. A thin layer of ozone high above us stops most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet light. The Indian scientist Anna Mani built instruments in the 1960s to measure ozone over India. Air also carries dust, soot, salt spray from the sea, pollen and living microbes. Water vapour turns into cloud drops around these tiny specks.
- e.g. A cold steel tumbler mists over because water vapour from the air turns to drops on it.
- e.g. A sunbeam in a dark room shows dust floating in the air.
- e.g. A mango tree builds wood and leaves from the carbon dioxide in air.
### Sea water is water with salts dissolved in it · *defined*
**Definition:** A litre of sea water holds about 35 grams of dissolved salts. That is about 3 and a half parts in every 100. The rest is water.

Most of the salt in the sea is common salt, the salt we eat. Its proper name is sodium chloride. About 85 grams in every 100 grams of sea salt are sodium chloride. The rest holds magnesium, sulfur, calcium and potassium. The magnesium salts taste bitter. That is why sea water tastes harsher than salty water made at home. In 1884 the chemist William Dittmar tested 77 samples of sea water. A ship called HMS Challenger had collected them from every ocean. Some samples were saltier than others, but the recipe of the salts was the same in all of them. This shows that the oceans are one body of water that keeps mixing. Sea water also holds dissolved gases. Fish breathe the dissolved oxygen. Sea water is slightly basic, with a pH of about 8.
- e.g. In A Bottle of Sea, 3 level teaspoons of salt in half a litre of water match the sea.
- e.g. The Dead Sea holds about 340 grams of salts in a litre, so no fish can live there.
- e.g. Your tears and your blood hold about 9 grams of salt in a litre, a quarter of the sea's share.
### Why the sea is salty and rivers are not · *defined*
**Definition:** Rivers carry a very small amount of dissolved rock to the sea. Water leaves the sea only by evaporating, and the salts stay behind.

River water tastes fresh, but it is not pure. Rain dissolves a little of the rock and soil it runs over, and the river carries that to the sea. The amount is too small to taste. The Sun then evaporates water from the sea. Only the water goes up. The salts cannot evaporate, so they stay. The water falls again as rain, dissolves a little more rock and runs back. This has gone on for billions of years. Some of the sea's salt also came from volcanoes and from hot springs on the sea floor. Lakes show the same rule. A lake with a river flowing out of it stays fresh, because its salts are carried away. A lake with no way out, such as Sambhar Lake in Rajasthan, turns salty. The sea is not getting saltier today. Salt also leaves the sea slowly. Some is buried in mud. Some is left as thick beds of rock salt when a shallow sea dries up.
- e.g. In The Plate That Got Saltier, fresh-tasting water left a salty plate after five days of drying.
- e.g. Pangong Lake in Ladakh has no river flowing out, and its water is salty.
- e.g. Pink rock salt is called induppu or sendha namak. A sea that dried up over 500 million years ago left it behind.
### Soil has four parts, and it is full of life · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil is a mixture of mineral grains, humus, water and air. In every 100 parts of good garden soil, about 45 are minerals, 25 water, 25 air and 5 humus.

The mineral grains are rock broken very small. They are called sand, silt or clay, from the largest to the smallest. Humus is what microbes leave when they break down dead plants and animals. It is dark and holds water well. The gaps between the grains hold water and air. So about half of a handful of good soil is not solid at all. Roots, earthworms and microbes breathe the air in the gaps. They breathe out carbon dioxide, so soil air holds far more carbon dioxide than the air above. Soil water is not pure either. It holds dissolved minerals such as nitrogen, potassium and calcium, which roots drink in. Clay and humus hold on to these minerals, so that rain does not wash them all away. Soil can be acid or basic. Most crops grow best when the soil pH is between 6 and 7 and a half.
- e.g. In Hundred Squares, 45 squares of the soil grid are minerals and 25 squares are left empty for air.
- e.g. The red laterite soil of Kerala and the Konkan coast is red with iron, like rust.
- e.g. Farmers spread lime on sour soil to raise its pH.
### Clean rain is a weak acid, and it slowly turns rock into soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in rain and makes a weak acid called carbonic acid. So clean rain has a pH of about 5.6, a little below 7.

Many people think clean rain is pure water with a pH of 7. It is not. A raindrop falls through air, and some carbon dioxide dissolves in it. Soda water is sour for the same reason, though it holds far more of the gas. The weak acid in rain cannot hurt your skin or a leaf. But it never stops falling. Over thousands of years it dissolves limestone and makes caves. It slowly changes the hard minerals of granite into soft clay. This slow change is called chemical weathering. The parts that dissolve are carried away by rivers to the sea. The parts that do not dissolve stay behind as soil. In the heavy rain of the Kerala coast, almost everything that can dissolve has been washed out. What stays is rich in iron and aluminium, and it forms the red rock called laterite. Francis Buchanan gave laterite its name in 1807, at Angadipuram in Kerala.
- e.g. The Borra Caves in Andhra Pradesh were hollowed out of limestone by water.
- e.g. Granite hills slowly crumble into the sandy, clay soil at their feet.
- e.g. Lightning makes a little nitric acid in rain. It reaches the soil as food for plants.
### How smoke makes acid rain · *defined*
**Definition:** Burning coal and fuel puts sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides into the air. In clouds these gases turn into sulfuric acid and nitric acid, which fall in rain.

Coal and oil hold a little sulfur. When they burn, the sulfur joins with oxygen and makes the gas sulfur dioxide. Very hot flames, as in engines and furnaces, also make the nitrogen and oxygen of the air join. The gases they make are called nitrogen oxides. Both kinds of gas rise from chimneys and exhaust pipes. In the air they react with oxygen and water over a few days. They become sulfuric acid and nitric acid, which are strong acids. Clean rain has a pH of about 5.6. Acid rain often has a pH between 4 and 5. A rain of pH 4.6 is ten times as acid as clean rain. The wind can carry the gases for hundreds of kilometres before they fall. So acid rain often falls far from the chimney that made it, sometimes in another country. Volcanoes also give out sulfur dioxide. But in the last 150 years, most of the extra acid has come from people burning coal and oil.
- e.g. In Smoke in the Jar, the gas from five match heads turns purple indicator water pinker.
- e.g. In the 1960s, smoke from other countries in Europe fell as acid rain on Sweden and Norway.
- e.g. The clouds of the planet Venus are made of drops of sulfuric acid.
### What acid rain harms, and why some places are protected · *defined*
**Definition:** Acid rain harms lakes, soils, forests and buildings of limestone or marble. Ground that holds limestone neutralises the acid, and ground of granite does not.

In a lake, acid water kills fish eggs and young fish first. Snails and other animals with shells cannot build their shells. In soil, acid rain washes away calcium and magnesium, which trees need. It also sets free aluminium, which harms roots and fish. On buildings, acid reacts with marble and limestone and dissolves the surface. Carvings lose their sharp edges. Not every place suffers alike. Limestone is calcium carbonate, and it reacts with acid and uses the acid up. A lake on limestone can take acid rain for years and stay healthy. A lake on granite has nothing to neutralise the acid. This is why the lakes of Sweden and Norway, which lie on granite, lost their fish. In India, dust from dry soil is rich in calcium. It floats in the air and neutralises much of the acid before the rain falls. In 1989 L. T. Khemani and his team in Pune reported that rain over most of India was not acid, for this reason. They did find acid rain near some factories and power stations.
- e.g. In the Lemon Drop Test, a sour drop dulls polished marble and leaves granite unmarked.
- e.g. In Two Lakes, crushed shell turns pink sour water back to purple, and glass marbles do not.
- e.g. The granite carvings of the Brihadisvara temple at Thanjavur are over 1,000 years old and still sharp.
### How acid rain was cut · *defined*
**Definition:** Acid rain falls when sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides go into the air. Where laws and machines stopped those gases at the source, the rain became less acid.

For many years, factories built taller chimneys so that the smoke would blow away. This kept the town's air cleaner, but the acid only fell somewhere else. The lasting answer was to stop the gases. A machine called a scrubber sprays powdered limestone and water into the smoke of a power station. The limestone reacts with the sulfur dioxide and holds it. The solid that is left is gypsum, and builders use it for wall boards and cement. Oil companies now take most of the sulfur out of petrol and diesel. Since April 2020, fuel sold in India must hold very little sulfur. Cars also carry a part called a catalytic converter, which breaks down nitrogen oxides. In North America and Europe, power stations now give out far less sulfur dioxide. It is less than one tenth of what they gave out in 1990. Rain there is less acid, and fish have come back to some lakes. Soils recover far more slowly. In parts of Asia, where more coal is burned each year, acid rain is still a danger.
- e.g. Sweden has spread powdered limestone on thousands of lakes to neutralise the acid.
- e.g. In 1996 the Supreme Court of India ordered factories near the Taj Mahal to stop burning coal or move away.
- e.g. Some of India's coal power stations have been fitted with scrubbers.
### The sea takes in carbon dioxide too · *defined*
**Definition:** The sea dissolves about a quarter of the carbon dioxide that people put into the air. This makes sea water slightly less basic, a change called ocean acidification.

A gas from the air dissolves in the sea just as it dissolves in a raindrop. People began burning coal in large amounts about 250 years ago. Since then the pH of the sea surface has fallen from about 8.2 to about 8.1. The sea is still basic, not acid. But a fall of 0.1 means about 30 parts in 100 more acid than before. Corals, clams, snails and many tiny floating beings build shells from calcium carbonate. In less basic water, building a shell takes them more work. In 1955 the Japanese chemist Katsuko Saruhashi worked out a way to find the carbon dioxide in sea water. It used the water's temperature, its pH and its saltiness. Her tables were used around the world for many years. Scientists now measure the sea's pH at fixed stations every month. They do not yet know how well each kind of shell builder will cope.
- e.g. A coral reef is built from calcium carbonate that coral animals take out of sea water.
- e.g. Cold sea water dissolves more carbon dioxide than warm sea water.
- e.g. A station near Hawaii has measured the sea's pH falling every year since 1988.
### Air, seas and soils on other worlds · *defined*
**Definition:** Other planets and moons have air, seas and soils too, but each has a different recipe from the Earth's.

The air of Venus is about 96 parts carbon dioxide in every 100, and it is very thick. High in that air are clouds made of drops of sulfuric acid. These drops fall as rain. But the air below is so hot that the drops evaporate before they reach the ground. The air of Mars is 95 parts carbon dioxide, but it is more than 100 times thinner than ours. Titan, a moon of Saturn, has air that is mostly nitrogen, like ours. Enceladus is a small icy moon of Saturn with a sea under its ice. In 2009 the Cassini spacecraft found grains of ice from that sea, and they held sodium salts. So that sea is salty too. In 2023 India's Chandrayaan-3 rover, Pragyan, tested the Moon's soil near its south pole and found sulfur in it. The Earth's own recipe is unusual in one way. So much oxygen is in our air only because plants, algae and bacteria keep making this gas.
- e.g. On Venus, acid rain falls from the clouds, but it evaporates before it reaches the ground.
- e.g. The sea inside Enceladus holds salts, as the Earth's sea does.
- e.g. Moon soil has no humus, no water between its grains and no air.
### Where did the Earth's water come from? · *debated*
The young Earth was very hot, and scientists once thought it must have been dry. So where did the water of the sea come from? Scientists test each idea by comparing water. Water holds two kinds of hydrogen atom, a light kind and a rare heavy kind. The share of the heavy kind is like a fingerprint. Scientists measure this fingerprint in comets and in meteorites, which are rocks that fall from space. Then they compare it with sea water. The answer is not settled.
- e.g. A comet is a ball of ice and dust from the cold outer part of the solar system.
- e.g. Some meteorites hold water locked inside their minerals.
- e.g. Volcanoes still bring up steam from deep inside the Earth.
- **Scientists who favour asteroids:** Water came with rocky asteroids that hit the young Earth. The water in one kind of meteorite has almost the same hydrogen fingerprint as sea water.
- **Scientists who favour the Earth's own rock:** The rocks that built the Earth already held enough hydrogen to make the seas. In 2020 Laurette Piani and her team measured this in meteorites made of that kind of rock.
- **Scientists who favour comets:** Icy comets brought the water. But in 2014 the Rosetta spacecraft found that one comet's water held three times too much heavy hydrogen. So comets were probably a small part.

## Mostly agreed
- Everywhere on the Earth, dry air is 78 parts nitrogen, 21 parts oxygen and nearly 1 part argon. *How we know:* In 1894 Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay found argon by weighing nitrogen very carefully. Air sampled today on mountains, at sea and in cities gives the same shares.
- The share of carbon dioxide in air is rising. It was about 2.8 parts in 10,000 before factories, and it is over 4.2 parts now. *How we know:* Charles David Keeling began measuring it on Mauna Loa in Hawaii in 1958, and the record has never stopped. Bubbles of old air trapped in polar ice give the earlier values.
- The salts in sea water are in the same proportions in every ocean. *How we know:* In 1884 William Dittmar analysed 77 samples that HMS Challenger had collected around the world from 1872 to 1876. Later measurements agree.
- Clean rain is slightly acid, with a pH of about 5.6. *How we know:* Pure water left open to clean air takes in carbon dioxide until its pH is about 5.6. Rain collected far from any smoke is close to this value.
- Acid rain comes mainly from sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides, and it can fall hundreds of kilometres from their source. *How we know:* In 1968 the Swedish scientist Svante Odén used years of rain records from across Europe. He showed the acid falling on Sweden came on winds from other countries. From 1963 Gene Likens found the same in the forests of New Hampshire in the United States.
- When less sulfur dioxide goes into the air, the rain becomes less acid. *How we know:* After a law of 1990, power stations in the United States cut their sulfur dioxide by more than nine tenths. Rain collected there over the same years became steadily less acid.

## How we found out
**Henry Cavendish, Lord Rayleigh (John William Strutt) and William Ramsay, all in Britain, Cavendish found his leftover bubble in 1785. Rayleigh and Ramsay announced argon in 1894..** For a hundred years, chemists were sure they knew what air was made of. It was nitrogen and oxygen, with a little carbon dioxide and water vapour. One clue did not fit. In 1785 Henry Cavendish used electric sparks to make the nitrogen and oxygen of air react, again and again. A small bubble of gas was always left over, and nothing would make it react. He wrote this down, and almost everyone forgot it. In the 1890s Lord Rayleigh weighed gases with great care. Nitrogen taken from air was always a little heavier than nitrogen made from chemicals. The difference was about one part in 200. Many people would have blamed the scales. Rayleigh and the chemist William Ramsay looked for the cause instead. They removed the oxygen and then the nitrogen from a sample of air. A heavier gas was left, and it would not react with anything. In 1894 they named it argon, from a Greek word meaning lazy. Nearly 1 part in every 100 parts of air is argon. It had been in every breath anyone had ever taken.  
*What people believed before:* That air was only nitrogen and oxygen, with a little carbon dioxide and water vapour. Rayleigh showed that nitrogen from air was heavier than pure nitrogen. With Ramsay he then separated the extra gas, argon.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A taller chimney solves the problem of smoke.** (Many engineers and governments from the 1950s to the 1970s.; *rejected*)  
  For: A tall chimney sends smoke high above the town, and the air in the streets below becomes cleaner.  
  Against: The gases do not go away. Wind carries them for hundreds of kilometres, and they fall as acid rain on other places. Svante Odén showed this for Sweden in 1968.
- **Clean rain is pure water with a pH of 7, so any acid in rain is pollution.** (A common belief, found in some older school books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rain forms from water vapour, which leaves salts and dirt behind when it evaporates.  
  Against: Rain dissolves carbon dioxide as it falls, so clean rain has a pH of about 5.6. Volcanoes and lightning add a little natural acid too.
- **The sea gets a little saltier every year, so its saltiness tells us the age of the Earth.** (Edmond Halley suggested it in 1715. John Joly worked it out in 1899 and got about 90 million years.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rivers do bring dissolved salts to the sea every year, and evaporation leaves them there.  
  Against: Salt also leaves the sea. It is buried in mud and in beds of rock salt. So the sea's saltiness stays about the same. Radioactive atoms in rocks show the Earth is about 4,500 million years old.
- **Acid rain is what turns the Taj Mahal yellow and brown.** (Many school books and news reports.; *debated*)  
  For: The Taj Mahal is marble, and acid does dissolve marble. Factories and a refinery near Agra gave out sulfur dioxide.  
  Against: In 2015 Mike Bergin, S. N. Tripathi and their team tested marble pieces left on the Taj Mahal. The colour came mostly from dust and soot particles stuck to the surface. Acid may add to the damage.
- **Living beings keep the air and the sea fit for life, as if the whole Earth were one living body.** (James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis, who called the idea Gaia in 1974.; *debated*)  
  For: Living beings make the oxygen in air and take carbon dioxide out of it. The share of oxygen has stayed near 21 parts in 100 for millions of years.  
  Against: Most scientists agree that life changes the air and the sea. Many do not agree that life steers them towards what suits life. Life has also caused disasters for other life.
- **Acid rain is a solved problem.** (Many people in North America and Europe.; *debated*)  
  For: Sulfur dioxide from power stations there has fallen by more than nine tenths since 1990, and rain is less acid.  
  Against: Soils that lost their calcium take many decades to recover. In parts of Asia, coal burning is still growing.

## Roots
In this unit you found what air, sea water and soil are made of. You also found why the sea keeps its salt. Here we ask what it means that some things stay and others pass through.

**Deep truth.** In The Plate That Got Saltier, each day's water tasted fresh, yet the plate ended up salty. The water left for the air every day, and the salt stayed. The sea keeps its salt the same way, and the air keeps nitrogen, which hardly reacts. So what a place is made of depends on what stays there, as well as on what arrives.  
*Habit it breaks:* We expect a place to be made mostly of whatever is poured into it most. Rivers pour far more fresh water than salt into the sea, yet the sea is salty. What stays longest builds up.  
*Evidence:* In The Plate That Got Saltier, 20 tablespoons of fresh-tasting water left one salty plate. A lake with a river flowing out stays fresh. A lake with no way out, such as Sambhar Lake, turns salty. Argon reacts with nothing and has built up to nearly 1 part in 100 of air.

- *who am i:* The calcium in your bones was once in rock. Rain dissolved that calcium, and it reached you through your food. It has stayed in you. The nitrogen in your last breath did not stay: you breathed it out unchanged.
- *what is life:* Living beings change the recipes around them. The oxygen in air is there only because plants, algae and bacteria keep making oxygen. Humus is the part of soil that living beings made.
- *what is this world:* Air, sea and soil keep passing things to each other. A gas from the air dissolves in rain. Rain dissolves rock. Rivers carry the dissolved rock to the sea, and the sea's water rises into the air again.

Air, sea water and soil look like three separate things. But the water of the sea was once vapour in the air. The salt of the sea was once rock in a hill. The clay in a field is what rain left when it carried the rest away. Each of the three is being made, all the time, out of the other two. Many sages taught that the world is one whole that appears as many things. The science here says something close and plain: you can name three recipes, but the ingredients keep moving between them.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** “लवणमेतदुदकेऽवधायाथ मा प्रातरुपसीदथा इति / तं होवाच । यद्दोषा लवणमुदकेऽवाधा अङ्ग तदाहरेति / तद्धावमृश्य न विवेद / मध्यादाचामेति । कथमिति । लवणमिति / तं होवाचात्र वाव किल तत्सोम्य न निभालयसेऽत्रैव किलेति” Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to put a lump of salt in water. The next morning the salt cannot be seen or picked out. But every sip, from the top, the middle and the bottom, tastes of salt. (This is A Bottle of Sea. The salt is still there after it dissolves, spread through all the water. Uddalaka uses it to point at something unseen that is present in everything. The dissolving is science, and what he points to is a teaching.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.10.1:** “इमाः सोम्य नद्यः पुरस्तात्प्राच्यः स्यन्दन्ते पश्चात्प्रतीच्यः / ताः समुद्रात्समुद्रमेवापियन्ति / स समुद्र एव भवति / ता यथा तत्र न विदुरियमहमस्मीयमहमस्मीति” The rivers of the east flow east and the rivers of the west flow west. They rise from the sea and go back to the sea, and become the sea. There they do not know 'I am this river' or 'I am that river'. (The verse describes the water cycle: the sea's water rises, falls as rain and returns by the rivers. In the sea, the water of the Kaveri and of the Ganga cannot be told apart. The verse uses this to speak about the self, which science does not test.)
- **Ecclesiastes 1:7, in the Hebrew Bible:** “כׇּל־הַנְּחָלִים הֹלְכִים אֶל־הַיָּם / וְהַיָּם אֵינֶנּוּ מָלֵא / אֶל־מְקוֹם שֶׁהַנְּחָלִים הֹלְכִים שָׁם הֵם שָׁבִים לָלָכֶת” All the rivers run into the sea, yet the sea is not full. To the place where the rivers come from, there they return again. (This is the cousin's puzzle in the story The Oldest Salt Pan, noticed more than 2,000 years ago. The sea does not fill up because water leaves it as vapour. The writer saw the pattern, and evaporation explains it.)
- Science (settled): In all this dissolving, evaporating and raining, no atom is made or lost. The sodium in the sea today is the same sodium that rain once washed out of rock.
- Science (debated): In 1974 James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis proposed that living beings keep the air and the sea steady. They called this one system Gaia. Scientists agree that life changes the air and the sea. They do not agree that life steers them.
- Other view, The Buddha's teaching of anicca, which means impermanence: Nothing stays. The salt of the sea is buried in time, lifted into hills as rock salt and washed back by rain. Even the nitrogen of the air is slowly caught by bacteria and lightning. On this view, 'what stays' only means what leaves slowly. A place is not made of anything fixed. It is only a pattern of arriving and leaving.
- Practice, Five breaths and a glass of water (3 min): Sit with a glass of drinking water in front of you. Breathe in slowly and out slowly, five times. Each breath in is mostly nitrogen, which you will give back unchanged. Each breath out carries a little water from your body into the room. Now take one sip. This water has been rain, river and sea many times. Sit quietly for a moment and notice what is passing through you and what is staying.
- Ask: In The Plate That Got Saltier, the water left the plate and the salt stayed. In you, what passes through and what stays?
- Ask: The water of the sea is replaced every few thousand years, and the salt stays for millions. Is it the same sea?
- Ask: You breathed out nitrogen exactly as you breathed it in. Was that nitrogen ever part of you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The water in the sea is replaced every few thousand years, but the salt stays for millions. So which one is the real sea, the water or the salt? (hint: Think of a school. The children change every few years, and the building stays. Which one is the school?)
- *deep:* All rain is a little acid. So where should we draw the line between clean rain and acid rain? (hint: Is the line a number, such as pH 5? Or is it whether the acid came from people?)
- *deep:* Argon was in every breath anyone ever took, and nobody knew until 1894. What else might be around us that nobody has measured yet? (hint: Argon was found because two careful weighings of nitrogen, which should have matched, differed by a tiny amount.)
- *big:* Smoke from one country falls as acid rain on another country. Who should pay to stop it? (hint: The country with the chimneys gets the electricity. The country with the lakes gets the damage.)
- *big:* The oxygen in the air was made by living beings. So is the air part of the living world or the non-living world? (hint: Without plants, algae and bacteria, the oxygen would slowly react with rocks and be gone.)
- *big:* In India, dust in the air neutralises acid in rain. The same dust harms lungs. Can one thing be both a harm and a help? (hint: Think about who is asking: a lake, a marble statue or a child with a cough.)

## Story: The Oldest Salt Pan
*Thenmozhi, her Aachi and her cousin Prabhu are made up. These facts are real: Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu has a very large number of salt pans. Tamil Nadu is one of the main salt-making states of India. Many of the workers who rake and carry the salt are women. Salt water is let into shallow pans, the sun evaporates the water, and the salt is raked into heaps. Many pans there use salty water pumped from under the ground as well as sea water. The Thamirabarani river rises in the Pothigai hills, gives Thoothukudi its drinking water and meets the sea at Punnaikayal.*

In the May holidays Thenmozhi carried the tea can to the salt pans every morning. Her Aachi had worked there since before Thenmozhi's mother was born.

The pans lay outside Thoothukudi, flat and square, hundreds of them, each as big as a room. By nine o'clock the white heaps of salt were too bright to look at.

That year her cousin Prabhu had come from Madurai. He was twelve, and he had a new atlas. He sat on the mud bund between two pans with the atlas open on his knees.

"Look," he said. "The Thamirabarani goes into the sea just south of here. The Vaigai goes in. The Kaveri, the Krishna, the Godavari, the Ganga. Every river in this book ends in the sea."

"So?" said Thenmozhi.

"So river water is sweet. Sweet water pours into the sea all day and all night. Every year the sea must be a little less salty. One day it will be as sweet as a river." He shut the atlas. "Then nobody will need salt pans. What will your Aachi do?"

Thenmozhi had bathed in the Thamirabarani at her aunt's house in Srivaikuntam. That water was sweet. She had swallowed enough of the sea to know that the sea was not. She could not find what was wrong with Prabhu's idea, and it stayed with her like a stone in a slipper.

She took the tea to Aachi. Aachi was pulling a long wooden rake through her pan. The grey water under the rake was only as deep as a finger, and the bottom of the pan was white.

"Aachi, will the sea turn sweet one day?" She told her what Prabhu had said.

Aachi drank her tea slowly. "How deep is the water in my pan?"

"One finger."

"How deep was it last week?"

"One finger."

"Come on Thursday, early. Watch what happens to the pan. Then ask me again."

On Thursday the pan had no water in it at all. It was a wet white crust from bund to bund. A man walked along the bund and lifted a wooden board out of a gap. Water slid in from the channel. It was sea water that had already stood for weeks in the outer pans. It spread across the crust until the pan was a finger deep again. Then he put the board back.

"He does that twice a week," said Aachi. "He has done it since February. How many times is that?"

Thenmozhi counted the weeks on her fingers. "About thirty times."

"Thirty times, and a finger deep each time. Stand thirty fingers one on top of the other."

Thenmozhi measured against her leg. Thirty fingers came up above her knee.

"That much water has come into my pan," said Aachi. "Has it ever overflowed?"

"No."

"Did anyone carry water out?"

"No. It dried."

"It went up," said Aachi, and she pointed her chin at the sky. "The sun takes the water. It does not take the salt. In forty years I have not seen one grain of salt rise into the air. The water comes and goes. The salt only comes." She pushed her rake, and the crust broke with a crunch. "That is why I have something to rake."

Thenmozhi sat down on the bund. A channel came in. Nothing went out, except what the sun lifted. She thought of the atlas. Every river in it was a channel. And the sea had no river running out of it, anywhere in the book.

One thing still did not fit. "Aachi, your channel is salty. A river is sweet."

"Sweet to your tongue," said Aachi. "Your tongue is not a fine balance." She wiped the steel plate from her lunch. She poured a little drinking water onto it from her bottle. It was Thamirabarani water, which came to the town in a pipe. She set the plate on the hot bund.

By the time the tea can was empty, the plate was dry. Where the water had been, there was a pale ring on the steel, as faint as breath on a mirror. Thenmozhi touched it with a wet finger and put the finger on her tongue. It hardly tasted of anything. But it was not nothing.

"That river runs over rock and soil all the way from the Pothigai hills," said Aachi. "It takes a little from them. Too little for your tongue. But a river does not stop."

A little every day, thought Thenmozhi. For longer than anyone has been alive to count. And nothing leaving but the water.

She found Prabhu under the neem tree with his atlas.

"You have it the wrong way round," she said. "The rivers are not washing the salt away. The rivers are bringing it." She told him about the board in the gap, the thirty fingers of water and the ring on the plate. "The sea is a salt pan. The rivers are its channels. The sun does to the sea what it does to Aachi's pan."

Prabhu opened the atlas and looked for a river that ran out of the sea. He looked for some time.

"Then all that water goes up," he said. "So where do the rivers get more?"

Thenmozhi pointed inland. Far away, over the hills, the afternoon clouds were piling up, grey underneath.

"It comes down again," she said. "Over there. Without the salt."

Behind them Aachi's rake scraped and crunched. By evening there was a new white heap at the corner of her pan, as high as Thenmozhi's waist.

**For the parent:** Prabhu thought fresh rivers must be washing the sea's salt away. Thenmozhi saw that water enters a salt pan again and again and leaves only as vapour, while the salt stays. Rivers bring a little dissolved rock to the sea, and the sun takes only the water. So the sea keeps the salt.

- Ask: Water kept coming into the salt pan, and the pan never overflowed. Where did the water go, and why did the salt not go with it?
- Ask: River water tastes fresh. What could you do to show that it still holds a little dissolved mineral?
- Ask: A lake has rivers flowing in and no river flowing out. Would you expect its water to be fresh or salty? Why?

### e1 · Smoke in the Jar: which part of a match makes water sour? (30 min)
- **question:** When a match burns, do its gases make water acid? If they do, which part is the cause: the head of the match or the wooden stick?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Three jars will hold plain air, the gas from burning match heads and the gas from burning wood. In which jar will the indicator water turn pinkest? Why do you think so?

### e3 · Two Lakes: which lake bed neutralises acid rain? (30 min, 2 days)

## Worksheets
### Three recipes and a sour rain (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Out of 100 parts of dry air, how many are nitrogen?  
   Options: 78 / 21 / 50 / 1  
   **Answer:** 78. *Why:* Nitrogen is 78 parts in every 100. Oxygen is 21 parts, and argon is nearly 1 part.
2. (fill) A litre of sea water holds about ____ grams of dissolved salts.  
   **Answer:** ['35']. *Why:* Sea water is about 3 and a half parts salts in every 100. That is about 35 grams in a litre.
3. (truefalse) Clean rain is pure water with a pH of exactly 7.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in rain and makes a weak acid. Clean rain has a pH of about 5.6.
4. (match) Match each mixture to its largest part.  
   **Answer:** Dry air → Nitrogen; Sea water → Water; Good garden soil → Mineral grains; The salts in the sea → Common salt. *Why:* Each mixture has one part that is far bigger than the rest. Air is mostly nitrogen, sea water is mostly water, and soil is mostly grains of broken rock.
5. (sort) Sort these sources of acid gases. Is each one natural, or made by people?  
   **Answer:** a volcano → Natural; lightning → Natural; a coal power station → Made by people; a diesel lorry → Made by people; a brick kiln → Made by people. *Why:* Volcanoes give out sulfur dioxide, and lightning makes nitrogen oxides. Most of the extra acid in rain today comes from burning coal and fuel.
6. (mcq) What is humus?  
   Options: The dark remains of dead plants and animals / Very fine sand / Water held between soil grains / A kind of soft rock  
   **Answer:** The dark remains of dead plants and animals. *Why:* Microbes break down dead leaves, roots and animals, and humus is what is left. It is dark and holds water well.
7. (order) Put these in order, to show how a coal fire far away harms a lake.  
   **Answer:** ['Coal burns in a power station', 'Sulfur dioxide rises from the chimney', 'Wind carries the gas far away', 'The gas turns into acid in a cloud', 'Acid rain falls on a lake']. *Why:* The sulfur in coal becomes sulfur dioxide gas. Wind carries it, it turns into acid in the cloud, and the acid falls in rain.
8. (mcq) Which of these does acid rain damage most?  
   Options: A marble statue / A granite pillar / A glass window / A glazed tile  
   **Answer:** A marble statue. *Why:* Marble is calcium carbonate, which reacts with acid and dissolves. Granite, glass and glazed tiles hardly react.
9. (truefalse) Acid rain always falls close to the chimney that made it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Wind can carry the gases for hundreds of kilometres before they fall, sometimes into another country.
10. (fill) Using up an acid with limestone or a base is called ____ the acid.  
   **Answer:** ['neutralising', 'neutralizing']. *Why:* Limestone reacts with an acid and uses it up. The water is then no longer acid.
11. (write) In The Plate That Got Saltier, each day's water tasted fresh. Why did the plate taste salty on day 6?  
   **Answer:** The river water held a very small amount of salt. Each day the water evaporated and the salt stayed on the plate. After five days the salt of 20 tablespoons was all in one place.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the water left and the salt stayed, and that small amounts added up.
12. (write) In Smoke in the Jar, the match-head jar turned pinkest. What does that tell you about coal smoke and rain?  
   **Answer:** Match heads hold sulfur, and burning sulfur makes a gas that turns water acid. Coal holds sulfur too. So the smoke of burning coal can make rain acid.. *Why:* A strong answer links sulfur in the match head to sulfur in coal, and the jar of water to rain.
13. (draw) Draw rain on a hill, a river, the sea and vapour rising. Show with dots where the salt goes.  
   **Answer:** Rain falls on a hill. A river with a few dots runs to the sea. The sea is full of dots. Arrows of vapour rise from the sea with no dots in them, and go back to the cloud.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows dots going into the sea with the river and none leaving with the vapour.
14. (perspective) A factory's smoke is making the town's air dirty. What should be done?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that the gases travel hundreds of kilometres, so a tall chimney only moves the harm. Another is that a scrubber costs money that someone has to pay.. *Why:* Each speaker has a real point. A strong answer uses a fact about how the gases travel or how a scrubber works.
15. (mcq) Why is a lake with no river flowing out usually salty?  
   Options: Salts arrive and only water leaves / Salt falls into it with the rain / Fish living there make salt / Its bed is always made of salt  
   **Answer:** Salts arrive and only water leaves. *Why:* Rivers bring a little dissolved rock. Water leaves such a lake only by evaporating, and the salts stay behind and build up.
### Follow the gas, follow the salt (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) You breathe in air with 78 parts nitrogen. What does your body do with the nitrogen?  
   Options: Breathes it out unchanged / Burns it for energy / Turns it into oxygen / Stores it in the bones  
   **Answer:** Breathes it out unchanged. *Why:* Nitrogen reacts with very few things. Your body breathes it out again, still 78 parts in 100.
2. (mcq) One rain has a pH of 4.6. Clean rain has a pH of 5.6. How do they compare?  
   Options: The first is 10 times as acid / The first is slightly more acid / They are equally acid / The clean rain is more acid  
   **Answer:** The first is 10 times as acid. *Why:* Each whole step down the pH scale means 10 times more acid. From 5.6 to 4.6 is one whole step.
3. (truefalse) Sea water is an acid.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Sea water is slightly basic, with a pH of about 8. Dissolved carbon dioxide is making it a little less basic.
4. (truefalse) Every ocean has the same recipe of salts, even though some seas are saltier than others.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Samples from every ocean have the salts in the same proportions. This shows that the oceans keep mixing.
5. (odd) Three of these are calcium carbonate. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Marble / Seashell / Limestone / Granite  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Marble, seashell and limestone are all calcium carbonate, which reacts with acid. Granite is made of other minerals that hardly react.
6. (sort) Sort these. Is most of each one found in air, in the sea or in soil?  
   **Answer:** argon → Air; ozone → Air; dissolved sodium → Sea; dissolved magnesium salts → Sea; humus → Soil; clay → Soil. *Why:* Argon and ozone are gases of the air. Sodium and magnesium are dissolved in the sea. Humus and clay are solid parts of soil.
7. (count) A litre of sea water holds 35 grams of salts. How many grams do 4 litres hold?  
   **Answer:** 140. *Why:* 35 grams in each litre, for 4 litres, is 35 times 4, which is 140 grams.
8. (order) Put these waters in order, from the least salty to the most salty.  
   **Answer:** ['rain', 'river water', 'sea water', 'Dead Sea water']. *Why:* Rain holds almost no salts. River water has dissolved a little rock. The sea has kept its salts for ages. The Dead Sea has no river flowing out of it. The hot sun keeps evaporating its water, so it is the saltiest.
9. (fill) The slow breaking of rock by rain, air and living beings is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['weathering']. *Why:* Rain is a weak acid. Over thousands of years it dissolves part of the rock, and what stays behind becomes soil.
10. (mcq) Why does crushed shell turn sour pink water back to purple?  
   Options: It reacts with the acid, using it up / It soaks up the pink colour / It makes the water colder / It adds more water  
   **Answer:** It reacts with the acid, using it up. *Why:* Shell is calcium carbonate. It reacts with the acid, so less acid is left and the indicator goes back to purple.
11. (write) In the Lemon Drop Test, marble was marked and granite was not. Explain why, using the words calcium carbonate.  
   **Answer:** Marble is calcium carbonate. Acid reacts with calcium carbonate and makes bubbles of gas. It dissolves a little of the surface, so the shine is lost. Granite is made of minerals that acid hardly touches.. *Why:* A strong answer names what marble is made of and says the acid reacted with it. It also says that granite did not react.
12. (write) A lake has rivers flowing in and one river flowing out. Explain why its water stays fresh.  
   **Answer:** The rivers bring a little dissolved rock. The river flowing out carries the same salts away again. So the salts do not build up.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the salts have a way out. It may compare this with the sea, where only water leaves.
13. (write) Carbon dioxide is only 4 parts in 10,000 of air. Give two reasons why it still matters.  
   **Answer:** Plants build their bodies from it. It dissolves in rain and makes rain a weak acid, which slowly breaks rock into soil. It also dissolves in the sea and helps to keep the Earth warm.. *Why:* A strong answer gives any two real jobs of carbon dioxide. A small share can still do a great deal.
14. (label) Label four parts of this magnified view of soil.  
   **Answer:** ['A mineral grain: a piece of broken rock, such as a grain of sand', 'Humus: a dark crumb made from dead plants and animals', 'Water: a thin film around the grains', 'An air gap: an empty-looking space between the grains']. *Why:* Soil has four parts. About half of good soil is grains and humus. The other half is the water and air in the gaps.
15. (perspective) India's air often carries a lot of dust from dry soil. Is this dust good or bad?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer sees that the same dust harms lungs and marble and also protects lakes. So the answer depends on what you are trying to protect.. *Why:* All three speakers are right about what they study. A strong answer weighs the harm against the help.
### What stays and what leaves (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Nitrogen taken from air weighed slightly more than nitrogen made from chemicals. What explained the difference?  
   Options: An unknown heavier gas in the air / A fault in the scales / Damp inside the jars / Oxygen left in the nitrogen  
   **Answer:** An unknown heavier gas in the air. *Why:* The nitrogen from air held a little argon, which is heavier. A small difference that keeps coming back is a clue, not a mistake.
2. (mcq) A lake on granite is turning acid. Which action stops the cause?  
   Options: Fitting scrubbers at power stations / Adding limestone to the lake / Adding more fish / Building a taller chimney  
   **Answer:** Fitting scrubbers at power stations. *Why:* A scrubber stops the gas that makes the acid. Limestone helps the lake for a time, but it is used up while the acid rain goes on.
3. (mcq) Which test would show that fresh-tasting river water holds dissolved minerals?  
   Options: Dry a plateful and look for a film / Look at it through a magnifier / Shake it hard in a bottle / Smell it  
   **Answer:** Dry a plateful and look for a film. *Why:* Dissolved minerals are too small to see and too few to taste. When the water evaporates they stay behind as a faint film.
4. (mcq) As the air gains carbon dioxide, sea water becomes less basic. Why?  
   Options: The gas dissolves and makes a weak acid / The sea gets warmer / Salt slowly turns into acid / Rivers bring more salt  
   **Answer:** The gas dissolves and makes a weak acid. *Why:* Carbon dioxide dissolves in the sea, as it does in a raindrop, and makes carbonic acid. The pH of the sea surface has fallen from about 8.2 to 8.1.
5. (truefalse) The sea gets saltier every year.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Rivers add salts, but salts also leave the sea slowly. They are buried in mud and in beds of rock salt. So the saltiness stays about the same.
6. (truefalse) Most of the oxygen in the air was made by living beings.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Plants, algae and some bacteria make oxygen from water, using sunlight. Without them the oxygen would slowly react with rocks and be used up.
7. (match) Match each problem to an answer that people really use.  
   **Answer:** Sulfur dioxide from a power station → A scrubber; Sulfur in diesel and petrol → Cleaner fuel; Nitrogen oxides from car engines → A catalytic converter; A lake that is already acid → Powdered limestone. *Why:* The first three stop the gases before they reach the air. Limestone treats the harm after it has happened.
8. (count) The salt from 20 tablespoons of river water is dissolved in 1 tablespoon. How many times as salty is it?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* The same salt is now in one twentieth of the water. So each drop holds 20 times as much salt.
9. (fill) A lake with no river flowing out loses its water only by ____.  
   **Answer:** ['evaporation', 'evaporating']. *Why:* Only water evaporates. The salts stay, so such a lake turns salty over time.
10. (write) Living beings add oxygen and carbon dioxide all day. Why is nitrogen still the largest part of air?  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen reacts with very few things, so it is hardly ever used up. It stays in the air for millions of years. Oxygen and carbon dioxide are used up almost as fast as they are made.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the share of a gas depends on how long the gas stays in the air.
11. (write) Taller chimneys made the air in towns cleaner. Explain why they did not solve acid rain.  
   **Answer:** A tall chimney only sends the gases higher. The wind carries them further, and they still turn into acid and fall in rain somewhere else.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the gases were moved and not removed.
12. (write) A match-head jar in Smoke in the Jar stayed purple. Give one reason other than 'smoke makes no acid'.  
   **Answer:** The water may have been hard, and hard water uses up a little acid. Or too few matches were used, or the gas escaped before the lid went on.. *Why:* A strong answer looks for a fault in the test before throwing away an idea that other tests support.
13. (write) Rock salt is mined from hills far from any sea. What does that tell you about those hills?  
   **Answer:** The salt was left when a sea or a salt lake dried up long ago. Later the salt beds were buried and lifted into hills. So that land was once under salt water.. *Why:* A strong answer reasons from the rule that salt stays when water evaporates.
14. (perspective) The sea's water is replaced every few thousand years. Its salt stays for millions of years. What is the sea?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer uses the fact that water leaves by evaporating and salt stays. It says what that means for which part is 'the sea'.. *Why:* This question has no single right answer. A strong answer uses how long each part stays.
15. (draw) Draw a power station, a cloud and a lake. Show how acid rain forms, and one way to stop it.  
   **Answer:** Smoke rises from the chimney. An arrow of wind carries it to a cloud, and rain falls on the lake. The change could be a scrubber on the chimney, cleaner fuel, or power made without burning coal.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the gas travelling before it falls. It puts the change at the source, not at the lake.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which list gives the three main gases of dry air, largest first?  
   Options: Nitrogen, oxygen, argon / Oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide / Oxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogen / Nitrogen, carbon dioxide, oxygen  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen, oxygen, argon. *Why:* Dry air is 78 parts nitrogen, 21 parts oxygen and nearly 1 part argon. Carbon dioxide is far less than 1 part.
2. (mcq) About how much of the air is carbon dioxide?  
   Options: 4 parts in 10,000 / 4 parts in 100 / 21 parts in 100 / About half  
   **Answer:** 4 parts in 10,000. *Why:* Carbon dioxide is rare in air. Breathed-out air holds about 4 parts in 100, which may be why many people choose that number.
3. (mcq) What is most of the salt in sea water?  
   Options: Common salt, the salt we eat / Sugar / Chalk / Baking soda  
   **Answer:** Common salt, the salt we eat. *Why:* About 85 grams in every 100 grams of sea salt are common salt, sodium chloride.
4. (mcq) Rivers taste fresh. How do they still make the sea salty?  
   Options: They bring a little dissolved rock / They wash salt off the beaches / They freeze and leave salt behind / They do not. Fish make the salt  
   **Answer:** They bring a little dissolved rock. *Why:* River water holds a very small amount of dissolved rock. It adds up, because the salts stay in the sea when the water evaporates.
5. (mcq) When sea water evaporates, what goes into the air?  
   Options: Only the water / The water and the salt together / Only the salt / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** Only the water. *Why:* Water turns into vapour and rises. Salt cannot evaporate in the sun, so it stays. That is how a salt pan works.
6. (mcq) Which four parts make up soil?  
   Options: Minerals, humus, water and air / Sand, stones, roots and worms / Rock, salt, water and ash / Clay, cement, water and air  
   **Answer:** Minerals, humus, water and air. *Why:* Soil is mineral grains, humus, water and air. About half of good soil is the water and air in the gaps.
7. (mcq) Why is clean rain slightly acid?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide dissolves in it / Salt from the sea rises into it / Sunlight turns water sour / All dust in the air is acid  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide dissolves in it. *Why:* A raindrop falls through air and dissolves a little carbon dioxide, which makes a weak acid. Salt does not rise with the vapour.
8. (mcq) Which two kinds of gas cause most acid rain?  
   Options: Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides / Oxygen and nitrogen / Argon and water vapour / Carbon dioxide and oxygen  
   **Answer:** Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides. *Why:* Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides turn into strong acids in clouds. Carbon dioxide makes only the weak acid of clean rain.
9. (truefalse) Gases from a chimney in one country can fall as acid rain in another country.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Wind carries the gases for hundreds of kilometres. Smoke from other countries in Europe fell as acid rain on Sweden and Norway.
10. (mcq) Two lakes get the same acid rain. Which lake stays healthier?  
   Options: The lake on limestone / The lake on granite / The deeper lake / Both stay the same  
   **Answer:** The lake on limestone. *Why:* Limestone reacts with the acid and neutralises it. Granite does not, so a lake on granite turns acid.
11. (mcq) What does acid rain do to a marble carving over many years?  
   Options: Dissolves the surface and blurs it / Makes the marble harder / Turns the marble into granite / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** Dissolves the surface and blurs it. *Why:* Acid reacts with the calcium carbonate of marble and dissolves a little each time. Sharp edges slowly become blurred.
12. (mcq) What is the lasting way to stop acid rain?  
   Options: Stop the gases at the chimney / Build taller chimneys / Add limestone to every lake / Cover the lakes  
   **Answer:** Stop the gases at the chimney. *Why:* Scrubbers and cleaner fuel stop the gases from reaching the air. Taller chimneys only move the acid, and limestone is used up.

## Applications
- Salt makers along the coasts of Gujarat and Tamil Nadu let sea water into shallow pans. The sun evaporates the water, and they rake up the salt.
- Scrubbers on power stations spray limestone into the smoke to catch sulfur dioxide. The gypsum that is left is made into wall boards.
- Oil companies take sulfur out of petrol and diesel, so that engines give out far less sulfur dioxide.
- Farmers test the pH of their soil. If it is too acid, they spread lime, which is powdered limestone, to neutralise it.
- Factories cool air until it turns to liquid, and then separate it. Hospitals use the oxygen, food packers use the nitrogen and welders use the argon.
- Chennai makes drinking water from sea water at plants in Minjur and Nemmeli. They push the water through very fine filters that hold back the salts.
- Scientists around the world collect rain and measure its pH every week. The records show where acid rain is growing and where it is shrinking.

## Spotted around us
- A puffed packet of chips or murukku. The gas inside is nitrogen, taken from air, and it keeps the food crisp.
- White heaps of salt beside square pans, on the coast road near Thoothukudi or Marakkanam, or in Kutch.
- A dull round patch on a polished marble floor, where lemon juice, tamarind or curd was once spilled.
- Red soil and red laterite stone walls in Kerala, coastal Karnataka and Goa. The red is iron oxide that the rain left behind.
- Pink rock salt, called induppu or sendha namak, in a kitchen jar. It is sea salt from a sea that dried up long ago.
- A very tall chimney at a power station or factory, with a pale plume drifting far downwind.
- A white crust inside a kettle or on a tap. It is minerals that the water dissolved from rock, left behind when the water evaporated.

## Open questions
- **Where did the Earth's water come from?** The water in some meteorites matches sea water well, and the water in one comet did not. But only a few comets and meteorites have been measured, and the young Earth's own rock may have held enough.
- **How salty was the sea when it first formed?** No sea water that old survives. Scientists study tiny drops trapped in ancient salt crystals. The oldest of these cover only part of the Earth's history.
- **How will shell builders cope as the sea becomes less basic?** In tanks, some corals and snails build thinner shells in such water, and some seem to manage. Nobody knows yet what will happen in the open sea over many years.
- **What has kept the oxygen in air close to 21 parts in 100 for millions of years?** Plants make oxygen, and burning and rotting use it up. Scientists have several ideas about what keeps the two in balance, such as forest fires, and they do not agree.
- **How long do soils take to recover from acid rain?** In some forests the rain has been cleaner for 30 years, and the soil is still short of calcium. New calcium comes only from the slow weathering of rock.
- **Is anything alive in the acid clouds of Venus?** In 2020 a team reported a gas called phosphine there, which living beings can make. Other teams could not find it. The question is still argued.

## Connected maths
- In The Plate That Got Saltier, you poured 4 tablespoons of river water on the plate each day. You did this for 5 days. On day 6 you dissolved all the salt in 1 tablespoon of water. How many times as salty as river water was that tablespoon? **20 times as salty.**. 4 tablespoons a day for 5 days is 4 × 5 = 20 tablespoons. All the salt from 20 tablespoons is now in 1 tablespoon. So each drop holds 20 times as much salt as a drop of river water. (queues `math.multiplication.L4`)
- In One Hundred Grains of Air, 100 grains stood for air: 78 of rice, 21 of dal and 1 mustard seed. You want a bigger model with 500 grains. How many grains of rice, of dal and of mustard do you need? **390 grains of rice, 105 grains of dal and 5 mustard seeds.**. 500 is 5 times 100, so every share is 5 times as big. 78 × 5 = 390, 21 × 5 = 105 and 1 × 5 = 5. Check: 390 + 105 + 5 = 500. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- About 4 parts in every 10,000 parts of air are carbon dioxide. How many grains would a model of air need, to hold just 1 grain of carbon dioxide? How many plates of 100 grains is that? **2,500 grains, which is 25 plates of 100 grains.**. 10,000 ÷ 4 = 2,500. So 4 grains in 10,000 is the same share as 1 grain in 2,500. And 2,500 ÷ 100 = 25 plates. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- The sea holds about 35 grams of salts in a litre. In A Bottle of Sea you put 3 level teaspoons of salt into half a litre of water. A level teaspoon holds about 6 grams. Was your bottle more salty or less salty than the sea? **Very slightly more salty: 18 grams, where the sea would have 17 and a half grams.**. Half a litre of sea water holds half of 35 grams, which is 17 and a half grams. 3 teaspoons of 6 grams are 18 grams. The difference is only half a gram, so the recipe is close enough. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- Each whole step down the pH scale means 10 times more acid. Clean rain has a pH of about 5.6. How many times as acid as clean rain is a rain of pH 3.6? **100 times as acid.**. From 5.6 down to 3.6 is two whole steps. One step is 10 times, and two steps are 10 × 10 = 100 times. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- In Two Lakes, one family crushed their shell as fine as rava, and Lake Limestone turned purple in 10 minutes. Another family used the same weight of shell in big pieces, and it took 40 minutes. How many times as long did the big pieces take? What could explain it? **4 times as long. Fine bits have far more surface touching the water.**. 40 ÷ 10 = 4. The acid can only react at the surface of the shell. The same shell crushed finer has much more surface, so it uses up the acid faster. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
