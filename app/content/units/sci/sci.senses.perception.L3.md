# Illusions: how your brain can be fooled about what you see
`sci.senses.perception.L3`

**Summary.** Two lines can both be 10 cm long, yet one looks clearly longer than the other. A ruler shows the truth, and the eyes are not broken. Seeing is done by the eyes and the brain together. The eyes catch light, and the brain works out sizes, distances, shapes and movement from clues, like what is around a thing and where shadows fall. An illusion is a time when those clues lead the brain to the wrong answer. Even after you know the answer, most illusions still fool you. Touch can be fooled too, and some animals, like butterflies with eyespots and the great bowerbird, use illusions to live their lives.

**Intro.** *Look at these two lines. The top one has arrowheads at its ends, like this: <——>. The bottom one has fins that open outwards, like this: >——<. Which line between the ends is longer? Now measure both with a ruler.* Both lines are the same length. Most people see the line with open fins as longer, and it still looks longer after they measure it. Your eyes caught the light correctly. It was your brain that decided how long the lines are, and here it used the fins as a clue and got it wrong. In this unit you find out what clues your brain uses, test how much an illusion fools the people in your home, and find out whether knowing the answer makes it go away.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what an illusion is: a time when what I see or feel is different from what is really there, as a ruler or another test shows.
1. I can explain that the eyes and the brain see together, and that the brain uses clues, like distance, surroundings and shading, to decide what I see.
2. I can run a fair test of the arrow-lines illusion, measure how much it fools different people, and record the results in a table.
3. I can show with my own test that knowing about an illusion usually does not stop me seeing it.
4. I can explain why still pictures shown one after another look like movement, and why the old answer, 'persistence of vision', is not right.
5. I can give examples of illusions of touch, and of animals that use illusions in their lives, like butterflies with eyespots and the great bowerbird.

## Terms
- **Illusion**: A time when what you see, hear or feel is different from what is really there. *e.g.* Two lines that are both 10 cm long, where one looks longer.
- **Optical illusion**: An illusion of seeing. 'Optical' means to do with the eyes and light. *e.g.* Railway tracks that seem to meet far away, though they stay the same distance apart.
- **Perception**: What your brain makes of what your senses pick up. It is the picture or feeling you end up with. *e.g.* You see a whole friend's face, though your eyes only catch light.
- **Brain**: The organ in your head that takes in signals from the senses, decides what they mean, and sends out signals to move your body. *e.g.* Your brain decides that a far-away bus is big, though it looks small.
- **Clue**: A piece of information the brain uses to make its best guess. Scientists also call this a cue. *e.g.* Lines that come closer together are a clue that something is far away.
- **Perspective**: The way things look smaller and lines seem to come together as they get further away. *e.g.* A long corridor in a block of flats seems to get narrower at the far end.
- **Size constancy**: Your brain's habit of seeing a thing as the same size, even when it is far away and makes a smaller picture in your eye. *e.g.* Your mother walking away down the road does not seem to shrink.
- **Contrast**: How different a thing looks from what is next to it. The brain judges brightness and colour by comparing. *e.g.* The same grey paper looks lighter on black and darker on white.
- **Shading**: How light and dark spread across a thing. The brain uses shading to tell bumps from dents. *e.g.* A circle that is light at the top and dark at the bottom looks like a bump.
- **Apparent motion**: Seeing movement when nothing really moves, because pictures in slightly different places are shown one after another. *e.g.* A film is a row of still pictures. Shown quickly, they look like moving people.
- **Frame**: One still picture in a film, a cartoon or a flip book. *e.g.* Most cinema films show 24 frames every second.
- **Eyespot**: A round mark on an animal's body that looks like a big eye. *e.g.* The peacock pansy butterfly, common in Indian gardens, has eyespots on their wings.
- **Camouflage**: Colours and patterns that help a living thing blend into what is around them, so they are hard to see. *e.g.* A garden lizard can turn brown on a dry branch.
- **Forced perspective**: A trick where near things and far things are arranged so that sizes look wrong. *e.g.* A photo where a person seems to hold a tall building between two fingers.

## Explanations
### What an illusion is · *defined*
**Definition:** An illusion is a time when what we see, hear or feel is different from what is really there. We find out what is really there by measuring or testing, for example with a ruler.

In an illusion, nothing is wrong with the eyes, ears or skin. They pick up what reaches them correctly. The mistake happens when the brain works out what that information means. Illusions are not rare tricks made only for puzzle books. They happen every day, for example when a train next to yours moves off and you feel your own train move.
- e.g. Two lines of exactly 10 cm, one with arrowheads and one with open fins. The open-fin line looks longer.
- e.g. Sitting in a train at Chennai Central, you feel your train start, but it is the train on the next track that is moving.
- e.g. The rising Moon looks bigger than the Moon high in the sky, though a photo shows they are the same size.
### The eyes and the brain see together · *defined*
**Definition:** The eyes catch light and turn it into nerve signals. The brain uses those signals, together with what it has learned about the world, to build what we see.

Light comes into each eye and lands on the retina, the thin layer at the back. The retina sends signals along the optic nerve to the brain. These signals only say how much light, and of what colour, landed at each spot. They do not say 'this is a mango, 30 cm away'. The brain works that out. It does this very fast, and nearly always it gets it right. That is why you can catch a ball or cross a road. An illusion is one of the rare times the brain's answer is wrong.
- e.g. The picture of a far-away bus on your retina is tiny, but you see a full-size bus.
- e.g. You see a whole room at once, though your eyes jump from spot to spot several times every second.
### The brain judges size from distance clues · *defined*
**Definition:** To judge how big something is, the brain also works out how far away it is. It uses clues such as lines that come together, things that hide other things, and how high something is in the view.

A coin held close makes a big picture in your eye. A car far away makes a small one. The brain knows this, so it makes far things look bigger than their picture in the eye. This is called size constancy. It means your mother walking down the road does not seem to shrink. But if the brain thinks something is far away when it is not, it makes that thing look too big. That is what happens in the railway-track drawing. Two equal strips of paper sit between two lines that come together. The brain reads the narrow end as far away, so the strip there looks longer. The Italian scientist Mario Ponzo described this in 1911, and it is called the Ponzo illusion.
- e.g. Two equal paper strips on a drawing of railway tracks. The one near the narrow end looks longer.
- e.g. From the 10th floor, people on the road look small, but you still see them as people of normal size far away.
### Why the arrow lines fool us · *debated*
In 1889 the German doctor Franz Carl Müller-Lyer published the arrow-lines drawing. A line with open fins looks longer than an equal line with arrowheads. Scientists agree that it fools almost everyone. They still argue about why.
- e.g. In tests, people often make the open-fin line 1 to 3 cm shorter than 10 cm before it looks equal, but results vary a lot from person to person.
- **Richard Gregory, a British scientist, and others:** The fins look like the edges of a corner. Open fins look like the inside corner of a room, which is far away. Arrowheads look like the outside corner of a building, which comes towards you. The brain makes the 'far' line look bigger, as it does on railway tracks.
- **Other scientists:** The illusion still works when the fins are circles or small squares, which do not look like corners at all. So they think the brain judges the length of the whole shape, fins and all, and mixes this into the length of the line.
- **Researchers who tested people around the world, in the 1960s:** People who grew up among square buildings and straight walls were fooled more than people who grew up with few straight walls and corners. This suggests the brain learns some of its clues from what it sees growing up. Other scientists say the tests were not fair, because the groups also differed in other ways.
### Light from above: bumps and dents · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain expects light to come from above, because sunlight and most lamps do. It uses this to decide whether a shaded shape sticks out or goes in.

Draw a circle that is light at the top and dark at the bottom, and it looks like a bump. Turn the page upside down, and the same circle looks like a dent. Nothing on the page changed. The brain assumed the light was coming from above, so it guessed which way the surface faces. The scientist V. S. Ramachandran, who was born in Tamil Nadu, studied this illusion and wrote about it in 1988.
- e.g. Shaded circles that look like bumps one way up and dents the other.
- e.g. A photo of footprints in sand, turned upside down, can look like raised feet.
### Still pictures that seem to move · *defined*
**Definition:** When still pictures with small differences are shown quickly one after another, the brain joins them into smooth movement. This is called apparent motion.

A film is a long row of still pictures called frames. Most cinema films show 24 frames every second. Nothing in a film really moves. For many years people said this works because each picture stays on the eye for a moment and blurs into the next. That is called 'persistence of vision'. In 1912 the scientist Max Wertheimer showed that this is not the answer. If pictures only blurred together, you would see two things at once, not one thing travelling. The brain builds the movement itself, and fills in the path in between.
- e.g. A flip book of a ball, which seems to bounce when you flip the pages.
- e.g. Two dots drawn on two pages. Flip between them, and one dot seems to jump across.
- e.g. Signs with rows of lights that switch on one after another seem to show a running arrow.
### Knowing the answer does not switch off the illusion · *idea*
After you measure the arrow lines, you know they are equal. Yet the open-fin line still looks longer. Most illusions work like this. The part of the brain that builds what you see does its work quickly and on its own, and it does not wait to ask what you know. Practice can weaken the arrow illusion a little. Scientists argue about how much what we know and expect can ever change what we see. Some say it changes seeing a lot, others say only a little.
- e.g. You know railway tracks stay the same width, yet they still look as if they meet.
- e.g. You know the train next to yours is moving, yet for a moment you still feel your own train move.
### Touch can be fooled too · *defined*
**Definition:** Illusions happen in all the senses, not only in seeing. The brain judges touch using a map of where each part of the skin usually is.

Cross your middle finger over your first finger. Now roll a pea between the two fingertips, with your eyes shut. Many people feel two peas. Normally, the outer sides of these two fingers never touch the same small thing at once. So when they both feel a pea, the brain decides there must be two. This was described by the Greek thinker Aristotle, more than 2,300 years ago, and it is called Aristotle's illusion.
- e.g. One pea between crossed fingers feels like two.
- e.g. A cold, dry steel plate can feel wet when you touch it.
### Animals that use illusions · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals live among other animals' eyes and brains, so some of them have bodies or habits that create illusions.

A small butterfly has a big problem: many birds want to eat them. The peacock pansy, common in Indian gardens, has large round eyespots on their wings. In experiments with the European peacock butterfly, small birds were often startled and backed away when the butterfly suddenly opened their wings and showed the eyespots. A bird's brain, like ours, takes two big round spots as a warning. Camouflage works the other way: colours and patterns help an animal disappear into the leaves or the ground. In Australia, male great bowerbirds lay out stones, bones and shells in front of the shelter they build to court females. In 2012 the scientist Laura Kelley and her colleague John Endler found that the males put small objects near the female and bigger ones further away. This makes the court look evenly patterned from where she watches. Males whose courts made a better illusion mated more often. Why it works so well is still being studied.
- e.g. A peacock pansy butterfly showing eyespots to a bird.
- e.g. A great bowerbird's court of grey stones, small near the shelter and larger further out.

## Mostly agreed
- Illusions fool almost everyone. They are not a sign of bad eyesight. *How we know:* The arrow lines and the railway-track drawing have been tested on thousands of people for more than 100 years.
- Seeing is done by the eyes and the brain together. The brain uses clues to work out size, distance, shape and movement. *How we know:* Illusions change when only the clues change, such as the fins on a line or the way a shaded circle is turned, while the light into the eye is the same kind.
- Films and cartoons work because the brain joins still pictures into movement. *How we know:* Max Wertheimer's tests in 1912, and many since, show people see one thing moving between two flashed lines, not two blurred lines.
- Most illusions keep working after you know the answer. *How we know:* People still see the arrow lines as unequal after measuring them. Practice weakens the illusion only a little.
- Illusions happen in touch, hearing and other senses, not only in seeing. *How we know:* Aristotle's crossed-finger illusion works for most people who try it.

## How we found out
**Max Wertheimer, with Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka, in Frankfurt, 1912.** When films came to cinema halls in the 1890s, people asked how still pictures could look like moving people. The popular answer was 'persistence of vision'. It said each picture stays on the eye for a moment and blurs into the next one. Toy makers had used this idea since the 1820s for spinning picture toys. In 1912, in Frankfurt, a scientist called Max Wertheimer tested how we see movement. He flashed a short line of light, and then a second line a little to one side, with a tiny gap of time in between. When the gap was long, people saw one line and then the other. When it was very short, they saw both lines at once. But at a gap in between, they saw something surprising: one line moving across, through the empty space between the two. Nothing had been there to see. If the pictures only blurred together on the eye, people would see two lines at once, not one line travelling. So the movement was being made by the brain. Two of the people in his tests, Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka, became famous scientists too. Many books still say films move because of persistence of vision, but scientists who study seeing say that is wrong.  
*What people believed before:* People thought films seem to move because each picture stays on the eye and blurs into the next ('persistence of vision'). Wertheimer showed that people see one line travelling between two flashed lines, which blurring cannot explain. The brain makes the movement.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Seeing is believing: if I saw it, it must be true.** (A very common saying; *rejected*)  
  For: Our eyes are right almost all the time, so we learn to trust them.  
  Against: The arrow lines, the railway tracks and the moving train show that the brain can give the wrong answer. A ruler or a careful test can catch it.
- **Films move because each picture stays on the eye and blurs into the next ('persistence of vision').** (Many school books, toy boxes and websites; *rejected*)  
  For: An image does fade slowly when you close your eyes after looking at a bright light.  
  Against: Blurring would show two things at once, not one thing moving. Wertheimer's tests in 1912 showed the brain builds the movement itself.
- **If you know about an illusion, it stops working.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Once you know the trick, you stop believing the lines are different.  
  Against: Measure the arrow lines and look again. The open-fin line still looks longer. What you believe changes, but what you see mostly does not.
- **Horizontal stripes make a person look wider.** (A common rule in clothes shops; *debated*)  
  For: Many people feel that stripes going across the body make it look broad.  
  Against: In a test in 2011, Peter Thompson and Kyriaki Mikellidou found that a figure with horizontal stripes looked slightly thinner and taller, as the scientist Hermann von Helmholtz had noticed with striped squares in the 1860s. Other studies found the effect depends on the body shape.
- **Some groups of people are not fooled by the arrow lines at all.** (Reports of tests around the world in the 1960s; *debated*)  
  For: In those tests, people who grew up without square buildings were fooled less.  
  Against: They were still fooled, only less. And the groups differed in schooling and in how used they were to pictures on paper, so it is hard to say what caused the difference.

## Roots
We have seen equal lines look unequal, still pictures seem to move, and one pea feel like two. Now we ask what illusions tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the arrow test, the ruler showed both lines were 10 cm long. Even after you knew, the line with open fins still looked longer. Knowing the answer did not change what you saw. So the part of you that sees can disagree with the part that knows.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk as if each of us is one single looker, so that knowing the truth should change what we see. The arrow test shows that seeing keeps its own answer, even while knowing holds another.  
*Evidence:* In the arrow test, people still copied the open-fin line longer than 10 cm after the ruler had shown all the lines were 10 cm. Scientists find the same in many illusions: knowing about them hardly weakens them.

- *who am i:* You are not one single looker. Different parts of your mind work at the same time, and they can give different answers.
- *what is life:* Every animal's brain judges the world from clues. A great bowerbird even lays out stones to play with a female's sense of distance.
- *what is this world:* The world you see is your brain's best judgement from light. A ruler, a test or another person helps you check it.

The same light reaches every eye that looks. Each brain, a bee's, a bowerbird's or yours, builds a picture of that light from the clues it uses. Inside you, too, seeing and knowing each build their own answer. None of these pictures is the whole of what is there. Comparing them, with a ruler or with each other, is how we come closer to it.

- **Udana 6.4 (Pali Canon), the blind men and the elephant:** Some men who could not see each touched one part of an elephant. One felt the leg and said an elephant is like a pillar. One felt the ear and said it is like a winnowing basket. Each was sure, and they quarrelled. (Each man's touch gave a true clue but a wrong whole. In an illusion, your eyes give true light, and your brain builds a wrong whole from the clues. Checking with a ruler is like asking the others what they felt.)
- **Gaudapada, Mandukya Karika 2.17:** A rope seen unclearly in the dark is taken to be a snake. When the rope is seen for what it is, the snake is gone. (In the rope story, seeing clearly removes the mistake. The arrow lines are different: even after you know the truth, the open-fin line still looks longer. The mistake and the knowing sit side by side.)
- Science (settled): Most illusions stay strong after you know about them. Practice weakens the arrow-lines illusion only a little.
- Science (debated): How much what we know and expect can change what we see is still argued over.
- Other view, Scientists who study how expectation shapes seeing: What we know changes what we see all the time. You can read a messy word in a sentence because you expect it. On this view, seeing and knowing are woven together, and the arrow lines are a rare exception, not the rule.
- Practice, Look twice (3 min): Let's put the arrow lines in front of us. Look at them quietly and breathe slowly, five times. Notice that the open-fin line looks longer. Now lay the ruler on them and read 10 and 10. Look again. Notice that it still looks longer. Say quietly, 'My eyes see one thing. I know another.' Sit with both for three more breaths.
- Ask: When the line still looks longer after you have measured it, which answer is yours: the one you see or the one you know?
- Ask: A bee, a bowerbird and you look at the same garden. Is one of you seeing it right?
- Ask: If your brain is always judging from clues, how can you check a judgement you do not notice it making?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your eyes caught the light correctly, but your brain got the length wrong. So who was fooled: your eyes, your brain, or you? (hint: Think about which part did the measuring, which part did the seeing, and which part knew the answer.)
- *big:* If your brain is always guessing from clues, how do you know any of the things you see are real? (hint: What did you use to check the arrow lines? What else could you use to check what you see?)
- *deep:* A film is only still pictures. Is the movement you see in a film real? (hint: Is there movement on the screen, or only in you? Does it matter, if everyone in the cinema hall sees it?)
- *big:* A bird is startled by a butterfly's eyespots. Is the bird fooled, or is the bird being careful? (hint: What would happen to a bird who ignored every eye-like shape?)
- *deep:* Why would a brain use shortcuts that can be wrong, instead of always measuring carefully? (hint: How long would it take you to measure everything before crossing a road?)
- *big:* If a bee, a bowerbird and you all look at the same garden, are you seeing the same world? (hint: Each brain uses its own clues. Is there a 'real' picture that none of you sees?)

## Story: The Train That Did Not Move
*The illusions in this story are real and easy to try: the train on the next track that seems to make your own train move, rails that seem to meet in the distance, the Moon that seems to follow a moving train, and the railway-track drawing (the Ponzo illusion). Kavya, Arjun, their mother and this journey are made up.*

Kavya was nine, and her brother Arjun was seven. They were going to Madurai on the night train from Chennai Egmore, with their mother. Amma worked for the railways. Her job was checking the signals that tell trains when to stop and when to go.

The train stood at the platform. Arjun had the window seat, and he pressed his nose to the glass. "I see everything," he said. "My eyes never get anything wrong."

Just then, the window filled with the side of another train. It began to slide past. "We're moving!" Arjun shouted. "We're going to Madurai!"

Kavya looked out of the window on the other side, at the platform. A tea seller stood by a pillar with his flask. The pillar did not move. The tea seller did not move. "We're not moving," she said. "It's the other train."

Arjun did not believe her. Then the other train slid away, and behind it was the next platform, perfectly still. Their own train had not moved at all.

"But I felt us go," said Arjun. "I really felt it."

Amma smiled. "Your eyes saw a whole window full of something moving. Your brain had to decide what was moving. Usually, when everything outside the window slides past, it is because you are moving. So your brain made that guess. This time, the guess was wrong."

Ten minutes later, the train really did move. Lights and poles and houses rushed past the window. Then Arjun saw the Moon, big and round above the buildings.

He watched it for a long time. "Amma," he whispered. "The Moon is following us."

Kavya watched too. The poles near the track flew past. The houses further away went past more slowly. The Moon stayed in the same place in the window. "The near things go fast and the far things go slowly," she said. "And the Moon is very, very far."

"That's it," said Amma. "The Moon is so far away that, however far we travel tonight, it stays in the same place in our window. So it looks as if it is coming with us."

In the morning the train stopped at a small station before Madurai. The children got down to stretch their legs. Arjun stood at the end of the platform and looked along the track. The two shining rails ran far into the distance, closer and closer together, until they seemed to meet.

"The rails get narrow over there," he said. "Won't the train fall off?"

Kavya laughed. "The rails are the same distance apart all the way. Otherwise the wheels wouldn't fit."

Amma took out her notebook. She drew two long lines that came closer together at the top, like the rails. She tore two small strips from the edge of the page, and used her measuring tape to make them exactly the same length, 3 cm each. She put one strip across the lines near the top, where they were close together. She put the other across near the bottom.

"Which strip is longer?" she asked.

"The top one," said Arjun at once. "Easy."

Kavya swapped the strips. Now the other strip, at the top, looked longer. Arjun's mouth fell open. He measured them with the tape himself. Both were 3 cm.

"So my eyes lie," he said slowly.

"Your eyes caught the light correctly," said Amma. "It was your brain that made a guess. Lines that come together are a clue that something is far away. So your brain thought the top strip was far away. Something far away that still looks that big must be really big. So it showed you a bigger strip."

Arjun looked at the drawing again. "But I know they're the same now," he said. "And the top one still looks longer!"

"Knowing doesn't switch it off," said Kavya. "I've tried."

"Your brain makes guesses like this many times every second," said Amma. "Nearly always, they are right. That is how you can catch a ball, or see that a bus far away is big, not tiny. An illusion is one of the rare times the guess is wrong. That is why scientists love illusions. They show the rules the brain uses."

The whistle blew, and they climbed back on. As the train rolled into Madurai, Arjun was drawing rails in his own notebook, with two strips of paper on them.

"I'm going to fool Paati," he said.

Kavya grinned. "First we measure," she said. "Then we fool."

**For the parent:** For the parent: in all three illusions, Arjun's eyes did their job correctly. His brain then used a clue that is usually right. When everything in the window moves, it is usually because you are moving. When lines come together, they are usually going into the distance, and something far away that makes a big picture in the eye must be big. Each time, the clue led to the wrong answer. The Moon seems to follow the train because it is so far away that its direction hardly changes as you travel, while near things sweep past. Knowing the truth did not stop the railway-track strips looking different, which is the point the child tests in the arrow-lines experiment.

- Ask: Why did Arjun feel his train move when it was standing still?
- Ask: How did Kavya find out which train was really moving?
- Ask: Why did the strip of paper near the top of the rails look longer?
- Ask: Arjun measured the strips and knew they were equal. What happened when he looked again?
- Ask: Kavya said, 'First we measure. Then we fool.' Why do you think she wanted to measure first?

### e1 · The Arrow Lines: how much does your brain stretch a line? (35 min)
- **question:** Two lines are both 10 cm long. One has arrowheads, and one has fins that open outwards. When people copy each line by eye, how long do they draw it? And does knowing the answer change that?
- **predict:** Guess first. When people copy each line by eye, will they draw it the right length, too long or too short? Will they still get it wrong after they know all the lines are 10 cm? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every line is exactly 10 cm, with fins of the same size, on the same kind of paper strip. Everyone copies in the same way, by eye, starting from a dot, with no ruler. Only the fins change. A plain line with no fins shows how well each person copies anyway, so we can see how much the fins added.
- **safety:** No special safety needs. Scissors for cutting the paper are used by a grown-up or with a grown-up's help.
- **record:** Fill in the table, one row for each person. Then circle the biggest difference from 10 cm.
- **results table:** Person | Plain line (cm) | Arrowhead line (cm) | Open-fin line (cm) | Open-fin line again, after knowing (cm)
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut each sheet in half the long way, to make long strips. 2. Lay a strip sideways. Starting 2 cm from the left edge, draw a line exactly 10 cm long. 3. Make 4 strips for each person. 4. Strip A: leave the line plain. 5. Strip B: add arrowheads at both ends, like <——>. 6. Strip C and strip D: add fins that open outwards at both ends, like >——<. 7. On each strip, put a dot 4 cm to the right of the line's right end.  
  *Parent says:* We'll make test strips. Every line is exactly 10 cm. Only the ends are different. Can you help me measure?  
  *Child mode:* Help draw the lines. Each one is exactly 10 cm. Strip B gets arrowheads. Strips C and D get open fins.
  - Note: Make each fin 2 cm long, at about the angle of the arms of a letter V. Measure the 10 cm only along the straight line, between the two points where the fins meet it. Keep strip D hidden until step 5. Each person needs their own 4 strips, so nobody sees another person's copy.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay strips A, B and C in front of your child. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Each person will copy each line by eye, starting at the dot. Will they draw it too long, too short or just right? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at strips A, B and C. Will people copy them too long, too short or just right? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen with the open-fin line, and why? (good: It will be drawn too long, because it looks longer.; Just right, because it is really 10 cm.; Any guess with a reason is good.)
  - Note: Write the guess down. Do not say whether it is right. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hide the ruler. 2. Give the first person strip A and a pencil. 3. Say: 'Start at the dot. Draw a straight line to the right, as long as the line on the left. Don't count the fins.' 4. Do the same with strip B, then strip C. 5. Write the person's name on each strip. 6. Repeat with each person.  
  *Parent says:* Start at the dot. Draw a line as long as the line on the left. Take your time. Don't count the fins.  
  *Child mode:* Start at the dot. Draw a line just as long as the other one. Don't count the fins. No ruler!
  - Note: Your child can be a tester too. Copy the lines yourself after your child, on your own strips. Let each person look back and forth as much as they like, but they must not use their fingers or the pencil to measure.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure each copied line with the ruler, from the dot to the end, to the nearest half centimetre. Write each number in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure every copy. How far from 10 cm is each one?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each copy with the ruler. Write the number in the table.
  - Check: Meena, a girl in Class 3, copied the open-fin line. Her copy was 11.5 cm long. The real line is 10 cm. What does this show? → **The open fins made the line look longer to Meena**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show everyone the ruler on strips A, B and C, so they see all three lines are 10 cm. 2. Give each person strip D, with open fins. 3. Say: 'You know it's 10 cm now. Copy it again.' 4. Measure and write it in the last column.  
  *Parent says:* Now everybody knows the truth. All the lines are 10 cm. Will knowing help? Let's try the open-fin line once more.  
  *Child mode:* Now you know every line is 10 cm. Copy the open-fin line again. Does knowing help?
  - Note: Ask people to copy what they see, not to correct on purpose. Some people will draw it a bit shorter than before. Most will still draw it longer than 10 cm.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the fins fool people about the line's length, even when they know. What I saw: plain lines were copied at about 10 cm. Arrowhead lines were copied shorter, about 8.5 cm, and open-fin lines longer, about 11.5 cm. After we knew, the open-fin copies were still about 11 cm. Why: the brain uses the fins as a clue to the length, and knowing does not switch that off. Your own numbers will be different. Any difference larger than the plain line's error counts.
### e2 · Crossed Fingers: can one pea feel like two? (20 min)
- **question:** With your eyes shut, can you tell whether one pea or two peas are touching your fingertips? Does crossing your fingers change your answer?
- **predict:** Guess first. With your fingers normal, will you get one and two right every time? With your fingers crossed, will you still get it right? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same fingers, the same peas and the same gentle touch every time. Do 10 tries with fingers normal and 10 with fingers crossed. Use a written list for whether it is one pea or two, so the helper does not give a clue. Only the crossing changes.
- **safety:** Do not put the peas in your mouth, nose or ears. Keep them away from babies and toddlers.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Count how many of the 10 were right with fingers normal, and how many with fingers crossed.
- **results table:** Try | Fingers normal or crossed | Really one or two? | I said | Right?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write a list of 20 tries. 2. For each try, toss a coin. Heads means one pea, tails means two peas. 3. Mark tries 1 to 10 'normal' and tries 11 to 20 'crossed'.  
  *Parent says:* We'll make a secret list. The coin decides one pea or two, so I can't give you a clue.  
  *Child mode:* The coin chooses one pea or two. Then nobody can guess the pattern.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* With your eyes shut, will you always know if it's one pea or two? What about with your fingers crossed? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Will you always know? What if your fingers are crossed? Why do you think so?
  - Note: Write the guess down. Don't explain anything yet.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child shuts their eyes and holds the first and middle fingers side by side. 2. For one pea, gently roll it between the two fingertips, so it touches both. 3. For two peas, touch one pea to each fingertip at the same time. 4. Your child says 'one' or 'two'. 5. Write it down. Do tries 1 to 10.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes shut. Fingers side by side. Is it one pea or two?  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Keep your fingers side by side. One pea or two?
  - Note: Roll the single pea gently back and forth for about two seconds, touching both fingertips. Keep the same light touch every time.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child crosses the middle finger over the first finger. 2. The single pea now goes in the small gap between the two crossed fingertips, touching the outer sides of both. 3. For two peas, touch one to each fingertip. 4. Do tries 11 to 20 and write the answers.  
  *Parent says:* Now cross your fingers. Same game. One pea or two?  
  *Child mode:* Cross your middle finger over your first finger. Shut your eyes. One pea or two?
  - Note: Most people feel two peas when one pea is rolled between crossed fingertips. It may help to roll the pea a little more. Some people feel one round pea that seems strangely wide.
  - Check: Farhan, a boy in Class 3, crossed his fingers. One pea was rolled between his fingertips, and he felt two. Why? → **His brain expects those two finger sides to touch two different things**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the right answers for tries 1 to 10 and for tries 11 to 20. Then ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* How many did you get right with fingers normal? With fingers crossed? What do you think now, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your right answers. Normal fingers? Crossed fingers? What do you think now? Why?
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: crossing my fingers fools my sense of touch. What I saw: with fingers normal I got 10 out of 10 right. With fingers crossed I got 6 out of 10, and most mistakes were feeling two peas when there was one. Why: my brain uses a map of where my fingers usually are, and crossing them puts the fingers in the wrong place on that map.

- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Take several photos, moving the near thing a little each time until they seem to touch.  
  *Parent says:* Keep trying. Move a little, then look at the photo.  
  *Child mode:* Take a photo. Look at it. Move a little. Try again.

## Applications
- Films, cartoons and video games show still frames quickly so that we see movement.
- Road markings: some cities paint lines closer and closer together before a speed breaker, so drivers feel they are going faster and slow down.
- Artists paint flat murals with perspective, so a wall seems to have steps or a window in it.
- Photographers and film-makers use forced perspective to make sets and actors look bigger or smaller.
- Tailors and clothes designers use stripes and patterns to change how tall or wide clothes look.
- Doctors and scientists use illusions to study how the brain works, and to check how vision develops in children.
- Running-light signs at shops and railway stations switch bulbs on one after another, so an arrow seems to move.
- Camouflage patterns are printed on soldiers' uniforms, copying the way animals hide.

## Spotted around us
- Railway tracks or a long straight road that seem to meet far away.
- A long corridor in your block of flats that seems to get narrower at the far end.
- The train next to yours at a station starts moving, and you feel your own train move.
- The Moon seeming to follow your car or bus at night.
- A running-light arrow on a shop sign or a railway station board.
- A peacock pansy butterfly showing their eyespots in a garden or a park.
- A rangoli or kolam drawn with shading, light on top and dark below, so that a flat drawing on the floor looks raised.

## Open questions
- **Why exactly do the arrow lines fool us?** Scientists have argued for more than 100 years. The corner idea, the whole-shape idea and others each explain some results but not all.
- **Why does the Moon look bigger near the horizon?** Photos show it is the same size, so it is an illusion. Scientists have several explanations about how the brain judges the Moon's distance, but none is agreed.
- **How much can what we know change what we see?** Some scientists say knowledge and expectation change seeing a lot. Others say the first stage of seeing works on its own. Experiments give different answers.
- **How do our separate guesses about colour, shape, size and movement join into one picture?** Different parts of the brain handle different features. How they come together into one experience is not understood.
- **What do other animals see in our illusions?** Some birds, fish and bees respond to some illusions as we do, and not to others. Testing what an animal sees is hard, because they cannot tell us in words.

## Connected maths
- The real line in the arrow test is 10 cm. Kavya copied the arrowhead line at 8.5 cm. How much too short was her copy? **1.5 cm too short.**. The real line is 10 cm and Kavya's copy is 8.5 cm. 10 − 8.5 = 1.5. Check by adding: 8.5 + 1.5 = 10. So her copy was 1.5 cm too short. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Three people copied the open-fin line. Kavya drew 11.5 cm, Amma 12 cm and Paati 11 cm. The real line is 10 cm. Who was fooled the most, and who the least? **Amma was fooled the most (2 cm too long). Paati was fooled the least (1 cm too long).**. Take 10 cm away from each copy. Kavya: 11.5 − 10 = 1.5 cm. Amma: 12 − 10 = 2 cm. Paati: 11 − 10 = 1 cm. 2 is the biggest and 1 is the smallest. But check each person's plain line too. If Amma also copied the plain line too long, part of her 2 cm is ordinary copying. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- In the crossed-fingers test, Revathi got 10 out of 10 right with fingers normal. With fingers crossed she got 6 out of 10 right. How many more did she get wrong with fingers crossed? What fraction of the crossed tries were wrong? **4 more wrong. 4 out of 10, or 4/10, of the crossed tries were wrong.**. Normal: 10 − 10 = 0 wrong. Crossed: 10 − 6 = 4 wrong. 4 − 0 = 4 more. 4 wrong out of 10 tries is the fraction 4/10, which is the same as 2/5. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- A cinema film shows 24 frames every second. How many still pictures does it show in 5 seconds? **120 pictures.**. 5 seconds of 24 pictures is 5 groups of 24. 24 + 24 + 24 + 24 + 24 = 120. Or 5 × 24 = 120. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Illusion Museum, 8 visitors looked at each illusion. The arrow lines fooled 7, the railway tracks fooled 6, the ring of circles fooled 5 and the bumps and dents fooled 8. Draw a bar chart. Which illusion fooled everyone? **The bumps and dents fooled all 8 visitors.**. Draw one bar for each illusion: 7, 6, 5 and 8. The tallest bar is the bumps and dents at 8, which is every visitor. The ring of circles fooled the fewest, 5. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)

## Warm-up

- Which part of you decides how long a line looks: your eyes or your brain? → Your brain, using what the eyes send it.

- Does knowing about an illusion make it go away? → Usually not. It still looks the same.
- How many still pictures does a cinema film usually show each second? → 24.
- Which way does your brain expect light to come from? → From above.
