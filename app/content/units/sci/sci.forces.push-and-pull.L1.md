# Pushes and pulls: what makes things start to move
`sci.forces.push-and-pull.L1`

**Summary.** A ball on the floor stays still until something pushes or pulls it. Your child pushes boxes, pulls toys, opens doors and watches ants, and finds that every push or pull can start a thing moving. A bigger push sends a thing further, and a heavy thing needs a bigger push. A push moves a thing away from you, and a pull moves it towards you. Scientists call both of them a force.

**Intro.** *Put a ball on the floor. Watch it for one whole minute. Does it move by itself?* The ball sat still. Nobody touched it, so it stayed where it was. Now give it a little push with one finger. Off it rolls! A push made it move. In this unit you find out what pushes and pulls do. You also spot them all around you: at home, in toys, in the playground and in big machines.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say if a thing was pushed or pulled, by looking at which way it moved: away from me or towards me.
1. By the end I can show that a push or a pull can make a still thing start to move. I know scientists call every push and pull a force.
2. By the end I can show that a bigger push makes a thing go further.
3. By the end I can say that a heavy thing needs a bigger push or pull to move it.
4. By the end I can spot pushes and pulls at home, in toys, in the playground and in machines.

## Terms
- **Push**: A push moves a thing away from you. *e.g.* You push a door to close it, and you push a swing to make it go.
- **Pull**: A pull moves a thing towards you. *e.g.* You pull a drawer open, and you pull a toy along on a string.
- **Force**: Force is the scientists' word for any push or any pull. *e.g.* When you kick a ball, your foot gives the ball a force.
- **Machine**: A machine is something people make to help them push or pull. *e.g.* A tractor pulls a trolley full of sugarcane. A bulldozer pushes soil.

## Explanations
### A push or a pull makes a still thing move · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing that is still stays still until something pushes or pulls it. Scientists call every push and every pull a force.

The ball on the floor stayed still for a whole minute. It started to roll only when your finger pushed it. The box in the box test stayed by the start line until your hand pushed it. A swing hangs still until someone pushes it. A pull works too: a toy on a string moves when you pull the string. Scientists use one word for every push and every pull. They call it a force.
- e.g. In the box test, the box stayed still until your hand pushed it.
- e.g. A football sits on the ground until someone kicks it.
- e.g. A bucket of water comes up from a well only when someone pulls the rope.
### Push means away, pull means towards · *defined*
**Definition:** A push moves a thing away from you. A pull moves a thing towards you. Which word you use depends on where you are standing.

When you close a fridge, the door moves away from you, so you push it. When you open it, the door comes towards you, so you pull it. In the Two-Sided Door test, you closed the same door twice. From one side you pushed it. From the other side you pulled it. The door swung the same way both times. So push and pull are both forces. The name tells us where the pusher or puller is standing.
- e.g. In the Two-Sided Door test, the door shut the same way when you pushed it and when you pulled it.
- e.g. A fishing boat on the beach can be pushed from the back or pulled by a rope from the front. Either way, it slides towards the sea.
- e.g. Opening a drawer is a pull. Closing it is a push.
### A bigger push sends a thing further · *defined*
**Definition:** A gentle push makes a thing move slowly, and it stops soon. A big push makes it move fast, and it goes further.

In the box test, your gentle push sent the empty box only a little way. Your big push sent the same box much further. A big push makes the box move faster. The floor rubs on the box and slows it down. A fast box takes longer to slow down, so it slides further before it stops. This is why you kick a ball hard to send it far.
- e.g. In the box test, the gentle push sent the box about one foot step. The big push sent it many steps.
- e.g. A bigger push on a swing sends it higher.
- e.g. A carrom striker flicked hard shoots across the board. A soft flick stops halfway.
### A heavy thing needs a bigger push or pull · *defined*
**Definition:** A heavy thing is harder to start moving than a light thing. It needs a bigger push or a bigger pull.

When you filled the box with rice, the same big push sent it only a short way. In the Pull-Along Train, the rubber band stretched a little for one box. It stretched much longer for three boxes. A longer stretch means a bigger pull. So heavy things need bigger pushes and pulls. That is why people push a stuck car together, and why a tractor, a big machine, pulls a heavy trolley.
- e.g. In the box test, the full box slid only a short way after a big push.
- e.g. In the Pull-Along Train, the rubber band stretched longest for three loaded boxes.
- e.g. Many people together push a heavy fishing boat down the beach. One person alone cannot.

## Mostly agreed
- Every push and every pull is a force. *How we know:* Scientists use the word force for all of them: a kick, a tug, the wind on a sail, a magnet pulling a pin, and the Earth pulling a mango down from a tree.
- A still thing starts to move only when a force acts on it. *How we know:* Anyone can test it. A ball left on a flat floor stays put. It moves when a push, a pull, a slope or a gust of wind acts on it.
- A heavier thing needs a bigger push or pull to move it the same way. *How we know:* An empty box slides far with one push, but a full one hardly moves. People use machines and many hands to move very heavy things.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, a Greek thinker; John Philoponus, a thinker in Alexandria, Egypt; Ibn Sina, a doctor and thinker in Central Asia and Persia; Isaac Newton, an English scientist, From about 350 BCE to 1687.** When you throw a ball, your hand pushes it. Then the ball leaves your hand and keeps flying. What keeps it going? About 2,350 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle gave an answer. He said the air behind a thrown stone keeps pushing it along. For hundreds of years, many people agreed with him. About 1,500 years ago, John Philoponus, a thinker in Alexandria in Egypt, said this was wrong. If air pushed stones along, he wrote, we could make a stone fly just by moving air hard behind it. But we cannot. He said the throw gives the stone something that keeps it going for a while. About 1,000 years ago, Ibn Sina, a doctor and thinker in Central Asia and Persia, built on this idea. He said the air does not help the stone along. It pushes against the stone and slows it down. In 1687, Isaac Newton in England wrote the rule we use today. A push or a pull starts a thing moving. Then it keeps moving until another push or pull slows it down, stops it or turns it.  
*What people believed before:* People thought the air behind a thrown stone keeps pushing it along. Philoponus showed that moving air cannot throw a stone, and Ibn Sina said the air only slows it down.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only people and other animals can push and pull.** (A common idea among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: People push doors, ants pull crumbs, and an elephant pushes a log. These are pushes and pulls we can see a body making.  
  Against: The wind pushes a sailing boat. A magnet pulls a steel pin without touching it. The Earth pulls a mango down from a tree. Pushes and pulls come from many places.
- **A heavy thing can never be moved.** (What it can feel like when a big box will not budge; *rejected*)  
  For: A child pushing a cupboard alone may not move it at all.  
  Against: A bigger push moves it: two people, a whole family, or a machine. Ships much heavier than houses are pulled by small, strong boats called tugboats.
- **After you throw a ball, the air behind the ball keeps pushing it along.** (The Greek thinker Aristotle, about 2,350 years ago, and many people after him; *rejected*)  
  For: The ball keeps flying after it leaves your hand, so it seems something must keep pushing it.  
  Against: The air does not push the ball along. It rubs against the ball and slows it down. A moving ball keeps going by itself until a push or pull slows it, stops it or turns it.

## Roots
We have pushed boxes, pulled toys and closed a door from both sides. Now we ask what the door that swung the same way both times tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You closed one door by pushing it from one side and pulling it from the other. The door swung the same way both times. A push is a force away from you, and a pull is a force towards you. So whether a force is a push or a pull depends on where you stand.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of push and pull as two opposite kinds of thing. The door test shows the same force on the same door gets either name. The name tells us where the person is standing.  
*Evidence:* In the Two-Sided Door, the child closed the same door twice, once pushing from inside and once pulling from outside. The door swung and shut the same way both times. In the story, Selvi's push and Muthu's pull both sent the boat towards the sea.

- *who am i:* You are a pusher and a puller. Every jump pushes the floor, and every hug pulls someone close.
- *what is life:* People and other animals move by pushing and pulling. An ant pulls a grain of rice, an elephant pushes a log, and a plant's roots push down through the soil.
- *what is this world:* Things in the world start to move when they are pushed or pulled. The wind pushes the clouds, and the Earth pulls every falling mango down.

Pushes and pulls come from many places: your hands, the wind, a magnet, the Earth and a line of ants. Scientists give them all one name, force. Every force is a meeting of two things, such as your hand and the door. Everything is aware in its own way, so the door meets your hand just as your hand meets the door. Many scientists say only living beings with nerves can feel a touch.

- **Tao Te Ching 2:** Long and short shape each other. High and low lean on each other. Front and back follow each other. (Push and pull are a pair like front and back. The same force on the same door is called a push from one side and a pull from the other.)
- **Udana 6.4 (a Buddhist text):** People who could not see each touched one part of an elephant. The one who felt the head said an elephant is like a pot. The one who felt the ear said it is like a winnowing basket. The one who felt a leg said it is like a post. Each told the truth about their own part. (A child inside the room says 'I pushed the door'. A friend outside says 'I pulled it'. Each is right about the same door, from where they stand.)
- Science (settled): Scientists call every push and every pull a force. A force can start a thing moving, stop it, or change the way it goes.
- Science (open): Gravity, the Earth's pull, works without touching. Nobody yet fully understands what gravity is at the very tiniest scale.
- Other view, Builders and engineers: For the thing being pushed or pulled, they are really different. A pull stretches a rope, and a push squashes a pillar. A rope can pull a boat, but it cannot push one. So builders always ask whether a part must take a push or a pull.
- Practice, Two hands, one push (3 min): Press your palms together in front of your chest. Push them gently into each other. Count slowly to five. Feel both arms. Now hook your fingers together and gently pull your hands apart. Count to five again. Let your hands rest on your knees. Which hand was pushing? Which hand was pulling?
- Ask: When your two hands pushed each other, which hand was doing the pushing?
- Ask: Can you think of something that is a push for one person and a pull for another?
- Ask: What else in this room can be pushed or pulled, and by whom?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the box test, your hand let go at the start line. Why did the box keep sliding for a while after that? (hint: Nothing pushed it after your hand let go. What slowed it down in the end? Try it on a mat and on tiles.)
- *deep:* Could you move a boat using only pulls? Could you move it using only pushes? (hint: Think of a rope at the front and hands at the back. Which way does the boat go each time?)
- *big:* Right now, is anything near you being pushed or pulled, with nobody touching it? (hint: Drop a spoon. What pulled it down? Think of the wind, a magnet and the Earth.)
- *big:* Can a push or a pull make a moving thing stop? (hint: Roll a ball across the floor, then catch it. What did your hands do?)

## Story: The Boat on the Sand
*Fishing families on the coasts of Tamil Nadu and Kerala really do push their boats down the beach together, and pull long nets in from the sea by hand, in a line on the rope. Paati means grandmother in Tamil. Selvi, Paati, Amma and Muthu are made up.*

Selvi was six. She lived in a fishing village by the sea in Tamil Nadu. Every morning she woke up to the sound of the waves.

Her grandmother, Paati, mended fishing nets on the sand. Paati knew every boat on the beach.

One morning, a blue boat lay high up on the sand, far from the water. Selvi's Amma and five other fishers were going out to sea in it.

'First the boat must get to the water,' said Paati. 'Everyone, push!'

The fishers put their hands on the back of the boat. They leaned and pushed. The boat slid a little way down the sand.

Selvi ran to help. She put both hands on the back of the boat and pushed as hard as she could. Her feet sank into the wet sand.

Her little cousin Muthu was four. He ran to the front of the boat and grabbed the rope.

'I'm helping too!' he shouted. He leaned back and pulled.

Selvi laughed. 'Muthu, you're doing it wrong. I'm pushing, and you're pulling!'

Paati looked at the boat. 'Is it going the wrong way?' she asked.

Selvi looked. The boat was sliding towards the sea. Her push sent it that way. Muthu's pull sent it that way too.

'From the back, you push the boat away from you,' said Paati. 'From the front, Muthu pulls it towards him. The boat goes to the sea both times.'

With one more big push, the boat splashed into the waves. Amma waved. The engine roared, and the boat went out to sea.

Later, Selvi tried to push an old wooden boat by herself. She pushed and pushed. It did not move at all.

'It's too heavy,' she said, and sat down on the sand.

'One push is small,' said Paati. 'Six pushes together are big. That is why we all push together.'

Selvi found an empty fish basket instead. One little push, and it slid right across the sand. 'A light thing needs only a little push,' she told Muthu.

In the evening, the fishers came back. Then it was time to bring in the big net. Out in the sea, a long net was full of fish. Two long ropes ran from it to the beach.

Everyone stood in a line on the rope: Paati at the front, then Selvi, then Muthu at the very end. They pulled together, hand over hand.

Slowly, slowly, the net came up out of the sea. Silver fish jumped and flapped on the sand.

On the way home, Selvi stopped. A line of ants was crossing the path. Four ants were pulling a grain of rice. One more ant pushed from behind.

'Look, Muthu,' said Selvi. 'They're doing it like us. Some pull and some push.'

At home, Selvi went into the house first. Muthu was still outside. She pushed the door to close it.

At the same moment, Muthu grabbed the handle from outside and pulled.

The door swung shut with a soft bang.

'I pushed it!' said Selvi.

'No, I pulled it!' said Muthu.

Paati smiled at them both. 'You are both right,' she said. 'It all depends on where you stand.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: Selvi finds that her push from the back and Muthu's pull from the front both move the boat the same way, towards the sea. Push or pull depends on where you stand. She also finds that a heavy boat needs many pushes together, while a light basket needs only one small push.

- Ask: Selvi pushed the boat and Muthu pulled it. Which way did the boat go?
- Ask: Why could Selvi not move the old wooden boat by herself?
- Ask: How did the fishers bring the big net out of the sea?
- Ask: At the end, who closed the door, Selvi or Muthu? Why does Paati say they are both right?

## Games
### g1 · Push or Pull? The quick-hands card game (10 min)
- **rules:** Mix the 12 cards and put them face down.; One person is the caller. The others are the players. A grown-up reads for a caller who cannot read yet.; The caller turns over the top card, keeping the answer on the back hidden, and reads the front out loud.; Every player shows a sign as fast as they can: an open palm moving away for PUSH, a fist moving in for PULL.; The caller reads the answer on the back. A player who showed the right sign and can say why gets a point.; For a card that says BOTH, show both signs.; After 3 cards, the next person becomes the caller. With 2 people you take turns. With 4 people everyone calls 3 cards.; When all 12 cards are read, add up your points. The most points wins. If you tie, you all win.

## Experiments
### e1 · Little Push, Big Push: how far will the box slide? (15 min)
- **question:** Will a big push send the box further than a gentle push? Will a full box slide as far as an empty one?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the gentle push or the big push send the box further? Will the full box go as far as the empty one? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same box, the same floor and the same start line every time. We let go of the box at the start line each time. We try each push twice, because one try can be luck.
- **record:** Draw the start line and the marks. Draw a small box at each place it stopped. Count the foot steps and write the number, or a grown-up writes it.
- **results table:** Push | My guess: near or far? | How far it went (in my foot steps)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the dupatta across the floor as a start line. 2. Put the empty box just behind the line. 3. Show your child how to kneel and push, letting go at the line.  
  *Parent says:* This box is still. Will it move by itself? Let's wait and see. Now watch: I push it, and I let go at the line.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the box. Is it moving? Now watch the push.
  - Note: Clear the floor of toys and furniture. A mat or carpet stops the box too fast, so use bare tiles or stone. Keep feet out of the box's path. Push big, but not as hard as you can, so the box does not hit a wall.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess before any pushes. Write or draw their guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* You will give the box a gentle push, and then a big push. Which one will send it further? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Gentle push or big push: which goes further? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The big push, because it is stronger.; The gentle one, because it goes slowly and keeps going.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Don't say what will happen.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child gives the empty box a gentle push and lets go at the line. 2. Put a coin where it stops. 3. Then a big push. 4. Put a spoon where it stops. 5. Count foot steps, heel to toe, from the line to each mark.  
  *Parent says:* First a gentle push. Let go at the line. Where did it stop? Now a big push. Where did it stop this time? Let's count the steps.  
  *Child mode:* Give a gentle push. Mark where it stops. Now a big push. Mark it. Count your steps.
  - Ask: Which push sent the box further? (good: The big push. It went much further.; The gentle push stopped near the line.)
  - Note: If the box spins or tips, push it from the middle of its back. Help your child walk heel to toe so each step is the same size.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the gentle push and the big push once more each. Mark the gentle one with the second coin and the big one with the second spoon. Compare with the first marks.  
  *Parent says:* Let's do each push one more time. One try can be luck. Did it stop near the same place?  
  *Child mode:* Try each push again. Did the box stop near the same mark?
  - Note: The second tries will not land on exactly the same spot. That is normal. The big push should still go clearly further.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the box with rice and close the lid. 2. Ask your child to guess. 3. Your child gives the full box a big push, as big as before. 4. Put the chappal where it stops and count steps.  
  *Parent says:* Now the box is full of rice. If you push it just as hard, will it go as far as before? Why do you think so? Let's try.  
  *Child mode:* The box is full now. Will it go as far? Why? Push it as hard as before.
  - Ask: What happened to the full box? (good: It didn't go far. It was heavy.; I had to push much harder and it still stopped soon.)
  - Note: A full box goes a much shorter way with the same big push. Your child may also feel that it is harder to push.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what your child found, and why. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* What did the big push do? What did the full box do? Why do you think that happened?  
  *Child mode:* What did the big push do? What did the full box do? Why?
  - Ask: What did you find? (good: A big push sends the box further.; A heavy box needs a bigger push.)
  - Note: Wait for your child's own claim and evidence first. Then say it plainly: a bigger push sends a thing further, and a heavy thing needs a bigger push.
  - Check: Kavya, a girl in Class 1, pushes an empty box gently, then hard. Which push sends it further? → **The hard push**
### e2 · The Pull-Along Train: does a longer train need a bigger pull? (15 min)
- **question:** Does a train of three boxes need a bigger pull than one box? How can we see the pull?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will one box or three boxes need a bigger pull? Will the rubber band stretch more or less? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same rubber band, the same string and the same floor each time. We pull slowly each time. Each box has one book inside, so every box weighs about the same.
- **safety:** Keep the rubber band away from faces and pull slowly, so it does not snap back at anyone.
- **record:** Draw the rubber band for one box, two boxes and three boxes, as long as it really got.
- **results table:** Train | My guess: short or long stretch? | How long the rubber band got (draw it)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one book in one box. 2. Tie the string to the front of the box. 3. Loop the rubber band onto the free end of the string. 4. Your child hooks one finger in the band.  
  *Parent says:* This box is our train. When you pull, the rubber band will stretch. A longer stretch means a bigger pull.  
  *Child mode:* Hook your finger in the rubber band. When you pull, watch it stretch.
  - Note: Make a small hole in the box with a pencil and tie the string through it, or tie the string all the way round the box.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child pulls slowly until the box starts to slide. Lay the paper strip beside the band and mark how long it got.  
  *Parent says:* Pull slowly. Watch the band. Stop when the box starts to move. How long did the band get? Let's mark it.  
  *Child mode:* Pull slowly. Watch the band. Stop when the box moves. Let's mark it.
  - Note: The band stretches most just before the box starts to slide. That moment is the one to mark.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess before adding boxes.  
  *Parent says:* Now we add two more boxes, each with a book. Will the band stretch more, or less, or the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three boxes now. Will the band stretch more or less? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: More, because three boxes are heavier.; The same, because it's the same band.)
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie on a second box and pull. Mark the band. 2. Tie on a third box and pull. Mark the band.  
  *Parent says:* Two boxes now. Pull slowly and watch. Now three boxes. Is the band longer or shorter than before?  
  *Child mode:* Pull two boxes. Watch the band. Now pull three. Is it longer?
  - Ask: What did the band do? (good: It stretched longer with more boxes.; Three boxes made it the longest.)
  - Note: If the band gets very long, stop and use a thicker band, or two bands side by side.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the three marks together. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the three marks. What do they tell you about the pull?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the marks. Which train needed the biggest pull?
  - Check: The rubber band stretched longest for three boxes. What does that tell us? → **Three boxes needed the biggest pull**

## Activities
### a1 · Push Hands, Pull Hands: watch the home (10 min)
- **record:** Draw one push and one pull you saw at home.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Teach the two signs. PUSH: open palm, moving away from your body. PULL: closed fist, moving towards your body.  
  *Parent says:* Here are our two signs. A push moves things away, so the palm goes away. A pull moves things towards you, so the fist comes in.  
  *Child mode:* Push: open hand, move it away. Pull: close your fist, bring it in.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do one job at a time while your child watches. 2. Try the fridge, a drawer, a switch, a curtain and the washing line. 3. Your child shows a sign for each.  
  *Parent says:* Watch me. Am I pushing or pulling? Show me the sign. How do you know?  
  *Child mode:* Watch. Push or pull? Show the sign. How do you know?
  - Ask: How do you know opening the fridge is a pull? (good: The door came towards you.; You pulled the handle to you.)
  - Note: Sliding a curtain can be both a push and a pull. Let your child show both signs if they see both.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* One question about the kitchen.  
  *Child mode:* Answer one question.
  - Check: A girl called Meena closes a kitchen drawer. Is that a push or a pull? → **A push**
### a2 · The Two-Sided Door: push it shut, pull it shut (8 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up stays at the door. Keep fingers away from the edge of the door and the gap by the hinges, so they cannot get caught. Close the door slowly.
- **record:** Draw the door from above, as a bird looking down would. Draw yourself pushing on one side, and pulling on the other.
- **s1 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the door halfway. 2. Your child stands on the side where the door will move away from them. 3. They push the door slowly shut, with a flat hand in the middle.  
  *Parent says:* Put your flat hand on the door. Push it slowly until it shuts. Did the door move away from you or towards you?  
  *Child mode:* Put your flat hand on the door. Push it slowly shut. Did it move away from you?
  - Note: Stand by the door edge yourself, so small fingers stay away from the gap.
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the door halfway again. 2. Your child goes to the other side and holds the handle. 3. They pull the door slowly shut.  
  *Parent says:* Now go to the other side. Hold the handle and pull the door slowly until it shuts. Which way did the door swing this time?  
  *Child mode:* Go to the other side. Pull the handle slowly. Which way did the door swing?
  - Ask: Which way did the door swing each time? (good: The same way both times.; It shut the same way, but I pushed once and pulled once.)
  - Note: The door swings the same way both times, and closes the same way. The only change is where your child stood.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what changed and what stayed the same. Then ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* The door went the same way both times. So why did we call it a push once and a pull once?  
  *Child mode:* Same door, same way. Why was it a push once and a pull once?
  - Ask: Why? (good: Because I was on a different side.; From one side it went away from me. From the other side it came towards me.)
  - Note: Let your child explain first. Then say it plainly: push and pull are both forces, and the word depends on where you stand.
  - Check: Ravi pushes a door shut from inside. His sister Anu pulls the same door shut from outside. Which way does the door go? → **The same way both times**
### a3 · Ant Helpers: who pushes, who pulls? (10 min)
- **record:** Draw an ant pulling food, and an ant pushing food.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a line of ants near the kitchen, a windowsill or a balcony. 2. Put the food a hand-width away from the line.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a line of ants. Let's give them a crumb, close to their path. Then we watch quietly.  
  *Child mode:* Put the crumb near the ants. Then watch quietly.
  - Note: Most Indian homes have a few ants. If none come in ten minutes, leave the crumb and look again after an hour. If you have no ants today, skip this one and try another day.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Watch together. Use the magnifying glass if you have one.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the ants. Is one pulling the crumb towards herself? Is one pushing from behind? How can you tell?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the ants. Who is pulling? Who is pushing?
  - Ask: What are the ants doing? (good: Some pull the crumb with their mouths.; One is pushing it from the back.; Lots of them carry it together.)
  - Note: Ants grip food with their jaws and walk backwards, pulling, or walk forwards, pushing. Several ants often move a big crumb together, like the fishers who push the boat in the story.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* One question about the ants.  
  *Child mode:* Answer one question.
  - Check: Many ants move a big crumb together. Why? → **A big crumb needs a bigger pull than one ant can give**
### a4 · The Bedsheet Ride: your body pushes and pulls (10 min)
- **safety:** Use a smooth floor with nothing sharp nearby. The grown-up pulls slowly, in a straight line, and never near stairs.
- **record:** Draw yourself on the bedsheet ride, and draw who is pulling.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child jumps on the spot three times, then pushes a wall with both hands.  
  *Parent says:* Jump three times. What do your feet push on? Now push the wall. Where do you feel it?  
  *Child mode:* Jump three times. Your feet push the floor. Now push the wall.
  - Ask: What did your feet push when you jumped? (good: The floor.; I pushed down and went up.)
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the bedsheet on the floor. 2. Your child sits on one end. 3. Hold the other end and pull slowly in a straight line.  
  *Parent says:* Sit on the sheet and hold on. I'm going to pull it slowly. Am I pulling you towards me, or pushing you away?  
  *Child mode:* Sit on the sheet. Hold on. Is the grown-up pulling you or pushing you?
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Swap. You sit on the sheet. Your child pulls.  
  *Parent says:* Now you pull me. Lean back and pull hard. Can you move me? Why is it harder?  
  *Child mode:* Now you pull the grown-up. Pull hard. Can you move them? Why is it hard?
  - Ask: Why was it harder to pull me? (good: You are heavier than me.; A heavy thing needs a bigger pull.)
  - Note: Your child may move you a little, or not at all. Either result shows that a heavier load needs a bigger pull.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* One question about the ride.  
  *Child mode:* Answer one question.
  - Check: When you jump, what do your legs push on? → **The floor**

### p1 · Make a Pull-Along Toy (25 min, 2 days)
- **record:** Draw your toy. Draw an arrow from the toy to you for the pull, and from you to the toy for the push.
- **schedule:** day 1: Make and decorate the toy, and tie on the pull string. (15 min); day 2: Pull the toy on tiles and on a mat, then push it with the stick. (10 min)
- **s1 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up makes a small hole in the front of the box with a pencil point. 2. Your child decorates the box. 3. Tie the string through the hole.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make a toy that moves when you pull it. What will it be: a train, an auto, a cart or an animal?  
  *Child mode:* Choose what your toy will be. Decorate the box. Then we tie on the string.
  - Note: Make the hole yourself, pointing away from your hand.
  - Wait: Leave the glue to dry until tomorrow.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child pulls the toy on tiles, then on a mat or carpet, and says which was easier.  
  *Parent says:* Pull your toy on the tiles. Now pull it on the mat. Which was easier? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Pull your toy on the tiles. Now on the mat. Which was easier?
  - Ask: Which floor was easier? (good: The tiles. They are smooth.; The mat. It slowed the toy down.)
  - Note: The rough mat rubs the box more, so it needs a bigger pull. This rubbing is called friction, and children meet it properly in a later unit.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the old pencil to the back of the toy as a push stick. Your child pushes the toy with it.  
  *Parent says:* Now push the toy with the stick. Is the toy going away from you or towards you? So is it a push or a pull?  
  *Child mode:* Push the toy with the stick. Is it going away from you? Push or pull?
  - Check: You poke the toy with the stick and it moves away from you. Is that a push or a pull? → **A push**

## Challenges
### c1 · Bring Teddy Home (15 min)
- **problem:** Your teddy sits at the far end of a table, just out of reach. You must stay standing at this end. How can you bring the teddy to you?
- **your turn:** Find two different ways to bring Teddy home. Which way was easier? Was it a push or a pull?
- **constraints:** You must stay where you are standing.; You may not climb on the table.; Use only things from home.
- **general:** Many flats have a long hook-pole that pulls clothes down from a high rod.; People use a long hook to pull mangoes down from a tree.
- **creative:** Slide a ruler or long spoon past Teddy, then sweep it back so Teddy comes towards you.; Pull the whole bedsheet slowly, and the teddy rides along on top.; Hook the teddy's arm with the hanger and pull it in.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem. Spread a spare bedsheet on a smooth table. Sit the teddy at the far end, just out of reach.  
  *Parent says:* Teddy is over there. You have to stay here, and you can't climb up. How can you bring Teddy to you?  
  *Child mode:* Teddy is far away. Stay here. How can you get Teddy?
  - Ask: What could you use? (good: The hanger to hook it.; Pull the sheet.)
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child tries their ideas. Help only if asked.  
  *Parent says:* Try your idea. Did it work? Is your hanger pulling Teddy towards you, or pushing Teddy away?  
  *Child mode:* Try your idea. Is it a push or a pull? Did it work?
  - Note: Pulling the bedsheet works well and surprises many children. If an idea fails, ask what could make it work.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask which way worked best, and why. Then the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Which way was easiest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Which way was easiest? Why?
  - Check: You pull the bedsheet towards you, and the teddy comes too. Why? → **The sheet pulls the teddy along with it**

## Worksheets
### Push or Pull? (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (sort) Is it a push or a pull? A grown-up reads each one. Circle the pushes in red and the pulls in blue.  
   **Answer:** Opening a cupboard → Pull; Kicking a football → Push; Pressing a doorbell → Push; Pulling a bucket up from a well → Pull; Pushing a shopping trolley → Push; Taking a book off a shelf → Pull. *Why:* A push moves a thing away from you. A pull moves it towards you. A strong answer says which way the thing moved.
2. (match) Draw a line from each machine to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Tractor → Pulls a trolley full of sugarcane; Bulldozer → Pushes soil and stones; Crane → Pulls heavy things up; Tugboat → Pulls a big ship into the harbour. *Why:* Machines are made to push or pull for us. A tractor and a tugboat pull, a bulldozer pushes, and a crane pulls up with its cable.
3. (mcq) Tara, a girl who plays carrom, flicks a striker softly, then flicks it hard. Which time does the striker go further?  
   Options: The gentle push / The hard push / Both the same  
   **Answer:** The hard push. *Why:* A harder flick is a bigger push. The striker moves faster, so it goes further before it stops.
4. (draw) Draw yourself pushing something. Show it moving away from you.  
   **Answer:** A child pushing a thing, such as a swing or a ball, with the thing moving away.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the child's hands or feet on the thing, and the thing going away.
5. (draw) Draw yourself pulling something. Show it coming towards you.  
   **Answer:** A child pulling a thing, such as a drawer or a toy on a string, with the thing coming closer.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the child holding the thing and the thing coming nearer.
6. (truefalse) When you pull a drawer open, the drawer moves away from you.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A pulled drawer slides out towards you. A pull moves a thing towards you.
7. (odd) Three of these are pulls. Which one is a push?  
   Options: Opening a drawer / Pulling a suitcase along / Closing a door / Pulling a kite string  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Closing a door moves it away from you, so it is a push. The others bring something towards you.
8. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: why did the full box not slide as far as the empty box?  
   **Answer:** It was heavy. The same push was not big enough to send it far.. *Why:* A strong answer says the full box was heavier, and a heavier thing needs a bigger push.
9. (perspective) Nandini, aged 6, and her friend Farhan, aged 5, closed a bedroom door together. Nandini was inside the room. Farhan was outside, holding the handle.  
   **Answer:** Both are right. From inside, the door moved away from Nandini, so she pushed. From outside, it came towards Farhan, so he pulled.. *Why:* A strong answer notices where each child stood. The door moved the same way, and push or pull depends on where you stand.
10. (label) Look at the playground. A grown-up points to each child. Say PUSH or PULL for each, and the grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** ['push', 'pull', 'push', 'pull']. *Why:* The swing and the roundabout move away from the hands, so those are pushes. The toy car and the boy's body come towards his hands, so those are pulls.
### Pushes and Pulls All Around (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (order) In the Pull-Along Train, put the trains in order, from the shortest rubber band stretch to the longest.  
   **Answer:** ['1 box', '2 boxes', '3 boxes']. *Why:* More boxes are heavier, so they need a bigger pull. A bigger pull stretches the band longer.
2. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it: how did the rubber band show you how big your pull was?  
   **Answer:** When I pulled harder, the band stretched longer. Three boxes made it stretch the most.. *Why:* A strong answer links a longer stretch to a bigger pull.
3. (perspective) Ishita and Gauri are talking about pushes and pulls in Hyderabad.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason. For Gauri: both are forces. For Ishita: a floppy rope can only pull, so for a rope they really are different.. *Why:* Both girls see something true. Pushes and pulls are both forces. But some things, like a rope, can only pull, so builders care which one a thing must take.
4. (mcq) A cricket ball lies still on the grass. Which of these will NOT start it moving?  
   Options: A kick / A strong wind / Looking at it hard  
   **Answer:** Looking at it hard. *Why:* A still thing starts to move only when a push or a pull acts on it. Looking is not a push or a pull.
5. (draw) Draw a machine you have seen, such as a tractor, a crane or a bulldozer. Show if it pushes or pulls, and which way the thing moves.  
   **Answer:** Any machine drawn with the thing it moves, and an arrow showing the push or the pull.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the machine touching the thing and the thing moving away (push) or towards the machine (pull).
6. (truefalse) When you kick a ball, the air behind the ball keeps pushing it along.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Once the ball leaves your foot, nothing pushes it along. The air rubs against the ball and slows it down.
7. (match) Draw a line from each living being to the push or pull they make.  
   **Answer:** An ant → Pulls a grain of rice; A working elephant → Pushes a big log; A plant's root → Pushes down through the soil; A girl on a swing → Pulls on the chains. *Why:* People, other animals and plants all push and pull. Ants pull food, elephants push logs, and roots push their way through soil.
8. (label) Look at the street. Say PUSH or PULL for each person, and a grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** ['push', 'pull', 'pull']. *Why:* The woman moves the cart away from her, so she pushes. The wagon and the trolley follow behind, towards the hand that holds them, so those are pulls.
9. (odd) Which one moves a thing only by touching it?  
   Options: A magnet pulling a steel pin / The Earth pulling a mango down / Your hand pushing a door  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Your hand must touch the door to push it. A magnet and the Earth can pull things without touching them.
10. (write) A friend says a very heavy cupboard can never be moved. Say out loud what you would tell your friend, and a grown-up writes it down.  
   **Answer:** It can be moved with a bigger push: many people pushing together, or a machine.. *Why:* A strong answer says a heavy thing needs a bigger push or pull, and says how to get one.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A girl called Diya drags a mat across the room by its corner, walking backwards. Is that a push or a pull?  
   Options: A pull / A push / Neither  
   **Answer:** A pull. *Why:* The mat comes towards Diya, so it is a pull.
2. (mcq) A boy called Arjun pushes a swing. Which way does the swing move?  
   Options: Towards Arjun / Away from Arjun / It stays still  
   **Answer:** Away from Arjun. *Why:* A push moves a thing away from you.
3. (mcq) A ball sits on a flat floor. Nobody touches it. What happens?  
   Options: It rolls away by itself / It jumps up / It stays still  
   **Answer:** It stays still. *Why:* A still thing stays still until a push or pull moves it.
4. (mcq) What do scientists call any push or pull?  
   Options: A force / A machine / A toy  
   **Answer:** A force. *Why:* Force is the scientists' word for every push and every pull.
5. (mcq) A girl called Bornali wants her toy car to go as far as it can. What should she do?  
   Options: Give it a tiny push / Hold it still / Give it a big push  
   **Answer:** Give it a big push. *Why:* A bigger push makes the car go faster, so it goes further.
6. (mcq) A girl called Thenmozhi pushes her little sister on a swing. Which push sends the swing higher?  
   Options: A smaller push / A bigger push / Both the same  
   **Answer:** A bigger push. *Why:* A bigger push makes the swing move faster, so it goes higher.
7. (mcq) Which is harder to push across the floor?  
   Options: A bucket full of water / An empty bucket / Both are the same  
   **Answer:** A bucket full of water. *Why:* The full bucket is heavier, and a heavy thing needs a bigger push.
8. (mcq) Why do many fishers push a big boat down the beach together?  
   Options: The sand is too hot / One person would push it too far / The boat is heavy and needs a big push  
   **Answer:** The boat is heavy and needs a big push. *Why:* Many pushes together make a big push, big enough to move a heavy boat.
9. (mcq) Which machine is made to pull?  
   Options: A bulldozer / A tractor with a trolley / A road roller  
   **Answer:** A tractor with a trolley. *Why:* A tractor pulls a trolley behind it. A bulldozer pushes soil, and a road roller presses the road flat.
10. (mcq) In the playground, a girl called Lakshmi pushes a roundabout. What does her push do?  
   Options: It makes the roundabout start to turn / It pulls the roundabout towards her / Nothing  
   **Answer:** It makes the roundabout start to turn. *Why:* Her push moves the roundabout away from her hands, so it starts to turn.

## Applications
- Cranes on building sites pull heavy loads up with strong cables.
- Tugboats pull and push huge ships into harbours, such as the ports of Chennai and Kochi.
- Astronauts on the space station mostly move around by pushing off walls and pulling on handles.

## Spotted around us
- A tractor in Punjab or Tamil Nadu pulling a trolley piled high with sugarcane.
- A vegetable seller in Mysuru pushing a cart on wheels down the street.
- Fishers on a beach in Kerala pulling in a long net together.
- The Rath Yatra in Puri, Odisha, where huge crowds pull giant wooden chariots with long ropes.
- A metro or lift door in Kolkata or Bengaluru that slides open and shut, pushed and pulled by a motor.

## Open questions
- **How do a group of ants agree which way to pull a big crumb?** Many ants pull together without bumping into each other's way. Scientists have found that some ants seem to steer the group, but they are still studying how the whole group decides.
- **What is the Earth's pull, gravity, really?** We can measure gravity very well, and it pulls without touching. But scientists still do not fully understand what gravity is at the very tiniest scale.
- **Are there pushes and pulls nobody has found yet?** Scientists know a few basic forces that make every push and pull. Some are still looking for new ones, and nobody knows if they will find any.

## Connected maths
- In the box test, Kavya, a girl in Class 1, gave a gentle push that sent the box 2 foot steps. Her big push sent it 7 foot steps. How many more steps did the big push go? **5 more steps.**. Count on from 2 to 7: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. That is 5 more. So the big push sent the box 5 steps further. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- Pooja, a girl in Class 1, watched people at home for ten minutes. She saw 6 pushes and 4 pulls. Did she see more pushes or more pulls? How many did she see in all? **More pushes. 10 in all.**. 6 is more than 4, so she saw more pushes. 6 and 4 make 10, so she saw 10 pushes and pulls in all. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L1`)
- In the Pull-Along Train, Revathi, a girl in Class 1, marked the rubber band on a paper strip three times. Which mark is the longest? Which train made it? **The longest mark is from the train of 3 boxes.**. Lay the marks side by side, all starting from the same end of the strip. The 3-box mark reaches furthest, so it is the longest. The longest stretch means the biggest pull. (queues `math.length.comparing-length.L1`)
