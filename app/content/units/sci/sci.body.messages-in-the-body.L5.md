# Hormones: the chemical messages behind alarm, growth and sleep
`sci.body.messages-in-the-body.L5`

**Summary.** Your heart can race while you sit perfectly still. You grow about half a centimetre a month, and you do not notice it happening. You get sleepy at nearly the same time each night. What is doing all this? In this lesson you will test whether pressure alone can speed up your pulse, find out how weak a taste your tongue can still pick up, and change the light in your evenings to see what happens to your sleepiness. You will meet the glands that send chemical messages through your blood, and find out why the same message gets different answers in different parts of you.

**Intro.** *Think of the last time something gave you a fright. Maybe a pressure cooker whistled just behind you, or a dog barked, or a glass nearly slipped from your hand. Where in your body did you feel it? Point to every place. Then answer this: the fright was over in one second, so why did your heart keep thumping for a minute or more?* Most people point to many places: the chest, the tummy, the hands, the legs, the mouth. That is odd. One small event changed many far-apart parts of you, almost together. And the thumping went on long after the danger had gone. Your body has two kinds of message. Nerve signals are fast and go to one place. Hormones are chemicals that travel more slowly, in the blood, and reach everywhere. In this lesson you look closely at hormones. You find out where hormones are made, how such tiny amounts can do so much, and how they control three things in your life: your alarm, your growth and your sleep.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can point to the main endocrine glands on a body outline and name one hormone that each one makes.
1. By the end I can explain what adrenaline does in an alarm, why nerves start the alarm first, and why the alarm takes minutes to fade.
2. By the end I can explain why one hormone gets different answers from different cells, using the idea of receptors.
3. By the end I can describe how growth is controlled by growth hormone, thyroid hormone and the growth plates, and why growing stops.
4. By the end I can explain how a body clock, light and melatonin set the time I feel sleepy, and what sleep pressure adds.
5. By the end I can trace an order from the hypothalamus to the pituitary to another gland, and say why 'master gland' is only partly right.
6. By the end I can explain how hormones work in tiny amounts, and what doctors do when a gland makes too little.
7. By the end I can plan a fair test on my own body, repeat it, say what it shows and say honestly what it cannot show.

## Terms
- **Hormone**: A hormone is a chemical that some cells make and release, usually into the blood. It travels round the body and changes what certain other cells do. *e.g.* Adrenaline is a hormone. It leaves the adrenal glands, travels in the blood and makes the heart beat faster and harder.
- **Gland**: A gland is a group of cells whose job is to make a substance and release it. Some glands release through a tube, as sweat glands and saliva glands do. Endocrine glands have no tube. They release straight into the blood. *e.g.* A saliva gland sends saliva down a tube into your mouth. The thyroid gland sends its hormone straight into the blood passing through it.
- **Endocrine system**: The endocrine system is all the glands and cells that make hormones, taken together. 'Endocrine' comes from Greek words that mean 'to separate within', because the hormones are released inside the body. *e.g.* The pituitary, the thyroid, the adrenal glands, the pineal gland, parts of the pancreas, the ovaries and the testes all belong to the endocrine system.
- **Receptor**: A receptor is a protein on or inside a cell that one kind of hormone fits. When the hormone joins the receptor, the receptor changes shape, and that starts changes inside the cell. *e.g.* Heart muscle cells carry receptors for adrenaline. A cell with no adrenaline receptor does not answer adrenaline at all.
- **Target cell**: A target cell is any cell that has the receptor for a certain hormone, and so can answer that hormone. *e.g.* Liver cells are target cells for adrenaline. When adrenaline arrives, they release glucose into the blood.
- **Adrenal glands**: The adrenal glands are two small flat glands, one sitting on top of each kidney. The inner part makes adrenaline. The outer part makes other hormones, such as cortisol. *e.g.* When you get a fright, nerves from your spinal cord signal the inner part of each adrenal gland, and adrenaline enters your blood within seconds.
- **Adrenaline**: Adrenaline is the hormone the adrenal glands release in an alarm or in hard exercise. It gets the body ready for sudden effort. It is also called epinephrine. *e.g.* Adrenaline makes the heart beat faster and harder, opens the airways in the lungs wider, and makes the liver release glucose.
- **Hypothalamus**: The hypothalamus is a part of the brain about the size of an almond, low down in the middle. It is where the nervous system and the endocrine system meet. It is made of nerve cells, and some of those nerve cells release hormones. *e.g.* The hypothalamus sends 'releasing hormones' down a short stalk to the pituitary gland just below it.
- **Pituitary gland**: The pituitary is a gland the size of a pea that hangs under the hypothalamus on a short stalk. It makes growth hormone, and it makes hormones that tell other glands to work. *e.g.* The pituitary releases a hormone that travels in the blood to the thyroid gland and tells it to make more thyroid hormone.
- **Growth hormone**: Growth hormone is a hormone from the pituitary gland. It makes bones grow longer and muscles grow bigger in children. It is released in bursts, and the biggest burst usually comes soon after deep sleep begins. *e.g.* Growth hormone tells the liver to make a second hormone. Together they make the cartilage cells in the growth plates divide.
- **Growth plate**: A growth plate is a thin layer of cartilage near each end of a long bone in a child. Cartilage is the firm, bendy material in your ear. The bone gets longer here. At the end of the teenage years the plate turns to bone and growing in height stops. *e.g.* On an X-ray of a ten-year-old's hand, the growth plates show as dark gaps near the ends of the finger bones. A grown-up's hand has no gaps.
- **Thyroid gland**: The thyroid is a gland shaped like a butterfly at the front of the neck, below the voice box. Its hormone sets how fast cells use energy. A baby's brain and a child's bones need it to grow properly. *e.g.* A child whose thyroid makes too little hormone grows slowly and feels tired and cold. A daily tablet of the missing hormone puts this right.
- **Iodine**: Iodine is a chemical element. The thyroid gland cannot make its hormone without it, because each molecule of the hormone holds three or four iodine atoms. We get iodine from food and from iodised salt. *e.g.* The packet of salt in most Indian kitchens says 'iodised'. A tiny amount of an iodine compound has been added to it.
- **Pineal gland**: The pineal gland is a gland about the size of a grain of rice, deep in the middle of the brain. It makes melatonin when it is dark. *e.g.* In the evening, as the light fades, the body clock lets the pineal gland start making melatonin.
- **Melatonin**: Melatonin is the hormone the pineal gland releases at night. It tells every part of the body that it is dark outside. Bright light in the evening holds it back. *e.g.* In a child who sleeps at about nine, melatonin begins to rise in the early evening and is highest in the middle of the night.
- **Body clock**: The body clock is a group of about 20,000 nerve cells in the hypothalamus that keeps time. It runs a cycle of roughly 24 hours by itself. Morning light sets it right each day. Scientists call a cycle of about a day a circadian rhythm. *e.g.* People who travel from Chennai to London feel sleepy at the wrong time for some days. Their body clock is still on Indian time.
- **Sleep pressure**: Sleep pressure is the need for sleep that builds up the longer you stay awake. It falls again while you sleep. It is separate from the body clock. *e.g.* After a long afternoon nap you are not sleepy at bedtime. The nap has taken away some of your sleep pressure.
- **Cortisol**: Cortisol is a hormone from the outer part of the adrenal glands. It rises in the last hours of the night and is highest soon after you wake. It helps release stored fuel for the day. It also rises during long-lasting stress. *e.g.* Your cortisol is rising before your alarm rings. Your body has started getting ready for the day while you are still asleep.
- **Steroid hormone**: A steroid hormone is a hormone that the body makes from cholesterol, a fatty substance. It is small and fatty, so it can pass through the oily skin of a cell. Inside, it switches certain genes on or off. *e.g.* Cortisol and oestrogen are steroid hormones. Their effects come slowly and last for hours or days.
- **Amplifying**: Amplifying means making a small signal bigger. One hormone molecule on one receptor sets off a chain of steps inside the cell. Each step switches on many molecules of the next step. *e.g.* One adrenaline molecule on a liver cell can lead to the release of many thousands of glucose molecules.
- **Half-life**: The half-life of a hormone is the time the body takes to clear half of it from the blood. A hormone with a short half-life gives a short message. *e.g.* Adrenaline has a half-life of only a few minutes. So a few minutes after a fright, most of it has gone.
- **Puberty**: Puberty is the time when a child's body changes into a grown-up body. It begins when the hypothalamus tells the pituitary to wake the ovaries or the testes. They then make much more of their hormones. *e.g.* Puberty usually begins between 8 and 13 in girls and between 9 and 14 in boys. It brings a few years of fast growth.

## Explanations
### The endocrine system: glands that release hormones into the blood · *defined*
**Definition:** The endocrine system is the set of glands and cells that make hormones. A hormone is a chemical that is released into the blood, travels round the body and changes what certain cells do.

Start at the top. The hypothalamus and the pituitary sit under the brain. The pineal gland is in the middle of the brain. The thyroid is at the front of the neck. The two adrenal glands sit on the kidneys. The pancreas lies behind the stomach, and small groups of its cells make insulin. Lower down are the ovaries in girls and the testes in boys. Why does the body need this system when it already has nerves? A nerve reaches only the cells it touches. Some jobs need a message to reach nearly every cell and to last for hours or years, such as growing. Blood already goes everywhere, so a chemical in the blood is a cheap way to tell the whole body one thing. This list of glands is a simplification. The stomach, the fat under your skin, the heart and the kidneys also release hormones. Scientists have named more than fifty human hormones, and they are still finding more.
- e.g. The thyroid gland, with its hormone that sets how fast cells use energy.
- e.g. The adrenal glands, with adrenaline from the inner part and cortisol from the outer part.
- e.g. The stomach, which is not called a gland but releases a hormone that makes you feel hungry.
### Adrenaline: the alarm that nerves start and a hormone carries on · *defined*
**Definition:** Adrenaline is a hormone released by the inner part of the adrenal glands during fright, excitement or hard exercise. It prepares the body for sudden effort.

When your brain judges that something is urgent, it signals a set of nerves called the sympathetic nerves. These nerves reach the heart, the skin and the gut directly, so the heart speeds up in about a second. The same nerves reach the inner part of the adrenal glands. Within seconds the glands release adrenaline into the blood. Adrenaline then does the same kind of work as those nerves, and it reaches cells that nerves do not touch. The heart beats faster and harder. The airways widen. The liver releases glucose, which is fuel. Blood is sent to the big muscles and away from the skin and the gut. All this is useful if you must run or fight. The American scientist Walter Cannon described it in 1915 and called it the 'fight or flight' response. Why does the thumping last after the danger has gone? A nerve signal stops in a moment. Adrenaline stays in the blood until the body has broken it down, and that takes a few minutes.
- e.g. A dog barks behind you. Your heart is pounding before you have turned round, and it is still pounding a minute later.
- e.g. Before a race or a stage performance, your hands feel cold and your mouth feels dry. Blood has been moved away from the skin, and less saliva is being made.
- e.g. In the Sitting-Still Alarm Test, most children's pulse rises during the timed sums, though no muscle is working hard.
### One hormone, different answers: the receiving cell decides · *defined*
**Definition:** A hormone does not carry an instruction inside it. What happens depends on the receptor a cell has, and on what that cell is built to do.

Adrenaline makes heart muscle beat harder. The same adrenaline makes the muscle in the wall of the gut relax. It narrows the small blood vessels in the skin and widens those in the big muscles. How can one chemical do opposite things? In 1948 the American scientist Raymond Ahlquist proposed the answer. Cells carry different kinds of adrenaline receptor. He called them alpha and beta. Each kind starts a different chain of events inside the cell. So the meaning of the message is set by the receiver. Other hormones show the same thing. Thyroid hormone makes a child's bones grow, and the same hormone makes a tadpole's tail shrink away. Melatonin rises in the dark in humans, who then get sleepy. It also rises in the dark in rats and hamsters, who are active at night and are then wide awake. This is why calling melatonin 'the sleep hormone' is not quite right. It is a signal that says 'it is dark', and each kind of body answers in their own way.
- e.g. Adrenaline: heart muscle speeds up, gut muscle relaxes, liver cells release glucose.
- e.g. Thyroid hormone: a child's leg bones lengthen, and a tadpole's tail is taken apart.
- e.g. An asthma inhaler holds a medicine shaped to fit one kind of adrenaline receptor, the kind that opens the airways.
### The chain of command: hypothalamus, pituitary and the glands below · *defined*
**Definition:** The hypothalamus releases hormones that tell the pituitary what to do. The pituitary releases hormones that tell other glands what to do. Those glands report back through the blood.

The hypothalamus is part of the brain, and it gets news from all over the body and from the senses. It sends tiny amounts of 'releasing hormones' down a short stalk to the pituitary. The pituitary answers by releasing its own hormones into the blood. One goes to the thyroid. One goes to the outer part of the adrenal glands. Two go to the ovaries or testes. One, growth hormone, goes to the liver, the bones and the muscles. Each step makes more hormone than the step before, so a very small signal from the brain becomes a large effect in the body. The pituitary is often called the 'master gland'. That is only partly right. It takes its orders from the hypothalamus. And when the thyroid has made enough hormone, that hormone itself tells the hypothalamus and the pituitary to ease off. So the orders run in a circle. Some glands are not in this chain at all. The adrenal glands release adrenaline on a signal from nerves, and the pancreas releases insulin when it senses sugar.
- e.g. Hypothalamus to pituitary to thyroid: three glands and three hormones, one after another.
- e.g. When there is plenty of thyroid hormone in the blood, the pituitary sends less of its 'make more' hormone.
- e.g. Adrenaline skips the chain. Nerves signal the adrenal glands directly, which is why it is so quick.
### How growth is controlled · *defined*
**Definition:** Children grow taller because cartilage cells in the growth plates of their bones divide, and bone then replaces the cartilage. Growth hormone and thyroid hormone keep this going. At the end of puberty the plates turn to bone and growth in height stops.

Between about four years old and puberty, most children grow about 5 to 6 centimetres a year. The growing happens in the growth plates, thin layers of cartilage near the ends of the long bones. Growth hormone from the pituitary drives it. Growth hormone acts on the plates directly. It also tells the liver to release a second hormone, which makes the cartilage cells divide. Thyroid hormone is needed as well, and so are food and sleep. Growth hormone comes in bursts through the day and night. In 1968 Yasuro Takahashi and his colleagues sampled the blood of sleeping volunteers every 30 minutes. They found that the biggest burst begins when deep sleep begins. So 'you grow in your sleep' is partly true. It is also a simplification, because some growth hormone is released by day, and one late night does not make anyone shorter. In puberty, the ovaries and testes make far more of their hormones, and growth speeds up for a few years. Then one of those hormones, oestrogen, makes the growth plates turn to bone. Boys make oestrogen too, in smaller amounts, and it closes their growth plates as well.
- e.g. A girl who was 128 centimetres on her ninth birthday is about 134 centimetres on her tenth.
- e.g. At the fastest point of puberty, girls grow about 8 centimetres in a year and boys about 9 and a half.
- e.g. In 1994 doctors described a man of 28 whose body could not answer oestrogen. His growth plates had never closed, and he was still growing.
### The thyroid: the pace of your cells, and why salt is iodised · *defined*
**Definition:** The thyroid gland makes a hormone that sets how fast cells use energy. Growth and brain development depend on it. The gland needs iodine to make the hormone.

Nearly every cell in your body is a target cell for thyroid hormone. With too little of it, cells work slowly. A child with too little grows slowly, feels cold and tired, and may find it hard to concentrate. A baby with too little, before or after birth, can be left with lasting harm to the brain. Each molecule of thyroid hormone holds iodine atoms, so no iodine means no hormone. A child of ten needs about 120 millionths of a gram of iodine a day. That is too small an amount to see. In many hill regions and river plains the soil holds little iodine, so the food grown there holds little too. The gland then swells as it tries harder, and the swelling in the neck is called a goitre. From 1956, the Indian doctor Vulimiri Ramalingaswami and his colleagues ran a long trial in the Kangra Valley of Himachal Pradesh. Villages that got salt with added iodine had far fewer goitres after some years. Villages that got plain salt did not change. India began a national goitre programme in 1962, and today nearly all salt sold for eating is iodised.
- e.g. The word 'iodised' on the salt packet in your kitchen.
- e.g. Many hospitals test a drop of blood from a newborn baby's heel to check the baby's thyroid hormone, because early treatment protects the brain.
- e.g. A tadpole with no thyroid hormone stays a tadpole and never becomes a frog.
### How sleep is timed: a clock, light, melatonin and sleep pressure · *defined*
**Definition:** Two things together decide when you feel sleepy. One is the body clock, which sends out melatonin when it is dark. The other is sleep pressure, which builds up the longer you are awake.

The body clock is a small group of nerve cells in the hypothalamus. It keeps a cycle of about 24 hours by itself. In the evening it lets the pineal gland start making melatonin. Melatonin travels in the blood and tells every organ that night has come. It does not knock you out. It helps the body shift towards sleep, and it works together with sleep pressure. Sleep pressure is a separate thing. It rises through the day and falls during sleep. The Swiss scientist Alexander Borbély put the two together in 1982, and his picture is still used. How does the clock know it is evening? Special cells in the back of the eye measure brightness, and blue light affects them most. They are not the cells you see with. They report straight to the clock. Bright light in the evening tells the clock it is still day, and the pineal gland holds melatonin back. In 1980 Alfred Lewy's team showed this in people. Later studies found that young children's melatonin falls very strongly in bright evening light. Towards morning, cortisol rises and melatonin falls, and the body gets ready to wake.
- e.g. On a camping trip with no electric light, most people get sleepy earlier than at home.
- e.g. After a flight from Chennai to London, the clock in the hypothalamus is still about five hours ahead for some days.
- e.g. A late afternoon nap lowers sleep pressure, so bedtime sleepiness comes later, though melatonin rises at its usual time.
### The clock runs by itself, and the spinning Earth made it · *defined*
**Definition:** The body clock keeps going with no light and no clock on the wall. Each of its cycles is close to one turn of the Earth.

In 1999 Charles Czeisler and his colleagues kept volunteers for weeks in rooms with no clues about the time. Their body clocks ran a cycle of 24 hours and 11 minutes on average. So the clock is very nearly right, and a little slow. Morning light corrects it each day. Where is the clock kept? Inside each clock cell, a few genes make proteins. The proteins build up and then switch their own genes off. The proteins break down, and the genes switch on again. One round takes about a day. Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young worked this out in fruit flies and received the Nobel Prize in 2017. The same kind of clock ticks in nearly all your cells, in the liver, the skin and the gut. The clock in the hypothalamus keeps them in step. Why do living beings have clocks at all? The Earth has turned once a day for billions of years. Light, warmth and danger have come and gone on that beat. Bodies that got ready ahead of time did better. Plants, fungi and some bacteria keep daily clocks too.
- e.g. A person in a cave with no watch still sleeps and wakes about once every 24 hours.
- e.g. A touch-me-not plant kept in the dark still folds and opens their leaves roughly on time for a few days.
- e.g. Your body temperature is lowest in the last hours of the night, whether you are asleep or awake.
### Tiny amounts, big effects: how a cell amplifies a hormone · *defined*
**Definition:** Hormones work at amounts far too small to taste or see. A cell answers because each receptor that is switched on starts a chain inside the cell, and each step of the chain is bigger than the last.

At night the melatonin in a child's blood is about one part in ten thousand million. That is less than a pinch of salt stirred into a hundred big water tankers. How can so little do anything? First, receptors fit their hormone closely, so they catch it even when it is rare. Second, the cell amplifies. Adrenaline does not enter a liver cell. It joins a receptor on the outside. The receptor then switches on helper proteins inside. Each helper starts the making of many small molecules called second messengers. These switch on enzymes, and each enzyme releases many glucose molecules. The American scientist Earl Sutherland found the second messenger in the 1950s and received the Nobel Prize in 1971. Hormones of a second kind work differently. Steroid hormones and thyroid hormone go inside the cell and switch genes on or off. That is slower, and the effect lasts much longer. Measuring such tiny amounts was impossible until 1959. Then the physicist Rosalyn Yalow and the doctor Solomon Berson, in New York, invented a method that can find a millionth of a millionth of a gram in a small sample of blood. Yalow received the Nobel Prize in 1977. Berson had died in 1972.
- e.g. In the Ten Times Weaker test, most tongues lose the taste of sugar by the second or third tumbler. A hormone receptor would still catch it at the tenth.
- e.g. Adrenaline acts in seconds because it only has to switch on proteins that are already there.
- e.g. Cortisol acts over hours because cells must read genes and build new proteins.
### Puberty: the hormones that change a child's body · *defined*
**Definition:** Puberty begins when the hypothalamus starts sending bursts of a releasing hormone to the pituitary. The pituitary then signals the ovaries or the testes, and they make much more oestrogen or testosterone.

This chain of three is fully built in a baby. Then it goes quiet for the whole of childhood. Some time between 8 and 13 in girls, and between 9 and 14 in boys, the hypothalamus starts it up again. Oestrogen and testosterone are steroid hormones. They switch on genes in many kinds of cell, so over a few years the body grows fast, changes shape, grows new hair and becomes able to make babies. Both hormones are found in both girls and boys, in different amounts. What starts the hypothalamus up? In 2003 two research teams found families in which puberty never began, and traced it to one gene. The gene is for a receptor on the hypothalamus cells. The signal that fits it is called kisspeptin, and without it the chain stays quiet. But what tells the kisspeptin cells that the time has come is still not known. Body size, food and genes all play a part. The age at which puberty starts is different for every child, and a wide range is healthy.
- e.g. Two friends of the same age may start their fast growth two or three years apart, and both are healthy.
- e.g. Girls usually have their fastest growth about two years earlier than boys.
- e.g. The same hormone that speeds growth in puberty, oestrogen, also ends it by closing the growth plates.
### When a gland makes too little, and how people replace the hormone · *defined*
**Definition:** If a gland makes too little of a hormone, the cells that depend on it do not get their message. Doctors can often measure the hormone in blood and give the missing hormone as medicine.

A child whose pituitary makes too little growth hormone grows much more slowly than other children. A child whose thyroid makes too little hormone grows slowly and feels tired. A child whose pancreas makes no insulin has type 1 diabetes. In each case the missing hormone can be given. Thyroid hormone is a small, tough molecule and survives the stomach, so it can be a tablet. Insulin and growth hormone are proteins. The stomach would digest them like food, so they must be injected. Insulin was first used in Toronto in January 1922, on a boy of 14 named Leonard Thompson. Frederick Banting, Charles Best, James Collip and John Macleod did the work. Only Banting and Macleod were given the Nobel Prize in 1923, and they shared their prize money with the other two. Most short children do not have a hormone problem. They have short parents, or they are growing on their own later timetable. A doctor checks by plotting heights on a growth chart over many months.
- e.g. A daily thyroid tablet for a child whose gland makes too little.
- e.g. Insulin injections or an insulin pump for a child with type 1 diabetes.
- e.g. A growth chart in a child's health record, with a height marked every six months.
### Hormones are older than glands: plants, insects and single cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Chemical messages between cells are found in nearly all living beings, including those with no blood and no glands.

Plants make hormones. One of them, ethylene, is a gas. A ripening banana gives off ethylene, and the gas makes the fruits nearby ripen too. That is why one ripe banana in a bag of raw mangoes hurries them along. Another plant hormone, auxin, gathers on the shaded side of a shoot and makes the cells there grow longer, so the shoot bends towards light. Insects grow by hormones as well. A caterpillar sheds their skin when one hormone rises. A second hormone keeps them a caterpillar. When the second one falls, the next change makes a pupa and then a butterfly. Even single cells signal with chemicals. Yeast cells release a chemical that other yeast cells answer before they join together. Some of our own hormone molecules are very old. Melatonin has been found in plants and in bacteria. One idea is that it began, billions of years ago, as a chemical that protected cells from damage, and only later became a night signal. That idea is not proven.
- e.g. Put a ripe banana in a paper bag with a raw banana or a raw mango, and the raw fruit ripens sooner.
- e.g. A silkworm caterpillar moults four times as a caterpillar, each time on a hormone signal.
- e.g. A money plant on a windowsill bends towards the light because auxin gathers on the shaded side.
### Does your pounding heart cause the fear, or does the fear cause the pounding heart? · *debated*
Most people would say: I saw the dog, I felt afraid, and so my heart pounded. In 1884 the American thinker William James turned this round. He said the body changes first, and the feeling of fear is our sensing of those changes. Scientists have argued about it ever since. In 1962 Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer injected students with adrenaline without telling them what it would do. They reported that the students' feelings depended on what was going on around them. In 1979 Gary Marshall and Philip Zimbardo repeated the test and did not get the same result. So that famous experiment is in doubt. In the Sitting-Still Alarm Test you can ask yourself: when my pulse was up, did I feel nervous, excited or nothing much?
- e.g. Your heart pounds before a race and before a scary film. The body change is much the same, but you call one excitement and the other fear.
- e.g. Some people feel their heart race after strong coffee and feel no emotion at all.
- **William James (1884):** The body changes come first. We feel afraid because we sense our heart pounding and our muscles tensing. Without the body changes there would be a thought of danger but no feeling.
- **Walter Cannon (1927):** The same body changes happen in fear, anger and excitement, and they are too slow. So the body cannot be what tells these feelings apart. The brain makes the feeling and the body alarm at the same time.
- **Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer (1962):** A feeling needs two things: a stirred-up body, and the mind's explanation of why. The same pounding heart can become fear or joy, depending on what you think is going on. Later tests did not all agree with theirs.
- **Lisa Feldman Barrett and others today:** The brain is always predicting what the body will need and reading the body's signals. A feeling is the brain's best guess at what those signals mean right now, built from past experience.
### Do screens before bed harm sleep, and is the light to blame? · *debated*
Bright evening light holds back melatonin. That is settled. Whether the light from a phone or tablet is bright enough to matter much is still argued over. In 2015 Anne-Marie Chang and her colleagues had 12 adults read for four hours before bed, on a bright tablet for some nights and from a printed book for others. On tablet nights their melatonin rose about an hour and a half later, and they took about ten minutes longer to fall asleep. Other scientists point out that four hours at full brightness is a lot. A phone screen is far dimmer than daylight. In everyday life, they say, screens cost sleep mainly because people stay up later, and because games and videos keep the mind busy. The Dim Evening Test in this lesson changes only the room light, so that you can see what light alone does for you.
- e.g. A child who watches a film until 10:30 has lost an hour of sleep, whatever the light did.
- e.g. A dim, warm bedside lamp gives the eye far less blue light than a white tube light on the ceiling.
- **Anne-Marie Chang, Charles Czeisler and colleagues (2015):** Light from a screen in the evening delays the body clock, holds back melatonin and makes people less alert the next morning. They measured all three.
- **Michael Gradisar and other sleep researchers:** The effect of screen light on falling asleep is small, a matter of minutes. What matters more is the time taken from sleep and how stirring the content is. Dimming the screen is less important than stopping on time.

## Mostly agreed
- Hormones are real chemicals in the blood, present in tiny amounts, and they act only on cells that have receptors for them. *How we know:* Since 1959, the method of Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson has let scientists measure hormones in blood directly. Raymond Ahlquist's idea of different receptors, from 1948, led to medicines that fit one receptor and not another, and those medicines work.
- The inner part of the adrenal glands releases adrenaline, which raises the heart rate and blood pressure. *How we know:* In 1894 George Oliver and Edward Schäfer in London showed that an extract of adrenal gland sharply raised blood pressure. In 1900 Jokichi Takamine and his assistant Keizo Uenaka purified the active chemical as crystals. Uenaka did the key laboratory work and is rarely named.
- Growth hormone is released in bursts, and the largest burst is tied to the start of deep sleep. *How we know:* Yasuro Takahashi, David Kipnis and William Daughaday measured it in 1968 by taking blood every 30 minutes through the night. When they delayed sleep, the burst was delayed too.
- The thyroid needs iodine, and adding iodine to salt prevents goitre. *How we know:* In the Kangra Valley trial that began in 1956, goitre fell sharply in the zones given iodised salt and stayed the same in the zone given plain salt.
- Bright light at night holds back melatonin in humans. *How we know:* Alfred Lewy and his colleagues showed it in 1980 by measuring melatonin in volunteers who were woken at night and put in bright light. It has been repeated many times, in adults and in children.
- The human body clock runs a cycle very close to 24 hours with no outside clues. *How we know:* Charles Czeisler's team measured it in 1999 in 24 people kept away from all time clues. The average was 24 hours and 11 minutes.
- Oestrogen closes the growth plates in both girls and boys. *How we know:* In 1994 doctors reported a man of 28 whose cells had no working oestrogen receptor. He was 204 centimetres tall, his growth plates were still open and he was still growing.

## How we found out
**René Descartes, Carey McCord and Floyd Allen, Aaron Lerner and his team, and Alfred Lewy and his team, 1649 (Descartes), 1917 (McCord and Allen), 1958 (Lerner) and 1980 (Lewy).** In 1649 the French thinker René Descartes wrote that the pineal gland was the main seat of the soul. He chose it because it is single and sits in the middle of the brain. Later, many doctors decided the opposite. They said it was a leftover with no job. In 1917 two Americans, Carey McCord and Floyd Allen, fed ground-up cow pineal glands to tadpoles. The tadpoles' dark skin soon turned pale. So the gland held a powerful chemical. Aaron Lerner, a skin doctor at Yale University, hoped the chemical might treat skin diseases. His team worked through more than 250,000 cow pineal glands. In 1958 they isolated a few thousandths of a gram of it and named it melatonin. It did almost nothing to human skin. But other scientists soon found that animals make it in the dark. In 1980 Alfred Lewy's team showed that bright light at night switches off melatonin in people. The gland turned out to be the body's signal that night has come.  
*What people believed before:* First, that the pineal gland is the seat of the soul. Later, that it is a useless leftover. The pale tadpoles showed that the gland makes a powerful chemical, and measurements in the dark and in the light showed that the chemical is a night signal.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The pituitary is the master gland that controls all the other glands.** (Many older textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: The pituitary does send hormones that drive the thyroid, the outer part of the adrenal glands, the ovaries and the testes.  
  Against: The pituitary takes its orders from the hypothalamus. The glands it drives send their own hormones back to slow it down. And adrenaline, insulin and melatonin are released with no order from the pituitary at all.
- **Melatonin is the hormone that makes you sleep.** (A common saying, and the label on some bottles; *debated*)  
  For: In humans melatonin rises about two hours before the usual bedtime, and sleepiness follows.  
  Against: Rats and hamsters are active at night, and their melatonin rises at night too. So melatonin signals darkness, and each animal answers differently. In studies, melatonin tablets shift the body clock, but they make people fall asleep only a little sooner. Children should take them only if a doctor says so.
- **Children grow only while they are asleep.** (Many grandparents, and some books; *debated*)  
  For: The biggest burst of growth hormone each day begins when deep sleep begins.  
  Against: Smaller bursts come in the daytime as well. Growth depends on hormone levels over weeks and months, and on food and genes. The bone-building that growth hormone starts goes on all day.
- **We do not tremble because we are afraid. We are afraid because we tremble.** (William James, 1884; *debated*)  
  For: The heart can speed up within a second, before a person has had time to think. People often say 'I only realised how scared I was when I saw my hands shaking.'  
  Against: The body's alarm is much the same in fear, anger and excitement, so the body alone cannot tell the feelings apart. A key experiment in this area, from 1962, did not give the same result when others repeated it.
- **Blue light from screens is the main reason screens spoil sleep.** (Many news articles; *debated*)  
  For: The eye cells that report to the body clock are most affected by blue light, and a 2015 study found later melatonin and slower sleep after four hours of tablet reading.  
  Against: A screen is far dimmer than daylight, and the measured delay in falling asleep was about ten minutes. Many sleep scientists think later bedtimes and exciting content do more harm than the light does.
- **Only animals have hormones.** (A natural first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: The word was first used in 1905 for chemicals carried in the blood of animals.  
  Against: Plants make hormones such as auxin and ethylene and carry them in sap or release them into the air. Fungi and single cells signal to each other with chemicals too.

## Roots
We have learned that hormones carry news through the blood, and that each cell answers in their own way. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Sitting-Still Alarm Test, one alarm made your heart speed up and, for many children, the mouth go dry. Adrenaline tells heart muscle to work harder and gut muscle to rest. Melatonin rises in the dark in a child, who gets sleepy, and in a rat, who wakes. So a hormone has no order written in it. Each cell that receives it decides what it means.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a message holds its meaning inside it, the way a parcel holds a gift. So we name hormones after one effect: the 'sleep hormone', the 'growth hormone', the 'stress hormone'. But the same molecule gets opposite answers from different receivers.  
*Evidence:* In the Alarm Test, the pulse rose, and for many children the mouth felt drier at the same time. Adrenaline speeds the heart and relaxes the gut, because the two carry different receptors. Thyroid hormone lengthens a child's bones and takes apart a tadpole's tail. Melatonin rises at night in animals who sleep at night and in animals who wake at night. A man with no working oestrogen receptor kept growing, though the hormone was there.

- *who am i:* You are trillions of cells who all get the same news: it is night, there is danger, it is time to grow. Each kind answers in their own way. What you call 'I feel sleepy' is all those answers together.
- *what is life:* Living beings send each other chemical messages, from yeast cells to banana plants to the glands in you. Being alive includes sending, receiving and answering.
- *what is this world:* Your sleep is timed by a clock that the turning Earth shaped, and that the Sun's light sets each morning. The causes of your yawn at night reach all the way to the sky.

You began as one cell. That cell divided into many kinds: heart cells, liver cells, bone cells, gut cells. One small gland now releases one kind of molecule into the blood, and the many kinds of cell each make something different of it. In the heart it becomes a faster beat. In the liver it becomes sugar. In the gut it becomes a pause. The many answers add up to one body that runs, grows or sleeps as a whole.

- **Thirukkural 423:** “எப்பொருள் யார்யார்வாய்க் கேட்பினும் அப்பொருள் / மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்ப தறிவு.” Whatever is heard, and from whoever's lips, wisdom is to find its true meaning. (Valluvar puts the work of meaning with the one who hears. A cell does this with a hormone. The same adrenaline arrives at the heart and at the gut, and each works out what it means there.)
- **Tao Te Ching 11:** “三十輻共一轂，當其無，有車之用。 / 埏埴以為器，當其無，有器之用。” Clay is shaped into a pot, and it is the empty space inside that makes the pot useful. (A receptor is a shaped hollow, and a hormone does nothing until it meets one. And the pineal gland's signal is made by an absence. It is the lack of light that says 'night'.)
- **Ludwig Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations, section 43:** For a large class of cases, the meaning of a word is its use in the language. (Wittgenstein said a word has no meaning sitting by itself. Its meaning is what people do with it. A hormone is like that. Its meaning is what each cell does with it.)
- Science (settled): One hormone produces different effects in different cells, depending on the receptor each cell carries. Raymond Ahlquist showed this for adrenaline in 1948, and medicines built on the idea work.
- Science (open): Every cell senses, decides and acts. Whether a single cell answering a hormone has any experience of doing so is a question that science has no agreed way to test.
- Other view, Many biologists: A cell does not work out a meaning. A receptor is a protein, and when a hormone joins it, chemistry follows. Evolution fixed long ago what each kind of cell does, the way a lock is cut before any key arrives. And the hormone is not empty of meaning. Its shape decides which receptors it can fit, so the message and the receiver share the work.

## Story: What the Water Told Them
*A made-up story. Girija, Suhas, Shanta Atte and the two rock pools are imagined. Dharwad is a real city in Karnataka. The science is real. Asian common toads lay strings of eggs in rain pools at the start of the monsoon. In 2017 Santosh Mogali, Srinivas Saidapur and Bhagyashri Shanbhag of Karnatak University in Dharwad reported that tadpoles of this toad, kept in water that was slowly lowered, became toadlets sooner and at a smaller size than tadpoles kept in steady water. The story stretches the difference a little, so that it is easy to see. The change from tadpole to toad is driven by thyroid hormone. Work on other species, by Robert Denver in the United States, showed that falling water raises a stress hormone in the tadpole, and that this pushes thyroid hormone up.*

The first real rain of June came at night, and by morning the toads had been.

Behind the house, where the hill showed its bare rock, there were two hollows that held water after rain. They were the same size, each about as wide as a bullock-cart wheel and as deep as Girija's hand was long. In each one lay strings of black dots in clear jelly, looped round and round like a dropped necklace.

'That one is mine,' said Suhas, pointing to the hollow nearer the tamarind tree. He was seven and claimed things quickly.

'Then this one is mine,' said Girija. She was ten, and she had already begun to worry.

She had reason. Last year the rain had come like this, with one great night of water, and then it had stopped for eighteen days. The hollows had dried to a grey crust. She remembered what had been in the crust.

In four days the dots had tails. In a week the pools were full of tadpoles, each smaller than a grain of cooked rice, black and busy. They scraped at the green fuzz on the rock all day long.

The rain did not come back.

On the eighth morning Girija took a piece of chalk from her school bag and drew a line on the rock just at the edge of her water. By evening the water had drawn back from the line by the width of her thumb. So she took the small steel pot to the well, filled it, carried it up the slope and poured it in, slowly, down the side of the rock so that the tadpoles would not be knocked about. The water came back to the line.

'You have to fill yours too,' she told Suhas.

'Tomorrow,' said Suhas.

She filled hers every evening after that. Five pots, sometimes six. The rope of the well made a red mark across her palms. Her chalk line stayed wet.

Suhas filled his twice, and then there was a cricket match, and then he forgot. His water went down a thumb's width each day. A pale ring showed on the rock where it had been. Then a second ring inside the first.

'They will die,' Girija said. 'You know that. They will be stuck in the mud like last year.'

Suhas looked at his pool and looked unhappy, and went to play.

Their aunt came on Sunday. Shanta Atte worked at the university, in the building where the zoology people kept their tanks, and she knew how to keep four hundred tadpoles alive on boiled spinach. Girija took her up the slope and showed her both pools. She showed her the chalk line, and the steel pot, and her palms.

Atte crouched by Suhas's pool for a long time. It was hardly deeper than a finger now.

'Look at them,' she said. 'Not at the water. At them.'

Girija looked. Her brother's tadpoles looked different, and she needed a moment to see why. They had legs. Two thin back legs each, folded against the tail like a pair of bent pins. Some had the bumps of front legs under the skin.

She ran to her own pool. Her tadpoles were bigger than his. They were fat and glossy, and they had no legs at all, only two specks where legs might one day be.

'Mine are behind,' she said. It came out as a wail. 'I carried all that water and mine are behind!'

'Are they?' said Atte. She did not say anything else about it. She asked what was for lunch.

Nine days later Suhas's pool was a film of water over mud, and the mud was hopping. Toads no bigger than Girija's smallest fingernail, each a perfect toad, with a stub of tail still on some of them. They climbed the pale rings on the rock and went off into the grass under the tamarind. By evening there was only mud, and nothing was stuck in it.

Suhas was unbearable at dinner. 'Mine are finished. Mine are toads. Yours are still babies.'

Girija did not answer. She was thinking about the two pools. The eggs had come on the same night. The sun was the same, and the green fuzz on the rock was the same. Only one thing had been different, and she had carried it up the hill herself, five pots every evening.

She had thought the water was only the place where the tadpoles lived. But his tadpoles had known their pool was going. Nobody had told them. The water had told them, a thumb's width a day, and they had hurried. They had left early, and they had left small.

And hers? Every evening she had brought her pool back up to the chalk line. Every evening she had been telling them something too. There is time, she had been saying. Stay. Eat. Grow.

They were not behind. They had done exactly what she had told them.

The next evening she went up with the steel pot and stood at her pool for a while. Then she bent and rubbed out the chalk line with her thumb, and drew a new one a little lower down. She poured in two pots, only to the new line.

The evening after that, she drew it lower again.

On the sixth day there were legs.

Her toads left twelve days after her brother's. She put one on her palm beside a mark she had made on her finger for the size of his. Hers was clearly bigger.

'Mine were first,' said Suhas.

'Mine are bigger,' said Girija.

They looked at Atte, who was shelling peas and seemed not to have heard.

That night it rained on the hill, hard, for the first time in five weeks. In the morning both hollows were full to the brim, and there were new strings of black dots in each.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Girija thinks of the water as a home that she must keep safe. The turn comes when she sees that the water is also a message. A tadpole becomes a toad when thyroid hormone rises in their body. A tadpole senses that the pool is shrinking, and a stress hormone then pushes the thyroid hormone up early. So the change comes sooner, at a smaller size. Girija's tadpoles got no such signal, so they kept eating and growing. Neither set did anything wrong. Each tadpole timed the change by a hormone, and the hormone answered the news from outside. Children's bodies do the same when hormones time growth, sleep and puberty by food, light and the seasons.

- Ask: The two pools had the same eggs, the same sun and the same food. What was the one thing that differed?
- Ask: Why might leaving the pool early and small be a good choice for a tadpole whose pool is drying?
- Ask: Why might staying longer and leaving bigger be a good choice for a tadpole whose pool stays full?
- Ask: Girija said her filling had been 'telling them something'. What do you think she meant?
- Ask: What news from outside does your own body use to time things, such as feeling sleepy or feeling hungry?

  *Parent says:* The pulse is the push of blood each time your heart beats. Let's practise counting it for 30 seconds.  
  *Child mode:* Rest your arm on the table. Your helper finds your pulse. Count the beats for 30 seconds together.
  - Note: Use two fingers, not the thumb, because the thumb has a pulse of its own. If you lose count, start the 30 seconds again.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the plan: rest, then calm counting, then racing a timer on sums. 2. Ask what the pulse will do in the race. 3. Ask how long it will take to come back. 4. Write down both guesses and the reasons.  
  *Parent says:* You will sit still the whole time. When you race the timer, what will your pulse do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what your pulse will do in the race. Guess how long it takes to come back. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: It will stay the same, because I am not moving.; It will go up, because I feel my heart thump when I am nervous.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Both of these reasons are sensible.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sits quietly for 3 minutes without talking. 2. Count the pulse for 30 seconds. 3. Wait half a minute and count again. 4. Ask whether the palms are dry or damp, and whether the mouth is wet or dry.  
  *Parent says:* Sit quietly and breathe normally. I will count your resting pulse twice.  
  *Child mode:* Sit quietly for 3 minutes. Your helper counts your pulse twice. Notice your palms and your mouth.
  - Note: If the two counts differ by more than 3 beats, wait another minute and count a third time. Use the two that are closest.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start a one-minute timer. 2. Your child counts aloud from 1, slowly, about one number each second. 3. Count the pulse during the last 30 seconds. 4. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Count aloud from 1, nice and slowly. There is no hurry and nothing to get wrong.  
  *Child mode:* Count aloud from 1, slowly, for one minute. Your helper counts your pulse.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start a one-minute timer. 2. Your child counts backwards from 200 in threes, aloud, as fast as possible. 3. Count the pulse during the last 30 seconds. 4. When the timer rings, your child sits silent. 5. Count for 30 seconds straight away, and again at 1, 2 and 3 minutes. 6. Ask about palms and mouth.  
  *Parent says:* Now race the timer. Count backwards from 200 in threes. Try to get below 140 before it rings. Go!  
  *Child mode:* Count backwards from 200 in threes, fast. Try to pass 140. Then sit silent while your helper counts.
  - Note: Keep your fingers on the wrist throughout. Do not correct mistakes in the sums. The pressure should come from the timer, not from you. For a child who finds threes easy, use sevens.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check that the pulse is back near the resting count. 2. Repeat the race with a new task: count backwards from 300 in fours. 3. Count the pulse during the last 30 seconds. 4. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* One try can be a fluke, so we race once more with a new sum. Ready? Go!  
  *Child mode:* Race the timer once more. Count backwards from 300 in fours. Your helper counts your pulse.
  - Note: The second rise is often smaller than the first, because the task is no longer new. That is worth noticing and writing down.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Multiply every 30-second count by 2. 2. Mark each one on a line graph in the order it was taken. 3. Join the marks.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn every count into beats a minute and put them on a graph.  
  *Child mode:* Multiply each count by 2. Mark each one on a graph. Join the marks with a line.
- **s8 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what your child claims, what they saw and why they think it happened. 2. Ask what else changed besides the pulse. 3. Only then read the parent note together. 4. Write three sentences in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me your claim, your evidence and your reason. What did your pulse do, and what did your palms and mouth do?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. Say what you saw. Say why you think it happened. Write three sentences.
  - Ask: No muscle was working hard. So what told your heart to speed up? (good: My brain decided the race was urgent and sent a signal.; Nerves to the heart, and adrenaline from the adrenal glands.)
  - Ask: Your heart sped up, but did your mouth make more saliva or less? (good: Less. It felt drier.; So the same alarm made one part do more and another part do less.)
  - Note: After your child has answered: in most children the pulse rises by 5 to 20 beats a minute in the race and is back near resting within 1 to 3 minutes. The brain judged the task urgent and signalled the sympathetic nerves. They speed the heart within a second or so, and they also signal the adrenal glands to release adrenaline. Be honest about the limit of this test. It cannot show how much of the rise was nerves and how much was adrenaline. Studies that sampled blood during mental sums found that adrenaline about doubles, which is still a small amount. So here the nerves probably did most of the work. In a real fright or a hard run, far more adrenaline is released. If the pulse did not rise at all, that is a real result too. Some children stay calm in this task.
  - Check: Your pulse rose while you sat still and raced the timer. What does that show? → **The body can sound an alarm from a thought alone**
### e2 · The Dim Evening Test: does evening light change when you get sleepy? (70 min, 7 days)
- **question:** If the lights are dim for the last hour and a half before bed, do you get sleepy sooner and fall asleep faster than on a bright evening?
- **predict:** Guess first. On dim evenings, will you feel more sleepy, less sleepy or the same at bedtime? Will you fall asleep sooner, later or in the same time? Write your guess, and write the reason for it.
- **fair test:** Change only the light. On all six evenings, eat dinner at the same time and switch the lights off for sleep at the same time. On all six evenings, use no phone, tablet or television in the last hour and a half. Do the same quiet things: read a paper book, draw, play a board game or talk. Take no nap in the day, and no tea, coffee or cola after 4 in the afternoon. Take turns: bright, dim, bright, dim, bright, dim. That way, a tiring day is as likely to fall on one kind of evening as on the other.
- **safety:** On dim evenings, keep enough light to walk about safely. Do not cover a hot bulb with cloth or paper. The grown-up checks on the child quietly and does not wake them.
- **record:** Fill in one row of the table each evening and add the morning score the next day. On day 7, find the mean sleepiness score and the mean minutes to fall asleep for the three bright evenings and for the three dim evenings. Draw two bars for each. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Evening | Bright or dim | Sleepiness score 15 minutes before lights-off (1 to 5) | Time of first yawn | Minutes to fall asleep | Next morning: how easy was waking? (1 to 5)
- **schedule:** day 1: Plan the six evenings and write your guess. Evening 1 is bright. Fill in the first row. (20 min); day 2: Morning: write how easy waking was. Evening 2 is dim. Fill in the row. (6 min); day 3: Morning score. Evening 3 is bright. Fill in the row. (6 min); day 4: Morning score. Evening 4 is dim. Fill in the row. (6 min); day 5: Morning score. Evening 5 is bright. Fill in the row. (6 min); day 6: Morning score. Evening 6 is dim. Fill in the row. (6 min); day 7: Last morning score. Find the mean for bright evenings and for dim evenings. Draw a bar chart. Write your claim, evidence and reasoning. (20 min)
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Agree on one dinner time and one lights-off time for all six evenings. 2. Explain the two kinds of evening. 3. Ask for a guess and the reason. 4. Write the guess in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* For six evenings only the light will change. Will dim light make a difference to your sleepiness? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Plan your six evenings. Guess what dim light will do. Say why. Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Dim light is like sunset, so my body may think it is night.; It will make no difference, because I get sleepy when I am tired.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Choose a lights-off time you can truly keep for six evenings.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the table with six rows. 2. Write 'bright' or 'dim' against each evening, taking turns. 3. Draw the sleepiness scale: 1 is wide awake, 3 is a little sleepy, 5 is eyes closing. 4. Choose the dim lamp and try it.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw the table and the sleepiness scale. Then we will try the dim lamp to check that we can still see.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the table. Mark each evening bright or dim. Draw the sleepiness scale from 1 to 5.
  - Note: A good dim lamp lets you read large print but makes small print hard. Put it low and behind the child, not at eye level.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. An hour and a half before lights-off, set the light as planned and put screens away. 2. Note the time of the first yawn. 3. Fifteen minutes before lights-off, ask for the sleepiness score. 4. After lights-off, look in every 5 minutes and note when your child is asleep.  
  *Parent says:* It is time to set the light for this evening. Tell me your sleepiness score when I ask, from 1 to 5.  
  *Child mode:* Set the light for this evening. Put screens away. Give your sleepiness score when asked.
  - Note: To check for sleep, whisper your child's name once from the door. No answer and slow, even breathing counts as asleep.
  - Wait: Do this step on each of the six evenings.
- **s4 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On waking, ask how easy it was to wake up, from 1 to 5. 2. Write the score in last evening's row.  
  *Parent says:* How easy was it to wake up this morning? 1 is very hard and 5 is very easy.  
  *Child mode:* When you wake, score how easy it was. 1 is very hard. 5 is very easy.
  - Wait: Do this step on the morning after each evening.
- **s5 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add the three bright scores and divide by 3. 2. Do the same for the three dim scores. 3. Do the same for minutes to fall asleep. 4. Draw the bars. 5. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. 6. Then read the parent note together.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me your claim, your evidence and your reason. Did the light make a difference for you? How sure are you?  
  *Child mode:* Find the mean for bright and for dim evenings. Draw the bars. Say what you found and why.
  - Ask: You knew which evenings were dim. Could that have changed your scores? (good: Yes. I expected to be sleepy, so I may have scored higher.; The minutes to fall asleep are fairer, because someone else measured them.)
  - Ask: Three evenings of each kind is a small number. What would make you more sure? (good: More evenings of each kind.; Other children doing the same test and getting the same result.)
  - Note: After your child has answered: special cells in the eye report brightness to the body clock. Bright evening light tells the clock it is still day, and the pineal gland holds back melatonin. In dim light, melatonin rises on time. Many families find more sleepiness and quicker sleep on dim evenings, but not all do. Three evenings of each kind is few, and one late party or one exciting day can swamp the effect. If your bars are equal, say so. That is an honest result.
  - Check: Why were screens put away on bright evenings as well as on dim ones? → **So that only the room light differed**
### e3 · Ten Times Weaker: how little sugar can your tongue still find? (25 min)
- **question:** Each tumbler has ten times less sugar than the one before. Which is the last tumbler that still tastes sweet to you?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which will be the last tumbler you can taste as sweet: tumbler 1, 2, 3 or 4? Write your guess, and write the reason for it.
- **fair test:** Change only the amount of sugar. Use the same drinking water, the same kind of tumbler and the same amount in each. Rinse the spoon before each transfer, so that no extra sugar is carried over. Taste without seeing the labels, because knowing the answer changes what people taste. Include one tumbler of plain water to catch guessing. Rinse your mouth with plain water between tastes. Do the whole tasting twice.
- **record:** Fill in the table for both rounds. Circle the weakest tumbler that you called sweet in both rounds. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you found, and why you think tumbler 4 has sugar in it though you cannot taste it.
- **results table:** Tumbler | Sugar in the water | Round 1: sweet or not? | Round 2: sweet or not?
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 10 tablespoons of water in tumbler 1 and stir in 4 level teaspoons of sugar. 2. Put 9 tablespoons of water in tumblers 2, 3 and 4. 3. Move 1 tablespoon from tumbler 1 to tumbler 2 and stir. 4. Rinse the spoon. 5. Move 1 tablespoon from 2 to 3, then from 3 to 4, rinsing each time. 6. Put 10 tablespoons of plain water in tumbler W.  
  *Parent says:* Each tumbler will have ten times less sugar than the one before it. We rinse the spoon each time so that no extra sugar sneaks across.  
  *Child mode:* Make tumbler 1 with sugar. Move one spoon from each tumbler to the next. Rinse the spoon each time.
  - Note: Tumbler 1 is about one part sugar in ten, like a very sweet sherbet. Tumbler 2 is one part in a hundred. Tumbler 3 is one in a thousand. Tumbler 4 is one in ten thousand.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand each tumbler on its paper slip. 2. Ask which tumbler will be the last to taste sweet. 3. Ask for the reason. 4. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Which will be the last tumbler that you can still taste as sweet? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the last tumbler that will taste sweet. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Tumbler 4, because there is still some sugar in it.; Tumbler 2, because after that there is too little to notice.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child turns away. 2. Slide the tumblers into a new order, keeping each on its slip. 3. Your child tastes one teaspoon from each and says 'sweet' or 'not sweet'. 4. Your child rinses with plain water between tastes. 5. Write the answers down, then shuffle and repeat.  
  *Parent says:* Taste each one and tell me: sweet, or not sweet? Rinse your mouth before the next. You will not know which is which.  
  *Child mode:* Turn away while the tumblers are shuffled. Taste each one. Say sweet or not sweet. Rinse between tastes.
  - Note: Give a clean teaspoon, or rinse it, for every taste. If your child calls the plain water sweet, they are guessing. Do one more round.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the tumblers to show the slips. 2. Circle the weakest tumbler called sweet in both rounds. 3. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning. 4. Ask whether tumbler 4 has sugar in it. 5. Only then read the parent note together.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me your claim, your evidence and your reason. Which was the last one you could taste? Is there sugar in tumbler 4?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the labels. Circle the last tumbler you tasted as sweet. Say what you found and why.
  - Ask: You cannot taste tumbler 4. How do you know there is sugar in it? (good: I put it there myself, one spoon from tumbler 3.; Not tasting something does not mean it is absent.)
  - Ask: If you kept going, which tumbler would be as weak as melatonin in your blood at night? (good: About tumbler 10.; Six more tumblers after tumbler 4.)
  - Note: After your child has answered: most children taste tumbler 2 and lose the sugar at tumbler 3. Some lose it at tumbler 2. Your tongue finds sugar with receptors on taste cells. They belong to the same large family of receptors as the ones that catch adrenaline. But hormone receptors hold their hormone far more tightly. At night, melatonin in the blood is about one part in ten thousand million. That would be tumbler 10 in your row. Cells still answer it, because their receptors catch it and the cell then amplifies the signal.
  - Check: Tumbler 4 tastes like plain water. What is the best thing to say about it? → **It has sugar, but too little for my tongue**

## Applications
- Iodised salt in nearly every Indian kitchen prevents goitre and protects babies' brains.
- Doctors measure hormones such as thyroid hormone and insulin in a small sample of blood, and replace a hormone that is missing.
- Children with type 1 diabetes inject insulin or wear an insulin pump.
- An asthma inhaler holds a medicine that fits one kind of adrenaline receptor and opens the airways.
- A person with a dangerous allergy carries an adrenaline injection pen, because adrenaline opens the airways and raises the blood pressure within minutes.
- Fruit sellers ripen bananas and mangoes on time with ethylene, the plant hormone gas.
- Pilots, nurses and factory workers on night shifts plan their light and sleep by what is known about the body clock.
- Some hospitals keep the lights low at night in rooms for newborn babies, so that the babies get a clear day and a clear night.

## Open questions
- **What tells the hypothalamus that it is time to start puberty?** The signal that switches the chain on, kisspeptin, was found in 2003. But nobody knows how the cells that make it work out that the body is ready. Body size, food and genes all seem to matter.
- **Do children grow smoothly, or in sudden jumps with still days in between?** In 1992 Michelle Lampl measured babies every day and reported growth in sudden bursts. Other scientists measured in other ways and saw smoother growth. Measuring a living child to a fraction of a millimetre is very hard.
- **What is sleep for?** We know how sleep is timed far better than we know why bodies need it. Clearing waste from the brain, saving energy and sorting memories are all good ideas with some evidence. None is agreed to be the whole answer.
- **What was melatonin first used for, billions of years ago?** Melatonin is found in bacteria and plants, who have no pineal gland. One idea is that it began as a chemical that protected cells from damage. Nobody can test directly what a molecule did so long ago.
- **How many hormones does the human body make?** New ones are still being found. The hunger hormone ghrelin was discovered only in 1999. Fat, bone, muscle and the gut all release signals that are still being worked out.
- **When your heart pounds and you feel afraid, which causes which?** The body's alarm and the feeling arrive so close together that they are hard to pull apart. Experiments that tried, such as injecting adrenaline, have given mixed results.

## Connected maths
- In the Sitting-Still Alarm Test, a helper counted 42 beats in 30 seconds at rest and 50 beats in 30 seconds during the race. How many beats a minute is each? How big was the rise? **84 beats a minute at rest and 100 beats a minute in the race. The rise was 16 beats a minute.**. 30 seconds is half a minute, so multiply each count by 2. 42 × 2 = 84. 50 × 2 = 100. The rise is 100 − 84 = 16 beats a minute. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L5`)
- After a fright there are 32 units of extra adrenaline in the blood. The body clears half of what is there every 2 minutes. How much is left after 8 minutes? What fraction of the start is that? **2 units are left. That is one sixteenth of the starting amount.**. 8 minutes is 4 lots of 2 minutes, so the amount halves 4 times. 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2. Two out of 32 is 2 ÷ 32 = one sixteenth. Notice that it never drops straight to zero. Each step removes half of what is left. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In Ten Times Weaker, tumbler 1 has 1 part sugar in 10. Each tumbler is ten times weaker than the one before. What is tumbler 4? Melatonin in the blood at night is about 1 part in 10,000,000,000. Which tumbler would be that weak? **Tumbler 4 has 1 part sugar in 10,000. Melatonin would be tumbler 10.**. Each step multiplies the number by 10, which adds one zero. Tumbler 1 is 1 in 10, tumbler 2 is 1 in 100, tumbler 3 is 1 in 1,000 and tumbler 4 is 1 in 10,000. So the tumbler number is the same as the number of zeros. 10,000,000,000 has ten zeros, so it is tumbler 10. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- Nila was 122 centimetres tall at 8, 127.5 centimetres at 9 and 133 centimetres at 10. How much did she grow each year? If she keeps the same speed, how tall will she be at 11? Give one reason the real answer could be higher. **She grew 5.5 centimetres each year. At the same speed she would be 138.5 centimetres at 11. It could be higher if her puberty growth spurt begins.**. 127.5 − 122 = 5.5. 133 − 127.5 = 5.5. So her speed is steady at 5.5 centimetres a year. 133 + 5.5 = 138.5 centimetres. A prediction like this assumes that nothing changes. In puberty, the ovaries release more hormone and growth speeds up to about 8 centimetres a year for a while. (queues `math.big-ideas.change-and-motion.L5`)
- In the Dim Evening Test, Meenakshi took 30, 20 and 25 minutes to fall asleep on bright evenings. She took 15, 25 and 20 minutes on dim evenings. Find the two means. Then say how sure she can be that dim light helped. **The mean is 25 minutes for bright evenings and 20 minutes for dim evenings. She cannot be very sure, because the two sets of numbers overlap.**. Bright: 30 + 20 + 25 = 75, and 75 ÷ 3 = 25 minutes. Dim: 15 + 25 + 20 = 60, and 60 ÷ 3 = 20 minutes. The dim mean is 5 minutes lower. But look at the single evenings. One dim evening took 25 minutes and one bright evening took 20. With only three of each, a gap of 5 minutes could be chance. More evenings would make her surer. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- A body clock can move about 1 hour a day. Dr Revathi's clock must move 5 and a half hours for London. How many days is that? If she moves it 3 hours before she flies, how many days are left after she lands? **About 5 and a half days in all, so she is fully shifted on the sixth day. If she moves 3 hours first, 2 and a half hours are left, which takes about 2 and a half days.**. At 1 hour a day, 5.5 hours takes 5.5 ÷ 1 = 5.5 days. If 3 hours are done before the flight, 5.5 − 3 = 2.5 hours are left, and that takes 2.5 days. Planning ahead does not make the clock move faster. It moves some of the days to before the trip. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is a hormone? → A chemical message that cells release into the blood. It changes what certain other cells do.
- Which glands release adrenaline, and where are they? → The adrenal glands, one on top of each kidney.
- Why does a hormone change some cells and not others? → Only cells that have the receptor for that hormone answer it.
- Which gland makes melatonin, and when? → The pineal gland, in the dark.
- Where does a child's bone grow longer? → At the growth plates, thin layers of cartilage near the ends of the bone.
- Which element does the thyroid need to make its hormone? → Iodine. That is why salt is iodised.
- Which part of the brain gives orders to the pituitary gland? → The hypothalamus.
- What two things together make you sleepy at night? → The body clock, with its night signal melatonin, and sleep pressure, which builds up while you are awake.
