# Mass extinctions: how most kinds of life ended five times, how life came back, and what is happening now
`sci.evolution.fossils-and-extinction.L5`

**Summary.** Five times, most of the kinds of living beings on Earth died out in a short time. Yet after each of those times, life filled the world again. How did so few survivors do that, and how long did it take? In this lesson you wipe out a paper world with coins and refill it with a die. You also roll for fossils in a paper cliff, to see why a sudden ending can look slow in the rocks. Then you compare the extinctions of today with the five great ones.

**Intro.** *Big dinosaurs filled the land for more than 130 million years. Mammals lived beside them all that time, and almost all stayed smaller than a cat. Then the big dinosaurs ended, and within about ten million years there were mammals as big as cows. Why did the mammals wait so long, and what let them change so fast?* You already know that fossils are traces of old life, and that the big dinosaurs died out. In this lesson you go further. You find out that dying out is normal, and that most kinds that ever lived are gone. You learn about the five times when most kinds ended together. These are called mass extinctions. You test with coins and a die how a nearly empty world fills up again, and who fills it. You find out why the last fossil of an animal is almost never the last animal. Then you look at today. Kinds are ending faster now than is normal, and scientists argue about how to name what is happening.

## Terms
- **Species**: A species is one kind of living being. Members of a species can have young together, and their young can have young too. *e.g.* The house crow and the jungle crow look alike, but they are two species. The Asian elephant is one species.
- **Extinction**: Extinction is the end of a species. It happens when the last member dies and none are left anywhere. *e.g.* The dodo of Mauritius is extinct. The last sure sighting of a living dodo was in 1662.
- **Background extinction**: Background extinction is the slow, steady ending of species that goes on all the time. A few species end in every stretch of a million years. *e.g.* A snail species that lives in one lake dies out when the lake dries over thousands of years. Nothing else in the world changes.
- **Mass extinction**: A mass extinction is a time when most species on Earth die out in a short stretch of time. Many scientists use the measure of three out of every four species gone within about two million years. *e.g.* About 66 million years ago, about three out of four species died out. The big dinosaurs and the ammonites were among them.
- **Fossil record**: The fossil record is all the fossils that have been found, together with the rock layers they lie in. It is our only direct record of life long ago. *e.g.* The rocks around Ariyalur in Tamil Nadu hold shells of sea animals. They are part of the record of a sea that was there in the time of the dinosaurs.
- **Boundary layer**: A boundary layer is a thin layer of rock that marks the change from one age to the next. The fossils below it differ from the fossils above it. *e.g.* A thin layer of clay, about as thick as a finger, marks the end of the age of dinosaurs in rocks around the world.
- **Iridium**: Iridium is a rare metal. Earth's surface rocks hold very little of it, and asteroids hold much more. *e.g.* The clay layer from 66 million years ago holds far more iridium than the rocks above and below. That was the first clue to an asteroid strike.
- **Flood basalt**: A flood basalt is a huge sheet of lava that poured out of long cracks in the ground, again and again, over thousands of years. Basalt is the dark rock that the lava became. *e.g.* The Deccan Traps of Maharashtra are a flood basalt. The flat-topped, stepped hills around Mahabaleshwar are layers of that lava.
- **Ocean acidification**: Ocean acidification means the sea becoming more acid. It happens when a lot of the gas carbon dioxide dissolves in sea water. Animals then find it harder to build shells. *e.g.* Soda water is water with carbon dioxide dissolved in it. It tastes a little sour because the gas makes a weak acid.
- **Detritus**: Detritus is dead plant and animal matter, such as fallen leaves, rotting wood and dead bodies. *e.g.* Earthworms, millipedes and many insects eat detritus. They can keep feeding even when no plant is growing.
- **Niche**: A niche is one way of making a living in one kind of place. It covers what a species eats, where they live and when they are active. *e.g.* Eating leaves from the tops of tall trees is a niche. Giraffes fill that niche in Africa today. Long-necked dinosaurs filled a niche like that once.
- **Recovery**: Recovery is the time after a mass extinction when the number of species grows again. New species arise from the survivors. *e.g.* After the big dinosaurs ended, the number of mammal species grew for millions of years.
- **Adaptive radiation**: An adaptive radiation is the splitting of one group into many new species in a fairly short time, each fitted to a different niche. It often happens when many niches are empty. *e.g.* After 66 million years ago, small mammals gave rise to bats, whales, horses, elephants and many more.
- **Lazarus species**: A Lazarus species is a species that people thought was extinct and then found alive. The name comes from an old story about a man who came back from death. *e.g.* Jerdon's courser is a bird of Andhra Pradesh. No scientist saw one from 1900 to 1986. Then one was found alive near Kadapa.
- **Extinction rate**: The extinction rate is how many species end in a given time, out of a given number of species. *e.g.* One estimate of the normal rate is 2 extinctions among 10,000 species in 100 years.
- **Extinct in the wild, and extinct in one country**: A species is extinct in the wild when the only ones left live in zoos or farms. A species can also be gone from one country and still live in another. *e.g.* Cheetahs were declared extinct in India in 1952. Cheetahs still lived in Africa and in Iran.

## Explanations
### Background extinction: species are always ending · *defined*
**Definition:** Background extinction is the slow ending of species, a few at a time, that goes on in every age.

No species lasts for ever. In the fossil record, a species usually lasts between one million and ten million years. Mammal species often last only one to three million years. Then the species ends, or changes so much that scientists give the descendants a new name. Why does a species end? A lake dries. A new predator arrives. A rival eats the same food and does it better. The climate shifts and the forest the species needs turns to grassland. While some species end, others split into new ones, so the number of species stays roughly level or slowly rises. Scientists estimate that more than 99 out of every 100 species that ever lived are now extinct. That is an estimate, because most species left no fossils at all. So extinction is the usual end of a species. It does not mean the species did something wrong.
- e.g. In the Hundred Squares activity, you colour only 1 square out of 100 for the species alive today.
- e.g. Trilobites were sea animals with jointed armour. Thousands of trilobite species came and went over about 270 million years. Each species lasted a few million years.
- e.g. Our own species, Homo sapiens, is about 300,000 years old. That is young for a species.
### Mass extinctions: the five great ones · *defined*
**Definition:** A mass extinction is a time when most species on Earth die out within a short stretch of geological time, all over the world and in many groups at once.

In 1982 two scientists in Chicago, David Raup and Jack Sepkoski, counted the fossil families of sea animals through time. Sepkoski had spent years gathering the list from books and papers. Their graph showed the number of kinds slowly rising, with five sharp drops. People now call them the Big Five. Each one killed about three quarters of all species or more. The percentages are estimates, and different studies give different numbers. Each of the five had a different cause, but the causes share something. In each one the air, the sea or the climate changed faster than living beings could keep up with. Later counts show some smaller drops too. So the Big Five are the largest of many extinction events. They are not a separate kind of event.
- e.g. End of the Ordovician, about 444 million years ago. Great ice sheets grew and the sea level fell, then rose again. Almost all life was in the sea then. About 85 out of 100 species ended.
- e.g. Late Devonian, about 370 million years ago. This one came in several steps over a long time. Sea life was hit hardest. The causes are still argued. One idea is that the first forests changed the air and cooled the Earth.
- e.g. End of the Permian, about 252 million years ago. Huge volcanoes in Siberia heated the Earth and made the sea acid. This was the worst of all.
- e.g. End of the Triassic, about 201 million years ago. The land began to split where the Atlantic Ocean is now, and lava poured out. Afterwards, dinosaurs became the main large land animals.
- e.g. End of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago. An asteroid struck, and the Deccan volcanoes were erupting. About 3 out of 4 species ended.
### The worst one: the end of the Permian · *defined*
**Definition:** The end-Permian extinction, about 252 million years ago, is the largest extinction known. About 8 out of 10 species in the sea ended, and about 7 out of 10 species of land animals with backbones.

In what is now Siberia, lava poured out of the ground over a huge area. The eruptions sent enormous amounts of carbon dioxide into the air. Carbon dioxide is a gas that traps heat, so the Earth warmed by several degrees. Much of the gas dissolved in the sea and made the water more acid. Animals who build shells and skeletons from the minerals in sea water could no longer build them well. Warm water also holds less oxygen, so large parts of the sea had too little oxygen to breathe. On land, many places dried out. The main die-off took about 60,000 years. That sounds long. But against the age of the Earth it is very short, like one second in a whole day. Older books say 96 out of 100 sea species died. Newer counts give about 81, because an earlier, separate extinction had been mixed in. The trilobites ended for ever. So did many groups of corals and sea lilies. Even insects, who came through the other mass extinctions well, lost many groups.
- e.g. After this extinction, one plant-eater about the size of a pig was the most common land animal with a backbone for a time. This animal is called Lystrosaurus. In some rock beds of South Africa, about 95 out of 100 fossils of land animals with backbones are Lystrosaurus.
- e.g. Lystrosaurus fossils are also found in India, in the Panchet rocks of the Damodar valley in West Bengal and Jharkhand. India, Africa and Antarctica were joined in one land then.
- e.g. Sea life took about 10 million years to become rich again. Land animals with backbones took even longer.
### The end of the Cretaceous: the asteroid and the Deccan volcanoes · *debated*
**Definition:** About 66 million years ago, about three quarters of all species died out. An asteroid about 10 kilometres wide struck where Mexico is now, at a time when the Deccan volcanoes of India were erupting.

The asteroid strike is not in doubt. It left a buried crater about 180 to 200 kilometres wide, called Chicxulub. It left a thin layer of clay rich in iridium all over the world. It also left grains of quartz with tiny cracks that only a huge shock can make. Dust and soot blocked the sunlight. Computer models suggest the dark lasted from some months to about two years. Without light, plants and the tiny floating algae of the sea could not make food, so food chains broke from the bottom. The Deccan eruptions are not in doubt either. Their lava still covers about 500,000 square kilometres of western and central India. Volcanic gases can warm the Earth and make rain and sea water acid. The argument is about which of the two did most of the killing. Dating the lava is hard. Two teams dated it in 2019 with two methods, and their answers did not agree on when the biggest eruptions came.
- e.g. At Anjar in Kutch, Gujarat, layers rich in iridium lie in lake mud between Deccan lava flows. So lava was already flowing in India before the asteroid's dust settled there.
- e.g. In Meghalaya, the rocks beside the Um Sohryngkew River hold a layer with much more iridium than the layers around it.
- e.g. Rajasaurus was a meat-eating dinosaur about as long as a small bus. Their bones were found at Rahioli in Gujarat, in rocks from the last few million years before the end.
- **Most earth scientists:** The asteroid was the main cause. In 2010 a group of 41 scientists reviewed twenty years of evidence and came to this view. In 2020 a study of old sea temperatures found that the big changes fit the time of the strike, not the eruptions.
- **Gerta Keller of Princeton University and her co-workers:** The Deccan eruptions were the main cause. Keller has studied tiny sea fossils for decades. She argues that many species were already in trouble before the strike.
- **A middle view:** The eruptions had already put life under strain, and the asteroid then did most of the killing. Many scientists think both mattered and still argue about the shares.
### Who lived through the asteroid strike, and why · *defined*
**Definition:** A mass extinction does not kill at random. At the end of the Cretaceous, survival depended mostly on body size, on food and on shelter.

On land, almost no animal with a backbone heavier than about 25 kilograms survived. A large animal needs a lot of food every day, and there was almost none. Animals who fed on living plants died, and so did the meat-eaters who fed on them. Many survivors belonged to food chains that begin with detritus. Insects, worms and snails ate dead leaves and rotting wood. Small mammals, small birds, lizards and frogs ate the insects, worms and snails. Animals who could shelter in burrows or under water came through the first heat and the cold that followed. Seeds and spores could wait in the soil. Rivers and lakes did better than the open sea, because dead matter washing in from the land kept feeding them. So the features that had made a species successful stopped helping. Great size had been an advantage for millions of years, and now it was a danger. Small size, a wide diet and a hiding place had been ordinary features, and now they mattered most. Luck mattered too. Many small mammal species and most kinds of birds also died.
- e.g. Crocodiles and turtles are an exception to the size rule. They live in water and can go for months without food.
- e.g. Among birds, the survivors seem to have been ground-living birds. One explanation is that the forests burned or died, so tree-living birds lost their homes.
- e.g. In the sea, the ammonites died out. They were shelled relatives of squids, and their young floated among the tiny algae. The nautilus, a relative who lives in deep water, survived and is still alive.
- e.g. The first plants to cover the bare ground were ferns. Fern spores are so common in the rock just above the clay layer that scientists call it the fern spike.
### Recovery: how a nearly empty world fills up again · *defined*
**Definition:** Recovery is the growth in the number of species after a mass extinction. The survivors spread, and over a very long time their descendants split into many new species that fill the empty niches.

A mass extinction leaves many niches empty. Nobody eats the tall leaves, and nobody hunts in the shallow sea. A world full of species leaves little room for a new one, because a new kind must do a job better than someone who already does it. An emptied world is different. The survivors meet few rivals, so many of their varieties can live and have young. Over many thousands of generations, natural selection fits different groups of descendants to different niches, and one species becomes many. This is an adaptive radiation. It is fast compared with normal times, and still very slow for us. A study by James Kirchner and Anne Weil in 2000 looked at the whole fossil record of sea life. The number of species took about 10 million years to recover after an extinction, whether the extinction was large or small. The lost species never come back. New and different species take their places.
- e.g. In your Empty Board Test, no group can add a species while the board is full. After the extinction, the survivors fill the board in a few rounds, and the group marked D never returns.
- e.g. At Corral Bluffs in Colorado, fossils show the first million years after the asteroid. The mammal survivors were about the size of a rat. About 100,000 years later there were mammals of about 6 kilograms. After about 700,000 years, some weighed nearly 50 kilograms.
- e.g. Mammals were not the only winners. Today there are more than 6,000 species of mammals and about 11,000 species of birds. Birds are dinosaurs, so the age of dinosaurs has not fully ended.
- e.g. Some groups survive a mass extinction and then fade away in the next few million years. Scientists call such a group a 'dead clade walking'. A clade is one branch of the family tree of life.
### The last fossil is not the last animal · *defined*
**Definition:** The last fossil found of a species almost always comes from some time before the species really ended. The rarer the fossils, the bigger the gap is likely to be.

Very few animals become fossils, and people find only a few of those. So the youngest fossil of a species is the last one that was buried, kept and found. The real last animal lived later, and sometimes much later. Two scientists, Philip Signor and Jere Lipps, pointed out what follows from this in 1982. Suppose many species die on the same day. The common ones leave fossils right up to that day. The rare ones leave their last fossil well below it. A person reading the cliff from the bottom up sees the species vanish one after another. A sudden ending looks like a slow one. This is called the Signor-Lipps effect. Your Last Fossil Test shows it with a die. The same effect works at the start of a species too. The first fossil found is later than the first animal.
- e.g. For years, the highest dinosaur bones found in the rocks of western North America lay about three metres below the iridium layer. Some scientists said the dinosaurs had gone before the asteroid. Wider searches later found bones closer to the layer.
- e.g. In the 1980s Peter Ward studied ammonites in sea cliffs in Spain. At first they seemed to stop well below the boundary. After more years of searching in nearby cliffs, he found ammonites right up to it.
- e.g. The fix is to search more rock, in more places, and to use maths to estimate the likely gap.
### Lost and found: Lazarus species · *defined*
**Definition:** A Lazarus species is one that was thought extinct and was then found alive. Not being seen is weak evidence that a species is gone.

Scientists call a species extinct only when careful searches, in the right places and seasons, have failed for many years. The rule of the Red List, the world list of species at risk, is that there must be no reasonable doubt that the last one has died. This is hard to be sure of. A small animal who is active at night in thick scrub can be missed for a hundred years. So some species come back from the list of the lost. Finding one is a joy, and a warning as well. A species missed for a century is almost always very rare, and can still end soon.
- e.g. The coelacanth is a large fish with fleshy fins. The youngest fossils known were about 66 million years old, so scientists thought coelacanths had died out with the big dinosaurs. In 1938 Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer found a fresh one on a fishing boat in South Africa.
- e.g. Jerdon's courser is a night bird of the scrub forest near Kadapa in Andhra Pradesh. The last record was in 1900. In January 1986 a bird trapper named Aitanna caught one, and Bharat Bhushan of the Bombay Natural History Society confirmed the find. The state then made the place a sanctuary. In August 2025 birdwatchers recorded the bird's call again, after more than fifteen years without a record.
- e.g. The dodo is not a Lazarus species. People have searched the small island of Mauritius for more than 300 years. In about 1625, the Mughal court painter Ustad Mansur painted a living dodo. That painting is among the best pictures we have of the bird. DNA from a museum dodo shows that the Nicobar pigeon of India's Nicobar Islands is the closest living relative.
### Extinction today: measuring the rate · *defined*
**Definition:** The extinction rate today is the number of species ending in our time, compared with the number that would end in normal times.

To know whether today is unusual, scientists compare two numbers. The first is the background rate from the fossil record. A careful study in 2015, led by Gerardo Ceballos in Mexico, used a high value for it on purpose, so as not to exaggerate. The value was 2 extinctions for every 10,000 species in 100 years. At that rate, about 9 species of animals with backbones should have ended since 1900. The second number is the count of real losses. On a cautious count, 477 such species are gone or very probably gone since 1900. That is about 50 times the normal number. Other studies give from tens of times to 1,000 times, because each method counts differently. The causes are known. People clear the places where species live, hunt and fish too much, carry species to new places, pollute, and heat the climate. Each cause is something people do, so people can also do less of it.
- e.g. In October 2025 the Red List had looked at 172,620 species. Of these, 48,646 were at risk of extinction. That is more than one in four of the species checked.
- e.g. The passenger pigeon of North America once numbered in billions. Hunting and forest clearing ended them. The last one, a female named Martha, died in a zoo on 1 September 1914.
- e.g. Most of the millions of species on Earth have never been checked. Most insects have not even been named.
### Are we in a sixth mass extinction? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that species are ending much faster than normal because of what people do. They disagree about whether to call this a mass extinction yet.

A mass extinction, by the usual measure, means about three out of four species gone. Since the year 1500, fewer than 1,000 extinctions have been recorded, among well over a million named species. Many more have surely been missed. Even so, the losses so far are far below three out of four. The worry is the rate. If the rate stays as high as now, and the species at risk today are lost, the count could reach the level of the Big Five in some hundreds or thousands of years. In the life of the Earth that would be very sudden. So the two sides agree on most of the facts. They differ on which word fits them.
- e.g. In 2011 a team led by Anthony Barnosky wrote that the losses so far are serious, but do not yet make a mass extinction. They added that losing the species now at risk would bring one within a few centuries.
- e.g. India shows both sides. The cheetah was lost from India by 1952. The tiger, the Asiatic lion and the one-horned rhinoceros were close to ending and have grown in number under protection.
- **Gerardo Ceballos, Paul Ehrlich and co-workers (2015):** A sixth mass extinction is already under way. The rate is far above normal, and waiting for three quarters of species to die before using the name would be waiting too long.
- **Anthony Barnosky and co-workers (2011), and Stuart Pimm:** It has not happened yet. The rate is alarming, but most species are still here, so a mass extinction can still be prevented.
- **Some palaeontologists:** The fossil record mostly shows sea animals with shells, counted over millions of years. Today's counts are mostly of birds and mammals over a few hundred years. The two are hard to compare fairly.
### If life's history ran again, would the same beings appear? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists ask whether the living world we have was bound to happen, or depends on accidents such as an asteroid strike.

If the asteroid had missed, big dinosaurs might still rule the land, and large mammals might never have appeared. That would mean no elephants, no whales and no humans. The way things turn out when they depend on accidents is called contingency. Other scientists point to convergence. Convergence means that unrelated animals often arrive at the same answer to the same problem. Nobody can run the history of life twice, so this stays an argument about evidence. Your Empty Board Test gives a small taste of it. Run it twice with the same rules, and a different group may end up with the most squares.
- e.g. Wings arose separately in insects, in the flying reptiles called pterosaurs, in birds and in bats.
- e.g. Dolphins are mammals and ichthyosaurs were reptiles. Both ended up with the body shape of a fast fish.
- **Stephen Jay Gould, in Wonderful Life (1989):** Chance rules. Replay the tape of life from the start, and the result would be utterly different each time. Humans would almost surely not appear.
- **Simon Conway Morris, in Life's Solution (2003):** There are only so many good ways to make a living. Eyes, wings and large brains have arisen many times, so something like them would arise again.
### Following the idea further: atoms, the sky and what nobody knows · *idea*
**Definition:** The causes of mass extinctions reach from single atoms to the space around the Earth.

Start with atoms. A molecule of carbon dioxide is one carbon atom joined to two oxygen atoms. When it dissolves in water, it joins with a water molecule and makes a weak acid called carbonic acid. That one small reaction, repeated without number, made the end-Permian sea acid. It is happening again. Since about 1850 people have burned coal and oil, and the surface of the sea has become a little more acid. Next, energy. The asteroid was moving at many kilometres each second. When it stopped, its energy of movement turned into heat, shock and flying rock. Then the sky. The iridium in the clay layer came from space. Earth moves among countless rocks, and large strikes are very rare but not over. Space agencies now track the large rocks that pass near the Earth. Last, the open questions. Nobody knows why the Late Devonian extinction came in steps. Nobody knows how many species are alive today. Estimates run from a few million to many millions.
- e.g. In 2022 the DART spacecraft struck a small asteroid on purpose and changed its path a little. It was the first test of pushing an asteroid aside.
- e.g. Soda water turns blue litmus paper red, because dissolved carbon dioxide makes carbonic acid.

## Mostly agreed
- Most species that have ever lived are extinct. *How we know:* Rock layers of every age hold fossils of species that are not alive today. A species usually lasts a few million years in the record, and life is more than 3,500 million years old.
- At least five times, most species died out within a short stretch of geological time. *How we know:* Counts of fossil groups through the rock layers show five sharp drops. Raup and Sepkoski published the first such count in 1982, and later counts with far more fossils show the same five.
- A large asteroid struck the Earth about 66 million years ago. *How we know:* There is a buried crater in Mexico of that age. A layer rich in iridium, with shocked quartz, lies in rocks of that age around the world, including in India.
- The end-Permian extinction happened at the time of huge eruptions in Siberia. *How we know:* The lava and the extinction layer have been dated with uranium in tiny crystals. The dates match to within some tens of thousands of years.
- After a mass extinction, new species arise from the survivors, and the lost species do not return. *How we know:* The fossils above each extinction layer belong to different species from those below. No trilobite appears above the end-Permian layer anywhere.
- Species are ending faster today than the normal rate, mainly because of what people do. *How we know:* The recorded losses of birds, mammals, amphibians and other animals with backbones since 1900 are many times the number expected from the fossil record.

## How we found out
**Walter Alvarez, Luis Alvarez, Helen Michel and Frank Asaro in California. Glen Penfield and Antonio Camargo, and later Alan Hildebrand, for the crater., The iridium paper came out in 1980. The crater was first noticed in 1978 and confirmed in 1991..** For 150 years, most geologists held that the Earth changes only slowly. Charles Lyell taught this in the 1830s, and Charles Darwin agreed. They thought sudden mass deaths in the rocks were only gaps in the record. In the late 1970s Walter Alvarez was studying a thin clay layer at Gubbio in Italy. Below it lay tiny sea fossils of the dinosaurs' time, and above it almost none. He asked his father, the physicist Luis Alvarez, how to tell how long the clay took to form. They decided to measure iridium, which falls from space as dust at a steady rate. The chemists Helen Michel and Frank Asaro did the measuring. The clay held about thirty times more iridium than the rock around it. That was far too much for slow dust. In 1980 the four proposed that an asteroid had struck. Many geologists objected and asked where the crater was. In fact Glen Penfield and Antonio Camargo, who were searching for oil in Mexico, had found a buried ring 180 kilometres wide in 1978. Almost nobody listened to them. In 1991 Alan Hildebrand's team connected the ring to the clay.  
*What people believed before:* That the Earth and its life change only slowly, so a sudden worldwide die-off could not be real. The iridium layer and the crater showed that one day can change the history of life.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Deccan volcanoes of India, not the asteroid, were the main killer 66 million years ago.** (Gerta Keller of Princeton University, with co-workers including Indian geologists; *minority*)  
  For: The eruptions were enormous and went on for hundreds of thousands of years. Some of the tiny sea fossils Keller studies seem to decline before the strike. In India, iridium lies between lava flows, so lava was flowing before the dust settled.  
  Against: The best dates put the extinction at the time of the strike. A 2020 study of sea temperatures found the big changes at the strike, not at the eruptions. A decline before the strike may be partly the Signor-Lipps effect.
- **The dinosaurs were already dying out slowly before the asteroid came.** (Many palaeontologists before the 1990s, and a few today; *debated*)  
  For: In some places fewer kinds of dinosaur bones are found in the last few million years before the end.  
  Against: Rare fossils always seem to stop early. Careful searches in North America found dinosaur bones close to the boundary. Where the record is good, many kinds of dinosaurs lived right up to the end.
- **Mass extinctions come round every 26 million years or so, driven by something in space.** (David Raup and Jack Sepkoski, in 1984; *rejected*)  
  For: Their count of sea fossils seemed to show extinction peaks at regular gaps. Some astronomers suggested a faint companion star of the Sun that sends comets our way.  
  Against: No such star has been found, though telescopes have searched the whole sky. Better dating of the rocks made the regular pattern much weaker.
- **A sixth mass extinction has already begun.** (Gerardo Ceballos, Paul Ehrlich and others, in 2015; *debated*)  
  For: Animals with backbones have been ending about 50 times faster than normal since 1900, on a cautious count. More than one in four of the species checked are at risk.  
  Against: Fewer than 1 in 100 named species are recorded as lost so far. A mass extinction means about 3 in 4. So others say it is a danger ahead and has not happened yet.
- **Extinction is natural, so losing species today does not matter.** (A view often heard in arguments about protecting nature; *rejected*)  
  For: It is true that extinction is the normal end of every species, and that life recovered after each mass extinction.  
  Against: Recovery took about 10 million years each time. Our whole species is only about 300,000 years old. A lost species never returns. Also, today's losses have a cause that can choose to stop.

## Roots
We have learned how most kinds of life ended five times, and how the survivors filled the world again. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Empty Board Test, no group could add a species while the board was full. The extinction emptied most squares, and the few survivors filled them within a few rounds. The group that had held most of the board never came back. So the species alive today are not the best that ever lived. They descend from whoever got through, and they grew into the room that the lost ones left.  
*Habit it breaks:* We speak of extinct animals as failures. We say 'dead as a dodo', and we call something old-fashioned a dinosaur. We speak as if living species had won a fair race. But the big dinosaurs filled the land for more than 130 million years, and ended because the world changed in a day. Calling the living 'winners' and the extinct 'losers' hides how much depended on what the world did.  
*Evidence:* On your board, the mammals held 4 squares for as long as the board was full. They held about 12 after the refill, and in some runs they held none. In the real record, no land animal with a backbone heavier than about 25 kilograms came through the asteroid strike, except some crocodiles and turtles. Mammals stayed small beside the dinosaurs for more than 100 million years. Within about 700,000 years of the strike, some weighed nearly 50 kilograms.

- *who am i:* You are a mammal. Your ancestors of 66 million years ago were small animals who got through the dark months, perhaps in burrows. If the asteroid had missed the Earth, there might be no people at all.
- *what is life:* Almost every species ends, yet life has not stopped once in more than 3,500 million years. Life goes on by branching into new species from whoever is left.
- *what is this world:* The world changes. The sea, the air and the climate shift, sometimes slowly and sometimes in a day. Living beings change them too. The first forests did, and people are doing so now.

All living beings descend from one beginning. Over a very long time, that one line branched into millions of species. Five times, most of the branches were cut off together. Each time the line of life itself was not cut. The few branches left grew and split, and the world was full of many kinds again. They were different kinds each time. So the many kinds are not fixed. They come and go, and the one line of life runs on through all of them. You are part of that line.

## Story: The Fish Under the Sharks
*This is a true story, told with some imagined details. Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, Captain Hendrik Goosen, the trawler Nerine, the date, the fish and the colours of the fish, the taxi, the mortuary and the cold store that refused the fish, the taxidermist Robert Center, the letter and sketch to Dr J. L. B. Smith, his telegram, his visit on 16 February 1939 and the name Latimeria chalumnae are all real. The words people say to each other are imagined, except Smith's telegram and his sentence 'There was not a shadow of a doubt', which he wrote later. Marjorie's thoughts, the taxi driver's manner and the last conversation about the rocks are imagined.*

The telephone rang in the little museum at East London, on the coast of South Africa. It was the twenty-second of December, 1938, and very hot. Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer was thirty-one. She ran the museum almost alone, and she was busy mounting the bones of a fossil reptile before the holiday.

The call was from the docks. Captain Goosen's fishing boat, the Nerine, had come in. He often kept strange fish aside for her. Would she like to look?

She nearly said no. The bones were not finished, and the docks were far. But the crew had been kind to her museum all year, so she went.

On the deck lay a slippery heap of sharks and rays, already drying in the sun. She looked it over and saw nothing she needed. Then, under the heap, she saw one blue fin.

She moved the sharks aside. The fish beneath was as long as she was tall. The scales were as hard as armour. The colour was a pale mauve-blue, flecked with white, with a silver-green shine that moved as she moved. And the fins were wrong. Four of them stood out from the body on thick, fleshy stalks, almost like short legs.

'What is this one?' she asked.

'No idea,' said an old sailor. 'Thirty years at sea, and I never saw one. This fish snapped at the captain's hand.'

She did not know what the fish was either. She only knew that she must not lose this fish.

The taxi driver did not want a dead fish of fifty-seven kilograms on his back seat. She told him she would take another taxi, then. He sighed, and they lifted the fish in together.

At the museum she searched every book she had. No picture matched. The chairman of the museum looked in at the door and said the fish was only a rock cod. She did not think so. A rock cod does not have fins on stalks.

The heat was the enemy now. The museum had no cold room and far too little preserving liquid. She took the fish to the town mortuary, where bodies are kept cold. The man in charge would not have a fish among them. She took the fish to the cold store where the town kept its meat and fruit. They refused as well.

So she went to the one man who could help a little. Mr Center was a taxidermist. His work was to preserve the skins of animals. He wrapped the fish in bedsheets and newspapers soaked in preserving liquid. That would slow the rotting, though it could not stop it.

That evening she wrote to Dr Smith, who knew a great deal about the fishes of South Africa. She could not send him the fish, so she drew a sketch for him. She drew the armoured scales and the strange tail with the extra little tuft at the end. She drew the four fins on their stalks. Then she posted the letter and waited.

No answer came. Dr Smith was far away on holiday, and the letter sat waiting for him. Christmas passed. The fish began to leak oil through the sheets, and the smell grew. On the twenty-seventh, Mr Center told her plainly that the inside could not be saved. If she wanted to keep anything, he must skin the fish now and throw the soft parts away. She said yes, and it felt like losing.

On the third of January a telegram came. MOST IMPORTANT PRESERVE SKELETON AND GILLS. It was a week too late. The gills had gone out with the rubbish.

Dr Smith reached East London on the sixteenth of February. He walked into the room where the stuffed fish stood, and he stopped. He walked all the way round. He touched the scales. When he spoke, his voice was not steady.

'There was not a shadow of a doubt,' he wrote afterwards. He told her the name of the family. Coelacanths.

'I looked in every book,' said Marjorie. 'Why was this fish in none of them?'

'You looked in the books of living fish,' he said. 'These are in the books of fossils. I have seen them only in stone. They were swimming before there were any dinosaurs. The youngest stone one is as old as the last dinosaurs. After that there are no more of them, in any rock, anywhere. Everyone agreed they had died out together with the dinosaurs.'

She looked at the fish, and at the fins on their stalks.

'Then where have they been all this time?'

'Here,' said Dr Smith. 'In deep water, among rocks, where no mud settles to bury a fish and keep the bones for us. They did not stop living. The rocks stopped keeping a record of them, and we took that to mean they were gone.'

Marjorie thought of the heap of sharks, and the one blue fin. She had almost stayed at her desk.

'So one is not enough,' she said. 'We need the gills. We need the inside. We need another fish.'

'We do,' he said. 'And now we know that there is another one to find.'

He named the fish Latimeria, after her. It took fourteen years to find the second one.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the key idea is that having no record of something is different from that thing being gone. Fossil coelacanths stop in rocks about 66 million years old. People read the missing fossils as extinction. But fossils form only where bodies are buried quickly, and the deep rocky slopes where these fish live bury almost nothing. The same idea runs through the Last Fossil Test: the last fossil found is not the last animal. The story also shows a woman without a science degree doing careful science. She noticed what did not fit, refused the easy name of rock cod, and kept the evidence as well as she could.

- Ask: Why did scientists think coelacanths were extinct?
- Ask: The fish had been alive all along. So why were there no fossils of them from the last 66 million years?
- Ask: The chairman said the fish was a rock cod. What did Marjorie see that told her otherwise?
- Ask: Why was the stuffed skin not enough? What could the gills and the inside have shown?
- Ask: Can you think of another living being who might be alive now though nobody has seen them for years? How would you search?

### e1 · The Empty Board Test: after a mass extinction, who fills the world again? (35 min)

  - Ask: The mammals had a die on the full board too. What changed for them after the extinction? (good: There were empty squares next to them.; Nothing about the mammals changed. The room around them changed.)
  - Note: In most homes the mammals go from 4 squares to between 8 and 16, and the two runs end differently. Write down what you really got. The reason: a new species needs an empty niche. The full board had none, so no roll could help. The extinction emptied about three quarters of the board, and the survivors had room on every side. The filled squares rise quickly at first and then slowly, because the last empty squares are hard to reach. The lost group never returns, because new species come only from living ones. The model leaves a lot out. Real refilling took about 5 to 10 million years. Real species also change as they spread, so the new mammals were not copies of the old ones.
  - Think: If you ran this world a hundred times, in how many would the mammals end with the most squares?
### e2 · The Last Fossil Test: if five species died on the same day, would the rocks show it? (30 min)
- **question:** Five species all die out at the same moment, at the top of layer 20 of a paper cliff. Some left many fossils and some left few. When you search the cliff, in which layer is the last fossil of each?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. All five species end at the top of layer 20. In which layer will you find the highest fossil of the commonest species? In which layer for the rarest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All five species lived through every layer and ended at the same moment. You search every layer in the same way, with one roll of the same die. Only one thing differs between the species: how many numbers on the die count as a find.
- **record:** Draw the cliff as a table with 20 rows and 5 columns. Tick the highest layer where each species is found. For cliffs 2 and 3, write only the layer number of the highest find.
- **results table:** Species | Numbers that count as a find | Highest fossil, cliff 1 | Cliff 2 | Cliff 3
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain that the paper will be a cliff of 20 rock layers. 2. Say that five species all die at the very top, on the same day. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason for the commonest and the rarest species. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Five species all end on the same day, at the top of this cliff. Species A left lots of fossils. Species E left very few. In which layer will you find the last fossil of each? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Five species end on the same day. Guess the layer of the last fossil of each. Say why.
  - Ask: Why might the last fossil of E not be in the top layer? (good: E is rare, so there may be no fossil of E in that layer.; You might not find one even if E was alive then.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say every last fossil will be in layer 20, because that is when the species died.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a table with 20 rows and 5 columns. 2. Number the rows from 20 at the top to 1 at the bottom. 3. Head the columns A, B, C, D and E. 4. Draw a thick red line across the top. 5. Under each heading, write the rolls that count as a find.  
  *Parent says:* The top row is the youngest rock. A is found on a roll of 1 to 5. B on 1 to 4. C on 1 to 3. D on 1 or 2. E only on a 1.  
  *Child mode:* Draw 20 rows and 5 columns. Number them from 20 at the top. Draw a red line on top.
  - Note: Every species was alive in every layer. The die only decides whether a fossil of that species was made, kept and found in that layer.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Begin with species A in layer 20. Roll the die. 2. If the roll counts as a find, put a tick and stop searching for A. 3. If not, put a dash and go down one layer. Roll again. 4. Do the same for B, C, D and E. 5. If you reach layer 1 with no find, write 'none found'.  
  *Parent says:* We only want the highest fossil of each species, so stop at the first tick. Start at the top each time and work down.  
  *Child mode:* Start at layer 20. Roll. A find gets a tick. No find gets a dash. Then go down.
  - Note: Species A is usually ticked in layer 20. Species E is often ticked five or more layers lower. About 1 time in 40, E is not found at all.
  - Check: In your cliff, species E has dashes in layers 20 to 15 and a tick in layer 14. When did E really die out? → **At the top of layer 20, with the others**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Search a second cliff in the same way. You need not draw it. Count down the layers aloud as you roll. 2. Write only the layer of the highest find for each species. 3. Do the same for a third cliff.  
  *Parent says:* One cliff can be a fluke. Let's search two more cliffs. Count down from 20 as you roll, and write the layer of the first find.  
  *Child mode:* Search two more cliffs. Count down from 20 as you roll. Write the layer of each first find.
  - Note: Three cliffs show the pattern better than one. In a single cliff, E can be found in layer 20 by luck.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For cliff 1, draw a bar for each species. The height of the bar is the layer of the highest find. 2. Draw the red line across the chart at 20. 3. For each species, find the highest find in any of the three cliffs. Write it beside the bar.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a bar up to the last fossil of each species. Then look at all three cliffs together. What is the highest layer for each?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each species. Draw the red line at 20. Find the best of three cliffs.
  - Note: The bars for cliff 1 usually step down from A to E. The best of three cliffs is usually much closer to 20 for every species.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cover the red line with a ruler. 2. Ask what a fossil hunter who saw only cliff 1 would say about how these species ended. 3. Ask your child what they guessed, what they saw and why it happened. 4. Then read the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Suppose you found only this cliff and knew nothing else. Would you say the five species ended together, or one after another? Now tell me what you guessed, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Hide the red line. Do the species seem to end together? Say what you saw and why.
  - Ask: All five ended on the same day. Why does the cliff make it look slow? (good: The rare ones were not found in the top layers.; No fossil found does not mean none was alive.)
  - Ask: How did searching three cliffs change the picture? (good: The highest finds moved up towards layer 20.; More searching makes the gap smaller.)
  - Note: The reason: a rare species leaves few fossils, so there are many layers where they lived and nothing was found. Their last fossil lies below their real end. Common species leave a fossil in nearly every layer, so their last fossil lies close to the real end. A sudden ending therefore looks like a slow one. Scientists call this the Signor-Lipps effect. The cure is more searching. The same thinking explains how a fish could be missing from 66 million years of rock and still be alive.
  - Think: Is the first fossil of a species the first animal of that species? What would the die say?

### p1 · The Dark Week: who gets through a time without sunlight? (50 min, 8 days)

  *Child mode:* Look at all three plates. Who came through the dark best? What if the dark lasted months?
  - Ask: After the asteroid strike, the dark lasted for months. Which plants could come back? (good: The ones waiting as seeds or spores in the soil.; Not the ones that were already growing.)
  - Note: Most of the dry seeds should have sprouted, as if nothing had happened. The pale seedlings should be turning green. This is why the first plants after the strike grew from spores and seeds, and why animals who could eat seeds had food.
  - Check: Sunlight is blocked for many months. Which has the best chance of growing afterwards? → **A dry seed lying in the soil**

## Challenges
### c1 · The Thirty-Roll Search: plan a fossil hunt that gets closest to the truth (25 min)
- **problem:** Five species, A to E, all end at the top of layer 20. You may roll the die only 30 times in all. Plan your search so that the last fossil you find of each species is as close to layer 20 as possible.
- **your turn:** Now make your own plan for 30 rolls. Write it down before you roll. Run it and work out your score. Then explain to someone why your plan gave a smaller total gap than sharing the rolls equally, or why it did not.
- **constraints:** Each roll searches one layer for one species. You choose the species and the layer before you roll.; The finding rules are those of the Last Fossil Test. A is found on 1 to 5, B on 1 to 4, C on 1 to 3, D on 1 or 2, and E only on 1.; You may search the same layer again for the same species. Each new search costs one roll. It stands for digging more rock from that layer.; Your score is the total of the five gaps. A gap is 20 minus the highest layer where you found that species. A species never found scores a gap of 20. The lowest score is best.
- **general:** Share the rolls equally: 6 for each species, working down from layer 20, one roll for each layer.; Give the common species fewer rolls and the rare species more, still working down one layer at a time.; Stop searching for a species as soon as you find one fossil, and pass the spare rolls to the next species.
- **creative:** Search layer 20 again and again for the rare species, and move down only if you run short of rolls.; Search the rare species first, while you still have all your rolls, and leave the common ones for last.; Keep back five rolls for a last try at layer 20 for the species with the biggest gap.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the problem and the four rules aloud. 2. Work out one gap together as an example. A highest find in layer 14 gives a gap of 6. 3. Draw a cliff table with 20 rows and 5 columns.  
  *Parent says:* You have only 30 rolls for the whole cliff. The score is the total of the five gaps, and the lowest score wins. What is the gap if the highest find is in layer 14?  
  *Child mode:* Read the rules. You have 30 rolls. A gap is 20 minus your highest find. Draw the cliff.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Try the equal plan first. Give each species 6 rolls. 2. For each species, search layers 20, 19, 18, 17, 16 and 15, one roll each. Stop at the first find. 3. Write the five gaps and add them up.  
  *Parent says:* Let's try the plain plan first. Six rolls for each species, one layer at a time from the top. What is your total gap?  
  *Child mode:* Give each species 6 rolls, from layer 20 down. Stop at a find. Add up the five gaps.
  - Note: With this plan, species E is not found at all about one time in three, which costs a gap of 20. A total between 3 and 25 is usual.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to design a better plan and write it down. 2. Ask why they expect the plan to work. 3. Run the plan with exactly 30 rolls or fewer. 4. Write the five gaps and the total.  
  *Parent says:* Now design your own plan. Where were the rolls wasted last time? Where were there too few? Write the plan, tell me why, and then roll.  
  *Child mode:* Make your own plan for 30 rolls. Write it down and say why. Then roll and score.
  - Ask: Which species needs the most rolls, and why? (good: E, because E is found only on a 1.; The rare ones, because each search is less likely to find them.)
  - Note: Let your child find the strong idea alone if possible. It is to search layer 20 many times for the rare species. Spare rolls come from the common species, who are usually found in one or two rolls.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two scores side by side. 2. Ask which plan came closer to the truth, and why. 3. Ask what a real fossil hunter could do that is like searching layer 20 again.  
  *Parent says:* Which plan gave the smaller total gap? Why? What could a real scientist do that is like rolling again for the top layer?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the two scores. Which plan was closer to the truth? Say why.
  - Note: One good plan can lose to luck in a single try. If there is time, run each plan twice. A real scientist searches the same layer in many cliffs and digs more rock from the layers just below the boundary.
  - Check: You want to know when a rare species really ended. Where should most of your searching go? → **The top layers, searched again and again**

## Worksheets
### Mass Extinctions and Recovery (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a mass extinction?  
   Options: One species ending in one forest / Most species on Earth ending within a short time / Many animals dying in one cold winter / A species moving to a new place  
   **Answer:** Most species on Earth ending within a short time. *Why:* A mass extinction ends most species, all over the world, in a short stretch of geological time. One hard winter kills many animals but ends no species.
2. (fill) The slow, steady ending of species in normal times is called ____ extinction.  
   **Answer:** ['background']. *Why:* A few species end in every million years, even when nothing unusual happens. That steady loss is the background.
3. (truefalse) True or false: most of the species that ever lived are still alive today.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Scientists estimate that more than 99 out of 100 species that ever lived are extinct. They did not all live at the same time.
4. (order) Put the five mass extinctions in order, oldest first.  
   **Answer:** ['End of the Ordovician', 'Late Devonian', 'End of the Permian', 'End of the Triassic', 'End of the Cretaceous']. *Why:* They happened about 444, 370, 252, 201 and 66 million years ago.
5. (match) Match each mass extinction to a likely cause.  
   **Answer:** End of the Ordovician → ice sheets and a falling sea; End of the Permian → huge eruptions in Siberia; End of the Triassic → lava where the Atlantic opened; End of the Cretaceous → an asteroid strike, with eruptions in India. *Why:* Each one had a different cause. In each, the air, the sea or the climate changed too fast for most species.
6. (mcq) Which rare metal in a thin clay layer was the first clue to an asteroid strike?  
   Options: Iron / Gold / Copper / Iridium  
   **Answer:** Iridium. *Why:* Asteroids hold far more iridium than Earth's surface rocks. Iron is common in ordinary rock, so it would be no clue.
7. (sort) Sort these by what happened to them 66 million years ago.  
   **Answer:** big dinosaurs → Ended; ammonites → Ended; crocodiles → Lived on; small mammals → Lived on; mosasaurs → Ended; turtles → Lived on; ground birds → Lived on; frogs → Lived on. *Why:* Large animals and those who depended on fresh plants or floating algae ended. Small animals with shelter, and those in rivers, mostly lived.
8. (write) On a full board of 36 squares, no group could add a new species. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** A new species needs an empty niche to live in. On the full board every square was taken, so there was no room, whatever the die showed.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the limit was room, not the die or the group.
9. (truefalse) True or false: after a mass extinction, the lost species come back.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A lost species never returns. New and different species arise from the survivors.
10. (mcq) Which land animals mostly survived the asteroid strike?  
   Options: The largest plant-eaters / Small ones with shelter who ate insects or seeds / The largest meat-eaters / Animals who lived in treetops  
   **Answer:** Small ones with shelter who ate insects or seeds. *Why:* Small animals need little food, and insects and seeds were still there. Choosing the largest animals mixes up strength with what the dark months demanded.
11. (fill) A species thought to be extinct and then found alive is called a ____ species.  
   **Answer:** ['Lazarus']. *Why:* The coelacanth and Jerdon's courser are Lazarus species. Each was missed for a very long time.
12. (draw) Draw a cliff of rock layers with one boundary layer. Show different fossils below and above the boundary.  
   **Answer:** A cliff with several layers and one thin dark layer. The fossils below, such as ammonites, differ from the fossils above.. *Why:* A boundary layer marks a change of age. A strong drawing shows that some fossils stop at the layer and do not appear above.
13. (write) Five species end on the same day. Why is the last fossil of the rarest one found lower in the cliff?  
   **Answer:** A rare species leaves few fossils. In many layers they were alive and no fossil was found. So their last fossil lies below the layer where they really ended.. *Why:* A strong answer separates two things: when the species lived, and where a fossil happened to be found.
14. (odd) Three of these ended 66 million years ago. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Ammonite / Mosasaur / Triceratops / Nautilus  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The nautilus lived on in deep water and is alive today. The other three ended.
15. (perspective) Two children argue about extinct animals.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives evidence, such as how long the big dinosaurs or the trilobites lasted. It also says what the other child has right: some species do end because rivals do better.. *Why:* The second child is closer to what the rocks show for mass extinctions. In normal times, though, a species can lose out to others, so the first child is not wholly wrong.
### Who Survives, and How We Know (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why did shell-building sea animals suffer most at the end of the Permian?  
   Options: The sea froze solid / Dissolved carbon dioxide made the sea more acid / Fishing boats caught them / The sea dried up  
   **Answer:** Dissolved carbon dioxide made the sea more acid. *Why:* Carbon dioxide from the eruptions dissolved in the sea and made a weak acid. Shells are harder to build in more acid water. There were no people or boats then.
2. (write) Great size helped dinosaurs for millions of years. Explain why it became a danger after the asteroid strike.  
   **Answer:** A large body needs a lot of food every day. With sunlight blocked, plants died and food ran out. Large animals starved first. Small animals needed little and could shelter.. *Why:* A strong answer links size to food, and says that the same feature can help in one world and harm in another.
3. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Niche → a way of making a living in a place; Detritus → dead plant and animal matter; Recovery → the growth of species numbers after an extinction; Boundary layer → a thin rock layer between two ages. *Why:* These four words are needed to describe what happens before and after a mass extinction.
4. (truefalse) True or false: mammals first appeared after the big dinosaurs died out.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Mammals lived beside the dinosaurs for well over 100 million years. They were mostly small until the big dinosaurs ended.
5. (order) Put these in order, starting from the asteroid strike.  
   **Answer:** ['Dust and soot block the Sun', 'Ferns cover the bare ground', 'Forests grow back', 'Some mammals reach 50 kilograms']. *Why:* Darkness came first. Ferns grew from spores, then forests returned. Large mammals appeared hundreds of thousands of years later.
6. (mcq) In a refill game, a group holding 2 squares may add at most 2 more. What does this rule stand for?  
   Options: Small groups are lazy / The die is unfair / New species arise only from living species / Species grow bigger each round  
   **Answer:** New species arise only from living species. *Why:* Each new species splits off from one that already exists. So a group with few species can add only a few at a time.
7. (fill) The splitting of one group into many new species that fill empty niches is an adaptive ____.  
   **Answer:** ['radiation']. *Why:* After the big dinosaurs ended, mammals went through an adaptive radiation into bats, whales, horses and many more.
8. (sort) Sort each piece of evidence by the cause it points to.  
   **Answer:** a clay layer rich in iridium → Asteroid; quartz grains with shock cracks → Asteroid; a buried crater in Mexico → Asteroid; lava covering much of western India → Volcanoes; tiny beads of melted rock → Asteroid; gases that warm the whole Earth → Volcanoes. *Why:* A shock, a crater and melted beads come from a strike. Lava and heat-trapping gases come from eruptions.
9. (write) The last fossil of a rare animal lies five metres below a boundary layer. Give two possible explanations.  
   **Answer:** One: the animal ended earlier than the others. Two: the animal lived right up to the boundary, and no fossil from the top five metres has been found yet.. *Why:* Both are possible. More searching in more places is the way to tell them apart.
10. (label) Draw a time line from 500 million years ago to now. Label the five mass extinctions on it.  
   **Answer:** ['End of the Ordovician, about 444 million years ago', 'Late Devonian, about 370 million years ago', 'End of the Permian, about 252 million years ago', 'End of the Triassic, about 201 million years ago', 'End of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago']. *Why:* The gaps between them are uneven. Mass extinctions do not come on a timetable.
11. (mcq) About how long did the number of species take to recover after past extinctions?  
   Options: About 100 years / About 10,000 years / It never recovered / About 10 million years  
   **Answer:** About 10 million years. *Why:* A study of the whole record of sea life found about 10 million years. That is more than 30 times as long as our species has existed.
12. (truefalse) True or false: a species that nobody has seen for 50 years must be extinct.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Jerdon's courser was missed for 86 years and found alive. Small, rare animals who are active at night are easy to miss.
13. (perspective) Two scientists argue about what ended the big dinosaurs.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and names evidence. For the asteroid: the crater, the iridium layer and shocked quartz, all of the right age. For the volcanoes: the huge Deccan lava flows and their gases. It may also say that both played a part.. *Why:* Most scientists think the asteroid was the main cause. The lava is hard to date exactly, so the share of each is still argued.
14. (odd) Three of these have been linked to mass extinctions. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Growing ice sheets / Huge lava floods / An asteroid strike / A tiger hunting deer  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Ice, lava floods and an asteroid changed the whole Earth's air, sea or climate. A predator hunting prey is part of normal life and ends no species by itself.
15. (mcq) Why were rivers and lakes hit less hard than the open sea after the strike?  
   Options: Rivers have no animals / The dust did not reach rivers / River water is always warm / Dead matter washed in from the land fed them  
   **Answer:** Dead matter washed in from the land fed them. *Why:* River food chains can run on dead leaves and other detritus from the land. Food chains of the open sea begin with floating algae, who need light.
### Extinction Today and Big Questions (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Normally 2 species in 10,000 end every 100 years. How many of 40,000 species should end in 100 years?  
   **Answer:** 8 species. 40,000 is 4 lots of 10,000, and 4 × 2 = 8.. *Why:* This is the expected number for normal times. Real losses are compared with it.
2. (mcq) Since 1900, 477 species were lost where 9 were expected. About how many times the normal number is that?  
   Options: About 5 times / About 470 times / About 50 times / About 9 times  
   **Answer:** About 50 times. *Why:* 477 ÷ 9 = 53, which is about 50 times. Subtracting 9 from 477 gives the extra losses, not how many times.
3. (perspective) Two scientists argue about what to call today's extinctions.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses numbers. For 'already begun': losses are about 50 times normal, and over one in four checked species are at risk. For 'not yet': fewer than 1 in 100 named species are recorded lost, and a mass extinction means 3 in 4.. *Why:* The two agree on the facts and on the danger. They differ on the word. Seeing that is part of a strong answer.
4. (write) Someone says: 'Extinction is natural, so losing species today does not matter.' Answer with two facts.  
   **Answer:** Recovery after past extinctions took about 10 million years, far longer than people have existed. A lost species never comes back. Also, today's losses are many times the natural rate, and their cause is people, who can choose to stop.. *Why:* A strong answer accepts what is true in the claim, that extinction is natural, and then shows what the claim leaves out.
5. (truefalse) True or false: the five great mass extinctions are the only times many species died out together.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The record shows many smaller extinction events too. The five are the largest of them.
6. (mcq) What is the Signor-Lipps effect?  
   Options: Rare fossils stop below a species' real end / Fossils sink through rock over time / Large animals leave no fossils / Volcanoes destroy all fossils  
   **Answer:** Rare fossils stop below a species' real end. *Why:* The last fossil found is older than the last animal, and more so for rare species. So a sudden ending can look slow. Fossils do not move down through solid rock.
7. (match) Match each finding to what it most likely means.  
   **Answer:** No trilobites above one layer, anywhere → the whole group ended there; Fern spores crowd the rock above a layer → bare ground was being covered again; Mammal fossils get bigger above a layer → survivors were filling empty niches; Quartz grains with shock cracks → a huge sudden blow. *Why:* Each finding is evidence. A scientist asks what would have to be true for the rocks to look this way.
8. (order) Three species end together. Order them by how close their last fossil is likely to be to the true end.  
   **Answer:** ['Found on a roll of 1 to 5', 'Found on a roll of 1 to 3', 'Found only on a roll of 1']. *Why:* The more often a species is found, the smaller the gap between the last fossil and the true end.
9. (draw) Draw a branching tree of life. Show a mass extinction cutting most branches, and what grows afterwards.  
   **Answer:** A tree with many branches. A line across the tree ends most of them. A few branches pass the line and split into many new twigs. The cut branches do not regrow.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows three things: most branches end, a few continue, and the new twigs come only from those few.
10. (mcq) Suppose the asteroid had missed the Earth. Why might humans never have appeared?  
   Options: Mammals did not exist yet / Big dinosaurs would have kept the niches of large animals / The Earth would be too hot / Humans come from dinosaurs  
   **Answer:** Big dinosaurs would have kept the niches of large animals. *Why:* Mammals already existed, but stayed small while big dinosaurs held the niches for large land animals. Large mammals, including apes, arose only after those niches emptied.
11. (fill) Unrelated animals arriving at the same answer to the same problem is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['convergence']. *Why:* Dolphins and ichthyosaurs are unrelated, and both have the body shape of a fast fish. That is convergence.
12. (write) Wings arose separately in insects, birds and bats. What does that suggest about replaying the history of life?  
   **Answer:** It suggests that some answers, like wings, are likely to appear again, because flying is a good way to live. It does not show that the same animals would appear. Birds and bats are very different flyers.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the evidence for convergence and also sees its limit. Similar answers can come from very different beings.
13. (sort) Sort these statements: agreed by scientists, or still argued?  
   **Answer:** An asteroid struck 66 million years ago → Agreed; Which did more killing, asteroid or eruptions → Still argued; Species are ending faster than normal today → Agreed; Whether today is a mass extinction yet → Still argued; Lost species do not return → Agreed; Why the Late Devonian extinction came in steps → Still argued. *Why:* The events themselves are agreed. The shares of blame, the right name for today, and the cause of the Devonian extinction are still argued.
14. (truefalse) True or false: the first fossil found of a species shows exactly when that species began.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The first animals of a species lived before the first one that was buried, kept and found. The gap works at the start just as at the end.
15. (odd) Three of these help a living species stay unseen for decades. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Being active only at night / Living in the deep sea / Being very rare / Being large and living near towns  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A large animal near towns is seen often. Night habits, deep water and small numbers all make a species easy to miss.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which sentence describes background extinction?  
   Options: A few species end in every million years / Most species end in a short time / Species that ended come back / No species ever ends  
   **Answer:** A few species end in every million years. *Why:* Background extinction is the slow, steady loss of normal times. Most species ending in a short time is a mass extinction.
2. (mcq) Which of these would count as a mass extinction?  
   Options: A lake dries and one fish species ends / A flood kills many animals in one state / Three quarters of all species end within a short time / A forest loses half of the trees  
   **Answer:** Three quarters of all species end within a short time. *Why:* A mass extinction ends most species across the world. A flood or a lost forest kills many living beings, but that is different from ending most species.
3. (mcq) Which mass extinction was the largest?  
   Options: End of the Permian / End of the Cretaceous / End of the Triassic / End of the Ordovician  
   **Answer:** End of the Permian. *Why:* About 8 in 10 sea species ended about 252 million years ago. The end of the Cretaceous is the best known, because of the dinosaurs, but was smaller.
4. (mcq) Which mass extinction has clear signs of an asteroid strike?  
   Options: End of the Permian / Late Devonian / End of the Ordovician / End of the Cretaceous  
   **Answer:** End of the Cretaceous. *Why:* The crater and the iridium layer are both 66 million years old. The end of the Permian is linked to eruptions in Siberia.
5. (mcq) After a mass extinction, where do the new species come from?  
   Options: From the species that ended / From other planets / From the species that survived / From rocks and soil  
   **Answer:** From the species that survived. *Why:* Every new species splits off from a living one. That is why the group you wiped out never returned to your board.
6. (mcq) Why do new species arise faster after a mass extinction than before?  
   Options: The survivors try harder / The Sun is brighter / Fossils form faster / Many niches are empty  
   **Answer:** Many niches are empty. *Why:* With few rivals, more varieties can live and have young, and natural selection fits them to the empty niches. Trying harder does not change what is inherited.
7. (mcq) Which animal was most likely to survive the asteroid strike?  
   Options: A giant plant-eater / A small burrowing insect-eater / A giant meat-eater / A treetop bird  
   **Answer:** A small burrowing insect-eater. *Why:* A small animal needs little food, insects lived on, and a burrow gave shelter. Giants starved, and the forests that treetop birds needed were lost.
8. (mcq) In the dark months, why did food chains that begin with dead leaves keep going?  
   Options: Dead leaves make their own food / Dead matter was there without sunlight / Insects stopped eating / Dead leaves grew back  
   **Answer:** Dead matter was there without sunlight. *Why:* Fallen leaves and wood were already lying there, so the animals who eat them still had food. Chains that begin with living plants stopped when the plants could not make food.
9. (mcq) A rare species' last fossil lies eight layers below a boundary. What is the fairest conclusion?  
   Options: They surely ended eight layers early / They never existed / They may have lived up to the boundary / They are alive today  
   **Answer:** They may have lived up to the boundary. *Why:* Rare species leave gaps, so the last fossil is usually below the real end. Choosing 'surely ended early' reads the gap as the truth.
10. (mcq) Which finding points to an asteroid and not to volcanoes?  
   Options: Quartz grains with shock cracks / Thick layers of lava / Fern spores above the layer / Warming of the climate  
   **Answer:** Quartz grains with shock cracks. *Why:* Only a huge sudden shock cracks quartz in that way. Lava points to volcanoes, and fern spores and warming fit both causes.
11. (mcq) What do scientists agree about extinction today?  
   Options: Three quarters of species are already gone / No species has ended since 1900 / Species are ending faster than normal, because of people / The rate is the same as always  
   **Answer:** Species are ending faster than normal, because of people. *Why:* The rate is many times the normal one, and the causes are things people do. But most species are still alive, so three quarters gone is wrong.
12. (mcq) Fewer than 1 in 100 named species are recorded as lost. Why are scientists still worried?  
   Options: The rate of loss is far above normal / They enjoy worrying / Fossils are running out / An asteroid is coming  
   **Answer:** The rate of loss is far above normal. *Why:* If such a high rate went on for a few thousand years, the losses would reach the level of the five great extinctions. No large asteroid is known to be on its way.

## Applications
- People who protect wildlife must decide when to stop searching for a missing species. They use the idea from the Last Fossil Test: a rare species can go unseen for a long time and still be alive.
- Geologists who search for oil and gas use tiny fossils and boundary layers to tell the age of rock deep under the ground. The Chicxulub crater was first noticed by two men searching for oil.
- Space agencies track large rocks that pass near the Earth. In 2022 the DART spacecraft struck a small asteroid and changed its path, as a first test of pushing one aside.
- Scientists who study the sea measure how acid the water is, year after year. They compare today's change with the change at the end of the Permian.
- Seed banks keep dry seeds for many years. A dry seed can wait through a bad time, as seeds did after the asteroid strike.
- Climate scientists test their computer models on the great warmings of the past. If a model can reproduce what the rocks record, people can trust its forecasts more.
- The Red List tells governments which species need help first. India used such counts to plan the protection of tigers, lions and rhinoceroses.

## Spotted around us
- The crow or pigeon on your window grill is a living dinosaur. Their ancestors came through the asteroid strike.
- Hills that look like giant steps, seen from a train window. Each step can be one flow of old lava.
- Old forts built of dark basalt, the lava rock from the time of the last dinosaurs.
- A shaligram stone kept in some homes is often an ammonite fossil from the Gandaki river in Nepal. Ammonites ended 66 million years ago.
- A rock with shells of sea animals in it, from the time of the dinosaurs. A sea covered that land then.
- A tree trunk turned to stone, in a museum or a fossil park.
- After the monsoon, ferns are often the first plants on a damp wall or a cleared bank. Ferns were also first after the asteroid strike.
- A glass of soda water tastes a little sour. Carbon dioxide dissolved in the water has made a weak acid, as it does in the sea.
- News about cheetahs brought from Africa, and how they are doing in a park.

## Open questions
- **How much of the killing 66 million years ago was done by the asteroid, and how much by the Deccan eruptions?** The two happened within a few hundred thousand years of each other. The lava is hard to date exactly, and two methods give different orders for the biggest flows.
- **Why did the Late Devonian extinction come in several steps?** There are several ideas, such as the spread of the first forests, eruptions and changes in the sea. The rocks of that age are old and worn, so the evidence is thin.
- **How many species are alive on Earth today?** About two million have been named. Estimates of the real number run from a few million to many millions. Without that number, the count of species being lost is also uncertain.
- **If the history of life ran again, would beings like us appear?** Nobody can run the test. Some scientists point to chance events such as the asteroid, and others point to the many times the same body forms have arisen.
- **Why do some groups survive a mass extinction and then fade away over the next few million years?** It may be that too few species of the group were left, or that the new world no longer suited them. The fossil record is often too thin to tell.
- **Which species listed as extinct are still alive somewhere?** Nobody can search everywhere. Small, rare animals of the night, the deep sea and thick forest are missed for decades.

## Connected maths
- On your paper time line, 1 centimetre stands for 10 million years. The end-Ordovician extinction was 444 million years ago. How far from 'now' do you draw the line? Which is the bigger gap on the strip: Ordovician to Devonian, or Triassic to Cretaceous? **44.4 centimetres from 'now'. The gap from the Triassic to the Cretaceous is bigger.**. 444 ÷ 10 = 44.4 centimetres. Ordovician to Devonian is 444 − 370 = 74 million years, which is 7.4 centimetres. Triassic to Cretaceous is 201 − 66 = 135 million years, which is 13.5 centimetres. So the second gap is nearly twice as long. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- Before the extinction, mammals held 4 of the 36 squares on the board. When the board was full again, they held 12. What fraction of the board did they hold each time? The mammals had the same die before and after. So what made the difference? **One ninth before, and one third after. The difference was empty squares next to them.**. 4 out of 36 is 4 ÷ 36 = one ninth. 12 out of 36 is 12 ÷ 36 = one third. One third is three times one ninth. The die and the mammals did not change. The extinction emptied the squares around them, and that gave them room. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- In the Last Fossil Test, the rarest species is found only on a roll of 1. What is the chance of a find in any one layer? About how many layers must you search, on average, for one find? A species found on a roll of 1, 2 or 3 would need about how many? **The chance is 1 in 6. You need about 6 layers on average for the rarest species, and about 2 layers for the other.**. A die has 6 faces and only one of them is a 1, so the chance is 1 in 6. On average one find comes in every 6 tries. Rolls of 1, 2 or 3 are 3 faces out of 6, which is 1 in 2, so about 2 tries. That is why the last fossil of the rarest species usually lies about 5 layers below the top, and the other lies about 1 layer below. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- At the normal rate, about 9 species of animals with backbones should have ended since 1900. On a cautious count, 477 have. How many times the normal number is that? What fraction of the 40,000 species checked is 477, roughly? **53 times. About 1 in 84, which is a little more than 1 in 100.**. 477 ÷ 9 = 53. For the fraction, 40,000 ÷ 477 is about 84, so 477 is about 1 part in 84. The two answers go together. Species are ending about 50 times faster than normal, and yet about 83 out of every 84 are still here. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- After past extinctions, the number of species took about 10 million years to recover. Our species is about 300,000 years old. How many times longer is the recovery than the whole time people have existed? **About 33 times longer.**. 10,000,000 ÷ 300,000 is the same as 100 ÷ 3, which is about 33. So people would have to exist 33 times longer than they have so far, to see one full recovery. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- The number of filled squares after each round was 9, 17, 27, 33 and 36. Work out how many squares were added in each round. In which round was the refill fastest? Why did it slow down at the end? **8, 10, 6 and 3 squares were added. Round 2 was fastest. It slowed because few empty squares were left.**. 17 − 9 = 8, 27 − 17 = 10, 33 − 27 = 6, and 36 − 33 = 3. At first there are few species to split, so growth is moderate. Then more species means more new ones each round. At the end almost no empty squares are left, and some cannot be reached. A line chart of these numbers bends into the shape of a stretched letter S. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is a mass extinction? → A time when most species on Earth end within a short stretch of geological time.
- How many great mass extinctions do scientists count? → Five.
- Which was the largest, and what caused it? → The end of the Permian, about 252 million years ago. Huge eruptions in Siberia warmed the Earth and made the sea acid.
- Name two clues that an asteroid struck 66 million years ago. → A clay layer rich in iridium, and a buried crater in Mexico. Quartz with shock cracks is a third.
- Do lost species come back after a mass extinction? → No. New species arise from the survivors.
- Why is the last fossil of a species not the last animal? → Few animals become fossils and few fossils are found, so the species lived on after the last one we have.
- What is a Lazarus species? Name one. → A species thought extinct and found alive, such as the coelacanth or Jerdon's courser.
- About how long does the number of species take to recover after an extinction? → About 10 million years.
