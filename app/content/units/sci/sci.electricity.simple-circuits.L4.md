# Bulbs in a row and bulbs side by side: how the layout of a circuit changes what each part gets
`sci.electricity.simple-circuits.L4`

**Summary.** Add a second bulb to a working circuit, and the first bulb gets dimmer. Add it another way, and nothing changes at all. The bulbs and the cells are the same, so only the wiring can explain it. A circuit with one path is called a series circuit. A circuit with several paths is called a parallel circuit, and every home is wired that way. In this unit you test both with real bulbs, find out why a thin filament glows while a thick wire stays cool, and learn why the arrow in a circuit diagram points the wrong way. You also learn why a fuse is a thin wire that is meant to break.

**Intro.** *Try this with a grown-up. Light one torch bulb with two AA cells. Now put a second bulb into the same loop, one after the other. Both bulbs still glow, but each one is much dimmer than the single bulb was. The cells did not change. Where did the brightness go?* The wires decide how the push of the cells is shared. In this unit you build circuits with bulbs in a row and bulbs side by side, and you score how bright each one is. You unscrew one bulb to see which circuit survives. You find out why a bulb's thin filament glows and why a fuse is meant to melt. You also test an LED, which works only one way round, and you meet the guess that made every circuit diagram point the wrong way.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can build a series circuit and a parallel circuit with two or three bulbs, and say how bright the bulbs are in each.
1. I can predict what the other bulbs do when one bulb is taken out, in a series circuit and in a parallel circuit.
2. I can explain, using the idea of sharing the cell's push, why bulbs in a row are dimmer and bulbs side by side are not.
3. I can explain why a thin filament glows while the thick wire stays cool, using the word resistance.
4. I can draw a circuit diagram with the symbols for a cell, a bulb, a switch and a wire, and build a circuit from someone else's diagram.
5. I can say why the arrow in a circuit diagram is a guess from 1747, that electrons really move the other way and only slowly, and show that an LED lights one way round only.
6. I can explain what a short circuit is and how a fuse protects a home.
7. I can match switches in a row to 'both needed' and switches side by side to 'either one is enough', and design a circuit for each.

## Terms
- **Current**: How much electric charge flows past a point in the circuit each second. A bigger current means a bigger flow. *e.g.* When you add a second bulb in a row, the current in the loop gets smaller, so both bulbs glow dimly.
- **Voltage**: The size of the push that a cell gives to the charges in a circuit. It is measured in volts. *e.g.* One AA cell pushes with 1.5 volts. Two in a row push with 3 volts. A wall socket in India pushes with 230 volts.
- **Series circuit**: A circuit where the parts are joined one after the other, so there is only one path. The same current goes through every part. *e.g.* Old festival light strings are often wired in series. When one bulb breaks, the whole string goes dark.
- **Parallel circuit**: A circuit where the parts are joined side by side, so the electricity has more than one path. Each part gets the full push of the cell. *e.g.* A fan and a tube light in one room are wired in parallel. You can switch off the light and the fan keeps turning.
- **Branch**: One of the separate paths in a parallel circuit. *e.g.* In a circuit with two bulbs side by side, each bulb is on its own branch.
- **Filament**: The very thin coiled wire inside a bulb. It gets so hot that it glows. *e.g.* In a torch bulb you can see the filament as a tiny bright coil between two supports.
- **Resistance**: How much a part holds back the flow of electricity. A thin wire, a long wire or a pencil lead holds back more than a short thick copper wire. *e.g.* A bulb's filament has a lot of resistance. That is why it heats up and glows, while the copper wires to it stay cool.
- **Circuit diagram**: A drawing of a circuit that uses standard symbols and straight lines for wires, so that anyone in the world can read it. *e.g.* An electrician reads a circuit diagram to see which parts are in a row and which are side by side.
- **Symbol**: A small standard drawing that stands for a part. A circle with a cross inside is a bulb. A long line next to a short fat line is a cell. *e.g.* In every circuit diagram, a gap with a lever is the symbol for a switch.
- **Short circuit**: A path with almost no resistance that lets a very large current flow, because the wire joins the two ends of a cell, or two mains wires, directly. *e.g.* A loose wire that touches both ends of a cell makes a short circuit. The wire and the cell get hot very fast.
- **Fuse**: A thin piece of wire placed in a circuit on purpose. If too much current flows, the fuse heats up and melts first, and that breaks the circuit. *e.g.* In the fuse of a plug, a thin wire melts when a fault makes the current too big, and the appliance stops.
- **LED**: A light-emitting diode. It is a small light that makes light without a glowing filament, and it works only when the current goes through it one way. *e.g.* The small red light on a phone charger or a TV is an LED.
- **Electron**: A tiny particle with a negative charge, found in every atom. The moving charges in a metal wire are electrons. *e.g.* In a copper wire, electrons drift slowly along the wire when a cell is joined, less than a millimetre in a second.
- **Conventional current**: The direction that circuit diagrams use for current, from the plus end of the cell, round the circuit, to the minus end. It is a rule agreed long ago, and the electrons really move the opposite way. *e.g.* On a diagram the arrow leaves the plus end of the cell. The electrons in the wire move towards that plus end.

## Explanations
### Series circuit: one path, one push shared out · *defined*
**Definition:** In a series circuit the parts are joined one after the other. The same current goes through every part, and the cell's push is shared between them.

With one bulb on two cells, the whole push of 3 volts goes to that bulb. Add a second bulb in the same loop, and the two bulbs must share the 3 volts, about 1.5 volts each. Two bulbs also make the path harder to get through, so less current flows round the loop. Less current and less push on each bulb means both glow dimly. The current is the same size in every part of the loop, so the first bulb does not use up the electricity before the second. If any part is taken out, the loop is broken everywhere, and every part stops.
- e.g. Two bulbs in a row glow dimly, and both go out if one is unscrewed.
- e.g. Three AA cells in a torch are in series, so their pushes add up to 4.5 volts.
- e.g. A switch in a torch is in series with the bulb, so opening the switch breaks the loop.
- **An electrician:** Series means one path. If one part fails open, everything in the path stops, so electricians use series only where that is wanted, such as a switch with the light it controls.
### Parallel circuit: more paths, a full push for each · *defined*
**Definition:** In a parallel circuit the parts are joined side by side. Each part has its own path to the cell and gets the cell's full push.

Each bulb side by side has its own two wires to the cell, so each bulb gets the whole 3 volts and glows as brightly as a bulb alone. The current from the cell splits at one end and joins again at the other. The cell gives out the sum of the currents in all the branches. So two bulbs side by side draw twice the current from the cells, and three bulbs draw three times, and the cells run down faster. If one bulb is taken out, the other branches still make complete loops, so they keep glowing.
- e.g. Two bulbs side by side are both as bright as one bulb alone.
- e.g. All the lights and fans in a home are in parallel, so each gets the full 230 volts and each can be switched off alone.
- e.g. As an example, if a cell lasts about 6 hours with one bulb, it lasts roughly 3 hours with two bulbs side by side. Real times vary with the cell and the bulb.
- **An engineer:** Parallel gives every part the full push, and a fault in one branch does not stop the others. The price is that the cell or supply must give out more current in total.
### Why a thin filament glows and a thick wire stays cool · *defined*
**Definition:** Resistance is how much a part holds back the flow of electricity. Where resistance is high, the flowing charges give up more energy as heat.

The wires in your circuit are thick copper, which holds back the flow very little, so almost no energy is given up in them. The filament is very thin and made of tungsten. It holds the flow back a lot, so the charges give up much of their energy there as heat. The filament of a bulb on the mains gets hot, at about 2,500 degrees Celsius, which is hotter than lava. A hot enough thing glows, as a heated iron rod glows red. A torch bulb filament is not so hot but works the same way. The same current goes through the wire and the filament, but the filament is where the energy goes.
- e.g. The wires to a bulb stay cool while the filament glows.
- e.g. A long thin pencil lead in a loop makes the bulb dimmer, because the lead holds the flow back.
- e.g. A fuse is a thin wire on purpose: it holds back the flow so much that it melts first.
- **A bulb maker:** The bulb is filled with a gas that does not burn, or has air taken out, so the very hot filament does not burn up. Most of the energy still leaves as heat, and only a small part as light.
### Resistance and dimmers · *defined*
**Definition:** A longer or thinner conductor has more resistance than a shorter or thicker one made of the same material.

Imagine water flowing through a pipe. A long, narrow pipe lets less water through each second than a short, wide one, if the push is the same. Wires work in a similar way. If you add a long pencil lead into a loop with a bulb, the bulb glows less. If you slide the clip along the lead, you can change how much lead is in the loop, and so how bright the bulb is. A fan regulator or a light dimmer in a home does the same job, in a cleverer way.
- e.g. Sliding a clip along a pencil lead changes the bulb's brightness.
- e.g. A long thin extension wire holds back the flow more than a short thick one.
- e.g. A fan regulator changes how much current reaches the fan.
- **A physicist:** Water in a pipe is only a picture. It helps with the idea of flow, but a real wire works through the pushes between charged particles, and the picture breaks down in other cases.
### Circuit diagrams · *defined*
**Definition:** A circuit diagram is a drawing that uses standard symbols for the parts and straight lines for the wires.

A circuit is hard to read from a photograph, because the wires bend and cross. So engineers all over the world draw circuits with the same symbols. A cell is a long thin line next to a short thick line. The long line is the plus end. A bulb is a circle with a cross in it. A switch is a gap with a lever. A wire is a straight line. With a diagram you can see at once whether bulbs are in a row or side by side, and you can build a circuit that someone far away has drawn.
- e.g. Two bulbs in a row are drawn one after another on a single loop.
- e.g. Two bulbs side by side are drawn on two branches between the same two points.
- e.g. A switch is drawn as a gap with a lever, open or closed.
### Which way does electricity flow? · *defined*
**Definition:** In a circuit diagram the arrow for current goes from the plus end of the cell round to the minus end. This is called conventional current. In a metal wire the moving charges are electrons, and they move the opposite way.

In 1747 Benjamin Franklin had to guess which way electricity moved, and he guessed it moved from plus to minus. Everyone drew arrows that way. In 1897 J. J. Thomson found the electron, which has a negative charge. Later work showed that the moving charges in a metal wire are electrons. They move from the minus end of the cell to the plus end, the opposite way to the arrows. Engineers kept Franklin's arrow, because millions of diagrams and rules already used it. The answers come out right whichever way you picture the flow, as long as you stick to one.
- e.g. The arrow on a circuit diagram leaves the plus end of the cell.
- e.g. The electrons in the wire move towards the plus end.
- e.g. An LED works only when it is joined so that the current goes into its long leg.
- **A teacher:** Learn the diagram rule first, because that is what you will see in books and exams. Then remember that the electrons really go the other way.
### Electrons crawl, but the bulb lights at once · *defined*
**Definition:** The electrons in a wire drift very slowly, less than a millimetre each second. The push that sets them moving travels along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light.

When you close a switch, the bulb lights at once. This is not because an electron raced from the cell to the bulb. The wire is already full of electrons. When the cell pushes the electrons at its end, the push passes along the wire from one to the next almost instantly, like a row of marbles in a tube. Every electron in the loop starts to drift together, including the ones in the filament. Their drifting is slow, but the bulb does not need to wait for a particular electron to arrive.
- e.g. A long extension wire does not delay a bulb at its end.
- e.g. A row of marbles in a tube moves at once at both ends when you push one end.
- e.g. A call from Chennai to Delhi along a wire arrives in a fraction of a second.
- **A physicist:** A better picture is that the cell sets up an electric field along the whole wire, and the field pushes all the electrons at once.
### Short circuits and fuses · *defined*
**Definition:** A short circuit is a path with almost no resistance that lets a very large current flow. A fuse is a thin wire in a circuit that melts if the current gets too big, and so breaks the circuit.

If a bare wire joins the two ends of a cell, there is nothing to hold the flow back except the wire. The current is huge, and the wire, and the cell, heat up fast. At home the same can happen if two mains wires touch, and the heat can start a fire. So a fuse or a trip switch, called a miniature circuit breaker or MCB, is put in the circuit. It has a thin part that heats first. When the current is too big, the fuse melts or the MCB flips, and the loop is broken before the wires get dangerous. A fuse protects the wires. It does not protect a person who is touching a live wire.
- e.g. A fuse in the plug of an appliance melts when a fault makes the current too large.
- e.g. An MCB in the box near the main door trips and clicks off.
- e.g. A bulb with a wire across its two ends goes dark, because the current takes the easy path.
- **An electrician:** A fuse or an MCB is chosen to match the wire. A thin wire must have a small fuse. Putting in a bigger fuse, or a bit of wire in its place, is dangerous because the house wiring will then overheat first.
### Why homes are wired in parallel, and switches in series · *defined*
**Definition:** In a home, every light and appliance is on its own branch, in parallel, so each gets the full 230 volts. Each switch is in series with the light it controls.

If a home were wired in series, switching off one light would turn off every light. And each lamp would get only a small share of the 230 volts. So a home has a pair of wires from the supply, and each light, fan or socket is joined across that pair. Each is in its own branch. The switch for a light sits in the same branch as the light, one after the other. Opening the switch breaks only that branch.
- e.g. Switching off the kitchen light leaves the fan on.
- e.g. Two switches in a row, both closed, are needed to light a bulb.
- e.g. Two switches side by side, either closed, will light a bulb.
### Where does the energy of a circuit travel? · *idea*
The usual picture is that the electrons carry energy along the wire to the bulb. A second picture comes from John Henry Poynting, who worked it out in 1884. He showed that the energy of a circuit flows through the space around the wire, in the electric and magnetic fields, and then sinks into the wire and the bulb. Both pictures give the same results for any circuit you can build. Some physics teachers think the second picture is closer to what really happens. Others say it is not needed at this level. It is an idea to keep open, and not a thing to memorise.
- e.g. Energy reaches a bulb just as quickly through a long wire as through a short one.
- e.g. A power line carries energy over many kilometres with its electrons drifting very slowly.
- e.g. A wireless charger moves energy through the space between two coils.
- **The usual picture:** The energy travels inside the wire, carried along by the moving electrons.
- **Poynting's picture, 1884:** The energy travels in the fields around the wire, and the wire guides it to the bulb.

## Mostly agreed
- In a series circuit the same current goes through every part, and in a parallel circuit each branch gets the full voltage of the cell. *How we know:* Meters placed in different parts of circuits show the same current all round a series loop, and the same voltage across each branch of a parallel circuit.
- A bulb's filament glows because it holds back the current so much that it gets very hot. *How we know:* The colour of the light tells us the temperature. A filament in a mains bulb glows at about 2,500 degrees Celsius, and tungsten does not melt until 3,422 degrees.
- The moving charges in a metal wire are electrons, and they move from the minus end of the cell to the plus end. *How we know:* Thomson found the electron in 1897. Later experiments on metals showed that the charges carried in a wire are negative.
- A short circuit lets a very large current flow, and a fuse or MCB protects the wiring by breaking the circuit. *How we know:* Electrical safety standards require fuses and breakers matched to the wire, because overheated wires cause fires, and tests show they cut the current in time.
- Two switches in a row need both to be closed, and two switches side by side need only one. *How we know:* Anyone can test this with a cell, a bulb and two paper-clip switches. Claude Shannon showed in 1937 that this is how computer logic is built from switches.

## How we found out
**Benjamin Franklin in Philadelphia, and J. J. Thomson in Cambridge, 1747 (Franklin's guess) and 1897 (Thomson's electron).** In 1747, Benjamin Franklin in Philadelphia studied how rubbing made things pull on each other. He pictured one invisible fluid of electricity that flowed into some things and out of others. He called having too much 'plus' and too little 'minus'. He could not tell which way the fluid really moved, so he guessed that it moved from plus to minus. Everyone after him drew their arrows that way. In 1897, J. J. Thomson in Cambridge bent beams in glass tubes with magnets and charged plates. He showed the beams were tiny particles carrying the kind of charge Franklin had called minus. These are electrons. Later work showed that the moving charges in a metal wire are electrons too. So in a wire they move from minus to plus, opposite to the arrows. Engineers kept Franklin's arrow, because every diagram and rule already used it, and the sums come out right.  
*What people believed before:* Franklin guessed that the electric fluid moves from plus to minus, and everyone drew their circuits that way. Thomson's electron showed that the moving charges in a wire go from minus to plus.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The bulb uses up the electricity, so less comes out of the bulb than goes in.** (Many learners, and some old books.; *rejected*)  
  For: A cell does run out, and a bulb seems to 'use' electricity.  
  Against: Meters show the same current going into a bulb and coming out. What runs out of the cell is its stored energy, and the bulb turns that energy into heat and light.
- **In a row of bulbs, the first bulb is the brightest, because the electricity reaches it first.** (Many learners.; *rejected*)  
  For: It seems natural that the first bulb should get the most.  
  Against: The same current goes through every bulb in a row, so equal bulbs glow equally. Only the order along the wire is different.
- **The arrows in a circuit diagram show which way the electrons move.** (Many learners, and some books.; *rejected*)  
  For: The arrow goes round the circuit like a flow, and it is natural to think it shows what is moving.  
  Against: The arrow follows a rule that Benjamin Franklin guessed in 1747. In a metal wire the electrons move the opposite way.
- **Thomas Edison invented the electric light bulb.** (Many books and many people.; *debated*)  
  For: In 1879 and 1880 his team in the USA made a long-lasting lamp and built the wiring, meters and generators to sell light to whole streets.  
  Against: Joseph Swan in England showed a working lamp with a carbon filament in 1878 and 1879. Lewis Latimer, a Black draughtsman and engineer, patented a better way to make carbon filaments in 1882. Many people improved the bulb over many years.

## Roots
We have built circuits in rows and side by side, and seen the same bulb glow bright, dim or not at all. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The same bulb was bright alone, dim in a row, and dark with a gap. The bulb never changed. What it did was set by the loop it sat in. So a bulb has no brightness of its own.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'a bright bulb' and 'a dim bulb', as if brightness belonged to the bulb the way its size does. That label hides that the same bulb glows differently when its loop changes.  
*Evidence:* In Bulb Teams, one bulb scored 5 alone and about 2 with a second bulb in a row. Side by side, it scored 5 again. Unscrewing one bulb put out the others in a row but not the others side by side.

- *who am i:* Each nerve cell in your body fires or stays quiet depending on the signals from the nerve cells joined to it. Like a bulb, a nerve cell is set by its loop.
- *what is life:* A Venus flytrap shuts their trap only if a hair inside is touched twice within about 20 seconds. That is like two switches in a row, where both must be pressed.
- *what is this world:* A whole city, and India's national grid, are bulbs and machines side by side. Each gets a full push, and each can be switched off alone.

A cell is one push. The loop turns that one push into many different jobs: a glow in a filament, a hum in a fan, a ring in a bell. How the push is shared out depends on how the parts are joined. In a row, the parts share one push like one plate shared by a line of people. Side by side, every part draws from the same source at once. The world is joined in both ways. Some things depend on a single chain, and others on many paths at once.

- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18:** Whatever comes to be in dependence on other things has no self of its own. Nagarjuna called this emptiness, and meant that things exist through their conditions. (A bulb's glow comes to be through the cell, the wires and the other bulbs in its loop. Change the loop, and the glow changes. Nagarjuna said this is true of everything.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding, 1988, on interbeing:** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain, the tree and the logger inside the paper. Nothing exists by itself alone. (The light of a bulb contains the cell, the wire and the other bulbs of its loop, the way the paper contains the cloud.)
- Science (settled): In a series circuit the same current goes through every part and the cell's push is shared. In a parallel circuit each branch gets the full push. So a part's behaviour is set by how it is joined to the rest.
- Science (open): A nerve cell fires when the signals from the nerve cells joined to it add up enough. How these signals become a thought or a feeling is not known.
- Other view, Many physicists and engineers: A bulb does have a property of its own, its resistance. Everything else follows from that and from the cell, with one equation. Saying that brightness belongs to the whole loop only means you must know all the parts, and it is not a deeper truth.
- Practice, Follow the wire (3 min): Switch on a light and sit and look at it. Breathe in, and think of the wire behind the wall. Breathe out, and think of the wire in the street. Breathe in, and think of the pole and the power station. Breathe out, and ask: where does this loop begin, and where does it end? Stay with that question for three more breaths.
- Ask: Is a bulb's brightness a fact about the bulb, or a fact about the whole loop it sits in?
- Ask: Which things in your own life work like switches in a row, where everything is needed, and which like switches side by side, where any one is enough?
- Ask: If you join two circuits side by side, where does one end and the other begin?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The same bulb was bright alone, dim in a row and dark when there was a gap. Which of these is the real bulb? (hint: Think about what changed, and what stayed the same. Is brightness a fact about the bulb, or about the whole loop?)
- *deep:* In a row, the first bulb does not use up the electricity before it reaches the second. So what does each bulb take from the cell? (hint: The current is the same all round the loop. Think about what the cell gave to the charges at the start, and what they have less of after each bulb.)
- *deep:* The electrons in a wire move less than a millimetre in a second, yet a bulb lights the moment you close the switch. What is it that travels fast? (hint: Think about a long tube full of marbles. Push one in at one end.)
- *deep:* The same current goes through the thick wire and the thin filament. Why does only the filament glow? (hint: Where does the push of the cell get used up? Think of a crowd squeezing through a narrow gate.)
- *big:* India's power grid was joined into one national grid in 2013, and in 2012 a failure left about 600 million people without power. Is one big joined grid safer or riskier than many small ones? (hint: A joined grid can share power when one place fails. But a failure can also spread. List one good and one bad side.)
- *big:* Which things you depend on are like bulbs in a row, where one break stops everything, and which are like bulbs side by side? (hint: Think of a train with its carriages, a school day, a kitchen, and a village's water supply.)
- *big:* Franklin's guess about the direction of electricity turned out to be backwards, but engineers kept using it. When is it sensible to keep an old rule you know is a convention? (hint: Think about all the diagrams, books and machines that already use it. Think about which way the Sun 'rises' in the sentence we all use.)
- *big:* An electric eel stacks thousands of tiny cells in a row, and their small pushes add up to a very large one. What else in nature adds small pushes together? (hint: Think of a crowd clapping, an ant colony, ripples on a pond, or a million raindrops filling a river.)

## Story: Forty-Six Bulbs
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Old festival strings wired in one path do go dark when one bulb breaks. Many newer strings have a tiny extra wire in each bulb that closes the gap, so a string may not fail this way. Forty-six bulbs of about 5 volts each would use up the 230 volts of the mains. Only grown-ups ever touch a mains string, and the plug is pulled out first.*

On the evening of Karthigai Deepam, the lane in Madurai where Meenakshi lived looked like a different place. Every house had a row of clay lamps on the steps. The women had drawn kolams at every door. And across the lane, from the neem tree to the tea stall, Murugan mama had hung a long string of small coloured bulbs. There were forty-six of them. Meenakshi was nine, and she had counted them twice with him that afternoon.

At six o'clock the whole street gathered on the steps. Murugan mama pushed the plug into the socket by the pole, and the string came alive. It glowed red, yellow, green and blue. Everyone clapped. Meenakshi's grandmother, Paati, lit the biggest lamp on their step, and the flame leaned and settled.

Ten minutes later, there was a tiny pop, and the whole string went dark. Not one bulb. All forty-six, at the same moment. The clay lamps went on flickering, but the lane suddenly felt empty.

"Power cut!" someone shouted. But Paati pointed to the tube light over the tea stall, which was still shining, and to the TV in the house next door, which was still talking. "Not a power cut," she said. "Only our string."

Murugan mama checked the plug. He checked the box on the pole. He walked along the string, tugging each wire. "Maybe a rat chewed it," said one neighbour. "Maybe it is bad luck," said another. The big lamp ceremony was at seven o'clock, and Murugan mama was starting to sweat.

Meenakshi stood very still. She was remembering the science corner at school. Last week, Lakshmi miss had given her group a cell, two bulbs and some wires. They had put both bulbs into one loop. When Farhan had unscrewed one bulb, the other one had gone dark too. Farhan had said it was magic. Lakshmi miss had said, "No. There is one path, and you broke it."

"Mama," said Meenakshi, "is every bulb on this string joined one after the other, in one long path?"

"Of course," said Murugan mama. "One wire runs through all of them. That is how strings are made."

"Then it is like our school circuit. If one bulb is broken inside, the path is broken for every bulb. They all go out together." She looked at the string. "Mama, the string is not dead. Only one bulb is."

Murugan mama opened his mouth, and shut it again. Then he laughed. "But which one? There are forty-six!"

"First pull out the plug," said Paati, in the voice she used for important things. "Nobody touches a bulb on a string that is plugged in. This is the wall current. It is not a small cell." Murugan mama pulled the plug out by its plastic body, and set it on the step.

Meenakshi took her mother's phone and switched on the torch. "A good bulb has a tiny coil of thread inside, joined at both ends," she said. "A broken one has a gap in the thread." She and her friend Farhan took half of the string each. They held each bulb up to the torch light and looked in, one after another, down the lane. Murugan mama held the wire steady.

At the thirty-first bulb, Farhan stopped. "Here! The thread is broken. There is a gap in the middle."

Murugan mama had a spare bulb in his shirt pocket from last year. He twisted out the dead bulb and put in the new one. Paati checked that his hands were dry. Then he pushed in the plug.

All forty-six bulbs came on at once. The lane cheered so loudly that the crows flew out of the neem tree.

"Each bulb takes only a small share of the push, you know," said Murugan mama, when the noise had settled. "The wall pushes with two hundred and thirty volts, and that is divided between forty-six bulbs. That is why such tiny bulbs can live on it."

"Next year," said Meenakshi, "we could give every bulb its own pair of wires to the main wire. Then if one broke, only that one would go dark."

"More wire, more work," said Murugan mama.

"But the big lamp would start on time," said Paati. And she handed Meenakshi the first match.

**For the parent:** In a string wired in one path, one broken bulb breaks the path for every bulb. If each bulb has its own path, a broken bulb puts out only itself.

- Ask: Why does a whole string of bulbs wired in one path go dark when only one bulb is broken?
- Ask: How could you wire the same bulbs so that a broken bulb puts out only itself?
- Ask: Why must a mains string be unplugged before anyone touches the bulbs?

A good reason scores one more point.; The reader's job passes to the next player after each card.; Playing alone: say your answer and reason out loud, then check the back. Score your own points out of 48.; The game ends when all the cards have been read. The player with the most points wins. Playing alone, try to beat your last score.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: a circuit question on the front, and the answer with the reason on the back:** One cell and two equal bulbs in a row. How bright is each bulb, compared with one bulb alone? (back: Dimmer. The two bulbs share the cell's push, so each gets about half.); One cell and two equal bulbs side by side. How bright is each bulb, compared with one bulb alone? (back: The same as one bulb alone. Each bulb has its own path and gets the whole push.); Two bulbs are in a row. You unscrew one. What does the other bulb do? (back: It goes out. The only path is broken.); Two bulbs are side by side. You unscrew one. What does the other bulb do? (back: It stays lit. Its own path is still whole.); A cell, a bulb and a switch are in one loop. The switch is open. Is the bulb lit? (back: No. An open switch is a gap in the only path.); A cell and a bulb are in a loop. A thick wire is joined straight across the two sides of the bulb. Does the bulb light? (back: No. The current takes the easy path through the thick wire, and the bulb gets almost nothing. The wire and cell may get hot.); Three bulbs are in a row. The middle bulb has a broken thread. Do the other two light? (back: No. The break in the middle breaks the one path for all three.); Two cells are in a row, facing the same way, with one bulb. Is the bulb brighter or dimmer than with one cell? (back: Brighter. The pushes add up, 1.5 volts plus 1.5 volts makes 3 volts.); Two cells are in a row but facing opposite ways, with one bulb. What does the bulb do? (back: It stays dark. The pushes work against each other and cancel.); Two cells are joined side by side, with one bulb. Compared with one cell, how bright is the bulb and how long does the pair last? (back: The same brightness. The two cells last about twice as long, because they share the work.); A string of forty-six festival bulbs goes dark when one bulb breaks. How are the bulbs joined? (back: In a row, in one path. One break stops all of them.); A home has a fan and a tube light. The tube light is switched off. What does the fan do? (back: It keeps turning. They are in parallel, each on its own branch.); An LED is joined with its legs the wrong way round. Does it light? Is it broken? (back: It does not light, but it is not broken. Turn it round and it lights.); A bulb and two switches are all in one row. When does the bulb light? (back: Only when both switches are closed. Both are needed.); A bulb is joined with two switches side by side. When does the bulb light? (back: When either switch is closed, or both. One is enough.); The same current goes through a thick wire and a thin filament. Which one gets hotter? (back: The thin filament. It holds the flow back more, so more energy turns to heat there.); A wire joins the two ends of a cell directly, with nothing else in the loop. What happens? (back: A very large current flows. The wire and cell get hot fast. This is a short circuit, and it is dangerous.); A fuse in a house wire melts. Is that a good thing or a bad thing? (back: A good thing. It broke the circuit before the wires overheated.); Three bulbs are side by side. You take two of them out. What does the third do? (back: It glows just as bright as before. Its path is not changed.); A long pencil lead is added to a loop with a bulb. What happens to the bulb? (back: It gets dimmer. The lead holds back the flow.); On a circuit diagram, the arrow goes from the plus end of the cell round to the minus end. Which way do the electrons in the wire really go? (back: From minus to plus, the opposite way. The arrow is a rule Franklin guessed in 1747.); A torch has three AA cells in a row, all facing the same way. How many volts do they push with? (back: 4.5 volts. Each cell adds 1.5 volts.); An electric eel's body has thousands of tiny cells stacked in a row. Why does a row make a large push? (back: In a row, the small pushes add up, as the cells in a torch do.); When you close a switch, the bulb lights at once. Does an electron race from the cell to the bulb? (back: No. The wire is already full of electrons. They all start to drift together, very slowly.)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and shuffle them into a pile. Make a score sheet with each player's name. Show your child the front and the back of one card.  
  *Parent says:* Each card asks a question about a circuit. The back has the answer and the reason. The reader reads the front. Everyone else says an answer and a reason.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Shuffle them. Each card has a circuit question on the front.
  - Note: Cards are printed two-sided. If you cannot print two-sided, stick each answer on the back by hand.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first 8 cards. After each card the reader reads the back aloud. Score one point for the right answer and one more for a good reason. After card three, ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* The reader reads the front. Everyone says an answer and a reason. Then the reader reads the back. One point for the answer. One more for the reason.  
  *Child mode:* The reader reads a card. Say an answer and a reason. Check the back.
  - Ask: What do the cards where a bulb stays lit have in common? (good: Each bulb has its own path.; They are in parallel.; The break is in a different branch.)
  - Note: Let children argue their reasons before the back is read. A right answer with a weak reason earns one point. A good reason is worth the second.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the next 16 cards. Keep the scores. If a card is hard, draw the circuit on paper to work it out.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. If a card is hard, draw the circuit with the symbols. Is the bulb in a row, or side by side? Can the current find a whole loop?  
  *Child mode:* Keep playing. Draw the circuit if a card is hard.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the scores. Ask which card was the hardest, and why. Put the cards the child got wrong back in the pile and try them again.  
  *Parent says:* Add up the points. Which card was hardest? What would you draw to work it out? Let us try the hard ones again.  
  *Child mode:* Add up your points. Try the hard cards again.

## Experiments
### e1 · Bulb Teams: bulbs in a row or side by side? (40 min)
- **question:** What happens to the brightness of the bulbs when you add a second and a third bulb, in a row and side by side? What happens to the others when you take one out?
- **predict:** Before you build anything, guess. How bright will two bulbs in a row be, compared with one bulb alone? How bright will two bulbs side by side be? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same two fresh cells and the same kind of bulb every time. Test in the same dim room, after your eyes have rested for one minute. Only the layout changes. The single bulb is the control and always scores 5. Ask a second person to score the bulbs too, without seeing your scores. If you both give the same score, you can trust it.
- **safety:** Use only AA cells, never a wall socket or a plug. Disconnect the cells after each test. Bulbs can be warm after a minute, so touch only the holders. Do not use cells that look swollen or leaking.
- **record:** For each layout, write the score of each bulb from 0 to 5, and what the others did when you unscrewed one. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each layout.
- **results table:** Layout | My guess (0 to 5) | Brightness (0 to 5) | Do the bulbs look the same? | Unscrew one bulb: what do the others do?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the cells, the three bulbs and the wires. Ask for a guess about each layout before you build anything. Write each guess as a score from 0 to 5 in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will light one bulb, then two in a row, then three in a row. Then we will put bulbs side by side. How bright will each be, compared with one bulb alone? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Will two bulbs be dimmer? Will three? Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think two bulbs in a row will be that bright? (good: Two bulbs need more electricity, so each gets less.; They share the cell.; They will be brighter, because there are more bulbs.)
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Many children say two bulbs will be brighter, because there are more of them. Score the single bulb as 5. Write each guess as a score from 0 to 5. With 2 to 4 children, one builds, one scores, one keeps the table and one draws; swap jobs for each layout. Each child writes their own guess before anyone speaks.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Dim the room. Build one loop with the cell holder, a bulb and two wires. Wait one minute for your eyes to adjust. This glow is the control and scores 5.  
  *Parent says:* Switch off the room light and draw the curtains. Join the cells to one bulb. This is our control. Look at how bright it is. It scores five.  
  *Child mode:* Dim the room. Light one bulb. This bulb scores 5.
  - Note: Use fresh cells and 2.5 volt bulbs. If the room cannot be made dark, cup a hand round each bulb and score every layout in the same way. Never leave a circuit joined for more than a minute. The cells run down. Cheap bulbs are often not quite equal, so light each bulb alone first and use the three that look most alike. Damp weather can make clips and bulb holders touch badly, so press each joint firmly.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Join a second bulb into the same loop, so the electricity goes through one bulb and then the other. Score each bulb from 0 to 5. Write whether the two look the same.  
  *Parent says:* Put the second bulb in the same loop, one after the other. Is it brighter or dimmer than the single bulb? Is the first bulb brighter than the second? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Add a second bulb in the same loop. Score both bulbs. Is the first one brighter?
  - Ask: What do you notice about the first bulb and the second bulb? (good: They look the same.; Both are dimmer than one bulb alone.; Neither is brighter than the other.)
  - Note: Typically both bulbs glow equally, with a dull orange light, clearly dimmer than the single bulb. Exact brightness depends on your cells and bulbs. If neither glows, check that the bulbs are 2.5 volts and the cells are fresh, and that every wire is touching bare metal.
  - Check: Both bulbs in a row glow equally dimly. What does that show about the electricity going through the first bulb? → **The same flow goes through both bulbs, so the first one did not use it up**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Join a third bulb into the same loop. Score all three bulbs. They may glow only faintly, or not at all. Write down what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Add a third bulb in the same loop. What do you see? If you see no glow, look closely, and shade the bulb with your hand. No glow is a good result too.  
  *Child mode:* Add a third bulb in the row. Score it. No glow is a result too.
  - Ask: What pattern do you see as you add bulbs in a row? (good: Each bulb gets dimmer.; The more bulbs, the dimmer.; They all share the same cells.)
  - Note: With three 2.5 volt bulbs on 3 volts, each bulb gets about 1 volt. The glow may be a faint red thread, or nothing. Do not worry. Write down what you saw. Disconnect the cells.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take the loop apart. Join the left sides of two bulb holders with one short wire. Join the right sides with another. Join the cell holder to the left and right sides. Score both bulbs. Add a third bulb the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now give each bulb its own path. Join the two left sides with a wire, and the two right sides with a wire. Then join the cells to the left and the right. How bright are the bulbs now?  
  *Child mode:* Join the bulbs side by side. Each gets its own path. Score them.
  - Ask: How is this circuit different from the row? (good: There are two paths.; Each bulb has its own way to the cell.; The electricity can split.)
  - Note: Expect both bulbs to be about as bright as the single bulb, and a third bulb the same. Disconnect the cells after each test. Side by side, the cells run down faster, and the wires may feel a little warm. Use only the holder to touch hot bulbs.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Join two bulbs in a row again. Unscrew one bulb a little and watch the other. Then do the same with two bulbs side by side. Write down what the other bulb did each time.  
  *Parent says:* Predict first. If I unscrew one bulb in the row, what will the other bulb do? And side by side? Now try each, and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Unscrew one bulb. What does the other bulb do? Try the row, then side by side.
  - Note: In the row, both bulbs go out. Side by side, the other bulb stays as bright as before. Bulbs are usually only warm at 3 volts, but hold them by their holders. If the bulb in the row circuit stays lit when you unscrew the other, a wire is bridging the holder: check the wiring.
  - Check: A string of festival lights goes completely dark when one bulb fails. How are its bulbs most likely joined? → **In a row, in one single path**
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought (claim), what they saw (evidence), and why (reasoning). Write one line for each. Then ask them to draw both circuits.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why. Then draw both circuits.
  - Note: A strong answer says the same flow goes through every bulb in the row and the cells' push is shared between them, so each bulb gets less. Side by side, each bulb has its own path and gets the whole push. After they have said what they saw, explain it. Two bulbs make the path harder, so less flow goes round, and each bulb gets about half of the 3 volts. Side by side, each bulb gets all 3 volts, but the cells give out twice as much flow, so they run down about twice as fast.
  - Think: If the first bulb in a row does not use up the electricity, what makes the bulbs dim?
### e2 · Which Way Round? A bulb and an LED (28 min)
- **question:** Does a bulb light when you turn the cell round? Does an LED?
- **predict:** Before you test, guess. Will the bulb light with the cell turned round? Will the LED? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same cell and the same wires for every test. Only the direction changes. Try each way twice, because a loose wire can fool you. If the LED does not light either way, check the legs touch the faces of the cell and do not touch each other.
- **safety:** A coin cell can badly hurt a child who swallows it, so a grown-up holds it and keeps it from children under five. Put it back in its packet and tape the packet when done. Never join an LED straight to two AA cells, because the current is too big and the LED burns out.
- **record:** In the table, write what the bulb did and what the LED did with the cell each way round. Draw an LED and mark which leg is plus.
- **results table:** Part | Cell one way | Cell the other way | What I think this means
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bulb circuit, the LED and the coin cell. Keep the coin cell in your hand. Ask for a guess about each part, and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* A bulb and an LED are both lights. If I turn the cell round, will the bulb still light? Will the LED? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Does the cell's direction matter for a bulb? For an LED? Why?
  - Note: Do not correct any guess. Write the guesses in the table. A coin cell can be swallowed. Keep it in your hand or on a tray, and away from children under five.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Join a bulb to the cell holder, and watch it light. Score its brightness from 0 to 5. Turn the cell holder round, or swap the two wires on the cells. Score it again. Write down what you see both ways.  
  *Parent says:* Light the bulb. Now swap the two wires on the cells, so the cells face the other way. Does the bulb change? Is it as bright as before?  
  *Child mode:* Light the bulb. Swap the wires on the cells. What changes?
  - Ask: Does the bulb care which end of the cell the wire touches? (good: No, it lights both ways.; It looks the same both ways.)
  - Note: The bulb should glow just the same both ways. The filament does not mind which way the current goes.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up holds the coin cell. Look at the LED. Find the long leg and the short leg. Touch the long leg to the cell's flat side marked plus. Touch the short leg to the other face of the cell. Write down what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the LED by its legs. One leg is longer. Touch the long leg to the plus face of the cell. Touch the short leg to the other face. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up holds the cell. Long leg to plus. Short leg to minus. Look!
  - Note: The long leg is the plus leg. The coin cell has a plus face with writing on it and a flat minus face. If nothing happens, check the legs are touching the faces and not each other. A fresh coin cell typically gives a clear red glow. Keep the bent legs off the metal rim of the cell, because the rim joins to the plus face and the LED would be shorted and stay dark. If it is still dark, try the other LED or a new coin cell. With 2 to 4 children, only the grown-up holds the cell: the children take turns as the one who turns the LED, the one who records, and the one who draws. Put the cell back in its packet when you are finished.
- **s4 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the LED round, so the long leg touches the minus face and the short leg touches the plus face. Watch it. Turn it back. Do this twice. Write down what you see each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now turn the LED round the other way. Does it light? Turn it back. Is anything damaged? What does this tell you about how the electricity moves through an LED?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the LED round. Does it light? Turn it back.
  - Note: The LED will not light the wrong way round, and it is not damaged. The wrong way round stops the current.
  - Check: Your LED lit with the long leg on the plus face and stayed dark when turned round. What does this show? → **An LED lets current through in one direction only**
- **s5 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up tapes two LEDs to the coin cell facing opposite ways. One has its long leg on the plus face. The other has its long leg on the minus face. Look at which one lights. Then turn the coin cell over.  
  *Parent says:* Two LEDs, facing opposite ways, on one cell. Which one will light? Now I turn the cell over. What will happen? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Two LEDs face opposite ways. Which lights? Turn the cell over. Now which?
  - Note: Only one LED lights. When the cell is turned over, the other LED lights and the first goes dark. If both stay dark, check that each LED has one leg on each face.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why. Tell them that the arrow in circuit diagrams is a guess from 1747. Ask which way they think the electrons go.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Which part does not care which way round the cell is, and which part does?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why. Draw an LED with its long leg marked plus.
  - Note: A strong answer says the bulb's thin wire heats whichever way the current goes, but an LED has a special material inside that lets current through one way only. After they have answered, tell them that the arrow in a diagram, from plus to minus, was guessed by Benjamin Franklin in 1747. The electrons in a wire go the other way.
  - Think: If a bulb works both ways, but an LED works one way, what must be different inside an LED?

## Activities
### a1 · Draw It Like an Engineer: circuit diagrams (20 min)
- **record:** Keep your diagrams. Stick them in your notebook next to a photo of each circuit.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the four symbols on a piece of paper: cell, bulb, switch and wire. Say what each one means. Ask your child to copy each symbol twice.  
  *Parent says:* Engineers draw circuits with the same four symbols all over the world. A cell is a long line and a short fat line. The long line is the plus end. A bulb is a circle with a cross. A switch is a gap with a lever. A wire is a straight line.  
  *Child mode:* Learn four symbols. Cell, bulb, switch, wire. Copy each one twice.
  - Ask: Which end of the cell is the long line? (good: The plus end.)
  - Note: Write the symbols large on paper. Let your child copy them. The cell symbol can be tricky: long thin line for plus, short thick line for minus.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to draw three circuits from Bulb Teams as diagrams. Draw one bulb, two bulbs in a row, and two bulbs side by side. Use a ruler for the straight lines. Mark the plus end of the cell.  
  *Parent says:* Now draw each circuit you built, using the symbols. Use a ruler for the wires. In the row, the bulbs are on one loop. Side by side, each bulb has its own branch.  
  *Child mode:* Draw your three circuits with the symbols. Use a ruler for wires.
  - Note: If they did not do Bulb Teams, build the circuits quickly, or look at the picture steps of it. Check that the row has one loop and the side-by-side drawing has two branches joining the same two points.
  - Check: In a diagram, two bulbs are drawn on two separate branches between the same two points. Which circuit is this? → **Parallel**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap diagrams with a partner. Build the circuit that your partner drew, using only the diagram. Check that it lights. Write down any mistake in the diagram.  
  *Parent says:* Swap drawings with me. Build my circuit, using only my diagram. Does it light? If it does not, find out whether the mistake is in the drawing or in the building.  
  *Child mode:* Swap diagrams. Build your partner's circuit. Does it light?
  - Note: Mistakes are useful. A drawing with a gap, or a cell drawn with no wire to it, will not light. Let them find the fault themselves.
### a2 · Pencil Dimmer: slide the clip, change the glow (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up shaves the pencil with a blade, cutting away from their body, on a table, with the pencil held flat. Children do not use the blade. Keep the bare lead away from clothes: it marks them.
- **record:** Draw the circuit and write what the bulb did when the clip was near and when it was far.
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up carefully shaves the wood off one side of a pencil, to show about 12 centimetres of lead. Test the lead with a bulb and cells. If the bulb does not light, use a softer pencil.  
  *Parent says:* A pencil's lead is graphite. It carries electricity, but holds it back more than copper does. A grown-up will shave one side of the pencil to show the lead.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up shaves the pencil to show its lead. We will use the lead as a wire.
  - Note: Use a blade away from the body (a sharpener cannot expose the lead). Shave only one side, so the lead stays held by the other half; the lead is brittle, so go slowly. A soft pencil such as 4B carries more current than an HB. Start with a 2B or 4B. If the bulb never lights, use a softer one. If it stays equally bright wherever the clip is, the lead is too good a conductor: try an HB.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Join a clip from the cell holder to one end of the lead. Join the bulb to the other clip, and touch it to the lead near the first clip. Slide it slowly away. Stop at 2, 5, 8 and 11 centimetres. Score the bulb from 0 to 5 each time. Write the scores in a table.  
  *Parent says:* Join the circuit through the pencil lead. Start with the clip near the first end. Slide it slowly along the lead. What does the bulb do as the clip moves away?  
  *Child mode:* Slide the clip along the lead. Watch the bulb. What changes?
  - Ask: What happens to the bulb as the clip moves away? (good: It gets dimmer.; It glows less.; The longer the lead, the dimmer.)
  - Note: Typically the bulb glows less as more lead is in the loop, but how much depends on the pencil: a pencil lead is not a perfect dimmer. Press the clips firmly on the graphite. In a dim room the change is easier to see. If the change is too small, try a harder pencil, such as HB, or use two pencils end to end. If the bulb stays off, the lead is too hard or the loop is broken. With 2 to 4 children, one slides the clip, one measures, one scores and one writes the table; a second child can score without seeing the first score.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they saw and why. Then ask where in a home a dimmer or regulator is used.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you saw and why you think it happened. Where in our house does something change the brightness or speed with a knob?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you saw, and why. Where at home is there a knob like this?
  - Note: A strong answer says that more lead in the loop holds back the flow more, so less current reaches the bulb. A fan regulator and a light dimmer do the same job, in cleverer ways.
### a3 · Fuse Test: why a fuse is a thin wire (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up does all of this activity. The strand gets very hot and can burn skin and light paper. Use a metal tray, keep paper and cloth away, and have a bowl of water ready. Never do this with a wall socket, plug or phone charger. Sparks and tiny hot bits can fly: the adult keeps their face back, children watch from arm's length, and everyone stays at least one step from the tray. Steel wool fibres splinter skin, so wash hands after. The battery and the used strand stay hot for a minute.
- **record:** Draw the strand before and after, and write one sentence about why a fuse is a thin wire.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up sets up on a metal tray, away from paper, with a bowl of water nearby. Pull out a few strands of fine steel wool, about 5 centimetres long. Hold up one strand and a copper wire. Ask which will get hotter if the same current flows.  
  *Parent says:* Here is one thin strand of steel wool and one thick copper wire. If we send the same current through both, which one will get hotter? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up does this. Which gets hotter, the thin strand or the thick wire? Why?
  - Note: Do this only with a grown-up in charge. Keep the children at arm's length: one child or several can watch, predict, and count seconds aloud, one holding the timer and one writing results. Use a metal tray, a 9 volt battery, and a bowl of water close by. No paper, cloth or plastic near the tray.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up lays one thin strand across the two terminals of a 9 volt battery, holding the battery by its sides. The strand typically glows orange and breaks in a second or two. Count the seconds aloud. Then twist 5 or 6 strands into one bundle and try again. It lasts longer. Put the used strands on the tray. Disconnect and put the battery away.  
  *Parent says:* Watch. The grown-up touches the strand across the battery. What happens to the thin strand? Now think: a fuse is a thin wire like this one, put in a circuit on purpose.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up touches the strand to the battery. Watch the strand. What happens?
  - Note: Single thin strand: it typically glows and breaks within a second or two, often with a few sparks. The twisted bundle should last longer, which shows that thicker means more flow gets through before it melts. If nothing happens, the strand may be too thick or loose: use finer steel wool, press it onto both terminals, or try a fresh battery. If steel wool is not available, a foil strip with a narrow neck works in the same way. Do not hold it for long, because the battery gets warm. Never do this with a plug, wall socket or mobile phone charger. Let the strands cool on the tray, then put them in the bin.
  - Check: A fuse in a plug is a thin wire. Why is it thin on purpose? → **So that it heats up and melts first if the current gets too big**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child where the fuse or MCB box is in your home. Find it together, without touching it. Ask who must do anything with it.  
  *Parent says:* Where is the fuse or MCB box in our house? Let us find it. It protects the wires in the walls. Who is allowed to touch it?  
  *Child mode:* Find the MCB box at home. Look, do not touch. It protects the wires.
  - Note: A fuse or MCB protects the wires, but it does not protect a person who touches a live wire. Only an adult resets an MCB, and only after finding out why it tripped.
### a4 · Be the Festival Lights: a human circuit (15 min)
- **record:** Draw the ring and the two lanes as circuit diagrams, and mark where the break was.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Gather 5 people, or use toys as bulbs. Choose one person as the cell. Everyone else is a bulb. Give the cell a bag of 20 small things, such as dry beans, to be the charges.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to be a circuit. One person is the cell. The others are bulbs. The beans are the charges. The cell starts them moving round.  
  *Child mode:* We are a circuit. One of us is the cell. The rest are bulbs.
  - Note: With only two or three people, use soft toys or cushions as bulbs and let the child move the beans themselves. The beans are only for passing: they are not to be eaten, and keep them away from children under three who might swallow them.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make a ring. Pass one bag of beans from the cell, through each bulb in turn, and back to the cell. Everyone holds their hands out when the bag arrives. Now one bulb sits down and refuses to pass the bag. Watch what happens to the ring.  
  *Parent says:* This ring is a series circuit. The bag goes round, bulb to bulb. Now one bulb sits down. What happens to the bag? What happens to the others?  
  *Child mode:* Make a ring. Pass the bag round. One bulb sits down. What happens?
  - Ask: What happened when one person sat down? (good: The bag stopped.; Everyone stopped.; The loop was broken.)
  - Note: The bag stops at the one who sat down, and everyone behind has nothing to pass. This is why one broken bulb darkens a whole series string.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now make two lanes from the cell to a finish line and back, one bulb in each lane. The cell hands out two bags at the same time, one for each lane. One bulb sits down. Watch the other lane. Count how many beans the cell hands out in 30 seconds, first in the ring and then with the two lanes.  
  *Parent says:* Now each bulb has its own lane. The cell hands a bag to each lane. One bulb sits down. What does the other lane do? What is the cell doing now compared with before?  
  *Child mode:* Make two lanes. One bulb sits down. What does the other lane do?
  - Ask: What is different for the cell now? (good: It has to hand out two bags at once.; It works twice as hard.; It will get tired sooner.)
  - Note: The other lane carries on. The cell has to hand out two bags at once, so with two lanes it hands out about twice as many beans in the same 30 seconds as in the ring, which is why cells run down faster in parallel. Counts will not be exact. With one child and one parent, use toys for the bulbs and count the beans yourselves.
  - Check: In your acting, one lane's bulb sat down and the other lane carried on. What does that show about a parallel circuit? → **A break in one branch leaves the other branches working**

### c1 · Two Doors, One Bell: switches in a row and side by side (40 min)

  - Ask: Which problem needs both switches, and which needs either? (good: The safe needs both.; The bell needs either one.)

## Worksheets
### Rows, Branches and Symbols (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Two equal bulbs are joined in a row to one set of cells. Compared with one bulb alone, how bright is each bulb?  
   Options: Brighter than one bulb alone / Dimmer than one bulb alone / As bright as one bulb alone  
   **Answer:** Dimmer than one bulb alone. *Why:* In a row, the two bulbs share the cells' push, and less current goes round the loop. Each bulb glows dimly.
2. (mcq) Two equal bulbs are joined side by side to one set of cells. Compared with one bulb alone, how bright is each bulb?  
   Options: Brighter than one bulb alone / As bright as one bulb alone / Dimmer than one bulb alone  
   **Answer:** As bright as one bulb alone. *Why:* Side by side, each bulb has its own path and gets the whole push of the cells. Each glows as brightly as a single bulb would.
3. (truefalse) In a row of equal bulbs, the first bulb is always the brightest.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The same current goes through every bulb in a row, so equal bulbs glow equally. The order along the wire makes no difference.
4. (truefalse) If you unscrew one bulb from a parallel circuit, the other bulbs stay lit.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each bulb is on its own branch, so the other branches are still complete loops.
5. (fill) A circuit with only one path is called a ____ circuit.  
   **Answer:** ['series']. *Why:* Series means one after another, with one path. A break anywhere stops everything.
6. (fill) A circuit where each bulb has its own path to the cell is called a ____ circuit.  
   **Answer:** ['parallel']. *Why:* Parallel means side by side. Each bulb is on its own branch.
7. (match) Match each circuit symbol to the part it stands for.  
   **Answer:** a circle with a cross inside → a bulb; a long thin line next to a short fat line → a cell; a gap with a lever → a switch; a straight line → a wire. *Why:* Engineers all over the world use these same four symbols, so any diagram can be read anywhere. In the cell symbol, the long line is the plus end.
8. (sort) Sort each one by how its parts are wired.  
   **Answer:** an old festival string that goes dark when one bulb fails → In a row; a fan and a tube light in a room → Side by side; a switch and the light it controls → In a row; the lights in different rooms of a home → Side by side; three AA cells inside a torch → In a row; a front-door bell button and a back-door bell button that ring one bell → Side by side. *Why:* Where one break must stop everything, or where pushes add up, things are in a row. Where each part must work alone, they are side by side.
9. (mcq) A fan keeps turning when you switch off the tube light in the same room. What does that show about how they are wired?  
   Options: They are on separate branches, side by side / The fan has its own cell / They are in a row  
   **Answer:** They are on separate branches, side by side. *Why:* Switching off the tube light opens only its own branch. The fan's branch is still a complete loop, so the fan keeps turning.
10. (order) Put these in order, from the dimmest bulbs to the brightest.  
   **Answer:** ['three bulbs in a row', 'two bulbs in a row', 'one bulb alone']. *Why:* The more bulbs share the push in a row, the less each one gets. Three in a row are the dimmest, and one alone is the brightest.
11. (mcq) What is the job of a fuse in a circuit?  
   Options: To make the bulb brighter / To melt and break the circuit if the current gets too big / To make the current bigger  
   **Answer:** To melt and break the circuit if the current gets too big. *Why:* A fuse is a thin wire that heats first. If a fault makes the current too big, it melts and breaks the circuit before the other wires get hot.
12. (write) In your own words, explain why a bulb's thin filament glows but the thick copper wire joined to it stays cool.  
   **Answer:** The same current goes through both. The filament is very thin and holds back the flow a lot, so the charges give up much energy there as heat, and it gets hot enough to glow. The thick wire holds back the flow very little, so it stays cool.. *Why:* A strong answer says the current is the same in both, that the filament has high resistance, and that this is where the energy turns to heat and light.
13. (draw) Draw a circuit diagram of one cell and two bulbs side by side. Use the symbols and mark the plus end of the cell.  
   **Answer:** A cell with two bulbs on two separate branches between the same two points, drawn with a ruler, with the long line of the cell marked plus.. *Why:* A strong diagram shows two branches, one bulb on each, joined at both ends to the cell. If the bulbs are on one loop, the circuit is in series.
14. (label) Here is a circuit diagram of a cell, a switch and a bulb in one loop. Label each part.  
   **Answer:** ['a long thin line next to a short fat line: cell', 'a gap with a lever: switch', 'a circle with a cross: bulb']. *Why:* Each part has its own symbol: the cell is a long thin line beside a short fat one, the switch is a gap with a lever, and the bulb is a circle with a cross.
15. (mcq) You join an LED to a coin cell and it does not light. When you turn it round, it lights. What was wrong the first time?  
   Options: It was joined the wrong way round / The LED was broken / The coin cell was empty  
   **Answer:** It was joined the wrong way round. *Why:* An LED lets current through one way only. Turned round, it simply blocks the current. It is not damaged, and the cell was fine since it lit afterwards.
### Sharing the Push (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Three equal bulbs are joined in a row to cells that push with 4.5 volts. About how many volts does each bulb get?  
   Options: 13.5 volts / 4.5 volts / 1.5 volts  
   **Answer:** 1.5 volts. *Why:* In a row, the push is shared. 4.5 volts shared between 3 equal bulbs is 1.5 volts each.
2. (mcq) What is the best reason that two bulbs in a row are dimmer than one bulb alone?  
   Options: The first bulb uses up the electricity / The wires get tired / They share the push of the cells, and less current goes round  
   **Answer:** They share the push of the cells, and less current goes round. *Why:* The same current goes through both bulbs, so the first does not use it up. Each bulb gets only a share of the push, and the extra bulb makes the path harder, so the current is smaller.
3. (truefalse) In a row of bulbs, there is less current after the first bulb than before it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The current is the same everywhere in a series loop. If it were smaller after the first bulb, charges would be piling up or disappearing.
4. (fill) A wire joined straight across the two ends of a cell makes a ____ circuit.  
   **Answer:** ['short']. *Why:* Nothing holds back the flow, so the current is huge and the wire and cell heat up fast.
5. (count) A torch has 3 AA cells in a row, all facing the same way. Each cell pushes with 1.5 volts. How many volts is the push?  
   **Answer:** 4.5. *Why:* In a row, the pushes add up. 1.5 plus 1.5 plus 1.5 is 4.5 volts.
6. (count) A cell lasts 20 hours with one bulb. About how many hours does it last with 4 equal bulbs side by side?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* Side by side, each bulb draws its own current from the cell, so 4 bulbs drain it 4 times as fast. 20 divided by 4 is 5 hours.
7. (odd) Three of these are wired side by side. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: the lights in different rooms of a home / an old festival string that goes dark when one bulb fails / two doorbell buttons that ring the same bell / a fan and a tube light in one room  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* The old festival string has its bulbs in a row, with one path. The other three each have separate branches, so they work alone.
8. (mcq) Why do engineers still draw the arrow of current from plus to minus, when the electrons really move from minus to plus?  
   Options: Millions of diagrams and rules already use it, and the answers are right either way / Nobody has found out which way the electrons move / The electrons switch direction every second  
   **Answer:** Millions of diagrams and rules already use it, and the answers are right either way. *Why:* Franklin guessed the direction in 1747, long before electrons were found. Keeping the rule means old and new diagrams agree, and the results come out right.
9. (write) Zoya says: 'The first bulb in a row uses up some of the electricity, so the second bulb is dimmer.' Explain what is wrong with her idea.  
   **Answer:** In a row the same current goes through every bulb, so nothing is used up on the way. If the second bulb were dimmer, equal bulbs would not glow equally, but they do. Both bulbs are dim because they share the cells' push.. *Why:* A strong answer uses what was seen: both bulbs glowed equally, which shows the same current reached both.
10. (write) A bulb is joined to cells through a pencil lead. A short length of lead in the loop gives a bright bulb, and a long length gives a dim one. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Graphite holds back the flow of electricity. More lead in the loop holds it back more, so less current reaches the bulb, and the bulb is dimmer.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the word resistance, and says that a longer lead has more of it.
11. (draw) Draw a circuit with a cell, a bulb and two switches so that the bulb lights only when both switches are closed. Use the symbols.  
   **Answer:** A cell, a bulb and two switches all in one loop, one after the other.. *Why:* Both switches are on the only path, so both must be closed for the loop to be whole.
12. (label) Here is a circuit diagram of two bulbs side by side with a cell. Label the cell, the two branches and the two bulbs.  
   **Answer:** ['the long and short lines: cell', 'the top path: branch 1', 'the bottom path: branch 2', 'the bulb on branch 1: bulb A', 'the bulb on branch 2: bulb B']. *Why:* Each bulb is on its own branch. The two branches start and end at the same two points, joined to the cell.
13. (perspective) A family is deciding how to wire the new festival lights on their balcony.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and backs it with a fact, such as: a row breaks all at once, side by side stays lit, a mix saves wire and limits the damage. It also gives one fair point for another view.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Each wiring has a price. Series needs less wire but fails together. Parallel is steady but uses more wire and drains the cells faster.
14. (mcq) An electrician puts a much bigger fuse in a house circuit than the wires can safely carry. What is the danger?  
   Options: The fuse will melt too soon / The lights will be dimmer / The wires could overheat and start a fire before the fuse melts  
   **Answer:** The wires could overheat and start a fire before the fuse melts. *Why:* A fuse protects the wires by melting before they get too hot. A fuse that is too big does not melt in time.
15. (mcq) Why is a bulb's filament made so thin?  
   Options: A thin wire carries more current / A thin wire is cheaper to see / A thin wire holds back the flow, so it gets hot enough to glow  
   **Answer:** A thin wire holds back the flow, so it gets hot enough to glow. *Why:* A thin wire has high resistance. The charges give up their energy there as heat, which makes it glow.
### Fuses, Switches and Credit (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Two cells are joined in a row but facing opposite ways, with a bulb. What does the bulb do?  
   Options: It glows as bright as with one cell / It glows twice as bright / It stays dark, because the pushes cancel  
   **Answer:** It stays dark, because the pushes cancel. *Why:* Cells in a row add their pushes only if they face the same way. Facing opposite ways, the pushes work against each other and cancel.
2. (mcq) A string has 100 equal bulbs in a row, joined to the 230 volts of the mains. About how many volts does each bulb get?  
   Options: 23 volts / 230 volts / 2.3 volts  
   **Answer:** 2.3 volts. *Why:* The push is shared equally. 230 divided by 100 is 2.3 volts each.
3. (count) One cell, with one bulb, lasts 10 hours. Now two cells are joined side by side and run two bulbs side by side. About how many hours does the pair of cells last?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* Each bulb draws the same current as before. The two cells share the load, so each cell gives out only what it did when it ran one bulb. Each lasts about 10 hours.
4. (truefalse) A fuse protects a person who is touching a live wire.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A fuse protects the wires from overheating. It can take too long to melt to save a person, so electrical safety rules for people still apply.
5. (fill) In a circuit diagram, the arrow for current leaves the ____ end of the cell.  
   **Answer:** ['plus', 'positive']. *Why:* The arrow follows the rule Franklin guessed in 1747. The electrons really move the other way.
6. (match) Match each wiring to what it does.  
   **Answer:** two switches in a row with a bulb → the bulb lights only when both are closed; two switches side by side with a bulb → the bulb lights when either one is closed; a switch in the main wire of a string with its bulbs side by side → turns all the bulbs off; a switch in one branch of a string with its bulbs side by side → turns off only that branch's bulb. *Why:* Where a switch sits in the circuit decides what it controls. Whatever is in the same path as the switch is controlled by it.
7. (odd) Three of these are made to heat up or break when the current gets too big. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: a fuse / an MCB / a bulb's filament / a thick copper wire in a house  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A fuse, an MCB and a filament are made to heat or break. A thick copper wire is made to hold back the current as little as possible so it stays cool.
8. (mcq) Why does a wire joined straight across a cell get hot so fast?  
   Options: The cell makes heat on its own / Nothing holds back the flow, so a huge current goes through the thin wire / The wire is too long  
   **Answer:** Nothing holds back the flow, so a huge current goes through the thin wire. *Why:* A short circuit has almost no resistance, so the current is very large. A large current heats even a thick wire.
9. (write) An old festival string goes dark when one bulb fails. Explain, step by step, how you would find the broken bulb and fix it safely.  
   **Answer:** First a grown-up unplugs the string. Then look into each bulb with a torch for a gap in the thread, or swap each bulb with a spare one. Replace the broken bulb, check the plug and the wire, and plug in again.. *Why:* A strong answer starts with unplugging, since mains is 230 volts. It then finds the one bulb that broke the path. Everything lights again when it is replaced.
10. (write) A friend says: 'An electron races from the cell to the bulb really fast, which is why the bulb lights at once.' Is she right? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Not quite. The electrons in a wire drift very slowly, less than a millimetre in a second. The wire is already full of electrons. The push from the cell passes along the wire almost at once and sets all of them moving together, so the bulb lights straight away.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the slow drift of the electrons from the fast push that passes along the wire, like a row of marbles in a tube.
11. (draw) Draw a circuit diagram of a home with a light, a fan and a socket, each on its own branch with its own switch, so that each can be used alone.  
   **Answer:** Two supply lines with three branches between them: one with a switch and a light, one with a switch and a fan, one with a switch and a socket.. *Why:* Each part is on its own branch and its switch is in the same branch, in a row with it. Opening one switch leaves the others working.
12. (label) Here is a drawing of a torch bulb cut open. Label the glass, the filament, the two support wires and the metal base.  
   **Answer:** ['the clear dome: glass', 'the tiny coil at the middle: filament', 'the two thick wires holding the coil: support wires', 'the screw part at the bottom: metal base']. *Why:* Current goes in through the base, along a support wire, through the thin filament and out through the other support wire. The filament is where it glows.
13. (perspective) Two inventors and an engineer disagree about who should be remembered for the electric light bulb.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason, such as the first working lamp, the whole system, or the improvement that made it practical. It also notes that the bulb came from many people.. *Why:* Credit for an invention is often shared. Inventions are built by many people, and the best-known name is not always the only one.
14. (mcq) A circuit must light a bulb if either a door switch or a hand switch is closed. How should the two switches be wired?  
   Options: In a row / Side by side / Both on one side of the cell with no bulb between  
   **Answer:** Side by side. *Why:* Side by side, closing either switch makes a whole path. In a row, both would have to be closed.
15. (truefalse) A cell that runs three bulbs side by side will run down about three times as fast as the same cell running one bulb.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each bulb on its own branch takes its own current, so the cell must give out three times as much each second.

   Options: They glow the same / The bulbs in circuit Y / The bulbs in circuit X

   Options: Each is on its own branch, so a switch controls only its own branch / Switches only control lights / The fan has stronger wires  
   **Answer:** Each is on its own branch, so a switch controls only its own branch. *Why:* Home wiring is parallel. A switch in one branch breaks only that branch. If the home were in a row, switching off one light would put out everything.
3. (mcq) Three equal bulbs are in a row on cells that push with 6 volts. About how many volts does each bulb get?  
   Options: 18 volts / 6 volts / 2 volts  
   **Answer:** 2 volts. *Why:* The push is shared equally in a row. 6 volts divided between 3 bulbs is 2 volts each.
4. (mcq) What makes a bulb's filament glow while the thick wires joined to it stay cool?  
   Options: The thick wires have no current in them / The filament has more electrons / The filament is thin and holds back the flow a lot, so it gets very hot  
   **Answer:** The filament is thin and holds back the flow a lot, so it gets very hot. *Why:* The same current goes through both. Only the thin filament has high resistance, so the energy turns to heat and light there.
5. (mcq) In a circuit diagram, which symbol stands for a cell?  
   Options: A long thin line next to a short fat line / A circle with a cross inside / A gap with a lever  
   **Answer:** A long thin line next to a short fat line. *Why:* The cell is a long thin line, the plus end, next to a short fat line, the minus end. The circle with a cross is a bulb, and the gap with a lever is a switch.

   Options: They move the opposite way, from minus to plus / They move the same way as the arrow / They do not move at all  
   **Answer:** They move the opposite way, from minus to plus. *Why:* Franklin guessed the direction of the arrow in 1747. Later the electron was found, and it moves the other way.
7. (mcq) An LED does not light when you join it to a coin cell. What should you try first?  
   Options: Turn the LED round / Wet the legs / Join a second coin cell in a row  
   **Answer:** Turn the LED round. *Why:* An LED works only one way round. Turned the right way, its long leg is on the plus face. The wrong way round it blocks the current but is not harmed. Two coin cells in a row could burn it out.
8. (mcq) What does a fuse do?  
   Options: It melts and breaks the circuit when the current gets too big / It makes the current steady / It stores electricity  
   **Answer:** It melts and breaks the circuit when the current gets too big. *Why:* A fuse is a thin wire that heats first. It breaks the circuit before the wires in the walls get dangerously hot.
9. (mcq) A loose wire touches both ends of a cell and quickly gets hot. What is this called?  
   Options: A switch / A parallel circuit / A short circuit  
   **Answer:** A short circuit. *Why:* Nothing holds back the flow except the wire, so the current is huge and the wire heats up. It is dangerous and the wire should be removed at once.
10. (mcq) A safe light should come on only when two keys are both turned. How are the two key switches joined?  
   Options: In a row / Side by side / On opposite sides of the bulb with no wire  
   **Answer:** In a row. *Why:* In a row, the only path goes through both switches, so both must be closed.
11. (mcq) A bell should ring if either of two buttons is pressed. How are the buttons joined?  
   Options: Side by side / One button in the cell holder / In a row  
   **Answer:** Side by side. *Why:* Side by side, pressing either button makes a whole loop. Pressing both works too.
12. (truefalse) In two equal bulbs in a row, more current goes through the first bulb than the second.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The current is the same all round a series loop, so equal bulbs glow equally. They are dim because they share the push.

## Applications
- Wiring the lights, fans and sockets of a home in parallel, so each works alone at the full 230 volts.
- Putting a switch in series with the light it controls, so that opening the switch breaks the loop.
- Festival string lights, where bulbs are wired in a row, in groups, or side by side.
- Fuses and MCBs, which protect house wiring from short circuits and overloads.
- Torches and remote controls, which put cells in a row to add their pushes: 3 cells make 4.5 volts.
- LED indicator lights on chargers and televisions, which work only one way round.
- Computer chips, which join millions of tiny switches in rows and side by side to do logic, an idea shown by Claude Shannon in 1937.
- India's national power grid, which joins power stations and cities side by side and can share power when one place fails.
- Dimmers and fan regulators, which change the resistance in a loop to change the brightness or speed.
- Engineers such as Hertha Ayrton (1854 to 1923), who in 1899 became the first woman member of the Institution of Electrical Engineers, and Rajeshwari Chatterjee (1922 to 2010), who built up microwave engineering at the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru.

## Open questions
- **How do the electrical signals between billions of nerve cells become a thought or a feeling?** Scientists can follow the signals from nerve to nerve. Nobody yet knows how this adds up to what it feels like to think.
- **Can a material carry electricity with no resistance at everyday temperatures?** Superconductors, found in 1911, carry current with zero resistance, but only when very cold or under huge pressure. A material that works at room temperature and normal pressure has not been confirmed.
- **Why does every electron carry exactly the same amount of charge?** Physicists have measured the charge very precisely. Nobody has explained why it has this value, or why all electrons have the same one.
- **How can a whole country store electricity from the Sun for use at night, cheaply and cleanly?** Batteries, pumped water and other stores exist, but none is yet cheap and large enough everywhere. Engineers are still testing many ideas.
- **How small can a working switch be?** Chip makers now build switches only a few nanometres across. How small they can go, before quantum effects make them unreliable, is still being tested.

## Connected maths
- A festival string has 46 equal bulbs in a row, joined to the 230 volts of the mains. How many volts does each bulb get? A second string has 23 equal bulbs in a row. How many volts does each bulb get, and why is it more? **5 volts each in the first string. 10 volts each in the second. With fewer bulbs, each bulb has a bigger share of the same push.**. 230 divided by 46 is 5. 230 divided by 23 is 10. The same 230 volts is shared between fewer bulbs, so each share is bigger. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- One bulb on two cells gets 3 volts. Two bulbs in a row get 1.5 volts each. Three get 1 volt each. Four get 0.75 volts each. What do six bulbs in a row get each? What do you notice? **0.5 volts each. Each time you add a bulb, the 3 volts is divided into more equal shares, so each share gets smaller, and by less each time.**. Six equal shares of 3 volts is 3 divided by 6, which is 0.5. The shares go 3, 1.5, 1, 0.75, and so on. The drop is big at first and then smaller. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- One cell lasts 12 hours with one bulb. Each extra bulb side by side drains it by the same amount again. How long does it last with 2 bulbs, 3 bulbs, 4 bulbs and 6 bulbs side by side? What pattern do you see? **6 hours, 4 hours, 3 hours and 2 hours. The more bulbs, the shorter the time, and 12 divided by the number of bulbs gives the answer.**. Two bulbs use the cell twice as fast, so 12 divided by 2 is 6 hours. Three bulbs: 12 divided by 3 is 4. Four: 3. Six: 2. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A cell lasts 10 hours with one torch bulb. Anika uses the torch for 15 minutes each evening. How many evenings does the cell last? How many evenings would it last with two bulbs side by side? **40 evenings with one bulb, and 20 evenings with two bulbs side by side.**. 10 hours is 600 minutes. 600 divided by 15 is 40. With two bulbs side by side the cell lasts half as long, so 20 evenings. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- Look at the chart. Which layouts scored the same as one bulb alone? Another child predicts that four bulbs in a row would score 0 or 1. Do you agree? Give a reason from the chart. **Two side by side and three side by side scored 5, the same as one bulb. Yes, a score of 0 or 1 is sensible: the score fell from 5 to 2 to 1 as bulbs were added in a row, so four in a row would be 1 or 0.**. The chart shows 5 for one bulb, 2 for two in a row and 1 for three in a row. The scores go down each time a bulb is added, so four should be even lower. Side by side stays at 5 each time. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- Suppose an electric eel's body has 6,000 tiny cells in a row, and together they make about 600 volts. If every cell makes the same push, how much does each make? How many AA cells of 1.5 volts in a row would make 600 volts? **0.1 volts for each of the eel's cells. 400 AA cells in a row.**. 600 divided by 6,000 is 0.1 volts. 600 divided by 1.5 is 400 AA cells. The eel's cells are tiny, but there are many of them. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
