# Keeping clean: washing hands, bathing and brushing teeth
`sci.health.hygiene.L1`

**Summary.** Eat a biscuit, swish some water round your mouth, and look in a mirror. The front teeth look clean, but crumbs still sit in the grooves of the back teeth. Tiny living beings in your mouth eat those crumbs. Your child finds out what water, soap, a bath and a toothbrush each take away, and why. They learn when to wash their hands, how to brush the back teeth, and why we do it every day.

**Intro.** *Open your mouth wide in front of a mirror, and shine a torch inside. Look at the tops of your back teeth. Are they flat and smooth, or do they have little hills and grooves?* Your front teeth are thin and smooth. Your back teeth are wide, with bumps and grooves on top. The grooves help you grind food. They are also where food likes to hide. In this unit we find out where food and dirt stay on our bodies, and what takes them away. We test water, soap, a bath and a toothbrush.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say when to wash my hands: before I eat, after the toilet, after playing and after blowing my nose.
1. By the end I can wash my hands with soap, rubbing every part, for as long as it takes to sing Happy Birthday twice.
2. By the end I can say why I brush my teeth twice a day, and brush my back teeth as well as my front teeth.
3. By the end I can say that tiny living beings live in my mouth and on my skin, and that most of them do no harm.
4. By the end I can say why I take a bath: to wash off sweat, dust and old skin.
5. By the end I can make a guess, test it, and say what I saw.

## Terms
- **Hygiene**: Hygiene means the things we do to keep our bodies and homes clean, so we stay well. *e.g.* Washing your hands before you eat idli is hygiene.
- **Germs**: Germs are tiny beings, too small to see. Some kinds can make us ill if they get inside us. *e.g.* Germs on your hands can get into your mouth when you eat with your fingers.
- **Soap**: Soap is something we rub on our skin with water. It lifts oil, dirt and germs off, so the water can carry them away. *e.g.* A bar of soap makes white foam when you rub it on wet hands.
- **Plaque**: Plaque is a soft, sticky layer on your teeth. The tiny living beings in your mouth make it. *e.g.* In the morning, before you brush, your teeth feel fuzzy to your tongue. That fuzz is plaque.
- **Bristles**: Bristles are the thin, bendy hairs on a toothbrush. They reach into grooves and gaps. *e.g.* The bristles bend into the grooves on top of your back teeth.

## Explanations
### Why we wash our hands · *defined*
**Definition:** Washing hands with soap and water takes away dirt and germs, so they cannot get from our hands into our mouths.

Your hands touch door handles, toys, the floor and your nose. Germs from those things stay on your hands. If you eat with your fingers, the germs go into your mouth. Some can give you a tummy upset or a cold. Water alone washes off loose dirt. But skin is a little oily, and oil holds on to germs. Soap lifts the oil, and the water carries it away. Rub all six parts: palms, backs of the hands, between the fingers, backs of the fingers, thumbs, and fingertips in the palm. Rub for 20 seconds. That is Happy Birthday sung twice.
- e.g. Before you eat, and after you use the toilet, wash with soap.
- e.g. After playing outside, or touching a pet, wash with soap.
- e.g. After you blow your nose or sneeze into your hands, wash with soap.
- e.g. Florence Nightingale, a nurse in England, wrote in 1859 that every nurse should wash her hands very often during the day.
### What lives in your mouth, and why we brush · *defined*
**Definition:** Many kinds of tiny living beings live in every mouth. They eat leftover food on our teeth and make an acid. Brushing takes away the leftovers and the sticky plaque.

Your mouth is a home for tiny living beings called bacteria. They are too small to see. Most of them do you no harm. They eat what you eat, especially sugar. As they eat, they make an acid. An acid is something sour, like lemon juice. Over many days, acid can wear a hole in a tooth. The bacteria also make a sticky layer called plaque. Brushing takes off the food and the plaque. Food hides most in the grooves of the back teeth. So we brush every tooth, twice a day, for about two minutes.
- e.g. In the biscuit test, a swish of water cleaned the front teeth, but crumbs stayed in the grooves of the back teeth.
- e.g. Holes in children's teeth are found most often on the tops of the back teeth, where the grooves are.
- e.g. Around the age of six, new big back teeth come in behind the milk teeth. They are grown-up teeth, and they must last a whole life.
### Why we take a bath · *defined*
**Definition:** A bath washes sweat, dust, oil and old skin off the whole body.

All day, your skin makes sweat and a little oil. Dust from the air and the floor sticks to them. Your skin also loses old skin in tiny flakes, while new skin grows underneath. A bath with water and a little soap washes all of this away. Fresh sweat has almost no smell. The smell comes from tiny living beings on your skin, who eat the sweat. A bath washes most of the sweat away, so the smell goes too.
- e.g. In the white cloth test, the sole of a foot often left a grey mark before the bath, and almost none after it.
- e.g. After playing in the sun, you may feel sticky and want a second bath.
- e.g. Your skin feels sticky and salty after a hot game. That is dried sweat.
### Are the tiny beings on us friends or enemies? · *debated*
We often call all tiny living beings germs, as if they were all enemies. But most bacteria on your skin and in your mouth do you no harm. Many of them help you. Some keep harmful kinds away just by living in their place. Only a few kinds make us ill. So washing and brushing do not try to remove every tiny being. They keep the numbers small, and keep harmful kinds out of our mouths. Bacteria also send each other chemical signals. Scientists think these signals help them build a sticky layer like plaque.
- e.g. Plaque grows back on your teeth every night. Brushing keeps it thin.
- e.g. Bacteria on healthy skin help keep harmful bacteria from settling there.
- e.g. Curd is made by helpful bacteria.
- **Scientists who study the bacteria living on and in us:** Most of these bacteria are our neighbours, not our enemies. A body is a home for many beings, and good hygiene keeps that home in balance.
- **Many doctors and dentists:** Some of these bacteria do cause holes in teeth and illness. So we should think of them as germs to keep off, and wash and brush well every day.
- **Scientists who study how bacteria behave:** Bacteria sense each other and act together, which is why they build plaque only when there are many of them. Some scientists call this decision-making. Others call it automatic chemistry.
### Humans and other animals keep clean · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals, including humans, spend time every day cleaning their bodies.

Humans are not the only animals who keep clean. A cat licks her fur with a rough tongue. A crow dips into water and shakes her feathers. A sparrow rolls in dry dust, and the dust takes extra oil off her feathers. An elephant sprays water and mud over her back. An ant has a tiny comb on each front leg, and she pulls her feelers through it to clean them. Monkeys pick through each other's fur. Humans use water, soap and toothbrushes. Each animal cleans in the way that suits their body.
- e.g. A crow or pigeon may bathe in a dish of water left on a balcony.
- e.g. A cat licks her paws, then wipes her face with them.
- e.g. Elephants at temple and forest camps in Kerala and Karnataka are given baths in rivers.

## Mostly agreed
- Washing hands with soap and water stops many illnesses from spreading. *How we know:* In a study in Karachi, in Pakistan, families were given soap and shown how to wash their hands. Their children had about half as many tummy upsets with diarrhoea, and about half as many chest infections, as children in other families.
- Brushing twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste helps stop holes in teeth. *How we know:* Dentists have compared large groups of children for many years. Children who brushed twice a day with fluoride toothpaste got fewer holes in their teeth.
- Bacteria in the mouth make acid from sugar, and the acid wears away teeth. *How we know:* In 1890, Willoughby Miller showed in a laboratory that mouth bacteria given sugar make acid, and that the acid softens teeth.

## How we found out
**Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a cloth seller who made his own microscopes, and later the dentist Willoughby Miller, Leeuwenhoek saw bacteria from his teeth in 1683. Miller showed that mouth bacteria make acid from sugar in 1890..** For thousands of years, people in many lands believed a toothache came from a tiny worm inside the tooth. An old clay tablet from Babylon tells of the tooth worm. In Europe, many people still believed it about 300 years ago. Then, in 1683, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek looked at the white paste from his own teeth. He was a cloth seller in the Netherlands who made his own tiny microscopes. He mixed the paste with clean water. He saw a great many tiny living beings, moving about. Nobody had ever seen them before. He found no tooth worm. About 200 years later, in 1890, a dentist called Willoughby Miller tested what these beings do. In his laboratory he gave them sugar and bread, and they made an acid. The acid softened pieces of tooth. So a hole in a tooth is not made by a worm. It is made by acid from the bacteria in plaque, who eat our leftover food.  
*What people believed before:* That holes in teeth are made by a tooth worm. No one ever found a tooth worm. Leeuwenhoek saw tiny living beings in plaque instead, and Miller showed that their acid softens teeth.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Brushing hard cleans teeth better.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Scrubbing hard cleans a dirty floor or a pot faster.  
  Against: Teeth and gums are not a floor. Plaque is soft and comes off with gentle brushing. Brushing very hard can hurt the gums and wear the teeth. Dentists say: a soft brush, small gentle circles, and every part of every tooth.
- **Children are healthier if they play in some dirt, so too much cleaning is bad.** (Some scientists who study allergies; *debated*)  
  For: Children who grow up on farms, with animals and soil, get some allergies less often. Our bodies may need to meet many harmless microbes when we are young.  
  Against: This does not mean dirty hands are good. Washing hands before eating and after the toilet still stops many illnesses. Scientists are still working out which kinds of contact with nature help, and how.
- **Milk teeth fall out anyway, so they do not need brushing.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Milk teeth do fall out, between about age 6 and age 12.  
  Against: A hole in a milk tooth can hurt and make eating hard. Milk teeth hold the space for the grown-up teeth growing underneath. And the big back teeth that come in at about age six are grown-up teeth already.

## Roots
We have looked into our own mouths, and found where food stays after we eat. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves, and about who we share our bodies with.

**Deep truth.** In the biscuit test, crumbs stayed in the grooves of your back teeth after a rinse. Tiny living beings in your mouth eat those leftovers. So every time you eat, you feed them too. Brushing takes their leftovers away, so they make less of the acid that wears teeth.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a meal as ours alone, and of our mouth as only ours. But many kinds of living beings share every meal we eat, right there on our teeth.  
*Evidence:* After a water rinse, crumbs stayed in the grooves of the back teeth, and only the brush took them out. Every morning the fuzzy plaque was back, built overnight by the bacteria who live in the mouth.

- *who am i:* You are a home. Many kinds of tiny living beings live in your mouth and on your skin.
- *what is life:* Every living being eats and keeps their body in order: a bacterium, a cat licking her fur, and you with your toothbrush.
- *what is this world:* The water that washes you came from rain and rivers. It goes back to them, carrying what it washed off you.

One meal feeds many lives at once. The rice on your plate feeds you, and the crumbs left on your teeth feed the bacteria in your mouth. Your body is one home where many kinds of life live together. Keeping clean does not empty that home. It keeps the many lives in it in balance with you.

- **Thirukkural 298, by Thiruvalluvar:** Water makes the outside of the body clean. Truthfulness makes the inside clean. (Thiruvalluvar knew that water cleans the outside of the body. In the white cloth test, the bath water carried away dust we could not even see.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, Present Moment Wonderful Moment (1990), the verse for turning on the water:** Water flows from high in the mountains, and runs deep in the Earth. It comes to us, and keeps all life going. (The water in your tap came a long way, from rain and rivers, before it washed your hands. Every hand wash is a meeting with that water.)
- Science (settled): Scientists have found more than 700 kinds of bacteria in human mouths. One person carries many dozens of kinds at a time.
- Science (open): Bacteria send each other chemical signals, and build a sticky layer like plaque when there are many of them. How they do this together in a real mouth is still being worked out.
- Other view, Many microbiologists: Many microbiologists describe bacterial signals as chemistry that runs by itself, the way a match lights when it is struck. They say bacteria react to chemicals, but do not make choices.
- Practice, A tongue tour of your teeth (3 min): Close your eyes. Close your mouth. Now, slowly, let your tongue walk along your teeth. Start at the front. Feel how thin and smooth the front teeth are. Now walk to the back. Feel the wide back teeth, and the little grooves on top. Many tiny beings live here, in this home that is you. Take one slow breath in through your nose, and out. Open your eyes.
- Ask: If tiny beings eat every meal with you, is your mouth only yours?
- Ask: When you brush, are you looking after yourself, or the beings who live with you, or both?
- Ask: Where does the water that washed your hands go next?

## Story: The Tooth at the Back
*Revathi, her mother and her grandfather are made up. It is true that the first big grown-up back teeth usually come in at about age six, behind the milk teeth, and that their grooves are where food stays most.*

Revathi was six. She lived in Thrissur, in Kerala, with her mother and her grandfather. She called her grandfather Appooppan. Every morning, the three of them ate breakfast together on the floor of the kitchen.

One morning there was puttu, soft and white and steaming, with black kadala curry. Revathi loved puttu. She pressed it into the curry with her fingers and ate a big mouthful.

Then she stopped chewing. Her tongue had found something new. Right at the back of her mouth, behind all her small teeth, there was something hard and wide.

'Amma,' she said. 'There is a stone in my mouth.'

Amma laughed. She fetched the torch from the shelf. 'Open wide,' she said, and she shone the light inside.

'That is not a stone,' said Amma. 'That is a new tooth. It is a big grown-up tooth, coming in behind your milk teeth. Most children get one at about your age, on each side, top and bottom.'

Revathi went to the mirror by the door. Amma held the torch. Revathi pulled her cheek back with one finger. There it was: a wide white tooth, bigger than all the others. On top, it had little hills and valleys, like a tiny mountain range.

And in the valleys, there was puttu.

'There is breakfast in my tooth,' said Revathi.

'Swish some water and look again,' said Amma.

Revathi took a big sip from the steel tumbler. She swished it round her mouth, puffing out her cheeks, and spat into the sink. She looked again. Her front teeth were clean. But in the valleys of the new tooth, little white crumbs of puttu were still sitting there.

'The water went over the top,' said Revathi. 'It didn't go into the valleys.'

'Those valleys are called grooves,' said Amma. 'They help you grind food. But food hides in them. Tiny living beings in your mouth find it there and eat it. They make an acid as they eat, and the acid can make a hole in the tooth. So we have to clean the grooves.'

'How?' said Revathi.

Amma gave her the toothbrush. 'Put the bristles right on top of the back tooth,' she said. 'Brush gently, back and forth, in short little strokes. Let the bristles go down into the grooves.'

Revathi brushed. Then she looked in the mirror again, with the torch. The grooves were clean. She ran her tongue over the new tooth. It felt smooth and squeaky.

'This tooth has to last you your whole life,' said Amma. 'It will never be swapped for a new one.'

Revathi thought about that all day.

That evening, after dinner, Appooppan drank a glass of water, swished it round his mouth, and went to sit on the verandah. Revathi followed him with the torch.

'Appooppan,' she said. 'Open wide, please.'

Appooppan smiled and opened his mouth. Revathi shone the torch on his back teeth. They had grooves too, big ones. And in the grooves there was rice from dinner.

'Appooppan, the water went over the top,' said Revathi. 'The rice is still in the grooves.'

Appooppan looked surprised. 'I have rinsed with water after every meal for seventy years,' he said.

'Water is good,' said Revathi, the way Amma said it. 'But it doesn't reach the grooves. You need bristles.'

Appooppan cleaned his teeth every morning with a neem twig. He chewed one end until it was soft and spread out, like a little brush. Revathi watched him do it the next morning.

'You only do the front,' she said. 'Do the back teeth too. On the top. That is where the food hides.'

So Appooppan reached the soft end of the twig right to the back of his mouth. He rubbed the tops of his back teeth slowly. Revathi held the torch, and he looked in the mirror. 'Well,' he said. 'You are right. The back was hiding things from me.'

From then on, every night, Revathi and Appooppan stood at the sink together. Revathi had her toothbrush. Appooppan had his own brush now, a soft one that Amma bought him from the shop. Revathi counted while they brushed: the top left, the top right, the bottom left, the bottom right. The front teeth, and the back teeth, and the grooves.

And every morning, before they brushed, they both ran their tongues over their teeth, and made a face. 'Fuzzy,' said Revathi. 'Fuzzy,' said Appooppan. The tiny beings had been busy all night.

'Again,' said Revathi, and handed him his brush.

**For the parent:** Revathi sees that water runs over the top of a tooth, but bristles reach into the grooves where food hides. The part of the mouth we cannot see easily is the part that needs the most care.

- Ask: Why did the water clean Revathi's front teeth but not the grooves of her new tooth?
- Ask: Why did Amma say the new back tooth has to last a whole life?
- Ask: Why do you think the teeth felt fuzzy again every morning?
- Ask: Have you found any big new back teeth in your mouth yet?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Biscuit Test: what does a rinse clean, and what does a brush clean? (20 min)
- **question:** After we eat, does a swish of water clean all our teeth? Or does some food stay behind, and where?
- **predict:** After you eat a biscuit, will a swish of water wash all the crumbs away? Where do you think crumbs might stay? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We look at the same teeth each time, with the same torch and mirror. We look three times: after eating, after rinsing, and after brushing. Only one thing changes each time: what we clean with.
- **record:** A tooth map: a big U shape for the teeth. Colour where crumbs were after rinsing in one colour, and after brushing in the other.
- **results table:** Where I looked | After eating: crumbs? | After a water rinse: crumbs? | After brushing: crumbs?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shine the torch into your child's mouth while they hold the mirror. 2. Find the front teeth and the back teeth together. 3. Draw a big U on the paper. Mark F at the front and B at each back end.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look inside your mouth. These are your front teeth. They are thin and smooth. These are your back teeth. Look at the tops. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Look in the mirror. Find your front teeth. Now find your back teeth. Look at their tops.
  - Ask: What are the tops of the back teeth like? (good: Bumpy.; They have little hills and lines.; They are wide, with grooves.)
  - Note: The tops of the back teeth have grooves. Children often have never looked at them. Let your child take their time.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess before they eat. Wait for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Soon you will eat a biscuit. Then you will swish water round your mouth. Will the water wash all the crumbs away? Where might crumbs stay? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will eat a biscuit, then swish water. Will the water clean every tooth? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Water washes things, so it will all go.; The crumbs might stick in the bumpy bits.; The back is hard to reach.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Do not give the answer. Many children guess that water will wash everything away.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child eats the biscuit and chews it well. 2. No drinking yet. 3. Shine the torch and look at the front teeth, the tops of the back teeth, and between the teeth.  
  *Parent says:* Eat the biscuit and chew it well. Don't drink anything yet. Now open wide. Where can you see crumbs?  
  *Child mode:* Eat the biscuit. Chew it well. Don't drink yet. Open wide. Where are the crumbs?
  - Ask: Where are the crumbs? (good: On the back teeth.; In the grooves.; Between my teeth.)
  - Note: You will usually see brown paste on the tops of the back teeth, and a little on the front teeth and between them.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child takes a big sip of water. 2. They swish it hard round the mouth while you count to ten. 3. They spit it into the sink. 4. Do it once more. 5. Look again with the torch. 6. Colour any crumbs on the tooth map in the first colour.  
  *Parent says:* Take a big sip. Swish it all round while I count to ten. Spit. Once more. Now open wide. What has gone, and what is still there?  
  *Child mode:* Take a big sip. Swish it round. Count to ten. Spit. Do it again. Look. What is still there?
  - Ask: What did the water clean? What did it leave? (good: The front teeth are clean.; There are still crumbs in the grooves of the back teeth.; Some crumbs are still between my teeth.)
  - Note: Crumbs usually stay in the grooves of the back teeth and between the teeth. If the water cleaned everything, try again another day with a stickier food, such as a piece of chikki or a date.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child brushes with their toothbrush and a little water, or a smear of toothpaste. 2. Remind them to put the bristles on top of the back teeth. 3. Brush gently for about one minute. 4. Look again. 5. Colour anything left in the second colour.  
  *Parent says:* Now brush. Put the bristles right on top of the back teeth. Brush gently, back and forth. Now let's look. What is left?  
  *Child mode:* Brush your teeth. Put the bristles on top of the back teeth. Brush gently. Now look. What is left?
  - Ask: What did the brush clean that the water did not? (good: The grooves on the back teeth.; The crumbs at the back.; Almost everything.)
  - Note: The grooves are usually clean now. A tiny bit may stay between two teeth. A grown-up can clean between teeth with dental floss, if you have some.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why. Then read the tooth map together.  
  *Parent says:* So what do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think the water left crumbs in the grooves, and the brush took them out?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find out? What did you see? Why did the brush work better at the back?
  - Ask: Why did the brush clean the grooves? (good: The bristles go down into the grooves.; Water just goes over the top.; The brush rubs, and water only washes.)
  - Check: After the water rinse, where were crumbs most likely to stay? → **In the grooves of the back teeth**
  - Think: Tiny living beings in your mouth eat leftover crumbs. Where in your mouth would they find the most food?
### e2 · The White Cloth Test: what does a bath wash off? (15 min)
- **question:** Does our skin carry dirt we cannot see? Does a bath take it away?
- **predict:** Your skin looks clean. If we wipe it with a white cloth before your bath, will the cloth get dirty? What about after the bath? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We wipe the same three places each time: the back of the neck, the inside of the elbow and the sole of one foot. We use the same kind of cloth, made just damp, and wipe each place three times. Each place gets a new square.
- **record:** Lay the six squares on paper in two rows: before and after. Draw them, or keep them dry in an envelope.
- **results table:** Place on my body | Before the bath: mark on the cloth? | After the bath: mark on the cloth?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Before the bath, ask your child to guess. Wait for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Your skin looks clean. If I wipe it with a white cloth, will the cloth get dirty? Where might it get most dirty? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Your skin looks clean. Will a white cloth get dirty on it? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: I played outside, so I might be dusty.; My feet walk on the floor.; My skin looks clean, so the cloth will stay white.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Do not tell your child the answer.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dip a cloth square in the water and squeeze it until just damp. 2. Wipe the back of the neck three times. 3. Put the square on the paper. 4. Do the same with a new square on the inside of the elbow, and on the sole of one foot.  
  *Parent says:* I am wiping the back of your neck. One, two, three. Now your elbow. Now the bottom of your foot. Let's put the cloths in a row.  
  *Child mode:* Wipe the back of your neck three times. Then your elbow. Then the bottom of your foot. Put the cloths in a row.
  - Note: The sole of a foot that has walked barefoot at home almost always leaves a grey mark. The neck often leaves a faint brown or grey mark, especially after playing outside or a sweaty day.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child has their bath as usual, with soap. 2. After drying, wipe the same three places with three new damp squares. 3. Put them in a second row under the first.  
  *Parent says:* Now you have had your bath. Let's wipe the same places again, with new cloths. Neck, elbow, foot.  
  *Child mode:* After your bath, wipe the same three places. Use new cloths. Put them in a second row.
  - Note: The bath itself does not count in the minutes. Do this right after drying, before the feet walk on the floor again.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the two rows together. Ask your child what they think, what they see, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the two rows. Which cloths are dirtier? What do you think the bath took away? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both rows. Which cloths are dirty? What did the bath take away?
  - Ask: What did the bath wash off? (good: Dust.; Sweat and dirt.; The grey from my feet.)
  - Check: Which row of cloths was more likely to be grey? → **The row from before the bath**
### e3 · The Morning Tongue Test: does the fuzz come back? (10 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Our teeth feel fuzzy in the morning. After we brush, does the fuzz stay away, or does it come back?
- **predict:** After you brush tonight, will your teeth feel fuzzy again tomorrow morning? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We feel the same teeth, the back teeth, with the tongue each time. We feel them at the same time: right after waking up, and right after brushing.
- **record:** Two rows of two boxes. Draw a wavy line in a box for fuzzy, and a smooth line for smooth.
- **results table:** When | Before brushing: smooth or fuzzy? | After brushing: smooth or fuzzy?
- **schedule:** day 1: The night before, guess. In the morning, feel the back teeth before brushing. Brush. Feel again. Colour the boxes. (6 min); day 2: Do the same again. Has the fuzz come back? (4 min)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The night before, ask your child to guess. Wait for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Tomorrow morning, before you brush, feel your back teeth with your tongue. Will they feel smooth or fuzzy? And after you brush? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Tomorrow morning, feel your teeth with your tongue. Smooth or fuzzy? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: I brushed tonight, so they will be smooth.; They always feel funny in the morning.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On the first morning, before brushing, your child runs their tongue over the back teeth. 2. They draw a wavy or smooth line. 3. They brush. 4. They feel again and draw again.  
  *Parent says:* Before you brush, run your tongue over your back teeth. Smooth or fuzzy? Now brush. Feel again. Smooth or fuzzy?  
  *Child mode:* Feel your back teeth with your tongue. Smooth or fuzzy? Draw it. Now brush. Feel again. Draw it.
  - Note: Most people feel a soft fuzz in the morning, mainly near the gums. After brushing, the teeth feel smooth and squeaky.
  - Wait: Do the next step tomorrow morning, before brushing.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On the second morning, feel the back teeth again before brushing. 2. Draw it. 3. Brush and feel again. 4. Talk about what you found.  
  *Parent says:* You brushed last night. Is the fuzz back this morning? What do you think the fuzz is? Why does it come back?  
  *Child mode:* Feel your back teeth again. Is the fuzz back? What do you think the fuzz is?
  - Ask: Why did the fuzz come back? (good: Something grew on my teeth at night.; The tiny living beings in my mouth made it again.; It is plaque, and it grows back.)
  - Check: What is the fuzz on your teeth in the morning? → **Plaque, a sticky layer made by tiny living beings**

### Clean hands, clean teeth (core, 10 questions)
1. (draw) Remember the biscuit test? Draw a back tooth. Draw where the crumbs stayed after you swished water.  
   **Answer:** A wide tooth with grooves on top, and crumbs drawn inside the grooves.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the grooves on top of the back tooth and puts the crumbs inside them, because that is where water did not reach.
2. (sort) Colour the times to wash your hands green. Colour the other times yellow.  
   **Answer:** before eating → Wash your hands; after the toilet → Wash your hands; after blowing your nose → Wash your hands; listening to a song → No wash needed; after playing outside → Wash your hands; looking at the sky → No wash needed. *Why:* We wash before eating, after the toilet, after blowing our nose and after playing, because those are the times germs get on our hands or into our mouths. Listening and looking do not make hands dirty.
3. (mcq) Revathi in the story had a new big tooth at the back. Where was the puttu hiding?  
   Options: In the grooves on top of the tooth / On the tip of her tongue / Behind her ear  
   **Answer:** In the grooves on top of the tooth. *Why:* The new tooth had little hills and valleys on top. Food hides in those valleys, which are called grooves.
4. (order) Put the hand wash in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Wet your hands', 'Rub soap on every part', 'Rinse off the soap', 'Dry on a clean towel']. *Why:* First wet the hands so the soap can foam. Then rub every part. Then rinse the soap away with the dirt in it. Last, dry.
5. (match) Draw a line from each thing to what it cleans.  
   **Answer:** Toothbrush → Teeth; Soap and water → Hands; Nail cutter → Short nails; A bucket bath → The whole body. *Why:* A toothbrush reaches the grooves of the teeth. Soap lifts dirt off hands. A nail cutter makes short nails, which leave less space for dirt. A bath washes sweat and dust off the whole body.
6. (truefalse) Skin that looks clean has no dirt on it at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the white cloth test, skin that looked clean still made the cloth grey before a bath. Dust and sweat can be too thin to see.
7. (label) Here are two hands. Put a tick on the parts people often forget to wash: the thumbs, the fingertips and the backs of the hands.  
   **Answer:** Ticks on both thumbs, the fingertips and the backs of both hands.. *Why:* Most people rub their palms well, but miss the thumbs, the fingertips and the backs. A strong answer ticks all three.
8. (write) Say it out loud, and a grown-up writes it: why do we wash our hands before we eat?  
   **Answer:** Because germs on our hands can go into our mouth with the food.. *Why:* A strong answer says that our hands carry germs, and that eating with our hands takes them to our mouth.
9. (mcq) Nila, a girl of 6 in Madurai, only brushes her front teeth. What is she missing?  
   Options: The back teeth, where food hides / Nothing, the front is enough / Her tongue only  
   **Answer:** The back teeth, where food hides. *Why:* Food hides most in the grooves of the back teeth. Holes in children's teeth are found most often there. If you picked 'the front is enough', remember the biscuit test.
10. (draw) Draw yourself having a bath. Draw what the water is washing off you.  
   **Answer:** A child pouring water or under a shower, with dust, sweat drops or tiny flakes washing off.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the water carrying something away: sweat, dust, or tiny flakes of old skin.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) When should you wash your hands with soap?  
   Options: Before eating and after the toilet / Only when they look dirty / Only once a day  
   **Answer:** Before eating and after the toilet. *Why:* Germs are too small to see, so hands can carry them even when they look clean. Before eating and after the toilet are the most important times.
2. (mcq) How long should you rub soap on your hands?  
   Options: About 20 seconds, like singing Happy Birthday twice / One second / Ten minutes  
   **Answer:** About 20 seconds, like singing Happy Birthday twice. *Why:* About 20 seconds gives enough time to rub every part. One second misses most of the hand, and ten minutes is far more than needed.
3. (mcq) In the biscuit test, where did the crumbs stay after the water rinse?  
   Options: In the grooves of the back teeth / On the front teeth / On the tongue  
   **Answer:** In the grooves of the back teeth. *Why:* Water runs over the top of the back teeth without reaching into the grooves. Only the bristles of a brush reach in.
4. (mcq) Which of these should only you use?  
   Options: Your toothbrush / The bucket / The tap  
   **Answer:** Your toothbrush. *Why:* A toothbrush goes in your mouth and carries the tiny beings from your mouth. So everyone has their own.
5. (mcq) How many times a day should you brush your teeth?  
   Options: Twice: morning and night / Once a week / Only after sweets  
   **Answer:** Twice: morning and night. *Why:* Plaque grows back all the time, so dentists say to brush twice a day, for about two minutes, morning and night.
6. (mcq) What do the tiny living beings in your mouth eat?  
   Options: Leftover food, especially sugar / Your teeth / Only toothpaste  
   **Answer:** Leftover food, especially sugar. *Why:* They eat leftover food on your teeth. As they eat sugar, they make an acid, and the acid can wear a hole in a tooth over time. They do not eat the tooth itself.
7. (mcq) Most of the tiny living beings on your skin and in your mouth are...  
   Options: Harmless or helpful / All dangerous / Not alive  
   **Answer:** Harmless or helpful. *Why:* Only a few kinds make us ill. Most live with us quietly, and some help keep harmful ones away.
8. (mcq) Why do we take a bath?  
   Options: To wash off sweat, dust and old skin / To make our skin grow longer / Because water is warm  
   **Answer:** To wash off sweat, dust and old skin. *Why:* All day, sweat, oil and dust build up on the skin, and old skin flakes off. A bath washes them away.
9. (mcq) In the white cloth test, the sole of a foot was wiped before a bath and after it. What did the cloths most often show?  
   Options: The cloth from before the bath was greyer / Both cloths stayed white / The cloth from after the bath was greyer  
   **Answer:** The cloth from before the bath was greyer. *Why:* Before the bath, dust and sweat were on the skin, even when we could not see them. The bath washed them off.
10. (mcq) Before testing, Bindu, a girl of 6, said 'I think the water will wash all the crumbs away.' What was that?  
   Options: A guess, to check with a test / The answer / A mistake  
   **Answer:** A guess, to check with a test. *Why:* A guess made before a test is how scientists start. Then the test shows what really happens. A guess that turns out wrong is still a good guess.

## Applications
- Doctors and nurses wash their hands between every patient, so they do not carry germs from one person to the next.
- Cooks in restaurants and in school kitchens wash their hands before touching food.
- Dentists check children's back teeth, and can paint a thin coat into the grooves to keep food out.
- Global Handwashing Day, on 15 October each year, reminds people across the world to wash with soap.

## Spotted around us
- A wash basin near the door of many homes and restaurants, with a bar of soap beside it.
- Neem and babool twigs for cleaning teeth, sold in bundles at some markets.
- Crows and pigeons splashing in puddles or water dishes on hot days, then fluffing their feathers.
- Temple elephants being given their bath in a river or a tank, in Kerala and Tamil Nadu.
- Signs in school and hospital toilets that say to wash your hands.

## Open questions
- **How do the bacteria in plaque decide when to build their sticky layer?** Bacteria send each other chemical signals, and act together when there are many of them. Scientists are still working out how this works in a real mouth, with many kinds living together.
- **Is a little contact with dirt and soil good for a child?** Some studies find that children who meet more of nature's microbes get some allergies less often. Scientists still argue about why, and which kinds of contact help.
- **What is it like to be one of the tiny beings who live in a mouth?** They sense food, acid and each other, and they act on what they sense. What that is like for them, nobody knows.

## Connected maths
- In the biscuit test, you looked at 4 back teeth. After a water rinse, 3 of them still had crumbs in the grooves. How many were clean? **1 back tooth was clean.**. 4 teeth take away the 3 with crumbs leaves 1. So most of the back teeth kept crumbs after the rinse. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L1`)
- Malini brushes her teeth 2 times every day. How many times does she brush in 3 days? **6 times.**. 2 and 2 and 2 make 6. Count in twos: 2, 4, 6. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- On the Clean Week chart, the morning brush row has 7 coloured boxes, and the night brush row has 5. Which row is harder to remember? How do you know? **The night brush row. It has fewer coloured boxes: 5 is less than 7.**. 7 is more than 5, so the morning brush was done more often. The row with fewer boxes is the one that was forgotten more. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- Your mouth has 4 parts: top left, top right, bottom left and bottom right. You brush each part for 30 counts. Which pattern shows the right order to go round the mouth, so no part is missed? **Any order that visits each of the 4 parts once, for example top left, top right, bottom right, bottom left.**. There are many right answers. A good one goes round in a circle, so you always know which part comes next and none is missed. (queues `math.position.position-words.L1`)
