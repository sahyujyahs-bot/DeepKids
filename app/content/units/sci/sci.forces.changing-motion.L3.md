# Forces change shape, speed and direction
`sci.forces.changing-motion.L3`

**Summary.** Press a ball of dough and it stays flat. Squeeze a sponge and it springs back. Press a biscuit and it breaks. A force is a push or a pull. It can change a thing's shape by squashing, stretching, twisting or bending it. Forces also start, stop, speed up, slow down and turn moving things. A bouncing ball is squashed for a moment, too fast to see. Even a steel table changes shape a tiny bit when you press it. Pushes and pulls also shape your bones, trees and the Himalayas.

**Intro.** *Press your thumb on a table. Did the table change shape? You can't see any change. But it did bend, a tiny bit. Scientists can show it by bouncing a laser beam off a mirror lying on the table.* Get a ball of chapati dough. Squash it, stretch it, twist it and bend it. Each push or pull changes its shape, and the new shape stays. Now squeeze a sponge and let go. It springs back. Now press a biscuit. It cracks. The same push did three different things. In this unit you find out why, and how even hard things change shape when they are pushed.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that a force can change a thing's shape, and show four ways: squashing, stretching, twisting and bending.
1. I can sort things by what they do after a force: keep the new shape, spring back, or break.
2. I can run a fair test, like dropping clay from different heights, measure the change, and show it in a table and a bar chart.
3. I can explain that a bend is a squash on one side and a stretch on the other, and that folds and tubes make things stronger.
4. I can say that forces also start, stop, speed up, slow down and turn moving things, and that a bounce changes both shape and direction.
5. I can give examples of forces changing living things and the Earth: bones, trees, the Himalayas and the spinning Earth.
6. I can explain that shapes change because atoms are pushed closer or pulled apart, and say what scientists still do not know.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces can start or stop things, speed them up, slow them down, turn them, or change their shape. *e.g.* You push dough flat with a rolling pin. You pull a rubber band longer.
- **Shape**: The outline of a thing, and how it fills space. Round, flat, long and curved are all shapes. *e.g.* A ball of dough is round. A rolled chapati is flat.
- **Squash**: To push on something so it gets flatter or smaller. Scientists also call this compressing. *e.g.* Sitting on a cushion squashes it. Stamping on a can squashes it.
- **Stretch**: To pull on something so it gets longer or thinner. *e.g.* Pulling a rubber band. Pulling chewing gum. Pulling a piece of dough into a long snake.
- **Twist**: To turn one end of something one way while the other end turns the other way, or stays still. *e.g.* Wringing water out of a wet towel. Twisting a snake of dough like a rope.
- **Bend**: To make something curve. A bend squashes one side and stretches the other side at the same time. *e.g.* A bamboo pole bends in the wind. A ruler bends when you press its middle.
- **Material**: What a thing is made of. *e.g.* Wood, steel, rubber, clay, glass and cotton are materials.
- **Elastic**: Springs back to its old shape when the force stops. *e.g.* A sponge, a rubber band and a tennis ball are elastic.
- **Plastic change**: When a thing keeps its new shape after the force stops. In science, 'plastic' means 'stays changed'. It is not the stuff bottles are made of. *e.g.* Clay, chapati dough and a bent paper clip keep their new shapes.
- **Brittle**: Breaks or cracks instead of bending much. *e.g.* A biscuit, a papad and glass are brittle.
- **Crack**: A thin split in a thing. Brittle things break when a crack runs all the way across. *e.g.* A cracked tile. A cracked eggshell.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then you know what made the difference. *e.g.* Drop the same clay ball from different heights. Change only the height.
- **Speed**: How fast something moves. Something with more speed goes further in the same time. *e.g.* A bowled cricket ball has more speed than a rolled marble.
- **Direction**: The way something is moving or facing, like left, right, up or down. *e.g.* A batsman sends the ball back the other way. That changes its direction.
- **Mass**: How much matter is in a thing. Matter is what all things are made of. The more mass a thing has, the harder it is to start, stop or turn. *e.g.* A full school bag has more mass than an empty one. It is harder to swing round, and harder to stop.
- **Bounce**: When a moving thing hits something, squashes for a moment, then springs back and moves away. *e.g.* A tennis ball hitting the floor squashes a little flatter for a moment, then jumps back up.
- **Fold**: A sharp bend pressed into a sheet. Folds can make a thin sheet much stiffer. *e.g.* A paper fan. A wavy tin roof sheet. The wavy middle layer of a cardboard box.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* Clay, steel, your bones and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Bond**: The hold between two atoms. It acts a little like a tiny spring. Push the atoms closer and it pushes back. Pull them apart and it pulls back. *e.g.* When you bend a steel spoon a little, you are pulling and pushing on more of these tiny holds than anyone could count.
- **Bone**: The hard, living parts inside your body that hold you up. Bones are alive, and they slowly change shape to match the pushes and pulls on them. *e.g.* The bones in a tennis player's playing arm grow thicker than in the other arm.
- **Tectonic plate**: A giant slab of the Earth's rocky outer layer. The plates move very slowly, about as fast as your fingernails grow. *e.g.* India sits on a plate that is slowly pushing into Asia. That push built the Himalayas.
- **Gravitational wave**: A ripple in space itself. As it passes, it stretches and squashes space by a tiny amount. *e.g.* In 2015, scientists measured a gravitational wave from two black holes crashing together far away.

## Explanations
### Forces can change shape · *defined*
**Definition:** A force can change a thing's shape. There are four main ways: squashing, stretching, twisting and bending.

When you push or pull something, it does not just move. It also changes shape, at least a little. Pushing inwards squashes it. Pulling outwards stretches it. Turning its two ends opposite ways twists it. Pushing on its middle while its ends are held bends it. Often one action does two at once. Rolling a chapati with a rolling pin squashes the dough and stretches it wider.
- e.g. Squash: pressing a ball of chapati dough flat.
- e.g. Stretch: pulling a rubber band, or chewing gum.
- e.g. Twist: wringing out a wet towel, or opening a bottle cap.
- e.g. Bend: a bamboo in the wind, or a ruler pressed in the middle.
### Keep it, spring back, or break · *defined*
**Definition:** After a force stops, some things keep their new shape. Scientists call this a plastic change. Some spring back to their old shape. These are elastic. Some crack and break. These are brittle.

Clay and dough stay squashed, so they are good for making pots and chapatis. Sponges, rubber bands and balls spring back. A biscuit, a papad or glass cracks instead. The same thing can do all three. Bend a steel paper clip a little and it springs back. Bend it more and it stays bent. Bend it back and forth many times and it snaps. Springs and rubber bands are explored more in the unit 'Stretching and springs'.
- e.g. A thumbprint stays in clay. That is a plastic change.
- e.g. A squeezed sponge springs back. It is elastic.
- e.g. A papad snaps. It is brittle.
### A bigger force makes a bigger change · *defined*
**Definition:** The bigger the force, the bigger the change of shape, until the thing breaks.

Drop a clay ball onto a plate from higher up, and it hits the plate faster. The plate has to push harder to stop it. That bigger push squashes it more. So the flat patch on the bottom gets wider. Engineers use this rule all the time. They push and pull on materials with machines, and measure exactly how much they change. The chart shows made-up results from the clay drop test in this unit.
- e.g. A gentle press makes a small dent in dough. A hard press makes a deep one.
- e.g. A light child sitting on a cushion squashes it a little. A heavy grown-up squashes it more.
### Inside a bend: squashed and stretched at once · *defined*
**Definition:** When something bends, the inside of the curve is squashed and the outside is stretched. The middle is hardly changed at all.

Draw lines across a long sponge, then bend it. On the inside of the curve, the lines crowd together. On the outside, they spread apart. Every bending thing works like this, from a ruler to a bridge. So a thing resists bending best when it has lots of material far from its middle. A ruler lying flat bends easily. Stand it on its thin edge and it hardly bends at all, though it is the same ruler. Steel beams in buildings are shaped like the letter I for the same reason.
- e.g. Press the middle of a ruler lying flat, and it bends easily. Stand it on its edge, and the same press hardly bends it.
- e.g. Steel beams in buildings and railway tracks have an I shape.
- e.g. Bamboo is a hollow tube, so it is very stiff for how light it is.
### Shape can make things strong · *defined*
**Definition:** Changing the shape of a material can make it much stronger, without adding any more material. Folds, tubes and arches are the main tricks.

A flat sheet of paper sags under one coin. Fold the same sheet like a fan, and it can hold many coins. Each fold is a little stiff wall standing on its edge. Rolled tubes are strong too. Galileo, the Italian scientist, wrote about this in 1638. He noticed that hollow bones and hollow reeds are strong for how light they are. An arch carries a push along its curve, down to its two feet. The chart shows made-up results from the paper bridge challenge in this unit.
- e.g. Wavy tin roof sheets are much stiffer than flat ones.
- e.g. The wavy layer inside cardboard boxes makes them strong.
- e.g. An eggshell is a dome, a curved shell. It is very hard to crush by squeezing its two ends.
### Twisting, and how twists make rope · *defined*
**Definition:** A twist turns one end of a thing one way, while the other end turns the other way or stays still.

When you wring a wet towel, the twist squeezes the cloth tighter and tighter, and the water is pushed out. Thread and rope are made with twists too. Cotton wool is made of short fibres, each only about 2 to 4 centimetres long. Pull a loose strand of cotton wool, and the fibres slide apart. Twist the same cotton into a wick, and the twist squeezes the fibres against each other. Friction, the grip between touching things, then stops them sliding. So the twisted wick is far stronger. Every cotton thread is spun this way, on a charkha or in a mill. In Kerala, coir rope has been twisted from coconut husk fibres for centuries. You can read more about grip in the unit 'Friction'.
- e.g. Wringing out clothes on washing day.
- e.g. A diya wick, twisted from cotton wool.
- e.g. Coir rope, twisted from coconut fibres.
### Forces change speed and direction · *defined*
**Definition:** A force can start a thing moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down, or turn it in a new direction.

Think of one cricket ball. The bowler's push starts it and speeds it up. The bat's push turns it the other way. The grass slows it down. A fielder's hands stop it. Every one of these changes needs a force. The more mass a thing has, the bigger the force needed to change its speed or direction by the same amount. That is why a full trolley is harder to start and stop than an empty one. If no force acts, a moving thing keeps going at the same speed in a straight line. On Earth, friction and air usually slow things down, so we hardly ever see that. When two forces on a thing are equal and opposite, they cancel out, and its movement does not change. You can read about that in the unit 'Pushes and pulls'.
- e.g. A carrom striker hits a coin. The coin starts moving, and the striker slows and turns.
- e.g. A kite string's tug turns the kite.
- e.g. Brakes slow a bicycle.
### A bounce is a squash · *defined*
**Definition:** When a ball bounces, it squashes against the floor, then springs back. The springing back pushes it off in a new direction.

A ball hits the floor going down. The floor pushes up on it and squashes it a little flatter for a tiny moment, too fast to see. Then the ball springs back to round. As it does, it pushes down on the floor, and the floor pushes it back up. So the force that changed its direction also changed its shape. Wet the bottom of a tennis ball and drop it on newspaper. The wet circle shows how flat it got. Drop it from higher, and it hits faster and gets squashed flatter. A clay ball does not bounce at all. It stays squashed, so nothing pushes it back up. The chart shows made-up results from the wet ball print test.
- e.g. A tennis racket and a tennis ball both squash when they meet.
- e.g. A cricket ball squashes a little against the bat.
- e.g. A ball of dough lands with a flat thud and stays put.
### Going round needs a pull inwards · *defined*
**Definition:** To move in a circle, a thing needs a force that keeps pulling it towards the middle. That force keeps changing its direction.

Put a ball in a sock, tie a string to it, and swing it in a circle beside you. Your hand keeps pulling it inwards through the string, so it keeps turning. Let go, and it flies off in a straight line. The Moon goes round the Earth in the same way. The Earth's gravity keeps pulling it inwards. Isaac Newton, an English scientist, explained this in 1687. Without that pull, the Moon would fly off in a straight line into space. You can read more in the units about gravity.
- e.g. A merry-go-round pulls you inwards through the bar you hold.
- e.g. A cyclist leans into a turn, so the road can push them round.
- e.g. In a spinning washing machine, the drum pushes the clothes inwards, so they go round. The water goes straight on, out through the holes.
### Deep down: atoms pushed together and pulled apart · *defined*
**Definition:** Everything is made of atoms held to each other by bonds. Bonds act a little like tiny springs. A shape changes when atoms are pushed closer or pulled further apart.

Press a steel spoon or a table, and its atoms are pushed slightly closer. Their bonds push back, so it springs back when you let go. That is elastic. A sponge and a rubber band spring back too, but in other ways. A sponge's thin walls bend and then straighten. A rubber band is made of long, tangled chains of atoms that straighten out when pulled, then curl up again. In metals, whole rows of atoms can slide past each other into new places, and stay there. In clay and dough, tiny grains slide past each other in the same way, held together by water. That is a plastic change. In 1934, three scientists explained how rows of atoms in metals slip, one small step at a time. They were Geoffrey Taylor, Egon Orowan and Michael Polanyi, working separately. In brittle things like glass, the atoms cannot slide. So the push gathers at the tip of a tiny scratch, and a crack races across. In 1921, an English engineer, Alan Griffith, showed that tiny scratches are what make glass weak. That is why a glass-cutter scratches a line first, then snaps the glass along it.
- e.g. A glass shop scratches a line on a sheet of glass, then snaps it cleanly along the line.
- e.g. A gold bangle can be hammered thinner because rows of gold atoms slide without breaking.
### What is glass, really? · *debated*
Glass is hard like a solid. But its atoms are jumbled, like a liquid frozen in the middle of moving. You may hear that old church windows are thicker at the bottom because glass slowly flows downwards. Scientists tested this. It is not true. The old windows were made unevenly, and builders often put the thick edge at the bottom. A study in 1998 by Edgar Zanotto, a Brazilian scientist, worked out that glass at room temperature would take far longer than the age of the universe to sag. But what glass really is remains a puzzle. In 1995, a famous American physicist, Philip Anderson, called it perhaps the deepest unsolved problem about solids.
- e.g. Old Roman glass bowls, about 2,000 years old, have not sagged at all.
- e.g. A window pane at home is the same shape it was when it was made.
- **Many scientists:** Glass is a solid. Its atoms are jumbled, but they are stuck in place and do not flow.
- **Other scientists:** Glass is best thought of as a liquid that has slowed down so much it cannot flow in any time we can measure.
- **Still others:** Glass is its own kind of matter, neither a normal solid nor a liquid. Exactly why it goes stiff as it cools is still being studied.
### Living things grow to match the forces on them · *defined*
**Definition:** Bones, muscles and plants are alive. They slowly change shape and grow stronger where they are pushed and pulled, and weaker where they are not.

In 1892, a German doctor, Julius Wolff, wrote that bones reshape themselves to match the forces on them. The bones in a tennis player's playing arm grow thicker. Astronauts floating in space hardly press on their bones. So they can lose about 1 to 2 out of every 100 parts of their hip bones each month, and must exercise every day. Your spine squashes too. During the day, the soft pads between its bones are pressed down. So you are about 1 centimetre shorter at bedtime than when you wake up. Trees that sway in the wind grow thicker, sturdier trunks. In 1973, an American plant scientist, Mark Jaffe, gave this a name: thigmomorphogenesis. It means 'shape made by touch'. Trees tied too tightly to stakes grow weaker trunks.
- e.g. Children who run and jump grow stronger bones.
- e.g. Trees on windy hilltops are short and thick.
- e.g. You are a little taller in the morning than at night.
### Forces shape the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** The giant slabs of Earth's outer layer, called tectonic plates, push on each other. Over millions of years, these pushes fold, crack and lift rock into mountains.

The plate that India sits on moves north by about 4 to 5 centimetres a year. That is about as fast as fingernails grow. About 50 million years ago, it began to crash into Asia. The rock crumpled up like a rug pushed against a wall. That is how the Himalayas formed, and they are still rising by a few millimetres a year. Rock also bends slowly, until it suddenly snaps. That snap is an earthquake. The spinning Earth changes shape too. Its spin makes it bulge at the middle. It is about 43 kilometres wider across the equator than from pole to pole. Even the solid ground under you rises and falls each day, by up to about 30 centimetres, as the Moon and Sun pull on it.
- e.g. Fossil sea shells are found high in the Himalayas. The rocks they lie in were once under the sea.
- e.g. You can see bent and folded rock layers in road cuttings in hilly places.
### Forces shape moons, stars and space itself · *defined*
**Definition:** Gravity can squeeze, stretch and heat whole worlds. Very strong gravity can even stretch and squash space itself.

Io is one of Jupiter's moons. Jupiter's huge gravity stretches and squeezes Io as it goes round. All that squeezing heats Io's insides. So Io has more active volcanoes than any other place we know. Some dead stars are squashed so hard that their atoms are crushed together. These are called neutron stars. A teaspoon of one would weigh about as much as a small mountain. Near a black hole, gravity can stretch a star apart. Astronomers have seen flashes of light from stars being torn up like this. Space itself can be stretched and squashed too. In 2015, the LIGO detectors in America measured a ripple in space from two black holes crashing together. As it passed, it stretched and squashed their 4-kilometre-long arms by an amount hundreds of times smaller than a proton. A proton is one of the particles in the centre of an atom.
- e.g. Io's volcanoes throw material hundreds of kilometres up into space.
- e.g. LIGO is now joined by detectors in Italy and Japan. One is planned in India, called LIGO-India.
### The spaghetti puzzle · *defined*
**Definition:** Bend a stick of dry spaghetti until it snaps, and it nearly always breaks into three or more pieces, not two.

The famous physicist Richard Feynman once spent an evening trying to snap spaghetti into just two pieces. He could not. In 2005, two French scientists, Basile Audoly and Sébastien Neukirch, explained why. When the first break happens, the two halves spring back. That sends a wave of extra bending racing along each half, and it snaps them again. In 2018, a team at MIT in America found a trick. Twist the spaghetti about three-quarters of a turn or more first, then bend it slowly. Then it can snap into just two pieces. So a twist changes how a bend breaks.
- e.g. Snap dry spaghetti, a thin dry stick or dry noodles. Count the pieces.
- e.g. Then try twisting it about three-quarters of a turn before bending, and see what happens.

## Mostly agreed
- Forces can change the shape of things, by squashing, stretching, twisting or bending. *How we know:* Anyone can see it with dough, sponges and rubber bands. Engineers measure it in every material, with machines that push and pull.
- Materials answer forces in different ways. Some keep the new shape, some spring back, and some break. *How we know:* Clay, sponge and glass all react differently to the same push. Engineers choose materials for this reason, like rubber for tyres and steel for bridges.
- Everything changes shape a little when pushed, even steel and stone. *How we know:* Tall buildings sway in the wind. Bridges sag a little when trucks cross. Scientists can show a table bending under a gentle press by bouncing a laser beam off a mirror lying on it.
- A force is needed to change a thing's speed or direction. *How we know:* Isaac Newton wrote this down in 1687. Every spacecraft is steered using his rules, and they arrive where they are meant to.
- The spinning Earth bulges at the equator. *How we know:* Measurements from satellites show the Earth is about 43 kilometres wider across the equator than from pole to pole.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, and later Isaac Newton, an English scientist, Early 1600s to 1687.** For nearly 2,000 years, many people followed the ideas of Aristotle, a Greek thinker. He taught that a moving thing needs a push all the time to keep moving. Stop pushing a cart, and it stops. So stopping seemed natural, and only moving seemed to need a force. In Italy, in the early 1600s, Galileo Galilei rolled balls down sloping wooden tracks. He saw that a ball rolling down one slope rolls up a second slope to nearly the same height. When he made the second slope less steep, the ball rolled further to reach that height. So Galileo asked: what if the second track were flat? The ball would never reach its starting height, so it would keep rolling. He said a rolling ball slows down only because of friction and the air. He wrote this in a book in 1638. In 1687, Isaac Newton made it his first law of motion. A moving thing keeps going at the same speed in a straight line, unless a force acts on it. So a force is needed to change how a thing moves, not to keep it moving.  
*What people believed before:* A moving thing needs a push all the time to keep moving. Stop pushing, and it stops by itself.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Hard things like steel and stone don't change shape when you push them.** (Most of us think this without noticing.; *rejected*)  
  For: We cannot see a steel table or a stone wall change shape when we press it.  
  Against: They do change, just too little to see. A laser beam bounced off a mirror on a table moves when someone presses the table. Bridges sag when trucks cross them. The Himalayas are bent rock.
- **Old church windows are thicker at the bottom because glass slowly flows downwards.** (Many books and tour guides; *rejected*)  
  For: Some very old windows really are thicker at the bottom. And glass atoms are jumbled like a liquid's.  
  Against: Some old windows are thicker at the top or the side. They were made unevenly, and builders often put the thick edge at the bottom. Roman glass 2,000 years old has not sagged. In 1998, Edgar Zanotto worked out that glass at room temperature would take far longer than the age of the universe to flow.
- **If something isn't moving, no forces are acting on it.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: If nothing moves, it seems as if nothing is happening.  
  Against: A book on a table is pulled down by gravity and pushed up by the table, all the time. The forces are equal and opposite, so they cancel. Push on a wall and it doesn't move, but you can feel the force. And the wall changes shape a tiny bit.
- **To make something stronger, you must make it thicker or use more material.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: A thick stick is harder to break than a thin one.  
  Against: Shape matters as much as amount. One sheet of paper folded like a fan holds many coins. Galileo pointed out in 1638 that hollow bones and reeds are strong for how light they are. Bamboo, bicycle frames and steel I-beams use the same trick.
- **Mountains are fixed and never change shape.** (An everyday idea. Mountains look as if they have always been there.; *rejected*)  
  For: In a whole human lifetime, a mountain looks exactly the same.  
  Against: The Himalayas are still rising by a few millimetres a year, as India pushes into Asia. Fossil sea shells high in the mountains show the rock was once under the sea. Earthquakes are rock snapping after bending.
- **The science of how materials bend and stretch was built only by men, like Robert Hooke and Augustin Cauchy.** (Many older history books; *debated*)  
  For: Hooke, Navier, Cauchy and others did write much of the theory, and most famous names in the story are men.  
  Against: Sophie Germain, a French woman, taught herself mathematics in secret. She first wrote to scientists under a man's name, 'Monsieur LeBlanc'. In 1816 she won a top prize from the Paris Academy of Sciences, for maths about how bent and shaken metal plates vibrate. At first, as a woman, she was not allowed to attend the Academy's meetings. Her work had gaps, which others later filled, so historians still debate how much credit she should get.
- **Bones are dead and hard, like chalk.** (A common idea, because skeletons look dry and stony; *rejected*)  
  For: Bones are hard, and old skeletons in museums are dead.  
  Against: Inside you, bones are alive. They have blood and cells that build and remove bone. They grow thicker where they are pushed most. Astronauts in space lose bone because it is not pushed. A broken bone heals itself.

## Roots
We have been learning how forces squash, stretch, bend and turn things. Now we ask what this tells us about hard things, and about touching.

**Deep truth.** A ball dropped on wet paper leaves a print much bigger than its resting dot. It squashed flat on the floor, then sprang back up. The floor squashed too, far too little to see. So every bounce changes the shape of both things, even a stone floor.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort things into soft ones that change shape and hard ones that don't. But every material gives a little when pushed. Hard and soft differ only in how much they give.  
*Evidence:* In the wet ball prints test, the bounce left a wet circle much bigger than the resting dot, so the ball squashed to push off the floor. A laser dot bounced off a mirror on a table moves when you press the table, so the table bends too.

- *who am i:* Your bones grow stronger where they are pushed every day. Your body is shaped by the forces on it.
- *what is life:* Trees that sway in the wind grow thicker trunks. Living things respond to pushes and pulls by growing stronger where they feel them.
- *what is this world:* Mountains fold and the spinning Earth bulges. Even the hardest rock changes shape when it is pushed.

When you press a table, your thumb squashes a little, and so does the table. The push comes from both of them changing shape together. It is always shared: the table pushes your thumb exactly as hard as your thumb pushes the table. So nothing can touch without being touched back, and changed a little. So things are less separate than they look. Each thing is partly shaped by everything that presses on it.

- **Tao Te Ching 76:** A living plant is soft and bendy. A dead one is dry and snaps. So the stiff and hard break, and the soft and bending last. (In this unit, a bamboo bends in the wind and springs back, but a dry stick or a papad snaps. Things that can give a little under a force often last longer than things that cannot.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment DK B91:** You cannot step into the same river twice, because new water is always flowing past. (A steel table, a mountain and your bones all change shape under every push, mostly too slowly or too little to see. Heraclitus saw change even in things that look fixed.)
- Science (settled): A thing changes shape because its atoms are pushed closer together or pulled further apart.
- Science (settled): When two things push on each other, the two pushes are always equal and opposite. Isaac Newton wrote this down in 1687 as his third law of motion.
- Other view, Many physicists: A thumb and a table push on each other, but they stay two separate things. Squashing and pushing back are simply what atoms do. Calling it a sign that things are less separate adds nothing the physics needs.
- Practice, Feel a shared push (3 min): Hold your palms together in front of you, gently. Breathe in slowly. As you breathe out, press your palms a little harder. Feel each palm squash a little. Now breathe in and let go. Feel them spring back. Which hand was doing the pushing? Do it three times, slowly.
- Ask: When you press the table and it presses you back, which of you is doing the pushing?
- Ask: Can you touch anything without changing it, even a tiny bit?
- Ask: Is anything so hard that it never gives at all?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Does a steel table change shape when a fly lands on it? (hint: A bigger force makes a bigger change. So does a very tiny force make a very tiny change, or none at all?)
- *deep:* Why does a sponge spring back but dough stays squashed? What is different inside them? (hint: Look closely at a sponge. It is full of holes with thin walls. What happens to those walls when you squeeze? What happens inside dough when it moves?)
- *big:* Is it better for a thing to bend or to be stiff? (hint: A bamboo bends in a storm and does not snap. A bridge must stay stiff, so it does not sag under trucks. Which jobs need which?)
- *deep:* Your bones grow stronger where they are pushed. How does a bone sense where it is being pushed? (hint: Bone cells can sense being squeezed and stretched. Scientists are still finding out exactly how they decide where to build more bone.)
- *big:* If you protected something from every push and pull, would it stay strong? (hint: What happens to astronauts' bones in space? What happens to a tree tied tightly to a stake?)
- *deep:* When a ball bounces, where does its movement go while it is squashed flat for a moment? (hint: A squashed sponge pushes back on your hand. Could the squashed ball be holding its movement for a moment, then giving it back?)
- *big:* The Himalayas are still rising. Will they keep getting taller for ever? (hint: Rain, ice and rivers are wearing them down at the same time. Which do you think will win?)
- *deep:* Is anything in the universe completely rigid, so that it never changes shape at all? (hint: Even space itself stretches when a gravitational wave passes. Can you think of anything that can't change?)
- *big:* A potter, a goldsmith, a baker and a blacksmith all change shapes with force. Why do we call some of this 'art' and some 'work'? (hint: Who decides? Is rolling a perfect round chapati an art?)
- *big:* Some sculptors say a statue is already hidden inside the stone. Is a shape something we make, or something we find? (hint: When you squash dough, is the new shape new, or was it always possible?)
- *deep:* Why does dry spaghetti almost never break into just two pieces? (hint: When it first snaps, both halves spring back fast. What might that sudden spring do?)

## Story: The Bridge That Was Allowed to Sway
*The living root bridges of Meghalaya are real. Khasi and Jaintia people grow them from the roots of rubber fig trees, sometimes guiding the roots through hollow betel-nut trunks, and some have been used for well over a hundred years. Iba, Bah Kyrmen and the young bridge are made up. Trees that are swayed by wind really do grow thicker, sturdier stems. Whether root bridges grow thicker exactly where they are walked on has not been carefully measured. In the story it is a guess based on how trees behave.*

In the hills of Meghalaya, in the north-east of India, it rains more than almost anywhere else on Earth. Below the town of Sohra, a stream runs through a deep green gorge. Across it hangs a bridge that nobody built with a hammer. It was grown. Its floor and its rails are the living roots of a rubber fig tree, as thick as a man's leg, knotted together and furred with moss. People have walked across it for longer than anyone can remember.

Iba was nine, and she was in a hurry. Upstream, her uncle, Bah Kyrmen, was growing a new bridge. He had laid a hollow betel-nut trunk across the water like a long wooden gutter. Inside it, young roots from a fig tree on the bank were creeping across. They were pale and bendy, like cooked noodles.

'When will it be ready?' Iba asked.

'When you are as old as I am,' said Bah Kyrmen. 'Maybe older.'

Iba could not wait that long, so she decided to help. The young roots swung about in the wind. Where the gutter ended, they drooped and wandered. They looked untidy, like hair that had not been combed. So one morning Iba cut strips of cane and tied each root tightly to a straight bamboo pole. She tied them so well that they could not wobble at all.

'There,' she said. 'Now you will grow straight and strong.'

Her uncle looked at the tied roots for a long time. He did not untie them. 'Let us see,' he said.

On the far side, a few roots had escaped her. They hung loose over the water. Every gust pushed them about.

Then the monsoon came. Rain blew sideways off the hills. The stream roared brown and white. Every day after school, Iba ran up the path to check her bridge. The loose roots were thrashed and bent and tugged by the wind. Iba felt sorry for them. She tied her own roots even tighter, so the storm could not touch them.

The rains ended. The cold season came, bright and dry. One morning Iba took a piece of thread to measure her roots. She wrapped it round a tied root and pinched the thread where the ends met. Then she did the same with one of the wild roots on the far side. She held the two threads side by side.

The thread from the wild root was nearly twice as long. The tied roots were still pale and thin, as thin as the day she had tied them. The wild roots had grown thick and brown, with tough, wrinkled skin like her uncle's wrists.

'That's not fair,' Iba said. Her voice wobbled. 'I looked after mine.'

Bah Kyrmen did not laugh. He took her hand and walked her down the slippery steps to the old bridge. 'Put your hand here,' he said. He pressed her palm onto the roots in the middle, where the floor sagged lowest. That was where every foot had stepped for a hundred years. The roots there were the thickest of all. They had grown into each other until she could not tell where one ended and the next began.

'This is where it is pushed the most,' he said. 'Every time someone walks across, the roots bend a little and stretch a little. And they answer. They grow.' He tapped her tied roots on the way back up. 'A root that is never pushed has no reason to grow.'

Iba thought about the bamboo behind her house. In the storms they bent nearly to the ground, then sprang back. She thought about the dry stick she had snapped for the fire, which could not bend at all. She thought about her own legs. They used to ache on the steep steps to school. Now they ran up without stopping.

That evening, she cut the cane. The roots sprang free and swayed a little in the breeze, as if they were stretching after a long sleep. It felt strange to let them go, like letting go of a little brother's hand at the edge of a road.

The next day she did something else. She wove a tiny basket and hung it from the young roots, with one smooth river stone inside. The roots dipped under it, and swung. 'Something to carry,' she told them. Every week she added one more stone. Every week the roots bent down a little further. And every week, when she measured with her thread, they were a little thicker.

Bah Kyrmen watched her. 'Now,' he said, 'you are building.'

Years went by. The roots crossed the stream and wrapped round a tree on the other side. Bah Kyrmen laid flat stones between them for a floor. His hair turned white. Iba grew tall.

When Iba was nineteen, the young bridge was finally strong enough to hold a child. She was standing on the bank, wondering who should be the first to cross, when a small boy from the next village ran straight past her. He did not ask anyone. He ran out onto the roots.

The bridge dipped under his feet. It swayed, and creaked, and sprang back. The boy laughed out loud.

Iba opened her mouth to shout at him. Then she closed it again. She watched the roots bend under his weight and stretch, and come back. Under her breath, she said one word to them. 'Grow.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: every push and pull changes the shape of what it touches, even a little. The wind bends the roots, and a stone in a basket stretches them. Living things respond to those changes by growing stronger where they are pushed and pulled. Iba's tight ties protected the roots from every force, so they had no reason to grow, and stayed thin. Trees staked too tightly, and astronauts' bones in space, behave the same way. The bamboo that bends and the stick that snaps also hint at the difference between elastic and brittle. The story quietly asks about care that protects, and care that lets go.

- Ask: In the story, Iba tied the young roots so they could not move. Why did they stay thin?
- Ask: The wild roots were thrashed by the storm. Why did they grow thicker?
- Ask: Where were the roots thickest on the old bridge? Why there?
- Ask: Why did Iba hang a basket of stones from the roots?
- Ask: Iba nearly shouted at the boy, but didn't. Why do you think she stopped?
- Ask: Iba's legs stopped aching on the steep steps. Which part of your body got stronger because you used it a lot?

### e1 · The Clay Drop Test (25 min)

## Worksheets
### Shape Changers (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A force is…  
   Options: A push or a pull / Only something that makes things move fast / A kind of material / Something only machines make  
   **Answer:** A push or a pull. *Why:* A force is a push or a pull. Picking 'only makes things move fast' usually means forces are linked only with movement. But forces also squash, stretch, twist and bend things.
2. (mcq) You press a ball of dough flat with your hand. Which shape change is this?  
   Options: A squash / A stretch / A twist / A bend  
   **Answer:** A squash. *Why:* Pressing inwards on something makes it flatter. That is a squash. Picking 'stretch' is close, because the dough also spreads wider. But your push is inwards, so it is mainly a squash.
3. (mcq) You wring water out of a wet towel. Which shape change is this?  
   Options: A squash / A twist / A bend / No change  
   **Answer:** A twist. *Why:* Your two hands turn opposite ways. That is a twist. Picking 'squash' makes sense, because the twist does squeeze the water out. But the action itself is twisting.
4. (fill) When you pull a rubber band, it gets longer. This is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['stretch']. *Why:* Pulling outwards on something makes it longer. That is a stretch.
5. (sort) Sort these. After the force stops, does each one stay changed or spring back?  
   **Answer:** clay pressed with a thumb → Stays changed; a squeezed sponge → Springs back; a rolled chapati → Stays changed; a stretched rubber band → Springs back; a squashed drinks can → Stays changed; a pressed cushion → Springs back. *Why:* Clay, dough and cans keep their new shapes. Sponges, rubber bands and cushions are elastic, so they spring back.
6. (mcq) A papad snaps when you bend it. A word for things that break instead of bending is…  
   Options: Elastic / Brittle / Plastic / Stretchy  
   **Answer:** Brittle. *Why:* Brittle things crack and break instead of bending. Picking 'plastic' often comes from thinking of plastic bottles. In science, plastic means 'stays changed'.
7. (truefalse) In science, a 'plastic' change means the thing keeps its new shape after the force stops.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Scientists use 'plastic' to mean 'stays changed', like clay. It is not about the stuff bottles are made of.
8. (mcq) In the clay drop test, you drop the same clay ball from 10 cm and from 80 cm. Which drop makes the wider flat patch?  
   Options: 10 cm / 80 cm / Both the same / Neither, clay doesn't squash  
   **Answer:** 80 cm. *Why:* From higher up, the ball hits the plate faster. The plate pushes back harder, so it squashes the clay more. Picking 'both the same' means thinking the same clay must always squash the same amount. But from higher up, the push from the plate is bigger.
9. (mcq) In the clay drop test, what must stay the same to make it a fair test?  
   Options: The size of the ball, the clay and the plate / Nothing, everything can change / Only the height  
   **Answer:** The size of the ball, the clay and the plate. *Why:* In a fair test you change only one thing, here the height. Everything else stays the same. Picking 'only the height' mixes up what we change with what we keep the same.
10. (mcq) Forces can change how things move. Which of these is NOT something a force can do?  
   Options: Start a ball rolling / Turn a ball in a new direction / Make a ball stop / Make a ball move with no push or pull at all  
   **Answer:** Make a ball move with no push or pull at all. *Why:* Every change in movement needs a push or a pull. Picking 'turn a ball' means thinking a ball can turn by itself. It can't: turning needs a push from the side.
11. (mcq) A batsman hits a cricket ball back the way it came. What did the force from the bat change?  
   Options: Only the ball's colour / The ball's direction and speed / Nothing / Only the ball's shape  
   **Answer:** The ball's direction and speed. *Why:* The bat turned the ball round and usually sent it off faster. It also squashed the ball a tiny bit. Picking 'only shape' notices the squash but misses the change in movement.
12. (truefalse) A bouncing ball is squashed for a moment when it hits the floor.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The floor pushes up and squashes the ball a little flatter for a tiny moment. Then the ball springs back to round, pushing on the floor, and the floor pushes it back up. The wet ball prints test shows this.
13. (match) Match each job to the shape change it uses most.  
   **Answer:** Rolling a chapati → squash; Pulling a hair band onto your hair → stretch; Opening a bottle cap → twist; A diving board when you stand on its end → bend. *Why:* Rolling presses the dough flat. A hair band is pulled longer. A cap is turned. A diving board curves down.
14. (write) Name three workers who change the shape of materials with forces. Say what each one does.  
   **Answer:** For example: a potter squashes and pulls clay into a pot. A goldsmith hammers gold into a bangle. A baker kneads and stretches dough.. *Why:* Any three correct workers, each with the force they use, are right.
15. (draw) Draw a ball of dough before and after you squash it. Draw an arrow to show your push.  
   **Answer:** A round ball, then a flat round shape. The arrow points down into the dough.. *Why:* A squash is a push inwards, so the arrow points into the dough.
16. (write) Why does a sponge spring back but clay stays squashed? Give your best idea.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful idea. A good one: a sponge is full of holes with thin, springy walls. They bend when you squeeze, then straighten. In clay, tiny pieces slide to new places and stay there.. *Why:* Elastic things spring back into shape. In a plastic change, the tiny pieces inside move to new places and stay.
### Bends, Bounces and Fair Tests (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at the chart from the clay drop test. What pattern does it show?  
   Options: The higher the drop, the wider the flat patch / The higher the drop, the smaller the flat patch / Height makes no difference  
   **Answer:** The higher the drop, the wider the flat patch. *Why:* The bars get taller as the height goes up. So a higher drop squashes the clay more. Picking 'smaller' often means the bars were read the wrong way round. Taller bars mean wider patches.

   **Answer:** They crowd closer together. *Why:* The inside of a bend is squashed, so the lines crowd together. The outside is stretched, so they spread apart. Picking 'spread apart' mixes up the inside with the outside.
4. (mcq) You press the middle of a plastic ruler lying flat, and it bends easily. Stand the same ruler on its thin edge, and the same press hardly bends it. Why?

   **Answer:** On its edge, more of the ruler is far from the middle of the bend. *Why:* On its edge, the top and bottom of the ruler are far apart. 5. (mcq) Which sheet of paper can hold the most coins over a gap between two books?  
   Options: A flat sheet / The same sheet folded like a fan / A crumpled sheet / A torn sheet  
   **Answer:** The same sheet folded like a fan. *Why:* Each fold is a little stiff wall standing on its edge. So the fan is far stiffer. Picking 'flat' often means more paper touching the coins seems better. But a flat sheet bends easily.

Tennis balls and bamboo spring back. Glass and dry biscuits are brittle.

   Options: The same thing can be elastic for small forces and plastic for big ones / Paper clips are broken / Paper clips are only elastic / The second bend used less force  
   **Answer:** The same thing can be elastic for small forces and plastic for big ones. *Why:* Most materials spring back from small forces. A big enough force makes them stay changed, or break. Picking 'only elastic' misses what happened on the second bend.

   Options: Most of the steel is at the top and bottom, where a bend squashes and stretches most / It is cheaper to paint / It looks like the letter I for 'iron' / It makes them heavier  
   **Answer:** Most of the steel is at the top and bottom, where a bend squashes and stretches most. *Why:* A bend squashes the top and stretches the bottom most. So steel placed there resists bending best, without wasting steel in the middle. Picking 'iron' is a fun guess, but the shape is about bending, not spelling.
9. (mcq) Why is cotton wool twisted into a wick stronger than the same cotton pulled into a loose strand?  
   Options: The twist squeezes the fibres together, so friction stops them sliding apart / Twisting makes each fibre longer / Twisting adds glue / It isn't stronger  
   **Answer:** The twist squeezes the fibres together, so friction stops them sliding apart. *Why:* Twisting presses the short fibres against each other. Friction, the grip between touching things, holds them together, so they share the pull. Picking 'longer fibres' is a sensible guess, but each fibre stays the same length.
10. (mcq) A ball is swung round on a string. The string breaks. Which way does the ball go?  
   Options: Straight on, in the direction it was moving / It keeps going round in a circle / Straight back to the middle / Straight down  
   **Answer:** Straight on, in the direction it was moving. *Why:* With no string pulling it inwards, nothing turns it any more. So it flies off in a straight line. Picking 'keeps going round' is a very common idea. But going round needs a pull inwards all the time.
11. (mcq) A clay ball and a rubber ball are dropped from the same height. Only the rubber ball bounces. Why?  
   Options: The rubber ball springs back after squashing, but the clay stays squashed / The clay is heavier / Clay is not affected by forces / The rubber ball was dropped harder  
   **Answer:** The rubber ball springs back after squashing, but the clay stays squashed. *Why:* Both squash when they hit. The rubber ball springs back and pushes itself up. The clay stays squashed, so nothing pushes it back up. Picking 'clay is heavier' misses that weight is not the main difference here.
12. (truefalse) A steel table changes shape a tiny bit when you press on it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It is far too little to see. But a laser beam bounced off a mirror on the table moves when you press. That shows the table bends.
13. (order) Put these in order, from EASIEST to change the shape of by hand, to HARDEST: a stone, a rubber eraser, a steel spoon, dough.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Dough squashes with a light press. A rubber eraser needs a firm squeeze. A steel spoon needs a strong push to bend. A stone hardly changes at all by hand.
14. (write) You want to make a fair test of which drop height squashes clay most. Write what you will change, what you will measure, and what you will keep the same.  
   **Answer:** I will change the drop height. I will measure the width of the flat patch. I will keep the same size ball, the same clay, the same plate, and I will drop it, not throw it.. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same.
15. (forces) A ball of dough is being squashed between your two hands. Show the forces on it, with one arrow for each.  
   Options: left hand pushes / right hand pushes / gravity  
   **Answer:** left hand pushes right, right hand pushes left, gravity down. *Why:* Each hand pushes inwards, towards the dough. Two pushes towards each other make a squash. Gravity also pulls the dough down.
16. (draw) Design a sandal sole that can bend when you walk but never snaps. Label which parts squash and which parts stretch when it bends.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful design. The top of the bend is squashed and the bottom is stretched, or the other way round when it bends the other way. Soft rubber, or grooves across the sole, help it bend.. *Why:* A bend squashes one side and stretches the other.
### From Atoms to Mountains (challenge) (challenge, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Deep down, why does a steel ruler spring back after a small bend?  
   Options: Its atoms are pushed closer on one side and pulled apart on the other, and the bonds between them pull them back / Air inside it pushes it back / Gravity pulls it back / Bending makes its atoms bigger  
   **Answer:** Its atoms are pushed closer on one side and pulled apart on the other, and the bonds between them pull them back. *Why:* Bonds between atoms act a little like tiny springs. Squash them or stretch them, and they pull the atoms back into place. Picking 'air inside' is a sensible guess for a sponge, but a steel ruler has no air holes. Bending does not make atoms bigger. It moves them closer or further apart.
2. (mcq) A gold bangle can be hammered into a new shape without breaking. A glass bangle cannot. Why?  
   Options: In gold, rows of atoms can slide past each other. In glass they cannot, so cracks spread / Gold is softer than everything / Glass is not made of atoms / Gold is heavier  
   **Answer:** In gold, rows of atoms can slide past each other. In glass they cannot, so cracks spread. *Why:* In metals, rows of atoms slip into new places one step at a time. In glass the atoms are jumbled and stuck, so a push gathers at tiny scratches and a crack races across. Picking 'gold is heavier' links strength with weight, but that is not the reason.
3. (mcq) Why does a glass shop scratch a line on glass before snapping it?  
   Options: The scratch is where a crack starts, so it breaks along the line / The scratch makes the glass softer / The scratch lets air in / It is only for decoration  
   **Answer:** The scratch is where a crack starts, so it breaks along the line. *Why:* Alan Griffith showed in 1921 that tiny scratches make glass weak, because the push gathers at their tips. A scratched line makes the crack go exactly where the glass-cutter wants. Picking 'softer' mixes up weak with soft.
4. (truefalse) Old church windows are thicker at the bottom because glass slowly flows down over hundreds of years.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The windows were made unevenly, and builders often put the thick edge at the bottom. Roman glass 2,000 years old has not sagged. Glass at room temperature would take far longer than the age of the universe to flow.
5. (mcq) Astronauts living in space for months lose some of their bone. Why?  
   Options: Floating, they hardly push on their bones, so the bones do not need to stay strong / Space air is poisonous to bones / Their bones melt in the cold / They eat too much  
   **Answer:** Floating, they hardly push on their bones, so the bones do not need to stay strong. *Why:* Bones reshape to match the forces on them. With almost no pushing, the body removes bone it doesn't seem to need. That is why astronauts exercise for about two hours a day. Picking 'cold' is a guess about space, but the space station is kept warm.
6. (mcq) You are measured as soon as you wake up, and again at bedtime. What will you find?  
   Options: I am about 1 cm shorter at bedtime / I am about 1 cm taller at bedtime / Exactly the same / About 10 cm shorter at bedtime  
   **Answer:** I am about 1 cm shorter at bedtime. *Why:* During the day, the soft pads between the bones of your spine are squashed by your weight. At night, lying down, they spring back. Picking '10 cm' is a good guess about the direction, but far too big.
7. (mcq) A tree tied tightly to a stake so they can never sway often grows a weaker trunk. Why?  
   Options: Trees grow thicker where wind bends them, and tight ties stop the bending / Stakes are poisonous / The tie stops water reaching it / It grows faster without wind  
   **Answer:** Trees grow thicker where wind bends them, and tight ties stop the bending. *Why:* Trees respond to bending by growing thicker, sturdier wood. Mark Jaffe named this in 1973: thigmomorphogenesis, 'shape made by touch'. Picking 'stops water' is a sensible worry, but water moves inside the trunk, not past the tie.
8. (mcq) Fossil sea shells are found high in the Himalayas. What does this tell us?  
   Options: The rock was once under the sea, and was pushed up by enormous forces / Birds carried the shells there / The sea was once as high as the mountains / Someone put them there  
   **Answer:** The rock was once under the sea, and was pushed up by enormous forces. *Why:* The plate India sits on pushed into Asia and crumpled the old sea floor up into mountains. Picking 'the sea was once that high' is a natural guess. But it was the land that rose, not the sea.
9. (fill) The plate that India sits on moves north by about 5 cm a year. In 20 years it moves about ____ cm.  
   **Answer:** ['100']. *Why:* 20 lots of 5 cm is 100 cm. That is 1 metre. 5 × 20 = 100.
10. (mcq) The spinning Earth is about 43 km wider across the equator than from pole to pole. What causes the bulge?  
   Options: Its spin makes the middle bulge out a little / The Moon pulls the equator / The equator is hotter, so it swells / Mountains at the equator  
   **Answer:** Its spin makes the middle bulge out a little. *Why:* As the Earth spins, the rock at the equator moves fastest, and it swings outwards a little. So the middle bulges out and the poles are a little flatter. Picking 'hotter' is a clever guess, since heat does swell things. But it is the spin.
11. (mcq) Jupiter's moon Io has more active volcanoes than anywhere else we know. Why?  
   Options: Jupiter's gravity squeezes and stretches it, and that heats its insides / It is very close to the Sun / It is made of fire / It is hit by lots of rocks  
   **Answer:** Jupiter's gravity squeezes and stretches it, and that heats its insides. *Why:* Squeezing and stretching something over and over warms it up, like bending a paper clip back and forth. Picking 'close to the Sun' is a natural guess, but Io is much further from the Sun than Earth is.
12. (mcq) In 2015, the LIGO detectors measured space itself being stretched and squashed. What caused it?  
   Options: Two black holes crashing together far away / A truck driving past / An earthquake / The Moon  
   **Answer:** Two black holes crashing together far away. *Why:* The crash sent a ripple through space called a gravitational wave. Its stretch was hundreds of times smaller than a proton. Picking 'a truck' or 'an earthquake' is sensible, because LIGO must ignore those shakes. Two detectors far apart helped scientists tell the difference.
13. (write) Dry spaghetti almost always snaps into three or more pieces. Scientists explained why in 2005. Try to explain it in your own words.  
   **Answer:** When it first snaps, the two halves spring back fast. That sends a wave of extra bending along each half, which snaps them again.. *Why:* The first break releases a sudden spring-back, which bends the pieces past breaking.
14. (write) Is it better to bend or to be stiff? Give one example where bending is better and one where being stiff is better.  
   **Answer:** For example: a bamboo that bends survives a storm, and a bendy ruler doesn't snap. But a bridge or a table should be stiff, so it doesn't sag when we use it.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Good answers give a reason for each side.
15. (write) Iba tied the young roots tightly so the storm couldn't touch them. They stayed thin. Was she wrong to protect them? Explain what you think.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer. For example: she wanted to help, but the roots needed to be pushed and pulled to grow strong. Protecting something from every force can keep it weak.. *Why:* This links the science to life. Living things grow stronger where they are pushed and pulled.
16. (draw) Draw what you think happens to the atoms inside a steel ruler when you bend it a little, and when you let go.  
   **Answer:** Atoms joined by little springs. While bent, they are closer together on the inside of the curve and further apart on the outside. When let go, they go back to their old spacing.. *Why:* Bonds between atoms act like tiny springs. Squashed or stretched, they pull back.
17. (write) Sophie Germain had to use a man's name to share her maths. Why do you think that was? Is it fair?  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer. For example: in her time, many people thought women should not do science, so she hid her name to be taken seriously. It was not fair.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Good answers show understanding of her situation.

## Applications
- Potters, bakers, goldsmiths, blacksmiths and sculptors all change shapes with forces.
- Car crumple zones squash on purpose in a crash, so the people inside are pushed more gently.
- Bicycle and cricket helmets have foam inside that squashes to protect the head.
- Steel I-beams, bamboo scaffolding and bicycle frames use shapes that resist bending.
- Wavy tin roof sheets and cardboard boxes are stiff because of their folds.
- Ropes, coir mats and woven cloth are strong because of twisting and friction between threads.
- Springs in mattresses, sofas, pens and weighing scales squash and spring back.
- Tyres, balls and shoe soles squash and spring back with every bounce and step.
- Glass-cutters scratch a line first, so the glass breaks exactly where they want.
- Doctors and physiotherapists use exercise to make bones and muscles stronger.
- Gardeners tie young trees loosely, so they can sway and grow strong trunks.
- Engineers test bridges and buildings to see how much they bend before they are built.
- Earthquake scientists measure how rocks slowly bend, to learn where they might snap.
- LIGO and other detectors measure space itself being stretched by gravitational waves.
- Sugarcane juice machines and oil presses squash and squeeze out juice and oil.

## Open questions
- **What is glass, really? Is it a solid, a very slow liquid, or something else?** Its atoms are jumbled like a liquid's but stuck like a solid's. Scientists still argue about why it goes stiff as it cools.
- **Can we predict exactly when and where something will break?** Cracks start at tiny flaws that are hard to find, and they spread in complicated ways. Engineers add big safety margins because of this.
- **Can we predict earthquakes?** Rock bends slowly for years, then snaps. Scientists can say where earthquakes are likely, but not the day they will happen.
- **How exactly do living cells feel being squashed and stretched?** In 2010, Ardem Patapoutian's team found tiny gates in cells, called Piezo channels, that open when the cell is stretched. He shared a Nobel Prize in 2021. How bones use this to decide where to grow is still being worked out.
- **How squashed can matter get? What is inside a neutron star?** Neutron stars are squashed harder than anything we can make on Earth. Scientists don't agree on exactly what their centres are made of.
- **Could we make materials that heal themselves, like bone does?** Scientists have made some self-healing plastics and concretes, but none come close to how well bone repairs itself.
- **Do living root bridges grow thicker exactly where people walk?** Trees grow thicker where they are bent. But nobody has carefully measured this on the root bridges of Meghalaya yet.
- **Could gravitational waves tell us what happened just after the universe began?** Scientists hope ripples in space from the very early universe might still be passing through us. Nobody has detected ripples from that early time yet.

## Connected maths
- In the clay drop test, the flat patch was 1 cm wide from 10 cm, 2 cm from 20 cm, 3 cm from 40 cm and 4 cm from 80 cm. Look at the chart. How much wider was the patch from 80 cm than from 10 cm? **3 cm wider.**. The patch from 80 cm was 4 cm wide. The patch from 10 cm was 1 cm wide. 4 take away 1 is 3. So it was 3 cm wider. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- You measured your height at bedtime: 129 cm. The next morning you were 130 cm. How much taller were you in the morning? **1 cm taller.**. 130 take away 129 is 1. So you were 1 centimetre taller in the morning. That is about the width of your little fingernail. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- In the paper bridge challenge, a flat sheet held 1 coin. A folded fan held 25 coins. Each coin weighs about 4 grams. How much more weight did the fan hold than the flat sheet? **96 grams more.**. The fan held 25 coins and the flat sheet held 1. 25 take away 1 is 24 more coins. Each coin is 4 grams. 24 × 4 = 96. So the fan held 96 grams more. Check: 20 × 4 = 80, and 4 × 4 = 16. 80 + 16 = 96. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- The plate India sits on moves north about 5 cm every year. How far does it move in 20 years? Give your answer in centimetres and in metres. **100 cm, which is 1 metre.**. Each year adds 5 cm. 20 lots of 5 cm is 5 × 20 = 100 cm. 100 cm make 1 metre. The real speed is about 4 to 5 cm a year. We used 5 to make the sum easier. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- You twist a lined paper strip. You turn one end a quarter turn, then another quarter turn, then another. How much have you turned it altogether? **Three quarters of a full turn.**. Each quarter turn is one quarter of a full turn. 1 quarter + 1 quarter + 1 quarter = 3 quarters. One more quarter turn would make a full turn, all the way round. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L3`)

## Warm-up
- Name the four ways a force can change shape. → Squash, stretch, twist and bend.
- Wringing a wet towel: squash, stretch, twist or bend? → Twist.
- Does a sponge stay squashed or spring back? → It springs back. It is elastic.
- In science, what does 'plastic' mean? → Keeps its new shape after the force stops, like clay.
- A biscuit that snaps instead of bending is called… → Brittle.
- Does a higher drop squash clay more or less? → More. It hits faster, so the push is bigger.
- When something bends, which side is squashed? → The inside of the curve.
- Name a force that changes a ball's direction. → A bat, a kick from the side, or the floor in a bounce.
- Are you taller in the morning or at night? → In the morning, by about 1 cm.
- What pushed the Himalayas up? → The plate India sits on, pushing into Asia.
- Is the Earth a perfect ball? → No. Its spin makes it about 43 km wider across the middle.
