# Sorting animals into groups: testing rules against the hard cases
`sci.classification.sorting-living-things.L4`

**Summary.** A bat flies like a crow, but her relatives are cats and cows. A dolphin swims like a shark, but she breathes air and feeds her baby milk. So how do scientists decide which animals belong together? They test every rule against the hard cases, such as the egg-laying platypus, the flightless penguin and the legless snake, and keep only the rules that do not break. This unit sorts animals with and without a backbone, then follows the family tree, where even the word fish hides a surprise.

**Intro.** *A bat, a crow and a cat. Which two belong together? Most people say the bat and the crow, because both fly. Look closer. The bat has fur, and her baby drinks her milk, just like a kitten. The crow has feathers and lays eggs.* Sorting by flying is a good sort if you are watching the evening sky. But the bat's fur and milk are clues to something else, which is her family. In this unit you test sorting rules on the hardest cases animals can offer, and you find rules that never break.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can sort animals into those with a backbone and those without, and say which half is far bigger.
1. I can name the five groups of animals with a backbone and give two clues that decide each group.
2. I can test a sorting rule against hard cases, and count the animals it leaves out and the animals it lets in.
3. I can explain why a dolphin is a mammal and not a fish, using clues about breathing and milk.
4. I can explain that the same bones in different shapes, such as a bat's wing and a human arm, show that animals share ancestors.
5. I can sort animals with no backbone into their big groups, using one clear question for each group.
6. I can explain why the word fish does not describe one family, and why scientists change their groups when new evidence comes.

## Terms
- **Vertebrate**: An animal with a backbone, which is a row of small bones or hard cartilage down the middle of the back. *e.g.* A rohu, a gecko, a crow and a cow are all vertebrates.
- **Invertebrate**: An animal with no backbone. About 19 of every 20 kinds of animal are invertebrates. *e.g.* An ant, a snail and an earthworm have no backbone.
- **Mammal**: An animal that feeds her babies milk from her own body, and has some hair at some time in her life. *e.g.* A cow, a bat and a dolphin are mammals.
- **Bird**: An animal with feathers, a beak and no teeth, who lays eggs with hard shells. *e.g.* A crow, a penguin and an ostrich are birds.
- **Reptile**: An animal with dry skin covered in scales or plates, who breathes with lungs all her life. *e.g.* A gecko, a snake, a crocodile and a sea turtle are reptiles.
- **Amphibian**: An animal with thin, moist skin, who lays soft eggs without a shell and starts life in water or in damp places. *e.g.* A frog and a caecilian are amphibians.
- **Fish**: An animal who lives in water, breathes with gills all her life and swims with fins. *e.g.* A rohu, a shark and a seahorse are fish.
- **Gills**: Thin folds in a fish's body that take oxygen out of the water. *e.g.* A rohu's gills sit under the flap on each side of her head.
- **Lungs**: Soft bags inside the body that take oxygen out of the air. *e.g.* A dolphin comes up to the surface because her lungs need air.
- **Rule**: A question about a feature that anyone can check, used to put animals into groups. *e.g.* 'Does she have feathers?' is a rule for birds.
- **Hard case**: An animal who tests a rule by breaking it, or by almost breaking it. *e.g.* A penguin is a hard case for the rule 'birds fly'.
- **Metamorphosis**: A big change of body shape during a life, such as a tadpole becoming a frog. *e.g.* A caterpillar goes through metamorphosis to become a butterfly.
- **Convergent evolution**: When animals who are not close relatives end up with a similar shape, because they solve the same problem. *e.g.* A shark and a dolphin both have a streamlined body for fast swimming.
- **Homologous**: Describes body parts built from the same bones in a shared ancestor, even when they do different jobs. *e.g.* A bat's wing and your arm are homologous.
- **Arthropod**: An animal with jointed legs and a hard skeleton on the outside of the body. *e.g.* An ant, a spider and a crab are arthropods.
- **Mollusc**: An animal with a soft body and one muscular foot, who often has a shell. *e.g.* A snail, a mussel and an octopus are molluscs.
- **Monotreme**: A mammal who lays eggs. Only the platypus and four kinds of echidna are monotremes. *e.g.* The platypus lays eggs and feeds her young milk.
- **Ancestor**: An animal in the past from whom later animals came. *e.g.* Fish, frogs, crows and cows all share a very old ancestor with a backbone.

## Explanations
### What makes a sorting rule good · *defined*
**Definition:** A good rule puts every animal of a group inside it and keeps every animal of other groups outside it. A hard case is an animal who tests a rule.

Take the rule 'animals that fly are birds'. A crow passes it. A bat breaks it, because the bat flies but is not a bird. A penguin breaks it too, because the penguin is a bird but cannot fly. So a rule can fail in two ways. It can leave out an animal that belongs, or it can let in an animal that does not. Scientists look for rules that fail in neither way. They often find that one clue is not enough, and that two clues together do the job.
- e.g. 'Animals that live in water are fish' lets in a dolphin, who is not a fish.
- e.g. 'Animals that fly are birds' lets in a bat and leaves out a penguin.
- e.g. 'Mammals have hair and feed milk' fits a platypus, a bat and a dolphin, so it holds up.
### Backbone or no backbone · *defined*
**Definition:** A vertebrate is an animal with a backbone, a row of small bones or hard cartilage down the middle of the back. An invertebrate is an animal with no backbone.

About 19 of every 20 named kinds of animal have no backbone. Insects alone are more than a million kinds, and beetles are about 400,000 of them. All the animals with a backbone, from sharks to crows to you, add up to roughly 75,000 kinds. A shark's backbone is made of cartilage, the bendy material in your nose and ears, and it still counts as a backbone. The counts change every year, as scientists name new kinds. So the split is real, but the half that includes us is the small half.
- e.g. A rohu, a crow and a cow each have a backbone.
- e.g. An ant, a snail, an earthworm and a jellyfish have none.
- e.g. Beetles alone have about five times as many kinds as all the animals with a backbone.
### The five groups of animals with a backbone · *defined*
**Definition:** The five groups are fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. Each group is decided by a few clues together, never by one clue alone.

Fish breathe with gills all their lives and swim with fins. Amphibians have thin, moist skin, lay soft eggs without a shell, and start life in water or damp places. Reptiles have dry skin with scales or plates, and breathe with lungs all their lives. Birds have feathers and lay hard-shelled eggs. Mammals feed their babies milk and have some hair at some time in their lives. Notice what is missing from this list. Where an animal lives, whether she flies, how many legs she has and whether she is born live are not in it, because each of those has hard cases.
- e.g. A gecko has dry scales and lungs, so she is a reptile.
- e.g. A frog has moist skin and jelly eggs, so she is an amphibian.
- e.g. A rohu has gills and fins, so she is a fish.
### Hard cases: animals that break the simple rules · *defined*
**Definition:** A hard case is an animal that breaks a simple rule. When a simple rule breaks, scientists do not throw away the group. They find a better rule.

A platypus lays eggs but feeds her young milk, so she is a mammal. A dolphin lives in water and has a fish-like shape, but she breathes air with lungs and feeds milk, so she is a mammal. A penguin cannot fly but has feathers and hard-shelled eggs, so he is a bird. A snake has no legs but has dry scales and lungs, so she is a reptile. A caecilian looks like a worm, but she has a backbone and moist skin, so she is an amphibian. A seahorse looks nothing like a fish, but she has gills and fins, so she is a fish. A climbing perch breathes air and wriggles over land, but she still has gills and fins, so she is a fish. A Russell's viper gives birth to live young, but she has dry scales and lungs, so she is a reptile.
- e.g. A bat flies, but she has fur and milk, so she is a mammal.
- e.g. A sea turtle lives in the sea, but she breathes air and lays eggs on the beach, so she is a reptile.
- e.g. An ostrich cannot fly, but she has feathers and lays hard-shelled eggs, so she is a bird.
### One animal, two shapes · *defined*
**Definition:** Metamorphosis is a big change of body shape during a life. Sorting uses the whole life of an animal, not one stage.

A tadpole has gills, a tail and no legs, and she lives in water. If you met only a tadpole, you might sort her with the fish. But she is a young frog. Her gills shrink, her lungs grow, her legs appear and her tail is taken back into her body. A caterpillar becomes a butterfly in the same way, and both are invertebrates. Some frogs, such as many bush frogs of the Western Ghats, skip the tadpole stage. Their eggs hatch into tiny frogs. So a good rule looks at an animal's whole life, and does not trust one stage.
- e.g. A tadpole breathes with gills like a fish, and a frog breathes with lungs and skin.
- e.g. A caterpillar has chewing mouthparts, and a butterfly has a long tube for drinking nectar.
- e.g. A bush frog's egg hatches straight into a tiny frog.
### Same job, same shape: why a dolphin looks like a shark · *defined*
**Definition:** Convergent evolution is when animals who are not close relatives end up with a similar shape, because they solve the same problem.

A shark, a dolphin and a penguin all have a smooth, pointed body and a fin or flipper on each side. Swimming fast through water pushes every swimmer towards that shape. Over a very long time, each one's young changed a little in each generation, and the shape that cut through water best was the one that kept going. The shark is a fish, the dolphin is a mammal and the penguin is a bird, so they sit on three different branches of the family tree. A bat's wing and a crow's wing are another pair. Both are for flying, but each kind of wing was built separately, from skin stretched over long fingers in the bat and from feathers in the crow. So scientists look past the shape, to clues that come from ancestors, such as milk, hair and the bones inside.
- e.g. A shark, a dolphin and a penguin have similar shapes but different breathing and babies.
- e.g. The wings of a bat, a crow and a butterfly all fly, but have three different builds.
- e.g. A snake, a caecilian and an earthworm are long and legless, yet sit in three different groups.
### Same bones, different jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Body parts built from the same bones in a shared ancestor are called homologous, even when they do different jobs.

Feel your upper arm. It holds one long bone. Turn your forearm and feel two bones side by side. Then come your wrist bones and your finger bones. A bat's wing has the same plan: one bone, two bones, wrist and very long finger bones with skin stretched between them. A dolphin's flipper has one bone, two bones, wrist and finger bones inside a smooth paddle. A crow's wing has the same plan, with fewer finger bones. A horse's leg is the same plan, standing on one long finger. These animals do not need the same bones to do their jobs, because a different design could work. The best explanation is that they inherited the plan from one four-limbed ancestor.
- e.g. Your arm, a bat's wing and a dolphin's flipper all have one upper bone and two lower bones.
- e.g. A horse runs on the tip of one long finger bone.
- e.g. A crow's wing has fewer finger bones, but the same plan.
### Groups inside groups: the backbone family tree · *defined*
**Definition:** The groups nest. Each inner group has all the clues of the group around it, and adds one new clue.

All animals with a backbone are vertebrates. Inside that group, frogs, reptiles, birds and mammals descend from animals with four limbs. A snake lost her legs later and a whale's legs became flippers, but their ancestors had four limbs. Inside that group, reptiles, birds and mammals descend from animals whose egg had a waterproof skin around the baby, so the egg could develop on dry land. Inside that group, only mammals feed milk and have hair. Fish sit at the outer edge, because they have the backbone but not the four limbs. The tree shows who is more closely related than who.
- e.g. A frog is inside the four-limbs room but not the sealed-egg room.
- e.g. A crow is inside the sealed-egg room, and so are a gecko and a cat.
- e.g. A dolphin is in the hair-and-milk room because her ancestors walked on land.
### Birds are living dinosaurs · *debated*
In 1861 a fossil called Archaeopteryx was found in Germany. She had feathers and wings like a bird, and teeth and a long bony tail like a small dinosaur. Since 1996, fossils from China have shown many small dinosaurs with feathers. Most scientists agree that birds are the living descendants of small, feathered dinosaurs. When an asteroid hit the Earth about 66 million years ago, the bird branch survived. The closest living relatives of birds are crocodiles. What scientists still argue about is the name. Should birds still be a group of their own next to the reptiles, or are they a branch inside the reptiles?
- e.g. A crow's scaly legs look like a lizard's skin.
- e.g. A crocodile builds a nest and guards her eggs, like a bird.
- e.g. A crow and a crocodile are closer relatives than a crocodile and a gecko.
- **A family-tree biologist:** A group should hold an ancestor and all of her descendants. Birds descend from dinosaurs, which are reptiles, so birds are a branch inside the reptiles.
- **A traditional teacher of the five groups:** Birds changed so much, with feathers, wings and a high body heat, that they deserve a group of their own. The five groups are easy to teach and easy to use.
- **A bird watcher:** On a morning walk, the five groups are what you need to know. The family-tree question is a different one, and both are useful.
### Is fish a real group? · *debated*
Fish are animals who live in water, breathe with gills and swim with fins. But there are three very different kinds: sharks with a skeleton of cartilage, bony fish such as the rohu with fins held by thin rays, and lobe-finned fish such as the lungfish, whose fins have thick bones and muscle. Lungfish have lungs too. In 2021 scientists read the genes of an Australian lungfish, and found that lungfish are the closest living relatives of animals who walk on land. In 2004 a team found Tiktaalik, a fossil about 375 million years old, with fins that held wrist bones and a neck. So a lungfish is a closer relative of a cow than of a rohu. If fish meant all the descendants of the first fish, it would have to include frogs, lizards, crows, cows and people.
- e.g. A lungfish breathes air and has fleshy fins with bones.
- e.g. Tiktaalik had a flat head, a neck and fins with wrist bones.
- e.g. A rohu and a shark are less closely related than a lungfish and a cow.
- **A family-tree biologist:** Fish is not one family, because the lungfish is closer to a cow than to a rohu. The word names a way of life in water.
- **A fisher or an aquarium keeper:** Fish is a very useful word. It tells you how to catch, keep and cook an animal who lives in water and breathes with gills.
- **A teacher of the five groups:** Fish is a good first group for children, as long as they learn that the group has hard cases and a surprising history.
### Animals with no backbone: six big groups · *defined*
**Definition:** Invertebrate is not one family. It only says what the animals lack. Scientists sort them into many separate groups.

Arthropods have jointed legs and a hard skeleton outside the body. Insects have six legs, spiders have eight and crabs have ten or more. Molluscs have a soft body and one muscular foot, and many have a shell. Annelids have soft bodies made of rings and no legs, such as the earthworm. Cnidarians have stinging tentacles around a mouth, such as jellyfish and corals. Echinoderms have spiny skin, tiny tube feet and arms in fives, such as the starfish. Sponges have bodies full of holes and stay in one place. A sponge has no brain or nerves, and some sponges can still slowly shrink when disturbed. An octopus has no backbone, but she solves puzzles and opens jars.
- e.g. Ask 'Does she have jointed legs?' and an ant, a spider and a crab answer yes.
- e.g. Ask 'Is her skin spiny, with arms in fives?' and a starfish answers yes.
- e.g. Ask 'Is her soft body in rings with no legs?' and an earthworm answers yes.
### How scientists check the groups today · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists check animal groups with three kinds of evidence: bones, fossils and DNA. Where all three agree, the group is trusted.

Bones show shared plans, like the arm bones. Fossils show animals in between, such as Tiktaalik. DNA lets scientists compare the genes of any two animals, even ones that look nothing alike. Mostly the three agree. Sometimes DNA gives a surprise. The closest living relatives of whales and dolphins are hippos. Fossils fit this. Early whales lived in the rivers and shores of what is now India and Pakistan about 50 million years ago, and the ones found in Pakistan had four legs. A fossil jaw called Himalayacetus, found in Himachal Pradesh and described in 1998 by Sunil Bajpai and Philip Gingerich, is one of the oldest early-whale fossils known.
- e.g. Bones show that your arm and a bat's wing share a plan.
- e.g. Fossils from Pakistan show early whales with four legs.
- e.g. DNA shows that a hippo is a closer relative of a dolphin than a cow is.

## Mostly agreed
- Animals with a backbone are sorted into five groups: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. *How we know:* Bones, eggs, breathing and DNA put almost every animal with a backbone clearly into one of the five, though a few hard cases need two or three clues.
- About 19 of every 20 named kinds of animal have no backbone. *How we know:* Scientists have named about 1.5 million kinds of animals and about 75,000 of them have a backbone. Insects alone are more than a million.
- Birds are the living descendants of small feathered dinosaurs. *How we know:* Fossils of Archaeopteryx and of many feathered dinosaurs from China show feathers, wishbones and nesting in animals that were not birds yet.
- A mammal is an animal who feeds her babies milk, and egg laying does not exclude her. *How we know:* The platypus and the echidnas lay eggs and feed milk, and William Caldwell showed in 1884 that they lay eggs.
- Whales and dolphins descend from four-legged land mammals. *How we know:* Fossils from India and Pakistan show early whales with legs, and DNA shows the hippo as the closest living relative.
- All animals with a backbone share one very old ancestor. *How we know:* They share the same body plan, the same genes for building the backbone and the same cell types, and fossils link the groups.

## How we found out
**George Shaw, William Caldwell, and the Aboriginal people who had always known, 1799 (the first skin described) and 1884 (the eggs shown).** In 1799 a dried skin of a strange animal reached Britain from New South Wales. It had fur, a flat bill like a duck and webbed feet. The scientist George Shaw wondered whether someone had sewn the parts together as a trick. It was real, and it was a platypus. The next puzzle was her eggs. Aboriginal people had long said the platypus lays eggs. Most European scientists did not believe it, because they thought every animal with fur and milk must give birth to live young. So for decades the question stayed open. In 1884 a young Scottish scientist, William Caldwell, hired many Aboriginal people to help him find platypuses and echidnas in Queensland. In late August he found a platypus egg. He sent a short telegram to a scientific meeting in Montreal. It said that these animals lay eggs. Scientists then changed the rule. A mammal is an animal that feeds milk. Live birth was not needed.  
*What people believed before:* That an animal with fur and milk must give birth to live young, so an animal that lays eggs could not be a mammal. Caldwell found the eggs in 1884, and scientists changed the rule to milk.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Fish are one natural family.** (School books and many people; *debated*)  
  For: Fish all live in water, breathe with gills and have fins, so they look like one group.  
  Against: A lungfish is a closer relative of a cow than of a rohu, so the word fish covers several branches of the tree and leaves out the land animals that came from one of them.
- **Birds are a separate group from reptiles.** (The traditional five-group system; *debated*)  
  For: Birds have feathers, wings, a high body heat and a very different life from lizards.  
  Against: Birds descend from dinosaurs, which are reptiles, and crocodiles are the closest living relatives of birds. A group of reptiles that leaves out birds leaves out some of the descendants.
- **Every mammal gives birth to live young.** (Many textbooks until the 1880s; *rejected*)  
  For: Almost every mammal does give birth to live young, including all cats, cows and people.  
  Against: The platypus and four kinds of echidna lay eggs. They have fur and feed milk, so they are mammals. The rule is milk, not live birth.
- **Animals that live in the sea and swim are fish.** (Many sailors and fishers through history; *rejected*)  
  For: Whales and dolphins swim, have flippers and a tail, and live in the sea.  
  Against: Aristotle wrote more than 2,300 years ago that dolphins breathe air and feed milk. Whales and dolphins are mammals.
- **Animals with no backbone are less clever, and feel less, than animals with a backbone.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Most are small and do not have a big brain.  
  Against: Octopuses solve puzzles, and a veined octopus was seen carrying coconut shells to use as a shelter. Bees can learn to count to four. Many scientists now say animals with no backbone feel and decide too.

## Roots
We have tested sorting rules against animals that break them, and followed the backbone family tree. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and about the world.

**Deep truth.** A lungfish is a closer relative of a cow than of a salmon. The fish branch that crawled onto land became frogs, lizards, birds, mammals and us. So the word fish names a way of life in water, not one family. By family, we are inside that branch.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture the five groups as five separate boxes, and fish as the box farthest from us. But boxes made from looks and ways of life can cut one family in two, and the family tree has no gap between fish and us.  
*Evidence:* In the rule test, 'lives in water' put a dolphin with the fish. A lungfish has lungs and fins with bones. Her genes are closer to land animals than to a salmon. And a 375-million-year-old fossil called Tiktaalik had fins with wrist bones.

- *who am i:* You are a mammal, and by family you are also part of the fish branch that crawled onto land. Your arm has the same bones as a bat's wing and a dolphin's flipper.
- *what is life:* Every animal with a backbone came from one very old ancestor. Fish, frogs, lizards, crows and cows are all her descendants, so they are cousins.
- *what is this world:* Most animals in the world have no backbone, 19 of every 20 kinds. The world is mostly theirs, and we are a small branch of the animals.

More than 500 million years ago, the first small swimmers with a backbone lived in the sea. Their young changed a little in each generation, and the branches split again and again. One branch grew jaws, one grew fleshy fins, and one crawled onto land. Later, eggs with a waterproof skin let animals breed away from water, and then feathers and milk appeared. When we sort by clues that come from ancestors, we are finding where the branches join. All animals with a backbone are one family, and the five groups are five of its branches.

- **Tattvartha Sutra 5.21, by Umaswati, a Jain text:** Living beings support one another. (The family tree shows that every animal with a backbone came from the same ancestor, and every animal depends on others for food, air and shelter.)
- **Karaniya Metta Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.8, verses 146 and 147, a Buddhist text:** May all beings be happy, whether weak or strong, large or small, seen or unseen, near or far, born or yet to be born. (This text sorts all beings by size and distance, and then gives the same wish to every one. A scientist's sort of the animals also ends by showing that none stands apart.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 42:** The Tao gives birth to One. One gives birth to Two. Two gives birth to Three. Three gives birth to all things. (The family tree starts as one ancestor and splits into two, then into many. This is how all groups of animals came out of one.)
- Science (settled): All animals with a backbone share one ancestor, and the lungfish is the closest living relative of animals who walk on land. Her genome was read in 2021.
- Science (debated): Whether birds should still count as their own group, or as a branch of the reptiles, is a question about names, and biologists still argue about it.
- Other view, Ernst Mayr, a well-known biologist of the 1900s, and others who agreed with him: A good group should reflect how animals live and how much they have changed, as well as who their ancestors were. By this view, birds are different enough from lizards to be a group of their own, and fish are a sensible group, even if the family tree cuts across them.
- Practice, Breathing with the animals (3 min): Sit still. Put one hand on your chest. Breathe in slowly and feel your lungs fill with air. A dolphin, a crow, a gecko and a cow breathe in the same air with lungs like yours. A frog breathes it through her skin as well. A fish takes the same oxygen from the water with gills. Breathe out slowly three times. Think of the same air going in and out of all of them.
- Ask: The lungfish is a fish, and she is closer to a cow than to a salmon. What do you think the word fish should mean?
- Ask: If all animals with a backbone came from one ancestor, are they all cousins? What would that change about how you look at a crow or a frog?
- Ask: Nobody drew the lines between the five groups before the animals existed. Who drew them, and why?

## Deep and big questions

- *big:* If we found animals on another planet, could we sort them into our five groups? What would you ask first? (hint: Our five groups came from one tree of life. Would aliens be on the same tree?)
- *deep:* A dolphin looks like a shark but is related to a hippo. Which tells you more about an animal: how she looks, or where she came from? (hint: Think about the shark, the dolphin and the penguin.)
- *big:* Of every 20 kinds of animals, 19 have no backbone. Why did people study the one that includes us first? (hint: Who made the names, and who were they looking at?)
- *deep:* If a sorting rule has exceptions, is the rule bad, or is the animal a hard case worth studying? (hint: Think about the platypus and the rule that mammals never lay eggs.)

## Story: The Heavy Catch in Palk Bay
*Selvi, her mother Amudha, Murugan Mama and this morning are made up. The science is real. Dugongs live in Palk Bay and the Gulf of Mannar, they breathe air and must come up every few minutes, and they sometimes drown in fishing nets. Dugongs are protected by law in India, and in 2022 Tamil Nadu set aside part of Palk Bay to protect them. Many fishers there call the dugong kadal pasu, which means sea cow.*

Selvi was nine, and in the summer holidays her mother let her come out on the boat. The boat was small and blue, with a wooden bench across the middle and a net folded up at the front. Her mother, Amudha, was the captain. Murugan Mama, her mother's cousin, pulled in the nets.

They left the small harbour before sunrise. By the time the sun was up, the water of Palk Bay was as green as a curry leaf and as flat as a plate.

At school that term, Selvi's class had been sorting animals. Her teacher had said something Selvi had not liked. 'Do not sort an animal by where she lives,' the teacher had said. 'Ask what she is made of, how she breathes and how her babies start.' Selvi had written it down, but she had thought, a fish is a fish. If an animal lives in the sea and swims, she is a fish. Why make it harder?

Now Murugan Mama stood up and began to haul in the net. He pulled, and stopped. He pulled again.

'Too heavy for fish,' he said. 'It must be a rock, or a big old tangle.'

Amudha leaned over the side to help. Then she went still. 'It is not a rock,' she said.

Selvi looked down. Under the boat, in the green water, something large and grey-brown was pressed into the net. She was as long as the boat's bench. She had a round, smooth body and a broad tail that spread out sideways, like a flat paddle. Two short flippers hung at her sides. She did not thrash. She lay very still, with the mesh across her back.

'Kadal pasu,' whispered Amudha. 'A sea cow.'

'She is a big fish,' said Murugan Mama. 'She is tired, that is all. We will tow her slowly to the shore, and cut her free in the shallows. The sea is her home. She can wait in the water.'

It sounded sensible. Selvi had thought the same words herself, a minute ago. But something in her head went back to the classroom. How does she breathe?

She leaned out as far as she dared. The animal's face was blunt, with whiskers like a cat's around a wide, downturned mouth. On top of her snout, just above the mouth, were two nostrils. They were not gill slits. They were small slits on top of her snout, and they were pushing up against the underside of the net, as if she were trying to reach the surface.

'Mama,' said Selvi. 'When did we pull her up?'

'A few minutes ago,' said her mother.

'How long can she wait?'

Murugan Mama laughed. 'A fish can stay in the sea all day, child.'

'But she does not have gills,' said Selvi. Her voice came out higher than she meant. 'She has nostrils, on the top. She is a mammal. She breathes air with lungs. Mama, I think she is drowning.'

Nobody spoke. The boat rocked.

'Look under her flipper,' said Selvi. 'Fish do not have that.' She was pointing at a small teat, the kind a mother mammal feeds her baby from. 'And look at her nostrils. A fish does not breathe through her nose.'

Amudha looked. She looked for exactly as long as it took to be sure. Then she took the big knife from the bench. 'Murugan,' she said, 'hold the net up. We cut it. Now.'

'The net is new,' said Murugan Mama. 'It cost four thousand rupees.'

'A net can be mended,' said Amudha. 'She cannot.'

The knife went through the mesh in four quick strokes. The net opened like a flower. For a moment, the animal did nothing. Then she rose, slowly, until her snout broke the surface. She breathed out with a long, wet sigh, and breathed in again. A small cloud of spray hung in the morning light.

Selvi found that she was holding her breath, and she let it go.

The great grey body turned and slid away under the water. A little way off, a much smaller shape, no bigger than a calf, came up to meet her. The two rose and sank together, side by side, and then the green water closed over them.

Nobody said anything for a while. Murugan Mama sat down on the bench and looked at his hands. 'Thirty years on this sea,' he said finally. 'I have always sorted by where they live. If the child had not looked at the nostrils, I would have towed her to shore and she would have been dead before we reached the shallows.'

'You did the best you could with the rule you had,' said Amudha. 'Now we have a better rule.'

That evening, Selvi opened her notebook and crossed out the line she had written in class. Underneath she wrote: Where she lives did not tell me what she needs. How she breathes did. Then, because she was a careful girl, she added one more line. I should check two clues, not one.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Murugan Mama sorted the animal by where she lives, so he decided she was a fish and could wait in the water all day. Selvi sorted her by how she breathes and how she feeds her young. Nostrils pushing up towards the air, and a teat for feeding a calf, showed a mammal, who needs to reach the air every few minutes. The clue that decided the rescue was not where the animal lives, but a feature that comes from her ancestors. A sorting rule is useful when it tells you what an animal needs. 'Lives in water' did not. 'Breathes air' did.

- Ask: Name one clue, other than where an animal lives, that shows a sea animal is a mammal and not a fish.
- Ask: A rule that sorts by where an animal lives can mislead. Think of another animal it would mislead you about.
- Ask: Why is it better to check two clues than one when you sort an animal?
- Ask: Can you think of a time when a name for a group made you expect the wrong thing?
- Ask: How would the rescue have gone if the sorting rule had been 'what does she eat'?

### e1 · The Rule Test: which sorting rules break? (33 min)

### e2 · Hold Your Breath: why must a dolphin come up for air? (22 min)
- **question:** How long can you hold your breath at rest, and after exercise? How long can air-breathing animals who live in water stay under?

  - Note: Hand-warm water is best. If the cubes are not equal, use the two most alike. Do the pressing on a table, not a glass.

  - Note: Expected: the powder is gone in about a minute or two, the pieces in a few minutes, and the whole cube takes longest, often 4 to 8 minutes. Stop at 10 minutes and write 'not finished' if the cube is still there. Cold water is slower, so use warm water.

  *Parent does:* Write the three times. Ask why the powder was fastest. Then say that a fish's gill is a stack of thin folds, so water can touch a huge area of thin skin.  
  *Parent says:* Which form dissolved first? What was different about the powder? Water can only act on sugar where they touch. A powder has far more places to touch.  
  *Child mode:* Which form was fastest? Why? Water works where it touches.
  - Ask: Why did the powder dissolve fastest? (good: There was more sugar touching the water.; Water could reach every grain.)
  - Note: The same idea explains gills. A fish's gill is made of thousands of thin folds, so a lot of thin skin touches the water at once and oxygen passes into the blood quickly. This is an analogy, because sugar and oxygen are different things.
  - Check: Why would a gill with many thin folds take in oxygen faster than a flat gill of the same size? → **More of the gill touches the water**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: the more places water can touch, the faster it works. What I saw: the powder dissolved before the pieces and the pieces before the whole cube. Why: the powder has far more surface touching the water.

### Sorting animals (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What do all animals with a backbone have in common?  
   Options: A row of bones or hard cartilage down the middle of the back / Four legs / Warm blood  
   **Answer:** A row of bones or hard cartilage down the middle of the back. *Why:* A backbone is a row of small bones or hard cartilage. A shark, a snake and a bird have no legs or do not have four, and many have cold blood, so those are not the shared clue.
2. (truefalse) Most kinds of animals have a backbone.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. About 19 of every 20 named kinds of animal have no backbone. Insects alone are more than a million kinds.
3. (fill) Animals with a backbone are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['vertebrates', 'vertebrate']. *Why:* Vertebrates are animals with a backbone. Animals with no backbone are called invertebrates.
4. (sort) Sort each animal by whether she has a backbone.  
   **Answer:** rohu → Backbone; snail → No backbone; crow → Backbone; ant → No backbone; cow → Backbone; jellyfish → No backbone; gecko → Backbone; starfish → No backbone. *Why:* A rohu, a crow, a cow and a gecko have a backbone. A snail, an ant, a jellyfish and a starfish have none.
5. (match) Match each animal group to a clue that helps decide it.  
   **Answer:** Fish → Breathes with gills all her life; Amphibian → Thin moist skin and jelly eggs; Reptile → Dry scales and lungs; Bird → Feathers; Mammal → Feeds her babies milk. *Why:* Each group is decided by a few clues together. These are the main clue for each group.

   Options: She breathes air with lungs and feeds her baby milk / She is big / She has a tail  
   **Answer:** She breathes air with lungs and feeds her baby milk. *Why:* Breathing air with lungs and feeding milk are the clues that decide the group. Size and a tail do not.

   **Answer:** Crow, because she has feathers and lays eggs. The other three are mammals with fur who feed milk.. *Why:* A bat has fur and feeds milk, so she goes with the cat and the cow, even though she flies like a crow.

   **Answer:** A bat, who flies but is a mammal. *Why:* A bat flies, so the rule lets her in, but she is a mammal. A crow follows the rule and a cow does not enter it.
9. (truefalse) A platypus lays eggs, so she cannot be a mammal.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A platypus has fur and feeds her young milk, and those are the clues that decide a mammal. Laying eggs does not rule her out.

### Sorting animals (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A shark and a dolphin both have a pointed, smooth body. What is the best explanation?

2. (mcq) Whales and dolphins have four-legged ancestors. Which evidence supports this?  
   Options: Fossils of early whales from India and Pakistan had legs / Whales can swim / Whales live in the sea  
   **Answer:** Fossils of early whales from India and Pakistan had legs. *Why:* Early whale fossils from Pakistan show animals with four legs, and an early whale jaw was found in India. DNA also shows that hippos are the closest living relatives.
3. (perspective) A family-tree biologist and a bird watcher disagree about whether birds count as reptiles.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side and says that the biologist's view is useful for who is related, and the bird watcher's view for finding animals in the field.. *Why:* The two views answer different questions. A family tree answers who is related, and a field guide answers how to find and name an animal.
4. (truefalse) If a rule has one exception, the whole group must be wrong.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A hard case shows that the rule is weak, not that the group is wrong. Scientists mend the rule with a better clue.
5. (mcq) Which pair are the closest relatives?  
   Options: A crow and a crocodile / A crocodile and a gecko / A crocodile and a frog  
   **Answer:** A crow and a crocodile. *Why:* Birds and crocodiles share a more recent ancestor than crocodiles and lizards do.
6. (fill) A rule can fail in two ways. It can leave out an animal that belongs, or it can ____ an animal that does not.  
   **Answer:** ['let in']. *Why:* A rule that lets in an animal who does not belong has failed, as well as one that leaves out an animal who does.
7. (sort) Sort each animal into the smallest room of the family tree that fits her.  
   **Answer:** rohu → Backbone only; frog → Four limbs only; gecko → Sealed egg but no milk; crow → Sealed egg but no milk; dolphin → Hair and milk; bat → Hair and milk. *Why:* A rohu has only the backbone. A frog has four limbs but no sealed egg. A gecko and a crow have sealed eggs and no milk. A dolphin and a bat have hair and milk.
8. (write) An octopus has no backbone, and she can open a jar. What does this say about the idea that animals with a backbone are the cleverest?  
   **Answer:** It shows the idea is not right. Cleverness is found in animals without a backbone too. A backbone is not what makes a mind.. *Why:* A strong answer says that an animal with no backbone can solve problems, so the backbone does not decide how clever an animal is.
9. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Shark, rohu, dolphin, seahorse.  
   **Answer:** Dolphin, because she is a mammal who breathes air and feeds milk. The other three are fish with gills.. *Why:* A shark, a rohu and a seahorse breathe with gills all their lives. A dolphin breathes air with lungs.
10. (match) Match each hard case to the rule she breaks.  
   **Answer:** Penguin → Animals that fly are birds; Platypus → Animals that are born live are mammals; Rohu → Animals with scales are reptiles; Dolphin → Animals that live in water are fish. *Why:* The penguin is a bird who cannot fly. The platypus is a mammal who lays eggs. The rohu has scales but is a fish. The dolphin lives in water but is a mammal.
11. (draw) Draw a shark, a dolphin and a penguin with the same smooth shape. Under each, write one clue that shows her real group.  
   **Answer:** Three similar shapes labelled with gills (shark), lungs and milk (dolphin), and feathers (penguin).. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the same shape three times and a different body clue under each that shows her real group.
12. (mcq) Why do fish gills have many thin folds?  
   Options: More of the gill touches the water, so more oxygen passes in / The folds make the fish heavier / The folds keep the water warm  
   **Answer:** More of the gill touches the water, so more oxygen passes in. *Why:* Oxygen passes in where a gill touches the water. Many thin folds give a big area in a small space, just as powdered sugar touches water in more places than a lump.
13. (count) Out of 16 animal cards, a rule leaves out 2 and lets in 3. How many animals break the rule?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* 2 plus 3 is 5. The rule breaks on 5 animals, whether it leaves them out or lets them in.
14. (write) A bat is in the 'can fly' group and in the 'feeds milk' group. Which group is more useful for finding her relatives? Explain.  
   **Answer:** The 'feeds milk' group, because milk is a clue that comes from a shared ancestor with mammals such as cats and cows. Flying has appeared in birds, insects and bats separately.. *Why:* A strong answer picks the milk group and says that flying appeared several times, while milk follows the family.
15. (truefalse) A caterpillar has a backbone.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A caterpillar is the young stage of an insect, and insects have no backbone.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) How many kinds of animals have a backbone, compared with those that have none?  
   Options: Only about 1 in 20 kinds have a backbone / About half have a backbone / Most have a backbone  
   **Answer:** Only about 1 in 20 kinds have a backbone. *Why:* About 19 of every 20 named kinds of animals have no backbone. The animals with a backbone are the small half.
2. (mcq) Which clue best shows that a bat is a mammal?  
   Options: She has fur and feeds her baby milk / She flies at night / She hangs upside down  
   **Answer:** She has fur and feeds her baby milk. *Why:* Fur and milk are the clues for a mammal. Flying and hanging upside down are things she does, and a bird or an insect can do them too.
3. (mcq) A penguin cannot fly. Why is he still a bird?  
   Options: He has feathers and lays hard-shelled eggs / He lives near the sea / He has two legs  
   **Answer:** He has feathers and lays hard-shelled eggs. *Why:* Feathers and hard-shelled eggs decide a bird. A penguin swims instead of flying.
4. (mcq) A rule leaves out an animal that belongs and lets in an animal that does not. What does this show?  
   Options: The rule is weak and needs a better clue / The group does not exist / The animals are wrong  
   **Answer:** The rule is weak and needs a better clue. *Why:* A rule that fails in both ways is weak. Scientists mend it by trying a body clue or two clues together.
5. (mcq) Which animal is a hard case for the rule 'reptiles lay eggs'?  
   Options: A Russell's viper, who gives birth to live young / A house gecko, who lays eggs / A crocodile, who lays eggs  
   **Answer:** A Russell's viper, who gives birth to live young. *Why:* A Russell's viper is a reptile with live young, so she breaks the rule. A gecko and a crocodile follow it.
6. (mcq) A Ganges river dolphin must come up for air every minute or two. Why?  
   Options: She has lungs and no gills / She is tired / She likes the sunlight  
   **Answer:** She has lungs and no gills. *Why:* A dolphin breathes air with lungs, so she must come to the surface. A fish has gills and can stay under.
7. (mcq) Why do a bat's wing and a human arm have the same bones in the same order?  
   Options: Both came from an ancestor with that plan / Both are used for flying / Both are used for holding  
   **Answer:** Both came from an ancestor with that plan. *Why:* The same plan used for different jobs is what you expect when both inherited the plan from one ancestor.
8. (truefalse) A tadpole should be sorted with the fish because she has gills.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A tadpole turns into a frog, and a fish keeps her gills for life. You must look at the whole life of an animal.
9. (mcq) Which question separates a snail from an ant?  
   Options: Does she have jointed legs and a hard outer skeleton? / Does she live on land? / Is she small?  
   **Answer:** Does she have jointed legs and a hard outer skeleton?. *Why:* An ant is an arthropod with jointed legs and a hard outer skeleton. A snail is a mollusc with a soft body and one muscular foot.
10. (mcq) Why is 'invertebrate' not one family?  
   Options: It only says what the animals lack, and they belong to many separate groups / All of them are the same shape / There are very few of them  
   **Answer:** It only says what the animals lack, and they belong to many separate groups. *Why:* The word names a missing backbone. Insects, snails and starfish are as different from each other as a fish is from a snail.
11. (mcq) What is surprising about a lungfish?  
   Options: She is a closer relative of a cow than of a rohu / She lives on land / She is a kind of frog  
   **Answer:** She is a closer relative of a cow than of a rohu. *Why:* A lungfish is a fish with lungs and fins that have bones. Her genes put her closer to land animals than to most fish.
12. (mcq) A scientist finds an animal that lays eggs and also has fur and feeds milk. What should scientists do about the rule 'mammals give birth to live young'?  
   Options: Mend the rule so that milk decides a mammal / Say the animal is not a mammal / Ignore the animal  
   **Answer:** Mend the rule so that milk decides a mammal. *Why:* When evidence breaks a rule, scientists mend the rule. This is what happened with the platypus, so a mammal is defined by feeding milk.

## Applications
- Vets treat reptiles, birds and mammals differently, because the groups differ in how they breathe, keep warm and feed.
- Snakebite doctors in India ask what kind of snake bit the patient, such as a viper, a cobra or a krait, because the bites harm the body in different ways.
- Conservation workers protect animals such as dugongs, who must surface to breathe, by setting up reserves and freeing animals from nets.
- Fisheries managers sort the catch by group, so that dolphins, turtles and sharks caught by accident can be released safely.
- Museums arrange skeletons by their matching bones, so visitors can see which animals share a plan.
- Drug researchers study animals with no backbone, such as cone snails, whose venom led to a painkiller.
- Farmers sort animals as ruminants, birds or fish to plan feed, housing and medicines.
- Wildlife researchers use DNA to tell which animals are related, and to find animals not yet named.

## Open questions
- **How many kinds of animals are there on Earth?** About 1.5 million kinds of animals have names. One estimate from 2011 suggests there may be about 8.7 million kinds of animals, plants and fungi with cell nuclei, and most are not named yet. Nobody knows.
- **Which animal branched off first from the rest: the sponge or the comb jelly?** Studies of DNA give different answers, and scientists still argue about it.
- **Why did some fish first crawl onto land?** Fossils show when it happened, about 375 million years ago, but scientists debate why. Ideas include drying pools, food on the shore and escape from big hunters.
- **Do animals with no backbone, such as the octopus, have minds like ours?** Nobody can see inside another mind. We judge by what an animal does, and octopuses solve problems, use tools and seem to learn.
- **What were feathers first for, before birds could fly?** Some small dinosaurs had feathers but could not fly. Scientists debate whether feathers first kept them warm, helped them show off or helped them glide.
- **If there is life on other planets, could we sort it into our groups?** We know only one tree of life on Earth. If life elsewhere began separately, our groups might not fit at all. We have no evidence yet either way.

## Connected maths
- In the Rule Test, rule 1 let in 6 of the 16 animal cards. What fraction of the cards is that? Write it in its simplest form. **3 out of 8.**. 6 out of 16 is the fraction 6 over 16. Divide the top and bottom by 2, and you get 3 over 8. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- The bar chart shows how many of the 16 animals broke each rule. How many more animals broke rule 1 than rule 4? **4 more animals.**. The bar for rule 1 is 6 and the bar for rule 4 is 2. 6 take away 2 is 4. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- Eight animals are put in two rings: Flies, and Has feathers. The animals are a crow, a bat, a penguin, an ostrich, a cow, a cat, a gecko and a cobra. How many animals are in neither ring? **4 animals.**. The crow is in both rings. The bat flies, so she is only in the Flies ring. The penguin and the ostrich have feathers, so they are only in the Has feathers ring. That is 4 animals in a ring. The cow, the cat, the gecko and the cobra are in neither ring, so 8 take away 4 is 4. (queues `math.logic.sets-and-venn.L4`)
- About 19 of every 20 kinds of animal have no backbone. A museum labels 400 kinds of animals in the same proportion. How many of them have a backbone? **20 kinds.**. 400 divided by 20 is 20 groups of 20. In each group of 20 kinds, 1 has a backbone, so 20 groups give 20 kinds with a backbone. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A Ganges river dolphin comes up to breathe every 2 minutes. How many times does she come up in one hour? **30 times.**. One hour is 60 minutes. 60 divided by 2 is 30. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- A child can hold her breath for 30 seconds. A sperm whale can stay under for 60 minutes. How many times longer is the whale's time? **120 times.**. 60 minutes is 60 times 60 seconds, which is 3,600 seconds. 3,600 divided by 30 is 120. Always put both times in the same unit first. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- In the Lump or Powder test, the whole sugar cube took 240 seconds to dissolve and the powder took 40 seconds. How many times faster was the powder? **6 times faster.**. 240 divided by 40 is 6. The powder was 6 times faster. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A child breathes out 20 times a minute at rest. How many breaths is that in one hour? Explain how you worked it out. **1,200 breaths.**. There are 60 minutes in an hour. 20 times 60 is 1,200. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- There are about 400,000 kinds of beetle and about 75,000 kinds of animals with a backbone. About how many times as many kinds of beetle are there? Round to the nearest whole number. **About 5 times as many.**. 400,000 divided by 75,000 is about 5.3, which rounds to 5. Beetles have about five times as many kinds as all the animals with a backbone. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
