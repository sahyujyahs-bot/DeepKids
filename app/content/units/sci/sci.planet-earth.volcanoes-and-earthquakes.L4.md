# Volcanoes and earthquakes: why the ground shakes and burns where it does
`sci.planet-earth.volcanoes-and-earthquakes.L4`

**Summary.** The ground under your feet feels still, yet it is travelling north-east by about 5 centimetres every year. The Earth's hard outer shell is broken into huge plates that move, stick and suddenly slip. In this lesson you pull a weighted box with a rubber band to find out if anyone can tell when it will jump. You follow India's own journey, from the lava floods of the Deccan to the rise of the Himalaya, the Bhuj earthquake, the 2004 tsunami and the volcano on Barren Island. You also find out what scientists can tell us before the ground shakes, and what nobody can.

**Intro.** *On the top of Mount Everest, the rock is full of sea shells. The highest place on Earth was once the bottom of a sea. How did a sea floor get nearly 9 kilometres up into the sky?* Nobody carried it there. The ground did it, moving more slowly than your fingernails grow. The same slow movement shook Bhuj in 2001, sent the sea over the coast of Tamil Nadu in 2004, and feeds a volcano that still smokes in the Andaman Sea. In this lesson you first draw the Earth to its true scale and see how thin the ground really is. Then you pull a heavy box with a rubber band and try to say when it will jump. That small test holds the biggest honest answer about earthquakes.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can describe the layers inside the Earth, and say how we know about them without ever having seen them.
1. I can explain what a plate is, how fast plates move, and what happens at the three kinds of plate edge.
2. I can explain an earthquake as stick, stretch and slip, and use the words focus, epicentre, seismic wave and magnitude.

4. I can use a map to show why most earthquakes and volcanoes lie in lines, and I can name places that do not fit the lines.
5. I can tell India's own story: the journey of the Indian plate, the Deccan Traps, the Himalaya, Bhuj in 2001, the tsunami of 2004 and Barren Island.

## Terms
- **Crust**: The thin, hard, outer layer of rock that we live on. It is 5 to 10 kilometres thick under the oceans and 30 to 70 kilometres thick under the land. *e.g.* The granite under Bengaluru is part of the crust.
- **Mantle**: The thick layer of hot rock under the crust. It is solid, but over millions of years it creeps like very stiff dough. *e.g.* The mantle goes down about 2,900 kilometres, which is most of the way to the core.
- **Core**: The centre of the Earth, made mostly of iron and nickel. The outer core is liquid. The inner core is solid. *e.g.* The inner core is hotter than 5,000 degrees Celsius, about as hot as the surface of the Sun.
- **Plate**: A huge slab made of the crust and the stiff top of the mantle. The Earth's outer shell is broken into about seven very large plates and many smaller ones. *e.g.* India, Sri Lanka and part of the Indian Ocean floor ride on the Indian plate.
- **Plate edge**: The place where two plates meet. Scientists also call it a plate boundary. Plates move apart, push together or slide past each other there. *e.g.* The Himalaya stand along the edge where the Indian plate meets the plate that carries most of Asia.
- **Fault**: A crack in rock where the two sides have moved past each other. *e.g.* The Bhuj earthquake of 2001 came from a fault hidden deep under Kutch.
- **Earthquake**: The shaking of the ground when stuck rock suddenly slips along a fault. *e.g.* Instruments pick up about half a million earthquakes every year. People feel only about one in five of them.
- **Focus**: The place under the ground where the rock first slips in an earthquake. *e.g.* The focus of the Bhuj earthquake was about 16 kilometres below the ground.
- **Epicentre**: The place on the surface straight above the focus. *e.g.* The epicentre of the 2004 earthquake was in the sea, west of the island of Sumatra.
- **Seismic wave**: A wave of shaking that travels through the Earth from an earthquake. P waves push and stretch the rock and arrive first. S waves shake it from side to side and arrive second. *e.g.* P waves from an earthquake in Japan reach India in about ten minutes.
- **Seismograph**: An instrument that draws the shaking of the ground as a wiggly line. *e.g.* A seismograph in Hyderabad can record an earthquake in Indonesia that nobody in Hyderabad feels.
- **Magnitude**: A number for the size of an earthquake at its source. Each step up of one whole number means ten times more ground movement. *e.g.* Bhuj in 2001 was magnitude 7.7. The earthquake of 2004 was magnitude 9.1.
- **Magma**: Melted rock under the ground, with gases mixed into it. *e.g.* Magma collects in a chamber a few kilometres under Barren Island.
- **Lava**: Magma that has come out onto the surface. *e.g.* The dark rock of the Deccan was once lava that flowed across the land.
- **Volcano**: An opening in the crust where magma, ash and gases come out, and the hill that builds up around it. *e.g.* Barren Island, 138 kilometres from Port Blair, is India's active volcano.
- **Tsunami**: A set of very long sea waves made when the sea floor suddenly moves, most often in an undersea earthquake. The word is Japanese for harbour wave. *e.g.* The tsunami of 26 December 2004 reached the coast of Tamil Nadu about two and a half hours after the earthquake.
- **Hot spot**: A place where volcanoes form far from any plate edge, above rock that is unusually hot. *e.g.* Hawaii sits on a hot spot in the middle of the Pacific plate.

## Explanations
### Inside the Earth: crust, mantle and core · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth has layers. From the outside in they are the crust, the mantle, the liquid outer core and the solid inner core.

It is about 6,370 kilometres from your feet to the centre of the Earth. The crust you stand on is the thinnest layer. If the Earth were a mango, the crust would be thinner than the skin. Under it lies the mantle, about 2,900 kilometres of hot rock. The mantle is solid. It is not a sea of lava. But it is so hot, and squeezed so hard, that over millions of years it creeps by a few centimetres a year. Below the mantle is the outer core, an ocean of liquid iron and nickel. At the centre is the inner core, a ball of solid metal that is hotter than 5,000 degrees Celsius. The heat comes from two places. Some is left over from the time the Earth formed. Some is made all the time by atoms deep in the rock that break apart and give out heat.
- e.g. The deepest hole ever drilled is the Kola borehole in Russia. It reached 12 kilometres in 1989, and it never left the crust.
- e.g. At Koyna in Maharashtra, Indian scientists have drilled 3 kilometres into the Deccan rock to study earthquakes.
- e.g. The liquid iron moving in the outer core makes the Earth a giant magnet, which is why a compass needle points north.
- **Seismologists, from Richard Oldham in 1906 to Inge Lehmann in 1936:** Nobody has seen below the crust. The layers were found by reading earthquake waves that had passed through the Earth. Oldham saw that waves slow down and bend deep inside, so there must be a core. Lehmann, working in Denmark, saw faint waves in a zone where none should arrive. She showed that they had bounced off a solid inner core.
- **Scientists who squeeze and heat rock in laboratories:** Solid and liquid are not two clean boxes. The same mantle rock rings like a bell when an earthquake wave passes in a second, so it is solid. Over a million years it flows. The answer depends on how long you watch.
- **Scientists who study what the core is made of:** The core is lighter than pure iron and nickel would be. Something else is mixed in. It may be sulphur, oxygen, silicon or hydrogen. Nobody has a sample, so this is still argued.
### Plates: the broken shell that moves · *defined*
**Definition:** A plate is a huge slab of the crust and the stiff top of the mantle. The plates move a few centimetres every year, carrying the land and the sea floor with them.

The hard outer shell of the Earth is not one piece. It is broken into about seven very large plates and many smaller ones, a little like the shell of a boiled egg that has been cracked all over. The plates are 50 to 250 kilometres thick. They ride on mantle rock that is softer and creeps. Most plates move between 2 and 10 centimetres a year, which is about as fast as your fingernails grow. That sounds like nothing. But in a million years, 5 centimetres a year adds up to 50 kilometres. Satellites now measure this movement directly. A marker fixed to the rock in Bengaluru moves about 5 centimetres towards the north-east every year, along with all of India. Two slow forces also change the land. Plates push, bend and lift it. Erosion is the slow wearing away of land by rain, rivers, wind and ice. The rocks keep the marks: layers laid down by water, with fossils in them (the remains or prints of living things), show what the land was like long ago.
- e.g. The Indian plate carries India, Sri Lanka and a large part of the Indian Ocean floor.
- e.g. The Pacific plate is the largest. It is almost all sea floor.
- e.g. A child who is 9 years old has travelled about 45 centimetres with the Indian plate since she was born.
- e.g. Rivers coming down from the Himalaya carry away rock that the plates have pushed up, and drop it on the plains.
- **Alfred Wegener, a German weather scientist, in 1912:** He said the continents were once joined and had drifted apart. He pointed to coastlines that fit, and to the same fossils on lands now far apart. Most geologists of his time rejected the idea, because he could not say what force could move a continent.
- **Scientists who say sinking plates do the pulling, for example Donald Forsyth and Seiya Uyeda in 1975:** Old sea floor is cold and heavy. Where it dives into the mantle, it sinks and drags the rest of its plate behind it. They found that plates with a long diving edge move fastest, so this pull seems to be the main force.
- **Scientists who say the creeping mantle does the carrying:** Hot mantle rock rises, spreads sideways and sinks again, very slowly. In this view the plates are carried along on top. Most scientists now think both things happen, and they still argue about how much each one does.
### Three things plates do at their edges · *defined*
**Definition:** Where two plates meet, they move apart, push together or slide past each other. Each kind of edge makes its own kind of land, earthquake and volcano.

Where plates move apart, hot mantle rock rises into the gap, melts and cools into new sea floor. This happens along the mid-ocean ridges, which form the longest mountain chain on Earth, almost all of it under water. Where plates push together, one of two things happens. If one plate is heavy sea floor, it dives under the other and sinks into the mantle. Water carried down with it helps the rock above to melt, and a line of volcanoes grows. If both plates carry light continent rock, neither can dive. The rock between them crumples and piles up into mountains. Where plates slide past each other, no rock is made or lost. The edges catch and stick, then jerk forward, and the ground shakes.
- e.g. Moving apart: the Carlsberg Ridge under the Arabian Sea, where the Indian plate moves away from Africa.
- e.g. Pushing together with one plate diving: under the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, where the Indian plate dives beneath the Burma plate.
- e.g. Pushing together with no plate diving: the Himalaya, where India meets Asia.
- e.g. Sliding past: the San Andreas fault in California.
- **School books and most maps:** They draw each plate edge as one thin line and give it one of three names. This is a good first map, and it explains most volcanoes and earthquakes.
- **Geologists who measure the ground with satellites, such as Richard Gordon and Seth Stein:** Many edges are not lines. They are belts hundreds of kilometres wide. The squeeze between India and Asia is spread from the Himalaya across Tibet and far into China. The Indian plate itself is slowly breaking under the Indian Ocean, and no single line marks the break.
### Why the ground shakes: stick, stretch and slip · *defined*
**Definition:** An earthquake happens when rock that was stuck along a fault suddenly slips. The energy stored in the bent rock is let go as waves of shaking.

Plates never stop moving, but their edges do not glide. Rock is rough, and friction holds the two sides of a fault together. So the plate keeps moving while its edge stays stuck. The rock near the fault bends, the way a ruler bends when you push its two ends. Rock can bend a little and store energy, just like a stretched rubber band. This can go on for a hundred years or more. Then the stuck patch gives way. The two sides jump past each other, by a few centimetres in a small earthquake and by 10 to 20 metres in a great one. The place where the slip starts is the focus. The slip can then run along the fault for hundreds of kilometres in a few minutes. The stored energy travels out as seismic waves, and that is the shaking people feel.
- e.g. In 2004 the fault off Sumatra slipped along about 1,300 kilometres. The slipping took nearly ten minutes.
- e.g. After a big earthquake, the rock nearby settles with many smaller earthquakes called aftershocks.
- e.g. A door that sticks and then flies open when you push harder is a small stick and slip.
- **Harry Fielding Reid, an American scientist, in 1910:** After the San Francisco earthquake of 1906, he compared old and new land surveys. The land on both sides of the fault had been bending slowly for decades, and it sprang back in the earthquake. He called this elastic rebound. It is still the main explanation.
- **Varahamihira, an astronomer of Ujjain, in the Brihat Samhita, in the 500s CE:** His chapter on earthquakes sets side by side the explanations of earlier scholars. They include giant animals living in the water, the tired elephants who hold up the Earth, winds crashing into each other, and a cause that cannot be seen. He recorded them without choosing one.
- **Peoples who live with earthquakes:** In Japan, stories tell of a giant catfish named Namazu who thrashes under the land. The Maori of New Zealand speak of Ruaumoko, the unborn child of the Earth mother, turning over. These stories kept the memory of past earthquakes alive for the people who came later.
### Seismic waves, and two ways to measure an earthquake · *defined*
**Definition:** Seismic waves carry the energy of an earthquake through the Earth. Magnitude measures the size of the earthquake at its source. Intensity measures how hard the ground shook at one place.

An earthquake sends out several kinds of waves. P waves push and stretch the rock along the way they travel, like a push sent along a spring toy. They are the fastest, at about 6 kilometres every second in the crust, and they pass through solids and liquids. S waves shake the rock from side to side. They travel at about 4 kilometres every second, and they do not pass through liquids. Surface waves are the slowest. They roll along the ground and do most of the damage. A seismograph draws all of them as a wiggly line. Seismologists use the line to work out the magnitude. Each step of one whole number means the ground moved ten times more, and about 32 times more energy was let go. Intensity is different. It describes what people felt and what broke at one place, so one earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities.
- e.g. The gap between the P wave and the S wave tells how far away the earthquake was, in the way the gap between lightning and thunder tells how far away a storm is.
- e.g. A magnitude 7 earthquake moves the ground 100 times more than a magnitude 5.
- e.g. The Bhuj earthquake shook the ground very hard near Bhachau and gently in Mumbai. It was the same earthquake, with the same magnitude.
- **Seismologists such as Hiroo Kanamori and Thomas Hanks, in 1979:** They measure an earthquake by how large the slipped patch of fault was and how far it slipped. This is called moment magnitude. It replaced the scale Charles Richter made in 1935, which could not tell the largest earthquakes apart.
- **Engineers and the people who lived through it:** For them the number that matters is how hard their own ground shook. A middle-sized earthquake under a city on soft soil can do more harm than a huge one far out at sea. The Latur earthquake of 1993 was only magnitude 6.2, and nearly 8,000 people died, because stone houses fell on sleeping families.
### Volcanoes: where melted rock reaches the top · *defined*
**Definition:** A volcano is an opening in the crust where magma, ash and gases come out, together with the hill of cooled lava and ash that builds up around the opening.

Most of the mantle is solid, so magma has to be made. Rock melts in a few kinds of places. It melts where hot rock rises and the squeeze on it drops. It melts where a diving plate carries water down, because wet rock melts more easily. Magma is lighter than the solid rock around it, so it rises and collects in a chamber. Magma has gases dissolved in it, like the fizz in a closed bottle of soda. When magma is runny, the gas bubbles escape easily. Lava pours out and spreads far, building wide, low hills. When magma is thick and sticky, the bubbles are trapped. The push of the gas builds until the magma is blown apart into ash, and a steep cone grows. People sort volcanoes into active, dormant and extinct. An active volcano has erupted in recent times. A dormant one is quiet but may erupt again. An extinct one is not expected to erupt again.
- e.g. Runny lava: the volcanoes of Hawaii, and the lava floods of the Deccan.
- e.g. Sticky magma: Krakatau in Indonesia, which blew itself apart in 1883.
- e.g. Barren Island in the Andaman Sea is active. Narcondam, its neighbour, is dormant.
- **Volcano scientists, for example at the Smithsonian's Global Volcanism Program:** They do not trust the words dormant and extinct. A volcano's life is far longer than written history. They count any volcano that has erupted in about the last 12,000 years as one that could erupt again. Barren Island was quiet for well over a century, and then it erupted in 1991.
- **People who live on volcanoes:** In Hawaii, the volcano is Pele, a powerful being who destroys land and makes new land. Farmers on the slopes of Merapi in Java know the mountain as a danger and also as the giver of their rich soil. They see one being with two sides, not a disaster.
- **Scientists who study the early Earth:** They see volcanoes as makers. Over billions of years, volcanoes brought up gases that helped to build the air, and rock that became the land. Where the Earth's first water came from is still argued.
### Why they happen where they do: lines on the map, and places that break the rule · *debated*
Mark the world's earthquakes and volcanoes on a map, and they do not fall everywhere. They fall in narrow belts, and the belts are the plate edges. The edge of the Pacific Ocean has so many that it is called the Ring of Fire. About three out of four active volcanoes on land are there, and most of the world's earthquakes. But some do not fit. Hawaii sits in the middle of a plate. So do the old volcanoes of the Deccan. These are called hot spots, and scientists argue about what feeds them. Earthquakes also strike inside plates. Bhuj is about 400 kilometres from the nearest plate edge. Latur and Koyna are deep inside the Indian plate. India is being squeezed as it pushes into Asia, and old faults far inside the plate can slip under that squeeze. Such earthquakes are rarer, and they are harder to forecast.
- e.g. On the line: Japan, Indonesia, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Nepal, Chile.
- e.g. Off the line: Hawaii, Bhuj in 2001, Latur in 1993, Koyna in 1967.
- e.g. At Koyna the earthquakes began after a large dam was filled, so people can set off earthquakes too.
- **W. Jason Morgan, in 1971, and most geologists today:** A hot spot is fed by a plume, a narrow column of hot rock rising all the way from the bottom of the mantle. The plate slides over it, and a chain of volcanoes is left behind, each older than the last.
- **Gillian Foulger and other scientists who doubt deep plumes:** Many hot spots do not show a clear column of hot rock below them. In this view the plate is being stretched and cracked from the side, and shallow rock melts and leaks up through the cracks.
- **Seismologists who study earthquakes inside plates, such as Harsh Gupta in India:** Places like Kutch are old tears in the plate from the time India broke away from other lands. They are weak places. The steady squeeze from the north loads them slowly, perhaps over thousands of years, and then one slips.
### India's journey: from Gondwana to the Himalaya · *defined*
**Definition:** India was once part of a great southern land called Gondwana. It broke away, crossed an ocean, and pushed into Asia. The Himalaya are the rock crumpled up between the two.

About 180 million years ago, India was joined to Africa, Antarctica, Australia and Madagascar. The name Gondwana comes from the land of the Gond people of central India, where the first clues were found in the rocks. Gondwana cracked apart. About 88 million years ago India left Madagascar behind and moved north, for a time at more than 15 centimetres a year. That is very fast for a plate. A sea called the Tethys lay between India and Asia. About 50 million years ago India reached Asia. Both carry light continent rock, so neither could dive under the other. The floor of the Tethys Sea was squeezed, folded and lifted. That is why sea shells lie in the rock at the top of Everest. India has not stopped. It still pushes north by 4 to 5 centimetres a year. Parts of the Himalaya rise a few millimetres every year, while rain, ice and rivers wear them down.
- e.g. Fossils of the same plant, Glossopteris, are found in the coal fields of India and in Africa, Australia and Antarctica.
- e.g. The six-sided lava columns of St Mary's Islands near Udupi formed about 88 million years ago, when Madagascar split away.
- e.g. The great Himalayan earthquakes, such as Kangra in 1905 and Nepal in 2015, happen because India is still pushing.
- **Geologists who read fossils and the magnetism frozen in old rocks:** Lava records the direction of the Earth's magnet when it cools. Old Indian lavas show that India once lay far south of the equator. Most studies put the first touch with Asia at 50 to 55 million years ago.
- **Jonathan Aitchison and other geologists, in 2007:** They argue that India first hit a chain of islands, and that the true meeting of the two continents came much later, about 35 million years ago. The date is still argued.
- **People who keep shaligram stones:** Black stones with a spiral inside are found in the Kali Gandaki river in Nepal. Many Hindus have kept them for centuries as a form of Vishnu. Geologists read the same stones as ammonites, sea animals who lived in the Tethys Sea more than 100 million years ago. One stone carries both meanings.
### The Deccan Traps: a flood of lava · *defined*
**Definition:** The Deccan Traps are a huge pile of basalt, which is cooled lava, covering much of western and central India. They formed in a burst of eruptions about 66 million years ago.

Not every volcano is a cone. About 66 million years ago, long cracks opened in the ground of India, and runny lava poured out in floods. One flow could spread for hundreds of kilometres before it cooled. Then the next flow covered it. This went on, in bursts, for several hundred thousand years. The lava pile is more than 2 kilometres thick near the Western Ghats. It still covers about half a million square kilometres, which is nearly one sixth of India. The layers have worn into steps on the hillsides. The word trap comes from a Swedish word for stairs. Over millions of years the basalt broke down into black soil, which holds water well and grows cotton and jowar. Most geologists think India was then passing over the hot spot that today feeds the volcano on Réunion island.
- e.g. The step-like hills at Mahabaleshwar and all along the Western Ghats in Maharashtra.
- e.g. The black soil of northern Karnataka, in Belagavi, Vijayapura, Kalaburagi and Bidar, and of western Telangana.
- e.g. The caves of Ajanta and Ellora, which were carved into the Deccan basalt.
- **Geologists who follow the hot spot trail, such as Vincent Courtillot:** As India moved north, the hot spot stayed behind and left a line of volcanic ridges on the sea floor, under Lakshadweep, the Maldives and Mauritius. The trail ends at Réunion, where a volcano erupts today.
- **Farmers of the Deccan:** For them the Traps are not a disaster of the past. They are regur, the black cotton soil. Farmers know how it swells when wet and cracks when dry, and which crops it suits. The lava is the ground of their living.
### Tsunami: when the sea floor moves the sea · *defined*
**Definition:** A tsunami is a set of very long waves made when a large piece of sea floor suddenly rises or drops and lifts the whole depth of the sea above it.

On 26 December 2004, at about half past six in the morning in India, the fault where the Indian plate dives under the Burma plate slipped. The slip ran for about 1,300 kilometres, from Sumatra to the Andaman Islands. The sea floor jumped up by several metres and pushed up all the water above it. In deep water a tsunami is less than a metre high, and ships hardly notice it. But it moves at about 700 kilometres an hour, as fast as a jet plane. Near the shore it slows down, and the water piles up into waves many metres tall. The waves reached the Nicobar Islands in minutes and the coast of Tamil Nadu about two and a half hours later. About 230,000 people died in 14 countries. More than 10,000 of them were in India. Often the sea first pulls far back from the beach. That is a warning made by the wave itself.
- e.g. Indira Point, the southern tip of India in the Nicobar Islands, sank by about 4 metres in the 2004 earthquake.
- e.g. Since 2007, the Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre in Hyderabad watches the Indian Ocean all day and all night.
- e.g. The earthquake was magnitude 9.1, the third largest ever recorded.
- **The people of Simeulue island, Indonesia, and the Onge and Jarawa of the Andaman Islands:** Simeulue was close to the earthquake of 2004. Its people had a word, smong, and songs from a tsunami in 1907. The songs say that when the ground shakes hard and the sea pulls back, you must run to the hills. Nearly everyone ran, and only seven of about 78,000 people died. Reports say the Onge and the Jarawa also moved to high ground in time.
- **Scientists at warning centres, such as the one at INCOIS in Hyderabad:** Seismographs find the earthquake within minutes. Pressure sensors on the deep sea floor feel the wave pass over them. Computers work out when it will reach each coast. In 2004 the Indian Ocean had no such system. People near the earthquake still have only minutes, so the old knowledge and the new system are both needed.
### What can we know before the ground shakes? · *idea*
Here is what we know so far. Nobody can say the day, the place and the size of an earthquake before it happens. Many people have tried for more than a hundred years, and no method has worked reliably. Scientists can do three other things. They can forecast. From old earthquakes and from the measured squeeze on the rocks, they can say that a region is likely to have a large earthquake some time in the coming decades. India's earthquake map is made this way. It puts the Himalaya, the Northeast, Kutch and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands in the highest danger zones. Much of South India is in lower zones, but lower does not mean zero. They can also warn. Sensors near the fault catch the first fast waves and send a message that outruns the slower, stronger waves. That gives people a few seconds to a minute. And engineers can build so that buildings bend and do not fall. What is missing is the reason one small slip stops and another grows into a giant. Nobody knows that yet.
- e.g. A forecast: 'This region is likely to have a strong earthquake within the next 50 years.'
- e.g. A warning: a phone alert that arrives 20 seconds before strong shaking.
- e.g. A prediction nobody can honestly make: 'An earthquake will strike this town next Tuesday.'
- **Robert Geller and other seismologists, writing in 1997:** They argue that earthquakes cannot be predicted at all. A fault is always close to slipping, and tiny differences nobody can measure decide when a slip starts and how far it runs. They say money is better spent on strong buildings.
- **Chinese scientists at Haicheng in 1975:** After many small earthquakes and reports of odd changes in wells, officials moved people out of their homes. A magnitude 7.3 earthquake struck hours later, and many lives were saved. But the next year an even deadlier earthquake at Tangshan came with no such signs, and nobody was warned.
- **Scientists who track animals, such as Martin Wikelski:** In 2020 his team reported that cows, sheep and dogs on a farm in Italy moved about more in the hours before nearby earthquakes. Other scientists say the study was small and the pattern must be tested in many more places.

## Mostly agreed
- Satellite markers fixed to rock on every continent move a few centimetres each year, and the directions match the plate map. *How we know:* Thousands of ground stations, including many across India, have recorded their own positions every day since the 1990s.
- The floor of the open ocean is young. Almost none of it is older than 200 million years, while some land rocks are 4,000 million years old. *How we know:* Ships have drilled and dated sea floor rock since 1968. It is youngest at the ridges and older on both sides, in matching stripes.
- A great earthquake makes the whole Earth ring like a struck bell. *How we know:* After the earthquake of 26 December 2004, seismographs all over the world showed the ground everywhere moving by about a centimetre, and the ringing lasted for weeks.
- About half of the heat flowing out of the Earth is made by atoms breaking apart in the rock. *How we know:* Detectors in Japan and Italy have counted tiny particles called neutrinos that come up from these atoms inside the Earth.
- The Deccan lava erupted very close to the time the giant dinosaurs died out, about 66 million years ago. *How we know:* Two teams dated the lava layers in 2019 with two different methods. Both placed the main eruptions within a few hundred thousand years of the die-off.
- People can set off earthquakes by filling large dams, pumping water into deep rock, or mining. *How we know:* At Koyna in Maharashtra, earthquakes began soon after the dam was filled in 1962 and still rise and fall with the water level. One in 1967 was magnitude 6.3.

## How we found out
**Alfred Wegener, Marie Tharp, and Frederick Vine, Drummond Matthews and Lawrence Morley, who explained the magnetic stripes, 1912 to 1968.** For a long time, geologists were sure that the continents had always stood where they are. To explain why the same fossils lay on both sides of an ocean, they imagined land bridges that had later sunk. It was a fair idea, because nobody had ever felt a continent move. In 1912 Alfred Wegener, a German weather scientist, said the continents had once been joined and had drifted apart. He was laughed at, because he could not name a force strong enough. He died in Greenland in 1930. In 1952 Marie Tharp, an American map-maker, was plotting depth readings from ships. Women were not allowed on the ships, so she worked from the numbers. She found a deep valley running down the middle of the ridge under the Atlantic, as if the sea floor were pulling apart there. Her own colleague first brushed it off as 'girl talk'. Then earthquakes were plotted on her map, and they fell along the same valley. In 1963 striped magnetic patterns in the sea floor showed that new rock is made at the ridges. By the late 1960s, most geologists had changed their minds.  
*What people believed before:* Geologists held that the continents never move, and that sunken land bridges explained matching fossils. Marie Tharp's map of a rift in the ocean ridge, the earthquakes along it and the magnetic stripes beside it showed that the sea floor spreads and the continents travel.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Deccan eruptions, and not only an asteroid, ended the giant dinosaurs.** (Gerta Keller of Princeton University and geologists who work with her in India; *debated*)  
  For: The eruptions poured out gases that could heat the air, then cool it, and make the sea acid. Fossils in some places show sea life already in trouble before the asteroid fell.  
  Against: A layer of asteroid dust lies around the whole world at exactly that age, and the crater is in Mexico. A large study in 2020 found that the main die-off matches the asteroid, with the lava playing a smaller part.
- **Animals sense an earthquake hours before it comes.** (Martin Wikelski and his team, and many people's own stories from Bhuj, Latur and the 2004 tsunami; *debated*)  
  For: Tagged farm animals in Italy grew restless before earthquakes that were close by. Many animals feel faint shaking and smell gases that humans miss, so a real signal is possible.  
  Against: A review of 180 published studies in 2018 found that most reports were stories told afterwards, with no record of how the animals behaved on ordinary days. Animals grow restless for many reasons.
- **Hot spots are fed by plumes of hot rock rising from the bottom of the mantle.** (W. Jason Morgan, who proposed it in 1971; *debated*)  
  For: It explains chains of islands that get older in a line, such as Hawaii, and the trail from the Deccan to Réunion. Earthquake waves show slow, hot rock deep under some hot spots.  
  Against: Gillian Foulger and others point out that under many hot spots no deep column is found, and some chains do not age in a neat line. They say cracks in a stretched plate can explain them.
- **Mangrove forests shielded villages from the 2004 tsunami.** (K. Kathiresan and N. Rajendran, who studied 18 villages near Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu in 2005; *debated*)  
  For: Villages behind thick mangroves lost fewer lives and less property than villages on open shore nearby. The tangled roots and trunks slow moving water.  
  Against: Alexander Kerr and Andrew Baird looked at the same numbers again. They found that a village's height above the sea and its distance from the shore explained most of the difference. Against the largest waves, trees may do little.
- **The mantle is a sea of melted rock, and the plates float on it like boats.** (Many drawings, films and older school books; *rejected*)  
  For: Lava comes out of volcanoes, so it is easy to think there is lava everywhere underneath. Drawings often colour the mantle bright orange and red.  
  Against: S waves pass through the mantle, and they do not pass through liquids. So the mantle is solid. It only creeps, and magma is made in a few small regions.
- **Someone can tell you the day an earthquake will strike.** (Astrologers, some messages passed around on phones, and a few scientists over the years; *rejected*)  
  For: At Haicheng in China in 1975, people were moved out hours before a large earthquake, and it looked as if prediction had arrived.  
  Against: No method has worked a second time. Tangshan in 1976, Bhuj in 2001 and Sumatra in 2004 all came without warning. When predictions are checked against records, they do no better than guessing.

## Roots
You have pulled a box that sat still and then jumped, and you have followed India across an ocean. Now we ask what a moving ground tells us about stillness, about ourselves and about the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Sticking Box test, your hand never stopped pulling, yet the box sat still and then jumped. The quiet wait and the jump were one movement. The ground under you is like that box. It is being carried and squeezed right now, by a few centimetres a year. An earthquake is the last second of a hundred quiet years.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort the world into still things and moving things. We call the ground solid and an earthquake sudden. That sorting comes from the length of our lives and the speed of our eyes. It does not come from the Earth.  
*Evidence:* In the Sticking Box test the rubber band stretched all through the wait, so the jump was being prepared the whole time. Satellite markers show India moving about 5 centimetres every year. Sea shells lie in the rock at the top of Everest.

- *who am i:* The iron in your blood and the calcium in your bones came out of rock that volcanoes brought up and rivers ground down. You are a piece of the moving Earth that can stand up, look around and ask questions about the rest.
- *what is life:* Volcanoes gave the early Earth much of its air. Moving plates keep lifting fresh rock, and rain washes its minerals into soil and sea. Life and the restless ground have shaped each other for thousands of millions of years.
- *what is this world:* The world is a slow movement, and the things in it are that movement seen at one moment. A mountain is a wave of rock that takes fifty million years to rise and fall.

Follow one push. Heat deep in the Earth moves the mantle. The mantle and the sinking sea floor move the plates. The Indian plate carried a piece of Gondwana across an ocean, poured out the Deccan lava on the way, and lifted a sea floor into the Himalaya. The Himalaya make the rivers, the rivers make the plains, and the plains grow the rice on your plate. The same push broke the rock at Bhuj and lifted the sea in 2004. You cannot keep the parts you like and remove the parts you fear, because they are one movement. Consciousness is the ground that all of this arises in, and the rock, the river, the rice and the child who eats it are the same world showing up in different forms.

- **Isha Upanishad, verse 5:** “तदेजति तन्नैजति तद्दूरे तद्वन्तिके । / तदन्तरस्य सर्वस्य तदु सर्वस्यास्य बाह्यतः ॥” It moves, and it moves not. It is far, and it is near. It is inside all this, and it is outside all this. (The sage says that moving and not moving can both be true of one thing. The floor under you is like this: at rest to your hand, travelling to a satellite.)
- **Dhammapada, verse 277:** “Sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā ti, / yadā paññāya passati; / atha nibbindati dukkhe, / esa maggo visuddhiyā.” All things that are put together are impermanent. One who sees this with wisdom grows weary of suffering. This is the path to purity. (The Buddha taught that nothing made of parts stays as it is. A mountain range is put together by a collision and taken apart by rain. Even the Himalaya are an event.)
- **Heraclitus of Ephesus, as reported in Plato's Cratylus 402a:** Everything moves on and nothing stays at rest. You could not step twice into the same river. (Heraclitus chose a river because its movement is easy to see. Plate science says the same of the river bank, the bed rock and the continent, at a speed too slow for the eye.)
- Science (settled): No place on the Earth's surface is fixed. Every continent and every piece of sea floor is moving, and satellites measure the movement every day.
- Science (debated): James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis proposed in 1974 that living beings, air, sea and rock work together as one system that keeps the Earth fit for life. They called it Gaia. Scientists accept that life and rock change each other, and they argue about whether the whole can be called one living system.
- Science (open): Some scientists think moving plates are needed for a planet to keep life going over a very long time, because plates recycle the materials life uses. Only one planet with life is known, so this cannot be checked yet.
- Other view, Most geologists, and the philosophers called physicalists: Plates are rock moved by heat and weight, and nothing more is needed to explain them. Still and moving are only words for slow and fast. The rock has no awareness, and the Earth is not one being. The feeling that it all hangs together is a feeling in a human brain, and it is not a fact about the planet.
- Practice, Standing on the moving ground (3 min): Stand with bare feet on the floor and let your arms hang. Feel your weight press down through your heels and your toes. Close your eyes and breathe slowly five times. The floor feels still. Now remember that this floor, this building and this whole land are travelling north-east. They move about as fast as your fingernails grow. Stay with both things at once for five more breaths. Then open your eyes.
- Ask: If the ground is always moving, is there anything at all that is truly still? How would you look for it?
- Ask: The push that makes the earthquake also makes the mountain, the river and the rice. Can you want one without the other?
- Ask: Your bones were once rock. Where does the Earth end and where do you begin?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The floor feels perfectly still, and instruments say it is moving. Which one is true, and how would you decide? (hint: Think about a clock's hour hand. Does it move? How do you know?)
- *deep:* The mantle is solid in one second and flows over a million years. So what do the words solid and liquid really mean? (hint: Think of honey from the fridge, wet sand, and a ball of dough left on a plate overnight.)
- *big:* We know an earthquake will come to the Himalaya, but not when. How should people live with something that is certain and unknown at once? (hint: Think of other things in your life that are sure to happen on a day nobody knows.)
- *big:* The same lava that buried the land became the black soil that feeds people. Is a volcano good or bad? (hint: Ask who is answering, and when: a deer on that day, or a farmer 60 million years later.)
- *deep:* Nobody has ever seen the Earth's core. How sure can we be about a thing we know only through waves? (hint: Think of how you know what is inside a wrapped gift when you shake it.)
- *big:* If the plates stopped moving, there would be no earthquakes. Would the Earth be a better home for life, or a worse one? (hint: Think about where mountains, rivers, fresh soil and the gases of the air come from.)
- *big:* The people of Simeulue were saved by a song. Is a song knowledge in the same way that a seismograph reading is? (hint: Think about what each one remembers, how long it lasts, and who can use it.)

## Story: The Night the Needle Moved
*A made-up story. Meenu, Kaveri, Imran and Thatha are imagined, and so is this earthquake. The place is real: the Indian Tsunami Early Warning Centre in Hyderabad has watched the Indian Ocean day and night since 2007. The science is real too. Waves from an earthquake near the Nicobar Islands take about four minutes to reach Hyderabad, and an earthquake whose fault slips sideways lifts very little water.*

Meenu was nine, and she was meant to be asleep on the sofa at the back of the big room.

Her aunt Kaveri worked nights in Hyderabad, in a room full of screens that watched the Indian Ocean. Tonight there was nobody at home to stay with Meenu, so she had come along with a pillow and a book.

The room was quiet. On the largest screen, thin lines ran from left to right, one line for each seismograph. There was a line for Port Blair, a line for Chennai, a line for Hyderabad itself. All of them were nearly flat.

'Each line is the ground at one place,' Kaveri had told her. 'When the ground is quiet, the line is quiet.'

A young man called Imran sat at the next desk. It was his second week. Kaveri was teaching him the work, and he wrote everything she said in a small notebook.

At seven minutes past two, the line for Port Blair jumped.

It did not wiggle. It leapt into tall, crowded zigzags that filled its whole strip. A soft alarm began to sound. Kaveri put down her tea.

'Where?' said Imran.

'Wait,' said Kaveri. 'Watch the other lines. The waves will tell us.'

Meenu sat up. The line for Chennai was still flat. The line for Hyderabad was still flat. She put her hand on the floor. It felt just as it always felt.

Then the Chennai line jumped. And a little after that, about four minutes after the first alarm, the Hyderabad line began to shake too.

Meenu stared at it. That line was this building. That line was the floor under her hand. The ground beneath her was moving, right now, and she felt nothing at all.

'Near the Nicobar Islands,' said Kaveri, reading the screen. 'About two thousand kilometres from us. Magnitude 7.6. It is under the sea.'

Imran had gone pale. 'Under the sea. So a tsunami is coming.'

'We do not know that yet,' said Kaveri. 'Tell me what we need to find out.'

He looked at his notebook. 'If the sea floor went up or down.'

'Yes. A tsunami is not made by shaking. It is made when the sea floor lifts the water. If the two sides of the fault only slid past each other, sideways, the water above stays where it is.' She was already typing. 'The first waves say this one slid mostly sideways. But I will not trust one sign. There is a pressure sensor on the sea floor west of the Nicobars. If a wave is crossing the ocean, the sensor will feel the extra water pass over it.'

They waited. Meenu had never seen grown-ups wait like that. Nobody spoke. On the coast of Tamil Nadu, her grandfather was asleep in his house two streets from the beach at Nagapattinam. Thatha had lost his boat and two of his friends to the sea in 2004. He did not like to talk about that day.

A new line appeared on Kaveri's screen. It rose by a tiny amount and came down again.

'Three centimetres,' said Kaveri, and she let out a long breath. 'A wave of three centimetres in the open sea. That will harm nobody. We send the message: earthquake recorded, no tsunami threat to India.'

Imran sent it. His hands were not quite steady.

Later, when the alarm was off and fresh tea had come, he asked the question Meenu wanted to ask. 'Madam, could we have known? Yesterday, I mean. That it would happen tonight.'

'No,' said Kaveri. 'Nobody could. We know that the Indian plate is diving under those islands, about four centimetres every year. We know the rock there is being bent. We can say that the place will break again and again. We cannot say the night. I have done this work for fifteen years and I still cannot. What we can do is be awake when it happens.'

Meenu looked at the Hyderabad line. It had gone flat again.

'Athai,' she said, 'the floor really moved? Here?'

'By less than the width of a hair. But yes. The rock under the Nicobars and the rock under this sofa are one rock, Meenu. When it breaks there, it trembles here. The line only shows us what our bodies are too slow and too large to notice.'

In the morning Meenu rang her grandfather before he could hear the news from anyone else.

'Thatha, there was an earthquake in the night near the Nicobars. A big one. But the sea floor slid sideways. It did not lift the sea. So there is no wave. I watched them check.'

He was silent for a moment. 'It slid sideways,' he said slowly. 'In all these years nobody told me there were two kinds.' Then he asked, 'And how did you know before my radio did?'

'Because the floor in Hyderabad shook,' said Meenu. 'I had my hand on it. I just could not feel it.'

After the call she sat on the steps outside and pressed both palms flat on the warm stone. It felt completely still. She kept her hands there for a long time, wondering what else was moving that she had always called still.

**For the parent:** Meenu's own floor moved with an earthquake two thousand kilometres away, and her hand felt nothing. The ground that seems separate and still is one connected rock, and it is always carrying some movement that we are too slow to feel.

- Ask: Meenu felt nothing, and the line showed the floor moving. Which would you trust, your hand or the line? Why?
- Ask: Kaveri could not say which night the earthquake would come, but she was ready when it did. Is being ready a kind of knowing?
- Ask: What else around you might be moving that you have always called still?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Sticking Box: can you tell when it will jump? (35 min)
- **question:** If you pull a heavy box with a slow, steady pull on a rubber band, does the box move steadily? Can you tell when it will move next?
- **predict:** Before you pull, guess. Will the box glide along with your hand, or will it wait and then jump? Will every wait be the same length? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same box, the same rubber bands, the same cloth and the same slow pull of 1 centimetre for each count are used all the way through. Only the weight in the box changes: one packet in Round 1 and two packets in Round 2. Each round goes on for 8 jumps, because one jump alone can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A stretched rubber band can snap back. Pull low along the floor and keep faces away from the line of the band. If the band stretches to three times its slack length and the box has still not moved, stop. Use a thicker band, or two bands side by side.
- **record:** Fill in one row of the table for each jump, 8 rows for each round. Then write three lines: what I thought, what I saw, and why I think it happened.
- **results table:** Round and weight | Jump number | Counts waited before the jump | Front of the box at (cm) | Size of the jump (cm)
- **e1s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the box, the rubber bands and the cloth. Say that you will pull the band very slowly and steadily. Write down the guess and the reason before anything is pulled.  
  *Parent says:* I will pull this band slowly, 1 centimetre at a time, and never stop. Will the box glide along with my hand? Or will it wait and jump? Will every wait be the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A hand will pull the band slowly and never stop. Guess what the box will do. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: What is holding the box back when the band first pulls? (good: Friction.; The cloth grips the bottom of the box.; Its weight presses it onto the cloth.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say the box will slide smoothly because the pull is smooth. Write the guess and the reason at the top of the table.
- **e1s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put one packet in the box and close it. Tie the string round the box like a parcel. To make the chain, push one band halfway through the other. Pass it through its own loop and pull tight. Loop the chain onto the string at the front. Spread the cloth flat and lay the tape beside it. Put the front of the box at 0.  
  *Parent says:* The box is a patch of rock that is stuck. The band is rock that can bend and store energy. My hand is the plate, and a plate never stops. Try one pull to see that the band holds.  
  *Child mode:* Put one packet in the box. Tie the string round it. Hook on the rubber bands. Put the front of the box at 0.
  - Note: You need about 1.5 metres of clear floor. Hold the cloth flat with your feet or with books on its corners, so that only the box can move. Do one trial pull. The box should stay put while the band stretches, then jerk forward by 2 to 10 centimetres. If it glides without jumping, add the second packet or use a rougher cloth such as a towel. If the band becomes thin and pale before the box moves, use two bands side by side.
- **e1s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 1. Your child hooks one finger in the end of the band and rests that hand on the floor. The hand moves 1 centimetre along the tape for each count, about one count a second. Count aloud. When the box jumps, write the count and the place of the front of the box. Start again from 1. Go on for 8 jumps.  
  *Parent says:* Move your hand one centimetre for each count. Do not speed up and do not stop. Keep counting. When the box jumps, I will write your count and where the box is now. Then start again from one.  
  *Child mode:* Pull 1 centimetre for each count. Count aloud. When the box jumps, say your count. Then start again from 1.
  - Ask: Were the waits all the same? Were the jumps all the same size? (good: No, some waits were longer.; Some jumps were small and some were big.; They were close to each other but not the same.)
  - Note: With one 1 kilogram packet on a cotton sheet, waits of about 6 to 15 counts and jumps of 3 to 10 centimetres are typical. Yours will differ, so write what you see. A tiny shuffle of under 1 centimetre still counts as a jump. Write it down, because real faults have small slips too. Work out each jump size by taking away the box's last place from its new place. With 2 to 4 children, share the jobs: puller, counter, box watcher and writer.
- **e1s4 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 2. Put the second packet in the box and start again from 0. Pull in the same way for 8 jumps. This time your child also calls 'now' one count before the jump is expected. Put a tick in the margin for each call that was right.  
  *Parent says:* Now the box is twice as heavy. Pull just as before. This time, try to call 'now' one count before it jumps. You have watched 8 jumps already, so use what you learned.  
  *Child mode:* Add the second packet. Pull as before. Call 'now' just before you think it will jump.
  - Ask: How did the heavier box change the waits and the jumps? (good: The waits were longer.; The jumps were bigger.; The band stretched further before the box moved.)
  - Note: A heavier box presses harder on the cloth, so the band must stretch further before it wins. Most families see longer waits and bigger jumps. Most children get only one or two calls right out of 8, and some get none. That is the result. Do not help them to do better.
  - Check: During a wait, the box is not moving. Where is the energy of your pull going? → **Into the stretched rubber band**
- **e1s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the two columns of waits together. Find the shortest and the longest wait in each round. Ask your child to write what they thought, what they saw and why. Only then talk about real faults.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did the table show? Why do you think the box behaved like that? Then tell me this. What could you say about the next jump, and what could you not say?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Find the shortest wait and the longest wait. Write what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer says the pull was steady but the box moved in jumps, because friction held it until the band pulled hard enough. It says the waits were roughly alike but never exactly the same. So you could say 'it will jump again, in about 10 counts', but not 'it will jump on count 10'. After your child has said this, give the link. Seismologists can say a fault will slip again, and roughly how often. They cannot say the day. Then say what the model leaves out. A real fault is kilometres deep, nobody can see how stretched it is, and one slip can set off the fault next to it. So real earthquakes are far less regular than your box.
  - Think: You could watch the rubber band stretch. Nobody can see the rock stretch 20 kilometres down. What could scientists measure from the surface instead?
### e2 · Runny or sticky: which one traps the bubbles? (25 min)
- **question:** When air is blown into a runny liquid and into a thick, sticky one, which lets the bubbles out faster? Which one builds the steeper hill when it is poured?
- **predict:** Guess before you blow. In which tumbler will the bubbles be bigger? In which will they last longer after you stop? Which liquid will make the taller heap on a plate? Give a reason for each guess.
- **fair test:** Both tumblers are the same size and are filled to the same height. The same person blows through the same kind of straw, gently, for a slow count of 5. One tablespoon of each liquid is poured from the same height onto the same kind of plate. Only the liquid changes. Each test is done three times.
- **record:** Fill in the table for water and for batter. Draw the two heaps from the side. Then write what you thought, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Liquid | Size of the bubbles | Seconds until the bubbles are gone | Width of the puddle (cm) | Shape of the heap
- **e2s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stir 6 tablespoons of atta or besan with 6 tablespoons of water until smooth. It should drop slowly off the spoon. Half fill one tumbler with it and the other with water. Ask for the three guesses and write them down with the reasons.  
  *Parent says:* One liquid is runny and one is sticky. I will blow air into each. Where will the bubbles be bigger? Where will they last longer? Which will make a taller heap on a plate? Tell me why.  
  *Child mode:* Mix the flour and water until smooth. Half fill both tumblers. Make your three guesses and give reasons.
  - Note: If the batter runs off the spoon like milk, add a spoon of flour. If it will not drop at all, add a spoon of water. Do not explain anything about volcanoes yet.
- **e2s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a straw to the bottom of the water. Blow gently for a slow count of 5, then stop. Time how long the bubbles take to vanish. Do the same in the batter with the other straw. Do each one three times and write the results.  
  *Parent says:* Blow gently, the same each time, and never suck. Watch the size of the bubbles. Listen to them. When you stop, I will time how long they stay.  
  *Child mode:* Blow gently for a count of 5. Never suck. Watch the bubbles and listen. Count how long they stay.
  - Ask: What was different about the bubbles in the batter? (good: They were bigger.; They rose slowly and burst with a plop.; Some stayed in the batter after I stopped.)
  - Note: In water the bubbles are small, rise at once and are gone in about a second. In batter the air collects into large bubbles that push the surface up into a dome and burst with a plop, sometimes throwing specks. Some stay trapped for many seconds. Blowing hard into the water makes it splash, which spoils the test, so keep the breath gentle. Keep the tumblers on a plate to catch drips.
  - Check: In which liquid did the air find it harder to get out? → **The thick batter**
- **e2s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold a level tablespoon of water 2 centimetres above the middle of one plate and tip it out. Do the same with batter on the other plate. Wait 30 seconds. Measure the width of each puddle and look at both from the side.  
  *Parent says:* Now we pour. One spoon of each, from the same height. Wait for thirty counts. Which has spread wider? Which stands taller? Bend down and look from the side.  
  *Child mode:* Pour one spoon of each on a plate. Wait 30 seconds. Measure how wide each one spread.
  - Note: The water runs out into a wide, very thin sheet, often 10 centimetres or more across. The batter makes a heap only 4 to 6 centimetres across, with a rounded top. Pour a second and a third spoon onto the same spot to see the heap grow.
- **e2s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to write what they thought, what they saw and why. Then link it to volcanoes together. Afterwards pour the batter and water into the bin or a compost pot, because the straws have been in mouths. Wash the tumblers and plates.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you guessed, what you saw and why you think it happened. Now think of magma with bubbles in it. What would runny magma do at the top of a volcano? What would sticky magma do?  
  *Child mode:* Write what you thought, what you saw, and why. Then think: what would sticky magma do?
  - Note: A good answer says the sticky liquid held the air longer and made a taller heap. Then give the link. Runny magma lets its bubbles go, so lava pours out and spreads into wide, low hills or floods, as in Hawaii and the Deccan. Sticky magma traps its bubbles until the push blows it apart into ash, and it piles into a steep cone, as at Krakatau. Then say what the test leaves out. Real magma is hotter than 700 degrees Celsius. Its bubbles are not blown in. They come out of the magma itself when the squeeze on it drops, the way fizz appears when a soda bottle is opened.
  - Think: If you shook a closed soda bottle and opened it, which volcano would it be like? What is the batter in that picture?

### a2 · Biscuit plates: three ways for plates to meet (15 min)
- **record:** Draw the three edges from the side, with arrows for which way the biscuits moved, and write the name of a real place under each.
- **a2s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wash hands. Spread the curd on the plate. Lay two biscuits on it, edges touching. Press down lightly and slide them slowly apart by 2 centimetres. Look at what happens in the gap.  
  *Parent says:* These biscuits are two plates. The curd is the soft rock under them. Before you move them apart, guess what will happen in the gap. Now do it slowly.  
  *Child mode:* Lay two biscuits on the curd. Press lightly. Slide them slowly apart. Watch the gap.
  - Ask: What came up in the gap, and what would it be in the real Earth? (good: Curd came up.; Hot rock rising from below.; New sea floor.)
  - Note: The curd wells up between the biscuits. In the Earth, hot mantle rock rises into the gap, part of it melts, and it cools into new sea floor. This is happening now at the Carlsberg Ridge under the Arabian Sea.
- **a2s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take two new biscuits. Dip one edge of each in water for 3 seconds. Lay them on the curd and push the wet edges slowly together. Then take a dry biscuit and push it against another dry one. Press its edge down a little so that it slides underneath.  
  *Parent says:* Now two plates push together. First with soft edges. What do the edges do? Now with a hard edge pressed down. Where does that plate go?  
  *Child mode:* Wet one edge of two biscuits. Push the wet edges together. Then push a dry one under another.
  - Ask: Which real place is like the crumpled wet edges? (good: The Himalaya.; Where India pushes into Asia.)
  - Note: The wet edges crumple and rise into a ridge. That is how the Himalaya formed, where two continents met and neither could dive. The dry biscuit sliding under is like sea floor diving into the mantle, as the Indian plate does under the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
- **a2s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take two dry biscuits. Hold them edge to edge, pressing the edges together firmly. Now try to slide one forward and the other back. Feel what your hands feel. Draw the three edges, then eat the dry biscuits.  
  *Parent says:* Press the edges together hard and slide them past each other. Does it slide smoothly? What do you feel just before it moves? What falls off?  
  *Child mode:* Press two dry biscuits edge to edge. Slide one forward and one back. Feel what happens.
  - Ask: What did you feel in your fingers just before the biscuits moved? (good: They were stuck.; I had to push harder and harder.; Then they jerked and crumbs broke off.)
  - Note: The edges catch, the push builds, and then they jerk, with crumbs breaking off. That jerk is a small earthquake. Say that curd and biscuits show the shapes well, but they move in seconds. The real thing takes millions of years.
  - Think: Which of the three edges would you least like to live beside? Which would you most like to visit?

### a4 · Ready at home: a shake drill and a look round the flat (15 min)
- **record:** Write a family plan on one page: the safe spot in each room, the things you decided to move, and where you will meet outside. Put it on the fridge or by the door.
- **a4s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit together. Ask what your child would do if the floor began to shake right now. Listen to the whole answer before you say anything.  
  *Parent says:* Imagine the floor starts shaking now, hard enough that it is difficult to stand. What would you do in the first five seconds? Why that?  
  *Child mode:* Imagine the floor starts shaking now. Say what you would do first. Say why.
  - Note: Many children say they would run outside. In strong shaking people are hurt mostly by falling objects, breaking windows and stairs, so running is often the riskier choice. Hold that thought for the next step.
- **a4s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* In each room, choose the safest spot: under a sturdy table, or low beside an inside wall, away from windows and tall cupboards. Practise in each room. Drop to your knees and cover your head and neck with your arms. Hold on while a grown-up counts slowly to 20.  
  *Parent says:* India's disaster authority teaches three words: drop, cover, hold. Drop to your knees so you are not thrown down. Cover your head and neck. Hold on until the shaking stops. Let us find the best spot in this room.  
  *Child mode:* Find the safest spot in each room. Drop to your knees. Cover your head and neck. Hold on for a count of 20.
  - Ask: Why is under a strong table better than beside the window? (good: Things can fall on the table and not on me.; Windows can break.; The table protects my head.)
  - Note: If there is no table, crouch beside a bed or an inside wall and cover the head and neck. In bed, stay there and cover the head with a pillow. Once the shaking stops, walk out by the stairs to open ground. Never use the lift. Be honest that experts discuss one case. In a weak building, a person on the ground floor who is a few steps from open ground may do better to get out at once.
- **a4s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Walk through the home together and only look. Find heavy things kept high: boxes on cupboards, pictures or shelves above beds, a television that could tip. A grown-up decides what to move lower and does the moving later.  
  *Parent says:* Now be a detective. If this room shook hard, what could fall? What is above your pillow? What is above the sofa? Show me, and I will decide what to move.  
  *Child mode:* Walk through your home and look. Find heavy things kept high up. Show a grown-up.
  - Note: The child only points. The lifting and any fixing of cupboards to walls is a grown-up's job for later. The most useful single change is to clear heavy things from above beds.
- **a4s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Agree on an open place outside to meet after any earthquake. Then learn the beach rule together and say it aloud. Write the family plan.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the beach rule. If the ground shakes hard or for a long time, or if the sea pulls far back, leave the beach at once. Go to high ground or far inland, and tell others. Do not wait for anyone to announce it.  
  *Child mode:* Choose where your family will meet outside. Learn the beach rule and say it aloud.
  - Ask: The sea has pulled back and the wet sand is full of shells. What do you do? (good: Leave at once for high ground.; Go far from the sea and tell others.; I stay away from the bared sand.)
  - Note: In 2004 many people walked out onto the bared sea bed to look. A girl of ten named Tilly Smith, on a beach in Thailand, remembered a school lesson, told her parents, and the beach was cleared in time. The first wave is often not the largest, so stay away for several hours.
  - Think: Being ready does not stop an earthquake. What does it change?

### c1 · The Lemon Tower: build something that stands when the ground moves (35 min)
- **problem:** Build a tower from newspaper and tape that holds a lemon at least 30 centimetres above a tray. Then shake the tray. The tower passes if the lemon is still up after 10 seconds of strong shaking. There are many good answers, and builders in different parts of India found different ones.
- **your turn:** Now change the rules yourself. Make the tower twice as tall, or carry two lemons, or use only 5 sheets. Or invent a way nobody has listed here. Draw your design first and say why you think it will work.
- **constraints:** Use only 10 sheets of newspaper and 1 metre of tape.; The lemon must sit at least 30 centimetres above the tray.; The tower stands on a tray, a large steel plate or a chopping board that someone can slide.; You may tape the tower's feet to the tray with 4 small pieces from your 1 metre.
- **general:** A wide base and a narrow top. A tower that is wider at the bottom is harder to tip.; Triangles. A square frame leans over into a diamond when it is pushed. A slanting strip across it makes two triangles, which hold their shape.; A light top. Keep the weight low. The more weight is high up, the harder the shaking throws it about.
- **creative:** Let the ground move without the building. A tower standing on round pencils stays nearly in place while the tray slides under it. Engineers call this base isolation. The hospital at Bhuj was rebuilt in this way after 2001.; Go round. The bhunga houses of Kutch are round, with a light roof. Builders there chose the shape after an earthquake in 1819, and many bhungas stood in 2001 when newer buildings fell.; Bend, do not break. In the timber-laced walls of Kashmir and the bamboo and reed houses of Assam, light wooden frames flex and soak up the shaking. A rigid wall cracks.

  *Parent does:* Read the problem and the four rules together. Give your child paper and a pencil. Ask for a sketch of the tower before any newspaper is touched, with one sentence on why it will stand.  
  *Parent says:* You have 10 sheets, 1 metre of tape and a lemon. The lemon must stay 30 centimetres up while I shake the tray. Draw your idea first. What will stop it from tipping?

  - Ask: What do you think will fail first when the tray shakes? (good: The legs will bend.; It will tip over.; The lemon will roll off.)

  *Parent says:* Roll the tubes tight, because a tight tube is much stiffer than a loose one. Build your design. Tell me if you change your mind about something as you go.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the newspaper into tight tubes. Tape them. Build your tower. Check that it is 30 centimetres tall.
  - Note: Tear the tape by hand, or have pieces ready in advance. A sheet rolled from the corner makes a tube about 70 centimetres long that can be folded or torn into shorter pieces. If the tape is running out, suggest slotting one tube inside another.
- **c1s3 (8 min)**

  - Ask: Where did the tower bend or break first? (good: At the bottom of the legs.; At a joint.; It leaned into a diamond shape.)
  - Note: Keep the shaking the same for every tower, so that the test is fair. Most first towers pass gentle and fail at medium, usually by leaning over at the joints or tipping from the base.
- **c1s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the usual solutions and the surprising ones together. Choose one change and make it. Test again at the level that failed. Then try one more thing. Untape the feet and stand the tower on a small book resting on 6 round pencils. Shake the tray gently along the direction the pencils roll.  
  *Parent says:* Builders have found many answers. Some make the building stiffer. Some let it bend. Some let the ground move without it. Which one do you want to try? What happened to the lemon on the pencils?  
  *Child mode:* Choose one change and make it. Test again. Then stand the tower on round pencils and shake.

Engineers aim for a building that may be damaged but does not fall, so that the people inside can walk out.

## Worksheets
### The moving ground (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which layer of the Earth do we live on?  
   Options: The crust / The mantle / The outer core  
   **Answer:** The crust. *Why:* We live on the crust, the thin outer layer. The mantle begins tens of kilometres below us, and nobody has ever reached it.
2. (fill) The layer of liquid metal deep inside the Earth is the outer ____.  
   **Answer:** ['core']. *Why:* The outer core is liquid iron and nickel. The inner core, at the very centre, is solid.
3. (truefalse) The mantle is a sea of melted rock.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The mantle is solid rock that creeps very slowly. We know because S waves pass through it, and S waves do not pass through liquids.
4. (label) Draw a slice of the Earth from the surface to the centre. Label the crust, mantle, outer core and inner core.  
   **Answer:** Four layers in the right order, with the crust drawn as the thinnest and the mantle as the thickest.. *Why:* The order from outside in is crust, mantle, outer core, inner core. A strong drawing makes the crust a thin line, because it is less than one hundredth of the way down.
5. (mcq) What is a plate?  
   Options: A huge moving slab of crust and upper mantle / A layer of lava under the ground / A kind of earthquake wave  
   **Answer:** A huge moving slab of crust and upper mantle. *Why:* A plate is a slab of the crust and the stiff top of the mantle. If you chose lava, remember that the rock under the plates is solid and only creeps.
6. (match) Match each plate movement to what it makes.  
   **Answer:** Plates move apart → New sea floor forms in the gap; A sea-floor plate pushes against another plate → One plate dives and volcanoes grow above; Two continents push together → Rock crumples into high mountains; Plates slide past each other → The edges stick, then jerk. *Why:* Each kind of edge leaves its own mark. Gaps fill with new rock, diving plates feed volcanoes, continents crumple, and sliding edges stick and slip.
7. (order) Put these in order to tell how an earthquake happens.  
   **Answer:** ['The plates keep moving', 'The rock at the fault stays stuck', 'The rock bends and stores energy', 'The stuck rock suddenly slips', 'Waves of shaking spread out']. *Why:* The plates never stop, but friction holds the fault. The rock bends like a stretched rubber band until the stuck patch gives way, and the stored energy travels out as waves.
8. (mcq) What do we call the place on the surface straight above the start of an earthquake?  
   Options: The epicentre / The focus / The crater  
   **Answer:** The epicentre. *Why:* The epicentre is on the surface. The focus is the place underground where the slip begins. A crater belongs to a volcano.
9. (fill) Melted rock that is still under the ground is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['magma']. *Why:* It is magma while it is underground and lava once it comes out onto the surface.
10. (truefalse) Lava is magma that has reached the surface.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It is the same melted rock. Only the name changes when it comes out.
11. (sort) Sort each word by where it belongs.  
   **Answer:** fault → Earthquake; magma → Volcano; seismic wave → Earthquake; vent → Volcano; epicentre → Earthquake; ash → Volcano; plate edge → Both; crater → Volcano. *Why:* Faults, epicentres and seismic waves describe an earthquake. Magma, vents, ash and craters describe a volcano. Plate edges are where most of both are found.
12. (mcq) Why does the edge of the Pacific Ocean have so many volcanoes and earthquakes?  
   Options: Many plate edges run along it / The water there is the deepest / It is the hottest ocean  
   **Answer:** Many plate edges run along it. *Why:* Sea-floor plates dive under other plates all round the Pacific. Depth and warmth of the water do not make earthquakes. The moving rock does.
13. (write) The ground feels still. Give two pieces of evidence that it is moving.  
   **Answer:** Any two of these: satellite markers move a few centimetres a year. Sea shells are found on Himalayan peaks. Earthquakes show sudden slips. The sea floor is youngest at the ridges. Matching fossils lie on lands now far apart.. *Why:* A strong answer gives evidence someone measured or found, and does not only say that a book says so.
14. (write) In your own words, why can nobody tell the day of the next earthquake?  
   **Answer:** A good answer says the stuck rock is deep and cannot be seen, that nobody can measure how close it is to slipping, and that tiny differences decide when it goes. It may use the Sticking Box as an example.. *Why:* We know where the squeeze is building, but not the breaking point of the rock. In the Sticking Box test the waits were never exactly the same, even though you could see the band.
15. (perspective) Two farmers live on the slope of a volcano that last erupted 90 years ago.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side, gives a reason, and names something to find out, such as how often the volcano erupts, if it is being watched, and how fast people could leave.. *Why:* Both are right about a fact. Volcanic soil is rich, and a quiet volcano can wake. Millions of people around the world make this choice, and many stay and keep a plan to leave quickly.
16. (count) A plate moves 4 centimetres every year. How many centimetres does it move in 25 years?  
   **Answer:** 100. *Why:* 4 times 25 is 100 centimetres, which is 1 metre. In one long human life a plate moves about as far as a child is tall.
### India on the move (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (order) Put India's journey in order, from the oldest event to the newest.  
   **Answer:** ['India is part of Gondwana', 'India splits from Madagascar', 'The Deccan lava floods pour out', 'India touches Asia', 'The Himalaya rise']. *Why:* India left Gondwana, lost Madagascar about 88 million years ago, crossed a hot spot about 66 million years ago and met Asia about 50 million years ago. The Himalaya have been rising since.
2. (mcq) What are the Deccan Traps mostly made of?  
   Options: Basalt, which is cooled lava / Sand laid down by rivers / Old coral reefs  
   **Answer:** Basalt, which is cooled lava. *Why:* They are layer upon layer of basalt from lava floods. Sandstone and coral rock form in water, in a quite different way.
3. (truefalse) Barren Island in the Andaman Sea is an active volcano.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It erupted in 1991 after well over a century of quiet, and it has erupted on and off ever since.
4. (fill) The tsunami of 2004 began with a giant earthquake under the sea near the island of ____.  
   **Answer:** ['Sumatra']. *Why:* The fault off Sumatra, in Indonesia, slipped along about 1,300 kilometres, as far north as the Andaman Islands.
5. (mcq) What makes most tsunamis?  
   Options: A sudden jump of the sea floor / A very strong wind / The pull of the Moon  
   **Answer:** A sudden jump of the sea floor. *Why:* A tsunami starts when the sea floor lifts or drops and moves all the water above it. Wind makes ordinary waves. The Moon's pull makes tides, which are a different thing.
6. (match) Match each place to what you would find there.  
   **Answer:** Bhuj, Gujarat → A large earthquake far inside a plate; St Mary's Islands, Karnataka → Six-sided columns of old lava; Barren Island, Andaman Sea → A volcano that still erupts; Mahabaleshwar, Maharashtra → Hills cut into steps of basalt; Kali Gandaki river, Nepal → Ammonite fossils in black stones. *Why:* Each place holds one piece of India's story: old cracks that still slip, lava from the split with Madagascar, a diving plate, the Deccan floods and the lost Tethys Sea.
7. (count) A tsunami crosses deep ocean at 700 kilometres an hour. A coast is 1,400 kilometres away. How many hours until it arrives?  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 1,400 divided by 700 is 2 hours. That is enough time to clear a coast, if a warning is sent and people know what to do.
8. (mcq) How much more does the ground move in a magnitude 7 earthquake than in a magnitude 6?  
   Options: 10 times more / Twice as much / A little more  
   **Answer:** 10 times more. *Why:* Each whole step in magnitude means ten times more ground movement. If you chose 'a little more', remember that the scale multiplies and does not add.
9. (truefalse) An earthquake has one magnitude, but the shaking it causes differs from place to place.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Magnitude measures the size at the source. How hard the ground shakes at your home depends on your distance and on the soil under you.
10. (fill) Rain, rivers, wind and ice wear the land down slowly. This is called ________.  
   **Answer:** erosion. *Why:* Erosion is the slow wearing away of land. Plates lift and fold land. Erosion wears it down. Both happen at the same time.
11. (sort) Sort each clue: does it belong to runny lava or to sticky magma?  
   **Answer:** bubbles escape easily → Runny; builds a wide, low hill → Runny; blasts out clouds of ash → Sticky; builds a steep cone → Sticky; spreads in floods, as in the Deccan → Runny; traps its bubbles until it bursts → Sticky. *Why:* Runny lava lets its bubbles go and flows far. Sticky magma holds its bubbles until the push blows it apart, and what falls builds a steep cone.
12. (write) Bhuj is far from any plate edge. Explain how it could still have a great earthquake.  
   **Answer:** A good answer says that India is squeezed as it pushes into Asia, that old cracks from the time India broke from other lands lie under Kutch, and that one of them slipped under the squeeze.. *Why:* The push at the edge is felt far inside the plate. Old faults are weak places, and they can store the squeeze for a very long time before they slip.
13. (draw) Draw India meeting Asia from the side. Show the Himalaya. Star one place where rock sticks and slips.  
   **Answer:** A slab from the left sliding under or against a slab on the right, with crumpled mountains above the join and a star on the sloping surface between the two.. *Why:* The Indian plate pushes under the edge of Asia along a sloping fault. The rock above crumples into the Himalaya, and the great earthquakes start on that sloping surface.
14. (perspective) About 66 million years ago the giant dinosaurs died out. Scientists do not fully agree why.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view, gives one piece of evidence for it, and names a test, such as dating the lava layers more exactly or finding fossils from just before the asteroid.. *Why:* This is a live debate. The asteroid has the most support, but the lava's part is still being measured. No answer here is marked wrong if it gives evidence.
15. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Bhuj, Latur, Koyna, Kathmandu.  
   **Answer:** Kathmandu, because it lies along a plate edge, in the Himalaya. The earthquakes at Bhuj, Latur and Koyna happened far inside the Indian plate.. *Why:* Three of these places surprised people because they are far from the edge. Any other answer with a true reason also counts.
16. (write) A friend says South India is safe from earthquakes. What would you tell her? Give an example.  
   **Answer:** A good answer says the danger is lower but not zero, and gives an example: Latur in 1993, Koyna in 1967, Coimbatore in 1900, or the tsunami of 2004 that reached Tamil Nadu and Kerala from far away.. *Why:* Rare is not the same as never. Earthquakes inside a plate come seldom, so people are less prepared for them.
17. (fill) The name Gondwana comes from the land of the ____ people of central India.  
   **Answer:** ['Gond', 'Gonds']. *Why:* Geologists named the old southern land after the region of the Gond people, where the first rocks of that age were studied.
### Read the evidence (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does a P wave reach a seismograph before an S wave from the same earthquake?  
   Options: It travels faster / It sets off earlier / It is stronger  
   **Answer:** It travels faster. *Why:* Both leave at the same moment. P waves travel at about 6 kilometres a second in the crust and S waves at about 4, so the P wave wins the race.
2. (count) A P wave travels 6 kilometres a second. A seismograph is 300 kilometres away. After how many seconds does it arrive?  
   **Answer:** 50. *Why:* 300 divided by 6 is 50 seconds. Seismologists use arrival times like this from many stations to find where an earthquake began.
3. (write) After a great earthquake, S waves never arrive on the far side of the Earth. What does this tell us, and why?  
   **Answer:** A good answer says there is a liquid layer deep inside, the outer core, because S waves shake rock from side to side and a liquid does not spring back sideways.. *Why:* The missing S waves are a shadow cast by the liquid outer core. This is how people learned about a layer nobody has seen.
4. (truefalse) People have drilled through the crust and into the mantle.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The deepest hole is about 12 kilometres deep, and the crust under land is about 35 kilometres thick. Everything known about the mantle comes from waves and from rock that volcanoes bring up.
5. (mcq) Where is the rock of the sea floor youngest?  
   Options: At the mid-ocean ridges / Beside the continents / It is the same age everywhere  
   **Answer:** At the mid-ocean ridges. *Why:* New sea floor forms at the ridges and moves away on both sides, so it gets older with distance. This was strong evidence that plates move.
6. (write) Fossils of one plant, Glossopteris, lie in India, Africa, Australia and Antarctica. Give two possible explanations. Which fits better?  
   **Answer:** One: the lands were once joined and later moved apart. Two: the seeds crossed the oceans, or land bridges once joined the continents and later sank. A good answer says which fits better and why, for example that the rocks and many other fossils match too.. *Why:* Both ideas were held by real scientists. The joined-lands idea won because the sea floor shows no sunken bridges, and because the plates can now be measured moving.
7. (match) Match each person to what they found.  
   **Answer:** Inge Lehmann → The Earth has a solid inner core; Alfred Wegener → The continents were once joined; Marie Tharp → A rift valley runs along the ocean ridge; Zhang Heng → An instrument that sensed distant earthquakes, in 132 CE; Richard Oldham → Earthquake waves show the Earth has a core. *Why:* These five worked in Denmark, Germany, the United States, China and India. Oldham did his work on earthquake waves at the Geological Survey of India, after the Assam earthquake of 1897.
8. (fill) A place where volcanoes form far from any plate edge, above unusually hot rock, is called a hot ____.  
   **Answer:** ['spot']. *Why:* Hawaii and Réunion sit on hot spots. What feeds them is still argued.
9. (truefalse) The volcanoes of Hawaii sit on the edge of a plate.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Hawaii is in the middle of the Pacific plate, thousands of kilometres from any edge. It is one of the places that breaks the rule.
10. (perspective) Can other animals sense an earthquake before it comes?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view and describes a test, such as tagging animals in many places for years and counting how often they were restless with no earthquake.. *Why:* Both sides are careful scientists. One has found a pattern on one farm. The other asks for the same pattern in many places before believing it. Neither has been shown wrong.
11. (order) Put the steps of a tsunami warning in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The sea floor jumps in an earthquake', 'Seismographs record the waves within minutes', 'A sensor on the sea floor feels the wave pass', 'The warning centre sends an alert', 'People on the coast move to high ground']. *Why:* Seismic waves travel much faster than the tsunami, so the earthquake is found first. The sea-floor sensor then confirms that a wave was really made, and the alert goes out.
12. (mcq) How does an earthquake alert on a phone work?  
   Options: Sensors catch the first fast waves and send word ahead / It predicts the earthquake a day before / It watches how animals behave  
   **Answer:** Sensors catch the first fast waves and send word ahead. *Why:* The earthquake has already begun. Sensors near it catch the P waves, and a radio message outruns the slower, stronger waves by a few seconds. It is a warning and not a prediction.
13. (draw) Draw what lies under Barren Island. Label the diving plate, where rock melts, and the magma's path.  
   **Answer:** A sloping slab going down under the island, a patch of melting above the slab at depth, and a pipe or chain of blobs rising from it to the volcano.. *Why:* The Indian plate dives under the Burma plate there. Water from the diving slab helps the rock above it to melt, and the magma rises to feed the volcano. A drawing with these three parts is a good model, even if the shapes differ.
14. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Deccan Traps, Barren Island, St Mary's Islands, the Himalaya.  
   **Answer:** The Himalaya, because they are sea-floor rock that was folded and lifted. The other three are made of lava.. *Why:* Three were built by volcanoes and one by a collision. Any other answer with a true reason also counts.
15. (write) Design a fair test of this claim: dogs bark more before earthquakes. What would you record, and for how long?  
   **Answer:** A good answer records barking every day for a long time, on ordinary days as well as before earthquakes, in many homes, and has the records written down before anyone knows an earthquake came.. *Why:* A memory after the event is not evidence, because people remember the barking that fits. A fair test counts the ordinary days too.
16. (sort) Sort each statement: is it settled, or still argued by scientists?  
   **Answer:** Plates move a few centimetres a year → Settled; The outer core is liquid → Settled; Hot spots are fed by plumes from the deep mantle → Still argued; Deccan lava helped to end the giant dinosaurs → Still argued; India was once joined to Antarctica → Settled; Farm animals sense earthquakes hours ahead → Still argued. *Why:* Settled does not mean it can never change. It means the evidence is strong and has been checked many ways. The argued ones still need better evidence.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) How do we know what the inside of the Earth is like?  
   Options: From how earthquake waves travel through it / From holes drilled to the centre / From photographs taken underground  
   **Answer:** From how earthquake waves travel through it. *Why:* Earthquake waves bend, slow down or stop as they pass through different layers. The deepest hole is only 12 kilometres deep, out of 6,370.
2. (mcq) Which layer of the Earth is the thickest?  
   Options: The mantle / The crust / The inner core  
   **Answer:** The mantle. *Why:* The mantle is about 2,900 kilometres thick. The crust is the thinnest layer, and the inner core is a ball about 1,220 kilometres in radius.
3. (mcq) About how fast do plates move?  
   Options: As fast as fingernails grow / As fast as a person walks / They stay where they are  
   **Answer:** As fast as fingernails grow. *Why:* Plates move 2 to 10 centimetres a year. If you chose 'they stay', remember that satellites measure the movement every day.
4. (mcq) What happens where two plates carrying continents push together?  
   Options: High mountains rise / A new ocean opens / A hot spot forms  
   **Answer:** High mountains rise. *Why:* Continent rock is too light to dive, so it crumples and piles up, as in the Himalaya. A new ocean opens where plates move apart.
5. (mcq) What makes the ground shake in an earthquake?  
   Options: Stuck rock suddenly slipping / Wind rushing out of caves / The core spinning faster  
   **Answer:** Stuck rock suddenly slipping. *Why:* Rock bends while a fault is stuck and then slips. The wind idea is very old and was given up when faults were studied.
6. (truefalse) The focus of an earthquake is underground, and the epicentre is on the surface above it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The slip begins at the focus. The epicentre is the point on the map straight above.
7. (mcq) Why do some volcanoes explode?  
   Options: Sticky magma traps the gas / They are taller than others / Their lava is colder than ice  
   **Answer:** Sticky magma traps the gas. *Why:* Thick magma holds its bubbles until the push blows it apart. Runny magma lets the gas out, and its lava flows quietly.
8. (mcq) A volcano has been quiet for 300 years. What is the most truthful thing to say?  
   Options: It may still erupt again / It is certainly dead / It was never a volcano  
   **Answer:** It may still erupt again. *Why:* A volcano's life is far longer than 300 years. Barren Island was quiet for well over a century before 1991.
9. (mcq) Where do most earthquakes and volcanoes happen?  
   Options: Along the edges of plates / Evenly all over the Earth / Only in hot countries  
   **Answer:** Along the edges of plates. *Why:* Marked on a map, they fall into narrow belts along plate edges. Iceland and Alaska are cold and have plenty of both.
10. (mcq) Which of these lies far from any plate edge?  
   Options: Latur / Kathmandu / The Nicobar Islands  
   **Answer:** Latur. *Why:* Latur is deep inside the Indian plate, which is why its earthquake in 1993 surprised people. The other two lie along plate edges.
11. (mcq) What built the Himalaya?  
   Options: India pushing into Asia / A giant volcano / The Deccan lava floods  
   **Answer:** India pushing into Asia. *Why:* The sea floor between India and Asia was squeezed and lifted. That is why the high rocks hold sea shells and no lava.
12. (mcq) Where is India's active volcano?  
   Options: Barren Island, in the Andaman Sea / Mahabaleshwar, in Maharashtra / Bhuj, in Gujarat  
   **Answer:** Barren Island, in the Andaman Sea. *Why:* Barren Island sits above a diving plate. Mahabaleshwar is old Deccan lava that stopped flowing about 66 million years ago.
13. (mcq) Which of these can scientists do today?  
   Options: Warn seconds after an earthquake starts / Name the day a week ahead / Stop a fault from slipping  
   **Answer:** Warn seconds after an earthquake starts. *Why:* Sensors catch the first waves and send an alert ahead of the strong shaking. Nobody can name the day or stop the slip.
14. (mcq) Rain and rivers wear a hill down a little every year, and carry the grains away. What is this called?  
   Options: Erosion / Lava / A seismic wave  
   **Answer:** Erosion. *Why:* Erosion is the slow wearing away of land by rain, rivers, wind and ice. Lava is melted rock that has reached the surface, and a seismic wave carries an earthquake's energy. Neither wears land away.
15. (mcq) The floor starts to shake hard while you are indoors. What do you do first?  
   Options: Drop, cover your head, hold on / Run to the lift / Stand at the window to look  
   **Answer:** Drop, cover your head, hold on. *Why:* Most injuries come from falling things and broken windows. Get low under something sturdy and protect your head and neck. Lifts can jam.

## Applications
- Tsunami warning: the centre at INCOIS in Hyderabad watches seismographs and sea-floor sensors day and night, and sends alerts to every coastal state.
- Building rules: India's earthquake map tells engineers how strong a school, a hospital or a bridge must be in each district.
- Phone alerts: phones and sensors in parts of India can now warn people a few seconds before strong shaking arrives.
- Heat from the ground: in volcanic places such as Iceland and Indonesia, hot rock underground makes steam that drives power stations. India is testing this at Puga in Ladakh.
- Farming: the black soil made from Deccan basalt grows much of India's cotton, jowar and tur dal.
- Stone to build with: basalt was carved into the caves of Ajanta, Ellora and Elephanta, and granite into the temples of Thanjavur and Mamallapuram.
- Finding metals: copper, gold and many other metals collect where magma once rose, so geologists use the plate map to decide where to search.

## Spotted around us
- A pumice stone for scrubbing feet. It is volcanic froth that cooled with its gas bubbles still inside, which is why it floats on water.
- The granite of a grinding stone or a kitchen counter. It was magma that cooled slowly, deep underground, and never reached the surface.
- Black soil in the fields of northern Karnataka, Maharashtra and Telangana, with wide cracks in the dry season. It is Deccan lava that has broken down.
- Blue boards along the beaches of Tamil Nadu, Kerala and Odisha that point the way to higher ground in a tsunami.
- The notice beside a lift that says: in case of earthquake or fire, do not use the lift.
- Hot springs, such as Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh and Bakreshwar in West Bengal, where water heated deep in the crust comes up.
- The stepped, flat-topped hills seen from a train through the Western Ghats between Mumbai and Pune. Each step is one old lava flow.

## Open questions
- **Why does one small slip on a fault stop, while another grows into a giant earthquake?** Both start in the same way. Nobody can see the stuck patches kilometres underground, and tiny differences in the rock seem to decide the outcome.
- **When did plates first begin to move, and why does only the Earth have them?** Venus is nearly the same size and has no moving plates. Estimates for the Earth's first plates range from 4,000 million to under 1,000 million years ago, because so little rock from that time is left.
- **What feeds the hot spots, such as the one that made the Deccan Traps?** Earthquake waves give only a blurred picture of the deep mantle, and the pictures can be read as deep plumes or as shallow melting.
- **How much did the Deccan eruptions add to the death of the giant dinosaurs?** The lava and the asteroid came within a few hundred thousand years of each other, and rock dating is not yet fine enough to pull their effects apart.
- **Do other animals sense something before an earthquake, and if so, what?** Large earthquakes are rare and cannot be arranged, so it takes many years of tagging animals in many places to get enough fair records.
- **When will the next great Himalayan earthquake come?** Measurements show the squeeze is building, but nobody can measure how close the deep rock is to its breaking point.

## Connected maths
- The Indian plate moves about 5 centimetres every year. A girl is 9 years old. How far has the ground under her home travelled since she was born? **45 centimetres.**. 5 times 9 is 45. That is about the length of her arm. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- At 5 centimetres a year, how many years does the Indian plate take to move 1 kilometre? One kilometre is 100,000 centimetres. **20,000 years.**. 100,000 divided by 5 is 20,000. The first farming villages are only about 10,000 years old. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L4`)
- In your scale drawing, 1 centimetre stands for 500 kilometres. The mantle is about 2,900 kilometres thick. How many centimetres thick is it on the drawing? **5.8 centimetres.**. 2,900 divided by 500 is 5.8. On the same drawing the crust is less than 1 millimetre. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L4`)
- Each step of 1 in magnitude means the ground moves 10 times more. How many times more does the ground move in a magnitude 8 earthquake than in a magnitude 5? **1,000 times more.**. From 5 to 8 is three steps. 10 times 10 times 10 is 1,000. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- P waves travel about 6 kilometres every second and S waves about 4. A town is 120 kilometres from an earthquake. How many seconds pass between the P wave and the S wave? **10 seconds.**. 120 divided by 6 is 20 seconds for the P wave. 120 divided by 4 is 30 seconds for the S wave. 30 take away 20 is 10. (queues `math.time.speed.L4`)
- In the Sticking Box test, the waits before four jumps were 9, 12, 7 and 12 counts. What was the average wait? Was any wait exactly the average? **10 counts. No wait was exactly 10.**. 9 and 12 and 7 and 12 make 40. 40 divided by 4 is 10. None of the four waits was 10. (queues `math.data.averages.L4`)
- The deepest hole ever drilled is 12 kilometres deep. The centre of the Earth is about 6,400 kilometres down. About what fraction of the way did the drill go? **About 1 part in 500.**. 6,400 divided by 12 is about 533, so the hole is about one five-hundredth of the way. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- The Deccan lava covers about 500,000 square kilometres. India's land is about 3,300,000 square kilometres. Is the lava nearer to one tenth, one sixth or one third of India? **Nearest to one sixth.**. One tenth of 3,300,000 is 330,000. One sixth is 550,000. One third is 1,100,000. 500,000 is closest to 550,000. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)

## Warm-up
- Name the layers of the Earth from the outside in. → Crust, mantle, outer core, inner core.
- Is the mantle solid or liquid? → Solid rock that creeps very slowly.
- About how fast do plates move? → A few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow.
- What is the difference between magma and lava? → Magma is underground. Lava has come out onto the surface.
- What built the Himalaya? → India pushing into Asia.
- Where is India's active volcano? → Barren Island, in the Andaman Sea.
- Can anyone tell the day of an earthquake in advance? → No. Scientists can say where one is likely, but not when.
- The sea suddenly pulls far back from the beach. What do you do? → Leave at once for high ground and tell others.
- What does each step of 1 in magnitude mean? → The ground moves ten times more.
