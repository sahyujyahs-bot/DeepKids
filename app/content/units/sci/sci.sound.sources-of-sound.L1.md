# Where sounds come from, and what makes them loud or soft
`sci.sound.sources-of-sound.L1`

**Summary.** A closed bottle of rice sits on the table in silence. Shake it, and it rattles. Every sound comes from something moving: hands clapping, rain hitting a window, a crow calling, steam rushing out of a pressure cooker. The thing that makes a sound is called its sound source. Your child hunts for sound sources at home, tests which home-made shaker is loudest, and finds that a harder shake or a harder hit makes a louder sound.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes. Sit very still and listen for one whole minute. How many sounds can you hear? What is making each one?* Even a quiet room is full of sounds: the fan, a bird outside, a scooter in the street, your own breathing. Every one of those sounds is made by something that is moving. In this unit you hunt for the things that make sounds. You also find out what makes a sound loud, and what makes it soft.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what a sound source is: the thing that makes a sound.
1. By the end I can name sound sources at home and outside: people, other animals, things, and wind and rain.
2. By the end I can show that a sound comes from something moving, and that a still thing makes no sound.
3. By the end I can make the same thing sound loud or soft, by shaking or hitting it harder or more gently.
4. By the end I can find a sound source with my eyes closed, by listening carefully.

## Terms
- **Sound**: A sound is anything we hear with our ears. *e.g.* A dog barking, a temple bell ringing and your friend's voice are all sounds.
- **Sound source**: A sound source is the thing that makes a sound. *e.g.* When a pressure cooker whistles, the pressure cooker is the sound source.
- **Listen**: To listen is to hear on purpose. You keep still and pay attention to the sounds. *e.g.* Close your eyes and listen. Can you hear the fan?
- **Loud**: A loud sound is a big sound. You can hear it even from far away. *e.g.* Thunder, a bus horn and the drums at a temple festival are loud.
- **Soft**: A soft sound is a small, gentle sound. Soft sounds are also called quiet sounds. *e.g.* A whisper, a clock ticking and leaves rustling are soft.
- **Voice**: Your voice is the sound you make when you talk, sing or shout. It starts in your throat, and your mouth shapes it. *e.g.* When you sing at Onam or say good morning, you are using your voice.

## Explanations
### Every sound has a source · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound source is the thing that makes a sound.

In the listening minute, every sound you heard came from somewhere. The whirr came from the fan. The caw came from a crow. The honk came from a scooter. Each of these is a sound source. Sometimes you can see the source, like a hand that claps. Sometimes it is hidden, like a neighbour's mixie behind a wall. Then you can find it by following the sound. As you walk towards the source, the sound gets louder.
- e.g. A ceiling fan whirring
- e.g. A crow cawing at the window
- e.g. A neighbour's mixie behind the wall
- e.g. Your own voice when you talk
### A sound comes from something moving · *defined*
**Definition:** Every sound is made by something moving. When it stops moving, its sound stops.

In the Shaker Test, the bottles were silent while they sat on the table. When you shook them, the chana and the rice hit each other and the sides of the bottle, and you heard a rattle. Your hands make a clap only when they move and meet. Rain makes a sound when the drops hit the window. A crow calls by pushing air out from deep in the chest. Some moving is too small and too fast to see. Inside a phone, a tiny part moves back and forth very fast to make the sound you hear. In Class 3 you will learn the word for this fast shaking: vibrate.
- e.g. Chana hitting the inside of a bottle
- e.g. Two hands clapping
- e.g. Raindrops hitting a window
- e.g. A crow pushing air out to call
### Loud and soft sounds · *defined*
**Definition:** A loud sound is a big sound, and a soft sound is a small one. The harder something hits, shakes or blows, the louder its sound.

In the Shaker Test, a gentle shake made a soft rattle. A hard shake made a loud one. The same bottle can make a loud sound or a soft sound, depending on how hard you shake it. What hits matters too. Hard chana made a loud rattle. Soft cotton made almost no sound. And what it lands on matters. A spoon dropped on the bare floor goes clang. The same spoon dropped on a pillow makes almost no sound. A sound also gets softer as you move away from its source.
- e.g. Shaking the chana bottle hard, then gently
- e.g. A whisper and a shout
- e.g. A spoon landing on the floor and on a pillow
- e.g. A drum hit hard and hit softly
### Sound sources all around us · *defined*
**Definition:** Sound sources can be people, other animals, things that people make, or nature itself.

People make sounds with their voices, their hands and their feet. Other animals make sounds too, in many ways. A crow and a cow call with their breath. A bee buzzes because her wings beat very fast. A cricket chirps by rubbing his wings together. Things that people make are sound sources too: a mixie, a bell, a bus, a pressure cooker. And nature makes sounds with no one helping: wind in the trees, rain on a roof, waves on a beach, thunder in a storm. Some sound sources are alive, and some are not.
- e.g. A person singing
- e.g. A cow mooing
- e.g. A bee buzzing
- e.g. A mixie grinding
- e.g. Rain on a tin roof
- e.g. Waves on the beach at Puri
### Why do so many birds sing in the early morning? · *debated*
In many Indian cities, crows, mynas, bulbuls and koels are loudest around sunrise. Scientists call this the dawn chorus. They agree that birds sing to tell other birds who they are and where they live, and to find a mate. They still argue about why so much of the singing happens so early. Here are the main ideas.
- e.g. Crows cawing at sunrise
- e.g. A koel calling 'koo-ooo' in summer
- e.g. Bulbuls and mynas chattering in the morning
- **Some scientists:** Early morning is too dark to look for food well, so it is a good time to sing instead.
- **Other scientists:** The air is often calm and still at dawn, so a song may carry further. Studies do not all agree that it does.
- **Other scientists:** A bird who sings loudly first thing in the morning shows the neighbours that she or he is healthy and still there.

## Mostly agreed
- Every sound is made by something moving. *How we know:* Anyone can test it. A bottle of rice sitting still makes no sound. Shake it, and it rattles at once.
- A harder hit or a harder shake makes a louder sound. *How we know:* In the Shaker Test, the same bottle of chana was loud when shaken hard and soft when shaken gently.
- Many animals make sounds to call each other, including fish. *How we know:* From the 1940s, the scientist Marie Poland Fish recorded the sounds of many kinds of fish and other sea animals, one kind at a time.

## How we found out
**Marie Poland Fish (1900 to 1989), an American scientist who studied sea animals, and her team, From the 1940s.** For a long time, many people thought the sea was a silent place, and that fish made no sounds. During the Second World War, sailors in the American navy listened to the sea with underwater microphones. They heard grunts, croaks, drumming and crackling. The noise made it hard to listen for submarines. So the navy asked a scientist, Marie Poland Fish, to find out what was making the noise. She and her team kept fish and other sea animals in tanks, and recorded one kind at a time. Croaker fish drum with special muscles against a bag of gas inside their bodies. Tiny snapping shrimp crackle by snapping one big claw shut so fast that it makes a popping bubble. Marie Poland Fish recorded the sounds of many kinds of sea animals. She showed that the sea is full of sound sources. Many kinds of croaker live along India's coasts too.  
*What people believed before:* The sea is silent, and fish make no sounds. Recording one kind of sea animal at a time showed that croakers drum and snapping shrimp crackle.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only living things make sounds.** (A common idea among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: Many sounds we notice are voices: people talking, dogs barking, birds calling.  
  Against: Rain, wind, thunder and a falling spoon all make sounds, and none of them is alive. A sound comes from something moving, alive or not.
- **Big things always make louder sounds than small things.** (A common idea among children, and many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: An elephant's trumpet and a bus horn are big, loud sounds.  
  Against: A tiny cricket can fill a room with chirping. A pillow is big, but dropping it makes almost no sound. And elephants walk very quietly, on soft, padded feet. How hard something moves, and what it hits, matter more than its size.
- **Fish make no sounds.** (Many people, for a long time; *rejected*)  
  For: We hear nothing when we look at fish in a tank or a pond.  
  Against: Many fish make sounds underwater. Croaker fish drum with special muscles. Most of their sounds stay in the water, so people on land rarely hear them.
- **A phone makes sound with nothing moving.** (A common idea, because we cannot see anything move; *rejected*)  
  For: A phone looks still when it plays music.  
  Against: Inside the phone's speaker, a tiny part moves back and forth very fast. Put your finger lightly on a speaker while music plays, and you may feel it buzz.

## Roots
We have shaken bottles, dropped spoons and listened with our eyes closed. Now we ask what sounds tell us about the world, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Your bottle of chana made no sound while it sat still. Shake it, and it rattled at once. Every sound you found came from something moving. So whenever you hear a sound, something somewhere is moving right now.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk as if a sound belongs to a thing, as in 'the bell's ring'. Yet a still bell is silent. A sound comes from something happening: a moment when something moves.  
*Evidence:* In the Shaker Test, every shaker was silent on the table and rattled only while it was shaken. In the listening minute and in Sound Detective, each sound led back to something moving: a fan, a bird, keys, paper, water.

- *who am i:* You are a sound source too. When you talk, sing or clap, part of you moves, and other people hear it.
- *what is life:* Many living beings make sounds to find each other: a calf calling her mother, crows warning their family, crickets chirping at night.
- *what is this world:* The world is always moving, so it is full of sounds: wind, rain, waves, birds and traffic. Even a quiet room has a fan or a fridge moving.

Your still hands made no sound, and your moving hands did. The chana, the bell and the crow were the same: each sound came from something moving. Everything that moves is aware in its own way, the chana and the bottle too. Many scientists disagree: they say only beings with nerves are aware.

- **Hakuin Ekaku, a Zen teacher in Japan (1686 to 1769), who gave this question to his students:** Two hands clap, and there is a sound. What is the sound of one hand? (In the Body Band, a clap came only when two hands moved and met. Hakuin asked this question to make his students stop and listen deeply. It has no quick answer.)
- **Hatha Yoga Pradipika, chapter 4, on listening to nada, the inner sound:** Sit still and listen closely. The text says the sounds you hear at first are loud, like the sea or drums. As you keep listening, they grow finer, like a flute or a humming bee. (In the listening minute, you heard the loud sounds first. The longer you stayed still, the more soft sounds you noticed.)
- Science (settled): Every sound is made by something moving back and forth. When the movement stops, the sound stops.
- Science (settled): Many animals make sounds that people rarely notice, like fish drumming underwater.
- Other view, Many scientists today: They say chana, bottles and bells are not aware of anything. To them, a sound is only moving things pushing on the air. They think awareness belongs to living beings with nerves or brains.
- Practice, Still hands, moving hands (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Hold your hands still in your lap. Can you hear them? Now rub your palms together very slowly. Listen. Now rub them fast. Is the sound louder? Now stop, and hold your hands still again. Listen to the quiet. Where did the sound go?
- Ask: Your still hands made no sound, and your rubbing hands did. Was the sound in your hands, or in the rubbing?
- Ask: If everything in the world stopped moving, what would you hear?
- Ask: When you hear a crow, you know a crow is there and calling. What else can a sound tell you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The shakers in the Shaker Test were silent while they sat on the table. Where was the rattle before you shook one? (hint: Was the rattle waiting inside the bottle? Or did it begin when the chana started hitting the sides?)
- *deep:* Can anything move without making any sound at all? (hint: Wave your hand very slowly through the air. Is there a sound too soft for you to hear? Some animals, like bats and dogs, can hear sounds that people cannot.)
- *deep:* When you talk, what part of you moves to make the sound? (hint: Your breath comes up from your chest. Your throat, your tongue and your lips all move as you speak.)
- *big:* A crow calls to other crows. What do you think she is telling them? (hint: Crows call to warn each other about danger, like a cat nearby, and to keep in touch with their family. Listen to how a crow's call changes when a cat walks past.)
- *big:* If you could hear every sound in the whole world at once, what would it be like? (hint: Think of all the rain, the waves, the people talking and the animals calling, everywhere, all at the same time.)

## Story: The Ting in the Night
*Paati means grandmother in Tamil. Kavya, Arjun, Amma, Paati and Selvi are made up.*

Kavya was six. She lived in a flat on the fourth floor in Madurai, with her Amma, her grandmother Paati, and her little brother Arjun, who was four.

One night the whole flat was dark and quiet. Amma was asleep. Paati was asleep. Even the fan was switched off, because the night was cool after the rain.

Then Kavya heard it. Ting.

She lay very still and listened. Ting. Ting.

Arjun sat up in his bed. "Akka," he whispered. Akka means big sister. "What is that? Is it a ghost?"

"I don't know yet," said Kavya. "But Paati always says that every sound comes from something. If we find what is moving, we find the sound."

Ting.

Kavya got out of bed and held Arjun's hand. They walked on tiptoe, so that their own feet would not make a sound. In the hall, the ting was a little louder. Near the kitchen door, it was louder still.

"It's in the kitchen," whispered Kavya. She switched on the small light.

The tap over the sink was dripping. A drop of water grew fat at the end of the tap. Then it fell. It landed in an empty steel bucket under the tap. Ting!

"There's your ghost," said Kavya. "A drop of water, falling on the steel."

Arjun laughed out loud. Then he put both hands over his mouth. "Shh! We'll wake Paati."

"Too late," said a voice. Paati was standing in the doorway, smiling. "Who is making a concert in my kitchen at midnight?"

"The tap!" said Arjun. "The drops hit the bucket. Ting, ting, ting."

Paati turned the tap as tight as it would go. The drops came more slowly, but they did not stop. Ting.

"This old tap needs mending," said Paati. "Selvi the plumber can come tomorrow. But how can we make it quiet tonight, so we can all sleep?"

Arjun thought hard. "Put a pillow in the bucket!"

"My pillow is not going in a bucket," said Paati.

Kavya looked around the kitchen. On the hook by the stove was a soft cotton towel. She folded it and laid it in the bottom of the bucket.

They waited. The drop grew fat. It fell. This time there was no ting. There was only a tiny pat, so soft they could hardly hear it.

"The drop still falls," said Kavya slowly. "But now it lands on something soft. So the sound is soft."

Paati nodded. "When something hits hard steel, you hear a loud ting. When it hits a soft towel, you hear only a little pat."

They all went back to bed. Kavya listened in the dark. She could not hear the tap at all now. She heard Arjun breathing, slow and sleepy. Far away, a dog barked once.

The next morning, Selvi the plumber came with her bag of tools. She opened the tap and put in a new rubber ring, called a washer. She closed it again. Arjun watched the end of the tap for a long time. No drop grew there. No drop fell.

"No more ting," said Arjun, a little sadly.

"No more moving water," said Kavya. "So no more sound."

That evening, Kavya drew a picture for her sound diary. She drew the tap, the drop, the steel bucket and the towel. Next to the steel bucket she drew big zigzag lines for a loud sound. Next to the towel she drew one tiny line for a soft sound.

Arjun looked at the picture. "Akka, can we find more sounds tomorrow?"

"Yes," said Kavya. "And for every one, we'll find what is moving."

**For the parent:** Every sound comes from something moving. The falling drop made a loud ting on hard steel and only a soft pat on a soft towel. When the tap was mended and no drop fell, the sound stopped.

- Ask: What was making the ting in the night?
- Ask: How did Kavya find where the sound was coming from?
- Ask: Why did the drop make only a soft pat when it fell on the towel?
- Ask: After Selvi mended the tap, the ting stopped for good. Why?

## Games
### g1 · Sound Detective: what made that sound? (12 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the cards and put them in a pile, front side down. Choose a detective and a sound maker.; The sound maker turns over the top card and reads the front quietly. If the sound maker cannot read yet, the grown-up whispers it to them.; Then the detective sits with their back turned and eyes closed.; The sound maker makes that sound, two times.; The detective says what made the sound, and what moved.; The detective turns round. The sound maker shows the back of the card. If the detective was right, they keep the card. If not, the card goes to the side.; With 2 players, swap roles after six cards. With 3 or more, everyone else is a detective, the first to say the right answer keeps the card, and the sound maker's turn passes to the next person after each card.; The game ends when the pile is finished. The player with the most cards is the best detective. If it is a tie, you are all best detectives.
- **Printable cards: 12 Sound Detective cards:** Shake a bunch of keys. (back: Keys. The keys hit each other.); Tear a sheet of newspaper. (back: Paper. The paper rips apart.); Pour water from a jug into a steel tumbler. (back: Water. The water splashes into the tumbler.); Clap your hands twice. (back: Hands. Your hands hit each other.); Close a door gently. (back: A door. The door hits its frame.); Tap a pencil on a table. (back: A pencil. The pencil hits the table.); Open and close a zip on a bag. (back: A zip. Its little teeth click together.); Rub your hands together fast. (back: Hands. Your hands rub against each other.); Crumple a plastic bag. (back: A plastic bag. It crinkles as it folds.); Blow out a long breath: whoosh. (back: Breath. Air rushes out of your mouth.); Drop a coin on the floor. (back: A coin. It hits the floor and bounces.); Shake a closed bottle with chana inside. (back: A bottle of chana. The chana hits the inside of the bottle.)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the rules together. Decide who is the first detective. Collect what the cards need. You need keys, newspaper, a jug of water and a steel tumbler. You also need a pencil, a bag with a zip, a plastic bag, a coin and a closed bottle with chana inside.  
  *Parent says:* You are the detective. Close your eyes and listen. You have to tell me what made the sound, and what moved.  
  *Child mode:* You are the detective. Close your eyes. What made the sound?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play six cards with your child as detective. After each guess, ask what moved, then let them turn and look.  
  *Parent says:* Listen. What made that sound? What moved? Turn round and see if you were right.  
  *Child mode:* Listen. What made that sound? What moved? Now turn and look.
  - Ask: What moved to make the keys jingle? (good: The keys.; The keys hit each other.)
  - Note: The tricky cards are the breath, the rubbing hands and the plastic bag. If your child is stuck, make the sound once more, a little louder.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap roles for the last six cards. Whisper each card to your child, then close your eyes and guess.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are the sound maker. I will whisper the card to you. Make the sound, and I will guess.  
  *Child mode:* Now you make the sounds. Can you trick me?
  - Check: In every sound in this game, what was always happening? → **Something was moving**
### g2 · Stamp, Tiptoe, Freeze (8 min)
- **rules:** The drummer turns a plastic bucket or a big steel vessel upside down, and beats it with a wooden spoon.; The drummer takes a card and plays what it says.; Loud beats: players stamp around with big, heavy steps.; Soft beats: players tiptoe, quiet as a cat.; No sound: players freeze like statues until the next beat.; Anyone who moves in the silence swaps places with the drummer, and the new drummer plays the cards that are left. With one player, the player and the grown-up swap.; The game ends when all eight cards are played. Shuffle them and play again if you like.
- **Printable cards: 8 drummer cards:** LOUD: 4 big beats; SOFT: 4 tiny beats; STOP: no sound, count to 5; LOUD: 6 big beats; SOFT: 2 tiny beats; LOUD, then SOFT; SOFT, then LOUD; STOP: no sound, count to 5
- **s1 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the bucket upside down. Show one loud beat and one soft beat.  
  *Parent says:* This is a loud beat. This is a soft beat. When it is loud, stamp. When it is soft, tiptoe. When it stops, freeze.  
  *Child mode:* Loud: stamp. Soft: tiptoe. No sound: freeze.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play through the eight drummer cards. Then shuffle them and play again if there is time.  
  *Parent says:* Listen. Is it loud or soft? Now it has stopped. Freeze!  
  *Child mode:* Listen. Loud or soft? Silence: freeze!
  - Note: Watch whether your child changes their steps as soon as the sound changes. That shows careful listening.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let your child be the drummer. Ask how they make a beat loud or soft.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are the drummer. How will you make a loud beat? How will you make a soft one?  
  *Child mode:* You are the drummer now. Make loud beats and soft beats.
  - Ask: How do you make a loud beat? (good: Hit the bucket hard.; Bang it with a big swing.)
  - Ask: How do you make a soft beat? (good: Hit it gently.; Just touch it lightly.)

## Experiments
### e1 · The Shaker Test: which shaker is loudest? (25 min)
- **question:** Does what is inside a shaker change how loud it sounds? And does shaking harder make it louder?
- **predict:** Look at the four shakers: chana, rice, cotton and nothing. Which will be loudest? Which will be softest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All four shakers are the same kind of bottle or box. Chana and rice are two big spoons each. The cotton is two balls, which fill about the same space. The empty one has nothing in it. Only what is inside is different. We shake each one five times, the same way.
- **safety:** Chana and rice are small. Watch younger children so they do not put them in their mouth, nose or ears. Close the caps tightly.
- **record:** Draw the four shakers in a line, from the loudest to the softest. Draw big zigzag lines around the loudest one, and tiny lines, or none, around the softest.
- **results table:** Shaker | My guess: loud, middle or soft? | What I heard: loud, middle or soft?
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put two big spoons of chana in the first bottle, through the paper funnel. 2. Put two big spoons of rice in the second. 3. Push two cotton balls into the third. 4. Leave the fourth empty. 5. Close all the caps tightly.  
  *Parent says:* We are making four shakers. Each one gets something different inside. Let's draw a picture label for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Help fill the bottles. Close the caps tight. Draw a picture label for each.
  - Note: Picture labels help a child who cannot read yet: a bean, a grain of rice, a cloud of cotton and an empty circle.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put all four shakers on the table. Ask your child to sit very still, lean close and listen for ten seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Our shakers are sitting on the table. Put your ear close and listen very carefully. Can you hear them?  
  *Child mode:* Put your ear close to the shakers. Can you hear them?
  - Ask: What do you hear? (good: Nothing.; They make no sound when they sit still.)
  - Note: The still shakers are silent. Let your child notice this, and come back to it at the end. Don't explain it yet.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to put the shakers in a line, from 'will be loudest' to 'will be softest'. 2. Draw or write the guesses in the table. A grown-up writes for a child who cannot write yet.  
  *Parent says:* Soon you will shake each one. Which will be loudest? Put it at this end. Which will be softest? Put it at the other end. Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which shaker will be loudest? Which will be softest? Line them up. Why?
  - Ask: Why did you put that one first? (good: The chana is big and hard.; The rice has lots and lots of grains.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is good. Many children think the rice will be loudest, because there are so many grains. Don't say what will happen.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child shakes each shaker five times, the same way. 2. Hide all four under the dupatta. 3. Your child closes their eyes. 4. Shake one at a time under the dupatta. 5. Each time, your child says loud, middle or soft.  
  *Parent says:* Shake each one five times. Now close your eyes. I will shake one under the dupatta. Is it loud, middle or soft? Which one do you think it is?  
  *Child mode:* Shake each one five times. Now close your eyes. Is it loud, middle or soft?
  - Ask: Which was the loudest? (good: The chana.; The beans were loudest.)
  - Ask: Which was the softest? (good: The cotton.; The empty one made almost no sound.)
  - Note: Usually the chana is loudest, and the rice makes a softer, hissing rattle. The cotton and the empty bottle are nearly silent. If two sound alike, shake them one after the other, again. Write what your child heard in the table.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child the chana shaker. 2. Ask them to shake it very gently. 3. Then very hard. 4. Then very gently again.  
  *Parent says:* Now shake the chana one very gently. Now shake it hard. Now gently again. What changes?  
  *Child mode:* Shake it gently. Now shake it hard. What changes?
  - Ask: When was it loudest? (good: When I shook it hard.; A hard shake made a big sound.)
  - Check: How do you make the chana shaker louder? → **Shake it harder**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the chana shaker and the cotton shaker. 2. Give your child one chana and one cotton ball. 3. Ask them to tap each one on the table.  
  *Parent says:* Tap the chana on the table. Now tap the cotton. Which one makes a sound?  
  *Child mode:* Tap a chana on the table. Now tap the cotton. Which makes a sound?
  - Ask: What did you hear? (good: The chana goes tak.; The cotton is soft, so it makes almost no sound.)
  - Note: This shows why the cotton shaker was so quiet. Hard things hitting make loud sounds. Soft things hitting make soft sounds.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to tell you three things. Write them down in your child's words. Ask what the shakers did while they sat still.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you hear? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think. Say what you heard. Say why.
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this. 'The shakers were silent when they sat still. The chana was loudest. Shaking hard made it louder. Hard chana makes a loud sound, and soft cotton makes a soft sound.'
### e2 · Soft Landing: what makes a falling spoon quiet? (15 min)
- **question:** When a steel spoon falls, does what it lands on change how loud the sound is?
- **predict:** We will drop a spoon onto the bare floor, a folded newspaper, a folded towel and a pillow. Which will be loudest? Which will be softest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same spoon every time. We drop it from the same height, your knee. We let it fall, and never throw it. At the end we change the height on purpose, to see what it does.
- **record:** Draw the four landing places in a row. Draw big zigzag lines next to the loudest landing, and one tiny line next to the softest.
- **results table:** What the spoon lands on | My guess: loud or soft? | What I heard: loud or soft?
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the folded newspaper, the folded towel and the pillow on the floor in a row. Leave a patch of bare floor next to them.  
  *Parent says:* Here are four landing places for our spoon: the bare floor, the newspaper, the towel and the pillow.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the four landing places. Touch each one.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to point to the landing that will be loudest, and the one that will be softest. Write or draw the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which landing will make the loudest sound? Which will make the softest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which will be loudest? Which will be softest? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the pillow will be soft? (good: It is squishy.; It is soft, so the spoon will not bang.)
  - Note: Don't say what will happen. Any guess with a reason is good.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds the spoon at knee height over each landing place. 2. They let it fall, without throwing. 3. Drop it twice on each one. 4. Your child says loud or soft each time.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the spoon at your knee. Let it go. Listen. Was it loud or soft? Let's try it twice on each place.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the spoon at your knee. Let it go. Loud or soft?
  - Ask: Which was the loudest landing? (good: The bare floor.; It went clang on the floor.)
  - Ask: Which was the softest? (good: The pillow.; On the pillow I could hardly hear it.)
  - Note: The bare floor is loudest. The newspaper is a little softer, the towel is softer still, and the pillow is nearly silent.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child drops the spoon on the bare floor from knee height. 2. Then from as high as their shoulder.  
  *Parent says:* Now drop it on the floor from your knee. Now from your shoulder. Which was louder?  
  *Child mode:* Drop it from your knee. Now drop it from your shoulder. Which was louder?
  - Ask: Which drop was louder? (good: From my shoulder.; The higher drop hit the floor harder.)
  - Check: The spoon was dropped from a knee and from a shoulder. Which drop was louder? → **From the shoulder**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to tell you three things. Write them down in your child's words.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you hear? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think. Say what you heard. Say why.
  - Note: A strong answer: 'The floor was loudest and the pillow was softest. The spoon hits the hard floor hard. The soft pillow catches it gently.'

## Worksheets
### Sound Sources (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (sort) A grown-up reads each sound. Who or what made it? Draw a line to the right group: people, other animals, or things.  
   **Answer:** A baby crying → People; A cow mooing → Other animals; A doorbell ringing → Things; A mother singing → People; A bee buzzing → Other animals; A mixie grinding → Things. *Why:* Every sound has a source. People make sounds with their voices. Other animals call, buzz and moo. Things that people make, like bells and mixies, make sounds when they move.
2. (odd) Three of these are making a sound right now. Which one is silent? Circle it.  
   Options: A ringing bell / A barking dog / A spoon lying still on a table / Two hands clapping  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The spoon is lying still, so it makes no sound. The bell, the dog and the hands are all moving, so we hear them.
3. (match) A grown-up reads the words. Draw a line from each sound to its source.  
   **Answer:** Caw caw → A crow; Whistle → A pressure cooker; Moo → A cow; Tring tring → A bicycle bell. *Why:* Each sound tells us its source. A crow caws, a pressure cooker whistles when steam rushes out, a cow moos and a bicycle bell rings when you press it.
4. (mcq) In the Shaker Test, a child shook the chana shaker gently, then hard. When was it louder?  
   Options: When she shook it gently / When she shook it hard / Both were the same  
   **Answer:** When she shook it hard. *Why:* A hard shake makes the chana hit the bottle harder, so the sound is louder. A gentle shake makes a soft sound.
5. (draw) Draw one thing in your home that makes a loud sound. Then draw one thing that makes a soft sound.  
   **Answer:** One loud sound source, such as a pressure cooker, a mixie or a doorbell, and one soft sound source, such as a clock, a fan or a whisper.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a real sound source for each, and draws something that moves to make the sound.
6. (truefalse) Your own body can be a sound source when you clap or stamp.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. When you clap, your hands move and meet. When you stamp, your foot hits the floor. Anything that moves can make a sound, and your body moves.
7. (write) Say it out loud, and a grown-up writes it down. When the shaker rattled, what was moving?  
   **Answer:** The chana, or the rice, was moving inside the bottle. It hit the other grains and the sides of the bottle.. *Why:* A strong answer names what moved inside the shaker, and says that it hit something.
8. (label) Look at the kitchen. A grown-up points to each thing. Say 'loud', 'soft' or 'silent', and the grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** ['loud', 'loud', 'soft', 'silent']. *Why:* The pressure cooker and the mixie make loud sounds. A drip makes a soft sound. The spoon is not moving, so it is silent.
9. (count) Look at the street picture. Count the sound sources that are making a sound. Write the number.  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* There are 5 sound sources making sounds: an auto honking, a vegetable seller calling, a dog barking, a crow cawing and a temple bell ringing. The parked bicycle is not making a sound, because nothing is moving it.
10. (perspective) Divya, a girl in Class 1, wants to call her friend from across the playground. Three children have ideas.  
   **Answer:** Any choice with a good reason. A shout is loud and goes far but can disturb others. A clap is loud and saves your voice, but your friend may not know it is for her. Walking over is quiet and clear but takes longer.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason, and fairly says what is good about another idea. Each idea makes a different sound source loud or soft.
### Loud, Soft and Silent (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (order) A grown-up reads these steps of the Shaker Test. Number them 1, 2 and 3 in the right order.  
   **Answer:** ['Guess which shaker will be loudest', 'Shake each shaker five times and listen', 'Line up the shakers from loud to soft']. *Why:* First we guess, then we test by shaking and listening, then we line them up by what we heard.
2. (mcq) Meera, a girl in Class 1, rings a bell on the table. Then she walks to the other side of the room and listens. How does the ring sound from far away?  
   Options: Louder than close up / Softer than close up / Just the same  
   **Answer:** Softer than close up. *Why:* A sound gets softer as you move away from its source. That is why you can find a hidden sound by walking towards where it gets louder.
3. (mcq) A phone is playing a song. Where is the sound coming from?  
   Options: A tiny part inside the phone that moves very fast / The air in the room, all by itself / Your ears  
   **Answer:** A tiny part inside the phone that moves very fast. *Why:* Inside the phone's speaker, a tiny part moves back and forth very fast. That moving part is the sound source. We cannot see it move, but it is moving.
4. (truefalse) Only living things can make sounds.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Rain, wind, thunder and a falling spoon all make sounds, and none of them is alive. Anything that moves can make a sound.
5. (fill) A thing that makes a sound is called a sound ___. A grown-up can help write the word.  
   **Answer:** source. *Why:* A sound source is the thing that makes a sound, like a bell, a crow or your own voice.
6. (match) A grown-up reads the words. Each of these animals makes a sound in a different way. Draw a line to show how.  
   **Answer:** A bee → Wings beating very fast; A cricket → Rubbing his wings together; A crow → Calling with her breath; A woodpecker → Tapping a beak on wood. *Why:* Animals make sounds in many ways. A bee's buzz comes from her fast-beating wings. A male cricket rubs his wings together to chirp. A crow pushes air out to call. A woodpecker hammers on wood with a beak.
7. (write) Your friend says big things are always louder than small things. A cricket is tiny, and a pillow is big. Is your friend right? Say why, and a grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** No. A tiny cricket can chirp loudly enough to fill a room. A big pillow makes almost no sound when it falls. How hard something moves, and what it hits, matter more than its size.. *Why:* A strong answer uses an example of a small, loud thing or a big, quiet thing to show the idea is wrong.
8. (draw) Draw yourself tapping a steel dabba twice: once to make a soft sound and once to make a loud sound. Show how your hand moves each time.  
   **Answer:** Two drawings. For the soft sound, a small, gentle movement and small sound lines. For the loud sound, a big swing and big sound lines.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that a bigger, harder movement makes a louder sound.
9. (perspective) Meera, a girl aged 8, watches rain fall on a window with her mother and her grandfather. They disagree about the sound source.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that both the drops and the glass move when a drop hits. Rain sounds different on a window, a tin roof and a leaf, so what the drops land on matters too.. *Why:* A sound often comes from two things meeting. The drop moves, and the glass it hits moves too. A strong answer gives a reason and fairly says what is right about another view.
10. (write) Guess before you try. Would a shaker full of coins be louder or softer than a shaker full of cotton? Why? Say it out loud, and a grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** Louder. Coins are hard and heavy, so they hit the bottle hard. Cotton is soft, so it hits gently and makes almost no sound.. *Why:* A strong answer uses what the Shaker Test showed: hard things hitting make loud sounds and soft things make soft sounds.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a sound source?  
   Options: The ear that hears a sound / The thing that makes a sound / A very loud sound  
   **Answer:** The thing that makes a sound. *Why:* A sound source is the thing that makes a sound, like a bell or a crow. The ear hears the sound, but it does not make it.
2. (mcq) A pressure cooker whistles in the kitchen. What is the sound source?  
   Options: The pressure cooker / A listener's ear / The kitchen window  
   **Answer:** The pressure cooker. *Why:* The sound comes from the pressure cooker, as steam rushes out of the top.
3. (mcq) Which of these sound sources is not alive?  
   Options: A crow cawing / Rain on the roof / A cow mooing  
   **Answer:** Rain on the roof. *Why:* Rain is not alive, but it still makes a sound when the drops hit the roof. The crow and the cow are living beings who call with their breath.
4. (mcq) A temple bell hangs still. Nobody touches it. What do you hear from the bell?  
   Options: A loud ring / A soft ring / Nothing  
   **Answer:** Nothing. *Why:* A still bell makes no sound. It rings only when something moves it, like a hand pulling the rope.
5. (mcq) Farhan, a boy in Class 1, claps his hands. What moved to make the sound?  
   Options: His ears / His hands / The floor  
   **Answer:** His hands. *Why:* His hands moved and hit each other. That is what made the clap.
6. (mcq) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 1, wants a louder sound from her drum. What should she do?  
   Options: Hit it harder / Hit it more gently / Stop hitting it  
   **Answer:** Hit it harder. *Why:* A harder hit makes a louder sound. A gentle hit makes a soft sound, and no hit makes no sound.
7. (mcq) Remember the Shaker Test, with chana, rice, cotton and an empty bottle. Which shaker with something inside made the softest sound?  
   Options: The one with chana / The one with rice / The one with cotton  
   **Answer:** The one with cotton. *Why:* Cotton is soft, so it hits the bottle gently and makes almost no sound. Hard chana makes the loudest rattle.
8. (mcq) Why is a whisper a soft sound?  
   Options: Only a little air moves, gently / A whisper has no source / Your ears are closed  
   **Answer:** Only a little air moves, gently. *Why:* When you whisper, only a little air moves out of your mouth, gently. A shout pushes out much more air, much harder.
9. (mcq) Your eyes are closed. You hear keys jingling. What is moving?  
   Options: Nothing is moving / Only your ears / The keys, hitting each other  
   **Answer:** The keys, hitting each other. *Why:* Every sound comes from something moving. Jingling keys are moving and hitting each other.
10. (mcq) Sneha, a girl in Class 1, hears a sound behind a door, but she cannot see what made it. How can she find the sound source?  
   Options: Walk towards where the sound gets louder / Cover her ears / Walk away from the sound  
   **Answer:** Walk towards where the sound gets louder. *Why:* A sound gets louder as you get closer to its source. Following the sound leads you to it, as the children did in the story.

## Applications
- Horns on buses, autos and scooters, and bells on bicycles, warn people to move out of the way.
- A pressure cooker whistles when steam rushes out. The whistles tell the cook how the cooking is going.
- Doctors listen to your heart with a stethoscope. Each heartbeat makes a sound as the little doors inside the heart, called valves, close.
- School bells, fire alarms and ambulance sirens are made loud on purpose, so that everyone hears them.
- Scientists put microphones in the sea to listen to fish, whales and dolphins, and to learn how they are living.

## Spotted around us
- At Thrissur Pooram in Kerala, long rows of drummers play the chenda together, and the sound fills the town.
- During Durga Puja in Kolkata, drummers play the dhak, a big drum hung from the shoulder.
- In summer, the koel calls 'koo-ooo' loudly from trees in cities across India. The loud caller is the male, a black bird with red eyes.
- At Bohag Bihu in Assam, people play the dhol and blow the pepa, a pipe made from a buffalo horn.
- In many homes, a bell rings in the morning puja, and a pressure cooker whistles while lunch is cooking.

## Open questions
- **What are crows saying to each other?** Scientists know crows have many different calls, for danger, for food and for their family. Nobody yet understands everything the calls mean.
- **How many kinds of fish make sounds?** Scientists keep finding fish who make sounds, and many kinds of fish have never been listened to carefully.
- **Why do so many birds sing most at dawn?** There are several good ideas, such as calm morning air or too little light to find food. Scientists are still testing which ones are right.

Guessing hidden sounds and matching sounds to sources became Sound Detective. Body percussion and call-and-response rhythm became My Body Band. The journal of sounds became the sound map and the sound diary in the story. The founder's vibration experiments (rubber-band guitar, rice on a drum, straw reeds) belong to the Level 3 unit, so this unit does not repeat them.
- Curriculum entry sci.sound.sources-of-sound.L1 (Where do sounds come from? Loud and soft sounds around us), Cambridge Stage 1 unit 6.1. The Level 3 unit sci.sound.sources-of-sound.L3 teaches vibration, sound waves and the medium, with the ruler, foil-ball and atta-drum experiments, the coin line, Katy Payne and the elephants, and the tree-in-the-forest puzzle. This unit only prepares for those: it says sound comes from something moving, and names 'vibrate' as a word for Class 3.
- Marie Poland Fish (1900 to 1989) catalogued sounds of fish and other marine animals for the US Office of Naval Research from the 1940s, at the Narragansett Marine Laboratory, University of Rhode Island. Fish, M. P. and Mowbray, W. H., Sounds of Western North Atlantic Fishes (Johns Hopkins Press, 1970).
- Croakers and drums (family Sciaenidae) make sound with sonic muscles that vibrate the swim bladder.
- Snapping shrimp: the snap makes a cavitation bubble whose collapse makes the sound. Versluis, Schmitz, von der Heydt and Lohse, 'How snapping shrimp snap: through cavitating bubbles', Science 289 (2000).
- Field crickets: males chirp by rubbing a file on one forewing against a scraper on the other (stridulation).
- The dawn chorus: hypotheses include low light for foraging, better sound transmission at dawn and signalling of fitness. None is fully settled.
- The koel's loud 'koo-ooo' call is the male's. The male Asian koel is glossy black with red eyes.
- Hakuin Ekaku (1686 to 1769) gave the koan of the sound of one hand. Hatha Yoga Pradipika, chapter 4, describes nadanusandhana: inner sounds heard first like the ocean, clouds and drums, later like the flute, the vina and bees.
