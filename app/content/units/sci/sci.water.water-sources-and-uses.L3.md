# Where our water comes from, and when it is safe to drink
`sci.water.water-sources-and-uses.L3`

**Summary.** Almost all the water we use starts as rain. Some rain flows into rivers and lakes. Some soaks into the ground and fills the tiny gaps between grains of soil and rock. That is groundwater. Wells and borewells reach down to it. People store water in matkas, tanks, ponds and stepwells, and clean it before they drink it. Water that looks clear can still carry germs and dissolved salts, because both are too small to see.

**Intro.** *Fill a glass from the tap and hold it up to the light. It looks like nothing but water. Where was this water last week? And is there anything else in it?* Most children say the water comes from the tap. But a tap makes no water. It only lets out water that is already in the pipe. The pipe brings it from a tank, a well or a river, and those were filled by rain. In this unit you follow your water back to where it started. You also dry spoonfuls of clear water on a plate, to see what was hiding in them.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name where the water in my home comes from, such as a river, a lake, a well or a borewell, and how it reaches the tap.
1. I can say what rain does when it lands: it soaks in, runs off or dries up. I can show which ground lets it soak in.
2. I can explain groundwater, and why wells go dry when people pump faster than rain refills the ground.
3. I can describe ways people store water, from matkas and stepwells to overhead tanks, and explain why a matka keeps water cool.
4. I can explain why clear water is not always clean, and name ways to make water safe: settling, filtering, boiling and chlorine.
5. I can measure how much water my family uses in litres, and show it in a table and a bar chart.
6. I can explain the problems of too little water and of dirty water, and say who they hurt most.
7. I can explain that the water we use is very old and goes round and round. I can describe how satellites weigh groundwater, and how water was found on the Moon.

## Terms
- **Source**: The place where something comes from. A source of water is the place we get it from. *e.g.* A river, a lake, a well and a borewell are all sources of water.
- **Rainwater**: Water that falls from clouds. It is where almost all the fresh water we use begins. *e.g.* In the monsoon, rainwater fills ponds, lakes and wells.
- **Fresh water**: Water with very little salt in it. People, plants and most land animals need fresh water to live. *e.g.* River water, lake water and rainwater are fresh. Sea water is salty, not fresh.
- **Surface water**: Water we can see on top of the land, in rivers, lakes, ponds and reservoirs. *e.g.* The Kaveri, Vembanad Lake in Kerala and your village pond are all surface water.
- **Reservoir**: A big lake made by people, usually by building a wall called a dam across a river. It stores water for a city or for farms. *e.g.* Many big cities get their water from reservoirs far away.
- **Groundwater**: Water hidden under the ground, in the tiny gaps between grains of soil, sand and rock. It is rain that soaked in. *e.g.* The water in a well or a borewell is groundwater.
- **Aquifer**: A layer of rock, sand or gravel under the ground that holds lots of groundwater, like a giant sponge. *e.g.* Many borewells in India are drilled down into an aquifer.
- **Well**: A hole dug down into the ground until it reaches groundwater. Water seeps into it from the sides and the bottom. *e.g.* An old village well with a rope and a bucket.
- **Borewell**: A deep, narrow hole drilled into the ground, with a pipe and a pump that bring up groundwater. *e.g.* Many homes and farms in India pump water from a borewell with an electric motor.
- **Absorb (soak in)**: To take in water, the way a sponge or soil does. *e.g.* Rain soaks into a field, but not into a cement road.
- **Run-off**: Rainwater that flows away over the ground instead of soaking in. *e.g.* On a tarred road, rain runs off into the drain.
- **Evaporate**: When liquid water slowly turns into water vapour, an invisible gas, and goes into the air. *e.g.* Wet clothes on a line dry because the water evaporates.
- **Matka**: A clay pot for keeping drinking water. Clay has tiny holes, so a little water seeps out and evaporates, which cools the water inside. In Tamil it is called a mann paanai. *e.g.* In summer, many Indian homes keep a matka in a shady corner.
- **Stepwell**: A deep well with stone stairs going down to the water. People walked down further as the water level fell in the dry season. *e.g.* Rani ki Vav in Patan, Gujarat, and Chand Baori in Abhaneri, Rajasthan, are famous stepwells.
- **Tanka**: A covered underground tank used in the Thar desert. Rain falling on a clean, sloping courtyard runs into it and is stored for months. *e.g.* A family in a desert village drinks tanka water all through the dry season.
- **Johad**: A curved wall of earth built across a slope to hold back rainwater, so it has time to soak into the ground. *e.g.* Villagers in Alwar, Rajasthan, built many johads to refill their wells.
- **Dissolve**: When something mixes into water so completely that you cannot see it any more. It is still there. *e.g.* Stir salt or sugar into water and it dissolves.
- **Germs**: Living things far too small to see, such as bacteria and viruses. Some of them make people ill. *e.g.* Germs in dirty water can cause tummy illnesses such as cholera.
- **Cholera**: An illness caused by a germ that lives in water dirtied by human waste. It causes very bad diarrhoea and can kill quickly if not treated. *e.g.* In 1854, a doctor in London, John Snow, showed that cholera spread through water from one pump.
- **Purify**: To make water clean and safe to drink. *e.g.* Boiling water, adding chlorine and using a water purifier are ways to purify water.
- **Chlorine**: A chemical added in very small amounts to drinking water to kill germs. Bleaching powder, used to clean village wells, contains it. *e.g.* Water from a city treatment plant often has a slight swimming-pool smell from chlorine.
- **Molecule**: The tiniest bit of something that is still that thing. A water molecule is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom. Scientists write it H2O. *e.g.* One drop of water has more molecules than there are grains of sand on all of Earth's beaches.
- **Litre**: A unit for measuring how much liquid something holds. A big water bottle often holds 1 litre. *e.g.* A bucket often holds about 15 litres.

## Explanations
### Where our water comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** Almost all the fresh water we use starts as rain. We take it from two places: water on the land, called surface water, and water under the ground, called groundwater.

Rain fills rivers, lakes and ponds. That is surface water, water we can see. Rain also soaks into the ground. That is groundwater, hidden water. Every home gets its water from one of these, or from both. A city usually pipes water from a river, a lake or a reservoir far away. A village often pumps groundwater from a well, a borewell or a handpump. Some homes buy water from a tanker, a truck that carries water. A few coastal cities, such as Chennai, also take salt out of sea water in big plants, but that costs a lot of electricity.
- e.g. Bengaluru gets most of its water from the Kaveri river, more than 100 kilometres away.
- e.g. A farmhouse near Coimbatore may pump all its water from a borewell.
- e.g. In summer, many families in big cities also buy water from tankers.
### What rain does when it lands · *defined*
**Definition:** When rain lands, it soaks in, runs off, or evaporates. Which one happens depends mostly on the ground it lands on.

Rain can do three things. It can soak into the ground. It can run off, flowing over the ground into drains, streams and rivers. Or it can sit in a puddle and slowly evaporate, turning into invisible vapour in the air. What happens depends on the ground. Soil, sand, grass and forest floors have tiny gaps that water trickles into. Cement, tar, tiles and roofs have no gaps, so rain runs off. A forest is best of all. Leaves slow the rain, and roots make channels into the soil.
- e.g. Rain on a grassy field soaks in. Rain on a tarred road runs into the drain.
- e.g. A city with roads and buildings everywhere floods quickly in heavy rain, because little water can soak in.
- e.g. A puddle on a sunny day is gone by evening. Its water went into the air.
### Groundwater: water hiding in the gaps · *defined*
**Definition:** Groundwater is rainwater that has soaked into the ground. It fills the tiny gaps between grains of soil, sand and rock.

Dig into wet sand at a beach, and the hole slowly fills with water. That water was hiding between the grains. Groundwater is like that, but under the whole land. It is not usually a big underground lake. It fills tiny gaps between grains of soil, sand and rock, like water in a sponge. A layer that holds a lot of groundwater is called an aquifer. A well or borewell is a hole that reaches down into this wet layer. Groundwater moves very slowly, sometimes only a few centimetres in a day. Some of the water in deep borewells fell as rain thousands of years ago.
- e.g. Dig a hole in wet sand at the beach. Water seeps in from the sides.
- e.g. A well gets almost all its water from groundwater seeping in, not from rain falling straight into it.
- e.g. Most drinking water in Indian villages is groundwater.
### How water reaches your tap · *defined*
**Definition:** City water is taken from a river, lake or groundwater, cleaned at a treatment plant, then pumped through pipes to homes. Overhead tanks let gravity push it down to the taps.

In most cities, water travels a long way. First it is taken from a river, a lake or a reservoir. Then it goes to a treatment plant, a place that cleans the water. There the water sits still in big tanks, so mud sinks. It is passed through beds of sand and gravel, which catch smaller bits. A tiny amount of chlorine is added to kill germs. Then pumps push the clean water through pipes under the roads. In many buildings, a motor lifts it to an overhead tank on the roof. From there, gravity pulls it down the pipes to your tap. In a village, a borewell pump may lift groundwater straight into a tank.
- e.g. The overhead tank on your roof uses gravity. Water flows down from it, so taps work even when the motor is off.
- e.g. If a tap upstairs is weaker than one downstairs, it is because the water has less height to fall.
- e.g. After the water is used, it goes down the drain, into sewers, and often back into a river.
### Storing water, then and now · *defined*
**Definition:** Storing water means keeping it safely for later. People store rain and groundwater in pots, tanks, ponds, wells and lakes behind dams.

Rain in most of India comes mainly in about four months of monsoon. People need water all twelve months. So for thousands of years, people have stored it. About 4,500 years ago, the Harappan city of Dholavira in Gujarat had huge stone reservoirs to hold rainwater. Mohenjo-daro had hundreds of brick wells. Villages built ponds and tanks, and cleaned out the mud together every year. In the Thar desert, families built tankas, covered underground tanks. In western India, people built stepwells, so they could walk down to the water as it fell lower in summer. Women paid for some of the grandest stepwells. Rani ki Vav in Patan was built by Queen Udayamati nearly 1,000 years ago. Today we also use overhead tanks, sumps, steel drums and giant reservoirs behind dams.
- e.g. A matka keeps a family's drinking water for the day.
- e.g. Rani ki Vav is so beautiful that it is printed on India's 100 rupee note.
- e.g. A village pond stores monsoon rain, and helps refill the wells around it.
### Why a matka keeps water cool · *defined*
**Definition:** A matka cools water because water seeps through its clay and evaporates from the outside. Evaporating water carries heat away.

Clay looks solid, but it is full of tiny holes. A little water slowly seeps through and wets the outside of the matka. That water evaporates into the air. To evaporate, water needs heat. It takes that heat from the pot and from the water inside. So the water inside gets cooler. It is the same reason you feel cold when you step out of a bath with wet skin. A matka works best in dry, breezy weather, because water evaporates faster in dry air. On a sticky, humid day it cools much less.
- e.g. Sweat cools your body the same way, as it evaporates off your skin.
- e.g. A wet cloth wrapped round a steel bottle also cools the water inside.
- e.g. A matka in a breezy spot cools better than one in a closed cupboard.
### Clear is not the same as clean · *defined*
**Definition:** Water that looks clear can still carry germs and dissolved substances that we cannot see. Clear water is not always safe water.

Water can carry three kinds of things. The first kind is bits you can see, like mud, leaves and sand. These make water cloudy. The second kind is germs, such as bacteria and viruses. They are far too small to see. Hundreds of bacteria could sit side by side across the full stop at the end of this sentence. The third kind is dissolved things, such as salt, or harmful substances like arsenic and too much fluoride. They mix in completely and vanish from sight. So a glass of water can look perfect, smell fine and taste sweet, and still make you ill. The only way to know is to test it, or to make it safe before drinking.
- e.g. Sea water looks clear, but it is full of dissolved salt.
- e.g. In 1854 in London, people loved the clear, cold water from one pump. It was spreading cholera.
- e.g. In some villages, clear groundwater carries arsenic or too much fluoride that nobody can see.
### Making water safe to drink · *defined*
**Definition:** Settling and filtering remove bits you can see. Boiling, chlorine and strong sunlight kill germs. Special filters are needed to remove dissolved substances like salt or arsenic.

Different dirt needs different cleaning. Settling means leaving water still, so heavy bits sink to the bottom. Filtering means passing water through cloth, sand or a filter that catches bits. Neither of these reliably removes germs. Boiling water until big bubbles roll, and keeping it boiling for a minute or more, kills germs. Chlorine kills germs too. Even strong sunlight can kill many germs in a clear bottle left in full sun for about six hours. Scientists in Switzerland tested this, and the World Health Organization accepts it. Boiling does not remove salt, arsenic or fluoride. To take those out, people need special filters, or a purifier called reverse osmosis, RO for short. Many RO machines also throw away a lot of water as they work.
- e.g. Many Indian families boil their drinking water, or use a filter or purifier.
- e.g. City treatment plants settle, filter and add chlorine.
- e.g. Boiling sea water does not make it drinkable. The salt stays behind in the pot.
### How much water a home uses · *defined*
**Definition:** A home's water use is measured in litres for each person each day. Bathing, flushing and washing use far more water than drinking.

Most of a home's water is not for drinking. Each person drinks only about 2 or 3 litres a day. Far more goes on bathing, flushing toilets, washing clothes and dishes, and cleaning floors. India's planners say a city home with drains needs about 135 litres for each person, each day. India's Jal Jeevan Mission, started in 2019, aims to give every village home a tap with at least 55 litres for each person each day. Some families use far more than this, and some manage on far less. A dripping tap can waste many litres a day without anybody noticing. You can find out your own family's number by counting.
- e.g. One bucket bath uses about 15 litres. A long shower can use 50 litres or more.
- e.g. An old toilet flush can use 10 litres. Many newer toilets have a half-flush button.
- e.g. Washing a car with a running hose can use more than 100 litres.
### Too little water · *defined*
**Definition:** Water becomes short when rain is low, or when people take water out faster than rain refills it.

Rain in India is very uneven. Mawsynram in Meghalaya gets nearly 12 metres of rain in a year, taller than a three-storey house. Parts of the Thar desert in Rajasthan get about 20 centimetres, about the length of a pencil. Rain also comes in a few months, not all year. And there is a second problem. India pumps up more groundwater than any other country in the world. In many places, people pump it out faster than rain soaks back in. Then the water level falls, and wells and borewells go dry. In 2019, Chennai's main lakes almost dried up. Trains had to carry water to the city from Jolarpettai, more than 200 kilometres away. When water is short, the poorest families, and the women and girls who fetch water, suffer first.
- e.g. A village borewell that once struck water at 30 metres may now need to go down 100 metres or more.
- e.g. In the summer of 2019, special trains carried millions of litres of water to Chennai.
- e.g. Paving over fields and filling in ponds means less rain soaks in to refill the ground.
### Weighing hidden water from space · *defined*
**Definition:** The GRACE satellites measured tiny changes in Earth's gravity. These showed where underground water was being lost.

How can anyone know how much water is hidden underground? In 2002, the United States and Germany launched two satellites, nicknamed Tom and Jerry. They chased each other around the Earth, about 220 kilometres apart. Water is heavy, so it pulls with a tiny bit of gravity. When the satellites flew over a place with more water, its pull tugged the first satellite ahead a tiny bit, and the gap changed. The satellites measured changes far thinner than a hair. From this, scientists worked out where water was being gained and lost. In 2009, a team led by Matthew Rodell showed that groundwater under north-west India, in Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana and Delhi, was being used up faster than rain refilled it.
- e.g. New twin satellites, launched in 2018, carry on the same job.
- e.g. The satellites can feel a whole region's groundwater getting lighter, year after year.
### Why is there arsenic in some well water? · *debated*
In the early 1980s, doctors in Kolkata saw patients with dark spots and hard patches on their skin. A skin doctor, K. C. Saha, traced the cause to arsenic, a poison, in their tubewell water. Since then, arsenic has been found in groundwater in West Bengal, Bangladesh, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Assam and other places. Dipankar Chakraborti, a scientist at Jadavpur University in Kolkata, spent decades testing tens of thousands of wells. Scientists agree the arsenic was not poured in by people. It comes naturally from mud washed down from the Himalayas long ago. The harder question is why it gets into the water, and whether pumping makes it worse.
- e.g. Arsenic has no colour, taste or smell. Only a test can find it.
- e.g. After testing, many tubewells are painted green if the water is safe and red if it is not.
- **Most scientists today:** The arsenic sits stuck to tiny grains of rust in the mud. Living things too small to see, called microbes, eat parts of the mud where there is no air. As they do this, the rust breaks down and lets the arsenic go into the water.
- **Charles Harvey and others, in a study in Bangladesh published in 2002:** Pumping huge amounts of groundwater for farms pulls new water and food for the microbes down into the ground. So pumping may be making more arsenic come loose.
- **Other scientists:** Pumping matters in some places but not others. The arsenic in many wells was released long before pumps arrived. The full answer is different from village to village, and is still being studied.
### What a water molecule is, and why water dissolves so much · *defined*
**Definition:** A water molecule is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom. Its ends carry tiny electric charges, so it pulls many other substances apart and dissolves them.

Zoom in to a drop of water, far past any microscope, and you reach molecules. Each one is one oxygen atom with two hydrogen atoms stuck on, like a head with two ears. Scientists write it H2O. The oxygen end has a tiny negative electric charge. The hydrogen ends have a tiny positive charge. Opposite charges attract. So water molecules attract the tiny charged parts of salt, sugar and many other things, and pull them apart. Those parts spread out between the water molecules and can no longer be seen. This is why water dissolves so many things. It is also why water is so hard to keep clean.
- e.g. Stir salt into water. Water molecules pull the salt apart, and it disappears.
- e.g. Oil does not dissolve in water, because oil molecules have no charged ends for water to pull on.
### Your water is very, very old · *idea*
Water does not get used up. It goes round and round. Rain falls, flows, soaks in, is drunk, is breathed out, evaporates and falls again. So the water in your glass has been in clouds, seas, rivers, plants and animals before you. Go back further. The hydrogen atoms in water were made in the Big Bang, about 13.8 billion years ago, when the universe began. The oxygen atoms were made inside stars. In 2014, a team led by Ilsedore Cleeves worked out something surprising. Up to half of the water on Earth may be older than the Sun itself. It may have formed as ice in a cold cloud of gas and dust, before our Sun was born.
- e.g. The water in your glass may once have been in a dinosaur, a cloud over the ocean, and a mango tree.
- e.g. When you breathe on a cold mirror, the mist is water leaving your body to start a new journey.
- **Scientists, about the atoms:** It is settled that hydrogen was made in the Big Bang and oxygen in stars. It is also settled that water goes round and round on Earth.
- **Scientists, about water older than the Sun:** This comes from computer models of how water forms in space. It is a strong idea, but not yet proved.
### Where did Earth's water come from? · *debated*
Earth has a lot of water. It covers about 70 out of every 100 parts of the planet's surface. But nobody knows for sure where it came from. Scientists can test ideas by looking at a rare, heavier kind of hydrogen in water. Water from different places in space has different amounts of it, like a fingerprint. So scientists compare the fingerprint of Earth's oceans with water from comets, asteroids and old rocks.
- e.g. In 2014, a spacecraft called Rosetta reached a comet. Its water had a different fingerprint from Earth's oceans.
- e.g. Some meteorites, rocks that fall from space, contain water locked inside them.
- **Some scientists:** Icy comets crashed into the young Earth and brought water. But the Rosetta comet's water did not match, so comets may have brought only a small part.
- **Many scientists:** Rocky asteroids from further out in the solar system brought most of it. Some meteorites from them have water that matches Earth's fingerprint well.
- **Laurette Piani and her team, in 2020:** The rocks that built the Earth may have held enough water already. They studied rare meteorites like Earth's building blocks, and found enough hydrogen to fill the oceans several times.
### Water beyond Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Water has been found on the Moon, on Mars and on several icy moons of Jupiter and Saturn, mostly as ice or locked in rock.

Water is not only on Earth. In 2009, scientists studying data from India's first Moon mission, Chandrayaan-1, found a thin trace of water on the Moon's surface. It was in the dust, not in lakes. Dark craters near the Moon's poles, where sunlight never reaches, hold ice. Mars has ice at its poles and under its ground. Far out in the solar system, Jupiter's moon Europa and Saturn's moon Enceladus seem to have huge oceans under thick shells of ice. In 2005, a spacecraft called Cassini saw jets of water spraying out of Enceladus into space. Wherever there is liquid water, scientists ask a big question. Could something live there?
- e.g. India's Chandrayaan-1 carried an instrument made by NASA, the American space agency. It found signs of water in the Moon's dust.
- e.g. Enceladus sprays water into space from cracks near its south pole.
### Water inside living bodies · *defined*
**Definition:** More than half of the human body is water. The kidneys clean the body's fluids every day.

Every living thing needs water. More than half of your body weight is water. A newborn baby is about three-quarters water. Your blood is mostly water. It carries food and oxygen to every part of you. You lose water every day when you breathe out, sweat and go to the toilet. So you must drink to replace it. Your two kidneys are your body's own water-cleaning plant. Each day an adult's kidneys clean about 180 litres of fluid. That is about 12 buckets of 15 litres. They keep most of it, and send out the waste as urine.
- e.g. Breathe onto a cold steel plate. The mist is water from inside you.
- e.g. When you are thirsty, your body is telling you it needs water back.
- e.g. Cacti live where rain may not come for months. They store water in their thick, fleshy stems, so they can keep living through the dry months.

## Mostly agreed
- Almost all the fresh water people use begins as rain. *How we know:* River levels, lake levels and well levels all rise after the monsoon and fall in the dry months. Weather stations across India measure the rain every day.
- Groundwater is rain that soaked into the ground. It fills the gaps in soil and rock, and it refills slowly. *How we know:* Wells rise after rain soaks in. Scientists measure water levels in thousands of wells across India. Satellites that weigh water with gravity show groundwater falling where too much is pumped.
- Germs in dirty water cause illnesses such as cholera and typhoid. *How we know:* In 1854, John Snow showed that people who drank from one London pump fell ill with cholera. Later, scientists saw the cholera germ under the microscope. Cities that cleaned their water saw these illnesses drop.
- Boiling kills germs, but it does not remove dissolved substances like salt or arsenic. *How we know:* Boil salty water until it dries up. The salt stays in the pot. Tests on boiled well water still find the arsenic.
- Water goes round and round. It is not destroyed, only moved and changed. *How we know:* Water that evaporates from a plate in the sun is not lost. It is in the air as vapour, and it comes down again as rain or dew.

## How we found out
**K. C. Saha, a skin doctor in Kolkata, and Dipankar Chakraborti of Jadavpur University, 1983, and the years of testing after it.** In the 1970s, many children in West Bengal and Bangladesh fell ill from germs in pond and river water. So governments and UNICEF, a United Nations group that helps children, paid for millions of tubewells. A tubewell is a narrow pipe pushed deep into the ground, with a handpump on top. Its water came from under the ground, away from the germs, and it was clear and cool. Fewer children got ill from germs. Nobody tested the water for anything else. In 1983, a skin doctor in Kolkata, K. C. Saha, saw patients with dark spots and hard, rough patches on their hands and feet. He traced the cause to arsenic, a poison, in their tubewell water. The arsenic had dissolved from the mud under the ground. Later, Dipankar Chakraborti and his team at Jadavpur University tested tens of thousands of wells. Today, wells in many villages are tested and painted green if safe and red if not.  
*What people believed before:* Clear water from under the ground, away from the germs, must be safe to drink. Patients' skin, and then tests on the wells, showed that it can carry arsenic dissolved from the rocks.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Water that is clear, cold and tastes good is safe to drink.** (Most people, most of the time; *rejected*)  
  For: Dirty-looking or smelly water is often unsafe. So it feels sensible to trust clear, sweet water.  
  Against: Germs and dissolved poisons are invisible. In 1854, London's favourite pump, known for its sweet water, spread cholera. Clear groundwater in parts of Bengal carries arsenic.
- **Illnesses like cholera come from bad smells in the air.** (Most doctors in Europe in the 1850s. They called the bad air 'miasma'.; *rejected*)  
  For: Cholera struck poor, crowded, smelly parts of cities most. People did not yet know about germs.  
  Against: John Snow showed that cholera followed the water, not the smell. A widow far away in Hampstead, where the air was fresh, died after drinking water from the Broad Street pump. The same year, Filippo Pacini in Italy saw the cholera germ under a microscope.
- **A well gets its water from rain falling into it, or from an underground river.** (A common picture in many people's minds; *rejected*)  
  For: Wells rise after rain. And some caves really do have underground streams.  
  Against: Very little rain falls straight into a well. Almost all well water seeps in from groundwater, which fills tiny gaps in soil and rock all around. Underground rivers are found in some limestone caves, but most groundwater is not a river.
- **Water is the 'universal solvent': it dissolves everything.** (Many science books and websites; *rejected*)  
  For: Water dissolves more substances than almost any other liquid, such as salt, sugar, many gases and many rocks over time.  
  Against: Water does not dissolve oil, wax, sand or plastic. Saying 'everything' goes too far. 'It dissolves a great many things' is true.
- **Big dams are the best way to give everyone water.** (Many engineers and governments, especially in the 20th century; *debated*)  
  For: Big dams store huge amounts of water for cities and farms, and make electricity. Many cities depend on them.  
  Against: Big dams can flood villages and forests, and force thousands of families to move. People like Anupam Mishra and Rajendra Singh showed that many small ponds, tanks and johads built by villagers can also store rain and refill wells. Most experts now say both small and big ways are needed.
- **When John Snow had the pump handle taken off, the cholera stopped.** (Many books and films; *debated*)  
  For: It is a great story, with one dramatic moment. The handle was taken off on 8 September 1854.  
  Against: Deaths were already falling by then, partly because many people had fled the area. Snow himself said so. Removing the handle may have stopped a second wave. His real proof was his careful map and his count of who drank which water.
- **Bottled water is always safer than water from a tap or a filter at home.** (Many advertisements; *debated*)  
  For: Sealed bottled water from a careful company is usually clean.  
  Against: Some bottled water has been found unsafe in tests. Water boiled or purified properly at home is also safe. And millions of plastic bottles end up as rubbish in rivers and seas.

## Roots
We have been learning where our water comes from, how we store it and when it is safe. Now we ask what the water in our glass tells us about the world it came through.

**Deep truth.** Plain tap water left a faint white ring when it dried. That ring is minerals, dissolved by the water from rock and soil on its way to you. Water picks up a little of every place it passes through. So each glass of water carries a trace of where it has been.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'plain water' or 'just water', as if water were empty. But almost no water on Earth is only water. It carries a little of the ground, the rock and the air it has passed through.  
*Evidence:* In the Invisible Ring, glass A and glass B looked the same, but B left a thick salt ring and plain tap water left a faint ring of minerals. Arsenic in Bengal tubewells came the same way, dissolved from the mud under the ground.

- *who am i:* More than half of you is water. Your blood carries salts and food to every part of you, the way groundwater carries minerals from rock.
- *what is life:* Every living thing we know needs water. Water carries food into a plant's roots and into every one of your cells.
- *what is this world:* Rain soaks through soil and rock, takes a little of them with it, and flows on to wells, rivers and the sea.

Rain is almost pure when it falls. As it soaks through soil and rock, it dissolves tiny amounts of them. Rivers carry these dissolved minerals to the sea, and over millions of years this helped make the sea salty. So the water in your glass, a well and the sea are joined by one journey, and each carries traces of the land it crossed.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the salt cannot be seen. But when Shvetaketu sips from the top, the middle and the bottom, the water tastes salty everywhere. (In the Invisible Ring, the salt in glass B could not be seen either. Drying the water showed that it was still there, spread all through the glass.)
- **Tao Te Ching 8:** The best way to live is like water. Water helps all things and does not fight them. It flows to the low places that people look down on. (Water sinks into the ground, the lowest place, and that is exactly where it is stored and kept safe for wells and roots.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), on 'interbeing':** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see a cloud in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, no tree, and no paper. (Look closely at a glass of water and you can find the rain, the rock it soaked through and the river or well it came from. The faint ring it leaves when it dries is part of that story.)
- Science (settled): Rivers carry minerals dissolved from rocks into the sea. Over millions of years, this is one main reason the sea is salty.
- Science (settled): Scientists can often tell which rocks groundwater has passed through by testing the minerals dissolved in it.
- Other view, Many chemists: Water itself is only H2O, two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. The minerals are other substances mixed into it, not part of the water. On this view, water carries things, but water itself keeps no record of where it has been.

Any taste comes from minerals it picked up from the ground. Think back along its journey: the tap, the pipe, the tank, a river or a well, the soil, and the rain. Now swallow slowly.

## Story: The Sweetest Water in London
*Real: the Broad Street pump and its famous water, the cholera outbreak of 1854, Mrs Susannah Eley in Hampstead who had the pump's water brought to her and died of cholera, her niece from Islington, the brewery workers and the workhouse with their own wells, Dr John Snow's questions and map, and the pump handle taken off on 8 September 1854. In real life, Snow learned about Mrs Eley from one of her sons. Tilly, the carter Mrs Pike, and their talk with Snow are made up.*

In the summer of 1854, the best water in London came out of a pump on Broad Street. Everybody said so. It was cold even in August. It was as clear as a window. People walked past two other pumps just to fill their jugs there.

Tilly was nine, and that pump was her job. Every morning she pumped the long iron handle until a big stone bottle was full. She pushed the cork in tight and lifted the bottle onto Mrs Pike's cart. Then she rode beside Mrs Pike, out of the crowded streets and up the long green hill to Hampstead.

In Hampstead, in a quiet house with roses by the door, lived old Mrs Eley. Her sons ran a factory on Broad Street. She had moved away to the fresh air of the hill, but she would drink no water except Broad Street water. 'Hampstead water tastes of nothing,' she told Tilly, every single morning. 'Broad Street water tastes of something.' Then she gave her a biscuit.

At the end of August, a terrible illness came to Broad Street. It was called cholera. It came fast, with a burning thirst and a sickness of the stomach. Some people who were well at breakfast were dead by night. The church bell rang and rang. Families piled their chairs onto carts and fled.

The doctors said it came from bad air. Soho smelled of drains, horses and rubbish, and in those days most people thought illness floated in on a stink. Tilly's mother tied a cloth soaked in vinegar over her nose. 'Breathe through that,' she said, 'and don't you dare stop for anybody.'

So Tilly did not stop. She pumped the bottle full and rode up the hill, where the air smelled of roses and cut grass. Up there, nobody had cholera. Up there, Tilly felt safe.

On Thursday she left the bottle at Mrs Eley's door, as always. Mrs Eley's niece was visiting from Islington, and she waved at Tilly from the window with a glass in her hand.

On Saturday, the curtains of the house were closed. Mrs Pike took off her hat and spoke to the maid. When she climbed back onto the cart, her face was grey. 'Cholera,' she said quietly. 'Both of them. The old lady, and the niece.'

Tilly could not understand it. All the way down the hill, she kept thinking. The bad air of Soho could not have floated all the way up here. The wind had been blowing the other way all week. Nobody else on the hill was ill. What had travelled from Broad Street to that house with the roses?

Then she looked down at her feet. In the straw of the cart sat the empty stone bottle, the one she had filled on Thursday.

Tilly felt sick. She had carried that bottle herself, every single day.

That afternoon, Tilly saw a thin, serious man walking slowly along Broad Street. He had a notebook. He was knocking on doors, and at every door he asked the same strange question. 'Where did this family get its water?' People said he was Dr Snow, and that he was a bit odd. Everybody knew it was the air.

Tilly ran after him. Her words came out in a tumble: the bottle, the cart, the hill, the roses, the old lady, the niece with the glass at the window.

Dr Snow stopped writing. He looked at Tilly for a long moment. 'Hampstead,' he said. 'Fresh air, and no cholera. And the only thing from Broad Street in that house was the water.' He wrote it down very carefully. 'Do you know the brewery on this street?' he asked. 'Seventy men work there. Not one has fallen ill. They drink beer, and the brewery has its own well. The workhouse round the corner has hundreds of people and its own well too. Hardly any of them are sick.'

'But the pump water is the best in London,' said Tilly. 'Everyone says so. It's so clear.'

'Yes,' said Dr Snow. 'It is very clear.' He closed his notebook. 'Whatever is making people ill is far too small to see. So we cannot ask our eyes. We have to ask the water where it went, and who drank it.'

That evening, Dr Snow went to the men who looked after the parish. He showed them his notes. On 8 September, they agreed to take the handle off the Broad Street pump.

People grumbled. They had to walk further, to a pump whose water was warmer and tasted of nothing. The next morning, Tilly stood beside the pump with no handle. A small boy came along with an empty jug, frowning. She pointed him down the road to the other pump.

'That water doesn't taste nice,' he said.

'No,' said Tilly. 'It doesn't.' And she walked with him, carrying the jug.

Later, Dr Snow drew a map of the streets. He put a little black bar for each person who had died. The bars piled up thickly round the Broad Street pump, like iron filings round a magnet. A young priest called Henry Whitehead, who at first thought Snow was wrong, went from house to house checking. In the end he found that a sick baby's nappies had been washed into a leaking pit right next to the well.

Today, on that same street in London, there is a copy of the old pump. It has no handle.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the clue that cracks the mystery is distance. The cholera reached Mrs Eley's house on a hill with fresh air, where nobody else was ill, and the only thing from Broad Street in that house was the water. So the illness was not in the air. It was in water that looked, smelled and tasted perfect. Germs are too small to see, so clear water is not always clean water. The way to find danger was to trace where the water came from and who drank it.

- Ask: Why did people trust the Broad Street pump more than any other?
- Ask: Mrs Eley lived far away, in fresh air, and nobody near her was ill. Why was that the big clue?
- Ask: The brewery men and the workhouse people mostly stayed well. What did they have that the others did not?
- Ask: Dr Snow said, 'We cannot ask our eyes.' What did he mean?
- Ask: Henry Whitehead set out to prove Dr Snow wrong, and changed his mind. Have you ever changed your mind because of evidence?

## Worksheets
### Water Detective (core) (core, 13 questions)
1. (mcq) Where does almost all the fresh water we use begin?  
   Options: In the tap / As rain / In the overhead tank / In the sea  
   **Answer:** As rain. *Why:* Rain fills rivers, lakes and the ground. Taps and tanks only pass water on. Picking the tap or the tank means stopping the trail too early. The sea is salty, so we cannot drink it without removing the salt.
2. (fill) Water hidden under the ground, in the gaps between grains of soil and rock, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['groundwater', 'ground water']. *Why:* Groundwater is rain that soaked into the ground. Wells and borewells pull it up.
3. (mcq) In Rain on Four Grounds, you pour the same mug of water on a tub of soil, a tub of sand, a tub of gravel and a tile. Guess first: where will most of the water run off?  
   Options: The sand / The gravel / The tile  
   **Answer:** The tile. *Why:* A tile has no gaps for water to go into, so the water runs off its edges. Sand, gravel and soil have gaps, so water soaks in. Gravel and sand soak it up fastest, because their gaps are biggest.
4. (sort) Sort these grounds. Does rain mostly soak in, or run off?  
   **Answer:** a grassy field → Soaks in; a tarred road → Runs off; a forest floor → Soaks in; a tin roof → Runs off; a sandy beach → Soaks in; a tiled courtyard → Runs off. *Why:* Soil, sand, grass and forest floors have gaps for water to trickle into. Roads, roofs and tiles have none.
5. (match) Match each water source to what it is.  
   **Answer:** River → water flowing over the land towards the sea; Borewell → a deep, narrow hole with a pump that brings up groundwater; Reservoir → a big lake made by building a dam across a river; Tanker → a truck that carries water to homes. *Why:* These are the main places our water comes from. Rivers and reservoirs are surface water. Borewells bring up groundwater. Tankers carry water from somewhere else.
6. (mcq) Why does water in a clay matka stay cool?  
   Options: Clay is made of something cold / The matka keeps the sunlight out / Water seeps through the clay and evaporates, taking heat away / The water inside slowly turns to ice  
   **Answer:** Water seeps through the clay and evaporates, taking heat away. *Why:* Evaporating water carries heat away, so the water inside cools. 'Clay is cold' is a common guess. But a dry clay pot left in the sun gets hot like anything else. Any covered pot keeps sunlight out, but that does not explain why a matka is cooler than a steel pot.
7. (mcq) Old stepwells in India have long stone stairs going down to the water. Why were the stairs built?  
   Options: So people could reach the water even when its level fell in the dry season / Only to make the well look beautiful / To make the water cold  
   **Answer:** So people could reach the water even when its level fell in the dry season. *Why:* In the dry months the groundwater level falls, and the water in the well sinks lower. The stairs let people walk down to it, however low it went. Many stepwells are also beautiful, but the stairs were built for reaching the water.
8. (mcq) Which way kills germs in water?  
   Options: Leaving it to settle / Pouring it through a cloth / Boiling it until big bubbles roll / Putting it in a steel tumbler  
   **Answer:** Boiling it until big bubbles roll. *Why:* Boiling kills germs. Settling and a cloth remove bits you can see, but germs are far too small to be caught that way. Most slip right through the cloth.
9. (fill) A bucket holds about 15 litres. A girl uses 2 full buckets of water for her bath. She uses ____ litres.  
   **Answer:** ['30', 'thirty']. *Why:* 2 lots of 15 litres is 15 + 15 = 30 litres. A litre is a unit for how much liquid something holds. A big water bottle often holds 1 litre.
10. (write) Write the journey of the water in your home, from where it really begins to your tap. Use arrows between the steps.  
   **Answer:** Any journey with a real source and real steps. For example: rain filled the river → the river water went to a treatment plant → pipes took the water to our sump → the motor lifted it to the overhead tank → it came down to our tap.. *Why:* A strong answer starts at a real source, such as a river, a lake or a borewell, and follows each step to the tap.
11. (write) Anu, a girl in Class 2, says: 'Our water comes from the overhead tank, so we will never run out.' What has she missed?  
   **Answer:** The tank does not make water. It is filled by a motor from a sump, a borewell or the city pipe. If the river, the lake or the groundwater runs low, the tank stays empty too.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the water back past the tank to its real source, and says that the source can run low.
12. (write) Name two places in your home or street where you could catch or save rain. Say how.  
   **Answer:** Any two sensible ideas. For example: put a bucket under the drainpipe to water the plants, or leave a patch of soil unpaved so rain can soak in.. *Why:* Catching rain from roofs, and leaving soil open so rain soaks in, both save water. A strong answer says how each idea would work.
13. (draw) Draw a well and the ground around it. Show where the groundwater is, and how it gets into the well.  
   **Answer:** The drawing shows water filling the gaps in soil or rock below the ground, with arrows showing it seeping into the well from the sides and the bottom.. *Why:* Well water seeps in from the groundwater all around. Very little falls straight in as rain.
### Water in Real Life (stretch) (stretch, 12 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at the chart from the Cool Matka Test. Which container kept the water coolest?  
   Options: The steel bottle / The bottle in a wet cloth / The clay matka  
   **Answer:** The clay matka. *Why:* The matka's bar is the shortest, at 24 °C. On this chart a shorter bar means cooler water. Picking the steel bottle usually means reading the tallest bar as the best.
2. (mcq) On a hot, dry day the matka water is cool. On a sticky, humid day in the monsoon, it is hardly cooler than the room. Why?  
   Options: The clay gets wet from the rain / Water evaporates slowly when the air is already full of water vapour / Matkas only work in summer  
   **Answer:** Water evaporates slowly when the air is already full of water vapour. *Why:* The matka cools because water evaporates from its outside. Humid air already holds a lot of vapour, so evaporation is slow, and less heat is carried away. This is also why wet clothes dry slowly in the monsoon.
3. (mcq) In the jar well, the water level fell after 5 pumps. Then you covered the soil with plastic and poured more rain on top. What happened?  
   Options: The level rose a lot / The level did not rise, because the rain could not soak in / The jar overflowed  
   **Answer:** The level did not rise, because the rain could not soak in. *Why:* The plastic stopped the rain soaking down, just like cement over real ground. So nothing refilled the groundwater. Picking 'rose a lot' means thinking rain reaches the well whatever covers the ground.
4. (sort) What does each way of cleaning water do? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** settling → Removes bits you can see; a cloth filter → Removes bits you can see; boiling → Kills germs; chlorine → Kills germs; a sand filter → Removes bits you can see; strong sunlight on a clear bottle for 6 hours → Kills germs. *Why:* Settling and filters catch bits that are big enough to sink or get stuck. Boiling, chlorine and strong sunlight kill germs.
5. (mcq) Why does a city with lots of roads and buildings often flood in heavy rain?  
   Options: Cities get more rain than villages / Buildings make rain / Rain cannot soak into cement and tar, so it all runs off at once  
   **Answer:** Rain cannot soak into cement and tar, so it all runs off at once. *Why:* Hard ground has no gaps, so the rain runs off all at once and overfills the drains. Cities usually do not get more rain than villages. The difference is the ground, not the sky.
6. (match) Match each water store to what it is.  
   **Answer:** Tanka → a covered underground tank in the Thar desert; Stepwell → a deep well with stairs down to the water; Matka → a clay pot that keeps drinking water cool; Overhead tank → a tank on a roof that sends water down the pipes. *Why:* These are ways Indian homes and villages store water, old and new.
7. (order) Put the steps of a city water treatment plant in order.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* First the water is collected. Then heavy bits settle, smaller bits are filtered out, germs are killed with chlorine, and only then is it sent to homes.
8. (mcq) Look at the chart from a Family Water Count. Which job used the most water?  
   Options: Drinking / The toilet / Bathing / Washing clothes  
   **Answer:** Bathing. *Why:* Bathing has the tallest bar, at 150 litres. The toilet is second, at 120 litres. Picking drinking means guessing from what we think matters most, not reading the chart.
9. (perspective) In a village near Kolar, in Karnataka, the wells are drying up. Three people disagree about what to do.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses the jar well as evidence: pumping lowered the water for the whole jar, not just near the straw. It answers back fairly from another side, for example Selvi: a limit must still let farmers earn a living.. *Why:* This is a real problem in many parts of India. Groundwater is shared, but each farmer needs it. There is no single right answer, so listen for evidence and fairness.
10. (write) A family boils their drinking water every morning. Then they keep it in an open bucket on the floor and dip a tumbler in to drink. By evening, is the water still safe? What would you tell them to change?  
   **Answer:** It may not be safe any more. Germs can get back in from dust, flies, and hands or tumblers dipped into the bucket. Keep the boiled water covered, up off the floor, and pour it out with a ladle or a tap instead of dipping in.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that boiling only kills the germs that are there at that moment. Germs can come back in, so clean water must also be stored safely.
11. (write) In the Invisible Ring, even the tap water left a faint ring on the dark plate. Does that mean the tap water is unsafe? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Not by itself. The ring shows that the tap water has some dissolved minerals, such as calcium. Small amounts of these are normal and harmless. The ring tells you something is dissolved, but not what it is or whether it is harmful. Only a proper test can tell that.. *Why:* A strong answer separates 'something is dissolved' from 'something harmful is dissolved'. Almost all water has some minerals in it.
12. (write) Choose one job that uses lots of water in your home. Write two ways your family could use less for that job.  
   **Answer:** For example, bathing: use a bucket instead of a shower, and turn off the tap while soaping. Any two real ideas are good.. *Why:* Small daily changes in the biggest water jobs save the most water. A strong answer picks a big job, not drinking.
### Water Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 11 questions)
1. (mcq) A village well dried up after the forest on the hills above it was cut down. The hills became bare and hard. Why did the well dry up?  
   Options: The well was dug too deep / Rain ran off the bare hills before it could soak in and refill the groundwater / Trees drink all the rain, so cutting them should have helped  
   **Answer:** Rain ran off the bare hills before it could soak in and refill the groundwater. *Why:* Forest floors slow rain down and let it soak in. Bare, hard ground makes it run away. So less water refilled the ground. Trees do use water, but forests help far more rain soak in than they use.
2. (mcq) Scientists measured the groundwater under north-west India using two satellites. What did the satellites actually measure?  
   Options: Photographs of underground lakes / Tiny changes in the pull of gravity / The temperature of the soil  
   **Answer:** Tiny changes in the pull of gravity. *Why:* Water is heavy, so it adds a tiny bit to the Earth's pull of gravity. When groundwater is pumped out, the pull gets slightly weaker, and the satellites feel it. Satellites cannot see under the ground. Groundwater is also not mostly in lakes.
3. (truefalse) Some of the water on Earth may be older than the Sun.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True, scientists think. A 2014 study worked out that up to half of Earth's water may have formed as ice in space before the Sun was born. It is a strong idea, but not yet proved.
4. (order) Put these places in order, from the most rain in a year to the least.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Mawsynram gets nearly 12 metres. Mumbai gets about 2 metres. Delhi gets about 80 centimetres. Jaisalmer gets about 20 centimetres.
5. (write) Sohra, also called Cherrapunji, in Meghalaya, is one of the wettest places on Earth. Yet in winter many families there run short of drinking water. How can that be?  
   **Answer:** Almost all the rain falls in a few monsoon months. The hills are steep and much of the land is bare rock or thin soil, so the rain runs off fast instead of soaking in. Little is stored in tanks or in the ground. So in the dry winter months the springs and streams run low.. *Why:* A strong answer says that how much rain falls matters less than whether it soaks in or is stored. It challenges the idea that lots of rain always means lots of water.
6. (sort) A family boils all their drinking water. Which problems does boiling fix, and which does it not fix?  
   **Answer:** cholera germs → Boiling fixes it; arsenic from a deep well → Boiling does not fix it; germs from a dirty pond → Boiling fixes it; too much fluoride → Boiling does not fix it; salt from the sea → Boiling does not fix it; worm eggs → Boiling fixes it. *Why:* Boiling kills living things such as germs and worm eggs. It cannot remove things that are dissolved, such as arsenic, fluoride and salt. Those stay in the pot when water boils away.
7. (perspective) A city must decide how people should pay for water.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a real reason, then looks at the poorest family under each idea. For example: free water helps the poor family but may leave the pipes leaking, so they might get dirty water. Paying for everything may leave them thirsty. Some Indian cities already give a little free water to every home and charge for more.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. This is a real choice that cities make. Listen for reasons and for thinking about fairness.
8. (write) Design a fair test to find out whether a matka cools water better in a breezy place or a still place. What will you keep the same? What will you change?  
   **Answer:** Use two matkas of the same size and clay, filled with the same water at the same time. Put one in a breeze and one in still air, both in the shade. Measure both after the same time. Change only the breeze.. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. Everything else stays the same, so you know the breeze made the difference.
9. (draw) Draw the journey of one water molecule over a very long time. Show at least five places it has been, from space to your glass.  
   **Answer:** Any drawing with a sensible order, such as: ice in space, an icy comet or rock, the sea, a cloud, rain, a river, groundwater, a tree, a dinosaur, a well, your glass.. *Why:* Water goes round and round for billions of years. It is never new and never used up.
10. (mcq) In 2019, Chennai's main lakes nearly dried up. How did the city get water?  
   Options: Trains carried water from Jolarpettai, more than 200 kilometres away / It waited for snow / It used sea water just as it was  
   **Answer:** Trains carried water from Jolarpettai, more than 200 kilometres away. *Why:* Special trains carried millions of litres of water to Chennai every day. Sea water cannot be drunk until the salt is removed, which needs special plants and lots of electricity.
11. (truefalse) Many people say India is short of water mainly because it does not rain enough. Is that true?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. India gets a lot of rain, but mostly in a few months. In many places more groundwater is pumped out than rain puts back, and rain on hard ground runs away to the sea. Saving and soaking in rain matters as much as how much falls.

   **Answer:** An overhead tank. *Why:* An overhead tank is filled by a motor from a sump, a borewell or a pipe. Rivers and groundwater are real sources, filled by rain.

   **Answer:** Rain held in tiny gaps between grains of soil and rock, like water in a sponge. *Why:* Groundwater fills tiny gaps, like water in a sponge. 'A big lake' is a very common picture, but it is mostly wrong. Puddles are on top of the ground, not in it.
3. (mcq) Rain falls on four places. Where will it soak in fastest?  
   Options: A cement floor / A tin roof / Sand / A tiled courtyard  
   **Answer:** Sand. *Why:* Sand has big gaps between its grains, so water rushes in. Cement, tin and tiles have no gaps, so rain runs off.

   Options: Pouring more rain on top / Pumping water up the straw / Leaving it alone

   **Answer:** Water must seep through tiny holes in the clay and evaporate on the outside. *Why:* The cooling comes from water evaporating on the outside of the pot. A shiny glaze blocks the tiny holes, so no water seeps out, and the pot stays as warm as a steel one.

## Applications
- City water treatment plants settle, filter and add chlorine to river water before it reaches our taps.
- Overhead tanks use gravity to push water down to taps, even when the power is off.
- Matkas and clay coolers keep water and food cool without electricity.
- Rainwater harvesting: gutters and pipes lead roof rain into tanks or back into the ground.
- Tankas, kunds, johads, ponds and stepwells have stored rain in India for centuries.
- Water testing kits check wells for germs, arsenic and fluoride.
- Desalination plants, such as those near Chennai, take salt out of sea water.
- Farmers use drip irrigation, which gives each plant small drops at their roots, to save water.
- Satellites that weigh water with gravity show where groundwater is being used up.
- Doctors give oral rehydration solution, clean water with sugar and salt, to save people with diarrhoea.
- Fire engines, hospitals and factories all need large stores of clean water.
- Hydroelectric dams use falling water to make electricity.
- Bleaching powder is added to village wells to kill germs, especially after floods.
- Water meters measure how many litres each home uses.

## Open questions
- **Where did Earth's water come from in the first place?** Comets, asteroids and Earth's own rocks are all possible. The chemical fingerprints do not yet settle it.
- **How much groundwater is under India, and how fast is it refilling?** It is hidden, it moves slowly and it differs from village to village. Satellites and well records give only a rough picture.
- **Can we predict a good or a poor monsoon months ahead?** The monsoon depends on oceans, winds and land together, far away and nearby. Forecasts are getting better, but they are still often wrong.
- **Why does arsenic come loose into some wells and not others nearby?** Microbes, mud and pumping all seem to matter, but the mix is different in every place.
- **Is there life in the hidden oceans of icy moons like Europa and Enceladus?** They seem to have liquid water, and some of the chemicals life needs. Nobody has looked inside yet. Missions to Europa are on their way.
- **How can we make clean drinking water cheap for everyone, without using lots of energy or wasting water?** Boiling uses fuel, RO wastes water and removing salt from the sea uses lots of electricity. Scientists are testing new filters and materials.
- **Could all life everywhere need liquid water, or could some life use something else?** Every living thing we know needs water. But we have only ever studied life on one planet.

## Connected maths
- A tap drips 50 millilitres in 10 minutes. How much does it drip in one hour? How much in one whole day of 24 hours? Give the day's answer in litres and millilitres. **300 millilitres in one hour. 7,200 millilitres in a day, which is 7 litres and 200 millilitres.**. One hour has 6 lots of 10 minutes. So in one hour the tap drips 6 × 50 = 300 millilitres. A day has 24 hours. 24 × 300 = 7,200 millilitres. 1,000 millilitres make 1 litre. So 7,000 millilitres is 7 litres, and 200 millilitres are left over. That is about half a bucket, from one small drip. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Look at the family water chart. Bathing used 150 litres, the toilet 120, washing clothes 80, dishes and cooking 60, cleaning the floor 20 and drinking 10. How many litres did the family use altogether in the day? **440 litres.**. Add the bars two at a time. 150 + 120 = 270. 80 + 60 = 140. 20 + 10 = 30. Then 270 + 140 = 410, and 410 + 30 = 440. So the family used 440 litres in one day. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)
- A family of 4 used 440 litres of water in one day. If each person used the same amount, how many litres did each person use? Is that more or less than the 135 litres a day that city planners allow for each person? **110 litres each. That is 25 litres less than 135 litres.**. Share 440 litres equally among 4 people. 440 ÷ 4 = 110, because 4 × 110 = 440. So each person used 110 litres. 135 take away 110 is 25. So each person used 25 litres less than 135. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- A bucket holds 15 litres. One bucket bath uses one full bucket. A shower runs for 10 minutes and uses 6 litres every minute. Which uses more water, and by how much? **The shower uses more. It uses 60 litres, which is 45 litres more than the 15-litre bucket.**. The shower runs for 10 minutes at 6 litres a minute. 10 × 6 = 60 litres. The bucket bath uses 15 litres. 60 take away 15 is 45. So the shower uses 45 litres more. That is 3 more whole buckets. (queues `math.weight-capacity.capacity.L3`)
- Look at the thermometer from the Cool Matka Test. Each big number is 10 degrees, and each small mark is 1 degree. What does it read? The water started at 30 °C. How many degrees did it cool? **It reads 24 °C. The water cooled by 6 degrees.**. The liquid stops after the 20 mark, at the fourth small mark. Each small mark is 1 degree. So it reads 20 + 4 = 24 °C. The water started at 30 °C. 30 take away 24 is 6. So the matka cooled the water by 6 degrees. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- Riya, a girl in Class 3, did Rain on Four Grounds with more water. She poured 8 cups of water onto bare soil, one cup at a time. 6 cups soaked in. 2 cups ran off. What fraction of the water ran off? **2 out of 8, which is the same as 1 quarter.**. 2 cups out of 8 ran off. That is 2 eighths. Put the 8 cups into 4 equal groups of 2. One group ran off. So 2 eighths is the same as 1 quarter. The other 3 quarters soaked in. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- Use the rain rule: 1 millimetre of rain on 1 square metre of roof gives 1 litre. A roof is 40 square metres. In one storm, 25 millimetres of rain fall. How many litres could the roof catch? How many 15-litre buckets is that, roughly? **1,000 litres. That is more than 66 buckets.**. Each square metre catches 25 litres, one for each millimetre. 40 square metres catch 40 × 25 litres. 40 × 25 = 1,000 litres. One bucket holds 15 litres. 15 × 66 = 990, so 1,000 litres is a little more than 66 buckets. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
