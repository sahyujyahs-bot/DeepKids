# Where food comes from: tracing a meal back to plants and the Sun
`sci.food.where-food-comes-from.L3`

**Summary.** Almost every food on your plate was made by something alive, mostly by plants. Even curd goes back, through bacteria and a cow, to the grass the cow ate. Only salt and water come from the non-living Earth. Follow almost any food back far enough and you reach a plant using sunlight. Indian families have also kept old knowledge about food, like the six tastes, and science agrees with some of it.

**Intro.** *Look at your plate at your next meal. Pick one thing on it. Now ask: where was this a year ago? Was it alive? Was it in the air, in the soil, or in the sea?* Most children say food comes from the shop. That is true, but it is only the last step. Before the shop there was a farm, a dairy, a tree or a fishing boat. Before the farm there was a seed. This unit follows food back, step by step, to where it begins. On the way, you split open a seed and find a tiny plant inside. You find out what turns milk into curd overnight. And you meet deep-sea animals whose food chain does not start with sunlight.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can trace a food back to where it came from: a plant, an animal, a fungus or tiny living thing, or the non-living Earth.
1. I can say which part of a plant a food comes from: root, stem, leaf, flower, fruit or seed.
2. I can name foods we get from animals, and say what those animals eat.
3. I can explain that every food chain starts with plants, and that plants make food from sunlight, air and water.
4. I can run a fair test, changing only one thing, and record my results in a table and a bar chart.
5. I can describe old Indian knowledge about food, like the six tastes and eating with the seasons. I can say which parts science agrees with and which are still argued about.
6. I can give big examples from the edge of science. Deep-sea vent life eats chemicals instead of sunlight. Seed banks keep food safe for the future. My own body is rebuilt from what I eat.

## Terms
- **Food**: Anything a living thing takes in to get energy and to build their body. *e.g.* Rice gives you energy to run. Dal helps build your muscles.
- **Plant**: A living thing that makes their own food from sunlight, air and water. They grow rooted in one place. *e.g.* A rice plant, a mango tree and a tulsi plant.
- **Root**: The part of a plant that grows under the ground. It holds the plant in place and takes in water from the soil. *e.g.* Carrot, radish (mooli) and beetroot are roots we eat.
- **Stem**: The part of a plant that holds the plant up and carries water and food. Some stems grow under the ground. *e.g.* Sugarcane is a stem. A potato is a fat underground stem.
- **Leaf**: The flat green part of a plant that catches sunlight and makes food. *e.g.* Palak, methi, cabbage and curry leaves are leaves we eat.
- **Flower**: The part of a plant that makes seeds. Some flowers and flower buds are food. *e.g.* Cauliflower and banana flower are flowers. A clove is a dried flower bud.
- **Fruit**: The part of a plant that grows from a flower and holds the seeds. *e.g.* Mango, guava and banana are fruits. So are tomato, bhindi and brinjal.
- **Seed**: A baby plant inside a tough coat, with a store of food beside them. A dry seed is dormant: they rest until they get water. *e.g.* Rice, wheat, moong, peanuts and jeera are all seeds.
- **Grain**: The seed of a grass plant that we eat. *e.g.* Rice, wheat, ragi, jowar, bajra and maize are grains.
- **Pulses**: The dried seeds of bean and pea plants. We cook them as dal. *e.g.* Moong, toor, masoor, chana and rajma are pulses.
- **Fungus (more than one: fungi)**: A living thing that is not a plant and not an animal. They cannot make food from sunlight. They feed on dead or living things around them. *e.g.* Mushrooms are fungi. So is the yeast that makes bread rise.
- **Microbe**: A living thing far too small to see without a microscope. Some microbes make us ill. Many help us. *e.g.* Tiny microbes turn milk into curd and make idli batter rise.
- **Bacteria**: One kind of microbe. Each one is a single living cell. Bacteria live almost everywhere: in soil, water, air and inside our bodies. *e.g.* Bacteria called lactobacillus turn milk into curd.
- **Fermentation**: When microbes slowly change a food by feeding on it. The food often turns sour, bubbly or thick. *e.g.* Idli batter left overnight rises and smells sour. That is fermentation.
- **Mineral**: A non-living material that comes from rocks, soil or the sea, such as salt. Our bodies need small amounts of some minerals, like iron and calcium. *e.g.* Salt is a mineral. Plants take up iron and calcium from the soil, and we get them by eating the plants.
- **Photosynthesis**: How a green plant makes food. Their leaves use the energy of sunlight to join carbon dioxide from the air with water, making sugar. *e.g.* A mango leaf in the sun is making food right now.
- **Food chain**: A list of who eats whom, starting from a plant. *e.g.* A cow eats grass. You drink the cow's milk. So the chain is grass, then cow, then you.
- **Starch**: A kind of food that plants make and store, mostly in seeds, roots and underground stems. It gives us energy. *e.g.* Rice, potato, chapati and ragi are full of starch.
- **Crop**: A plant that farmers grow in large amounts. *e.g.* Rice, wheat, sugarcane and cotton are crops.
- **Seasonal food**: Food that grows and is fresh at a certain time of year. *e.g.* Mangoes in summer. Carrots, peas and sarson in winter.
- **Rasa**: The old Sanskrit word for taste. Old Indian books named six rasas. *e.g.* Sweet, sour, salty, pungent (spicy), bitter and astringent (the dry, puckery feel of raw banana).
- **Nectar**: A sweet juice made inside flowers. Bees drink it and turn it into honey. *e.g.* A bee sipping from a mustard flower is collecting nectar.
- **Farming**: Growing plants and keeping animals for food, instead of only finding them in the wild. People began farming about 11,000 years ago. *e.g.* Before farming, people gathered fruit, roots and seeds, and hunted animals.
- **Seed bank**: A safe store where seeds of many kinds of crop are kept, so they are never lost. *e.g.* The Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway keeps more than a million packets of seeds deep inside a frozen mountain.
- **Hydrothermal vent**: A crack in the deep sea floor where very hot, mineral-rich water gushes out. Strange animals live around it without any sunlight. *e.g.* Giant tube worms live beside vents about 2.5 kilometres under the Pacific Ocean.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of, far too small to see. *e.g.* Some of the atoms in your dal will become part of your body.
- **Energy**: What it takes to move, grow, keep warm or change. Food carries energy that first came from the Sun. *e.g.* The energy in a banana lets you run and jump.

## Explanations
### What food is, and the four places it comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** Food is anything a living thing takes in to get energy and to build their body. Our food comes from four kinds of places: plants, animals, fungi and microbes, and the non-living Earth.

Look at any meal and sort it. Rice, dal, vegetables, fruit and spices come from plants. Milk, curd, ghee, eggs, fish and honey come from animals. Mushrooms are fungi, and curd and idli batter are changed by microbes, tiny living things. Salt and water are the odd ones out. They were never alive. They come from the sea, the rocks and the rain. So almost all of our food was once part of something alive.
- e.g. A plate of idli, sambar and coconut chutney: rice and urad dal (plants), tiny microbes that made the batter rise, tamarind and vegetables (plants), salt (the Earth), water (the Earth).
- e.g. A glass of lassi: milk from a cow or buffalo, microbes that turned the milk into curd, sugar from sugarcane, and water.
- e.g. A mushroom masala: mushrooms (fungi), onion and tomato (plants), oil from mustard or groundnut seeds, and salt.
### Which part of the plant are we eating? · *defined*
**Definition:** We eat all six parts of plants: roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits and seeds. From each plant we usually eat only one or two parts.

A plant is one living thing doing many jobs. The root takes in water and often stores food. The stem holds the plant up and carries water. Leaves make food from sunlight. Flowers make seeds. Fruits hold the seeds. Seeds are baby plants with food packed around them. People found out long ago which parts of which plants are good to eat. Some surprises: a potato is not a root. It is a fat underground stem, and its 'eyes' are buds that can grow shoots. Ginger and haldi are underground stems too. Cinnamon is the bark of a tree. A clove is a dried flower bud. Saffron is the thread-like middle part of a crocus flower.
- e.g. Roots: carrot, radish, beetroot, sweet potato.
- e.g. Stems: sugarcane, potato, ginger, haldi, lotus stem (kamal kakdi).
- e.g. Leaves: palak, methi, cabbage, curry leaves, tea leaves.
- e.g. Flowers: cauliflower, banana flower, cloves, saffron.
- e.g. Fruits: mango, banana, tomato, bhindi, brinjal, chilli, pepper.
- e.g. Seeds: rice, wheat, all dals, peanuts, jeera, mustard, coffee beans.
### Is a tomato a fruit or a vegetable? · *debated*
This sounds like a silly question, but people really argue about it. The answer depends on who is doing the sorting. Botanists have a clear meaning for 'fruit'. 'Vegetable' is a cooking word, not a science word.
- e.g. Bhindi, brinjal, cucumber, pumpkin, chilli and tomato all grow from flowers and hold seeds.
- e.g. Nobody puts brinjal in a fruit salad, even though a botanist calls it a fruit.
- **Botanists, the scientists who study plants:** A fruit is the part that grows from a flower and holds the seeds. So tomato, bhindi and brinjal are fruits. 'Vegetable' is not a science word at all. It just means any other plant part we eat.
- **Cooks:** A fruit is sweet and eaten raw or as a dessert. A vegetable is cooked in a poriyal or a curry. So a tomato is a vegetable.
- **Judges of the United States Supreme Court, in 1893:** A family of tomato importers argued that tomatoes were fruits, so they should not pay the tax on imported vegetables. The judges agreed that botanists call tomatoes fruits. But they ruled that in everyday speech a tomato is a vegetable, so the tax had to be paid.
### Food from animals, and what the animals ate · *defined*
**Definition:** Some of our food comes from animals: milk and everything made from it, eggs, honey, meat and fish. Every one of those animals got their own food from plants, or from animals that ate plants.

Milk comes from cows, buffaloes and goats. From milk we make curd, paneer, butter, ghee and khoa. Hens lay eggs. Fish, prawns and crabs come from rivers and the sea. Honey is made by honeybees. They drink sweet nectar from flowers and store it in little wax cells in their hive. They fan it with their wings until most of the water evaporates. That means the water turns into vapour and goes into the air. One worker bee makes only about a twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in her whole life. So every spoon of honey is the work of many bees and many thousands of flowers. Now ask what each animal ate. A cow eats grass and fodder. A hen eats grain. A bee drinks nectar. A fish eats algae, tiny water creatures or smaller fish. So animal food is really plant food that has passed through an animal first.
- e.g. Ghee comes from butter, which comes from milk, which came from a cow, which ate grass.
- e.g. Honey comes from bees, which drank nectar from flowers.
- e.g. Some families eat no animal food at all. Some eat milk but no eggs or meat. Families make different choices for health, belief and kindness.
### Food made by fungi and microbes · *defined*
**Definition:** Fungi and microbes are living things that are not plants and not animals. Mushrooms are fungi we eat. Many microbes help make our food, by fermenting it.

Mushrooms are not plants. They have no green leaves and cannot make food from sunlight. They feed on dead leaves, wood or soil. Scientists have found that fungi are actually more closely related to animals than to plants. Microbes are even smaller living things. Put a spoon of curd into warm milk and leave it overnight. Bacteria from the old curd feed on the sugar in the milk. They make lactic acid, which turns the milk thick and sour. By morning there are billions of them, and the milk has become curd. Idli and dosa batter rise and turn sour for the same reason. Bacteria and yeasts feed on the soaked rice and urad dal. Yeasts are tiny fungi, each just one cell. People in India have used microbes to make curd, idli, dhokla, kanji and appam for many hundreds of years. They did it long before anyone knew microbes existed.
- e.g. Curd is milk changed by bacteria.
- e.g. Bread and bhatura rise because yeast makes tiny bubbles of gas.
- e.g. Idli batter left overnight rises and smells sour.
### Salt and water: food that was never alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Salt and water are the two common foods that do not come from plants, animals or microbes. They come from the non-living Earth.

Water falls as rain, flows in rivers and sits under the ground. Our bodies are more than half water, and we lose some every day in sweat, breath and urine. Salt is a mineral. Most of India's salt comes from Gujarat. There, people let sea water into shallow fields called salt pans. The Sun dries the water away and leaves white salt crystals behind. Rock salt, sendha namak, is dug out of the ground. It is the salt from seas that dried up millions of years ago. Our bodies also need tiny amounts of other minerals, like iron and calcium. Plants take them up from the soil, and we get them by eating the plants.
- e.g. Salt pans in the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, and the Sambhar salt lake in Rajasthan.
- e.g. A cucumber is about 95 parts water out of every 100. So even plant food is mostly water from the Earth.
### Every food chain begins with a plant, and every plant begins with the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** A food chain is a list of who eats whom. On land, every food chain starts with a plant, and in water most start with tiny algae. Plants and algae make food using the energy of sunlight. So the energy in almost all food first came from sunlight.

Follow your curd back. Curd came from milk. Milk came from a cow. The cow ate grass. The grass plants made themselves out of sunlight, air and water. Follow fish curry back. The fish ate smaller fish. The smaller fish ate tiny floating algae. The algae made food from sunlight. Every chain on land and in the sunlit sea ends at a plant or an alga, and then at the Sun. There is a rough rule too. When a cow eats grass, only about a tenth of the grass's energy ends up in the cow's body. The rest is used for moving, keeping warm and living. So it takes a lot of grass to feed one cow. Feeding people on plants directly needs much less land.
- e.g. Sun, then grass, then cow, then milk, then you.
- e.g. Sun, then algae in the sea, then small fish, then big fish, then fish curry.
- e.g. Sun, then mustard flower, then bee, then honey, then you.
### What plants make their food from · *defined*
**Definition:** Green plants make their food from air, water and sunlight. Their leaves take in a gas from the air called carbon dioxide. Their roots take in water. Sunlight gives the energy to join them into sugar. This is called photosynthesis.

Most people guess that plants eat soil. About 380 years ago, a scientist named Jan Baptist van Helmont, in what is now Belgium, tested this. He weighed some dry soil and put the soil in a pot. He planted a small willow tree in the pot and gave the tree only rainwater. Five years later, the tree had grown about 30 times heavier. But the soil had lost only a tiny pinch of weight. So the tree had not eaten the soil. Van Helmont thought the tree was made of water. Scientists later found that most of the tree's dry wood was built from carbon dioxide, a gas in the air. A wheat grain, a mango and a grain of rice are mostly built out of air and water, joined together using the energy of sunlight. The soil gives only small amounts of minerals.
- e.g. A tall coconut tree is built mostly from air and water.
- e.g. A few months ago, the rice on your plate was carbon dioxide gas in the air over a paddy field.
### What is inside a seed · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed is a baby plant inside a tough coat, with a store of food beside them. When we eat grains, dal and nuts, we are eating that stored food.

Soak a chana or a peanut overnight and gently split it in two. You will find two fat halves and, at one end, a tiny curled baby plant. The fat halves are their food store. They are full of starch, protein and oil that the mother plant packed for them. A dry seed is dormant, which means resting. They can stay that way for months, or even years, as long as they are dry and cool. When they get water and warmth, they start to grow, using their food store to make a root and a shoot. That is why seeds are such good food for us. They are small, they keep for a long time, and they are packed with energy. Rice, wheat, ragi, bajra, all the dals, peanuts, and even jeera and mustard are seeds.
- e.g. Soaked moong grows a little white root in a day or two.
- e.g. A whole grain of brown rice has a tiny baby plant at one end. Polishing white rice rubs most of that baby plant off.
- e.g. People have stored seeds for months between harvests since farming began.
### You are built out of your food · *defined*
**Definition:** Your body is made of atoms. Almost all of them came from the food you ate, and the water and air you took in. Your body keeps replacing its old parts with new parts built from food.

When you eat, your body breaks food down into tiny pieces. It uses some for energy, like fuel. It uses the rest as building blocks. The lining of your gut is rebuilt every few days. Each red blood cell lasts only about 120 days, about four months, and is then replaced with a new one. So the blood in you now was built from food you ate in the last few months. The carbon atoms in today's rice were carbon dioxide in the air over a paddy field last season. Before that, they may have been in a tree, a dinosaur, or the breath of someone long ago. Atoms do not wear out. They just move from one living thing to the next. And apart from hydrogen, most of those atoms were first made inside stars that burned out before the Sun was born.
- e.g. The dal you eat this week helps build the muscles you use next month.
- e.g. A carbon atom in your fingernail may once have been in a mango, a cow and the air.
### Life with no sunlight: food at the bottom of the sea · *defined*
**Definition:** Deep under the sea, where sunlight never reaches, some living things get their food from chemicals instead of light. They live around hot springs on the sea floor called hydrothermal vents.

In 1977, scientists dived more than 2 kilometres down to the floor of the Pacific Ocean in a small submarine called Alvin. They were studying hot springs on the sea floor. To their amazement, they found crowds of life there: giant tube worms taller than a child, white crabs and huge clams. There are no plants down there, because there is no light. So what is at the start of the food chain? Microbes. They take chemicals from the hot vent water and use them to make food, the way plants use sunlight. This is called chemosynthesis, which means making with chemicals. The giant tube worms have no mouth and no stomach. Billions of these microbes live inside their bodies and feed them from within. Some scientists think the very first life on Earth may have begun near vents like these. That is still an idea, not proven.
- e.g. Giant tube worms near vents can grow taller than a grown-up.
- e.g. Some microbes in soil and in deep rocks also make food from chemicals, without sunlight.
### Six tastes or five? Old Indian knowledge meets today's science · *debated*
About 2,000 years ago, the Charaka Samhita, one of the oldest books of Ayurveda, India's traditional medicine, named six tastes, or rasas. They are madhura (sweet), amla (sour), lavana (salty), katu (pungent or spicy), tikta (bitter) and kashaya (astringent, the dry puckery feel of raw banana or strong tea). It taught that a good meal should have all six in balance. A festive thali often still does. Today, scientists study the taste cells on the tongue. They agree with some of the old list and explain other parts differently.
- e.g. A thali with sweet payasam, sour lemon pickle, salty papad, spicy chutney, bitter karela and astringent raw banana poriyal has all six rasas.
- e.g. When you bite a chilli, your mouth feels hot, even though the chilli itself is not hot.
- **The Charaka Samhita, an Ayurveda book about 2,000 years old:** There are six tastes. Each one has its own effect on the body. A healthy meal balances all six.
- **Taste scientists today:** The tongue has taste cells for five basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. Umami is a savoury taste, found in ripe tomatoes, mushrooms and cheese. It was named in 1908 by Kikunae Ikeda, a Japanese chemist. Spicy is not a taste at all. Chilli switches on nerves that sense heat and pain. David Julius shared a Nobel Prize in 2021 partly for finding the nerve sensor that chilli switches on. Astringent is a touch feeling, a drying of the mouth.
- **Scientists still arguing:** Some think there may be more basic tastes, such as a taste for fat. So nobody can yet say for sure how many tastes there are.
### Eating with the seasons and the place · *debated*
In every part of India, people have long eaten what grows nearby, at that time of year. Ayurveda books describe how food should change with each of the six seasons, or ritus. This idea is called ritucharya. Grandparents say things like 'eat curd rice and buttermilk in summer' and 'eat til and jaggery in winter'. Some of this matches what science knows. Some has not been tested.
- e.g. Summer: watermelon, tender coconut, buttermilk, aam panna and cucumber, all very watery.
- e.g. Winter: sarson ka saag, gajar halwa, bajra roti, til laddoo and peanuts, all filling and rich.
- e.g. Rice and fish in Bengal and Kerala, where there are many rivers and plenty of rain. Bajra and jowar in dry Rajasthan, where little water falls.
- **Old Ayurveda books and many families:** Each season changes the body, so food should change too. Eating seasonal, local food keeps the body in balance with nature.
- **Nutrition scientists:** Seasonal food that grew nearby is often fresher and cheaper. Some vitamins, like vitamin C, slowly break down after a fruit is picked. Watery summer foods help replace the water we sweat out. Rich winter foods give energy. But many specific old rules have never been tested carefully, so we cannot say if they are right.
- **Some farmers and environmental scientists:** Local, seasonal food often needs less fuel to transport and store. But this is not always true. It depends on how the food was grown and moved.
### How people started farming, and how our kitchen travelled the world · *defined*
**Definition:** For most of human history, people found their food in the wild. Farming began about 11,000 years ago, separately in several parts of the world. Since then, crops have travelled across the world with traders.

Before farming, people gathered fruit, roots, seeds and honey, and they hunted and fished. Then, in a few places, people began to sow seeds and keep animals. One of the earliest farming villages in South Asia is Mehrgarh, in what is now Pakistan. People there grew barley and wheat and kept cattle about 9,000 years ago. Later, crops travelled. Ragi first grew in East Africa and reached India about 4,000 years ago. Chilli, potato, tomato, peanut and maize all came from the Americas. They reached India only about 400 to 500 years ago, many of them on Portuguese ships. Before that, Indian food got its heat from black pepper and long pepper. Black pepper grows wild in the forests of Kerala, and traders took it all the way to ancient Rome. People in India were also among the first to turn sugarcane juice into sugar crystals. The English word 'sugar' comes, through Persian and Arabic, from the Sanskrit word 'sharkara'.
- e.g. Aloo, tamatar and mirchi are in almost every Indian kitchen, but none of them grew in India 600 years ago.
- e.g. Black pepper from Kerala was once so valuable that it was called black gold.
### How will everyone be fed in the future? · *debated*
There are more than 8 billion people on Earth. In the 1960s, India did not grow enough grain and feared famine. Scientists, including M. S. Swaminathan, worked with farmers to grow new kinds of wheat and rice that gave much bigger harvests. This was called the Green Revolution, and within a few years India could feed itself. But it used a lot of water, fertiliser and pesticide. Today people argue about what to do next.
- e.g. In Punjab, so much water is pumped up for rice that the water under the ground is falling year after year.
- e.g. Millets like ragi, jowar and bajra need much less water than rice. The United Nations named 2023 the International Year of Millets, after India proposed it.
- e.g. Seed banks, like the one in Svalbard in Norway, store seeds of many crops in case any are lost.
- **Supporters of the Green Revolution:** New high-yield seeds and fertiliser saved millions of people from hunger. Science must keep making better crops.
- **Critics, like the Indian writer and activist Vandana Shiva:** The Green Revolution harmed soil and water, and pushed out India's many old local crops. Farming should go back to many crops, local seeds and fewer chemicals.
- **M. S. Swaminathan himself, later in life:** He called for an 'Evergreen Revolution': growing more food while caring for the soil, water and small farmers.
- **Other scientists:** Some try new foods: meat grown from animal cells, insects (about 2 billion people already eat them), or seaweed and algae. Whether these will become common is still open.

## Mostly agreed
- Almost all of our food comes from living things: plants, animals, fungi and microbes. Salt and water are the main foods that come from the non-living Earth. *How we know:* Trace any food back, step by step. You always reach a plant, an animal, a fungus, a microbe, the sea or the rocks.
- Green plants make their food from air, water and sunlight. The soil gives them only small amounts of minerals. *How we know:* Van Helmont's willow tree grew about 30 times heavier, but their soil hardly lost any weight. Later scientists showed plants take in carbon dioxide from the air and need light to grow.
- On land and in the sunlit sea, every food chain begins with plants or algae, which use sunlight. *How we know:* Animals cannot make food from light. Every animal we eat ate plants, or ate other animals that ate plants.
- Seeds hold a baby plant and a store of food, mostly starch, protein and oil. *How we know:* Split a soaked seed and you can see the baby plant. A drop of iodine turns the food store blue-black, which shows starch is there.
- Microbes turn milk into curd and make batters rise. *How we know:* Warm milk with a spoon of curd sets by morning. Boiled milk with no curd added does not. Under a microscope, curd is full of bacteria.
- Some living things at deep-sea vents get their food from chemicals, not sunlight. *How we know:* Scientists in the submarine Alvin first saw these communities in 1977. Since then, hundreds of vent sites have been studied.

## How we found out
**Louis Pasteur, Justus von Liebig and Eduard Buchner, 1857 to 1897.** For thousands of years, people made curd, bread and idli without knowing why the food changed. In the 1830s, a few scientists looked at fermenting liquids under microscopes and saw tiny yeast cells. But the famous German chemist Justus von Liebig said fermentation was only a chemical breakdown, like rotting. The yeast cells, he said, were not doing the work. In 1857, the French scientist Louis Pasteur studied liquids that were turning sour, like souring milk. Under his microscope he found living cells far smaller than yeast. When he added a few of them to a fresh sugary liquid, they multiplied, and the liquid turned sour. Without them, it stayed fresh. He showed that each kind of fermentation is done by its own kind of living microbe. In 1897, Eduard Buchner found that juice squeezed out of yeast, with no living cells left inside, could also ferment sugar. It held enzymes, chemicals the yeast makes. So both sides were partly right: living microbes do the work, using chemicals they make.  
*What people believed before:* Fermentation is only a chemical breakdown, like rotting, and the tiny living cells in it play no part.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Food comes from the shop.** (Many young children, and many grown-ups in cities who never see a farm; *rejected*)  
  For: The shop is where we buy it, and where we last saw it before it reached home.  
  Against: The shop is only the last step. Every food in the shop came from a farm, a sea, a forest, a dairy or a salt pan. Someone grew it, caught it or made it.
- **Plants get their food from the soil.** (Most people, for thousands of years, including the Greek thinker Aristotle; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants grow in soil, and they die if they are pulled out of it.  
  Against: Van Helmont's willow grew huge while their soil hardly changed. Plants make their own food from air, water and sunlight. Soil gives them water, a firm place to stand and a little of some minerals.
- **Mushrooms are a kind of plant.** (Many older books, and most people; *rejected*)  
  For: Mushrooms grow out of the ground, they cannot walk, and they are sold with vegetables.  
  Against: Mushrooms have no green leaves and cannot make food from sunlight. They feed on dead things like wood and leaves. Studies of their cells show fungi are more closely related to animals than to plants.
- **Spicy food and worry cause stomach ulcers, painful sores inside the stomach.** (Most doctors, until the 1980s; *rejected*)  
  For: People with ulcers often felt worse after spicy food or a worrying day.  
  Against: In 1982, two doctors in Australia, Robin Warren and Barry Marshall, found that most ulcers are caused by a bacterium. Other doctors did not believe them. So in 1984 Marshall drank a cup of the bacteria himself. His stomach became sore and swollen, and medicine that kills bacteria cured him. They won a Nobel Prize in 2005. Spicy food can still upset some stomachs, but it is not the main cause of ulcers.
- **Old food traditions are just superstition, and only modern science knows about food.** (Some people today; *debated*)  
  For: Some old rules have no evidence behind them, and a few are wrong.  
  Against: Many traditions came from careful watching over hundreds of years. People made curd and idli with microbes long before microbes were discovered. Eating millets in dry places and watery foods in summer makes good sense. The fair way is to test each old idea, not to accept or reject them all.
- **The tongue has separate areas: sweet at the tip, salty and sour at the sides, bitter at the back.** (School books all over the world, for most of the 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: A drawing called the 'tongue map' was printed in thousands of books, based on a German study from 1901.  
  Against: The study only found small differences. An American psychologist, Edwin Boring, redrew it in 1942 so that it looked like separate zones. In 1974, Virginia Collings tested it carefully and found every taste can be sensed all over the tongue. You can test it yourself with sugar water on the back of your tongue.
- **M. S. Swaminathan and a few scientists fed India.** (Many history books and news stories; *debated*)  
  For: Swaminathan led the work that brought high-yield wheat and rice to Indian farms. He won the first World Food Prize, in 1987.  
  Against: Millions of farmers, many of them women, took the risk of growing the new seeds and did the work. Earlier scientists helped too. E. K. Janaki Ammal, one of India's first women botanists, bred sweeter sugarcane in Coimbatore in the 1930s. Credit belongs to many hands.

## Roots
We have been following food back to where it comes from: plants, animals, tiny living things, the Earth and the Sun. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Sort any meal, and almost every food on it was made by something alive. Plants made the rice and dal, a cow made the milk, and bacteria made the curd. In the curd test, warm milk with no curd added stayed runny, because no bacteria were working on the milk. So almost everything you eat was, a short time ago, part of another living thing.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'I made dinner', or that food comes from the shop. Cooking and buying are real work, but they only change or move food that plants, animals and microbes made.  
*Evidence:* In Kitchen Detective, everything except salt and water went on a plate for a living thing. In the curd test, milk set into curd only where living bacteria from old curd were added and kept warm.

- *who am i:* You are built out of what you ate: rice, dal, milk and fruit, rebuilt into you.
- *what is life:* Living things make food and pass it on. A plant stores food in their seed, a cow turns grass into milk, and your body turns both into you.
- *what is this world:* In this world, grass stores the Sun's energy, a cow turns the grass into milk, and the milk helps build a child who can wonder about it all.

Most food starts as sunlight caught by a leaf. A wheat plant uses that energy to build their grain. A cow uses grass to build milk, and curd bacteria use milk to build more bacteria. Your body uses all of these to build you. Each living thing takes what another made and builds themself from it, so every meal links many lives into one chain.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2.1 and 3.10.6 (the Bhrigu Valli):** The young Bhrigu asks his father what is truly real. His father tells him to find out for himself, by looking deeply. The first thing Bhrigu understands is that food is the great reality. From food all beings are born. By food they live. Into food they return. At the end of the teaching, the one who understands sings: 'I am food, I am food, I am food. I am the eater of food.' (In this unit, curd came from milk, milk from grass, and grass from sunlight, air and water. Bhrigu's teaching says the same: every eater is made of food, and in the end becomes food for others.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), the sheet of paper:** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see a cloud in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, without rain no tree, and without the tree no paper. He called this 'interbeing': nothing exists by itself alone. (Look deeply at a chapati and you see the Sun, the rain, the wheat field, the farmer and the miller. The chapati is made of things that are not chapati.)
- **Robin Wall Kimmerer, Braiding Sweetgrass (2013), the chapter 'The Honorable Harvest':** Robin Wall Kimmerer, a Potawatomi botanist, shares the Indigenous teaching she calls the Honourable Harvest. Take only what you need. Never take the first one you find, and never take more than half. Share what you take, and give thanks for it. (In the story, the seed keepers did not eat the seeds, so the crops could be grown again. Farmers everywhere save part of each harvest as seed for the next sowing.)
- Science (settled): Almost every food chain begins with sunlight caught by plants or algae. The atoms in your body came from your food, water and air, and they are always being replaced.
- Science (idea): Some scientists think the very first life may have begun at deep-sea vents, feeding on chemicals, before any plant existed.
- Other view, Many biologists, and the Charvaka thinkers of ancient India: Many biologists say eating is chemistry: food is broken into small pieces and rebuilt into a body. On this view, the chain from the Sun to your plate is energy and atoms moving, and there is nothing more to it. The Charvaka thinkers also taught that only matter is real, and that the self ends when the body dies.
- Practice, The Slow Bite (3 min): Take one small piece of chapati, a raisin or a slice of fruit. Hold it in your palm and just look at it for a moment. Close your eyes. Think of the Sun that shone on it. Think of the rain, the soil and the farmer. Now put it in your mouth, but don't chew yet. Feel it on your tongue. Chew very slowly, and swallow. This food is now becoming you. Sit quietly for three breaths.
- Ask: Where does your food stop and you begin?
- Ask: Can you find any food on your plate today that no living thing helped to make?
- Ask: If your body is always being rebuilt from food, what part of you stays the same?

## Story: A Field in Every Packet
*The siege of Leningrad (September 1941 to January 1944) is real. So is Nikolai Vavilov's great seed collection at the Institute of Plant Industry on St Isaac's Square, his seed-collecting journeys to Ethiopia, Afghanistan and the Americas, his arrest in 1940 and his death of hunger in prison in 1943. Several of the Institute's workers died of hunger during the siege while guarding the seeds. Accounts differ on how many. In the worst weeks of that winter, the bread ration for children really fell to 125 grams a day. Nina, her mother Vera and the night with the peas are made up.*

In the winter of 1941, the city of Leningrad was shut in like a box. An army had closed every road. No trains came in with flour. No lorries came in with potatoes. The snow fell and fell, and the city grew quieter and thinner every day.

Nina was ten. Each morning she stood in the bread queue with her mother and came home with a slice of bread no bigger than her palm. It was dark and damp and tasted of sawdust. That was all there was for the whole day. She ate it in tiny bites, making each bite last, the way you make a story last when you know it is nearly over.

Nina's mother, Vera, worked in a grand old building on St Isaac's Square. Inside, it was the strangest library in the world. It had no books. Its shelves held boxes, and the boxes held packets, and the packets held seeds. Wheat from the mountains of Ethiopia. Peas from the valleys of Afghanistan. Beans and maize from Mexico, potatoes from the high Andes, rice from everywhere rice had ever grown. A scientist called Nikolai Vavilov had spent his life travelling the world to collect them. He was not there now. He had been arrested the year before, and nobody would say where he was.

Their flat had no heating, so Nina came to work with her mother. The seed library had no heating either, but at least there were people. They moved slowly, in coats and scarves and gloves with the fingertips cut off. They spoke softly, as if loud words cost too much. Their faces had gone grey and sharp.

One afternoon Nina stood in front of a shelf of rice. She did a sum in her head, and it made her angry. 'There are tonnes of seeds here,' she said. 'Tonnes. And people are dying in the street. Why don't you just cook them?'

Her mother did not get cross. She only looked tired. 'If we cook them, we eat for a week,' she said. 'And then the war ends, and the farmers come to us for seeds, and the shelves are empty. Then there is no wheat, no peas, no rice. Not next year. Not ever.'

Nina did not understand. Food was food. You were hungry, and there it was.

That night, the cold was so bad that her mother fell asleep at her desk with her head on her arms. It was Nina's turn to keep watch. The rats were the real danger. They were starving too. They chewed through boxes and ate whatever they found. Nina sat with a small lamp and a broom, and listened to the building creak.

Then she heard it. A scratching, a skitter, and then a soft patter like rain. She lifted the lamp. A box on a low shelf had been gnawed open, and a packet inside had split. Green-grey peas lay spilled across the floor.

Nina knelt and gathered them into her lap, one by one. Her stomach twisted. She put a single pea in her mouth. The pea sat on her tongue, hard as a pebble. It tasted of dust, and very faintly of summer.

Then she remembered something.

Long before the hunger, in the autumn, her mother had soaked a pea from their kitchen in a cup of water overnight. In the morning she had split the pea with her thumbnail and held the halves under the lamp. Inside were two fat pale halves, and tucked at one edge, a tiny curled thing with two folded leaves, like a sleeping baby holding their knees. 'That is the plant,' her mother had said. 'And these two fat halves are their lunch. Their mother packed this lunch before they were born. When we eat peas, we are eating the baby's lunchbox.'

Nina took the pea out of her mouth and held the little seed up to the lamp. There was a baby in there. Asleep. Waiting for water and warmth and spring.

She began to count on her cold fingers. Plant one pea, and they grow into a plant. One plant makes perhaps five pods. Each pod holds perhaps six peas. That is thirty peas. Plant those thirty, and there are thirty plants. Thirty plants make nine hundred peas. And the year after that…

She stopped, because the number had become too big to hold.

In her mouth, the pea would be gone in one swallow. In the ground, the pea was a field.

Nina wiped the pea on her sleeve and put the seed back in the packet. Then she found every other pea on the floor, even the one that had rolled under the radiator. She counted them twice. Forty-seven. She tied the packet tight with a piece of string, and on the paper, in her best handwriting, she wrote: 47. All here.

In the morning her mother woke stiff and blinking. She saw the box, and the tied packet, and the number written on it. She looked at Nina for a long moment. Then she took a pencil, and beside the 47 she wrote her own initials, very small, as if signing something important.

The siege lasted two more winters. Some of the people in the seed library did not live to see the end of the siege. They died of hunger at their desks, beside sacks of rice and boxes of seeds they never opened. Nikolai Vavilov, the man who had gathered the seeds so that nobody would ever starve, died of hunger in a prison far away.

But the seeds survived. After the war, they went out from St Isaac's Square to farms across the country, packet by packet. They were sown in spring soil. They woke up and ate their lunch and pushed out roots and shoots, and grew, and made more seeds, and those seeds made more.

Somewhere, a pea plant is climbing a stick in a garden right now. Their seeds came, many generations later, from seeds that lived through that winter. Now they turn their leaves to the Sun.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Nina's turn comes when she sees that seeds are not only food. Each seed is a living baby plant with a store of food, waiting to grow. Eaten, a seed is one mouthful. Planted, it becomes a plant that makes many more seeds. Most of our food is seeds or other plant parts. Every bit of it comes from living things that keep making more of themselves. That is why farmers have always kept back their best seeds, even in hungry years.

- Ask: Why did the scientists in the story not cook the seeds, even though they were starving?
- Ask: What did Nina remember about the inside of a pea? What were the two fat halves for?
- Ask: Nina counted: one pea could become thirty, then nine hundred. How did she work that out?
- Ask: Why do you think Nina's mother wrote her initials next to Nina's number?
- Ask: Is there anything in your home that is kept for later, instead of being used now? Why?

### e1 · Milk to Curd: what do curd bacteria need? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Milk turns into curd because of tiny living things called bacteria. Do they need a spoon of old curd to start, and do they need warmth?
- **predict:** Before you start, read how the three katoris will be set up: A has curd and stays warm, B has no curd and stays warm, C has curd and goes in the fridge. Guess which ones will be thick curd by morning, and which will still be runny milk. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use the same milk, the same amount in each katori, and the same size katoris. Change only one thing at a time: whether we add curd, or where we keep it.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the milk and pours it. Let it cool until it is just warm. Eat only the curd from katori A. Throw away katori B after the test, because milk left out without curd can go bad.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all three katoris. Then draw a bar for each katori, as tall as its tilt score.
- **results table:** Katori | What we added | Where we kept it | Tilt test in the morning (0 runny, 1 thick like custard, 2 sets and holds) | Smell: sour or fresh?
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Set up three katoris of warm milk: A with curd in a warm place, B with no curd in a warm place, C with curd in the fridge. (13 min); day 2: Do the tilt test on all three. Fill in the table. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (12 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child a bowl of milk and a bowl of curd. 2. Ask how one becomes the other.  
  *Parent says:* This is milk. This is curd. Tomorrow morning, some milk will have turned into curd. How do you think that happens?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the milk. Look at the curd. How does milk become curd? Say your guess.
  - Ask: How does milk become curd? (good: You add a bit of old curd.; You leave it overnight.; Something in the curd changes the milk.)
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up boils the milk and lets it cool until it feels just warm on the wrist. 2. Pour the same amount into three katoris. 3. Label them A, B and C with tape.  
  *Parent says:* Each katori gets the same amount of milk. That keeps the test fair.  
  *Child mode:* Wait for a grown-up to pour the warm milk. Each katori gets the same amount. Label them A, B and C.
  - Note: Milk that is too hot kills the bacteria. Milk that is too cold makes them very slow. Just warm, like a baby's bottle, is right.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir 1 teaspoon of curd into A. 2. Add nothing to B. 3. Stir 1 teaspoon of curd into C. 4. Put A and B in a warm corner. Put C in the fridge.  
  *Parent says:* A gets curd and stays warm. B gets no curd and stays warm. C gets curd but goes in the cold fridge.  
  *Child mode:* Put a spoon of curd in A and C. Put nothing in B. A and B stay warm. C goes in the fridge.
  - Note: In cold weather, keep katoris A and B warm all night. Wrap them in a thick towel, or put them in a closed casserole. Curd sets in about 6 to 8 hours when it is warm, and much more slowly when it is cold.
  - Check: Katoris A and B both stay warm. What is the only difference between them? → **A has curd added, B does not**
  - Wait: Leave the katoris overnight, about 8 hours.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Next morning, bring out all three. 2. Tilt each one slowly. 3. Score it 0 if it flows like milk. Score it 1 if it moves like thick custard. Score it 2 if it sets and holds. 4. Smell each one. 5. Write it all in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let's tilt each one gently. Does it flow, wobble, or hold still?  
  *Child mode:* Tilt each katori slowly. Does it flow, wobble or hold? Give it 0, 1 or 2. Smell it. Write it down.
  - Note: Typical results: A sets well (score 2) and smells pleasantly sour. B is still runny (0). If the milk was not boiled well, B may turn a little sour by itself. C is runny or barely thick (0 or 1), because the cold makes the bacteria very slow.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar chart together. One bar for each katori, as tall as its tilt score.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn the scores into bars. Which katori made the best curd?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each katori. Make it as tall as its score. Which one is tallest?
  - Check: Katori A, with curd in a warm place, set into curd. Katori C, with curd in the fridge, did not. What does that tell us? → **The curd bacteria need warmth to work**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?

### e2 · Sprouting Moong: do seeds need water to grow? (35 min, 3 days)

### e3 · The Iodine Test: which foods have starch? (25 min)
- **question:** Plants pack food called starch into their seeds, roots and underground stems. Which of our foods have starch in them? Do foods from animals have any?

  - Check: The paneer stayed brown-orange. What does that tell us? → **Paneer has no starch**
- **s4 (4 min)**

  - Think: A ripe banana tastes sweeter than a raw one. Where did its starch go?
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think, what did you see, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: plants store starch in their seeds and underground parts, but animal foods do not have starch. What I saw: rice, chapati, besan and potato turned blue-black, but paneer did not. ### Food Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (write) Pick one food you ate today. Trace it back, one step at a time, until you reach the Sun. Write each step.  
   **Answer:** For example: my idli was made from rice and urad dal batter. The rice and urad dal are seeds from plants in a field. The plants made their food using sunlight. So: idli, batter, rice and urad dal, plants, Sun.. *Why:* A strong answer goes step by step, with no jumps, and ends with a plant using sunlight. If the food is from an animal, the answer goes one step further, to what the animal ate.
2. (sort) Sort these foods onto four plates. Where did each one come from?  
   **Answer:** rice → Plant; mango → Plant; egg → Animal; fish → Animal; mushroom → Fungi and microbes; baker's yeast → Fungi and microbes; salt → Non-living Earth; water → Non-living Earth. *Why:* Rice and mango grow on plants. Eggs come from hens, and fish are animals. Mushrooms and yeast are fungi. Salt and water were never alive. Only the last plate holds things that were never alive.
3. (match) Match each food to the part of the plant we eat.  
   **Answer:** radish → root; sugarcane → stem; methi → leaf; banana flower (vazhaipoo) → flower; guava → fruit; moong → seed. *Why:* A radish is a root. Sugarcane is a stem. Methi is a leaf. Vazhaipoo is the flower of the banana plant. A guava is a fruit. Moong is a seed.
4. (write) Farhan, a boy in Class 3, says, "Paneer comes from a cow, not from a plant. So paneer has nothing to do with plants." What has he missed?  
   **Answer:** He has missed the step before the cow. The cow made the milk from the grass and fodder she ate. Grass is a plant that grew using sunlight. So paneer is plant food that passed through a cow.. *Why:* A strong answer goes one step further back than the animal, to what the animal ate.
5. (truefalse) A mushroom is a kind of plant.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A mushroom is a fungus. Fungi have no green leaves and do not make food from sunlight. They feed on dead wood and leaves. Fungi are a separate kind of living being.
6. (mcq) In the Milk to Curd test, which katori set into thick curd by morning?  
   Options: Milk with curd added, kept warm / Milk with nothing added, kept warm / Milk with curd added, kept in the fridge  
   **Answer:** Milk with curd added, kept warm. *Why:* The curd bacteria need to be added, and they need warmth to work. If you picked 'nothing added', you may think milk sets by itself. If you picked 'the fridge', remember that cold slows the bacteria down.
7. (write) A girl finds mushrooms growing on a damp log in a dark corner of the garden. A tulsi plant kept in a dark cupboard turns pale and weak. Why can the mushrooms grow in the dark when the tulsi cannot?  
   **Answer:** The tulsi is a plant and makes her food from air and water using sunlight, so in the dark she cannot make new food. Mushrooms are fungi. They do not make food from light. They feed on the dead wood of the log, so they can grow in the dark.. *Why:* A strong answer says how each one gets food: the plant makes food with light, the fungus takes food from dead things.
8. (fill) Milk turns into curd because of tiny living beings called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['bacteria', 'microbes']. *Why:* Bacteria from a spoon of old curd feed on the sugar in warm milk. They make an acid that turns the milk thick and sour.
9. (order) Put this food chain in order, starting from the beginning.  
   **Answer:** ['Sun', 'grass', 'cow', 'milk', 'curd']. *Why:* The Sun gives grass the energy to make food. The cow eats the grass. The cow makes milk. Bacteria turn the milk into curd.
10. (sort) Sort these into grains, which are seeds of grass plants, and pulses, which are seeds of bean and pea plants.  
   **Answer:** rice → Grains; moong → Pulses; wheat → Grains; chana → Pulses; ragi → Grains; rajma → Pulses. *Why:* Rice, wheat and ragi are seeds of grass plants, called grains. Moong, chana and rajma are seeds of bean and pea plants, called pulses. Both are seeds.
11. (truefalse) When we eat dal, we are eating seeds.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Dals are the dried seeds of pulse plants. Soak whole moong and they will sprout a root.
12. (draw) Draw a soaked chana seed split open. Label the baby plant and the food store.  
   **Answer:** A split seed with a small curled baby plant at one edge, and the two big halves labelled 'food store'.. *Why:* A seed holds a baby plant and a store of food. The big halves hold the food the baby plant uses when they start to grow.
13. (match) Match each food to the living thing that helped make it.  
   **Answer:** honey → honeybee; curd → bacteria; egg → hen; paneer → cow or buffalo; bread → yeast. *Why:* Bees make honey from nectar. Bacteria turn milk into curd. A hen lays an egg. Paneer is made from the milk of a cow or buffalo. Yeast makes bread rise.
14. (truefalse) A hen is an animal, so an egg has nothing to do with plants.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A hen eats grain and other plant food, and makes the egg from that food. So an egg is plant food that has passed through an animal.
15. (mcq) Which of these food chains is the longest?  
   Options: Sun, algae, small fish, big fish, fish curry / Sun, rice plant, rice / Sun, sugarcane, jaggery / Sun, mango tree, mango  
   **Answer:** Sun, algae, small fish, big fish, fish curry. *Why:* The fish curry chain has five links. Each of the others has only three. Every extra animal in a chain adds one more link.
### Food Journeys (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (match) Each masala comes from one part of a plant. Match each masala to that part.  
   **Answer:** jeera → seed; cinnamon → bark; haldi → underground stem; clove → flower bud; curry leaves → leaf; black pepper → fruit. *Why:* Jeera is a seed. Cinnamon is bark, the outside of a tree's stem. Haldi is an underground stem. A clove is a dried flower bud. Curry leaves are leaves. Each peppercorn is a small dried fruit.
2. (mcq) A botanist is a scientist who studies plants. To a botanist, which of these is a fruit?  
   Options: Bhindi / Potato / Radish / Keerai  
   **Answer:** Bhindi. *Why:* Botanists call any part that grows from a flower and holds seeds a fruit. Bhindi is full of seeds, so it is a fruit, even though a cook calls it a vegetable. Potato is a stem, radish is a root and keerai is leaves.
3. (write) Ghee comes from a cow. Write the whole chain, going back from ghee to where its energy began.  
   **Answer:** Ghee is made from butter. Butter is made from milk. The cow made milk from the grass she ate. The grass made their food using sunlight. So the chain is: Sun, grass, cow, milk, butter, ghee.. *Why:* A strong answer reaches the Sun with no missing steps. Every animal food goes back to a plant, and every plant goes back to the Sun.
4. (mcq) In the iodine test, which food turned blue-black?  
   Options: Cooked rice / Paneer / Salt / Sugar  
   **Answer:** Cooked rice. *Why:* Rice is a seed, full of the starch the plant packed for their baby. Iodine turns blue-black on starch. Paneer comes from milk and salt from the Earth. Sugar is a clever trap: sugar comes from a plant, but sugar is a different kind of food from starch.
5. (truefalse) Animals store their food as starch, just like plants.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Starch is how plants store food. That is why paneer did not turn blue-black in the iodine test. Animals store food mostly as fat, and a little as glycogen, a starch-like store in the liver and muscles.
6. (fill) Making food with the help of microbes, like curd and idli batter, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['fermentation']. *Why:* In fermentation, microbes feed on a food and slowly change it. The food often turns sour, bubbly or thick.
7. (write) In a warm Chennai kitchen, idli batter rises overnight. In a cold Delhi winter, it often stays flat unless it is kept somewhere warm. Use what you found in the Milk to Curd test to explain why.  
   **Answer:** Idli batter rises because microbes in it feed and make gas. In the curd test, the bacteria worked only when the milk was warm. The cold fridge katori stayed runny. So in a cold kitchen the batter microbes work very slowly, and the batter stays flat.. *Why:* A strong answer links the two: both are microbes at work, and both need warmth.
8. (perspective) Three girls are arguing about where curd comes from.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one and backs it up, for example with the Milk to Curd test, where milk with no curd added stayed runny. It then admits that the others are right too, because curd needs a plant, an animal and microbes.. *Why:* All three are partly right. Curd needs grass that grew in sunlight, a cow who made milk, and bacteria who turned the milk sour and thick. The strength is in the reasons.
9. (mcq) Honey is sweet. Where did that sweetness first come from?  
   Options: Sugar made by a flowering plant, using sunlight / The bee's own body fat / The wax of the hive  
   **Answer:** Sugar made by a flowering plant, using sunlight. *Why:* Bees drink nectar, a sugary juice made by flowers. The plant made that sugar using the energy of sunlight. The bees thicken it into honey, but the sweetness began in the plant.
10. (sort) Sort these foods into usual summer foods and usual winter foods in north India.  
   **Answer:** mango → Summer; sarson ka saag → Winter; buttermilk → Summer; gajar halwa → Winter; watermelon → Summer; til laddoo → Winter. *Why:* Mangoes and watermelons ripen in summer, and buttermilk cools us. Mustard greens and carrots grow in winter, and til laddoo gives lots of energy in the cold.
11. (mcq) In the Windowsill Farm, the seedlings in the dark cupboard grew tall, thin and pale yellow. What were they living on?  
   Options: The food packed in their seeds / Food they made from light / The soil / Food from the dark  
   **Answer:** The food packed in their seeds. *Why:* In the dark, a seedling cannot make new food. They live on the food stored in their seed, stretching to look for light. 'The soil' is the old idea that plants eat soil, which van Helmont's willow tree showed was wrong.
12. (truefalse) A dry moong seed sitting in a jar in the kitchen is not alive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A dry seed is alive, but resting. Scientists call this dormant. Give the seed water and warmth, and the baby plant inside wakes up and grows. A dead seed would never sprout.
13. (draw) Draw a whole plant and label six parts: root, stem, leaf, flower, fruit, seed. Next to each label, write a food from your own kitchen that is that part. Try not to use radish, sugarcane, methi, vazhaipoo, guava or moong.  
   **Answer:** Root under the soil, for example carrot or beetroot. Stem, for example potato, an underground stem. Leaves, for example curry leaves or keerai. A flower, for example cauliflower. A fruit, for example tomato or brinjal. Seeds, for example rice or peanut.. *Why:* Every part of a plant can be food. A strong drawing puts each part in the right place and finds a new, real food for each one.
14. (order) Put the steps of making salt in the salt pans in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Sea water flows into shallow fields', 'The Sun dries the water', 'White salt crystals are left behind', 'Workers rake the white salt into heaps']. *Why:* Sea water is let into the fields. The Sun dries the water away, and the salt that was in the water stays behind as crystals. Then the workers rake it into heaps.
15. (mcq) About how long ago did people begin farming?  
   Options: About 11,000 years ago / About 100 years ago / About 500 years ago / Before there were any people  
   **Answer:** About 11,000 years ago. *Why:* Farming began about 11,000 years ago, in several parts of the world. Before that, people gathered wild plants and hunted. The Green Revolution in India was only about 60 years ago.
### From the Sun to the Sea Floor (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Before 1977, scientists thought every food chain began with sunlight. Then they found crabs and tube worms living around hot vents deep under the sea, where no light reaches. Why was this a surprise? What feeds these animals?  
   **Answer:** It was a surprise because no plant can grow without light, so there seemed to be nothing to start a food chain. The animals are fed by microbes that make food from chemicals in the hot vent water, the way plants use sunlight.. *Why:* A strong answer says why scientists expected nothing, and names the microbes that use chemicals instead of light. This is called chemosynthesis.
2. (write) About 380 years ago, a scientist called van Helmont grew a willow tree in a pot for five years. The tree got about 30 times heavier, but the soil hardly changed. What does this tell us about what plants are made of?  
   **Answer:** The tree did not get their weight from the soil, because the soil stayed almost the same. So the weight must have come from what the tree took in: water and, as later scientists found, air. Plants build themselves mostly from air and water, using the energy of sunlight.. *Why:* Van Helmont thought it was all water. Later scientists found most of a plant's weight comes from carbon dioxide gas in the air. A strong answer uses the evidence: the soil did not change.
3. (mcq) A cow eats a field of grass. About how much of the grass's energy ends up stored in the cow's body?  
   Options: About a tenth / All of it / About half / None  
   **Answer:** About a tenth. *Why:* This is a rough rule. Most of the grass's energy is used by the cow for walking, keeping warm, breathing and living. Only about a tenth is stored in her body. 'All of it' forgets that a cow uses energy all day, just like you.
4. (write) Only about a tenth of the energy passes on at each step of a food chain. Use this rough rule to explain why feeding people on milk and meat takes more land than feeding them on grains and dal.  
   **Answer:** When people eat grains and dal, they get the plant's energy directly. When a cow eats the plants first, she uses up most of the energy. Only about a tenth is left in her milk and meat. So to get the same energy from animal food, many more fields of plants are needed.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the tenth rule: every extra step in a food chain loses most of the energy, so shorter chains need less land.
5. (perspective) People disagree about how much milk, eggs and meat we should eat, now that we know animal food takes more land.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason from the unit, for example the tenth rule, or that cows can turn grass people cannot eat into milk. It then gives the other side its due. It may suggest a middle way, such as less animal food, or animals fed on leftovers.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. Each view has true facts behind it, and families and countries weigh them differently.
6. (truefalse) The red blood cells in your body now were built from food you ate in the last few months.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each red blood cell lasts only about 120 days, about four months. Your body keeps making new ones, built from what you eat.
7. (write) In the story A Field in Every Packet, the real scientists of the Leningrad seed collection in 1941 were starving, but they did not eat their seeds. Give two reasons why saving the seeds was so important.  
   **Answer:** First, each seed is a baby plant that can grow into a plant that makes many more seeds. Eaten, a seed is one mouthful, but planted, a seed becomes a field. Second, the collection held many kinds of crops from all over the world. If they were lost, farmers might never get them back.. *Why:* Seeds are both food and the future of food. A strong answer gives both reasons: one seed grows into many, and many kinds of crops must not be lost.
8. (mcq) In the iodine test, sugar did not turn blue-black. Sugar comes from sugarcane, a plant. What does that tell us?  
   Options: Not every plant food is starch / Sugar is from an animal / The iodine was broken / Sugarcane is not a plant  
   **Answer:** Not every plant food is starch. *Why:* Plants make many kinds of food: sugar, starch, oil and protein. Iodine shows only starch. 'The iodine was broken' is a good question to ask. The potato test at the start showed the iodine worked.
9. (mcq) You want to find out if the added curd is what turns milk into curd. Which test is best?  
   Options: Compare warm milk with curd to warm milk without curd / Compare warm milk with curd to cold milk without curd / Taste lots of curd / Use a bigger katori  
   **Answer:** Compare warm milk with curd to warm milk without curd. *Why:* To test one thing, change only that one thing. Both katoris are warm, so only the curd is different. Cold milk without curd changes two things at once, so you could not tell which one mattered.
10. (sort) Sort these by what was at the very start of their food chain.  
   **Answer:** chapati → Sunlight; honey → Sunlight; tube worm → Chemicals from a deep-sea vent; salt → Never alive; fish curry → Sunlight; vent crab → Chemicals from a deep-sea vent. *Why:* Chapati, honey and fish curry all trace back to plants or algae using sunlight. Tube worms and vent crabs depend on microbes that use chemicals from the vents. Salt was never alive.
11. (write) Some people say old food traditions are just superstition. Others say they are always right. What do you think? Give one example that supports your answer.  
   **Answer:** Any answer with a reason and an example. For example: some old traditions were clever, because people made curd and idli with microbes long before microbes were discovered. But each old rule should be tested, because some have never been checked.. *Why:* The fair way is to test each idea, not to accept or reject them all. A strong answer gives a real example and a reason.
12. (mcq) Millets like ragi, jowar and bajra are being grown more again in India. What is one big reason?  
   Options: They need much less water than rice / They grow without any sunlight / They are new crops that were never grown in India before  
   **Answer:** They need much less water than rice. *Why:* Millets grow well with little rain, while rice needs a lot of water. In places like Punjab, pumping water for rice is making the water under the ground fall. Millets are not new: people in India have grown them for thousands of years. Like every plant, they need sunlight.
13. (truefalse) Fungi, like mushrooms, are more closely related to animals than to plants.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Studies of their cells and their genes show that fungi and animals share a closer ancestor than fungi and plants do. That surprised many people, because mushrooms grow in one place like plants.
14. (write) The carbon atoms in your rice were carbon dioxide in the air a few months ago. Where might they go after you eat them? Write two places.  
   **Answer:** Some will become part of my body, like my blood or muscles. Some I will breathe out as carbon dioxide, and a plant may take them in again to make new food.. *Why:* Atoms do not wear out. They move from air to plant to animal and back to the air, again and again. A strong answer names one place in the body and one way back to a plant.
15. (fill) Microbes at deep-sea vents make food from chemicals instead of sunlight. This is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['chemosynthesis']. *Why:* Chemosynthesis means making food with chemicals. Plants do the same job with sunlight, which is called photosynthesis.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these foods comes from a plant?  
   Options: Jaggery / Ghee / Egg / Paneer  
   **Answer:** Jaggery. *Why:* Jaggery is made from sugarcane juice, so it comes from a plant stem. Ghee and paneer come from milk, and eggs come from hens. Picking ghee often happens because ghee is yellow and oily like plant oil, but ghee is made from butter.
2. (mcq) Which plate does salt belong on?  
   Options: Plant / Animal / Fungi and microbes / The non-living Earth  
   **Answer:** The non-living Earth. *Why:* Salt is a mineral from the sea or from rocks. It was never alive. Picking 'plant' sometimes happens because salt is found in so many plant dishes.
3. (mcq) What part of the plant is a carrot?  
   Options: Root / Stem / Fruit  
   **Answer:** Root. *Why:* A carrot is a root. It grows straight down, stores food and has no eyes or buds. Picking 'stem' often comes from knowing that a potato is a stem, and guessing all underground food is the same.
4. (mcq) What part of the plant is cauliflower?  
   Options: Leaf / Flower / Root  
   **Answer:** Flower. *Why:* Cauliflower is a big bunch of flower buds packed tightly together. The name even says flower. Picking 'leaf' is a natural guess, because a cauliflower comes wrapped in big green leaves. But the white part we eat is the flower buds.
5. (mcq) When we eat rice, dal or peanuts, which part of the plant are we eating?  
   Options: Seeds / Roots / Leaves  
   **Answer:** Seeds. *Why:* Rice, dal and peanuts are all seeds: baby plants with their food packed around them. Picking 'roots' happens with peanuts, because they grow under the ground. But a peanut is a seed inside a pod.
6. (mcq) A cow gives milk. What did the cow eat to make her milk?  
   Options: Grass and fodder / Milk / Nothing  
   **Answer:** Grass and fodder. *Why:* A cow makes milk from the grass and fodder she eats. That is why animal food is really plant food that has passed through an animal. Picking 'nothing' means imagining milk just appears inside the cow.
7. (mcq) What is at the very start of almost every food chain on land?  
   Options: A plant, using sunlight / A lion / A shop / A cow  
   **Answer:** A plant, using sunlight. *Why:* Every food chain on land starts with a plant, which makes food from sunlight. Picking 'a cow' means starting in the middle. The cow ate grass first.
8. (mcq) What do plants make most of their own body from?  
   Options: Air and water / Soil / Fertiliser  
   **Answer:** Air and water. *Why:* Plants use sunlight to join carbon dioxide from the air with water. Picking 'soil' is the old idea that van Helmont tested. His tree grew huge, but the soil in their pot hardly changed.
9. (mcq) In the Milk to Curd test, why do we keep everything the same except one thing?  
   Options: So we know which change made the difference / Because it looks neater / To use less milk  
   **Answer:** So we know which change made the difference. *Why:* If only one thing changes, that one thing must have caused the difference. That is a fair test. Picking 'looks neater' misses the reason. A test can be messy and still fair, or neat and still unfair.
10. (mcq) Look at the bar chart from the sprouting test. Which plate had more sprouted seeds?  
   Options: The wet cloth / The dry cloth / They were the same  
   **Answer:** The wet cloth. *Why:* The wet cloth's bar is much taller: 18 seeds sprouted, and none on the dry cloth. Seeds need water to sprout. Picking 'the same' might mean the chart was not read. The dry bar is at zero.
11. (mcq) Old Indian books named six tastes, or rasas. Which one of these is in that list?  
   Options: Astringent, the dry puckery feel of raw banana / Crunchy / Cold / Colourful  
   **Answer:** Astringent, the dry puckery feel of raw banana. *Why:* The six rasas are sweet, sour, salty, pungent, bitter and astringent. Astringent is the dry feeling from raw banana or strong tea. Crunchy and cold are about texture and temperature, not taste.
12. (mcq) Why do many families eat watery foods like watermelon and buttermilk in summer?  
   Options: They help replace the water we lose in sweat / They are only sold in summer by law / They make us sweat more  
   **Answer:** They help replace the water we lose in sweat. *Why:* On hot days we sweat a lot, and watery foods help replace that water. This is old knowledge that science agrees with. Picking 'make us sweat more' turns the idea upside down.
13. (mcq) What did scientists find at deep-sea vents in 1977?  
   Options: Animals living without sunlight, fed by microbes that use chemicals / Plants growing in the dark / Nothing alive at all  
   **Answer:** Animals living without sunlight, fed by microbes that use chemicals. *Why:* At the vents, microbes make food from chemicals in the hot water, and whole communities of animals depend on them. Before 1977, most scientists thought every community of animals depended on sunlight. Picking 'plants growing in the dark' means forgetting that plants need light.
14. (mcq) Why do seed banks, like the one in Svalbard in Norway, keep seeds very dry and very cold?  
   Options: So the baby plants stay dormant and alive for many years / To make the seeds taste better / To kill the seeds  
   **Answer:** So the baby plants stay dormant and alive for many years. *Why:* Water and warmth start a seed growing. Kept dry and cold, the baby plant inside stays dormant and alive for a very long time. Picking 'to kill the seeds' is the opposite of the plan. A dead seed can never be grown again.

## Applications
- Farming: knowing which part of a plant we eat tells farmers what to grow, when to harvest, and which seeds to save.
- Dairies, like the milk co-operatives of Gujarat, turn milk into curd, butter, ghee and paneer, using microbes for some of them.
- Idli, dosa, dhokla, appam, bread and pickles all use microbes to ferment food.
- Seed banks, like the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway and India's national gene bank in New Delhi, keep crop seeds safe for the future.
- Plant breeders cross different kinds of a crop to grow sweeter sugarcane, rust-proof wheat or rice that needs less water.
- Salt pans in Gujarat and Tamil Nadu use sunlight to dry sea water and make salt.
- Beekeepers keep bee boxes near mustard and sunflower fields. The bees make honey and help the crops make seeds.
- Doctors and nutrition scientists use what we know about food to plan meals for schools, like India's midday meals.
- Space agencies grow plants under lamps, so astronauts can one day grow their own food on long journeys.
- Kitchen gardens, terrace farms and windowsill microgreens let city families grow some of their own food.
- Scientists studying hot springs and deep-sea vents have found microbes with heat-proof enzymes, now used in laboratories all over the world.
- Composting returns peels and leftovers to the soil, which feeds the next plants.

## Open questions
- **How did the very first living things get their food, before there were any plants?** Some scientists think life began at deep-sea vents, feeding on chemicals. Others think it began in warm pools on land. Nobody was there to see, and the clues are four billion years old.
- **How many basic tastes are there?** Most scientists now accept five. Some argue for more, such as a taste for fat. It depends on finding special taste cells for each one.
- **How can we grow enough food for everyone without harming the soil, water and air?** Scientists, farmers and governments disagree about the best mix of new seeds, old seeds, chemicals, millets and new foods.
- **Which old food traditions are good for health, and which are not?** Many traditional rules have never been tested carefully. Testing them fairly takes many years and many people.
- **Exactly how do the trillions of microbes in our gut help us use our food?** Scientists have only begun to study them in detail. They know the microbes matter, but not yet all the ways.
- **How long can a dry, dormant seed stay alive?** Some seeds sprout after hundreds of years, but most do not. Seed banks are still learning how long each kind of seed lasts.
- **Will meat grown from cells, insects or algae ever become everyday food?** These foods already exist, but nobody yet knows if they will become cheap, safe and popular enough.
- **Is there life in the dark oceans under the ice of moons like Europa, feeding on chemicals like vent life does?** Some moons of Jupiter and Saturn have hidden oceans, and maybe vents. Space missions are being sent to look, but nobody knows yet.

## Connected maths
- In the sprouting test, 50 grams of dry moong was soaked overnight. In the morning it weighed 100 grams. How many grams of water did the seeds take in? How many times heavier were they? **The seeds took in 50 grams of water. They were 2 times as heavy.**. The seeds started at 50 grams. They ended at 100 grams. 100 take away 50 is 50, so they took in 50 grams of water. And 100 is 2 lots of 50, so the soaked seeds weighed 2 times as much as the dry seeds. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L3`)
- In a sprouting test, a child counted the sprouted seeds on the wet cloth each day: after 1 day, 3 seeds. After 2 days, 12 seeds. After 3 days, 18 seeds. Draw a bar chart. On which day did the most new seeds sprout? **The second day. 9 new seeds sprouted that day. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each day, as tall as its count. To find the new seeds each day, subtract the day before. Second day: 12 take away 3 is 9 new seeds. Third day: 18 take away 12 is 6 new seeds. 9 is more than 6 and more than 3, so the second day had the most new sprouts. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In Kitchen Detective, a family sorted 20 kitchen things. 13 were from plants, 3 from animals, 2 from fungi and microbes, and 2 from the Earth. Make a pictograph where one 🌿 stands for 1 thing. Is more than half of their food from plants? **Yes. 13 out of 20 is more than half, because half of 20 is 10.**. Draw 13 symbols in the plant row, 3 in the animal row, 2 in the fungi and microbes row and 2 in the Earth row. Check: 13 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 20. Half of 20 is 10. 13 is more than 10, so more than half of the food came from plants. (queues `math.data.pictographs.L3`)
- In the Plant-Part Thali, a child opened a pea pod and counted 6 peas. Suppose one pea plant grows 5 pods like this one. How many peas is that? If every one of those peas were planted and grew the same, how many pea plants would there be next year? **30 peas, and so 30 pea plants next year.**. 5 pods with 6 peas in each is 5 groups of 6. 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 30. So one plant makes 30 peas. Each pea is a baby plant, so planting all 30 could give 30 new pea plants. This is the sum Nina did in the story. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In Trace Your Dinner, a child counted the people on each food's chain: chapati 6 people, dal 5 people, curd 4 people, banana 5 people. How many people helped bring her dinner altogether? **20 people.**. Add the four chains: 6 + 5 = 11. 11 + 4 = 15. 15 + 5 = 20. So 20 people helped. That does not even count the ones we cannot see, like the people who made the tractor or the cooking gas. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)

## Warm-up

- What turns milk into curd? → Tiny living things called bacteria.
- Is a mushroom a plant? → No. Mushrooms are fungi.
- Name a food that was never alive. → Salt, or water.
- Who makes honey, and from what? → Honeybees, from the nectar of flowers.
- What is at the start of almost every food chain? → A plant, using sunlight.
- What is inside a seed? → A baby plant and their store of food.
- Which came to India from the Americas: chilli or black pepper? → Chilli. Black pepper grows wild in Kerala.
- What lives at deep-sea vents without any sunlight? → Microbes that make food from chemicals, and the tube worms, crabs and clams that depend on them.
