# Curious people: how discoveries begin with a question
`sci.how-we-know.stories-of-discovery.L1`

**Summary.** A yellow haldi stain on a white shirt can turn red in the wash. Why? In this unit you will test which kitchen liquids turn haldi red, hear about curious people like Tu Youyou, and play a true-or-false game about who found out what.

**Intro.** *Here is an old cloth with a yellow haldi stain. When a stain like this is washed, it can turn red. What do you think turns it red?* Many grown-ups have seen a haldi stain turn red in the wash, but most never ask why. Asking why, and then trying things to find out, is what scientists do. In this unit you become a scientist in your own kitchen. You also hear true stories of curious people who found out new things.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what a scientist does: notices something, asks a question, and tries things to find out.
1. By the end I can say that anyone who is curious can be a scientist, girls and boys, from any place.
2. By the end I can find out which kitchen liquids turn haldi red, by testing one at a time.
3. By the end I can tell the story of Tu Youyou, who found a medicine for malaria.
4. By the end I can name a discovery from India and one from another country.
5. By the end I can make a guess, test it, and say what I saw.

## Terms
- **Curious**: Being curious means wanting to know more about something. A curious person asks lots of questions. *e.g.* Meenakshi, a girl in this unit's story, was curious about why the haldi stain went red, so she asked her grandmother.
- **Scientist**: A scientist is a person who finds out how the world works. They look closely, ask questions and test their ideas. *e.g.* Tu Youyou was a scientist in China. She found a new medicine for malaria.
- **Discovery**: A discovery is finding out something about the world that people did not know before. *e.g.* Finding out that soap turns haldi red was a discovery for you.
- **Test**: A test is a way to find out if your guess is right. You try it and see what happens. *e.g.* You guess that lemon will turn haldi red. You try it, and it stays yellow.

## Explanations
### What a scientist does · *idea*
Scientists notice things. Then they ask a question, like 'Why did the haldi go red?' They make a guess. Then they try things to see if the guess is right. Last, they tell other people what they found, so others can try it too. There is no single fixed order. Sometimes a scientist tries things first and asks questions later. Scientists still argue about whether there is one right way to do science.
- e.g. You noticed haldi went red with soap. You asked which liquids do it. You tested each one.
- e.g. C. V. Raman, a scientist from India, saw the deep blue sea from a ship in 1921. He asked why the sea is so blue. He spent years finding out.
- e.g. Babies are curious too. When a toy does something surprising, babies pick it up and test it again.
### Anyone curious can be a scientist · *defined*
**Definition:** A scientist is anyone who finds out about the world by looking closely, asking questions and testing ideas.

Scientists are not a special kind of person who is born clever. They are people who stay curious and keep trying. Some started as children. Mary Anning was about 12 when she and her brother found bones on a beach in England. They were the bones of a huge sea animal that lived long ago. Some scientists had little money and no big laboratory. Tu Youyou did not have a doctorate, the top university degree, but she found a medicine that has saved millions of lives. Women and girls have always done science, even when people tried to stop them.
- e.g. Mary Anning dug up fossils on the beach as a girl, in about 1811 and 1812.
- e.g. Janaki Ammal, from Thalassery in Kerala, grew new kinds of sugarcane that grow well in Indian fields.
- e.g. Tu Youyou, from China, found a malaria medicine by reading old books and testing.
### Why soap turns haldi red · *defined*
**Definition:** Haldi has a yellow colouring in it. Soap and cooking soda change this colouring to red. Lemon juice changes it back to yellow.

The yellow in haldi comes from a colouring called curcumin. Curcumin is yellow in plain water, salt water, sugar water and lemon juice. Soap and cooking soda change it, so it looks red. Add lemon juice, and it turns yellow again. Scientists call things like soap and cooking soda 'bases'. A base changes the colour of curcumin. They call sour things like lemon 'acids'. An acid changes it back. You will learn more about bases and acids when you are older. Some mild liquid hand washes do not turn haldi red. That is a real result too.
- e.g. A haldi stain on a white shirt turns red when you rub soap on it.
- e.g. A drop of soap water on haldi paper makes a red spot.
- e.g. A drop of lemon juice on the red spot turns it yellow again.
### Discoveries come from everywhere · *defined*
**Definition:** A discovery is finding out something about the world that people did not know before. People in every part of the world have made them.

People in every land have asked questions and found answers. Aryabhata, in India about 1,500 years ago, wrote that the Earth spins round. Tu Youyou, in China, found a medicine for malaria in 1971. C. V. Raman, in Kolkata, found something new about light in 1928. Each discovery is shared, so people everywhere can use it and test it again. A discovery made in one country helps people in every country.
- e.g. Malaria medicine found in China is now used in India and many other countries.
- e.g. In the year 499, Aryabhata, in India, wrote that the Earth spins.
- e.g. Ronald Ross, working in Secunderabad, near Hyderabad, found in 1897 that mosquitoes carry malaria.

## Mostly agreed
- Anyone who is curious and tests their ideas can find out new things. *How we know:* Mary Anning found famous fossils as a girl. Tu Youyou found a medicine without a doctorate. Children find things out at home every day by trying them.
- Soap and cooking soda turn haldi red, and lemon juice turns it yellow again. *How we know:* Anyone can test this at home with haldi, soap, cooking soda and lemon. It works the same way in every kitchen.
- A guess becomes knowledge only after it is tested. *How we know:* Tu Youyou guessed that heat was spoiling the medicine. She made it without much heat, and it worked. Her test showed her guess was right.

## How we found out
**Tu Youyou, a scientist in China, and her team, using a clue from Ge Hong, a doctor in China about 1,700 years ago, 1969 (Tu begins the search), 1971 (the low-heat medicine works in mice and monkeys), 1972 (first tests in people), 2015 (the Nobel Prize).** Malaria is an illness that people get from mosquito bites. In 1969, a scientist called Tu Youyou, in Beijing, China, was asked to find a new medicine for it, because the old medicines were no longer working well. Tu and her team read very old books and tested hundreds of plant recipes. Most did not work. One plant, sweet wormwood, worked only sometimes. Tu read its recipe again, in a book by a doctor called Ge Hong, written about 1,700 years ago. It said to soak the leaves in water and squeeze out the juice. It did not say to boil them. Most plant medicines were made by boiling. Tu wondered if heat was spoiling the medicine. So she made it a new way, without much heat. In 1971 it worked very well on mice and monkeys. Then she and her team took it themselves first, to check it was safe. The medicine is called artemisinin, and it has since saved millions of lives. In 2015 Tu won the Nobel Prize, though she never had a doctorate.  
*What people believed before:* Most people made plant medicines by boiling them, so boiled sweet wormwood was tried first, and it worked only sometimes. Tu guessed the heat was spoiling the medicine. The medicine she made without much heat worked very well in mice and monkeys. That showed the heat had been the problem.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Scientists are special people who are born clever.** (A common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Famous scientists did amazing things, so it seems they must be very different from us.  
  Against: Scientists learn by asking, trying and making mistakes, like everyone. Mary Anning started as a girl collecting shells and fossils on the beach. Many scientists say they kept going because they were curious, not because they were born knowing.
- **Old medicine books always have the right answer.** (Some people today; *rejected*)  
  For: Tu Youyou found the clue to her malaria medicine in a book about 1,700 years old.  
  Against: Tu Youyou's team tested hundreds of old recipes against malaria. Most did not work. Only careful testing showed which one did. An old book can give a clue, and a test shows if the clue is right.
- **New ideas that sound strange must be wrong.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Many strange ideas do turn out to be wrong when people test them.  
  Against: Some strange ideas turned out to be right. Many people laughed at the idea that stones fall from the sky. Then scientists studied stones that many people had seen fall, and agreed that it was true. A strange idea must be tested, not laughed at or believed straight away.

## Roots
We have tested haldi in our kitchen and heard about curious people from India and the world. Now we ask what being curious tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Haldi Test, the haldi turned red where soap touched it, and stayed yellow with lemon, salt and sugar. Many people see haldi stains turn red in the wash. Seeing it does not tell you what made it red. Asking and testing told you. So a discovery can be waiting in something everyone sees every day.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think discoveries are hidden far away, found only by special people in laboratories. But haldi stains have been turning red in washing buckets for a very long time. What was missing was someone asking why and trying one thing at a time.  
*Evidence:* In the Haldi Test, the child guessed first, then put six kitchen liquids on haldi paper, one in each box. Only soap and cooking soda turned it red. In the story, Paati had seen it for sixty years, and found out what did it only when Meenakshi asked and tested.

- *who am i:* You are a curious being. Questions pop into your head without you trying, and you can test them.
- *what is life:* Every living being explores. A baby tests a surprising toy, a crow pokes at something new, and a plant's roots search the soil for water.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of things nobody has asked about yet. Even your kitchen holds answers waiting for a question.

A baby tests a toy that surprised them. A crow turns over something new. An ant tries a new path. Many living beings explore the world around them. Scientists still disagree about how much each of them wonders. Everything is conscious, and thinking is not only done by brains. People are animals too, and our questions are one kind of exploring among many.

- **Plato, Theaetetus 155d:** “μάλα γὰρ φιλοσόφου τοῦτο τὸ πάθος, τὸ θαυμάζειν· οὐ γὰρ ἄλλη ἀρχὴ φιλοσοφίας ἢ αὕτη” Socrates, a thinker in ancient Greece, said that philosophy, the love of wisdom, begins with wonder. He said it has no other beginning. (Your haldi test began with wonder: why did the stain go red? The wondering came first, and the test came next.)
- **Kena Upanishad 1.1:** “ॐ केनेषितं पतति प्रेषितं मनः / केन प्राणः प्रथमः प्रैति युक्तः” The Kena Upanishad, an old Indian teaching, begins with a question: 'By whom is the mind sent out?' It is even named after its first word, 'kena', which means 'by whom'. Asking is where it starts. (The Kena Upanishad starts with a question, the way a scientist does. You started with a question too, and the question led you to the answer.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129.7 (the Nasadiya Sukta):** “इयं विसृष्टिर्यत आबभूव यदि वा दधे यदि वा न। / यो अस्याध्यक्षः परमे व्योमन्त्सो अङ्ग वेद यदि वा न वेद” This very old hymn from the Rig Veda, called the Nasadiya Sukta, asks how the world began. It ends by saying that perhaps even the highest one does not know. It is honest about not knowing. (Scientists also say 'we don't know yet' when they don't. Not knowing is where every new question, and every discovery, begins.)
- Science (settled): Even babies are curious. In one study, babies of about one year old tested toys that had done something surprising. They dropped or banged them to find out more.
- Science (open): Many animals explore new things, including crows, rats and octopuses. Scientists are still finding out how curiosity works in each of them.
- Other view, Many scientists: Many scientists say only animals with brains can be curious. They say a plant's roots growing towards water follow a rule built into their cells, and that this is not the same as wondering.

Smell it. Now close your eyes and take one slow breath. Open them. Tell me one thing you wonder about it.
- Ask: Where do your questions come from?
- Ask: Is there anything nobody will ever be able to find out?

## Story: The Red Spot on Arjun's Shirt
*Meenakshi, Arjun and Paati are made up. Haldi really does turn red with washing soap, and yellow again with lemon.*

It was a Sunday morning in Madurai. Meenakshi was six. Her big brother Arjun was nine. They lived with Amma, Appa and Paati in a flat on the second floor.

At lunch, Arjun ate sambar and rice very fast. A big drop of sambar fell on his white school shirt. It left a bright yellow spot.

"Oh no," said Arjun. "That's my school shirt."

"Haldi," said Paati. "The sambar has haldi in it. Haldi is very yellow, and it sticks."

After lunch, Paati took the shirt to the bathroom. Meenakshi went with her. Paati wet the shirt and rubbed the yellow spot with a bar of washing soap.

Meenakshi stared. "Paati! The spot is going red!"

It was true. Where the soap touched the yellow, it turned red, like kumkum.

"Yes," said Paati. "Haldi always does that with soap. I have seen it since I was a little girl."

"But why?" asked Meenakshi.

Paati stopped rubbing. She thought for a moment. "Do you know, I never asked," she said. "I have washed a hundred haldi stains. I always saw it go red. But I never asked why."

"Can we find out?" asked Meenakshi.

Paati smiled. "Let's try. First, what do you think makes it red?"

Meenakshi thought. "Maybe it's the water," she said. "Or maybe it's the rubbing."

"Good guesses," said Paati. "How can we check?"

They went to the kitchen. Paati mixed a little haldi with water in a small steel katori. It was bright yellow. She painted some of it on an old white paper with her finger.

Meenakshi dipped her finger in plain water. She touched the yellow paper. The spot stayed yellow. "Not the water," she said.

She rubbed the paper hard with a dry finger. It stayed yellow. "Not the rubbing."

Then she wet her finger and rubbed it on the bar of soap. She touched the paper. A red spot came up at once.

"It's the soap!" she shouted.

Arjun came into the kitchen. "What are you two doing?"

"Finding out why your shirt went red," said Meenakshi. "It's the soap. Watch." She made another red spot.

"What else can turn it red?" asked Arjun.

"Let's find out," said Meenakshi. Now she was the teacher.

They tried salt water. It stayed yellow. They tried sugar water. Yellow. They tried lemon juice. Yellow.

Then Paati took the tin of cooking soda that Amma kept for making bajji. She mixed a pinch with water. Meenakshi dabbed it on the paper. The spot turned orange-red.

"Soap and cooking soda," said Meenakshi slowly. "Those two make it red. The others don't."

Arjun looked at the red spots. "Can you make it yellow again?"

Meenakshi did not know. She tried lemon juice on a red spot. Slowly, the red went pale. Then it was yellow again.

"It came back!" said Arjun.

That evening, Appa came home. Meenakshi showed him the paper. She showed him the red spots and the yellow spots, and told him what each one was.

"Who taught you this?" asked Appa.

"Nobody," said Meenakshi. "I asked a question. Then we tried things."

Paati laughed. "Meenakshi taught me," she said. "Sixty years of washing, and today I found out something new."

Appa smiled. "You know who does that? Scientists. They ask a question, and then they try things to find out."

Meenakshi looked at her yellow and red paper. "Then I'm a scientist," she said.

"And so is Paati," said Arjun.

That night, Meenakshi lay in bed and looked at the ceiling fan. Why does it make a whirring sound? she wondered. She smiled in the dark. Tomorrow she would find out.

**For the parent:** Meenakshi sees the haldi stain turn red and asks why. By trying one thing at a time, she finds that soap and cooking soda turn haldi red. She realises that asking and trying is what scientists do, and that her grandmother, who saw it for sixty years, is finding out with her.

- Ask: What did Meenakshi notice about Arjun's shirt?
- Ask: How did Meenakshi find out it was the soap, and not the water or the rubbing?
- Ask: Paati had seen haldi go red for sixty years. Why did she find out what turned it red only on that day?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Haldi Test: which kitchen liquids turn haldi red? (20 min)
- **question:** A haldi stain can turn red in the wash. Which kitchen liquids turn haldi red?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will plain water, lemon juice, salt water, sugar water, soap water and cooking soda water turn the haldi red? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same haldi paper for every liquid. We put one drop of each liquid in its own box. We use a clean cotton bud or a clean spoon tip each time. Only the liquid changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the lemon.
- **record:** The haldi paper itself, with each box named, and the results table with a guess and a result for each liquid.
- **results table:** Liquid | My guess: red or yellow? | What I saw: red or yellow?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the haldi powder and the six liquids you will use. 2. Draw the results table together. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason for each liquid. Write the guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Sometimes a haldi stain turns red in the wash. We will test six kitchen liquids to see which one does that.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the six liquids. Make your guesses before we try.
  - Ask: Which ones do you think will turn it red? Why? (good: Lemon, because it is strong.; Not water, because water just makes it wet.; Salt, because it is like soap.)
  - Note: Don't say what will happen. Many children guess lemon will turn it red, because lemon is strong and sour. Write every guess down.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a little of each of these in its own katori: plain water, lemon juice, salt water and sugar water. 2. Rub the wet soap bar in a katori of water until it is cloudy. 3. Stir a pinch of cooking soda into water.  
  *Parent says:* I am making soap water and soda water. You can stir the salt and the sugar into water. Stir until they disappear.  
  *Child mode:* Help stir the salt and the sugar into water.
  - Note: Use a bar of washing soap or bathing soap, or washing powder. Some mild liquid hand washes and shampoos do not turn haldi red.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix half a teaspoon of haldi with three spoons of water. 2. Paint it thickly on the paper with fingers or a brush. 3. Draw six boxes in pencil and write the name of a liquid in each.  
  *Parent says:* Paint the whole paper yellow. Make it nice and thick. Now we draw six boxes, one for each liquid.  
  *Child mode:* Paint the paper yellow with your fingers or a brush.
  - Note: The paper can stay damp. The colour change shows even better on damp haldi. Haldi stains clothes and fingers, so wear old clothes.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dip a clean cotton bud in plain water. 2. Dab it in the first box. 3. Wait and watch for ten seconds. 4. Write red or yellow in the results table. 5. Do the same for each liquid, with a clean bud each time.  
  *Parent says:* Put one drop in its own box and watch it. Is it red or yellow? Now do the next liquid, with a clean bud.  
  *Child mode:* Dab one liquid in its box. Watch. Is it red?
  - Note: Soap water turns the haldi deep red at once. Cooking soda water usually turns it orange-red. Water, lemon, salt and sugar leave it yellow. If the soap box stays yellow, try a different soap bar or washing powder. Dab small drops, far apart, so the boxes do not run into each other.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the paper and the table together. Ask which liquids turned the haldi red and how your child knows.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the paper and the table. Was any guess different from what happened?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your guesses and your results. Which ones were different?
  - Ask: Which ones turned it red? How do you know? (good: Soap and cooking soda.; The soap one went the reddest.)
  - Check: In the Haldi Test, the soap box went red and the lemon box stayed yellow. What did the test show? → **Soap turns haldi red, and lemon does not**
### e2 · Back to Yellow: can the red spot change back? (12 min)
- **question:** Once soap has turned haldi red, can we turn it yellow again?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the red spot stay red for ever? Could lemon, water or salt water change it back? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We add a few drops of a different liquid to each red spot. All the red spots were made with the same soap water. Only the liquid we add changes.
- **record:** Take a photo of the paper, or draw three spots and colour each one the way it looked after you added a liquid.
- **results table:** Liquid added to the red spot | My guess | What I saw
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Dab soap water on the haldi paper in three places, far apart, with a cotton bud. Each spot turns red.  
  *Parent says:* Here are three red spots. Soap made them red. Can we turn one back to yellow?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the red spots. Think about what you tried in the haldi test.
  - Ask: What could turn it yellow? (good: Water could wash it away.; Lemon, because lemon kept it yellow before.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add a few drops of plain water to spot one and salt water to spot two. Add just two or three drops of lemon juice to spot three. Watch for half a minute.  
  *Parent says:* Put water on the first red spot, salt water on the second and lemon on the third. Watch closely.  
  *Child mode:* Add one liquid to each red spot. Watch.
  - Note: The lemon spot fades to orange and then yellow. It may need a few more drops. Water and salt water leave the spot red.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rub the wet soap bar on the yellow lemon spot. If it stays yellow, rub more soap on. Talk about what you saw.  
  *Parent says:* Now rub soap on the lemon spot. Guess first, then watch.  
  *Child mode:* Rub soap on the lemon spot and watch the colour.

### Being a Scientist (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (sort) Which liquids turned haldi red in your test? Put each in a box.  
   **Answer:** soap water → Turned red; lemon juice → Stayed yellow; salt water → Stayed yellow; cooking soda water → Turned red; plain water → Stayed yellow. *Why:* A strong answer comes from the child's own haldi paper, not from memory. If their soap box stayed yellow, they may have used a mild hand wash.
2. (draw) Draw your haldi paper. Colour the boxes that went red.  
   **Answer:** A yellow paper with red boxes for soap and cooking soda.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows which liquid was in each box, and colours only those that changed.
3. (mcq) In the story, Meenakshi rubbed haldi paper hard with a dry finger, and it stayed yellow. Then soap turned it red. What did the rubbing show?  
   Options: Rubbing is not what turns haldi red / Haldi never changes / Soap is not needed  
   **Answer:** Rubbing is not what turns haldi red. *Why:* She tried the rubbing on its own, and nothing changed. So the red came from something else: the soap.
4. (match) Draw a line from each person to what they found.  
   **Answer:** Tu Youyou → a medicine for malaria; Mary Anning → fossil bones of a sea animal; Janaki Ammal → new kinds of sugarcane; You → which liquids turn haldi red. *Why:* Each of these people was curious, asked a question and found something out. That includes you.
5. (truefalse) Only grown-ups can make discoveries.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Mary Anning found famous fossils as a girl. You made a discovery in the haldi test.
6. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: how do you know soap turns haldi red?  
   **Answer:** I put soap water on haldi paper and the spot went red. The other boxes stayed yellow.. *Why:* A strong answer says what the child saw in the test. 'Paati said so' is not enough.
7. (fill) Fill the gap: A scientist notices something, asks a ____, and tries things to find out.  
   **Answer:** question. *Why:* A scientist starts with a question. Then she tries things to find the answer.
8. (label) Here is a girl being a scientist. Put an E on the part of her that notices. Put a Q on her question. Put an H on the part of her that tests.  
   **Answer:** E on her eyes, Q on the question mark above her head, H on her hands holding the cotton bud.. *Why:* Scientists notice with their senses, ask questions and test with their hands and tools.
9. (odd) Which one is NOT a question?  
   Options: Why is the sky blue? / How do ants find sugar? / The fan is on. / Where do clouds go?  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 'The fan is on' tells us something. The others ask something. Scientists start with questions.
10. (perspective) Two friends in Chennai talk about scientists.  
   **Answer:** Many children agree with the second child, because they did the haldi test at home and found something out. The first child is right that many scientists work in laboratories and learn a lot.. *Why:* A strong answer uses evidence, like Mary Anning on the beach or the haldi test in the kitchen. It is also fair to the other side.
### Curious People (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (order) Put the steps of the Haldi Test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Guess which liquids will turn it red', 'Paint the paper with haldi', 'Dab each liquid in its own box', 'Say what you found']. *Why:* Scientists guess first, then test, then say what they found.
2. (draw) Draw the red spot after you added lemon juice in Back to Yellow.  
   **Answer:** A yellow spot, or a pale orange one turning yellow.. *Why:* Lemon juice turns the red haldi colouring yellow again. A strong drawing shows the spot yellow.
3. (mcq) In The Secret Red Message, your hidden soap word turned red when you brushed haldi water on it. What does that show?  
   Options: The soap was still there, though you could not see it / Haldi can read / The paper was red  
   **Answer:** The soap was still there, though you could not see it. *Why:* The dried soap was hard to see, but it was still on the paper. The haldi showed where it was.
4. (truefalse) A strange new idea must be wrong.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A strange idea is not always wrong, and not always right. Scientists test it to find out.
5. (fill) Fill the gap: A guess becomes knowledge only after we ____ it.  
   **Answer:** test. *Why:* A guess is an idea. A test shows whether the idea is right.
6. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: what is one question you wonder about? How could you test it?  
   **Answer:** For example: Does a wet cloth dry faster in the sun? I could hang one in the sun and one in the shade and check which dries first.. *Why:* A strong answer has a real question and a test that changes only one thing.
7. (perspective) A girl in Hyderabad and her grandfather, Thatha, talk about old books.  
   **Answer:** Neither is fully right. An old book can give a good clue, but many old ideas turn out to be wrong. The way to know is to test them.. *Why:* A strong answer says that an old idea and a new idea both need testing. It gives each person credit for part of their view.
8. (truefalse) Scientists share what they find, so other people can test it too.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A discovery made in one country, like the malaria medicine, can be tested and used in every country.
9. (label) Here is a map of the world. Put a star on India, on China and on England. Great scientists have made discoveries in all three. What does that tell you?  
   **Answer:** Stars on India, China and England. Discoveries come from every part of the world.. *Why:* A strong answer finds the three countries and says that science belongs to people everywhere.
10. (match) Match each thing to what it does to haldi.  
   **Answer:** soap → turns it red; lemon juice → keeps it yellow; cooking soda → turns it orange-red; lemon on a red spot → turns it yellow again. *Why:* Soap and cooking soda turn haldi red. Lemon keeps it yellow, and turns red haldi yellow again.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does a scientist do?  
   Options: Notices, asks questions and tests ideas / Only reads books / Only wears a white coat  
   **Answer:** Notices, asks questions and tests ideas. *Why:* Scientists find out by noticing, asking and testing. Anyone can do these things, with or without a white coat.
2. (mcq) Who can be a scientist?  
   Options: Only grown-up men / Anyone who is curious and tests their ideas / Only people from far away  
   **Answer:** Anyone who is curious and tests their ideas. *Why:* Girls, boys, women and men from every country have made discoveries. Mary Anning started as a girl.
3. (mcq) In the haldi test, which liquid turned the haldi red?  
   Options: Lemon juice / Sugar water / Soap water  
   **Answer:** Soap water. *Why:* Soap turns haldi red. Lemon juice and sugar water leave it yellow.
4. (mcq) In the haldi test, why did each liquid get its own box?  
   Options: So we could tell which liquid made the colour change / To make the paper look nice / Because the paper was too big  
   **Answer:** So we could tell which liquid made the colour change. *Why:* If two liquids touched the same box, we would not know which one changed the colour. One liquid in each box keeps the test fair.
5. (mcq) A scientist is looking for a new medicine. Where could she find a clue?  
   Options: In old books about medicine, which she then tests / On the Moon / In a dream, with no test  
   **Answer:** In old books about medicine, which she then tests. *Why:* Old books hold what people tried long ago. A clue from them is a start, but it must be tested. Tu Youyou found her clue this way.
6. (mcq) Tu Youyou guessed that heat was spoiling the medicine. How did she find out if she was right?  
   Options: She asked a king / She made it without much heat and tested it / She stopped looking  
   **Answer:** She made it without much heat and tested it. *Why:* She tested her guess. The medicine made without much heat worked.
7. (mcq) Where do discoveries in science come from?  
   Options: People in every country / Only one country / Only rich countries  
   **Answer:** People in every country. *Why:* Anyone who asks questions and tests them can find things out. Great scientists have come from India, China, England and everywhere else.
8. (mcq) You guess that salt turns haldi red. What should you do?  
   Options: Believe your guess / Test it with salt water on haldi paper / Ask a friend to guess too  
   **Answer:** Test it with salt water on haldi paper. *Why:* A guess needs a test. If you try salt water on haldi, you will see it stays yellow.
9. (mcq) A red soap spot is on yellow haldi paper. You add lemon juice. What happens?  
   Options: It turns black / It turns yellow again / It gets more red  
   **Answer:** It turns yellow again. *Why:* Lemon turns the red haldi colouring back to yellow.
10. (mcq) Long ago, Aryabhata said the Earth spins round. What do we see every day that fits his idea?  
   Options: The Sun seems to rise and set / The Earth looks flat / The Earth is a square  
   **Answer:** The Sun seems to rise and set. *Why:* As the Earth spins, our side turns towards the Sun and away again. So the Sun seems to rise and set. Aryabhata in India said the Earth spins.

## Applications
- Doctors test new medicines carefully before people use them, the way Tu Youyou did.
- Cooks and grandmothers know many kitchen facts, like haldi turning red with soap. Each fact started with someone noticing.
- Scientists share what they find, so people in other countries can test it and use it.
- Asking questions and testing helps at school, at home and in every job.
- Malaria medicines found by testing are used in India and many other countries.

## Open questions
- **Why are some people more curious than others?** Scientists still argue about this. Some think it is how we are born, and some think it is how we are brought up. Most think it is both.
- **What will be the next big discovery?** Nobody knows. A big discovery is something nobody knows yet, so nobody can say what it will be.
- **What is curiosity like for a crow, an octopus or a rat?** Crows, octopuses, rats and many other animals explore new things. Scientists are still finding out what curiosity is like for each of them.

## Connected maths
- In the story, Meenakshi tested 6 liquids on haldi. 2 turned it red. How many stayed yellow? **4 liquids stayed yellow.**. 6 liquids in all. 2 went red. 6 take away 2 is 4. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- You guessed that 4 liquids would turn haldi red. Only 2 did. Was your guess too many or too few? By how many? **Too many, by 2.**. 4 is more than 2. 4 take away 2 is 2. You guessed 2 more than really turned red. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- Here is a list of the colours from a haldi test: red, yellow, yellow, red, yellow, yellow. Make tally marks for red and for yellow. Which colour came up more? **Red: 2 tally marks. Yellow: 4 tally marks. Yellow came up more.**. Count each colour and make one mark for each. Yellow has 4 marks and red has 2, so yellow came up more. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L1`)
