# Straight, round, back and forth: the paths moving things take
`sci.motion.kinds-of-motion.L2`

**Summary.** Make a bunch of keys go round and round on a string. Look down from above, and you see a circle. Put your eyes at the same height, and the same keys seem to go back and forth, like a swing. Things move in three main paths: straight, round, and back and forth. Your child finds all three at home, sorts things that move by themselves from things that are moved, and tests whether they can walk straight with their eyes shut.

**Intro.** *Draw a straight line in the air with your finger. Now draw a circle. Now wave your finger back and forth. Those are the three paths.* A path is the way a thing goes as it moves. A ball rolled down a corridor goes in a straight line. The tip of a fan blade goes round and round. A swing goes back and forth. Some things start moving by themselves, like an ant or a crow. Others move only when something pushes or pulls them, like a ball. In this unit you hunt for all three paths, and test one surprise: can a round path look like a back-and-forth one?

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can name the three paths things move in: straight, round, and back and forth.
1. By the end I can find things at home that move in each path, and things that move in two paths at once.
2. By the end I can show that a round motion, seen from the side, looks like back and forth.
3. By the end I can sort things into those that move by themselves and those that are moved by a push or pull.
4. By the end I can say why I need my eyes to keep walking in a straight line.
5. By the end I can make a guess, test it, count what I see, and say what I found.

## Terms
- **Path**: A path is the way a thing goes as it moves. You can draw it as a line. *e.g.* The path of a ball rolled along the corridor is a straight line.
- **Straight motion**: Straight motion is moving along a straight line, without turning. *e.g.* A drawer sliding open, a lift going up, and a train on a straight track all move straight.
- **Round motion**: Round motion is going round and round a middle point, along a circle. Scientists call it circular motion. *e.g.* The tip of a fan blade and a seat on a giant wheel both go round.
- **Back-and-forth motion**: Back-and-forth motion is going one way, then back the other way, again and again. *e.g.* A swing, a cradle and a toothbrush scrubbing your teeth all move back and forth.
- **Vibrate**: To vibrate is to move back and forth very fast, often too fast to see. Vibrating things often make a sound. *e.g.* A ruler flicked on the edge of a table vibrates and buzzes.
- **Moves by themselves**: A thing that moves by themselves starts, stops and turns with no push or pull from outside. Living beings move by themselves, using energy from their food. *e.g.* An ant walking to a sugar grain and a crow flying off the grille both move by themselves.
- **Is moved**: A thing is moved when a push or a pull from something else starts it or keeps it going. A push or a pull is called a force. *e.g.* A ball moves when your foot kicks it. A curtain moves when the wind blows it.

## Explanations
### Three paths · *defined*
**Definition:** Most moving things move in one of three paths: straight, round, or back and forth.

In straight motion, a thing goes along a line and does not turn. In round motion, a thing goes round a middle point, along a circle. In back-and-forth motion, a thing goes one way and then back again, over and over. A swing, a cradle and a ruler buzzing on a table all move back and forth. Back-and-forth motion that is very small and fast is called vibrating.
- e.g. A drawer slides straight out and straight back in.
- e.g. A clock's hands go round the middle of the clock.
- e.g. A cradle rocks back and forth.
### One thing, two paths at once · *defined*
**Definition:** Different parts of one thing can move in different paths at the same time.

When a cycle rides along a straight road, the whole cycle moves straight. At the same time, its wheels and pedals go round. The rider's knees go up and down, back and forth. So when we ask how something moves, we need to ask which part we are watching.
- e.g. An auto goes straight along the road while its wheels go round.
- e.g. A sewing machine's wheel goes round while its needle goes up and down.
- e.g. When you walk straight down the corridor, your arms swing back and forth.
### Round motion seen from the side · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing going round in a circle looks like it goes back and forth when you watch from the side, at the same height.

From above, you saw the keys go round in a circle. From the side, you could not see them coming towards you or going away. You saw only their left and right. So they seemed to go back and forth, like a swing. One round looked like one back and forth. Their shadow on the wall did the same.
- e.g. In the keys test, 10 rounds from above matched about 10 back-and-forths from the side.
- e.g. A fan's blade tips go round. A gecko on the wall, level with the fan, would see them go back and forth.
- e.g. Galileo saw Jupiter's moons go back and forth beside Jupiter. They are going round Jupiter, and we see their circles from the side.
### Moving by themselves, or being moved · *debated*
**Definition:** Living beings start, stop and turn by themselves. Most things that are not alive start moving only when a push or a pull starts them.

An ant decides where to walk, and a crow decides when to fly. Living beings use energy from their food to move their own bodies. A ball, a door and a curtain start moving when something pushes or pulls them: a foot, a hand or the wind. Some things carry a motor or an engine, like a fan, a lift or an auto. They start when electricity or fuel turns the motor, without anyone pushing. People still argue about what 'by themselves' really means.
- e.g. A pigeon walks along the window ledge by herself.
- e.g. A leaf moves when the wind blows it.
- e.g. A ceiling fan goes round when electricity turns its motor, and slows down and stops when the power goes.
- **Aristotle, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,350 years ago:** Living beings move themselves, and everything else is moved by something. Even an animal moving herself is moved by a part of her own body.
- **Many physicists today:** Every mover needs a push from something. An ant moves forward by pushing the floor back with her feet. A fish pushes the water. So nothing starts moving with no push at all.
- **Many biologists:** What is special about living beings is that they choose when and where to move, and use their food's energy to do it.

## Mostly agreed
- Things move in straight, round and back-and-forth paths, and one thing can move in more than one path at once. *How we know:* A cycle goes straight along the road while its wheels and pedals go round. A sewing machine's wheel goes round while its needle goes up and down.
- A round motion watched from the side looks like back and forth. *How we know:* Keys going round on a string look like a swing from the side, and so does their shadow on a wall. Jupiter's moons, which go round Jupiter, look from Earth as if they go back and forth beside it.
- Living beings use energy from their food to move themselves. *How we know:* Muscles need food to work. After running and playing for a long time, you feel tired and hungry.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, and Simon Marius, a German astronomer, January 1610.** For a very long time, many people in Europe believed that everything in the sky went round the Earth. On 7 January 1610, Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, pointed his new telescope at the planet Jupiter. Beside it he saw three tiny bright points, in a straight line. At first he took them for stars. But the next night they had moved. Within a week he had seen four. Night after night, they moved back and forth along the same line, never going far from Jupiter. Sometimes one vanished behind Jupiter and later came out again. Galileo worked out what this meant. The four points were moons, going round and round Jupiter. We see their round paths from the side, so they seem to go back and forth. Here were things in the sky going round something that was not the Earth. Galileo wrote about them in a little book, The Starry Messenger, in March 1610. A German astronomer, Simon Marius, said he had seen the moons at about the same time. The names we use for them today, Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, are the ones Marius printed.  
*What people believed before:* Everything in the sky goes round the Earth. At first, Galileo also took Jupiter's moons for ordinary stars.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A round path and a back-and-forth path are always easy to tell apart.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: From above, a circle and a swing look very different.  
  Against: In the shadow game, the shadow of keys going round looked almost the same as the shadow of keys swinging. Galileo first took Jupiter's moons for stars going back and forth in a line.
- **Only living beings move by themselves.** (A common idea, close to what Aristotle taught; *debated*)  
  For: Ants, crows and people start and stop when they choose. A ball lies still until someone kicks it.  
  Against: A fan, a lift and an auto start without anyone pushing them, using electricity or fuel. In 1827, Robert Brown, a Scottish plant scientist, saw tiny specks from pollen jiggling in water. Then he saw specks of ground-up rock and glass jiggle too. Later scientists found that water particles, too small to see, bump the specks all the time.
- **Plants stay still. They do not move by themselves.** (A common idea, because plants move too slowly for us to notice; *rejected*)  
  For: A potted plant seems to stay in the same place all day.  
  Against: Seedlings by a window lean towards the light in a day or two, and lean back if you turn the pot. The touch-me-not plant folds up their leaves within seconds of a touch.

## Roots
We have found straight, round and back-and-forth paths, and watched keys on a string from two places. Now we ask what the keys test tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** From above, the keys on your string went round in a circle. From the side, they went back and forth, once for every round. The keys kept the same path all the time. So round and back-and-forth can be one motion, seen from two places.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort motions into kinds, such as round and back and forth, as if each kind were a separate thing. The keys test showed that one motion fits two kinds, depending on where it is watched from.  
*Evidence:* In the keys test, 10 rounds seen from above matched about 10 back-and-forths seen from the side, and the shadow on the wall went back and forth too.

- *who am i:* You move yourself. You decide to walk, turn and stop, and your eyes keep you on your path.
- *what is life:* Living beings start, stop and turn by themselves. An ant, a crow and a mustard seedling each move towards what they need.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of round motions: fans, wheels, and the Moon round the Earth. From the side, many of them look like back and forth.

We sorted motions into three kinds: straight, round, and back and forth. Then one bunch of keys showed two of the kinds at once. The kinds are names we give from where we watch, and one motion can fit more than one of them. Everything is conscious, and every watcher meets the world from their own place. A gecko on the wall, an ant on the floor and you each see the same fan move in a different path.

- **Vaisheshika Sutra 1.1.7, by Kanada:** Kanada, an early Indian thinker, listed five kinds of motion: moving up, moving down, drawing in, spreading out, and going along. (Like us, Kanada sorted motions by the way they go. His list is different from ours. People can sort the same motions in more than one way.)
- **Udana 6.4, a Buddhist text, and the Jain teaching of anekantavada, many-sidedness:** Some blind men each touch one part of an elephant. One says an elephant is like a rope, and another says like a wall. Each is right about the part he touched. (From above you saw a circle, and from the side a line. Each view told the truth about the keys from one place.)
- **Aristotle, Physics, Book 7, chapter 1:** Aristotle, a Greek thinker, taught that everything that moves is moved by something. (In Who Starts It?, we asked the same question about each moving thing: what made it start?)
- Science (settled): A swing's back-and-forth motion and a round motion seen from the side follow the same pattern. Scientists use circles to work out how swings and springs move.
- Science (settled): Jupiter's four big moons go round Jupiter. From Earth we see their circles from the side, so they seem to go back and forth.
- Other view, Many physicists: The keys had one real path, a circle. The back-and-forth line was only how it looked from one place, so it is not a second kind of motion.
- Practice, Back and forth with your breath (3 min): Sit up tall. Put one hand on your tummy. Breathe in slowly and feel your tummy go out. Breathe out and feel it come back in. That is back and forth. Now draw a slow circle in the air with one finger. Go up as you breathe in, and down as you breathe out. Do it five times. Seen from the side, your finger goes only up and down.
- Ask: The keys went round, and they went back and forth. Which one was the real path, or were both real?
- Ask: When you watch the fan from your bed, what path would a gecko on the wall see?
- Ask: Does anything start moving with nothing at all to start it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The keys went round the whole time. Why did they seem to go back and forth from the side? (hint: From the side, could you tell when the keys were coming towards you or going away?)
- *deep:* Why did your walk drift off the line when your eyes were shut? (hint: With your eyes open, what tells you that you have started to turn?)
- *big:* The Earth goes round the Sun. Seen from very far away, from the side, what path would the Earth seem to take? (hint: Think of the keys seen from the side, and of Jupiter's moons.)
- *big:* When you decide to get up and walk, where does the start of that walk come from? (hint: Think about what you ate, and what you wanted to do.)

## Story: Revathi and the Shadow on the Wall
*Hyderabad is a real city in Telangana. Ammamma means mother's mother in Telugu. An oonjal is a swing seat that hangs in many South Indian homes. Revathi, Arjun, Amma and Ammamma are made up. Galileo Galilei was a real scientist who saw Jupiter's moons in 1610.*

Revathi was seven. She lived on the fourth floor of a block of flats in Hyderabad, with her Amma and her Ammamma. Ammamma is her grandmother. That week her cousin Arjun was staying with them. He was six.

One evening, the lights went out. The ceiling fan kept turning, slower and slower, and then it stopped.

"Power cut," said Amma. She put her phone on the dining table, with its torch shining at the white wall.

Ammamma's bunch of keys hung on a long red thread from her waist. Revathi untied them. "Can I play with these, Ammamma?"

"Gently," said Ammamma. "And keep them away from faces."

Revathi stood near the wall and held the thread. She moved her hand in a tiny circle. The keys went round and round below it. Their big black shadow moved on the wall.

Arjun was on the sofa. "You're swinging them," he said. "Like an oonjal."

"I'm not swinging," said Revathi. "They're going round. Look down at them."

"I can only see the shadow," said Arjun. "And it's going back and forth."

Revathi frowned. She gave Arjun the keys. "You do it. Make a tiny circle with your hand. Slowly." She showed him how. Then she sat on the sofa, where Arjun had been.

She looked at the wall. The shadow went to the left, then to the right, then to the left again. It looked exactly like a swing.

"Are you swinging them?" she asked.

"No! You told me to go round," said Arjun.

Revathi got up and looked down at the keys from above. They were going round in a circle. She sat down on the sofa again. Back and forth. She got up again. Round.

"It's the same keys," she said slowly. "From the top, they go round. From the side, they go back and forth."

"So which is it?" asked Arjun.

"Let's test," said Revathi. "I'll shut my eyes. You make them swing, or go round. Then I'll open my eyes and look only at the shadow. I'll guess which."

She guessed five times. She was right twice and wrong three times. "I can't tell from the shadow!" she said. "I have to look from the top."

Ammamma laughed. "A long time ago, a scientist called Galileo looked at Jupiter through a telescope. He saw little moons going back and forth beside Jupiter. He worked out that they were really going round it. We see their circles from the side."

Just then a moth flew in from the balcony. She flew round and round the torch. On the wall, an ant was walking in a straight line towards the kitchen.

"Who is pushing the ant?" asked Arjun.

"Nobody," said Revathi. "She moves by herself. She decides where to go."

"And the keys?" asked Ammamma.

"The keys go only where my hand moves them," said Revathi. "And the moth moves by herself, like the ant."

With a click, the power came back. The fan began to turn again.

"The fan moved by itself!" said Arjun.

"No," said Revathi. "The electricity came back. It turns the motor inside the fan. When the power went, the fan slowed down and stopped."

That night, Revathi lay in bed and looked up at the fan. From below, the blades went round in a circle. On the wall near the ceiling sat a little house gecko.

"Ammamma," Revathi whispered. "The gecko is level with the fan. I think the gecko sees the blades go back and forth."

"You may be right," said Ammamma. "Go to sleep now."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Arjun sees the shadow go back and forth, and Revathi sees the keys go round. Both are right about what they see. Revathi tests it by guessing from the shadow alone, and finds she cannot tell. One motion looks round from above and back and forth from the side. Then the ant, the moth, the keys and the fan show the difference between moving by yourself, being moved, and having a motor.

- Ask: Why did Arjun think Revathi was swinging the keys?
- Ask: How did Revathi test whether she could tell round from back and forth?
- Ask: Who moved by themselves in the story, and what was moved?
- Ask: Why did the fan start again?

## Games
### g1 · Three Paths: the card game (10 min)
- **rules:** Write STRAIGHT, ROUND and BACK AND FORTH on three sheets of paper. These are the mats.; A grown-up reads one card out loud.; The player draws the path in the air with a finger, then puts the card on a mat.; The grown-up reads the back. A right answer wins the card.; If the player gives a good reason for a different mat, that wins too.; Play until all 12 cards are won.
- **Printable cards: 12 moving cards:** A Mumbai local train on a straight track (back: STRAIGHT); A seat on a giant wheel at a mela (back: ROUND); A cradle rocking in the hall (back: BACK AND FORTH); A lift going up (back: STRAIGHT); The tip of a ceiling fan blade (back: ROUND); A toothbrush scrubbing teeth (back: BACK AND FORTH); A drawer sliding open (back: STRAIGHT); The hands of a clock (back: ROUND); An oar rowing a boat in the Kerala backwaters (back: BACK AND FORTH); A coconut falling from a tree (back: STRAIGHT, straight down); A cycle on a straight road (back: STRAIGHT for the cycle, ROUND for the wheels); A sewing machine needle (back: BACK AND FORTH, up and down)
- **Printable tokens: 3 mats:** STRAIGHT mat; ROUND mat; BACK AND FORTH mat
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the three mats. 2. Draw each path in the air together.  
  *Parent says:* Here are three mats. Draw a straight line in the air. Now a circle. Now back and forth.  
  *Child mode:* Draw each path in the air. Straight. Round. Back and forth.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the cards one by one. 2. Your child draws the path in the air and puts the card on a mat.  
  *Parent says:* A seat on a giant wheel. Draw its path in the air. Which mat does it go on?  
  *Child mode:* Listen. Draw the path in the air. Put the card on a mat.
  - Ask: Why did you put the cycle on two mats? (good: The cycle goes straight, but the wheels go round.; Different parts move in different paths.)
  - Note: Some cards fit two mats. Praise a reason that says which part moves which way.
- **s3 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the cards on each mat.  
  *Parent says:* Which mat has the most cards? Did any card fit two mats?  
  *Child mode:* Count the cards on each mat.
  - Check: An oar pushes a boat through the water, again and again. What path does the oar take? → **Back and forth**
### g2 · Who Starts It? The quick-hands card game (10 min)
- **rules:** Agree on three hand signs: hands on your chest for BY THEMSELVES, pushing palms for PUSHED OR PULLED, and rolling fists for MOTOR.; A grown-up reads one card.; Everyone shows a sign as fast as they can.; The grown-up reads the back. Everyone with the right sign gets a point.; A player who gives a good reason for a different sign also gets a point.; Play until all 12 cards are read.
- **Printable cards: 12 mover cards:** An ant walking to a grain of sugar (back: BY THEMSELVES); A football after a kick (back: PUSHED); A ceiling fan (back: MOTOR); A crow flying off the window grille (back: BY THEMSELVES); A kite in the sky at Sankranti (back: PUSHED by the wind, and PULLED by the string); An auto on the road (back: MOTOR, an engine); You, walking to the kitchen (back: BY THEMSELVES); A door closing in the wind (back: PUSHED by the wind); A lift going up (back: MOTOR); A fish swimming in a pond (back: BY THEMSELVES); A pulled cart of vegetables (back: PULLED by the person); Mustard seedlings leaning to the window (back: BY THEMSELVES, very slowly)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Teach the three hand signs and practise each one.  
  *Parent says:* Hands on your chest means it moves by itself. Pushing palms means a push or pull. Rolling fists means a motor.  
  *Child mode:* Learn the signs. Chest, pushing palms, rolling fists.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the cards one by one. 2. Everyone shows a sign. 3. Read the back and give points.  
  *Parent says:* A kite in the sky at Sankranti. What starts it moving? Show me your sign!  
  *Child mode:* Listen. What starts it moving? Show your sign fast!
  - Ask: Why did you show pushing palms for the kite? (good: The wind pushes it.; The string pulls it.)
  - Note: The seedlings card surprises many children. Plants move by themselves, but slowly, over hours and days.
- **s3 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the points.  
  *Parent says:* Who has the most points? Which card made you think the hardest?  
  *Child mode:* Count your points. Which card was hardest?
  - Check: A fish swims to the top of the pond for food. What starts the fish moving? → **The fish, by themselves**

## Experiments
### e1 · Round or Back and Forth? Keys on a string, seen from two places (22 min)
- **question:** Keys go round and round in a circle on a string. What path do you see when you watch from the side, with your eyes at the same height as the keys?
- **predict:** Guess first. From the side, will you see a circle, a straight line, or something else? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same string, the same keys and the same slow speed every time. Only the place we watch from changes. We count 10 rounds each time, so we can compare the counts.
- **safety:** Keep the keys going slowly, and keep everyone's face at least one big step away from them.
- **record:** Draw the keys seen from above, with their path. Then draw them seen from the side, with their path. Write the two counts.
- **results table:** Where I watched from | My guess | The path I saw | How many I counted
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie the keys to one end of the string. 2. Hold the other end so the keys hang just below your knee. 3. Move your hand in a tiny, slow circle until the keys go round in a circle about the size of a plate.  
  *Parent says:* Watch. I move my hand in a tiny circle. The keys go round in a big circle under my hand.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the keys. They go round and round under the hand.
  - Note: Small, steady hand circles make a neat circle. If the keys start to swing back and forth instead, stop them and start again with a smaller circle.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep the keys going round. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* If you get down low and look from the side, what will you see? A circle, a line, or something else? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. From the side, what path will the keys make? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: A circle, because they go round.; A line, because I will only see one side.; I don't know. I will have to look.)
  - Note: Write the guess in your child's own words. Do not explain yet.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands next to you and looks straight down at the keys. 2. Keep the keys going round. 3. Count 10 rounds together, out loud.  
  *Parent says:* Look straight down from the top. What path do the keys make? Count each time they come back to your side.  
  *Child mode:* Look down at the keys. What shape do they make? Count 10 rounds.
  - Ask: What path did you see from the top? (good: A circle.; Round and round.)
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lies on the floor 2 big steps away, eyes as low as the keys. 2. Keep the keys going round exactly as before. 3. Your child says 'tick' each time the keys reach the left end. 4. You count 10 rounds from above.  
  *Parent says:* Now put your eyes as low as the keys. Say tick each time they reach the left end. What path do you see now?  
  *Child mode:* Lie down. Look from the side. Say tick at the left end.
  - Ask: What path did you see from the side? (good: They go side to side.; Back and forth, like a swing.; A straight line.)
  - Note: From the side, the keys seem to go only left and right, like a swing. While you count 10 rounds, your child should count about 10 ticks. One round looks like one back and forth.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the room dim. 2. Put the phone torch on a table, shining at a plain wall. 3. Hold the keys between the torch and the wall, close to the wall. 4. Your child looks only at the shadow. 5. Make the keys go round, then swing them back and forth, in any order.  
  *Parent says:* Look only at the shadow on the wall. Am I making the keys go round, or swing back and forth? Tell me which.  
  *Child mode:* Look only at the shadow. Are the keys going round or swinging? Can you tell?
  - Note: The shadow of keys going round and the shadow of keys swinging look almost the same. A sharp-eyed child may notice that a swing's shadow rises a little at each end. That is a good find. Let your child try the shadow game on you too.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill in the table together. 2. Ask what your child saw, and why it looked different.  
  *Parent says:* From the top you saw a circle. From the side you saw back and forth. Were the keys doing two things, or one thing?  
  *Child mode:* From the top, a circle. From the side, back and forth. Did the keys change?
  - Ask: Why did the keys look different? (good: They went round all the time. I looked from a different place.; From the side I could not see them come closer and go away.)
  - Note: Now explain. From the side, you cannot see the keys coming towards you and going away. You only see them go left and right. So a round motion seen from the side looks like a back-and-forth motion.
  - Check: The keys went round in a circle on the string. What did you see from the side, with your eyes low? → **Back and forth, like a swing**
  - Think: Where else might something going round look like it goes back and forth?
### e2 · Eyes Shut, Walk Straight: can you stay on the line? (18 min)
- **question:** Can you walk 8 steps along a straight line with your eyes shut, and stay on it?
- **predict:** Guess first. With your eyes shut, will you stay on the line, or drift off it? If you drift, which way? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same line, the same start and the same 8 steps every time. Only the eyes change: open or shut. We try eyes open 2 times and eyes shut 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Clear the floor first. A grown-up walks beside the child all the time and stops them before any wall, door or corner.
- **record:** Draw the line. Draw a dot for each place you stopped: blue for eyes open, red for eyes shut.
- **results table:** Try | Eyes open or shut | How many hand-widths from the line I stopped
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a straight tile line, or stick down a strip of tape. 2. Clear the floor around it. 3. Show your child where to start.  
  *Parent says:* This is our straight line. You will stand at this end and walk 8 steps along it.  
  *Child mode:* This is the line. Stand at this end with your toes on it.
  - Note: A tile line in a hall or corridor works well. You need about 4 big steps of clear floor.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* First with eyes open, then with eyes shut. Will you stay on the line with your eyes shut? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* With your eyes shut, will you stay on the line? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Yes, because I know where the line is.; No, because I can't see it.; I might go a bit to one side.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child walks 8 steps along the line with eyes open. 2. Put a slip at their toes. 3. Measure the gap to the line in hand-widths. 4. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Walk 8 steps along the line with your eyes open. Stop. How far are your toes from the line?  
  *Child mode:* Eyes open. Walk 8 steps on the line. Stop. Look at your toes.
  - Note: With eyes open, most children stay on the line or very close to it.

## Applications
- Trains, lifts and drawers run on straight tracks or rails so they move in a straight line.

- Cradles and swings rock babies and children back and forth.
- Engineers use circles to work out how swings and springs move back and forth.

## Open questions
- **Why do people walk in circles when they cannot see?** Scientists tested people walking with their eyes covered in a big field, and many walked in circles. Each person drifted in their own way. Scientists are still working out exactly why the small turns build up.
- **How does a seedling sense which way the light is coming from?** Scientists have found light-sensing parts in plant cells, and a growth chemical that gathers on the shaded side of the stem. How a seedling puts all these signals together to choose the best way to bend is still being studied.
