# Animal body plans: rings, two halves, spirals and segments
`sci.animals.body-plans.L4`

**Summary.** A crow, a fish, an ant and a human all have a front end and two matching sides. A jellyfish has neither. Her body is a ring, with the mouth in the middle. Nearly every animal is built on one of a few layouts, called body plans: rings, two matching halves, spirals and rows of segments. Your child rolls a lopsided roller to find out why animals who travel have matching sides. They also test why a growing snail shell coils, and find four kinds of tissue in their own hand.

**Intro.** *A starfish has five arms and no head. So which way is forwards for a starfish?* Any way is forwards for a starfish. She can walk with any arm in front. You are built differently. Your eyes, nose and mouth are all at one end, and you walk that end first. Your left side matches your right, but your front does not match your back. Animal bodies are laid out in a few ways: in rings, in two halves, in spirals and in rows of repeated parts. In this unit you find out what each layout is good for. You roll a lopsided roller made of dough and a pencil. You tilt a tall dough shell and a coiled one. You also find four kinds of tissue in your own hand.

## Terms
- **Body plan**: The basic layout of an animal's body: which parts there are, and how they are arranged. *e.g.* A fish and a crow share one body plan. Each has a head end, a tail end and two matching sides.
- **Symmetry**: A shape has symmetry when one part of it matches another part. *e.g.* Your left hand matches your right hand. That is symmetry.
- **Radial symmetry**: Matching parts arranged round a middle point, like the spokes of a cycle wheel. *e.g.* A sea anemone has a ring of tentacles round her mouth.
- **Bilateral symmetry**: One line down the middle of the body, with a matching left side and right side. *e.g.* An ant, a gecko and a human all have bilateral symmetry.
- **Head end**: The front end of a two-sided animal, where the eyes, feelers, mouth and brain are gathered. *e.g.* A fish swims head end first, so her eyes and mouth meet the water first.
- **Segment**: One of a row of repeated parts along a body, each built on the same pattern. *e.g.* Each ring of an earthworm is one segment.
- **Spiral**: A line that winds round a middle point and moves further out with every turn. *e.g.* A snail's shell is a spiral. So is a murukku.
- **Larva**: The young form of an animal, when the young looks very different from the adult. *e.g.* A caterpillar is the larva of a butterfly. A starfish larva is a tiny swimmer with two sides.
- **Sponge**: An animal who lives fixed in one place, with a body full of small holes and no head or mouth. *e.g.* Natural bath sponges are the soft skeletons of sea sponges.
- **Cell**: The tiny living unit that every living body is made of. *e.g.* Your body has about thirty trillion cells. That is 30 lakh crore.
- **Tissue**: A group of cells of the same kind that are joined together and do one job. *e.g.* Muscle is a tissue. All the cells in a muscle shorten together to pull.
- **Organ**: A body part built from several tissues that work together. *e.g.* Your heart is an organ. It has muscle tissue, nerve tissue and a smooth lining.
- **Gene**: An instruction for building part of a body, passed from parents to their young. *e.g.* Genes called Hox genes mark out a body from head to tail.

## Explanations
### A body plan is the basic layout of a body · *defined*
**Definition:** A body plan is the basic layout of an animal's body: which parts there are, and how they are arranged.

Scientists have named more than a million kinds of animals. Yet almost all of them are built on a few layouts. Three questions sort most animals. Does the body have a front end? Do the left and right sides match? Are there parts that repeat in a row? A jellyfish has no front end, and her parts are arranged in a ring. A fish has a front end and two matching sides. An earthworm has a front end, matching sides and about a hundred repeated rings. A snail has a front end and matching sides, and she carries a shell that coils in a spiral. Humans are animals too. We have a front end, matching sides and a row of repeated bones down the back. This unit takes the plans one at a time. It also looks inside, at the tissues that every plan is built from.
- e.g. Rings: a sea anemone on a rock at Rameswaram.
- e.g. Two halves: a crow, a rohu fish, a house gecko and you.
- e.g. Segments: an earthworm, a centipede and a prawn.
- e.g. A spiral: the shell of a garden snail.
### Bodies built in rings: radial symmetry · *defined*
**Definition:** Radial symmetry means that matching parts are arranged round a middle point, like the spokes of a cycle wheel.

A sea anemone is fixed to a rock. Her mouth is on top, in the middle, with a ring of tentacles round it. Food may drift towards her from any side, and so may danger. So her body is the same all the way round, and every side is ready. A jellyfish has the same plan turned upside down. Her mouth hangs under the middle of the bell, and tentacles hang from the rim. She drifts and pulses, and she meets the water equally on every side. A ring body has a top and a bottom. It has no front, no back, no left and no right. Cut it through the middle along many different lines, and the two halves match. Ring animals have no head. Their nerve cells are spread through the body as a net, so the whole body senses and responds. Corals are ring animals too. Each coral animal, called a polyp, is like a tiny sea anemone sitting in a stone cup.
- e.g. A sea anemone in a rock pool catches a shrimp who touches any tentacle.
- e.g. A moon jellyfish has four matching parts round the middle of her bell.
- e.g. The corals of the Gulf of Mannar and Lakshadweep are built by millions of tiny ring-shaped polyps.
### Bodies built in two halves: bilateral symmetry · *defined*
**Definition:** Bilateral symmetry means that a body has one line down the middle, with a matching left side and right side.

A two-sided body has three pairs of sides. The front end differs from the tail end. The back differs from the belly. Only the left and the right match. So there is exactly one line that cuts the body into matching halves. An animal with this plan moves front end first. The front end meets food, danger and new places before the rest of the body does. So the eyes, the feelers, the nose and the mouth are gathered there, with a brain close behind them. That gathered front end is a head. Worms, insects, spiders, crabs, snails, fish, frogs, birds and mammals all have this plan. Out of every hundred kinds of animals, about ninety-nine are two-sided. The matching is on the outside. Inside a human, the heart leans to the left and the liver lies on the right.
- e.g. A house gecko runs up the wall head first, with two matching legs on each side.
- e.g. A dragonfly has two matching pairs of wings and two huge eyes at the front.
- e.g. You have two eyes, two ears, two arms and two legs, and one nose on the middle line.
### Why animals who travel have matching sides · *debated*
**Definition:** The usual explanation is that matching sides give equal pushes, so the animal travels straight.

An animal moves by pushing on the ground, the water or the air. Legs, fins and wings come in pairs, one on each side. If the left side pushed harder than the right, the animal would curve to the right. She would have to correct her path at every step. With matching sides the pushes are equal, and the path is straight. In the Lopsided Roller test you see this with a pencil and two balls of dough. The roller with equal balls goes nearly straight, and the roller with unequal balls curves. Top and bottom do not match in animals, because gravity pulls down and the ground is on one side only. Front and back do not match, because the animal goes one way. Left and right are the only pair that meet the same conditions. This is the usual explanation. It is hard to test on living animals, so scientists still argue about how two-sided bodies first arose.
- e.g. A rowing boat with one oar goes round in a circle.
- e.g. A fish has a matching fin on each side of her body.
- e.g. Most birds shed the matching feathers of both wings at the same time, so the two wings stay equal.
- **Gábor Holló and Mihály Novák, in a paper of 2012:** Matching sides let an animal travel straight and also turn quickly to either side. Two-sided bodies have lasted because they steer well.
- **John Finnerty, in a paper of 2005:** Two sides may first have helped the inside of the body, by giving food and water a clear path through. Easy travel may have come later.
### A starfish starts with two sides and becomes a star · *defined*
**Definition:** A larva is the young form of an animal, when the young looks very different from the adult.

An adult starfish has five arms round a middle, with the mouth underneath. She has no head that anyone can see, and any arm can lead when she walks. So a starfish looks like a ring animal. But a starfish egg hatches into a tiny, clear larva who swims in the open sea. The larva has a front end and two matching sides. After some weeks a small five-part star grows inside the larva. The star settles on the sea floor, and the rest of the larva's body shrinks away. Sea urchins grow up the same way. This tells us that the ancestors of starfish were two-sided animals, and the star shape came later. The ring of a starfish and the ring of a jellyfish arose separately. Starfish are closer relatives of fish and humans than of jellyfish. So the same shape does not prove that two animals are close relatives. Scientists also compare the young, and they compare the genes.
- e.g. A starfish larva is smaller than a grain of rice and swims with tiny beating hairs.
- e.g. A sea urchin's round shell has five matching parts, and her larva has two matching sides.
- e.g. A dolphin and a shark have the same shape. A dolphin is a mammal, and a shark is a fish.
### Bodies built in segments · *defined*
**Definition:** A segment is one of a row of repeated parts along a body, each built on the same pattern.

An earthworm's body is a row of about a hundred rings. Each ring is a segment with muscles and tiny bristles of its own. The worm squeezes a few segments at a time, and so she moves through the soil. A centipede has one pair of legs on each segment. A millipede has two pairs on most of hers. Repeating has two uses. A body can grow long by making the same part again and again. And a segment can be changed to do a new job. A prawn shows this well. Some of her segments carry feelers, some carry jaws, some carry walking legs and some carry swimming legs. An insect's body is made of segments joined into three parts: head, thorax and abdomen. The thorax is the middle part. It has three segments with one pair of legs each, which is why an insect has six legs. You are built in segments too. Run a finger down your backbone and feel the row of bumps. Each bump is one bone of the row, and your ribs repeat in pairs.
- e.g. A cooked fish breaks into flakes. Each flake is one block of muscle, and the blocks repeat from head to tail.
- e.g. The tail part of a prawn has six segments that you can count at the fish market.
- e.g. A human backbone starts as 33 small bones in a row.
### Bodies with a spiral: the snail's shell · *defined*
**Definition:** A spiral is a line that winds round a middle point and moves further out with every turn.

A snail's soft body has a head, two matching sides and a flat foot. Her shell is different. A snail never leaves her shell, and she never sheds one. As she grows, she adds new shell only at the opening, where her body meets the edge. Each new part is a little wider than the part before it. The shell also curves as it grows, so it winds round and round. The tiny tip in the middle is the shell she hatched with. If the shell grew straight, a big snail would carry a long, tall cone. A coil packs the same room into a low, compact shape, which is easier to carry. In Tall Cone or Coil you test this with dough. Most snail shells coil to the right. Hold a shell with the tip up and the opening facing you. If the opening is on your right, the shell is right-handed. The chank shell, or sangu, blown at temples is the shell of a sea snail. A chank that coils the opposite way is very rare. In Tamil it is called a valampuri sangu, and people prize it.
- e.g. A garden snail on a wet wall in the monsoon carries a shell with four or five turns.
- e.g. The apple snail of paddy fields has a round, right-handed shell.
- e.g. A shaligram stone from the Gandaki river in Nepal holds a coiled ammonite shell. Ammonites were sea animals who died out long ago.
### Sponges: animals with no fixed shape · *defined*
**Definition:** A sponge is an animal who lives fixed in one place. Her body is full of small holes, and she has no head or mouth.

A sponge lives fixed to a rock or a reef. Most sponges grow as a lump, a crust or a vase, and no two are quite alike. Water flows in through thousands of tiny holes and out through a few large ones. Cells lining the inside beat tiny hairs to keep the water moving, and they catch bits of food from it. A sponge has several kinds of cells, but she has no muscles, no nerves and no gut. Even so, a sponge senses what touches her and can slowly squeeze her whole body. In 1907 the American scientist H. V. Wilson pressed a living sponge through fine silk cloth. The sponge came apart into separate cells in a dish of sea water. Over the next days the cells crawled back together and built small new sponges. So each cell can find the others and take up a job again. For a long time people classed sponges with plants. In the 1820s Robert Grant in Edinburgh watched sponges pump water through their bodies, and showed that they are animals.
- e.g. A natural bath sponge is the soft skeleton of a sea sponge.
- e.g. Sponges of many colours grow on the reefs of the Andaman Islands.
- e.g. A sponge the size of a tumbler can pump many buckets of sea water in a day.
### Tissues: groups of cells that do one job together · *defined*
**Definition:** A tissue is a group of cells of the same kind that are joined together and do one job.

Every animal is made of cells, which are the tiny living units of a body. One cell is far too small to see. A human body has about thirty trillion of them, which is 30 lakh crore. Cells of one kind join up to make a tissue. Animals have four main kinds of tissue. Covering tissue makes sheets, such as the outside of your skin and the lining of your mouth. Scientists call it epithelial tissue. Connecting tissue holds parts together and supports them. Bone, fat, blood and the cords called tendons are all connecting tissues. Scientists call them connective tissue. Muscle tissue is made of long cells that can shorten, and it pulls parts so that they move. Nerve tissue carries signals quickly from one part of the body to another. Several tissues together make an organ, such as a heart, a stomach or an eye. So the order is cell, tissue, organ, whole animal. A snail, a prawn and a human are laid out very differently. Yet all three are built from the same four kinds of tissue.
- e.g. The mound at the base of your thumb is muscle tissue. It goes hard when you pinch.
- e.g. The cords that move on the back of your hand are tendons, a connecting tissue.
- e.g. The meat of a fish is muscle tissue, and the skin is covering tissue.
### Why are there so few body plans? · *debated*
**Definition:** Most body plans alive today were already present about 540 million years ago, and scientists argue about why.

Rocks about 540 million years old hold the first clear fossils of most animal groups alive today. This time is called the Cambrian. Animals with rings, with two sides, with segments and with shells were all there. Since then, many kinds of animals have appeared and died out. But almost no new body plan has appeared. In the 1980s scientists also found something surprising. Flies, mice and humans use the same family of genes to mark the body from head to tail. These are called Hox genes. A gene is an instruction for building part of a body, passed from parents to their young. Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard and Eric Wieschaus found many of the genes that lay out a fly's segments. They shared a Nobel Prize in 1995 with Edward Lewis. So very different bodies are laid out by very similar instructions. Why the plans are so few, and so old, is still argued.
- e.g. A fly's Hox genes sit in the same order as the body parts they mark, from head to tail.
- e.g. A mouse has matching genes that mark out her neck, chest and tail in the same order.
- **Scientists who study how bodies grow:** The layout is set very early, when the young animal is only a few cells. A change that early upsets everything built afterwards, so the plan stays.
- **Scientists who study ways of life:** The main ways of making a living were taken long ago. A new plan would have to compete with animals who are already good at each one.
- **Stephen Jay Gould, in his book Wonderful Life (1989):** Chance played a large part. Many early plans died out, and if history ran again a different set might survive.
- **Simon Conway Morris, in his book Life's Solution (2003):** Similar ways of life lead to similar bodies again and again. If history ran again, the plans would turn out much the same.

## Mostly agreed
- Nearly all kinds of animals have a front end and two matching sides. *How we know:* Scientists have named more than a million kinds of insects, spiders, crabs, worms, snails and animals with backbones. All of these are two-sided.
- A starfish begins life as a larva with two matching sides. *How we know:* Johannes Müller watched starfish and sea urchin larvae change into stars in the 1840s. Anyone with a microscope can watch the same change today.
- Animal bodies are made of cells, and the cells are grouped into tissues. *How we know:* Around 1800 the French doctor Xavier Bichat sorted the materials of the body into tissues. In 1839 Theodor Schwann showed with a microscope that animal tissues are made of cells.
- Separated sponge cells can come together again and rebuild a sponge. *How we know:* H. V. Wilson showed this in 1907, and many scientists have repeated the test since.
- Flies, mice and humans use the same family of genes to mark out the body from head to tail. *How we know:* In the 1980s scientists read these Hox genes in flies. They then found matching genes in mice, frogs and humans.
- In pond snails, one gene of the mother decides which way a young snail's shell coils. *How we know:* Breeding tests published in 1923 showed this pattern. In 2019 Reiko Kuroda's team in Japan switched the gene off, and the young snails coiled the opposite way.

## How we found out
**Georges Cuvier in France, then Johannes Müller and Rudolf Leuckart in Germany, Cuvier published his four groups in 1817. Müller studied the larvae in the 1840s, and Leuckart split the ring group in 1848..** In 1817 the French scientist Georges Cuvier sorted all animals into four groups by the layout of their bodies. He put starfish and jellyfish together in one group, called Radiata, because both are built in rings. For about thirty years most scientists agreed that ring-shaped animals were one family. In the 1840s the German scientist Johannes Müller towed a fine net through the sea near the island of Heligoland. He looked at his catch under a microscope. He found tiny, clear swimmers, each with a front end and two matching sides. He kept watching them. Inside each swimmer a small five-part star grew, and the two-sided body shrank away. The swimmers were young starfish and young sea urchins. So a starfish begins life with two sides, as a worm or a fish does. In 1848 Rudolf Leuckart split Cuvier's ring group into separate groups. Today scientists also compare genes. The genes show that starfish are closer relatives of fish and humans than of jellyfish.  
*What people believed before:* That all ring-shaped animals are one family. Müller found that a starfish begins life as a two-sided larva. So the ring shape of a starfish and the ring shape of a jellyfish arose separately.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Starfish and jellyfish belong in one group, because both are built in rings.** (Georges Cuvier, in his book on the animal kingdom of 1817.; *rejected*)  
  For: Both have matching parts round a middle, and neither has a head that anyone can see.  
  Against: A starfish larva has two matching sides, and a small star grows inside the larva. Genes also show that starfish are closer relatives of fish than of jellyfish.
- **Every two-sided animal has matching left and right sides.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Most animals look matched from the outside.  
  Against: A flatfish begins life matched. Then one eye moves across the head, and the adult lies on one side. A male fiddler crab has one huge claw. Inside a human, the heart, stomach and liver are not matched.
- **All animals share one body plan. An insect is laid out like an animal with a backbone, turned upside down.** (Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire, who argued this against Cuvier in Paris in 1830.; *debated*)  
  For: In the 1990s scientists found that the same genes mark belly and back in flies and in frogs. In frogs they work the other way up.  
  Against: Cuvier pointed to real differences. An insect's skeleton is on the outside, and the main nerve cord runs along the belly. Most scientists say the plans are related but are not one plan.
- **All the animal body plans appeared suddenly, about 540 million years ago.** (A common reading of the Cambrian fossils.; *debated*)  
  For: Most animal groups first show up as clear fossils in rocks of about that age.  
  Against: Older rocks hold earlier animals. Ikaria, a two-sided animal the size of a rice grain, lived about 555 million years ago. Scott Evans, Mary Droser and their team described the fossils in 2020. Soft bodies rarely become fossils, so the start may be older still.
- **A starfish has no head.** (Most textbooks, until recently.; *debated*)  
  For: No end of a starfish has eyes, a brain and a mouth together, and any arm can lead.  
  Against: In 2023 Laurent Formery and his team mapped the genes at work in a young starfish. Genes that mark the head in other animals were switched on along every arm. Genes that mark the trunk were mostly missing.

## Roots
In this unit you rolled a lopsided roller, tilted dough shells and found four tissues in your hand. Here we ask what the shape of a body tells us about the life that body leads.

**Deep truth.** In the Lopsided Roller test, the roller with unequal balls curved the same way on every roll. An animal who travels needs equal pushes on her left and right, so those two sides match. Her top and bottom differ, and her front and back differ, because gravity and travel treat them differently. So the layout of a body shows which directions matter in that animal's life.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat an animal's shape as a fact about the animal alone, like a name. The shape also describes the animal's world: where food comes from, which way gravity pulls, and which way she travels.  
*Evidence:* In the Lopsided Roller test, only the roller with matching balls went nearly straight. A sea anemone, who meets food from every side, is the same all the way round. A starfish larva who swims one way has two sides. The adult, who can creep any way, has five arms.

- *who am i:* Your face is at your front because your ancestors travelled head first. Your backbone repeats like the segments of a worm. Your hand holds the same four tissues as a frog's foot.
- *what is life:* Life builds millions of different bodies from a few layouts and four kinds of tissue. Each layout fits one way of meeting the world.
- *what is this world:* Gravity, water and the direction of travel leave their mark on every body. You can read part of an animal's world from the animal's shape.

The first animals were small groups of cells. Their descendants became rings, two-sided swimmers, coiled snails and long rows of segments. Under these different layouts, the same family of genes still marks head from tail in flies, mice and children. Many sages taught that one life takes many forms. The science here says something close and exact. Every animal body plan grew from one beginning, and the shared genes still show it.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** Uddalaka tells his son that by knowing one lump of clay, you know everything made of clay. The pot and the plate differ in name and shape, and the clay is the same. (The verse is about the ground of all things, and it does not mention animals. A prawn's body shows the same idea in a small way. Her feelers, jaws and legs are one repeated part, shaped differently on each segment.)
- **D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson, On Growth and Form (1917), chapter 1:** Thompson wrote that the form of any object, living or not, is a 'diagram of forces'. He meant that a shape shows the pushes and pulls that made it. (The Lopsided Roller test and Tall Cone or Coil both show a shape answering a force. Matching sides answer the need for equal pushes. A low coil answers the pull of gravity on a tall shell.)
- Science (settled): The same family of Hox genes marks out head to tail in flies, mice and humans. This is strong evidence that these very different bodies come from one ancestor.
- Science (debated): A study of starfish genes in 2023 suggested that a starfish's body is mostly a head, with almost no trunk. Scientists are still testing this.
- Other view, Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin, in a paper of 1979: They warned against explaining every feature of a body by its usefulness. Some features are left over from ancestors, and some are side effects of how a body grows. On this view a starfish's five arms may have no special reason. We should not read a purpose into every shape.
- Practice, Find your middle line (3 min): Stand still and close your eyes. Put one finger on the top of your head. Draw it slowly down your forehead, your nose, your chin and your chest to your navel. That is your middle line. Now lift both arms and feel that they weigh the same. Take one slow breath in and out. Then turn once round on the spot, slowly. Notice that you must turn to face each wall. A sea anemone never needs to turn.
- Ask: Your face is at your front because your ancestors travelled head first. Where might your eyes be if humans had always lived fixed in one spot?
- Ask: In the Lopsided Roller test, the shape of the roller decided the path. How much of what you do is decided by the shape of your body?
- Ask: A starfish has two sides when young and five arms when grown. Which shape is the starfish?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A starfish larva has two sides, and the adult is a star. Are they the same animal? (hint: Think about what stays the same when a body changes its whole layout.)
- *deep:* Your left and right sides match on the outside but not on the inside. Which is your real plan? (hint: Your two arms match, and yet your heart leans to the left.)
- *big:* If animals lived where there was no up or down, what body plans might they have? (hint: On Earth, gravity makes the top differ from the bottom in almost every animal.)
- *deep:* Is a body plan a real thing in nature, or a way that people sort animals? (hint: Cuvier's group of ring animals looked right for thirty years. Then starfish larvae showed that it was two groups.)
- *big:* Cars, boats and planes have matching sides. Did people copy animals, or did they meet the same problem? (hint: In the Lopsided Roller test, the roller with unequal balls curved to one side on every roll.)
- *deep:* Each of your cells is alive. So are you one living being, or trillions living together? (hint: H. V. Wilson's sponge cells lived apart in a dish, and then rebuilt a sponge.)

## Story: The Boat with No Front
*Ponni, her grandmother and her cousin are made up. These facts are real: round bamboo boats called parisal, or coracles, are used on the Kaveri river at Hogenakkal in Tamil Nadu. A parisal is woven from split bamboo and covered underneath with a tarred sheet. The paddler kneels at the rim and pulls the water towards her, and any part of the rim can lead.*

Ponni was ten, and she was ashamed of her grandmother's boat.

It was a parisal, as round as a dosa pan and wide enough for six people. Paati had woven it herself from split bamboo and covered the bottom with a tarred sheet. It had no pointed end and no back end. It had no left and no right. It sat on the Kaveri at Hogenakkal like a basket that someone had dropped.

In the summer holidays Ponni had stayed with her cousin Devika near Alappuzha. The boats there were long and narrow, with a raised nose at one end. They went down the canals as straight as a line of thread.

"A boat should know which way it is going," Ponni said when she came home.

Paati did not argue. She held out the paddle. "Take us across, then."

So every evening, in the slow green water above the falls, Ponni practised. She knelt at the rim and pulled the paddle through the water on her right. The parisal turned to the right, and went on turning. She paddled on her left. The parisal turned to the left. In a quarter of an hour she had made nine circles and travelled about the length of a goat.

"It only spins," she said.

"It spins because you paddle beside it," said Paati. "Reach out in front of your knees and pull the water straight towards you. Then the place where you kneel is the front."

Ponni tried. The parisal wobbled forwards, slowly, like a plate being pushed across a table. It was better than spinning. But she thought of Devika's long boat cutting down the canal, and she was not comforted.

Then the rain came to the hills upstream. For two days the Kaveri ran brown and quick. On the third morning Paati said the fish traps had to be lifted before the river took them.

The traps were tied among the black rocks below the bathing place. There the river split into narrow channels, and the water did not agree with itself. It pushed from the left in one place and from the right in the next. Paati knelt at the rim and worked the parisal into a channel hardly wider than the boat. Rock stood on both sides, close enough to touch.

At the end of the channel the first trap hung on its rope. Paati put down the paddle and took the rope in both hands. The trap was heavy with water and weed. She leaned back and hauled.

Ponni looked back the way they had come. A tree branch as long as a bus was coming down the channel towards them. It turned slowly as it came, and its wet twigs scraped along the rock.

"Paati," she said.

"I see it. My hands are full. Take us out by the side gap, there, behind you."

Ponni snatched up the paddle. The side gap was behind her left shoulder. She did what anyone on Devika's canal would do. She began to turn the boat round, to point its front at the gap.

The rim struck rock. She pushed off and tried the other way. The rim struck rock again. There was no room to turn anything. The branch was four boat-lengths away, then three.

Ponni stared at the round rim of the parisal. Every part of it looked the same. There was nothing to point.

Then she understood that there was nothing to turn, either.

She let the boat be. She crawled across the bamboo floor to the part of the rim nearest the gap, knelt there, reached the paddle out in front of her knees and pulled. The parisal did not swing round. It simply went that way, as if that part of the rim had always been the front. They slid sideways out of the channel and into the gap. Behind them the branch went past with a long scraping sound, and the water closed over the place where they had been.

Paati dropped the trap into the boat. Three small fish jumped inside the trap. She looked at Ponni for a moment and then went back to coiling the rope.

That evening the river was quieter. Ponni sat on the steps with her feet in the water. Near her toes a row of small fish held their place in the current. Every one of them pointed the same way, nose to the flow, and the two sides of each fish matched like the two sides of Devika's boat. When a shadow crossed them they shot off upstream, straight and fast, all in one direction.

A leaf came down the river, turning. Then the parisal came past, with Paati kneeling at the rim, bringing a neighbour's children home. A boy in the boat dropped his cap in the water on the far side. Paati did not turn the boat. She moved along the rim and pulled, and the parisal went to the cap.

On Sunday Devika telephoned. "Does your round boat still not know where it is going?" she asked.

"It does not need to know," said Ponni. "I know. And wherever I kneel, that is the front."

**For the parent:** A long boat has one front. It goes fast and straight, but it needs room to turn. A round boat is the same on every side, so any part of the rim can lead. Animal bodies follow the same two plans. A fish is built to go one way, head first. A sea anemone or a jellyfish is the same all the way round. She is ready on every side at once.

- Ask: Why could the round boat leave the narrow channel without turning round?
- Ask: Which animals are built like the long boat, and which like the round boat?
- Ask: When would a body with no front be more useful than a body with a head?

### g2 · Sit or Seek: a dice game of two bodies in two seas (15 min)
- **rules:** You need one die, 12 rajma beans or groundnuts as food, and two dough animals. The ring animal is a round ball. The front animal has a pointed head.; There are four rounds of 12 turns. Every round starts with the animal on the middle square.; Drifting sea. Each turn, roll the die. A 1 brings food from N, a 2 from E, a 3 from S and a 4 from W. A 5 or a 6 brings no food.; The ring animal has arms on every side. She eats every piece of food that arrives.; The front animal has one mouth, at her head. She eats only food that arrives on the side she faces. After each roll she may turn to face a new side.; Still sea. Put 8 beans on the board: one on each corner square, and one on the middle square of each edge. No die is needed for the front animal.; In the still sea, the front animal takes one action each turn. She either steps one square forwards or turns to face a new side. She eats a bean when she lands on that square.; In the still sea, the ring animal creeps slowly. Roll the die each turn. Only on a 6 may she move one square, in any direction.; Write each score on the score sheet. With two players, each plays all four rounds, and the higher total wins.
- **Printable board: The sea: a grid you draw:** Draw 5 rows of 5 squares on a sheet of paper. Make each square about 4 centimetres wide.; Mark the middle square with a star. Every round starts there.; Write N on the top edge and E on the right edge. Write S on the bottom edge and W on the left edge.
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Drifting sea, ring animal: food eaten in 12 turns ____; Drifting sea, front animal: food eaten in 12 turns ____; Still sea, ring animal: food eaten in 12 turns ____; Still sea, front animal: food eaten in 12 turns ____
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the grid and make the two dough animals. Read the rules together. Ask for a guess before the first roll.  
  *Parent says:* One animal is round, with arms on every side. The other has a head and one mouth. Which will eat more when food drifts in from all sides? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the grid. Make two dough animals. Which will eat more? Why?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the drifting sea. Play 12 turns with the ring animal, then 12 turns with the front animal. Write both scores.  
  *Parent says:* Roll the die. Which side did the food come from? Did your animal catch the food? For the front animal, choose which way to face next.  
  *Child mode:* Roll 12 times for each animal. Write how much each one ate.
  - Note: The ring animal usually eats about 8 pieces in 12 turns. The front animal usually eats about 2. Luck will move these numbers up or down.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the still sea. Put out the 8 beans. Play 12 turns with the front animal, then 12 turns with the ring animal. Write both scores.  
  *Parent says:* Now the food does not come to you. Plan a path for the front animal. Remember that a turn uses up one action.  
  *Child mode:* Put out 8 beans. Play 12 turns for each animal. Write both scores.
  - Note: A front animal with a good path eats 3 or 4 beans. The ring animal usually eats 0 or 1.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the four scores together. Ask which body won in each sea.  
  *Parent says:* Which body ate more in the drifting sea? Which ate more in the still sea? Where do real sea anemones live, and where do real fish hunt?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your four scores. Which body won in each sea?
  - Check: In Sit or Seek, which sea suits an animal who is the same on every side? → **The sea where food drifts in from all sides**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Lopsided Roller: do matching sides help a body go straight? (30 min)
- **question:** A roller is made of a pencil with a ball of dough on each end. Does it roll straight when the two balls match? What happens when one ball is smaller than the other?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which roller will go straightest? Will a lopsided roller turn towards the big ball or towards the small ball? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same dough and pencils of the same length for all three rollers. Push each pencil about 1 centimetre into each ball. Start every roll from the same line on the same ramp, and let go without pushing. Keep the small ball on the right for the main rolls. Roll each roller 5 times, because one roll can be a fluke. Only one thing changes: how well the two balls match.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows: matched roller, a little lopsided, very lopsided. For each roll, write how far from the line the roller stopped, and on which side. If the roller went right round, write 'circle'.
- **results table:** Roller | Roll 1 to roll 5: how many centimetres from the line, and on which side | What the path looked like
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a pencil and two lumps of dough, one large and one small. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will put a ball of dough on each end of a pencil and let it roll. Which roller will go straightest? Will a lopsided one turn towards the big ball or the small ball? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which roller goes straightest. Which way will a lopsided roller turn? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think it will turn that way? (good: The big ball is heavier.; The big ball goes further each time it turns.; The small ball is slower.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Many children expect the roller to turn towards the big ball.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make four balls 4 centimetres wide, one ball 3 centimetres wide and one ball 2 centimetres wide. Check each against the ruler. Push a pencil about 1 centimetre into each ball to make the three rollers. Dust the balls with dry flour.  
  *Parent says:* Roll each ball between your palms until it is round. Hold it beside the ruler to check the size. The two balls of the matched roller must be as equal as you can make them.  
  *Child mode:* Roll six balls of dough. Check each size with the ruler. Push them on to the pencils.
  - Note: Stiff dough works best. If the dough is sticky, knead in more dry atta. For the matched roller, make one lump and cut it exactly in half. That gives two equal balls.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rest one end of the tray on the thin book. Draw a chalk line on the floor, straight out from the middle of the low end, about 1 metre long. Mark a start line across the top of the ramp.  
  *Parent says:* This line shows where a roller would go if it rolled perfectly straight. We will start every roll from the same place.  
  *Child mode:* Make the ramp. Draw a straight line on the floor from the ramp.
  - Note: A line between two rows of floor tiles can be the straight line. If the rollers stop too soon, raise the ramp on a second book.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the matched roller across the start line, with its middle over the chalk line. Let go. Where it stops, measure from its middle to the chalk line. Write the distance and the side. Do this 5 times.  
  *Parent says:* Let go gently, with no push. Where did the middle of the roller stop? How far is that from the line, and on which side?  
  *Child mode:* Roll the matched roller 5 times. Measure how far from the line it stops.
  - Note: The matched roller usually stops within about 10 centimetres of the line, sometimes left and sometimes right. No hand-made roller is perfectly matched.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Roll the roller with the 4 centimetre and 3 centimetre balls 5 times, with the small ball on the right. Measure and write each result. Then do the same with the 4 centimetre and 2 centimetre roller.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the path. Is it a straight line or a curve? Which ball is on the inside of the curve?  
  *Child mode:* Roll the two lopsided rollers 5 times each. Watch which way they curve.
  - Note: The 4 and 3 roller makes a wide curve to the right. The 4 and 2 roller turns in a tight circle and may come back towards the ramp. Write 'circle' for that.
  - Check: In the Lopsided Roller test, which way did the roller with unequal balls curve? → **Towards the side with the small ball**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the 4 and 3 roller round, so that the small ball is on the left. Roll it 3 times. Then add dough to the small ball until it is 4 centimetres wide, and roll it 3 more times.  
  *Parent says:* Now the small ball is on the left. Which way will it curve this time? Now we make the small ball match the big one. What will happen?  
  *Child mode:* Put the small ball on the left. Roll it. Then make both balls match and roll again.
  - Note: With the small ball on the left, the roller curves left. That shows the curve comes from the roller and not from the floor. Once the balls match, the roller goes nearly straight again.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your results. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then tell me: why might a fish need two matching sides?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Why did the lopsided roller curve? (good: The big ball goes further in one turn.; One side moved faster than the other.; The two sides were not equal.)
  - Ask: What would happen to a fish whose left fins pushed harder than her right fins? (good: She would swim in a curve.; She would keep turning to the right.; She would have to keep correcting her path.)
  - Note: The reason: both balls are fixed to one pencil, so they turn together. In one turn a 4 centimetre ball rolls about 12 and a half centimetres along the floor. A 3 centimetre ball rolls only about 9 and a half. So the big ball's side gets ahead, and the roller curves towards the small ball. An animal's legs, fins or wings work in the same way. If one side pushes more than the other, the animal curves. With matching sides, the pushes are equal and the path is straight. The roller is a model, and a living animal can also correct her own path.
  - Think: A rowing boat has two oars. What happens if only one person rows, on one side?
### e2 · Tall Cone or Coil: which shell is easier to carry as it grows? (25 min)
- **question:** A snail's shell must get bigger as the snail grows. If the shell grew as a straight cone, would a taller cone fall over more easily? Does the same amount of shell stay on better when it is coiled?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Three dough cones are 6, 9 and 12 centimetres tall. Which will fall over first when the board is tilted? Will a coil made from the same dough as the tallest cone fall sooner or later? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Make every cone 3 centimetres wide at the base, and press each base flat. Stand each cone at the same spot on the same board. Lift the same end of the board slowly each time, about one finger-width each second. Measure the height of the lifted end at the moment the cone falls. Test each shape 3 times. For the coil, use exactly the dough of the 12 centimetre cone. Only the shape changes.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows: the 6 centimetre cone, the 9 centimetre cone, the 12 centimetre cone and the coil. Write the height of the lifted end for each of the three tries. If a shape did not fall, write 'did not fall' and the height you reached.
- **results table:** Shape | Height of the lifted end when the shape fell: try 1, try 2 and try 3 | The middle value of the three tries
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a snail shell or a picture of one, and a lump of dough. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* A snail's shell is a long cone that is wound into a coil. We will test straight cones and a coil on a tilted board. Which will fall first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which dough shell will fall first on a tilted board. Why?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make three dough cones, 6, 9 and 12 centimetres tall. Make each base 3 centimetres wide. Press each base on the table so that it is flat and the cone stands upright.  
  *Parent says:* Shape each cone like a carrot standing on its thick end. Check the height and the base with the ruler.  
  *Child mode:* Make three dough cones: 6, 9 and 12 centimetres tall. Flatten each base.
  - Note: If the tallest cone bends over, the dough is too soft. Knead in more dry atta and shape it again.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the 6 centimetre cone in the middle of the board. Your child lifts one end slowly. You hold the ruler upright at that end. Read the height when the cone falls. Do this 3 times. Then test the 9 centimetre cone and the 12 centimetre cone in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Lift slowly, about one finger-width each second. Stop the moment the cone tips. I will read the height.  
  *Child mode:* Lift one end of the board slowly. Stop when the cone falls. Write the height.
  - Note: If a cone slides before it falls, lay a sheet of paper on the board and try again. The shortest cone needs the steepest tilt.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take the 12 centimetre cone. Roll it into a long thin cone about 25 centimetres long. Wind it into a flat coil, with the thin tip in the middle. Lay the coil flat on the board and tilt the board 3 times.  
  *Parent says:* This coil is made of exactly the same dough as the tallest cone. Lift the board again. Does the coil fall at the same height?  
  *Child mode:* Roll the tallest cone long and thin. Wind it into a coil. Tilt the board again.
  - Note: The coil usually stays in place until the board is very steep, and then it slides. Write 'did not fall' and the height you reached.
  - Check: In Tall Cone or Coil, the coil was made from the dough of the tallest cone. Why? → **So that only the shape was different**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each shape, circle the middle value of the three tries. Put the four shapes in order, from the one that fell first to the one that fell last.  
  *Parent says:* Which shape fell at the lowest tilt? Which stayed on longest? What happened as the cones got taller?  
  *Child mode:* Circle the middle value for each shape. Which shape fell first?
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then tell me: what would a straight shell be like for a big, old snail?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Why does a taller cone fall over at a smaller tilt? (good: More of it is high up.; It leans past its base sooner.; The base is the same but the cone is taller.)
  - Ask: Why does the coil stay on? (good: It is low and wide.; All the dough is close to the board.)
  - Note: In most homes the 12 centimetre cone falls first and the 6 centimetre cone last. The coil does not fall at all. The reason: a tall cone has much of its weight high above a small base. A small tilt carries that weight past the edge of the base, and the cone falls. A coil keeps the same amount of shell low and close to the board. A snail's shell grows only at the opening, so a straight shell would become a longer and longer cone. Scientists think that coiling keeps a growing shell compact and easy to carry.
  - Think: A snail adds shell only at the opening. Where on the shell is the part she made as a baby?

### a1 · Three Pairs of Sides: which sides of a body match? (10 min)
- **record:** A table with three rows: me, another animal, and the tumbler. It has three columns: top and bottom, front and back, left and right. Write 'match' or 'differ' in each box.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the table. Your child stands up and compares three pairs of sides of their own body: top and bottom, front and back, left and right.  
  *Parent says:* Compare your head end with your feet. Do they match? Now compare your front with your back. Now compare your left side with your right side.  
  *Child mode:* Compare your top and bottom. Then front and back. Then left and right.
  - Ask: Which pair matches? (good: Only left and right.; My two arms and two legs match.; My front and back are different.)
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find one other animal: a crow or pigeon outside, an ant, a gecko, a fly or a pet. Check the same three pairs. If no animal is near, use a toy animal or a picture on a packet.  
  *Parent says:* Look at this animal's three pairs of sides. Which pair matches? Where are the eyes and the mouth?  
  *Child mode:* Find another animal. Check the three pairs of sides. Where are the eyes?
  - Note: Humans count as animals, so a family member will do. Every animal your child can find at home will have only left and right matching.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the tumbler upside down. Explain that a jellyfish is shaped like this, with the mouth under the middle and tentacles round the rim. Check the three pairs on the tumbler.  
  *Parent says:* This tumbler stands for a jellyfish. Does the top match the bottom? Turn it slowly. Can you find a front, a back, a left or a right?  
  *Child mode:* Turn a tumbler upside down. Does it have a front? Does it have a left side?
  - Note: The tumbler has a top and a bottom, but it is the same all the way round. That is radial symmetry. Every animal your child checked had only one matching pair, left and right. That is bilateral symmetry.
  - Check: A crow has a front and a back. Which pair of a crow's sides match each other? → **Left and right**
### a2 · Four Tissues in Your Hand (15 min)
- **record:** Trace round your hand on paper. Label where you found each of the four kinds of tissue: covering, muscle, connecting and nerve.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the back of the hand closely, with a magnifying glass if you have one. Put one drop of water on the skin and watch it.  
  *Parent says:* Your skin is a sheet that covers you. Look at the fine lines. Does the drop of water soak in or sit on top? A tissue that covers a surface is called covering tissue.  
  *Child mode:* Look closely at your skin. Put a drop of water on it. What does the drop do?
  - Note: The drop sits on top. The outer skin is many layers of flat cells packed closely together, and it keeps water out.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child rests one hand on the table, palm up. With the other hand, they press the mound at the base of the thumb. Then they pinch thumb and first finger together hard.  
  *Parent says:* Press the soft mound at the base of your thumb. Now pinch hard. What happens to the mound? That is muscle tissue. Its cells have all shortened at once.  
  *Child mode:* Press the mound below your thumb. Now pinch hard. What do you feel?
  - Ask: What changed when you pinched? (good: It went hard.; It got fatter and firmer.; It bulged.)
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child holds the hand flat, palm down, and wiggles the fingers as if playing a keyboard. Look for cords moving under the skin. Then press a knuckle and feel the bone.  
  *Parent says:* See the cords that move? They are tendons. Each one ties a muscle in your arm to a finger bone. Tendons and bones are connecting tissues.  
  *Child mode:* Wiggle your fingers. Watch the cords on the back of your hand. Press a knuckle.
  - Note: The muscles that move the fingers are mostly in the forearm. Ask your child to hold their forearm and wiggle the fingers, and they will feel those muscles working.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child closes their eyes. Touch one fingertip very lightly with the feather or thread. Ask which finger was touched. Then press on a fingernail for 3 seconds and let go.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes closed. Which finger did I touch? You knew at once, because nerve tissue carried a signal to your brain. Now watch your nail. It goes white, then pink again, as the blood comes back.  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Which finger was touched? Then press your nail and watch the colour.
  - Note: Blood is a connecting tissue too, although it is liquid. Its cells float in a watery fluid and travel to every part of the body.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Trace round the hand on paper. Label the four tissues and where each was found.  
  *Parent says:* Label your drawing: covering tissue, muscle tissue, connecting tissue and nerve tissue. Which did you see, and which did you only feel?  
  *Child mode:* Trace your hand. Label where you found each of the four tissues.
  - Check: In Four Tissues in Your Hand, the mound at the base of your thumb went firm when you pinched. Which tissue is it? → **Muscle tissue**
### a3 · One Segment, Many Times: a paper chain animal (15 min)
- **record:** Keep the paper animal. On the back, write what job you gave to each changed segment.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fold the paper strip backwards and forwards, like a fan, into 8 equal parts. Press the folds flat.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to build a long animal by drawing only one part. How many times do you think we will need to cut?  
  *Child mode:* Fold the strip like a fan into 8 equal parts.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On the top layer, draw an oval that touches both folded edges. Add one leg above and one leg below. Cut round the drawing through all the layers. Leave the folds joined where the oval touches them.  
  *Parent says:* Draw one segment with one pair of legs. Now cut through all the layers at once. Keep the two sides of the oval joined at the folds.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one oval with two legs. Cut it out. Do not cut the folds.
  - Note: Eight layers of notebook paper are stiff to cut. Help with the scissors if your child's hand gets tired.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Open the paper out. Count the segments and the legs.  
  *Parent says:* You drew one segment. How many did you get? How many legs? An earthworm grows a long body in this way, by making one kind of part again and again.  
  *Child mode:* Open the paper. Count the segments. Count the legs.
  - Ask: You drew one pair of legs. How many legs does the animal have? (good: 16 legs.; 8 pairs, because there are 8 segments.)
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Change some segments. Draw eyes and long feelers on the first one and trim its legs short. Snip the last one into a tail fan. Colour a few middle segments as walking legs and a few as swimming legs.

### c1 · Many Threads, One Lift: build a model tendon from sewing thread (20 min)
- **problem:** A tendon ties a muscle to a bone. It is a tissue made of thousands of thin fibres lying side by side. One fibre alone is weak. Your problem is the same. One sewing thread snaps when it lifts a bag that weighs 2 kilograms. Lift the bag with thread alone, using as few threads as you can.
- **your turn:** Now design your own. Find the smallest number of threads that holds the bag for 10 seconds. Draw how you arranged them. Then test one change, such as twisting, and write whether you needed fewer threads.
- **constraints:** Use ordinary sewing thread only. No string, wool or tape.; Each thread is 30 centimetres long before you tie it.; The bag must hang 2 centimetres above the floor for 10 seconds.; Use at most 10 threads.; Lift slowly. Do not jerk the bag.
- **general:** Tie several threads side by side between the rolling pin and the bag's handles.; Make every thread the same length, so that each one takes a share of the weight.; Spread the threads along the rolling pin so that they do not rub on each other.
- **creative:** Twist the threads together into one cord, as fibres are twisted in a rope.; Plait three groups of threads, as hair is plaited.; Tie short threads in two rows, so that if one thread snaps the others still hold.
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put the packet in the bag. Tie one 30 centimetre thread from the rolling pin to both handles. Your child lifts the rolling pin slowly until the bag is just off the floor.  
  *Parent says:* A tendon is made of thousands of thin fibres. Here one thread stands for one fibre. Will one thread hold the bag? Lift slowly, and keep your feet back.  
  *Child mode:* Tie one thread to the bag. Lift slowly. Does one thread hold?
  - Note: Most sewing thread snaps at about 1 kilogram or less, and a knot makes it weaker. If one thread does hold, add another kilogram to the bag. Keep the bag close to the floor so that it cannot fall far.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tie 3 threads side by side and test. If they snap, try 4, then 5. Each time, look at which thread snapped first.  
  *Parent says:* Try three threads. Did they all snap together, or did one go first? Look at the lengths. Which thread was tightest?  
  *Child mode:* Tie three threads side by side. Lift. Which thread snaps first?
  - Ask: Why did one thread snap before the others? (good: It was shorter, so it took all the weight.; The others were loose.; They were not sharing the weight.)
  - Note: The shortest thread goes tight first and carries the whole bag for a moment. It snaps, and then the next shortest does the same. Equal lengths are what make the threads share.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let your child choose their own design. They may match the lengths carefully, twist the threads or plait them. Test each design for 10 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Now use your own idea. How will you make every thread take a share? Count ten seconds aloud while the bag hangs.  
  *Child mode:* Build your own design. Lift the bag. Count to ten.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the smallest number of threads that held. Draw the design. Then ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* How many threads did you need? Your tendons work in the same way. Thousands of thin fibres lie side by side, all the same way, and share the pull.  
  *Child mode:* Write how many threads held the bag. Draw your design.
  - Check: In Many Threads, One Lift, one thread snapped but several equal threads held the bag. How is that like a tissue? → **Many alike cells share one job**

### Rings, halves, spirals and segments (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a body plan?  
   Options: The basic layout of an animal's body / A list of what an animal eats / The place where an animal lives / The colour of an animal's skin  
   **Answer:** The basic layout of an animal's body. *Why:* A body plan says which parts a body has and how they are arranged. Two animals with very different colours and homes can share one body plan.
2. (mcq) Which animal has a body built in rings round a middle point?  
   Options: A sea anemone / A crow / An earthworm / A house gecko  
   **Answer:** A sea anemone. *Why:* A sea anemone has a ring of tentacles round one mouth. A crow, an earthworm and a gecko each have a head end and two matching sides.
3. (fill) An animal with one head end and matching left and right sides has ____ symmetry.  
   **Answer:** ['bilateral']. *Why:* Bilateral symmetry means two matching sides. There is exactly one line that cuts such a body into matching halves.
4. (truefalse) A jellyfish has a left side and a right side.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A jellyfish is the same all the way round, so no part of her is a left or a right. She has a top and a bottom only.
5. (match) Match each animal to their body plan.  
   **Answer:** Jellyfish → Rings round a middle; Crow → Two matching halves with a backbone; Earthworm → A long row of segments; Garden snail → A spiral shell. *Why:* Ask three questions. Does the body have a front end? Do the sides match? Do parts repeat in a row? The snail also carries a coiled shell.
6. (sort) Sort these animals. Does each one have a head end?  
   **Answer:** fish → Has a head end; ant → Has a head end; frog → Has a head end; jellyfish → Has no head end; sea anemone → Has no head end; sponge → Has no head end. *Why:* An animal who travels front end first has eyes and a mouth gathered at that end. Animals who drift or stay fixed have no front, so they have no head.
7. (mcq) In the Lopsided Roller test, the roller with one big ball and one small ball curved. Why?  
   Options: The big ball rolls further each turn / The small ball is heavier / The floor slopes to one side / The pencil is too long  
   **Answer:** The big ball rolls further each turn. *Why:* Both balls turn together on one pencil. In one turn the big ball covers more floor than the small ball. So the big ball's side gets ahead, and the roller curves.
8. (truefalse) Insects, prawns and centipedes all have bodies built in segments.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. All three have a row of repeated parts. In an insect the segments are joined into a head, a thorax and an abdomen.
9. (order) Put these in order, from the smallest to the largest.  
   **Answer:** ['a cell', 'a tissue', 'an organ', 'a whole animal']. *Why:* Cells of one kind make a tissue. Several tissues make an organ. Many organs make a whole animal.
10. (fill) A group of cells of the same kind that do one job together is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['tissue']. *Why:* Muscle, nerve, covering tissue and connecting tissue are the four main kinds of tissue in animals.
11. (mcq) Where does a snail add new shell as she grows?  
   Options: At the rim of the opening / At the tip / All over the outside / Nowhere. She finds a new shell  
   **Answer:** At the rim of the opening. *Why:* A snail's body touches the shell at the opening, so new shell is added there. The tip is the oldest part. A snail never changes shells.
12. (write) Explain in two sentences why a sea anemone has tentacles all round her body.  
   **Answer:** A sea anemone is fixed to a rock, and food can drift towards her from any side. Tentacles all round mean that every side is ready to catch it.. *Why:* A strong answer links the ring shape to a life in one place. There, food and danger come from every side.
13. (write) In Tall Cone or Coil, the tall dough cone fell over first. What does this suggest about snail shells?  
   **Answer:** A straight shell would become a tall cone as the snail grew, and the cone would tip over easily. A coiled shell keeps the same amount of shell low and steady.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the result of the test: the taller the cone, the sooner it fell, and the coil stayed on.
14. (draw) Draw an ant from above. Draw the one line that cuts the ant into matching halves.  
   **Answer:** An ant seen from above. One straight line runs from the head to the tail end, through the middle of the body. Three legs and one feeler are on each side of the line.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the line from head to tail. A line across the middle is wrong, because the front half and the back half do not match.
15. (mcq) When you bend your thumb, which tissue is pulling?  
   Options: Muscle / Bone / Skin / Nail  
   **Answer:** Muscle. *Why:* Muscle tissue is the only tissue whose cells can shorten and pull. Bone is pulled, and skin covers the muscle.
### Why bodies are laid out this way (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) An animal lives fixed to a rock, and food drifts in from every side. Which plan suits her best?

   **Answer:** The front meets new things first. *Why:* An animal who travels meets food and danger with her front end first. Senses placed there give the earliest warning.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A starfish larva has a front end and two matching sides. The five-part star grows inside the larva later.

   **Answer:** Muscle tissue → Shortens to pull a part; Nerve tissue → Carries signals quickly; Covering tissue → Makes a sheet over a surface; Connecting tissue → Holds parts together and supports them. *Why:* Each tissue is a group of alike cells with one job.    **Answer:** A snail adds new shell only at the opening. So the first shell she made is pushed further and further from the opening. 12. (write) A sponge has many cells but no muscles or nerves. Name one thing a sponge can still do.  
   **Answer:** A sponge can pump water through her body and catch food from it. She can also sense a touch and slowly squeeze her body. Her separated cells can come back together.. *Why:* A strong answer gives something sponges are known to do. Pumping water, sensing touch and rebuilding from separate cells all count.
13. (perspective) A starfish has five arms and no head that anyone can see. Scientists describe her body in different ways.

14. (label) Label four sides of this fish: front end, tail end, back and belly.  
   **Answer:** ['Front end: where the mouth and eyes are', 'Tail end: where the tail fin is', 'Back: the upper side, towards the surface', 'Belly: the lower side, towards the river bed']. *Why:* A two-sided body has a front and a tail that differ, and a back and a belly that differ. ### Testing ideas about body plans (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Two animals share a ring shape. What is the best way to check whether they are close relatives?  
   Options: Compare their young and their genes / Compare their colours / Compare where they live / Count their arms  
   **Answer:** Compare their young and their genes. *Why:* Shape alone can mislead, as it did with starfish and jellyfish. Young forms and genes carry more of an animal's family history.

   Options: Repeated parts can change for different jobs / Every segment must stay the same / Segments are only for bending / Prawns have too many legs

## Applications
- Boats, cars, cycles and aeroplanes are built with matching left and right sides, so that they travel straight.
- A train is built in segments. Coaches of one design are joined in a row, and a coach can be added or taken off.
- Doctors look at a tiny piece of tissue under a microscope to find out whether the cells are healthy. This is called a biopsy.
- Doctors can grow sheets of skin tissue from a few of a patient's own cells, to cover a bad burn.
- Engineers build robots in segments, copied from worms and snakes, to crawl through pipes and fallen buildings.
- A ceiling fan, an umbrella and a parisal are built in a ring. Each must work the same way on every side.

## Open questions
- **Why do most starfish have five arms?** Five-part bodies are very rare outside the starfish and sea urchin group. Nobody knows what first fixed the number at five, or whether five has any special use.
- **Why do almost all snails coil to the right?** Scientists have found a gene that sets the direction of the coil in pond snails. They do not know why the right-handed form became so common in nearly every kind of snail.
- **What did the first two-sided animal look like?** The animal lived more than 550 million years ago and was small and soft. Very few fossils of such animals exist, so scientists argue from genes and from a handful of traces in rock.
- **Which animals are the oldest branch of the animal family tree, sponges or comb jellies?** Comb jellies have nerves and muscles, and sponges have neither. A gene study in 2023 put comb jellies on the oldest branch, and other studies put sponges there. The answer changes how we think nerves began.
- **Is a starfish mostly a head?** A gene study in 2023 found head genes at work along every arm of a young starfish. Scientists are still checking this in other kinds of starfish and in sea urchins.

## Connected maths
- Here are made-up results from the Lopsided Roller test, in centimetres from the line. The matched roller stopped at 6 left, 4 right, 9 right, 3 left and 5 left. The lopsided roller stopped at 38, 45, 41, 36 and 40, all to the right. Does the matched roller have a favourite side? Does the lopsided roller? **The matched roller has no favourite side. The lopsided roller always goes right.**. The matched roller went left 3 times and right 2 times, and never more than 9 centimetres. That is what chance looks like. The lopsided roller went right all 5 times, by about 40 centimetres. Five out of five on one side, and far from the line, points to a cause in the roller. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In a made-up run of Tall Cone or Coil, the 6 centimetre cone fell at a lift of 18 centimetres. The 9 centimetre cone fell at 14 centimetres, and the 12 centimetre cone at 10 centimetres. What is the pattern? Predict the lift for a 15 centimetre cone. **Each extra 3 centimetres of cone makes it fall 4 centimetres sooner. A 15 centimetre cone should fall at about 6 centimetres.**. 18 minus 14 is 4, and 14 minus 10 is 4. So the next step is 10 minus 4, which is 6. This is a prediction from a pattern. Real cones may not follow it exactly, so the next thing to do is to test it. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A house centipede has 15 segments with legs, and one pair of legs on each. How many legs does she have? Every centipede has an odd number of pairs of legs. Can any centipede have exactly 100 legs? **The house centipede has 30 legs. No centipede has exactly 100 legs.**. 15 pairs is 15 times 2, which is 30 legs. For 100 legs a centipede would need 100 divided by 2, which is 50 pairs. But 50 is an even number, and centipedes always have an odd number of pairs. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A moon jellyfish has 4 matching parts round her middle. One kind of coral polyp has 8 tentacles round the mouth. A full turn is 360 degrees. How far must each animal be turned to look the same as before? **The jellyfish 90 degrees, and the coral polyp 45 degrees.**. 360 divided by 4 is 90. 360 divided by 8 is 45. The more matching parts a ring animal has, the smaller the turn that brings her back to the same look. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- In a made-up game of Sit or Seek, the ring animal ate 8 pieces of drifting food in 12 turns. The front animal ate 2 in 12 turns. What fraction of the turns did each animal eat on? How many times as much did the ring animal eat? **The ring animal ate on two thirds of the turns and the front animal on one sixth. The ring animal ate 4 times as much.**. 8 out of 12 is the same as 2 out of 3. 2 out of 12 is the same as 1 out of 6. 8 divided by 2 is 4. The ring animal has four sides ready and the front animal has one. So four times as much is what the rules lead us to expect. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In a made-up run of Many Threads, One Lift, one thread snapped at about 500 grams. The bag weighs 2 kilograms. What is the smallest number of threads that could hold it? Why might a builder still need 6? **4 threads at the least. More may be needed because threads rarely share the weight equally.**. 2 kilograms is 2000 grams. 2000 divided by 500 is 4. That works only if every thread carries an equal share. If one thread is a little shorter, it carries more than 500 grams and snaps first. Extra threads give a margin. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L4`)

## Warm-up
- Name four animal body plans. → Rings, two matching halves, spirals and rows of segments.
- What is radial symmetry? → Matching parts arranged round a middle point, as in a sea anemone.
- What is bilateral symmetry? → One line down the middle, with a matching left side and right side.
- Why do animals who travel have a head? → The front end meets new things first, so the senses and the mouth are gathered there.
- What is a segment? → One of a row of repeated parts along a body.
- Where does a snail's shell grow? → At the rim of the opening. The tip is the oldest part.
- What is a tissue? → A group of cells of the same kind that do one job together.
- Does a starfish larva have two sides or five? → Two matching sides. The five-part star grows later.
