# Friction: which way it pushes, when we want it, and why it makes heat
`sci.forces.friction.L5`

**Summary.** A train engine as strong as four thousand horses can stand still on a wet hill, with its wheels spinning. The engine only turns the wheels. The force that moves the train forward is friction from the rail. Friction is a contact force between two surfaces, and it acts against sliding. So friction can slow a rolling marble or push a walker forward. It can also carry a steel bowl along on a moving cloth. Whenever surfaces do slide, friction turns movement into heat. Your child measures stopping distances and tests what makes a rubbed coin warmer. They also sort the friction we want from the friction we fight.

**Intro.** *Stand up and take one slow step forward. As you step, which way does your back foot push on the floor: forward or backward? Say what you think, and why.* It is hard to tell just by stepping. In the first activity, Finger Walk, you test it with two fingers and a loose sheet of paper. Many books say that friction is the force that slows things down. That is only half of what friction does. In this unit you find out which way friction pushes, and how to work it out for anything. You also find out where the movement goes when friction stops a rolling marble, and why brakes get hot.

## Terms
- **Friction**: Friction is a force between two surfaces that touch. It acts against sliding, or against the sliding that would happen without it. *e.g.* Friction between your chappal and the road stops your foot sliding when you walk.
- **Contact force**: A contact force is a force that acts only while two things touch. Friction, a push from your hand and the pull of a rope are contact forces. *e.g.* A carrom striker feels friction from the board only while the striker touches the board.
- **Non-contact force**: A non-contact force acts between things that do not touch. Gravity and the force of a magnet are non-contact forces. *e.g.* A mango falls from the tree because gravity pulls the mango down, though nothing touches the mango.
- **Force diagram**: A force diagram is a simple drawing of one thing with an arrow for each force on it. Each arrow points the way that force acts. A bigger force gets a longer arrow. *e.g.* A sliding book gets three arrows. Gravity points down, the table's push points up, and friction points backward.
- **Static friction**: Static friction is friction between two surfaces that are not sliding over each other. It grows to match a push, up to a limit. *e.g.* You push a heavy almirah gently and it stays still. Static friction is pushing back just as hard as you push.
- **Sliding friction**: Sliding friction is friction between two surfaces that are sliding over each other. It is usually a little smaller than the biggest static friction. *e.g.* A carrom coin sliding across the board slows down because of sliding friction.
- **Rolling resistance**: Rolling resistance is the force that slows a rolling ball or wheel. Most of it comes from the surface and the wheel being squashed a little as they roll. It is usually far smaller than sliding friction. *e.g.* A marble rolls a long way on a hard floor and stops soon on a soft towel.
- **Traction**: Traction is friction that pushes a walker or a wheel forward. It happens when a foot or a wheel pushes backward on the ground without slipping. Every walking animal moves by traction, including us. *e.g.* On wet mud a wheel has little traction, so the wheel spins and the auto stays where it is.
- **Lubricant**: A lubricant is something slippery put between two surfaces to make friction smaller. Oil, grease, soap, wax and talcum powder are lubricants. *e.g.* A drop of oil on a door hinge stops the squeak, because the metal parts slide on oil.
- **Kinetic energy**: Kinetic energy is the energy a thing has because it is moving. A faster thing has more of it, and so does a heavier thing. *e.g.* A cycle at twice the speed has four times the kinetic energy.
- **Thermal energy**: Thermal energy is the energy of the tiny particles of a thing as they jiggle. When a thing has more thermal energy, it is hotter. In everyday talk we call it heat. *e.g.* After you brake hard on a cycle, the wheel rim is warm. The cycle's kinetic energy became thermal energy.
- **Wear**: Wear is the slow loss of material from a surface that rubs against another surface. *e.g.* The heel of an old chappal is thinner on one side, because the road has worn the rubber away.
- **Drag**: Drag is the force from air or water that acts against a thing moving through it. Air resistance and water resistance are the two kinds of drag. *e.g.* You feel drag when you cycle fast, and when you wade through a pool.
- **Streamlined**: A streamlined shape is smooth and narrow at the front, so air or water flows round it easily. A streamlined thing has less drag. *e.g.* A kingfisher's long, pointed beak is streamlined. She dives into water with hardly a splash.
- **Upthrust**: Upthrust is the upward push of water or air on a thing that is in it. Upthrust acts even when the thing is still. It is a contact force, but it is not a kind of friction. *e.g.* Push an empty bottle down into a bucket of water. The push you feel against your hand is upthrust.
- **Stopping distance**: Stopping distance is how far a moving thing travels before it stops. *e.g.* A cycle going fast has a longer stopping distance than a cycle going slowly.
- **Mean**: The mean is one number that stands for several measurements. Add the measurements and divide by how many there are. It is also called the average. *e.g.* Three runs of 22, 26 and 24 centimetres add up to 72. The mean is 72 divided by 3, which is 24 centimetres.

## Explanations
### Friction is a contact force · *defined*
**Definition:** Friction is a force between two surfaces that touch. It is a contact force, so it stops acting as soon as the surfaces part.

Forces come in two kinds. Contact forces need touching: a push from a hand, the pull of a rope, friction, air resistance, water resistance and upthrust. Non-contact forces act across a gap: gravity and the forces of magnets and electric charge. Friction acts along the surfaces, where they meet. In a force diagram we draw friction as an arrow lying along the surface. Friction depends on both surfaces, never on one alone. The same chappal grips a dry road well and a wet marble floor badly.
- e.g. A sliding carrom coin slows down on the board. Flick the coin off the edge, and that friction ends at once.
- e.g. Gravity pulls a falling mango with nothing touching the mango. Friction cannot do that.
- e.g. In a force diagram of a sliding book, the friction arrow lies along the table top and points backward.
### Which way friction acts · *defined*
**Definition:** Friction acts along the touching surfaces. On each surface it points against the way that surface is sliding over the other, or would slide without friction.

To find the direction, ask one question. Which way is this surface sliding over the other one, or trying to slide? Friction on it points the opposite way. A book sliding to the right on a table gets friction to the left. A book resting on a tilted board would slide down, so friction on the book points up the slope. Friction always comes as a pair. The table pushes the sliding book to the left. The book pushes the table to the right, just as hard. We seldom notice the second push, because the table is held by the floor.
- e.g. A child going down a slide moves down the slope, so friction on the child points up the slope.
- e.g. Press a book against a wall with a flat hand. The book would slide down, so friction from the wall points up.
- e.g. In Finger Walk, your fingers pushed the loose paper backward, and the paper slid backward. The paper's friction on your fingers pointed forward.
### Friction can start a movement · *defined*
**Definition:** Traction is friction that pushes a walker or a wheel forward. Friction can also carry a thing along on a moving surface.

When you step forward, your back foot pushes backward on the floor. Without friction the foot would slide backward. So friction on your foot points forward, and that push moves you. A wheel does the same. The engine turns the wheel. The bottom of the wheel pushes backward on the road, and the road pushes the wheel forward. On ice, mud or wet rails the wheel slides and spins, and the vehicle stays where it is. Friction also carries things. Pull a cloth slowly and a steel bowl standing on the cloth moves with it. Only the cloth touches the bowl, so friction from the cloth moved the bowl. In every case friction acts to stop two touching surfaces sliding over each other.
- e.g. In the Riding Katori, a small steel bowl on a cloth moved when the cloth was pulled slowly. Friction speeded the bowl up.
- e.g. A train carries dry sand. On wet rails the loco pilot blows sand under the wheels, so the wheels grip the rails again.
- e.g. A snake's belly scales grip the ground more when they slide backward than forward. She pushes back on the ground and friction moves her forward.
- e.g. A box on the back of a lorry speeds up with the lorry. Friction from the lorry's floor pushes the box forward.
### Static friction: friction when nothing slides · *defined*
**Definition:** Static friction is friction between surfaces that are not sliding. It grows to match the push on a thing, up to a limit.

Push a heavy almirah gently and it does not move. Static friction pushes back exactly as hard as you push, so the two forces are balanced. Push harder and static friction grows too. But it has a limit. Past that limit the almirah starts to slide, and sliding friction takes over. Sliding friction is usually a little smaller, so the almirah feels easier once it moves. Walking and driving use static friction, because a sole or a tyre that grips does not slide. That is why a spinning wheel grips less than a wheel that is just holding.
- e.g. A book resting on a tilted board stays put. Static friction up the slope balances the part of gravity that pulls the book down the slope.
- e.g. A knot stays tied because static friction stops the string sliding over itself.
- e.g. A driver on a wet road brakes gently, so the tyres keep gripping and do not skid.
### Useful friction and unwanted friction · *defined*
**Definition:** Useful friction is friction that stops a slide we do not want. Unwanted friction is friction that works against a slide we do want. It is the same force both times.

One bicycle has both. We want the tyres to grip the road and the brake pads to grip the rim. We want the shoes to grip the pedals. We do not want friction in the axles or between the links of the chain. There it wastes the rider's effort as heat. To make friction bigger, people use rubber, rough surfaces, tread, a harder press, sand, or chalk on the hands. To make friction smaller, people use oil, grease, polished surfaces, wheels, ball bearings or a cushion of air. No surface is good or bad by itself. A smooth floor is right for dancing and wrong for a wet bathroom.
- e.g. Weightlifters and rock climbers rub chalk on their hands. Chalk soaks up sweat, so the hands grip better.
- e.g. A carrom board is dusted with fine powder, so the coins slide further.
- e.g. A screw holds two pieces of wood because of friction on its thread. With no friction, every screw and nut would work loose.
- e.g. On a cycle, the brake pads need friction and the axle next to them needs oil.
### Friction turns movement into heat · *defined*
**Definition:** When surfaces slide, friction turns kinetic energy into thermal energy. The energy changed is the friction force multiplied by the distance of sliding.

Energy is never destroyed. A moving thing has kinetic energy, and friction changes that energy into thermal energy in both surfaces. So a bigger friction force makes more heat, and a longer rub makes more heat. A stopping thing is the same story. A soft towel pushes hard against a rolling marble, so the marble's energy is used up in a short distance. A bedsheet has a smaller force, so the same energy lasts for a longer distance. A marble that starts twice as high has twice the energy, so it rolls about twice as far. Speed matters even more. Twice the speed means four times the kinetic energy. So a cycle or a car at twice the speed needs about four times the distance to brake.
- e.g. In the Stopping Distance Test, the marble started at the 10 and the 20 centimetre marks. From 20 it rolled about twice as far.
- e.g. In the Warm Coin Test, 80 strokes warmed the coin more than 20 strokes. A firm press warmed it more than a light press.
- e.g. A cycle rim feels warm after hard braking down a slope. The cycle's kinetic energy is now heat in the rim and the pads.
- e.g. A lorry coming down a long ghat road uses a low gear, so that the brakes do not overheat.
### Why rubbing makes things hot · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat is the jiggling of the atoms and molecules of a thing. Sliding surfaces tug on each other's atoms and let go, and that sets the atoms jiggling faster.

Two surfaces really touch only at many tiny spots. At each spot the atoms of one surface hold on to the atoms of the other. As the surfaces slide, each spot is stretched, and then the atoms snap free. They are left shaking, like a plucked string. That shaking is thermal energy. It spreads from atom to atom into the rest of each surface, and then into the air. The spreading goes one way. The shaking atoms never push the surfaces back to where they started. Scientists agree on this much. They are still working out the details. One is how much of the energy goes first into atoms, and how much into electrons.
- e.g. Rub your palms together for ten seconds and they are warm.
- e.g. A match lights because friction heats one tiny spot on the match head enough to start the chemicals burning.
- e.g. A drill bit is too hot to touch after it has made a hole in a wall.
### Friction wears surfaces away · *defined*
**Definition:** Wear is the loss of tiny pieces from surfaces that rub. Every sliding contact causes some wear.

At the tiny spots where two surfaces touch, sliding can tear small pieces off. Usually the softer surface loses more. Sometimes we want wear. A pencil writes because friction pulls thin flakes of graphite off the tip and leaves them on the paper. An eraser works because the rubber grips those flakes and wears away into crumbs that carry them off. More often wear is a cost. Brake pads, tyres, shoe soles and the parts of engines all wear out and must be replaced. Oil helps twice. It makes friction smaller, and it keeps the surfaces apart so they wear more slowly.
- e.g. The steps of an old temple or railway station dip in the middle. Millions of feet have worn the stone away.
- e.g. A cycle's brake pads become thinner every month.
- e.g. The dust from worn tyres is washed off roads by the rain and ends up in rivers and the sea.
### Air resistance and water resistance · *defined*
**Definition:** Air resistance and water resistance are forces from air and water that act against a thing moving through them. Together they are called drag.

Drag is like friction in three ways. It is a contact force, it acts against the movement, and it turns kinetic energy into heat. It is also different. Solid friction changes little with speed, but drag grows fast as speed grows. Drag also depends on shape and on how big a front the thing pushes through the air or water. A streamlined shape has less drag. Upthrust is a different force. It is the upward push of water or air on a thing that is in it. Upthrust acts even on a thing that is still, and it points up, not against the movement. A boat floats because upthrust balances gravity. The boat slows down because of water resistance.
- e.g. Sweep a flat hand through a bucket of water, then a hand held edge-on. The flat hand is much harder to move. You try this in Hand in the Bucket.
- e.g. Racing cyclists crouch low, so they push a smaller front through the air.
- e.g. An empty bottle pushed under water pushes back up on your hand even when you hold it still. That is upthrust, not water resistance.
### Is rolling resistance really friction? · *debated*
**Definition:** Rolling resistance is the force that slows a rolling ball or wheel. People disagree about whether to call it friction.

A rolling marble does not slide over the towel, so the usual cause of friction is missing. Yet something slows the marble. As the marble rolls, it presses a small dip into the cloth, and the cloth springs back behind it. The fibres bend and rub against each other, and that takes energy from the marble and turns it into heat. A hard floor is squashed far less, so a marble rolls much further on it. Whatever name we use, the force acts where two things touch, it acts against the movement, and it makes heat.
- e.g. A cycle with soft tyres is harder to pedal, because the soft tyres squash more as they roll.
- e.g. A steel train wheel on a steel rail squashes very little. That is one reason a train needs so little energy for each tonne it carries.
- e.g. In the Stopping Distance Test, the marble stopped sooner on a soft towel than on a thin bedsheet.
- **Many school science books:** They call it friction. It acts between two touching surfaces and against the movement, and that is what friction means at this level.
- **Many engineers:** They call it rolling resistance and keep it apart from friction. It comes mostly from squashing and springing back, not from surfaces sliding.

## Mostly agreed
- Friction acts only where two surfaces touch, and it acts against sliding between them. *How we know:* Thousands of pull and slope tests show this, from Leonardo da Vinci's notebooks more than 500 years ago to laboratories today.
- Friction acts on both surfaces at once, with equal forces in opposite directions. *How we know:* This is Newton's third law, published in 1687. You can see it when loose paper slides backward under walking fingers.
- Walkers and wheels move forward because of friction from the ground. *How we know:* On ice, wet mud or oily rails, feet slip and wheels spin. Railway engines carry sand to put grip back on wet rails.
- Friction turns kinetic energy into heat, and no energy is lost on the way. *How we know:* In the 1840s James Joule stirred water with paddles turned by falling weights. The same amount of work always gave the same amount of heat.
- A vehicle at twice the speed needs about four times the distance once the brakes are on. *How we know:* Kinetic energy grows with the speed multiplied by itself. Road tests of braking cars agree with this.
- Drag from air and water grows quickly with speed and depends on shape. *How we know:* Engineers measure drag on models of cars, planes and ships in wind tunnels and water tanks.

## How we found out
**John Blenkinsop and Matthew Murray, who built the toothed railway. William Hedley, Timothy Hackworth and Jonathan Forster, who built Puffing Billy at Wylam., 1811 (Blenkinsop's toothed rail), about 1812 (Hedley's test carriage) and 1813 (Puffing Billy).** Two hundred years ago, many engineers believed that a smooth iron wheel could never grip a smooth iron rail. They thought an engine pulling heavy wagons would only spin its wheels. So in 1811 John Blenkinsop patented a rail with teeth along one side. He worked at a coal mine near Leeds in England. His engines, built by Matthew Murray, pulled themselves along with a toothed wheel. Around 1812 William Hedley, a manager at Wylam coal mine, decided to test the belief. He built a test carriage that men drove by hand. He loaded it with weights and hooked coal wagons behind it. With little weight on it, the wheels spun. With more weight pressing the wheels onto the rails, the smooth wheels gripped and pulled the wagons. The friction had grown with the weight. In 1813 Hedley, with Timothy Hackworth and Jonathan Forster, built Puffing Billy, a steam engine with smooth wheels. Puffing Billy now stands in the Science Museum in London. Almost every train in the world runs on smooth wheels.  
*What people believed before:* That smooth iron wheels cannot grip smooth iron rails well enough to pull a heavy load. Hedley's loaded test carriage showed that they can, if enough weight presses the wheels down.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Friction always acts against the way a thing is moving.** (Many school science books; *rejected*)  
  For: It is true for a sliding box, a rolling ball and a braking cycle. These are the examples most books use.  
  Against: Friction on a walker's foot points forward, the way the walker moves. So does friction on a steel bowl carried along by a pulled cloth. The full rule is that friction acts against sliding between two surfaces.
- **The energy that friction takes from a moving thing is lost for ever.** (A common way of speaking; *rejected*)  
  For: A cyclist who brakes cannot get her speed back from the warm brake pads. For her, that energy is gone.  
  Against: The energy still exists as heat. James Joule showed in the 1840s that work always turns into a fixed amount of heat. The heat only spreads out, so it is hard to use again.
- **A vehicle can be stopped without turning all its movement into heat.** (Engineers of electric trains and cars; *accepted*)  
  For: In regenerative braking the turning wheels drive the motors backward, and the motors make electricity. Delhi Metro trains feed braking energy back into the overhead wires.  
  Against: Some energy still becomes heat. Every train and car also keeps ordinary friction brakes for the last few metres and for emergencies.
- **A heavier vehicle always needs a longer distance to brake.** (Most drivers; *debated*)  
  For: A heavier vehicle has more kinetic energy at the same speed. Loaded lorries really do take longer to stop, because their brakes and tyres reach their limits.  
  Against: More weight also presses the tyres harder on the road, so the friction grows by about the same share. In simple tests, a heavy and a light sliding block stop in about the same distance.
- **A world with no friction would be a better world for machines.** (A popular belief; *rejected*)  
  For: One study in 2017 estimated that about one fifth of the energy the world uses is spent on overcoming friction. Worn parts cost a great deal to replace.  
  Against: With no friction, no wheel could push on a road and no brake could work. No screw, nut or knot would stay tight. Engineers aim for the right friction in each place, not for none.

## Roots
We have learned that friction is a contact force that acts against sliding, and that it turns movement into heat. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In Finger Walk, your fingers walked forward and the loose paper slid backward. Your fingers pushed the paper back, so friction pushed your fingers forward. In the Stopping Distance Test, friction slowed the marble until it was as still as the towel. Both times, friction worked to make two touching surfaces move together, whether that meant starting or stopping.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call friction 'the force that slows things down'. But friction has no direction of its own. It acts against sliding between two surfaces, so it slows some things and speeds others up.  
*Evidence:* In Finger Walk, loose paper slid backward under walking fingers. In the Riding Katori, a steel bowl on a cloth began to move when the cloth was pulled. Nothing else touched the bowl. In the Stopping Distance Test, a rolling marble came to rest on a towel.

- *who am i:* Every step you take is a push from the ground. You move through the world by pushing on it and being pushed back.
- *what is life:* Every animal that walks, crawls or climbs moves by friction. A snake's belly scales grip the ground, and the ground pushes her forward.
- *what is this world:* Friction never belongs to one thing. It exists only where two surfaces meet, and it pushes on both of them equally.

Friction is one force, and we meet it in many forms. It is the grip of a shoe, the squeal of a brake and the warmth of rubbed hands. It is also the thin heel of an old chappal. Underneath, each of these is the same thing. The atoms of two touching surfaces hold on to each other with electric forces. One kind of holding between atoms shows up as grip, heat and wear, depending on what the surfaces are doing.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 2 (paraphrased):** Difficult and easy complete each other. Long and short shape each other. Before and after follow each other. (We call friction useful on a brake and unwanted in an axle, yet the force is the same. 'Useful' and 'unwanted' describe what we want at that moment. Each word has meaning only beside the other.)
- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18 (paraphrased):** Whatever arises by depending on other things has no existence of its own, apart from them. (A chappal has no friction by itself, and neither has a floor. Friction arises only when the two meet, and it changes when either one changes.)
- Science (settled): Every force comes as a pair. When one surface pushes on another, the second pushes back just as hard (Newton's third law, 1687).
- Science (settled): Energy is never destroyed. Work done against friction turns into the same amount of heat (James Joule, 1840s).
- Science (open): How sliding atoms pass their movement on as heat is still being worked out in detail.
- Other view, Many physicists: Friction has no aim. It is the sum of countless tiny electric forces between the atoms of two surfaces. Saying that friction 'works to' make surfaces move together is only our way of describing how the sums come out.
- Practice, Feel the floor push you (3 min): Stand with bare feet on the floor. Let's close our eyes and feel the soles of our feet. Now lean forward very slowly, as if you were about to take a step. Feel your toes press backward against the floor. The floor is pushing you forward just as hard. Now open your eyes. Take five slow steps, and notice the floor's push in every one.
- Ask: Friction pushes you forward at every step. So when you walk, who is moving you: you, or the floor?
- Ask: The same friction is useful in a brake and unwanted in an axle. Is 'useful' in the friction, or in what we want?
- Ask: Friction exists only where two surfaces meet. Is there anything about you that exists only when you meet someone or something else?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The floor pushes you forward at every step. So when you walk, who moves you: your legs, or the floor? (hint: Try the newspaper under your foot again. What do your legs do, and what does the floor do?)
- *big:* The same force is useful on a brake and unwanted in an axle. Is 'useful' a fact about friction, or about us? (hint: Think about who is doing the wanting in each case.)
- *deep:* Friction needs two surfaces. Does a chappal by itself have any grip? (hint: Think of the same chappal on a dry road and on a wet marble floor.)
- *deep:* When a marble stops, its energy has become heat that spreads into the room. Could that heat ever start the marble rolling again? (hint: Have you ever seen a still marble start to roll because the room was warm?)
- *big:* About one fifth of the world's energy goes on overcoming friction. Whose job is it to waste less of it? (hint: Think of a cyclist who oils a chain, a driver, an engineer and a government.)
- *big:* Tyres and brakes wear into dust that ends up in rivers and the sea. Is the friction we use on roads free? (hint: Somebody, somewhere, breathes or drinks what is rubbed off.)

## Story: A Handful of Sand
*A made-up story. The science and the railway are real. The Braganza Ghat runs between Kulem in Goa and Castle Rock in Karnataka. It is a very steep railway climb. Trains usually climb it with two engines in front and one pushing at the back, and the Dudhsagar waterfall falls beside the track. Indian engines carry sand to drop on slippery rails. Yazhini, Shanta Chithi, the passengers and this journey are imagined.*

The train left Kulem station in the rain. It was July, and Yazhini was ten. She sat with her nose against the window bars, because somewhere up this hill was the Dudhsagar waterfall.

Her aunt, Shanta Chithi, sat across from her with a steel tiffin box on her knees. Chithi drove goods trains for the railway. Today she was only a passenger, going home to Hubballi on leave.

The hill ahead was called the Braganza Ghat. For twenty-six kilometres the track climbed through wet forest. It was a very steep railway climb. At Kulem the railway had joined one more engine to the back of the train, to push.

"How many engines do we have?" Yazhini asked.

"Three. Two in front and one pushing from behind," said Chithi. "Each one is as strong as about four thousand horses."

Yazhini tried to picture twelve thousand horses. She could not. The train climbed slowly, through one tunnel and then another. Rain ran down the bars. Wet leaves lay stuck to the sleepers, and on the shining tops of the rails.

Then the forest opened, and there was the waterfall. White water came down the whole height of the mountain, so close that spray blew in through the window. Everyone in the coach stood up to look.

The train slowed. It slowed some more. Then a sound came from the front of the train, a rising whirr, like a mixer running with nothing in the jar. The coach shook. Outside, the trees stood perfectly still.

"We have stopped," said a man by the door. "Three engines, and they are still too weak for this rain. They should have sent a bigger one."

"The train is too heavy," said a woman with a basket of bananas. "Half of us should get down and walk."

Yazhini listened to the whirr. It reminded her of something. That morning she had run across her grandmother's wet veranda in her socks, to catch the auto. Her legs had gone very fast. She had gone nowhere at all. Then she had sat down hard on the red floor.

My legs were not weak, she thought. My legs were working. The floor was not holding my feet.

"Chithi," she said slowly, "I don't think the engines are weak. I can hear the wheels turning. They are turning, and the train is standing still. So the wheels are slipping on the rails, like my socks on Paati's veranda."

Chithi put the lid back on her tiffin box. "Go on," she said.

"An engine only turns its wheels," said Yazhini. "The wheel pushes backward on the rail. If the rail holds the wheel, the rail pushes the train forward. But the rail is wet, and there are leaves on it. So the wheel just slides round. Nothing is pushing us."

"And if you were the loco pilot?" asked Chithi. "Would you ask for a fourth engine?"

Yazhini shook her head. "A fourth engine would spin too. I would not make the wheels stronger. I would make the rail hold them. I would put something rough under the wheels."

Chithi smiled. "Listen," she said.

The whirr had dropped to a low growl. Then from the front came a long hiss of air.

"First the pilot lowers the power," said Chithi. "A wheel that is spinning grips even less than a wheel that is holding. Then she presses the sand button. Every engine carries boxes of dry sand. Air blows the sand down a pipe, onto the rail, just in front of the wheel."

The coach gave a small jerk. A tree trunk slid past the window, then another. The waterfall began to move away behind them.

"There," said the man by the door. "They have switched on more power at last."

"Less," said Yazhini, but only Chithi heard her.

At the top of the ghat the train stopped at Castle Rock, and the pushing engine was taken off. Yazhini climbed down and walked along the wet platform to the front. She bent to look at the place where a great steel wheel stood on the rail.

The wheel was more than a metre across. But the place where it touched the rail was tiny, smaller than a one-rupee coin. On the rail beside it lay a little pale dust, the last of the crushed sand.

"All those horses," she said, "and they push through a patch that size."

"Twelve thousand horses," said Chithi, "and a handful of sand."

Yazhini walked back along the platform. It was wet, so she walked carefully. At each step she felt her sandal press backward on the stone, and the stone push her on.

**For the parent:** For the parent: an engine does not push a train. The engine turns the wheels, and the wheels push backward on the rails. Friction from the rails pushes the train forward. This forward friction is called traction. On wet, leafy rails the friction is too small, so the wheels spin and all the engine's power does nothing. More power makes it worse, because a spinning wheel grips less than a wheel that is holding. Sand makes the friction bigger, and the train moves. The same thing happens under a shoe at every step.

- Ask: The engines were working, yet the train stood still. What was missing?
- Ask: Why did Yazhini think of her socks on the wet veranda?
- Ask: Which way does the wheel push on the rail? Which way does the rail push on the wheel?
- Ask: Why did the loco pilot lower the power before she used the sand?
- Ask: Where else have you seen a wheel or a foot slip? What gave back the grip?

### g1 · Which Way Is the Friction? (20 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the cards and put them in a pile, question side up. Each player takes a paper arrow.; One player reads the top card aloud and shows with a hand which way the thing is moving.; On the count of three, every player lays down their arrow to show the way friction pushes. For 'none', keep the arrow in your hand.; Turn the card over. Every player who was right and can give the reason scores one point.; After 20 cards, the player with the most points wins.; Playing alone: score one point for each card you get right. Try to reach 16 out of 20, then play again.
- **Printable cards: 20 situation cards to cut out (the answer is on the back):** A carrom coin slides across the board to the right. Which way does friction push the coin? (back: To the left. The coin slides to the right over the board, and friction acts against that slide.); A girl starts to walk forward. Which way does friction from the floor push her back foot? (back: Forward. Her foot pushes backward on the floor. Friction stops the foot sliding back, so it points forward.); A cycle is braking on a road. Which way does friction from the road push the tyres? (back: Backward. The braked tyres try to slide forward over the road, and friction acts against that.); A book rests on a tilted desk and does not slide. Which way does friction push the book? (back: Up the slope. Without friction the book would slide down the slope.); A steel bowl stands on a cloth. The cloth is pulled slowly to the left, and the bowl rides along. Which way does friction push the bowl? (back: To the left. Friction from the cloth carries the bowl along, the same way it moves.); A suitcase rides on a level airport belt at a steady speed. How much friction pushes the suitcase along? (back: None. The suitcase and the belt move together at a steady speed, so nothing is trying to slide.); An airport belt starts up with a suitcase lying on it. Which way does friction push the suitcase? (back: Forward, the way the belt moves. Friction speeds the suitcase up until it moves with the belt.); A lorry speeds up, with a box on its flat back. The box does not slide. Which way does friction push the box? (back: Forward. Without friction the box would be left behind and slide off the back.); A lorry brakes, with a box on its flat back. The box does not slide. Which way does friction push the box? (back: Backward. Without friction the box would keep going and slide towards the driver's cabin.); A child goes down a playground slide. Which way does friction push the child? (back: Up the slide. The child slides down, and friction acts against that slide.); You press a book against a wall with a flat hand, and the book stays up. Which way does friction from the wall push the book? (back: Up. Gravity would slide the book down the wall, and friction acts against that.); A car speeds up. The engine turns the front wheels. Which way does friction from the road push those tyres? (back: Forward. The bottom of each tyre pushes backward on the road, so the road's friction pushes the tyre forward.); A football rolls across grass and slows down. Which way does the grass push the ball? (back: Backward. The grass bends and rubs under the ball, and that force acts against the rolling.); You push a heavy almirah gently to the right, and it does not move. Which way does friction push the almirah? (back: To the left, exactly as hard as your push. This is static friction.); A climber grips a rope with both hands and hangs still. Which way does friction from the rope push her hands? (back: Up. Gravity would slide her hands down the rope, and friction acts against that.); A train tries to start on wet rails, and its wheels spin. Which way does friction from the rail push the wheels? (back: Forward, but too weakly. The bottom of each wheel slides backward over the rail, so friction points forward.); A match head is dragged to the right along the side of the box. Which way does friction push the match head? (back: To the left. That friction also heats the match head until it lights.); A swimmer pushes off the wall and glides forward. Which way does water resistance push her? (back: Backward. Water resistance acts against her movement through the water.); A girl cycles fast through still air. Which way does air resistance push her? (back: Backward. Air resistance acts against her movement through the air.); A box stands on a level floor, and nobody pushes it. How much friction acts on the box? (back: None. Nothing is trying to slide the box, so static friction has nothing to match.)
- **Printable tokens: A paper arrow for each player:** Arrow for player 1; Arrow for player 2; Arrow for player 3; Arrow for player 4
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 20 cards and the arrows. 2. Shuffle the cards. 3. Put them in a pile, question side up.  
  *Parent says:* Each card describes one thing. We will work out which way friction pushes on that thing. Sometimes the answer is a surprise.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and the arrows. Shuffle the cards. Put them in a pile.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the top card aloud. 2. Count to three. 3. Everyone lays down an arrow. 4. Turn the card over and read the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Ask yourself one question. Which way would this thing slide if there were no friction? Friction pushes the other way.  
  *Child mode:* Read the card. Which way would it slide with no friction? Point your arrow the other way.
  - Ask: A lorry speeds up with a box on its back. Why does friction on the box point forward? (good: Without friction the box would be left behind and slide backward.; Friction acts against that slide, so it points forward.)
  - Note: The useful question is always the same: which way would it slide with no friction? If your child points against the movement every time, ask that question again for the walker and the lorry cards.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the points. 2. Lay out the cards that anyone got wrong. 3. Sort all 20 cards into two heaps: friction against the movement, and friction with the movement or none.  
  *Parent says:* Which cards surprised us? In which ones did friction point the same way as the movement?  
  *Child mode:* Count your points. Which cards surprised you? Sort the cards into two heaps.
  - Check: A girl starts to walk forward. Which way does friction from the floor push her back foot? → **Forward, because her foot pushes backward on the floor**
### g2 · Shove Coin: stop on the line (20 min)
- **rules:** Mark a start line and a target line 60 centimetres apart on a smooth table. Use a pencil, chalk or strips of paper tape.; Put a coin on the start line. Shove it once with one finger, in one quick push.; Score 3, 2, 1 or 0 by where the coin stops. Each player has five shoves in a round.; Round 1 is on the bare table. Round 2 is on the table dusted with a pinch of talcum powder. Round 3 is on a bedsheet pulled flat.; Add up each round. The player with the most points after three rounds wins.; Playing alone: try to beat your own score for each round on a second go.
- **Printable board: The target, drawn with pencil or chalk on the table:** Start line: at the near edge of the table; Target line: 60 centimetres from the start line; 3 points: the coin stops touching the target line; 2 points: the coin stops within one hand-width of the line; 1 point: the coin stops within two hand-widths of the line; 0 points: the coin stops further away, or falls off the table
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet for three rounds:** Round 1, bare table: five shoves each. Points: __; Round 2, table dusted with powder: five shoves each. Points: __; Round 3, on a bedsheet: five shoves each. Points: __; Which round needed the hardest shove? __; Which round needed the gentlest shove? __
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose a smooth table at least 1 metre long. 2. Mark the start line and the target line 60 centimetres apart. 3. Give each player a coin of the same kind. 4. Let each player try three practice shoves.  
  *Parent says:* The aim is to stop the coin on the far line. Your finger gives the coin its speed. After that, only friction acts along the table.  
  *Child mode:* Mark two lines 60 cm apart. Take a coin. Try three practice shoves.
  - Note: A dining table, a carrom board or a smooth floor all work. A coin can scratch polished wood, so put the lines on a surface that will not be spoiled.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play round 1 on the bare table, five shoves each. 2. Write down the points. 3. Ask how hard the shove had to be.  
  *Parent says:* Round 1 is on the bare table. How hard a shove gets the coin to the line and no further?  
  *Child mode:* Play round 1. Shove the coin five times. Write your points.
  - Ask: What stops the coin after your finger lets go? (good: Friction between the coin and the table.; Sliding friction turns the coin's movement into heat.)
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sprinkle a pinch of talcum powder between the lines and spread it with a dry hand. 2. Ask for a guess: harder or gentler shoves now? 3. Play round 2. 4. Wipe the table, lay a bedsheet flat over it and play round 3.  
  *Parent says:* The powder changes the friction. Will you need a harder shove or a gentler one? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Dust the table with powder. Guess first: harder shove or gentler? Play round 2. Then play round 3 on a bedsheet.
  - Note: With powder the coin slides much further, so most players overshoot at first. On the bedsheet a much harder shove is needed. Wipe the powder up with a damp cloth afterwards, because a powdered floor is slippery.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add up the points for each round. 2. Fill in the last two lines of the score sheet. 3. Talk about what changed.  
  *Parent says:* On which surface did the coin need the hardest shove? What does that tell us about the friction there?  
  *Child mode:* Add up your points. Which surface needed the hardest shove? Why?
  - Check: On a table dusted with powder, the same shove sends a coin further. Why? → **The powder makes friction smaller, so the coin's energy lasts longer**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Stopping Distance Test: how far does a marble roll before it stops? (40 min)
- **question:** How does the surface change the distance a rolling marble needs to stop? And if the marble starts twice as high up the ramp, how much further does it roll?
- **predict:** Guess first. On which will the marble stop sooner, a bath towel or a cotton bedsheet? Then guess again. If the marble starts twice as far up the ramp, how far will it roll? Choose the same distance, twice as far, or more than twice as far. Write both guesses and your reason for each.
- **fair test:** In part 1, change only the surface. Start the marble at the 20 centimetre mark every time. In part 2, change only the start mark, and keep the towel. Always let go of the marble without a push. Use the same marble, the same ramp and the same pile of books. Do every run 3 times and find the mean, because one run can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Pick up the marble after every run, so that nobody steps on it. Keep marbles away from children under three.
- **record:** Fill in the table with every run and every mean. Draw a bar chart of the three surfaces, and a line graph of the three start marks. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Surface and start mark | Run 1 (cm) | Run 2 (cm) | Run 3 (cm) | Mean (cm)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the cardboard strip along its length to make a V-shaped channel. 2. Rest one end on a pile of books 6 centimetres high, and tape it there. 3. Spread the towel flat on the floor and rest the bottom end of the channel on it. 4. Mark the channel 10, 20 and 30 centimetres from the bottom end.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to roll a marble down this channel and see how far it goes. First let's build the ramp and mark three starting places.  
  *Child mode:* Fold the cardboard into a V. Rest the top on the books. Rest the bottom on the towel. Mark 10, 20 and 30 cm from the bottom.
  - Note: Measure the marks along the channel from its bottom end. The bottom end must lie on the cloth, so the marble meets no step. A toy car needs no channel: use a tray or a hardback book as the ramp.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the towel and the bedsheet. 2. Ask for the first guess and its reason. 3. Ask for the second guess, about starting twice as high, and its reason. 4. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* On which cloth will the marble stop sooner? And if it starts twice as far up, how much further will it roll? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which cloth stops the marble sooner. Guess what starting twice as high does. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The towel is soft and rough, so it has more friction.; A higher start makes the marble faster, so it needs longer to stop.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children expect the higher start to make only a small difference.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the marble on the 30 centimetre mark with one finger. 2. Lift the finger straight up. 3. Watch where the marble stops. 4. If it rolls off the end of the towel, take one book away and try again.  
  *Parent says:* This is a trial run from the top mark. We need the marble to stop on the towel. Lift your finger straight up, and do not push.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the marble on the top mark. Lift your finger. Do not push. Does it stop on the towel?
  - Note: If the marble stops within 10 centimetres, add a book. Once the ramp is right, do not change it again. Then roll the marble once onto the bare floor, to see how far it goes with little friction.
- **s4 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start the marble at the 20 centimetre mark. 2. Measure from the end of the channel to the marble. 3. Do 3 runs on the towel and write each one down. 4. Lay the bedsheet flat in place of the towel and do 3 runs. 5. Do 3 runs on your third surface.  
  *Parent says:* Part 1 changes only the surface. Start at the 20 centimetre mark every time. Measure in a straight line from the end of the channel.  
  *Child mode:* Start at the 20 cm mark each time. Do 3 runs on the towel. Do 3 on the bedsheet. Do 3 on your own surface.
  - Note: Pull the bedsheet smooth and hold its corners down with books. If the marble rolls off the end of the sheet, write 'more than' and the length of the sheet.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the towel back. 2. Ask your child to guess the distances from the 10 and the 30 centimetre marks. They can use the result from the 20 centimetre mark. 3. Do 3 runs from the 10 centimetre mark. 4. Do 3 runs from the 30 centimetre mark.  
  *Parent says:* Part 2 changes only the start mark. From the 20 centimetre mark you already have a mean. What do you expect from 10 and from 30? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Put the towel back. Guess the distance from the 10 cm mark. Guess for the 30 cm mark. Do 3 runs from each.
  - Ask: What do you expect from the 10 centimetre mark, and why? (good: About half as far, because the marble starts half as high.; Less, because the marble is slower at the bottom.)
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each row, add the three runs and divide by 3. 2. Draw a bar chart of the three surfaces. 3. Draw a line graph of stopping distance against start mark for the towel.  
  *Parent says:* Let's find the mean for each row. Then we draw one bar for each surface, and one point for each start mark.  
  *Child mode:* Add the three runs. Divide by 3. Draw a bar for each surface. Draw a point for each start mark.
  - Note: If one run is very different from the other two, ask what happened. Roll it again and say so in the table. A mean hides a mistake, so look at the three runs first.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for the three sentences. 2. Ask them to draw the marble on the towel with a friction arrow. 3. Only then read the explanation together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then draw the marble on the towel. Which way does the friction arrow point?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened. Draw the marble and a friction arrow.
  - Ask: The marble had the same speed at the bottom each time in part 1. Why did it stop sooner on the towel? (good: The towel pushes against the marble with a bigger force.; The towel takes the marble's energy away in a shorter distance.)
  - Ask: In part 2, where did the extra distance come from? (good: A higher start gives the marble more energy.; The towel takes away about the same energy each centimetre, so more energy lasts longer.)
  - Note: Expect about double the distance from double the start mark, not exactly double. Real cloth gives results between about one and a half and two and a half times.
  - Check: In the Stopping Distance Test, the marble started twice as far up the ramp and rolled about twice as far. Why? → **The marble had about twice the energy to lose**
### e2 · The Warm Coin Test: what makes friction make more heat? (30 min)
- **question:** Does a coin get warmer if you rub it for more strokes? Does it get warmer if you press harder? And can a judge tell without being told what was done?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which will be warmest: a coin rubbed for 20 strokes, 40 strokes or 80 strokes? Will a firm press or a light press make the coin warmer? Write your guesses and the reason for each.
- **fair test:** Use coins of the same kind, and start each test with a cool coin. Rub between the same two lines, 20 centimetres apart, at the same speed. In the first test change only the number of strokes. In the second test change only the press. The judge keeps their eyes closed and is not told what was done. Then the judge's guess cannot change the score.
- **safety:** A coin rubbed hard for 80 strokes can be hot enough to sting. Touch it to the skin for one second only. Never press a hot coin on anybody as a joke.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Test | Strokes | Press | Warmth score (0 to 3) | Did the judge know?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw two lines 20 centimetres apart on the cardboard. 2. Put the three coins on the steel plate. 3. Agree the scores: 0 is cool, 1 is the same as skin, 2 is warm, 3 is hot. 4. Practise 10 strokes at about 4 strokes each second.  
  *Parent says:* We will rub a coin and feel how warm it gets. One trip from line to line is one stroke. Let's agree what the scores 0, 1, 2 and 3 mean.  
  *Child mode:* Draw two lines 20 cm apart. Put the coins on the plate. Learn the scores 0 to 3. Practise 10 fast strokes.
  - Note: Hold the coin flat under two fingertips. The cardboard protects the table and holds the heat in the coin.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask which number of strokes will make the warmest coin, and why. 2. Ask whether a firm or a light press will make more heat, and why. 3. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* Which coin will be warmest: 20 strokes, 40 strokes or 80 strokes? Will pressing harder change the warmth? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which coin will be warmest. Guess what a firm press does. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: More strokes means more rubbing, so more heat.; Pressing harder makes more friction, so more heat.)
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The judge closes their eyes and holds out a wrist. 2. The rubber picks 20, 40 or 80 strokes in secret and rubs with a firm press. 3. The rubber touches the coin to the judge's wrist for one second. 4. The judge gives a score. 5. Repeat with a cool coin until all three numbers are done.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes. I will not tell you how many strokes I did. Tell me the score when you feel the coin.  
  *Child mode:* The judge closes their eyes. Rub the coin 20, 40 or 80 strokes. Touch the judge's wrist for one second. Write the score.
  - Note: Take turns as rubber and judge. Put each used coin back on the steel plate for two minutes, because the plate cools it. If nothing feels warm, rub faster and press harder, or double every number of strokes.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take a cool coin. 2. Rub it for 40 strokes with a light press, as if you might tear the cardboard. 3. The judge scores it. 4. Rub a cool coin for 40 strokes with a firm press. 5. The judge scores it. Do the two in a secret order.  
  *Parent says:* Now the strokes stay the same and only the press changes. I will do the light press and the firm press in a secret order.  
  *Child mode:* Rub 40 strokes with a light press. Then rub 40 strokes with a firm press. The judge scores each one.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to choose one more change. 2. Ask what they expect, and why. 3. Test it with 40 firm strokes and a judge with closed eyes.  
  *Parent says:* Now you design a test. You could try a drop of oil on the cardboard, a smooth steel plate, or longer strokes. What do you expect, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Choose one more change. Guess what it will do. Say why. Test it with 40 strokes.
  - Note: Oil makes the friction smaller, so the coin usually stays cooler. The difference can be small. If the judge feels no difference, write that down as the result.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child for the three sentences. 3. Only then read the explanation together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Where did the heat in the coin come from?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Where did the heat in the coin come from? (good: From the movement of my hand.; Friction turned the kinetic energy of the rubbing into heat.)
  - Ask: Why did the judge keep their eyes closed? (good: So the judge could not guess the answer from what they saw.; If you expect a coin to be hot, it can feel hotter.)
  - Check: In the Warm Coin Test, a firm press warmed the coin more than a light press. Why? → **A firm press makes a bigger friction force**

### a1 · Finger Walk: which way does friction push? (10 min)
- **safety:** Do the foot test sitting on a chair. Do not stand or run on loose paper, because it slips.
- **record:** Draw the walking fingers and the paper. Add one arrow for the push of the fingers on the paper, and one for the friction on the fingers.

  - Note: The paper slides backward, and the fingers hardly move forward. Press down a little, as a foot does.

  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sits on a chair on a smooth floor. 2. Put a sheet of newspaper under one foot. 3. Your child pushes with that foot as if starting to walk forward.

  *Child mode:* Sit on the chair. Put one foot on the newspaper. Push as if to walk. Watch the paper.
  - Ask: Which way does your foot push on the floor when you walk forward? (good: Backward.; My foot pushes the floor back, and the paper showed it.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the corners of the plain paper to the table. 2. Your child walks two fingers forward again. 3. Ask what pushes the fingers forward now. 4. Your child draws the fingers with two arrows.  
  *Parent says:* Now the paper cannot slide. Your fingers push it backward as before. So what pushes your fingers forward?  
  *Child mode:* Tape the paper down. Walk your fingers again. What pushes them forward now? Draw two arrows.
  - Note: One arrow shows the fingers pushing the paper backward. The other shows friction from the paper pushing the fingers forward. The two arrows are the same length.
  - Check: In Finger Walk, the loose paper slid backward when the fingers walked forward. What does that show? → **The fingers push backward on the paper**
### a2 · The Riding Katori: can friction start a thing moving? (15 min)
- **record:** Draw the katori three times: for the slow pull, the sudden stop and the fast pull. Add a friction arrow to each drawing.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the cloth on the table and stand the katori on it. 2. Ask what the katori will do when the cloth is pulled slowly, and why. 3. Your child pulls the cloth slowly and steadily towards themself.  
  *Parent says:* I will not touch the katori. What will it do when you pull the cloth slowly? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what the katori will do. Say why. Pull the cloth slowly towards you.
  - Ask: Only the cloth touched the katori. So which force made the katori move? (good: Friction from the cloth.; Friction pushed the katori the same way the cloth moved.)
  - Note: The katori rides along with the cloth. Let your child say which force moved it before you say so.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child pulls the cloth steadily, then stops it suddenly. 2. Watch the katori. 3. Ask which way friction pushed the katori this time.  
  *Parent says:* Pull steadily, then stop the cloth all at once. Watch the katori. Which way did friction push it just now?  
  *Child mode:* Pull steadily. Stop the cloth suddenly. Watch the katori. Which way did friction push it?
  - Note: The katori slides forward a little over the stopped cloth and then stops. Now friction pushes it backward. In both steps, friction worked to make the katori and the cloth move together.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the katori near the far edge of the cloth. 2. Your child grips the near edge with both hands and pulls it out very fast, straight and level. 3. Compare with the slow pull.  
  *Parent says:* Now pull the cloth out as fast as you can. Guess first. Will the katori ride along this time?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Pull the cloth out very fast. Did the katori ride along?
  - Note: The katori stays almost where it was. Friction has a limit, and it cannot speed the katori up that quickly. Use an empty katori, so nothing spills.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws the katori for all three pulls. 2. They add a friction arrow to each drawing. 3. Ask what is the same about all three.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the three pulls and the friction arrow for each. Friction pointed a different way each time. What was it doing every time?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the three pulls. Add a friction arrow to each. What did friction do every time?
  - Check: A katori stands on a cloth. The cloth is pulled slowly and the katori moves with it. Which force moved the katori? → **Friction from the cloth**
### a3 · Green and Red: a friction map of one machine (15 min)
- **safety:** Keep fingers away from the chain and the spokes while a wheel is turning.
- **record:** Keep the drawing with its green and red marks. Under it write one sentence for each mark, saying what the friction does there.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the bicycle safely. 2. Your child draws it from the side, big enough to fill the page. 3. Together, name the parts: tyres, brake pads, rim, pedals, chain, axles, handle grips and seat.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw the cycle and name its parts. Then we will hunt for every place where two surfaces touch.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the cycle from the side. Fill the page. Name each part.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child touches each place where two surfaces meet. 2. For each place ask: do we want these to slide, or to grip? 3. Mark green for grip wanted and red for sliding wanted. 4. Find at least four green places and three red ones.  
  *Parent says:* Touch each place where two surfaces meet. Do we want them to grip here, or to slide easily? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Touch each place where two surfaces meet. Grip wanted is green. Sliding wanted is red. Find seven places.
  - Ask: The brake pad and the axle are a hand's width apart. Why does one need friction and the other need oil? (good: The pad must grip the rim to stop the wheel.; The axle must turn easily, or the rider's effort is wasted as heat.)
  - Note: Green: tyres on the road, brake pads on the rim, shoes on the pedals, hands on the grips, the seat. Red: the axles, the chain links, the pedal axle and the brake cable in its tube. On a door, the hinge and bolt are red and the handle is green.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick one green place and one red place. 2. Ask what would go wrong if the friction there were the other way round. 3. Ask how people made the friction bigger or smaller at each place.

### Friction: direction, use and heat (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What must be true before friction can act between two things?  
   Options: They must touch / They must be moving / They must be heavy / They must be rough  
   **Answer:** They must touch. *Why:* Friction is a contact force, so the two surfaces must touch. Choosing 'moving' forgets static friction, which acts on things that are still.
2. (truefalse) True or false: friction can act on a thing that is not moving.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Static friction holds a book still on a tilted board. A strong answer gives an example like this.
3. (fill) Friction acts against ____ between two surfaces that touch.  
   **Answer:** ['sliding']. *Why:* Friction acts against sliding, or against the sliding that would happen without it. 'Movement' is close, but friction can also push a thing the way it moves.
4. (sort) Sort these forces into contact forces and non-contact forces.  
   **Answer:** friction → Contact force; gravity → Non-contact force; air resistance → Contact force; a magnet's force on a pin → Non-contact force; upthrust → Contact force; water resistance → Contact force. *Why:* Friction, air resistance, water resistance and upthrust act only on things that touch a surface, air or water. Gravity and a magnet's force act across a gap.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Traction → friction that pushes a walker or a wheel forward; Lubricant → something slippery that makes friction smaller; Drag → the force from air or water against a moving thing; Wear → the slow loss of material from a rubbing surface; Static friction → friction between surfaces that are not sliding. *Why:* These words are used all through the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
6. (draw) Draw a book sliding to the right across a table. Add the friction arrow and label it.  
   **Answer:** A box on a line, with an arrow along the table pointing to the left, labelled 'friction'.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the arrow along the table top, pointing against the slide. An arrow pointing down or to the right shows a mix-up with gravity or with the push.

   Options: The towel pushed back with a bigger force / The marble was slower on the towel's ramp / The bedsheet made the marble heavier / The towel had less friction  
   **Answer:** The towel pushed back with a bigger force. *Why:* The marble had the same energy at the bottom each time. The towel used that energy up in a shorter distance, so its force against the marble was bigger.

We want easy sliding in the axles and the chain, so those are oiled.
9. (truefalse) True or false: a drop of oil on a hinge makes the friction there bigger.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Oil is a lubricant. The metal parts slide on a thin layer of oil, so the friction is smaller and the squeak stops.

   **Answer:** The fingers push backward on the paper. The paper is loose, so it slides backward. When the paper is held still, friction from the paper pushes the fingers forward.. *Why:* A strong answer says which way the fingers push, and that friction on the fingers points the other way, forward.
12. (fill) When friction stops a sliding thing, its kinetic energy turns into ____.  
   **Answer:** ['heat', 'thermal energy']. *Why:* Friction turns kinetic energy into thermal energy, which we call heat. The energy is not destroyed.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The suitcase and the belt move together at a steady speed, so nothing is trying to slide. Friction is needed only when the belt speeds up, slows down or turns.
6. (count) A cycle brakes with a friction force of 40 newtons and stops in 5 metres. How many joules become heat?  
   **Answer:** 200. *Why:* Energy turned into heat is force multiplied by distance. 40 × 5 = 200 joules.
7. (mcq) A train's wheels spin on wet rails. Why does the loco pilot lower the power before using sand?  
   Options: A spinning wheel grips less / The engine needs to rest / Sand works only on a cold rail / Less power makes the train lighter  
   **Answer:** A spinning wheel grips less. *Why:* Sliding friction is smaller than the biggest static friction. So a wheel that spins grips less than one that holds. Lowering the power lets the wheel stop slipping, and the sand then adds grip.
8. (fill) A wheel that grips the road and pushes a car forward is using ____.  
   **Answer:** ['traction', 'friction']. *Why:* Traction is friction that pushes a wheel or a walker forward.
9. (sort) Sort each one by the force that mainly slows it: friction between solids, or drag from air or water.  
   **Answer:** a cycle with its brakes on → Friction between solids; a parachute coming down → Drag from air or water; a ship after the engine stops → Drag from air or water; a sliding carrom coin → Friction between solids; a kingfisher diving into a pond → Drag from air or water; one palm rubbed on the other → Friction between solids. *Why:* Brake pads, a carrom coin and rubbed palms are solids sliding on solids. A parachute, a ship and a diving bird are slowed by air or water.
10. (draw) A box rides on a lorry speeding up to the right. Draw friction on the box and on the floor.  
   **Answer:** On the box: an arrow pointing right, labelled 'friction on box'. On the lorry's floor: an arrow of the same length pointing left, labelled 'friction on floor'.. *Why:* Friction comes as a pair. The floor pushes the box forward, and the box pushes the floor backward just as hard.
11. (order) A cyclist pedals up to speed and then brakes. Put the energy changes in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Energy from food is stored in her muscles', 'Her muscles turn it into the kinetic energy of the cycle', 'The brakes turn the kinetic energy into heat in the pads and rims', 'The heat spreads into the air']. *Why:* Energy is never lost. It changes form at each step, and the last form is heat spread through the air.
12. (write) In the Warm Coin Test, the judge kept their eyes closed. Why does that make the result more trustworthy?  
   **Answer:** A judge who knows a coin was rubbed 80 times expects it to be hot. That judge may give a higher score. With closed eyes the judge can use only what the skin feels. So the scores come from the coin and not from the judge's guess.. *Why:* A strong answer says that expecting a result can change what a person reports. Hiding the treatment removes this.
13. (match) Match each scene to the kind of friction or drag in it.  
   **Answer:** A pen held between the fingers → static friction; A carrom coin crossing the board → sliding friction; A marble rolling on a towel → rolling resistance; A kite pulled along by the wind → air resistance; A fish swimming in a pond → water resistance. *Why:* Each scene has surfaces that hold, slide or roll, or a thing moving through air or water.
14. (odd) Three of these changes make a rubbed coin warmer. Which one keeps it cooler?  
   Options: More strokes / Longer strokes / A firmer press / A drop of oil  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* More strokes, longer strokes and a firmer press all raise the force or the distance, so more energy becomes heat. Oil makes the friction force smaller.
15. (write) Lorries use a low gear on a long downhill ghat road. Explain why, using friction and heat.  
   **Answer:** Going downhill, a lorry would keep gaining kinetic energy. Brakes turn that energy into heat by friction. On a long slope the brakes get so hot that they stop gripping well. In a low gear the engine holds the lorry back, so the brakes do less and stay cooler.. *Why:* A strong answer says where the heat comes from, and why too much heat in the brakes is dangerous.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a contact force?  
   Options: Gravity on a falling mango / Friction on a sliding coin / A magnet's force on a pin / The Earth's pull on the Moon  
   **Answer:** Friction on a sliding coin. *Why:* Friction acts only where two surfaces touch. The other three act across a gap, so they are non-contact forces.
2. (mcq) A carrom coin slides to the right. Which way does friction push the coin?  
   Options: To the left / To the right / Downward / Upward  
   **Answer:** To the left. *Why:* The coin slides to the right over the board. Friction acts against that slide, so it points to the left.
3. (mcq) You start to walk forward. Which way does friction from the floor push your back foot?  
   Options: Forward / Backward / Downward / There is no friction  
   **Answer:** Forward. *Why:* Your foot pushes backward on the floor and would slide backward without friction. So friction on the foot points forward. Choosing 'backward' means using the rule 'friction is against movement', which is only half right.
4. (mcq) A train's wheels spin on wet rails, and the train stays still. What is missing?  
   Options: Enough friction between wheels and rails / Enough power in the engine / Enough weight in the coaches / Enough air in front  
   **Answer:** Enough friction between wheels and rails. *Why:* The engine is turning the wheels, so power is not the problem. The rails must push the wheels forward by friction, and wet rails give too little.
5. (mcq) In a stopping distance test, why do you roll the marble three times on each surface?  
   Options: One run can be a fluke / The marble needs to warm up / Three is a lucky number / The surface changes each time  
   **Answer:** One run can be a fluke. *Why:* A small push or a wrinkle can spoil one run. Three runs and a mean give a number you can trust more.
6. (mcq) Which pair names useful friction first, and unwanted friction second, on a bicycle?  
   Options: Brake pads, then the axle / The axle, then brake pads / The chain, then the tyres / The axle, then the chain  
   **Answer:** Brake pads, then the axle. *Why:* Brake pads must grip the rim, so friction there is useful. An axle must turn easily, so friction there wastes the rider's effort.
7. (mcq) One coin is rubbed for 80 strokes and another for 20, in the same way. Which is warmer, and why?  
   Options: The 80-stroke coin: longer rubbing, more heat / The 20-stroke coin: less time to cool / Both the same: friction force was equal / Neither: coins cannot get warm  
   **Answer:** The 80-stroke coin: longer rubbing, more heat. *Why:* Energy turned into heat is friction force multiplied by distance rubbed. Four times the strokes is four times the distance, so more heat.
8. (mcq) After a rolling ball stops, where has its kinetic energy gone?  
   Options: Into heat in the ball, floor and air / Nowhere: it was destroyed / Back into the hand that rolled it / Into the ball's weight  
   **Answer:** Into heat in the ball, floor and air. *Why:* Friction and air resistance turn kinetic energy into heat. The heat spreads out, so we do not notice it, but the energy still exists.
9. (mcq) A cycle goes twice as fast and brakes in the same way. About how far does it need to stop?  
   Options: Four times as far / Twice as far / The same distance / Half as far  
   **Answer:** Four times as far. *Why:* Twice the speed means four times the kinetic energy. The same braking force needs four times the distance to turn it all into heat.
10. (mcq) Which force from water acts on a boat even when the boat is still?  
   Options: Upthrust / Water resistance / Sliding friction / Traction  
   **Answer:** Upthrust. *Why:* Upthrust pushes up on anything in water, moving or still. Water resistance acts only when the boat moves through the water.
11. (mcq) Which of these is still not fully understood by scientists?  
   Options: Exactly how sliding atoms turn movement into heat / Whether friction makes heat / Whether oil makes friction smaller / Whether friction needs touching surfaces  
   **Answer:** Exactly how sliding atoms turn movement into heat. *Why:* Scientists agree that friction makes heat, that oil lowers friction and that friction is a contact force. The exact steps by which sliding atoms pass on their energy are still being studied.

## Applications
- Brakes on cycles, autos, cars and trains press a pad against a turning rim, disc or wheel. Friction turns the vehicle's kinetic energy into heat.
- Railway engines are made heavy and carry sand. The rails can push a train forward only through friction with the wheels.
- The grooves in a tyre let rainwater escape, so that rubber still touches the road in the monsoon.
- Ball bearings and oil cut the unwanted friction in ceiling fans, mixers, cycles and engines. About one fifth of the world's energy is used up overcoming friction.
- Metro trains and electric cars use regenerative braking. The motors slow the wheels and turn part of the kinetic energy back into electricity.
- Fast trains, racing cycles and swimmers' caps are streamlined, to make air resistance or water resistance smaller.
- A wet grinder crushes soaked rice and urad dal for idli batter by friction between heavy stones. The batter comes out slightly warm.

## Open questions
- **At the level of atoms, how exactly does sliding turn into heat?** The energy goes into shaking atoms, and some goes into electrons. The shares have been measured only for a few special materials.
- **What happens in the instant when a still thing starts to slide?** Fast cameras show tiny cracks racing along the touching surfaces just before sliding starts. Nobody can yet predict them for everyday surfaces.
- **How much harm does the dust from tyres and brakes do?** Tyre dust is one of the largest sources of tiny plastic pieces in rivers and seas. Its effect on fish, people and other animals is still being measured.
- **Why do faults in the Earth become so slippery during an earthquake?** In laboratory tests, rock sliding fast loses most of its friction, perhaps because friction heats the rock. Nobody can watch a real fault deep underground.
- **How much of the energy lost to friction can the world save?** Engineers think better surfaces and oils could save a large share. How fast that can happen in real engines and factories is not known.

## Connected maths
- In the Stopping Distance Test, three runs on a bedsheet were 92, 100 and 96 centimetres. What is the mean? **96 centimetres.**. Add the three runs: 92 + 100 + 96 = 288. Divide by the number of runs: 288 ÷ 3 = 96. So the mean is 96 centimetres. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- The marble's mean stopping distance was 24 centimetres on a towel and 96 centimetres on a bedsheet. How many times as far did it roll on the bedsheet? What does that say about the two forces? **4 times as far. The force from the towel was about 4 times as big.**. 96 ÷ 24 = 4. The marble had the same energy at the bottom both times. The towel used up that energy in a quarter of the distance. Energy is force multiplied by distance, so the towel's force was about 4 times as big. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- On a towel, the marble's mean stopping distances were 12, 24 and 36 centimetres. The start marks were 10, 20 and 30 centimetres. What do you expect from a 40 centimetre mark on a longer ramp? **About 48 centimetres.**. Each extra 10 centimetres of start adds 12 centimetres of rolling. The pattern is 12, 24, 36, so the next number is 48. Real results will be close to this, not exact. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A cyclist brakes. The friction force on the wheels is 50 newtons, and the cycle stops in 8 metres. How many joules of kinetic energy turned into heat? **400 joules.**. Energy turned into heat is the friction force multiplied by the distance. 50 × 8 = 400. One joule is the energy used when a force of 1 newton acts through 1 metre. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- A cycle at 10 kilometres an hour stops in 2 metres with the brakes on hard. Twice the speed means 4 times the kinetic energy, and 3 times the speed means 9 times. How far does the cycle need at 20 and at 30 kilometres an hour? **About 8 metres at 20 kilometres an hour, and about 18 metres at 30.**. The brakes give the same force, so the distance grows with the energy. At 20: 4 × 2 = 8 metres. At 30: 9 × 2 = 18 metres. Three times the speed needs nine times the distance. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Four runs of a marble on a towel were 23, 25, 24 and 61 centimetres. Which run looks wrong? What is the mean of the other three? What should you do about the odd run? **The 61 centimetre run looks wrong. The mean of the other three is 24 centimetres. Roll that run again and write down what happened.**. Three runs are within 2 centimetres of each other, and 61 is far away. Perhaps the marble got a push, or missed the towel. 23 + 25 + 24 = 72, and 72 ÷ 3 = 24. If 61 were included, the mean would be about 33, which matches none of the runs. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
