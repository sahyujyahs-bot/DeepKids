# Migration: the animals that travel across the world every year
`sci.animal-behaviour.migration.L3`

**Summary.** Every year, an Arctic tern flies from the top of the world to the bottom and back, about 70,000 kilometres. Humpback whales swim thousands of kilometres between cold seas full of food and warm seas where their calves are born. Monarch butterflies make a journey so long that it takes their great-grandchildren to finish it. These yearly journeys are called migration. Animals migrate to find food, warmth and safe places to raise their young, and many young birds find the right part of the world on their first journey, alone.

**Intro.** *Each winter, a small grey bird called a curlew sandpiper lands on a mudflat at Kodiakkarai, at the tip of Tamil Nadu. She has flown there from the far north of Russia, more than 6,000 kilometres away. Some of these birds are only a few months old. They have never seen Kodiakkarai, and no grown-up bird showed them the way. So how did they know where to go?* Animals of every kind make long journeys each year: birds, whales, butterflies, dragonflies, turtles, fish, and people too. In this unit you will meet the record travellers, find out why they go, and test two ways of finding your way in your own home.

## Terms
- **Migration**: A long journey that an animal makes at the same time each year, from one place to another and back again. *e.g.* Every winter, demoiselle cranes fly from Central Asia over the Himalayas to Rajasthan. In spring they fly back. That is migration.
- **Migrate**: To make a migration. An animal that migrates is called a migrant. *e.g.* Barn swallows migrate to South India for the winter, and leave again in spring.
- **Route**: The way a traveller goes from one place to another. *e.g.* The route of a monarch butterfly runs from Canada, through the United States, to the mountains of Mexico.
- **Flyway**: A wide path across the world that many kinds of migrating birds use every year. *e.g.* India lies on the Central Asian Flyway. Millions of birds come down it each winter, from Russia and Central Asia to Indian lakes and coasts.
- **Breeding ground**: The place where an animal lays eggs or gives birth, and raises their young. *e.g.* The curlew sandpiper's breeding ground is the Arctic tundra in the far north of Russia.
- **Wintering ground**: The place where a migrating animal spends the cold months, often far from where they breed. *e.g.* Chilika Lake in Odisha is a wintering ground for hundreds of thousands of ducks and other water birds.
- **Stopover**: A place on the route where a traveller rests and eats before going on. *e.g.* Many birds flying from Central Asia stop at lakes in Gujarat and Rajasthan, eat for a few days, and then fly further south.
- **Navigate**: To find your way from one place to another. The finding of the way is called navigation. *e.g.* A young cuckoo navigates all the way to Africa on her first journey, alone.
- **Landmark**: Something you can see, hear or smell that tells you where you are: a hill, a river, a building or a coast. *e.g.* On the way to school, the big banyan tree and the temple gopuram are landmarks that tell you that you are nearly there.
- **Direction**: The way something is pointing or moving: towards the sea, towards the sunrise, north or south. *e.g.* From Kodiakkarai, the Arctic coast of Russia lies to the north. A bird flying there must keep heading north for thousands of kilometres.
- **Inborn**: Something an animal has from birth, without being taught. Scientists also say innate. *e.g.* A young cuckoo is raised by other birds. Yet she flies the right way to Africa, so the direction must be inborn.
- **Tailwind**: A wind that blows from behind, the same way you are going. It pushes you along. A wind in your face is a headwind. *e.g.* Cycling with a tailwind is easy. Cycling into a headwind is hard work.
- **Generation**: All the animals or people born at about the same time. Your parents are one generation, you are the next, and your grandparents are the one before. *e.g.* The monarch journey north takes three or four generations of butterflies.
- **Tracker**: A tiny machine fixed to an animal. The tracker records where the animal goes, and sometimes sends that record to scientists by satellite. *e.g.* Scientists fixed trackers on Amur falcons in Nagaland and followed them all the way to southern Africa.
- **Krill**: Tiny shrimp-like animals that live in huge numbers in cold seas. Many whales eat them. *e.g.* A blue whale can eat several tonnes of krill in a day in the cold seas near Antarctica.

## Explanations
### What migration is · *defined*
**Definition:** Migration is a long journey that an animal makes at about the same time every year, from one place to another, and back again.

A crow flying across the city to find food is not migrating. She comes home the same evening. A migration is a big, regular journey that follows the seasons. Most migrants live in two homes: one where they raise their young, and one where they spend the rest of the year. Some go north and south. Some go up mountains in summer and down in winter. Some fish swim from the sea up a river.
- e.g. Demoiselle cranes spend the summer in Central Asia and Mongolia, where they raise their chicks. They spend the winter in India, and thousands of them come to the village of Khichan in Rajasthan.
- e.g. Hilsa fish live in the Bay of Bengal. In the monsoon, many swim up rivers such as the Hooghly to lay their eggs.
- e.g. A house crow stays in the same city all year. She is not a migrant.
### Why animals migrate · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals migrate to be where food, warmth and safe places for their young are, at the time of year when they need them.

In the far north, summer brings long days, plenty of insects and space to nest. Then winter comes. The ground freezes and the food is gone. So many birds raise their chicks in the north in summer and fly south for the winter, where there is still food. Whales do something similar in the sea. The cold seas near the poles are full of food in summer, while warm seas are safer places for a newborn calf. Many scientists think the main reason is food, not cold. A bird with enough food can survive a lot of cold.
- e.g. Curlew sandpipers nest in the Arctic, where the summer brings swarms of insects for their chicks. In winter they come to Indian coasts, where the mud is full of worms and tiny shellfish.
- e.g. Humpback whales eat krill in cold seas, then swim to warm seas where their calves are born.
### What the journey costs · *defined*
**Definition:** Migration costs a lot of energy and is dangerous. Many travellers die on the way. It is still worth it for those who make it, because of what waits at the other end.

Before a long flight, a bird eats and eats, and stores the food as fat under her skin. The fat is fuel for the journey. On the way there are storms, hunters, tall glass buildings, bright city lights and lakes that have dried up. A young bird on her first journey is in the most danger. So why go? Because staying would be worse. A bird who stays where the food runs out may starve. Every journey is a choice between two risks.
- e.g. In 2022, a young bar-tailed godwit flew from Alaska to Tasmania without stopping, more than 13,000 kilometres in 11 days. She could not eat, drink or rest on the way.
- e.g. A humpback whale mother may eat almost nothing for months while she is in the warm sea with her calf, living on her fat.
### The record travellers · *defined*
**Definition:** Some animals make journeys of thousands of kilometres every year. Scientists know this because they have followed them with rings, tags and trackers.

The Arctic tern makes the longest yearly journey known. In 2010, scientists fixed tiny trackers to terns in Greenland. The birds flew to Antarctica and back, about 70,000 kilometres in a year. A tern can live 30 years, so in a lifetime she may fly as far as the Moon and back three times. Humpback whales swim up to about 8,000 kilometres each way between cold seas and warm seas. Monarch butterflies fly up to about 4,000 kilometres from Canada and the United States to the mountains of Mexico.
- e.g. An Arctic tern sees two summers every year: the Arctic summer and the Antarctic summer.
- e.g. Bar-headed geese fly over the Himalayas, some of the highest mountains on Earth, to spend the winter in India.
### A journey longer than one life · *defined*
**Definition:** For some insects, one journey takes several generations. The children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren carry it on.

In autumn, monarch butterflies fly south to the mountains of Mexico. These autumn monarchs live about 8 months, much longer than summer monarchs, who live only a few weeks. In spring they fly north, lay eggs on the way, and die. Their children fly further north and lay eggs. It takes three or four generations to reach Canada. Then, in autumn, the great-great-grandchildren fly all the way back to the same mountains in Mexico, a place none of them has ever seen.
- e.g. A monarch who reaches Canada in summer is the great-granddaughter of the monarch who left Mexico in spring.
- e.g. Painted lady butterflies also travel over many generations, between Africa and Europe.
### Two ways to find the way: a direction, or a place · *defined*
**Definition:** A traveller can find the way by keeping a direction for a certain distance, or by knowing the place itself. Young birds on their first journey use mostly the first. Older birds also know places.

Picture walking to a bowl by counting 6 steps from a doorstep. That works only if you start from the doorstep. If someone moves you without telling you, the same 6 steps take you to the wrong spot. But if you can see the bowl, you can walk to it from anywhere. Young birds on their first journey carry a direction and a distance inside them from birth. Older birds have learned the places too, so they can correct a wrong start.
- e.g. In the 1950s, the Dutch scientist A.C. Perdeck caught thousands of starlings in the Netherlands in autumn and flew them to Switzerland. The young ones kept their usual direction and ended up in Spain and the south of France. The adults turned towards their real winter home in the north of France and England.
- e.g. A young common cuckoo is raised by other birds and never meets her parents. She still flies to Africa alone, in the right direction.
### What tells a bird which way to go? · *debated*
Scientists agree that migrating birds use several clues: where the Sun is, the patterns of the stars at night, the Earth's magnetism, smells and landmarks such as coasts and mountains. They still argue about how birds sense the Earth's magnetism, and about which clue matters most.
- e.g. Birds kept in a cage at migration time hop towards the side of the cage that points the way they would fly.
- e.g. A compass needle lines up with the Earth's magnetism. Many birds seem able to sense the same magnetism.
- **Many scientists who study the eyes of birds:** A protein in a bird's eyes, called cryptochrome, changes with the Earth's magnetism. So the bird may see the magnetism as a faint pattern over what she sees.
- **Other scientists:** Some birds may sense magnetism with nerves in their beak, or in their ears. More than one sense may be at work.
- **Scientists who study smell:** Some sea birds seem to find their way by smells carried on the wind. When they cannot smell, they get lost more often.
### Riding the wind · *defined*
**Definition:** A wind blowing the way a traveller is going, a tailwind, carries them along and saves energy. Many migrants wait for the right wind before they set off.

A small bird or insect flies only a little faster than a strong wind. So the wind makes a big difference. With a tailwind, a bird covers more ground with the same fuel. With a headwind, she may go nowhere. Bird scientists watching radar see many more birds take off on nights when the wind blows their way. Large birds such as storks and eagles also ride on rising warm air, called thermals, so they can glide for hours without flapping.
- e.g. Globe skimmer dragonflies arrive in the Maldives each year with the winds from India, in October and November.
- e.g. In your paper butterfly test, the same butterfly travelled further from the same height when the fan blew behind it.
### People migrate too · *defined*
**Definition:** Humans are animals, and many people make yearly journeys with the seasons, for the same reasons other animals do: food, water and work.

Some families of shepherds walk their flocks to new grass every year. In the summer the high mountain meadows have grass, and in winter the plains do. Many workers travel to cities for part of the year and go home for harvest or festivals. Long ago, before farming, all humans moved with the seasons, following food.
- e.g. Gaddi shepherds in Himachal Pradesh take their sheep and goats up to high meadows in summer and down to the warmer valleys in winter.
- e.g. Dhangar shepherds in Maharashtra move their flocks across the Deccan in the dry months, looking for grass and water.
- e.g. Some Kuruba shepherds in Karnataka walk their sheep to new grazing land each year.
### How we know where they go · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists follow migrating animals with numbered rings on legs, paper tags on wings, tiny trackers that talk to satellites, radar, and the help of many ordinary people.

A ring is a tiny metal or plastic band with a number. If the same bird is caught or found again somewhere else, the number tells where she was before. Trackers are smaller than ever, but they must be very light, or they will slow the animal down. Ordinary people help a lot. Bird watchers all over India count birds and share their lists online, so scientists can see when the travellers arrive and leave.
- e.g. In 2013, scientists fixed trackers on Amur falcons in Nagaland and followed them across the Arabian Sea to southern Africa.
- e.g. Volunteers in America and Canada stuck tiny paper tags on monarch butterflies, which led to the discovery of their winter forests in Mexico.

## Mostly agreed
- Many kinds of birds, whales, fish, insects, turtles and mammals, including humans, make long journeys at about the same time each year. *How we know:* Rings, tags and trackers have followed these animals for over a hundred years, and bird watchers count their arrival every season.
- Animals migrate mostly to be where there is food and a safe place to raise their young at that time of year. *How we know:* Migrants arrive where food is plentiful and leave as it runs out. Where food stays all year, many of the same kinds stay put.
- Many young birds on their first journey fly in an inborn direction, without being shown the way. *How we know:* Young cuckoos raised by other birds fly to Africa alone. Young starlings moved to Switzerland kept their usual direction and missed their usual winter home.
- Migrating birds use several clues to find their way, including the Sun, the stars, the Earth's magnetism and landmarks. *How we know:* In experiments, birds change the direction they try to fly when scientists change the star pattern above them in a planetarium, or change the magnetism around them.
- Lost resting places, bright lights at night, glass buildings and hunting kill many migrating animals. *How we know:* Scientists count birds killed at lit buildings, and see numbers fall where wetlands on the route have dried up or been built over.

## How we found out
**Norah and Fred Urquhart, Canadian scientists; Catalina Aguado and Ken Brugger, who found the winter forests; thousands of volunteer taggers; and the local people of the Mexican mountains, who knew about the butterflies all along, 1950s (the tagging began) and January 1975 (the winter forests were found).** For a long time, nobody knew where the monarch butterflies of North America went in winter. Every autumn, millions of them flew south from Canada and the United States, and then they seemed to vanish. Many people did not believe that a butterfly, as light as a flower petal, could fly thousands of kilometres. In the 1950s, Norah Urquhart and Fred Urquhart, two scientists in Canada, asked ordinary people for help. Thousands of volunteers stuck tiny paper tags on the wings of monarchs. Each tag had a number and an address. When someone found a tagged butterfly, they posted the tag back and said where they found it. Year by year, the tags pointed further south, towards Mexico. In January 1975, Catalina Aguado, a young woman from Mexico, and Ken Brugger, an American who was helping the Urquharts, climbed into the mountains of central Mexico. There they found fir trees covered in millions of resting monarchs. The local people had known about them for generations. Later, butterflies carrying tags from the United States were found in those forests. A butterfly really could fly that far.  
*What people believed before:* Many people did not believe that a butterfly could fly thousands of kilometres. Tags posted back from further and further south, and then tagged butterflies found in the Mexican forests, showed that monarchs really do.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A butterfly is too light and fragile to fly thousands of kilometres.** (Many people, before the monarch's winter forests were found in 1975; *rejected*)  
  For: A butterfly weighs about half a gram, and summer butterflies live only a few weeks.  
  Against: Tagged monarchs have been found thousands of kilometres from where they were tagged. The autumn monarchs live about 8 months, long enough for the journey.
- **Every young bird learns the route by following their parents.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Young cranes and geese do fly with their families, and learn the stopovers on the way.  
  Against: Young cuckoos never meet their parents, and young curlew sandpipers fly south weeks after the adults have left. They still reach the right part of the world.
- **Migrating animals always go north in summer and south in winter.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Many birds that come to India do fly south in autumn and north in spring.  
  Against: Some birds move up mountains in summer and down in winter. Blue tiger butterflies in South India move between the plains and the Western Ghats. Arctic terns go to the far south for the southern summer.
- **Globe skimmer dragonflies cross the ocean from India to East Africa and back every year.** (Charles Anderson, a marine biologist in the Maldives, in 2009; *debated*)  
  For: Huge numbers of globe skimmers appear in the Maldives each October, with the winds from India, though the islands have almost no fresh water for them to breed in. Later studies of the dragonflies' wings supported a long journey.  
  Against: No one has followed a single dragonfly the whole way. The round trip would take about four generations, and parts of it are still guesses.
- **Birds sense the Earth's magnetism with a protein in their eyes.** (Many scientists who study bird senses; *debated*)  
  For: A protein called cryptochrome in birds' eyes changes with magnetism. Some birds lose their magnetic sense in some colours of light.  
  Against: No one has yet shown exactly how the bird turns this into a sense of direction. Other senses in the beak or the ear may help too.

## Story: The Ring at Kodiakkarai
*Curlew sandpipers really do breed in the Arctic tundra of Russia and spend the winter on Indian coasts, including Point Calimere (Kodiakkarai), where bird scientists have ringed waders for many years. In this kind of bird, the adults leave the Arctic first and the young fly south weeks later, on their own. Lost young birds far from their usual range are found more often than lost adults. The starling experiment Chitti mentions was done by A.C. Perdeck in the Netherlands and published in 1958. Harini, Arun and Chitti Selvi are made up.*

Every winter, Harini's aunt went to work at Kodiakkarai. Kodiakkarai is at the very tip of Tamil Nadu, where the land points out into the sea. Its other name is Point Calimere. Harini called her aunt Chitti, which means mother's younger sister in Tamil. This year, for the first time, Harini and her little brother Arun went with her.

Chitti Selvi was a bird scientist. In the cool months, the mudflats at Kodiakkarai fill with small grey birds. They run along the edge of the water, poking the wet mud with long, thin, curved beaks. 'These are curlew sandpipers,' Chitti said. 'Each one weighs about as much as a small tomato.'

Before sunrise, Chitti and two other scientists, Mary and Nandini, had put up a long, soft net across the mudflat. It was so fine that it was hard to see. A few sandpipers flew into it and were caught, gently, like a ball in a cloth. The scientists lifted them out one by one with careful fingers. Harini held her breath.

Chitti weighed each bird in a little cloth bag and measured her wing. Then she slipped a tiny metal band onto one leg and squeezed it shut with special pliers. 'This is a ring,' she said. 'It has a number on it, like a car's number plate. If anyone catches this bird again, anywhere in the world, the number tells them where she has been.'

Then Nandini held up a bird and laughed. 'This one already has a ring!' Chitti read out the number, and Mary looked it up in a thick notebook. There was a long silence. Then Mary said, 'We ringed her right here. On this same mudflat. Two winters ago.'

Harini looked at the small grey bird. 'So she has been here before?' 'Yes,' said Chitti. 'Since then she has flown to the far north of Russia twice, to the Arctic, more than six thousand kilometres away. She laid her eggs there on the open ground, in the short Arctic summer. Then she flew all the way back. Not just to India. To this mudflat.'

'How did she find it?' asked Harini. 'There are thousands of beaches.' Chitti let the bird go, and she flew off low over the water. 'She knows this place,' said Chitti. 'The shape of the coast, the lighthouse, perhaps the smell of the mud. Older birds remember places.'

Arun was frowning. 'But what about the baby birds? The ones who have never been here?' Chitti smiled. 'That is the strange part. In this kind of bird, the parents leave the Arctic first. The young ones stay behind and eat and grow. A few weeks later, they fly south on their own. Nobody shows them the way.'

'Then how do they know where to go?' asked Arun. 'Scientists think the young are born knowing which direction to fly, and for about how long,' said Chitti. 'It is like a rule inside them: fly this way, for this many nights. Most of them reach the right part of the world by following that rule.'

Harini thought about this all through lunch, which was fish curry and rice at a small hotel by the jetty. Then she asked, 'Chitti, what if a storm blows a young bird a long way sideways?' 'Then she may keep flying her direction from the wrong place,' said Chitti. 'She would land somewhere new, far from her kind. Bird watchers sometimes find a lost young bird like that. Older birds are much better at finding their way back, because they know the places.'

That evening, on the verandah of the forest guest house, Harini had an idea. She put a steel tumbler at the far end of the verandah. 'Arun, this is the mudflat. You are a young sandpiper. Stand on the doorstep.' Arun stood on the doorstep, and Harini taught him to walk straight to the tumbler. It took him 6 steps.

Then she tied her dupatta over his eyes. 'Don't peek. Walk your 6 steps.' Arun walked 6 steps and stopped. The tumbler was right by his toes. 'Easy,' he said.

'Again,' said Harini. This time she led him around in a little circle, still with his eyes covered. Then, very quietly, she stood him two big steps to the left of the doorstep, facing the same way as before. 'Walk your 6 steps.' Arun walked 6 steps and stopped. Harini laughed. The tumbler was far away to his right.

Arun pulled off the dupatta. 'That's not fair! You moved me!' 'That is what the storm did to the young bird,' said Harini. 'Your steps were right. Your start was wrong.'

'Do it once more,' said Arun. Harini moved him again, to a new spot. This time, before she covered his eyes, she let him look for three seconds. He turned a little, and walked straight to the tumbler.

Arun stood still for a moment. 'So I knew two different things,' he said slowly. 'I knew the steps. And when I peeked, I knew the place.' 'That is it exactly,' said Chitti, who had been watching from the doorway. 'More than sixty years ago, a Dutch scientist found the same thing with starlings. The young ones knew a direction. The old ones also knew the place.'

The next morning, a big flock of sandpipers lifted off the mudflat all at once, turned in the light, and settled again further along. Somewhere among them was the bird with the old ring. Harini wondered where the young ones would go in spring, and whether, two winters from now, one of them would come back to this very mud, knowing the place.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Arun's counted steps worked only from the usual start, like a young bird's inborn direction. Moved without knowing, he missed. One look at the tumbler let him find it from anywhere, like an older bird who knows the place. Knowing a way and knowing a place are two different kinds of knowing.

## Experiments
### e1 · Moved in the Night: do your steps still find the bowl from a new start? (30 min)
- **question:** If you learn to reach a bowl by counting your steps, can you still find the bowl when someone moves your starting spot without telling you?
- **predict:** Guess first. When your eyes are covered and you start from a new spot, will your usual steps still take you to the bowl? If not, how far away will you stop? And if you peek at the bowl for three seconds first, what will happen? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every walk uses the same number of steps, the same cloth over your eyes, and your normal walking speed. Only one thing changes: where you start, and whether you peek. Try each way 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** The walker cannot see. Clear the floor of toys, slippers and wires first. A grown-up walks beside the child every time, ready to stop them before a wall or a table.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the floor from above: the bowl, the two starts, and a dot where you stopped each time.
- **results table:** Try | Where you started | Did you peek? | How far from the bowl you stopped (in foot-lengths)
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear the floor. 2. Put the bowl at one end. 3. Put a newspaper sheet about 5 or 6 normal steps away, facing the bowl. This is the usual start. 4. Put a second sheet about one metre to one side of the first. This is the secret start. Don't point it out.  
  *Parent says:* Here is our lake, and here is where a young bird starts her journey. Let's set it up carefully first.  
  *Child mode:* Put the bowl at the end of the room. This is the lake. Stand on the newspaper. That is your start.
  - Note: One metre to the side is about 4 grown-up foot-lengths. If your room is small, put the bowl 4 steps away and move the secret start 3 foot-lengths.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand your child on the usual start, facing the bowl. 2. Let them walk to the bowl with eyes open, three times. 3. Count the steps together. Write the number down.  
  *Parent says:* Walk to the lake with your eyes open. Count your steps out loud. Do it three times, so the number stays in your head.  
  *Child mode:* Walk to the bowl. Count your steps out loud. Do it three times. How many steps is it?
  - Ask: How many steps does it take you to reach the bowl? (good: The same number each time, about 5 or 6.; It was 6 the first time and 6 again.)
  - Note: Ask them to take normal steps, not giant ones, so the count stays the same.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Before any test, ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* Soon I will cover your eyes and maybe move you. Do you think your steps will still find the lake? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Your eyes will be covered. You might be moved. Will your steps still find the bowl? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen if I move you without telling you? (good: I will miss the bowl, because my steps go the same way.; I will still find it, because I remember where it is.)
  - Note: Don't say which guess is right. The test will answer it.
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cover your child's eyes with the cloth. 2. Turn them slowly round twice and walk them about the room for a few seconds, as in the next step. Then stand them on the usual start, facing the bowl. 3. Say 'Walk your steps.' 4. When they stop, measure how far their toes are from the bowl in your foot-lengths. 5. Do it 3 times and write each number.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes covered. You are on the usual start. Walk your steps, then stop. I'll measure how close you are.  
  *Child mode:* Eyes covered. Walk your steps. Stop. How close are you to the bowl?
  - Note: Walk beside them with a hand ready. Most children stop within 1 or 2 foot-lengths of the bowl. A step drifting a little to one side is normal.
- **s5 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cover your child's eyes. 2. Turn them slowly round twice, then walk them about the room for a few seconds. 3. Quietly stand them on the secret start, facing the same way as the usual start. 4. Say 'Walk your steps.' 5. Measure and write. 6. Do this 3 times. Each time, walk them about first.  
  *Parent says:* I'm going to walk you about a little. Then you start again. Walk your steps, and stop.  
  *Child mode:* Keep your eyes covered. I will walk you about. Then walk your steps and stop.
  - Note: Make sure the secret start faces exactly the same way as the usual start. Children usually stop 3 to 5 foot-lengths to one side of the bowl. That is the result to look for.
  - Check: From the secret start, you walked your usual steps. Where did you stop? → **About as far to one side of the bowl as the start had been moved**
- **s6 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand your child on the secret start with eyes open. 2. Let them look at the bowl for three seconds. 3. Cover their eyes. 4. Say 'Now find the bowl.' 5. Measure and write. 6. Do this 3 times.  
  *Parent says:* This time you get a quick peek at the lake first, only three seconds. Then find it with your eyes covered.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the bowl for three seconds. Now eyes covered. Find the bowl!
  - Ask: What did you do differently after the peek? (good: I turned a little towards the bowl before walking.; I walked to where I saw it, not just my steps.)
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the three rows of numbers together. Ask your child to say what they found as a claim, the evidence and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Look at our numbers. Say what you found, which numbers show it, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your numbers. What did you find? Which numbers show it? Why?
  - Ask: Why did your steps miss the bowl from the secret start, but not after the peek? (good: The steps only know which way and how far, so a new start sends them to a new place.; When I peeked, I knew where the bowl was, so I could find it from anywhere.)
  - Note: A strong answer says that the steps carried a direction and a distance, and that the peek gave the place. This is the difference between a young bird and an older one.
  - Think: A young bird on her first journey flies a direction she was born with. What would happen to her if a storm moved her?
### e2 · Wind at Your Back: how much further does a tailwind carry a paper butterfly? (25 min)
- **question:** How much further does a falling paper butterfly travel when a wind blows from behind?
- **predict:** Guess first. With no wind, where will the butterfly land? With the fan blowing behind it, how far will it go: a little further, twice as far, or more? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same butterfly, dropped from the same height, from the same spot, every time. Keep the fan at the same speed and the same distance. Only the wind changes. Drop it 3 times each way, because one drop can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up sets up and switches on the fan. Keep fingers and paper away from the blades.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the two kinds of drop side by side, with an arrow for the wind.
- **results table:** Drop | Wind | How far from the spot below the hand it landed (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pinch the paper square in the middle to make two wings. 2. Clip the middle with a paper clip. 3. Switch off the ceiling fan and close the windows.  
  *Parent says:* Let's make a butterfly. The clip is her body. We switch off the ceiling fan, so only our own wind blows.  
  *Child mode:* Pinch the paper in the middle. Clip it. Now you have a butterfly!
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand in one spot. 2. Hold the butterfly at shoulder height with your arm straight out. 3. Put a coin on the floor right under your hand.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the butterfly with your arm straight out. The coin marks the spot right under it.  
  *Child mode:* Hold out your arm. Put a coin under your hand. That is the start spot.
  - Note: Keep the same height every time. Shoulder height with a straight arm is easy to repeat.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Where will the butterfly land with no wind? And with the fan behind her? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Where will she land with no wind? And with wind behind? Why?
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Drop the butterfly with no fan on. 2. Measure from the coin to where it landed, in centimetres. 3. Do it 3 times. Write each number.  
  *Parent says:* Let go, don't throw. Then measure how far it landed from the coin.  
  *Child mode:* Let go gently. Don't throw! Measure from the coin. Do it 3 times.
  - Note: With no wind, the butterfly usually lands within about 20 or 30 cm of the coin, wobbling on the way down.
- **s5 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the fan about one metre behind the dropper, pointing the way the butterfly will go. 2. Switch it on at its lowest speed. 3. Drop the butterfly from the same height. 4. Measure from the coin. 5. Do it 3 times.  
  *Parent says:* Now the fan is our tailwind. Same height, same spot. Let go, and measure again.  
  *Child mode:* Now the fan blows behind you. Let go from the same spot. Measure. Do it 3 times.
  - Note: On the lowest fan speed, the butterfly often lands one to two metres away. If she flies off the table or the fan is too strong, move the fan further back.
  - Check: With the fan blowing behind, the butterfly landed much further from the coin. Why? → **The moving air pushed her along while she fell**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the numbers together. Work out how many times further the wind carried her. Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* How many times further did she go with the wind? What does that tell us about tiny travellers like dragonflies?  
  *Child mode:* How much further did she go with the wind? What does that mean for a dragonfly?
  - Ask: Why do many migrating birds and insects wait for a tailwind before they set off? (good: The wind carries them further for the same work.; They save fuel, so they can go further before they need to eat.)
  - Think: A globe skimmer dragonfly weighs less than a paper clip. How could she cross the sea?

### c1 · The Lightest Tracker: how much can a flyer carry? (30 min)
- **problem:** A tracker that is too heavy can tire a bird or make her fall behind. Find out how much extra weight a paper plane can carry before it flies much shorter, and where to put the weight so it flies best.
- **your turn:** Now design the best place and the lightest tracker for your plane. Draw it, test it 3 times, and write how far it flew compared with no tracker.
- **constraints:** Use one paper plane for every test.; The 'tracker' is paper clips, taped on.; Throw from the same spot, with the same gentle push, 3 times for each test.
- **general:** Scientists use a rule of thumb: a tracker should weigh no more than about 3 parts in every 100 of the bird's weight.; Trackers are fixed near the middle of the back, close to where the body balances, so they don't pull the bird off balance.; Solar panels on trackers mean smaller batteries, so the tracker can be lighter.
- **creative:** Fix the clips on the middle of the plane instead of the nose or tail, and see if it balances better.; Make a lighter 'tracker' by cutting one clip in half, or using a scrap of foil instead.; Some scientists don't put trackers on birds at all. They use radar, cameras or the songs birds make at night.

## Applications
- Bird scientists and forest staff protect wetlands like Chilika Lake, Pulicat Lake and Point Calimere, because birds from far away depend on them.
- Countries along a flyway agree to protect the travellers together, because one bird needs safe places in many countries.
- Some cities dim building lights in the migration months so night-flying birds are not confused.
- Weather radar is used to see big nights of bird migration, and to keep birds and aeroplanes apart near airports.
- Villagers in Khichan, Rajasthan, feed thousands of demoiselle cranes every winter, and visitors come to see them.
- In Bangladesh, fishing is banned for some days each year while hilsa swim up the rivers to lay their eggs.
- Volunteers count birds in Indian cities and villages and share the lists online, helping scientists see when travellers arrive.
- Engineers design road tunnels and bridges for travelling animals, like the crab bridges on Christmas Island.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how do birds sense the Earth's magnetism?** A protein in their eyes may be involved, and nerves in the beak or ear may help. Nobody has yet shown the whole way it works.
- **Do globe skimmer dragonflies really cross the ocean between India and Africa?** The evidence points that way, but nobody has followed a single dragonfly the whole route.
- **How is a young bird's direction and distance passed down from her parents?** Experiments show it is inborn, but scientists are still finding out how it is carried in the body.
- **How do monarch great-great-grandchildren find the very same mountain forests?** They use the Sun to keep their direction, but how they reach those exact forests is not fully known.
- **How do whales find their way across thousands of kilometres of open ocean?** Some humpbacks swim in very straight lines for days. Scientists think they may use the Sun, the Earth's magnetism or the stars, but nobody is sure.
- **Where do many small birds that visit India actually spend the summer, and which route do they take?** Many are too small to carry trackers, so their routes are still being worked out from rings and bird counts.

## Connected maths
- In the bowl test, Harini's three walks from the usual start ended 1, 2 and 1 foot-lengths from the bowl. Her three walks from the secret start ended 4, 5 and 4 foot-lengths away. On average, how many more foot-lengths away was she from the secret start? **About 3 foot-lengths further.**. From the usual start: 1 + 2 + 1 = 4, shared over 3 walks is a little more than 1 each. From the secret start: 4 + 5 + 4 = 13, a little more than 4 each. 4 take away 1 is 3. So she stopped about 3 foot-lengths further away, about as far as she had been moved. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In the paper butterfly test, the butterfly landed 15 cm, 25 cm and 20 cm from the coin with no wind. With the fan behind, she landed 120 cm, 160 cm and 140 cm away. Is the tailwind effect real, or could it be a fluke? **Real. Every wind drop was further than every no-wind drop.**. The longest no-wind drop was 25 cm. The shortest wind drop was 120 cm. Even the worst wind drop was nearly 5 times the best no-wind drop. If the wind made no difference, the two sets would overlap. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- A humpback whale swims about 8,000 km from a cold sea to a warm sea, and the same distance back. How far is that in one year? **16,000 km.**. 8,000 km there and 8,000 km back. 8,000 + 8,000 = 16,000 km. India is about 3,200 km from Kashmir to Kanyakumari, so that is about five times the length of India. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- Monarch butterflies leave Mexico in March. The journey north takes about 4 generations, and each generation lives about 1 month. In about which month do they reach Canada? **About July.**. Start in March and count 4 months on: April, May, June, July. So they reach Canada in about July. The exact timing changes from year to year. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- Scientists use a rule that a tracker can weigh no more than 3 grams for every 100 grams of bird. A pigeon weighs about 300 grams. What is the heaviest tracker she should carry? **9 grams.**. 300 grams is 3 groups of 100 grams. Each group allows 3 grams. 3 groups of 3 grams is 9 grams. A small sandpiper of about 60 grams could carry less than 2 grams. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
