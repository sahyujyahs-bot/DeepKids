# The water cycle: where rain comes from and where it goes
`sci.water.water-cycle.L3`

**Summary.** Every raindrop was once water on the land or in the sea. The Sun warms that water, and some of it evaporates into an invisible gas called water vapour. High up the air is cold, so the vapour condenses into tiny drops, and billions of drops make a cloud. The drops grow and fall as rain, hail or snow, and rivers carry the water back to the sea. Salt cannot evaporate, so rain is fresh even though most of it rose from the salty sea.

**Intro.** *Wet your hand and press it flat on a dry floor tile in the sunshine. Watch your handprint. Count slowly. Where is the water going? It did not soak into the tile.* In a minute or two, your handprint vanishes. The water has not been destroyed. It has gone into the air, as an invisible gas. It might come back down as rain next week, a hundred kilometres away. Every drop of rain you have ever seen went up first. This unit follows water on its round journey: up from the sea, into the clouds, down as rain, along rivers and back to the sea. You will make a real cloud in a jar, and get fresh water out of salty water using only sunshine.

## Terms
- **Water cycle**: The journey water makes again and again. It goes up from the sea and land into the air, comes down as rain or snow, and flows back to the sea. It has no start and no end. *e.g.* The rain on your roof today may have been in the Arabian Sea last week.
- **Liquid**: One of the forms a material can take. A liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. *e.g.* Water in a glass, milk in a cup and oil in a bottle are liquids.
- **Gas**: One of the forms a material can take. A gas spreads out to fill all the space it can. Most gases are invisible. *e.g.* The air around you is a mix of gases. You cannot see it, but you can feel it as wind.
- **Water vapour**: Water as a gas. It is completely invisible. There is always some in the air around you. *e.g.* On a sticky monsoon day, the air is full of water vapour. That is why your clothes take so long to dry.
- **Evaporate**: When liquid water slowly turns into water vapour and goes into the air. Heat, wind and sunshine make it faster. *e.g.* Wet clothes on a line dry because their water evaporates.
- **Condense**: When water vapour cools down and turns back into tiny drops of liquid water. It happens on cold surfaces, and in air that is cooling. *e.g.* Drops appear on the outside of a steel tumbler full of ice. They came out of the air.
- **Steam**: Steam is the water vapour that comes off boiling water. Like all water vapour, it is invisible. The white cloud you see above a kettle is tiny drops that have already condensed. *e.g.* Look closely at a boiling kettle's spout. Right next to it there is a clear gap. The white mist starts a little further away.
- **Cloud**: A huge crowd of tiny water drops, or tiny ice crystals, floating in the air. Each drop is far too small to see by itself. *e.g.* A white cloud over the hills is made of billions and billions of tiny drops.
- **Condensation nucleus (cloud seed)**: A tiny speck of dust, salt, smoke or pollen floating in the air. Water vapour condenses onto it to make a cloud drop. The plural is condensation nuclei. *e.g.* Over the sea, tiny grains of salt from sea spray become the seeds of cloud drops.
- **Dew**: Drops of water that form on cool grass, leaves and cars on a still night. The water comes out of the air, by condensation. *e.g.* On a winter morning, the grass in the park is wet even though it did not rain.
- **Fog and mist**: A cloud that sits on the ground. Mist is a thin one. Fog is a thick one you can hardly see through. *e.g.* In winter, Delhi and Punjab often have thick fog in the early morning, and trains run late.
- **Precipitation**: Any water that falls from clouds to the ground: rain, drizzle, hail or snow. It is a long word for 'falling water'. *e.g.* Rain in Mumbai, hail in a summer storm in Bihar and snow in Kashmir are all precipitation.
- **Hail**: Balls of ice that fall from big storm clouds. They grow as they are tossed up and down inside the cloud. *e.g.* A hailstorm can dent car roofs and damage crops, even on a hot day.
- **Transpiration**: Plants giving off water vapour from tiny holes in their leaves. The water comes up from the roots, through the stem. *e.g.* A big tree can put many buckets of water into the air on one hot day.
- **Molecule**: The smallest piece of water that is still water. Each water molecule is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom. One drop has more molecules than there are grains of sand on all the beaches of India. *e.g.* When a puddle evaporates, its molecules fly off into the air one by one.
- **Energy**: What it takes to make something move, warm up or change. Energy never disappears. It only changes from one kind into another. *e.g.* The Sun's heat gives water the energy to evaporate. That energy comes out again when the vapour turns back into drops.
- **Monsoon**: A season of strong, wet winds that blow from the sea onto the land and bring heavy rain. In most of India, the monsoon comes from June to September. *e.g.* The monsoon usually reaches Kerala around the first of June. Farmers wait for it to sow their crops.
- **Rain shadow**: A dry area on the far side of mountains. The wet wind drops most of its rain on the near side, so little is left. *e.g.* Mumbai, on the sea side of the Western Ghats, gets about three times as much rain as Pune, on the far side.
- **Humid**: Humid air has a lot of water vapour in it. It feels sticky, and sweat does not dry easily. *e.g.* Chennai in summer is humid. A Rajasthan summer is dry.
- **Salt pan**: A flat, shallow pond where sea water is left in the sun. The water evaporates and the salt is left behind for people to collect. *e.g.* Most of India's salt comes from salt pans in Gujarat.
- **Solar still**: A simple machine that uses sunshine to make fresh water from salty or dirty water. The water evaporates, condenses on a cover and drips into a cup. *e.g.* You will build one with a bowl, a cup and cling film.
- **Methane**: A gas we use as fuel. On Earth it is a gas, but on Saturn's very cold moon Titan it is a liquid, and it falls as rain. *e.g.* The piped gas that some city kitchens cook with, and biogas made from cow dung, are mostly methane.

## Explanations
### What the water cycle is · *defined*
**Definition:** The water cycle is the journey water makes round and round. It rises from the sea and the land into the air, forms clouds, falls as rain or snow, and flows back to the sea.

The water cycle has four big steps. First, the Sun warms water, and some of it rises into the air as an invisible gas. This is evaporation. Second, high up the air is cold, and the gas turns back into tiny drops that make clouds. This is condensation. Third, the drops grow and fall as rain, hail or snow. This is precipitation. Fourth, the water gathers in rivers, lakes, the ground and the sea. Then it starts again. The cycle has no beginning and no end. You can join it anywhere. Almost no water leaves the Earth, and almost no new water arrives. The same water just keeps going round.
- e.g. A puddle dries in the sun. Its water is now in the air, and will fall as rain somewhere.
- e.g. The Ganga carries rain and melted snow from the Himalaya to the Bay of Bengal. From the sea, the water rises again.
- e.g. Your breath on a cold mirror makes a tiny cloud, and it dries up again in seconds.
### Evaporation: water going into the air as a gas · *defined*
**Definition:** Evaporation is liquid water slowly turning into water vapour, an invisible gas, and mixing into the air. It happens at any temperature, not only when water boils.

Water is made of tiny particles called molecules. They are always jiggling and bumping into each other. At the top of a puddle, some molecules jiggle so fast that they break free and fly off into the air. That is evaporation. Warmth makes the molecules jiggle faster, so more of them escape. Wind blows the escaped molecules away, so they don't fall back in. A wide puddle has a bigger surface than a narrow glass, so more molecules can escape at once. Dry air takes up vapour more easily than sticky, humid air. So clothes dry fastest on a hot, windy, dry day, spread out flat.
- e.g. Wet clothes on a terrace dry faster in the sun and wind than in a closed bathroom.
- e.g. A wide plate of water dries up long before the same water in a narrow glass.
- e.g. In the Rajasthan summer, a wet floor dries in minutes. In a sticky Mumbai monsoon, it can take an hour.
### Water vapour is invisible, and so is real steam · *defined*
**Definition:** Water vapour is a gas, and it is completely invisible. The white mist above a kettle or a pressure cooker is not vapour. It is a crowd of tiny liquid drops that have already condensed.

The air in your room right now has water vapour in it. You cannot see it, smell it or feel it. Look closely at a kettle as it boils. Right at the spout there is a small clear gap. That gap is the real, invisible steam. A little further out, the steam meets cooler air and turns back into tiny drops. Those drops are what you see as white mist. So the white puff from a pressure cooker whistle is really a tiny cloud. Clouds in the sky are made the same way.
- e.g. The clear gap just above a kettle spout is the invisible steam.
- e.g. On a cold morning in Shimla, you can 'see your breath'. You are seeing tiny drops, not vapour.
- e.g. A drying shirt sends water into the air, but you never see it go.
### Condensation: water coming back out of the air · *defined*
**Definition:** Condensation is water vapour cooling down and turning back into tiny drops of liquid water. It happens when vapour touches something cold, or when air cools as it rises.

Cold makes molecules slow down. When slow molecules of vapour bump into each other, they stick together and make a drop. That is condensation, and it is evaporation run backwards. So any cold surface collects water from the air. A steel tumbler of ice gets wet on the outside. The inside of a lid over hot rice drips. The grass gets dew on a cool night. Air that rises up a mountain or high into the sky also cools, and its vapour condenses into clouds.
- e.g. A cold steel tumbler gets wet on the outside on a sticky day. The drops came from the air, not through the steel.
- e.g. The underside of the lid on a pot of hot dal is covered in drops.
- e.g. Car windows mist up from the inside when a whole family breathes in a closed car.
### Clouds: tiny drops on specks of dust · *defined*
**Definition:** A cloud is a huge crowd of tiny water drops, or tiny ice crystals, floating in the air. Each drop forms around a speck of dust, salt, smoke or pollen.

Vapour finds it hard to make a drop out of nothing. It needs something to stick to. In the air there are billions of tiny specks: dust, salt from sea spray, smoke and pollen. Vapour condenses onto them, and each speck grows into a cloud drop. A Scottish scientist, John Aitken, showed this in 1880. He filtered all the dust out of air in a jar, and then it would not make a cloud. Cloud drops are tiny, about one hundredth of a millimetre across. They are so small and light that rising air holds them up, and they fall very slowly. But a cloud is not light. An American cloud scientist, Peggy LeMone, worked out that one ordinary puffy cloud holds about 500,000 kilograms of water. That is as heavy as about 100 elephants, spread out over a huge space.
- e.g. Smoke from an agarbatti, an incense stick, helps the cloud in a jar form much faster.
- e.g. Over the sea, tiny grains of salt from sea spray are the seeds of many cloud drops.
- e.g. Clouds look white because their billions of drops scatter sunlight. Very thick clouds look grey or dark because less light gets through.
### How cloud drops become raindrops · *debated*
A raindrop is about 100 times wider than a cloud drop. So it takes about a million cloud drops to make one raindrop. Scientists agree on two ways this happens. But in warm clouds over India, rain often forms faster than these ideas can fully explain. How exactly it happens so fast is still being studied.
- e.g. Rain can pour out of a big monsoon cloud within half an hour of it forming.
- e.g. In cold clouds, high up, rain often starts as snow and melts on the way down.
- e.g. One raindrop holds about as much water as a million cloud drops.
- **Alfred Wegener (1911), Tor Bergeron (1933) and Walter Findeisen (1938), German and Swedish weather scientists:** In the cold top of a cloud, ice crystals and supercooled drops sit side by side. Supercooled drops are drops colder than freezing that have not frozen yet. The ice crystals grow by taking water from the drops. They become heavy snowflakes, fall, and melt into rain on the way down.
- **Many weather scientists, for warm tropical clouds:** In warm clouds there is no ice. Bigger drops fall a little faster, bump into smaller ones and swallow them. This is called collision and coalescence. It works best when the cloud has a few big seeds, like grains of sea salt.
- **Scientists studying clouds today, including in India:** Swirling, bumpy air inside clouds, called turbulence, may throw drops together much more often. That could explain why rain forms so fast. Exactly how much turbulence matters is still argued about. Computers and cloud experiments are testing it.
### Rain, drizzle, hail and snow · *defined*
**Definition:** Precipitation is any water falling from clouds: rain, drizzle, hail or snow. Which one falls depends on how cold the cloud and the air below it are.

Drizzle is very small drops from low, thin clouds. Rain is bigger drops. Snow is ice crystals that stay frozen all the way down, as in Kashmir and Ladakh in winter. Hail is balls of ice from huge storm clouds. Strong rising winds keep them up inside the cloud, where they collect layer after layer of ice, like an onion. Raindrops are not shaped like teardrops. Small ones are round. Bigger ones are squashed flat at the bottom, like a burger bun, by the air they fall through. Very big drops wobble and break apart before they reach the ground.
- e.g. A cut-open hailstone shows rings, like an onion. Each ring is a layer of ice it collected while the winds kept it up in the cloud.
- e.g. Snow falls in Gulmarg in winter. The same kind of cloud over Delhi would give rain.
- e.g. Drawings of raindrops as teardrops are wrong. Real falling drops look like little buns.
### Why rain is fresh but the sea is salty · *defined*
**Definition:** When sea water evaporates, only the water goes up into the air. The salt stays behind. So rain is fresh water, even though most of it rose from the salty sea.

Salt is dissolved in the sea, about 6 teaspoons in every litre. When the Sun warms the sea, water molecules break free and fly up. Salt cannot evaporate at these temperatures, so it is left behind. This is why salt pans work: people let sea water evaporate in the sun and rake up the salt. Rivers carry tiny amounts of salt from rocks into the sea. The water leaves again as vapour, but the salt cannot. Over hundreds of millions of years, this has made the sea salty. A solar still copies the sky. It lets salty water evaporate, catches the vapour on a cover, and collects fresh drops.
- e.g. Gujarat's salt pans make most of India's salt, just by letting sea water evaporate.
- e.g. Rain water caught in a clean bowl is not salty, even at the seaside.
- e.g. A salty tear or sweat dries to leave a white mark on your skin or shirt. The water left, the salt stayed.
### Plants put water into the air · *defined*
**Definition:** Transpiration is plants giving off water vapour through tiny holes in their leaves. The water is pulled up from the roots, through the stem, to the leaves.

A plant drinks through its roots. Most of that water does not stay in the plant. It travels up the stem and leaves through tiny holes, too small to see, on the underside of leaves. As this water evaporates, it pulls more water up behind it, like sipping through a straw. A big tree can give off more than a hundred litres on a hot day. So a forest puts a huge amount of invisible water vapour into the air. Some of the rain that falls far inland, in Central India or the Amazon rainforest, has already been through the leaves of trees on the way. Scientists think more than half of the water rising from land comes out through plants. Exactly how much is still argued about.
- e.g. A clear bag tied over a leafy branch fills with mist in an hour.
- e.g. A wet patch under a tree is cooler, partly because its leaves give off water.
- e.g. In the Amazon, water goes from rain to tree to cloud to rain again, many times, as the winds carry it inland.
### The Sun's energy drives the water cycle · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun's energy drives the water cycle. Water needs energy to evaporate. When the vapour condenses into cloud drops, that energy comes out again as heat.

To break free and fly off, water molecules need energy, and they take it from the Sun's warmth. That is why wet skin feels cold: evaporation takes heat away. The energy is not lost. It travels up inside the vapour. When the vapour condenses in a cloud, the energy comes out again, and warms the air around it. Warm air rises, so the cloud grows taller. This is how a small cloud can grow into a huge thundercloud in an afternoon. Cyclones over the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea get their great strength this way, from warm sea water that evaporates and condenses. Gravity does the other half of the work. It pulls rain down and makes rivers flow to the sea.
- e.g. Wet hands feel cold in a breeze, because evaporation takes heat away.
- e.g. A tall thundercloud on a summer afternoon is powered by energy from vapour turning into drops.
- e.g. Cyclones form only over warm seas, and they get weaker once they move over land.
### The monsoon: India's giant water cycle · *defined*
**Definition:** The monsoon is a season of wet winds that blow from the sea onto the land and bring heavy rain. In most of India, it comes from June to September and gives most of the year's rain.

In early summer, the land of India gets very hot. Wind starts blowing in from the cooler Indian Ocean and Arabian Sea, carrying huge amounts of water vapour. When this wet air meets mountains, like the Western Ghats or the hills of the North-East, it is pushed upwards. Rising air cools, the vapour condenses, and rain pours down on the sea side of the mountains. Little is left for the far side, which stays drier. That dry side is called a rain shadow. The monsoon usually reaches Kerala around 1 June and covers all of India by about 8 July. Mawsynram and Sohra, also called Cherrapunji, in Meghalaya, get more than 11 metres of rain in a year. They are among the wettest places on Earth.
- e.g. Mumbai, on the sea side of the Western Ghats, gets about three times as much rain as Pune, on the far side.
- e.g. Farmers across India wait for the monsoon before sowing rice, cotton and many other crops.
- e.g. The rivers of the Western Ghats, like the Kaveri, swell after the monsoon reaches the hills.
### What really drives the monsoon · *debated*
Everyone agrees on what the monsoon does. Why it happens is a harder question. Scientists have given different answers, and they still argue about how much each one matters.
- e.g. Small sea breezes blow onto the land on hot afternoons at Chennai or Mumbai beaches. Is the monsoon a giant version?
- e.g. The Tibetan plateau, a huge high land north of the Himalaya, gets very hot in summer.
- **Edmond Halley, an English scientist, in 1686, and many school books:** The monsoon is a giant sea breeze. In summer, the land heats up faster than the sea. Hot air over the land rises, and cooler, wet air from the sea flows in to take its place.
- **Many weather scientists today:** The sea breeze idea is only part of it. Around the Earth's middle there is a belt of rising air and rain. In summer, this rain belt moves north, over India, following the Sun. The monsoon is that rain belt arriving.
- **Other scientists:** The high, hot Tibetan plateau and the Himalaya act like a giant heater and a wall. They help pull the monsoon in and keep it there. How much this matters compared with the rain belt is still debated.
### How long water stays in each place · *defined*
**Definition:** Water stays in different parts of the cycle for very different times. On average, a drop stays in the air for only about 9 days, but it can stay in the sea for thousands of years.

The water cycle has fast parts and slow parts. Water in the air comes down again in about 9 days on average. Water in a river reaches the sea in a few weeks or months. In the deep sea, a drop may stay for about 3,000 years before it evaporates again. Deep groundwater can wait thousands of years. The ice deep in Antarctica is the slowest of all. Scientists have drilled out Antarctic ice that froze about 800,000 years ago, and found tiny bubbles of ancient air inside it.
- e.g. Rain from a monsoon cloud today was probably sea water less than two weeks ago.
- e.g. A drop that sinks into the deep sea may not rise again for 3,000 years.
- e.g. Some ice in Antarctica has been frozen since long before the first humans.
### People are part of the water cycle now · *defined*
**Definition:** People now change the water cycle a lot. Dams, canals, borewells and cities move huge amounts of water, and a warmer world puts more water vapour into the air.

Most water cycle pictures show only sea, clouds, mountains and rivers. In 2019, a team of scientists led by Benjamin Abbott looked at hundreds of these pictures. Most of them left people out completely. But people store rivers behind dams, pump up groundwater for farms, and cover land with cement so rain runs off. A warmer world matters too. Warmer air can hold more water vapour, about 7 more parts in every 100 for each degree of warming. So when it rains, it can pour harder. In 2017, Roxy Mathew Koll and others found that days of very heavy rain over central India had become about three times more common since 1950.
- e.g. A dam on a river holds back water that would have flowed to the sea.
- e.g. Water pumped from a borewell to a rice field evaporates or soaks in, instead of staying underground.
- e.g. Some cities flood in the monsoon and run short of water in summer.
### Living things in the clouds · *idea*
Some tiny living things, too small to see, are carried high into the clouds by the wind. Some kinds of bacteria are very good at helping water freeze. In 2008, Brent Christner and other scientists tested fresh snow from several countries. Many of the specks that started its ice crystals came from living things, including bacteria. These specks help ice crystals start forming in clouds at temperatures where water would usually stay liquid. Some scientists think this could help the bacteria. They ride up from leaves, help make rain or snow, and fall back down onto new plants. This idea is called bioprecipitation. Scientists are still finding out how much it really matters for the world's rain.
- e.g. A single type of bacteria that lives on leaves is one of the best ice-makers known.
- e.g. Fresh snow collected in several different countries had these ice-making specks from living things in it.
### Rain on other worlds · *defined*
**Definition:** Other worlds have their own weather cycles. On Titan, Saturn's biggest moon, it rains liquid methane, which fills rivers and lakes.

Titan is so cold, about 180 degrees below zero, that water is rock-hard ice there. But methane, which is a gas on Earth, is a liquid on Titan. It evaporates from lakes, makes clouds, and falls as slow, heavy rain. It carves rivers and fills seas. In January 2005, a probe called Huygens landed on Titan and photographed river channels and pebbles. Another spacecraft, Cassini, flew past Titan many times and found big lakes and seas near its poles. Venus has clouds of sulphuric acid, a strong acid. Acid drops fall from them, but the air below is so hot that they evaporate long before they reach the ground. So going up, coming down and going round is not something only water does.
- e.g. Titan is the only place we know, other than Earth, with rain, rivers and lakes on its surface today.
- e.g. On Venus, acid drops fall from the clouds but evaporate high above the ground.
### How people found out that rain feeds rivers · *defined*
**Definition:** We know that rivers and springs are fed by rain and melting snow, because people measured the rain and the rivers and found the rain was more than enough.

For about two thousand years, many learned people thought rain was far too little to keep rivers flowing all year. They believed rivers were fed by sea water rising through secret passages under the mountains. In 1580, Bernard Palissy, a French potter who never went to university, wrote that rivers and springs come only from rain soaking into the ground. Scholars took little notice of him. About a hundred years later, Pierre Perrault measured the rain falling around the top of the river Seine for three years. In 1674, he showed it was about six times more than the river carried. Edmond Halley then measured how fast water evaporates from a pan. He showed that the Sun lifts enough water from the sea to make all the rain.
- e.g. Palissy was right, but he had no measurements, so few people believed him.
- e.g. You can do Perrault's kind of work at home: measure the rain with a bottle, and the evaporation with a plate.

## Mostly agreed
- Water goes round and round between the sea, the air and the land. Very little water leaves the Earth or arrives from space. *How we know:* Scientists measure rain, river flow and evaporation all over the world. Year after year, the water that goes up and the water that comes down roughly balance.
- Clouds are made of tiny liquid drops or ice crystals, not of water vapour. *How we know:* Aeroplanes fitted with instruments fly through clouds and collect the drops. Vapour is invisible, but clouds can be seen, because their drops scatter light.
- When sea water evaporates, the salt stays behind. So rain is fresh water. *How we know:* Salt pans have made salt this way for thousands of years. Rain water tested near the sea has almost no salt in it.
- The Sun's energy drives evaporation, and that energy comes out again when vapour condenses into clouds. *How we know:* Wet skin cools as water evaporates. Weather balloons show that air warms up inside growing clouds, where vapour is condensing.
- Rivers and springs are fed by rain and melting snow. *How we know:* In 1674, Pierre Perrault showed that the rain around the river Seine was about six times more than the river carried. River levels across India rise after the monsoon rain.
- Warmer air can hold more water vapour, so a warmer world gets heavier downpours. *How we know:* This follows from basic physics, and weather records show it. Studies of India's rain since 1950 find more days of very heavy rain over central India.

## How we found out
**Bernard Palissy, Pierre Perrault and Edmond Halley, 1580 to 1687.** For about two thousand years, many learned people in Europe believed rain was far too little to keep rivers flowing all year. They thought sea water travelled through hidden passages under the mountains and came out as springs. In 1580, Bernard Palissy, a French potter who had never been to university, wrote that springs and rivers come only from rain soaking into the ground. Few scholars listened, because he had no measurements. About ninety years later, Pierre Perrault measured the rain falling on the land around the start of the river Seine for three years. He also worked out how much water the river carried. In 1674, he showed that the rain was about six times more than the river carried. Soon after, Edmond Halley measured how fast water evaporates from a warmed pan. He worked out that the Sun lifts enough water from the sea to make all the rain. Careful measuring, not arguing, settled the question.  
*What people believed before:* Rivers and springs are fed by sea water rising through hidden passages under the mountains, because rain is far too little.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Clouds are made of water vapour, or steam.** (Many children, and many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Clouds are made of water, and they float like a gas.  
  Against: Water vapour is invisible. Clouds can be seen because they are made of tiny liquid drops or ice. The white mist above a kettle is tiny drops too, not vapour.
- **Water only evaporates when it boils.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Boiling water clearly turns into steam, and we use heat to dry things fast.  
  Against: A puddle dries on a cool day, and wet clothes dry in the shade. Water evaporates at any temperature. Heat only makes it faster.
- **Rivers and springs are fed by sea water rising through passages under the mountains.** (Many learned people in Europe for about two thousand years, until the 1600s; *rejected*)  
  For: Rivers flow all year, even in dry months. People could not believe rain was enough.  
  Against: Bernard Palissy argued against it in 1580. Pierre Perrault measured it in 1674, and found the rain was about six times what the river carried. Springs are fresh, not salty, too.
- **The water cycle is a neat circle of sea, clouds, rain and rivers, untouched by people.** (Most water cycle pictures in books and on websites; *rejected*)  
  For: It is a simple way to show the main journey.  
  Against: In 2019, scientists led by Benjamin Abbott checked hundreds of these pictures, and most left people out. Today, dams, borewells, farms and cities move huge amounts of water.
- **Cloud seeding can make it rain whenever we want.** (Some news reports, and some companies that sell it; *debated*)  
  For: Since 1946, when Vincent Schaefer made snow in a cold box with dry ice, seeding clouds with tiny specks has made some clouds rain or snow more.  
  Against: Seeding only works on the right kind of cloud, which must already be close to raining. Scientists at the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology in Pune tested it over Solapur in Maharashtra. They found it can add some rain, but only from suitable clouds. It cannot make rain from a clear sky.
- **Forests draw wet air in from the sea, like a pump.** (Anastassia Makarieva and Victor Gorshkov, two Russian scientists, in 2007. They call it the 'biotic pump'.; *minority*)  
  For: Forests give off huge amounts of water vapour. When it condenses, the air pressure drops, and that could draw wet air in from the sea. Inland forests like the Amazon really do recycle rain many times.  
  Against: Many weather scientists agree that forests recycle rain. But they doubt that forests pull in sea air as strongly as the biotic pump says. The argument is still going on.
- **Raindrops are shaped like teardrops.** (Almost every drawing of rain; *rejected*)  
  For: Drops dripping off a tap look like teardrops, so it seems natural.  
  Against: High-speed photos show falling raindrops are round when small and flattened like a bun when bigger. The teardrop shape only appears as a drop clings to a tap, just before it falls.

## Roots
We have been learning how water goes up into the air, turns into clouds and comes down as rain. Now we ask what the water cycle tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The air in an 'empty' room holds water you cannot see. Cool that air, and drops appear on a cold plate. A cloud forms the same way: the water was already in the air, and cooling made it visible. So seeing nothing does not mean nothing is there.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call air that looks clear 'empty', and we think a cloud is water arriving in the sky. But water vapour is invisible, so our eyes cannot tell us whether water is there.  
*Evidence:* In A Cloud in a Jar, a cloud appeared in clear air as soon as the icy plate cooled it. Your breath misted a cold plate, then vanished into the air again. In The Cold Tumbler, clear, plain drops came out of the room's air.

- *who am i:* More than half of your body is water. Every breath you breathe out carries some of it into the air, where nobody can see it.
- *what is life:* Life is part of the water cycle. Plants lift water from the soil and give it to the air. Animals drink the rain and give it back.
- *what is this world:* A world where the sea becomes sky, and the sky becomes rivers, again and again. Much of this journey happens where we cannot see it.

The same water is sea, then invisible vapour, then a cloud, rain, a river and part of a neem tree. We give each of these a different name, but it is one water changing form. DeepKids believes the whole world is like this: one source taking many forms, some seen and some unseen. The water cycle is a place where you can watch one water become many things, and come back again.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.10.5–6:** The Upanishad describes a journey. One becomes air, then smoke, then mist, then a cloud. The cloud rains down, and one is born again as rice, barley, plants and trees. (The Upanishad is about what happens to a person after death, not about weather. But it pictures life travelling with water: from the sky, as rain, into plants. That is the path the water cycle really takes.)
- **Heraclitus, a Greek thinker, about 2,500 years ago (Fragment 12, as numbered by the scholars Diels and Kranz):** As people step into the same rivers, different waters keep flowing past them. (A river keeps its name, but its water is always new. A cloud is the same: its drops keep drying up at the edges while new ones form.)
- **Ecclesiastes 1:7, in the Hebrew Bible:** All the rivers run into the sea, yet the sea is not full. The rivers go back to the place they came from, and flow again. (The writer had noticed the puzzle: rivers pour into the sea for ever, but it never overflows. The water cycle is the answer.)
- Science (settled): On average, a water molecule stays in the air for only about 9 days before it falls again. The water in a cloud is always new.
- Science (settled): The water in your body is replaced again and again. Studies that trace water with special labels show that an adult swaps a few litres of body water every day, out of about 40 litres.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India: The Charvakas said that only what we can perceive with our senses is real. For them the world is just earth, water, fire and air mixing, with no one source behind it. The water cycle, they would say, is simply water and heat doing what they do, and nothing more needs to be added. It is an honest view, and many careful people hold something like it today.
- Practice, Make a cloud with your breath (3 min): Hold a steel plate or a mirror close to your mouth. Breathe out slowly onto it with an open mouth, like a sigh. Watch the mist appear. Now stay still and watch it shrink, from the edges in, until it is gone. That mist was inside you a moment ago. Now it is in the air of this room. You cannot see it, but it is still there. Breathe again. Watch it come, and watch it go. Where does your breath end and the air begin?
- Ask: If you cannot see water vapour, how do you know it is there?
- Ask: Is a cloud new water in the sky, or water that was already there, now showing?
- Ask: When your breath goes into the air, is it still yours?
- Ask: What else might be all around you that you cannot see?

## Story: The Sky Under the Lid
*Real: wooden dhows from Mandvi in Kutch did sail to Arabia and East Africa, using the monsoon winds, and there are salt pans along the Kutch coast. Ships really did make fresh water from sea water by boiling it and catching the steam. Amina, her father, the dhow Noor, the cook Yusuf and this voyage are made up.*

Amina was ten, and she had never seen so much water and so little to drink.

The dhow was called Noor. She was a wooden ship from Mandvi, on the coast of Kutch, with one huge sail shaped like a slice of melon. Amina's father was her captain. They were sailing to Muscat, across the Arabian Sea, with a load of rice and cotton cloth. It was Amina's first voyage, and for six days the wind had pushed them along as if it were in a hurry too.

On the seventh day, the wind stopped.

It did not slow down. It simply stopped, the way a song stops. The great sail hung down like a tired elephant's ear. The sea went flat and shiny, as smooth as a steel plate, and it stayed that way.

On the ninth day, the sailors found that the biggest water barrel had cracked. Most of their drinking water had leaked into the bottom of the ship and mixed with the sea.

'How much is left?' asked her father.

Yusuf the cook tipped the last good barrel and listened to the slosh. 'Three days,' he said. 'Four, if nobody washes and nobody cries.'

Amina did not cry. But she was very thirsty, and all around her, as far as she could see, there was water. Blue water, green water, water so bright in the sun it hurt to look at. She leaned over the side and dipped her hand in. She licked one finger. It was so salty it stung.

'Don't,' said old Haroon, who had sailed for forty years. 'Drink the sea and the sea will drink you.' He told her about sailors who had gone mad with thirst and jumped in. Amina wished he had not.

That night, Yusuf cooked rice. He had no fresh water to spare for cooking, so he boiled the rice in sea water, in his big copper pot. He covered the pot with a heavy brass plate to keep the heat in. Amina sat beside him, hugging her knees, watching the little fire in its box of sand.

When the rice was done, Yusuf lifted the plate. Its underside was covered in shining drops. A few of them ran together and fell, plop, onto Amina's wrist.

Without thinking, she licked it.

Then she sat very still.

She licked her wrist again. She looked at the drops on the plate. She took one on her fingertip and put it on her tongue.

It was not salty. It tasted of nothing at all. It tasted of water.

'Yusuf,' she said. 'You cooked the rice in sea water.'

'I know I did. It will be salty. Eat it anyway.'

'But the drops on the lid aren't salty.'

Yusuf laughed, tiredly. 'Don't play games, little one.' But he touched a drop and tasted it. He stopped laughing.

Amina was thinking of home. Near Mandvi there were salt pans, long flat ponds where the salt workers let the sea in and closed it off. Then they waited. Day after day, the sun stood over the ponds, and the water went. Nobody carried it away. It went up, into the air, and left only salt behind, white and crunchy, in heaps taller than her father. Her mother used to say the sun was the thirstiest thing in the world. It drank the sea, but it would not drink the salt.

So where did the sun's drink go? Up. Into the air. And when it touched something cold, like this brass plate, it came back as water. Fresh water. The salt had stayed behind in the pot.

'The fire drinks the water out of the sea,' Amina said slowly, 'and the lid gives it back. Without the salt.'

Her father came over. Haroon came over. Everyone came over.

They worked all night. Yusuf filled the copper pot with sea water and kept the fire low and steady. Her father tilted the brass plate over it, so that the drops ran to one edge. Amina held a small cup under that edge. Haroon kept a wet cloth on top of the plate, dipping it again and again in the sea, because Amina said a cold lid would catch more. She did not know why. She only remembered that the cold steel tumblers at home grew drops on hot afternoons, and warm ones did not.

The drops came slowly. Plop. Plop. Plop. It took most of an hour to fill one small cup. They used up nearly a whole sack of firewood that night, and the cups they filled were not much. But each cup was a cup more than the sea would have given them.

For three days they boiled the sea. The sailors took turns at the fire. Nobody went mad. Nobody jumped in. The last barrel lasted, with the cups added, a little longer than Yusuf had said.

On the third evening, Amina was holding the cup when Haroon touched her shoulder and pointed. Far away, low over the flat sea, the sky had changed. Clouds were building up out of nothing. They rose and rose into towers, gold at the top from the setting sun, dark grey at the bottom.

Amina looked at the clouds, and then at the brass plate dripping into her cup.

'It's the same,' she whispered. 'The sun is heating the whole sea. And up there is the cold lid.'

That night the wind came back, and with it came the rain. Real rain, fat and cold, drumming on the deck and filling every bowl, every bucket and every empty barrel. The sailors danced in it with their mouths open. Amina stood at the rail and let it run down her face. It was not salty. It tasted of nothing at all. It tasted of water.

She thought of the salt pans at home, and the white heaps of salt, and the water that had risen out of them every day of her life. She had always thought it was simply gone. Now she knew where it went. It went up, and became this.

Somewhere under these same clouds, she thought, was Mandvi. Maybe it was raining there too, on her mother's roof, with water that had been this very sea a few days ago.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the key to the story is that when sea water evaporates, only the water goes up. The salt stays behind. Amina knows this from the salt pans, and she sees it again in the fresh drops on the lid of the rice pot. A cold surface turns the vapour back into drops. That is condensation. Her pot and plate are a small copy of the whole water cycle: the Sun heats the sea, the vapour rises, it condenses in the cold sky, and fresh rain falls. The story also quietly asks who is listened to: a ten-year-old girl noticed what the grown sailors had missed.

- Ask: Yusuf cooked the rice in sea water. Why were the drops on the brass plate not salty?
- Ask: What did the salt pans at home help Amina understand?
- Ask: Why did Amina want a wet cloth on top of the plate?
- Ask: At the end, Amina says the clouds and the plate are 'the same'. What did she mean?
- Ask: The sailors listened to a ten-year-old girl. Has anyone ever listened to an idea of yours? What happened?

### e5 · The Evaporation Race (30 min)
- **question:** What makes water evaporate faster: sunshine, wind, or a wide surface?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put the four places in order, from the one that will dry first to the one that will dry last: sun, shade, fan, narrow glass in the shade. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Put exactly the same amount of water in each, 2 teaspoons. Use three identical steel plates and one narrow glass. Start them all at the same time. Change only one thing for each: sun, shade, wind from a fan, or a narrow container.
- **safety:** Keep fingers away from the fan. Put the plates where nobody will step on them.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart, with one bar for each place. Make each bar as tall as its number of minutes.
- **results table:** Place | Time started | Time it was dry | Minutes to dry
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put exactly 2 teaspoons of water on each plate and into the glass. 2. Put plate 1 in the sun. Put plate 2 in the shade. Put plate 3 in the shade, in front of a fan. 3. Put the glass in the shade next to plate 2. 4. Write down the start time.  
  *Parent says:* This is a race. The winner is the puddle that disappears first. Which one will win?  
  *Child mode:* Put 2 spoons of water on each plate, and in the glass. One in the sun. One in the shade. One by a fan. The glass in the shade. Go!
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check every 15 minutes. 2. When a plate is completely dry, write down the time. 3. Work out the minutes it took. 4. If the glass still has water after 3 hours, write 'more than 180 minutes'.  
  *Parent says:* Let's check our race. Has any puddle gone yet? Where do you think its water went?  
  *Child mode:* Check every 15 minutes. When a plate is dry, write down the time. Is the glass dry yet?
  - Note: On a hot summer day, the sunny plate often dries in about 30 to 45 minutes. The fan plate is usually next, and the shade plate takes much longer. The narrow glass usually takes the longest, often until the next day. In cool or humid weather everything is slower, so use 1 teaspoon instead.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart, with one bar for each place.  
  *Parent says:* Each place gets one bar. The taller the bar, the longer that water took to evaporate. Which bar is the shortest?  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each place. Make it as tall as its minutes. The shortest bar won the race!
  - Check: In the Evaporation Race bar chart, the shortest bar shows the place where water… → **Evaporated fastest**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: sunshine, wind and a wide surface all make water evaporate faster. What I saw: the sunny plate dried in 40 minutes, the fan plate in 60, the shade plate in 120, and the glass took more than 3 hours. Why: heat makes water molecules jiggle faster so more escape, wind blows them away, and a wide puddle has a bigger surface for them to escape from.

### c1 · Beat the Sun: keep the water from evaporating (30 min)
- **problem:** On a hot day, ponds, tanks and reservoirs lose lots of water to evaporation. Your job: leave 10 spoons of water in the sun for a whole day, and have as much left as possible at the end.
- **your turn:** Now invent your own way to beat the Sun. Draw it, build it, and test it against the open bowl for a whole day. How many spoons did you save?
- **constraints:** Every container starts with exactly 10 teaspoons of water.; It must stay in the same sunny place as an open bowl, the 'control', for the whole day.; You can only use things you find at home.; At the end, count the spoons left using the same spoon.
- **general:** Cover the water with a lid or cling film, so the vapour can't escape.; Put it in a deep, narrow container, so less water is at the top.; Shade it with a plate, a cloth or leaves.
- **creative:** Float a few drops of cooking oil on top. A thin layer of oil slows evaporation. Scientists have tested floating layers like this on reservoirs.; In Gujarat, solar panels have been put on top of canals since 2012. They make electricity and shade the water, so less of it evaporates.; Old stepwells keep water deep down, in cool shade, far below the hot air. Could you make a tiny stepwell?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 10 teaspoons of water in each bowl. 2. One bowl stays open. It is the control, the bowl you compare with.  
  *Parent says:* This open bowl shows what the Sun does if we don't stop it. Your ideas have to beat it.  
  *Child mode:* Put 10 spoons of water in each bowl. One bowl stays open. Your ideas must beat it!
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* List three ideas before building anything. Think about what made water evaporate fastest in the Evaporation Race.  
  *Parent says:* Remember the race. Sun, wind and a wide surface all made water go faster. How can we block each one?  
  *Child mode:* Think like an engineer. Draw three ideas first. What made water dry fastest in the race? Block it!
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Build your three ideas. 2. Put them all in the same sunny spot as the open bowl, in the morning. 3. Leave them all day.  
  *Parent says:* They must all sit in the same sun, for the same time, so the test is fair.  
  *Child mode:* Build your ideas. Put them all in the sun with the open bowl. Leave them all day.
  - Wait: Leave the bowls in the sun until evening.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* In the evening, count the spoons left in each bowl, using the same spoon. Write the numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count. How many spoons did the open bowl lose? Which idea saved the most?  
  *Child mode:* Count the spoons left in each bowl. Which idea saved the most water?
  - Note: On a hot, sunny day, the open bowl often loses half its water or more. A covered bowl usually loses almost none, though drops form under the lid. If it is cool or cloudy, leave them a second day. If you used oil, do not pour that water on plants. Wipe the bowl with soap.
  - Check: In Beat the Sun, a bowl covered with cling film kept almost all its water. Why? → **The vapour condensed on the film and dripped back, instead of escaping into the air**
  - Think: Big reservoirs lose a lot of water to evaporation. Should we cover them? What might go wrong?
### c2 · The Fog Catcher: water for a dry hill (30 min)
- **problem:** Some dry places, high in the hills by the sea, get almost no rain, but thick fog drifts past most mornings. Design a net that catches water from the fog and drips it into a pot.
- **your turn:** Now design your own fog catcher. Draw it, build it and test it. How many drops can you catch in 3 minutes of 'fog'?
- **constraints:** It must be cheap, light and easy to build.; It must stand up on its own, or hang from something.; It must lead the drops into one container.; Test it with 'fog' from a spray bottle in front of a fan.
- **general:** Hang a fine net upright, facing the fog, like a volleyball net.; Put a gutter or a tray along the bottom to catch the drips.; Use a net with many fine threads, so lots of tiny drops hit it.
- **creative:** In Chile, in the dry Atacama desert, villages have used big fog-catching nets since the 1980s.; In Morocco, a group called Dar Si Hmad set up fog nets in 2015 to bring water to mountain villages.; A beetle in the Namib desert tilts its back into the morning fog. Drops gather on its back and roll down to its mouth.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set the fan on low, pointing at a wall or a window. 2. Spray mist from the bottle in front of the fan, so it blows like fog. 3. Feel the 'fog' on your hand.  
  *Parent says:* Fog is a cloud on the ground, billions of tiny drops. Feel it on your hand. Your job is to catch it.  
  *Child mode:* Turn the fan on low. Spray mist in front of it. That is your fog! Feel it on your hand.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw three designs before building anything.  
  *Parent says:* Think about this. Tiny fog drops pass through big holes. What kind of net will catch them?  
  *Child mode:* Draw three fog catchers. Think: what will tiny drops bump into?
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Build each design. 2. Stand it in the fog, with a tray underneath. 3. Spray 30 times in front of the fan. 4. Count the drops in the tray, or measure them with a spoon.  
  *Parent says:* Test each catcher with exactly the same fog: 30 sprays each. Which one catches the most?  
  *Child mode:* Test each catcher with 30 sprays. Count the drops in the tray. Which one wins?
  - Note: A fine onion net or mosquito net usually works best. Drops gather on the threads, join into bigger drops and run down. A solid cloth gets wet but drips less. Wipe up the floor afterwards, so nobody slips.
  - Check: In the Fog Catcher challenge, why does a net with fine threads catch more water than a net with big holes? → **More tiny drops bump into the threads, stick and join into bigger drops**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare your design with the usual solutions and the creative ideas.  
  *Parent says:* A beetle in the Namib desert solved this long ago. Which idea would you copy for a real village?  
  *Child mode:* A desert beetle drinks fog that gathers on its back! Which idea would you use for a real village?
  - Think: Nature had millions of years to find water in dry places. What else could we learn from desert plants and animals?

## Worksheets
### Water Cycle Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is the water cycle?  
   Options: The journey water makes round and round between the sea, the air and the land / A bicycle that runs on water / Water going down the drain / Rain falling once  
   **Answer:** The journey water makes round and round between the sea, the air and the land. *Why:* The water cycle is water's round journey: up into the air, down as rain or snow, and back to the sea. Picking 'rain falling once' sees only one step of the journey.
2. (fill) When water slowly turns into an invisible gas and goes into the air, we say it ____.  
   **Answer:** ['evaporates', 'evaporate', 'evaporation']. *Why:* Evaporation is liquid water turning into water vapour. It happens to puddles, wet clothes and the sea.
3. (fill) When water vapour cools and turns back into drops, we say it ____.  
   **Answer:** ['condenses', 'condense', 'condensation']. *Why:* Condensation is vapour turning back into liquid drops. It happens on cold things, and high in the sky where clouds form.
4. (mcq) A wet handprint on a sunny tile disappears. Where did the water go?  
   Options: Into the air, as invisible water vapour / Into the tile / It was destroyed  
   **Answer:** Into the air, as invisible water vapour. *Why:* The water evaporated into the air. Tiles have almost no holes, so it did not soak in. Picking 'destroyed' means thinking water can vanish. It only changes form and moves.
5. (mcq) What is a cloud made of?  
   Options: Billions of tiny drops of water or ice crystals / Water vapour, an invisible gas / Smoke / Cotton  
   **Answer:** Billions of tiny drops of water or ice crystals. *Why:* Clouds are tiny drops or ice. Water vapour is invisible, so a cloud we can see cannot be made of vapour alone. Picking 'vapour' is the most common mix-up, even among grown-ups.
6. (order) Put the steps of the water cycle in order, starting from the sea.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Warmth makes water evaporate. Rising vapour cools and condenses into cloud drops. They grow and fall. Rivers carry the water back to the sea, and it starts again.
7. (truefalse) Water only evaporates when it boils.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Puddles and wet clothes dry on cool days too. Water evaporates at any temperature. Heat just makes it faster.
8. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Evaporation → water going up into the air as vapour; Condensation → vapour turning back into drops; Precipitation → water falling from clouds as rain, hail or snow; Transpiration → plants giving off water from their leaves. *Why:* These are four steps of the water cycle. Each has its own long name, but each describes something you can see.
9. (mcq) A steel tumbler of ice gets wet on the outside. Where do the drops come from?  
   Options: From the air: vapour condenses on the cold steel / The water leaks through the steel / The ice melts through the steel  
   **Answer:** From the air: vapour condenses on the cold steel. *Why:* The drops are clear and not salty, though the water inside was coloured and salty. So they came from the air. Picking 'leaks' is very common. But steel has no holes for water to get through.
10. (sort) Sort these. Is it evaporation or condensation?  
   **Answer:** wet clothes drying on a line → Evaporation; dew on the grass at dawn → Condensation; a puddle shrinking in the sun → Evaporation; drops under a lid on a pot of dal → Condensation; mist on a mirror after a bath → Condensation; a wet floor drying after mopping → Evaporation. *Why:* Evaporation is water going into the air, so things dry. Condensation is water coming out of the air, so things get wet.
11. (mcq) Why is rain water not salty, when most of it rose from the salty sea?  
   Options: Only water evaporates. The salt stays in the sea. / Clouds filter the salt out / Rain washes the salt away  
   **Answer:** Only water evaporates. The salt stays in the sea.. *Why:* Salt cannot evaporate at these temperatures, so only fresh water rises. Picking 'clouds filter it' means thinking of a cloud like a sieve. The salt never got to the cloud at all.
12. (mcq) Which will dry fastest?  
   Options: A wet shirt spread on a line in sun and wind / A wet shirt rolled up in a bucket in the shade / A wet shirt folded in a closed bathroom  
   **Answer:** A wet shirt spread on a line in sun and wind. *Why:* Sun gives energy, wind blows the vapour away, and spreading out gives more surface. All three speed up evaporation. Rolled or folded shirts have very little surface open to the air.
13. (fill) A season of wet winds from the sea that brings heavy rain to most of India from June to September is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['monsoon', 'Monsoon']. *Why:* The monsoon brings most of India's rain in about four months.
14. (truefalse) Plants put water into the air through their leaves.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. This is transpiration. A bag tied over a leafy branch fills with mist and drops.
15. (write) Draw or write the water cycle. Use the words evaporate, condense and rain. Use arrows.  
   **Answer:** Any drawing or writing that shows water rising from the sea or land as vapour, condensing into a cloud, and falling as rain back to the land and the sea. For example: the Sun warms the sea, water evaporates, it condenses into a cloud, it rains on the hills, and a river carries it back to the sea.. *Why:* A good answer goes all the way round and comes back to where it started.
16. (write) Name two places at home where you have seen condensation. Say what was cold in each one.  
   **Answer:** Any two good examples. For example: the outside of a cold water bottle from the fridge, which was cold. The bathroom mirror after a hot bath, which was cooler than the steamy air.. *Why:* Condensation happens where vapour meets something colder.
### Water Cycle in Real Life (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at the chart from the Evaporation Race. Which place made water evaporate fastest?  
   Options: The sun / The fan in the shade / The shade / The narrow glass in the shade  
   **Answer:** The sun. *Why:* The sun's bar is shortest, at 40 minutes. A short bar means the water vanished quickly. Picking the glass usually means reading the tallest bar as the winner. Here, the tallest bar took longest.
2. (fill) In the Evaporation Race, the shade plate took 120 minutes to dry and the sunny plate took 40 minutes. The shade plate took ____ times as long.  
   **Answer:** ['3', 'three']. *Why:* 120 divided by 40 is 3. You can check: 40 + 40 + 40 = 120.
3. (mcq) In the Evaporation Race, the plate in the shade and the narrow glass in the shade had the same water and the same air. Why did the glass take much longer?  
   Options: Less water was at the top, open to the air / Glass is colder than steel / The glass had more water in it  
   **Answer:** Less water was at the top, open to the air. *Why:* Water evaporates from its top surface. A wide plate spreads the water out, so much more of it is at the top. Picking 'more water' misses the fair test: both had exactly 2 teaspoons.
4. (mcq) In the Cloud in a Jar, the cloud was much thicker with smoke from an agarbatti. Why?  
   Options: The vapour condensed onto the smoke specks / Smoke is made of water / The smoke made the jar warmer  
   **Answer:** The vapour condensed onto the smoke specks. *Why:* Vapour needs a speck to make a drop. More specks means more drops and a thicker cloud. Real clouds form on dust, salt and smoke. Picking 'smoke is water' mixes up the seed with the drop.
5. (mcq) The white mist that puffs out of a pressure cooker whistle is…  
   Options: Tiny drops of water, a little cloud / Invisible water vapour / Smoke from the rice  
   **Answer:** Tiny drops of water, a little cloud. *Why:* Real steam is invisible. As soon as it meets cooler air, it condenses into tiny drops, and those drops are what you see. Picking 'invisible vapour' is close. That vapour is there too, right at the whistle, but you can't see it.
6. (sort) Sort these. Which part of the water cycle is it?  
   **Answer:** a neem tree on a hot day → Water going up; hail in a summer storm → Water coming down; the sea under the hot Sun → Water going up; snow on a Himalayan peak → Water coming down; wet clothes on a terrace → Water going up; monsoon rain on the Western Ghats → Water coming down. *Why:* Trees, the sea and wet clothes give water vapour to the air. Hail, snow and rain bring water back down.
7. (mcq) Mumbai gets about three times as much rain as Pune, though they are close. Why?  
   Options: Wet winds drop most of their rain on the Western Ghats before reaching Pune / Pune is further from the Sun / Pune's clouds are made of dust  
   **Answer:** Wet winds drop most of their rain on the Western Ghats before reaching Pune. *Why:* Wet sea air is pushed up the Western Ghats, cools and rains on the sea side. Little water is left for the far side, the rain shadow. Picking 'further from the Sun' is a common guess, but the distance makes no difference here.
8. (truefalse) Raindrops are shaped like teardrops.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Small raindrops are round. Bigger ones are flattened at the bottom, like a burger bun. The teardrop shape only appears as a drop clings to a tap.
9. (mcq) In the leaf-in-a-bag experiment, why did we tie an empty bag next to the leafy one?  
   Options: To check that the water came from the leaves, not from the air / To catch more water / Because one bag might break  
   **Answer:** To check that the water came from the leaves, not from the air. *Why:* The empty bag is the control. It stayed dry, so the water must have come from the leaves. Without it, you could not be sure.
10. (match) Match each place to about how long water usually stays there.  
   **Answer:** The air → about 9 days; A river → weeks to months; The deep sea → about 3,000 years; Antarctic ice → up to hundreds of thousands of years. *Why:* Water rushes through the air but waits a very long time in the sea and in ice.
11. (mcq) A small puffy cloud shrinks and vanishes in 10 minutes. Did it rain?  
   Options: No, its drops evaporated back into the air / Yes, it rained somewhere else / No, the cloud floated into space  
   **Answer:** No, its drops evaporated back into the air. *Why:* Most clouds never rain. Their drops evaporate at the edges, and if they evaporate faster than new drops form, the cloud vanishes. Picking 'it rained' means thinking every cloud must rain.
12. (fill) On a cold, clear night, water from the air condenses on grass and leaves. In the morning we call these drops ____.  
   **Answer:** ['dew']. *Why:* Dew forms by condensation on cool surfaces at night, and evaporates when the Sun warms them.
13. (mcq) Where does a big thundercloud get the energy to grow so tall?  
   Options: From vapour condensing into drops, which gives out heat / From lightning / From the wind blowing it  
   **Answer:** From vapour condensing into drops, which gives out heat. *Why:* The Sun's energy went into the water when it evaporated. When the vapour condenses in the cloud, that energy comes out as heat. Warm air rises, so the cloud grows taller. Picking lightning gets it backwards: the cloud makes the lightning.
14. (write) Your solar still made fresh water from salty water. Explain how, using the words evaporate and condense.  
   **Answer:** The Sun warmed the salty water, and some of it evaporated into the air in the bowl. The salt stayed behind. The vapour touched the cooler cling film and condensed into fresh drops. They ran down to the low point and dripped into the cup.. *Why:* A good answer says that only the water rose, and that it turned back into drops on the cool film.
15. (write) A farmer is glad when the monsoon comes, but a family in a city is worried. Why might they feel differently about the same rain?  
   **Answer:** For example: the farmer needs rain to grow rice and fill the wells. The city family worries because streets made of cement and tar cannot soak up the rain, so their road may flood.. *Why:* Any answer that gives a reason for each person is good. The same rain can help or hurt, depending on the land it falls on.
### Water Cycle Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Rain often falls out of warm monsoon clouds faster than scientists' older ideas can explain. What is one new idea being studied?  
   Options: Swirling, bumpy air throws drops together more often / The drops are pulled down by the Moon / Lightning glues drops together  
   **Answer:** Swirling, bumpy air throws drops together more often. *Why:* Scientists think turbulence, swirling air inside clouds, may make tiny drops bump and join much faster. They are still testing this. Picking the Moon mixes this up with the tides.
2. (mcq) In 1880, John Aitken filtered all the dust out of air in a jar. What happened when he tried to make a cloud?  
   Options: Almost no cloud formed / A thicker cloud formed / It rained inside the jar  
   **Answer:** Almost no cloud formed. *Why:* Without specks to condense on, vapour could not easily make drops. This showed that clouds need dust, salt or smoke. Picking 'thicker' might seem right, because the air was cleaner. But clean air makes clouds less easily.
3. (truefalse) A puffy white cloud weighs almost nothing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Peggy LeMone, a cloud scientist, worked out that an ordinary puffy cloud holds about 500,000 kilograms of water. That is as heavy as about 100 elephants. It floats because its drops are tiny and spread over a huge space, and rising air holds them up.
4. (mcq) About how many cloud drops join to make one raindrop?  
   Options: About a million / About ten / About a hundred  
   **Answer:** About a million. *Why:* A raindrop is about 100 times wider than a cloud drop. A drop 100 times wider holds 100 × 100 × 100 = 1,000,000 times as much water. Picking 'a hundred' only counts the width, not the whole size.
5. (mcq) Pierre Perrault measured the rain around the river Seine in the 1670s. He found the rain was about six times what the river carried. What did this prove?  
   Options: Rain is more than enough to feed rivers / Rivers are fed by the sea underground / Most rain falls into the sea  
   **Answer:** Rain is more than enough to feed rivers. *Why:* If the rain is six times what the river carries, the river does not need any secret sea water. The rest of the rain soaks in, evaporates or is drunk by plants. Picking 'fed by the sea' was the old belief that Perrault's numbers overturned.
6. (mcq) On Titan, Saturn's biggest moon, what falls as rain?  
   Options: Liquid methane / Water / Sulphuric acid  
   **Answer:** Liquid methane. *Why:* Titan is about 180 degrees below zero, so water is hard ice there. Methane is a liquid at that cold, and it rains, fills rivers and makes lakes. Picking sulphuric acid mixes Titan up with Venus.
7. (mcq) Warmer air can hold more water vapour. What does this mean for heavy rain as the world warms?  
   Options: Downpours can get heavier / It will stop raining / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** Downpours can get heavier. *Why:* More vapour in the air means more water to fall when it rains. Studies of central India since 1950 found days of very heavy rain had become about three times more common. Picking 'stop raining' gets it backwards.
8. (write) Some people say forests 'pull' rain inland from the sea. Others say forests only recycle rain that already came. Design a way to find out which is right.  
   **Answer:** For example: compare the rain in places just inland from the sea that have forests with similar places without forests, over many years. Or measure how much vapour comes from the trees and where the wind carries it. A fair test changes only the forest, and keeps the distance from the sea and the mountains the same.. *Why:* A good answer compares places that differ in only one thing, the forest.
9. (mcq) A drop of water stays in the air for about 9 days, and in the deep sea for about 3,000 years. Why so different?  
   Options: The sea is huge and the water leaves it slowly. The air holds little water, and it rains out fast. / Sea water is heavier / The Sun does not shine on the deep sea  
   **Answer:** The sea is huge and the water leaves it slowly. The air holds little water, and it rains out fast.. *Why:* The time a drop stays depends on how much water a place holds and how fast it flows out. The air holds very little and lets it go quickly. The sea holds a lot and loses it slowly, mostly from its top.
10. (sort) Sort these statements about the water cycle.  
   **Answer:** clouds are made of tiny drops, not vapour → Settled: scientists agree; how drops grow into rain so fast in warm clouds → Still argued about; rain is fresh because salt does not evaporate → Settled: scientists agree; whether forests pull rain in from the sea → Still argued about; how much bacteria in clouds help make snow → Still argued about; the Sun's energy drives evaporation → Settled: scientists agree. *Why:* Science has a settled middle and an open edge. Knowing which is which is part of thinking like a scientist.
11. (mcq) Most water cycle pictures show only sea, clouds, rain and rivers. What did scientists in 2019 say was missing?  
   Options: People: dams, borewells, farms and cities / The Moon / Fish  
   **Answer:** People: dams, borewells, farms and cities. *Why:* People now move huge amounts of water, but most pictures leave us out. Picking fish is a reasonable thought, since animals are part of the cycle too. But the big missing piece was people.
12. (write) Heraclitus said that new waters keep flowing past people stepping into the same river. Is a river the same river from one day to the next? Give a reason for each side.  
   **Answer:** A balanced answer. For example: yes, because its banks, its name and its path stay the same. No, because all of its water is different water from yesterday.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. What counts is giving a reason for each side.
13. (write) Is a cloud a thing, or something that keeps happening? Use something you saw in this unit.  
   **Answer:** For example: a cloud is something happening, because in Cloud Watch my cloud kept changing and then vanished, and in the jar it formed and disappeared in a minute. Or: it is a thing, because I can see it, name it and draw it.. *Why:* Any answer with a reason from what you saw is good.
14. (mcq) In the jar garden project, you closed the lid and never watered it, yet the plants lived. Why?  
   Options: The same water kept going round: evaporating, condensing and dripping back into the soil / Plants don't need water / Water came in through the glass  
   **Answer:** The same water kept going round: evaporating, condensing and dripping back into the soil. *Why:* The jar is a closed water cycle. Water evaporates from the soil, leaves give off vapour, it condenses on the glass and runs back down. The Earth works in a similar way.
15. (draw) Draw a mountain with the sea on one side. Show the wet wind, the clouds, where the rain falls, and the dry rain shadow. Label each part.  
   **Answer:** The wind blows from the sea up the near slope. Clouds and rain are on the sea side. The far side is dry and labelled rain shadow.. *Why:* Wet air cools as it rises up the mountain, so it rains on the sea side. Little water is left for the far side.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is the water cycle?  
   Options: Water going round and round between the sea, the air and the land / Rain falling once and then stopping / Water made in factories  
   **Answer:** Water going round and round between the sea, the air and the land. *Why:* Water rises, falls and flows back, again and again. Picking 'falling once' sees only one step. Almost no new water is made. The same water keeps going round.
2. (mcq) A puddle dries up on a sunny day. What happened to its water?  
   Options: It evaporated into the air as invisible vapour / It was destroyed by the Sun / It all soaked into the cement  
   **Answer:** It evaporated into the air as invisible vapour. *Why:* The water went into the air. Water is never destroyed by drying. Picking 'soaked in' can be partly true on soil, but cement has almost no holes.
3. (mcq) A cold tumbler of ice gets wet on the outside. What is this called?  
   Options: Condensation / Evaporation / Melting  
   **Answer:** Condensation. *Why:* Vapour from the air cools on the cold steel and turns into drops. That is condensation. Picking 'melting' means thinking the ice got through the steel. The drops are not salty or coloured like the water inside, so they came from the air.
4. (mcq) What are clouds made of?  
   Options: Tiny drops of water or ice crystals / Water vapour, an invisible gas / Smoke  
   **Answer:** Tiny drops of water or ice crystals. *Why:* Clouds are made of tiny drops or ice. You can see them, and vapour is invisible. Picking 'vapour' is the most common mistake, even in some books.
5. (mcq) In the Cloud in a Jar, what did the smoke do?  
   Options: It gave the vapour tiny specks to condense on / It made the water boil / It turned into rain  
   **Answer:** It gave the vapour tiny specks to condense on. *Why:* Vapour needs specks to make drops. Smoke, dust and sea salt are the seeds of real cloud drops. Picking 'turned into rain' mixes up the seeds with the water.
6. (mcq) Why is rain fresh, when the sea is salty?  
   Options: Only water evaporates. The salt stays behind. / Clouds filter out the salt / Rain comes from rivers, not the sea  
   **Answer:** Only water evaporates. The salt stays behind.. *Why:* Salt cannot evaporate at everyday temperatures, so only fresh water rises. Picking 'filter' means imagining the cloud as a sieve. Picking 'rivers' forgets that most rain rose from the sea.
7. (mcq) In Sea in a Bowl, the water in the cup tasted…  
   Options: Not salty / Saltier than the bowl / Exactly as salty as the bowl  
   **Answer:** Not salty. *Why:* Only water rose and condensed on the cling film. The salt stayed in the bowl. If it tasted salty, some bowl water splashed into the cup.
8. (mcq) Look at the Evaporation Race results. Which made water dry fastest?  
   Options: The sunny plate / The shady plate / The narrow glass in the shade  
   **Answer:** The sunny plate. *Why:* The sunny plate's bar is shortest, 40 minutes. A short bar here means a quick dry. Picking the glass means reading the tallest bar as the winner.
9. (mcq) In the Evaporation Race, to make the test fair, what must be the same for every plate?  
   Options: The amount of water / The place / Nothing  
   **Answer:** The amount of water. *Why:* Every puddle started with exactly 2 teaspoons. Only the place changed. Picking 'the place' gets it backwards: the place is the one thing we change on purpose.
10. (mcq) A clear bag tied over a leafy branch fills with mist. Where does the water come from?  
   Options: From the leaves: plants give off water / From rain / From the plastic  
   **Answer:** From the leaves: plants give off water. *Why:* Water comes up from the roots and leaves through tiny holes in the leaves. That is transpiration. The empty bag next to it stayed dry, which shows the water came from the leaves.
11. (mcq) Why does Mumbai get much more rain than Pune?  
   Options: Wet winds drop their rain on the Western Ghats before reaching Pune / Mumbai is nearer the Sun / Pune has no clouds ever  
   **Answer:** Wet winds drop their rain on the Western Ghats before reaching Pune. *Why:* Wet sea air rises up the mountains, cools and rains on the sea side. The far side is a rain shadow. Distance from the Sun makes no difference here.
12. (mcq) When does the monsoon usually reach Kerala?  
   Options: Around 1 June / Around 1 January / Around 1 October  
   **Answer:** Around 1 June. *Why:* The monsoon usually arrives in Kerala around the first of June, and covers all of India by about 8 July. October is when it is usually leaving.
13. (mcq) Where does the energy to lift water into the sky come from?  
   Options: The Sun / The Moon / The wind alone  
   **Answer:** The Sun. *Why:* The Sun warms water so its molecules break free and rise. That energy comes out again when vapour condenses in clouds. Wind helps carry vapour, but the energy to evaporate it comes from the Sun.
14. (mcq) Do scientists fully know how tiny cloud drops grow into raindrops so fast in warm clouds?  
   Options: No, they are still studying it / Yes, it is completely solved / Yes, the Moon pulls them together  
   **Answer:** No, they are still studying it. *Why:* Scientists know drops bump and join, and that ice helps in cold clouds. But in warm monsoon clouds, rain often forms faster than expected. Swirling air may be part of the answer. It is still being studied.
15. (mcq) On Titan, Saturn's biggest moon, it rains…  
   Options: Liquid methane / Water / Sand  
   **Answer:** Liquid methane. *Why:* Titan is so cold that water is rock-hard ice. Methane is liquid there, and it rains, makes rivers and fills lakes. So a 'water cycle' can happen with other liquids too.

## Applications
- Salt pans in Gujarat and Tamil Nadu make salt by letting sea water evaporate in the sun.
- Solar stills make drinking water from salty or dirty water in dry places and on lifeboats.
- Desalination works near Chennai remove the salt from sea water to make drinking water for the city.
- Weather satellites, such as India's INSAT satellites, photograph clouds to forecast rain and cyclones.
- India's weather office uses the monsoon's path to tell farmers when to sow.
- Pressure cookers and steamers cook food with water vapour.
- Clothes dryers and drying racks on terraces use sun, heat and wind to speed up evaporation.
- Air conditioners and dehumidifiers take water out of the air by condensation. That is why an AC drips outside.
- Fog nets catch drinking water from fog in dry mountains, in Chile and Morocco.
- Cloud seeding tries to make suitable clouds give a little more rain.
- Cooling towers at power stations cool water by evaporation.
- Distilled water for car batteries, hospitals and laboratories is made by boiling water and condensing the steam.
- Kannauj in Uttar Pradesh makes a perfume called mitti attar, which smells of first rain, by heating baked clay and condensing the vapour.
- Solar panels on top of canals in Gujarat make electricity and cut evaporation.
- Sealed jar gardens and greenhouses recycle their own water.

## Open questions
- **How do tiny cloud drops grow into raindrops so fast in warm clouds?** Rain often forms faster than older ideas predict. Swirling air may throw drops together, but it happens inside clouds, at a tiny scale, and is very hard to measure.
- **How will the Indian monsoon change as the world warms?** Heavy rain days are already more common in central India. But the monsoon depends on the oceans, the land and the winds together, and forecasts for 50 years ahead still disagree.
- **How much does cloud seeding really work?** It is hard to know how much a cloud would have rained anyway. Tests in India and elsewhere find small effects, only in suitable clouds.
- **Do forests pull rain inland from the sea, or only recycle it?** Forests clearly recycle rain. Whether they also pull in wet air from the sea, as the 'biotic pump' idea says, is still argued about.
- **How much do tiny living things in clouds help make rain and snow?** Bacteria that make ice have been found in snow from many countries. Nobody yet knows how much they change the world's rain.
- **Why are clouds the hardest part of predicting future climate?** Clouds both cool the Earth, by blocking sunlight, and warm it, by trapping heat. Which wins depends on the kind of cloud, its height and the place. Computers struggle with them, because clouds are small and change fast.
- **Could there be other worlds with water cycles like ours?** Scientists have found many planets around other stars. A few might have seas and clouds, but our telescopes can only just begin to check.

## Connected maths
- In the Evaporation Race, the sunny plate dried in 40 minutes and the shady plate took 120 minutes. How many times as long did the shady plate take? **3 times as long.**. The shady plate took 120 minutes. The sunny plate took 40 minutes. 120 divided by 40 is 3. You can check: 40 + 40 + 40 = 120. So in the shade, the water took 3 times as long to evaporate. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Your solar still collects about 5 millilitres of fresh water every hour. How much does it collect in 4 hours? A person needs about 2 litres, which is 2,000 millilitres, to drink in a day. How many stills like yours would one person need for one day of 4 sunny hours? **20 millilitres in 4 hours. One person would need 100 stills.**. Each hour gives 5 millilitres. 4 hours give 4 × 5 = 20 millilitres. One person needs 2,000 millilitres. 2,000 divided by 20 is 100. So one person would need 100 bowls like yours. That is why real solar stills are big, and why fresh water is precious. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Look at the measuring spoon picture. In the leaf-in-a-bag experiment, you poured the water from the bag into a 5 millilitre teaspoon. It filled the spoon halfway. How many millilitres did the branch give off? **About 2 and a half millilitres.**. A full teaspoon holds 5 millilitres. Half of 5 is 2 and a half. So the branch gave off about 2 and a half millilitres in 3 hours. A big tree has thousands of branches, so it can give off many litres in a day. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- Mumbai gets about 2,200 millimetres of rain in a year. Pune gets about 700 millimetres. Draw a bar chart of the two cities. How much more rain does Mumbai get? **Mumbai gets about 1,500 millimetres more rain than Pune. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each city. Mumbai's bar goes up to 2,200 and Pune's to 700. To find the difference, take away: 2,200 − 700 = 1,500. So Mumbai gets about 1,500 millimetres more. That is one and a half metres more rain in a year. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- On average, water stays in the air for about 9 days before it falls. Let's round that to 10 days. About how many times could the same water go up into the air and come down again in one year of 360 days? **About 36 times.**. Each trip takes about 10 days. In 360 days, there are 360 ÷ 10 = 36 lots of 10 days. So, if it kept going straight back up, it could make about 36 trips. Real water often waits in a river, the ground or the sea in between, so most water makes far fewer. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
- After playing Round the World Drop, a child counted the beads on their bracelet: 4 blue (sea), 3 white (cloud), 2 green (river) and 1 light green (plant). Make a tally chart and a bar chart. Which place did the drop visit most? **The sea, with 4 beads. Here is the bar chart.**. Count each colour and make a tally: sea 4, cloud 3, river 2, plant 1. Then draw one bar for each place. The sea's bar is the tallest, at 4. That fits the real world, because most of Earth's water is in the sea. 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 beads, one for each turn. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
