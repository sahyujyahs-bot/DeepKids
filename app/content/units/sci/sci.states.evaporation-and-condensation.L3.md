# Evaporation and condensation: where water goes when things dry
`sci.states.evaporation-and-condensation.L3`

**Summary.** A puddle on the balcony is gone by the afternoon, but its water still exists. It has evaporated: it has turned into water vapour, a gas we cannot see, and mixed into the air. When water vapour touches something cold, it turns back into drops of water. This is condensation, and it is why a cold bottle from the fridge gets wet on the outside. Both happen at the same time at every wet surface. A wet thing dries only when more water leaves it than lands back on it. That is why clothes dry slowly when the air is already damp.

**Intro.** *In Chennai in November, a washed school uniform can stay damp on the line for two days. In May, the same uniform dries in about an hour. The cloth is the same, and so is the water in it. So what is different?* The answer is in the air around the cloth. In this unit you find out where water goes when something dries, and how it comes back as drops on cold things. You test it yourself with two wet cloths, one in an open box and one in a closed box. By the end you can say why a monsoon day dries clothes so slowly.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what evaporation is: liquid water turning into water vapour, a gas we cannot see, even when the water is not boiling.
1. I can say what condensation is: water vapour turning back into drops of liquid water when it touches something cold.
2. I can explain that water leaves a wet thing and lands back on it all the time, and that it dries only when more leaves than lands back.
3. I can name what makes things dry faster: warmth, moving air, spreading out and dry air. I can explain why damp air or a closed box slows drying.
4. I can spot evaporation and condensation at home: under a pot lid, on a cold bottle, on a bathroom mirror and from an AC pipe.
5. I can plan a fair test that changes only one thing, and use what I saw as evidence.
6. I can tell how William Wells showed that dew does not fall from the sky.

## Terms
- **Evaporation**: Liquid water turning into water vapour, a gas. It happens at the surface of water, even when the water is cool. *e.g.* A wet floor dries after it is mopped, because its water evaporates.
- **Water vapour**: Water as a gas. Its particles are spread far apart and move freely through the air, so we cannot see it. *e.g.* The air in your room has water vapour in it right now, even though it looks clear.
- **Gas**: A state of matter whose particles are far apart and fly about freely. A gas spreads out to fill whatever space it is in. *e.g.* Air is a mixture of gases. Water vapour is one of them.
- **Condensation**: Water vapour turning back into drops of liquid water. It happens when the vapour touches something cold. *e.g.* Drops form on the outside of a cold water bottle from the fridge.
- **Particle**: A tiny piece of a material, far too small to see. Water is made of countless water particles. *e.g.* One drop of water holds more water particles than there are stars in our galaxy.
- **Humidity**: How much water vapour is in the air. Air with a lot of water vapour is called humid. *e.g.* On a rainy monsoon day in Kochi, the humidity is high, and clothes dry slowly.
- **Saturated**: Air is saturated when it has so much water vapour that water lands back on a wet thing as fast as it leaves. *e.g.* The air inside a closed box with a wet cloth soon becomes saturated, so the cloth stays wet.
- **Dew**: Drops of water that form on grass, leaves and other things that cool down on clear nights. The water comes from water vapour in the air. *e.g.* In the morning you may find dew on a parked scooter's seat.
- **Fog and mist**: A cloud of tiny water drops floating in the air near the ground. They form when damp air cools and its water vapour condenses. *e.g.* On winter mornings in Delhi, fog can hide the end of the street.
- **Boiling**: When water is heated until bubbles of water vapour form inside it and rise. At sea level, water boils at 100 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Water bubbles hard in a pan of boiling rice.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* In the Closed Box Test, the only difference between the two boxes is the lid.

## Explanations
### What evaporation is · *defined*
**Definition:** Evaporation is liquid water turning into water vapour, a gas. It happens at the surface of the water, at any temperature.

Water is made of tiny particles that are always moving and bumping into each other. Some move faster than others. At the surface, the fastest particles can break away from the rest and fly off into the air. That is evaporation. It does not need boiling. A puddle on a cool, cloudy day still evaporates, only more slowly. Warmth makes the particles move faster, so more of them escape each second.
- e.g. A mopped floor dries without anyone heating it.
- e.g. Sweat on your skin evaporates, and that cools you down.
- e.g. Vadams and papads dry on a sunny terrace because the water in them evaporates.
### Where the water goes · *defined*
**Definition:** When water evaporates, it is not destroyed. The same water particles are in the air as water vapour.

Water vapour is invisible, so it looks as if the water has vanished. But the particles are still there, spread out among the particles of the air. The air in every room holds some water vapour. You can get it back as drops by cooling it, for example on a cold bottle or a cold lid. In the Closed Box Test you trap the vapour and see it come back.
- e.g. Breathe on a cold mirror. The water vapour in your breath turns back into a mist of drops.
- e.g. Water evaporated from the Bay of Bengal falls again as rain.
### What condensation is · *defined*
**Definition:** Condensation is water vapour turning back into liquid water. It happens when water vapour touches something colder than the air.

A cold surface takes warmth from the water particles that hit it. They slow down and stick together into tiny drops. Many tiny drops look like a dull mist. As more join, the drops grow and may run down. The water does not come through the cold thing. It comes from the air around it.
- e.g. Drops collect under the lid of a pot of cooking dal.
- e.g. Spectacles mist up when you step out of an air-conditioned room into a hot, damp street.
- e.g. A bathroom mirror mists over during a hot bath.
### Leaving and landing back, at the same time · *defined*
**Definition:** At every wet surface, water particles evaporate and condense at the same time. A wet thing dries only when more particles leave than land back.

Water vapour particles in the air fly about in every direction. Some hit the wet cloth and stick, so they condense back into it. In open air, the vapour from the cloth drifts away, so far fewer particles land back than leave. The cloth dries. In a closed box, the vapour cannot get away. Soon as many particles land back each second as leave. The air in the box is then saturated, and the cloth stays wet, even though water is still leaving it.
- e.g. A wet cloth in a closed box stays wet for days.
- e.g. A towel left folded in a heap stays damp inside, where the air cannot move.
### What makes things dry faster · *defined*
**Definition:** Things dry faster when they are warm, when the air around them moves, when they are spread out, and when the air is dry.

Warmth makes water particles move faster, so more escape. Moving air carries the damp air away from the wet thing, so fewer particles land back. Spreading a cloth out gives the water more surface to escape from. Dry air has little water vapour to land back. On a rainy monsoon day, the air is humid, so water lands back almost as fast as it leaves. That is why clothes take so long to dry.
- e.g. A uniform dries faster under a fan than on a chair in a still room.
- e.g. A sari dries faster spread along the line than bunched up.
- e.g. Clothes dry fast in hot, dry May and slowly in the damp of the northeast monsoon.
### Boiling, and the white cloud above a pan · *defined*
**Definition:** Boiling is evaporation so fast that bubbles of water vapour form inside the water. The white cloud above a boiling pan is tiny drops of water, not water vapour.

At sea level, water boils at 100 degrees Celsius. Then water vapour forms inside the water, not only at the top, and rises as bubbles. Just above the pan, the water vapour is invisible. Look closely and there is a clear gap just above the spout of a kettle. A little higher, the vapour meets cooler air and condenses into tiny drops. These drops are the white cloud people call steam. Higher still, the drops evaporate again and the cloud vanishes.
- e.g. Watch a kettle spout: there is a clear gap, then a white cloud, then nothing.
- e.g. On top of Mount Everest, water boils at only about 70 degrees Celsius, because the air presses on it less.
### Dew and fog · *defined*
**Definition:** Dew is water vapour that condenses on surfaces that have cooled down at night. Fog is water vapour that condenses into tiny drops floating in the air near the ground.

On a clear night, grass, leaves and car roofs give off their warmth to the open sky and cool down. When they become cold enough, water vapour from the air condenses on them as dew. Clouds act like a blanket, so on cloudy nights things cool less and less dew forms. Wind mixes in warmer air, which also means less dew. When the air near the ground itself cools enough, its vapour condenses into tiny floating drops. That is fog, a cloud at ground level.
- e.g. Dew on a car windscreen on a clear winter morning.
- e.g. Morning mist in Ooty and Kodaikanal that lifts as the Sun warms the air.
### Living things that drink the fog · *defined*
**Definition:** Some living things get water by letting water vapour or fog condense on their bodies.

In the Namib Desert in Africa, it almost never rains. Many beetles there need water to stay alive, and there are no puddles to drink from. On some mornings, fog rolls in from the sea. Then one kind of beetle climbs to the top of a sand dune. They tip their body forward, with their head down and their back raised into the fog. Fog drops collect on their back, join into bigger drops and run down to their mouth. Scientists first described this fog-basking in 1976.
- e.g. Fog-basking beetles in the Namib Desert.
- e.g. People in some dry, foggy places hang large nets that catch fog drops and drip them into tanks.

## Mostly agreed
- When water evaporates, it becomes water vapour, an invisible gas. The water is not destroyed. *How we know:* Water vapour can be turned back into the same amount of liquid water by cooling it. Scientists have weighed this carefully.
- Evaporation happens at any temperature, not only when water boils. *How we know:* Puddles dry on cool, cloudy days. A wet cloth dries in a room with no heater.
- Condensation happens when water vapour touches something cold enough. *How we know:* Cold bottles, cold lids and cold mirrors collect drops from the air, while warm ones stay dry.
- At a wet surface, evaporation and condensation happen at the same time. When they balance, the surface stops drying. *How we know:* A wet cloth in a closed box stays wet, though drops appear on the lid. Scientists measure the same balance very exactly in sealed containers.
- Dew forms from water vapour in the air. It does not fall from the sky. *How we know:* William Wells showed in 1814 that grass cools below the air temperature first, and dew forms after. Covered wool collected very little dew.

## How we found out
**William Charles Wells, a doctor in London, His experiments in the years before 1814, when he published An Essay on Dew.** For centuries, people said that dew 'falls' at night, as if it were a very fine rain from the sky. Some scientists also thought that the dew itself made the grass cold. William Charles Wells, a doctor in London, decided to test these ideas. Night after night, he laid small tufts of wool, weighed first, in different places: on the open grass, on raised boards, and under covers. In the morning he weighed them again, to see how much dew each had collected. He also measured the temperature of the grass and of the air above it. Wool under a cover collected very little dew. Far more dew formed on clear, still nights than on cloudy or windy ones. And the grass always became colder than the air before any dew appeared. In 1814 Wells published An Essay on Dew. He showed that dew does not fall. On clear nights, grass and leaves lose warmth to the open sky and cool down. Then water vapour from the air condenses on them. The cold comes first, and the dew comes after.  
*What people believed before:* Dew falls from the sky like a fine rain, and the dew makes the grass cold. Wells showed that the grass cools first, and water vapour from the air then condenses on it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **When a puddle dries, its water is gone for ever.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: The water is no longer visible anywhere.  
  Against: Trap the vapour in a closed box, and it turns back into drops on the lid. The water was in the air all the time.
- **The drops on a cold bottle leaked through the bottle.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: The drops appear on the bottle, so they seem to come from inside it.  
  Against: A closed bottle of coloured water gets plain, clear drops. An empty cold steel tumbler gets drops too. The water comes from the air.
- **The white cloud above a boiling kettle is water vapour.** (A very common idea, even in some books; *rejected*)  
  For: Everyone calls it steam, and it comes out of boiling water.  
  Against: Water vapour is invisible. Look just in front of the spout: there is a clear gap before the white cloud starts. The cloud is tiny drops that formed when the vapour cooled.
- **Dew falls from the sky at night, like a very fine rain.** (A belief held by many people for centuries; *rejected*)  
  For: Dew appears overnight on things that are open to the sky.  
  Against: William Wells found that dew forms on things only after they become colder than the air. Things under a cover get little dew because they stay warmer, not because the dew could not reach them.
- **Water evaporates only when it is hot or in the sun.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Things do dry much faster in the sun.  
  Against: Clothes dry indoors, at night and on cloudy days. Warmth only makes evaporation faster.

## Roots
We have seen water leave a wet cloth, wait in the air and come back as drops. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the closed box, the wet cloth stayed wet for hours. Yet drops kept forming under the cold lid. So water was still leaving the cloth, and the same amount was landing back. A thing dries only when more water leaves it than lands back on it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of drying as a one-way trip, where water leaves and is gone. We also call a cloth simply 'wet' or 'dry'. But water leaves and lands back on every wet thing all the time. What we see is which of the two is bigger.  
*Evidence:* In the Closed Box Test, the cloth in the closed box stayed wet while the cloth in the open box dried. Ice on the lid made drops form inside, so water vapour from the cloth filled the box. When the lid came off, the same cloth dried.

- *who am i:* Every breath you breathe out carries water vapour from inside you into the room. A cold mirror shows it coming back as drops.
- *what is life:* Every plant and animal loses water to the air and must take more in. A beetle in the Namib Desert drinks the fog that condenses on their back.
- *what is this world:* The air around you always holds water you cannot see. Every puddle, sea and leaf is giving water to the air and taking some back.

A glass of water on a table looks completely still. Yet every moment, countless particles leave its surface and countless others land back. When the two are equal, they add up to zero change, and nothing seems to happen. A little warmth or a breeze tips the balance, and the water leaves for the sky. It comes back somewhere else as dew or rain. One water, always moving, shows up as puddles, clouds, breath and drops under a lid.

- **Tirukkural 17, in the chapter on the excellence of rain:** Even the wide sea will lose its richness if the clouds take up its water and do not give it back as rain. (The sea stays full because water that evaporates from it comes back as rain. Tiruvalluvar saw that the taking and the giving back must both go on, like the leaving and landing back in the closed box.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.16:** What is not real never comes to be. What is real never stops being. (The Gita says this about the true self. Science found something like it for water. A puddle seems to vanish into nothing, but its water still exists as vapour, and comes back when it is cooled.)
- Science (settled): At any water surface, particles evaporate and condense at the same time. In a closed container the two become equal, and scientists call this a dynamic equilibrium.
- Science (settled): Evaporation and condensation never make or destroy water. They only change it between liquid and gas.
- Other view, Many physicists: Particles leaving and landing back is simply what countless particles do when they move about at random. It is a useful fact about water. Reading a lesson about life or balance into it says more about us than about water.
- Practice, Watch your breath come back (3 min): Hold this cold steel plate close to your mouth. Breathe out slowly on it. Watch the mist appear. Now watch it fade. That mist was inside you a moment ago. Now it is part of the room's air again. Let's do it three times, slowly. Then sit still for a moment and feel the air going in and out.
- Ask: When the cloth in the closed box stayed wet, was anything happening to it?
- Ask: The water in your breath leaves you and joins the room. When does it stop being your water?
- Ask: A glass of water loses some particles and gains others all the time. Is it the same water tomorrow?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the closed box, water kept leaving the wet cloth and landing back. Is a glass of water standing on the table ever really still? (hint: Think about the water particles at the top of the glass. What are the fastest ones doing?)
- *deep:* You cannot see the water vapour in your room. How could you prove to a friend that it is there? (hint: What did a cold bottle or a cold lid do?)
- *deep:* A towel stays damp in a bathroom, but dries on a breezy balcony. Is 'dry' something a towel is, or something that happens between the towel and the air? (hint: In the Closed Box Test, the same cloth stayed wet in one box and dried once the lid came off.)
- *big:* The water you breathe out joins the air of the room, and may become dew on a leaf tonight. When does it stop being your water? (hint: Think about where the water in you came from before you drank it.)
- *big:* Every water particle has been through clouds, seas, plants and animals many times. Is any water on Earth new? (hint: Evaporation and condensation change where water is, but do they make or destroy water?)

## Story: The Uniform in the Bag
*Chennai gets most of its rain from the northeast monsoon, from October to December, and the air is very humid then. Nila, her family and this week are made up.*

It had rained in Chennai for four days. The rain drummed on the balcony of Nila's flat, five floors up in Velachery. The roads below were rivers, and school had been closed on Monday. Now it was Tuesday evening, and school was opening again tomorrow.

Nila was nine. She had two school uniforms. One was in the wash basket, still muddy from Saturday. The other hung on the balcony line, where Amma had put it on Sunday. Nila touched the sleeve. It was cold and heavy, as damp as when it was hung up.

'Two days, and it is still wet!' she said. 'In May it dried in one hour.'

Appa looked up from his laptop. 'The rain is spraying it,' he said. 'Bring it in. We will keep it safe from the rain.' He took a big plastic bag from the kitchen, put the uniform inside, and tied the top. Then he laid the bag on the cool floor of the bedroom. 'There,' he said. 'Now nothing can make it wetter.'

Paati was sitting on the sofa with her coffee. She raised her eyebrows, but she said nothing.

In the morning, Nila ran to the bedroom and untied the bag. The uniform was just as damp as before. It smelled a little musty, like an old cupboard. And something else was strange. The inside of the bag was covered in drops of water, all over the side that had lain on the floor.

Nila sat down and looked at the drops. The bag was tied shut, so no rain could have got in. She looked at the outside of the bag. It was dry. So the water on the inside must have come from the uniform.

'Paati,' she said, 'the uniform's water came out. But the uniform is still wet.'

Paati put down her coffee. 'Where could the water go, inside a tied bag?' she asked.

Nila thought about it. The water had left the cloth as water vapour. It had nowhere to go, so the air in the bag filled up with it. Where the bag touched the cold floor, the vapour had turned back into drops. 'It could not go anywhere,' Nila said slowly. 'So it just went back into the cloth. Some came out, and some went back in.'

Paati smiled. 'It is like your idli steamer,' she said. 'When I lift the lid, the water is on the lid, not gone.'

Nila looked out at the balcony. It was still raining. The air out there was wet too, full of water vapour, so even outside, water was landing back on the uniform almost as fast as it left. She needed air that was drier, and air that kept moving, so the damp air would be carried away.

'The fan!' she said. She switched the ceiling fan in the hall to full speed. Paati showed her how to roll the uniform tightly in a dry bath towel and press on it with both hands, so the towel soaked up the water that dripped. Then Nila hung the uniform on a hanger right under the fan, with the arms spread out, and opened the hall windows on both sides.

Every half hour she touched the collar. At first it was cold and wet. Then it was cool and damp. By lunchtime it felt only a little cool. By three o'clock it was dry.

When Appa came to see, Nila explained it to him. 'In the bag, the water came out and went back in, the same amount. So it could never dry. Out here, the fan blows the wet air away. More water leaves than comes back.'

Appa scratched his head. 'So I made a little rainy day inside the bag,' he said.

'A very small one,' said Nila. 'With no way out.'

That night the rain stopped for a while. Nila went to the balcony and ran her finger across the railing. It was wet, though no rain was falling. She knew now where that water had come from.

**For the parent:** For the parent: water evaporates from a wet cloth all the time, and water vapour from the air condenses back onto it all the time. In the tied bag, the vapour had nowhere to go. The air in the bag soon became saturated, and as much water went back into the cloth as left it, so the uniform could not dry. The drops on the inside of the bag, where it lay on the cold floor, showed that water had really left the cloth. On a rainy day the outside air is humid too, so drying is slow even on the line. The fan carries the damp air away from the cloth and brings in other air, so far more water leaves than lands back. Rolling the cloth in a dry towel first removes much of the water without any evaporation at all. The wet railing at night is condensation from the damp air onto metal that cooled down after dark.

- Ask: Why did the uniform stay wet inside the tied bag?
- Ask: How did Nila know that the drops inside the bag came from the uniform, and not from the rain?
- Ask: Why did the uniform dry under the fan, though it was still raining outside?
- Ask: Paati gave Nila a clue instead of the answer. Why do you think she did that?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Closed Box Test: can a wet cloth dry with the lid on? (40 min)
- **question:** Does a wet cloth dry inside a closed box? If not, why not?
- **predict:** Guess first. We will put a wet cloth in a box with the lid on, and another in a box with the lid off. Which will dry first? Will the closed one dry at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two cloth squares cut from the same old cloth, the same size. Wet them both and squeeze each one the same way. Use two boxes of the same kind, side by side in the same place. Only the lid is different.
- **safety:** Safe to do alone, except cutting the cloth: a grown-up uses the scissors.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw both boxes at the end, with arrows for water leaving the cloth and landing back on it.
- **results table:** Time | Open box: wet, damp or dry? | Closed box: wet, damp or dry? | Drops on the walls or lid of the closed box? Where?
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts the old cloth into two squares the same size. 2. Dip both squares in the bowl of water. 3. Squeeze each one tightly, once, until it stops dripping. 4. Lay each one flat at the bottom of a box.  
  *Parent says:* We will give two wet cloths the same start. Same cloth, same water, same squeeze.  
  *Child mode:* Dip both cloths in water. Squeeze each one hard, once. Lay one flat in each box.
  - Note: Squeezing once each, as hard as your child can, keeps the two cloths about equally wet. Coloured cloth is best, because wet cloth looks darker than dry cloth, so you can see the change through the box.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press the lid firmly onto one box. 2. Lay the other lid beside its box. 3. Put both boxes side by side, out of direct sun and not right under a fan. 4. Write down the time.  
  *Parent says:* Now the only difference is the lid. Which cloth will dry first? Will the closed one dry at all? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Close one box. Leave the other open. Put them side by side. Write the time. Which will dry first? Why?

  - Note: Wait for a guess and a reason before saying anything. Many children say the closed one will dry too, only more slowly. That is a good guess to test.

  - Note: The open cloth usually dries in 2 to 4 hours. On a very humid monsoon day it can take longer. If it is still damp after 4 hours, put both boxes in front of a fan for an hour.   - Wait: Check every 30 minutes for about 3 hours.

  - Check: Drops appeared on the inside of the lid, under the ice. Where did that water come from? → **Water vapour from the wet cloth, in the air of the box, condensed on the cold lid**

  - Ask: Water left this cloth, because we saw drops on the lid. So why is it still wet? (good: Water went back into the cloth.; The air in the box got full, so as much water came back as left.)
  - Note: Let your child puzzle over this. Do not explain yet. The key clue is that water clearly left the cloth, yet the cloth stayed wet.

  - Note: It will dry now, in about the same time the first cloth took. Nothing about the cloth changed. Only the air around it changed.

### e2 · The Damp Air Hunt: which room has the dampest air? (25 min)

  *Child mode:* Which place misted fastest? Was your guess right? Where did that vapour come from?
  - Check: The tumbler misted fastest in the bathroom just after a bath. Why? → **Hot bath water evaporated, so the bathroom air had a lot of water vapour**

## Worksheets
### Where Did the Water Go? (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Meena mops the kitchen floor. An hour later, the floor is dry. Where did the water go?  
   Options: Into the floor tiles / Into the air, as water vapour / It was used up and is gone / Down the drain  
   **Answer:** Into the air, as water vapour. *Why:* The water evaporated. It became water vapour, an invisible gas, and mixed into the air. Picking 'used up and gone' means thinking water can be destroyed. It cannot: a cold surface can turn the vapour back into drops.
2. (mcq) Farhan takes a cold water bottle out of the fridge. Soon its outside is covered in drops. Where did the drops come from?  
   Options: Water leaked through the bottle / Water vapour in the room's air condensed on the cold bottle / The fridge sprayed the bottle / The bottle is melting  
   **Answer:** Water vapour in the room's air condensed on the cold bottle. *Why:* The cold bottle cools the water vapour in the air that touches it, and the vapour condenses into drops. A closed bottle cannot leak, and a steel bottle gets drops too.
3. (truefalse) Water evaporates only when it boils.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Water evaporates at any temperature. A mopped floor dries without being heated. Boiling is just very fast evaporation.
4. (truefalse) Water vapour is a gas that we cannot see.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Water vapour particles are far apart and move freely, so the air looks clear even when it holds a lot of water vapour.
5. (fill) When liquid water turns into water vapour, we say it ____.  
   **Answer:** ['evaporates']. *Why:* Evaporation is liquid water becoming a gas.
6. (fill) When water vapour turns back into drops of water, it is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['condensation']. *Why:* Condensation happens when water vapour touches something cold.
7. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Evaporation → water turning into a gas; Condensation → water vapour turning back into drops; Water vapour → water as an invisible gas; Dew → drops that form on cool things on clear nights. *Why:* These four words come up all through the unit.
8. (sort) Sort these. Is each one evaporation or condensation?  
   **Answer:** wet clothes drying on a line → Evaporation; drops under the lid of a rice pot → Condensation; a puddle shrinking in the sun → Evaporation; spectacles misting when you step out of an AC room → Condensation; a wet kolam drawing drying on the doorstep → Evaporation; dew on a scooter seat in the morning → Condensation. *Why:* In evaporation, water leaves something wet and goes into the air. In condensation, water comes out of the air onto something cold.
9. (mcq) In the Closed Box Test, the cloth in the open box dried, but the cloth in the closed box stayed wet. What was different for the closed cloth?  
   Options: It had more water to begin with / The air in its box filled up with water vapour, so as much water landed back as left / It was in a colder place / Cloth cannot dry in plastic  
   **Answer:** The air in its box filled up with water vapour, so as much water landed back as left. *Why:* Both cloths got the same water and the same place. Only the lid was different. In the closed box the vapour could not drift away, so water landed back as fast as it left.
10. (order) Put the steps of the Closed Box Test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Wet and squeeze two cloths the same way', 'Put one cloth in a closed box and one in an open box', 'Check both cloths every 30 minutes', 'Put ice on the closed lid and look for drops']. *Why:* First give both cloths the same start, then change only the lid, then watch, and last look for the trapped water vapour.
11. (write) Why does a wet towel dry faster when you spread it out than when you leave it in a heap?  
   **Answer:** Spread out, more of the towel touches the air, so more water can evaporate, and the damp air can drift away. In a heap, damp air is trapped between the folds, so water lands back.. *Why:* A strong answer says that spreading gives more surface and lets the damp air get away.
12. (write) Kavya says the Sun has destroyed the water in the puddle on her balcony. What would you tell her, and what could she do to check?  
   **Answer:** The water was not destroyed. It evaporated into the air as water vapour. She could put some water in a closed box in the sun and see drops form on the lid, or hold a cold plate over a warm puddle.. *Why:* A strong answer says the water is still there as vapour, and gives a test that brings it back as drops.
13. (draw) Draw the closed box at the end of the Closed Box Test. Show the cloth, the drops, and arrows for water leaving the cloth and landing back.  
   **Answer:** A box with a wet cloth at the bottom, drops under the lid, and arrows going up from the cloth and the same number coming back down.. *Why:* The arrows show the two happening at once. The same number up and down means the cloth stays wet.
14. (mcq) Which way will dry a wet handkerchief fastest?  
   Options: Folded in a heap in a closed cupboard / Spread out under a fan / Folded on a table / Inside a plastic bag  
   **Answer:** Spread out under a fan. *Why:* Spread out, the handkerchief has lots of surface for water to leave from, and the fan carries the damp air away. The other three all trap damp air around the cloth.
15. (perspective) Lakshmi's school uniform is still damp at 7 o'clock on a rainy morning. School starts at 9. Three people in her family each have an idea.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one and explains it with the science. For example, the iron is fastest because heat makes water evaporate very quickly, but a grown-up must do it and it uses electricity. Then it gives a fair point for another idea, such as the fan being free and safe, or the spare uniform needing no rush at all.. *Why:* All three can work, and each has a cost. The fan is slow on a humid day. The iron is fast but needs a grown-up and electricity. The spare uniform means wearing an older one.
### Damp Air and Cold Surfaces (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)

6. (mcq) Divya wears spectacles. She walks out of a cold, air-conditioned shop into a hot, damp street in Kochi. What happens to her spectacles?  
   Options: They mist up / They get hot and dry / Nothing happens  
   **Answer:** They mist up. *Why:* Her spectacles are still cold from the shop. The street's damp air touches them, and its water vapour condenses into a mist of drops.
7. (odd) Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Wet clothes drying / A puddle shrinking / Drops forming on a cold bottle / Sweat drying on your skin  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Drops forming on a cold bottle is condensation: water coming out of the air. The other three are evaporation: water going into the air.
8. (fill) A weather report says 'humidity 90 per cent'. Humidity tells us how much ____ ____ is in the air.  
   **Answer:** ['water vapour']. *Why:* Humidity is about the water vapour in the air. 90 per cent means the air is close to saturated, so drying will be slow.
9. (write) In the Closed Box Test, your friend says the drops under the ice came from the ice leaking through the lid. How could you show that the water came from inside the box?  
   **Answer:** The drops were on the inside of the lid, and the ice was in a bowl on the outside. You could colour the water in the ice bowl with turmeric. If the drops inside are clear, they did not come from the ice.. *Why:* A strong answer uses evidence: where the drops were, and a test that could tell the two waters apart.
10. (label) Label the drawing of a pot of dal cooking with a lid on: where water evaporates, where water vapour moves, where it condenses, and where drops fall back.  
   **Answer:** Evaporates at the top of the dal. Water vapour moves up through the space in the pot. It condenses on the underside of the lid. Drops fall back into the dal.. *Why:* A covered pot is a tiny water cycle: the same water goes up and comes back down.
11. (mcq) Which of these would dry a wet cloth fastest?  
   Options: Warm air that is still / Cool air that is still / Warm air with a breeze / Cool, damp air  
   **Answer:** Warm air with a breeze. *Why:* Warmth makes more particles escape, and a breeze carries the damp air away so fewer land back. Both together are fastest.
12. (sort) Sort these. Does each one speed up drying or slow it down?  
   **Answer:** a fan → Speeds up drying; a lid → Slows down drying; sunshine → Speeds up drying; folding in a heap → Slows down drying; damp, rainy air → Slows down drying; spreading out wide → Speeds up drying. *Why:* Warmth, moving air and spreading out help water leave and get away. Lids, heaps and damp air make more water land back.
13. (perspective) An air conditioner drips a bucket of water a day onto the balcony of Sumathi's flat. Three people say what to do with it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer explains that the water condensed from the room's air. It then weighs saving water for plants against the neighbour's worry about dirt. That worry is partly right, because of dust and mould inside the AC.. *Why:* Each view has a point. The water is not from a tap or drain, so it is not sewage, but it is not clean enough to drink.
14. (draw) Draw a cold tumbler of ice water in a room. Draw dots for water vapour particles in the air, and show what happens when they touch the tumbler.  
   **Answer:** Dots spread out in the air, and near the tumbler some dots joining into drops on its surface.. *Why:* The drawing should show the water coming from the air onto the cold surface, not out of the tumbler.
15. (write) On a clear night, dew forms on a scooter parked in the open, but not on one parked under a shed. Why?  
   **Answer:** In the open, the scooter loses warmth to the clear sky and gets colder than the air, so water vapour condenses on it. Under the shed, the roof stops it cooling as much, so it stays too warm for dew.. *Why:* A strong answer says dew needs a surface that has cooled down, and the shed stops that cooling. Wells found the same with covered wool.
### Leaving and Landing Back (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A glass jar with a little water at the bottom is closed tightly and left on a table for a week. What happens to the amount of water?  
   Options: It all evaporates / It stays nearly the same, because as many particles land back as leave / It grows bigger every day / It turns into ice  
   **Answer:** It stays nearly the same, because as many particles land back as leave. *Why:* A tiny amount evaporates until the air in the jar is saturated. After that, leaving and landing back are equal, so the water stays almost the same for weeks.
2. (write) In the Closed Box Test, the cloth stayed wet. Was evaporation still happening from the cloth? Use what you saw as evidence.  
   **Answer:** Yes. Drops formed under the cold lid, so water had left the cloth as vapour. The cloth stayed wet because the same amount of water landed back. When the lid came off, it dried.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the drops as evidence that water was leaving, and explains why the cloth stayed wet anyway.
3. (mcq) Why does a puddle dry faster on a windy day?  
   Options: The wind is cold / The wind blows away the damp air above the puddle, so fewer particles land back / The wind pushes the water into the ground / The wind is made of water  
   **Answer:** The wind blows away the damp air above the puddle, so fewer particles land back. *Why:* Above a puddle, the air gets damp. The wind replaces it with other, drier air, so fewer water particles land back.
4. (count) In Sumathi's Drying Diary, her handkerchief took 2 hours to dry on Monday and 6 hours on a rainy Wednesday. How many more hours did it take on Wednesday?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 6 minus 2 is 4. The rainy day took 4 hours more, three times as long, because the humid air made water land back on the cloth.
5. (truefalse) When water evaporates, its particles are destroyed.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The same particles are still there, spread out in the air as water vapour. Cooling brings them back as drops.
6. (truefalse) The air you breathe out has water vapour in it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Breathe on a cold mirror or plate, and the vapour condenses into a mist.
7. (write) In the salt pans near Thoothukudi, sea water is let into wide, shallow ponds and left in the sun. Why are the ponds wide and shallow, and not narrow and deep?  
   **Answer:** Water evaporates from its surface. A wide, shallow pond has a big surface, so the water evaporates quickly and leaves the salt behind.. *Why:* A strong answer links the size of the surface to how fast water evaporates.
8. (perspective) A village farm pond loses a lot of water to evaporation every summer. Three people have ideas.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer explains how the chosen idea slows evaporation, for example a cover traps the vapour like a lid, or shade keeps the water cooler. It then names an honest cost, such as harm to the fish, or the price of panels.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off. Covers and shade save water, but a pond is also a home for living things, and money matters too.
9. (mcq) On which kind of night will the most dew form on the grass?  
   Options: A cloudy, windy night / A clear, still night / A rainy night / A foggy afternoon  
   **Answer:** A clear, still night. *Why:* On a clear night, grass loses warmth to the open sky and cools a lot. Still air lets it stay cold. William Wells found the most dew on such nights.
10. (write) Design a way to collect drinking water from damp air without using electricity. Draw it and explain how it works.  
   **Answer:** For example, a large mesh net facing the foggy wind, with a gutter below, or a sheet that cools at night with a jar under it. Fog drops or dew collect on it and run into a container.. *Why:* A strong design uses condensation: it needs a surface that the damp air or fog touches, and a way for drops to run into a container.
11. (order) Water from a wet uniform on a balcony ends up as dew on a car roof the next morning. Put the steps in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Water evaporates from the uniform', 'The water vapour mixes into the air', 'At night, the car roof cools down', 'Water vapour condenses on the cold roof as dew']. *Why:* Evaporation puts the water into the air. It waits there as vapour until a cold surface turns it back into drops.
12. (match) Match each place to what is happening to water there.  
   **Answer:** A bathroom mirror after a hot bath → condensation; Salt pans in the sun → evaporation; An AC drain pipe dripping → condensation; Papads drying on the terrace → evaporation. *Why:* Mirrors and the cold parts inside an AC collect water from the air. Salt pans and papads lose water into the air.
13. (fill) When air holds so much water vapour that as many particles land back on a wet thing as leave it, scientists say the air is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['saturated']. *Why:* Saturated air is why the cloth in the closed box could not dry.
14. (draw) Draw two wet cloths side by side, one in open air and one in a closed box. Use arrows to show particles leaving and landing back. Make your arrows show which cloth dries.  
   **Answer:** The open cloth has many arrows going up and away and only a few coming back. The closed cloth has the same number going up and coming back.. *Why:* The difference in arrows is the whole idea of the unit: a thing dries when more leaves than lands back.
15. (mcq) In the Namib Desert it almost never rains. Why does a beetle there stand on top of a sand dune on foggy mornings, with their back raised?  
   Options: To warm up in the sun / So fog drops collect on their back and run down to their mouth to drink / To look out for enemies / To dig a hole  
   **Answer:** So fog drops collect on their back and run down to their mouth to drink. *Why:* The beetle needs water and has no puddles to drink from. Fog drops condense and join on their back, then run down to their mouth. The dune top is where most fog blows past.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is evaporation?  
   Options: Water vapour turning into drops / Liquid water turning into water vapour / Water turning into ice / Water soaking into the ground  
   **Answer:** Liquid water turning into water vapour. *Why:* Evaporation is liquid turning into gas. Picking 'water vapour turning into drops' mixes it up with condensation, its opposite.
2. (mcq) What is condensation?  
   Options: Water vapour turning back into liquid water on something cold / Water getting hot / Water drying up / Ice melting  
   **Answer:** Water vapour turning back into liquid water on something cold. *Why:* Condensation is gas turning back into liquid. It needs something cold, like a bottle from the fridge.
3. (mcq) Can water evaporate without boiling?  
   Options: No, it must reach 100 degrees Celsius / Yes, at any temperature, only faster when warm / Only in the sun / Only at night  
   **Answer:** Yes, at any temperature, only faster when warm. *Why:* Puddles and mopped floors dry without boiling. Warmth only makes evaporation faster.
4. (mcq) Anjali puts a wet cloth in a box and closes the lid tightly. What will happen after a day?  
   Options: The cloth will be dry / The cloth will still be wet, with drops on the inside of the box / The cloth will be frozen / The box will be empty  
   **Answer:** The cloth will still be wet, with drops on the inside of the box. *Why:* Water leaves the cloth, but the vapour cannot get out. The air in the box becomes saturated and as much water lands back as leaves.
5. (mcq) Why does a fan help a wet uniform dry?  
   Options: The fan makes the air hotter / The fan carries the damp air away from the cloth, so fewer water particles land back / The fan pushes the water out through the cloth / The fan makes the uniform lighter  
   **Answer:** The fan carries the damp air away from the cloth, so fewer water particles land back. *Why:* A fan does not heat the air. It replaces the damp air next to the cloth with other air, so more water leaves than lands back.
6. (mcq) Which day is best for drying clothes?  
   Options: A rainy, still day / A warm, breezy, dry day / A cold, foggy morning / A humid, cloudy day  
   **Answer:** A warm, breezy, dry day. *Why:* Warmth helps particles escape, the breeze carries the damp away, and dry air has little water vapour to land back.
7. (mcq) Rani lifts the lid off a pot of cooking rice. Drops of water hang under the lid. Where did they come from?  
   Options: From the steel of the lid / From the rice water, which evaporated and condensed on the cooler lid / From the air in the kitchen only / From the gas flame  
   **Answer:** From the rice water, which evaporated and condensed on the cooler lid. *Why:* Hot water in the pot evaporates fast. The lid is cooler than the vapour, so the vapour condenses on it.
8. (mcq) Where does the water dripping from an AC pipe come from?  
   Options: From the water tank / From water vapour in the room's air, condensed on the cold parts inside the AC / From rain / The AC makes it from electricity  
   **Answer:** From water vapour in the room's air, condensed on the cold parts inside the AC. *Why:* The room's air is blown over very cold metal inside the AC, and its water vapour condenses there and drips out.
9. (mcq) Thenmozhi wants to test whether a fan makes a cloth dry faster. Which is a fair test?  
   Options: One cloth under a fan in the sun, and one with no fan in the shade / Two identical wet cloths in the same place, one with a fan and one without / One big towel under a fan, and a small hanky without one / One cloth under a fan today, and one without tomorrow  
   **Answer:** Two identical wet cloths in the same place, one with a fan and one without. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. The other choices change two things at once: the sun, the size, or the day.
10. (mcq) What did William Wells find out about dew?  
   Options: Dew falls from the sky like fine rain / Grass cools down first, then water vapour from the air condenses on it / Dew comes up out of the soil / Dew is made by the Moon  
   **Answer:** Grass cools down first, then water vapour from the air condenses on it. *Why:* Wells weighed tufts of wool and measured temperatures. The grass always became colder than the air before dew formed.
11. (mcq) The white cloud above a boiling kettle is made of what?  
   Options: Water vapour / Tiny drops of liquid water / Smoke / Air  
   **Answer:** Tiny drops of liquid water. *Why:* The vapour itself is invisible. It condenses into tiny drops in the cooler air, and those drops make the white cloud.
12. (mcq) A wet cloth in the open air is drying. What is happening at its surface?  
   Options: Water is only leaving / Water is leaving and landing back, but more leaves than lands back / Water is only landing back / Nothing is happening  
   **Answer:** Water is leaving and landing back, but more leaves than lands back. *Why:* At every wet surface both happen at once. The cloth dries because more particles leave than land back.

## Applications
- Drying clothes, vadams, papads, chillies and grain in the sun and wind.
- Salt pans, such as those near Thoothukudi and in Gujarat, where sea water evaporates and leaves salt behind.
- Clay pots and matkas, which cool water as a little of it seeps out and evaporates.
- Air conditioners, which take water vapour out of a room's air by condensing it on cold pipes.
- Weather forecasts, which report humidity and warn of fog for drivers, trains and airports.
- Solar stills, which make clean drinking water by evaporating dirty or salty water and condensing the vapour.
- Fog nets in some dry, foggy places, which catch drops from the air for drinking water.
- Sweating, which cools your body as the sweat evaporates.

## Open questions
- **How do tiny cloud drops grow into raindrops so quickly in warm clouds, like the monsoon clouds over India?** Cloud drops are far too small to fall. Scientists know they join together, but the details of how this happens so fast are still being studied.
- **Where did Earth's water first come from?** Some of it may have come from icy rocks and comets, and some may have been inside the rocks that formed the Earth. Scientists are still comparing the evidence.
- **As the Earth warms, how will evaporation and rainfall change over India?** Warmer air can take up more water vapour, so heavy rain may become heavier. Exactly how each region's monsoon will change is still uncertain.

## Connected maths
- In the Closed Box Test, Priya put the cloths in the boxes at 10:00 in the morning. The cloth in the open box was dry at 12:30. How long did it take to dry? **2 hours and 30 minutes.**. From 10:00 to 12:00 is 2 hours. From 12:00 to 12:30 is 30 minutes more. So it took 2 hours and 30 minutes, which is 150 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Sumathi kept a Drying Diary for 6 days. Her handkerchief took these times to dry: Monday 2 hours, Tuesday 3 hours, Wednesday 6 hours, Thursday 5 hours, Friday 2 hours, Saturday 1 hour. On two days it rained. Which two days do you think they were? Tomorrow is forecast to be hot, dry and windy. Predict roughly how long it will take. **Wednesday and Thursday were probably the rainy days. Tomorrow it should take about 1 hour, or a little less.**. The two longest times, 6 hours and 5 hours, stand out from the rest. Rainy days have humid air, which slows drying. Saturday, the fastest day, took 1 hour. A hot, dry, windy day should be as fast as that, or faster. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In the Damp Air Hunt, the cold tumbler misted after 4 seconds in the bathroom and after 20 seconds in the bedroom. How many times as long did it take in the bedroom? What does that suggest about the two rooms? **5 times as long. The bathroom air had much more water vapour.**. 20 divided by 4 is 5. Check: 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 20. The faster the mist appears, the more water vapour is hitting the tumbler, so the bathroom air was far damper. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
- An air conditioner drips half a litre of water every hour on a humid night. It runs for 8 hours. How much water does it drip? A bucket holds 5 litres. Will it overflow? **4 litres. No, the bucket will not overflow, with 1 litre of space left.**. Half a litre each hour for 8 hours: 8 halves make 4 whole litres. 5 minus 4 leaves 1 litre of space. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Two wet towels hang in the same room. Towel A is spread out flat and has 4 times as much surface touching the air as towel B, which is folded. Towel A dried in 1 hour. Arun says towel B will take exactly 4 hours. Is that a fair guess, and why might it take even longer? **It is a fair first guess, but it may take longer than 4 hours.**. With a quarter of the surface, water can leave only a quarter as fast, so about 4 times as long is a good first guess. But inside the folds, damp air is trapped, so water lands back too. That can slow it down even more. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
