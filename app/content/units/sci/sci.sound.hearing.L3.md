# Our ears: how we hear, how to protect hearing, and the sounds animals hear that we can't
`sci.sound.hearing.L3`

**Summary.** A phone can be playing music while you hear nothing at all. Every ear has a quietest sound it can catch, and softer sounds pass it by. Your ear works as a chain. The ear flap collects sound, the eardrum vibrates, three tiny bones pass the vibration on, and hair cells in the cochlea turn it into signals for the brain. People hear only a band of pitches, and the top of that band drops as we grow older. Loud sound can break hair cells, and in people they never grow back, so ears need protecting. Bats, moths, cats and dogs hear sounds too high for us, and elephants and pigeons hear sounds too low for us. Each of them hears what their own life needs.

**Intro.** *Try this. Play a song on a phone and turn the volume all the way down, but leave the song playing. Put the phone on a table across the room. Can you hear it? The song is playing, and the phone is sending out sound. So why can't you hear it?* Your ears catch only sounds that are loud enough, and only sounds of certain pitches. In this unit you find the quietest sound you can hear, and you test which ear covers block sound best. You follow a sound from your ear flap to your brain. You find out why very loud sounds can harm hearing for ever, and how to keep your ears safe. You also meet animals that hear sounds we never notice: bats, moths, elephants and more.

## Terms
- **Ear flap**: The curved part of the ear on the outside of your head. It collects sound and sends it into the ear hole. Its scientific name is the pinna. *e.g.* Cup your hand behind your ear flap, and a fan's hum sounds louder, because you collect more sound.
- **Ear canal**: The short tube from the ear hole to the eardrum. In a grown-up it is about 2.5 cm long. *e.g.* Earwax is made in the walls of the ear canal.
- **Eardrum**: A thin, tight skin at the end of the ear canal. It vibrates when sound reaches it, like the skin of a tabla. *e.g.* When someone claps, the vibrating air makes your eardrum vibrate too.
- **Vibrate**: To move quickly back and forth. Every sound starts with something vibrating. *e.g.* A veena string vibrates when it is plucked, and so does your eardrum when the sound reaches you.
- **The three tiny bones**: Three tiny bones behind the eardrum, called the hammer, the anvil and the stirrup. They pass the vibration from the eardrum to the cochlea. The stirrup is the smallest bone in your body. *e.g.* The stirrup is about 3 millimetres long, smaller than a grain of rice.
- **Cochlea**: A tube coiled like a snail shell, deep inside your head. It is filled with liquid and lined with hair cells. *e.g.* The cochlea is about the size of a pea.
- **Hair cell**: A cell in the cochlea with a tiny tuft on top. When sound makes the tuft bend, the cell sends a signal along a nerve. Each ear has about 15,000 of them. *e.g.* Different hair cells pick up high sounds and low sounds.
- **Auditory nerve**: The nerve that carries signals from the hair cells to the brain. Auditory means to do with hearing. *e.g.* The brain gets signals along the auditory nerve and you hear the pressure cooker whistle.
- **Earwax**: A soft, sticky yellow-brown substance made in the ear canal. It traps dust and tiny insects, and slowly moves out of the ear on its own. *e.g.* A little earwax at the ear hole is normal and healthy. It is not dirt.
- **Pitch**: How high or low a sound is. A thing that vibrates faster makes a higher pitch. *e.g.* A koel's call has a high pitch. A big drum has a low pitch.
- **Hertz**: The unit for counting vibrations each second. 1 hertz is one vibration in one second. *e.g.* A young child can hear from about 20 hertz up to about 20,000 hertz.
- **Ultrasound**: Sound with a pitch too high for people to hear, above about 20,000 hertz. *e.g.* Many bats hunt with ultrasound calls.
- **Infrasound**: Sound with a pitch too low for people to hear, below about 20 hertz. *e.g.* The lowest part of an elephant's rumble is infrasound.
- **Quietest sound you can hear**: The softest sound that your ears can just catch. Scientists call it your hearing threshold. A sound softer than this reaches you, but you do not hear it. *e.g.* In the volume test, Kavya heard the song first at volume step 3. Step 3 was her quietest sound at that distance.
- **Decibel**: The unit for measuring how loud a sound is. A whisper is about 30 decibels. A mixer grinder is about 80 to 90 decibels. *e.g.* Sounds above about 85 decibels, heard for a long time, can harm hearing.
- **Hearing loss**: When a person hears less than before, in one or both ears. It can come from age, illness or loud sound. *e.g.* Many grandparents find it harder to hear high sounds, like the 's' in words.
- **Audiologist**: A trained person who tests hearing and helps people who hear less. *e.g.* An audiologist plays soft beeps through headphones and asks you to raise your hand at each one.
- **Bone conduction**: Hearing through the bones of your head. The bones carry vibrations straight to the cochlea. *e.g.* When you hum with your ears shut, the hum sounds louder, because it reaches you through your bones.
- **Echolocation**: Finding things by making a sound and listening to the echo that bounces back. An echo is a sound that bounces off something and comes back. *e.g.* Bats and dolphins find their food by echolocation.

## Explanations
### How the ear turns a sound into a signal · *defined*
**Definition:** Sound is a vibration of the air. The ear passes the vibration inwards from the eardrum to the cochlea, and hair cells there turn it into nerve signals for the brain.

When a bell rings, it vibrates, and the air around it vibrates too. Your ear flap collects some of that vibrating air and sends it down the ear canal. At the end, the eardrum vibrates. The three tiny bones pass the vibration on to the cochlea. The bones make the push stronger, because the cochlea is full of liquid and liquid is harder to move than air. In the cochlea the liquid ripples, and the tufts on the hair cells bend. Each bend makes a hair cell send a signal along the auditory nerve. Only then does the brain make the sound you hear.
- e.g. A pressure cooker whistle: vibrating steam, then air, eardrum, three bones, cochlea, hair cells, nerve, brain.
- e.g. Your mother calling you from the kitchen reaches your brain through the same chain in much less than one second.
### The quietest sound you can hear · *defined*
**Definition:** Every ear has a quietest sound it can catch, called the hearing threshold. A softer sound still reaches the ear, but it does not make a strong enough signal to be heard.

In the volume test, the phone was playing at the lowest steps, but you heard nothing. The sound was there in the air. It was too soft for your hair cells to send a clear signal. When the volume went up, you heard it. Covering the ear raised the step you needed, because less sound got in. An audiologist tests hearing this way. They play soft beeps of different pitches and find the quietest one you hear for each pitch.
- e.g. A ticking wall clock you can hear at night, but not in the day when the road is busy.
- e.g. A child who hears a whisper from 3 metres away, while a grandparent needs the speaker to come closer.
### Pitch, and the band of sounds people hear · *defined*
**Definition:** Pitch depends on how many times each second a thing vibrates. People hear from about 20 to about 20,000 vibrations each second, called 20 to 20,000 hertz.

A big drum skin vibrates slowly and makes a low pitch. A small bell vibrates very fast and makes a high pitch. Young people hear from about 20 hertz up to about 20,000 hertz. Sounds above that are called ultrasound. Sounds below it are called infrasound. They are real sounds, but our hair cells do not respond to them. Different hair cells pick up different pitches. Those for high sounds sit near the start of the coil in the cochlea, and those for low sounds sit deep inside it.
- e.g. A mosquito's whine is a few hundred hertz. You hear it well.
- e.g. A dog whistle is above 20,000 hertz. A dog hears it, and you hear nothing.
### Why the highest sounds go first as we grow older · *defined*
**Definition:** The hair cells for the highest pitches wear out first, so most people hear less of the top of the band as they grow older.

High-pitch hair cells sit at the start of the cochlea, where every sound passes. Over many years, more of them stop working. A child may hear a tone of 17,000 hertz that most grown-ups over 40 cannot. Many grandparents hear deep voices well but miss high sounds, like the 's' and 'f' in words. So 'fifty' and 'sixty' can sound the same to them. Loud places and some illnesses make this happen sooner.
- e.g. A grandmother who hears the temple drum but misses the microwave beep.
- e.g. In the family pitch test, the children hear the tone for longer than the parents.
### Loud sound and hair cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Very loud sound, or loud sound for a long time, can bend and break hair cells. In people, broken hair cells never grow back.

Loudness is measured in decibels. Talking is about 60 decibels. Sounds above about 85 decibels, heard for hours, slowly harm hearing. Very loud sounds close by, like a firecracker bursting near you, can harm it at once. After a loud wedding band or crackers, your ears may ring or sound muffled. That is a warning that hair cells were hurt. Most of it gets better in a day or so, but damage that happens again and again adds up. Birds can grow new hair cells, but people cannot.
- e.g. If you must shout to be heard by someone an arm's length away, the place is loud enough to harm hearing over time.
- e.g. Earphones at full volume for hours each day can cause hearing loss that lasts.
### Keeping your ears safe · *defined*
**Definition:** You protect your hearing by keeping loud sound short, keeping away from its source, covering your ears, and never pushing anything into the ear canal.

Moving away from a loud sound helps a lot, because sound spreads out and gets weaker with distance. Covering the ears helps, and a cover that seals the ear hole helps most. With earphones, keep the volume low enough to hear someone talking next to you. Earwax keeps the canal clean and moves out by itself as you chew and talk. A cotton bud can push wax deeper or scratch the eardrum. Doctors say to put nothing in your ear, not a bud, a hairpin, a matchstick or oil. If an ear hurts, leaks or feels blocked, see a doctor.
- e.g. Standing far back and pressing your ear flaps shut when crackers burst at Deepavali.
- e.g. Wiping only the outside of the ear with a cloth after a bath.
### Hearing through your bones · *defined*
**Definition:** Vibrations can reach the cochlea through the bones of the head, without passing through the eardrum. This is called bone conduction.

When you talk, your voice reaches your cochlea in two ways: through the air into your ears, and through the bones of your head. The bones carry the low parts of your voice well. That is why your voice sounds deeper to you than to others, and why a recording of your voice sounds strange. When you hum with your ears shut, the hum gets louder. Less sound escapes from the closed ear canal, and the hum still arrives through the bones.
- e.g. Your recorded voice sounds higher and thinner than the voice you hear when you talk.
- e.g. Bhramari, the humming bee breath of yoga, is done with the ears shut, and the hum fills your head.
### Animals that hear what we can't · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals hear pitches that people cannot. Each one hears the sounds that matter for their way of living.

A moth flying at night has a problem: bats hunt moths in the dark. Many moths have ears on their bodies that hear the bats' ultrasound calls. When a bat comes close, the moth dives or zigzags away. Bats themselves call in ultrasound and listen to the echoes to find insects. A mouse is hunted by owls and cats, and mice call to each other in ultrasound. Cats and dogs hear higher sounds than we do. Elephants live in herds that spread out across a forest. Their low rumbles, partly infrasound, travel far, and other elephants hear them from kilometres away. A cricket hears with ears on her front legs.
- e.g. A dog hears a dog whistle that people cannot.
- e.g. Pigeons hear infrasound, far below the lowest sound we can hear.
### What does a bat hear when the echoes come back? · *idea*
A bat hears echoes of their own calls and knows where a moth is, how fast she flies and even her size. Nobody knows what this is like for the bat. Does it feel like seeing, or like hearing, or like something people have no word for? In 1974 the philosopher Thomas Nagel asked this question in an essay called 'What Is It Like to Be a Bat?'. Scientists can measure what a bat's ears and brain do. They have no way yet to know what the bat experiences. Some blind people learn to click with their tongue and find walls and doorways from the echoes. They describe it in their own ways.
- e.g. A bat catches a moth in the dark in less than a second.
- e.g. Some blind people find a doorway by clicking and listening to the echo.

## Mostly agreed
- The ear passes sound from the eardrum, through three tiny bones, to hair cells in the cochlea, which send signals to the brain. *How we know:* Doctors and scientists have studied ears for centuries. Georg von Békésy watched the inside of the cochlea move with sound, and won the Nobel Prize in 1961.
- In people, hair cells in the cochlea do not grow back once they are broken. *How we know:* Doctors find damaged hair cells in the ears of people who worked for years in loud places. Birds can grow new hair cells. This was found in the late 1980s, and scientists are now trying to learn why people cannot.
- Young people hear from about 20 to about 20,000 hertz, and the top of that band drops with age. *How we know:* Audiologists test millions of people with beeps of different pitches. The same pattern shows up all over the world.
- Many animals hear pitches people cannot: bats, moths, mice, cats and dogs hear ultrasound, and elephants and pigeons hear infrasound. *How we know:* Scientists record these sounds with special microphones and test how animals react to them.
- Loud sound for a long time causes hearing loss that lasts. *How we know:* The World Health Organization studies hearing all over the world. It says that more than a billion young people are at risk from loud music and noisy places.

## How we found out
**Lazzaro Spallanzani in Italy, Louis Jurine in Switzerland, Georges Cuvier in France, and Donald Griffin and George Pierce in America, 1793 to 1794, and 1938.** In 1793, in Italy, Lazzaro Spallanzani wondered how bats fly at night without hitting anything. He covered the eyes of some bats, and they still flew around his room easily. In 1794 a Swiss surgeon, Louis Jurine, tried plugging bats' ears with wax instead. Now the bats bumped into walls. Spallanzani repeated the test and found the same. So hearing mattered. But nobody could hear any sound from the bats. The famous French scientist Georges Cuvier said bats must feel the air with the skin of their wings, by touch. Most people accepted that for more than a hundred years. Then in 1938, in America, a student named Donald Griffin brought bats to the physicist George Pierce. Pierce had built a machine that could pick up sounds too high for people to hear. When the bats flew in front of it, the machine was full of their calls. Bats call in ultrasound and listen to the echoes. Griffin later named this echolocation. The wrong idea came from one mistake: people thought that a sound they could not hear did not exist.  
*What people believed before:* Bats find their way by touch, feeling the air with their wings. Jurine's wax plugs showed that bats need their ears. Pierce's machine showed that bats make sounds too high for people to hear, and use the echoes to find their way.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Earwax is dirt, so ears must be cleaned out with cotton buds.** (A very common idea in many homes; *rejected*)  
  For: Earwax looks like dirt, and it is yellow or brown. Cotton buds are sold for ears.  
  Against: Earwax is made by the ear on purpose. It traps dust and keeps the canal from drying out. It moves out on its own as you chew and talk. A cotton bud often pushes wax deeper and can scratch the canal or the eardrum.
- **Putting oil in the ears is good for everyone.** (A home remedy in many families; *rejected*)  
  For: Oil can soften hard wax, and doctors sometimes advise ear drops to soften wax.  
  Against: Doctors say to put nothing in the ear unless a doctor asks. If the eardrum has a tiny hole, or the ear has an infection, oil can make it worse. A healthy ear does not need oil.
- **Only old people get hearing loss.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Hearing does get worse for most people as they grow old.  
  Against: Loud music, earphones, firecrackers and noisy work can cause hearing loss in young people too. Some babies are born deaf or hearing less.
- **If a sound does not hurt, it cannot harm your ears.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Very loud sound does hurt, and pain is a good warning.  
  Against: Sound around 85 to 100 decibels does not hurt, but hours of it every day slowly break hair cells. Many people find out only years later.
- **Animals with big ear flaps hear best.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Big ear flaps collect more sound. A rabbit or a fox turns them to catch faint sounds.  
  Against: Bats, moths and crickets hear very well with tiny ears or none we can see. A moth's ears are on her body, and a cricket's are on her front legs. Hearing depends most on what is inside the ear.

## Roots
We have been learning how the ear catches sound, how to protect it, and which sounds other animals hear. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** At the lowest volume steps, the phone was playing, yet you heard nothing. The sound reached your ears, but it was too soft for them to catch. Bats, cats and elephants catch sounds that human ears miss. So a room that seems silent to you still has sounds in it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a room is 'silent' when we hear nothing, as if no sound were there. But silent only tells us about our own ears. The same room can be full of sound for a cat, a bat or a mouse.  
*Evidence:* In the volume test, the phone was playing at step 1, but nobody heard it until a higher step. In the pitch test, a 20,000 hertz tone played while most people heard nothing, and a dog would hear it easily.

- *who am i:* You are a listener with your own band of hearing. Your ears catch some sounds and miss others, and your band changes as you grow.
- *what is life:* Every creature hears what their own life needs. A moth hears the bat, a cat hears the mouse, and an elephant hears the herd far away.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of vibrations, far more than any one creature hears. Each listener hears their own part of it.

One room holds one set of vibrations. A child, a grandfather, a cat and a mouse in that room each hear a different part of them. None of them hears all of it, and each hears what matters for their life. The same world shows up as many worlds of sound, one for each listener.

- **Kena Upanishad 1.2:** The teacher says there is something that is the ear of the ear and the mind of the mind. It is what hears through the ear. (The ear passes on vibrations, and the hair cells send signals. But something in you hears. The Upanishad asks what that listener is, a question science has not answered.)
- **Udana 6.4, a Buddhist text from the Pali Canon: the blind men and the elephant:** Some blind men each touch one part of an elephant. One feels the ear and says an elephant is like a winnowing basket. One feels the leg and says it is like a pillar. Each is sure he alone is right. (A person, a bat and an elephant each hear one band of the sounds in the same forest. Each band is real, and none is the whole.)
- **John Cage, Silence, 1961:** The composer John Cage sat in a room built to block all sound. He still heard two sounds, from his own body. He wrote that there is no such thing as empty time or empty space: there is always something to hear. (In the volume test you found that a room can be playing sound while you hear nothing. Cage found that even the quietest room still has sound.)
- Science (settled): People hear from about 20 to 20,000 hertz. Bats, mice, cats and moths hear far above that, and elephants and pigeons hear below it.
- Science (open): What hearing is like from the inside, for a person or a bat, is still an open question for science and philosophy.
- Other view, Some philosophers: A sound is the experience of hearing. Vibrations nobody hears are only vibrations, not sounds. On this view, an empty forest at night has movement in the air, but no sound until someone listens.
- Practice, Find the quietest sound (3 min): Let's sit very still and close our eyes. Listen for the loudest sound you can hear. Now listen for a softer one. Now the softest one you can find. Hold up a finger for each new sound. Then, think of the sounds in this room that none of us can hear. A cat or a bat might be hearing them right now.
- Ask: If a sound plays and nobody hears it, is it still a sound?
- Ask: A bat and a person stand in the same forest. Are they hearing the same forest?
- Ask: As your hearing changes over your life, is the world changing, or are you?

## Story: Appooppan and the Missing S
*A made-up story set in Kochi. The hearing test is described as audiologists really do it.*

Kavya was nine, and she lived in Kochi with her mother and her grandfather. She called him Appooppan, which means grandfather in Malayalam. For thirty years he had worked in a coir factory, where big machines twisted coconut fibre into rope all day.

Lately, Appooppan had started to get things wrong. Kavya told him her new teacher was called Miss Sumathi. He said, 'Miss Umathi? What a strange name.' She asked him for six bananas, and he brought her fifty paise. When the washing machine beeped, he didn't move. When her mother called from the kitchen, he heard every word.

'He hears Amma but not me,' Kavya said one evening. 'Is he ignoring me?'

'No,' said her mother. 'I think his ears are changing. We will go and see someone who knows.'

On Saturday they took the bus to a clinic in Ernakulam. The audiologist was a young woman called Dr Fathima. She sat Appooppan in a small quiet room with thick walls and gave him a pair of headphones.

'I will play some beeps,' she said. 'Some will be very soft. Each time you hear one, even a tiny one, raise your hand.'

Kavya watched through a window. Dr Fathima pressed keys on her laptop. A low beep came first. Appooppan's hand went up. She made it softer and softer, until his hand stayed down. Then she did the same with a higher beep, and a higher one.

On the screen, a line of dots appeared. For the low beeps, the dots sat near the top, close to a normal line. For the high beeps, the dots dropped down, far below it.

'This is called an audiogram,' Dr Fathima said, showing them. 'Each dot is the quietest beep he heard at that pitch. He hears low sounds well. High sounds have to be much louder before he hears them.'

'But the beeps were playing, even when his hand was down?' asked Kavya.

'Yes. The sound reached his ears. It was just too soft for him to catch.'

Dr Fathima drew a snail shell on a piece of paper. 'This is the cochlea, inside the ear. It is lined with tiny hair cells. The ones for high sounds sit here, at the start, where every sound passes. Over many years, many of them stop working. Thirty years of loud machines made it happen sooner.'

'Will they grow back?' Kavya asked.

'Not in people,' said Dr Fathima. 'A bird can grow new ones. We can't. That is why we protect our ears.'

Kavya thought about it on the bus home. The sound of 's' in 'Sumathi' and 'six' is a high hiss. Appooppan never heard the hiss, so 'six' sounded like nothing he knew. Her own voice was higher than Amma's. The washing machine beep was high too. Everything he missed was high.

That night she tried something. She sat in front of Appooppan, where he could see her face. She turned off the fan. She didn't shout, because shouting only made her voice higher and harder to understand. She spoke a little slower and a little lower. 'Appooppan, can I have SIX bananas?'

He laughed. 'Six! I heard it that time.'

A month later, Appooppan got two small hearing aids. They made high sounds louder, just for him. The first morning, he sat on the step and said, 'Kavya, what is that noise?'

She listened. It was a pair of sparrows on the wire. They had been chirping there every morning all year.

'Those are sparrows, Appooppan,' she said. 'They were always there. You just couldn't hear them.'

That evening, Kavya put a small packet of foam earplugs in the drawer by the door. 'For the temple festival,' she said. 'For the chenda drums. For both of us.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: the sound in the room was the same for Kavya and her grandfather. What differed was what each of their ears could catch. The hair cells for high pitches sit at the start of the cochlea and wear out first, and loud noise speeds this up. Facing the person, cutting background noise and speaking clearly and a little lower helps more than shouting.

- Ask: Why did Appooppan hear his daughter but often not Kavya?
- Ask: When Appooppan's hand stayed down, was the beep still playing? How do you know?
- Ask: Why did 'six' sound strange to him?
- Ask: What did Kavya do to help him hear, and why did each thing help?
- Ask: Why did Kavya buy earplugs for herself too?

## Games
### g1 · Who Hears It? A card game of sounds and listeners (20 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the listener cards. Each player takes two. Playing alone, take all eight.; Shuffle the sound cards and put them face down.; Turn over the top sound card and read it out. Don't look at the back yet.; Each player says which of their listeners can hear it, and why.; Turn the card over to check the band. Each right answer scores 1 point.; If a sound is heard by none of the people cards but by an animal, that player says how this hearing helps the animal's life, for 1 more point.; Play until all 10 sound cards are used. The highest score wins.
- **Printable cards: 8 listener cards (each shows which bands of pitch they hear):** A child: MIDDLE (all the sounds people hear); A grandparent: MIDDLE, but often not its very top; An elephant: VERY LOW and the lower part of MIDDLE; A pigeon: VERY LOW and the lower part of MIDDLE; A dog: MIDDLE and HIGH; A cat: MIDDLE, HIGH and VERY HIGH; A mouse: MIDDLE, HIGH and VERY HIGH; A moth: HIGH, where bats call
- **Printable cards: 10 sound cards (the band is on the back):** A veena string being plucked (back: MIDDLE); A mosquito whining near your ear (back: MIDDLE); A child saying 'six' (back: MIDDLE, with a high 's'); A tone of 17,000 hertz (back: top of the MIDDLE); The lowest part of an elephant's rumble (back: VERY LOW); The lowest rumble of a far-away storm (back: VERY LOW); A dog whistle (back: HIGH); A bat hunting a moth (back: HIGH); A baby mouse calling for their mother (back: VERY HIGH); A temple bell (back: MIDDLE)
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player names across the top; One row for each round; A box for the total

### g2 · The Ear Road: a board game from ear flap to brain (20 min)

## Experiments
### e1 · The Quietest Sound: which ear cover blocks the most? (30 min)
- **question:** Which ear cover blocks the most sound: cupped hands, a thick folded towel, or the small ear flaps pressed shut with a fingertip?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which cover will block the most sound: cupped hands, a folded towel, or pressing the small flaps shut? Which will block the least? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Keep everything the same except the ear cover: the same phone, the same song, the same distance of 3 metres, the same quiet room. The helper turns the volume up one step at a time, slowly and evenly. The listener keeps their eyes shut so they can't see the phone. Try each cover twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Nothing goes inside the ear. Press only the small flap in front of the ear hole. Keep the phone at least 3 metres away, and stop when the listener hears the song. The volume never needs to be loud.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw one bar for each ear cover, as tall as the volume step it needed.
- **results table:** Ear cover | Volume step when first heard (try 1) | Try 2 | How many steps more than open ears?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off the fan, the TV and anything humming. 2. Put the chair at one end of the room. 3. Measure 3 metres and put the phone there, speaker facing the chair. 4. Start a song and turn the volume all the way down.  
  *Parent says:* The song is playing, but the volume is at zero. We will turn it up one step at a time. You tell me the moment you hear it.  
  *Child mode:* Sit on the chair. The phone is 3 metres away. A song is playing very, very softly. Can you hear it yet?
  - Note: Pick a song with steady sound, not a quiet start. Check that the phone does not lock or pause. On most phones, pressing the volume button once moves one step. Counting steps is easier if the phone shows a volume bar.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Soon you will cover your ears in three ways. Which do you think will block the most sound? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which cover blocks most: hands, a towel, or pressing the flaps shut? Why?
  - Ask: Which cover do you think will block the most, and why? (good: The towel, because it is thick and soft.; Pressing the flaps, because it closes the hole.; Any guess with a reason.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The listener shuts their eyes. 2. Press the volume up one step every 3 seconds. Count the steps out loud in your head. 3. When the listener raises a hand, write down the step. 4. Turn it back to zero. Do it once more.  
  *Parent says:* Shut your eyes. Raise your hand the moment you hear the song, even a tiny bit.  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Listen hard. Raise your hand when you hear the song.
  - Note: Once in a while, wait without pressing anything for a few seconds. If a hand goes up then, the listener is guessing. Ask them to raise it only when they are sure. This is what audiologists do too. If the listener hears step 1 straight away, move the chair further back, or put the phone in the next room with the door open.
- **s4 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now test each ear cover the same way, twice each. 2. Cupped hands: palms held over both ear flaps. 3. Towel: folded thick and held over both ears with both hands. 4. Ear flaps: press the small flap in front of each ear hole shut with a fingertip. 5. Write each step in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Same phone, same place, same song. Only the ear cover changes. Raise your hand when you hear it.  
  *Child mode:* Cover your ears the new way. Shut your eyes. Raise your hand when you hear it.
  - Note: Show the small flap: it is the bump of cartilage just in front of the ear hole. Pushing it back over the hole closes the hole without putting anything inside. If the listener cannot hear the song even at full volume with a cover, write 'more than full' and move closer for every test.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each cover, take away the open-ears step from its step. 2. Write the difference in the last column.  
  *Parent says:* Which cover needed the most extra steps? That one blocked the most sound. Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Which cover needed the most steps? That one blocked most. Were you right?
  - Check: Arjun heard the song at step 2 with open ears and at step 7 with his ear flaps pressed shut. What does that tell you? → **Pressing the flaps blocked sound, so he needed a louder song to hear it**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the results. Then go back to step 1 of the volume, with the song still playing and ears open.  
  *Parent says:* Look. At step 1 the song is playing, but you can't hear it. What is happening to that sound?  
  *Child mode:* The song plays at step 1. You can't hear it. Where is the sound going?
  - Ask: Why did one cover block more than another? (good: It closed the ear hole, so sound couldn't get in round the edges.; Hands leave gaps, and sound goes through gaps.)
  - Ask: At step 1, is there sound in the room? (good: Yes, the phone is making sound, but it is too soft for my ears.; Yes, a cat might hear it.)
  - Note: Usually the pressed flaps need the most extra steps, because they seal the ear hole. Cupped hands leave gaps, and sound slips round them. A towel blocks more when pressed tight. Your family's results may differ, and that is fine. Ask what they found, not what the book says.
  - Think: Is a room ever truly silent?
### e2 · Who Hears the Highest Note? A family pitch test (25 min)
- **question:** Who in our family can hear the highest pitch: the children or the grown-ups?
- **predict:** Guess first. Who in your family will hear the highest note? Who will stop hearing it first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Everyone sits at the same distance from the same phone, with the same volume. The tone goes up in the same steps for everyone. Each person listens with eyes shut, so they don't copy anyone. Test each person twice.
- **safety:** A grown-up sets the volume first, at 1,000 hertz, to a comfortable talking level. Never turn it up for the high notes. Don't use earphones for this test. Stop if anyone finds the sound unpleasant.
- **record:** Write each person's name, age and the highest pitch they heard. Put them in order from the highest to the lowest.
- **results table:** Person and age | Highest pitch heard, in hertz (try 1) | Try 2
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the tone generator. 2. Set it to 1,000 hertz. 3. Set the volume to a comfortable talking level. 4. Put the phone on a table 1 metre from where everyone sits.  
  *Parent says:* This plays one pure note. The number is how many times each second the speaker vibrates. Let's hear 1,000 first.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the note. Its speaker vibrates 1,000 times a second. Is it high or low?
  - Note: Use the device's own speaker, not earphones. Leave the volume at this level for the whole test. Higher notes can sound sharp, so keep it moderate.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask everyone for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will make the note higher and higher. Who do you think will hear it the longest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Who will hear the highest note? You, or the grown-ups? Why?
  - Ask: Who do you think will hear the highest note, and why? (good: Me, because children hear better.; Amma, because she is bigger.; Any guess with a reason.)
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start at 8,000 hertz. 2. Raise it by 1,000 hertz every 5 seconds. 3. Everyone listens with eyes shut and raises a hand when the note disappears. 4. Write down the number for each person. 5. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Shut your eyes. Raise your hand when you can't hear the note any more.  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. The note goes higher. Raise your hand when it disappears.
  - Note: Phone speakers often can't play very high notes well. If everyone stops at the same number, the speaker may be the limit, not the ears. Then try a laptop or a speaker. Many grown-ups over 40 stop somewhere between 12,000 and 16,000 hertz. Many children still hear 17,000 or more.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put everyone in order from the highest pitch heard to the lowest. Look for a pattern with age.  
  *Parent says:* Who heard the highest? Who stopped first? Do you see a pattern with age?  
  *Child mode:* Who heard the highest note? Is there a pattern with age?
  - Check: Meenakshi, aged 8, hears the note up to 18,000 hertz. Her grandmother stops at 9,000. Why? → **Hair cells for the highest pitches wear out first as people grow older**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play 20,000 hertz for a few seconds, then switch it off.  
  *Parent says:* Most of us hear nothing now, but the speaker is still vibrating. A dog or a cat would hear this easily.  
  *Child mode:* The speaker is still vibrating. Can you hear it? A dog could!
  - Ask: Is there a sound in the room right now? (good: Yes, the speaker is vibrating, but it is too high for us.; For a dog, yes.)
  - Think: What else in our home might make sounds too high for us?
### e3 · Through the Door: do low or high sounds pass better? (20 min)
- **question:** When a door is shut, does a low note or a high note get through better?
- **predict:** Guess first. Behind a shut door, will the low note or the high note sound quieter? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same phone, the same volume and the same place for both notes. Listen from the same spot, first with the door open, then shut. Only the pitch and the door change.
- **safety:** Keep the volume at a comfortable talking level. Don't hold the phone near anyone's ear.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Circle the note that lost more loudness when the door shut.
- **results table:** Note | Door open: how loud (3 big, 2 medium, 1 soft, 0 nothing) | Door shut: how loud | How much quieter?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the phone on a table inside the room. 2. Set the volume to a comfortable level. 3. Stand outside, 2 metres from the open door.  
  *Parent says:* We will play a low note and a high note. First, guess: which will the door block more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will the door block the low note or the high note more? Why?
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The helper plays 200 hertz. 2. Score how loud it sounds with the door open. 3. Shut the door and score it again. 4. Do the same with 4,000 hertz.  
  *Parent says:* Score it: 3 for big, 2 for medium, 1 for soft, 0 for nothing.  
  *Child mode:* Listen. Give it a score from 0 to 3. Now the door shuts. Score it again.
  - Note: The helper must not change the volume between notes. Low notes often sound softer than high notes at the same volume, because ears are less sensitive to them. That is why we compare how much each note drops when the door shuts.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out how much each note dropped.  
  *Parent says:* Which note dropped more when the door shut?  
  *Child mode:* Which note lost more when the door shut?
  - Check: Lakshmi hears her neighbour's music through the wall. She hears the drum beat but not the words. Why? → **Walls and doors block high sounds more than low sounds**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about elephants.  
  *Parent says:* Elephants call with very low rumbles. Why might low sounds be useful for a herd spread across a forest?  
  *Child mode:* Elephants rumble very low. Why is low good in a big forest?
  - Ask: Why might low rumbles help an elephant herd? (good: Low sounds pass through trees and travel far.; The herd can stay in touch from far away.)
  - Note: In your test the high note usually drops more. If both dropped the same, the door may have a big gap. Try a heavier door.

### a6 · Why Do Ears Pop? The tube to your throat (5 min)
- **safety:** Don't blow hard with your nose pinched. Swallowing gently is enough.
- **record:** Write one sentence about when your ears have popped before.
- **s1 (2 min)**

### c2 · Talk So Appooppan Can Hear (25 min)
- **problem:** Many grandparents hear low sounds but miss high ones, like the 's' in 'six'. Shouting often makes it worse. Find the ways of talking that work best.
- **your turn:** Choose the three ways that worked best in your test. Teach them to your family.
- **constraints:** No machines.; You must test each idea, not just guess.; The listener must not see a written word.
- **general:** Face the person, so they can see your lips.; Turn off the fan, the TV and the mixer.; Speak a little slower and a little lower, not louder.

- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The listener presses their ear flaps shut, to hear less, like someone with hearing loss.  
  *Parent says:* With your flaps shut, you will hear less. Now let's find which way of talking helps most.  
  *Child mode:* Press your ear flaps shut. Now you hear less.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say five words from behind the listener, in a normal voice. 2. Then face them. 3. Then turn off the fan and speak slowly. 4. Then shout. 5. Count how many words they get right each time.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me each word you hear. I will count the right ones.  
  *Child mode:* Say each word you hear. Your partner counts the right ones.
  - Note: Shouting makes the voice higher and blurs the sounds, so it often scores worst. Facing the person usually helps most.

## Applications
- Hearing tests by audiologists, and newborn hearing checks in hospitals
- Hearing aids, which make sounds louder for the pitches a person misses
- Cochlear implants, which send electric signals straight to the auditory nerve
- Ear protectors for factory workers, traffic police and people at airports
- Bone conduction headphones, which rest on the bone in front of the ear
- Ultrasound scans in hospitals, using sound far too high for us to hear
- Bat detectors, which slow down or lower bat calls so people can hear them
- Soundproof rooms and thick doors in recording studios

## Open questions
- **Why can birds grow new hair cells, but people cannot?** Scientists are studying the genes that switch this on in birds and fish. They hope one day to help people with hearing loss.
- **Why do some people hear a ringing that never stops, called tinnitus?** It often follows hearing loss, but nobody fully knows how the brain makes a sound that isn't there, or how to stop it.
- **What is it like for a bat to hear echoes?** Scientists can measure what a bat's ears and brain do. Nobody has a way to know what the bat experiences.
- **How do hundreds of bats calling at once in a cave not confuse each other?** Bats seem to change their calls to avoid each other, but scientists are still working out exactly how.

## Warm-up
- Which part of the ear vibrates first? → The eardrum.
- Name the three tiny bones. → The hammer, the anvil and the stirrup.
- What is the snail-shaped part of the ear called? → The cochlea.
- Do hair cells in people grow back? → No. Birds' do, but people's don't.
- Name an animal that hears ultrasound. → A bat, a dog, a cat, a mouse or a moth.
- Name an animal that hears infrasound. → An elephant or a pigeon.
- What does earwax do? → It traps dust and keeps the ear canal healthy.
- What is the arm's-length test? → If you must shout to be heard at an arm's length, the place is loud enough to harm hearing.
