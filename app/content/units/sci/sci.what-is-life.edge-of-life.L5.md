# Viruses and the edge of life: can one definition sort the living from the non-living?
`sci.what-is-life.edge-of-life.L5`

**Summary.** A virus can copy itself, yet it is not a cell, so is it alive? In this lesson you will play a copying game to see how mistakes build up, test 20 cases against NASA's definition of life, and walk a scale to find out how small a virus is.

**Intro.** *Doctors say that soap 'inactivates' a cold virus. Many of them avoid the word 'kills'. Can you kill something if nobody agrees that it is alive?* A teaspoon of sea water from Marina Beach in Chennai holds about fifty million viruses. Almost none of them can harm you. Nearly all of them infect the bacteria of the sea. A virus is a set of instructions wrapped in a coat. Alone, a virus particle takes in nothing and makes nothing. Inside the right cell, the cell reads the instructions and builds more viruses. So the same virus looks like a speck of dust in one place and like a living being in another. In this lesson you will find out why scientists cannot agree about viruses, what NASA means by the word 'life', and whether life on another world might be built in a different way.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a virus is made of, and what a cell has that a virus lacks.
1. I can describe, step by step, how a cell copies a virus.
2. I can explain why viruses evolve, using copying, mistakes and selection.
3. I can state NASA's working definition of life and explain its three parts.
4. I can give reasons for and against calling a virus alive.
5. I can explain why a definition of life is a tool, and name cases that strain it.
6. I can describe how life on another world might differ, and what it would still need.
7. I can plan a fair test, predict with a reason, and say what my results show.

## Terms
- **Virus**: A virus is a set of instructions, called a genome, wrapped in a coat of protein. A virus can be copied only inside a living cell. *e.g.* The virus that causes a common cold is copied inside the cells that line your nose.
- **Virion**: A virion is one complete virus particle outside a cell. It is the form in which a virus travels from one cell to the next. *e.g.* A sneeze carries thousands of virions in tiny drops of water.
- **Genome**: A genome is the full set of instructions for building a living being or a virus. It is written as a long chain of chemical letters. *e.g.* Your genome has about 3,000 million letters. The genome of a flu virus has about 13,500.
- **DNA and RNA**: DNA and RNA are the two chemicals that genomes are written in. Each is a long chain made of four kinds of small units, which we call letters. *e.g.* Your genome is DNA. The genomes of the flu virus and the measles virus are RNA.
- **Protein**: A protein is a chain of small units called amino acids, folded into a shape that does a job. Cells build proteins by reading genes. *e.g.* The coat of a virus is made of proteins. So are your muscles and the keratin of your nails.
- **Capsid**: A capsid is the protein coat of a virus. It is built from many copies of the same few proteins, and it protects the genome inside. *e.g.* The capsid of tobacco mosaic virus is a hollow rod made of 2,130 copies of one protein.
- **Envelope**: An envelope is a thin layer of fat that some viruses carry outside their capsid. The virus takes it from the membrane of the cell that made them. *e.g.* Flu viruses and measles viruses have an envelope. Polio viruses do not.
- **Host cell**: A host cell is a living cell that a virus gets into and that then builds new viruses. *e.g.* For tobacco mosaic virus, the host cells are the leaf cells of tomato, tobacco and chilli plants.
- **Receptor**: A receptor is a molecule on the outside of a cell that has a job for the cell. A virus can enter only a cell whose receptor fits the virus's coat, as a lock fits a key. *e.g.* The measles virus fits a receptor found on human cells, so measles spreads only among humans.
- **Ribosome**: A ribosome is a tiny machine inside a cell that reads instructions and joins amino acids into proteins. Every cell has ribosomes. No virus has them. *e.g.* One bacterium holds thousands of ribosomes. They build virus proteins when a virus's instructions arrive.
- **Mutation**: A mutation is a change in a genome. Most mutations are copying mistakes, where one letter is swapped, lost or added. *e.g.* Flu viruses pick up mutations every year, which is why last year's flu vaccine may not fit this year's virus.
- **Darwinian evolution**: Darwinian evolution is change over generations that happens when copies differ a little, the differences are passed on, and some copies leave more copies than others. *e.g.* A virus with a mutation that helps them enter cells leaves more copies, so that mutation becomes common.
- **Self-sustaining**: Self-sustaining means keeping yourself going. A self-sustaining system takes in energy and materials and uses them to build and repair its own parts. *e.g.* A curd bacterium takes in milk sugar and builds new parts from it. A virus particle on a spoon does not.
- **Working definition**: A working definition is a rule that people agree to use for now, because it is useful. They expect to change it when they learn more. *e.g.* NASA's definition of life is a working definition, written to guide the search for life on other worlds.
- **Bacteriophage**: A bacteriophage, or phage, is a virus whose host cells are bacteria. Phages are the most common viruses on Earth. *e.g.* The phage called T4 infects the gut bacterium E. coli and looks like a tiny moon lander.
- **Giant virus**: A giant virus is a virus as large as a small bacterium, with hundreds or thousands of genes. *e.g.* Mimivirus has about 1,000 genes. A flu virus has about 12.
- **Viroid**: A viroid is a short loop of RNA with no coat at all. A viroid carries no instructions for any protein, yet plant cells make copies of them. *e.g.* A viroid of only 246 letters killed millions of coconut palms in the Philippines.
- **Inactivate**: To inactivate a virus is to damage them so that no cell can copy them any more. *e.g.* Soap inactivates a flu virus by pulling apart the envelope of fat around them.
- **Nanometre**: A nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre. Scientists measure viruses in nanometres. *e.g.* A flu virus is about 100 nanometres across. Ten thousand of them in a row would cross one millimetre.
- **Astrobiology**: Astrobiology is the science that studies how life begins, and whether there is life beyond Earth. *e.g.* Astrobiologists study the hot springs of Puga valley in Ladakh to learn how life copes with harsh places.

## Explanations
### What a virus is made of · *defined*
**Definition:** A virus is a genome of DNA or RNA inside a coat of protein called a capsid. Some viruses also have an outer envelope of fat.

A virus has very few parts. In the middle is the genome, a chain of chemical letters that holds the instructions. Around the genome is the capsid, a coat built from many copies of the same few proteins. Why so few kinds of protein? A genome this short has room for only a few instructions, so one small protein is made again and again and stacked into a shell. Some viruses, such as flu and measles, also wear an envelope. This is a thin layer of fat that the virus takes from the membrane of the cell that built them. Viruses are very small. Most are between 20 and 300 nanometres across, and a nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre. A light microscope cannot show a thing that small, because the waves of light are wider than most viruses. So scientists use an electron microscope, which uses a beam of electrons in place of light.
- e.g. Tobacco mosaic virus is a stiff rod 300 nanometres long. It has one strand of RNA with about 6,400 letters, inside 2,130 copies of one coat protein.
- e.g. A flu virus has 8 short pieces of RNA, about 13,500 letters in all, inside an envelope with spikes.
- e.g. In 1964 June Almeida, a Scottish scientist who had left school at 16, took the first electron microscope pictures of a human coronavirus.
### What a cell has that a virus particle does not · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell has ribosomes to build proteins and a way to get energy from food or light. A virus particle has neither.

Compare a virus with the smallest living cell. A cell has a membrane that takes in food and keeps the inside different from the outside. A cell has thousands of ribosomes, the machines that read instructions and build proteins. A cell breaks down food to get energy, and stores that energy in a small molecule called ATP. With these, a cell builds every part of a new cell. A virus particle has instructions and a coat, and usually nothing else. They have no ribosomes, so they cannot build a single protein. They make no ATP, so they have no supply of energy. This is why a virus particle outside a cell takes in nothing and builds nothing. It is also why antibiotics do not cure a cold. An antibiotic works by jamming a part that bacteria have, such as the bacterial ribosome or the bacterial cell wall. A virus has no such part to jam.
- e.g. A curd bacterium in warm milk takes in milk sugar and divides about once an hour. A virus particle in the same milk stays just as they are.
- e.g. Scientists can grow bacteria in a dish of broth. Nobody has ever grown a virus in broth without living cells in it.
- e.g. Flu viruses for vaccines are grown inside fertilised hens' eggs, because the viruses need living cells.
- e.g. Venkatraman Ramakrishnan, who was born in Chidambaram in Tamil Nadu, helped to map the ribosome atom by atom. The maps show where antibiotics jam the bacterial ribosome. He shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2009.
### How a cell copies a virus · *defined*
**Definition:** A virus is copied in six steps: attach, enter, uncoat, copy and build, assemble, and leave.

First the virus particle bumps into a cell by chance. If a protein on the virus's coat fits a receptor on the cell, the two stick together. This is the attach step. A receptor is there for the cell's own use, for example to take in food or to receive signals. The virus fits it the way a copied key fits a lock. Second, the virus or their genome gets inside. Third, the coat comes off and the genome is free. Fourth, the cell's ribosomes read the virus's genome in the same way as the cell's own genes, and build virus proteins. The cell also copies the virus genome many times. Fifth, the new coat proteins and the new genomes join up into new particles. In many viruses they do this by themselves, because their shapes and electric charges fit together. Sixth, the new particles leave. Some burst the cell open, and some bud out through the membrane and take a piece of it as an envelope. The fit between coat and receptor explains why each virus has only certain hosts. A virus whose key fits no lock on your cells cannot infect you.
- e.g. A T4 phage lands on a bacterium, injects their DNA, and about 25 minutes later 100 to 200 new phages burst out.
- e.g. Tomato mosaic virus has no way through a plant's cell wall. They enter through a tiny wound, such as a broken leaf hair.
- e.g. You can eat a tomato from a plant with mosaic virus. Your cells have no receptor that fits this virus.
### Why viruses evolve, and why some evolve fast · *defined*
**Definition:** Viruses evolve because their genomes are copied with small mistakes, the mistakes are passed on, and some copies get into more cells than others.

Evolution needs three things. There must be copies. The copies must differ a little, and the differences must be passed on. And some copies must leave more copies than others. Viruses have all three. When a cell copies a virus genome, the copying enzyme sometimes puts in a wrong letter. A copying enzyme is a protein that builds the new chain, one letter at a time. Your own cells check each new letter and repair most mistakes, so only about one letter in a thousand million ends up wrong. Most RNA viruses bring a copying enzyme that does no checking. It gets about one letter wrong in every ten thousand to hundred thousand. One infected cell makes thousands of copies, so many of the new viruses differ from the parent by a letter or two. Most changes do harm, and those viruses are not copied again. A few changes help, for example by hiding the virus from the body's defences. Those viruses are copied more, and soon they are the common kind. Nothing in this needs the virus particle to do any work. The cell does the copying, and the world does the choosing.
- e.g. Flu viruses change enough each year that a new flu vaccine is made for most years.
- e.g. During the Covid-19 pandemic, scientists read millions of virus genomes and watched new variants appear and spread.
- e.g. Coronaviruses have an unusual checking enzyme. It lets them keep a genome of 30,000 letters, which is very long for an RNA virus.
### NASA's working definition of life, word by word · *debated*
In 1994, a group of scientists advising NASA needed a definition to guide the search for life on other worlds. They agreed on this one: 'Life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution.' The biochemist Gerald Joyce wrote it down, and he says that Carl Sagan suggested the idea. Take it one part at a time. 'Chemical system' means a group of chemicals that act on one another. This leaves out computer programs, which are patterns in a machine. 'Self-sustaining' means the system keeps itself going. It takes in energy and materials and builds its own parts. 'Capable of Darwinian evolution' means it makes copies that differ a little and pass the differences on, so that its kind can change over generations. Why these three? A flame is chemical and keeps itself going, but it has no instructions to pass on, so a flame fails the third part. A virus is chemical and evolves, but a virus particle builds nothing by themselves, so a virus fails the second part. Of all the things we know, only cells pass all three. The definition has problems. A mule cannot have young, so one mule cannot evolve. The definition must be about a kind of being over many generations, and that makes it hard to use on one sample from Mars. And no living thing is fully self-sustaining. You cannot make your own food, and you need plants to do it. So 'self-sustaining' is a matter of degree.
- e.g. A candle flame: chemical, keeps itself going while the wax lasts, but cannot evolve. Two parts out of three.
- e.g. A cold virus on a door handle: chemical, their kind evolves, but they do not keep themselves going. Two parts out of three.
- e.g. A curd bacterium: chemical, self-sustaining and able to evolve. Three parts out of three.
- **Gerald Joyce and the NASA group, 1994:** The definition is a useful guide for now. Evolution is the one thing that life does and that nothing else in chemistry does, so a test for life should look for it.
- **Carol Cleland, a philosopher of science:** It is too early to define life. We know only one kind, the life of Earth, and all of it is related. A definition made from one example may describe only that example.
- **Scientists who design space missions:** A probe cannot wait for generations to watch evolution. It needs signs it can measure in a day, such as molecules too complicated to form without life.
### The great debate: is a virus alive? · *debated*
Scientists agree about what a virus is made of and what a virus does. They disagree about which word fits. The disagreement is not about facts. It is about which test for life matters most. If you think life means doing your own chemistry, a virus fails. If you think life means carrying instructions that evolve, a virus passes. A third group points out that we may be looking at the wrong object. A seed in a jar and the plant in the field are the same being at two stages. Perhaps the virus particle is like the seed, and the infected cell is the virus's active stage. You can test each view against the hard cases in this lesson. Ask which cases the view sorts well, and which ones it sorts oddly.
- e.g. A doctor says a vaccine holds 'live virus' or 'killed virus'. She is using the words of life.
- e.g. A chemist can store tobacco mosaic virus as crystals in a bottle. She is treating the virus as a chemical.
- e.g. Both of them are describing the same particle correctly.
- **David Moreira and Purificación López-García, 2009:** Viruses are not alive. They have no chemistry of their own and they do not copy themselves, because cells copy them. Many of their genes were taken from their hosts. Life is made of cells, and viruses are something cells produce.
- **Patrick Forterre, 2010:** Viruses are alive, but the particle is only their travelling stage. The living stage is the infected cell, which he calls a virocell. A virocell takes in food, makes energy and builds, all under the virus's instructions.
- **Many biologists today:** 'Alive' has no sharp edge. Stones, prions, viroids, viruses, bacteria that live only inside other cells, and free-living bacteria form a long slope. It is more useful to say what each one can do than to argue about one word.
### Giant viruses, tiny cells, viroids and prions: the cases in between · *defined*
**Definition:** Between a stone and a free-living cell there are many kinds of things: prions, viroids, small viruses, giant viruses, and cells that can live only inside other cells.

For a long time, the rule seemed simple. Viruses were tiny and had a handful of genes, and cells were big and had thousands. Then the gap began to fill from both sides. In 1992, a microbe was found in a water cooling tower in Bradford in England. The microbe was large enough to see with a light microscope, so scientists filed them as a bacterium. In 2003, Bernard La Scola and his team in France showed that the microbe was a virus, and gave them the name mimivirus. Mimivirus has about 1,000 genes, more than some bacteria have. A few of those genes are for parts of the protein-building system, which no virus was supposed to have. Mimivirus still has no ribosomes, so the virus still needs a cell. In 2008, the same team found a small virus, Sputnik, that can be copied only inside the copying factory of a giant virus. From the other side, scientists found bacteria that have lost most of their genes. Carsonella lives inside the cells of a plant-sucking insect and has only 182 genes. Carsonella cannot live outside those cells, yet every biologist calls them a living cell. Below the viruses there are simpler things still. A viroid is a bare loop of RNA, a few hundred letters long, with no coat. A prion is a wrongly folded protein that makes normal proteins of the same kind fold wrongly too. A prion has no genome at all, yet prions spread.
- e.g. Mimivirus: about 1,000 genes, no ribosomes, copied only inside an amoeba.
- e.g. Carsonella: 182 genes, has ribosomes, lives only inside insect cells.
- e.g. Potato spindle tuber viroid: 359 letters of RNA, no coat, no protein, found by Theodor Diener in 1971.
### Viruses sense, signal and wait · *debated*
Some phages do not burst their host cell straight away. They can slip their genome into the bacterium's own genome and wait there, and they are copied each time the bacterium divides. So each phage has two paths: burst out now, or hide and wait. In 2017, a team led by Rotem Sorek in Israel found how one group of phages chooses. Each time one of these phages infects a bacterium, the infected cell lets out a small signal molecule. The team named it arbitrium, from the Latin word for decision. Later phages measure how much arbitrium is around. If there is a lot, many bacteria have already been infected and few hosts are left. Then the phage hides and waits. If there is little, the phage bursts out. So these viruses send a signal, read it and act on it. Bacteria respond too. Many bacteria keep short pieces of the DNA of phages that attacked them, in a part of their genome called CRISPR. With these pieces they recognise the same phage later and cut that phage's DNA.
- e.g. A phage that finds few hosts left hides in the bacterium's genome and waits for better times.
- e.g. A bacterium who survives a phage keeps a piece of that phage's DNA and passes the piece on to their daughter cells.
- e.g. Scientists turned CRISPR into a tool for editing genes. Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this in 2020.
- **What the evidence shows:** These phages sense a signal, compare its amount and take one of two paths. That is sensing, deciding and acting, done with a few molecules. Viruses think in this simple way, as cells do.
- **Many biologists:** These are chemical switches. A molecule binds a protein, and the protein turns a gene on or off. They would rather not use the word 'decide', because nobody has shown any awareness behind it.
### Where did viruses come from? · *debated*
Nobody knows where viruses came from. Viruses leave no fossils, and their genomes change so fast that old traces are wiped out. There are three main ideas. All three may be true for different viruses. What scientists do agree on is that viruses are very old. Viruses infect every branch of cellular life, and some virus coat proteins have the same folded shape in viruses of bacteria and viruses of humans. That points to viruses that already existed when the first cells were alive, more than 3,000 million years ago.
- e.g. A coat protein with the same fold is found in a phage of bacteria and in a virus of humans, which suggests one very old ancestor.
- e.g. Mimivirus has genes for protein-building, which could be leftovers from a cell ancestor or could have been picked up from hosts.
- e.g. Some genes in cells can jump from place to place in the genome. A jumping gene that gained a coat would look much like a virus.
- **The escape idea:** Viruses began as pieces of a cell's genome that gained a coat and became able to move from cell to cell.
- **The shrinking idea:** Viruses began as small cells that lived inside larger cells. Over a very long time they lost every part they could borrow from the host, as Carsonella is doing now.
- **The virus-first idea, argued by Eugene Koonin and others:** Things like viruses were copying themselves before the first true cells existed. On this view, viruses did not come from cells. They are as old as life, or older.
### What viruses do in the living world · *defined*
**Definition:** Viruses are the most numerous biological things on Earth. They recycle nutrients in the sea, move genes between living beings, and have left their genes in every genome, including yours.

Most people meet viruses only as illness. That is a small part of what viruses do. A millilitre of surface sea water holds about ten million virus particles. Almost all of them are phages, whose hosts are bacteria. Every day, viruses, mostly phages, kill about a fifth of all the tiny living things in the sea. Most of these are bacteria. The burst cells spill their contents into the water, and other microbes feed on them. So viruses keep carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus moving through the sea. Viruses also carry genes from one host to another. And sometimes a virus genome gets stuck in the genome of an egg or sperm cell, and is passed to every descendant. About 8 of every 100 letters in the human genome came from such viruses. Some of those old virus genes now do jobs for us. A gene called syncytin once made the envelope protein of a virus. Today it builds a layer of the placenta, the organ that feeds a baby before birth. The placenta as we know it could not form without that old virus gene.
- e.g. There are thought to be about ten times as many viruses in the sea as bacteria.
- e.g. In 1896, Ernest Hankin reported that water from the Ganga and the Yamuna destroyed cholera bacteria, even after filtering. Phages are one possible cause, and scientists still debate it.
- e.g. Indian farmers spray a virus called HaNPV on chickpea and cotton. The virus infects only the caterpillars of the pod borer moth.
### Life as we do not know it: what could be different on another world? · *idea*
All life on Earth is related, so we have only one example of life. It uses water as its liquid, carbon chains as its frame, and DNA or RNA for its instructions. Which of these are needed, and which are only how things happened here? Start with the liquid. Chemicals must meet in order to react, and they meet easily when they are dissolved in a liquid. Water is very good at dissolving salts, sugars and many other chemicals. Titan, a moon of Saturn, has lakes of liquid methane and ethane at about 179 degrees below zero. These are oily liquids. Salt and sugar do not dissolve in them, and neither does DNA. Life in such a lake would need different molecules and a different kind of membrane. Next, the frame. A carbon atom makes four bonds, so carbon atoms join into long chains and rings. Silicon also makes four bonds, but chains of silicon atoms break apart in water, and silicon joined with oxygen becomes solid rock. So most scientists think carbon is the likelier frame. Last, the instructions. In 2012, a team led by Philipp Holliger in Cambridge built six new chemicals, called XNAs, that can store instructions and be copied with changes. So DNA and RNA are not the only chemicals that can do this job. What would probably stay the same? A liquid, a supply of energy, a boundary, and instructions that are copied with small mistakes.
- e.g. NASA's Dragonfly, a flying robot, is planned to leave for Titan in 2028 and to arrive in 2034.
- e.g. In 2015, scientists at Cornell worked out on a computer a membrane that might form in liquid methane. A study in 2020 found that it probably would not form.
- e.g. Carol Cleland and Shelley Copley asked in 2005 whether a second kind of life might exist on Earth unnoticed, because all our tests look for our own kind.
### How could we recognise life that is not like ours? · *debated*
A test that looks for DNA will miss life that uses another chemical. So some scientists look for signs that do not depend on what the life is made of. One idea is called assembly theory, from Lee Cronin, Sara Walker and their teams. They count the smallest number of joining steps needed to build a molecule. Simple molecules form by chance. A molecule that needs more than about 15 steps, and is found in large numbers, was probably made by something that follows instructions. In a study in 2021, only samples from living things held such molecules. Other scientists say the step count has not been tested widely enough. The need for a clear test was shown in 1976. NASA's two Viking landers put food marked with radioactive carbon onto Mars soil. Gas came off the soil, as it would if microbes were eating the food. But another instrument found no carbon molecules in the soil at all. Gilbert Levin and Patricia Straat, who ran the test, argued that it had found life. Most scientists think that harsh chemicals in the soil broke down the food. In 2008 a later lander found such a chemical, perchlorate, in Mars soil.
- e.g. A protein is a chain that needs hundreds of joining steps, and a cell makes millions of identical copies. That would not happen by chance.
- e.g. The Viking test gave a positive result that most scientists now explain without life.
- e.g. A sample from a far world may show something that is neither clearly alive nor clearly not, as a virus does on Earth.
- **Lee Cronin, Sara Walker and their teams:** Life is the only thing that makes many copies of very complicated molecules. Counting building steps is a test for life that works whatever the life is made of.
- **Scientists who doubt it:** Some minerals and some reactions without life can also make complicated things. A single number is too simple, and it has been tried on too few samples.
### Why the definition matters in practice · *defined*
**Definition:** A definition of life is a tool for making decisions, for example about medicines, about space missions and about what laboratories may build.

It can seem that arguing over a word changes nothing. A virus does what a virus does, whatever we call them. But definitions guide what people do. In medicine, knowing that a virus has no chemistry of their own tells doctors what will work. Antibiotics do nothing against viruses. So doctors block a step of the copying. A vaccine teaches the body to make antibodies. An antibody is a protein that sticks to the virus's coat, so that the coat no longer fits the receptor. In space science, the definition decides which instruments fly. A probe built to look for DNA cannot find life made of something else. Space agencies also clean their landers very carefully, so that Earth's microbes are not carried to Mars and mistaken for Mars life. In the laboratory, scientists can now build viruses from chemicals. In 2002, Eckard Wimmer's team built polio virus from mail-ordered pieces of DNA, using the published genome. If a thing can be made from bottled chemicals, was a living thing made? Laws about what may be built depend on such answers.
- e.g. A doctor does not give an antibiotic for a cold, because a cold is caused by a virus.
- e.g. India's Covaxin was made from whole coronaviruses that had been inactivated with a chemical.
- e.g. In 2024, a group of scientists asked the world not to build 'mirror' bacteria, whose molecules would be mirror images of ours, because our bodies' defences might not recognise them.

## Mostly agreed
- A virus is a genome of DNA or RNA inside a protein coat, and some viruses have an envelope of fat as well. *How we know:* Electron microscopes show the particles. X-ray studies, such as Rosalind Franklin's work on tobacco mosaic virus in the 1950s, showed where the RNA sits inside the coat.
- A virus can be copied only inside a living cell. *How we know:* In over a hundred years, nobody has grown a virus in food alone. Viruses multiply only when living cells are present, for example in hens' eggs or in dishes of cells.
- The genome carries the instructions, and the coat does not. *How we know:* In 1952, Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase showed that a phage's DNA goes into the bacterium while most of the coat stays outside, and new phages are still made.
- Viruses evolve by copying mistakes and selection. *How we know:* Scientists read the genomes of flu viruses and coronaviruses from patients every week. They see new mutations appear, and they see some of them become common.
- Every kind of cellular life has viruses: bacteria, plants, fungi and animals, including us. *How we know:* Wherever scientists have looked, from hot springs to the deep sea, they have found viruses that infect the cells living there.
- There is no agreed definition of life. *How we know:* In 2011, Edward Trifonov collected 123 different definitions of life from scientific writing. No single one is accepted by everyone.

## How we found out
**Wendell Stanley, who crystallised a virus. Frederick Bawden and Norman Pirie, who found its RNA. Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase, who showed that the genome carries the instructions., 1935 (Stanley's crystals), 1936 (Bawden and Pirie) and 1952 (Hershey and Chase).** In 1935, the American chemist Wendell Stanley made crystals of tobacco mosaic virus. He reported that the virus was a pure protein. At that time most scientists believed that genes were made of protein. DNA and RNA seemed too simple, with only four kinds of unit. In 1936, Frederick Bawden and Norman Pirie in England found that the virus also held a little RNA. Few people thought this mattered. In 1952, at Cold Spring Harbor in New York, Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase tested the question with phages. Chase was 24 years old. They grew one batch of phages with radioactive sulphur, which goes only into protein. They grew another batch with radioactive phosphorus, which goes only into DNA. They let each batch infect bacteria, and then spun the mixture in a kitchen blender to shake the empty coats off. The sulphur stayed outside the bacteria. The phosphorus went inside, and new phages were made. So the instructions were in the DNA. Hershey shared a Nobel Prize in 1969. Chase did not.  
*What people believed before:* That a virus is pure protein, and that genes are made of protein. Hershey and Chase showed that a phage's DNA enters the bacterium and directs the making of new phages, while most of the protein coat stays outside.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A virus is alive, and the infected cell is the virus's living body.** (Patrick Forterre, a French biologist, 2010; *debated*)  
  For: An infected cell feeds, makes energy and builds under the virus's instructions. Calling the particle 'the virus' is like calling a seed 'the plant'.  
  Against: The ribosomes, the energy and the membrane all belong to the cell. On this view, one cell would be two living beings at once, and it is unclear where one ends.
- **Viruses are not alive and do not belong on the tree of life.** (David Moreira and Purificación López-García, 2009; *debated*)  
  For: Viruses have no chemistry of their own, cells do all the copying, and many virus genes were taken from hosts. A tree of life should show cells only.  
  Against: Viruses have genes found in no cell, they evolve, and they have shaped the genomes of every living being. Leaving them out hides a large part of life's history.
- **Giant viruses are a fourth great branch of life, beside bacteria, archaea and cells with a nucleus.** (Didier Raoult and colleagues, around 2010; *minority*)  
  For: Giant viruses have some very old genes for protein-building that seem to branch off on their own.  
  Against: Later studies found that giant viruses picked up most of these genes from their hosts at different times. Most scientists do not accept a fourth branch.
- **The Viking landers found life on Mars in 1976.** (Gilbert Levin and Patricia Straat, who ran the Viking test; *minority*)  
  For: Mars soil gave off gas when fed, and soil that had been heated first did not. That is how soil with microbes behaves on Earth.  
  Against: No carbon molecules were found in the same soil. Mars soil contains perchlorate, a harsh chemical that can break down the food without any life.
- **Antibiotics help you get over a cold.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: People sometimes feel better after taking one. But a cold ends by itself in about a week.  
  Against: Antibiotics jam parts that only bacteria have. A cold virus has no such parts. Taking antibiotics when they are not needed also helps tough bacteria to spread.
- **We should stop trying to define life.** (Carol Cleland, a philosopher of science, and others; *debated*)  
  For: People could not define water correctly until they knew what it is made of. We know only one kind of life, so any definition now is a guess from one example.  
  Against: Missions and laws need some rule to work with today. A working definition can be useful even if it is later replaced.

## Roots
We have learned what a virus is, why scientists cannot agree whether viruses are alive, and how NASA defines life. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Copying Chain, the pencil line did no work at all. You did all the copying. Yet the line changed over ten generations, and when you kept the longest copy each time, the line grew steadily longer. A cell copies a virus in the same way. So a thing can evolve without doing any of the work of living.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think that a thing must first be alive, and that evolving is something living things then do. But evolution needs only copies, small mistakes in the copying, and some copies being copied more than others. That is why NASA's definition needs two separate parts, 'self-sustaining' and 'capable of evolution'. A virus has the second without the first.  
*Evidence:* In the Copying Chain, a line copied from copy to copy wandered far from 100 millimetres, and a line chosen for length grew by a few millimetres each generation. The line never copied itself. Flu viruses change every year in the same way, though a virus particle makes no energy and builds nothing.

- *who am i:* About 8 of every 100 letters in your genome came from viruses that infected your ancestors. One old virus gene helped build the placenta that fed you before you were born.
- *what is life:* No living being keeps going entirely alone. A virus needs a cell, you need plants, and plants need the Sun. What differs is how much each one borrows.
- *what is this world:* The world has stones, prions, viroids, viruses, tiny bacteria and banyan trees, with something in between each pair. The sorting into two boxes, living and non-living, is done by people.

A virus, a bacterium and a grain of sand are built from the same kinds of atoms, and those atoms were made in stars. From that one stock of atoms come things that do no chemistry of their own, things that are copied, and things that copy themselves. There is no step on the way where something new is added from outside. The many kinds of things, and the many kinds of minds, come out of the same one source, and our words 'living' and 'non-living' are a rough sorting of them.

- **Udana 6.4 (the Buddha's story of the blind men and the elephant, also told by Jain teachers to explain anekantavada, many-sidedness):** “ediso, deva, hatthī seyyathāpi kumbho. / ediso, deva, hatthī seyyathāpi suppo. / ediso, deva, hatthī seyyathāpi thūṇo.” Several blind men each touch one part of an elephant. One says an elephant is like a pillar, and another says like a fan. Each is right about his part, and wrong to think it is the whole. (One scientist touches the virus particle and says 'a chemical'. Another touches the infected cell and says 'alive'. Each definition of life describes one side of the same thing correctly.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 1 (paraphrased):** “道可道，非常道，名可名，非常名。” The name that can be spoken is not the lasting name. Names are how we divide the world into ten thousand things. (The word 'alive' was in use long before anyone had seen a cell or a virus. The world did not promise to fit the word. A virus shows us where the word runs out.)
- **J.C. Bose, Response in the Living and Non-Living (1902):** Jagadish Chandra Bose measured how metals, plants and muscles respond to being struck, tired and poisoned. He found the same kinds of response in all three, and wrote that he could find no sharp break between the living and the non-living. (Bose's measurements of plants were real and ahead of their time. His wider idea is not accepted by most scientists. Viruses, viroids and prions, which he never knew, sit exactly where he said there was no break.)
- Science (settled): About 8 percent of the human genome came from viruses, and the placenta is built with a gene that was once a virus's envelope gene (Mi and others, Nature, 2000).
- Science (settled): Viruses evolve by the same copying mistakes and selection as every living being.
- Science (debated): Whether a virus is alive, and whether the infected cell should count as the living virus.
- Science (open): Whether anything like awareness goes with the sensing and signalling that viruses and single cells do. Nobody knows how to test this yet.
- Other view, Most biologists, and thinkers of the Charvaka school in ancient India: Many biologists say the line is real and sharp enough. A thing with ribosomes and its own supply of energy is alive, and everything else is not. A few odd cases at the border do not remove the border, just as dusk does not make day and night the same. Most brain scientists also think that awareness needs a brain. The Charvaka thinkers taught that only matter exists, and that life and mind appear when matter is arranged in the right way, as the power to make people drunk appears when grain is fermented.

Now feel your stomach. Your last meal was grown by plants and cooked by someone. Tiny beings in your gut are helping you to digest it right now. Now hold your breath gently and count to ten. Feel how soon you need the outside again. Breathe normally. Open your eyes. Tell me one thing you did today with no help from any other living being.
- Ask: A virus is copied only inside a cell, and you stay alive only with air, food and water from outside. So is being alive something you have, or something you are given all the time?
- Ask: A pencil line can evolve if someone copies it. Does that make evolution less special, or copying more special?
- Ask: People made the word 'alive' before they knew about cells or viruses. If the world does not fit the word, which one should change?

## Story: The Tenth Pot
*A made-up story. Nila, Kathir, Paatti and Dr Vasuki are imagined. The science is real. Tomato mosaic virus is a close relative of tobacco mosaic virus. They spread from plant to plant in sap, on hands and tools, and the leaves show a patchwork of light and dark green about ten days to two weeks later. Washing hands with soap between plants is the advice given to growers. The tomatoes are safe to eat. The reasoning that a poison should get weaker as it is passed on, while this sickness does not, is how Martinus Beijerinck argued in 1898 that the cause multiplies inside the plant. The Agricultural College and Research Institute in Madurai is a real college.*

Paatti grew ten tomato plants on the terrace in Madurai, in ten grow bags, in one straight row along the parapet wall. Each bag stood on its own two bricks, so that no water ran from one to the next.

Nila had one job in the row. Every morning before school she pinched out the side shoots, the small green shoots that sprout where a leaf joins the stem. Paatti had shown her how. Thumb and finger, a quick snap. Pot one, pot two, pot three, all the way down to pot ten. By the end her fingertips were green and smelled of tomato leaf.

In the first week of the holidays her cousin Kathir came to stay. He was eight, and he followed her up the stairs each morning and gave his opinion on everything.

It was Kathir who saw the first pot. 'This one is ill,' he said.

The new leaves of pot one had come out patchy. Light green and dark green lay side by side on the same leaf, like a kolam drawn in two greens. Some leaves were narrow and curled.

'Something bad is in the soil,' said Kathir. 'A poison. Or bad water.'

That seemed sensible. Paatti scraped off the top soil of pot one and put in fresh compost. She gave pot one water from a separate mug. Nila went on pinching, pot one to pot ten.

Twelve days later pot two had the two greens. A few days after that, pot three. Then pot four.

'The poison is spreading,' said Kathir.

'How?' said Nila. 'The bags do not touch. The water does not run between them. You saw the bricks.'

'Through the air, then.'

'Then why pot two before pot nine? Air does not go down a row in order.'

Kathir had no answer, and for once he said so.

That evening Paatti made rasam, and Nila watched her drop a marble of tamarind into the water. She thought about it all through dinner. Afterwards she lined up ten steel tumblers on the kitchen counter and filled them with water. She squeezed tamarind into the first, until it was brown and sour. Then she poured a spoonful from the first tumbler into the second, a spoonful from the second into the third, and on down the line.

'Taste,' she told Kathir.

He tasted the first and pulled a face. The second was a little sour. The third tasted of almost nothing. By the fifth it was plain water.

'That is what a poison would do,' said Nila. 'Each time it is passed on, there is less of it. Pot one would be very ill. Pot two a little ill. Pot four would be fine.' She took him up to the terrace with a torch. 'Now look.'

Pot four was as patchy as pot one. If anything, the leaves were worse.

'So it is not being shared out,' said Nila slowly. 'It is not getting less. Each plant has as much as the one before. Whatever it is, more of it is being made inside every plant.'

Kathir thought about this. 'Then it is alive,' he said. 'Only living things make more of themselves. It is an insect. Or a mould.'

They borrowed Paatti's reading lens and looked at every leaf of pot four, top and underside. No insects. No eggs. No fluff of mould. Only leaf, in two greens.

'Something too small for the lens,' said Nila. 'Something that goes from pot one to pot two to pot three, in that order, and does not fly.'

She was holding a leaf as she said it. She looked at the leaf. Then she looked at her own thumb and finger, still faintly green from the morning.

'Oh,' she said.

'What?'

'Me. I go from pot one to pot two to pot three. Every morning. In that order. With sap on my fingers.'

Kathir stared at her hand as if it might bite him.

The next morning Nila did the row differently. She carried up a mug of water, a cake of soap and a towel. She began at pot ten, where the leaves were still one clean green. She pinched the shoots of pot ten and washed her hands with soap. Pot nine, and soap again. Pot eight. Kathir timed her and complained that it took four times as long.

They waited. Paatti said waiting was the longest part of gardening. Twelve days passed, and then two weeks. Pots five, six and seven turned patchy, one after another, because Nila had touched them before she understood. Pots eight, nine and ten stayed one clean green.

Paatti rang her old friend Dr Vasuki, who taught at the agricultural college, and held the phone so that both children could hear.

'Tomato mosaic,' said Dr Vasuki at once. 'A virus. Far too small for your lens, or for my microscope either. You worked it out the way the first virus scientists did. A poison thins out. This does not. And you were lucky with the last three pots. By the end of the row there was little sap left on your fingers.'

'So Kathir was right,' said Nila. 'It is alive.'

There was a pause on the line. 'In the dried sap on your fingers,' said Dr Vasuki, 'they do nothing at all. No feeding, no growing. You could keep that sap in a tin for years. Inside a leaf cell, the cell reads their instructions and builds thousands more. Are they alive? People in my college have argued about that since before I was a student. I change my mind about once a year.'

'What about the tomatoes?' asked Kathir. 'Can we eat them?'

'Certainly. These viruses fit a tomato cell. They do not fit yours.'

After the call Nila sat on the terrace step and looked at her washed hands. The same thing had been on her fingers and in the leaf. On her fingers it was like dust. In the leaf it was like a living being. She could not make the two fit into one word, and she found that she did not mind.

Paatti picked six tomatoes from pot one and went down to make rasam.

**For the parent:** A poison gets weaker each time it is passed on, because the same amount is shared out among more plants. The sickness in Paatti's row did not get weaker. So more of it was being made inside every plant. That is the mark of a virus: a cell copies them, so there is always as much as before. Nila also found that the same virus did nothing in dried sap on her fingers and was copied thousands of times inside a leaf cell. Whether to call them alive depends on which of those two places you look at.

- Ask: A poison gets weaker as it is passed along. Why did the sickness in the row not get weaker?
- Ask: Why did the sickness move down the row in order, and not jump to pot nine?
- Ask: Why did washing with soap between plants protect pots eight, nine and ten?
- Ask: On Nila's fingers the virus did nothing. In the leaf, they were copied. Would you call them alive? Why?
- Ask: Dr Vasuki changes her mind about once a year. Is that a good or a bad thing in a scientist?

### e1 · The Copying Chain: what happens when copies are copied? (45 min)
- **question:** If a line is copied from copy to copy for ten generations, how far does it change? And what happens if, in every generation, only the longest of three copies is copied again?

  *Child mode:* Now copy the original every time. Look, count to three, turn it over, draw. Do this for B1 to B10.
  - Note: Set B is the control. It shows how big one copying mistake is, when mistakes cannot add up.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start from the original again. 2. Your child draws three copies of it on three strips, looking afresh before each one. 3. Line up the left ends of the three lines and keep the longest. 4. Write 'P1' on its back. 5. Draw three copies of P1 and keep the longest as P2. 6. Go on until P8.  
  *Parent says:* This time the rule is that only the longest copy gets copied again. Draw three copies, keep the longest, and copy that one. We do this for eight generations.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three copies of the parent. Line up their ends. Keep the longest and name it P1. Copy P1 three times. Keep the longest again. Go on to P8.
  - Ask: You are trying to draw every line the same length. So can the line still get longer? (good: Yes, because some copies come out a bit long by mistake, and we keep those.; Each kept mistake becomes the new starting point.)
  - Note: Your child should still try to copy each line exactly. They must not draw longer on purpose. Put the two copies that were not kept in a separate pile.
- **s6 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now bring out the ruler. 2. Measure A1 to A10, B1 to B10 and P1 to P8 in millimetres, from end to end in a straight line. 3. Write each length in the table. 4. For chain A and for set B, find the line that is furthest from 100 millimetres.  
  *Parent says:* Now we measure. Which line in chain A is furthest from 100 millimetres? Which line in set B is furthest? Which of the two gaps is bigger?  
  *Child mode:* Measure every line in millimetres. Write each one in the table. Find the line furthest from 100 in chain A. Find the furthest in set B.
  - Note: Set B lines usually stay within about 10 millimetres of 100. Chain A usually wanders 15 to 40 millimetres away. A chain can also wander away and come back. If chain A ends within 5 millimetres of 100, look at its furthest point, or draw a second chain.
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws a line graph of chain A and of part 2. 2. Ask for the three sentences. 3. Ask who did the copying and who did the choosing. 4. Only then read the explanation cards together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why. Then tell me this. Did the line copy itself? Who copied it, and who chose the longest?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the graph, or lay the strips side by side in order. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: Why did chain A wander further than set B? (good: In chain A each mistake was copied into the next line, so mistakes added up.; In set B each copy started again from the true length.)
  - Ask: In part 2 the line got longer every generation. Did the line do anything to make that happen? (good: No. I copied it and I chose the longest.; It only needed copies, small mistakes and a rule about which copy is kept.)
  - Note: In part 2, expect a gain of about 3 to 8 millimetres in most generations, so about 130 to 160 millimetres at P8. This is the main idea of the unit. A virus genome is copied by a cell, with small mistakes, and the virus's surroundings 'keep' the copies that get into more cells.
  - Check: In part 2 of the Copying Chain, the line grew longer each generation. What made it grow? → **Small copying mistakes, and keeping the longest copy**
### e2 · Two Liquids: what can water dissolve, and what can oil dissolve? (40 min)
- **question:** Does cooking oil dissolve the same things that water dissolves?
- **predict:** Guess first, for each of the four things: salt, sugar, turmeric and ghee. Will it dissolve in water? Will it dissolve in oil? Write eight guesses as a tick or a cross, and give a reason for at least two of them.
- **fair test:** Change only the liquid. Use the same amount of liquid in every glass, 2 tablespoons. Use the same small amount of each thing, a quarter of a teaspoon. Stir each glass 30 times, and wait the same 5 minutes before you look. Use a clean, dry spoon for each glass, because one drop of water in the oil changes the result.
- **safety:** Wipe up spilt oil at once, because an oily floor is slippery. The oil can be strained and used for cooking the same day.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For each glass, write what you saw: gone and clear, clear but coloured, cloudy, lying at the bottom, or floating. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and what pattern you found.
- **results table:** Thing added | In water: what I saw | In oil: what I saw | Dissolved in water? | Dissolved in oil?
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set out 8 clear glasses in two rows of four. 2. Put 2 tablespoons of water in each glass of the back row. 3. Put 2 tablespoons of oil in each glass of the front row. 4. Write salt, sugar, turmeric and ghee on four slips and lay one in front of each pair.  
  *Parent says:* We have two liquids, water and oil. We will stir the same four things into each. Let's set out the glasses first.  
  *Child mode:* Set out 8 glasses in two rows. Put 2 tablespoons of water in four. Put 2 tablespoons of oil in four. Label the four pairs.
  - Note: The glasses and spoons must be dry before the oil goes in. If you have only 4 glasses, do the water round first, then wash and dry them well.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what 'dissolve' means. 2. Ask for the eight guesses. 3. Ask for a reason for at least two of them. 4. Your child writes the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* When something dissolves, it spreads through the liquid in pieces too small to see. Which of these four will dissolve in water? Which in oil? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess for each thing. Will it dissolve in water? Will it dissolve in oil? Write a tick or a cross. Give a reason for two of them.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Salt and sugar vanish in water when we cook, so I think water dissolves them.; Ghee is oily, so I think it will mix with oil and not with water.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Most children are sure about water, and are guessing about oil.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add a quarter teaspoon of salt to the first glass of water. 2. Stir 30 times. 3. Do the same with sugar, turmeric and ghee in the other three glasses of water. 4. Leave them to stand.  
  *Parent says:* Stir each one 30 times, and count aloud. What do you see happening in each glass as you stir?  
  *Child mode:* Add salt to the first glass of water. Stir 30 times. Do the same with sugar, turmeric and ghee. Leave them to stand.
  - Note: In a cold room, ghee stays in hard lumps in water. That is fine. It is a real result.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take a dry spoon. 2. Add a quarter teaspoon of salt to the first glass of oil and stir 30 times. 3. Do the same with sugar, turmeric and ghee. 4. Leave them to stand, and note the time.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same four things go into oil, with a dry spoon. Watch closely. Is the oil doing what the water did?  
  *Child mode:* Take a dry spoon. Add salt to the first glass of oil. Stir 30 times. Do the same with sugar, turmeric and ghee. Note the time.
  - Note: If the ghee is hard, it mixes into the oil slowly. Give that glass 10 minutes and stir again.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wait 5 minutes from the last stir. 2. Hold white paper behind each glass. 3. For each glass, your child says whether the liquid is clear or cloudy, and whether anything lies at the bottom or floats. 4. They fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look through the side of each glass. Is the liquid clear or cloudy? Has it changed colour? Is anything lying at the bottom or floating on top?  
  *Child mode:* Wait 5 minutes. Hold white paper behind each glass. Is the liquid clear or cloudy? Is anything at the bottom? Fill in the table.
  - Note: Turmeric is the hard one. In both liquids some powder settles, because turmeric powder is mostly plant fibre and starch. Look at the liquid above the powder. The oil is clear and strongly yellow, because the yellow colour has dissolved. The water is cloudy and pale, because fine bits are floating in it without dissolving.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for the three sentences. 2. Ask what pattern your child sees in the table. 3. Ask what a living cell on a world with oily lakes could not use. 4. Only then read the explanation together.  
  *Parent says:* What did you think would happen, and what did you see? Which things did water dissolve, and which did oil dissolve? Do you see a pattern?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Which things did water dissolve? Which did oil dissolve?
  - Ask: What pattern do you see? (good: Water dissolved salt and sugar, and oil did not.; Oil dissolved the ghee and the yellow of the turmeric, and water did not.)
  - Ask: Titan's lakes are an oily liquid with no water. Could a living thing there use salt and sugar as our cells do? (good: No, because salt and sugar would lie at the bottom and never meet.; It would need other chemicals that dissolve in that liquid.)
  - Note: Chemists say 'like dissolves like'. Water dissolves salts and sugars. Oily liquids dissolve fats and oily colours. Your cells are full of salts, sugars, proteins and DNA dissolved in water. The membrane round each cell is made of fat, which is why the water inside does not dissolve it away.
  - Check: In Two Liquids, salt vanished in water but lay at the bottom of the oil. What does that tell us about life in an oily lake? → **It would need chemicals that dissolve in that liquid**

- **general:** Make a small paper cap that fits over the tip of the virus key, so that the tip no longer fits the lock. This is how an antibody works.; Stick a blob of tape on the tip of the key.
- **creative:** Cut several loose copies of the lock and scatter them. The keys stick in the loose locks and never reach the cell. Scientists test such 'decoy receptors' as medicines.; Make a cap that fits only the notch pattern on the virus key, so that the friendly key is left alone.; Make a clip that holds two virus keys together, so that neither can turn into the lock.
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut a cardboard square 10 centimetres wide. 2. Cut a stepped notch 2 centimetres deep into one edge. This is the lock. 3. Cut a strip whose end fits the notch exactly. This is the virus key. 4. Cut a second strip with a narrower tip that also slides into the notch. This is the friendly key.  
  *Parent says:* The notch is a receptor on a cell. The cell uses it every day. The first strip is a virus's spike. The second is something the cell needs. Both fit.  
  *Child mode:* Cut a cardboard lock with a stepped notch. Cut a virus key that fits it. Cut a friendly key that also fits.
  - Note: Cardboard from a cereal box cuts easily with ordinary scissors. Help with the notch if the cardboard is thick. Draw a zigzag on the virus key so the two keys are easy to tell apart.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the rules: the lock stays whole and uncovered, and the friendly key must still fit. 2. Your child picks an ordinary idea, such as a paper cap for the key. 3. They say why it should work. 4. They build it and test it five times with both keys.  
  *Parent says:* Which idea will you try first? Why should it work? Remember to test the friendly key as well.  
  *Child mode:* Rules: the lock stays whole and uncovered, and the friendly key must still fit. Pick one idea. Say why it should work. Build it. Test both keys five times.
  - Ask: Why is it better to block the key than to cover the lock? (good: The cell needs the lock for their own work.; A cap on the key stops only the virus.)
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child designs and builds a blocker of their own. 2. They test it five times. 3. Now snip a small corner off the key's tip. The key must still enter the lock. 4. Test the blocker on the changed key.  
  *Parent says:* Now I am a copying mistake. I change the tip of the key a little, and it still fits the lock. Does your blocker still hold on to it?  
  *Child mode:* Design your own blocker. Build it and test it five times. Now the key's tip is changed. Does your blocker still fit?
  - Note: A cap made to fit the old tip usually falls off the new one. This is what happens with flu. The virus's spike changes by mutation, last year's antibodies fit less well, and a new vaccine is made.
  - Check: A vaccine gives the body antibodies that fit a virus's spike. Why might the same vaccine work less well some years later? → **Mutations have changed the shape of the spike**
### c2 · The Mistake-Proof Message: design a way to copy without errors (35 min)
- **problem:** A message of 24 letters, made only of A, U, G and C, must be copied six times in a chain, each copy made from the last copy. Each copyist may look at the parent for only 5 seconds at a time. Design a system so that copy 6 matches the original exactly.
- **your turn:** Now design your own copying system. Write its rules in three lines. Run it for six copies and count the wrong letters in copy 6. How many seconds did your system add to each copy? Was it worth it?
- **constraints:** Only the first copy may be made from the original. After that, each copy is made from the last copy.; A copyist may look at the parent for 5 seconds, then must cover it before writing. They may look again as many times as they like.; You may add extra marks or extra letters to the message, as long as they are copied too.
- **general:** Break the message into groups of three letters with small gaps, so that each look takes in one or two groups.; After writing, compare the copy with the parent letter by letter and correct any mistake. This is proofreading.
- **creative:** Add a check number at the end, such as how many A's the message has. If the count in a copy is wrong, there is a mistake somewhere.; Make two copies each time, compare them with each other, and look again only where they differ.; Give a second person the job of checker, who never copies and only compares.
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the message on a slip. 2. Your child looks at it for 5 seconds, covers it and writes what they remember. They may look again, 5 seconds at a time. 3. They copy that copy, and so on, six times. 4. Compare copy 6 with the original and count the wrong letters.  
  *Parent says:* First we need the number to beat. Copy the message six times, each from the last copy, working fast. How many letters are wrong at the end?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the message for 5 seconds. Cover it. Write what you remember. Copy your copy. Do this six times. Count the wrong letters.
  - Note: Ask your child to work quickly and without checking. Most children get 2 to 6 wrong letters by copy 6. If there are none, ask them to go faster.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the usual ideas: groups of three, and proofreading each copy. 2. Your child picks one and says why it should help. 3. They copy six times again using it. 4. They count the wrong letters and note the extra time.  
  *Parent says:* Which idea will you try? Why should it cut the mistakes? Time yourself as well, because checking is not free.  
  *Child mode:* Pick one idea. Say why it should help. Copy six times with it. Count the wrong letters. Note the time it took.
  - Ask: Why does checking each copy stop the mistakes adding up? (good: A mistake is caught before it is copied into the next one.; Each copy starts from a correct parent again.)
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child designs their own system and writes its rules. 2. They run six copies and count the wrong letters. 3. Compare all three runs: mistakes and time. 4. Read the note together and ask whether a virus would want a perfect system.  
  *Parent says:* Your cells check their DNA copies and fix nearly every mistake. Most RNA viruses do no checking. Is that only a weakness? What could a virus gain from mistakes?  
  *Child mode:* Design your own system. Write its rules. Run six copies. Count the wrong letters.
  - Note: Your cells proofread and repair their DNA, so only about one letter in a thousand million ends up wrong. Checking costs time and energy. Most RNA viruses skip it and get about one letter wrong in ten thousand to a hundred thousand. Most of those copies fail. But a few have a change that hides them from the body's defences, and that is how such viruses keep ahead.
  - Check: Most RNA viruses make far more copying mistakes than your cells do. What is one result? → **They evolve faster**

### What a virus is, and what NASA means by life (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which two parts does every virus have?  
   Options: A genome and a protein coat / A nucleus and a membrane / Ribosomes and a cell wall / A mouth and a gut  
   **Answer:** A genome and a protein coat. *Why:* Every virus has a genome of DNA or RNA and a protein coat called a capsid. Some also have an envelope of fat. A nucleus, a membrane and ribosomes are parts of cells.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a virus particle has ribosomes of their own.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Ribosomes are the machines that build proteins, and only cells have them. A virus's proteins are built by the host cell's ribosomes.
3. (fill) A virus can be copied only inside a living ____.  
   **Answer:** ['cell', 'host cell']. *Why:* A virus has instructions but no ribosomes and no supply of energy. Only a living cell can read the instructions and build new viruses.
4. (order) Put the steps by which a cell copies a virus in order.  
   **Answer:** ["The virus's coat fits a receptor and sticks to the cell", 'The virus or their genome gets inside', 'The coat comes off and the genome is free', 'The cell copies the genome and builds virus proteins', 'New particles put themselves together', 'The new particles leave the cell']. *Why:* The order is attach, enter, uncoat, copy and build, assemble, leave. The cell cannot read the genome until the coat is off, and nothing can leave until new particles have formed.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Genome → the full set of instructions; Capsid → the protein coat of a virus; Envelope → an outer layer of fat on some viruses; Receptor → a molecule on a cell that a virus's coat fits; Mutation → a change in a genome. *Why:* These five words are used all through the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
6. (mcq) Why is it safe to eat a tomato from a plant with mosaic virus?  
   Options: Our cells have no receptor that fits this virus / Cooking always destroys every virus / The virus stays in the roots / Tomato viruses are too large to swallow  
   **Answer:** Our cells have no receptor that fits this virus. *Why:* A virus can enter only a cell whose receptor fits their coat. This virus fits tomato cells and does not fit ours. The virus is in the fruit too, so 'stays in the roots' is not the reason.
7. (sort) Sort these: does only a cell have it, or do a cell and a virus particle both have it?  
   **Answer:** ribosomes → Only a cell has it; a genome → Both have it; proteins → Both have it; a way to get energy from food → Only a cell has it; a membrane that takes in food → Only a cell has it; instructions that can mutate → Both have it. *Why:* A virus particle shares the genome, the proteins and the ability to mutate. What a virus lacks is the machinery: ribosomes, a supply of energy and a membrane that takes in food.
8. (truefalse) True or false: antibiotics inactivate cold viruses.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Antibiotics jam parts that bacteria have, such as the bacterial ribosome. A virus particle has no such parts, so antibiotics do nothing to them.
9. (mcq) In the Copying Chain, which lines wandered furthest from 100 millimetres?  
   Options: Copies of the original / Lines drawn with the ruler / All wandered equally / Copies of the last copy  
   **Answer:** Copies of the last copy. *Why:* When each line is copied from the last copy, each new mistake is added to all the earlier ones. Copies made from the original start afresh each time, so they stay close.
10. (write) In the Copying Chain, mistakes added up from copy to copy. Why did that not happen when you copied the original?  
   **Answer:** Each copy of the original starts from the true length, so it carries only one mistake. In the chain, each copy starts from a line that already holds all the earlier mistakes, and adds one more.. *Why:* A strong answer says where each copy starts from. A copy of a copy inherits the old mistakes. A copy of the original does not.
11. (fill) NASA's definition: life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian ____.  
   **Answer:** ['evolution']. *Why:* The full definition is 'a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution'. Evolution means the kind can change over generations, through copies that differ a little.
12. (mcq) Which part of NASA's definition does a virus particle fail?  
   Options: Self-sustaining / Chemical system / Capable of evolution / None of them  
   **Answer:** Self-sustaining. *Why:* A virus is chemical and their kind evolves. But a virus particle takes in no energy and builds nothing, so they do not keep themselves going. Choosing 'capable of evolution' forgets that flu viruses change every year.
13. (label) On a drawing of a flu virus cut in half, label the genome, the capsid and the envelope.  
   **Answer:** ['Genome: the coiled strands in the middle', 'Capsid: the protein coat round the genome', 'Envelope: the outer layer of fat with spikes']. *Why:* The genome is innermost, the capsid surrounds it and the envelope is the outside layer. Not all viruses have an envelope, but a flu virus does.
14. (write) Give one reason for calling a virus alive, and one reason against.  
   **Answer:** For: a virus carries genes, is copied and evolves. Against: a virus particle makes no energy and builds nothing, and can be copied only by a cell.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a real fact on each side. Other good reasons for: some viruses sense signals and choose a path. Against: a virus can be stored as crystals.
15. (perspective) Here are two views about what soap does to a flu virus.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a word and ties it to a test for life, such as 'a particle that does nothing cannot be killed' or 'something that evolves is alive enough to be killed'.. *Why:* Both words are used by scientists. The choice shows which test for life the speaker has in mind.
### Evolving, and the cases in between (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Why do most RNA viruses evolve faster than your own cells?  
   Options: They are kept warmer / They have many more genes / Their copying enzyme does not check for mistakes / They are much heavier  
   **Answer:** Their copying enzyme does not check for mistakes. *Why:* Your cells check and repair their copies. Most RNA viruses bring a copying enzyme that does no checking, so nearly every new copy has a changed letter. More differences give selection more to work on.
2. (truefalse) True or false: every mutation harms the virus that carries it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most mutations do harm or make no difference, but a few help, for example by hiding the virus from antibodies. Viruses with those few are copied more.
3. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how a new kind of flu virus becomes common.  
   **Answer:** ['A cell copies a flu genome and gets one letter wrong', 'The changed letter alters the shape of the spike a little', 'Old antibodies fit the changed spike badly', 'The changed virus enters more cells and is copied more', 'The changed kind becomes the common one']. *Why:* A copying mistake comes first. It matters only if it changes something, here the spike. Then selection does the rest: the copies that escape the antibodies are copied more.
4. (fill) A virus whose host cells are bacteria is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['bacteriophage', 'phage']. *Why:* Bacteriophage means 'bacteria eater'. Phages are the most common viruses on Earth, and nearly all the viruses in sea water are phages.
5. (mcq) A candle flame is chemical and keeps itself going. Why can a flame not evolve?  
   Options: It is too hot / It needs air / It has no instructions that are copied with changes / It lasts too short a time  
   **Answer:** It has no instructions that are copied with changes. *Why:* Evolution needs copies that differ and pass the differences on. A new flame lit from an old one carries nothing over from it. A flame's shape depends only on the wick, the wax and the air now.
6. (sort) Each of these fails one part of NASA's definition. Sort them by the part they fail.  
   **Answer:** a cold virus particle → Fails 'self-sustaining'; a candle flame → Fails 'capable of evolution'; a viroid → Fails 'self-sustaining'; a red blood cell → Fails 'capable of evolution'; a mimivirus particle → Fails 'self-sustaining'; a forest fire → Fails 'capable of evolution'. *Why:* Viruses and viroids carry instructions that evolve, but do no chemistry of their own. Flames and red blood cells keep going for a while, but have no genome to pass on with changes.
7. (match) Match each thing to what it is.  
   **Answer:** Viroid → a bare loop of RNA with no coat; Prion → a wrongly folded protein that spreads its shape; Giant virus → a virus with hundreds of genes; Phage → a virus whose hosts are bacteria; Virion → one virus particle outside a cell. *Why:* These are the things at the edge of life. Each has less than a cell has, and they differ in what they still keep.
8. (mcq) Carsonella has ribosomes but can live only inside insect cells. What does this show about 'self-sustaining'?  
   Options: It is a matter of degree / Carsonella is really a virus / Insects are not alive / Ribosomes are not needed for life  
   **Answer:** It is a matter of degree. *Why:* Carsonella builds their own proteins but takes most other things from the host. So there is no clean split between 'keeps itself going' and 'does not'. Carsonella is still a cell, because they have ribosomes and a membrane.
9. (truefalse) True or false: a giant virus with 1,000 genes can build proteins without a cell.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Even mimivirus has no ribosomes. Having many genes does not help if there is no machine to read them.
10. (write) Explain why a mule is a problem for NASA's definition of life.  
   **Answer:** A mule cannot have young, so one mule cannot evolve. Yet a mule is clearly alive. So 'capable of evolution' must be about a kind of being over generations, and cannot be tested on one body.. *Why:* A strong answer says both things: the mule fails if you test one body, and the fix is to apply the definition to kinds. That fix makes the definition hard to use on a single sample.
11. (mcq) In Soap and the Fatty Coat, soap kept oil spread through water. What does soap do to a flu virus?  
   Options: Dissolves the genome / Pulls apart the envelope of fat / Feeds the virus / Makes the virus heavier  
   **Answer:** Pulls apart the envelope of fat. *Why:* A soap molecule holds fat at one end and water at the other. It pulls the fat of the envelope apart, and the spikes in the envelope are lost with it. Without spikes the virus cannot enter a cell.
12. (write) In Pori Rafts, grains joined with nobody arranging them. How is that like a virus's coat forming? How is it different?  
   **Answer:** Alike: the parts come together by themselves, with no builder. Different: pori is pulled by the water surface and makes a different raft each time. Coat proteins fit by shape and charge, and make the same coat every time.. *Why:* A strong answer gives one likeness and one difference. The difference matters: a model shows one feature of the real thing and gets others wrong.
13. (draw) Draw a slope from 'stone' to 'free-living bacterium'. Place a prion, a viroid, a flu virus and Carsonella along the slope.  
   **Answer:** From the stone end: prion, viroid, flu virus, Carsonella, then the free-living bacterium.. *Why:* A prion has no genome. A viroid has a genome and no coat. A flu virus has a genome and a coat. Carsonella has ribosomes too, but cannot live outside a host. Each one has a little more than the one before.
14. (perspective) Here are three views about whether a virus is alive.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view and names a weak spot. For example, the biochemist's view sorts a dry seed oddly, and the virologist's view makes one cell into two living beings.. *Why:* Each view is held by real scientists. Each sorts most cases well and at least one case oddly, which is why the debate continues.
15. (odd) Three of these can be copied only inside a cell. Which one can live without a host?  
   Options: A flu virus / A viroid / A mimivirus / A curd bacterium  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A curd bacterium needs only milk, warmth and time. Flu viruses, viroids and mimiviruses all need a living cell to copy them.
16. (fill) A protein on a virus's coat fits a ____ on the cell, as a key fits a lock.  
   **Answer:** ['receptor']. *Why:* A receptor is a molecule on the cell's surface that has a job for the cell. A virus can enter only if their coat fits that receptor.
### Other worlds, and what a definition is for (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) In Two Liquids, salt dissolved in water and not in oil. What would that mean for life in an oily lake?  
   Options: It would have to stir harder / It would taste salty / It would need different chemicals / It would need no chemicals  
   **Answer:** It would need different chemicals. *Why:* Chemicals must be dissolved to meet and react. Salts and sugars do not dissolve in oily liquids. So life there could not be built from the same chemicals as ours.
2. (write) Your cells are wrapped in a membrane of fat. Use your Two Liquids results to explain why fat suits the job.  
   **Answer:** The inside and the outside of a cell are both watery. In Two Liquids, ghee did not dissolve in water. So a layer of fat stays whole in water, and keeps the cell's salts and sugars in.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the result that fat and water do not dissolve each other. A membrane made of something that dissolved in water would fall apart.
3. (truefalse) True or false: scientists have built chemicals other than DNA and RNA that can store instructions.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 2012 a team built six such chemicals, called XNAs, and showed that they can be copied with changes and selected. So DNA and RNA are not the only possible instruction chemicals.
4. (mcq) Why do most scientists think carbon is a likelier frame for life than silicon?  
   Options: Carbon chains stay whole in water / Silicon is very rare / Carbon atoms are heavier / Silicon cannot make bonds  
   **Answer:** Carbon chains stay whole in water. *Why:* Both atoms make four bonds. But long chains of silicon break apart in water, and silicon joined with oxygen becomes rock. Silicon is common, so 'very rare' is not the reason.
5. (fill) The science that studies whether there is life beyond Earth is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['astrobiology']. *Why:* Astrobiology joins astronomy and biology. It also studies how life began on Earth, because that tells us where else to look.
6. (sort) Sort these: probably needed by any life, or perhaps only the way Earth's life does it?  
   **Answer:** a liquid for chemicals to meet in → Probably needed by any life; a supply of energy → Probably needed by any life; DNA → Perhaps only Earth's way; instructions copied with small mistakes → Probably needed by any life; water as the liquid → Perhaps only Earth's way; a boundary that keeps the inside in → Probably needed by any life. *Why:* This is the usual view, and it is a guess from one example. A liquid, energy, a boundary and copied instructions seem hard to do without. Which liquid and which instruction chemical may differ.
7. (mcq) Viking's fed Mars soil gave off gas. Why do most scientists doubt that it was life?  
   Options: No carbon molecules were found in the soil / The landers never reached Mars / Mars has no soil / The gas was only steam  
   **Answer:** No carbon molecules were found in the soil. *Why:* Life as we know it is built from carbon molecules, and another Viking instrument found none. Mars soil also holds harsh chemicals that can break down the food and give off gas without life.
8. (write) A probe cannot wait generations to watch evolution. Suggest one sign of life it could measure in a day. Say why.  
   **Answer:** One answer: look for very complicated molecules in large numbers, because only something following instructions makes many identical complicated molecules. Another: gas given off when the sample is fed, with a heated sample as the control.. *Why:* A strong answer names a sign and the reason it points to life. It is even better if it says what could give a false alarm.
9. (order) Put these in order from the fewest genes to the most.  
   **Answer:** ['A viroid (none)', 'A flu virus (about 12)', 'Carsonella (182)', 'A mimivirus (about 1,000)', 'A human (about 20,000)']. *Why:* The surprise is in the middle. A giant virus has more genes than the smallest bacterium. So the number of genes does not sort viruses from cells.
10. (mcq) About 8 of every 100 letters in your genome came from viruses. What does that show?  
   Options: We are 8 percent ill / Viruses are small humans / Our genome is shrinking / Viruses left their genomes in our ancestors  
   **Answer:** Viruses left their genomes in our ancestors. *Why:* Some viruses put their genome into the host's genome. When that happened in an egg or sperm cell long ago, the virus's letters were passed down to every descendant.
11. (truefalse) True or false: a definition made from one example may describe only that example.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. All life on Earth is related, so we have one example. We cannot yet tell which features every kind of life must have.
12. (write) In 2002, scientists built polio virus from bottled chemicals. Did they make something alive? Argue your answer.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. One answer: no, because a virus particle is a chemical, and chemists make chemicals. Another: yes, because the built virus is copied by cells and evolves like any other virus.. *Why:* A strong answer says which test for life it is using. The same event counts as 'making life' under one definition and not under another.
13. (perspective) Here are two views about defining life.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer sees that the two want different things. The scientist needs a tool to work with. The philosopher wants the truth about what life is.. *Why:* Both can be right. A working definition is a tool for now. Using it does not mean believing that it is final.
14. (draw) Draw a living being for a world with no liquid water. Label their liquid, energy supply, boundary and instructions.  
   **Answer:** Any being with the four labels and a reason for each. For example: a flat being in a methane lake, with energy from chemicals in the lake, a boundary that does not dissolve in methane, and a chain molecule as instructions.. *Why:* A strong drawing gives a reason for each label. The boundary must not dissolve in the being's liquid, and the instructions must be something that can be copied.
15. (match) Match each world to the liquid found there.  
   **Answer:** Earth's seas → salty water; Titan's lakes → liquid methane and ethane; Europa, under the ice → a salty water ocean; Venus's clouds → drops of sulphuric acid. *Why:* Only Earth has liquid water lying on its surface. Europa's water is hidden under ice, and the other two liquids are very unlike water.
16. (odd) Three of these are parts of NASA's definition of life. Which one is not?  
   Options: A chemical system / Self-sustaining / Capable of evolution / Made of cells  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* NASA's definition does not mention cells. That was on purpose, because life elsewhere might not be built from cells like ours.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these does a virus particle have?  
   Options: A genome / Ribosomes / A nucleus / Mitochondria  
   **Answer:** A genome. *Why:* A virus has a genome and a protein coat. Ribosomes, a nucleus and mitochondria are parts of cells.
2. (mcq) Why do antibiotics not work against a cold?  
   Options: A virus has none of the parts they jam / A cold is too mild / Antibiotics are too weak / There are too many viruses  
   **Answer:** A virus has none of the parts they jam. *Why:* Antibiotics jam parts that bacteria have, such as their ribosomes and cell walls. A cold is caused by a virus, which has none of these parts.
3. (mcq) What must fit together before a virus can enter a cell?  
   Options: The virus's coat and the cell's receptor / The sizes of the virus and the cell / Their temperatures / Their colours  
   **Answer:** The virus's coat and the cell's receptor. *Why:* A protein on the virus's coat must fit a receptor on the cell, as a key fits a lock. That is why each virus has only certain hosts.
4. (mcq) What builds the proteins of a new virus?  
   Options: The virus particle / The virus's envelope / The cell's receptor / The host cell's ribosomes  
   **Answer:** The host cell's ribosomes. *Why:* A virus has no ribosomes. The host cell's ribosomes read the virus's genome and build the virus's proteins. Choosing 'the virus particle' forgets that a particle builds nothing.
5. (mcq) Which three things does evolution need?  
   Options: Food, water and air / Cells, a brain and legs / Heat, light and time / Copies, small differences, unequal success  
   **Answer:** Copies, small differences, unequal success. *Why:* Evolution happens whenever there are copies, the copies differ a little in ways that are passed on, and some leave more copies than others. Nothing else is needed.
6. (mcq) In the Copying Chain, you kept the longest of three copies each time. What happened to the line?  
   Options: It stayed at 100 millimetres / It grew steadily shorter / It vanished / It grew steadily longer  
   **Answer:** It grew steadily longer. *Why:* Each copy came out a little long or short by mistake. Keeping the longest each time kept the 'long' mistakes. The line did nothing: copying and choosing did the work.
7. (mcq) NASA says life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of what?  
   Options: Fast movement / Darwinian evolution / Speech / Living in water  
   **Answer:** Darwinian evolution. *Why:* The definition ends 'capable of Darwinian evolution'. It means the kind can change over generations through copies that differ.
8. (mcq) Which of these passes all three parts of NASA's definition?  
   Options: A candle flame / A cold virus particle / A salt crystal / A curd bacterium  
   **Answer:** A curd bacterium. *Why:* A bacterium is chemical, keeps themselves going and evolves. A flame cannot evolve, a virus particle does not sustain themselves, and a crystal does neither.
9. (mcq) Scientists who call viruses alive point to which fact?  
   Options: Viruses make their own energy / Viruses have ribosomes / Viruses carry genes that evolve / Viruses grow in plain broth  
   **Answer:** Viruses carry genes that evolve. *Why:* Viruses carry genes, are copied and evolve. The other three statements are false, and they are the reasons other scientists give for saying viruses are not alive.
10. (mcq) Why is NASA's definition called a 'working definition'?  
   Options: It is used only at work / It has been proved true / It describes working animals / It is useful now and may change  
   **Answer:** It is useful now and may change. *Why:* A working definition is a rule people agree to use for now because it is useful. Scientists expect to change it when they learn more.
11. (mcq) Why might life in an oily lake need different chemicals from ours?  
   Options: Oily lakes are too wet / Oil contains no atoms / Salts and sugars do not dissolve in oil / Our chemicals are too heavy  
   **Answer:** Salts and sugars do not dissolve in oil. *Why:* Chemicals must dissolve in the liquid to meet and react. The salts and sugars our cells use do not dissolve in oily liquids.
12. (mcq) In the Two Liquids test, what was the one thing you changed on purpose?  
   Options: The amount of liquid / The number of stirs / The liquid / The waiting time  
   **Answer:** The liquid. *Why:* In a fair test you change one thing and keep the rest the same. Here only the liquid changed, so any difference was caused by the liquid.

## Applications
- Vaccines. A vaccine shows the body a weakened virus, an inactivated virus or one virus protein, so the body makes antibodies that fit the real virus's coat.
- Medicines against viruses. Since a virus has no chemistry of their own, these medicines block one step of the copying, such as the virus's copying enzyme.
- Hand washing. Soap pulls apart the fatty envelope of flu viruses and coronaviruses, and washes other viruses off the skin.
- Tracking outbreaks. Scientists read virus genomes from patients and use the mutations to work out how an illness is spreading.
- Farming without poison sprays. Indian farmers spray a virus that infects only pod borer caterpillars, and leaves bees, birds and people alone.
- Phage therapy. Doctors in Georgia and a few other countries treat some infections with phages, which burst the harmful bacteria. It is being tested more widely.
- Planning space missions. A definition of life decides which instruments a probe carries to Mars, Europa or Titan.
- Editing genes. CRISPR, the tool scientists use to change genes, was copied from the way bacteria recognise and cut the DNA of phages.

## Spotted around us
- On your vaccination card: 'OPV' is a live, weakened polio virus and 'IPV' is an inactivated one.
- At the vegetable market: a ladies' finger or papaya leaf with a net of yellow veins or a patchwork of two greens has a plant virus.
- At the sink: soap breaking up the grease on a plate is doing what it does to the fatty envelope of a flu virus.
- At the beach, every handful of sea water holds millions of phages and the bacteria they infect.
- On a chickpea or cotton field: a farmer spraying HaNPV is using a virus to protect the crop.
- In the news: a 'new variant' of flu or Covid is a virus whose genome has picked up mutations that help them spread.
- At the doctor's: when the doctor says 'it is a virus, so no antibiotics', she is using what you learned about what a virus lacks.
- In the night sky: Saturn can be seen without a telescope for part of each year. Its moon Titan has lakes of liquid methane.

## Open questions
- **Are viruses alive?** Scientists agree on the facts and disagree on the test. It may never be settled by evidence, because it depends on what we decide the word should mean.
- **Where did viruses come from?** Viruses leave no fossils, and their genomes change too fast to keep old traces. Escaped genes, shrunken cells and a world of viruses before cells are all still possible.
- **Is there a second kind of life anywhere, even on Earth?** Every living thing found so far shares one ancestor. Our tests look for our own chemistry, so a different kind of life could have been missed.
- **Could we recognise life built from other chemicals?** Tests such as counting building steps are new and have been tried on few samples. Nobody has a second kind of life to test them on.
- **What is the smallest set of genes that can keep a cell going?** In 2016 scientists built a cell with 473 genes, and did not know what about a third of those genes do. Some are still a mystery.
- **Does anything like awareness go with the sensing and choosing that viruses and single cells do?** Phages measure a signal and take one of two paths. Whether there is any experience behind that, nobody knows how to test.

## Connected maths
- In Walk the Scale, everything is 10,000 times its real size, so 1 millimetre stands for 100 nanometres. A tobacco mosaic virus is 300 nanometres long. A mimivirus is 700 nanometres across. How big is each in the model? **The tobacco mosaic virus is 3 millimetres long. The mimivirus is 7 millimetres across.**. Every 100 nanometres becomes 1 millimetre. 300 ÷ 100 = 3, so the rod is 3 millimetres long. 700 ÷ 100 = 7, so the mimivirus is 7 millimetres across. A flu virus is a 1 millimetre dot, so the mimivirus is 7 times as wide. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- In a made-up Copying Chain, the lines of chain A measured 104, 97, 103, 110, 108, 115, 112, 119, 123 and 121 millimetres. The lines of set B measured 103, 96, 101, 105, 98, 102, 94, 100, 104 and 99. For each, find the biggest gap from 100. How many times as big is chain A's gap? **Chain A's biggest gap is 23 millimetres. Set B's is 6 millimetres. Chain A's gap is nearly 4 times as big.**. In chain A the line furthest from 100 is 123, and 123 − 100 = 23. In set B the furthest is 94, and 100 − 94 = 6. 23 ÷ 6 is a little less than 4. Chain A wandered further because each copy passed its mistake on to the next. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In part 2 of a made-up Copying Chain, the kept lines measured 100, 106, 111, 118, 123 and 130 millimetres for generations 0 to 5. About how much did the line gain each generation? Predict the length at generation 10. **About 6 millimetres each generation. At generation 10 the line should be about 160 millimetres.**. From 100 to 130 is a gain of 30 millimetres in 5 generations. 30 ÷ 5 = 6 millimetres each generation. Five more generations add about 5 × 6 = 30. 130 + 30 = 160. Real results will be close to this, not exact. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- One phage enters a bacterium. Thirty minutes later, 100 new phages burst out, and each enters a new bacterium. How many phages are there after 30, 60 and 90 minutes? Why can this not go on for long? **100 after 30 minutes, 10,000 after 60 minutes and 1,000,000 after 90 minutes. It stops when the phages run out of bacteria.**. Each round multiplies the number by 100. 1 × 100 = 100. 100 × 100 = 10,000. 10,000 × 100 = 1,000,000. One more round would need a million bacteria. Soon there are too few hosts, and the numbers fall. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- A flu virus genome has about 13,500 letters. Suppose the copying gets 1 letter wrong in every 10,000, which is a round figure. One infected cell makes 1,000 copies. About how many wrong letters are there in all those copies together? **About 1,350 wrong letters, which is one or two in each copy.**. 1,000 copies of 13,500 letters is 13,500,000 letters. One in every 10,000 is wrong. 13,500,000 ÷ 10,000 = 1,350. Shared among 1,000 copies, that is between one and two wrong letters in each copy. So nearly every new flu virus differs a little from the parent. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Your genome has about 3,000 million letters. About 8 percent came from viruses. The parts that hold instructions for proteins make up about 1.5 percent. How many letters is each? How many times as large is the virus share? **240 million letters came from viruses. 45 million letters hold protein instructions. The virus share is more than 5 times as large.**. 1 percent of 3,000 million is 30 million. 8 percent is 8 × 30 = 240 million. 1.5 percent is 30 + 15 = 45 million. 240 ÷ 45 is a little more than 5. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
