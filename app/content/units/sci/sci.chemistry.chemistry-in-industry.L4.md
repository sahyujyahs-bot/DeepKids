# Fertilisers, medicines, fuels and paints: how chemistry makes them, and why the amount matters
`sci.chemistry.chemistry-in-industry.L4`

**Summary.** A tablet that cures a grown-up can harm a small child. Why does the amount matter so much? In this lesson you will find a fuel, a medicine, a paint and a plant food at home, test three paints, and compare one dose in two bodies.

**Intro.** *A farmer's urea, a paracetamol tablet, the gas in a cylinder and the paint on a wall look nothing alike. Where does each of them come from?* None of the four is dug up or picked ready to use. People make each one by chemical reactions, starting from air, rock, crude oil or a plant. In this lesson you find a fuel, a medicine, a paint and a plant food in your own home. You mix three paints and test which one stays on. You line up things made from crude oil, from cooking gas to candle wax. You also test why the same dose is weak in a big body and strong in a small one.

## Terms
- **Raw material**: A substance taken from nature that a factory starts with. *e.g.* Air, water, rock, crude oil and plants are raw materials.
- **Product**: The useful substance that a factory sets out to make. *e.g.* Urea is the product of a urea factory.
- **By-product**: Another substance that is made at the same time as the product. Some by-products are useful, and some are waste. *e.g.* Making hydrogen from natural gas also makes carbon dioxide, as a by-product.
- **Chemical industry**: All the factories that make new substances by chemical reactions. *e.g.* A refinery in Kochi and a medicine factory in Hyderabad are both part of the chemical industry.
- **Fertiliser**: A substance added to soil to give plants the nutrients they need. *e.g.* A farmer near Thanjavur spreads urea on her paddy field.
- **Nutrient**: A substance that a living being must take in to live and grow. The three main plant nutrients in fertiliser are nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. *e.g.* The letters N, P and K on a fertiliser bag stand for nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
- **Urea**: A white solid that is rich in nitrogen. It is the most used nitrogen fertiliser in India. Your own body also makes urea, and it leaves in your urine. *e.g.* A 45 kilogram bag of urea holds about 21 kilograms of nitrogen.
- **Fuel**: A substance that gives out heat when it burns. *e.g.* LPG, petrol, firewood and candle wax are all fuels.
- **Crude oil**: A thick, dark liquid found deep under the ground or the sea bed. It is a mixture of many substances. *e.g.* Crude oil is pumped from under the sea off Mumbai.
- **Hydrocarbon**: A substance whose molecules are made only of hydrogen atoms and carbon atoms. *e.g.* Each molecule of propane, in LPG, has 3 carbon atoms and 8 hydrogen atoms.
- **Refinery**: A factory that separates crude oil into its useful parts. *e.g.* The refinery at Digboi in Assam has worked since 1901.
- **Medicine**: A substance that prevents an illness, treats it or eases its signs. *e.g.* Paracetamol brings down a fever.
- **Dose**: The amount of a medicine taken at one time. *e.g.* A label gives a smaller dose for a child than for a grown-up.
- **Pigment**: A fine coloured powder that does not dissolve. It gives a paint its colour. *e.g.* Red ochre is a pigment made from a soft red rock.
- **Binder**: The part of a paint that holds the pigment together and sticks it to the surface. *e.g.* In Three Paints, white glue is the binder that keeps the colour powder on the card.
- **Solvent**: The liquid in a paint that makes it thin enough to spread. It evaporates as the paint dries. *e.g.* Water is the solvent in most wall paint.

## Explanations
### Where fertilisers, medicines, fuels and paints come from · *defined*
**Definition:** The chemical industry takes a few raw materials from nature. It changes them, by chemical reactions, into substances that people use.

A chemical reaction changes substances into new substances. The atoms stay the same, and they join up in a new way. A chemical factory does this in very large amounts. It starts with raw materials. There are only a few kinds: air, water, rocks and salts, crude oil and natural gas, and living beings such as plants and moulds. The substance the factory sets out to make is its product. Most reactions also make other substances, called by-products. A factory needs energy too, and most of that energy still comes from burning fuel. Four families of products are in this lesson. Fertilisers feed crops. Medicines treat illness. Fuels give energy. Paints colour and protect surfaces. Each one reaches your home after many steps. The paracetamol in a tablet is made in a few reactions from a substance called phenol. Phenol itself is made from crude oil.
- e.g. The nitrogen in urea comes from the air.
- e.g. The white pigment in wall paint is made from a black beach sand.
- e.g. LPG is separated from crude oil and natural gas.
### Fertilisers: putting back what the harvest takes away · *defined*
**Definition:** A fertiliser is a substance added to soil to supply nutrients that plants need. The three main nutrients are nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.

A plant makes sugar from air, water and sunlight. To build leaves, roots and seeds, she also needs nutrients from the soil. She needs nitrogen most of all, and then phosphorus and potassium. Their chemical symbols are N, P and K. When a crop is harvested, the nutrients inside the crop leave the field. A field that is harvested year after year grows poorer, unless something puts the nutrients back. Dung, compost and bean crops put some back. Fertilisers from factories put back more, and quickly. Urea supplies nitrogen. In every 100 kilograms of urea there are 46 kilograms of nitrogen. Phosphorus fertiliser is made from phosphate rock, which is mined at Jhamarkotra in Rajasthan. Potassium fertiliser is called potash. It is mined from the salts of ancient seas that dried up. India buys nearly all its potash from other countries. A fertiliser bag often carries three numbers, such as 19:19:19. They show how rich the fertiliser is in N, P and K, in that order.
- e.g. A 45 kilogram bag of urea holds about 21 kilograms of nitrogen.
- e.g. A bag marked 0:0:60 holds potash, with no nitrogen and no phosphorus.
- e.g. Since 2015, urea sold to farmers in India is coated with neem oil. The coating slows the release of nitrogen in the soil.
### Nitrogen: from the air to the field · *defined*
**Definition:** Air is about four fifths nitrogen gas, but plants cannot use nitrogen in that form. Factories join it with hydrogen to make ammonia, and ammonia is made into urea.

Nitrogen is all around every plant. In every 100 litres of air, about 78 litres are nitrogen gas. But the two atoms in a nitrogen molecule hold each other very tightly. A plant has no way to pull them apart. Some bacteria can pull them apart, such as those who live in the root swellings of beans. Lightning can pull them apart too. In 1909 the German chemist Fritz Haber found a way to do it in a laboratory. He squeezed nitrogen and hydrogen together at a very high temperature, over a metal catalyst. A catalyst is a helper that speeds up a reaction and is not used up. The two gases joined to make ammonia. By 1913 the engineer Carl Bosch had built a factory that did this in huge amounts. Today the hydrogen comes mostly from natural gas and steam. The ammonia is then joined with carbon dioxide to make urea. Scientists estimate that food grown with this nitrogen feeds about half the people on Earth. Making it uses a great deal of fuel.
- e.g. The protein in a grain of wheat holds nitrogen that may have been in the air one year earlier.
- e.g. Beans, peas and groundnuts get nitrogen from bacteria in their root swellings.
- e.g. A flash of lightning joins a little nitrogen with oxygen, and rain carries it to the soil.
### Fuels: energy stored in substances · *defined*
**Definition:** A fuel is a substance that gives out heat when it burns. Most fuels used today come from crude oil, natural gas and coal.

Crude oil formed from tiny living beings of the sea who died many millions of years ago. Mud buried them. Heat and pressure slowly changed their remains into oil and gas. Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons. A hydrocarbon molecule is a chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen atoms attached. Some chains are short and some are long. A refinery separates them by boiling. It heats the crude oil, and the vapours rise up a tall tower. Short chains boil at low temperatures, and they are collected near the top. Long chains are collected lower down. The shortest chains have 3 or 4 carbon atoms. They make LPG, the cooking gas. Petrol has chains of about 5 to 12 carbon atoms. Diesel has chains of about 12 to 20 carbon atoms, and candle wax has about 20 to 40. The thickest part is bitumen, the black tar that binds a road. The longer the chain, the thicker the substance is and the harder it is to boil. Refineries also supply the starting substances for plastics, paints and many medicines.
- e.g. The refinery at Jamnagar in Gujarat is the largest in the world.
- e.g. LPG has no smell of its own. A strong-smelling substance is added so that people notice a leak.
- e.g. Petroleum jelly and candle wax are both made from crude oil.
### What burning a fuel makes · *defined*
**Definition:** When a hydrocarbon fuel burns, it joins with oxygen from the air. The reaction makes carbon dioxide and water, and it gives out heat.

Burning is a chemical reaction between a fuel and oxygen. The carbon atoms of the fuel join with oxygen and become carbon dioxide. The hydrogen atoms join with oxygen and become water vapour. No atom is lost. Burning 1 kilogram of LPG makes about 3 kilograms of carbon dioxide, because oxygen from the air has been added. A flame that gets too little air also makes soot and a poisonous gas called carbon monoxide. Carbon dioxide is not poisonous in small amounts. You breathe it out, and plants take it in. But carbon dioxide holds heat in the air. In 1856 the American scientist Eunice Foote filled glass tubes with different gases and stood them in the sun. The tube of carbon dioxide grew the hottest. She wrote that more of this gas in the air would make the Earth warmer. People now burn so much fuel that the Earth really is getting warmer. So chemists work on fuels that add less carbon dioxide. Biogas is made by microbes from dung and food waste. Ethanol is made from sugarcane, and in India it is mixed into petrol. Hydrogen burns to give only water.
- e.g. A cold steel plate held above a candle flame mists over with water and turns black with soot.
- e.g. A yellow, smoky stove flame is getting too little air. A blue flame is burning cleanly.
- e.g. A full home cylinder holds 14.2 kilograms of LPG. Burning all of it makes about 43 kilograms of carbon dioxide.
### Medicines: substances that change how a body works · *defined*
**Definition:** A medicine is a substance that prevents an illness, treats it or eases its signs. It works by joining to particular molecules in the body or in a germ.

Many medicines were first found in other living beings. Willow bark eases pain, and chemists made aspirin from the substance in the bark. The bark of the cinchona tree holds quinine, which treats malaria. In 1928 Alexander Fleming saw that bacteria died near a mould called Penicillium. That mould gave us penicillin. Later, a mouldy cantaloupe from a market in Peoria, in the United States, gave a mould that made far more penicillin. A woman known as Moldy Mary is credited with finding it, but who she was is debated. In 1972 Tu Youyou and her team in China drew a malaria medicine, artemisinin, out of the sweet wormwood plant. She had read in an old Chinese book that the plant should be soaked in cold water. So she stopped boiling the plant, and the medicine was no longer destroyed. In India, Asima Chatterjee studied the chemistry of medicinal plants for most of her working life. Once chemists know which molecule does the work, a factory can make that molecule. A factory can also change the molecule a little, to make it stronger or safer. Yellapragada Subbarow, born in Andhra Pradesh, led teams that made medicines for cancer and for filaria. A new medicine is tested for years, first in a laboratory and then on thousands of volunteers. Most new medicines fail these tests. India's factories make a large share of the world's low-cost medicines and vaccines.
- e.g. Quinine was once grown for India in cinchona plantations in the Nilgiri hills.
- e.g. A medicine for childhood blood cancer comes from the periwinkle plant, called nithyakalyani in Tamil.
- e.g. Chalky antacid tablets are made from limestone, a rock.
### The dose: why the amount decides · *defined*
**Definition:** The dose is the amount of a medicine taken at one time. Too small a dose does nothing, the right dose helps, and too large a dose harms.

A medicine spreads through the blood and the water of the body. A bigger body has more blood and water, so the same amount is spread thinner. That is why a dose is often worked out from body mass. A label may give so many milligrams for each kilogram. A child's body is smaller than a grown-up's, so a child's dose is smaller. A child's liver and kidneys also handle some medicines differently. So only the label or a doctor decides a child's dose. The same rule of amount holds outside medicine. Your body needs salt, yet a few large spoonfuls eaten at once can make a person very ill. About 500 years ago a Swiss doctor called Paracelsus put the rule in one line. Everything is a poison, he wrote, and only the dose makes a thing not a poison. Fertiliser follows the rule too. A little feeds a plant. Too much draws water out of the roots, and the rest washes into ponds and rivers.
- e.g. A syrup for children and a tablet for grown-ups may hold the same medicine in different amounts.
- e.g. One spoon of plant food in a bucket of water feeds pot plants. One spoon in a cup of water can burn their roots.
- e.g. A little carbon dioxide in the air keeps the Earth warm enough to live on. More makes the Earth too warm.
### Paint: pigment, binder and solvent · *defined*
**Definition:** A paint is a mixture of three main parts. The pigment gives the colour. The binder holds the pigment and sticks it to the surface. The solvent makes the paint thin enough to spread.

A pigment is a fine coloured powder that does not dissolve. The oldest pigments are coloured earths. Thousands of years ago, people painted the rock shelters of Bhimbetka in Madhya Pradesh with red ochre. Red ochre is a soft rock coloured by iron oxide, the same substance as rust. Today the most used pigment is a white one, titanium dioxide. It is made from a black sand that is mined on the beaches of Kerala and Tamil Nadu. A pigment alone is only dust, and it falls off when it dries. The binder is what makes a paint stay. In the caves of Ajanta, the painters mixed their pigments with glue. Lac is a resin made by tiny lac insects. It binds the colours on the wooden toys of Channapatna. In most wall paint today the binder is a kind of plastic made from crude oil. The solvent is the liquid that carries the pigment and the binder. In water-based paint the solvent is water. Some paints use other liquids, such as turpentine, which have a strong smell. A paint also protects. A coat of paint keeps air and water away from steel, so the steel does not rust.
- e.g. A chalk line on a slate rubs off, because chalk has pigment and no binder.
- e.g. A wax crayon line stays, because the wax binds the pigment.
- e.g. The red on a Channapatna toy does not come off on your hands, because lac holds it.
### How a paint hardens · *defined*
**Definition:** A wet paint becomes a hard coat in one of three ways. Its solvent evaporates, or its binder reacts with a gas in the air, or it cools.

Water-based wall paint holds its binder as tiny particles of plastic floating in water. As the water evaporates, the particles are pressed together and join into one thin sheet. The sheet traps the pigment. Once the sheet has formed, water no longer dissolves it. Oil paint hardens in another way. Its binder is an oil, such as linseed oil, that reacts with oxygen from the air. The oil molecules link to each other and form a tough solid. This is a chemical reaction, so oil paint goes on hardening after it feels dry. Lime wash is the white chuna on many older walls. It hardens by a reaction too. Lime takes in carbon dioxide from the air and turns into calcium carbonate, the substance of chalk and limestone. Lac hardens in the third way. It melts when it is warmed, and it sets hard again as it cools.
- e.g. A freshly painted room smells for days, as the last of the solvent leaves.
- e.g. Nail polish dries in minutes, because its solvent evaporates fast.
- e.g. A lime-washed wall often leaves white powder on a sleeve, because lime wash holds its pigment only weakly.
### By-products, waste and pollution · *defined*
**Definition:** A by-product is a substance made along with the product. Pollution is a substance in the air, water or soil in an amount that harms living beings.

No factory makes only what it wants. Atoms cannot vanish, so every atom that goes in must come out somewhere. A good factory finds a use for its by-products. A urea factory makes carbon dioxide when it makes hydrogen. It then uses that carbon dioxide to make the urea. Other by-products are harder to use. Smoke, waste water and unwanted solids can harm people, other animals and plants. Products can harm too, after they leave the factory. Nitrogen fertiliser that the crop does not take up washes into ponds. There it feeds algae. When the algae die, the microbes who rot them use up the oxygen in the water, and fish die. Lead compounds were once common in paint. Lead damages the growing brain of a child, and no safe amount is known. Since 2017, house paint sold in India may hold only a trace of lead. A factory can also fail. In December 1984 a poisonous gas leaked from a pesticide factory in Bhopal, and thousands of people died. Chemists today try to design reactions that use less energy, make less waste and need no dangerous substances. This work is called green chemistry. When people change a material, they weigh the good it does against the harm it may do to the air, water or soil. Factory nitrogen has raised harvests a great deal, but the extra that washes into a pond can harm the fish and plants in it.
- e.g. A sugar mill burns its crushed cane as fuel and sells its molasses to make ethanol.
- e.g. A pond below a heavily fertilised field turns green with algae.
- e.g. Old paint that is flaking off a wall may hold lead, so it should not be scraped by children.
### Could the world be fed without factory fertiliser? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that factory nitrogen has greatly raised harvests. They disagree about how far farming could do without it.

Before 1913, all the nitrogen in crops came from dung, compost, bean crops, lightning and a little mined salt. The world then held fewer than 2 billion people. Today it holds more than 8 billion. Factory fertiliser is one reason that so many can be fed. It also pollutes water, and making it burns a great deal of natural gas.
- e.g. A paddy field that gets nitrogen fertiliser usually gives much more grain than a field that gets none.
- e.g. In 2016 Sikkim was declared India's first fully organic state, farming without factory fertiliser.
- **Scientists who count nitrogen, such as Vaclav Smil, and Jan Willem Erisman and his team in 2008:** About half of the nitrogen in the world's food now comes from factories. Without it, today's farmland could feed only about half of the people alive. Farming without it would need far more land, and forests would have to be cleared.
- **Farmers and scientists who work on organic and natural farming:** Much factory nitrogen is wasted, because crops often take up less than half of what is spread. Bean crops, compost and careful use can replace a large part of it. Wasting less food would lower the need further.

## Mostly agreed
- Substances made by living bodies can also be made in a laboratory from non-living materials. *How we know:* In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler heated a mineral salt called ammonium cyanate and got urea. It was the same as the urea in urine.
- Nitrogen from the air can be made into fertiliser, and about half the world's food is now grown with it. *How we know:* Fritz Haber made ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen in 1909, and Carl Bosch's factory opened in 1913. In 2008 Jan Willem Erisman's team estimated that this nitrogen feeds about 48 people in every 100.
- Burning hydrocarbon fuels adds carbon dioxide to the air, and carbon dioxide holds heat. *How we know:* Eunice Foote showed in 1856 that a tube of carbon dioxide warms more in sunlight than a tube of air. The share of carbon dioxide in the air has risen by about half since factories began.
- A substance that helps at one dose can harm at a larger dose. *How we know:* Every medicine label gives a largest safe dose. Hospitals treat people each year who took too much of a useful medicine, such as paracetamol or iron tablets.
- A pigment needs a binder to stay on a surface. *How we know:* In Three Paints, colour powder mixed with water alone rubs off when dry. Tests on old wall paintings, such as those at Ajanta, find glue mixed with the pigments.
- Lead in paint harms children, and no safe amount of lead has been found. *How we know:* In 1979 Herbert Needleman's team measured lead in children's milk teeth. Children with more lead did less well in class. The World Health Organization says no level of lead is known to be safe.

## How we found out
**Friedrich Wöhler in Germany, his teacher Jöns Jacob Berzelius in Sweden, and William Perkin in England, Wöhler made urea in 1828. Perkin made his purple dye in 1856..** Two hundred years ago, most chemists believed in a 'life force'. They thought that substances of living bodies, such as sugar and urea, could be made only inside a living being. A chemist could take such substances apart, but could never build them. In 1828 a young German chemist, Friedrich Wöhler, was heating a salt called ammonium cyanate. Chemists counted this salt as a mineral substance. White crystals formed. He tested them and found that they were urea, the same substance that kidneys make. He wrote to his teacher, Jöns Jacob Berzelius, that he could make urea without needing a kidney. The old belief did not end that day. But chemists went on to build one substance of living bodies after another. In 1856 William Perkin, aged 18, tried to make the medicine quinine from chemicals in coal tar. He failed, and found a purple dye. Dye factories grew from his discovery, and medicine factories grew from the dye factories. Today factories make urea from air, natural gas and water, by the million tonnes.  
*What people believed before:* That the substances of living bodies can be made only inside a living being, by a life force. Wöhler made urea from a mineral salt. Later chemists made many more such substances.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Natural substances are safe, and substances made in a factory are harmful.** (A common belief, and many advertisements.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some factory products have done great harm, and many plant remedies have been used for centuries.  
  Against: A molecule is the same wherever it was made. Urea from a factory and urea from urine cannot be told apart. Snake venom and some wild mushrooms are natural and deadly.
- **The dose alone makes the poison.** (Paracelsus, a Swiss doctor, in 1538, and most scientists since.; *accepted*)  
  For: Salt, water, iron and every medicine help at one amount and harm at a larger one.  
  Against: The rule needs care. For lead, no safe amount has been found. The same dose also acts differently on a baby, a child and a grown-up.
- **More fertiliser always gives a bigger harvest.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: A field with no fertiliser often gives a small crop, and the first bag raises the crop a lot.  
  Against: A crop can take up only so much. Extra fertiliser is washed away or lost to the air. Much too much draws water out of the roots and harms the plant.
- **Farming with compost and bean crops alone could feed everyone.** (Many organic farmers and some farm scientists.; *debated*)  
  For: Much factory nitrogen is wasted today. Bean crops and compost can supply nitrogen without burning natural gas.  
  Against: Scientists who count nitrogen estimate that factory nitrogen grows about half the world's food. Replacing all of it would need much more farmland.
- **Hydrogen is a fuel with no pollution.** (Some engineers and companies.; *debated*)  
  For: Hydrogen burns to give only water, with no carbon dioxide.  
  Against: Most hydrogen today is made from natural gas, and that step gives off carbon dioxide. Hydrogen is clean only if it is made with clean energy.
- **Felix Hoffmann alone invented aspirin.** (The account Bayer long told, first printed in 1934.; *debated*)  
  For: Hoffmann's laboratory notes of August 1897 record that he made the substance.  
  Against: Arthur Eichengrün, his senior at Bayer, later wrote that the work was done under his direction. Eichengrün was Jewish, and his name was left out during Nazi rule. In 2000 the historian Walter Sneader judged his claim likely to be true.

## Roots
In this lesson you saw how chemistry makes fertilisers, medicines, fuels and paints. Here we ask what makes one substance a help at one time and a harm at another.

**Deep truth.** In One Dose, Two Bodies, two drops made the small glass dark and left the big pot pale. The drops were the same, and only the amount of water around them changed. A medicine spreads through a body in the same way, and plant food through the water in soil. So a substance helps or harms depending on its amount and on the size of the body.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort substances into good ones and bad ones: medicine and poison, plant food and pollution. The sorting leaves out the amount. Urea feeds a paddy field and kills the fish in the pond below.  
*Evidence:* In One Dose, Two Bodies, the pot needed about 20 drops to match the glass with 2. The big pot's dose made a small glass far too dark. In Feed the Field, 2 beans raised the harvest, and the third bean only reached the pond.

- *who am i:* Scientists estimate that about half the nitrogen in people's bodies today passed through a fertiliser factory. Before that, it was in the air.
- *what is life:* Every living being takes in substances, and needs each one in an amount that fits their size. Too little starves a body, and too much harms it.
- *what is this world:* Air, rock, crude oil and plants are made of the same kinds of atoms as medicines, fuels and paints. A factory only changes which atoms are joined to which.

A nitrogen atom may be in the air one year and in a bag of urea the next. A year later it is in a grain of rice. Then it is in your muscle. It is the same atom each time, with different partners. The same is true of amounts. Urea in the right amount is food for a field. The same urea in the pond is pollution. Many sages taught that everything comes from one source. The science here says something close and plain: fertiliser, medicine and poison are made of the same few kinds of atoms. The joining and the amount make the difference.

- **Tirukkural 949, in the chapter on medicine:** “உற்றான் அளவும் பிணியளவும் காலமும் / கற்றான் கருதிச் செயல்.” Thiruvalluvar says that a learned healer first weighs three things: the measure of the patient, the measure of the illness and the season. Only then does the healer act. (This is the same finding as One Dose, Two Bodies, written in Tamil about 1,500 years ago or more. The right amount depends on who receives it. The verse is advice to healers, and it was not the result of a measured test.)
- **Paracelsus, Septem Defensiones (Seven Defences), third defence, written in 1538:** All things are poison, and nothing is without poison. The dose alone makes a thing not a poison. (Paracelsus was a doctor answering people who accused him of giving poisons as medicines. His line is still the first rule of the science of poisons. Modern scientists add that for a few substances, such as lead, no safe dose has been found.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 9:** “持而盈之，不如其已。” To keep filling a bowl to the brim is not as good as stopping in time. (In Feed the Field, the third bean added nothing to the harvest and went into the pond. The text is about how to live. Farmers and doctors have found that the same holds for fertiliser and medicine.)
- Science (settled): For almost every substance tested, the effect on a body grows with the dose. Scientists draw this as a curve for each substance, and they use it to set safe limits.
- Science (settled): The atoms in factory urea are the same kinds of atoms as those in the urea your body makes. No test can tell the two apart.
- Science (debated): Some scientists think that very small doses of some harmful things can do a body good. This idea is called hormesis, and scientists disagree about how often it is true.
- Other view, Many scientists who study poisons and public health: The line 'the dose makes the poison' can be misused. Factories have used it to say that a little of their waste does no harm. For lead, and for some substances that cause cancer, no safe amount has been found. The same dose also acts differently on a baby before birth, a child and a grown-up. On this view, some substances are simply harmful, and the safest dose of them is none.
- Practice, Three breaths of air (3 min): Sit still and put one hand on your belly. Breathe in slowly. Four fifths of that breath is nitrogen. It goes in, and it comes out again unchanged. Breathe in again. One fifth is oxygen, and your body keeps a little of it. Breathe a third time. You cannot use the nitrogen in the air you breathe. The nitrogen in your body reached you another way, in your food. Sit quietly for five more breaths.
- Ask: In One Dose, Two Bodies, the same drops were strong in the glass and weak in the pot. Is anything strong or weak by itself?
- Ask: Urea feeds the paddy field and harms the pond below it. Is urea a good substance or a bad one?
- Ask: The nitrogen in your body was once in the air, and some of it passed through a factory. Is it yours, the air's or the factory's?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your body needs salt, and too much salt can make you ill. So is salt a food or a poison? (hint: Think about what changed between the helpful salt and the harmful salt. Was it the salt itself, or something else?)
- *deep:* Urea from a factory and urea from your own body are the same substance. Does it matter where a substance was made? (hint: Could any test tell the two apart? If none can, what is the difference?)
- *deep:* Some of the nitrogen in your muscles passed through a fertiliser factory. Is that part of you natural or made? (hint: A nitrogen atom in a fertiliser is the same atom that was in the air. Only the atoms it is joined to have changed.)
- *big:* Ammonia from Haber's reaction grows food for billions. It was also used to make explosives in a war. Is a discovery good or bad? (hint: Think about a kitchen knife. Who decides what a knife does?)
- *big:* A factory gives a town jobs and medicines. The people who live beside it breathe its smoke. Who should decide where a factory is built? (hint: Think about who gains, who bears the harm, and whether they are the same people.)
- *big:* Crude oil took millions of years to form. People may burn most of it within a few hundred years. What do we owe the people who come after us? (hint: Think about the oil itself, and also about the carbon dioxide that burning it leaves in the air.)

## Story: The Top That Left Its Colour Behind
*Bhagya, Kiran and Ajji are made up. These facts are real: Channapatna in Karnataka is known for wooden toys turned on a lathe. Many are made of a pale wood called hale mara, or ivory wood. The colour is carried in lac, a resin made by lac insects. The lac is softened over heat and mixed with colour. A stick of it is pressed against the spinning wood. The heat of rubbing melts the lac. The toy is polished with a dry leaf of talegiri, the screw pine. Families in Karnataka set out steps of dolls at Dasara.*

Bhagya had made the top herself, and by evening it had to be red.

She lived in Channapatna, on the road between Bengaluru and Mysuru, where half the street made toys. Her Ajji had turned wood on a lathe for forty years. That week Ajji had let Bhagya hold the chisel for the first time. Out of a block of pale hale wood had come a small top, a little lopsided, with a fat belly and a sharp foot.

Tomorrow was the first day of Dasara. Every house on the street would set out its steps of dolls. Bhagya wanted her top on the lowest step, shining red.

"Ajji, will you colour it?"

"After the order is done," said Ajji, without looking up. Sixty rattles were going to a shop in Mysuru, and forty-one of them were still bare wood.

Bhagya could not wait for forty-one rattles. She took a pinch of kumkum from the box beside the lamp and mixed it with water in a coconut shell. She painted the top with the frayed end of a twig. The red was so deep that she laughed aloud.

She set the top in the sun, and in a short while it was dry.

Her cousin Kiran came by with a string. "Let me spin it," he said. He was a year younger than Bhagya, and he could spin a top on his palm, which she could not.

He wound the string and threw. The top hummed across the cement floor. Where it had landed there was a small red ring, like a bangle drawn in chalk. When the top fell over, Kiran picked it up and looked at his fingers. They were red. So was the string.

The top was pink, with pale patches where his hand had been.

"Your colour is running away," he said.

"It is not thick enough. That is all." She mixed the kumkum again, as thick as dosa batter this time, and laid it on in a heavy coat. It took longer to dry. When it did, it cracked like the mud of a dry pond, and flakes fell off before anyone touched it.

She tried once more, with coconut oil in place of water. The colour went on smoothly and did not crack. It also did not dry. An hour later the top was still greasy, and it left a red print on the step where she put it down.

Bhagya sat in the doorway with the sticky top in her lap and watched her grandmother work. She had watched a thousand times. This time she watched the colour.

Ajji had no brush and no water. A rattle spun on the lathe so fast that its shape blurred. Ajji held a short red stick against it and pressed hard. The stick squealed, and a warm smell rose from it. Red flowed off the stick and around the wood in a band, as ghee spreads on a hot dosa. Then Ajji took the stick away and pressed a dry talegiri leaf against the spinning rattle. The red began to shine like a wet stone.

Ajji stopped the lathe and dropped the rattle into the basket. It landed on the others with a click. Nothing came off on anything.

"Ajji. Why does your red stay, and mine run away?"

Ajji looked at the top, and at Bhagya's red hands, and did not laugh. "What did you put on it?"

"Kumkum, with water. Then more kumkum. Then kumkum with oil."

"Kumkum is a powder," said Ajji. "Water carries a powder to the wood, and then the water goes away into the air. What is left? Powder, sitting on the wood as dust sits on a shelf. Nothing is holding it."

"And the oil?"

"Coconut oil holds it, but coconut oil never turns hard. You need something that carries the colour, and then turns hard and keeps it."

She opened a tin. Inside were lumps the colour of dark honey. "Lac. Tiny insects make it on the branches of trees in the forest. They cover themselves with it."

Ajji held a lump on an iron rod over the coals until it sagged like warm jaggery. Then she pushed the kumkum box towards Bhagya. "You mix. Fold it in."

Bhagya sprinkled the red powder on the soft lac and folded it over with a wooden stick, again and again. The powder disappeared into the lac. The lump turned red all the way through. Ajji rolled it on a stone into a stick as long as a finger and left it to cool. In a few minutes it was hard enough to tap on the floor.

"Now the colour is inside something," said Ajji. "Wash your top and bring it."

Bhagya scrubbed the top until it was pale wood again. Ajji fixed it on the lathe. She put the red stick in Bhagya's hand and closed her own hand over it. "Press. Harder. The rubbing makes the heat, and the heat melts the lac."

The stick squealed. Bhagya felt it soften against the wood, and she saw red run round the belly of her top. Ajji gave her the talegiri leaf, and the red began to shine.

When the top had cooled, Bhagya rubbed it hard on the inside of her wrist. Her wrist stayed brown.

Kiran wound the string and threw. The top hummed across the floor for a long time. Afterwards they both bent down to look. There was no ring on the cement.

"Is there lac on our front door too?" Kiran asked. "That is blue, and it does not come off."

Bhagya went to the door and scratched it with a fingernail. Then she looked at her nail, which was clean. "Something is holding it," she said. "I don't know what. But it will not be only colour."

**For the parent:** Bhagya's kumkum was only colour, so it rubbed off as soon as the water dried. Ajji folded the same kumkum into lac, which melts, carries the colour and sets hard. A colour stays on a surface only when a binder holds it there.

- Ask: The water carried the kumkum to the wood. Why did the kumkum not stay when the water dried?
- Ask: Coconut oil held the colour, but the top stayed greasy. What must a binder do that coconut oil does not do?
- Ask: Kiran asked about the blue front door. How could you find out what holds its colour?

### e1 · Three Paints: what keeps the colour on the card? (35 min)
- **question:** A colour powder mixed with water makes something that looks like paint. Does the colour stay on when it dries? Does rice paste or white glue in the mix change that?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. When the three paints are dry, which will lose the most colour when you rub it? Which will lose the most when you wipe it with a damp cloth? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same colour powder, 1 level teaspoon, in every paint. Paint all the stripes on one card, the same size, and dry them together for the same time. Rub each stripe 10 times with a clean, dry fingertip, pressing equally hard. Wipe each stripe 5 times with a clean part of the damp cloth. Only one thing changes: what is mixed with the powder.
- **safety:** Do not taste the paints. Crush chalk gently so that the dust does not fly, and wash your hands afterwards. Cover the table with newspaper.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows, for paint A, paint B and paint C. Write the dry rub score and the damp wipe score for each paint.

  *Parent says:* Paint two stripes of each paint. One stripe is for the dry rub, and one is for the damp wipe. Keep the coats thin and even.  
  *Child mode:* Draw three rows with a ruler: A, B and C. Paint two stripes in each row.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the card flat under a fan. Wait 30 minutes. A stripe is dry when it no longer feels cool to a light touch at its edge.  
  *Parent says:* Now the water must leave. Where is the water going? We will come back in half an hour.  
  *Child mode:* Put the card under a fan. Wait 30 minutes.
  - Note: Use the wait for the story or the flashcards. On a damp day, or if paint B is thick, the stripes may need an hour.

### e2 · One Dose, Two Bodies: why is a child's dose smaller? (25 min)
- **question:** A small glass of water and a big pot of water each get the same 2 drops of colour. Do they end up looking the same? How many drops does the pot need to match the glass?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. The pot holds ten times as much water as the glass. Will 2 drops colour both the same? How many drops will the pot need in all to match the glass? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both bodies hold the same tap water. Every drop comes from the same dropper, held the same way. Always compare in two clear glasses of the same size, filled to the same height, over white paper. A deep layer of coloured water looks darker than a shallow one, so the heights must match. Stir before you compare. Only one thing changes: the amount of water that the drops are spread through.
- **safety:** Do not drink the coloured water. Food colour and ink stain clothes, so cover the table with newspaper. This is a model with colour. Never test with real medicine.
- **record:** Write the number of drops in the pot each time you compare. Beside each number write paler, the same or darker.
- **results table:** Drops in the pot so far | The pot water beside the small glass: paler, the same or darker?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour 100 millilitres of water into one glass. Pour 1 litre into the pot. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* The glass is a small body, like a child's. The pot is a big body, like a grown-up's. The pot holds ten times as much water. Each will get 2 drops of colour. Will they look the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The glass is a small body. The pot is a big body. Each gets 2 drops. Will they match? Why?
  - Ask: How many drops do you think the pot will need to look like the glass? (good: Ten times as many, so 20.; More than 2, because there is more water.; Any number with a reason.)
  - Note: Half a 200 millilitre steel tumbler is 100 millilitres. Five full tumblers make 1 litre. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 2 drops of colour in the glass and 2 drops in the pot. Stir both. Fill the second glass from the pot to the same height as the first. Stand the two glasses on white paper.  
  *Parent says:* Both bodies have had the same dose, 2 drops. Look at the two glasses from the side. What do you see? Where did the colour in the pot go?  
  *Child mode:* Put 2 drops in each. Stir. Fill the second glass from the pot. Compare them.
  - Note: To drop from a straw, dip it in the colour and close the top with a finger. Lift it, then ease the finger to let one drop fall. Let your child practise over a plate first. If you have no food colour or ink, dissolve 3 teaspoons of instant coffee in 6 teaspoons of water. Then use a quarter teaspoon of this coffee wherever a step says 2 drops.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour the sample back into the pot. Add 2 more drops to the pot and stir. Fill the second glass from the pot again and compare. Write paler, the same or darker. Repeat until the pot water matches the small glass.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the sample back. Add two more drops to the pot and stir. Fill the glass again to the same height. Is the pot water paler, the same or darker?  
  *Child mode:* Add 2 drops to the pot. Stir. Fill the glass and compare. Write what you see.
  - Note: To save time, your child may add 4 drops at a time until the colours are close. Most families find a match between 18 and 22 drops.
  - Check: In One Dose, Two Bodies, why must both glasses be filled to the same height? → **A deeper layer of colour looks darker**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Empty the second glass and put 100 millilitres of fresh water in it. Add the pot's full number of drops to this glass. Stand it beside the first small glass.  
  *Parent says:* This new glass is another small body. We will give it the big body's dose. How does it look beside the first glass?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the second glass with fresh water. Add the pot's number of drops. Compare.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the notes together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. The drops were the same each time. What made them look weak in the pot and strong in the glass?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: The pot needed about ten times as many drops as the glass. Why ten? (good: The pot has ten times as much water.; The same drops are spread through more water.; To be equally strong, the dose must grow with the body.)
  - Ask: What does the last glass tell you about a child taking a grown-up's dose? (good: It would be far too strong for a small body.; The same amount is too much when the body is smaller.)
  - Note: The reason: the colour spreads evenly through all the water. Ten times the water makes each drop ten times weaker. A medicine spreads through the blood and the water of a body in a similar way. So a bigger body needs a bigger dose for the same effect. A grown-up's dose is too strong for a child. A real body is more than a pot of water. The liver and kidneys of a child also handle medicines differently. So a child's dose is never guessed. It comes from the label or a doctor.
  - Think: A farmer waters one pot plant and a whole field. What should she think about before she adds plant food to each?

### c1 · The Slow Tablet: let the colour out late, but not too late (30 min)

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. When the water evaporates, only loose powder is left. A paint needs a binder to hold the pigment.

   **Answer:** urea → Fertiliser; potash → Fertiliser; LPG → Fuel; diesel → Fuel; paracetamol → Medicine; penicillin → Medicine; lime wash → Paint; wall emulsion → Paint. *Why:* Fertilisers feed plants, fuels are burned for heat, medicines treat illness, and paints colour and protect surfaces.

7. (order) Put these in order, from the shortest carbon chain to the longest.  
   **Answer:** ['LPG', 'Petrol', 'Diesel', 'Candle wax']. *Why:* LPG has 3 or 4 carbon atoms in a chain, and petrol has about 5 to 12. Diesel has about 12 to 20, and candle wax about 20 to 40.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The carbon atoms join with oxygen to make carbon dioxide, and the hydrogen atoms join with oxygen to make water.

   Options: A child's body is smaller / Children dislike bitter tastes / Medicine is costly / Children are never very ill  
   **Answer:** A child's body is smaller. *Why:* A medicine spreads through the body. In a smaller body the same amount is stronger, so a child needs less.
10. (fill) A substance that a factory makes along with its product is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['by-product']. *Why:* A by-product is made at the same time as the product. Some by-products can be used, and some are waste.

12. (write) In One Dose, Two Bodies, 2 drops looked dark in the glass and pale in the pot. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The pot held ten times as much water as the glass. The same drops were spread through more water, so each part of the water got less colour.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the amount of colour was the same, and that the amount of water was different.

### Why each product works as it does (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A refinery separates crude oil into its parts. What does it use to separate them?  
   Options: Their different boiling points / Their different colours / A strong magnet / A fine sieve  
   **Answer:** Their different boiling points. *Why:* Short carbon chains boil at low temperatures and long chains at high temperatures. So each part is collected at its own height in the tower.
2. (mcq) Water-based wall paint dries on a wall. What happens to its water?  
   Options: It evaporates into the air / It turns into pigment / It sinks into the binder / It burns away  
   **Answer:** It evaporates into the air. *Why:* The water is the solvent. It evaporates, and the binder particles join into a sheet that holds the pigment.
3. (mcq) Why does lime wash turn hard on a wall?  
   Options: It reacts with carbon dioxide from the air / It cools down / It soaks up sunlight / Its water turns to stone  
   **Answer:** It reacts with carbon dioxide from the air. *Why:* Lime takes in carbon dioxide and becomes calcium carbonate, the substance of chalk and limestone. This is a chemical reaction.
4. (mcq) A crop takes up half of the urea spread on a field. Where can the other half go?  
   Options: Into ponds, rivers and the air / Back into the bag / Into the grain anyway / Nowhere, because it vanishes  
   **Answer:** Into ponds, rivers and the air. *Why:* Atoms cannot vanish. Nitrogen that the crop does not take up is washed away by rain or lost to the air as gases.
5. (truefalse) Carbon dioxide is harmful in any amount, however small.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. You breathe it out, and plants need it. A little in the air keeps the Earth warm enough to live on. The harm comes from too much.
6. (truefalse) Burning 1 kilogram of LPG makes less than 1 kilogram of carbon dioxide.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It makes about 3 kilograms. The extra mass is oxygen from the air, which joins with the carbon of the fuel.

   **Answer:** water-based wall paint → The solvent evaporates; nail polish → The solvent evaporates; oil paint → It reacts with a gas in the air; lime wash → It reacts with a gas in the air; lac on a wooden toy → It cools. *Why:* Wall paint and nail polish lose their solvent. 8. (odd) Three of these medicines were first found in a plant or a mould. Which is the odd one out?

   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Quinine comes from cinchona bark, penicillin from a mould and artemisinin from sweet wormwood. Chalky antacid is made from limestone, a rock.
9. (count) A made-up medicine needs 10 milligrams for each kilogram of body mass. How many milligrams suit a 25 kilogram child?  
   **Answer:** 250. *Why:* 10 milligrams for each of 25 kilograms is 10 times 25, which is 250 milligrams.
10. (order) Put the steps of making and using urea in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Take nitrogen from the air', 'Join nitrogen with hydrogen to make ammonia', 'Join ammonia with carbon dioxide to make urea', 'Spread the urea on the field']. *Why:* Nitrogen gas is first made into ammonia. The ammonia is then made into urea, a solid that is easy to carry and spread.
11. (write) Some people think three bags of fertiliser must grow three times the crop of one bag. Explain what really happens.  
   **Answer:** The first bag raises the harvest a lot. A crop can take up only so much, so the extra is washed into ponds or lost to the air. Much too much can draw water out of the roots and harm the plants.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a crop has a limit, and says where the extra fertiliser goes.
12. (write) Why does a flame need plenty of air to burn cleanly? Say what it makes with too little air.  
   **Answer:** Burning joins the fuel with oxygen from the air. With enough oxygen, a flame makes carbon dioxide and water. 13. (write) A plant medicine stopped working after the plant was boiled. Give one reason why heat might matter.

14. (perspective) Is a natural substance safer than one made in a factory?

1. (mcq) Fertilisers, medicines, fuels and paints are all made in which way?  
   Options: By chemical reactions from raw materials / By digging them up ready-made / By picking them from trees / By melting old ones down  
   **Answer:** By chemical reactions from raw materials. *Why:* Each is made by changing a raw material into a new substance. The raw material may be air, rock, crude oil or a plant.
2. (mcq) Which of these is a raw material?  
   Options: Crude oil / Petrol / Paracetamol / Wall paint  
   **Answer:** Crude oil. *Why:* Crude oil is taken from nature as it is. Petrol, paracetamol and wall paint are all products made from raw materials.
3. (mcq) Why do farmers add nitrogen fertiliser to a field?  
   Options: Harvests carry nitrogen away / Soil holds no air / Rain water is too clean / Seeds dislike water  
   **Answer:** Harvests carry nitrogen away. *Why:* The nitrogen inside a crop leaves the field with the harvest. Fertiliser puts nitrogen back for the next crop.
4. (mcq) What can happen when too much fertiliser washes into a pond?  
   Options: Algae grow and fish die / The pond freezes over / The water turns salty / The fish grow larger  
   **Answer:** Algae grow and fish die. *Why:* The extra nitrogen feeds algae. When the algae die and rot, the oxygen in the water is used up, and fish cannot breathe.
5. (mcq) What is a fuel?  
   Options: A substance that gives heat on burning / Any liquid that flows / A gas found only in air / Any substance with a smell  
   **Answer:** A substance that gives heat on burning. *Why:* A fuel stores energy and gives it out as heat when it burns. A fuel can be a gas, a liquid or a solid.
6. (mcq) Which two substances does burning LPG mainly make?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide and water / Oxygen and nitrogen / Soot and salt / Hydrogen and sand  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide and water. *Why:* LPG is made of carbon and hydrogen. Joined with oxygen, the carbon becomes carbon dioxide and the hydrogen becomes water.
7. (mcq) Which of these medicines was first found in a mould?  
   Options: Penicillin / Quinine / Aspirin / Artemisinin  
   **Answer:** Penicillin. *Why:* Penicillin comes from the mould Penicillium. Quinine, aspirin and artemisinin each began with a plant.
8. (mcq) Why are new medicines tested on many volunteers before they are sold?  
   Options: To check they work and are safe / To make them taste better / To use up spare tablets / To give them a colour  
   **Answer:** To check they work and are safe. *Why:* A substance may work in a glass dish and still fail or harm in a body. Only careful tests on many people can show that.
9. (mcq) The same dose goes into a small body and a big body. In which is it stronger?  
   Options: The small body / The big body / Equally strong in both / It has no strength in either  
   **Answer:** The small body. *Why:* In a small body the dose is spread through less blood and water. This is what the dark small glass showed in One Dose, Two Bodies.
10. (mcq) What does the solvent in a paint do?  
   Options: Makes the paint thin enough to spread / Gives the paint its colour / Sticks the pigment to the wall / Stops the wall from cracking  
   **Answer:** Makes the paint thin enough to spread. *Why:* The solvent carries the pigment and the binder, and then evaporates. The pigment gives colour and the binder does the sticking.
11. (mcq) What is pollution?  
   Options: A substance present in a harmful amount / Anything made in a factory / Any gas at all / Any by-product at all  
   **Answer:** A substance present in a harmful amount. *Why:* A substance pollutes when there is enough of it, in the air, water or soil, to harm living beings. The amount matters.
12. (truefalse) Urea can be made only inside a living body.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In 1828 urea was made in a laboratory from a mineral salt. Today factories make it from air, natural gas and water.
13. (mcq) A factory makes a fertiliser that raises harvests, but some of it washes into a pond. What is the best way to think about it?  
   Options: Weigh the benefit, the bigger harvest, against the cost, the harm in the pond, and look for ways to cut the harm / It is all good, because a bigger harvest is all that matters / It is all bad, so no farmer should ever use it / Nothing to weigh, because pond water cleans itself at once  
   **Answer:** Weigh the benefit, the bigger harvest, against the cost, the harm in the pond, and look for ways to cut the harm. *Why:* A change can have a benefit and a cost at the same time. The bigger harvest is real, and so is the harm in the pond. Good thinking weighs both and looks for ways to cut the harm.

## Applications
- Farmers get a soil test before they buy fertiliser. The test shows which nutrient the field lacks, so that only that nutrient is added.
- Urea sold in India is coated with neem oil. The coating slows the release of nitrogen, so more of it reaches the crop.
- A medicine label gives the dose by age or by body mass. A syrup comes with a measuring cap so that the dose is exact.
- Petrol in India has ethanol mixed into it. The ethanol is made from sugarcane and grain, so a little less crude oil is burned.
- House paint sold in India since 2017 may hold only a trace of lead. Older paint that is flaking is removed with care for this reason.
- Ships, bridges and bicycles are painted to keep air and water away from the steel. Then the steel does not rust.

## Spotted around us
- A red LPG cylinder in the kitchen. Its gas was separated from crude oil or natural gas. A smell was added so that a leak can be noticed.
- White sacks of urea stacked at a fertiliser shop before the monsoon sowing. Nearly half of each sack is nitrogen that was taken from the air.
- Three numbers, such as 19:19:19, on a packet of plant food at a nursery. They show how rich it is in nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
- A strip of tablets with the dose printed on the back. Look for the letters 'mg', which stand for milligrams.
- A lime-washed wall that leaves white powder on your sleeve, next to a painted door that leaves none. The door's paint has a stronger binder.
- Black sand on a beach. Some of it is the mineral from which the white pigment of paint is made.

## Open questions
- **Can ammonia be made from air without high heat, high pressure and natural gas?** Some bacteria make ammonia at the temperature of the soil, using an enzyme. Chemists have not yet matched this in a factory.
- **Can a fuel be made in huge amounts that adds no carbon dioxide to the air?** Hydrogen, biofuels and fuels made from captured carbon dioxide all work in small amounts. Each is still costly or needs a great deal of clean energy.
- **Why does the same medicine work well in one person and poorly in another?** Bodies differ in how fast they break a medicine down. Scientists can explain this for some medicines, but not yet for most.
- **Where will new medicines against resistant bacteria come from?** Bacteria become resistant faster than new antibiotics are found. Scientists are searching in soil microbes, the deep sea and insects, with few successes so far.
- **What happens to paint after it flakes off walls, ships and roads?** Flakes of paint break into very small plastic particles. Nobody yet knows how much of this reaches the sea, or what it does to living beings there.

## Connected maths
- In One Dose, Two Bodies, a glass with 100 millilitres of water got 2 drops of colour. The pot holds 1 litre. How many drops does the pot need to be equally strong? How many would a bucket of 5 litres need? **The pot needs 20 drops. The bucket needs 100 drops.**. 1 litre is 1,000 millilitres, which is 10 times 100 millilitres. So the pot needs 10 times 2 drops, which is 20. 5 litres is 50 times 100 millilitres, and 50 times 2 is 100. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A made-up medicine needs 10 milligrams for each kilogram of body mass. A child has a mass of 25 kilograms and a grown-up 60 kilograms. Find both doses. How many times too strong is the grown-up's dose for the child? **The child needs 250 milligrams and the grown-up 600 milligrams. The grown-up's dose is 2.4 times too strong for the child.**. 10 times 25 is 250, and 10 times 60 is 600. 600 divided by 250 is 2.4. So the grown-up's dose is more than twice what the child's body should get. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- Burning 1 kilogram of LPG makes about 3 kilograms of carbon dioxide. A full home cylinder holds 14.2 kilograms of LPG. About how much carbon dioxide does one cylinder make? How can the gas weigh more than the fuel? **About 42.6 kilograms. The extra mass is oxygen from the air.**. 14.2 times 3 is 42.6. Each carbon atom in the fuel joins with two oxygen atoms from the air. So the carbon dioxide holds the fuel's carbon and also the air's oxygen. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In Feed the Field, one farmer uses 2 beans every season and another uses 3. In six seasons the rain is heavy twice. Work out each farmer's bags. How many beans does each send to the pond? **The 2-bean farmer keeps 20 bags and sends 2 beans to the pond. The 3-bean farmer keeps 18 bags and sends 6 beans.**. With 2 beans: four light seasons give 6 minus 2, which is 4 bags each, so 16. Two heavy seasons give 4 minus 2, which is 2 bags each, so 4. That makes 20, and 2 beans washed away. With 3 beans: every season gives 6 minus 3, which is 3 bags, so 18. One bean goes to the pond every season, so 6. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- In 100 kilograms of urea there are 46 kilograms of nitrogen. A farmer spreads two 45 kilogram bags. Her crop takes up half of the nitrogen. How many kilograms of nitrogen does the crop not take up? **About 20.7 kilograms.**. Two bags are 90 kilograms of urea. 46 hundredths of 90 is 41.4 kilograms of nitrogen. Half of 41.4 is 20.7. So about 20.7 kilograms are left to wash away or escape to the air. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In Three Paints, a child scored the colour that came off: paint A scored 2 dry and 2 wet. Paint B scored 1 dry and 2 wet. Paint C scored 0 dry and 1 wet. Which paint would you choose for a card kept indoors? Which for a pot left in the rain? Explain. **Indoors, paint B or paint C would do. In the rain, only paint C comes close, and even paint C lost a little.**. Indoors only the dry score matters. B scored 1 and C scored 0, so both hold far better than A. In rain the wet score matters. A and B both scored 2, so both would wash off. C scored 1, so it is the best of the three, but not yet rainproof. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
