# Waste after the bin: what rots, what stays, and where it all goes
`sci.waste.compost-and-disposal.L5`

**Summary.** Plastic bags and banana peels both go in the bin, but a hundred years later only one is gone. Why? In this lesson you will sort things that were once alive from things that never were, use water to sort look-alike plastics, and find out if a shiny packet is metal, plastic or both.

**Intro.** *The waste truck comes every morning, and the bin is empty again. Where is that waste tonight? Where will it be when you are fifty years old?* People say they throw waste 'away'. But every peel, bottle and packet goes to a real place and stays made of the same atoms. Some of it rots within weeks. Some of it can be melted and made into new things. Some of it lies in a dump for hundreds of years, or is burnt and goes into the air. In this lesson you find out which waste does which, and why. You also find out what your own family's waste is made of.

## Learning outcomes

6. By the end I can weigh my family's waste for a week, chart it and plan to make less.
7. By the end I can argue with evidence about city waste, and name one thing nobody knows yet.

## Terms
- **Waste**: Anything a person decides they no longer want and throws out. The same thing can be waste to one person and useful to another. *e.g.* An old newspaper is waste in your home. The kabadiwala pays for it, because a paper mill can use it.
- **Environment**: Everything around us, whether nature made it or people did. Soil, water, air, plants, animals, houses and streets are all part of it. *e.g.* A pond, a neem tree and the road in front of your house are all part of your environment.
- **Organic waste**: Waste that came from living beings, such as plants and animals. Decomposers can eat it, so it rots. *e.g.* Vegetable peels, leftover rice, dry leaves, paper, cotton cloth and wood are organic waste.
- **Inorganic waste**: Waste that did not come from living beings. It is made from rocks, sand and metal ores, and decomposers cannot eat it. *e.g.* Glass bottles, steel tins, aluminium foil and broken tiles are inorganic waste. Waste rules also put plastic in this group.
- **Biodegradable**: Able to be broken down by decomposers into soil, water and gases. *e.g.* A banana leaf plate is biodegradable. In a compost pot, decomposers finish eating one in a few weeks.
- **Non-biodegradable**: Not able to be broken down by decomposers, or broken down so slowly that it lasts for lifetimes. *e.g.* A plastic carry bag is non-biodegradable. Sunlight cracks it into small pieces, but the pieces stay plastic.
- **Decomposer**: A living being who feeds on dead plants, dead animals and droppings, and turns them into soil and gases. Most decomposers are fungi and bacteria. *e.g.* The white threads in a compost pot are fungi. They are eating the peels.
- **Segregation**: Keeping different kinds of waste apart from the moment they are thrown. India's waste rules ask every home to keep three kinds apart. *e.g.* A home with a wet bin, a dry bag and a small box for hazardous waste is segregating.
- **Compost**: Dark, crumbly matter that is left when decomposers have eaten organic waste with plenty of air. It feeds plants. *e.g.* A pot of peels mixed with dry leaves becomes compost in about two months.
- **Methane**: A gas with no colour and no smell that burns. Some microbes make it when they break down organic waste where there is no air. *e.g.* Deep inside a dump there is no air. The food waste there gives off methane, which can catch fire.
- **Biogas**: A mix of methane and carbon dioxide that people make on purpose and burn as fuel. Microbes make it from wet waste in a closed tank with no air. *e.g.* A biogas plant in a village turns cow dung and kitchen waste into gas for cooking.
- **Open dump**: A piece of land where mixed waste is tipped and left in the open. Nothing lies under the waste and nothing covers it. *e.g.* Ghazipur in Delhi and Deonar in Mumbai are open dumps that have grown into hills.
- **Landfill**: A place built for burying waste safely. It has a liner underneath, pipes to take out dirty water and gas, and a cover of soil. *e.g.* In a well-built landfill, each day's waste is pressed down and covered with soil.
- **Leachate**: The dirty liquid that drains out of a heap of waste. Rainwater soaks through the waste and carries dissolved matter with it. *e.g.* The dark liquid at the bottom of a kitchen bin is leachate. Under a dump, it can reach the groundwater.
- **Pesticide**: A chemical sprayed on crops to kill insects or other pests. Too much of it can poison soil, water and useful living beings. *e.g.* A farmer sprays pesticide on cotton plants. Some of it washes into the soil with the next rain, and earthworms there die.
- **Incineration**: Burning waste in a closed furnace at very high heat. Some plants use the heat to make electricity. *e.g.* A waste-to-energy plant incinerates mixed waste. It still leaves ash, and it sends gases up its chimney.
- **Recycling**: Breaking a used thing down into its material, and making new things from that material. *e.g.* Old glass bottles are crushed, melted and blown into new bottles.
- **Downcycling**: Recycling a material into something of lower quality, which often cannot be recycled again. *e.g.* Clear drink bottles are often made into polyester fibre for cloth. That cloth is not made back into bottles.
- **Resin code**: The number from 1 to 7 inside a small triangle on a plastic thing. It tells which kind of plastic the thing is made of. *e.g.* A drink bottle has a 1 on its base. A takeaway box often has a 5.
- **Density**: How much matter is packed into each bit of space. A material denser than water sinks in water, and a less dense one floats. *e.g.* A piece of drink bottle is denser than water and sinks. A piece of its cap is less dense and floats.
- **Multi-layer packaging**: A packet made of thin layers of different materials stuck together, such as plastic, metal and paper. *e.g.* A chips packet is plastic film with a very thin coat of aluminium. A juice carton is paper, plastic and foil.
- **Hazardous waste**: Waste that can poison, burn or cut living beings, even in small amounts. It must be kept apart from other waste. *e.g.* Used batteries, old medicines, tube lights, paint tins and insect spray cans are hazardous waste.
- **E-waste**: Electrical and electronic things that are thrown out. E-waste holds useful metals and also harmful ones. *e.g.* An old phone, a dead charger and a broken remote are e-waste. They go to an e-waste collector, not the dry bin.
- **Microplastic**: A piece of plastic smaller than 5 millimetres. Big plastic things crack into microplastics, which do not rot. *e.g.* A thermocol plate rubbed between the hands breaks into tiny white beads. Each bead is still plastic.
- **Waste hierarchy**: A list of what to do about waste, with the best choice first: refuse and reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, and last of all dispose. *e.g.* Carrying a steel bottle is reducing. It is better than recycling a plastic bottle, because no waste is made.

## Explanations
### Organic and inorganic waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Organic waste came from living beings, and decomposers can break it down. Inorganic waste came from rocks, sand and metal ores, and decomposers cannot break it down.

Fungi and bacteria have fed on dead plants and animals for hundreds of millions of years. They have enzymes, which are tiny tools that cut food into pieces small enough to take in. So anything built by a living being has someone who can eat it. That is why peels, paper, cotton, wood and leather rot. Glass is melted sand, and metals come from ores dug out of rock. No decomposer feeds on them, so they do not rot. Organic does not mean wet. Paper and cotton are dry, but they are organic. Plastic is harder to place. It is made from oil, and oil formed from tiny sea beings who died millions of years ago. But factories join its atoms into very long chains that almost no decomposer can cut. So waste rules put plastic with inorganic waste.
- e.g. Organic waste includes vegetable peels, leftover food, dry leaves, flowers, paper, cardboard, cotton, jute, wood and hair.
- e.g. Inorganic waste includes glass bottles, steel tins, aluminium foil, broken cups and broken tiles.
- e.g. Plastic includes milk packets, bottles, wrappers and thermocol. It is made from ancient living beings, but it is treated as inorganic waste because it does not rot.
- e.g. In Once Alive or Never Alive, an eggshell and a coconut shell are organic, though they rot slowly.
### What decomposers do to organic waste, with air and without air · *defined*
**Definition:** With plenty of air, decomposers turn organic waste into compost, carbon dioxide, water and heat. Without air, other microbes turn it into methane, carbon dioxide and strong-smelling gases.

Decomposers who use air work fast, and their heap smells like damp earth. This is composting. A good part of the mass of the peels leaves as carbon dioxide and water vapour. The rest stays as dark compost. Deep inside a packed dump, the air is soon used up. A different set of microbes takes over there. They work slowly and give off methane. Methane burns, so it can start fires inside a dump. Methane also traps heat in the air. Over a hundred years, methane warms the Earth about 28 times as much as the same mass of carbon dioxide. A biogas plant uses the same microbes on purpose. It keeps wet waste in a closed tank, collects the methane and burns it as fuel. The slurry that is left goes to fields as manure. A village biogas plant can be fed cow dung, dry leaves, crop leftovers such as stalks and husks, and kitchen scraps. The family cooks on the gas and spreads the leftover manure on the field, so the matter goes back to the soil.
- e.g. A balcony compost pot with dry leaves mixed in has air, and makes compost in about two months.
- e.g. Food waste buried under tonnes of other waste has no air, and makes methane for many years.
- e.g. Many homes in Indian villages cook on biogas made from cow dung in a tank beside the house.
- e.g. Indore sends wet waste to a large plant that makes gas for city buses.
### Open dumps and landfills: what happens under the heap · *defined*
**Definition:** An open dump is waste tipped on bare land. A landfill is built to hold waste. It has a liner below, pipes for dirty water and gas, and a cover of soil.

Rain falls on a dump and soaks down through the waste. On the way it picks up rotting matter, salts, and metals from batteries and paints. The dark liquid that drains out is called leachate. Under an open dump, leachate seeps into the soil and can reach the groundwater that wells and borewells draw from. Inside the heap, buried food waste makes methane, and the heap can smoulder for weeks. A heap that grows too tall and steep can also slide. A well-built landfill is designed to stop each of these. A layer of pressed clay and a thick plastic sheet line the bottom. Pipes carry the leachate to a treatment tank. Other pipes collect the methane. Each day's waste is covered with soil. Even so, a landfill only stores the waste. Someone must look after it for many years after it is full.
- e.g. At Ghazipur in Delhi, the dump grew into a hill some 50 to 65 metres high, and reports differ. In 2017 a part of it slid down and killed two people.
- e.g. In 2016 the Deonar dump in Mumbai burned for days, and the smoke could be seen in pictures from space.
- e.g. In A Dump That Does Not Leak, you build a small lined dump and test whether it leaks.
### Burning does not destroy waste · *defined*
**Definition:** When waste burns, its atoms join with oxygen and leave as gases and smoke. A little ash stays behind. No atom is destroyed.

A sheet of paper weighs far more than the ash it leaves. The missing matter did not vanish. Most of it went into the air as carbon dioxide and water vapour, with some smoke. Smoke is made of tiny solid specks that reach deep into the lungs. Burning plastic sends out more than smoke. Some plastics hold chlorine, and when they burn in an open fire they make poisons called dioxins. So a fire moves waste from the ground into the air that everyone breathes. Antoine Lavoisier, a French chemist, showed in the 1780s that matter is never lost in burning. He weighed everything before and after, in sealed glass vessels. Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier worked with him, kept the records and drew the equipment.
- e.g. A heap of dry leaves burnt on the street becomes smoke, gases and a handful of ash.
- e.g. Dump fires are often started by methane, and can burn for days.
- e.g. In Where Did the Peel Go, you draw what leaves a burning peel and where it ends up.
### Plastic is many materials: the number in the triangle · *defined*
**Definition:** 'Plastic' is a name for many different materials. The resin code is a number from 1 to 7 in a small triangle. It tells which kind a thing is made of.

Every plastic is made of very long chain molecules, but the chains differ. In a drink bottle, marked 1, the chains hold carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms and pack closely. That plastic is denser than water, so a piece of it sinks. In a bottle cap or a milk packet, the chains hold only carbon and hydrogen, like candle wax. Those plastics are less dense than water, so they float. Different plastics also melt at different temperatures and do not mix well when melted. A batch with two kinds in it makes a weak, cloudy plastic. So a recycling factory wants one kind at a time. The triangle on a thing only names the kind. It is not a promise that the thing will be recycled.
- e.g. Code 1 is PET. It is used for water and soft drink bottles, and it sinks in water.
- e.g. Code 2 is HDPE. It is used for shampoo bottles and many bottle caps, and it floats.
- e.g. Code 3 is PVC. It is used for pipes and some shrink labels, and it sinks.
- e.g. Code 4 is LDPE. It is used for milk packets and carry bags, and it floats.
- e.g. Code 5 is PP. It is used for takeaway boxes, many caps and straws, and it floats.
- e.g. Code 6 is PS. It is used for some disposable cups and spoons, and for thermocol. Solid pieces sink slowly in plain water, and float in salty water.
- e.g. Code 7 is used for all other plastics, and for mixtures of plastics.
### Recycling, downcycling and what is never recycled · *defined*
**Definition:** Recycling makes new things from the material of old ones. How many times a material can be recycled depends on the material.

Glass and metals can be melted and remade again and again. The new thing is as good as the old. Making a can from old aluminium takes about one twentieth of the energy of making it from ore. Paper is different. Each time it is pulped, its fibres get shorter, so paper can be recycled only a few times, often about four to seven. Plastic is usually downcycled. A clear bottle becomes fibre for cloth or filling, and that product is not recycled again. Multi-layer packets are the hardest. Their layers of plastic, metal and paper are stuck together and cannot be pulled apart cheaply. Scientists counted all the plastic made up to 2015. Only about 9 kilograms in every 100 had been recycled. About 12 had been burnt. About 79 were in dumps and landfills, or loose in the environment.
- e.g. An aluminium can sold as scrap can be back in a shop as a new can within months.
- e.g. A newspaper becomes an egg tray or a cardboard box.
- e.g. A chips packet is light and keeps food crisp, but almost no factory can recycle it.
- e.g. In Scrunch and Peel, you find three materials in one juice carton.
### How waste harms soil, water, air and living beings · *defined*
**Definition:** Waste harms when it reaches a place where it blocks, poisons or is eaten. The harm depends on what the waste is made of and where it ends up.

The environment is everything around us, whether nature made it or people did. It includes soil, water, air, plants, animals, houses and streets. When waste is thrown out properly, our surroundings stay clean and healthy for every living being. On land, plastic sheets in the soil stop water soaking in and roots spreading. Cows, dogs and goats who feed at open bins swallow plastic bags with the food inside them. The bags stay in a cow's stomach, and she can starve with a full belly. In water, plastic bags block drains, and streets flood. Organic waste harms water too. When food waste is tipped into a pond, microbes multiply to eat it. They use up the oxygen dissolved in the water, and fish die. Leachate from dumps carries salts and metals into wells. In the air, burning waste adds smoke and poisons, and dumps add methane. In the sea, sunlight and waves crack plastic into microplastics. Fish, birds and turtles swallow them. Microplastics have now been found in salt, in drinking water and in human blood. Soil is harmed in other ways too. Polythene and glass do not rot, so they stay in a field for years. Some farmers spray too much chemical pesticide, which is a poison made to kill insects. Rain carries it into the soil, where it also kills earthworms and the microbes that keep soil healthy. Some factories let waste water with metals and chemicals run into drains and fields. In such soil, seeds may not sprout, or roots may take up the poison. Then the crops grow weak, or carry the poison into the food. Soil that has been harmed by things added to it is called polluted soil.
- e.g. In July 2005, very heavy rain flooded Mumbai. Drains choked with plastic bags made it worse, and the state then banned the thinnest bags.
- e.g. Vets in India have removed many kilograms of plastic bags from the stomachs of street cows.
- e.g. People who live beside dumps breathe the smoke and often drink the water from nearby wells.
- e.g. A farmer near a factory drain finds that her wheat seeds do not sprout well, and a soil test finds metals in the field.
### Keeping soil clean · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil stays clean when less waste and poison reach it, and when what does reach it can rot and feed it.

Poisoned soil takes many years to heal, so it is better to stop the harm early. Sorting and recycling waste means less plastic and glass is buried in fields. Factories should clean their waste water and not let it run into drains and rivers. Farmers in India have used safer ways for a very long time. They crush neem leaves and seeds, soak them in water and spray the liquid to keep many insects off the crops. They spread compost and cow dung manure, which feed the soil and help it hold water. These do not stop every pest, so some farmers use a little chemical spray when they must. Scientists test how well the old methods work, and many farmers are returning to them.
- e.g. A farmer sprays a neem-leaf mixture on her brinjal plants instead of a chemical pesticide.
- e.g. A village pit where cow dung and dry leaves rot into manure for the next crop.
- e.g. A factory that treats its waste water before letting it out.
### Hazardous waste and e-waste at home · *defined*
**Definition:** Hazardous waste can poison, burn or cut, even in small amounts. E-waste is any thrown-out thing that used electricity. Both are kept apart from wet and dry waste.

A used battery holds metals such as lead, cadmium or lithium. In a dump its case rusts open, and the metals leak into the leachate. A crushed lithium battery can also catch fire in a waste truck. Tube lights and CFL bulbs hold a little mercury, which is set free when they break. Old medicines, paint, insect sprays and strong cleaners are poisons. Broken glass, blades and needles cut the hands of the people who sort waste. So these go in a separate, closed box, and blades and glass are wrapped in thick paper and marked. Used sanitary pads and nappies are wrapped in paper too. E-waste is a special case. An old phone holds lead and also copper, silver and gold. A tonne of old phones holds many times more gold than a tonne of gold ore. So e-waste goes to an authorised e-waste collector, who can recover the metals safely.
- e.g. Hazardous waste includes batteries, tube lights, CFL bulbs, old medicines, paint tins, insect spray cans, blades and broken thermometers.
- e.g. E-waste includes phones, chargers, earphones, remotes, toys with circuits and old computers.
- e.g. India makes millions of tonnes of e-waste every year, the third most of any country.
### The waste hierarchy and India's waste rules · *defined*
**Definition:** The waste hierarchy puts the choices about waste in order. Refuse and reduce come first, then reuse, then recycle, then recover, and dispose comes last.

The order follows one idea. The earlier the step, the less matter has to be moved, melted or stored. Refusing a carry bag makes no waste at all. Reusing a jar needs only washing. Recycling needs collecting, sorting and melting. Recovering means getting something back from what cannot be recycled, such as compost or biogas from wet waste. Disposing in a landfill gets nothing back. India's Solid Waste Management Rules of 2016 follow this order. They ask every home to keep three kinds of waste apart: wet waste, dry waste and domestic hazardous waste. Flats and offices that make a lot of waste must compost their own wet waste. Other rules make the companies that sell things in plastic packets pay to collect that plastic back. In 2022 India also banned some plastic things that are used once, such as plastic straws and thermocol plates.
- e.g. To refuse and reduce, carry a cloth bag and a steel bottle, and buy loose vegetables.
- e.g. To reuse, keep glass jars for dal, and pass on outgrown clothes.
- e.g. To recycle, sell paper, metal, glass and clean plastic to the kabadiwala.
- e.g. To recover, compost the wet waste, or send it to a biogas plant.
- e.g. To dispose, send only what is left to a landfill.
### How two Indian cities changed · *defined*
**Definition:** A city handles its waste well when three things happen. Homes segregate the waste. Wet waste is composted or made into gas nearby. Dry waste is sorted and sold.

Indore in Madhya Pradesh once had bins overflowing on its streets. From 2016 the city removed the street bins and sent a truck to every door each day. The truck has separate boxes, so wet and dry waste never mix. Wet waste goes to compost plants and to a large biogas plant. Dry waste goes to sorting centres. Indore was ranked India's cleanest city in the national survey every year from 2017, for at least seven years in a row. Alappuzha in Kerala took another path. In 2012 the people of the village beside its dump blocked the trucks, because the dump was spoiling their water. The town then asked each home and each street to compost its own wet waste. People used pipes, pots and shared bins. Both cities began with the same step, which was keeping wet and dry waste apart at home.
- e.g. In Indore, one truck comes to every door, with separate boxes for each kind of waste.
- e.g. In Alappuzha, there are compost bins in homes and at street corners, and a small biogas plant where there is room.
- e.g. In The Last Landfill, you run a city and choose what to build first.
### Cleanliness begins with our own habits · *defined*
**Definition:** A habit is something we do again and again without thinking. A clean neighbourhood begins with the habits of each home.

Keep two bins at home and label them. One is for organic waste, such as peels and leftover food. The other is for inorganic waste, such as packets, bottles and tins. Hospitals use bins of several colours for their waste, but two bins are a good start at home. Do not litter, and do not spit on the road, because spit can spread germs. Do not burn tyres or polythene, because the smoke is poison. If you have room, compost the wet waste in a pot or a pit. Children learn from what they see at home. A child who sorts the waste can teach a younger brother or sister, and also the grown-ups. A slogan is a short sentence that is easy to remember, and it can carry these habits to the whole street.
- e.g. Two buckets by the kitchen door, one labelled 'organic' and one labelled 'inorganic'.
- e.g. A girl who shows her little brother which bin the mango peel goes in.
- e.g. A school wall painted with a slogan about keeping the street clean.
### The people who sort our waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Waste pickers and sorters collect what others throw out, sort it by material and sell it to recyclers. Most of India's recycling begins with their hands.

Estimates say that somewhere between one and a half and four million people in India earn their living from waste. Many of them are women. They walk the streets and the dumps and pick out paper, metal, glass and plastic. Skilled sorters can tell one plastic from another by its sound when squeezed, its feel and the way it bends. This work saves cities a great deal of money and landfill space. But the work is risky. Mixed waste hides broken glass, blades, needles and rotting food. In Pune, waste pickers own a cooperative called SWaCH, which has worked with the city since 2008. Its members collect segregated waste from homes for a fee, with gloves, carts and identity cards. When a home keeps its waste clean and separate, the person who sorts it is safer and earns more.
- e.g. A milk packet that is rinsed and dried can be sold. A packet full of sour milk is thrown on the dump.
- e.g. Broken glass wrapped in newspaper and marked 'glass' does not cut anyone's hand.
- e.g. In The Scrap Price Chart, you find out what each material earns for the person who collects it.
### Should a city burn its waste to make electricity? · *debated*
A waste-to-energy plant burns mixed waste in a furnace and uses the heat to make electricity. Some Indian cities have built such plants, and people disagree about them.
- e.g. Delhi has several waste-to-energy plants. People living near the one at Okhla have gone to court about its smoke.
- e.g. Sweden and Japan burn a large part of their waste in plants with strong filters.
- e.g. Indian city waste is about half wet waste, which burns poorly.
- **Engineers who support the plants:** The dumps are full and growing. Burning shrinks waste to about a tenth of its volume and makes electricity. Modern plants have filters that catch most of the smoke and poisons.
- **Residents and waste pickers who oppose them:** Indian waste is wet and burns badly. So plants add fuel, or burn paper and plastic that could be recycled. Filters are costly to run and are not always kept working. The ash is hazardous and still needs a landfill.
- **Many waste scientists:** Burning has a small place, for the dry waste that truly cannot be recycled. It should come after segregation, composting and recycling, and never replace them.
### There is no 'away': the same atoms go round · *defined*
**Definition:** Matter is not made or destroyed when waste rots, burns or is buried. Its atoms only move to a new place and join in new ways.

The Earth has had almost the same atoms for more than four billion years. Living beings pass them round. A leaf falls, decomposers eat it, and its atoms go into soil, air and new plants. In a forest there is no waste heap, because every leftover is food for someone. Human waste breaks this pattern in two ways. First, we gather huge amounts in one place, faster than decomposers can work. Second, we make materials such as plastic that decomposers have never met. So the question about any waste is where its atoms will go next, and how long that will take. The same is true beyond the Earth. Tens of thousands of pieces of old rockets and dead satellites now circle the Earth, and nobody planned where they would go.
- e.g. The carbon in a banana peel can be in the air next month, and in a spinach leaf next year.
- e.g. A glass bottle thrown in a lake is still glass for a very, very long time.
- e.g. A plastic bag does not rot. It cracks into smaller and smaller pieces of the same plastic.

## Mostly agreed
- Matter is never destroyed. When waste rots or burns, its atoms move into the air, the water or the soil. *How we know:* Chemists since Lavoisier in the 1780s have weighed things before and after burning in sealed vessels. The total mass stays the same.
- Organic waste that rots without air gives off methane, and methane traps heat in the air. *How we know:* Gas collected from pipes sunk into landfills is about half methane. Satellites can now see methane rising from large dumps, including ones in India.
- Plastic does not rot away in any time people have measured. It cracks into smaller pieces. *How we know:* Plastic things many decades old are still pulled out of the sea with their printing readable. Microplastics are found from mountain snow to the deep sea.
- Burning waste in the open sends smoke and poisonous gases into the air. *How we know:* Smoke from open fires and burning dumps holds fine specks and gases such as dioxins, which people nearby breathe in.
- Waste that is kept apart at home is much easier to compost and to recycle. *How we know:* Cities such as Indore and Alappuzha compost or recycle most of their waste, and both began with segregation at home. Mixed waste gives dirty compost and paper nobody will buy.
- Glass and metals can be recycled again and again without getting worse. *How we know:* Melted glass and melted aluminium are the same materials as before. Much of the aluminium ever made is still in use today.

## How we found out
**Lois Gibbs, a mother with no training in science, and Beverly Paigen, a cancer scientist who helped her, The waste was buried from 1942 to the early 1950s. The door-to-door survey was made in 1978, and the clean-up law was passed in 1980..** In the 1940s, a chemical company in Niagara Falls, in the United States, needed somewhere to put its waste. It tipped more than 20,000 tons of chemicals into an old, unfinished canal called Love Canal. In 1953 it covered the canal with clay and soil. People believed that waste buried under clay would stay where it was put. The land was sold for one dollar, and a school and houses were built on it. In the 1970s, after years of heavy rain and snow, the buried chemicals rose. Dark, oily liquid seeped into basements and gardens. Lois Gibbs lived there, and her young son kept falling ill. In 1978 she went from door to door and asked each family about their health. Beverly Paigen, a scientist, helped her mark the answers on a map. The illnesses lay along old stream beds that ran out from the canal, where water had carried the chemicals. Officials first dismissed the survey. Later the government moved more than 900 families away. In 1980 the United States passed a law that makes companies pay to clean up old dumps.  
*What people believed before:* That waste buried under a cover of clay stays where it is put. Rain and groundwater moved the chemicals out along old stream beds. A map of who fell ill showed where they had gone.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Burning waste gets rid of it.** (A common belief, and the reason many people burn leaves and litter; *rejected*)  
  For: The heap really does shrink to a little ash, and the smell and flies go.  
  Against: The matter has only moved. Most of it is now in the air as gases and smoke, which people breathe. Burning plastic also makes poisons. Dry leaves can be composted instead.
- **If a packet has the triangle of arrows on it, it will be recycled.** (Many shoppers; *rejected*)  
  For: The triangle looks like the recycling sign, and some marked things, such as drink bottles, are widely recycled.  
  Against: The number in the triangle only names the kind of plastic. Whether a thing is recycled depends on someone collecting it, sorting it and finding a buyer. Up to 2015, only about 9 kilograms of every 100 kilograms of plastic had been recycled.
- **A paper bag is always better for the Earth than a plastic bag.** (A common belief. Studies by Britain's Environment Agency in 2011 and others tested it; *debated*)  
  For: Paper rots, and a loose paper bag does not choke a cow or a turtle. Plastic bags last for lifetimes.  
  Against: Making a paper bag takes more water and energy than making a thin plastic bag. Trees are cut for it too. The British study found that a paper bag must be used at least 3 times to harm the climate less. A cotton bag must be used more than 100 times.
- **Food waste is natural, so it does no harm wherever it is thrown.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Food waste rots and becomes soil. In a compost pot or a field it feeds plants.  
  Against: The place matters. In a pond, rotting food uses up the oxygen and fish die. Deep in a dump, it makes methane and leachate. At an open bin it brings rats and flies.
- **Litter is the fault of the people who drop it, so they alone should fix it.** (Many companies and city notices say this. People who study waste often disagree; *debated*)  
  For: Every wrapper on the road was dropped by someone. If each person used a bin, streets would be clean.  
  Against: A person can only choose among the packets that are sold. A company that sells a snack in a packet nobody can recycle has already decided what waste there will be. India's rules now make such companies pay to collect their plastic back.
- **Plastic is the worst material, and we should stop using it.** (Some campaigners; *debated*)  
  For: Plastic lasts for lifetimes, harms animals and breaks into microplastics that are now found everywhere.  
  Against: Plastic is light, cheap and safe for blood bags, syringes, helmets and water pipes. A glass bottle is much heavier, so lorries burn more fuel to carry it. The harm comes mostly from plastic that is used once for a few minutes.

## Roots
We have learned where waste goes and why plastics must be sorted kind by kind. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Float Tank Test, a bottle piece sank and a cap piece floated in the same water. Both were clean, hard plastic, so 'plastic' is a name for many different materials. Melted together, different plastics make a weak material that few factories will buy. So plastic becomes recyclable only when someone has sorted it, kind by kind.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'plastic' as if it were one material, and 'dry waste' as if it were one heap. We also read the small triangle on a packet as a promise that it will be recycled. The triangle only names the kind of plastic. Whether a thing is recyclable depends on the work of sorting, and on who does that work.  
*Evidence:* In the Float Tank Test, pieces marked 1 sank and pieces marked 2, 4 and 5 floated. Water sorted twenty look-alike flakes in under a minute, which eyes could not do. Of all the plastic made up to 2015, only about 9 kilograms in every 100 were ever recycled.

- *who am i:* Your body is built from atoms that were once in peels, leaves and other bodies. One day those atoms will be part of someone else.
- *what is life:* Living beings pass their materials round. For almost everything that living beings make, there is a decomposer that can eat it. For most of what factories make, there is not.
- *what is this world:* The Earth keeps the same atoms. Nothing you throw out leaves the world. It only changes its place and its form.

The Earth has one stock of atoms, and everything is made from it. The same carbon atoms have been air, leaf, peel, soil and child, many times over. Plastic is made of that carbon too, taken from sea beings who died millions of years ago. So a banana peel and a milk packet are made from the same stock of atoms, joined in two different ways. Decomposers can take the peel apart. For most plastics, almost no living being can yet do the same.

- **Thich Nhat Hanh, in his teaching on roses and garbage, found in several of his books:** Look deeply at a rose and you see the garbage that the rose will become in a few days. Look deeply at garbage and you see a rose that it can become. A good gardener is not afraid of garbage, because she knows how to turn it into compost. (This is what a compost pot does. In about two months, decomposers turn peels into food for a plant. The teaching fits organic waste well. It does not fit a chips packet, which no gardener can turn into a rose.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.16:** “नासतो विद्यते भावो नाभावो विद्यते सतः। / उभयोरपि दृष्टोऽन्तस्त्वनयोस्तत्त्वदर्शिभिः।।” What is not real never comes to be, and what is real never stops being. (The Gita says this about the self, not about waste. Science found something like it about matter: the atoms in waste never stop existing. They only move and join in new ways.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 1:** “ॐ ईशा वास्यमिदँ सर्वं यत्किञ्च जगत्यां जगत् / तेन त्यक्तेन भुञ्जीथा मा गृधः कस्यस्विद्धनम्” Everything in this moving world belongs to the whole. Enjoy what you are given by letting go, and do not grab what is another's. (The first step of the waste hierarchy is to take less. The Earth's materials are shared with every other being, and what we take and throw stays in their world too.)
- Science (settled): Matter is neither made nor destroyed when things rot or burn. Antoine and Marie-Anne Lavoisier showed this by careful weighing in the 1780s.
- Science (settled): Different plastics have different densities and do not mix well when melted, so they must be sorted before recycling.
- Science (open): Some bacteria and fungi have been found that can slowly break down certain plastics. Whether they could ever deal with the world's plastic is not known.
- Other view, Many engineers and economists: Waste is a problem of engineering, and using less is not the main answer. Sweden, Japan and Singapore handle their waste with sealed landfills, clean furnaces and good machines for sorting. Poor countries need those tools more than they need lessons about buying less. With good engineering, people can keep the benefits of cheap packaging and still have clean cities.
- Practice, Hold it before you throw it (3 min): Before you throw the next thing out, hold it in both hands. Take one slow breath. Say what it is made of. Say how long you used it. Say where it will be tomorrow, and where it will be in a hundred years. Take one more slow breath. Then put it in the right bin.
- Ask: In the Float Tank Test, water could tell two plastics apart when your eyes could not. What else might look the same to us and be different?
- Ask: A thing becomes 'waste' the moment someone stops wanting it. Did the thing change at that moment, or did the person?
- Ask: Your atoms were other things before they were you. Does that change how you think about a peel or a packet?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A newspaper is waste in your home and goods in the kabadiwala's shop. So is 'waste' a kind of thing, or a decision someone makes? (hint: Think of one thing that was waste in your home last week. Who would have wanted it?)
- *deep:* If atoms are never destroyed, where are the atoms of a peel you threw out a year ago? Could some be in you? (hint: Follow the peel in Where Did the Peel Go. Some atoms went into the air, and plants take in air.)
- *deep:* Plastic is made from oil, and oil formed from sea beings who died millions of years ago. Is plastic natural? (hint: Ask what 'natural' means. Is it about where the atoms came from, or about who put them together?)

## Story: The Drum of Water
*Yazhini, Vasanthi Athai, Selvi Akka, Kathir and their sorting shed are made up. The science is real. Drink-bottle plastic sinks in water and takeaway-box plastic floats. Recycling factories really do separate plastic flakes in tanks of water. Skilled sorters really can tell plastics apart by their sound and feel. 'Athai' is Tamil for a father's sister, 'akka' means older sister and 'thambi' means younger brother.*

Every May, when school closed, Yazhini went to stay with her aunt in Coimbatore. Her aunt, Vasanthi Athai, ran a sorting shed for dry waste behind the vegetable market. Eleven women worked there. Lorries brought sacks from four thousand homes, and the women turned the sacks into neat bales that factories paid for.

Yazhini loved the shed. It smelled of dust and old paper, not of rot, because the homes on this route kept their wet waste apart.

Athai had one skill that Yazhini thought was magic. She could sort plastic with her eyes shut.

"Give me anything," Athai would say, and tie a towel over her eyes. Yazhini put a water bottle in her hands. Athai squeezed it once. It crackled, sharp and loud. "Bottle plastic. Number one." A shampoo bottle gave a dull knock. "Number two." A takeaway box bent with a soft click. "Five."

"How do you know?" Yazhini asked, every time.

"Thirty years," said Athai. "They look alike, kanna. They are not alike. Each one has its own voice. And the factory wants each one alone. Bottle plastic melted with box plastic comes out cloudy and weak. Nobody buys that."

That summer the shed had a new machine. The women had saved for two years to buy it. It was a green shredder, as tall as Yazhini, and it chopped plastic into flakes the size of a fingernail. A factory in Tiruppur paid nearly double for flakes, because flakes were easy to wash and melt.

On Thursday, a lorry was due at four o'clock to take the first load of clear bottle flake. Two hundred kilograms.

At noon Yazhini heard Athai's voice go very quiet, which was worse than shouting.

Kathir, the new helper, stood by the shredder with an empty sack in each hand. He was nineteen and had worked in the shed for six days. One sack had held clear bottles. The other had held clear takeaway boxes. He had tipped both into the machine together.

"They were both clear," he said. "I thought clear goes with clear."

On the floor lay one glittering heap of flakes. Athai picked up a handful and let it run through her fingers. Every flake was clear. Every flake was the size of a fingernail.

She squeezed one beside her ear. Then another. Yazhini watched her face. A flake was too small to crackle. It had no voice.

"Can we pick them apart?" Kathir asked.

Selvi Akka, the oldest sorter, laughed without smiling. "There are more flakes in that heap than there are people in Coimbatore. Start now, thambi. You will finish when you are a grandfather."

They tried anyway. All the women sat round the heap. Some flakes bent a little more than others, if you pressed them with a thumbnail. After half an hour, Yazhini had a pile the size of a lemon, and she was not sure of half of it. The heap looked the same as before.

"Mixed flake sells for a quarter of the price," Athai said. "If it sells." She did not look at Kathir. Kathir looked at the floor.

Yazhini's hands were grey with dust and stuck all over with flakes. She went out to the big blue drum by the tap, where everyone washed, and plunged her hands in up to the wrists.

The flakes came off her skin. She watched them, because there was nothing else to watch.

Some of them sank. They tipped and slid down through the water like tiny panes of glass, and settled on the bottom of the drum.

The others did not sink. They stayed at the top. She pushed one under with her finger. It came straight back up.

Yazhini stood very still. Then she ran inside and took a whole bottle and a whole box from the sacks by the door. She snipped a piece from each with Athai's big scissors and dropped both into the drum.

The bottle piece sank. The box piece floated.

She did it again with two more pieces, because one try can be a fluke. Sink. Float.

"Athai!" she shouted. "Athai, come and see. The water can tell them apart."

Vasanthi Athai came out, wiping her hands on her sari. She looked into the drum for a long moment. She pushed a floating flake down with one finger and watched it rise. Then she turned round.

"Selvi! Bring every drum and bucket we have. Kathir, the hose. And the big sieve from the kitchen, the one for draining rice."

They filled five drums. They poured the flakes in, a bucket at a time, and stirred with a stick so that no air bubble could hold a flake up. The box flakes rose in a pale layer. Kathir skimmed them off with the rice sieve. The bottle flakes lay on the bottom like wet sand, and the women scooped them out and spread them on sacks in the sun.

The May sun dried them in an hour. At ten minutes to four, Selvi Akka tied the last sack.

The lorry driver weighed the load and wrote in his book. "Clean flake," he said. "Cleaner than the big units send."

When he had gone, Kathir came and stood beside Yazhini at the drum. "Show me," he said. "From the beginning. Why does one sink?"

Yazhini cut two new pieces and put one in each of his hands. "Feel them. They are the same size. But the bottle piece is heavier than the same amount of water, so it sinks. The box piece is lighter than the same amount of water, so it floats. Your eyes can't see that. The water can."

Kathir dropped them in and watched.

Athai sat down on an upturned crate. "Thirty years I have listened to plastic," she said. "And all that time the answer was standing here in the washing drum."

The water in the drum grew still. One last flake, caught on the rim, slipped in. Yazhini watched to see which way it would go.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn comes when the flakes wash off Yazhini's hands and some sink while others float. Two plastics that look exactly alike are different materials with different densities. Bottle plastic is denser than water, and box plastic is less dense. Athai's ears could tell whole bottles apart, but only water could sort a heap of tiny flakes. Recycling works only when each material is alone, and density is one way to get it alone.

- Ask: Kathir thought clear plastic goes with clear plastic. What does a recycling factory need to know that colour does not tell?
- Ask: Why could the women not sort the heap of flakes by hand in time?
- Ask: Why did Yazhini drop a second pair of pieces in the drum before she called her aunt?
- Ask: The women stirred the drums with a stick. What could go wrong if they had not?
- Ask: What other mixed-up things could you separate with a bucket of water? What could you not?

### e3 · The Crush Test: how much of our dry waste is air? (25 min)
- **question:** A waste truck carries dry waste by the truckload. How much of that load is really waste, and how much is air inside bottles and boxes?
- **predict:** Guess first. When every bottle, box and carton in the bucket is flattened, how high will the same waste come: about the same, about half, or less than half? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** The same pieces of waste go into the same bucket both times. Nothing is added or taken away. Both times the waste is dropped in loosely and not pressed down from above.
- **safety:** Do not use tins or glass, because they have sharp edges when crushed. Use only rinsed, dry waste, and wash your hands with soap at the end.
- **record:** Write both heights in the table. Divide the first height by the second. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:**  | Height of waste in the bucket (cm) | Number of pieces
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the pieces of dry waste. 2. Drop them one by one into the bucket, without pressing. 3. Stand the ruler inside the bucket, on the bottom. 4. Read the height at the top of the waste and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Drop each piece in gently. Don't press. Now, how high does our waste come in the bucket?  
  *Child mode:* Count the pieces. Drop them in the bucket gently. Do not press. Measure how high the waste comes. Write the height.
  - Note: If the waste rises above the rim, take a few pieces out and count again. If it is uneven, measure to where most of the top lies.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain that every piece will be flattened and put back. 2. Ask how high the same waste will come then. 3. Ask for the reason. 4. Write the guess as a number of centimetres.  
  *Parent says:* We will flatten every piece and put it all back. How high will it come then? About the same, about half, or less? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Every piece will be flattened and put back. Look at the full bucket. Write your guess in centimetres.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tip the waste out. 2. Take the cap off each bottle, press the bottle flat under a shoe, and screw the cap back on. 3. Open the flaps of each carton and box and fold it flat. 4. Stack the cups inside each other. 5. Fold each packet flat. 6. Lay all the pieces back in the bucket, without pressing down.  
  *Parent says:* Take the cap off first, so the air can get out. Press the bottle flat, then put the cap back so it stays flat.  
  *Child mode:* Take the cap off each bottle. Press it flat under your shoe. Put the cap back on. Fold boxes flat. Stack the cups. Lay everything back in the bucket.
  - Note: A capped bottle will not flatten, because the air cannot get out. That is worth letting your child find out.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the ruler in the bucket again and read the new height. 2. Write it in the table. 3. Divide the first height by the second height. 4. Say the answer as 'so many times lower'.  
  *Parent says:* Read the new height. Now divide the first height by this one. How many times lower is the flattened waste?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the new height. Write it down. Divide the first height by the new height.
  - Note: Most families find the flattened waste is 3 to 5 times lower. With many bottles the difference is larger. With mostly paper it is smaller.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 2. Ask what was in the bucket the first time, besides waste. 3. Ask what this means for a waste truck.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both heights. Tell your grown-up what you saw and why it happened.
  - Ask: The amount of plastic and card did not change. So what left the bucket? (good: Air. The bottles and boxes were mostly full of air.; The space inside and between the pieces.)
  - Ask: What does this mean for the truck that collects dry waste? (good: A truck full of loose bottles is carrying mostly air.; If people flattened their waste, one truck could do the work of three or four.)
  - Check: In the Crush Test, flattened waste filled far less of the bucket. What changed? → **The space the waste takes up**

  - Note: In many Indian homes wet waste is about half the mass or more. Studies of Indian cities find a few hundred grams of waste for each person in a day. Your number may be lower or higher.

Agree on one change for the next week. 4. Write a short report with the chart and the plan: two or three sentences, or say it and write it for your child.

### c1 · A Dump That Does Not Leak (35 min)
- **problem:** Build a model dump that holds 100 millilitres of 'rain' for 10 minutes without one drop leaking out below. Then find a way to take the dirty water out.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the bottle and covers the cut edge with tape. Use only fresh, cooled tea leaves. Put them in the wet waste afterwards and wash your hands with soap.
- **your turn:** Now design your own liner. Lay out the layers first and say what each one does. Build it, pour the rain and time 10 minutes. Then make one pinhole in your plastic layer and test again. Does your design still hold?
- **constraints:** The 'waste' is 2 tablespoons of today's used tea leaves, cooled. Add a pinch of turmeric and a pinch of salt.; The dump is the top half of a plastic bottle, turned upside down with the cap off. It stands in a glass tumbler.; The liner may use newspaper, cloth, a piece of carry bag, or kneaded atta dough, which stands for clay.; Pour the 100 millilitres slowly, over about one minute.
- **general:** Line the funnel with a piece of plastic carry bag, pressed well into the neck.; Use two layers, so that a hole in one is covered by the other.; Press atta dough into the neck as a plug of 'clay'.
- **creative:** Put dough under the plastic sheet, so that a pinhole in the plastic still does not leak. Real landfills use clay under plastic for this reason.; Slope the liner so that the dirty water runs to one low corner. Lift it out with a straw, holding a finger over the top end.; Add a 'roof' over the waste so that less rain gets in. Real landfills are covered with soil and a cap when they are full.
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the bottle top upside down in a tumbler, with the cap off. 2. Mix the tea leaves, turmeric and salt and put them in the funnel. 3. Ask what will come out when it rains on this dump, and why. 4. Pour 100 millilitres of water slowly over the waste. 5. Look at the water in the tumbler.  
  *Parent says:* This is a dump with nothing under it. If rain falls on it, what will come out at the bottom? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put the tea leaf 'waste' in the funnel. Pour the water slowly. Look in the tumbler.
  - Note: Yellow-brown water runs into the tumbler. It carries colour from the tea and turmeric, and salt that cannot be seen. This is leachate. Do not taste it.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty and rinse the funnel and the tumbler. 2. Ask your child to lay out the layers of a liner on the table and say what each one is for. 3. Build the liner inside the funnel. 4. Put fresh tea leaf 'waste' on top of the liner.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are the engineer. What will you put under the waste, so that no dirty water reaches the tumbler? Why that?  
  *Child mode:* Rinse the funnel. Lay out your liner layers and say what each one does. Build the liner inside the funnel. Put new 'waste' on top.
  - Note: Newspaper and cloth soak through within a minute. Plastic holds unless there is a gap at its edge. Dough holds well if it is pressed against the sides all the way round.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the funnel in a clean, dry tumbler. 2. Pour 100 millilitres of water slowly over the waste. 3. Time 10 minutes and count any drops that fall. 4. If it leaks, find where, mend the design and test again.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the rain slowly. Now we wait ten minutes and count every drop. If it leaks, where is the water getting through?  
  *Child mode:* Stand the funnel in a dry tumbler. Pour the rain slowly. Wait 10 minutes. Count the drops. If it leaks, mend it and test again.
  - Ask: If your liner leaked, where did the water get through? (good: At the edge, where the plastic did not reach the side.; Through the newspaper, because paper soaks up water.)
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look inside the funnel. The dirty water is now sitting in the dump. 2. Ask what should be done with it. 3. Try to take it out with a straw or a spoon, without moving the waste. 4. Compare your model with a real landfill.  
  *Parent says:* If your liner held, where is the dirty water now? It has not gone. What would you do with it in a real landfill?  
  *Child mode:* Look inside the funnel. Try to take the dirty water out with a straw.
  - Note: A real landfill has pipes at the bottom that carry the leachate to a tank, where it is cleaned. A liner without pipes fills up like a bathtub and overflows.
  - Check: A landfill has a perfect liner but no pipes. It rains for years. What happens to the leachate? → **It collects until it overflows the liner**
### c2 · The One-Material Pack: keep a snack crisp for two days (30 min, 3 days)
- **problem:** Design and build a pack that keeps four pieces of roasted papad crisp for two days. Every part of the pack must be a single material that rots, can be reused or can be recycled.

## Worksheets
### Where Waste Goes (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) What makes a piece of waste 'organic'?  
   Options: It is wet / It is brown / It smells bad / It came from a living being  
   **Answer:** It came from a living being. *Why:* Organic waste was made by a plant or an animal, so decomposers can eat it. Choosing 'wet' is a common slip. Paper and cotton are dry and still organic.
2. (truefalse) True or false: newspaper is organic waste, although it is dry.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Paper is made from trees, so decomposers can eat it. A strong answer adds that dry paper still goes in the dry waste, to be recycled first.
3. (fill) The dirty liquid that drains out of a heap of waste is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['leachate']. *Why:* Rain soaks through the waste and carries dissolved matter with it. Under an open dump, leachate can reach the groundwater.
4. (sort) Sort these into organic waste and inorganic waste.  
   **Answer:** tea leaves → Organic; glass jar → Inorganic; cotton rag → Organic; steel tin → Inorganic; dry leaves → Organic; aluminium foil → Inorganic; newspaper → Organic; broken tile → Inorganic. *Why:* Tea leaves, cotton, dry leaves and newspaper were made by plants. Glass, steel, foil and tile were made from sand, ore and clay.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Segregation → keeping kinds of waste apart; Compost → what decomposers leave when they have air; Methane → a gas made when waste rots without air; Landfill → a lined place built for burying waste; Recycling → making new things from old material. *Why:* These five words are needed to talk about where waste goes. A strong answer could give an example of each.
6. (mcq) A piece of drink bottle sinks in water. A piece of its cap floats. Why?  
   Options: They are two different plastics / The cap is coloured / The bottle piece is bigger / The cap has tiny holes  
   **Answer:** They are two different plastics. *Why:* The bottle and the cap are different materials. Bottle plastic is denser than water, and cap plastic is less dense. Colour and size make no difference to floating.
7. (write) In the Float Tank Test, water sorted 20 mixed flakes. Explain how, using the word 'density'.  
   **Answer:** The bottle flakes are denser than water, so they sank. The box flakes are less dense than water, so they floated. I skimmed the floating flakes off the top, and the two plastics were apart.. *Why:* A strong answer says which plastic is denser than water and which is less dense, and how that separated them.
8. (draw) Draw a lined landfill. Show the liner, the waste, the soil cover and a pipe for leachate.  
   **Answer:** A pit with a liner along the bottom and sides, layers of waste inside and soil on top. A pipe at the lowest point leads to a tank.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the liner under all the waste and the pipe at the lowest point, where leachate collects.
9. (odd) Three of these can be melted and remade again and again. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Glass bottle / Aluminium can / Chips packet / Steel tin  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Glass, aluminium and steel are single materials that can be remelted without end. A chips packet is plastic and metal stuck together, so no factory can remelt it.
10. (truefalse) True or false: burning a heap of dry leaves destroys the matter in them.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The matter leaves as carbon dioxide, water vapour and smoke, and a little stays as ash. No atom is destroyed.
11. (order) Put the waste hierarchy in order, with the best choice first.  
   **Answer:** ['Refuse or reduce', 'Reuse', 'Recycle', 'Recover, as compost or biogas', 'Dispose in a landfill']. *Why:* The earlier the step, the less matter has to be moved, melted or stored. Refusing makes no waste at all.
12. (fill) The number in the small triangle on a plastic thing is called the resin ____.  
   **Answer:** ['code']. *Why:* The resin code names the kind of plastic, from 1 to 7. It is not a promise that the thing will be recycled.
13. (fill) Everything around us, made by nature or by people, is called our ____.  
   **Answer:** ['environment']. *Why:* The environment includes soil, water, air, plants, animals, houses and streets.
14. (perspective) A family has a big heap of dry leaves swept from the yard. Two neighbours disagree about it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that burning moves the leaves into the air as smoke and gases, which everyone breathes. It also takes the other side seriously: a loose heap can shelter pests, so the leaves should go in a covered compost pit or bin.. *Why:* Both neighbours want a clean, safe yard. The science shows where the leaves go in each plan.
15. (mcq) Which of these is hazardous waste?  
   Options: A used battery / A banana peel / A cardboard box / A glass jar  
   **Answer:** A used battery. *Why:* A battery holds metals that can leak and poison soil and water. The other three are ordinary wet or dry waste.
16. (write) Your family will keep two bins at home. Write the label for each bin, and name three things that go in each. Where will you keep them?  
   **Answer:** Organic: peels, leftover food, a banana leaf. Inorganic: a chips packet, a steel tin, a glass bottle. I will keep them by the kitchen door, where everyone passes.. *Why:* A strong answer puts things that once lived in one bin and things that never rot in the other, and chooses a place that is easy to use.
17. (write) Rain falls on an open dump for many years. Explain how this can spoil a well nearby.  
   **Answer:** Rainwater soaks down through the waste and picks up salts, rotting matter and metals. This leachate seeps into the soil under the dump and reaches the groundwater. The well draws from that groundwater.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the water step by step: rain, through the waste, into the soil, into the groundwater, into the well.
### Sorting, Rotting and Leaking (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Deep inside a dump there is no air. Which gas does rotting food make there?  
   Options: Methane / Oxygen / Steam / Nitrogen  
   **Answer:** Methane. *Why:* Without air, a different set of microbes takes over, and they give off methane. Methane burns and traps heat in the air.
2. (write) A chips packet springs open after you scrunch it. Kitchen foil stays in a ball. What does this tell you?  
   **Answer:** The chips packet is mostly plastic film, which springs back. Kitchen foil is metal, which stays bent. So the silver inside the packet is only a very thin coat of metal on plastic.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the result as evidence about the material, and does not judge by the silver colour.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a triangle of arrows on a packet means the packet will be recycled.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The number inside only names the kind of plastic. Recycling happens only if someone collects, sorts and buys it.
4. (fill) A piece of plastic smaller than 5 millimetres is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['microplastic']. *Why:* Big plastic things crack into microplastics. They are too small to pick up, and they do not rot.
5. (sort) Sort these by what should be done with each.  
   **Answer:** vegetable peels → Compost; newspapers → Sell for recycling; used batteries → Hazard box; tea leaves → Compost; aluminium cans → Sell for recycling; old medicines → Hazard box; glass jars → Sell for recycling; a broken tube light → Hazard box; dry flowers → Compost. *Why:* Organic kitchen waste can be composted. Clean single materials can be sold. Things that poison or cut are kept apart in a closed box.
6. (mcq) Flattened dry waste filled a quarter of the bucket it had filled before. What left the bucket?  
   Options: Plastic / Mass / Air / Cardboard  
   **Answer:** Air. *Why:* Bottles and boxes are mostly air inside. Flattening pushes the air out. The plastic, the card and their mass all stay.
7. (draw) Draw one banana peel in a compost pot. Add arrows to show where its matter goes.  
   **Answer:** A peel in a pot with decomposers on it. One arrow goes up to the air, labelled carbon dioxide and water vapour. One arrow goes down, labelled compost.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows matter going two ways: into the air as gases and into the soil as compost. It shows decomposers doing the work.
8. (perspective) A city's dump is full. The council must decide what to do with the waste. Three people speak.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer notes that about half of Indian city waste is wet, which composts well and burns poorly. It may still allow burning for the dry waste that nobody can recycle. It takes the engineer's point seriously: the dump is full and something must be done now.. *Why:* This is a real argument in Indian cities. Each speaker has part of the truth, and the evidence about wet waste matters most.
9. (order) Food waste is tipped into a small pond. Put what follows in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Microbes multiply to eat the food', 'The microbes use up the oxygen in the water', 'Fish cannot get enough oxygen', 'Many fish die']. *Why:* Rotting uses oxygen. In a pond there is only a little oxygen dissolved in the water, and the fish need the same oxygen.
10. (write) Leftover food is organic and rots. Explain why it can still do harm in a dump.  
   **Answer:** Deep in a dump there is no air. The food rots slowly there and gives off methane, which can start fires and traps heat in the air. The liquid from the rotting food also joins the leachate.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the place decides the harm. The same food in a compost pot with air would do good.
11. (match) Match each kind of waste to the harm it can do.  
   **Answer:** Plastic bags in a street drain → streets flood in heavy rain; Food waste in a pond → fish die for lack of oxygen; A battery in an open dump → metals leak into the groundwater; Plastic burnt in a street fire → poisonous smoke in the air; A plastic bag at an open bin → a cow swallows it with the food. *Why:* Each harm comes from what the waste is made of and where it ends up. A strong answer could say how each could be prevented.
12. (match) Match each action to how it keeps soil clean.  
   **Answer:** Neem-leaf spray on crops → keeps insects off without a strong poison; Compost and cow dung manure → feeds the soil with rotted waste; Sorting and recycling waste → less plastic and glass buried in fields; A factory cleans its waste water → metals and chemicals stay out of fields. *Why:* Each action stops a different kind of harm to soil before it starts.
13. (mcq) Paper can be recycled about five to seven times. Why not for ever?  
   Options: Its fibres get shorter each time / The ink runs out / Paper turns into plastic / Factories refuse old paper  
   **Answer:** Its fibres get shorter each time. *Why:* Each time paper is pulped, its plant fibres break and get shorter. Short fibres cannot hold a sheet together. Glass and metal do not have this limit.
14. (odd) Three of these are multi-layer packaging. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Juice carton / Chips packet / Glass jar / Shampoo sachet  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A glass jar is one material. The carton, the chips packet and the sachet are thin layers of different materials stuck together.
15. (label) Label the four parts of the landfill: the liner, the leachate pipe, the gas pipe and the soil cover.  
   **Answer:** ['Liner: the layer of clay and plastic under all the waste', 'Leachate pipe: at the lowest point of the liner', 'Gas pipe: standing up through the waste', 'Soil cover: on top of the waste']. *Why:* Each part stops one harm. The liner and its pipe protect the groundwater. The gas pipe takes the methane out. The cover keeps rain, flies and birds off.
16. (write) Four people made 4,200 grams of wet waste in 7 days. Find the amount for each person in a day.  
   **Answer:** 150 grams. First find one day: 4,200 ÷ 7 = 600 grams. Then share it among 4 people: 600 ÷ 4 = 150 grams.. *Why:* A strong answer shows both steps: divide by the days, then by the people.
17. (order) Put the steps of a village biogas plant in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Cow dung and dry leaves are put into the tank', 'Microbes eat the waste with no air', 'Gas collects in the tank', 'The family cooks on the gas', 'The leftover manure goes to the field']. *Why:* The microbes work inside a closed tank, and both the gas and the manure are used.
### No Such Place as Away (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) People say waste is 'thrown away'. Use what you know about atoms to explain why there is no 'away'.  
   **Answer:** Atoms are not destroyed when waste rots, burns or is buried. They only move to the air, the water or the soil, or stay where they are. So every bit of waste is still somewhere on the Earth.. *Why:* A strong answer names at least two places where the matter goes, and says that no atom stops existing.
2. (perspective) A snack company sells chips in a packet that nobody can recycle. Three people speak about the empty packets.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the finding that a multi-layer packet cannot be separated or remelted. It notes that the buyer did not choose the material. It is fair to the manager: the packet really does save food and fuel.. *Why:* India's rules now make companies collect their plastic back, but people still argue about how well this works.
3. (mcq) A solid piece of plastic sinks in plain water and floats in strong salt water. What does this show?  
   Options: It is denser than plain water only / It is denser than both liquids / It is less dense than both / Salt makes plastic lighter  
   **Answer:** It is denser than plain water only. *Why:* Its density lies between the two. It is a little denser than plain water and less dense than salt water. The salt changes the water, not the plastic.
4. (write) A sack holds chopped bottle bits and cap bits, all mixed. Design a way to separate them.  
   **Answer:** Tip the bits into a tank of water and stir. The bottle bits are denser than water and sink. The cap bits are less dense and float. Skim off the floating bits and scoop out the sunk ones.. *Why:* A strong answer uses density and says which bits go where. It mentions stirring, so that air bubbles do not hold bits up.
5. (truefalse) True or false: a well-lined landfill makes waste harmless for ever.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A landfill only stores waste. Its leachate and gas must be piped out and treated for many years after it is full. Liners can also fail.
6. (mcq) About half of Indian city waste is wet. Why is that a problem for a plant that burns waste?  
   Options: Wet waste burns poorly / Wet waste is too heavy to lift / Wet waste has no atoms / Wet waste is hazardous  
   **Answer:** Wet waste burns poorly. *Why:* Much of the heat goes into boiling off the water. So the plant needs extra fuel, or dry waste that could have been recycled. Wet waste composts well instead.
7. (write) A plastic bottle and a steel tumbler both last hundreds of years. Why is only the bottle a waste problem?  
   **Answer:** The tumbler is used for all the years it lasts, so it does not become waste. The bottle is used for a day and then lies as waste for hundreds of years. The problem is the gap between the use time and the lasting time.. *Why:* A strong answer compares use time with lasting time for both things, and does not blame lasting alone.
8. (fill) Recycling a material into a lower-quality thing that is not recycled again is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['downcycling']. *Why:* A clear bottle made into polyester fibre is downcycled. The fibre is not made back into bottles.
9. (sort) Sort each thing by where most of its matter is one year later.  
   **Answer:** a peel burnt in a street fire → In the air; finished compost dug into a pot → In the soil; a glass bottle in a lake → Still the same thing; methane from a dump → In the air; a plastic spoon in a field → Still the same thing; ash from burnt leaves → In the soil. *Why:* Burning and rotting without air send matter into the air. Compost and ash join the soil. Glass and plastic stay as they are.
10. (mcq) A paper bag takes more water and energy to make than a plastic bag. When is paper better?  
   Options: Always, whatever is done with it / Never / When it is reused several times / Only when it is white  
   **Answer:** When it is reused several times. *Why:* A study in Britain found that a paper bag must be used 3 times to harm the climate less. Paper also rots if it is littered, which plastic does not. So the answer depends on how the bag is used.
11. (order) A used battery is thrown on an open dump. Put what follows in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The steel case rusts open', 'Metals leak out of the battery', 'Rain washes the metals down through the waste', 'The metals reach the groundwater', 'People draw the water from a well']. *Why:* Hazardous waste harms through a chain of small steps. Keeping the battery out of the dump breaks the chain at the start.
12. (write) Plastic is made from oil, which formed from ancient sea beings. Is plastic waste organic or inorganic? Argue for one.  
   **Answer:** Either answer can be strong. Organic: its atoms came from living beings, and chemists call carbon chains organic. Inorganic: no decomposer can eat it, so it behaves like glass and metal in a dump. The better answer says which meaning of the word it is using.. *Why:* A strong answer chooses one, gives a reason from the science, and says what the other side has right.
13. (match) Match each rule or action in India to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Three bins at home → keeps wet, dry and hazardous waste apart; The 2022 ban on some single-use plastic → stops plastic straws and thermocol plates; Companies must collect their packets back → puts the cost on those who chose the packet; Large housing societies compost on site → keeps wet waste out of the dump; Authorised e-waste collectors → recover metals safely from old phones. *Why:* Each rule acts at a different step. A strong answer could say which step of the waste hierarchy each one belongs to.
14. (odd) Three of these are near the top of the waste hierarchy. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Carrying a cloth bag / Refilling a steel bottle / Mending a torn school bag / Burying waste in a landfill  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The first three reduce or reuse, so no waste is made. Burying is disposal, the last step, which gets nothing back.
15. (label) Label where a banana peel's matter goes in each place: compost pot, deep dump, fire and cow.  
   **Answer:** ['Compost pot: into the soil as compost, and into the air', 'Deep dump: into the air as methane, and into leachate', 'Street fire: into the air as gases and smoke', 'Cow: into her body, her breath and her dung']. *Why:* In none of the four does any matter stop existing. A strong answer names a place for the matter in every box.
16. (write) A good slogan is short, true and easy to remember. Write a slogan against burning polythene, and give one fact that makes it true.  
   **Answer:** For example: 'Burn leaves into compost, not plastic into smoke.' It is true because burning polythene gives off poisonous smoke.. *Why:* A strong answer is short and kind to read, and the fact behind it is real.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which pair is organic waste?  
   Options: A glass jar and a steel tin / A milk packet and a bottle / A newspaper and a cotton rag / Foil and a broken tile  
   **Answer:** A newspaper and a cotton rag. *Why:* Newspaper is made from trees and cotton from a plant, so decomposers can eat both. They are dry, but organic does not mean wet.
2. (mcq) Why can decomposers eat a peel but not a plastic bag?  
   Options: The peel is softer / They have enzymes for what living beings make / The bag is too big / Plastic is too cold  
   **Answer:** They have enzymes for what living beings make. *Why:* Decomposers have had hundreds of millions of years to fit their enzymes to plants and animals. Plastic is new, and its long chains fit almost no enzyme. Softness makes no difference. A hard coconut shell rots.
3. (mcq) Peels rot in a compost pot with plenty of air. What do they become?  
   Options: Methane and leachate / Ash and smoke / Small pieces of plastic / Compost, carbon dioxide and water  
   **Answer:** Compost, carbon dioxide and water. *Why:* With air, decomposers turn peels into compost, carbon dioxide and water. Methane and leachate come from rotting without air, as in a dump.
4. (mcq) A sheet of paper weighs 5 grams. Its ash weighs far less. Where is the rest?  
   Options: In the air, as gases and smoke / It was destroyed by the fire / Hidden inside the ash / It turned into light only  
   **Answer:** In the air, as gases and smoke. *Why:* The atoms joined with oxygen and left as carbon dioxide, water vapour and smoke. Choosing 'destroyed' means thinking that matter we cannot see has stopped existing.
5. (mcq) Why can a big dump catch fire by itself?  
   Options: Plastic gets hot in the rain / Glass rots and makes heat / Buried food waste makes methane, which burns / Leachate is a fuel  
   **Answer:** Buried food waste makes methane, which burns. *Why:* Deep in a dump there is no air, so rotting food gives off methane. Methane burns easily and can smoulder inside the heap for weeks.
6. (mcq) Two plastic flakes look the same. In water, one sinks and one floats. What can you say for sure?  
   Options: One is older than the other / One is dirtier than the other / They are the same plastic / They are different materials  
   **Answer:** They are different materials. *Why:* The same water treated them differently, so their densities differ, and so they are different materials. Age and dirt do not change whether a plastic floats.
7. (mcq) Why does a recycling factory want one kind of plastic at a time?  
   Options: Mixed plastics are heavier / One kind looks prettier / Mixed plastics melt into a weak material / Mixed plastics rot in the machine  
   **Answer:** Mixed plastics melt into a weak material. *Why:* Different plastics melt at different temperatures and do not mix well. The result is cloudy and weak, and few buyers want it.
8. (mcq) A juice carton is paper, plastic and metal foil stuck together. Why is it hard to recycle?  
   Options: The layers cannot be pulled apart cheaply / Paper cannot be recycled / Metal cannot be melted / It is too heavy to carry  
   **Answer:** The layers cannot be pulled apart cheaply. *Why:* Each of the three materials can be recycled alone. Stuck together in thin layers, they need special machines to separate, and few places have them.
9. (mcq) Where should a used battery go?  
   Options: In the wet waste / In a separate closed box for hazardous waste / In the dry bag with paper / Down the drain  
   **Answer:** In a separate closed box for hazardous waste. *Why:* A battery holds metals that leak into soil and water. It is kept apart and given to a collection point. In the dry bag it can be crushed in the truck.
10. (mcq) Four people made 14 kilograms of waste in 7 days. How much is that for each person in a day?  
   Options: 2 kilograms / 500 grams / 3.5 kilograms / 200 grams  
   **Answer:** 500 grams. *Why:* One day: 14 ÷ 7 = 2 kilograms. One person: 2 kilograms ÷ 4 = 500 grams. Choosing 2 kilograms stops after the first step.
11. (mcq) Which choice is highest on the waste hierarchy?  
   Options: Recycling a plastic bottle / Burning a plastic bottle / Burying a plastic bottle / Carrying water in a steel bottle  
   **Answer:** Carrying water in a steel bottle. *Why:* Carrying a steel bottle means no plastic bottle is made or thrown at all. Reducing comes before recycling, because nothing has to be collected or melted.
12. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still not know about plastic?  
   Options: Whether it floats / What it is made from / How long it really lasts in nature / Whether it can melt  
   **Answer:** How long it really lasts in nature. *Why:* Plastic has been in wide use for less than a hundred years, so nobody has watched it for longer. The figures of hundreds of years are worked out from short tests.
13. (mcq) A farmer's field is next to a factory drain. Her wheat grows weak. Which could be the reason?  
   Options: The soil has too many earthworms / The wheat is too well watered / Metals and chemicals from the drain have got into the soil  
   **Answer:** Metals and chemicals from the drain have got into the soil. *Why:* Poisons in the soil can stop seeds sprouting, or be taken up by the roots. Earthworms help soil, and they die when soil is poisoned.
14. (mcq) Which action keeps a field's soil cleaner?  
   Options: Burning polythene bags at the edge of the field / Burying plastic bags in the soil / Spraying a neem-leaf mixture and spreading compost  
   **Answer:** Spraying a neem-leaf mixture and spreading compost. *Why:* Neem spray and compost use things that rot, and they feed the soil. Burning polythene makes poison smoke, and buried plastic stays for years.
15. (mcq) Which of these is part of the environment?  
   Options: A pond, a road and a tulsi plant / Only forests and rivers / Only things that people have made  
   **Answer:** A pond, a road and a tulsi plant. *Why:* The environment is everything around us, whether nature made it or people did. A pond and a tulsi plant came from nature, and the road was made by people.
16. (mcq) A home has two labelled bins, organic and inorganic. Where do a mango peel and a dry chips packet go?  
   Options: The peel in the organic bin, the packet in the inorganic bin / Both in the organic bin / The peel in the inorganic bin, the packet in the organic bin  
   **Answer:** The peel in the organic bin, the packet in the inorganic bin. *Why:* A peel came from a plant and decomposers can eat it. A chips packet is plastic and does not rot.
17. (mcq) Which of these can go into a village biogas plant?  
   Options: Glass bottles and steel tins / Plastic bags and old batteries / Cow dung, dry leaves and crop leftovers  
   **Answer:** Cow dung, dry leaves and crop leftovers. *Why:* Microbes can eat dung, leaves and crop leftovers, and they make gas. They cannot eat glass, steel or plastic, and batteries poison the tank.

## Applications
- Plastic recycling plants wash chopped bottles in long tanks of water. The bottle flakes sink, and the cap and label flakes float off the top.
- Scrap yards pull steel out of mixed metal with a large magnet. Aluminium is not magnetic, so it stays behind and is sold apart.
- Biogas plants in villages and cities keep wet waste in closed tanks. They collect the methane for cooking, for electricity and for buses.
- Engineers build landfills with a liner of clay and plastic, and with pipes. The pipes take out the leachate and the methane to be treated.
- Large housing societies in Indian cities compost their own wet waste, and use the compost in their gardens.
- Authorised e-waste recyclers take old phones apart and recover copper, silver and gold from them.
- Some shops sell oil, grains and soap loose, into the buyer's own container. No packet is made at all.

## Open questions
- **How long does plastic really last in nature?** Plastic has been in wide use for less than a hundred years, so nobody has watched it for longer. The figures of hundreds of years come from short tests, and plastics differ.
- **Can microbes or enzymes be found, or made, that eat plastic fast enough to matter?** In 2016 scientists in Japan found a bacterium that slowly eats the plastic of drink bottles. Others are building faster enzymes. Nobody knows yet whether this can work for whole cities, or for other plastics.
- **What do microplastics do inside a human body?** Microplastics have been found in human blood and other parts of the body. Scientists do not yet know how much harm they do, because the studies are new and hard to do.
- **What should be done with multi-layer packets?** They save food and fuel, but they cannot be recycled cheaply. Engineers are testing packets made of one plastic only, and ways to dissolve the layers apart. No answer is in wide use yet.
- **Who will clean up the waste that is already circling the Earth?** Tens of thousands of pieces of old rockets and dead satellites orbit the Earth. A few test missions have tried to catch one. Nobody has agreed who should pay.

## Connected maths
- The table shows a family's wet waste on 7 days. What is the total, and what is the average for one day? **The total is 5,600 grams. The average is 800 grams a day.**. Add the seven days: 750 + 900 + 650 + 800 + 1,100 + 700 + 700 = 5,600 grams. Divide by 7 days: 5,600 ÷ 7 = 800 grams. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L5`)
- The bar chart shows one family's waste in a week, in grams. What percentage of the total is wet waste? **56 percent.**. Add the five kinds: 5,600 + 1,400 + 700 + 300 + 2,000 = 10,000 grams. Wet waste is 5,600 out of 10,000. Divide both by 100: 56 out of 100, which is 56 percent. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L5`)
- A city has 1,000,000 people. Each person makes 400 grams of waste a day. How many tonnes is that in a day? A truck carries 8 tonnes. How many truckloads? **400 tonnes a day, which is 50 truckloads.**. 1,000,000 × 400 grams = 400,000,000 grams. There are 1,000 grams in a kilogram, so that is 400,000 kilograms. There are 1,000 kilograms in a tonne, so that is 400 tonnes. 400 ÷ 8 = 50 truckloads. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- In the Crush Test, loose waste came up to 28 cm in the bucket. Flattened, it came up to 7 cm. A truck carries the loose dry waste of 500 homes. How many homes' flattened waste could it carry? **2,000 homes.**. 28 ÷ 7 = 4, so the flattened waste takes a quarter of the space. The truck can hold 4 times as much waste: 500 × 4 = 2,000 homes. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In Breaks Up, Not Away, the pieces doubled each round: 2, 4, 8. How many pieces are there after 10 rounds? The square was 16 cm by 16 cm. What is the area of each piece then? **1,024 pieces, each of a quarter of a square centimetre.**. Keep doubling: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024. The square's area is 16 × 16 = 256 square centimetres. 256 ÷ 1,024 = 0.25 square centimetres. The total area is still 256. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A scrap dealer pays 12 rupees for 1 kg of newspaper and 100 rupees for 1 kg of aluminium. Which earns more: 3 kg of newspaper or half a kilogram of aluminium cans? **The aluminium cans, by 14 rupees.**. Newspaper: 3 × 12 = 36 rupees. Aluminium: half of 100 = 50 rupees. 50 − 36 = 14 rupees more for the cans, though they weigh one sixth as much. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L5`)
- A peel is used for 1 day and lasts about 30 days as waste. A plastic bottle is used for 1 day and lasts about 450 years. How many times longer than the peel does the bottle last? **5,475 times longer.**. Change the years to days: 450 × 365 = 164,250 days. Divide by the peel's 30 days: 164,250 ÷ 30 = 5,475. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
