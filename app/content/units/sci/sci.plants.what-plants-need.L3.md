# What plants need, and how to test it fairly
`sci.plants.what-plants-need.L3`

**Summary.** Moong grown in a dark cupboard grows taller than moong on a windowsill. But the plants are pale and thin, and they fall over. So what does a plant really need? They need light, water, air, warmth and minerals from the soil, each in the right amount. Your child finds this out with fair tests, which change only one thing at a time. They grow moong in the light and in the dark, and measure them with a ruler every few days. They can also test too little and too much water, and cold against warmth.

**Intro.** *Have you seen moong sprouts grown in a closed box, or a potato that has sat in a dark kitchen basket? Their shoots are white or pale yellow, not green. Why?* Sprouts grown in a closed tiffin, and potato shoots in a dark basket, come out long and pale. They are alive, and they grow fast. The only thing they were missing was light. So what does a plant really need? Is it light, water, warmth or soil? And how could you find out for sure, without guessing? In this unit you find out with fair tests, changing one thing at a time.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a plant needs to grow well: light, water, air, warmth and minerals, each in the right amount.
1. I can plan and run a fair test of light, water or warmth. I change only one thing and keep everything else the same.
2. I can measure a plant's height in centimetres and millimetres, at the same time each day. I can write it in a results table.
3. I can show my results in a bar chart and write a conclusion: what I think, what I saw, and why.
4. I can read a plant's clues, like tall and pale, wilting, or yellow with soggy soil, and say which need is missing.
5. I can explain that more is not always better. Too much water, too much heat or too much cold harms a plant, and each kind of plant has their own 'just right'.
6. I can give big examples. Plants sense light and warmth with special molecules. People grow plants in space. Scientists still have open questions about how plants sense warmth.

## Terms
- **Need**: Something a living thing must have to stay alive and healthy. *e.g.* You need food, water and air. A plant needs light, water, air, warmth and minerals.
- **Seedling**: A very young plant that has just grown out of their seed. *e.g.* A moong seedling has a thin stem and two small round leaves.
- **Growth**: Getting bigger. A plant grows taller, grows more leaves and grows longer roots. *e.g.* A moong seedling can grow from 1 centimetre to 10 centimetres tall in a week.
- **Light**: What lets us see. For a plant, light is also the energy they use to make their own food. *e.g.* Sunlight through a window. A plant on a windowsill leans towards it.
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold something is. We measure it in degrees Celsius, written °C. *e.g.* A cool room in winter might be 18 °C. A hot afternoon in May might be 40 °C.
- **Thermometer**: A tool that measures temperature. A coloured liquid or a number shows how hot it is. *e.g.* A room thermometer on the wall shows 28 °C.
- **Minerals (nutrients)**: Substances in soil that a plant needs in tiny amounts, such as nitrogen and iron. They dissolve in water, and the roots take them in with the water. *e.g.* Compost and cow dung add minerals to soil.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then you know what caused the result. *e.g.* To test light, put two cups of the same seedlings in the light and the dark, and give both the same water.
- **The thing we change**: In a fair test, the one thing you choose to change on purpose. Scientists call it the variable. *e.g.* In the light test, the thing we change is light: one cup gets light, one does not.
- **The thing we measure**: In a fair test, what you look at or measure to see what happened. *e.g.* The height of the seedlings, in centimetres.
- **Prediction**: A careful guess about what will happen, with a reason, made before you test. *e.g.* I predict the dark seedlings will be smaller, because plants need light.
- **Centimetre and millimetre**: Units for measuring length. A centimetre is about the width of your fingernail. A millimetre is much smaller: 10 millimetres make 1 centimetre. *e.g.* A moong seedling 6 centimetres and 5 millimetres tall is 65 millimetres tall.
- **Results table**: A neat table where you write what you measured, so nothing is forgotten. *e.g.* A table with a row for each cup and a column for each day.
- **Bar chart**: A picture of numbers, with one bar for each thing. The taller the bar, the bigger the number. *e.g.* One bar for the light cup and one for the dark cup, as tall as their seedlings.
- **Conclusion**: What you found out, said in a sentence or two. It uses what you saw as proof. *e.g.* We found that seedlings in the dark grew tall, pale and floppy. This shows plants need light to grow well.
- **Wilt**: When a plant droops and goes floppy, usually because they do not have enough water. *e.g.* A tulsi plant left without water on a hot day wilts by the evening.
- **Waterlogged**: When soil is so soaked that there is no air left in it. Roots in waterlogged soil cannot breathe. *e.g.* A pot standing in a full dish of water for a week becomes waterlogged.
- **Leggy (etiolated)**: Tall, thin, pale and floppy, because a plant grew without enough light. Scientists call this etiolated. *e.g.* Moong grown in a dark cupboard comes out leggy and yellow.
- **Chlorophyll**: The green substance in leaves that catches light. A plant makes it only when they get light. *e.g.* Leaves are green because of chlorophyll. Sprouts grown in a closed box are pale, because they have not made it yet.
- **Just right range**: The temperatures, or amounts of water or light, in which a kind of plant grows best. Below it or above it, the plant grows slowly or is harmed. *e.g.* Moong grows well between about 25 °C and 35 °C. Wheat likes it cooler.
- **Photoreceptor**: A special molecule inside a plant that notices light. One kind, called phytochrome, also notices warmth. *e.g.* Photoreceptors tell a seedling under a box that it is dark.
- **Enzyme**: A tiny helper molecule inside living things that makes changes happen faster. Enzymes work faster when warm and slower when cold. *e.g.* Enzymes in a sprout help them use their stored food to grow.
- **Crescograph**: A machine invented by Jagadish Chandra Bose in India. It made a plant's growth look thousands of times bigger, so you could see the plant grow minute by minute. *e.g.* Bose showed with it that a plant's growth speeds up and slows down when they are warmed, cooled or touched.
- **Hydroponics**: Growing plants with their roots in water mixed with minerals, and no soil at all. *e.g.* Some farms on city rooftops grow lettuce in pipes of mineral water.

## Explanations
### What a plant needs · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant needs light, water, air, warmth and minerals from the soil to live and grow well. They need each one in the right amount.

Think of everything you need to live: food, water, air and a place that is not too hot or too cold. A plant needs nearly the same things. But they do not eat food the way you do. They make their own food in their leaves, using light. That is why light is on their list. The soil gives the plant water, a few minerals and something to hold on to. The unit Food from light explains how the leaves make the food. The unit Plant parts shows which part does what.
- e.g. A tulsi pot on a sunny balcony, watered every morning, stays green and bushy.
- e.g. Moong sprouts grown in a closed tiffin are pale. They have water and air, but no light.
- e.g. A money plant in a jar of water grows for months with no soil at all, because the water carries a few minerals.
### A fair test changes only one thing · *defined*
**Definition:** A fair test changes only one thing, called the variable, and keeps everything else the same. Then any difference in the results was caused by that one thing.

Imagine you put one cup of seedlings in a sunny window and another in a dark cupboard. But you also forget to water the cupboard cup. A week later the cupboard seedlings are dead. Was it the dark, or the thirst? You cannot tell, because two things changed. In a fair test you choose one thing to change. You keep everything else the same: the same kind of seeds, the same soil, the same cups and the same water. You measure the same way each time, and test more than one plant, because one plant can be a fluke.
- e.g. Light test: same seeds, same soil, same water. Only the light is different.
- e.g. Water test: same seeds, same soil, same window. Only the amount of water is different.
- e.g. Warmth test: same sprouts, same wet cotton, same dark boxes. Only the temperature is different.
### Light: why the dark seedling grows tall and yellow · *defined*
**Definition:** Without light, a seedling cannot make food or the green colour called chlorophyll. They use their stored food to grow tall and thin, searching for light. Scientists call this etiolation.

A seed carries a small store of food. A seedling in the light uses it to open green leaves quickly. Then the leaves catch light and make more food. A seedling in the dark cannot make food. So they spend their stored food on growing their stem as tall and as fast as they can, towards where light might be. They do not spend food on opening their leaves or making chlorophyll. That is why they end up tall, thin, yellow and floppy. When the stored food runs out, they die. Put them back in the light in time, and they start to turn green within a day. The chart shows made-up results from the light test.
- e.g. Moong in a dark cupboard grows taller than moong on a windowsill, but the plants are yellow and fall over.
- e.g. Potatoes kept in a dark bag grow long, white, spidery shoots.
- e.g. A plant in a dim corner leans towards the window and grows long gaps between their leaves.
### Water: just right, not as much as possible · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant needs enough water to keep their cells full and firm, but not so much that their roots cannot get air. Too little water makes them wilt. Too much water makes their roots rot.

A plant is mostly water. Their tiny cells are full of water, like balloons full of air. The full cells push against each other, and that holds the leaves and soft stems up. With too little water, the cells go soft, like a balloon going flat, and the plant wilts. Water also carries minerals from the soil and is used to make food. But roots need air too. Root cells use oxygen from the air, just as your cells do. Soil has tiny air spaces between its grains. If the soil is always soaked, water fills every space. Then the roots cannot breathe, start to rot and turn brown, and the plant goes yellow. How water travels up a plant is in the unit How water moves up a plant.
- e.g. A tulsi left without water on a hot day wilts. After a good drink they stand up again within hours.
- e.g. A pot standing in a dish of water for a week often has yellow leaves and soft, smelly roots.
- e.g. Pots have a hole in the bottom so extra water can run out.
### Warmth: growing faster, slower, or not at all · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants grow faster when they are warm and slower when they are cold. Too much heat harms them. Each kind of plant has a range of temperatures where they grow best.

Inside every living cell, tiny helpers called enzymes do the work of growing. They join and cut tiny pieces to build new cells. Enzymes work faster when they are warm, and very slowly when they are cold. So a plant in the cold hardly grows. They are not dead. They are waiting. Warm them up and they start again. But too much heat is harmful. The plant loses water fast and wilts. In great heat, the enzymes change shape and stop working, a bit like egg white turning solid in a hot pan. So for each kind of plant there is a best range of temperatures. Below it, growth is slow. Above it, the plant is harmed. The chart shows the usual pattern.
- e.g. Moong sprouts in a fridge hardly grow. Moved to a warm room, they start growing again.
- e.g. In a hot May afternoon, many balcony plants droop, and then recover in the cool evening.
- e.g. In the Himalayas, many plants only grow in the short warm summer.
### Each kind of plant has their own just right · *defined*
**Definition:** Every kind of plant needs light, water and warmth, but each needs different amounts. These depend on the place their wild ancestors came from.

A cactus and a fern need very different amounts of water. A cactus lives in dry, sunny places and stores water in their fat stem. Water them every day and they rot. A fern lives in damp, shady places. Put them in full summer sun and their leaves burn. Indian farmers know this well. Wheat is sown at the start of the cool winter, and rice at the start of the hot, wet monsoon. Each crop is planted when the weather is just right for it.
- e.g. Aloe vera and cactus need only a little water. Money plant and ferns like more water and some shade.
- e.g. Wheat grows in the cool winter in Punjab. Rice grows in the hot, wet monsoon.
- e.g. Apples grow in cool hills like Himachal Pradesh. Coconuts grow on hot coasts like Kerala.
### The shortest plank: the need in shortest supply · *debated*
Imagine a wooden bucket made of planks, with one plank much shorter than the rest. Pour water in. It can only fill up to the top of the shortest plank. In the 1800s, two German scientists said plants are like this. A plant can only grow as well as their scarcest need allows. Give a plant more water when what they really lack is light, and they will not grow any better. Scientists still argue about how true this simple picture is.
- e.g. A plant in a dark corner will not grow better with extra fertiliser. Light is their shortest plank.
- e.g. A farmer's field may have plenty of rain but very little nitrogen in the soil. Adding nitrogen helps more than adding water.
- **Carl Sprengel, a German scientist, in 1828, and Justus von Liebig, another German scientist, around 1840:** A plant's growth is limited by whichever need is in shortest supply. Sprengel wrote this first. Liebig made it famous, and for a long time it was called Liebig's law of the minimum.
- **Many plant scientists and ecologists today:** It is a useful rule, but too simple. Often two or three needs run short at the same time, and they affect each other. For example, a plant with more light may also need more water and minerals.
### Measuring growth: watching a plant grow minute by minute · *defined*
**Definition:** To measure growth, measure the same thing, in the same way, at the same time, again and again. The difference between two measurements is how much the plant grew.

Plants grow too slowly for our eyes to see. So scientists measure them. You measure with a ruler, from the soil to the top of the plant, at the same time every day. Then you take away yesterday's height from today's height. The answer is how much the plant grew that day. More than a hundred years ago, in Kolkata, Jagadish Chandra Bose built a machine called the crescograph. It made a plant's growth look thousands of times bigger. A needle drew the growth as a line on a glass plate. Bose showed that growth is not steady. It speeds up and slows down from minute to minute, when the plant is warmed, cooled, touched or given a drop of chemical.
- e.g. A moong seedling might grow about 1 centimetre in a day. That is less than half a millimetre each hour.
- e.g. Bamboo is one of the fastest growing plants. Some kinds can grow tens of centimetres in a day.
- e.g. Cut a paper strip as tall as a seedling every day and stick the strips in a row. Each day's strip is a little longer.
### Warmth deep down: moving molecules · *defined*
**Definition:** Temperature tells how fast the tiny molecules in something are moving. Warmer molecules move faster and bump into each other more often, so the work of growing goes faster. But too much heat breaks the plant's helper molecules.

Everything is made of tiny pieces called molecules, far too small to see. They are always moving. When something is warm, its molecules move faster. When it is cold, they move slowly. Inside a plant, enzymes are the tiny helpers that build new cells. They work when other molecules bump into them and get joined or cut. In the warm, more bumps happen each second, so growing goes faster. In the cold, bumps are rare, so growing almost stops. But each enzyme is folded into a special shape. Too much heat shakes it so hard that it unfolds and stops working. That is why great heat harms a plant.
- e.g. Sugar dissolves faster in hot water than in cold water, because the hot water's molecules move faster and bump into the sugar more.
- e.g. An egg white turns solid and white when cooked, because heat unfolds its molecules. It never turns back.
### How does a plant know they are in the dark, or the warm? · *idea*
A plant has no eyes and no skin like ours. But they have special molecules called photoreceptors that notice light. One family of them is called phytochrome. It works like a tiny switch. Red light flips it one way. Darkness, and the deep red light that filters through other leaves, slowly flips it back. So a seedling can tell if they are in the light, in the dark, or in the shade of other plants. In 2016, two teams of scientists found something surprising. Phytochrome flips back faster when it is warm. So the same molecule is a light sensor and a thermometer. Scientists think plants have other warmth sensors too. How they all work together is still being worked out.
- e.g. A seedling under a box senses that it is dark, and grows tall towards where light might be.
- e.g. A plant in the shade of taller plants senses the changed light, and stretches up to get out of their shade.
- **Two teams of plant scientists in 2016. One was led by Philip Wigge in Cambridge, England. One was led by Jorge Casal in Buenos Aires, Argentina.:** They found that phytochrome B, a light-sensing molecule, also changes faster when it is warm. So it helps the plant feel temperature.
- **Other scientists since then:** They have found more molecules in plants that change when it gets warm. Plants seem to use several sensors together. Which ones matter most, in which plants, is still open.
### Do plants need soil? · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants do not need soil itself. They need what soil gives them: support, water, air for the roots and minerals. Growing plants in mineral water without soil is called hydroponics.

Most plants grow in soil, so it is easy to think they need it. But think about what soil gives a plant. It holds the roots, so the plant does not fall over. It holds water, with air between its grains. And it has small amounts of minerals. If you give a plant those things another way, they do not need soil at all. In the 1860s, two German scientists, Julius Sachs and Wilhelm Knop, grew plants with their roots in water mixed with minerals. In the 1930s, an American scientist, William Gericke, grew huge tomato plants this way and called it hydroponics. Many city farms in India now grow lettuce and herbs like this.
- e.g. A money plant cutting grows roots and leaves in a jar of water on a windowsill.
- e.g. Rooftop farms grow lettuce in plastic pipes of mineral water.
- e.g. Plants grow out of cracks in walls and temple towers, with only a little dust and rainwater.
### Light comes from a star · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants use light from the Sun. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes to travel from the Sun to the Earth.

The light falling on a leaf on your windowsill comes from the Sun, a star. It left the Sun about 8 minutes ago and crossed 150 million kilometres of empty space. Its energy was made deep in the Sun's centre, where hydrogen particles crash together and join. This is called nuclear fusion. That energy spent tens of thousands of years, or more, working its way out from the centre before it flew off as light. Only a tiny part of the sunlight that reaches the Earth is caught by leaves. But that tiny part feeds nearly every living thing, including you. When you eat an idli, you are eating sunlight that a rice plant caught.
- e.g. A leaf in the morning catches light that left the Sun only 8 minutes earlier.
- e.g. The energy in your breakfast came from sunlight caught by a plant.
### Plants in space and on the Moon · *defined*
**Definition:** To grow plants away from Earth, people must give them everything Earth gives for free. That means light, water, moving air, the right warmth and minerals.

Astronauts on long journeys will need to grow food. So scientists test what plants need away from Earth. On the International Space Station there is a small garden called Veggie. It gives plants mostly red and blue light from lamps, with a little green, and water through soft pillows. A fan moves the air, because in space warm air does not rise. In August 2015, astronauts there ate red lettuce they had grown themselves. In January 2019, a Chinese spacecraft called Chang'e 4 carried cotton seeds to the far side of the Moon in a sealed can. A cotton seed sprouted, the first ever on the Moon. But the freezing Moon night arrived, and the experiment ended. In 2022, scientists in Florida grew small plants in real Moon soil brought back by Apollo astronauts. The plants grew, but slowly, and they showed signs of stress.
- e.g. The Veggie garden on the space station lights its lettuce with mostly red and blue lamps, which give a pink glow.
- e.g. Cotton sprouted on the Moon in 2019, but could not survive the freezing Moon night.
- e.g. Plants grown in real Moon soil in 2022 were smaller than the same plants grown in volcanic ash from Earth, which is often used to copy Moon soil.
### Do plants grow better with music or talking? · *debated*
Many people say their plants grow better when they talk or sing to them. Some books have said so too. Scientists ask: was the test fair? A person who talks to their plants usually also visits them more, waters them more carefully and notices problems sooner. They also breathe out a little carbon dioxide and moist air near the leaves. So more than one thing changes. A fair test would need two groups of plants treated exactly the same, except for the sound.
- e.g. Some people play music to plants in their greenhouses.
- e.g. A plant that is talked to every day probably also gets watered and checked every day.
- **Some gardeners and a few popular books, such as The Sound of Music and Plants by Dorothy Retallack, in 1973:** Plants grow better with gentle music and worse with loud rock music.
- **Most plant scientists:** Those tests were small and not fair enough. Other things changed too. Careful tests have not shown that music makes plants grow better.
- **Heidi Appel and Rex Cocroft, two scientists in the USA, in 2014:** Plants do respond to some vibrations. They shook the leaves of thale cress, a small weed that scientists often study, with a recording of the vibrations caterpillars make when they chew. Later, when real caterpillars fed on these plants, the plants made more of their bitter defence chemicals. So plants can sense some sounds, but this is not the same as liking music.

## Mostly agreed
- Plants need light, water, air, suitable warmth and minerals to grow well. *How we know:* Farmers and gardeners have seen this for thousands of years. Scientists have tested each need with fair tests, taking it away and keeping everything else the same.
- Seedlings grown in the dark grow tall, thin and pale, and die when their stored food runs out. *How we know:* Anyone can repeat the light test with moong in a week. The pale colour is because plants make chlorophyll only in light.
- Roots need air. Waterlogged soil harms most plants. *How we know:* Roots kept in soaked soil turn brown and rot. Some plants do live in water, like rice, lotus and the mangroves of the Sundarbans. They have special air channels or breathing roots that carry air down.
- Plants grow faster when warm and slower when cold, and each kind of plant has their own best range of temperature. *How we know:* Growth has been measured at different temperatures in many experiments. Farmers sow each crop in the season that suits it.
- We can only trust a test of what plants need if it changes one thing and uses more than one plant. *How we know:* When scientists repeat careful fair tests in different places, they get the same answers. Unfair tests give answers that do not repeat.

## How we found out
**Jan Baptist van Helmont, in what is now Belgium (1600s); John Woodward, an English doctor (1699), Van Helmont's willow in the early 1600s, written up in 1648; Woodward's mint test in 1699.** In the 1600s, Jan Baptist van Helmont, a scientist in what is now Belgium, grew a willow tree in a pot of soil for five years. He gave the tree only rainwater. The tree gained about 74 kilograms, but the soil lost only about 57 grams. So he decided that a plant is made from water alone. In 1699, John Woodward, an English doctor, tested this idea. If plants are made only of water, he thought, then the purest water should grow them best. He put sprigs of spearmint in glass jars of different water: rainwater, water from the river Thames, and water with some garden soil stirred in. For several weeks he weighed the plants and the water they used. The mint in the muddiest water grew the most. The mint in clean rainwater grew the least. So plants take something from the soil, carried in the water. Today we call these things minerals. Woodward also found that most of the water a plant takes in goes out through their leaves into the air.  
*What people believed before:* That a plant is made from water alone. Woodward's mint grew better in muddy water than in pure rainwater, so something from the soil helps them grow. Woodward then went too far the other way and thought plants are made mostly of soil. Later scientists found that most of a plant's weight comes from the air, as the unit Food from light explains.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The more water you give a plant, the better they grow.** (A very common idea, especially among people who love their plants; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants wilt and die without water. So it seems that more must be better.  
  Against: Roots need air. In always-soaked soil, water fills the air spaces, the roots cannot breathe and they rot. Gardeners often say that more house plants die from too much water than from too little.
- **A taller seedling is a healthier seedling.** (Many of us, without thinking about it; *rejected*)  
  For: Healthy plants do grow. Height is easy to measure and compare.  
  Against: In the dark, seedlings grow tallest of all, because they are stretching to find light. They are pale, thin and floppy. Health shows in green colour, firm stems and open leaves as well as height.
- **Plants get their food from the soil.** (Most people, for most of history; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants grow in soil, and dry soil or poor soil gives poor plants.  
  Against: In the 1600s, Jan Baptist van Helmont, a scientist in what is now Belgium, grew a willow tree in a pot for 5 years. The tree gained about 74 kilograms, but the soil lost only about 57 grams. The plant makes their food in their leaves, from light, air and water. The unit Food from light tells this story.
- **Plants need soil to live.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Almost all the plants we see grow in soil.  
  Against: A money plant grows in a jar of water. Hydroponic farms grow lettuce in mineral water, and astronauts grew lettuce in space in soft pillows. Plants need what soil gives, not soil itself.
- **Justus von Liebig discovered that a plant grows only as well as their scarcest need allows.** (Many older textbooks, which call it Liebig's law of the minimum; *debated*)  
  For: Liebig, a famous German scientist, wrote about it around 1840 and made the idea famous around the world.  
  Against: Carl Sprengel, a less famous German scientist, published the idea in 1828, about twelve years earlier. Historians now often call it the Sprengel and Liebig law of the minimum.
- **Plants grow better if you talk or sing to them.** (Many gardeners, and some popular books; *minority*)  
  For: Many people say their talked-to plants do well.  
  Against: Talking usually comes with more care, more watering and more checking, so the test is not fair. Careful fair tests have not shown that music helps growth, though plants can sense some vibrations.

## Roots
We have been finding out what a plant needs, with fair tests of light, water and warmth. Now we ask what a plant's needs tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The same moong seeds grew short and green in the light, and tall and pale in the dark. The seeds came from one packet and got the same water. The light they sensed decided how they grew. So one kind of seed can grow into very different plants, depending on what they find where they grow.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a living thing's shape comes from inside them, already set in the seed. But a seed brings only part of the plan. The light, water and warmth they meet finish the rest.  
*Evidence:* In the light test, moong in the light grew short and green, with open leaves. The same seeds under the box grew tall, pale and thin, with leaves still closed. Back in the light, the pale ones began to turn green within a day.

- *who am i:* Your body also changes with where and how you live. Skin gets darker in strong sun, and muscles grow stronger with use.
- *what is life:* A living thing keeps sensing the world and changing to fit it. Too little or too much of a need changes how they grow.
- *what is this world:* The light that shapes a seedling on your windowsill left the Sun about eight minutes earlier. So a star 150 million kilometres away helps shape a plant in your home.

A moong seed holds the start of a plant: a tiny root, a tiny shoot and a store of food. But the plant they become depends on what they meet. Light, water, warmth and air all change their shape as they grow. A living thing and their world keep making each other. A seedling senses their place from their first day, and grows to fit it.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1:** From the Self came space. From space came air. From air came fire, and from fire came water. From water came earth, from earth came plants, and from plants came food. (The Upanishad puts plants after air, fire, water and earth, as if they are made from all of them. Our tests give the same list from science: a plant needs air, warmth and light, water, and minerals from the earth.)
- **Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, the Buddha's first teaching:** The Buddha said that two extremes, too much comfort and too much hardship, do not lead anywhere good. He taught a middle way between them. (The Buddha was talking about how people live, not about plants. But our water test has the same shape: no water and too much water both harm a moong seedling, and they grow best in between.)
- Science (settled): The same seeds can grow into very different shapes in different light, water and warmth. Biologists call this power to change shape to fit the surroundings plasticity.
- Science (settled): Sunlight takes about eight minutes to travel from the Sun to a leaf.
- Other view, Jain thinkers: Jain thinkers teach that each plant has their own soul, called a jiva, separate from every other soul. On this view, a seedling turns to the light because they are a living being in their own right, not because they and the world are one.
- Practice, Sun and shade (3 min): Let's stand in a patch of sunlight by a window for one minute. Close your eyes and feel the warmth on your face and arms. Then step into the shade and stand still for one minute. Breathe slowly. What changed on your skin? A plant senses this difference too, in their own way.
- Ask: The same seed can grow short and green, or tall and pale. Which one shows what a moong plant really looks like?
- Ask: A seedling in the dark spent their stored food on reaching for light. Were they choosing?
- Ask: Your body changes with sun and use, like a seedling changes with light. What has the place you live in shaped in you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A seedling in the dark grows tall instead of green. Is that a mistake, or a clever plan? (hint: Imagine you are in a dark tunnel with your last packed lunch. You see a crack of light far above. What would you do?)
- *deep:* Why can't a plant just walk to the light, or to water, as an animal can? How do they cope instead? (hint: Watch how a plant on a windowsill leans, and how roots spread. Plants move by growing.)
- *big:* In the water test, the moong standing in water had the most water, but they did worse than the moong given a spoonful a day. Is more always better? (hint: Think of the cups standing in water. What else is good in the right amount, but harmful when there is too much?)
- *deep:* The same moong seeds grew short and green in the light, and tall and pale in the dark. Which one shows what a moong plant really looks like? (hint: Is there one true shape, or does the shape always depend on where they grow? Think about how your skin changes in strong sun.)
- *big:* The Buddha taught a middle path, between too much and too little. Plants seem to live on a middle path too. Where else in life is the middle the best place to be? (hint: Think about food, sleep, screen time, and how hard to push a friend on a swing.)
- *big:* In our light test, we kept some seedlings in the dark until they were weak. Is it fair to the plants? When is it right to do experiments on living things? (hint: What do we learn? Can we give the dark plants back their light at the end? Would you test on an animal?)
- *deep:* The light that grew your idli left the Sun about 8 minutes before the rice plant caught it. So are you made of sunlight? (hint: Follow your food back step by step: idli, batter, rice plant, leaf, sunlight.)
- *deep:* If people one day live on the Moon or Mars, what will they need to bring for plants, that Earth gives for free? (hint: Make a list: light, air, water, warmth, minerals. Which ones does the Moon have?)
- *big:* Carl Sprengel had an idea first, but Justus von Liebig made it famous. Who should get the credit for an idea: the person who has it first, or the person who spreads it? (hint: Has someone ever said your idea louder than you, and got the praise?)
- *big:* A plant cannot tell us in words what they need. How can we listen to something that does not use words? (hint: A plant doctor reads clues: drooping, yellow, leaning. A baby cannot use words either. How do people find out what a baby needs?)

## Story: A Stripe of Sun
*Zoya, Nani, Rukmini Mami, Arjun and the contest are made up. What happens to Zoya's moong is real: you can see it in this unit's light test.*

In Zoya's lane in Triplicane, in Chennai, the buildings stood very close together. Neighbours could pass a bowl of kesari from one balcony to the next without leaning. Sunlight reached the lane for only about an hour each day. At eleven o'clock a thin yellow stripe slid down the wall opposite Zoya's window. For a while it touched the outer edge of the iron grille around her window. Then it was gone before lunch.

In the May holidays, Rukmini Mami from the ground floor made an announcement on the staircase. Every child in the building would get a paper cup of soil and five moong beans from her own kitchen. In two weeks there would be judging, right there on the landing. 'The tallest plant wins,' she said. She held up a mango so ripe that it smelt like a festival. 'A Malgova, from Salem. One.'

Arjun from the fourth floor carried his cup up to the terrace, where the sun beat down all day long. Kavitha's mother put hers on the kitchen window, next to the pressure cooker. Zoya put hers on her own windowsill, between Nani's spectacles case and a jar of buttons, facing the wall.

On the third day, two pale loops pushed up through Zoya's soil, like the handles of tiny bags. By the fifth day the loops had straightened and shot up past the rim of the cup. Zoya measured them every evening with her school ruler, from the soil to the very top. She wrote the numbers on the back of an old calendar. Four centimetres. Seven. Ten and a half.

Every evening she climbed to the terrace to look at Arjun's cup. His plants were short. They were hardly past the rim. 'Mine is winning,' she told Nani, who sat by the window every afternoon doing the crossword. 'Hm,' said Nani. That was what she said when she did not agree, but wanted you to find out for yourself.

Arjun's moong was short, but the plants were thick, and dark green. Each stem held up two round leaves, open flat like hands waiting for something to be put into them. Zoya's moong was tall, but the plants were a pale, washed-out yellow-green. Their leaves were small and only half open. And every stem leaned the same way, towards the window, as if they were listening for a voice outside.

On the eighth day, Zoya came home from her cousin's house and found her tallest plant lying across the rim of the cup. Nobody had knocked them. They had simply folded, like a tired arm. She stood them up and leaned them against a pencil. In the morning, two more had fallen.

At eleven o'clock the stripe of sun arrived on the wall opposite. Zoya was sitting with her chin on the windowsill when it came, so she saw. The plants did not move, or not that she could see. But for the first time she noticed exactly where they were pointing. They pointed at the bright stripe. All of them, like people on a railway platform leaning out to look down the line for the train. They had been pointing at it all along.

Zoya looked at her calendar of numbers. Four, seven, ten and a half, fourteen, eighteen. They did not look like winning numbers any more. They looked like someone running. Running and running down a long dark corridor towards a door far away, spending everything they had. Each moong bean had carried a little food inside, enough to get started. Arjun's plants had spent theirs on opening green leaves, and then the sun had fed them. Hers had never found enough sun. So they had spent every crumb on getting taller.

Zoya went into the kitchen and came back with Ammi's biggest steel thali, the one kept for guests. She leaned out to the edge of the grille, where the stripe of sun touched it, and tilted the thali. A bright, wobbly circle of light landed on the windowsill. It slid across the buttons, and onto the cup. 'Ammi will want that back by dinner,' said Nani, without looking up. But she put down her pen and held the thali steady while Zoya tied it to the grille with string.

That afternoon, Zoya sowed five more moong in a second cup and set it where the circle of light fell. Every day at eleven she turned the thali a little, to follow the sun. It was like turning the pages of a slow book.

On judging day, eleven cups stood in a row on the landing. Rukmini Mami walked along them with her tailor's tape. Zoya's first cup was still the tallest, even propped on their pencil: nineteen centimetres. Arjun's was nine. Kavitha's had got too hot next to the cooker and given up. 'Zoya!' said Rukmini Mami, and held out the mango.

Zoya did not take it. Her face was burning. 'They are tall because they are hungry,' she said. Everyone looked at her. She tried again. 'This is not the best plant. This is the plant that was looking hardest. Arjun's is the one that found what they were looking for.'

Rukmini Mami looked at the yellow plant leaning on their pencil for a long time. Then she looked at Arjun's, short and green and stubborn. She rolled up her tape. 'Then next year,' she said slowly, 'I will need a better ruler.'

Arjun got the mango. He cut it on the terrace, and the juice ran down eleven chins. But Zoya hardly tasted her slice. She was thinking of the second cup on her windowsill downstairs. Its plants were only as tall as her little finger. They were green as a parrot. And they were leaning, every one of them, towards an old steel thali full of sun.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Zoya's plant grew tallest because they were starving for light. A seedling in dim light spends their stored food on stretching their stem, and stays pale with folded leaves. Scientists call this etiolation. So height alone is the wrong measure of how well a plant is doing. Light was the need in shortest supply, and Zoya fixed it by bouncing sunlight in with a thali. The story also asks what we choose to measure, and whether the person with the biggest number has really done best.

- Ask: Zoya's moong grew the tallest. Why weren't they the healthiest?
- Ask: Which way were all Zoya's plants leaning? What were they looking for?
- Ask: How did the steel thali help the second cup of moong?
- Ask: Rukmini Mami said she would need 'a better ruler' next year. What else could she measure, to find the healthiest plant?
- Ask: Have you ever won, or lost, because of the wrong kind of measuring? For example, the most marks but not the best understanding?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Light Test: what happens in the dark? (45 min, 8 days)
- **question:** Do moong seedlings need light to grow well? What happens to them in the dark?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After a week, will the seedlings in the dark be taller or shorter than the ones in the light? Will they be green or another colour? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Change only the light. Use 6 cups that are the same, the same soil, and 5 moong seeds in each cup. Put all 6 cups side by side near the same bright window, out of strong afternoon sun, so the box does not get hotter than the open cups. Cover 3 of them with a cardboard box. Water every cup with the same tablespoon of water each morning. Measure every cup at the same time of day. We use 3 cups for each, because one cup could be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up pokes the holes in the cups. Wash hands after touching soil. Do not eat the seeds.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw two bar charts: one of the heights on Day 7, and one of the number of open green leaves.
- **results table:** Cup | Day 3: height of the tallest seedling (cm) | Day 5: height (cm) | Day 7: height (cm) | Colour of the leaves | Open green leaves on the tallest seedling
- **schedule:** day 1: Sow 5 soaked moong in each of 6 cups. Write your prediction. Put 3 cups under the box and 3 beside it in the light. (15 min); day 2: Water every cup with 1 tablespoon. Put the box back quickly. Do this every morning until Day 7. (2 min); day 3: Water, then measure the tallest seedling in each cup. Write the heights and colours. (5 min); day 4: Water every cup the same. (2 min); day 5: Water and measure again. (5 min); day 6: Water every cup the same. (2 min); day 7: Measure, count the open green leaves on the tallest seedling in each cup, compare and draw a bar chart. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (12 min); day 8: Put the dark cups in the light. Look again tomorrow. Do they turn green? (2 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the soaked moong in a bowl. 2. Ask your child to predict what will happen in the dark, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Some seedlings will grow in the light, and some in the dark. In a week, which do you think will be taller? Which will be greener?  
  *Child mode:* Some moong will grow in the light. Some will grow in the dark. Guess: which will be taller? Which will be greener? Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think that? (good: Plants need light, so the dark ones will be small.; They might grow up to look for light.)
  - Note: Most children predict the dark seedlings will be smaller. That is a fine prediction. The surprise is part of the lesson.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill 6 cups with soil to about one finger-width below the rim. 2. Press 5 soaked moong into each cup, about as deep as your fingernail. 3. Water each cup with 1 tablespoon. 4. Label 3 cups LIGHT and 3 cups DARK. 5. Stand all 6 on a tray near a bright window, out of strong afternoon sun. 6. Cover the 3 DARK cups with the box.  
  *Parent says:* We'll keep everything the same except one thing. Same cups, same soil, same seeds, same water, same window. The only difference is the box.  
  *Child mode:* Fill 6 cups with soil. Push 5 moong into each one. Give each cup 1 spoon of water. Put a box over 3 cups. Everything else stays the same.
  - Ask: What is the one thing we are changing? (good: Light.; Whether the cup is under the box.)
  - Ask: What are we keeping the same? (good: The soil, the seeds, the cups, the water, the window.)
  - Note: Moong usually comes up in 2 or 3 days in a warm room. In a cold winter room it can take 5 days or more, so add those days to the plan. If nothing has come up by Day 6, sow again in a warmer place.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every morning, lift the box for as short a time as you can. Water every cup with the same tablespoon of water. Put the box back straight away.  
  *Parent says:* We lift the box quickly, so the dark cups get as little light as possible. And every cup gets exactly the same water.  
  *Child mode:* Lift the box. Give every cup 1 spoon of water. Put the box back fast. The dark cups must stay dark.
  - Wait: Water every morning. Measure on Day 3 and Day 5.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 3 and Day 5: 1. Find the tallest seedling in each cup. 2. Stand the ruler on the soil beside them, with the 0 at the soil. 3. Read the number at the highest point of the seedling. 4. Write the height and the colour in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Put the zero of the ruler right at the soil. Read the number at the very top of the tallest seedling.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the ruler on the soil. Zero goes at the bottom. Read the number at the top of the tallest seedling. Write it down.
  - Note: Dark seedlings often grow with a hooked top. Measure to the highest point, not along the bend. If your child finds it hard to read the small marks, round to the nearest centimetre for now.
  - Check: Where should the 0 on the ruler go when you measure a seedling? → **At the soil**
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 7: 1. Take the box off. 2. Line up all 6 cups. 3. Measure the tallest seedling in each cup. 4. Count the open green leaves on that tallest seedling. 5. Look closely at the stems and colours.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look at all of them together. Which are taller? Which are greener? Which are stronger?  
  *Child mode:* Take the box off. Line up all the cups. Measure the tallest seedling in each. Count their open green leaves. What do you notice?
  - Ask: What do the dark seedlings look like? (good: Tall, thin and yellow.; Their leaves are tiny and closed.; They are falling over.)
  - Ask: Was your prediction right? (good: Any honest answer.)
  - Note: Usually the dark seedlings are taller, but yellow or white, thin and floppy, with small folded leaves. The light seedlings are shorter, thicker and green, with open leaves. This surprises most children.
  - Check: The dark seedlings grew taller than the light seedlings. Does that mean they are healthier? → **No. They are tall because they are stretching to find light, and they are pale and weak**
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the Day 7 heights. Use the middle height of the 3 LIGHT cups and of the 3 DARK cups.  
  *Parent says:* To find the middle height, put the 3 numbers in order and take the middle one. Then draw one bar for LIGHT and one for DARK.  
  *Child mode:* Put the 3 LIGHT heights in order. The middle one is your LIGHT bar. Do the same for DARK. Draw both bars.
  - Note: With the made-up numbers, LIGHT is 8, 8, 9, so the middle is 8. DARK is 13, 14, 15, so the middle is 14. Using the middle value stops one odd cup from fooling us.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. First, what you think now. Second, what you saw. Third, why you think it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: plants need light to grow well. What I saw: the dark seedlings were 14 cm tall but yellow and floppy, with no open green leaves. The light ones were 8 cm, green and strong. Why: without light they could not make food or green colour. So they used their stored food to stretch up and look for light.
- **s8 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 8, move the dark cups into the light. Look again the next day.  
  *Parent says:* Let's give the dark seedlings their light back. What do you think will happen by tomorrow?  
  *Child mode:* Put the dark cups in the light. Look tomorrow. Do they turn green?
  - Note: Within a day, the yellow seedlings usually start to turn green, because they make chlorophyll once they have light. Very weak seedlings that have fallen over may not recover. Giving the light back is a kind way to end the test, too.
  - Think: The dark seedlings had no eyes. How did they know they were in the dark?
  - Wait: Look again after 1 day.
### e2 · The Water Test: too little, just right, too much (50 min, 14 days)
- **question:** How much water do moong seedlings need? Is more water always better?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which cups will do best: no water, just right water, or soaked all the time? Which will do worst? Write your guesses and reasons.
- **fair test:** Change only the water. Grow all 9 cups of seedlings the same way for 5 days first. Then give 3 cups no water, and 3 cups 1 tablespoon every morning. Stand the last 3 cups in a deep dish of water that always comes up to the level of the soil inside the cups, so the soil stays soaked to the top. Keep the cups side by side on the same windowsill. Measure every cup at the same time of day.
- **safety:** A grown-up pokes the holes in the cups. Wash hands after touching soil. Tip out the SOAKED dish and fill it with fresh water every second day, so mosquitoes cannot breed in it. Throw away soggy, smelly soil outside or in the dustbin, not in the kitchen sink.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar chart of the middle height of each group on Day 14. Draw what the roots looked like in each group.
- **results table:** Cup | Day 6: height (cm) | Day 10: height (cm) | Day 14: height (cm) | Leaves: firm, drooping, yellow or dry? | Soil: dry, damp or soggy?
- **schedule:** day 1: Sow 5 soaked moong in each of 9 cups. Water every cup with 1 tablespoon and put them all on the windowsill. (12 min); day 3: Water all 9 cups the same. Do this every morning until Day 5. (2 min); day 6: Measure all cups. Write your predictions. Start the three treatments: none, just right, soaked. (12 min); day 8: Water the JUST RIGHT cups. Tip out the SOAKED dish and fill it with fresh water. Write one line about each group. (3 min); day 10: Water, change the dish water and measure. (5 min); day 12: Water, change the dish water and look closely at the leaves. (3 min); day 14: Final measuring. Tip one cup from each group out to look at the roots.   *Parent says:* First we grow all 9 cups exactly the same way, so they start the test the same.  
  *Child mode:* Fill 9 cups with soil. Push 5 moong into each. Give each 1 spoon of water. Put them all on the windowsill.
  - Wait: Let the seedlings grow for 5 days. Water all 9 cups the same, 1 tablespoon every morning.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 6: 1. Measure the tallest seedling in each cup and write it down. 2. Label 3 cups NONE, 3 JUST RIGHT and 3 SOAKED. 3. Stand the 3 SOAKED cups in a deep dish. Fill the dish with water up to the soil inside the cups. 4. Put all the cups back side by side.  
  *Parent says:* Now the test begins. These get no water at all. These get 1 spoon every morning. And these stand in water all the time. Which do you think will do best?  
  *Child mode:* Measure every cup. Put labels on: NONE, JUST RIGHT, SOAKED. Stand the SOAKED cups in a dish of water. Which will do best? Guess.
  - Ask: Which do you think will do best? Why? (good: Just right, because too much is bad too.; Soaked, because plants love water.)
  - Note: Many children predict the soaked cups will do best. Let them. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every morning, water the JUST RIGHT cups with 1 tablespoon. 2. Give the NONE cups nothing. 3. Every second day, tip out the SOAKED dish. Fill it again with fresh water, up to the soil inside the cups. 4. Press a finger into the soil of one cup from each group and write: dry, damp or soggy.  
  *Parent says:* Feel the soil. Dry, damp or soggy? Write it down for each group.  
  *Child mode:* Water only the JUST RIGHT cups. Change the dish water every second day. Feel the soil. Dry, damp or soggy?
  - Note: Changing the dish water matters. Mosquitoes need several days in still water to grow from eggs, so fresh water every second day stops them.
  - Wait: Repeat until Day 14.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 8 or 10, look closely at the leaves in each group together.  
  *Parent says:* Let's be plant doctors. What do the leaves look like in each group?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the leaves in each group. Are they firm, drooping, yellow or dry?
  - Check: The NONE cups have drooping, floppy leaves. What is the plant telling us? → **They need water**
- **s5 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 14: 1. Measure the tallest seedling in each cup. 2. Gently tip one cup from each group onto newspaper. 3. Wash the soil off the roots in a bowl of water. 4. Compare the roots.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look at the hidden part, the roots. What do the roots look like in each group?  
  *Child mode:* Measure every cup. Then tip out one cup from each group. Wash the roots gently. Compare them.
  - Ask: What do the SOAKED roots look like? (good: Brown and soft.; They smell bad.; Fewer white root hairs.)
  - Ask: What do the JUST RIGHT roots look like? (good: White and spread out.; Lots of tiny hairs.)
  - Note: Usually the NONE seedlings droop within 2 or 3 days, then dry up and turn brown, with thin dry roots. JUST RIGHT seedlings are green and firm, with white, spreading roots. SOAKED seedlings are often yellowing and shorter, with brown, soft roots. In some weather the soaked cups survive for longer. That is real science too. Write down what you actually saw.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the middle height in each group on Day 14.  
  *Parent says:* One bar for each group. Put the three heights in order and use the middle one.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for NONE, JUST RIGHT and SOAKED. Use the middle height of each group.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. First, what you think now. Second, what you saw. Third, why you think it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: moong needs some water, but not too much. What I saw: the JUST RIGHT seedlings were 13 cm and green. The NONE ones dried up, and the SOAKED ones were 8 cm and yellow with brown roots. Why: without water the cells go soft. In soaked soil the roots cannot get air, so they rot.
  - Think: Rice grows standing in water. Why don't the rice plants drown like our moong? (Hint: rice has air channels that carry air down to their roots.)
### e3 · The Warmth Test: cold, cool or warm? (40 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Do moong sprouts grow faster when they are warm or when they are cold?
- **predict:** Guess first. Some sprouts go in the fridge, some in a room cupboard and some in a warm spot. After 3 days, which will have grown longest? Write your guess and reason.
- **fair test:** Change only the warmth. Use 3 steel katoris or bowls that are the same, each with the same folded wet cloth and 10 sprouts. The fridge is dark inside, so cover all 3 bowls with a plate to make them all dark. Put the same amount of water in each. Measure all three at the same time each day.
- **safety:** For the warm spot, use a closed cupboard in the warmest room, or the top of the fridge near the back, where warm air comes out. Never use the stove, the oven, a heater or a flame. Do not eat the sprouts afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For each place, take the first total away from the last total. Draw one bar for each place.
- **results table:** Place | Temperature (°C) | Day 1: total length of 5 sprouts (cm) | Day 2 (cm) | Day 3 (cm) | Growth in 3 days (cm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up the three bowls. Measure the sprouts. Put one bowl in the fridge, one in a room cupboard and one in a warm spot. (15 min); day 2: Measure the 5 longest sprouts in each bowl. Read the temperature in each place. (6 min); day 3: Measure again. (6 min); day 4: Final measuring. Work out how much each bowl grew. Draw a bar chart. Move the fridge bowl into the room and watch. (13 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the sprouted moong and ask for a prediction.  
  *Parent says:* We'll keep some sprouts cold, some cool and some warm. Which do you think will grow the most in 3 days?  
  *Child mode:* Some sprouts go in the fridge. Some go in a cupboard. Some go somewhere warm. Guess: which will grow most?
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold a wet cloth flat in each bowl. 2. Wet each with exactly 4 teaspoons of water. 3. Put 10 sprouts on the cloth in each bowl. 4. Measure the 5 longest sprouts in each bowl, from the tip of the root to the top, and add them up. 5. Cover each bowl with a plate.  
  *Parent says:* The fridge is dark inside. So we cover all three bowls with a plate, to make them all dark. That way only the warmth is different.  
  *Child mode:* Put a folded wet cloth in 3 bowls. Add 4 spoons of water to each. Put 10 sprouts in each. Measure 5 sprouts. Cover each bowl with a plate.
  - Ask: Why do we cover all three bowls, not just the fridge one? (good: So they are all dark.; So only the warmth is different.)
  - Note: Adding up 5 sprouts gives one number for each bowl, and evens out small and big sprouts. Sprouts of 1 cm, 2 cm, 1 cm, 1 cm and 1 cm add up to 6 cm. Lift each sprout gently, because their root may stick to the cloth.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one bowl in the fridge, not the freezer. 2. Put one bowl in a cupboard in a room. 3. Put one bowl in a warm spot, like a cupboard in the warmest room or the top of the fridge. 4. If you have a thermometer, leave it in each place for 5 minutes and read it.  
  *Parent says:* Let's read the temperature in each place. The fridge is about 5 degrees. A room in India is often between 20 and 32 degrees.  
  *Child mode:* Put one bowl in the fridge. Put one in a cupboard. Put one somewhere warm. Read the thermometer in each place.
  - Note: Never use the stove, oven, a heater or a flame for the warm spot. If your warm spot turns out no warmer than the cupboard, the test still works with two places: the fridge and the cupboard. In winter, a warm spot makes a bigger difference.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 2 and Day 3: take each bowl out, measure the 5 longest sprouts and add them up. Add 1 teaspoon of water if the cloth is drying. Put each bowl back quickly.  
  *Parent says:* Measure the 5 longest sprouts and add them up. Which bowl is growing fastest?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the 5 longest sprouts in each bowl. Add them up. Write the total. Put the bowl back fast.
  - Check: You add water to the warm bowl because its cloth is drying. What should you do for the other two bowls? → **Add the same amount, so the water stays the same in all three**
  - Wait: Measure every day for 3 days.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 4: 1. Measure one last time. 2. For each bowl, take the Day 1 total away from the last total. That is how much they grew. 3. Draw a bar for each place.  
  *Parent says:* Take the first total away from the last total. That tells us how much each bowl grew. Let's draw one bar for each.  
  *Child mode:* Take the first number away from the last number. That is how much they grew. Draw one bar for each place.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. First, what you think now. Second, what you saw. Third, why you think it happened.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: moong sprouts grow faster when they are warm. What I saw: in 3 days the fridge sprouts grew 1 cm. The cupboard ones grew 18 cm and the warm ones 24 cm. Why: warmth makes the tiny helpers inside the sprouts work faster.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Move the fridge bowl into the room cupboard. Look again in 2 days.  
  *Parent says:* Are the fridge sprouts dead, or only waiting? Let's warm them up and see.  
  *Child mode:* Put the fridge bowl in the cupboard. Look in 2 days. Are the sprouts dead, or only waiting?
  - Note: The cold sprouts usually start growing again in the warmth. Cold slowed them down. A few may have been harmed, because moong comes from warm places and does not like long cold.
  - Think: Our warm spot was best. Would an even hotter place, like a terrace at 46 degrees, be even better? Why not?
  - Wait: Look again after 2 days.
### e4 · Coloured Light: does the colour matter? (35 min, 7 days)
- **question:** Do seedlings grow differently under red, blue, green or clear see-through covers?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which colour of light will grow the greenest, strongest seedlings? Which will grow the weakest? Plants are green. Do you think they like green light best? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Change only the colour of the cover. Use 4 cups of seedlings sown on the same day, the same soil and the same water. Make 4 cone-shaped covers of the same size, from clear, red, blue and green cellophane. Stand the cups side by side in the same bright place.
- **safety:** Leave a small gap at the bottom of each cover so air can get in. Do not leave the cups in hot afternoon sun, because the covers can trap heat.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw each seedling in their colour of light.
- **results table:** Cover colour | Day 7: height (cm) | Leaf colour | Open green leaves | Leaning?
- **schedule:** day 1: Make 4 cellophane covers and put one over each cup. Write your predictions. (15 min); day 4: Water every cup the same each morning, and look through the covers. (5 min); day 7: Take off the covers. Measure, compare colour and leaves, and say what you think, what you saw and why. (15 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push 4 sticks or straws into the soil around each cup's edge. 2. Roll a sheet of cellophane into a tall cone or tube and tape it to the sticks. 3. Leave a small gap at the bottom for air. 4. Use one colour for each cup: clear, red, blue, green.  
  *Parent says:* Each cup gets a coloured window. The clear one lets all the colours of sunlight through. The others let mostly one colour through.  
  *Child mode:* Make a tall see-through hat for each cup. One clear, one red, one blue, one green. Leave a gap at the bottom for air.
  - Ask: Plants are green. Which colour do you think they like best? (good: Green, because they're green.; Maybe not green. Maybe they bounce green light away.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Every morning, lift each cover for a moment, water each cup with the same 1 tablespoon and put the cover back.  
  *Parent says:* Same water for every cup. Only the colour is different.  
  *Child mode:* Lift each hat. Give each cup 1 spoon of water. Put the hat back.
  - Wait: Water every morning. Look on Day 7.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On Day 7: 1. Take off the covers. 2. Measure the tallest seedling in each cup. 3. Compare leaf colour, stem thickness and how much each seedling leans.  
  *Parent says:* Which seedlings look most like the ones in full light? Which look most like the ones in the dark?  
  *Child mode:* Take off the hats. Measure each cup. Which seedlings are greenest and strongest? Which look like they were in the dark?
  - Note: Clear usually gives the sturdiest green seedlings. Under red they are often a little taller and thinner. Blue usually gives short, green seedlings. Green often looks most like the dark cups, taller and paler. Results at home vary. Cellophane sheets also let different amounts of light through, so this test is not perfectly fair. That is worth talking about.
  - Check: Plants are green because their leaves bounce back much of the green light. So, under green cellophane, what do the seedlings mostly get? → **The light they use least, so they grow as if in dim light**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why. Then talk about what made the test less than perfectly fair.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why? And was our test perfectly fair?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. First, what you think now. Second, what you saw. Third, why you think it happened. Then think: was the test perfectly fair?
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: plants use some colours of light better than others. What I saw: under green, the seedlings were taller and paler than under clear or red. Why: plants bounce back green light, so they get less of the light they use. The test was not perfectly fair, because the coloured sheets let different amounts of light through.
  - Think: Astronauts grow lettuce under red and blue lamps, which make a pink glow. Why not plain green lamps?

### a5 · Bose's Growth Magnifier (35 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up handles the pin. Tie the thread loosely, so it does not cut the plant.
- **record:** Write where the pointer was every 2 hours. Work out how far it moved, and how much the plant really grew.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child about Jagadish Chandra Bose and his crescograph.  
  *Parent says:* More than a hundred years ago, in Kolkata, Jagadish Chandra Bose built a special machine. It made a plant's growth look thousands of times bigger. Today we'll build a tiny cousin of it.  
  *Child mode:* Long ago in Kolkata, a scientist called Bose built a machine. It made plant growth look huge. Let's build a small one.
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up pushes the pin through the straw about 4 cm from one end. 2. Push the pin into the clay or cork on top of the stick, so the straw can swing like a seesaw. 3. Hang the lightest paper clip on the long end, so it tips down. 4. Tie one end of the thread loosely around the stem, just below the top leaves. 5. Tie the other end to the short end of the straw, so the thread is straight and just tight. 6. Stand the paper scale behind the long end of the straw.  
  *Parent says:* The short end is 4 centimetres from the pin. The long end is about 16 centimetres. So when the plant grows a little, the long end moves 4 times as far.  
  *Child mode:* With a grown-up, push a pin through the straw near one end. Balance it on clay. Tie the short end to the plant's top with thread. The long end is your pointer.
  - Note: The paper clip makes the long end heavier, so the short end keeps the thread tight. As the plant grows up, the short end can rise, and the long end drops along the scale. Use the lightest clip you have, so the thread does not pull the plant up.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do this in the morning. Mark where the pointer is now. Then mark it again every 2 hours until the evening.  
  *Parent says:* Let's mark the pointer every 2 hours. Is the plant growing, even while we cannot see it?  
  *Child mode:* Mark where the pointer is. Look again every 2 hours. Mark it again. Is it moving?
  - Note: A young seedling grows about half a millimetre to one millimetre each hour on a warm day. With this lever, the pointer moves 4 millimetres for every millimetre of growth. So over a whole day it should move 2 centimetres or more. If nothing moves, check that the thread is still tight. Keep the plant away from fans and wind, which make the pointer swing.
  - Wait: Check every 2 hours, from morning to evening.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out how much the plant really grew: the pointer moved 4 times as far as the plant grew.  
  *Parent says:* The pointer moved 12 millimetres. The lever makes it look 4 times bigger. So how much did the plant really grow?

But both missed the air: most of a plant's dry body is built from a gas in the air, using light.. *Why:* Each scientist had real evidence. Van Helmont was right that the soil gives very little weight. Woodward was right that the soil gives something plants need. Neither of them knew that plants build their bodies mostly from the air.
5. (mcq) In the light test, what shows that seedlings can sense light, even though they have no eyes?  
   Options: The seedlings in the dark grew in a different way: tall, pale, with folded leaves / The seedlings in the dark were wetter / Nothing shows it  
   **Answer:** The seedlings in the dark grew in a different way: tall, pale, with folded leaves. *Why:* The dark seedlings changed how they grew, so they must have sensed the dark. Plants sense light with special molecules called photoreceptors, which change when light falls on them.
6. (truefalse) Scientists have found that phytochrome B, a light-sensing molecule in plants, also changes faster when it is warm.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 2016, two teams of scientists found that phytochrome B also works like a thermometer. Plants seem to have other warmth sensors too, and scientists are still working out how they all fit together.
7. (mcq) In January 2019, cotton seeds sprouted on the far side of the Moon, inside a sealed can on the Chinese spacecraft Chang'e 4. Why did the experiment end after only a few days?  
   Options: The cotton was eaten / The long, freezing Moon night came, and it got far too cold / There was too much water  
   **Answer:** The long, freezing Moon night came, and it got far too cold. *Why:* A night on the Moon lasts about two Earth weeks and is extremely cold. Warmth is one of a plant's needs. Picking 'too much water' misses that the big problem on the Moon is the cold.
8. (mcq) Why does warmth make a sprout grow faster?  
   Options: Warm molecules move faster and bump into each other more often, so the work of growing goes faster / Heat is a kind of food for the sprout / Warmth makes the sprout lighter  
   **Answer:** Warm molecules move faster and bump into each other more often, so the work of growing goes faster. *Why:* Temperature tells how fast molecules are moving. Faster molecules meet more often, so the plant's helper molecules, called enzymes, work faster. Picking 'heat is food' mixes up warmth with food. The sprout's food is stored in the seed.
9. (truefalse) Very great heat can harm a plant, even when they have plenty of water.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Enzymes, the plant's helper molecules, are folded into special shapes. Too much heat shakes them out of shape, like egg white turning solid when cooked. Then they stop working.
10. (match) More is not always better. Match each 'too much' to what it does to a plant.  
   **Answer:** Too much water → the roots get no air and start to rot; Too much heat → the plant loses water fast and their enzymes stop working; Too much fertiliser → the salty soil draws water out of the roots; Too little light → the plant grows tall, pale and weak. *Why:* Every need has a just-right amount. Too little and too much can both harm a plant.
11. (write) Ishita says: 'If a little fertiliser is good for my plant, a lot must be even better.' What would you tell her? How could she test her idea fairly?  
   **Answer:** More is not always better. Too much fertiliser makes the soil salty, and that can damage roots. To test it, she could grow 3 cups with no fertiliser, 3 with a little and 3 with a lot. Everything else stays the same: seeds, soil, light and water. Then she measures and compares after two weeks.. *Why:* A strong answer challenges the 'more is better' idea and plans a test with three amounts, like the water test.
12. (write) Bose's growth magnifier makes a plant's growth look 4 times bigger. The pointer moved 20 millimetres in 10 hours. How much did the plant really grow in those 10 hours? How much is that in one hour?  
   **Answer:** 20 divided by 4 is 5. So the plant grew 5 millimetres in 10 hours. 5 millimetres shared between 10 hours is half a millimetre each hour.. *Why:* Divide the pointer's movement by how much the lever magnifies. Then share it between the hours.
13. (write) In the light test, we kept some seedlings in the dark until they were weak. Was that fair to the seedlings? Give one reason for and one reason against.  
   **Answer:** For: we learnt something important about what plants need, and we put the dark seedlings back in the light at the end, so most of them turned green. Against: we made living beings struggle on purpose. Any answer with a real reason on each side is good.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. The point is to weigh both sides.
14. (write) Astronauts plan to grow food on the Moon. List what the plants will need, and say where the astronauts could get each one.  
   **Answer:** Light: from the Sun during the long Moon day, or from lamps. Water: brought from Earth, or made from ice near the Moon's poles. Air: made and pumped into a sealed greenhouse. Warmth: heaters and covers, because Moon nights are freezing. Minerals: from Moon soil, with extra added.. *Why:* A strong answer covers every need from this unit, and notices that Earth gives most of them for free.
15. (write) Sumathi's fern on a sunny terrace has brown, crispy edges. Her cactus in a dark bathroom is pale and leaning. What is wrong with each plant? What would you do?  
   **Answer:** The fern is getting too much sun and heat. Move the fern to a shady, cooler place and keep their soil damp. The cactus is getting too little light. Move the cactus to a sunny window, and water them only a little.. *Why:* Each kind of plant has their own just right. The same place can be too much for one plant and too little for another.
16. (draw) Draw the shortest-plank bucket for Zoya's moong in the dim window. Label each plank with a need. Make the shortest plank the need that held the moong back.  
   **Answer:** A bucket with planks for light, water, air, warmth and minerals. The light plank is the shortest, and water spills out over it.. *Why:* Zoya's moong had water, air, warmth and soil, but very little light. So light was their shortest plank.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which list shows what a plant needs to grow well?  
   Options: Light, water, air, warmth and minerals / Only soil and water / Darkness, water and soil  
   **Answer:** Light, water, air, warmth and minerals. *Why:* Plants need light, water, air, warmth and minerals. Picking 'only soil and water' leaves out light, which plants use to make food, and air for their roots and leaves.
2. (mcq) What makes a test fair?  
   Options: You change everything at once / You change only one thing and keep everything else the same / You test only one plant  
   **Answer:** You change only one thing and keep everything else the same. *Why:* Changing only one thing tells you what caused the result. Picking 'test only one plant' misses that one plant can be a fluke.
3. (mcq) In the light test, you forget to water the dark cups. What is wrong with the test?  
   Options: Nothing / Two things changed, light and water, so you can't tell which caused the result / The cups were too small  
   **Answer:** Two things changed, light and water, so you can't tell which caused the result. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. Picking 'nothing' means noticing the planned change but not the accidental one.
4. (mcq) Where does the 0 on the ruler go when you measure a plant's height?  
   Options: At the soil / At the top of the cup / At the top of the plant  
   **Answer:** At the soil. *Why:* Height is measured from the soil up. Picking 'top of the cup' leaves out the part of the stem inside the cup.
5. (mcq) A seedling is 6 centimetres and 5 millimetres tall. How many millimetres is that?  
   Options: 65 millimetres / 11 millimetres / 6.5 millimetres  
   **Answer:** 65 millimetres. *Why:* Each centimetre is 10 millimetres. So 6 centimetres is 60 millimetres, and 5 more makes 65. Picking 11 means adding 6 and 5 without changing the units.
6. (mcq) Look at the chart. Which cup's seedlings grew taller?  
   Options: LIGHT / DARK / They were the same  
   **Answer:** DARK. *Why:* The DARK bar is taller: 14 cm against 8 cm. But remember, the dark ones were pale and weak. Picking LIGHT means answering 'which was healthier' instead of 'which was taller'.
7. (mcq) Which sentence is a good conclusion for the water test?  
   Options: Plants are nice. / We found that the JUST RIGHT seedlings grew tallest, at 13 cm. This shows moong needs some water, but not too much. / I watered the plants.  
   **Answer:** We found that the JUST RIGHT seedlings grew tallest, at 13 cm. This shows moong needs some water, but not too much.. *Why:* A good conclusion says what was found, uses a number as proof, and says what it shows. Picking 'I watered the plants' describes what you did, not what you found.
8. (mcq) A plant is tall, thin, pale and leaning towards the window. Which need is missing?  
   Options: Water / Light / Space  
   **Answer:** Light. *Why:* Leaning and pale are clues of too little light. Picking 'water' is common, but thirsty plants droop in dry soil.
9. (mcq) A plant has yellow leaves and their soil is always soggy and smells bad. What is wrong?  
   Options: Too little water / Too much water, so the roots can't get air / Too much light  
   **Answer:** Too much water, so the roots can't get air. *Why:* Yellow leaves can come from too little water or too much. Here the soil is soggy and smells bad, so the roots are getting too much water and no air. Picking 'too little water' means looking at the leaves but not at the soil.
10. (mcq) Is more water always better for a plant?  
   Options: Yes / No. Roots need air too, and soaked soil drowns them  
   **Answer:** No. Roots need air too, and soaked soil drowns them. *Why:* Each need must be just right. Picking 'yes' is one of the most common mistakes in caring for plants.
11. (mcq) Which is true about warmth and plants?  
   Options: The hotter, the faster they grow, with no limit / Warmth speeds up growth, but too much heat harms plants / Plants grow fastest in the fridge  
   **Answer:** Warmth speeds up growth, but too much heat harms plants. *Why:* Each plant has a just-right range. Picking 'no limit' misses that great heat harms the plant's helper molecules.
12. (mcq) Why is wheat sown in the cool winter in Punjab?  
   Options: Wheat grows best in cool weather / Wheat needs a flooded field / Wheat cannot grow in sunlight  
   **Answer:** Wheat grows best in cool weather. *Why:* Each crop has its own just right. Wheat likes it cool. Picking 'a flooded field' describes rice, not wheat.
13. (mcq) How does a seedling in the dark sense that it is dark?  
   Options: With tiny eyes on their leaves / With special light-sensing molecules, such as phytochrome / They cannot sense light  
   **Answer:** With special light-sensing molecules, such as phytochrome. *Why:* Plants have photoreceptor molecules that change in light. Picking 'eyes' gives plants an animal body part they don't have.
14. (mcq) Astronauts grow lettuce on the International Space Station, far from the Sun's light through a window. Which need do the station's pink-purple lamps give the plants?  
   Options: Light / Water / Minerals  
   **Answer:** Light. *Why:* The lamps give light, mostly red and blue, the colours leaves use most. Water and minerals come from a pillow of soil-like material. Picking 'water' mixes up what a lamp gives with what a watering can gives.
15. (mcq) Jagadish Chandra Bose built the crescograph, a machine that made a plant's growth look thousands of times bigger. What did it show?  
   Options: That a plant's growth speeds up and slows down from minute to minute / That plants never grow at night / That plants grow only when someone is watching  
   **Answer:** That a plant's growth speeds up and slows down from minute to minute. *Why:* Bose saw a plant's growth change within minutes, for example when they were touched or warmed. Plants respond to what is around them all the time. Picking 'never at night' is wrong: plants grow at night too.

## Applications
- Farmers sow each crop in the season whose warmth and rain suit it. Rabi wheat is sown in winter, and kharif rice in the monsoon.
- Greenhouses and polyhouses keep plants warm and protected, so farmers can grow vegetables out of season.
- Drip irrigation gives each plant just the right water, drop by drop, and saves a lot of water. Many Indian farms use it.
- Plant nurseries put sun-loving plants in full sun and shade-loving plants under green shade nets.
- Hydroponic farms on city rooftops grow lettuce, spinach and herbs in mineral water with no soil.
- Grow lights in vertical farms and space gardens often use red and blue lamps, the colours plants use most.
- Seed banks keep seeds cold and dry so they stay alive for many years.
- Fair tests are used by scientists who breed new crops, to find out which kind grows best in heat or drought.
- Weather forecasts help farmers decide when to sow and when to water.
- Space agencies are testing how to grow food for long journeys to the Moon and Mars.
- Gardeners use pots with drainage holes and add sand to heavy soil, so roots get air.
- Botanic gardens keep plants from deserts, rainforests and mountains alive by copying the light, water and warmth of their homes.

## Spotted around us
- Pale yellow grass under a brick, a mat or a parked car that has not moved for days.
- House plants leaning towards windows, in homes, shops and offices.
- Green shade nets over plant nurseries on the roadside, protecting young plants from strong sun.
- Wilting plants on a balcony on a hot May afternoon, standing up again in the cool evening.
- Long white sprouts on potatoes or onions kept in a dark kitchen basket.
- Rice fields standing in water in the monsoon, and wheat fields growing in the cool winter fog.
- Peepal and banyan saplings growing out of cracks in old walls and temple towers, with only a little dust and rainwater.
- Drip pipes and buried clay pots in gardens and farms, giving plants water slowly.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how does a plant feel warmth?** In 2016 scientists found that a light-sensing molecule, phytochrome B, also senses warmth. Since then they have found other warmth-sensing molecules. How they all work together is still being worked out.
- **Why does a plant's growth speed up and slow down from minute to minute?** Jagadish Chandra Bose saw this with his crescograph over a hundred years ago. Scientists now know many signals are involved, from light and warmth to water and the plant's own daily clock. But the whole picture is still not clear.
- **How will India's crops cope as summers get hotter?** In 2022, an early heat wave in March shrank India's wheat harvest. Scientists are breeding crops that cope better with heat, but nobody knows yet how well they will keep up.
- **Could we grow food in the soil of the Moon or Mars?** In 2022, small plants grew in real Moon soil, but slowly, and they showed signs of stress. Mars soil contains harmful salts. Scientists are still testing ways to make these soils safe for plants.
- **Do plants respond to sound in useful ways?** Some experiments show plants react to the vibrations of chewing insects, or of running water. Others find nothing. It is hard to test fairly, and scientists still argue.
- **How does a seedling decide how to share their stored food between roots, stem and leaves?** Scientists know that light, water and minerals all change the decision, through plant hormones. How a plant without a brain balances all of these is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- In the light test, a seedling was 3 cm tall on Day 3 and 8 cm tall on Day 7. How much did they grow between Day 3 and Day 7? **5 cm.**.
