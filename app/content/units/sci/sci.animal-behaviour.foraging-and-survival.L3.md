# Finding food and staying alive: how animals hunt, graze and hide
`sci.animal-behaviour.foraging-and-survival.L3`

**Summary.** Every animal, including us, has two jobs each day: find food, and not become someone else's food. Searching for food and getting it is called foraging. Plant eaters such as cows and elephants graze or browse for most of the day, because grass and leaves give little energy in each mouthful. Hunters chase their prey, wait in ambush, or build traps, and most of their hunts fail. Prey animals stay alive by watching, keeping still, running, living in groups and hiding. Hiding by matching the place where you rest is called camouflage, and it works only while the body and the place match.

**Intro.** *Cut 20 small strips from a newspaper and 20 from plain paper. Scatter them all over an open newspaper on the floor. Now pick up as many as you can in 20 seconds, one at a time. Which ones did you miss?* A bird hunting insects on a tree faces this test every day. So do the insects, who stay alive only if the bird misses them. In this unit you find out how animals find their food: by grazing, browsing, chasing, waiting and trapping. You also find out how prey animals stay alive, and why a body that is hidden in one place can be easy to see in another.

## Terms
- **Forage**: To forage is to search for food and get it, by finding, collecting or catching it. Every animal forages in their own way. *e.g.* A myna walking across a lawn and pecking at insects in the grass is foraging.
- **Predator**: A predator is an animal who hunts and eats other animals. *e.g.* A pond heron standing in a paddy field is a predator of small fish and frogs.
- **Prey**: Prey are the animals that a predator hunts and eats. *e.g.* Moths and mosquitoes are prey for the house gecko on your wall.
- **Herbivore**: A herbivore is an animal who eats only plants. *e.g.* Cows, goats, elephants and spotted deer are herbivores.
- **Carnivore**: A carnivore is an animal who eats mainly other animals. *e.g.* Tigers, spiders, frogs and kingfishers are carnivores.
- **Omnivore**: An omnivore is an animal who eats both plants and animals. *e.g.* Crows eat grain, fruit, insects and leftovers. Most humans are omnivores too.
- **Scavenger**: A scavenger eats animals that are already dead, or food that others have left behind. *e.g.* Vultures and crows are scavengers. They clear away dead animals.
- **Graze**: To graze is to eat grass or other low plants, bite after bite, while moving slowly along. *e.g.* Buffaloes grazing on the bank of a canal near Thanjavur.
- **Browse**: To browse is to eat leaves, shoots and twigs from bushes and trees. *e.g.* A goat standing on her back legs to reach the leaves of a bush is browsing.
- **Ambush**: An ambush is a hunt in which the predator waits, still and hidden, and attacks when the prey comes close. *e.g.* A mugger crocodile lying still in a river, with only her eyes above the water, is waiting in ambush.
- **Camouflage**: Camouflage is colour, pattern or shape that makes a body hard to see against the place where they are. *e.g.* An oakleaf butterfly with her wings closed looks just like a dry brown leaf.
- **Countershading**: Countershading means being darker on the back and paler on the belly. Light from above makes the back bright and leaves the belly in shadow, so the two even out and the body looks flat. *e.g.* A mackerel, called bangda in many Indian markets, is dark blue on top and silvery white underneath.
- **Alarm call**: An alarm call is a sound an animal makes when they spot danger. Others who hear it look up, run or hide. *e.g.* When crows spot a cat, they caw loudly again and again, and more crows fly in.
- **Herd**: A herd is a big group of animals, such as deer or cattle, who feed and move together. A group of birds is a flock, and a group of fish is a school. *e.g.* Spotted deer, called chital, graze in herds in Bandipur and Kanha.
- **Chewing the cud**: Cows, buffaloes, goats and deer swallow their food quickly, then later bring it back up into the mouth and chew it again. This is called chewing the cud. *e.g.* A cow lying on the road, chewing slowly with no food in front of her, is chewing the cud.

## Explanations
### What foraging is · *defined*
**Definition:** Foraging is searching for food and getting it: finding it, catching or collecting it, and eating it.

Every animal forages, from an ant to an elephant. Humans are animals, and we forage too. For most of human history, people gathered fruit, seeds and roots, fished and hunted. Many people still pick wild greens, fish and collect honey. Foraging always costs something. It takes time and energy, and it can be dangerous, because a hungry animal out in the open may be seen by a predator. So every forager balances two needs: eat enough, and stay safe.
- e.g. A purple sunbird visits flower after flower, drinking nectar with a long, curved beak.
- e.g. Flamingos in the Rann of Kutch dip their heads upside down in shallow water and strain out tiny animals and plants with their beaks.
- e.g. In the Nilgiris, Kurumba honey gatherers climb cliffs to collect honey from the nests of wild rock bees.
### Grazers and browsers eat for most of the day · *defined*
**Definition:** Herbivores who eat grass are called grazers. Herbivores who eat the leaves and twigs of bushes and trees are called browsers.

Grass and leaves are easy to find, but each mouthful gives little energy. They are also tough and slow to digest. So a plant eater must eat a lot, and spend much of the day eating. Cows, buffaloes, goats and deer have a stomach with four parts. Tiny living things called microbes live in it and help break down the tough plants. These animals swallow quickly while feeding. Later, while resting, they chew the cud, which grinds the food finer. Every moment spent with the head down to eat is a moment the animal cannot watch for danger. That is one reason grazers feed in herds.
- e.g. An adult Asian elephant feeds for most of the day and part of the night, and can eat well over 100 kilograms of plants in a day.
- e.g. A goat browses on a thorny bush, picking the leaves out between the thorns with her lips.
- e.g. Chital deer often feed under trees where grey langur monkeys are eating, and pick up the leaves and fruit the langurs drop.
### Three ways to hunt: chase, ambush and trap · *defined*
**Definition:** A predator who chases runs, flies or swims after prey. A predator in ambush waits, still and hidden, until prey comes close. A predator with a trap builds something that catches prey for them.

Chasing uses a lot of energy, so chasers usually hunt in short bursts, or hunt together. Dholes, India's wild dogs, hunt in packs and chase deer for a long way. Ambush hunters save energy by waiting. A pond heron stands still in shallow water, then stabs at a fish. A tiger creeps close through tall grass, keeping low, and then rushes. Trappers build. A spider spins a sticky web and waits. An antlion larva, the young of a winged insect, digs a cone-shaped pit in dry dust and waits at the bottom for an ant to slip in. Most hunts fail. A tiger often has to try many times before she makes a kill.
- e.g. A house gecko waits beside a tube light, where moths and flies gather, then darts.
- e.g. Dragonflies catch other insects in the air. In one study, some dragonflies caught their prey in more than 9 out of every 10 tries.
- e.g. Banded archerfish in the mangroves of the Sundarbans spit a jet of water to knock insects off leaves into the water.
### Scavengers and omnivores eat what they find · *defined*
**Definition:** A scavenger eats animals that are already dead, or food others leave. An omnivore eats both plants and animals.

Not every meat eater hunts. Vultures can spot a dead animal from high in the sky and clear the body away within hours. This stops the dead body rotting and spreading disease. Crows are both omnivores and scavengers. They eat grain, fruit, insects, eggs and leftovers. Because they eat so many kinds of food, crows do well almost anywhere, including in big cities. Humans are omnivores too.
- e.g. A crow carries a piece of chapati to a water tank and dips it to soften it.
- e.g. Kites circle over fish markets in Chennai and Kolkata, swooping down for scraps.
- e.g. Jackals eat fruit, insects and the leftovers of other hunters' kills.
### Camouflage: a match between a body and a place · *defined*
**Definition:** Camouflage is colour, pattern or shape that makes a body hard to see in the place where they are.

Camouflage works only against the right background. A green leaf insect is hidden among green leaves, but easy to see on a white wall. That is what your paper strips showed. Many camouflaged animals also choose where to rest. Some moths land on bark, then walk and turn until their pattern lines up with the cracks in the bark. A team of scientists in South Korea filmed this in 2012. Stripes and spots break up the outline of a body, so a hunter's eye cannot easily find the edge. Countershading, a dark back and a pale belly, evens out the shadow under the body.
- e.g. Oakleaf butterflies in India's forests look like a dry leaf when their wings are closed, with a line like the middle vein of a leaf.
- e.g. Ghost crabs on the beaches of Chennai and Puri are the colour of sand, and dash into their burrows when something comes near.
- e.g. A jungle nightjar lays her eggs on bare ground and sits on them all day, her streaky brown feathers matching the dead leaves around her.
### What does a deer see when a tiger is near? · *idea*
To us, a tiger is bright orange. But deer have eyes that see fewer colours than ours do. Scientists who studied deer eyes think that, to a deer, orange looks much like green. If they are right, a tiger in green grass is better hidden from a deer than from us. So camouflage depends on three things together: the body, the place, and the eyes that look. Many birds can also see ultraviolet light, which human eyes cannot see at all. A pattern that looks plain to us might stand out to a bird.
- e.g. Bees see ultraviolet patterns on flowers that we cannot see.
- e.g. A tiger's orange coat may look green to a deer in tall grass, but bright to a person in a jeep.
### Staying alive: watch, keep still, run and stay together · *defined*
**Definition:** Prey animals stay alive by noticing danger early, keeping still, running away, living in groups, and carrying protection such as shells or spines.

Many hunters notice movement first. So a prey animal who keeps still is harder to spot, as you found when the moving strip was seen first. Living in a group helps too. A herd or flock has many eyes, so someone spots the predator sooner, and each animal can spend more time eating. An alarm call warns everyone. Chital deer and langur monkeys listen to each other's alarm calls. Langurs sitting high in trees can see a tiger or leopard from far away, and chital have a sharp sense of smell. Some animals carry protection. A tortoise pulls into her shell, an Indian pangolin rolls into a ball of hard scales, and a porcupine raises and rattles sharp quills.
- e.g. In a study in England, a trained hawk caught fewer pigeons from big flocks than from small ones, because big flocks noticed the hawk and flew off sooner.
- e.g. A chital who senses danger stamps a front foot, gives a sharp alarm call and raises her tail to show its white underside.
- e.g. A frog in a pond freezes and stays very still when a heron's shadow passes.
### Hunters and prey shape each other · *defined*
**Definition:** Over a very long time, predators and prey change together. When prey get better at hiding or escaping, the predators who survive are the ones better at finding and catching them. Scientists sometimes call this an arms race.

This happens over many, many generations, not in one animal's life. Prey who were easy to see were eaten more often, so fewer of them had young. Over thousands of generations, the colours that stay hidden became common. Hunters who could not find enough prey also had fewer young. A deer's sharp ears and fast legs, and a tiger's stripes and quiet paws, make sense only together. Hunting is very old. The family of jellyfish, corals and sea anemones is the oldest group of animals known to hunt with venom. Their tentacles carry stinging cells that fire like tiny harpoons.
- e.g. Bats hunt moths by listening for echoes. Some moths can hear a bat's calls and drop to the ground or fly in loops to escape.
- e.g. A tiger's soft paw pads let her walk almost silently, and a deer's large ears can turn to catch tiny sounds.
### Do animals think and feel while they hunt and hide? · *debated*
Hunters and prey make decisions all the time: where to look, when to strike, when to give up, where to rest. A spider who catches little often moves her web somewhere else. A moth turns on the bark until she is hidden. A tiger who has been seen walks away instead of wasting a chase. These are choices made by thinking, feeling beings. Some scientists call much of this automatic behaviour, shaped over many generations. Scientists still argue about what each animal feels.
- e.g. A house gecko picks the spot beside the tube light, where insects gather, and comes back to it night after night.
- e.g. A chital who hears a langur's alarm call stops eating and looks towards the danger.
- **Many animal behaviour scientists:** Hunting and hiding are full of choices that change with the situation. Prey animals show fear in their bodies the way we do: their hearts race, and they freeze or run. It makes sense to say they think and feel.
- **Other scientists:** Much of hunting and hiding is behaviour an animal is born with, shaped over many generations. We should be careful not to assume an animal feels exactly what a human would feel in the same place.

## Mostly agreed
- Every animal must find food, and most must also avoid being eaten. Different animals do this in very different ways. *How we know:* Scientists have watched how thousands of kinds of animals feed, in the wild and in zoos.
- Camouflage helps prey survive. *How we know:* In experiments, wild birds were given paper or pastry 'insects' of different colours. They ate fewer of the ones that matched the background, just as in your paper caterpillar test.
- Grass and leaves give less energy in each mouthful than meat does, so plant eaters spend more of their day eating. *How we know:* Scientists have timed how long animals feed, and measured the energy in their food.
- Living in a group helps prey notice predators sooner. *How we know:* In a study in England, a trained hawk caught fewer pigeons from big flocks than from small ones. Studies of many birds and deer found that animals in bigger groups each look up less, yet the group notices danger sooner.
- Most hunts by tigers and lions end without a kill. *How we know:* People who have watched tigers and lions for years have counted many more failed hunts than successful ones.

## How we found out
**Abbott Thayer, an American painter, with his son Gerald Thayer, and later Hannah Rowland and her team in Britain, 1896 (the painted model birds), 1909 (the Thayers' book), and the 2000s (the pastry caterpillar tests).** Abbott Thayer was an American painter who loved birds. In the 1890s he noticed that many animals are darker on their backs and paler on their bellies. He worked out why. Light comes from the sky, so it brightens an animal's back and leaves the belly in shadow. A pale belly evens out that shadow, so the body looks flat and blends into the background. In 1896 he showed this to bird scientists with painted model birds. A model painted dark on top and pale below almost vanished. This is called countershading, and Thayer was right. But then he went too far. In a book he wrote with his son Gerald in 1909, he said that almost every colour on every animal is for hiding. He even said a flamingo's pink hides her against the sky at dawn or dusk. Other naturalists, including Theodore Roosevelt, said this was wrong, because some colours are for showing off or for warning. For about a hundred years, few people tested countershading with real hunters. Then Hannah Rowland and her team in Britain made small pastry caterpillars, coloured in different ways, and laid them on branches. Wild birds ate fewer of the countershaded ones.  
*What people believed before:* Abbott Thayer believed that almost every animal colour, even a flamingo's pink, is for hiding. Other naturalists showed that some colours are for showing off or warning. His idea of countershading was right, and a century later Hannah Rowland's tests with wild birds and pastry caterpillars showed that it really helps prey survive.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A tiger can catch any animal she chooses.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Tigers are big, strong and fast, and they are top predators in Indian forests.  
  Against: Most of a tiger's hunts fail. Deer hear, smell or see her, and run. Once she has been seen, she usually gives up. A tiger may go several days without a kill.
- **Prey animals are helpless.** (A common idea, from films that show only the kills; *rejected*)  
  For: Films and photos usually show the moment a hunter catches someone.  
  Against: Prey animals escape most attacks. They keep watch, give alarm calls, run, kick, hide, and live in groups. Films show the kills because they are rare and dramatic.
- **Chameleons change colour mainly to hide.** (A very common idea; *debated*)  
  For: A resting chameleon's greens and browns do help them hide among leaves.  
  Against: Studies by Devi Stuart-Fox, a scientist in Australia, and her colleagues suggest that chameleons change colour most when signalling to other chameleons, for example in a contest or when courting. Temperature matters too.
- **Every colour on every animal is there to hide them.** (Abbott Thayer, an American painter, in a book of 1909; *rejected*)  
  For: Many animals really are hidden by their colours. Thayer was right about countershading.  
  Against: Some colours are for showing off, such as a peacock's tail, or for warning that an animal tastes bad. A pink flamingo is not hidden against the sunset.
- **Chimpanzees eat only plants.** (What many scientists thought before 1960; *rejected*)  
  For: Chimpanzees eat mostly fruit and leaves.  
  Against: In 1960, Jane Goodall saw wild chimpanzees in Gombe, Tanzania, eating meat. Later she saw them hunt monkeys together. Our closest cousins are omnivores, like most humans.

## Roots
We have learned how animals find food, and how prey stay alive by watching, keeping still and hiding. Now we ask what hunting and hiding tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your newspaper strips were hard to find on newspaper, but easy to find on the plain sheet. The strips stayed exactly the same, and only the place under them changed. So hiding is a match between a body and a place. A moth hidden on bark is seen at once on a white wall.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say an animal 'is camouflaged', as if hiding were a part of their body, like their legs. But the same body is hidden in one place and seen in another. Hiding belongs to the body and the place together, and to the eyes that look.  
*Evidence:* In the Paper Caterpillars test, the hunter missed most newspaper strips on the newspaper, then caught most of them on the plain sheet. Nothing about the strips changed. Only the background did.

- *who am i:* You are an animal who forages too. Like a bird's, your eyes notice what moves and what does not match.
- *what is life:* Every living being needs food, and most animals must also avoid becoming food. Hunter and hunted each stay alive by noticing the other.
- *what is this world:* In this world, how a thing looks depends on where it is and who is looking. A tiger we call orange may look green to a deer.

Sunlight feeds the grass, the grass feeds the chital, and the chital feed the tiger. Over millions of years, the tiger's stripes and quiet paws were shaped by the deer's sharp eyes and ears. The deer's watchfulness was shaped by the tiger. Neither can be understood alone. One flow of sunlight and life runs through many bodies, and each body is shaped by meeting the others.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.10.6:** The one who understands sings: I am food, I am food, I am food. I am the eater of food, I am the eater of food, I am the eater of food. (Every forager in this unit is both. The chital eat grass and are food for the tiger. When the tiger dies, her body feeds vultures, beetles and the soil, and the soil feeds the grass.)
- **Thirukkural 490, in the chapter on knowing the right time:** When the time is not right, wait, as still as the heron. When the time is right, strike, as surely as the heron strikes. (This is hunting by ambush. The pond heron in the paddy field, the gecko by the tube light and the tiger in the grass all wait, still, and strike only when the prey comes close.)
- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18:** Whatever comes into being by depending on other things has no being of its own, apart from them. (Being hidden comes into being only from a body, a place and the eyes looking, all together. Take away any one of them, and the hiding is gone.)
- Science (settled): In experiments with wild birds and paper or pastry prey, prey who matched the background were eaten less often.
- Science (open): What another animal sees and feels when they look at the world can be studied only from outside, by testing their eyes and watching what they do.
- Other view, Many biologists: A moth's colour came about over many generations, because moths who were easier to see were eaten more often. That explains camouflage fully. It is a fact about bodies, backgrounds and hungry birds, and it carries no message about oneness.

For one minute, notice everything in this room that moves. It might be a curtain, a fan, a fly or a shadow. When the minute is up, tell me what you noticed first. Was it something still, or something moving?

- Ask: A deer may see a tiger's orange as green. Whose colour is the true one?
- Ask: The chital eat, and are eaten. Is anyone in the forest only an eater, and never food?
- Ask: When you freeze in a game of statues, what are you hiding from: the eyes, or the movement?

## Story: The Bird on the Jeep Track
*Bandipur Tiger Reserve in Karnataka, chital, grey langurs, jungle nightjars, house geckos and the way chital and langurs listen to each other's alarm calls are all real. Meenakshi, Arun, Lakshmi and this morning are made up.*

Meenakshi had waited all year for this. Her aunt, Lakshmi Chikkamma, was a forest watcher in Bandipur, in Karnataka. This summer, Meenakshi and her cousin Arun were allowed to ride with her on the morning round.

Arun was eleven. He had watched a hundred wildlife films on his tablet in Bengaluru, and he told everyone so. 'I know everything about tigers,' he said, as the jeep bumped along the red mud track. 'They are the best hunters in the world. They catch whatever they want.'

Lakshmi Chikkamma smiled and said nothing. She stopped the jeep, pointed at the track just ahead of the front wheel, and whispered, 'Tell me what you see.'

Arun looked. 'Dry leaves. Mud. Some stones.'

Meenakshi looked for a long time. One of the dry leaves had an eye. The eye blinked. 'A bird!' she whispered. 'Sitting right on the ground!'

It was a nightjar. Her feathers were brown, grey and black, in tiny streaks and spots, just like the dead leaves around her. 'She is sitting on her eggs,' said Lakshmi Chikkamma. 'Nightjars make no nest. They lay their eggs on bare ground. All day she sits still, and her feathers hide her. At dusk she flies up to catch moths.' She steered the jeep carefully around the bird, who did not move a single feather.

'How did you see her?' Arun asked, a little cross.

'I looked for something that did not belong,' said Meenakshi. 'Her eye was shiny. Leaves are not shiny.'

Further on, the track opened into a grassy meadow near a waterhole. A herd of chital, the spotted deer, was grazing. Above them, in a big tree, a troop of grey langur monkeys sat eating leaves. Every now and then a langur dropped a leafy twig, and a deer nibbled it up.

'Why do the deer stand under the monkeys?' Meenakshi asked.

'Watch how they eat,' said Lakshmi Chikkamma. 'Each deer takes a bite, chews, and lifts their head to look and listen. Then they bite again. Grass gives so little energy that they must eat for most of the day. But while a deer's head is down, they cannot see. So they eat together, and many eyes keep watch. The langurs sit high up, where they can see far. And the leaves they drop are a gift.'

Arun was counting. 'Twenty-two deer. Twenty-two times two is forty-four eyes. And the monkeys have more.'

Suddenly a langur gave a loud, harsh bark. Then another. Every chital lifted their head at once. One stamped a front foot. A deer gave a high, sharp call, and white tails went up all over the herd.

'Alarm calls,' whispered Lakshmi Chikkamma. 'The langurs have seen something. Now the deer are telling everyone, even the hunter, that they have seen her.' She switched off the engine. Nobody spoke.

Arun stared at the tall golden grass at the edge of the meadow. 'There's nothing there,' he whispered.

Meenakshi stared too. She tried her trick. What did not belong? Then she saw it. Two black stripes curved a little differently from the shadows of the grass stems. A round ear, with a white spot on the back, gave a flick. 'Tiger,' she breathed.

The tiger was lying flat, perhaps forty steps from the nearest deer. Her orange and black coat melted into the dry grass and the stripes of shadow between the stems. She must have been creeping closer for a long time. Now every deer was looking straight at her.

The tiger lifted her head and looked at the herd. The herd looked back. For a long moment, nothing moved. Then the tiger stood up, stretched, and walked slowly away into the trees.

Arun let out his breath. 'She didn't even try! I thought tigers caught whatever they wanted.'

'Most of a tiger's hunts fail,' said Lakshmi Chikkamma. 'She has to get very close without being noticed, and then rush. Once the deer have seen her, a chase would only waste her energy. A good hunter knows when to stop. She will try again somewhere else, perhaps tonight.'

'So the deer won,' said Arun.

'Today,' said Lakshmi Chikkamma. 'Tomorrow the tiger may win. She has to eat too. If tigers never caught a deer, there would be no tigers. If tigers caught every deer, there would soon be no deer, and then no tigers either.'

On the way back they passed the nightjar again, still as a leaf. This time Arun saw her first. 'I looked for the eye,' he said proudly. 'Meenakshi taught me.'

That night, at the forest quarters, a house gecko sat very still on the wall beside the tube light. A moth fluttered close. The gecko waited, and waited, and then darted. Meenakshi laughed. 'Look, Arun,' she said. 'A tiny tiger. She hunts by waiting too.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: the nightjar and the tiger were both hidden by camouflage, because their colours and patterns matched the dry leaves and grass where they lay. Meenakshi found them by looking for what did not match: a shiny eye, a stripe curving the wrong way, an ear that moved. The chital graze for most of the day because grass gives little energy, so they eat in a herd, where many eyes keep watch, and they feed near langurs, who see far from the treetops. Once the alarm calls showed the tiger that she had been seen, her ambush could no longer work, so she gave up rather than waste energy on a chase. Most hunts fail, and the hunter and the hunted both depend on the other to stay as they are.

- Ask: How did the nightjar stay safe without a nest?
- Ask: What trick did Meenakshi use to find the nightjar and the tiger?
- Ask: Why did the chital graze under the langurs' tree?
- Ask: Why did the tiger walk away without chasing the deer?
- Ask: Arun knew a lot from films, but Meenakshi spotted the animals first. What did she do that he did not?

### g2 · Freeze, Tiger! (15 min)

## Experiments
### e1 · Paper Caterpillars: which ones does a hungry hunter miss? (30 min)
- **question:** Do prey that match the background survive a hunter better than prey that stand out? And what happens when the background changes?
- **predict:** Guess first. On a newspaper, will you catch more newspaper strips or more plain strips in 20 seconds? On a plain sheet, which kind will you catch more of? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use 20 strips of each kind, all the same size. Hunt for the same time, 20 seconds, with one hand, one strip at a time. Someone else scatters the strips while the hunter's eyes are closed. Do 3 rounds on each background, because one round can be a fluke. Only the background changes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart with two bars for each background: newspaper strips caught and plain strips caught.
- **results table:** Background | Round | Newspaper strips caught | Plain strips caught
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold one newspaper sheet into four layers. 2. Cut strips about 3 cm long and 1 cm wide, through all four layers. 3. Stop when you have 20 newspaper strips. 4. Do the same with the plain paper, to make 20 plain strips. 5. Keep all 40 strips about the same size.  
  *Parent says:* These strips are caterpillars, and you are a hungry bird. Some caterpillars match the newspaper and some don't.  
  *Child mode:* Fold the newspaper. Cut 20 small strips. Cut 20 strips of plain paper too. Make them all the same size. These are your caterpillars.
  - Note: Cutting through four layers saves time. The strips do not need to be perfect. Choose plain paper the same colour as your plain background, for example white strips and a white bedsheet.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason before any hunting. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* On the newspaper, which caterpillars will you catch more of? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. On the newspaper, which strips will you catch more of? Why?
  - Ask: Which caterpillars will you catch more of on the newspaper, and why? (good: The plain ones, because they stand out.; Fewer newspaper ones, because they look like the background.)
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the second newspaper flat on the floor. 2. The hunter closes their eyes. 3. Scatter all 40 strips over the newspaper, well mixed. 4. Say 'Go!' and start a 20-second timer. 5. The hunter picks up strips one at a time with one hand. 6. Each strip goes into the bowl before the next. 7. Say 'Stop!' at 20 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes while I scatter the caterpillars. Ready? Pick them up one at a time, as fast as you can. Go!  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Wait for 'Go!' Pick up one strip at a time. Drop each one in the bowl. Be quick! Stop at 20 seconds.
  - Note: One hand and one strip at a time is like a bird pecking one insect at a time. It keeps every round fair. Stand up while hunting, so you look down from the same height each time.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the newspaper strips and the plain strips in the bowl. 2. Write both numbers in the table. 3. Put all 40 strips back on the newspaper. 4. Do 2 more rounds, so there are 3 rounds in all.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count what you caught. Then we'll hunt twice more, because one round could be a fluke.  
  *Child mode:* Count each kind in your bowl. Write the numbers. Put them all back. Hunt 2 more times.
  - Check: On the newspaper, which caterpillars were left behind most? → **The newspaper strips, because they matched the background**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take away the newspaper and spread the plain sheet on the floor. 2. Ask for a new guess and a reason. 3. Scatter all 40 strips on it while the hunter's eyes are closed. 4. Hunt for 20 seconds, 3 rounds, as before. 5. Write the numbers each time.  
  *Parent says:* The caterpillars have landed somewhere new. Which kind will you catch more of now? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Now hunt on the plain sheet. Guess first. Which kind will you catch more now? Why? Hunt 3 times. Write the numbers.
  - Ask: What changed between the two hunts? (good: Only the background changed.; The strips were the same, but the place under them was different.)
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A strong answer sounds like this. What I think: prey that match the background are harder for a hunter to find. What I saw: on the newspaper I caught about 15 plain strips and only 3 newspaper strips. On the plain sheet it was the other way round. Why: the newspaper strips matched the newspaper, so my eyes missed them. On the plain sheet they did not match, so they stood out.
### e2 · Still or Moving: which caterpillar do eyes find first? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a hunter spot hidden prey sooner when the prey moves than when the prey keeps still?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will you spot a newspaper caterpillar on a newspaper faster if the strip keeps still, or if the strip wriggles a little? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same newspaper strip on the same newspaper every time, so it is always equally well hidden. Only one thing changes: whether the strip moves. The hunter stands in the same place each time. Do 3 tries of each, because one try can be a fluke.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Add up the three still times and the three wriggling times, and compare them.
- **results table:** Try | Still strip: seconds to spot | Wriggling strip: seconds to spot
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread a newspaper on the floor. 2. Poke 3 small holes in it with a pencil, far apart. 3. Tape one end of a 60 cm thread to the end of a newspaper strip.  
  *Parent says:* We are making a caterpillar that can wriggle. The thread will run under the paper, so the hunter won't see you pull it.  
  *Child mode:* Help tape a thread to the end of one newspaper strip. The thread will go down through a hole and under the paper. Then the strip can wriggle.
  - Note: Use a light-coloured thread. Sit at the side of the newspaper and keep your hand still under the edge, so the hunter cannot see where you pull from.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Will you spot a hidden caterpillar faster if the strip keeps still, or if the strip wriggles a little? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Still or wriggling: which will you spot faster? Why?
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The hunter stands one big step from the newspaper and turns round. 2. Lay the strip on the newspaper about 2 cm from one hole, with the taped end nearer the hole. 3. Push the thread down through that hole and under the newspaper to where you sit. 4. Say 'Look!' and start the timer. 5. Stop the timer when the hunter points at the strip. 6. Do this 3 times, using a different hole each time. Keep the strip still every time.  
  *Parent says:* Turn round. Now look! Point the moment you spot the strip.  
  *Child mode:* Turn round. When you hear 'Look!', search for the strip. Point the moment you spot the strip.
  - Note: If your child takes longer than 30 seconds, write 'more than 30' and move on.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do 3 more tries, using the holes in a new order and threading the strip each time. 2. This time, give the thread a small tug about once every two seconds, so the strip jerks a little. 3. Time how long the hunter takes to point.  
  *Parent says:* Turn round again. Same game, but this time the caterpillar might be wriggling. Look!  
  *Child mode:* Turn round. Look again. Point the moment you spot the strip.
  - Check: Which caterpillar did you usually spot faster? → **The wriggling one**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this. What I think: moving gives hidden prey away. What I saw: the still strip took me 12, 9 and 15 seconds to find, but the wriggling one took only 2, 1 and 3 seconds. Why: my eyes noticed the movement straight away, even though the colour matched.

### c1 · A Feeder for Small Beaks (30 min)
- **problem:** When grain is put out for small birds such as sparrows, bigger birds such as crows and pigeons often eat most of it. Design a feeder from a plastic bottle that a small beak can reach into, but a big beak cannot.
- **your turn:** Now design your own small-beak feeder. Draw it, build it, and test it with the pencil and the marker. If you can, hang it at a window and watch who comes.
- **constraints:** Use only things from home.; A pencil is the small beak. A thick marker pen is the big beak.; The small beak must reach the grain. The big beak must not.; The feeder must not hurt any bird: no sharp edges and no wire loops.
- **general:** Cut feeding holes just big enough for the pencil, but too small for the marker.; Put a short perch under each hole, so only light birds can hold on.; Hang the feeder on a string, so it swings. Heavy birds find it harder to land on.
- **creative:** Make a cage of ice-cream sticks or rolled paper around the feeder, with gaps a sparrow can slip through but a crow cannot.; Put the holes low on the bottle and fill it with only a little grain, so a beak has to reach deep inside to get any.; Make a feeder that hangs under a small roof, so big birds cannot land on top.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the pencil and the marker. 2. Explain the problem.  
  *Parent says:* Big birds often eat all the grain before sparrows get any. Can you make a feeder only a small beak can use?  
  *Child mode:* The pencil is a sparrow's beak. The marker is a crow's beak. Make a feeder only the pencil can reach into.
- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws a design. 2. Together, mark the holes. 3. A grown-up cuts the holes with scissors. 4. Smooth any sharp edges with tape.  
  *Parent says:* Draw your idea first. Then we'll make the holes together. I'll do the cutting.  
  *Child mode:* Draw your feeder. Mark where the holes go. A grown-up cuts them. Cover sharp edges with tape.

## Applications
- Soldiers' uniforms and vehicles use camouflage patterns. Abbott Thayer's ideas helped start this in the First World War.
- Wildlife photographers sit in hides and keep still, so animals do not notice them.
- Farmers welcome ladybirds, spiders and birds who eat crop pests, instead of using so much poison.
- Forest departments count deer and other prey, to know whether a forest can feed its tigers.
- Bird feeders with small holes let sparrows and sunbirds eat without bigger birds taking everything.
- Fishing nets work like a spider's web: a trap that catches food without chasing it.
- Studying how cows and goats graze helps farmers decide how many animals a field can feed.
- Scientists copy countershading and disruptive patterns when they design things that should be hard to see.

## Open questions
- **Do prey animals feel fear the way we do?** Their bodies react like ours: hearts race, and they freeze or run. But nobody can feel what another animal feels, so scientists still argue.
- **How does a hunter decide when to give up?** Hunters seem to weigh how close they are, how tired they are and whether they have been seen. How they put all this together is not fully known.
- **What does the world look like through a deer's eyes, or a bird's?** Scientists can test which colours an eye can sense, but not what the animal experiences when they look.
- **How many animals hide in ways we cannot see?** Some animals may hide or show patterns in ultraviolet light, or hide their smell. Humans cannot sense these, so many may still be undiscovered.
- **How did hunting first begin?** The first hunters lived in the sea hundreds of millions of years ago and left few traces. Scientists piece the story together from fossils and from living animals such as jellyfish.
- **Does a camouflaged moth know that she is hidden?** Some moths turn on bark until they are better hidden. Whether they know they are hidden, or simply settle when the bark feels right under their feet, is not known.

Who collected more food? **Ravi, with about 60 grains' worth.**. 3 beans × 20 = about 60 rice grains' worth. Asha had 40 grains. 60 is more than 40, so Ravi collected more food, even though he picked up far fewer things. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- These are made-up numbers. In one month, a tiger tries 20 hunts and makes 2 kills. How many hunts fail? What fraction of her hunts work? **18 hunts fail. 2 out of 20 work, which is 1 out of every 10.**. 20 − 2 = 18 failed hunts. 2 out of 20 is the same as 1 out of 10, because 20 ÷ 2 = 10. So only 1 hunt in every 10 worked. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
