# How to measure a force, and why we all use newtons
`sci.forces.measuring-forces.L3`

**Summary.** How many coins in a cup does it take to pull a notebook across a table? You and a grown-up can get different numbers and both be right, because you used different coins. That is why scientists everywhere agreed to measure force in one shared unit, the newton. You will invent your own unit, then learn to measure force in newtons with a forcemeter, a spring tool. Last, you will meet forces from the tiny pull of one molecule to the huge pull between the Earth and the Moon.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes. Hold a 100 gram packet in one hand and a 1 kilogram packet of rice in the other. How many times harder does the big one pull your hand down? Now, how could you tell a friend in another city exactly how hard it pulls?* Words like 'heavy' and 'light' mean different things to different people. Your 'heavy' may be another person's 'light'. A shopkeeper selling rice, an engineer building a bridge and a doctor testing your grip need more than that. They need a number that means the same thing to everyone. To get one, people choose a unit and count it. You will start by making up your own unit of pull. Then you will find out what goes wrong when other people make up theirs.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can invent my own unit of pull, such as 'one coin', and use it to compare pulls.
1. I can explain why two careful people can get different numbers for one pull. I can say why we need one agreed unit.
2. I can say that forces are counted in newtons. One newton is about the pull of a small apple. I can explain that the newton is an agreement between people.
3. I can use a forcemeter properly. I check the zero, pull steadily, read the scale at eye level and write the unit.
4. I can explain how a forcemeter works: a spring stretches more when the pull is bigger.
5. I can make a guess with a reason and test it fairly. I can say what I think, what I saw and why.
6. I can explain that weight is a force, so the same bag would give a smaller number on the Moon.
7. I can say what a force does, and what a push is at the size of atoms. I can name something scientists still do not know about forces.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces make things start moving, stop, speed up, slow down, turn or change shape. *e.g.* You pull a drawer open. You push a door shut.
- **Size of a force**: How strong a push or a pull is. *e.g.* Lifting a pencil takes a small pull. Lifting a full bucket takes a big pull.
- **Measure**: To find how big something is by counting how many units it takes to match it. *e.g.* A pencil is 15 centimetres long, because 15 centimetre steps fit along it. A notebook's pull is 6 coins, because 6 coins in a cup start it sliding.
- **Unit**: One agreed amount that we count again and again. Centimetres are a unit of length. Newtons are a unit of force. *e.g.* In the coin-pull test, your unit was the pull of one coin. 'Six' meant six of those pulls.
- **Standard**: The thing everyone agrees to check their unit against, so that everyone's unit is the same size. *e.g.* From 1889 to 2019, one metal cylinder near Paris was the standard kilogram. Every other kilogram was checked against it.
- **Newton (N)**: The unit of force that scientists everywhere agreed to share. It is named after the scientist Isaac Newton. The short way to write it is N. *e.g.* A small apple of about 100 grams pulls down on your hand with about 1 newton.
- **Forcemeter**: A tool that shows how big a pull is. It has a spring inside, a hook at the bottom and a scale of numbers. People also call it a spring balance or a newton meter. *e.g.* Hook a school bag on a forcemeter. The pointer shows how hard the bag pulls down.
- **Spring**: A coil of wire that stretches when you pull it and goes back when you let go. A bigger pull makes it stretch further. *e.g.* There is a spring inside a ballpoint pen. Many doors have a long spring to pull them shut.
- **Scale**: The row of marks and numbers on a tool such as a ruler or a forcemeter. You read the number where the pointer stops. *e.g.* The scale on a ruler has centimetres. The scale on a forcemeter has newtons.
- **Pointer**: The line or arrow on a forcemeter or a dial that moves to show the reading. *e.g.* On a forcemeter, the pointer slides down the scale as you pull harder.
- **Reading**: The number you read on a tool such as a forcemeter, with its unit. *e.g.* The reading on the forcemeter is 4 newtons.
- **Zero**: The starting mark on a scale. Before you start, the pointer must sit exactly on zero. *e.g.* With nothing on the hook, the pointer should point to 0.
- **Range**: The smallest and the biggest force a tool can show. Pulling harder than its range can spoil the spring. *e.g.* A 0 to 10 newton forcemeter is good for an apple, but not for a bucket of water.
- **Calibrate**: To put the marks on a tool by testing it with things you already know. *e.g.* You hang a 100 gram packet on your homemade forcemeter. You mark that place '1 N', because 100 grams pulls with about 1 newton.
- **Estimate**: A careful guess of a size, made before you test. *e.g.* I estimate my water bottle pulls with about 5 newtons, because it feels like five apples. Then I test it.
- **Weight**: The pull of a planet on a thing. Weight is a force, so scientists count it in newtons. *e.g.* A 1 kilogram packet of rice has a weight of about 10 newtons on Earth.
- **Mass**: The amount of matter in a thing, that is, how much of it there is. It is counted in grams and kilograms. Your mass is the same on Earth and on the Moon. *e.g.* A packet of salt with a mass of 1 kilogram has the same salt in it everywhere.
- **Weighing scale**: A tool that shows how hard things press down. Kitchen scales and bathroom scales are weighing scales. In India they show grams and kilograms. *e.g.* You stand on a bathroom scale and it shows 25 kilograms.
- **Load cell**: A small block of metal inside digital scales. It bends a tiny bit when pressed. A chip turns that bend into a number. *e.g.* The digital scale at the vegetable shop has a load cell under its tray.
- **Electron**: A tiny particle in the outer part of every atom. Electrons repel each other: they push each other away. *e.g.* When you push a door, the electrons in your hand repel the electrons in the door. That push is what you feel.
- **Molecule**: A tiny group of atoms joined together. Atoms are the tiny building blocks of everything. *e.g.* Inside your body, some molecules walk along threads, pulling loads like porters.
- **Gravity**: The force by which every object pulls every other object towards it. The Earth's gravity pulls you down. The Moon's gravity pulls on the sea and makes the tides. *e.g.* Gravity pulls a mango down from the tree.

## Explanations
### A unit is one small pull, counted again and again · *defined*
**Definition:** To put a number on a pull, choose one small pull that never changes. Then count how many of them match the big pull. That small pull is your unit.

In the coin-pull test, each coin of one kind pulls down on the cup by the same amount. So 'six coins' means 'six of the same small pull'. That is all a unit is. You can choose almost anything, as long as it stays the same every time. Coins of one kind work. A mix of coins and buttons does not, because a button pulls less than a coin. People have always done this. Indian goldsmiths used the bright red seed of the gunja plant as a tiny unit of weight, called a ratti. One ratti is a little more than a tenth of a gram. Some jewellers still say 'ratti' today. Gunja seeds are poisonous if chewed, so never put one in your mouth.
- e.g. Six five-rupee coins started the notebook sliding. The unit was 'one five-rupee coin'.
- e.g. Counting your steps across a room uses 'one of my steps' as a unit of length.
- e.g. A ratti seed was a unit of weight for gold. People chose these seeds because they are small, hard and look very alike.
### Why two careful people get different numbers · *defined*
**Definition:** A number means nothing without its unit. Six big coins and twelve small coins can be exactly the same pull.

In the coin-pull test, you and a grown-up may get different numbers for the same notebook. Both of you can be right. You were counting different small pulls. To compare, you have to find how many of one unit make the other. It is like changing rupees into dollars. Now imagine every town using its own coins, buttons or stones. Nobody could compare a pull in Madurai with a pull in Patna. A builder could not order a rope strong enough for a bridge. That is why people agreed to share one unit of force.
- e.g. Suppose one five-rupee coin pulls as hard as two one-rupee coins. Then 6 big coins match 12 small ones.
- e.g. A grown-up's step is longer than yours. The room is the same, but the number of steps is not.
- e.g. Old Indian markets used the ser. But a ser in one town was often not the same as a ser in the next.
### The newton: one unit the whole world shares · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists everywhere count force in newtons. The short way to write newtons is N. One newton is about the pull of a small apple on your hand.

The newton is named after Isaac Newton, an English scientist. In a book of 1687, he explained how forces change movement. Countries agreed to call the unit 'the newton' in 1948. One newton is a small force. A small apple, or a 100 gram packet of tea, pulls on your hand with about 1 newton. So does 100 millilitres of water. A 1 litre bottle of water pulls with about 10 newtons. The newton is not something you can dig up or find in nature. It is an agreement. Because everyone keeps the same agreement, a pull in Madurai and a pull in Paris can be compared.
- e.g. A 100 gram packet of biscuits pulls down with about 1 newton.
- e.g. A 1 litre bottle of water pulls down with about 10 newtons.
- e.g. A Class 3 child who is 25 kilograms presses down on the floor with about 250 newtons.
### What does a force do? · *defined*
**Definition:** A force changes how something moves, or changes its shape. A force is not needed to keep something moving.

About 2,350 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle taught that things need a push to keep moving. Stop pushing a cart, and it stops. For about 2,000 years, most people agreed. Then Galileo Galilei, in Italy in the early 1600s, rolled balls down a slope and onto a smooth floor. The smoother the floor, the further the ball rolled. He reasoned that on a perfectly smooth floor it would roll for ever. The cart stops because friction pulls back on it, not because the push runs out. In 1687, Isaac Newton wrote this down as a rule. A force changes movement. This gave a way to say exactly how big a force is. One newton is the push that makes a 1 kilogram thing speed up by 1 metre per second, every second. This works only if nothing rubs or pulls back.
- e.g. A bigger kick makes the same football speed up faster.
- e.g. The same push makes an empty trolley speed up much more than a full one.
- e.g. In space, where nothing rubs, one gentle push sets a floating astronaut moving. She keeps going until something stops her.
### How a forcemeter works · *defined*
**Definition:** A forcemeter is a spring with a hook and a scale. The bigger the pull on the hook, the further the spring stretches. The pointer moves further along the scale.

Look inside a forcemeter and you find a spring. The top of the spring is fixed. The hook hangs from the bottom. When something pulls on the hook, the spring stretches, and a pointer fixed to it slides down the scale. Twice the pull gives twice the stretch. That is why the marks on a forcemeter are evenly spaced. An English scientist, Robert Hooke, published this rule in 1678. The marks are put on by hanging things whose pull is already agreed, such as 100 gram packets. So every forcemeter is set to the same shared unit. A spring only follows the rule if you don't pull too hard. Pull past its range and it stays stretched, and the forcemeter is spoiled.
- e.g. A luggage scale at the airport is a forcemeter for heavy bags.
- e.g. A shopkeeper's hanging spring balance for vegetables is a forcemeter marked in kilograms.
- e.g. You can make a forcemeter with a rubber band, a paper clip and a strip of card.
### Reading a forcemeter properly · *defined*
**Definition:** To read a forcemeter well, check the zero and hold it straight. Pull steadily and read at eye level. Count the small marks and write the unit.

A forcemeter gives a true number only if you use it well. First, check the zero. With nothing on the hook, the pointer must be exactly on 0. If it isn't, turn the little screw at the top until it is. Second, hold it straight and still. A swinging bag gives a wobbly reading. Third, pull slowly and steadily. A sudden jerk makes the pointer jump too high. Fourth, look at the pointer with your eyes level with it, not from above or below. Fifth, count the small marks between the big numbers. Last, write the number with its unit, for example '3 newtons'.
- e.g. If the pointer starts at 1 with nothing on it, every reading will be 1 newton too big.
- e.g. Reading from above or below can make the pointer seem to sit on the wrong mark.
- e.g. A 0 to 10 newton forcemeter with 20 small marks has one mark for every half newton.
### Weight is a force · *defined*
**Definition:** Weight is the pull of a planet on a thing. It is a force, counted in newtons. Mass is how much stuff a thing has, counted in kilograms.

When you hang a bag from a forcemeter, what is pulling it down? It is the Earth. The pull of a planet on a thing is called its weight. So weight is a force, and scientists count it in newtons. In shops, we say a bag of rice 'weighs 1 kilogram'. Strictly, the kilogram tells us how much rice there is. Scientists call that its mass. On Earth, every kilogram is pulled down with about 10 newtons. On the Moon, gravity is weaker. The same bag of rice would pull the forcemeter down only about one sixth as hard. The rice has not changed. Only the pull has. The unit on Weight and mass goes deeper into this.
- e.g. A 1 kilogram bag of rice reads about 10 newtons on Earth, and about 1.6 newtons on the Moon.
- e.g. Astronauts on the Moon could carry heavy packs easily, because their packs pulled down less.
- e.g. On Jupiter, the same bag of rice would pull down about two and a half times harder than on Earth.
### Putting a number on a push · *defined*
**Definition:** Weighing scales put a number on pushes. A spring or a bending metal block inside turns the push into a number.

A forcemeter shows pulls. So how do we put a number on a push? A bathroom scale is one answer. When you stand on it, you push down on it. Inside an old scale is a spring that gets squashed. Inside a digital scale is a load cell. That is a small block of metal that bends a tiny bit when pressed. A chip turns the bend into a number. Weighing scales show kilograms, not newtons. To turn the kilograms on a scale into newtons of push, multiply by 10. You can also see pushes without numbers. A balloon, a sponge or a ball of foam squashes more when the push is bigger.
- e.g. Pressing a bathroom scale with one finger might show 2 kilograms. That is a push of about 20 newtons.
- e.g. A punching game at a fair shows how hard a punch hits its pad.
- e.g. Car makers crash cars into walls fitted with sensors, to find how big the push is and make cars safer.
### What is a push, underneath? · *defined*
**Definition:** When you push something, the electrons on the outside of your atoms repel the electrons of its atoms. Almost every push and pull you feel is this one electric force.

Zoom in on your finger pressing a table. Your skin and the table are both made of atoms. Each atom has electrons on its outside. Electrons repel each other, which means they push each other away. So your finger never quite touches the table. The electrons push apart, and that push is what you feel. A string pulling a cup is the same force. The atoms of the string hold on to each other, so a pull at one end reaches the other. A spring, the grip of friction and the push of the wind are this force too. Even a forcemeter's spring works this way: its atoms pull back on their neighbours when you stretch it. In everyday life, only the pull of gravity is different. In the coin test, you measured friction, the grip between the notebook and the table. That grip comes from electric pushes and pulls between trillions of atoms.
- e.g. Press your palm on a table. The push you feel is electrons repelling electrons.
- e.g. The string in the coin test pulls because its atoms hold on to their neighbours.
- e.g. The weight of the coins comes from gravity, the one everyday force that is not electric.
### Tiny forces and huge forces · *defined*
**Definition:** Forces range from far less than one newton to many millions of newtons. The newton works for all of them.

Forces in our world range from very tiny to enormous. A sheet of paper pulls down with about one twentieth of a newton. A grown-up presses on the floor with about 600 newtons. A big saltwater crocodile, like the ones in Bhitarkanika in Odisha, can bite with about 16,000 newtons. The giant Saturn V rocket that carried astronauts to the Moon pushed off with about 34 million newtons. And the Earth and the Moon pull on each other with about 200 million million million newtons. Scientists count all of them in the same unit, the newton.
- e.g. A one-rupee coin pulls down with only a few hundredths of a newton.
- e.g. An Asian elephant weighs about 40,000 newtons.
- e.g. India's rockets, at Sriharikota, push off with millions of newtons.
### The tiniest forces, inside living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists can detect forces far smaller than a newton, using light beams and tiny bending needles.

Inside every cell in your body, tiny molecules walk along threads, carrying loads. They are like porters on a mountain path. One kind is called kinesin. How hard can one kinesin molecule pull? To find out, scientists used light as a pair of tweezers. A focused laser beam can hold a tiny bead and show how hard something tugs on it. These are called optical tweezers. Arthur Ashkin invented them, and won a Nobel Prize for them in 2018. With optical tweezers, scientists found that one kinesin molecule pulls with about 6 piconewtons. That is about 6 millionths of a millionth of a newton. Other scientists use a tiny needle that bends, called an atomic force microscope. It feels the push of single atoms.
- e.g. Your muscles work because millions of molecules each give a tiny pull at the same time.
- e.g. An atomic force microscope can feel the bumps of single atoms on a surface.
- e.g. Optical tweezers can stretch a single strand of DNA, the molecule that carries your body's instructions.
### Weighing the Earth with a twisting wire · *defined*
**Definition:** Cavendish measured the tiny gravity pull between lead balls with a twisting wire, in 1798. It let scientists work out the mass of the Earth.

Everything pulls on everything else with gravity. Even two balls on a table pull each other. But that pull is so tiny that nobody could feel it. In 1798, an English scientist, Henry Cavendish, measured it. He hung a rod with two small lead balls from a thin wire. He put two big lead balls nearby. The big balls pulled the small ones, and the wire twisted a tiny bit. The twisting wire worked like a very gentle forcemeter. The pull was tiny, about the weight of one fine grain of sand. Cavendish watched through a telescope from outside the room, so even the warmth of his body would not spoil it. From this, scientists could work out the mass of the whole Earth. The idea and the first apparatus came from another scientist, John Michell. He died in 1793, before he could use it.
- e.g. A twisting wire can show forces far too small for a spring.
- e.g. Cavendish's measurement let people work out the mass of the Earth for the first time.
### Who decides how big a unit is? · *defined*
**Definition:** Since 2019, the kilogram, and so the newton, is fixed by numbers from nature, not by one metal object.

A newton depends on the kilogram, the metre and the second. So who decides how big a kilogram is? From 1889 to 2019, the kilogram was one real metal cylinder, made of platinum and iridium. It was kept locked in a vault near Paris, under three glass covers. People called it Le Grand K. By agreement, it was exactly 1 kilogram, whatever happened to it. Countries had copies. India's copy is kept at the National Physical Laboratory in New Delhi. But over about 100 years, the copies and the original slowly drifted apart, by about 50 millionths of a gram. Nobody could say which had changed. So in 2018, at a meeting in Versailles in France, countries voted to change the rule. Since 20 May 2019, the kilogram is fixed by a constant of nature, called the Planck constant. Even here there is an agreement. People chose the exact number for it, so the new kilogram matched the old one. A machine called a Kibble balance can turn that number into a real kilogram. It balances a weight against the push of a magnet on an electric current. Bryan Kibble, a British scientist, invented it in 1975.
- e.g. The metre is now fixed by the speed of light, not by a metal bar.
- e.g. A country with a Kibble balance can now make its own kilogram, without visiting Paris.
### Fair weights in India's history · *defined*
**Definition:** Fair trade needs weights everyone agrees on. India has used standard weights since the Indus Valley cities, about 4,500 years ago.

People have needed fair weights for thousands of years. About 4,500 years ago, traders in the Indus Valley cities used stone weights. They were carefully cut into little cubes. The sizes followed a pattern: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64, and then bigger steps. The same sizes were used in cities hundreds of kilometres apart. Much later came the Arthashastra, an old Indian book about running a kingdom. It describes an officer who checked the scales and weights in markets. For a long time, India used units like the tola, the ser and the maund. They were not the same everywhere. In 1958, India began to change to grams and kilograms. Today, Legal Metrology officers still check shop scales and put a seal on honest ones.
- e.g. Indus Valley stone weights have been found at Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and Lothal.
- e.g. Some grandparents still say 'one ser of milk' or 'one tola of gold'.
- e.g. Look for a small seal or stamp on the scales at your vegetable shop.
### What is a force, really? · *debated*
We can put very exact numbers on forces. But what is a force, deep down? How does the Earth pull a mango without touching it? Scientists have given different answers, and the question is not finished.
- e.g. A magnet pulls a pin across a gap. Nothing we can see joins them.
- e.g. A forcemeter inside a falling lift would read zero, even though gravity is still there.
- **Isaac Newton, in 1687 and 1713:** A force is whatever changes how a thing moves. Newton worked out exactly how gravity pulls. But he said he did not know how it reaches across empty space, and he would not guess.
- **Albert Einstein, in 1915:** Gravity can be pictured without any pull. Heavy things like the Earth bend space and time around them. Other things move along those bends. A falling mango is simply following them.
- **Particle physicists today:** Electric and magnetic forces are carried by tiny particles of light, called photons, passing between things. Many think gravity might be carried by a particle too, called a graviton. Nobody has ever found one.
### Does measuring change what you measure? · *idea*
A heavy forcemeter adds its own pull to what it measures. A cold thermometer cools the tea a tiny bit. Scientists always try to make their tools disturb things as little as possible. But at the size of atoms, there seems to be a limit. Quantum physics, the science of the very tiniest things, says that measuring something always nudges it a little. In 2014, physicists in California measured a force of about 42 yoctonewtons. One yoctonewton is so small that a million million million million of them make just one newton. They found their measurement was close to that limit. Whether we can ever beat it, in clever ways, is still being worked on.
- e.g. A spring balance hanging on a spring balance adds its own weight to the reading.
- e.g. Shining light on a tiny thing to see it also gives it a tiny push.
- **Most physicists:** There is a limit to how gently you can measure. It comes from quantum physics.
- **Physicists building new tools:** Clever tricks can get past the usual limit in some experiments. How far this can go is still being explored.

## Mostly agreed
- Forces are counted in newtons, all over the world. *How we know:* Countries agreed to name the unit 'newton' in 1948. Every science book and forcemeter in the world uses it.
- A number tells you nothing until you know its unit. *How we know:* In the coin-pull test, the same notebook needed 6 big coins or 12 small coins. In 1999, a space probe called the Mars Climate Orbiter was lost. One team's computer gave its numbers in an old British unit, and another team read them as if they were in newtons.
- A spring stretches evenly as the pull grows, until you pull it too hard. *How we know:* Hang 100 gram packets on a spring one at a time. Each packet adds about the same stretch. Robert Hooke published this in 1678.
- On Earth, every kilogram is pulled down with about 10 newtons. More exactly, it is 9.8 newtons. *How we know:* Hang a 1 kilogram packet on a good forcemeter. It reads just under 10 newtons. Scientists have checked this very carefully all over the planet.
- The same thing has a smaller weight on the Moon, but the same mass. *How we know:* From 1969 to 1972, astronauts on the Moon bounced along with heavy packs. On Earth those packs would have been hard to carry. Their packs still held exactly the same things.
- Scales and forcemeters must be checked against agreed standards to be fair. *How we know:* In India, Legal Metrology officers test shop scales with standard weights and seal the honest ones. Scientists check their own tools against national standards, like the kilogram copy kept in New Delhi.

## How we found out
**French scientists in 1795, and the countries that share the kilogram, 1795 to 2019.** For most of history, every town had its own weights. A ser in one market was not a ser in the next. So people often argued about how much they had bought. In 1795, France made a new rule. A gram would be the mass of a tiny cube of water, 1 centimetre on each side. Water is the same everywhere, so anyone could check it. In 1889, many countries agreed to share one kilogram. It was a metal cylinder, kept locked near Paris. Each country got a copy. India's copy is in New Delhi. People thought the metal would never change. But years later, the copies were weighed against it again. They no longer matched exactly. So in 2019, scientists stopped using the metal cylinder. Now the kilogram is set by a fixed number from physics. Anyone with the right machine can check it, anywhere in the world.  
*What people believed before:* People believed one guarded lump of metal would stay exactly the same for ever. When the copies were checked years later, they had drifted apart by a tiny amount.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Things need a push to keep moving.** (Aristotle, about 2,350 years ago, and most people without thinking about it; *rejected*)  
  For: A cart stops when the bullock stops pulling. A ball stops rolling. Everything we see seems to need a push to keep going.  
  Against: Galileo saw that a ball rolls further on a smoother floor. Things stop because friction and air pull back on them. In space, where nothing rubs, a spacecraft keeps moving for years with its engines off.
- **A number tells you the true size of a thing, all by itself.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: Numbers look exact. '12' seems to say exactly how big the pull is.  
  Against: The same notebook needed 6 big coins or 12 small coins. A number only says how many units fit. Until 2019, even the kilogram was simply the mass of one metal cylinder.
- **If nothing moves, there is no force.** (Many children, and many grown-ups without noticing; *rejected*)  
  For: We usually notice forces when something starts moving.  
  Against: Push hard on a wall through a balloon. The wall doesn't move, but the balloon squashes flat. A book lying on a table is pulled down by the Earth. At the same time, the table pushes it up. Put a bathroom scale under it and you can see the push as a number.
- **Your hands can tell you exactly how heavy something is.** (A common belief. Many traders once judged loads by lifting them.; *rejected*)  
  For: With practice, people get quite good at guessing weights.  
  Against: Tired arms make things feel heavier. A small box feels heavier than a big box of the same weight. Augustin Charpentier, a French doctor, wrote about this in 1891. That is why fair trade uses scales.
- **A bathroom scale measures how much of you there is.** (Most people, most of the time; *rejected*)  
  For: The scale shows kilograms, and kilograms measure mass, the amount of stuff.  
  Against: The scale really shows how hard you push down on it. Bend your knees fast and the number jumps. Press on a table beside you and it drops. On the Moon it would show a much smaller number, though you'd still be all there. The scale is just set to turn your push into kilograms, as if you were standing still on Earth.
- **Isaac Newton worked out the rules of forces all by himself.** (Many storybooks; *debated*)  
  For: Newton's book of 1687 brought the rules together in one place, and the unit of force is named after him.  
  Against: Newton built on Galileo, Kepler and others. Robert Hooke wrote to Newton about how gravity might weaken with distance, and felt Newton gave him too little credit. Newton himself wrote that he stood 'on the shoulders of giants'.
- **Gravity is a force, just like a pull on a rope.** (Newton's way of seeing it, still used in most science books; *debated*)  
  For: It works for nearly everything, from dropping mangoes to sending rockets to the Moon.  
  Against: Albert Einstein showed in 1915 that gravity can be seen as bent space and time. A forcemeter falling freely reads zero. His view explains some things Newton's can't, like a slow wobble in the orbit of the planet Mercury.

## Roots
We have been inventing units of pull, counting coins and water, and meeting the newton. Now we ask what a number like '5 newtons' really is, and what that says about us and the world.

**Deep truth.** One notebook needed 6 big coins to start sliding, or 12 small coins. The pull was the same, so the number changed only because the coin changed. A number for a force tells you how many units fit, so it depends on the unit people choose. That is why it is useful only when everyone counts in one agreed unit, like the newton.  
*Habit it breaks:* We usually think a number like 12 tells us how big a pull is. In fact a number only says how many units fit, so it means nothing without its unit.  
*Evidence:* In the coin-pull test, a child and a grown-up counted different numbers of coins for the same notebook. Both counts were right, because they used different coins as their unit.

- *who am i:* Your hand can feel that one pull is bigger than another. To tell someone else exactly how big it is, you both need to share one unit.
- *what is life:* A crow feels the push of the wind, and a root senses which way is down, without any numbers. People invented numbers for forces so they could share and compare what they sense.
- *what is this world:* The Earth pulled mangoes down long before anyone had a unit for force. The pull is part of nature. The number in newtons is something people added, by agreeing on a unit.

Before anyone measured it, the notebook's pull was just a pull, with no number. To count it, people choose one small pull as the unit, such as one coin, one ratti seed or one newton. Then they count how many of that one unit fit. Every number in newtons, from a molecule's tug to the pull between the Earth and the Moon, counts that same one unit. Because everyone starts from the same one, a number in Madurai can be compared with a number in Paris.

- **Rig Veda 1.164.46:** Truth is one. The wise call it by many names. (One notebook's pull was counted as 6 big coins, 12 small coins or about half a newton. The numbers changed with the unit. The pull itself stayed the same.)
- **Quran 55:7–9:** The heavens were raised, and a balance was set. So do not cheat the balance. Weigh fairly, and do not make the balance short. (A balance is a promise between people. In the story Ten Packets of Salt, one wrong pointer cheated every porter, until a shared standard showed it.)
- Science (settled): Since 20 May 2019, the kilogram, and so the newton, is fixed by the Planck constant, a constant of nature. People still chose the exact number it is given, so the new kilogram matched the old one. A Kibble balance can turn that number into a real kilogram anywhere.
- Science (debated): Units are clearly chosen by people. Philosophers of science still argue about the laws of nature. Are they simply found in nature, or partly shaped by the way people choose to describe and count things?
- Other view, Protagoras, a Greek thinker of about 2,400 years ago, as Plato reports in the Theaetetus: Protagoras said that each person is the measure of all things. What feels heavy to you is heavy for you. On this view, the feeling in your own hand is the truest measure. A shared unit leaves out how differently each of us feels the same load.
- Practice, How many coins can your hand count? (3 min): Close your eyes and hold out one open hand, palm up. I will put coins on it, one at a time, very gently. Say 'now' each time you feel your hand get heavier. Breathe slowly while you wait. Open your eyes. Did you feel every coin, or did some go unnoticed? Your hand felt the weight, but could it count it? What would it need?
- Ask: If everyone forgot the newton tomorrow, would the notebook still need the same pull? Then what would we have lost?
- Ask: Your hand could feel the coins but not count them. Is a pull you feel the same thing as a pull you count?

## Story: Ten Packets of Salt
*Made up. The village, Pushpa, Basanti, Mohan Lal, Diwan and Kamla Didi are imagined. Porters really do carry loads on mountain treks in Uttarakhand, often paid by the load. Spring balances really have a zero screw that can be set wrongly. Sealed packets must by law weigh what they say, and Legal Metrology officers check scales.*

In the mountains of Uttarakhand, a stony path climbs from a small village all the way to a glacier, a river of ice. Every spring, trekkers came from the cities to walk it. They brought tents and cooking pots, sacks of rice and tins of ghee. They did not carry these things themselves. The porters of the village did.

Pushpa was nine. Her mother, Basanti, was a porter. Each morning, before the sun climbed over the ridge, Pushpa walked with her to the walnut tree at the edge of the village. There the contractor, Mohan Lal, weighed the loads. He had a brass spring balance as long as his forearm. Inside it was a coiled spring. When a basket hung from its hook, the spring stretched, and a little pointer slid down a row of numbers.

Mohan Lal paid the porters by the kilo. 'Fifteen,' he would call, squinting at the pointer as a basket swung below it. 'Eighteen. Fourteen.' His clerk wrote every number in a long book. At the end of the season, the porters would be paid for every kilo in the book.

Basanti bent and slipped the strap of her basket over her forehead. She stood, and the basket rose onto her back. Pushpa saw her mother's neck go tight, and her knees tremble for a moment before they steadied.

'Fourteen,' said Mohan Lal.

That evening, Basanti sat by the fire and rubbed her neck. 'Fourteen,' she said softly. 'It felt like twenty-five.'

'Maybe you were tired, Ma,' said Pushpa.

'Maybe,' said her mother. 'A tired back is a poor judge. And Mohan Lal's balance is made of brass. Brass does not get tired.'

But Pushpa kept thinking. All the porters were saying the same thing. Old Diwan, who had carried loads for thirty years, said the baskets had grown heavier, or else he had grown weaker. The young men said nothing at all. They did not want to sound weak.

The next morning, Pushpa stood very close to the walnut tree. Mohan Lal hung his balance from a low branch, with nothing on the hook. Pushpa looked at the pointer. Then she looked again. It was not on the zero. It sat a little way above the zero, in the empty space at the top where there were no numbers at all.

She tugged her mother's sleeve. 'Ma, the pointer is in the wrong place.'

Basanti glanced at Mohan Lal and hushed her. 'Don't make trouble, beti.'

Mohan Lal laughed. 'So the little one wants to read my balance! Go and play.' His thumb slid across the brass. When Pushpa looked again, she could not see the pointer at all.

All day, she turned it over in her mind. A balance gives you a number. But how do you check the balance? You would need something whose weight everybody already agreed on. Something nobody could argue with.

Then she thought of Kamla Didi's tea shop. Kamla Didi sold salt in sealed packets. Each one was printed with the words 1 kg. They came from a factory far away, and by law every packet had to hold what it said. Every family in the village had cooked with that salt for years.

That evening, Pushpa stood at the tea shop counter. 'Didi, I don't want to buy salt,' she said. 'I want to borrow it. Only until tomorrow morning.'

Kamla Didi raised her eyebrows. Then, without asking why, she counted ten packets into a cloth bag. 'Ten packets. Ten kilos,' she said. 'Bring back every one.'

The bag was so heavy that Pushpa had to carry it in her arms, like a sleeping baby goat. It was heavier than her school bag full of books. By the time she reached the walnut tree, her arms were aching and the porters were already lining up.

'Mohan Lal-ji,' she said, loud enough for everyone to hear. 'Please weigh my load.'

The porters laughed, kindly. Mohan Lal laughed too. He hooked her bag onto his balance and peered at it. 'Five kilos,' he said. 'A little girl's little load.'

'There are ten packets of salt in that bag,' said Pushpa. Her voice shook, but she kept going. 'Each one says one kilo. Kamla Didi sold them. All of you have cooked with them.'

She tipped the packets out onto a blanket, one at a time. The porters began to count with her. One. Two. Three. By the end, the whole line was counting. 'Eight. Nine. Ten.'

Nobody spoke. Then old Diwan stepped forward. He lifted the brass balance off the branch and held it up to the morning light, with nothing on its hook. Everyone could see the pointer now, sitting high in the empty space above the zero.

'Five kilos missing from a bag of ten,' Diwan said slowly. 'And five from my basket. Every morning. For how long?'

Mohan Lal began to say that factories cheat too, that the packets were probably light. But just then Kamla Didi came up the path. She was carrying her own shop balance, the old kind with two pans and iron weights. She set it on a flat rock. She put five packets of salt in one pan and a five-kilo iron weight in the other. The pans swung, and settled, and hung perfectly level.

Two different ways of weighing. And they agreed.

Mohan Lal did not look at anybody. He turned a small screw at the top of his balance, and the pointer slid down until it sat exactly on the zero. Then he hooked on Basanti's basket.

'Nineteen,' he said quietly.

That season, every porter put a few rupees into a tin. At the end of the summer, they bought their own spring balance from the town. They hung it on a nail on the walnut tree. And every morning after that, before the first basket went on the hook, somebody would call out across the cold air: 'Pushpa! Check the zero!' And Pushpa would look, and nod, and the weighing would begin.

**For the parent:** A spring balance is a forcemeter. It turns a pull into a number by stretching a spring. But the number is only fair if the balance agrees with something everyone already trusts. Pushpa does what scientists call calibrating. She tests the balance with ten packets whose weight everybody has agreed on. Then a second, different balance agrees with them. Her mother's tired back could not prove anything, because a feeling belongs to one person. Ten sealed packets belonged to everyone. That is the whole idea of a unit: a number means something only because people share what it counts.

- Ask: Basanti said her basket felt like twenty-five kilos. Why couldn't her feeling prove that the balance was wrong?
- Ask: What was wrong with Mohan Lal's balance before anything was hung on it?
- Ask: Why did Pushpa choose sealed packets of salt to test the balance? Why not stones from the path?
- Ask: Kamla Didi checked the salt with a different kind of balance. Why did it matter that two different tools agreed?
- Ask: Why do you think the porters asked Pushpa to check the zero every morning, and not Mohan Lal?
- Ask: Why didn't the young porters say anything about the heavy loads at first?

### e4 · Invent a Unit: how many coins to pull a notebook? (40 min)
- **question:** How many coins does it take to start a notebook sliding? How does the number change for two or three notebooks? And will someone using different coins get the same number?
- **predict:** Before you start, make three guesses, and give a reason for each one. How many coins will one notebook need, and why? Will two stacked notebooks need the same number, a few more, or twice as many? Why do you think so? Will a grown-up using a different kind of coin get the same number as you? Why or why not?
- **fair test:** Change only one thing at a time. Use the same table, string and cup every time. In your own tests, use coins of only one kind. Put the notebook in the same place, the same way round. Drop each coin in gently, so a bump doesn't start the slide. Do every test 3 times and use the middle number, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up pokes the holes in the cup. Put a cushion under the cup, so the coins land softly when the notebook slides. Keep coins away from babies and toddlers, who might swallow them.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar for each test: one notebook, two notebooks and three notebooks. Under the chart, write the grown-up's number for one notebook, and the kind of coin they used.
- **results table:** What the cup pulled | My guess (coins) and why | Try 1 (coins) | Try 2 (coins) | Try 3 (coins) | Middle number (coins)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one notebook on the table. 2. Ask your child to push it gently with one finger until it slides. 3. Ask how big that push was, as a number. Do not suggest an answer.  
  *Parent says:* Push this notebook with one finger until it slides. Now tell me, how big was that push? Can you say it as a number?  
  *Child mode:* Push the notebook with one finger until it slides. How big was your push? Can you give it a number?
  - Ask: What could we count, to put a number on a pull? (good: Coins.; How many things it takes to pull it.; How far a rubber band stretches.; Something that is always the same.)
  - Note: Most children say 'small' or 'not much'. Some say a number in grams or kilograms. Ask what that number counts. The point is to feel that a push has no number on it until we choose something to count.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the notebook about 30 cm from the table edge. 2. Tape one end of the string to the middle of its front edge. 3. Run the string straight over the table edge. 4. Tie the other end to the cup's handle, so the cup hangs about 10 cm below the edge. 5. Put a cushion on the floor under the cup.  
  *Parent says:* Today the coins will do the pulling, and we will count them. Our unit of pull is one coin. Scientists named their unit after a person. What shall we call ours?  
  *Child mode:* Tape the string to the notebook. Hang the cup over the table edge. Put a cushion underneath. Your unit is one coin. Give it a name!
  - Note: The cup and string must be light. If the notebook slides before any coins go in, the cup is too heavy. Use a lighter cup, or a heavier notebook. Children love naming the unit after themselves. Let your child choose the name, and use it all through the test.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for two guesses, each with a reason. 2. Write both in the table. 3. Do not say whether they are right.  
  *Parent says:* How many coins do you think one notebook will need? Why? And two notebooks stacked: the same, a few more, or twice as many? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many coins one notebook needs. Then guess for two notebooks. Say why each time. Write your guesses.
  - Ask: Why do you think two notebooks will need that many coins? (good: Two notebooks are heavier, so they press harder on the table.; It is twice the notebook, so twice the coins.; They are the same kind of notebook, so the same number.)
  - Note: Every guess with a reason is a good guess. 'The same' and 'twice as many' are both common. Keep the guesses. Your child will check them in a few minutes.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Drop coins into the cup one at a time, gently. 2. Count out loud. 3. Stop the moment the notebook starts to slide. 4. Write the number. 5. Put the notebook back in the same place. 6. Do it 3 times in all.  
  *Parent says:* One coin at a time, gently. Count with me. Stop the moment it moves.  
  *Child mode:* Drop in one coin at a time. Count out loud. Stop when the notebook moves. Do it 3 times.
  - Note: The three numbers may differ by a coin or two. That is normal. Write all three and use the middle one. If a bump starts the slide early, that try doesn't count. If one notebook needs more than 12 coins, use a thinner notebook.
  - Check: In the coin-pull test, your three tries were 6, 7 and 9 coins. Which number is the fairest one to use? → **7, the middle one**
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stack a second notebook exactly on top of the first. 2. Read out your child's guess for two notebooks. 3. Do the coin test 3 times. 4. Then stack three notebooks and test again, if you have enough coins. 5. Write every number.  
  *Parent says:* Here is your guess for two notebooks. Let's see what the coins say.  
  *Child mode:* Stack two notebooks. Do the coin test 3 times. Then try three. Was your guess right?
  - Note: Usually two notebooks need about twice the coins, and three need about three times. The stack presses down harder, so the tiny bumps of the cover and the table grip each other more. This grip is called friction. Don't say this yet. Let the numbers show it, and ask why at the end.
  - Think: Look at your numbers for one, two and three notebooks. Do they grow by about the same amount each time?
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Before you start, ask your child to guess, with a reason, whether you will get the same number. 2. Test one notebook 3 times with your different coins. 3. Your child counts. 4. Write your middle number next to theirs.  
  *Parent says:* I'm going to test one notebook with these different coins. Will I get the same number as you? Why do you think so? Now count for me.  
  *Child mode:* Now a grown-up tests one notebook with different coins. Guess: will they get your number? Why? Then count their coins.
  - Ask: We got different numbers for the same notebook. Who is right? (good: Both of us. We used different coins.; Both. My coins are heavier, so I needed fewer.; We can't compare until we know how many of your coins make one of mine.)
  - Note: Lighter coins mean more coins for the same pull. To compare the two units, rest a ruler on a round pencil. Put one big coin on one end. Add small coins to the other end until it balances. Coins from different years weigh different amounts, so the answer may not be a whole number.
  - Check: In the coin-pull test, you needed 6 big coins to slide the notebook. A grown-up needed 12 small coins for the same notebook. What does this show? → **It was the same pull, counted in different units**
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what would happen if every family used its own coins, buttons or stones. Let your child answer before you say anything.  
  *Parent says:* A child in another city does this test with buttons. She says 'eleven'. Is her notebook's pull bigger than ours? What would we both need, to compare?  
  *Child mode:* A child in another city uses buttons and says 'eleven'. Is her pull bigger than yours? What would you both need?
  - Ask: What would you and that child need, to compare your pulls? (good: The same unit.; We both use the same kind of thing.; A unit that everyone agrees on.)
  - Note: This is the reason the whole world agreed on one unit of force, the newton. Let your child say it first, in their own words. The explanation cards come after this.
- **s8 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, which numbers show it. Third, why I think it happened. Then read their guesses from step 3 together.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now, about stacking notebooks or about units? Which numbers show it? And why do you think it happened? Now let's read your first guesses. Would you change them?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think now. Which numbers show it. Why it happened. Then read your first guesses again.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: each extra notebook needs about the same number of extra coins. What I saw: one notebook took 6 coins, two took 12 and three took 17. Why: the stack presses harder on the table, so it grips more. Another good answer is about units. What I think: a number is no use without its unit. What I saw: I needed 6 coins and a grown-up needed 12 for the same notebook. Why: her coins were lighter.
### e1 · The Pull Detective: which pull at home is biggest? (30 min)
- **question:** Which pull at home needs the biggest force: a door, a drawer, a bag or a chair?
- **predict:** Before you use the forcemeter, put the six pulls (the fridge door, a drawer, a chair, a school bag, a cupboard door and a slipper) in order, biggest first. Say why you put the first one first and the last one last.
- **fair test:** Pull every thing the same way: slowly and steadily, straight along the forcemeter. Read the biggest number just as the thing starts to move. Test each pull 2 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Pull gently and slowly. Never pull a tall cupboard or bookshelf that could tip over. Don't pull past the biggest number on the forcemeter, or the spring may be spoiled.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart, with one bar for each pull. Make each bar as tall as its biggest reading.
- **results table:** What we pulled | My guess (order, 1 to 6) | Try 1 (newtons) | Try 2 (newtons) | Biggest reading (newtons)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the six pulls. They are the fridge door, a drawer, a chair, a school bag, a cupboard door and a slipper. 2. Ask them to put them in order, biggest pull first. 3. Write the order in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to be pull detectives. Which of these do you think needs the biggest pull? Put them in order, biggest first, and tell me why.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the six things. Which needs the biggest pull to move? Put them in order. Say why. Write your guesses down.
  - Ask: Why did you put that one first? (good: It is heavy.; It is stuck tight.; The fridge door has a magnet that holds it shut.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the forcemeter upright with nothing on the hook. 2. Ask your child to read it. 3. If the pointer is not on zero, turn the screw at the top until it is.  
  *Parent says:* Before we start, we check the zero. Look at the pointer with nothing on the hook. Is it exactly on the 0?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the forcemeter up. Nothing on the hook. Is the pointer on 0? If not, ask a grown-up to fix it.
  - Check: With nothing on the hook, your forcemeter's pointer is on 1 newton. What will happen to your readings? → **They will all be 1 newton too big**
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie the string loop to the first handle. 2. Hook the forcemeter into the loop. 3. Hold it in a straight line with the pull. 4. Pull slowly and steadily. 5. Read the biggest number as the thing starts to move. 6. Do it twice and write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Pull slowly, like a snail. Watch the pointer climb. Tell me the biggest number just as it starts to move.  
  *Child mode:* Hook on. Pull slowly and straight. Watch the pointer. Say the biggest number as it moves. Do it twice.
  - Note: Keep your eyes level with the pointer when you read it. A sudden jerk makes the pointer jump too high, so the test would not be fair. For the school bag, lift it straight up off the floor slowly. If a pull goes past the end of the scale, stop, and write 'more than' the biggest number.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the biggest readings, with one bar for each pull.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn the numbers into a picture. Each pull gets a bar. The taller the bar, the bigger the pull.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each pull. Make it as tall as its biggest number. Which bar is tallest?
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the forcemeter's order with the guessed order in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Were your guesses right? Which pull surprised you most?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your guesses. Look at your bars. Which one surprised you?
  - Note: Ask your child to explain any surprise before you explain it. If the fridge door needed a big pull, one reason is the magnetic strip in its rubber seal, which pulls the door shut.
  - Think: Which pull surprised you most? Why do you think it needed so much, or so little?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* So, which pull was biggest? Which numbers show it? Why do you think that one needed so much?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. Which pull was biggest. Which numbers show it. Why you think so.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the fridge door needs a big pull. What I saw: it needed 18 newtons, but the drawer only needed 5. Why: there is a magnet in the fridge door pulling it shut.
### e2 · What Does a Bathroom Scale Really Measure? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a bathroom scale show how much of you there is, or how hard you push on it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. You stand on the scale and press down on a table beside you. Will the number go up, go down or stay the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Stand in the same place on the scale each time, with both feet. Change only one thing at a time: pressing down on the table, pulling up on it, or bending your knees. Read the number each time when it settles, except for the knee test.
- **safety:** A grown-up stands beside the child the whole time. Use a strong table that cannot tip. Don't jump on the scale.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For each kilogram, write about 10 newtons beside it. For each test, draw yourself on the scale with an arrow for your push on it.
- **results table:** What I did | My guess (up, down or same) | Number on the scale (kg) | Push in newtons (kg times 10)
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the scale on a hard floor next to a strong table. 2. Your child stands still on it with both feet. 3. Read and write the number when it settles.  
  *Parent says:* Stand still on the scale. What number does it show? Let's write it down.  
  *Child mode:* Stand still on the scale. Read the number. Write it down.
  - Note: A child of 25 kilograms is pressing down with about 250 newtons. Help multiply by 10. Many digital scales freeze the first number they show. If yours does, step off, then step back on while already pressing or pulling on the table, and read the new number. A dial scale shows every change straight away, and is best for the knee bend.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to guess what will happen. 2. They press down hard on the table top with both palms, still standing on the scale. 3. Read the number.  
  *Parent says:* Guess first. If you press down on the table, will the number go up, down, or stay the same? Why? Now try it.  
  *Child mode:* Guess first, and say why. Then press down hard on the table. What does the number do?
  - Note: Wait for the guess and the reason before your child presses. The number goes down, because the table now holds up part of your child.
  - Check: You stand on a bathroom scale and press down hard on a table beside you. What happens to the number? → **It goes down**
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child gently lifts up on the underside of the table edge with their fingertips. 2. Read the number. 3. Then they bend their knees quickly, once, and watch the needle or digits.  
  *Parent says:* Now gently pull up under the table edge. What do you think the number will do? Why? Try it. Then bend your knees quickly and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Gently pull up under the table edge. Watch the number. Then bend your knees fast. Watch again.
  - Note: Pulling up on the table makes the number go up, because the table pushes your child down. Bending the knees makes the number dip, then jump up as they stop. Nobody got heavier or lighter.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the big question and let your child answer before you explain.  
  *Parent says:* Did you get any heavier or lighter just now? Did any of you go away, or any more of you arrive? So what is the scale really showing?  
  *Child mode:* Did any of you disappear? So what does the scale show? Say what you think.
  - Ask: What does the scale really show? (good: How hard I push on it.; My push, not how much of me there is.; The force of my feet on it.)
  - Note: Let your child answer first. Then you can say it: the scale shows how hard you push on it. The amount of you never changed.
  - Think: On the Moon, would the scale show a bigger number, a smaller number, or the same?
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think the scale shows? Which numbers show it? And why did the number change when you pressed the table?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What the scale really shows. Which numbers show it. Why it changed.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the scale shows my push. What I saw: it showed 25 standing still, 18 pressing the table and 29 pulling up. Why: the table took some of my push, or added to it. There was always the same amount of me.
### e3 · Can Your Hands Be Fooled? The Big Box and the Small Box (25 min)
- **question:** If two boxes pull a forcemeter with exactly the same force, will they feel the same when we lift them?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will people say the big box feels heavier, the small box feels heavier, or that they feel the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Make both boxes pull the forcemeter to exactly the same number. Use the same kind of string loop on both. Ask each person to look at both boxes and lift them the same way, one in each hand, by the string loops. Swap the boxes between hands for each new person. Ask at least 3 people, and more if you can.
- **safety:** Keep coins away from babies and toddlers, who might swallow them.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Count how many people chose each answer. Draw a bar chart.
- **results table:** Person | Which felt heavier: big, small or same?
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie a string loop to the big empty box with tape. 2. Hang it on the forcemeter and read its pull. 3. Tie a loop to the small box. 4. Fill the small box with coins until it pulls exactly the same number. 5. Tape it shut.  
  *Parent says:* These two boxes must pull with exactly the same force. Keep adding coins until the forcemeter shows the same number for both.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the big box. Read the number. Fill the small box until it shows the same number. Tape it shut.
  - Note: An empty shoe box pulls with about 2 newtons. If the big box is very light, tape a few coins inside it, spread out so they don't rattle. What matters is that both boxes show the same number.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what people will say, and write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Both boxes pull the forcemeter the same. Will people feel that? What do you think they will say, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Both boxes pull the same. What will people say? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think people will say that? (good: Big things are usually heavier.; They pull the same, so they will feel the same.; Small things feel heavier in your hand.)
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask each person to look at both boxes, then lift them, one in each hand, by the string loops. 2. Don't tell them what you did. 3. Ask: 'Which feels heavier?' 4. Write each answer in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Please lift these two boxes. Which one feels heavier to you?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a person to lift both boxes. Ask which feels heavier. Write their answer.
  - Note: Most people say the small box feels heavier, often much heavier. A few may say they feel the same, and that is a real result too. Some even refuse to believe the forcemeter. People must see the boxes: the trick works because they can see which box is bigger.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the answers and draw a bar for each one.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count. How many people said small? How many said big? How many said the same?  
  *Child mode:* Count the answers. Draw one bar for small, big and same.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. Can our hands be fooled? Which answers show it? And why do you think people felt the small box was heavier?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What you think about hands. Which answers show it. Why it happened.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: hands can be fooled. What I saw: both boxes showed 3 newtons, but 5 of 6 people said the small one felt heavier. Why: one common explanation is that our brains expect big things to be heavier, so the big box surprised them by being light. Scientists still argue about the full reason.

## Activities
### a1 · The Squash-o-meter: how big is that push? (15 min)
- **safety:** Don't blow the balloon too full. Keep it away from sharp corners. Push the bucket gently, so water doesn't spill.
- **record:** Draw the balloon for each push: a little bit squashed, half squashed or very squashed. Write small, medium or large force.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Blow up a balloon to about the size of a big mango and tie it. 2. Show your child how to hold it in both hands and push something with the balloon, not with the hands.  
  *Parent says:* Today you push things with the balloon, not with your hands. Watch the balloon each time. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the balloon in both hands. Push things with the balloon. Watch it. What do you notice?
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child pushes five things through the balloon. They are a plastic cup, a book, a chair, a full bucket of water and the wall. They push each one until it moves, or as hard as they can for the wall.  
  *Parent says:* Push each thing until it just starts to move. What does the balloon do? Which push squashes it most? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Push the cup. Then the book, the chair, the bucket and the wall. How squashed is the balloon each time?
  - Ask: The wall didn't move. Was there still a force? (good: Yes, the balloon squashed flat.; Yes, a big push, but the wall is too strong to move.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to put the five pushes in order, smallest to biggest. Then they say small, medium or large for each.  
  *Parent says:* Put the pushes in order. Which was the smallest? Which was the biggest? Could the balloon tell us an exact number? Why not?  
  *Child mode:* Put the pushes in order, smallest first. Could a balloon tell you an exact number?
  - Think: A balloon shows big and small, but not an exact number. What tool could give us a number?
### a2 · Feel One Newton (15 min)
- **record:** Draw your one newton kit. Write '1 N' beside each thing that pulls with about 1 newton.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one 100 gram packet on your child's open palm. 2. Say that it pulls down with about 1 newton. 3. Then put a small apple on the other palm.  
  *Parent says:* Hold this packet on your open hand. It has 100 grams of tea in it. Scientists say it pulls down with about 1 newton. Now hold the apple. Does it pull more, less or the same?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the packet on your open hand. Feel it pull down. That is about 1 newton.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child a 1 litre bottle of water. 2. Say it pulls with about 10 newtons. 3. Then let them hold the 1 kilogram rice packet. 4. Ask how many 100 gram packets would make the same pull.  
  *Parent says:* This bottle pulls with about 10 newtons. How many tea packets do you think would pull as hard as this bottle? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the water bottle. It pulls about 10 newtons. How many tea packets would match it?
  - Ask: How many 100 gram packets make 10 newtons? (good: 10 packets, because each one is about 1 newton.; 10, because 10 times 100 grams is 1 kilogram.)
  - Note: A 1 litre bottle holds about 1 kilogram of water. On Earth, each kilogram pulls with about 10 newtons. More exactly, it is 9.8 newtons, but 10 is close enough for us.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child walks around the kitchen and finds three other things that feel like about 1 newton. They check each one on a kitchen scale or its packet label: about 100 grams.  
  *Parent says:* Find three more things that feel like about 1 newton. Then let's check them on the scale or the label.  
  *Child mode:* Find three things that feel like 1 newton. Check them. Are they about 100 grams?
  - Think: A small apple is about 1 newton on Earth. Would it still be 1 newton on the Moon?
### a3 · Read It Right: the forcemeter drill (15 min)
- **record:** Write each reading with its unit, for example '3 and a half newtons'.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On the long strip, draw a line down the middle. Write 0 at the bottom. Going up, make a mark every 2 cm and write 1, 2, 3 up to 10. 2. Make a small mark halfway between each pair of numbers. These are the half-newton marks. 3. Fold the short strip round the long one like a sleeve. Tape its ends together loosely, so it slides up and down. This is the slider. 4. Fold the small piece of card into a little block. Tape it on the front of the slider, and tape the toothpick across the block. The toothpick is the pointer. It stands about 1 centimetre in front of the scale, like the pointer on a real forcemeter.  
  *Parent says:* We're going to make a pretend forcemeter. The pointer slides, just like the real one.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a scale on the long strip. Mark 0 to 10. Add a half mark between numbers. Make a slider. Tape a toothpick on it.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Slide the pointer to a reading, without your child seeing. 2. They read it, with their eyes level with the pointer. 3. Do 8 readings: 2, 5, 7 and a half, 0, 3 and a half, 9, 1 and a half, 10.  
  *Parent says:* Count the big numbers first. Then count the little marks after it. Each little mark is half a newton.  
  *Child mode:* Read the big number first. Then count the small marks. Each one is half a newton. Say the unit too.
  - Check: The pointer is on the small mark halfway between 7 and 8. Each small mark is half a newton. What is the reading? → **7 and a half newtons**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let your child look at the toothpick pointer from high above, then from below, then level. Ask which one looks right.  
  *Parent says:* Look from up high. Now from down low. Now straight on. Does the number seem to change? Which one do you trust? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Look from high up. Then from low down. Then straight on. Which way is true?
  - Note: Because the toothpick stands in front of the scale, it seems to sit on a different mark when you look from above or below. Only the level view is true. A real forcemeter's pointer also stands a little in front of its scale.
  - Think: Why do you think scientists always say 'eye level'?
### a4 · Tug of War on Two Forcemeters (15 min)
- **safety:** Pull gently and slowly. Never let go suddenly while the springs are stretched. Stay below the biggest number on the scale.
- **record:** Write both readings for 4 different tugs. Draw an arrow on each side.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check both forcemeters read zero. 2. Hook them together, hook to hook. 3. Each person holds one by its top ring.  
  *Parent says:* Two forcemeters, hooked together. When we pull, will both show the same number, or will the stronger person's show more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Hook the two forcemeters together. Hold yours. Will both show the same number? Why?
  - Note: Most children guess that the grown-up's forcemeter will show more. Wait for the guess and the reason before anyone pulls.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pull gently. The grown-up tries to pull harder than the child. 2. Hold still and read both. 3. Try 4 different tugs. 4. Write both readings each time.  
  *Parent says:* I'll pull harder. Now read both forcemeters. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Pull gently. Hold still. Read both numbers. Are they the same?
  - Ask: Why do both forcemeters always show the same number? (good: Each one pulls on the other.; When I pull it, it pulls me back just as hard.; The pull goes through both springs.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hook one forcemeter to a door handle by its ring. Your child pulls on the other one. Read both.  
  *Parent says:* Now the door is my partner. Pull. Does the door's forcemeter show the same number as yours?  
  *Child mode:* Hook one to a door handle. Pull the other. Is the door pulling back?
  - Note: The door pulls back with exactly the same force. Every pull comes with an equal pull back. Isaac Newton wrote this as his third law of motion. The unit on Pushes and pulls explores it more.
  - Think: If you pull on the door with 5 newtons, how hard is the door pulling you?
### a5 · Your Tiffin on the Moon (15 min)
- **record:** Write the pull on Earth, the Moon and Jupiter. Draw the three tiffins.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the empty tiffin on the kitchen scale and press its zero button, so the scale counts only the rice. 2. Weigh 600 grams of rice into the tiffin. 3. Your child holds it. 4. Say that on Earth the rice pulls with about 6 newtons.  
  *Parent says:* Hold your lunch. On Earth it pulls your hand down with about 6 newtons. The Moon pulls things down about one sixth as hard. How do you think your lunch would feel there? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the tiffin. Here on Earth, the rice pulls about 6 newtons. The Moon pulls only one sixth as hard. How would it feel there?
  - Note: A steel tiffin is heavy by itself, so use a light plastic one or a polythene bag. No kitchen scale? Use sealed packets instead. For Earth, hold a 500 gram and a 100 gram packet together. For the Moon, hold one 100 gram packet. For Jupiter, hold a 1 kilogram and a 500 gram packet together.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask: 'What is one sixth of 600 grams?' 2. Pour rice out until the scale shows 100 grams. 3. Your child holds it. 4. Say that this is how your full tiffin would feel on the Moon.  
  *Parent says:* On the Moon, your full tiffin would pull like this. Feel it. But how much rice would really be inside the tiffin on the Moon?  
  *Child mode:* Pour out rice until 100 grams are left. That is your full lunch on the Moon. Feel how light!
  - Ask: On the Moon, would there be less rice in your tiffin? (good: No, the same rice. The Moon just pulls it less.; The mass is the same. Only the weight is smaller.)
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill a bag with 1,500 grams of rice. 2. Your child holds it. 3. Say this is how the same 600 gram tiffin would feel on Jupiter, about two and a half times heavier.  
  *Parent says:* Jupiter is a giant planet. Its pull is about two and a half times stronger than Earth's. This is how your lunch would feel there.  
  *Child mode:* Hold this bag. It is your lunch on Jupiter. Two and a half times heavier!
  - Think: Your lunch felt different in three places. Did your lunch change, or did the planet change?
### a6 · Who Checks the Scales? A market visit (30 min)
- **record:** Draw three different scales you saw. Write what each one weighs or shows, and whether you found a seal.
- **s1 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Walk through a vegetable market or visit a kirana shop. 2. Help your child find different kinds of scales: digital, hanging spring balances and two-pan balances.

  *Child mode:* Look for scales. Count how many kinds. Which ones have a spring? Which have two pans?
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Look closely at a scale with your child and find the seal or stamp of the Legal Metrology department. Ask the shopkeeper's permission first.  
  *Parent says:* This little seal means a government officer tested this scale with standard weights. Why do you think that matters?  
  *Child mode:* Look for a little seal or stamp on the scale. It shows someone checked it.
  - Note: Legal Metrology officers test shop scales with standard weights and seal the honest ones. Scales are meant to be checked regularly. If you can't find a seal, that is fine. Talk about why checking matters.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child ask the shopkeeper two questions. 'How do you know your scale is right?' 'Has anyone checked it?'  
  *Parent says:* Let's ask the shopkeeper how they know their scale is right.  
  *Child mode:* Ask the shopkeeper: how do you know your scale is right?
  - Think: Why might a shopkeeper want an honest scale, even if nobody checks?
### a7 · Swap Coins for Water: a unit anyone can share (15 min)
- **record:** Write how many millilitres of water started the notebook sliding. Divide by 100 to get newtons. Write it next to your coin number.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty the coins out of the cup and hang it again. 2. Ask your child to guess how much water will start the notebook sliding, and why.  
  *Parent says:* A child in another city can't use our coins. But she has water. How much water do you think it will take to slide one notebook? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Now you will use water, not coins. How much water will start the notebook sliding? Guess, and say why.
  - Ask: How did you make your guess? (good: I thought about how heavy the coins felt.; The cup was nearly full of coins, so maybe half a cup of water.; Water is lighter than coins, so more of it.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour water into the hanging cup 10 millilitres at a time, gently, without touching the cup. 2. Make a mark on paper for each 10 millilitres. 3. Stop when the notebook starts to slide. 4. Add up the millilitres. 5. Do it 2 more times and use the middle number.  
  *Parent says:* Ten millilitres at a time. Mark each one. Stop when it slides.  
  *Child mode:* Pour in 10 millilitres. Make a mark. Pour again. Stop when the notebook slides. Add up your marks.
  - Note: One thin notebook often needs between 30 and 60 millilitres. Pour slowly near the end. A cough syrup cap usually has a 10 millilitre mark. If yours only shows 5, pour two caps each time. If it needs more than the cup holds, use a bigger paper cup.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child that 100 millilitres of water pulls down with about 1 newton, anywhere on Earth. 2. Ask them to turn their millilitres into newtons.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a fact people everywhere share: 100 millilitres of water pulls down with about 1 newton. So how many newtons did our notebook need?  
  *Child mode:* 100 millilitres of water pulls with about 1 newton. How many newtons did your notebook need?
  - Note: 100 millilitres of water is 100 grams, and the Earth pulls 100 grams down with just under 1 newton. The cup and string add a tiny pull too. It is small, so we leave it out.
  - Check: In the water-pull test, the notebook slid when the cup held 50 millilitres of water. 100 millilitres pulls with about 1 newton. How big was the pull? → **about half a newton**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask why water might be a better unit than coins. Let your child answer first.  
  *Parent says:* Could a child in another city repeat this test exactly? Why might water be a better unit than our coins?  
  *Child mode:* Could a child in another city repeat this test? Why is water better than your coins?
  - Ask: Why is water a good thing to share? (good: Water is the same everywhere.; Anyone can get water and fill a 100 millilitre cup.; Coins are different in different countries.)
  - Note: Today the kilogram is fixed by a number from nature instead of by an object.
  - Think: Water changes a tiny bit when it is hot or cold. Would a perfect unit need something that never changes at all?

## Projects
### p1 · Build Your Own Forcemeter (45 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up pushes in the drawing pin. Don't stretch the rubber band near anyone's face. If it is stretched thin and white, it may snap, so stop adding packets.
- **record:** Draw your forcemeter and label the band, the hook, the bag and the scale. Write a table: Thing, My forcemeter (N), Check (N).
- **schedule:** day 1: Build the forcemeter. Mark the scale from 0 to 5 newtons with 100 gram packets. (25 min); day 2: Test five things with your forcemeter. Check them with a kitchen scale or a real forcemeter. Improve your forcemeter. (20 min)
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the card strip upright to a table leg or a door frame. 2. A grown-up pushes the drawing pin through the top of the card into the wood. If the leg or frame is metal, hang the band from a door handle instead, with the card taped just behind it. 3. Hang the rubber band from the pin. 4. Open a paper clip into an S-shape and hang it from the band.  
  *Parent says:* We're going to build a real forcemeter. The rubber band will be our spring.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the card upright. Hang the rubber band from the pin. Hook a bent paper clip on the bottom.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hang the empty bag on the paper clip. 2. Your child draws a line on the card exactly where the bottom of the band is. 3. They write 0 beside it.  
  *Parent says:* The empty bag is our starting point. Draw a line where the bottom of the band is. That is our zero.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the empty bag. Draw a line where the band ends. Write 0.

   Options: It speeds up twice as fast / It moves at the same steady speed / It speeds up just as fast as with 1 newton / It stops  
   **Answer:** It speeds up twice as fast. *Why:* A force changes how a thing moves. Twice the push on the same thing gives twice the speeding up. Picking 'steady speed' is the old idea that a push only keeps things going. Newton showed that a push changes the speed.
14. (order) Put these from the smallest force to the biggest: a small apple pulling on your hand, a feather pulling on your hand, the pull between the Earth and the Moon, a Saturn V rocket pushing off the ground.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* A feather pulls with less than one hundredth of a newton, and a small apple with about 1 newton. The Saturn V rocket pushed with about 34 million newtons. The Earth and the Moon pull on each other with about 200 million million million newtons. Most people guess the rocket is biggest, because a launch looks so huge.
15. (truefalse) A forcemeter that shows the same number every time you hang the same bag must be showing the right number.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A forcemeter can be out by the same amount every time. Giving the same answer again and again only shows it is steady. To know it is right, you must check it against something everyone trusts, like Pushpa's sealed salt packets.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Sneha, a girl in Class 3, invents a unit of pull called 'one eraser'. What must all her erasers be like?  
   Options: The same kind and size, so each one pulls the same / Any erasers at all / Brightly coloured  
   **Answer:** The same kind and size, so each one pulls the same. *Why:* A unit only works if every one is the same size. Picking 'any erasers' means a big and a small eraser would count as the same, so her numbers would mean nothing.
2. (mcq) A bridge builder in Chennai sends her numbers to a factory in Pune. Why must both of them use newtons?  
   Options: So the numbers mean the same thing in both places / Because newtons are cheaper / Because Chennai is bigger than Pune  
   **Answer:** So the numbers mean the same thing in both places. *Why:* A shared unit means a pull written in Chennai is the same pull when it is read in Pune. Without it, the factory could make parts that are too weak.
3. (mcq) Which of these pulls down on your hand with about 1 newton?  
   Options: A small apple / A school bag full of books / A feather  
   **Answer:** A small apple. *Why:* A small apple of about 100 grams pulls with about 1 newton. A full school bag is more like 30 or 40 newtons. A feather is far less than 1 newton.
4. (mcq) The newton is named after Isaac Newton. Who decided how big one newton is?  
   Options: People agreed on it together, using the kilogram, the metre and the second / Newton measured it inside an apple / Nature made it that size  
   **Answer:** People agreed on it together, using the kilogram, the metre and the second. *Why:* The newton is an agreement between people. It was named in 1948, long after Newton died in 1727. Picking 'nature' mixes up the force, which is real, with the unit we count it in, which people chose.
5. (mcq) On this forcemeter, the numbers go 0, 5, 10, 15, 20. Each small mark is 1 newton. What is the reading?  
   Options: 13 newtons / 3 newtons / 15 newtons  
   **Answer:** 13 newtons. *Why:* The pointer is 3 small marks past 10, and each mark is 1 newton, so it reads 13 newtons. Picking 3 means only the small marks were counted. Picking 15 means the reading was rounded to the nearest number.
6. (mcq) A boy reads a forcemeter from far below, looking up at the pointer. What can go wrong?  
   Options: The pointer looks shifted, so he may read the wrong mark / The spring stretches more / Nothing can go wrong  
   **Answer:** The pointer looks shifted, so he may read the wrong mark. *Why:* Looking from below or above makes the pointer seem to sit on a different mark. Reading with your eyes level with the pointer gives the true reading.
7. (mcq) Why does a forcemeter use a spring, and not a stiff steel rod?  
   Options: A spring stretches more for a bigger pull, so the pointer can show the size / A rod would be too heavy / Springs are prettier  
   **Answer:** A spring stretches more for a bigger pull, so the pointer can show the size. *Why:* The stretch is what shows the size of the pull. A stiff rod would hardly stretch at all, so the pointer would not move.
8. (mcq) A spring stretches 2 centimetres when it is pulled with 1 newton. How far does it stretch for 3 newtons, inside its range?  
   Options: 6 centimetres / 3 centimetres / 2 centimetres  
   **Answer:** 6 centimetres. *Why:* Each newton adds about 2 centimetres, so 3 newtons give 3 times 2, which is 6 centimetres. Robert Hooke found this rule in 1678. Picking 3 means the newtons were read as centimetres.
9. (mcq) Before the coin test, you guessed how many coins you would need, and said why. Why say a reason, not just a number?  
   Options: So after the test you can see whether your reason was right / To make the test take longer / Because a reason changes the result  
   **Answer:** So after the test you can see whether your reason was right. *Why:* A guess with a reason can be checked. If the result surprises you, your reason was wrong somewhere, and you learn what to think again.
10. (mcq) A girl tests two notebooks. She slides the thin one on a smooth table, and the thick one on a rough mat. What is unfair about her test?  
   Options: She changed two things at once, so she can't tell which one made the difference / Nothing is unfair / She should use more coins  
   **Answer:** She changed two things at once, so she can't tell which one made the difference. *Why:* In a fair test, you change only one thing. She changed the notebook and the surface, so if the numbers differ, she can't say why.
11. (mcq) On the Moon, a bathroom scale shows a 30 kilogram girl as about 5 kilograms. Did she lose 25 kilograms of her body?  
   Options: No. The scale shows her push, and the Moon pulls her less / Yes, the Moon makes people smaller / The scale must be broken  
   **Answer:** No. The scale shows her push, and the Moon pulls her less. *Why:* A bathroom scale measures how hard you push down on it. The Moon pulls about one sixth as hard, so she pushes about one sixth as hard. All of her is still there.
12. (mcq) You press your palm on a table. Deep down, what pushes your hand back?  
   Options: The electrons in the table push away the electrons in your hand / Tiny springs inside the table / Air trapped under your hand  
   **Answer:** The electrons in the table push away the electrons in your hand. *Why:* Electrons push other electrons away. So your hand and the table push apart, and that push is what you feel. Picking 'tiny springs' is a fair picture, but the real spring is the electric push between atoms.
13. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still not know?  
   Options: What gravity really is, deep down / How to measure a pull in newtons / Whether a spring stretches when it is pulled  
   **Answer:** What gravity really is, deep down. *Why:* Measuring forces is well understood. What gravity is, deep down, is still an open question. Newton, Einstein and physicists today give different answers.

## Applications
- Shops use weighing scales and spring balances to sell rice, dal and vegetables fairly.
- Airports use luggage scales to check that bags are not too heavy for the plane.
- Doctors use a grip meter to find how hard you can squeeze. It shows how strong your hands are after an illness.
- Engineers test ropes, cables and climbing gear by pulling them with huge force meters until they break.
- Car makers crash cars into walls fitted with force sensors, to make seatbelts and airbags safer.
- Cranes have load sensors that stop them lifting more than is safe.
- Engineers check the pull in the cables that hold up bridges like the Bandra-Worli Sea Link in Mumbai.
- Rocket scientists at Sriharikota test the push of rocket engines while they are tied down on the ground.
- Digital kitchen scales have a load cell that feels a push. Some phones can also feel how hard you press the screen.
- Sports scientists find out how hard a footballer kicks or a cricketer hits, to improve training.
- Atomic force microscopes feel the push of single atoms, to make computer chips and new materials.
- Optical tweezers feel the pull of single molecules, helping doctors understand how cells and muscles work.
- Legal Metrology officers check shop scales with standard weights, so nobody is cheated.
- Scientists map the tiny changes in gravity's pull to find water, oil and minerals underground.

## Open questions
- **What is a force, really, deep down?** Newton could describe gravity but not explain it. Einstein said gravity is bent space and time. Particle physicists think forces are carried by particles. Nobody has joined these into one picture.
- **Does a graviton, a particle that carries gravity, exist?** Many physicists think it should. It would be so hard to catch that nobody knows how to build an experiment to find one.
- **Why do measurements of gravity's strength disagree?** Scientists have measured the strength of gravity, often called big G, many times since Cavendish. The best results still disagree more than they should. Nobody is sure why.
- **How small a force can we ever measure?** Quantum physics says measuring always nudges things a little. Clever new tools can get around some limits. Nobody knows how far this can go.
- **Exactly why does a small box feel heavier than a big box of the same weight?** The size-weight illusion was described in 1891. Scientists still argue about how much comes from what we expect, and how much from how we grip and lift.
- **How do millions of tiny molecular pulls work together to make a muscle strong and smooth?** Scientists can find one molecule's pull. How so many of them work together so well is still being studied.
- **Is the strength of gravity the same everywhere in the universe, and at every time?** As far as we can tell, yes. But scientists keep testing it. If it changed even a little, our ideas about the universe would have to change.
- **Why does the pull needed to slide something grow so evenly with its weight?** Leonardo da Vinci noticed this rule around 1500, and Guillaume Amontons wrote it down in 1699. It works well. But why it works, at the size of atoms, is still being studied. It seems to depend on how many tiny bumps really touch.

## Connected maths
- Look at the forcemeter in the picture. The big numbers are whole newtons. Each small mark between them is half a newton. What is the reading? **3 and a half newtons.**. The pointer is past the 3, but before the 4. It sits on the small mark halfway between them. Each small mark is half a newton. So the reading is 3 newtons and half a newton more. That is 3 and a half newtons. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- In the coin-pull investigation, Meera, a girl in Class 3, needed 6 five-rupee coins to start her notebook sliding. Her grandmother tested the same notebook with one-rupee coins and needed 12. On a ruler balance, one five-rupee coin balanced two one-rupee coins. Did they find the same pull, or different pulls? **The same pull. 6 five-rupee coins pull as hard as 12 one-rupee coins.**. One five-rupee coin pulls as hard as 2 one-rupee coins. So 6 five-rupee coins pull as hard as 6 lots of 2 one-rupee coins. 6 times 2 is 12. Meera's grandmother needed 12. The numbers were different, but the pull was the same. Only the unit was different. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Here are made-up results from the coin-pull investigation. One notebook needed 6 coins. Two stacked notebooks needed 12. Three needed 17. Spot the pattern. About how many coins would four notebooks need? Why? **About 23 coins, because each extra notebook needs about 6 more coins.**. From 1 notebook to 2, the coins went up by 6. From 2 to 3, they went up by 5. So each notebook adds about 5 or 6 coins. 17 add 6 is 23. Real results are never perfectly even, so 22, 23 or 24 are all good predictions. If you have a fourth notebook, test your prediction. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- In the story Ten Packets of Salt, Mohan Lal's balance was set wrongly. Ten 1 kilogram packets of salt showed as only 5 kilograms. Basanti's basket showed 14 kilograms on the same balance. Something is odd. What was going wrong, and how heavy was the basket really? **The balance showed 5 kilograms too little every time. The basket was really 19 kilograms.**. The ten packets were agreed to be 10 kilograms, but the balance said 5. So it was 5 kilograms short. Its pointer started in the wrong place, so every reading was short by the same 5. To fix a reading, add 5 back. 14 add 5 is 19. That is exactly what the balance showed once the zero was fixed. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In the forcemeter tug of war, Ayesha, a girl in Class 3, pulled against her father. Ayesha's forcemeter showed 5 newtons, and her father's showed 5 newtons too. Her father said, 'So together we are pulling with 10 newtons.' Is he right? Explain. **No. The pull through the two forcemeters is 5 newtons. Each forcemeter shows the same pull, seen from its own end.**. Ayesha pulls on her father with 5 newtons, and he pulls back on her with 5 newtons. Both forcemeters feel that one pull passing through them. Adding 5 and 5 counts the same pull twice. If he let go, both forcemeters would drop to 0 together. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In the Moon tiffin activity, your 600 gram tiffin pulled with about 6 newtons on Earth. The Moon pulls about one sixth as hard. Predict: about how hard would the same tiffin pull on the Moon? **About 1 newton.**. One sixth means sharing into 6 equal parts and taking one part. 6 newtons shared into 6 parts is 1 newton each. So on the Moon the tiffin pulls with about 1 newton, like a small apple on Earth. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)

Draw a bar chart. How much bigger is the fridge door's pull than the drawer's? **The fridge door's pull is 13 newtons bigger. Here is the bar chart.**.
