# How big is a force? Forcemeters and newtons
`sci.forces.measuring-forces.L3`

**Summary.** This unit is about how big a force is, and how we measure it. A force is a push or a pull. Some forces are tiny, like a feather landing on your hand. Some are huge, like a rocket lifting off. Our hands are not good at telling the size of a force, so people invented tools to measure it. A forcemeter is a spring with a hook and a row of numbers. The harder you pull, the more the spring stretches. Forces are measured in newtons. One newton is about the pull of a small apple on your hand. From the tiny pull of one molecule inside your body to the pull between the Earth and the Moon, scientists measure them all in newtons.

**Intro.** *Hold a sealed 100 gram packet in one hand and a 1 kilogram packet of atta in the other. Close your eyes. How many times harder is the big one pulling your hand down? Now how could you find out for sure?* People have always known that some pushes are small and some are big. But 'big' and 'small' are not good enough. A shopkeeper selling rice, a builder hanging a bridge and a doctor testing your grip all need a number. So people found a way to turn a pull into a number. They used something that stretches the same way every time: a spring. This unit is about that trick. It goes from your kitchen all the way to the forces inside your cells and the pull of the Moon.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. Forces make things start moving, stop, speed up, slow down, turn or change shape. *e.g.* You pull a drawer open. You push a door shut.
- **Size of a force**: How strong a push or a pull is. Scientists also call it the strength of the force. *e.g.* Lifting a pencil takes a small pull. Lifting a full bucket takes a big pull.
- **Measure**: To find out how big something is by comparing it with a unit that everyone agrees on. *e.g.* You measure a pencil with a ruler. It is 15 centimetres long.
- **Unit**: An agreed amount we count in. Centimetres are a unit of length. Newtons are a unit of force. *e.g.* A road is 5 kilometres long. A pull is 5 newtons.
- **Newton (N)**: The unit for measuring force. It is named after the scientist Isaac Newton. The short way to write it is N. *e.g.* A small apple of about 100 grams pulls down on your hand with about 1 newton.
- **Forcemeter**: A tool that measures pulls. It has a spring inside, a hook at the bottom and a scale of numbers. People also call it a spring balance or a newton meter. *e.g.* Hook a school bag on a forcemeter. The pointer shows how hard the bag pulls down.
- **Spring**: A coil of wire that stretches when you pull it and goes back when you let go. A bigger pull makes it stretch further. *e.g.* There is a spring inside a ballpoint pen. Many doors have a long spring to pull them shut.
- **Scale**: The row of marks and numbers on a measuring tool. You read the number where the pointer stops. *e.g.* The scale on a ruler has centimetres. The scale on a forcemeter has newtons.
- **Pointer**: The line or arrow on a measuring tool that moves to show the reading. *e.g.* On a forcemeter, the pointer slides down the scale as you pull harder.
- **Reading**: The number you see on a measuring tool when you use it, with its unit. *e.g.* The reading on the forcemeter is 4 newtons.
- **Zero**: The starting mark on a scale. Before you measure, the pointer must sit exactly on zero. *e.g.* With nothing on the hook, the pointer should point to 0.
- **Range**: The smallest and the biggest force a tool can measure. Pulling harder than its range can spoil the spring. *e.g.* A 0 to 10 newton forcemeter is good for an apple, but not for a bucket of water.
- **Calibrate**: To put the marks on a measuring tool by testing it with things you already know. *e.g.* You hang a 100 gram packet on your homemade forcemeter and mark that place '1 N'.
- **Estimate**: A careful guess of a size, made before you measure. *e.g.* I estimate my water bottle pulls with about 5 newtons. Then I measure it.
- **Weight**: The pull of a planet on a thing. Weight is a force, so scientists measure it in newtons. *e.g.* A 1 kilogram packet of atta has a weight of about 10 newtons on Earth.
- **Mass**: How much stuff a thing is made of. It is measured in grams and kilograms. Your mass is the same on Earth and on the Moon. *e.g.* A packet of salt with a mass of 1 kilogram has the same salt in it everywhere.
- **Weighing scale**: A tool for measuring how hard things press down. Kitchen scales and bathroom scales are weighing scales. In India they show grams and kilograms. *e.g.* You stand on a bathroom scale and it shows 25 kilograms.
- **Load cell**: A small block of metal inside digital scales. It bends a tiny bit when pressed. A chip turns that bend into a number. *e.g.* The digital scale at the vegetable shop has a load cell under its tray.
- **Molecule**: A tiny group of atoms joined together. Atoms are the tiny building blocks of everything. *e.g.* Inside your body, some molecules walk along threads, pulling loads like porters.
- **Gravity**: The pull that every thing has on every other thing. The Earth's gravity pulls you down. The Moon's gravity pulls the sea. *e.g.* Gravity pulls a mango down from the tree.

## Explanations
### Forces come in sizes · *defined*
**Definition:** Every force has a size. A force can be tiny, medium or huge. A bigger force makes a bigger change.

Some forces are small, like lifting a leaf. Some are huge, like a train pulling wagons. You can often see the size of a force by what it does. A bigger push squashes a balloon more. A bigger pull stretches a rubber band further. A bigger kick sends a football faster.
- e.g. A bird picking up a leaf uses a tiny pull. A horse pulling a cart uses a big pull.
- e.g. Push a balloon gently against a book, then hard against a wall. The balloon squashes more for the bigger push.
- e.g. When you pull a heavy school bag with a rubber band, the band stretches long. A light pencil box stretches it only a little.
### Feeling a force is not measuring it · *defined*
**Definition:** Our bodies can compare pushes and pulls roughly. But they cannot give an exact, fair number. Tools can.

Your muscles can feel a pull, but they are easily fooled. When you are tired, a bag feels heavier. When you have just lifted something heavy, a light thing feels lighter. Here is a strange trick. Take a big box and a small box that weigh exactly the same. Most people say the small one feels heavier. A French doctor, Augustin Charpentier, wrote about this in 1891. It is called the size-weight illusion. Even today scientists argue about exactly why it happens. So if we want to be fair and sure, we need a tool that does not get tired or fooled.
- e.g. After carrying a full bucket, an empty bucket feels as light as air.
- e.g. A small tin full of coins feels heavier than a big box of the same weight.
- e.g. Two people lift the same bag. One says it is heavy. The other says it is light.
### Measuring means comparing with a unit · *defined*
**Definition:** Force is measured in newtons. The short way to write newtons is N. One newton is about the pull of a small apple on your hand.

To measure anything, you compare it with a unit that everyone agrees on. Length has centimetres and metres. Force has newtons. The unit is named after Isaac Newton, an English scientist who explained how forces change movement. He wrote his famous book about forces in 1687. Scientists agreed to call the unit 'the newton' in 1948. One newton is a small force. It is about the pull of a small apple, or a 100 gram packet of tea, on your hand. Ten newtons is about the pull of a 1 litre bottle of water.
- e.g. A 100 gram packet of biscuits pulls down with about 1 newton.
- e.g. A 1 litre bottle of water pulls down with about 10 newtons.
- e.g. A Class 3 child who is 25 kilograms presses down on the floor with about 250 newtons.
### How a forcemeter works · *defined*
**Definition:** A forcemeter is a spring with a hook and a scale. The bigger the pull on the hook, the further the spring stretches, and the further the pointer moves along the scale.

Look inside a forcemeter and you find a spring. The top of the spring is fixed. The hook hangs from the bottom. When something pulls on the hook, the spring stretches. A pointer is fixed to the spring, so it slides down the scale. Twice the pull gives twice the stretch. That is why the marks on a forcemeter are evenly spaced. An English scientist, Robert Hooke, published this rule in 1678. The unit on Stretching and springs explores it more. But a spring only follows the rule if you don't pull too hard. Pull past its range and it stays stretched, and the forcemeter is spoiled.
- e.g. A luggage scale at the airport is a forcemeter for heavy bags.
- e.g. A shopkeeper's hanging spring balance for vegetables is a forcemeter marked in kilograms.
- e.g. You can make a forcemeter with a rubber band, a paper clip and a strip of card.
### Reading a forcemeter properly · *defined*
**Definition:** To read a forcemeter well: check the zero, hold it straight, pull steadily, read at eye level, count the small marks and write the unit.

A forcemeter only tells the truth if you use it well. First, check the zero. With nothing on the hook, the pointer must be exactly on 0. If it isn't, turn the little screw at the top until it is. Second, hold it straight and still. A swinging bag gives a wobbly reading. Third, pull slowly and steadily. A sudden jerk makes the pointer jump too high. Fourth, look at the pointer with your eyes level with it, not from above or below. Fifth, count the small marks between the big numbers. Last, write the number with its unit, for example '3 newtons'.
- e.g. If the pointer starts at 1 with nothing on it, every reading will be 1 newton too big.
- e.g. Reading from above can make 3 newtons look like 3 and a half.
- e.g. A 0 to 10 newton forcemeter with 20 small marks has one mark for every half newton.
### Weight is a force · *defined*
**Definition:** Weight is the pull of a planet on a thing. It is a force, measured in newtons. Mass is how much stuff a thing has, measured in kilograms.

When you hang a bag from a forcemeter, what is pulling it down? It is the Earth. The pull of a planet on a thing is called its weight. So weight is a force, and scientists measure it in newtons. In shops, we say a bag of rice 'weighs 1 kilogram'. Strictly, the kilogram tells us how much rice there is. Scientists call that its mass. On Earth, every kilogram is pulled down with about 10 newtons. On the Moon, gravity is weaker. The same bag of rice would pull the forcemeter down only about one sixth as hard. The rice has not changed. Only the pull has. The unit on Weight and mass goes deeper into this.
- e.g. A 1 kilogram bag of rice reads about 10 newtons on Earth, and about 1.6 newtons on the Moon.
- e.g. Astronauts on the Moon could carry heavy packs easily, because their packs pulled down less.
- e.g. On Jupiter, the same bag of rice would pull down about two and a half times harder than on Earth.
### Measuring pushes · *defined*
**Definition:** Pushes can be measured with weighing scales. A spring or a bending metal block inside turns the push into a number.

A forcemeter measures pulls. So how do we measure a push? A bathroom scale is one answer. When you stand on it, you push down on it. Inside an old scale is a spring that gets squashed. Inside a digital scale is a load cell. That is a small block of metal that bends a tiny bit when pressed. A chip turns the bend into a number. Scales in India show kilograms, not newtons. To change kilograms of push into newtons, multiply by 10. You can also see pushes without numbers. A balloon, a sponge or a ball of foam squashes more when the push is bigger.
- e.g. Pressing a bathroom scale with one finger might show 2 kilograms. That is a push of about 20 newtons.
- e.g. The cricket ball machine at a stadium measures how hard a bowler's ball hits a pad.
- e.g. Car makers crash cars into walls fitted with sensors, to measure the push and make cars safer.
### Tiny forces and huge forces · *defined*
**Definition:** Forces range from far less than one newton to many millions of newtons. The newton works for all of them.

Forces in our world go from unbelievably tiny to unbelievably huge. A sheet of paper pulls down with about one twentieth of a newton. A grown-up presses on the floor with about 600 newtons. A big saltwater crocodile, like the ones in Bhitarkanika in Odisha, can bite with about 16,000 newtons. The giant Saturn V rocket that carried astronauts to the Moon pushed off with about 34 million newtons. And the Earth and the Moon pull on each other with about 200 million million million newtons. Scientists measure all of them in the same unit.
- e.g. A one-rupee coin pulls down with only a few hundredths of a newton.
- e.g. An Asian elephant weighs about 40,000 newtons.
- e.g. India's rockets, at Sriharikota, push off with millions of newtons.
### A force is measured by what it does to movement · *defined*
**Definition:** One newton is the force that makes a 1 kilogram thing speed up by 1 metre per second, every second.

Isaac Newton found a deeper way to say how big a force is. A force changes how things move. A bigger force on the same thing makes it speed up faster. So scientists define one newton like this. It is the push that makes a 1 kilogram thing speed up by 1 metre per second, every second, if nothing else pushes or rubs. Imagine a 1 kilogram ball floating in space. Push it with 1 newton for one second. It moves 1 metre each second. Keep pushing for 2 seconds, and it moves 2 metres each second. A forcemeter uses a spring because it is easier. But deep down, a force is measured by how much it can change movement.
- e.g. A bigger kick makes the same football go faster.
- e.g. The same push makes an empty trolley speed up much more than a full one.
- e.g. In space, a tiny push keeps a floating astronaut speeding up until something stops it.
### Measuring the tiniest forces, inside living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists can measure forces far smaller than a newton, using light beams and tiny bending needles.

Inside every cell in your body, tiny molecules walk along threads, carrying loads. They are like porters on a mountain path. One kind is called kinesin. How hard can one kinesin molecule pull? To find out, scientists used light as a pair of tweezers. A focused laser beam can hold a tiny bead and measure how hard something tugs on it. These are called optical tweezers. Arthur Ashkin invented them, and won a Nobel Prize for them in 2018. That same year, Donna Strickland shared a Nobel Prize for her work on lasers. She was only the third woman ever to win the Nobel Prize in Physics. With optical tweezers, scientists found that one kinesin molecule pulls with about 6 piconewtons. That is about 6 millionths of a millionth of a newton. Other scientists use a tiny needle that bends, called an atomic force microscope. It feels the push of single atoms.
- e.g. Your muscles work because millions of molecules each give a tiny pull at the same time.
- e.g. An atomic force microscope can feel the bumps of single atoms on a surface.
- e.g. Optical tweezers can stretch a single strand of DNA, the molecule that carries your body's instructions.
### Weighing the Earth with a twisting wire · *defined*
**Definition:** Cavendish measured the tiny gravity pull between lead balls with a twisting wire, in 1798. It let scientists work out the mass of the Earth.

Everything pulls on everything else with gravity. Even two balls on a table pull each other. But that pull is so tiny that nobody could feel it. In 1798, an English scientist, Henry Cavendish, measured it. He hung a rod with two small lead balls from a thin wire. He put two big lead balls nearby. The big balls pulled the small ones, and the wire twisted a tiny bit. The twisting wire worked like a very gentle forcemeter. The pull was smaller than the weight of a grain of sand. Cavendish watched through a telescope from outside the room, so even the warmth of his body would not spoil it. From this, scientists could work out how heavy the whole Earth is. The idea and the first apparatus came from another scientist, John Michell. He died in 1793, before he could use it.
- e.g. A twisting wire can show forces far too small for a spring.
- e.g. Cavendish's measurement let people work out the mass of the Earth for the first time.
### Who decides how big a unit is? · *defined*
**Definition:** Since 2019, the kilogram, and so the newton, is fixed by numbers from nature, not by one metal object.

A newton depends on the kilogram, the metre and the second. So who decides how big a kilogram is? From 1889 to 2019, the kilogram was one real metal cylinder. It was made of platinum and iridium. It was kept locked in a vault near Paris, under three glass covers. People called it Le Grand K. Countries had copies. India's copy is kept at the National Physical Laboratory in New Delhi. But metal can slowly change. The copies and the original drifted apart by a tiny amount. So in 2019, scientists changed the rule. Now the kilogram is fixed by a number from nature, called the Planck constant. A machine called a Kibble balance can measure it. It balances a weight against a magnet's push. Bryan Kibble, a British scientist, invented it in 1975.
- e.g. The metre is now fixed by the speed of light, not by a metal bar.
- e.g. Any country with a Kibble balance can now make its own perfect kilogram.
### Fair measures in India's history · *defined*
**Definition:** Fair trade needs measures everyone agrees on. India has used standard weights since the Indus Valley cities, about 4,500 years ago.

People have needed fair measures for thousands of years. In the cities of the Indus Valley, about 4,500 years ago, traders used carefully cut stone weights, shaped like little cubes. The sizes followed a pattern: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64, and then bigger steps. The same sizes were used in cities hundreds of kilometres apart. Much later, the Arthashastra, an old Indian book about running a kingdom, described an officer who checked the scales and weights in markets. For a long time, India used units like the tola, the ser and the maund. They were not the same everywhere. In 1958, India began to change to grams and kilograms. Today, Legal Metrology officers still check shop scales and put a seal on honest ones.
- e.g. Indus Valley stone weights have been found at Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and Lothal.
- e.g. Some grandparents still say 'one ser of milk' or 'one tola of gold'.
- e.g. Look for a small seal or stamp on the scales at your vegetable shop.
### What is a force, really? · *debated*
We can measure forces very well. But what is a force, deep down? How does the Earth pull a mango without touching it? Scientists have given different answers, and the question is not finished.
- e.g. A magnet pulls a pin across a gap. Nothing we can see joins them.
- e.g. A forcemeter inside a falling lift would read zero, even though gravity is still there.
- **Isaac Newton, in 1687 and 1713:** A force is whatever changes how a thing moves. Newton worked out exactly how gravity pulls. But he said he did not know how it reaches across empty space, and he would not guess.
- **Albert Einstein, in 1915:** Gravity is not really a pull at all. Heavy things bend space and time around them. Other things follow the bends. A forcemeter in a falling lift reads zero, because nothing is holding the bag up.
- **Particle physicists today:** Electric and magnetic forces are carried by tiny particles flying between things. Many think gravity might be carried by a particle too, called a graviton. Nobody has ever found one.
### Does measuring change what you measure? · *idea*
A heavy forcemeter adds its own pull to what it measures. A cold thermometer cools the tea a tiny bit. Scientists always try to make their tools disturb things as little as possible. But at the size of atoms, there seems to be a limit. Quantum physics, the science of the very tiniest things, says that measuring something always nudges it a little. In 2014, physicists in California measured a force of about 42 yoctonewtons. One yoctonewton is so small that a million million million million of them make just one newton. They found their measurement was close to that limit. Whether we can ever beat it, in clever ways, is still being worked on.
- e.g. A spring balance hanging on a spring balance adds its own weight to the reading.
- e.g. Shining light on a tiny thing to see it also gives it a tiny push.
- **Most physicists:** There is a limit to how gently you can measure. It comes from quantum physics.
- **Physicists building new tools:** Clever tricks can get past the usual limit in some experiments. How far this can go is still being explored.

## Mostly agreed
- Forces are measured in newtons, all over the world. *How we know:* Scientists from many countries agreed on the newton in 1948. Every science book and forcemeter in the world uses it.
- A spring stretches evenly as the pull grows, until you pull it too hard. *How we know:* Hang 100 gram packets on a spring one at a time. Each packet adds about the same stretch. Robert Hooke published this in 1678.
- On Earth, every kilogram is pulled down with about 10 newtons. More exactly, it is 9.8 newtons. *How we know:* Hang a 1 kilogram packet on a good forcemeter. It reads just under 10 newtons. Scientists have measured this very carefully all over the planet.
- The same thing has a smaller weight on the Moon, but the same mass. *How we know:* Astronauts on the Moon in 1969 to 1972 bounced along with heavy packs that would have been hard to carry on Earth. Their packs still held the same stuff.
- Measuring tools must be checked against agreed standards to be fair. *How we know:* In India, Legal Metrology officers test shop scales with standard weights and seal the honest ones. Scientists check their own tools against national standards, like the kilogram copy kept in New Delhi.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If nothing moves, there is no force.** (Many children, and many grown-ups without noticing; *rejected*)  
  For: We usually notice forces when something starts moving.  
  Against: Push hard on a wall through a balloon. The wall doesn't move, but the balloon squashes flat. A book lying on a table is pulled down by the Earth and pushed up by the table, all the time. Put a bathroom scale under it and you can measure the push.
- **Your hands can tell you exactly how heavy something is.** (A common belief. Many traders once judged loads by lifting them.; *rejected*)  
  For: With practice, people get quite good at guessing weights.  
  Against: Tired arms make things feel heavier. A small box feels heavier than a big box of the same weight. Augustin Charpentier, a French doctor, wrote about this in 1891. That is why fair trade uses scales.
- **A bathroom scale measures how much of you there is.** (Most people, most of the time; *rejected*)  
  For: The scale shows kilograms, and kilograms measure mass, the amount of stuff.  
  Against: The scale really measures how hard you push down on it. Bend your knees fast and the number jumps. Press on a table beside you and it drops. On the Moon it would show a much smaller number, though you'd still be all there. The scale is just set to turn your push into kilograms, as if you were standing still on Earth.
- **Isaac Newton worked out the rules of forces all by himself.** (Many storybooks; *debated*)  
  For: Newton's book of 1687 brought the rules together in one place, and the unit of force is named after him.  
  Against: Newton built on Galileo, Kepler and others. Robert Hooke wrote to Newton about how gravity might weaken with distance, and felt Newton gave him too little credit. Newton himself wrote that he stood 'on the shoulders of giants'.
- **Gravity is a force, just like a pull on a rope.** (Newton's way of seeing it, still used in most science books; *debated*)  
  For: It works for nearly everything, from dropping mangoes to sending rockets to the Moon.  
  Against: Albert Einstein showed in 1915 that gravity can be seen as bent space and time. A forcemeter falling freely reads zero. His view explains some things Newton's can't, like a slow wobble in the orbit of the planet Mercury.
- **The big forces are the important ones.** (A common way of thinking; *rejected*)  
  For: Rockets, earthquakes and cranes are impressive, and they need big forces.  
  Against: Your heart, your muscles and every cell in your body work because of tiny pulls of a few piconewtons each, added up millions of times. Cavendish weighed the Earth by measuring a force smaller than a grain of sand's weight.

## Story: Ten Packets of Salt
*Made up. The village, Pushpa, Basanti, Mohan Lal, Diwan and Kamla Didi are imagined. Porters really do carry loads on mountain treks in Uttarakhand, often paid by the load, and spring balances really have a zero screw that can be set wrongly. Sealed packets must by law weigh what they say, and Legal Metrology officers check scales.*

In the mountains of Uttarakhand, a stony path climbs from a small village all the way to a glacier, a river of ice. Every spring, trekkers came from the cities to walk it. They brought tents and cooking pots, sacks of rice and tins of ghee. They did not carry these things themselves. The porters of the village did.

Pushpa was nine. Her mother, Basanti, was a porter. Each morning, before the sun climbed over the ridge, Pushpa walked with her to the walnut tree at the edge of the village. There the contractor, Mohan Lal, weighed the loads. He had a brass spring balance as long as his forearm. Inside it was a coiled spring. When a basket hung from its hook, the spring stretched, and a little pointer slid down a row of numbers.

Mohan Lal paid the porters by the kilo. 'Fifteen,' he would call, squinting at the pointer as a basket swung below it. 'Eighteen. Fourteen.' His clerk wrote every number in a long book. At the end of the season, the porters would be paid for every kilo in the book.

Basanti bent and slipped the strap of her basket over her forehead. She stood, and the basket rose onto her back. Pushpa saw her mother's neck go tight, and her knees tremble for a moment before they steadied.

'Fourteen,' said Mohan Lal.

That evening, Basanti sat by the fire and rubbed her neck. 'Fourteen,' she said softly. 'It felt like twenty-five.'

'Maybe you were tired, Ma,' said Pushpa.

'Maybe,' said her mother. 'A tired back is a poor judge. And Mohan Lal's balance is made of brass. Brass does not get tired.'

But Pushpa kept thinking. All the porters were saying the same thing. Old Diwan, who had carried loads for thirty years, said the baskets had grown heavier, or else he had grown weaker. The young men said nothing at all. They did not want to sound weak.

The next morning, Pushpa stood very close to the walnut tree. Mohan Lal hung his balance from a low branch, with nothing on the hook. Pushpa looked at the pointer. Then she looked again. It was not on the zero. It sat a little way above the zero, in the empty space at the top where there were no numbers at all.

She tugged her mother's sleeve. 'Ma, the pointer is in the wrong place.'

Basanti glanced at Mohan Lal and hushed her. 'Don't make trouble, beti.'

Mohan Lal laughed. 'So the little one wants to read my balance! Go and play.' His thumb slid across the brass. When Pushpa looked again, she could not see the pointer at all.

All day, she turned it over in her mind. A balance gives you a number. But how do you check the balance? You would need something whose weight everybody already agreed on. Something nobody could argue with.

Then she thought of Kamla Didi's tea shop. Kamla Didi sold salt in sealed packets. Each one was printed with the words 1 kg. They came from a factory far away, and by law every packet had to hold what it said. Every family in the village had cooked with that salt for years.

That evening, Pushpa stood at the tea shop counter. 'Didi, I don't want to buy salt,' she said. 'I want to borrow it. Only until tomorrow morning.'

Kamla Didi raised her eyebrows. Then, without asking why, she counted ten packets into a cloth bag. 'Ten packets. Ten kilos,' she said. 'Bring back every one.'

The bag was so heavy that Pushpa had to carry it in her arms, like a sleeping baby goat. It was heavier than her school bag full of books. By the time she reached the walnut tree, her arms were aching and the porters were already lining up.

'Mohan Lal-ji,' she said, loud enough for everyone to hear. 'Please weigh my load.'

The porters laughed, kindly. Mohan Lal laughed too. He hooked her bag onto his balance and peered at it. 'Five kilos,' he said. 'A little girl's little load.'

'There are ten packets of salt in that bag,' said Pushpa. Her voice shook, but she kept going. 'Each one says one kilo. Kamla Didi sold them. All of you have cooked with them.'

She tipped the packets out onto a blanket, one at a time. The porters began to count with her. One. Two. Three. By the end, the whole line was counting. 'Eight. Nine. Ten.'

Nobody spoke. Then old Diwan stepped forward. He lifted the brass balance off the branch and held it up to the morning light, with nothing on its hook. Everyone could see the pointer now, sitting high in the empty space above the zero.

'Five kilos missing from a bag of ten,' Diwan said slowly. 'And five from my basket. Every morning. For how long?'

Mohan Lal began to say that factories cheat too, that the packets were probably light. But just then Kamla Didi came up the path. She was carrying her own shop balance, the old kind with two pans and iron weights. She set it on a flat rock. She put five packets of salt in one pan and a five-kilo iron weight in the other. The pans swung, and settled, and hung perfectly level.

Two different ways of measuring. And they agreed.

Mohan Lal did not look at anybody. He turned a small screw at the top of his balance, and the pointer slid down until it sat exactly on the zero. Then he hooked on Basanti's basket.

'Nineteen,' he said quietly.

That season, every porter put a few rupees into a tin. At the end of the summer, they bought their own spring balance from the town. They hung it on a nail on the walnut tree. And every morning after that, before the first basket went on the hook, somebody would call out across the cold air: 'Pushpa! Check the zero!' And Pushpa would look, and nod, and the weighing would begin.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a spring balance is a forcemeter. It turns a pull into a number by stretching a spring. But a number is only as fair as the tool. The pointer must start on zero, and the tool must agree with something everyone trusts, a known standard. Pushpa does what scientists call calibrating: she tests the balance with ten packets whose weight is agreed. Her mother's tired back could not prove anything, because feelings are not measurements. Ten packets of salt could. The story also quietly asks who is believed, and who carries the load.

- Ask: Basanti said her basket felt like twenty-five kilos. Why couldn't her feeling prove that the balance was wrong?
- Ask: What was wrong with Mohan Lal's balance before anything was hung on it?
- Ask: Why did Pushpa choose sealed packets of salt to test the balance? Why not stones from the path?
- Ask: Kamla Didi checked the salt with a different kind of balance. Why did it matter that two different tools agreed?
- Ask: Why do you think the porters asked Pushpa to check the zero every morning, and not Mohan Lal?
- Ask: Why didn't the young porters say anything about the heavy loads at first?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Pull Detective: how big are the pulls at home? (30 min)
- **question:** Which pull at home needs the biggest force: a door, a drawer, a bag or a chair?
- **predict:** Before you measure anything, write the six pulls in order. Put the one you think needs the biggest force first, and the smallest last.
- **fair test:** Pull every thing the same way: slowly and steadily, straight along the forcemeter. Read the biggest number just as the thing starts to move. Measure each pull 2 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Pull gently and slowly. Never pull a tall cupboard or bookshelf that could tip over. Don't pull past the biggest number on the forcemeter, or the spring may be spoiled.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart, with one bar for each pull. Make each bar as tall as its biggest reading.
- **results table:** What we pulled | My guess (order, 1 to 6) | Try 1 (newtons) | Try 2 (newtons) | Biggest reading (newtons)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the six pulls: the fridge door, a drawer, a chair, a school bag, a cupboard door and a slipper. 2. Ask them to put them in order, biggest pull first. 3. Write the order in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to be pull detectives. Which of these do you think needs the biggest pull? Put them in order, biggest first.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the six things. Which needs the biggest pull to move? Put them in order. Write your guesses down.
  - Ask: Why did you put that one first? (good: It is heavy.; It is stuck tight.; The fridge door has a magnet that holds it shut.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the forcemeter upright with nothing on the hook. 2. Ask your child to read it. 3. If the pointer is not on zero, turn the screw at the top until it is.  
  *Parent says:* Before we measure, we check the zero. Look at the pointer with nothing on the hook. Is it exactly on the 0?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the forcemeter up. Nothing on the hook. Is the pointer on 0? If not, ask a grown-up to fix it.
  - Check: With nothing on the hook, your forcemeter's pointer is on 1 newton. What will happen to your readings? → **They will all be 1 newton too big**
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tie the string loop to the first handle. 2. Hook the forcemeter into the loop. 3. Hold it in a straight line with the pull. 4. Pull slowly and steadily. 5. Read the biggest number as the thing starts to move. 6. Do it twice and write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Pull slowly, like a snail. Watch the pointer climb. Tell me the biggest number just as it starts to move.  
  *Child mode:* Hook on. Pull slowly and straight. Watch the pointer. Say the biggest number as it moves. Do it twice.
  - Note: Keep your eyes level with the pointer when you read it. A sudden jerk makes the pointer jump too high, so the test would not be fair. For the school bag, lift it straight up off the floor slowly. If a pull goes past the end of the scale, stop, and write 'more than' the biggest number.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the biggest readings, with one bar for each pull.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn the numbers into a picture. Each pull gets a bar. The taller the bar, the bigger the pull.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each pull. Make it as tall as its biggest number. Which bar is tallest?
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the measured order with the guessed order in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Were your guesses right? Which pull surprised you most?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your guesses. Look at your bars. Which one surprised you?
  - Note: The fridge door often surprises children. A magnetic strip in the rubber seal pulls the door shut.
  - Think: Why do you think the fridge door needs a bigger pull than its size suggests?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like real scientists. Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the fridge door needs a big pull. What I saw: it needed 18 newtons, but the drawer only needed 5. Why: there is a magnet in the fridge door pulling it shut.
### e2 · Does the Scale Weigh You, or Your Push? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a bathroom scale measure how much of you there is, or how hard you push on it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. If you press down on a table beside you, will the number on the scale go up, go down or stay the same?
- **fair test:** Stand in the same place on the scale each time, with both feet. Change only one thing at a time: pressing down on the table, pulling up on it, or bending your knees. Read the number each time when it settles, except for the knee test.
- **safety:** A grown-up stands beside the child the whole time. Use a strong table that cannot tip. Don't jump on the scale.
- **record:** Fill in the table. For each kilogram, write about 10 newtons beside it. Draw an arrow for your push on the scale in each picture.
- **results table:** What I did | My guess (up, down or same) | Number on the scale (kg) | Push in newtons (kg times 10)
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the scale on a hard floor next to a strong table. 2. Your child stands still on it with both feet. 3. Read and write the number when it settles.  
  *Parent says:* Stand still on the scale. What number does it show? That's your push on the scale when you stand still.  
  *Child mode:* Stand still on the scale. Read the number. Write it down.
  - Note: A child of 25 kilograms is pressing down with about 250 newtons. Help multiply by 10.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to guess what will happen. 2. They press down hard on the table top with both palms, still standing on the scale. 3. Read the number.  
  *Parent says:* Guess first. If you press down on the table, will the number go up, down, or stay the same? Now try it.  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Then press down hard on the table. What does the number do?
  - Note: The number goes down. The table now holds up part of your child's weight, so they push less on the scale.
  - Check: You stand on a bathroom scale and press down hard on a table beside you. What happens to the number? → **It goes down**
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child gently lifts up on the underside of the table edge with their fingertips. 2. Read the number. 3. Then they bend their knees quickly, once, and watch the needle or digits.  
  *Parent says:* Now gently pull up under the table edge. What happens? Then bend your knees quickly and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Gently pull up under the table edge. Watch the number. Then bend your knees fast. Watch again.
  - Note: Pulling up on the table makes the number go up, because the table pushes your child down. Bending the knees makes the number dip, then jump up as they stop. Nobody got heavier or lighter.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the big question and let your child answer before you explain.  
  *Parent says:* Did you get any heavier or lighter just now? Did any of you go away, or any more of you arrive? So what does the scale really measure?  
  *Child mode:* Did any of you disappear? So what does the scale measure? Say what you think.
  - Ask: What does the scale really measure? (good: How hard I push on it.; My push, not how much of me there is.; The force of my feet on it.)
  - Think: On the Moon, would the scale show a bigger number, a smaller number, or the same?
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like real scientists. Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the scale measures my push. What I saw: it showed 25 when I stood still, 18 when I pressed the table and 29 when I pulled up. Why: the table took some of my push, or added to it, but there was always the same amount of me.
### e3 · Can Your Hands Be Fooled? The Big Box and the Small Box (25 min)

- **s1 (6 min)**

  *Parent says:* These two boxes must pull with exactly the same force. Keep adding coins until the forcemeter shows the same number for both.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the big box. Read the number. Fill the small box until it shows the same number. Tape it shut.
  - Note: An empty shoe box pulls about 2 to 3 newtons. If the big box is very light, put a little crumpled paper and a few coins inside it too. What matters is that both read the same.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what people will say, and write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Both boxes pull the same. Will people feel that? What do you think they will say?  
  *Child mode:* Both boxes pull the same. What will people say? Write your guess.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask each person to lift both boxes, one in each hand, by the string loops. 2. Don't tell them what you did. 3. Ask: 'Which feels heavier?' 4. Write each answer in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Please lift these two boxes. Which one feels heavier to you?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a person to lift both boxes. Ask which feels heavier. Write their answer.
  - Note: Most people say the small box feels heavier, often much heavier. Some even refuse to believe the forcemeter.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the answers and draw a bar for each one.

  *Child mode:* Count the answers. Draw one bar for small, big and same.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Let's finish like real scientists. Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Finish like a scientist. Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: hands can be fooled. What I saw: the forcemeter showed 3 newtons for both boxes, but 5 of 6 people said the small one felt heavier. Why: our brains expect big things to be heavier, so the big box surprised them by being light.

### a4 · Tug of War on Two Forcemeters (15 min)

### Force Measurer (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What do we measure forces in?  
   Options: Centimetres / Newtons / Forcemeters / Litres  
   **Answer:** Newtons. *Why:* Forces are measured in newtons. Picking centimetres or litres usually means force is mixed up with length or amount of water. Picking forcemeters mixes up the tool with the unit. The forcemeter is the tool. The newton is the unit.
2. (mcq) Which tool measures the size of a pull?  
   Options: A ruler / A thermometer / A forcemeter / A clock  
   **Answer:** A forcemeter. *Why:* A forcemeter measures pulls. A ruler measures length, a thermometer measures how hot something is, and a clock measures time.
3. (fill) The short way to write newtons is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['N']. *Why:* N stands for newton, named after Isaac Newton.
4. (mcq) Put these in order from the smallest force to the biggest: a bird picking up a leaf, a train pulling wagons, a girl lifting a book, a horse pulling a cart. Which comes first?  
   Options: The train / The horse / The girl / The bird  
   **Answer:** The bird. *Why:* The bird picking up a leaf uses the smallest force. The order is bird, girl, horse, train. Picking the train usually means the order was read from biggest to smallest.
5. (mcq) What does the pointer of this forcemeter show?  
   Options: 4 newtons / 4 and a half newtons / 5 newtons / 45 newtons  
   **Answer:** 4 and a half newtons. *Why:* The pointer is one small mark past 4, and each small mark is half a newton. So the reading is 4 and a half newtons. Picking 4 or 5 usually means the small marks were not counted.
6. (mcq) Before you use a forcemeter, what should you check first?  
   Options: That it is shiny / That the pointer is on zero with nothing on the hook / That the spring is very stretched / That it is the biggest forcemeter in the house  
   **Answer:** That the pointer is on zero with nothing on the hook. *Why:* Check the zero first. If the pointer starts in the wrong place, every reading will be wrong by the same amount. Picking 'the spring is stretched' would mean it is already spoiled.
7. (truefalse) A small apple pulls down on your hand with about 1 newton.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A small apple of about 100 grams pulls down with about 1 newton.
8. (truefalse) If something doesn't move, no force is acting on it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Push a wall with a balloon. The wall doesn't move, but the balloon squashes, so there is a big force. Forces can act on things that stay still.
9. (fill) Inside a forcemeter there is a ____ that stretches when you pull.  
   **Answer:** ['spring']. *Why:* A forcemeter has a spring inside. The harder the pull, the more it stretches.
10. (match) Match each tool to what it measures.  
   **Answer:** Forcemeter → pulls, in newtons; Ruler → length, in centimetres; Bathroom scale → how hard you press down; Measuring jug → amount of water, in millilitres. *Why:* Every measuring tool has its own job and its own unit.
11. (sort) Sort these into small forces and big forces.  
   **Answer:** lifting a pencil → Small force; pushing a car → Big force; turning a page → Small force; a rocket lifting off → Big force; a tractor pulling a trolley → Big force; blowing out a candle → Small force. *Why:* Small forces move light things a little. Big forces move heavy things, or move things fast.
12. (mcq) You pushed a balloon against a cup, a chair and a wall. When was the balloon most squashed?  
   Options: Pushing the cup / Pushing the chair / Pushing the wall / It was the same every time  
   **Answer:** Pushing the wall. *Why:* The wall needed the biggest push, so the balloon squashed most, even though the wall didn't move. Picking the cup usually means a moving thing is thought to need more force.
13. (mcq) A 1 litre bottle of water pulls down with about…  
   Options: 1 newton / 10 newtons / 100 newtons / 1,000 newtons  
   **Answer:** 10 newtons. *Why:* 1 litre of water has a mass of about 1 kilogram. On Earth, each kilogram pulls down with about 10 newtons. Picking 1 newton mixes the bottle up with the apple.
14. (write) Why is it better to measure a force with a forcemeter than to guess by lifting it?  
   **Answer:** My hands can be fooled. Tired arms make things feel heavier, and small heavy things feel heavier than big things that weigh the same. A forcemeter gives the same number every time.. *Why:* A good answer says that feelings change, but a spring stretches the same way for the same pull.
15. (draw) Draw a forcemeter. Label the spring, the hook, the pointer and the scale.  
   **Answer:** The drawing shows a spring inside a tube, a hook at the bottom, a pointer fixed to the spring and a scale of numbers beside it.. *Why:* These four parts work together. The pull on the hook stretches the spring, and the pointer moves along the scale.
16. (mcq) Why should you pull a forcemeter slowly and steadily?  
   Options: So it doesn't get dirty / Because a jerk makes the pointer jump too high / Because forcemeters are scared of speed / So the spring gets warm  
   **Answer:** Because a jerk makes the pointer jump too high. *Why:* A sudden jerk makes the pointer jump past the real reading for a moment. Pulling steadily gives a fair, true reading.

   **Answer:** One litre of water always has a mass of about 1 kilogram. Everybody can get water and measure it with a jug. Nobody owns it.. *Why:* The gram was first defined in 1795 using water. Water is a standard that nature gives everyone.
11. (mcq) Which of these is the biggest force?  
   Options: The Saturn V rocket lifting off / An elephant standing / The Earth and the Moon pulling on each other / A crocodile's bite  
   **Answer:** The Earth and the Moon pulling on each other. *Why:* The Earth and the Moon pull on each other with about 200 million million million newtons. The Saturn V pushed with about 34 million. Picking the rocket usually means things we can see seem bigger than the pull between worlds.
12. (write) Isaac Newton said he did not know how gravity reaches across empty space. Einstein said gravity is bent space and time. Others think it might be carried by a particle called a graviton. Which idea do you find easiest to imagine? Why?  
   **Answer:** Any answer with a reason is good. For example: I like Einstein's idea, because I can imagine a ball rolling into a dip in a stretched sheet.. *Why:* Scientists still don't agree on the deepest answer. That's an open question you could work on one day.
13. (order) Put these steps for using a forcemeter in the right order: read at eye level, check the zero, write the number with its unit, hook on and pull steadily.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* First check the zero, so the reading starts fair. Then pull, read and write it down.
14. (truefalse) The size-weight illusion means a big box always feels heavier than a small box of the same weight.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It is the other way round. The small box feels heavier, because we expect big things to be heavy.
15. (draw) Design a forcemeter that could measure the pull of a single sheet of paper. Draw it and label how it works.  
   **Answer:** For example, a long plastic ruler taped to a table edge, sticking out, with the paper hanging from the far end and a straw pointer to show the bend.. *Why:* A good design is sensitive: a small pull makes a big, easy-to-see movement.
16. (mcq) Why do scientists say 'about 10 newtons' for a 1 kilogram bag, and not exactly 10?  
   Options: Because the exact number is about 9.8 newtons on Earth / Because nobody has ever measured it / Because bags are different colours / Because newtons are not real  
   **Answer:** Because the exact number is about 9.8 newtons on Earth. *Why:* On Earth, 1 kilogram pulls down with about 9.8 newtons. It even changes a tiny bit from place to place. 10 is close enough for most everyday sums.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these needs the biggest force?  
   Options: Picking up a leaf / Lifting a school bag / Pulling a cart full of bricks  
   **Answer:** Pulling a cart full of bricks. *Why:* The cart full of bricks is heaviest, so it needs the biggest pull. Picking the leaf usually means the order was read backwards.
2. (mcq) Why can't we trust our hands to measure a force exactly?  
   Options: Hands are too small / Hands can be fooled, for example by tiredness or by the size of a box / Hands only feel pushes, never pulls  
   **Answer:** Hands can be fooled, for example by tiredness or by the size of a box. *Why:* Tiredness and the size-weight illusion fool our hands. Picking 'hands only feel pushes' is not true. You can feel a bag pulling your arm.
3. (mcq) What is the unit of force?  
   Options: The kilogram / The newton / The forcemeter  
   **Answer:** The newton. *Why:* The newton is the unit of force. The kilogram is a unit of mass. The forcemeter is the tool, not the unit.
4. (mcq) About how hard does a small apple pull down on your hand?  
   Options: 1 newton / 100 newtons / 1,000 newtons  
   **Answer:** 1 newton. *Why:* A small apple of about 100 grams pulls with about 1 newton. Picking 100 usually means grams and newtons were mixed up.
5. (mcq) What does this forcemeter read?  
   Options: 6 newtons / 6 and a half newtons / 7 newtons  
   **Answer:** 6 and a half newtons. *Why:* The pointer is on the small mark between 6 and 7. Each small mark is half a newton. So the reading is 6 and a half newtons.
6. (mcq) With nothing on the hook, a forcemeter's pointer is not on zero. What should you do?  
   Options: Use it anyway / Turn the zero screw until the pointer is on zero / Pull harder  
   **Answer:** Turn the zero screw until the pointer is on zero. *Why:* Set the pointer to zero first, or every reading will be wrong by the same amount.
7. (mcq) Why do we read a forcemeter with our eyes level with the pointer?  
   Options: Looking from above or below can make the reading look wrong / It is more polite / The numbers are only printed on one side  
   **Answer:** Looking from above or below can make the reading look wrong. *Why:* Looking at a slant makes the pointer seem to sit on a different mark. Eye level gives the true reading.
8. (mcq) What is inside a forcemeter that stretches when you pull?  
   Options: A magnet / A spring / A balloon  
   **Answer:** A spring. *Why:* A spring. The harder the pull, the further it stretches, and the further the pointer moves.
9. (mcq) If you pull a forcemeter twice as hard, what happens to the spring?  
   Options: It stretches about twice as far / It stretches the same / It gets shorter  
   **Answer:** It stretches about twice as far. *Why:* Twice the pull gives about twice the stretch, as long as you don't pull past the forcemeter's range. Robert Hooke published this in 1678.
10. (mcq) You measured six pulls at home and drew a bar chart. What does the tallest bar show?  
   Options: The pull that needed the biggest force / The thing that was tallest / The pull you measured first  
   **Answer:** The pull that needed the biggest force. *Why:* Each bar is as tall as the force needed. The tallest bar is the biggest pull. Picking 'the tallest thing' usually means the bar is seen as a picture of the object.
11. (mcq) A 1 kilogram bag of rice pulls a forcemeter with about 10 newtons on Earth. About what would it show on the Moon?  
   Options: About 10 newtons / About 1.6 newtons / About 60 newtons  
   **Answer:** About 1.6 newtons. *Why:* The Moon pulls about one sixth as hard. Picking 10 usually means weight is thought never to change. Picking 60 means multiplying by 6 instead of dividing.
12. (mcq) You stand on a bathroom scale and press down on a table beside you. The number goes down. Why?  
   Options: You lost some mass / The table holds up part of you, so you push less on the scale / The scale is broken  
   **Answer:** The table holds up part of you, so you push less on the scale. *Why:* The scale measures your push, not how much of you there is. The table now shares the job of holding you up.
13. (mcq) Which tool can measure the pull of a single molecule inside your body?  
   Options: A luggage scale / Optical tweezers, made of laser light / A rubber band  
   **Answer:** Optical tweezers, made of laser light. *Why:* Optical tweezers can feel pulls of a few piconewtons, millions of millions of times smaller than an apple's pull.
14. (mcq) Do scientists all agree on what gravity really is?  
   Options: Yes, it was settled long ago / No. Newton, Einstein and particle physicists give different pictures, and it is still being studied / Nobody has ever studied it  
   **Answer:** No. Newton, Einstein and particle physicists give different pictures, and it is still being studied. *Why:* We can measure gravity's pull very well. What it is, deep down, is still an open question.
15. (mcq) Two forcemeters are hooked together. You pull one with 5 newtons. What does the other one show?  
   Options: 0 newtons / 5 newtons / 10 newtons  
   **Answer:** 5 newtons. *Why:* Both show 5 newtons. Every pull comes with an equal pull back. Picking 10 usually means the two pulls were added together.

## Applications
- Shops use weighing scales and spring balances to sell rice, dal and vegetables fairly.
- Airports use luggage scales to check that bags are not too heavy for the plane.
- Doctors measure how hard you can squeeze with a grip meter, to check how strong your hands are after an illness.
- Engineers test ropes, cables and climbing gear by pulling them with huge force meters until they break.
- Car makers crash cars into walls fitted with force sensors, to make seatbelts and airbags safer.
- Cranes have load sensors that stop them lifting more than is safe.
- Bridge builders measure the pull on every cable of a bridge, like the Bandra-Worli Sea Link in Mumbai.
- Rocket scientists at Sriharikota measure the push of rocket engines while they are tied down on the ground.
- Digital kitchen scales and phone touch screens use tiny sensors that feel pushes.
- Sports scientists measure how hard a footballer kicks or a cricketer hits, to improve training.
- Atomic force microscopes feel the push of single atoms, to make computer chips and new materials.
- Optical tweezers measure the pull of single molecules, helping doctors understand how cells and muscles work.
- Legal Metrology officers check shop scales with standard weights, so nobody is cheated.
- Scientists measure the tiny changes in gravity's pull to find water, oil and minerals underground.

## Open questions
- **What is a force, really, deep down?** Newton could describe gravity but not explain it. Einstein said gravity is bent space and time. Particle physicists think forces are carried by particles. Nobody has joined these into one picture.
- **Does a graviton, a particle that carries gravity, exist?** Many physicists think it should. It would be so hard to catch that nobody knows how to build an experiment to find one.
- **Why do measurements of gravity's strength disagree?** Scientists have measured the strength of gravity, often called big G, many times since Cavendish. The best results still disagree more than they should. Nobody is sure why.
- **How small a force can we ever measure?** Quantum physics says measuring always nudges things a little. Clever new tools can get around some limits. Nobody knows how far this can go.
- **Exactly why does a small box feel heavier than a big box of the same weight?** The size-weight illusion was described in 1891. Scientists still argue about how much comes from what we expect, and how much from how we grip and lift.
- **How do millions of tiny molecular pulls work together to make a muscle strong and smooth?** Scientists can measure one molecule's pull. How so many of them work together so well is still being studied.
- **Is the strength of gravity the same everywhere in the universe, and at every time?** As far as we can tell, yes. But scientists keep testing it, because if it changed even a little, our ideas about the universe would have to change.

## Connected maths
- Look at the forcemeter in the picture. The big numbers are whole newtons. Each small mark between them is half a newton. What is the reading? **3 and a half newtons.**. The pointer is past the 3, but before the 4. It sits on the small mark halfway between them. Each small mark is half a newton. So the reading is 3 newtons and half a newton more. That is 3 and a half newtons. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- When you made your forcemeter, each 100 gram packet pulled with about 1 newton. A bag of dal has a mass of 700 grams. About how hard does it pull? **About 7 newtons.**. Every 100 grams pulls with about 1 newton. 700 grams is 7 lots of 100 grams. So it pulls with 7 lots of 1 newton, which is 7 newtons. Check: 7 times 100 is 700. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- In the pull detective experiment, these were the biggest readings: slipper 2 newtons, drawer 5 newtons, cupboard door 6 newtons, chair 15 newtons, fridge door 18 newtons, school bag 25 newtons. Draw a bar chart. How much bigger is the fridge door's pull than the drawer's? **The fridge door's pull is 13 newtons bigger. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each pull, as tall as its reading. Then compare the fridge door and the drawer. 18 take away 5 is 13. So the fridge door needs 13 newtons more. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In the story Ten Packets of Salt, Mohan Lal's balance was set wrongly. Ten kilograms of salt showed as only 5 kilograms. Basanti's basket showed 14 kilograms on the same wrong balance. How heavy was it really? **19 kilograms.**. The balance showed 5 kilograms too little every time. 10 packets showed 5, so 5 kilograms were missing. So add 5 back on to every reading. 14 add 5 is 19. That is what the balance showed once the zero was fixed. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In the Moon tiffin activity, your 600 gram tiffin pulled with about 6 newtons on Earth. The Moon pulls about one sixth as hard. About how hard would the tiffin pull on the Moon? **About 1 newton.**. One sixth means sharing into 6 equal parts and taking one part. 6 newtons shared into 6 parts is 1 newton each. So on the Moon the tiffin pulls with about 1 newton, like a small apple on Earth. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
- In Guess the Newtons, Riya guessed that a coconut pulls with 8 newtons. Arjun guessed 12 newtons. The forcemeter showed 10 newtons. Whose guess was closer? **Both were equally close. Each was 2 newtons away.**. Riya's guess was 8. 10 take away 8 is 2. Arjun's guess was 12. 12 take away 10 is 2. Both guesses were 2 newtons away from the real reading, so it is a tie.
