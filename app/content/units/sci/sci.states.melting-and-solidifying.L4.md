# Melting and solidifying: why salted ice melts faster and gets colder
`sci.states.melting-and-solidifying.L4`

**Summary.** Sprinkle salt on ice and it melts faster, yet the bowl gets colder than plain ice. Why? In this lesson you melt and set ghee and coconut oil, run a fair test with salt, sugar and plain ice, and find out what the particles are doing. You learn why every substance has its own melting point, and why a melting solid takes in heat without getting warmer. You also meet three puzzles that are still argued about: water that stays liquid below zero, glass that has no melting point, and how melting really begins.

**Intro.** *On a cool December morning in Bengaluru the coconut oil in the bottle is white and hard. By lunch it is clear and pours. Nobody heated it and nobody put it in the fridge. And here is a stranger thing: sprinkle salt on ice and the ice melts faster, but the bowl gets colder. How can something melt and get colder at once?* A solid melts at its own exact temperature, called its melting point. Ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius, coconut oil at about 24, candle wax at about 60 and iron at 1,538. You will melt and set a spoon of ghee, test what salt does to ice, and build a picture of the particles at the surface of a melting solid. Along the way you will find that some simple-looking questions are still open. Does water always freeze at 0? Is glass a solid? What is hot, and for whom?

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what melting, solidifying and freezing are, and that a pure substance melts and freezes at the same temperature.
1. By the end I can explain with particles what happens when a solid melts and when a liquid solidifies.
2. By the end I can use melting points to explain why ghee and coconut oil are solid on cool mornings and liquid on warm afternoons.
3. By the end I can explain why a melting solid takes in heat without getting warmer.
4. By the end I can explain why salt melts ice and makes it colder, using what my own test showed.
5. By the end I can say why melting and solidifying are reversible changes, and what does not come back.
6. By the end I can describe what is not settled or not obvious: supercooled water, glass, and how melting begins.
7. By the end I can plan a fair test: guess with a reason, change one thing, measure, and say what the test cannot tell.

## Terms
- **Melting**: Melting is a solid turning into a liquid when it takes in heat. *e.g.* A spoon of ghee turns clear and runny on a warm dosa.
- **Solidifying**: Solidifying is a liquid turning into a solid when it gives out heat. It is also called setting. *e.g.* Melted candle wax turns hard again a minute after it drips.
- **Freezing**: Freezing is another word for solidifying. People use it most for water, but scientists use it for any liquid, even melted iron. *e.g.* Water freezes into ice in the freezer. Melted bronze freezes while it is still glowing red.
- **Melting point**: The melting point is the temperature at which a solid melts. Each pure substance has its own. *e.g.* Ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius. Gold melts at 1,064 degrees.
- **Freezing point**: The freezing point is the temperature at which a liquid can turn solid. For a pure substance it is the same number as the melting point. *e.g.* Water can freeze at 0 degrees, the same temperature at which ice melts.
- **Particle**: A particle is a very tiny piece that a substance is made of, far too small to see. *e.g.* One small ice cube holds far more water particles than there are grains of sand on Marina Beach.
- **Pure substance**: A pure substance is made of only one kind of particle. It melts at one exact temperature. *e.g.* Pure water and pure gold are pure substances.
- **Mixture**: A mixture is two or more substances together. A mixture often melts bit by bit over a range of temperatures. *e.g.* Ghee is a mixture of many fats, so it goes soft and grainy before it turns fully clear.
- **Reversible change**: A reversible change is a change that can be undone, so that you get the same substance back. *e.g.* Melted coconut oil sets again in the fridge and is still coconut oil.
- **Hidden heat**: Hidden heat is the heat a solid takes in while it melts without getting any warmer. Scientists call it latent heat. *e.g.* Ice in a tumbler of water stays at 0 degrees until the last piece has melted.
- **Supercooled**: A supercooled liquid is colder than its freezing point but is still liquid, because freezing has not yet started. *e.g.* A bottle of very clean water can stay liquid at minus 5 degrees in the freezer, and then freeze in seconds when you knock it.
- **Seed of ice**: A seed is the first tiny group of particles that line up in the pattern of the solid. Freezing spreads out from a seed. *e.g.* A speck of dust in water can help a seed of ice to form.
- **Brine**: Brine is water with a lot of salt dissolved in it. Brine freezes at a lower temperature than plain water. *e.g.* The salty meltwater in a kulfi seller's pot of ice and salt is brine.
- **Glass (a solid without a pattern)**: Glass is a solid whose particles are jumbled, not in rows. Scientists call this an amorphous solid. It has no exact melting point. *e.g.* A glass bangle softens slowly when a bangle maker heats it, and can be bent into a ring.
- **Casting**: Casting is making an object by pouring a melted substance into a hollow shape and letting it solidify. *e.g.* Bronze figures in Swamimalai are cast by pouring melted metal into a clay mould.

## Explanations
### Melting, and the melting point · *defined*
**Definition:** Melting is a solid turning into a liquid as it takes in heat. The temperature at which this happens is the melting point of that substance.

A solid does not melt just because it is 'hot'. It melts when it reaches its own melting point. Ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius, which feels very cold to us. Coconut oil melts at about 24 degrees, butter at about 33, candle wax at about 60, and iron at 1,538. The metal mercury melts at minus 39 degrees, so it is a liquid in every room on Earth. Chemists use the melting point like a fingerprint. Two white powders that look the same can be told apart by the temperature at which each one melts.
- e.g. A cube of ice melts in a tumbler of water from the tap, because tap water is warmer than 0 degrees.
- e.g. A steel ladle does not melt in boiling sambar, because steel melts at about 1,400 degrees.
- e.g. The metal gallium melts at about 30 degrees, so a piece of it turns liquid in a warm hand.
- **Everyday view:** Things melt when they get hot, and 'hot' means hot to our skin. This works in the kitchen, but it hides the fact that ice melting in the fridge door is the same event as iron melting in a furnace.
- **Chemists:** Every pure substance has its own melting point. Hot and cold are only words for our skin. For ice, a room at 25 degrees is far above its melting point. For iron, the same room is more than 1,500 degrees below.
- **The Vaisheshika thinkers of ancient India (Vaisheshika Sutra 2.1.6 and 2.1.7):** They listed ghee, lac and beeswax, and then tin, lead, iron, silver and gold, as solids that become fluid when they meet fire. They said the flowing of water is its own nature, but the flowing of ghee or gold is caused by fire. Today we would say water is only a liquid because the Earth is warm enough.
### Solidifying, and the freezing point · *defined*
**Definition:** Solidifying is a liquid turning into a solid as it gives out heat. The freezing point of a pure substance is the same temperature as its melting point.

Cool a liquid to its freezing point and it can turn solid. For water that is 0 degrees. For melted candle wax it is about 60 degrees, so wax 'freezes' on your table while it is still too hot to touch. As a liquid solidifies it gives out heat to the things around it. This is the same amount of heat it took in when it melted. At exactly the melting point, the solid and the liquid can sit together without either one winning. A tumbler of ice and water, stirred well, stays at 0 degrees.
- e.g. Hot ghee poured into a steel jar sets from the cold walls of the jar inwards.
- e.g. Melted bronze in a mould turns solid at about 950 degrees, while it still glows red.
- e.g. Fruit growers in cold countries spray water on their trees before a frosty night. As the water freezes it gives out heat, which keeps the buds at 0 degrees and no colder.
- **Most school books:** The freezing point and the melting point are the same number, so water always freezes at 0 degrees. This is true for the balance point, where ice and water can sit together.
- **Physicists who study freezing:** Ice always melts at 0 degrees, but water does not always freeze at 0. Clean, still water can be cooled several degrees below 0 and stay liquid. Freezing has to get started, and melting does not. So the melting point is the dependable number.
### What the particles do when a solid melts · *idea*
**Definition:** In a solid the particles are held in place and shake on the spot. Heat makes them shake harder. At the melting point they shake hard enough to break out of their places and slide past each other.

Here is what we know so far. Pulls between the particles hold a solid together. Warming a solid makes its particles shake harder, and at the melting point the neat order breaks. The particles stay close together and still pull on each other, but now they can move past one another, so the substance flows. Cooling does the reverse: the particles slow down and the pulls lock them into place again. A substance with strong pulls between its particles, like iron, needs a high temperature to melt. A substance with weak pulls, like ice or wax, melts at a low one. The particles themselves do not melt. One water particle is the same in ice and in water. What is missing from this picture is the very first moment. Scientists do not fully agree on how and where melting starts.
- e.g. An ice cube melts from the outside in. The particles at the surface have fewer neighbours holding them, so they break away first.
- e.g. Iron needs 1,538 degrees to melt and wax only about 60, because the pulls between iron particles are far stronger.
- e.g. When water freezes, its particles lock into rings of six. That is why snowflakes have six arms.
- **Frederick Lindemann, a physicist, in 1910:** A solid melts when its particles shake so widely that they start to bump into their neighbours. That happens when the shaking is about one tenth of the distance between particles. This simple rule gives roughly the right melting point for many solids.
- **Physicists who watch surfaces, from the 1980s on:** Melting does not start everywhere at once. A thin loose layer forms on the surface a little below the melting point, and at cracks and flaws inside. Melting spreads from there. A perfect crystal with no surface would be very hard to melt on time.
- **The particle picture itself, looked at closely:** Drawings show particles as little balls. Real particles have no sharp edge, and they never stop moving, even in the coldest solid. The picture is useful and a little wrong.
### A melting solid takes in heat but does not get warmer · *defined*
**Definition:** While a pure solid melts, its temperature stays at the melting point. The heat going in is used to pull the particles out of their places, not to make them move faster. This heat is called hidden heat, or latent heat.

Put ice and water in a tumbler in a warm room and stir. Heat flows in from the room the whole time, but a thermometer in the tumbler stays at 0 degrees until the last piece of ice has gone. Only then does the water begin to warm. Melting takes a lot of heat. The heat needed to melt one ice cube would warm the same amount of water from 0 degrees all the way to 80 degrees. When the water freezes again, it gives the same heat back out. This is why ice is so good at keeping things cold. It takes in a great deal of heat and stays at 0 the whole time.
- e.g. A fish seller at Kasimedu harbour in Chennai packs fish in crushed ice. The ice takes in the heat of the day and stays at 0 degrees as long as any ice is left.
- e.g. An ice cube on your palm feels as if it bites, because it takes the heat for melting from your skin.
- e.g. Vaccines travel across India in boxes of ice packs. The melting ice holds the box at a steady cold temperature for many hours.
- **Joseph Black, in the 1760s:** He measured this heat first and named it latent, which means hidden. He thought of heat as an invisible fluid that soaked into the ice and hid there.
- **Physicists today:** Heat is not a fluid. It is energy moving from a warmer thing to a cooler one. While ice melts, the energy goes into pulling the particles apart from their places against the pulls between them. It is stored in how the particles are arranged, and it comes back out on freezing.
### Why ghee and coconut oil are solid on cool mornings · *defined*
**Definition:** A substance is solid when it is colder than its melting point and liquid when it is warmer. Coconut oil melts at about 24 degrees and ghee between about 28 and 40, and the air in a kitchen moves across these numbers.

Most things in a kitchen have melting points far from the weather. Steel melts at about 1,400 degrees and water at 0, so a katori is always solid and water is always liquid. Coconut oil and ghee are different. Their melting points sit right inside the temperatures of an Indian day. A December night in Bengaluru or Delhi falls below 24 degrees, so coconut oil solidifies in the bottle. By afternoon the kitchen is warmer than 24 and the oil has melted. Ghee is a mixture of many fats, and each fat has its own melting point. So ghee does not melt all at once. On a mild day some of its fats are solid grains and the others are liquid oil around them. That is the grainy ghee people prize. Sesame, groundnut and mustard oils have melting points near or below 0 degrees, so they stay liquid on the shelf all year.
- e.g. In Chennai in May the kitchen is at 32 degrees, so coconut oil never sets on the shelf.
- e.g. In Ooty or Shillong in January, coconut oil stays white and hard all day.
- e.g. A spoon of ghee from the fridge melts on your tongue, because the inside of your mouth is at about 37 degrees.
- **Cooks and ghee makers:** Good ghee has a grain. Cooks in many homes cool fresh ghee slowly and leave it still, because slow cooling gives large soft grains and quick cooling gives a smooth paste. They know this by practice, handed down in kitchens.
- **Food chemists:** Ghee is a mixture of many fats. The fats with high melting points solidify first as crystals, and the others stay liquid around them. Slow cooling gives the crystals time to grow large. The cooks' rule and the chemists' reason describe the same thing.
- **Plants and other living beings:** A coconut palm lives where it is warm, and makes a fat that is solid at 24 degrees. Mustard and flax grow through cold winters, and make oils that stay liquid near 0. Researchers have found this pattern in many seed oils, and think this helps seeds to use their stored oil in the weather they sprout in.
### Why salt melts ice, and makes it colder · *defined*
**Definition:** Salt dissolved in water lowers the temperature at which the water can freeze. Ice touching salty water melts even below 0 degrees, and the melting takes heat from the ice and everything around it.

Here is a simplified picture, and it is the one chemists use to explain this. At the surface of wet ice two things happen all the time. Water particles break away from the ice into the liquid, and water particles from the liquid join the ice. At 0 degrees the two happen equally fast, so the ice neither grows nor shrinks. Now add salt. It dissolves in the thin layer of water on the ice. Some of the particles that touch the ice are now salt, so fewer water particles join the ice each second. Just as many break away as before. More leave than join, so the ice melts. Breaking away takes heat. No one is heating the bowl, so the heat comes from the ice, the salty water and the bowl. They all get colder. The cooling goes on until leaving and joining are equal again, at a new, lower temperature. With plenty of salt this can be as low as minus 21 degrees. A colder bowl also draws heat from the warm room faster, which melts the ice faster still. Sugar does the same, but a spoon of sugar has about ten times fewer particles than a spoon of salt, because each sugar particle is much bigger. So sugar does less.
- e.g. A kulfi seller packs the metal cones in a pot of ice and salt. Plain ice at 0 degrees would only chill the sweet milk. Ice and salt at minus 15 freezes it.
- e.g. The sea around Antarctica is salty, so it stays liquid down to about minus 2 degrees.
- e.g. In cold countries salt is spread on icy roads. It stops working when the road is colder than about minus 21 degrees.
- **Everyday view:** Salt must be heating the ice, because the ice melts. Touching the bowl shows this cannot be right. The salted bowl is the coldest one.
- **Chemists:** Salt gives no heat. It tips the balance between particles leaving the ice and particles joining it. Any substance that dissolves in the water does this, and what matters most is how many particles it adds.
- **Kulfi and ice-cream makers:** People froze sweet milk with ice and salt long before anyone drew a particle. In a home with no freezer, this is still the way to make kulfi. The practice came first and the explanation came centuries later.
### Supercooling: liquid water below zero · *defined*
**Definition:** A supercooled liquid has been cooled below its freezing point without turning solid. It stays liquid until a first seed of the solid forms. Then it freezes very fast.

To freeze, some water particles must first line up in the pattern of ice. This first tiny group is called a seed. Just below 0 degrees a small seed is more likely to break up than to grow. A speck of dust, a scratch on the bottle or a hard knock helps a seed get big enough to grow. In very clean, still water there is nothing to help, so the water can stay liquid well below 0. Tiny drops of pure water in high clouds stay liquid down to about minus 40 degrees. When supercooled water does freeze, it gives out its hidden heat and warms back up to 0. Only part of it turns to ice, so you get slush. A solid is very hard to heat above its melting point, because its surface is always ready to melt. So ice melts on time, but water can freeze late.
- e.g. A sealed bottle of filtered water left still in a freezer for about two hours can be liquid at minus 5 degrees. A knock on the table turns it to slush in seconds.
- e.g. Many clouds over the Himalaya are made of supercooled drops. When they touch an aeroplane wing they freeze onto it at once.
- e.g. Rain that freezes the moment it lands on a cold road is supercooled rain.
- **Physicists:** Supercooling shows that freezing needs a start. It is a matter of chance when the first seed forms, so nobody can say the exact second a given bottle will freeze.
- **The bacteria Pseudomonas syringae, who live on leaves:** They build a protein on their outer skin that holds water particles in the pattern of ice. With this seed, water freezes at about minus 2 degrees. Leroy Maki and others reported this in 1974. Researchers think the frost opens the leaf, so that the bacteria can feed.
- **Fish of the Antarctic sea, and wood frogs of North America:** The fish make proteins that cling to tiny ice seeds and stop them from growing, as Arthur DeVries found in the 1960s. Wood frogs do the opposite. They let ice form between their cells in winter and thaw alive in spring, as Janet Storey and Kenneth Storey have studied for many years.
### Glass: a solid with no melting point · *debated*
**Definition:** Glass is a hard material whose particles are jumbled, as in a liquid, but stuck in place, as in a solid. It has no exact melting point. It softens little by little as it is heated.

Window glass is made by melting sand with soda and lime at about 1,500 degrees. As it cools it does not suddenly freeze at one temperature. It gets thicker and slower, like honey in a fridge, until its particles can no longer move past each other. They are stuck wherever they were, with no neat rows. Heat it again and it does not melt at a point. It softens slowly, which is why a bangle maker can bend it and a glass blower can blow it. Butter, chocolate and tar soften slowly too, but for a different reason: they are mixtures. Tar on a road in May shows the same puzzle. It looks solid, yet over weeks it flows. In an experiment begun in 1927 in Australia, a lump of pitch in a funnel has dripped only nine drops.
- e.g. A glass bangle maker in Firozabad softens a glass rod in a flame and winds it into rings.
- e.g. A road in Hyderabad in May shows tyre marks in the tar by evening.
- e.g. Sugar syrup cooled quickly sets into clear hard candy, which is a glass made of sugar. Cooled slowly, it forms sugar crystals.
- **A popular belief:** Glass is really a liquid that flows too slowly to see. The proof given is that very old window panes are thicker at the bottom.
- **Most materials scientists, such as Edgar Zanotto:** Glass is a solid. It keeps its shape and breaks like a solid. In 1998 Zanotto worked out that a window pane would need far longer than the age of the universe to flow by an amount you could see. Old panes are uneven because of the way they were made.
- **Physicists who study the glass change:** The honest answer depends on how long you watch. Over a day, pitch is a solid. Over ten years, it is a liquid. Nobody has yet shown whether glass is a true state of matter of its own, or a liquid that has simply become too slow to move.
### Melting and solidifying are reversible changes · *defined*
**Definition:** A reversible change is one that can be undone. Melting is undone by solidifying. The substance is the same before and after, and none of it is lost.

Melt a block of ice and freeze the water again, and you have the same water. No new substance was made, and the weight is the same. This is different from burning or cooking. A burnt matchstick or a cooked dosa cannot be turned back. Because melting is reversible, people can use the same metal, wax and glass again and again. Old steel, broken bangles and gold ornaments are melted and made into new things. Look closer, though, and one thing does not come back by itself: the shape and the inner pattern. A melted ice sculpture freezes into a block, not a sculpture. Melted and re-set chocolate turns dull and crumbly, because its fat crystals have formed in a different pattern.
- e.g. A goldsmith in Thrissur melts an old bangle and casts the same gold as a pair of earrings.
- e.g. Candle makers collect the drips and stubs of old candles and melt them into new candles.
- e.g. Weigh a closed bottle of ice, let it melt and weigh it again. The weight is the same.
- **The school science view:** Melting and solidifying are fully reversible. You get the same substance back, in the same amount. This is correct, and it is what makes recycling possible.
- **Sculptors, chocolate makers and metal workers:** The substance comes back but the form does not. How a liquid is cooled decides the grain, the shine and the strength of the solid. Fast-cooled and slow-cooled steel are the same substance and behave very differently.
### Melting is not the same as dissolving · *defined*
**Definition:** Melting needs one substance and heat: the solid becomes its own liquid. Dissolving needs two substances: a solid spreads out, particle by particle, into a different liquid.

Sugar stirred into cold water does not melt. It dissolves. The water is far colder than the temperature at which sugar turns liquid by heat. An ice cube in a tumbler of water melts, because it turns into its own liquid, water. To check which one you are seeing, ask two questions. How many substances are there? Did the solid need to reach its own melting point? Salt on ice is a special case in which both happen together. The salt dissolves in the wet layer on the ice, and that makes the ice melt.
- e.g. Jaggery in hot tea is dissolving. A lump of jaggery heated alone in a pan softens and turns liquid.
- e.g. Butter on a hot dosa is melting. Nothing else is needed but heat.
- e.g. Salt stirred into rasam is dissolving. Salt alone melts only at 801 degrees.
### Shrink or grow: what happens to the size · *defined*
**Definition:** Most liquids shrink a little when they solidify, because their particles pack closer together. Water does the opposite and grows by about one eleventh when it freezes.

Look at a jar of set ghee or a candle. The top has a dip in the middle. The liquid shrank as it solidified, and the last part to set sank inwards. Solid ghee is packed tighter than melted ghee, so a lump of solid ghee sinks in melted ghee. Water is different. Its particles lock into open rings of six, and these take up more room than the loose particles of liquid water. So ice takes up more room than the water it came from, and ice floats. A few other substances grow on freezing as water does, such as the metals gallium and bismuth.
- e.g. Ice cubes in a tray have a small bump on top, pushed up as the water grew.
- e.g. A full, closed bottle of water left in the freezer can bulge or split.
- e.g. A lake in Ladakh freezes from the top, because ice floats. The fish live in the liquid water underneath.
### Some solids skip the liquid · *defined*
**Definition:** Some solids change directly into a gas without becoming a liquid first. This change is called sublimation.

A camphor tablet left in an open box gets smaller each week and leaves no puddle. Its particles leave the solid one by one and go into the air, which is why you can smell it. Naphthalene balls in a cupboard do the same. Solid carbon dioxide, called dry ice, turns to gas at minus 78 degrees and never forms a liquid in the open air. But this is not the whole story. Squeeze carbon dioxide hard enough, as inside a fire extinguisher, and it is a liquid. So whether a solid melts or skips the liquid depends on the pressure around it, as well as on the temperature.
- e.g. Camphor tablets kept for puja slowly vanish from an open tin.
- e.g. Dry ice packed around ice cream for a long journey gives off white mist and leaves the box dry.
- e.g. Frozen clothes on a line in Kargil dry out, because the ice in them slowly turns straight to vapour.
- **Everyday view:** Camphor and dry ice are solids that do not melt. This is what we see in the open air.
- **Physicists:** Every one of these substances has a liquid form. We do not see it, because the air on Earth does not press hard enough. Deep inside the Earth the pressure is so great that the iron core is solid, though it is about as hot as the surface of the Sun. Inge Lehmann found this solid inner core in 1936 from earthquake waves.

## Mostly agreed
- Thermometers all over the world are set using melting and freezing points. *How we know:* The world's temperature scale, agreed in 1990, is fixed by a list of exact points. Among them are the melting point of gallium (29.7646 degrees) and the freezing points of tin, zinc, silver and gold. A laboratory in Delhi and one in Paris get the same numbers.
- Nothing is lost or gained in weight when a substance melts or solidifies. *How we know:* A sealed bottle of ice weighs the same after the ice has melted. Careful weighings have shown this since the 1700s, and you can check it on a kitchen scale.
- Pressing on a solid changes its melting point. *How we know:* Earthquake waves pass through the centre of the Earth in a way that only a solid allows. Inge Lehmann showed this in 1936. The iron there is about as hot as the surface of the Sun, but the huge pressure keeps it solid.
- Something dissolved in a liquid lowers its freezing point, and the number of dissolved particles matters most. *How we know:* Measured since the 1880s by François-Marie Raoult and many others. Sea water freezes at about minus 2 degrees. Water with as much salt as it can hold freezes at about minus 21.
- Very small pieces of a solid melt at a lower temperature than a big piece. *How we know:* In 1976 Philippe Buffat and Jean-Pierre Borel measured specks of gold a few millionths of a millimetre across. They melted hundreds of degrees below 1,064. In a tiny speck most particles are at the surface, where they are held less firmly.

## How we found out
**Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit, a maker of thermometers and other instruments, 1721 to 1724.** Three hundred years ago, people who studied nature were sure of one thing about water. Cool it to its freezing point and it turns to ice. This fitted everything they saw in ponds and buckets. Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit made thermometers in Amsterdam. On a bitterly cold night in 1721 he left a sealed glass ball of clean water outside. In the morning his thermometer showed that the air was far below the freezing point. But the water in the ball was still liquid. When he disturbed it, thin plates of ice shot through the water in a moment. He tried again and again, and reported it to the Royal Society in London in 1724. Water could be colder than its freezing point and still be liquid. Freezing needed something to start it. He also saw that a mixture of ice and water always came back to the same temperature. So the dependable point was the one where ice melts. That is why later thermometer makers, such as Anders Celsius in 1742, used melting ice to mark their scales.  
*What people believed before:* People thought water always turns to ice the moment it is cooled to its freezing point. Fahrenheit found clean, still water liquid at a temperature far below that. Freezing has to be started by a seed or a knock, but melting ice always holds the same temperature.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Old window panes are thicker at the bottom because the glass has slowly flowed down over hundreds of years.** (A popular belief, repeated in some books and by many tour guides; *rejected*)  
  For: Many panes in buildings 500 or more years old really are thicker along one edge, and glass does have jumbled particles like a liquid.  
  Against: Edgar Zanotto worked out in 1998 that such a flow would take far longer than the age of the universe. Old panes were made by spinning or blowing and came out uneven, and some are fitted with the thick edge at the top. Roman glass 2,000 years old and old telescope lenses have kept their shape.
- **Glass is not a true solid state. It is a liquid that has become too slow to move, and nothing sudden happens when it 'sets'.** (One group of physicists who study glass. Another group holds the opposite.; *debated*)  
  For: No sharp change is seen when a liquid turns to glass. The slowing is smooth, and a glass cooled more slowly sets at a slightly lower temperature, which a true freezing point would not do.  
  Against: Other physicists think a real hidden change lies underneath, and that we never reach it because the liquid becomes too slow first. Some computer models and experiments on very old amber and very stable glasses point this way.
- **The melting point of a substance is not one fixed number. It changes with pressure, with the size of the piece and with tiny amounts of other substances.** (Physicists and chemists who measure melting very carefully; *accepted*)  
  For: Specks of gold a few particles wide melt hundreds of degrees lower than a gold bangle. The Earth's iron core is solid at more than 5,000 degrees because of pressure. A pinch of salt lowers the melting point of ice.  
  Against: For a pure substance, in pieces big enough to see, at the air pressure we live in, the melting point is so steady that the world's thermometers are set by it. The fixed number is true, as long as you say the conditions.
- **Table sugar has no true melting point. When heated it starts to break down into new substances before it melts, and that is what caramel is.** (Shelly Schmidt and her group at the University of Illinois, in 2011; *debated*)  
  For: They found that the temperature at which sugar turns liquid depends on how fast it is heated. Heated very slowly, it turned liquid at a much lower temperature. A true melting point does not change with the speed of heating.  
  Against: Yrjö Roos and other food scientists answered that sugar does melt, at about 186 degrees, and that it also breaks down at the same time. They say the two changes overlap and can be told apart.
- **Deeply supercooled water can exist as two different liquids, one more tightly packed than the other.** (Peter Poole, Francesco Sciortino, Ulrich Essmann and Eugene Stanley proposed it in 1992; *debated*)  
  For: Computer models of water show two liquids. In 2020 a team led by Kyung Hwan Kim and Anders Nilsson used very fast X-ray flashes and reported seeing water switch between them, near minus 70 degrees.  
  Against: Water this cold freezes in less than a thousandth of a second, so the measurements are very hard to make and to repeat. Some scientists think the signs can be explained by tiny ice crystals forming.

## Roots
In the salt test, the salted ice melted fastest and its bowl became the coldest, though nobody heated it or cooled it. This part looks at what that shows about an ice cube that seems to be sitting still, and about you.

**Deep truth.** In the salt test, the salted ice melted fastest and also became the coldest. Salt gave the ice no heat. At the surface of ice, particles leave and join all the time, and salt tipped that balance. So an ice cube that looks still is a balance between leaving and joining. A solid stays solid only while everything around it keeps that balance with it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think 'solid' is something a thing has all by itself, the way it has a name. We also think a thing that looks still is doing nothing. The salt test questions both. The ice changed because of what touched it, and it was never still.  
*Evidence:* In the salt test, the bowl with salt gave the most meltwater and felt the coldest. In Ghee There and Back, the same spoon of ghee was liquid over warm water and solid over cold water within a few minutes. If your results were different, use what you saw.

- *who am i:* Your body is at about 37 degrees. At that temperature the fats in the thin skins of your cells are just soft enough to bend and let things through, and your bones are far below their melting point. You are a balance of solids and liquids, held steady every second. You do not hold it alone. The air, your food and the Sun's warmth keep it with you.
- *what is life:* Living beings do not just put up with melting points. They sense the cold and change themselves. When the water cools, fish and bacteria change the fats in their cell skins so that these stay soft. Wood frogs let ice form between their cells in winter and thaw alive in spring. Bacteria on leaves build seeds for ice.
- *what is this world:* The Earth sits at a temperature where water is ice on the Himalaya, liquid in the Kaveri and vapour in the monsoon cloud, all at once. A little warmer or a little cooler, and one of the three would be missing. The Ganga and the Indus are fed by melting ice and snow. What we call the world's solid things and flowing things are one set of particles, sorted by warmth.

The lesson began with coconut oil that is solid in the morning and liquid by lunch. Then salt, which is not warm, melted ice. Then water stayed liquid below zero until something gave it a start. In each case the substance alone did not decide what it was. The warmth of the room, what was dissolved in it, a speck of dust and how long we watched all took part. Solid and liquid are things a substance does together with its surroundings.

- **Vaisheshika Sutra 2.1.6 and 2.1.7, linked to the teacher Kanada:** The Vaisheshika thinkers taught that ghee, lac and beeswax, and also tin, lead, iron, silver and gold, become fluid through contact with fire. They taught that this flowing is caused from outside and is not the solid's own nature. Scholars do not agree on the date of the text. (In Ghee There and Back, the ghee flowed only while the warm water was under it. Take the warmth away and it set. Its flowing depended on what it touched.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.5:** The teacher Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu that by knowing one lump of gold, everything made of gold is known. The bangle and the ring are names given in speech. What is real in them, he says, is the gold. (A goldsmith melts a bangle and casts a ring, and the gold is the same gold. Melting and solidifying show what stays when the form changes. Uddalaka uses this to point at one reality under all forms.)
- **The Buddha, in the Udana 1.1:** The Buddha taught: when this is, that is. From the arising of this, that arises. Nothing stands by itself. Each thing is there because of the conditions around it. (The ice in your bowl was solid because the freezer had been cold. It melted because the room was warm, and it melted faster because salt was there. Change the conditions and the ice changes.)
- Science (settled): At the melting point, particles leave the solid and join it at equal rates. A solid that sits unchanged in its own liquid is a two-way exchange in balance.
- Science (settled): Dissolved particles lower the freezing point of a liquid, and melting takes in heat from the surroundings.
- Science (open): How melting first begins inside a solid, particle by particle, is not fully worked out.
- Science (debated): Whether glass is a true state of matter or a liquid that has become too slow to move is argued among physicists.
- Science (debated): Consciousness is the ground from which particles and their balances arise. Science has no test for this yet, and many scientists hold that particles come first and minds arise later, only in brains.
- Other view, Most chemists, and the Vaisheshika thinkers themselves: A melting point belongs to the substance. Pure water has the same one in every laboratory on Earth, which is why thermometers are set by it. On this view the world is made of many separate things, each with its own fixed properties. The 'balance' is only a count of separate particles bumping into each other. Nothing in the salt test needs the idea that things are one.
- Practice, Ice on the palm (3 min): Hold one ice cube on your open palm over a bowl. Close your eyes. Notice the cold spreading into your hand. The ice is taking heat from you to melt. Take three slow breaths. Now ask yourself where your hand got that heat from. Put the ice in the bowl and dry your hand.
- Ask: The ice cube was solid only while its surroundings were cold enough. Is there anything about you that is true only because of what is around you?
- Ask: A thing that looks still may be a balance of leaving and joining. What else around you might be a balance that only looks still?
- Ask: The gold stays when the bangle is melted. When you change, what stays?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* An ice cube in ice-cold water looks as if nothing is happening. If particles are leaving and joining it all the time, is it the same ice cube after an hour? (hint: Think of a queue at a ration shop that stays the same length while people join and leave. Is it the same queue?)
- *deep:* One water particle alone cannot be ice or water. How many particles do you think it takes before the words 'solid' and 'liquid' start to mean something? (hint: Can one person be a crowd? Can two? Where would you draw the line, and does a sharp line exist?)
- *deep:* If pitch is a solid when you watch it for a day and a liquid when you watch it for ten years, which one is it really? (hint: Think about a mountain. Does it look the same to you as it would to someone who could watch for a million years?)
- *deep:* For melted bronze, 900 degrees is cold enough to freeze. For ice, your hand is hot enough to melt. So what do 'hot' and 'cold' mean, and for whom? (hint: Whose skin, or whose particles, are we asking? Try saying 'hot' from the point of view of an ice cube.)
- *big:* The Earth is at a temperature where water can be ice, liquid and vapour all at once. What would life be like on a world where water was only ever ice? (hint: Think about what a liquid lets living beings do that a solid does not. Then ask whether another liquid could do the job.)
- *big:* A goldsmith melts your great-grandmother's bangle and makes a ring. Is the ring her bangle? (hint: What was the bangle: the gold, the shape, or the memory? Which of these went through the melting?)
- *big:* Your body keeps some of its fats just melted and its bones solid, every second, without you deciding. Who or what is keeping that balance? (hint: Notice your warm skin on a cold morning. Where does the warmth come from, and what would happen to you without it?)

## Story: The Lamp That Became a Dancer
**

Thenmozhi was ten, and she had grown up with the sound of hammers. Her aunt, Valli Chithi, made bronze figures in a workshop in Swamimalai, near the Kaveri river in Tamil Nadu.

In the May holidays her cousin Kabir came from Delhi. He was eleven and had never seen metal poured. 'Come before the sun is up tomorrow,' Thenmozhi told him. 'Chithi pours at dawn, when the air is cool. I will show you everything.'

Next morning she led him to a table at the back of the workshop. On it stood a dancer as tall as Thenmozhi's forearm, the colour of honey. Kabir touched her with one finger. She was smooth and a little soft.

'Wax,' said Thenmozhi. 'Beeswax mixed with tree resin and a little oil. Chithi shaped her with warm fingers. When the wax is warm it bends. When it cools it holds.'

'But a wax dancer will melt in the sun,' said Kabir.

'That is the plan,' said Thenmozhi. 'She has to melt.'

She showed him the next table. Here a second figure was hidden inside a thick coat of clay. 'Fine clay from the bed of the Kaveri goes on first,' she said. 'It is pressed into every line of the wax. Then comes thicker clay. We leave a hole at the bottom. Then it dries for days.'

Out in the yard, Valli Chithi was setting a dry clay mould beside a fire pit, with the hole facing down. 'Both of you stay behind the rope,' she said. They stayed behind the rope.

Soon a thin golden stream ran out of the hole into a pan. Kabir stared. 'The dancer is running away!'

'The wax is,' said Thenmozhi. 'Wax melts at about sixty degrees. That is not much hotter than a tumbler of tea. Clay does not care about sixty degrees. So now there is a dancer-shaped hole inside the clay. Nothing but air, in the shape of her.'

Kabir thought about a hole that had fingers and a nose. It made his head feel strange.

Across the yard a clay pot sat deep in glowing charcoal. A worker named Selvi was feeding it pieces of metal. In went a cracked lamp, a handful of old bells and some bars of copper.

'That is my grandmother's broken lamp,' said Thenmozhi. 'Chithi asked her first.'

'How hot is it in there?' asked Kabir.

'More than a thousand degrees. Copper will not melt below that.'

Kabir whistled. In Delhi in January, he said, the coconut oil in their kitchen turned white and hard in the bottle. His father stood the bottle in a mug of warm water every morning. Twenty-four degrees was enough to melt that.

'And ice needs only zero,' said Thenmozhi. 'Everything has its own number.'

Valli Chithi and Selvi lifted the pot with long tongs. The metal inside was bright orange and moved like thick buttermilk. They tipped it into the hole of the mould. It went in with a soft glugging sound. Then there was nothing to see. The mould just sat there.

'Now we wait for it to freeze,' said Thenmozhi.

Kabir laughed. 'Freeze? It is hotter than anything I have ever seen.'

'Chithi calls it freezing, and she is right,' said Thenmozhi. 'Listen. This bronze turns solid a little below a thousand degrees. So when it has cooled to nine hundred, it is a solid. It is still glowing red. It could still set wood on fire. But it is frozen.'

Kabir was quiet for a while. 'So for bronze, nine hundred degrees is cold.'

'Cold enough to freeze,' said Thenmozhi. 'And for ice, your hand is terribly hot. And wax is somewhere in between.'

'Then what is hot?'

'I asked Chithi that when I was seven,' said Thenmozhi. 'She said, hot for whom?'

They came back the next morning, when the mould was cool enough to hold. Valli Chithi gave Thenmozhi the small hammer. 'You open it.'

Thenmozhi tapped. The clay cracked and fell away in pieces. Inside, dark and rough and dusted with ash, stood the dancer. Every finger was there. The line of her eyebrow was there.

Kabir looked at her for a long time. 'Yesterday she was wax,' he said slowly. 'Then she was a hole. Now she is bronze. And the bronze was a lamp and some bells.'

'And the wax is in the pan,' said Thenmozhi. 'It set again overnight. Next week Chithi will shape that same wax into something new.'

'So where is the dancer?' asked Kabir. 'Is she the bronze? The bronze was here before her. Is she the shape? The shape was in wax first, and then in nothing at all.'

Thenmozhi began to answer, and stopped. She had opened forty moulds and had never once asked that. She looked at the pan of pale wax. She looked at her grandmother's lamp, which now had fingers and an eyebrow.

'Ask me again tomorrow,' she said. 'I need to think.'

**For the parent:** Kabir saw that bronze 'freezes' while it is still red-hot, so hot and cold are not fixed things. They depend on whose melting point you ask about. He also saw the same shape pass from wax to an empty space to bronze, while the bronze itself had been a lamp the day before.

- Ask: Thenmozhi said bronze at nine hundred degrees is frozen. Do you agree that this is freezing? Why?
- Ask: Where do you think the dancer is: in the bronze, in the shape, or somewhere else?
- Ask: The wax will be melted and shaped again, and so might the bronze one day. What stays the same through all the melting?

## Games
### g1 · The Melting Point Ladder (20 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the 24 cards and make a pile with the fronts up. Nobody looks at the backs.; Take the top card, read it aloud and turn it over. Put it in the middle of the table with the number side up. This is the start of the ladder.; Take turns. On your turn, read the front of the top card aloud. Say where it goes in the ladder: to the left of all the cards (colder), to the right of all (hotter), or between two cards. Give a reason.; Turn the card over. If your place was right, you score 1 point. Right or wrong, put the card in its correct place, number side up.; Two cards, ghee and window glass, have a stretch of temperatures. Their backs say which places count as right.; Keep the score with tally marks on paper. The ladder gets longer, so each turn is harder than the last.; The game ends when the pile is empty. The player with the most points wins. A tie is a shared win.; Playing alone, try to score at least 16 points out of 23.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: a substance on the front, and its melting point in degrees Celsius on the back:** Helium, used in party balloons (back: About minus 272, and only when squeezed hard. In the open it stays liquid down to the coldest temperature there is.); Oxygen, the part of air we breathe in (back: Minus 219); Nitrogen, most of the air (back: Minus 210); The alcohol in hand sanitiser (back: Minus 114); Mercury, the silver liquid in old thermometers (back: Minus 39); Sea water, as ice (back: About minus 2. The salt lowers it.); Ice, made from plain water (back: 0); Coconut oil (back: About 24); Gallium, a metal (back: About 30. It melts in a warm hand.); Ghee (back: From about 28 to about 40. Ghee is a mixture, so it melts bit by bit. Any place between coconut oil and candle wax is right.); Candle wax (back: About 60); Sulphur, a yellow solid (back: 115); Tin, used to join wires (back: 232); Lead, the metal in car batteries (back: 327); Zinc, the coating on iron buckets (back: 420); Aluminium, as in foil (back: 660); Window glass (back: No exact melting point. It softens from about 600 to 1,000. Any place between zinc and gold is right. Say why it has no single place.); Table salt, heated alone (back: 801); Silver (back: 962); Gold (back: 1,064); Copper (back: 1,085); Iron (back: 1,538); Sand (quartz) (back: About 1,700); Tungsten, the wire in old light bulbs (back: 3,422, the highest of any metal)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Shuffle the cards. Make a pile with the fronts up. Turn the first card over to start the ladder. Read the rules together.  
  *Parent says:* Each card names a substance. Its melting point is on the back. We will build a row from the coldest melting point to the hottest.  
  *Child mode:* Shuffle the cards and make a pile, fronts up. Turn over the first card to start the ladder.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play in turns until the pile is empty. Ask for a reason before each card is turned over. Keep the score with tally marks.  
  *Parent says:* Before you turn it over, tell me your reason. Have you seen this substance melt? Is it liquid or solid in our kitchen?  
  *Child mode:* Read the top card. Choose its place and say why. Then turn it over and check.
  - Ask: How did you decide where it goes? (good: It is a liquid in our room, so it melts below room temperature.; It is part of the air, so it must melt far below 0.; It is used for cooking pans, so it melts very high.)
  - Note: A good clue is the state at room temperature. Something that is part of the air melts far below 0. A liquid melts below about 25. A solid melts above that.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the finished ladder together. Ask which card surprised your child most, and what the two stretch cards have in common.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the whole ladder. Where is the biggest jump? Which card surprised you? Why do ghee and glass not have one number?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the whole ladder. Which card surprised you most? Why do ghee and glass have no single number?
  - Think: Where on this ladder would you put yourself? Is there one answer?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Salt Test: which ice melts most, and which bowl gets coldest? (35 min)
- **question:** If you sprinkle salt on ice cubes, do they melt faster or slower than plain ice? Does sugar do the same? And which bowl ends up the coldest?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess two things. Which bowl will have the most meltwater after 15 minutes: plain, salt or sugar? Which bowl will feel the coldest on the outside? Give a reason for each guess.
- **fair test:** All three bowls are the same size and made of the same steel. Each gets four ice cubes from the same tray, taken out at the same moment. The bowls stand side by side, away from the window and the fan, for the same 15 minutes. Only one thing changes: what is sprinkled on the ice. Each bowl has four cubes, so one odd cube matters less. If you have a second tray of ice, do the whole test twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then write three lines: what I think, what I saw, and why I think it happened.
- **results table:** Bowl | What was sprinkled | How the outside felt at 5 and 10 minutes | Teaspoons of meltwater at 15 minutes
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three bowls, the salt and the sugar. Ask for both guesses and a reason for each. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* One bowl gets salt, one gets sugar and one gets nothing. Which will melt the most in 15 minutes? Which bowl will feel the coldest? Tell me why you think so.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the three bowls. Guess which ice will melt the most. Guess which bowl will be coldest. Say why.
  - Ask: Which bowl will have the most meltwater, and why? (good: Salt, because I have heard salt melts ice.; Plain, because salt and sugar are not warm.; All the same, because the room is the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children say the bowl that melts most must be the warmest. Let the test answer that.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the bowls Plain, Sugar and Salt. 2. Put four ice cubes in each. 3. Sprinkle one level teaspoon of sugar over the Sugar bowl. 4. Sprinkle one level teaspoon of salt over the Salt bowl. 5. Start the clock.  
  *Parent says:* Each bowl gets four cubes from the same tray. Level the teaspoon with your finger, so that the sugar and the salt are the same amount. Start the clock now.  
  *Child mode:* Put four ice cubes in each bowl. Sprinkle one level spoon of sugar on one. Sprinkle one level spoon of salt on another. Start the clock.
  - Note: Work quickly so that all the ice starts at the same time. Keep the bowls a hand apart and away from sunlight and the fan.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wait and watch. 2. At 5 minutes and at 10 minutes, touch the outside of each bowl with one fingertip for two seconds. 3. Write how each one feels. 4. Look for dew or frost on the outside of the bowls.  
  *Parent says:* While we wait, look closely. Touch the outside of each bowl for two seconds. Which is the coldest? Is anything forming on the outside of the bowls?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the ice. At 5 and 10 minutes, touch each bowl outside for two seconds. Write which one is coldest.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the Salt bowl? (good: The ice looks wet and has holes in it.; It is the coldest to touch.; There is frost or a lot of dew on the outside.)
  - Note: On a damp day the Salt bowl may grow white frost on the outside. That shows it is below 0 degrees. Touch for two seconds only, because the Salt bowl is very cold.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At 15 minutes, take the Plain bowl. 2. Hold the ice back with a spoon and pour the meltwater into the tumbler. 3. Spoon the water back into the sink, counting level teaspoons. 4. Write the number. 5. Do the same for Sugar and then Salt.  
  *Parent says:* Now we measure. Hold the ice back and pour off only the water. Count the teaspoons. Write each number in the table before you do the next bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the water from one bowl into the tumbler. Keep the ice back. Count the water in teaspoons and write it down.
  - Note: If the room is cool and there is less than one teaspoon in the Plain bowl, wait 10 minutes more for all three bowls. The salty water is not for drinking. Pour it down the sink.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to write three lines: what I think, what I saw, why. Then ask what the test cannot tell us.  
  *Parent says:* Write what you think salt does to ice. Write what you saw that shows it. Then say why you think it happened. Was anything a surprise?  
  *Child mode:* Write three lines. What do I think? What did I see? Why did it happen?
  - Ask: The salted ice melted most but its bowl was coldest. How can both be true? (good: Melting takes heat, and it took the heat from the bowl.; The salt did not warm the ice. Something else made it melt.)
  - Ask: What can this test not tell us? (good: It cannot show the particles.; It does not tell us if more salt would melt more.; It does not tell us what happens in a freezer.)
  - Note: Read the card 'Why salt melts ice, and makes it colder' only after your child has written the three lines.
  - Check: Why did the Salt bowl get colder? → **Melting takes in heat from the bowl and the ice**
  - Think: Would salt melt ice inside a freezer at minus 18 degrees? How could you find out?
### e2 · The Flat Line: does melting ice get warmer? (30 min)
- **question:** A tumbler of ice and water and a tumbler of cold water stand in the same warm room. Do they warm up in the same way? What happens to the temperature when salt goes into the icy one?
- **predict:** Guess what each thermometer reading will be after 15 minutes. Will the icy tumbler warm up, stay the same or get colder? Then guess what two spoons of salt will do to its temperature. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Both tumblers are the same size and material and hold the same amount in total. They stand side by side. You stir each one for ten seconds before every reading, and read at the same times. Only one thing is different: one has ice in it. The salt goes in only at the end, as a second test.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then mark your readings as dots on squared paper, minutes along the bottom and degrees up the side, and join the dots for each tumbler.
- **results table:** Minutes | Tumbler A: ice and water (degrees) | Tumbler B: cold water only (degrees)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the two tumblers and the thermometer. Ask for a guess for each tumbler after 15 minutes, with a reason. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* Both tumblers will sit in this warm room. Heat will flow into both. What will each thermometer reading be after 15 minutes? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the temperature of each tumbler after 15 minutes. Say why you think so.
  - Note: Most children expect both to warm up, the icy one a little less. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put six ice cubes in tumbler A. 2. Add cold water up to half way. 3. Fill tumbler B to the same height with cold water. 4. Stir each for ten seconds. 5. Read the temperature of each and write it at 0 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Stir first, then read. Hold the tip of the probe in the middle of the water, away from the wall of the tumbler. Wait until the number stops changing.  
  *Child mode:* Fill both tumblers. Stir each one. Put the probe in the middle and read the number. Write it down.
  - Note: The probe has a pointed tip, so hand it over handle first and wipe it between tumblers.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every three minutes, stir each tumbler for ten seconds and read the temperature. 2. Write each reading in the table. 3. Go on until 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Read both tumblers every three minutes. Write the number straight away. Look at how much ice is left each time.  
  *Child mode:* Every three minutes, stir and read both tumblers. Write the numbers. Look at how much ice is left.
  - Ask: What is happening to the number in A, and to the ice in A? (good: The number stays at about 0.; The ice is getting smaller.; B is warming up but A is not.)
  - Note: Tumbler A should stay within one degree of 0 while ice is left. If it reads 2 or 3, stir for longer, because the water at the bottom warms first.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add two level teaspoons of salt to tumbler A. 2. Stir for one minute. 3. Read the temperature. 4. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Now the salt. Guess the number first. Then stir for a full minute and read. Look for a minus sign.  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Then stir two spoons of salt into tumbler A. Read the number. Is there a minus sign?
  - Note: With ice still in the tumbler, the reading usually falls to between minus 2 and minus 6. If all the ice has gone, add three fresh cubes before the salt.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to write three lines: what I think, what I saw, why. Pour the salty water down the sink.  
  *Parent says:* Heat went into both tumblers. Only one got warmer. Write where you think the heat in tumbler A went.  
  *Child mode:* Write three lines. What do I think? What did I see? Where did the heat go?
  - Ask: Heat flowed into A the whole time. Why did the number not go up? (good: The heat was used to melt the ice.; The ice got smaller instead of the water getting warmer.)
  - Check: When will tumbler A start to warm up? → **When the last ice has melted**
  - Think: The salt made the number go below 0. Where did that heat go?

### a1 · Ghee There and Back (12 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the warm water. It should be no warmer than a bath, so that a hand can rest in it.
- **record:** Draw the ghee and the coconut oil three times: at the start, over warm water and over cold water. Write one word under each drawing.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put half a teaspoon of ghee in one small bowl and half a teaspoon of coconut oil in the other. Ask your child to describe each one and guess what warm water will do.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the ghee and the oil. Is each one a solid or a liquid right now? Which will change first over warm water? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the ghee and the oil. Is each one solid or liquid now? Guess which will change first.
  - Note: If your kitchen is warm and both are already liquid, do the ice water step first and the warm water step second.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour warm water into one wide bowl. 2. Float both small bowls on it. 3. Watch for three minutes. 4. Ask where the melting starts.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the edge of the ghee. Does it melt all at once or bit by bit? Which one turned clear first?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to pour the warm water. Float the two bowls. Watch where the melting starts.
  - Ask: Did the ghee melt all at once? (good: No, the grains stayed for a while in the clear part.; It melted from the edge that touched the bowl.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put cold water and four ice cubes in the other wide bowl. 2. Move the two small bowls onto it. 3. Watch for three minutes. 4. Tilt each small bowl to see if it still flows.

  *Child mode:* Float the two bowls on the ice water. Watch the colour change. Tilt each bowl. Does it flow?
  - Ask: What did you see as it set? (good: The oil turned white from the edge.; The ghee went cloudy and thick.; It stopped flowing.)
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what was the same and what was different between the start and the end. Scrape the ghee and oil back for cooking.  
  *Parent says:* Is it the same ghee as before? What changed and what did not? What decided whether it was solid or liquid?  
  *Child mode:* Is it the same ghee as at the start? What changed? What made it change?
  - Think: The ghee got grainy and the oil did not. What might be different inside them?
### a2 · Dip or Bump? What happens to the size (20 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** If the coconut oil is solid, a grown-up stands the bottle in warm water first. Use plastic tumblers, because glass can crack in a freezer.
- **record:** Draw each tumbler from the side on both days. Show the line and the surface.
- **schedule:** day 1: Mark the levels, guess, and put the water in the freezer and the oil in the fridge. (10 min); day 2: Take both out, compare each surface with its line, and talk about why. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. If the oil is solid, stand its bottle in warm water until it is clear. 2. Half fill one tumbler with water and the other with oil. 3. Mark each level with the pen or a rubber band.  
  *Parent says:* Both are liquids now. Mark the line exactly at the surface. Bend down so that your eyes are level with it.  
  *Child mode:* Half fill one tumbler with water and one with oil. Mark each level exactly. A grown-up warms the oil if needed.

## Challenges
### c1 · Free the Coin: the fastest rescue from ice (30 min)
- **problem:** A coin is frozen in the middle of a block of ice. Get the coin out as fast as you can.
- **your turn:** Now think of a method that is not on either list, or join two methods together. Guess its time first. There is no single best answer: the fastest way in a hot kitchen in Chennai may not be the fastest in a cold one in Shimla.
- **constraints:** No warm or heated water, and no heater, iron or hair dryer.; No hitting, cutting or breaking the ice.; You may use only things from the list.; Each method gets its own ice block, and you time each one.
- **general:** Pour tap water slowly over the block. Tap water is 20 degrees or more above the melting point of ice, and moving water keeps bringing fresh heat.; Rub salt onto the block. The salt lowers the melting point at the surface, so the ice melts even while it is very cold.; Hold the block in your hands. Your skin is at about 34 degrees.
- **creative:** Lay the block on a steel plate and press it down. Metal carries heat from the room into the ice far faster than air does.; Fan the block hard. Moving air brings more of the room's heat to the ice than still air.; Sprinkle salt in one line across the block, so that it melts a groove straight down to the coin.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem and the rules aloud. Look at the things on the list. Ask your child to choose three methods and guess which will be fastest.  
  *Parent says:* The coin can come out only when the ice around it melts. Melting needs heat. Where could the heat come from? Choose three ways and guess the fastest.  
  *Child mode:* Read the rules. Think about where heat can come from. Choose three ways. Guess which is fastest.
  - Ask: Where can the heat for melting come from? (good: From tap water.; From my hands.; From the air in the room.; From a metal plate.)
  - Note: Dip each bowl in tap water for a few seconds to loosen the block before you start.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start the first method and the clock together. 2. Stop the clock when the coin comes free. 3. Write the time. 4. Do the same for the other two methods.  
  *Parent says:* Start the clock as you begin. Stop it the moment the coin is free. Write the time before you start the next one.  
  *Child mode:* Start the clock and the method together. Stop when the coin is free. Write the time. Then try the next way.
  - Note: If you have the hands, run all three at once to save time. Stop any method at 10 minutes and write 'more than 10'.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the three times. Ask why the fastest was fastest. Then read the two lists of solutions together.  
  *Parent says:* Which was fastest? What brought heat to the ice quickest? Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the three times. Why was the fastest one fastest?
  - Ask: Why does running water beat still air? (good: Water carries more heat than air.; It keeps moving, so fresh warm water keeps coming.)
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for one new method or a mix of two. Let your child guess its time. Try it another day with a fresh block.  
  *Parent says:* Now invent your own way, or join two together. Guess its time first. We will freeze another coin tonight.  
  *Child mode:* Invent your own way, or join two ways. Guess its time. Freeze another coin for tomorrow.
  - Think: Salt gave the ice no heat at all. So where did the heat come from when the salt melted the ice?

## Worksheets
### Melting and solidifying: the main ideas (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Butter on a hot dosa turns runny. Which change is this?  
   Options: Dissolving / Melting / Evaporating  
   **Answer:** Melting. *Why:* Melting. One substance, butter, turns into its own liquid because of heat. Dissolving would need a second substance, a liquid for the butter to spread into.
2. (mcq) Coconut oil melts at about 24 degrees. The kitchen is at 19 degrees. What is the oil?  
   Options: A solid / A liquid / A gas  
   **Answer:** A solid. *Why:* A solid. 19 is below 24, so the oil is colder than its melting point. If you chose liquid, compare the two numbers again: below the melting point means solid.
3. (mcq) Melted candle wax drips on a table and turns hard. Where did its heat go?  
   Options: Into the table and the air / It was used up and is gone / Deeper into the wax  
   **Answer:** Into the table and the air. *Why:* A liquid gives out heat as it solidifies, and the heat goes into whatever it touches. Heat is not used up. You can feel the table warm under a fresh wax drop.
4. (mcq) Iron melts at 1,538 degrees and wax at about 60. Which has stronger pulls between its particles?  
   Options: Iron / Wax / They are the same  
   **Answer:** Iron. *Why:* Iron. Stronger pulls hold the particles in place more firmly, so the solid must be much hotter before they break free.
5. (mcq) Sugar stirred into cold water seems to vanish. Which change is this?  
   Options: Melting / Dissolving / Freezing  
   **Answer:** Dissolving. *Why:* Dissolving. There are two substances, and the water is far colder than the temperature at which sugar turns liquid. If you chose melting, count the substances.
6. (truefalse) True or false: water is the only substance that can freeze.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Freezing means a liquid turning solid. Melted wax, ghee, gold and iron all freeze, each at its own temperature.
7. (truefalse) True or false: ice and liquid water are made of the same particles.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The particles are the same. In ice they are held in place, and in water they slide past each other.
8. (fill) The temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid is its ____ ____.  
   **Answer:** ['melting point']. *Why:* Each pure substance has its own melting point. For ice it is 0 degrees Celsius.
9. (fill) A change that can be undone, giving the same substance back, is a ____ change.  
   **Answer:** ['reversible']. *Why:* Melting is undone by solidifying, so both are reversible changes.
10. (match) Match each solid to its melting point in degrees Celsius.  
   **Answer:** Ice → 0; Coconut oil → about 24; Candle wax → about 60; Iron → 1,538. *Why:* Ice melts in a cool room, coconut oil on a warm day, wax near a flame and iron only in a furnace.
11. (sort) Sort each change: is it melting or dissolving?  
   **Answer:** ghee on hot rice → Melting; salt in rasam → Dissolving; an ice cube in lemonade → Melting; jaggery in tea → Dissolving; wax beside a flame → Melting; sugar in milk → Dissolving. *Why:* Melting needs one substance and heat. Dissolving needs a solid and a different liquid for it to spread into.
12. (order) Put in order what the particles do as ice from the freezer warms up and melts.  
   **Answer:** ['The particles shake a little on the spot', 'The particles shake harder and harder', 'Particles at the surface break out of their places', 'The particles slide past each other']. *Why:* Warming makes the particles shake harder. At the melting point the ones at the surface break free first, and then the whole solid becomes a liquid.
13. (write) The ghee is hard in the morning and soft by afternoon. Nobody touched it. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The morning was cooler than the melting points of the fats in ghee, so they were solid. The afternoon was warmer, so some or all of them melted.. *Why:* A strong answer compares the temperature of the room with the melting point. It may add that ghee is a mixture and softens bit by bit.
14. (draw) Draw the particles of wax as a solid and as a liquid. Show how they move.  
   **Answer:** Solid: circles touching in neat rows, with small shake marks. Liquid: circles still touching but jumbled, with short arrows showing them sliding past each other.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps the particles the same size and close together in both. Only the order and the movement change.
15. (write) You want to know if salt makes ice melt faster. What would you keep the same, and what would you measure?  
   **Answer:** Keep the same: the number and size of ice cubes, the bowls, the place and the time. Change only the salt. Measure the meltwater in teaspoons.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing only and names something that can be counted or measured.
### Hidden heat, salt and what comes back (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Ice and water are stirred in a tumbler in a warm room. What does a thermometer show while ice is left?  
   Options: About 0 degrees the whole time / A slow, steady rise / The same as the room  
   **Answer:** About 0 degrees the whole time. *Why:* About 0. The heat coming in is used to melt the ice, so the temperature holds at the melting point. A steady rise begins only when the ice has gone.
2. (mcq) What is the heat that goes into melting ice used for?  
   Options: Pulling particles out of their places / Making each particle bigger / Making each particle heavier  
   **Answer:** Pulling particles out of their places. *Why:* The heat pulls the particles out of their fixed places against the pulls between them. The particles do not change in size or weight.
3. (mcq) Why does a bowl of salted ice get colder than a bowl of plain ice?  
   Options: Salt is cold when it leaves the shelf / The extra melting takes heat from the ice and bowl / Salt keeps the room's heat out  
   **Answer:** The extra melting takes heat from the ice and bowl. *Why:* Salt makes more ice melt, and melting takes in heat. With no one heating the bowl, the heat comes from the ice, the bowl and the salty water. Salt on the shelf is at room temperature.
4. (mcq) A spoon of sugar melts less ice than a spoon of salt. What is the main reason?  
   Options: Sugar is sweet / A spoon of sugar has far fewer particles / Sugar is colder than salt  
   **Answer:** A spoon of sugar has far fewer particles. *Why:* What matters most is how many dissolved particles get in the way at the surface of the ice. Sugar particles are big, so a spoonful holds about ten times fewer.
5. (mcq) Ghee melts bit by bit and not at one temperature. Why?  
   Options: It is a mixture of many fats / It has been kept too long / Its particles are very large  
   **Answer:** It is a mixture of many fats. *Why:* Each fat in ghee has its own melting point. The low ones melt first and the high ones last, so ghee softens over a stretch of temperatures.
6. (truefalse) True or false: a sealed bottle of ice gets lighter when the ice melts.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Nothing leaves the sealed bottle, so the weight is the same. Try it on a kitchen scale.
7. (truefalse) True or false: a piece of bronze glowing red at 900 degrees can be a frozen solid.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Bronze turns solid a little below 1,000 degrees. At 900 it is below its freezing point, so it is solid, even though it glows.
8. (fill) Water that is colder than 0 degrees but still liquid is called ____ water.  
   **Answer:** ['supercooled']. *Why:* Supercooled water has not yet started to freeze, because no seed of ice has formed in it.
9. (fill) Salt dissolved in water makes the freezing point of the water ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lower']. *Why:* Salty water freezes below 0 degrees. Sea water freezes at about minus 2.
10. (match) Match each thing to what it does.  
   **Answer:** Salt on a kulfi seller's ice → makes the ice mix colder than 0 degrees; Crushed ice around fish → stays at 0 degrees while any ice is left; A goldsmith's furnace → takes gold above 1,064 degrees; A fridge at 5 degrees → sets coconut oil solid. *Why:* Each one uses a melting point: lowering it with salt, holding steady at it, going above it, or going below it.
11. (sort) A wax figure is melted and the wax sets again. Sort what comes back and what does not.  
   **Answer:** the substance, wax → Comes back; the weight → Comes back; the shape of the figure → Does not come back by itself; the fine lines on the surface → Does not come back by itself. *Why:* Melting is reversible for the substance and its amount. The form is lost, and has to be made again with a mould or with hands.
12. (order) Put these in order from the lowest melting point to the highest.  
   **Answer:** ['Mercury', 'Ice', 'Coconut oil', 'Candle wax', 'Iron']. *Why:* Mercury minus 39, ice 0, coconut oil about 24, candle wax about 60, iron 1,538. Mercury is liquid in a room, so its melting point must be below room temperature.
13. (write) Fish sellers pack fish in crushed ice, not in cold water. Use hidden heat to explain why ice is better.  
   **Answer:** Melting ice takes in a lot of heat and stays at 0 degrees until it has all melted. Cold water has no ice to melt, so the same heat warms it up quickly.. *Why:* A strong answer says the ice stays at 0 while it melts, and that melting takes in far more heat than warming water by a few degrees.
14. (perspective) Three people are talking about a glass window.  
   **Answer:** Any view can be defended. A strong answer gives evidence, such as glass keeping its shape for 2,000 years, or its particles being jumbled like a liquid's, and names something that would change the writer's mind.. *Why:* The second view fits what we can measure in a human lifetime. The first is right that the particles are jumbled, but old windows have not flowed. The third is the one physicists use for pitch and tar. Whether glass is a true state of matter is still argued.
15. (write) A friend says salt is warm, and that is why it melts ice. What test would show her it is not so?  
   **Answer:** Touch the outside of a bowl of salted ice and a bowl of plain ice, or put a thermometer in each. The salted one is colder.. *Why:* A strong answer names something to feel or measure, and compares the salted ice with plain ice.
### The edges: supercooling, glass, pressure and proof (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A clean bottle of water is at minus 5 degrees and still liquid. What does it need in order to freeze?  
   Options: To get much colder first / A seed, from a speck or a knock / More water in the bottle  
   **Answer:** A seed, from a speck or a knock. *Why:* It is already cold enough. It needs a first group of particles in the pattern of ice. A speck, a scratch or a knock helps one to form.
2. (mcq) Supercooled water flash-freezes into slush, not a hard block. Why?  
   Options: Freezing gives out heat, which warms it back to 0 / The bottle is too small / Slush is a different substance  
   **Answer:** Freezing gives out heat, which warms it back to 0. *Why:* As ice forms it gives out hidden heat. This warms the water back up to 0 degrees, and then no more can freeze until the freezer takes that heat away.
3. (mcq) The Earth's inner core is solid iron at more than 5,000 degrees. What keeps it solid?  
   Options: Huge pressure / Its great age / Ice mixed into it  
   **Answer:** Huge pressure. *Why:* Pressure raises the melting point of iron. At the centre of the Earth the pressure is millions of times that of the air, so iron stays solid far above 1,538 degrees.
4. (mcq) A tiny speck of gold melts at a lower temperature than a gold bangle. Why?  
   Options: Most of its particles are at the surface / Small things are always warmer / The speck is not real gold  
   **Answer:** Most of its particles are at the surface. *Why:* Particles at a surface have fewer neighbours holding them, so they break free more easily. In a tiny speck, most particles are at the surface.
5. (mcq) You want to mark 0 degrees on a new thermometer. Which would you trust more?  
   Options: Stirred, melting ice / Still water in a freezer, as it freezes / Either, they always agree  
   **Answer:** Stirred, melting ice. *Why:* Melting ice always holds 0 degrees. Still water can go below 0 before it freezes, so the moment of freezing is not a dependable mark.
6. (truefalse) True or false: camphor left in an open tin turns into a liquid first, and then into a gas.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the open air camphor goes straight from solid to gas and leaves no puddle. This is called sublimation.
7. (truefalse) True or false: water always turns to ice as soon as it is cooled to 0 degrees.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Clean, still water can stay liquid below 0. It freezes only when a seed of ice forms.
8. (fill) A solid turning straight into a gas, with no liquid in between, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['sublimation']. *Why:* Camphor, naphthalene balls and dry ice all do this in the open air.
9. (fill) Ice takes up ____ room than the water it was made from.  
   **Answer:** ['more']. *Why:* In ice the particles lock into open rings, which take up more room. That is why ice cubes have a bump and why ice floats.
10. (match) Match each scientist to what they found.  
   **Answer:** Daniel Fahrenheit → water can stay liquid below its freezing point; Inge Lehmann → the Earth has a solid inner core; Edgar Zanotto → old window glass has not flowed; Shelly Schmidt → sugar may break down before it melts. *Why:* Each finding showed that a simple statement about melting or freezing needed a closer look.
11. (sort) Sort these: does it melt at one exact temperature, or soften over a stretch?  
   **Answer:** pure gold → One exact temperature; ghee → Softens over a stretch; ice from pure water → One exact temperature; window glass → Softens over a stretch; chocolate → Softens over a stretch; pure tin → One exact temperature. *Why:* Pure substances with particles in neat rows melt at a point. Mixtures such as ghee and chocolate, and glass with its jumbled particles, soften over a stretch.
12. (order) Put the steps of casting a bronze figure in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Shape the figure in wax', 'Coat the wax in clay and let it dry', 'Heat the mould so that the wax runs out', 'Pour melted bronze into the hollow', 'Break the clay when the bronze has set']. *Why:* The wax melts at about 60 degrees and leaves a hollow. The bronze, melted at about 1,000 degrees, fills the hollow and solidifies in the shape the wax had.
13. (write) Is an ice cube sitting in ice-cold water doing nothing? Use particles to explain your answer.  
   **Answer:** No. Particles keep leaving the ice and others keep joining it. At 0 degrees the two happen equally fast, so the cube stays the same size.. *Why:* A strong answer says that leaving and joining go on all the time, and that the cube looks still because they are in balance.
14. (draw) Draw a graph of temperature against time for ice at minus 10 warming until it is water at 20 degrees.  
   **Answer:** The line rises from minus 10 to 0, runs flat at 0 while the ice melts, then rises again to 20.. *Why:* A strong graph has a flat stretch at 0 degrees. The heat going in there is melting the ice, not warming it.
15. (perspective) Two food scientists disagree about heating table sugar.  
   **Answer:** One good test is to heat sugar quickly and slowly and compare the temperature at which it turns liquid. Another is to cool the liquid and check whether it is still only sugar. A strong answer says what each side predicts.. *Why:* A true melting point should not depend on how fast you heat, which is the evidence on one side. If the cooled liquid were still pure sugar, that would support the other. Food scientists have not settled this.

   Options: Freezing, or solidifying / Melting / Burning  
   **Answer:** Freezing, or solidifying. *Why:* A liquid turning solid is freezing, at any temperature. If you chose melting, think about which way the change went: from liquid to solid.
2. (mcq) Pure water can freeze at 0 degrees. At what temperature does pure ice melt?  
   Options: 0 degrees / 4 degrees / 100 degrees  
   **Answer:** 0 degrees. *Why:* For a pure substance the melting point and the freezing point are the same temperature. 100 degrees is where water boils.
3. (mcq) What do the particles of a solid do at its melting point?  
   Options: Break out of their places and slide past each other / Stop moving / Split into smaller particles  
   **Answer:** Break out of their places and slide past each other. *Why:* The particles shake hard enough to break out of their places. They never stop moving, and they do not split. They are the same particles in the liquid.
4. (truefalse) True or false: when ice melts, each water particle melts too.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. One particle cannot be solid or liquid. Melting is a change in how the particles are arranged and how they move.
5. (mcq) Coconut oil melts at about 24 degrees. On which kitchen shelf is it solid?  
   Options: Shimla, at 8 degrees / Chennai, at 33 degrees / Kochi, at 30 degrees  
   **Answer:** Shimla, at 8 degrees. *Why:* Only 8 degrees is below the melting point of 24. In Chennai and Kochi the shelf is warmer than 24, so the oil is liquid.
6. (mcq) Mustard oil stays liquid through a Delhi winter. What does this tell you about its melting point?  
   Options: It is below the winter temperature / It is above 24 degrees / Mustard oil has none  
   **Answer:** It is below the winter temperature. *Why:* A substance is liquid when it is warmer than its melting point. So the melting point of mustard oil must be lower than a Delhi winter day.
7. (mcq) A tumbler of ice and water stands in the sun. Half the ice has gone. How warm is the water, if stirred?  
   Options: About 0 degrees / About 15 degrees / As warm as the air  
   **Answer:** About 0 degrees. *Why:* While any ice is left, the heat from the sun goes into melting it. The stirred water stays at about 0 degrees.
8. (mcq) You stir salt into a bowl of ice. What happens?  
   Options: It melts faster and gets colder / It melts faster and gets warmer / It melts more slowly and gets colder  
   **Answer:** It melts faster and gets colder. *Why:* Salt tips the balance towards melting, and melting takes in heat from the ice and the bowl. If you chose warmer, remember that salt gives no heat.
9. (mcq) Why does a kulfi seller mix salt into the ice around the kulfi?  
   Options: To make the ice mix colder than 0 degrees / To make the kulfi salty / To stop the ice from melting  
   **Answer:** To make the ice mix colder than 0 degrees. *Why:* Sweet milk freezes below 0 degrees, and plain ice is only at 0. Ice with salt can reach minus 15, which is cold enough. The salt makes the ice melt faster, not slower.
10. (mcq) A goldsmith melts a ring and casts a coin from it. What is the same before and after?  
   Options: The substance and its weight / The shape / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** The substance and its weight. *Why:* Melting and solidifying are reversible. The gold and its weight are unchanged. The shape is new, because the liquid took the shape of the mould.
11. (mcq) Which sentence about window glass is true?  
   Options: It softens slowly, with no exact melting point / It melts at one exact temperature / It has flowed down in old windows  
   **Answer:** It softens slowly, with no exact melting point. *Why:* The particles of glass are jumbled, so it softens over a stretch of temperatures. Careful sums show old panes have not flowed. They were made uneven.
12. (truefalse) True or false: scientists fully agree on how melting first starts inside a solid.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They agree on what melting is, but how the very first particles break free is still being studied.
13. (mcq) In a fair test of salt on ice, what is the only thing that should change?  
   Options: What is sprinkled on the ice / The size of the bowls / The number of ice cubes  
   **Answer:** What is sprinkled on the ice. *Why:* If the bowls or the number of cubes changed too, you could not tell what caused the difference. Change one thing and keep the rest the same.
14. (mcq) Your test gave 8 spoons of meltwater for salt and 3 for plain ice, once. What should you do next?  
   Options: Repeat the test and compare / Change the numbers to look neater / Stop, because once is enough  
   **Answer:** Repeat the test and compare. *Why:* One try can be a fluke. If a second try gives nearly the same result, you can trust it more. Results are never changed to look neater.

## Applications
- Casting: bronze figures, steel machine parts, gold ornaments and candles are all made by melting a substance, pouring it into a mould and letting it solidify.
- Using things again: steel, aluminium, glass, gold and wax can be melted and made into new objects, because melting is a reversible change.
- Keeping things cold: fish, medicines and vaccines travel in melting ice, which holds a steady 0 degrees for hours.
- Making kulfi and ice cream with ice and salt, which gets far colder than ice alone.
- Clearing icy roads and runways in cold places with salt.
- Setting thermometers: the world's temperature scale is fixed by the melting and freezing points of pure substances.
- Joining wires: solder is a metal mixture that melts at about 200 degrees, so it can join parts without melting them.
- Safety fuses: a thin wire in a fuse melts and breaks the path when too much electricity flows.
- Checking purity: chemists test a medicine by its melting point, because an impure sample melts lower and over a wider stretch.

## Open questions
- **How exactly does melting begin inside a solid?** The first steps involve a few particles and last less than a millionth of a second. Rules such as Lindemann's from 1910 give roughly the right temperature but do not describe what happens. New cameras that use flashes of electrons are only now starting to catch it.
- **Is glass a true state of matter, or a liquid that has become too slow to move?** To find out, a liquid would have to be cooled so slowly that the test would take longer than a human life. The physicist Philip Anderson wrote in 1995 that this may be the deepest unsolved problem in the science of solids. It is still unsolved.
- **Can anyone say exactly when a bottle of supercooled water will freeze?** The first seed of ice forms by chance among a few hundred particles. Scientists can give the odds, but not the moment. They also do not fully know how the proteins of some bacteria arrange water into a seed so well.
- **Does very cold water come in two different liquid forms?** The place to look is near minus 70 degrees, where water freezes in less than a thousandth of a second. Only a few experiments, with very fast X-ray flashes, have reached it, and scientists read the results differently.
- **At what temperature does the iron at the centre of the Earth melt?** Nobody can go there, and the pressure is very hard to copy in a laboratory. Different experiments give answers that differ by about a thousand degrees.

## Connected maths
- Put these melting points in order from the coldest to the hottest: 24, minus 39, 0, 60, minus 210, 30. **Minus 210, minus 39, 0, 24, 30, 60.**. Numbers below 0 come first, and the one furthest below 0 is the coldest. So minus 210 is colder than minus 39. Then come 0, 24, 30 and 60. These are nitrogen, mercury, ice, coconut oil, gallium and candle wax. (queues `math.number-sense.negative-numbers.L4`)
- Coconut oil melts at 24 degrees. At 7 in the morning the shelf is at 19 degrees. At 2 in the afternoon it is at 31. How many degrees below the melting point is the morning? How many above is the afternoon? **5 degrees below in the morning. 7 degrees above in the afternoon.**. 24 minus 19 is 5, so the morning is 5 degrees below and the oil is solid. 31 minus 24 is 7, so the afternoon is 7 degrees above and the oil is liquid. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L4`)
- In a Salt Test the plain bowl gave 3 teaspoons of meltwater, the sugar bowl 4 and the salt bowl 9. How many times as much as the plain bowl did the salt bowl give? Each bowl started with ice worth 12 teaspoons. What fraction of the salted ice melted? **3 times as much. Three quarters of the salted ice melted.**. 9 divided by 3 is 3, so the salt bowl gave 3 times as much. 9 out of 12 is the same as 3 out of 4, which is three quarters. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Tumbler B, with cold water only, read 8, 10, 11, 13, 14 and 15 degrees at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 and 15 minutes. Tumbler A, with ice, read 1, 0, 0, 0, 0 and 0. How many degrees did B warm in 15 minutes? How many did A warm? What does the line for A look like on a graph? **B warmed by 7 degrees. A did not warm at all, and in fact fell by 1. The line for A is flat along 0.**. 15 minus 8 is 7. A went from 1 to 0 and stayed there. On a graph, B is a line going up and A is a flat line along 0, because the heat going into A was melting ice. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- The funnel of pitch was opened in 1930. Drops fell in 1938, 1947, 1954, 1962, 1970, 1979, 1988, 2000 and 2014. How many years passed from 1930 to the ninth drop? About how many years is that for each drop? Are the gaps getting longer or shorter? **84 years. About 9 years for each drop. The last two gaps, 12 and 14 years, are the longest.**. 2014 minus 1930 is 84. 84 divided by 9 is a little over 9. The gaps are 8, 9, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9, 12 and 14 years. The last two are longer, and the scientists think air conditioning put into the building made the pitch cooler and slower. (queues `math.data.averages.L4`)
- The heat that melts one ice cube could warm the same amount of water by 80 degrees. By how many degrees could that heat warm twice as much water? Four times as much? **40 degrees for twice as much water. 20 degrees for four times as much.**. The same heat shared among twice as much water warms it half as much. Half of 80 is 40. Shared among four times as much, it warms it a quarter as much. A quarter of 80 is 20. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
