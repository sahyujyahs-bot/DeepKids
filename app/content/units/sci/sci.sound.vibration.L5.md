# Vibration: how strings, skins and air columns make sound
`sci.sound.vibration.L5`

**Summary.** Draw a card slowly along a comb and you hear separate ticks, but draw it fast and the ticks join into one steady note. Why? In this lesson you will count the pushes in each second with a comb, watch a slow string vibrate, and hear a spoon ring through a string.

**Intro.** *Pluck a rubber band stretched over a steel tumbler. It hums, and it looks like a blur. Touch it with one finger, and the hum stops at once. What was the band doing while it hummed? And why did it keep doing it after your finger let go?* The band was vibrating. It was moving back and forth far too quickly for your eyes to follow. In this lesson you slow that movement down until you can watch it. You turn separate ticks into a note with a comb. You work out how many pushes reach your ear each second. You make grains jump on a skin that nothing touches. You hear a spoon ring like a temple bell through a piece of string. By the end you can say what vibrates in a veena, a mridangam, a bansuri and your own throat.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a vibration is, using the words rest position, amplitude and frequency.
1. I can explain why a plucked string keeps moving back and forth, and what makes a vibration faster or slower.
2. I can explain how separate pushes become a steady note, and work out the pushes in each second, in hertz.
3. I can say what vibrates in a string instrument, a drum, a wind instrument and a voice.
4. I can describe how a vibration travels through a solid, a liquid or a gas to the eardrum.
5. I can plan a fair test, and name what I change, what I measure and what I keep the same.
6. I can explain where a vibration goes when a sound dies away, and why a soundboard makes a sound louder.

## Terms
- **Vibration**: A movement back and forth, repeated again and again. One vibration is one full trip: away, back through the middle, to the other side and back again. *e.g.* A plucked veena string vibrates. So does a drum skin after it is struck.
- **Rest position**: The place where a thing stays when nothing is moving it. A vibrating thing moves to each side of its rest position. *e.g.* A rubber band's rest position is the straight line it makes before you pluck it.
- **Amplitude**: The size of a vibration: how far the thing moves from its rest position. A bigger amplitude gives a louder sound. *e.g.* Pluck a string gently and the blur is thin. Pluck it harder and the blur is wide, and the sound is louder.
- **Frequency**: The number of vibrations in one second. A higher frequency gives a higher note. *e.g.* A honeybee's wings beat about 230 times a second. That frequency is the hum you hear.
- **Hertz**: The unit for frequency. One hertz means one vibration each second. It is written Hz, and is named after the German scientist Heinrich Hertz. *e.g.* A string that vibrates 440 times a second has a frequency of 440 hertz.
- **Springy (elastic)**: A springy thing goes back to its shape after you bend, stretch or squeeze it. Scientists call this being elastic. *e.g.* A stretched string, a tight drum skin and squeezed air are all springy. That is why all three can vibrate.
- **String**: A thin length of wire, gut or thread, held tight at both ends. When it is plucked, bowed or struck, it vibrates. *e.g.* The veena, the sitar, the violin and the guitar are string instruments.
- **Skin (membrane)**: A thin sheet stretched tight over a frame. Scientists call it a membrane. When it is struck, the whole sheet vibrates. *e.g.* The heads of a mridangam, a tabla and a chenda are skins. So is your eardrum.
- **Air column**: The air inside a tube or a pipe. This air can vibrate back and forth along the tube. *e.g.* In a bansuri, a nadaswaram or a bottle you blow across, the air column makes the note.
- **Reed**: A thin strip of cane or metal that flaps quickly when air is blown past it. It starts the air column vibrating. *e.g.* A nadaswaram and a shehnai have reeds made of cane. A harmonium has metal reeds.
- **Soundboard**: A wide, thin surface joined to a string. The string makes it vibrate, and the wide surface pushes much more air than the thin string can. *e.g.* The hollow jackwood body of a veena is its soundboard. Without it you would hardly hear the string.
- **Medium**: The material that a sound travels through. A medium can be a solid, a liquid or a gas. *e.g.* When someone calls you, the medium is air. For a whale's song, the medium is sea water.
- **Particles**: The tiny pieces that every material is made of. They are far too small to see. A sound travels when each particle pushes the next one. *e.g.* Air particles pass the push from a drum skin to your ear. Each particle moves only a tiny way and comes back.
- **Eardrum**: A thin skin about 9 millimetres across, at the inner end of the ear hole. Sound makes it vibrate. *e.g.* When a mridangam is played, your eardrum vibrates at the same frequency as the drum skin.
- **Damping**: Anything that takes the energy out of a vibration, so that it dies away. Touching, soft cloth and the air itself all damp a vibration. *e.g.* A sitar player rests a hand on the strings to stop them. That is damping.
- **Independent variable**: In a fair test, the one thing you change on purpose. *e.g.* In the Comb Test, the independent variable is the time the stroke takes.
- **Dependent variable**: In a fair test, the thing you watch or measure to see what happens. *e.g.* In the Comb Test, the dependent variable is what you hear: ticks, a rattle, a buzz or a note.
- **Control variables**: In a fair test, the things you keep the same, so that only one thing changes. *e.g.* In the Comb Test, the comb, the card and the 40 teeth stay the same for every stroke.

## Explanations
### What a vibration is · *defined*
**Definition:** A vibration is a movement back and forth past a rest position, repeated again and again.

Every sound starts with something vibrating. To describe a vibration you need two numbers. The amplitude says how far the thing moves from its rest position. The frequency says how many full trips it makes each second. Frequency is measured in hertz. Most sound sources vibrate too quickly to watch. A plucked string looks like a blur because it passes the same places hundreds of times a second. The blur is widest where the string moves most. The two numbers are separate. Pluck harder and the amplitude grows, so the sound is louder, but the frequency stays the same. A young person can hear frequencies from about 20 hertz to about 20,000 hertz.
- e.g. In the Slow String, a chain of rubber bands with a peg on it swung about 3 times a second. That was slow enough to watch.
- e.g. The lowest string of a guitar vibrates about 82 times a second.
- e.g. A mosquito's wings beat several hundred times a second, which is why the whine is so high.
### Why a thing keeps vibrating · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing vibrates when it is springy and has weight. Its springiness pulls it back towards its rest position, and its own movement carries it past.

Pull a string to one side and let go. The tight string pulls itself back towards the middle. As it goes, it speeds up. It is moving fastest at the moment it reaches its rest position, so it cannot stop there. It carries on to the other side. Now the string is pulled back the other way. It slows, stops for an instant and returns. This repeats until the energy of the pluck is used up. Two things set the frequency. A stronger pull back gives a faster vibration. More weight to move gives a slower vibration. A shorter string also has less to move, so it vibrates faster. The same two rules work for a drum skin and for the air in a pipe.
- e.g. In the Slow String, a clothes peg clipped to the chain made it swing more slowly. There was more weight to move.
- e.g. In the Slow String, stepping back to pull the chain tighter made it swing faster, until it was a blur.
- e.g. The low strings of a guitar or a violin are wound with fine wire. The wire adds weight, so a tight string can still vibrate slowly.
### How separate pushes become a note · *defined*
**Definition:** Each vibration gives the air one push. Above about 20 pushes a second, human ears stop hearing them one by one and hear a steady note.

In the Comb Test, each tooth bent the card and let it snap back. Each snap pushed the air once. At 5 or 10 pushes a second you heard every tick. At about 20 the ticks ran together into a rattle. At 80 you heard a low note. Nothing changed except how fast the pushes came. A higher frequency gives a higher note. The note of a string, a drum or a flute is made in the same way. The string pushes the air once in every vibration. So 440 vibrations a second send 440 pushes a second to your ear. In 1681 the English scientist Robert Hooke showed this with a toothed wheel. He held a card against the turning teeth. When the wheel turned faster, the note rose.
- e.g. A male cricket has a row of tiny teeth on one wing. He draws the edge of the other wing across it, as you drew the card across the comb.
- e.g. A zip makes a rising 'zzzip' when you pull it faster.
- e.g. A cat's purr is about 25 pushes a second. That is slow enough to feel each one with your hand.
### Strings, skins and air columns · *defined*
**Definition:** Most musical instruments make sound with one of three vibrating things: a tight string, a tight skin, or the air inside a tube.

A string is plucked, bowed or struck. Its note depends on its length, its tightness and its weight. A thin string moves very little air. So it is joined through a bridge to a soundboard or a hollow body. A skin is struck with a hand or a stick. Its note depends on its size, its tightness and its weight. The black patch on a tabla is a paste of rice flour and fine iron or stone powder. It adds weight in the middle of the skin. An air column is the air inside a tube. Something must start it: a reed, the player's lips, or a stream of breath across an edge. Air is springy and it has weight, so it vibrates back and forth along the tube. A longer column vibrates more slowly and gives a lower note.
- e.g. String instruments include the veena, the sitar, the sarod, the violin, the guitar, the tanpura and the ektara of the Bauls.
- e.g. Instruments with skins include the mridangam, the tabla, the chenda, the parai, the dhol and the pung of Manipur.
- e.g. Instruments with an air column include the bansuri, the nadaswaram, the shehnai, the conch and the trumpet. In the Palm Pipe, the air in a cardboard tube gave a note when you slapped the end.
### Your voice, and the voices of other animals · *defined*
**Definition:** A voice is made by two small folds in the voice box, called the vocal folds. Breath from the lungs blows them apart, and they spring shut again, many times a second.

The voice box is the bump you can feel at the front of your throat. Its proper name is the larynx. When you speak, the two folds open and close about 250 times a second in a child. That is about 200 times in a woman and about 120 times in a man. Each opening lets out one puff of air, and each puff is one push. The puffs then pass through the throat and the mouth, which are an air column. So a voice uses folds that act like a skin, and an air column that shapes the sound. Tighter, thinner folds vibrate faster and give a higher note. Humans are one animal among many who make sound this way. Cows, cats, dogs and frogs have vocal folds too. A bird sings with a different organ, deeper in the chest, called the syrinx. Insects mostly rub or beat hard parts of the body.
- e.g. In Sss and Zzz, your fingers felt a buzz on your throat for 'zzz' but not for 'sss'.
- e.g. In Sss and Zzz, your hum stopped when you pinched your nose, because the breath could no longer flow.
- e.g. A male cricket sings by rubbing his wings. A bee's hum is the beat of her wings.
- e.g. With your eyes shut, you can often tell the source. A cricket's chirp comes from rubbing wings. A dog's bark comes from vocal folds. A bird's song comes from the syrinx, deep in the chest.
### How the vibration reaches your ear · *defined*
**Definition:** A vibration travels through a medium because each particle pushes the next. The sound reaches you when the pushes make your eardrum vibrate.

Air is made of particles with spaces between them. When a drum skin moves outwards, it squeezes the air particles next to it closer together. Those particles push the ones beyond them, and so on across the room. Each particle moves only a tiny way and then comes back. The squeeze travels, and the air stays where it was. The same thing happens in water and in a solid, where the particles are closer together. In the Spoon on a String, the vibration ran along the string to your fingers. From there it passed through the bones of your head. Sound travels at about 340 metres a second in air and about 1,500 in water. In steel it travels at about 5,000 metres a second. Where there are no particles, as in space, there is nothing to pass the push on. At the ear, the pushes make the eardrum vibrate. Three tiny bones pass the vibration to the inner ear, which is full of liquid. Nerves carry signals from there to the brain.
- e.g. In the Thali and the Skin, the sound of a steel plate crossed the air. It made grains jump on a skin that nothing touched.
- e.g. In the Spoon on a String, the ring was louder and longer through the string than through the air.
- e.g. A whale's song travels through sea water for many kilometres.
### Where the vibration goes · *defined*
**Definition:** A vibration dies away because its energy leaves. Some energy goes into the air as sound, and the rest warms the vibrating thing and whatever touches it.

A pluck gives a string a fixed amount of energy. Every vibration passes a little of that energy to the air. The amplitude shrinks, the sound grows softer, and at last the string is still. Anything that takes the energy faster is called damping. A finger on a string takes the energy almost at once. A soundboard takes energy from the string quickly and gives it to the air. So an instrument with a soundboard is louder, but its note dies sooner. A hard table does the same for a buzzing phone. The phone hardly moves the air by itself, but the whole table top moves a great deal of air. A soft pad under the phone keeps the vibration out of the table.
- e.g. In the Blur and the Pinch, the tumbler stopped ringing the moment you pinched its rim.
- e.g. In Quiet the Buzz, the phone was loud on a steel plate and much quieter on a folded towel.
- e.g. A mixer-grinder stands on rubber feet, so that less of its vibration passes into the kitchen counter.
### Is everything vibration? · *debated*
Vibration is found far beyond music. The particles of every solid vibrate all the time, millions of millions of times a second. That movement is what we call heat. A quartz crystal in a watch vibrates 32,768 times a second and keeps the time. After a very large earthquake the whole Earth rings for weeks. Its slowest vibration takes about 54 minutes. The surface of the Sun rises and falls about every five minutes. Some people go further and say that everything is vibration. Here the views differ.
- e.g. Heat in a steel tumbler is the vibration of its particles.
- e.g. The Earth rang for weeks after the Sumatra earthquake of December 2004.
- e.g. String theory describes every particle as a tiny vibrating string. No experiment has tested it yet.
- e.g. A rustle of dry leaves in the wind is many leaves knocking and rubbing against each other, so it has no clear note.
- **Physicists who use quantum field theory:** Our best tested theory describes every particle as a ripple in a field that fills all of space. An electron is a ripple in the electron field. This is a very exact way of calculating, and it has passed every test so far.
- **String theorists:** Every kind of particle may be one kind of tiny string, vibrating in different ways. One veena string gives different notes in the same way. The mathematics is promising. Nobody has yet found a way to test the idea by experiment.
- **The musician and scholar Sharngadeva, about 800 years ago:** In the first chapter of his book on music, the Sangita Ratnakara, sound (nada) is treated as the root of music and, in a deeper sense, of all things. This is a teaching about meaning. It was not offered as a measurement.
- **A careful critic:** A ripple in a field, the jiggling of particles and the swing of a string are different things. Calling them all 'vibration' uses one word for many ideas. The word should not be stretched until it explains nothing.

## Mostly agreed
- Every sound source vibrates. When the vibration stops, the sound stops. *How we know:* Touch any ringing thing and the sound ends as the movement ends. High-speed cameras show strings, drum skins and vocal folds moving back and forth.
- The note we hear depends on the frequency, which is the number of vibrations each second. *How we know:* In 1681 Robert Hooke held a card against a turning toothed wheel. When the wheel turned faster, the note rose. The Comb Test shows the same thing.
- Sound needs a medium. It travels through solids, liquids and gases, and it cannot cross empty space. *How we know:* About 1660, Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke pumped the air out of a glass jar with a bell inside. The bell still moved, but its sound grew much fainter.
- A bigger vibration gives a louder sound, and it does not change the note. *How we know:* A string plucked gently and the same string plucked hard give the same note. Only the width of the blur and the loudness change.
- A vibration dies away because its energy passes into the air as sound and into the material as heat. *How we know:* A string joined to a soundboard is louder and stops sooner than the same string held between two fingers. The energy of the pluck is spent faster.

## How we found out
**Galen, a Greek doctor in Rome, Antoine Ferrein, a French doctor, and Manuel García, a Spanish singing teacher, Galen wrote about 1,800 years ago. Ferrein reported his tests in 1741. García saw his own vocal cords in 1854 and reported it in 1855..** For a very long time, doctors thought the voice worked like a wind instrument. The Greek doctor Galen, about 1,800 years ago, compared the voice box to the mouthpiece of a blown pipe. On this idea the blown air made the note. In 1741 the French doctor Antoine Ferrein tested it. He took voice boxes from dead bodies and blew air through the windpipe. They gave a sound. When he stretched the two small bands inside the voice box tighter, the note rose. So the bands were vibrating, as the strings of a violin do, with the air acting like a bow. He named them vocal cords. Nobody had yet seen them move in a living person. In 1854 Manuel García, a Spanish singing teacher, held a small dentist's mirror at the back of his own throat. He used a second mirror to shine sunlight on it. He saw his vocal cords open and close as he sang. Today we know they are soft folds and not strings. Breath blows them apart, and they spring shut again.  
*What people believed before:* That the voice is like a flute, with only the air in the windpipe making the note. Ferrein showed that tightening the bands in the voice box raises the note, so the bands are part of the source.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Sound is a stream of tiny particles thrown out by the thing that makes the sound.** (The Greek thinker Epicurus and the Roman poet Lucretius, more than 2,000 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: A sound does seem to leave its source and arrive at the ear, as a thrown ball does. A louder sound could be more particles.  
  Against: A bell inside a jar goes quiet when the air is pumped out, though the bell still moves. Thrown particles would cross the empty jar easily. So sound needs the air to carry it.
- **'Sound travels best through solids' is only half the story.** (Physicists who study sound; *accepted*)  
  For: Sound is faster in a solid, and a string or a rail keeps it from spreading out. That is why the spoon rang so loudly through the string. But sound in the air hardly gets into a solid or a liquid at all. Almost all of it bounces off the surface. That is why a shut window keeps out street noise.  
  Against: Many school books say only that solids carry sound best, then liquids, then gases. That is true once the vibration is inside the material.
- **Vibrations slower than 20 a second are not sound.** (A common line in school books; *debated*)  
  For: Human ears hear nothing below about 20 vibrations a second, however strong the vibration is.  
  Against: Elephants and pigeons hear vibrations slower than that. The air does the same thing at 15 vibrations a second as at 50. Scientists call these slow vibrations infrasound. Whether to call them 'sound' depends on who is listening.
- **Plants hear.** (Plant scientists such as Heidi Appel, Rex Cocroft and Lilach Hadany; *debated*)  
  For: Plants sense vibrations and act on them. In a 2014 study, thale cress plants made more defence chemicals after feeling the vibrations of a caterpillar chewing. In a 2019 study, evening primrose flowers made sweeter nectar within three minutes of the sound of a flying bee.  
  Against: Some scientists say that responding to a vibration is not the same as hearing. They want the studies repeated by other teams. Claims that music makes plants grow better rest on weak tests.
- **Everything in the universe is made of tiny vibrating strings.** (String theorists, since the 1970s; *debated*)  
  For: In the mathematics, one kind of string vibrating in different ways could give every known particle, and gravity too.  
  Against: After fifty years there is still no experiment that can test it. Many physicists say an idea that cannot be tested is not yet science.

## Roots
In this lesson you turned separate ticks into a note, and watched a slow string pass its rest position. Here we ask what a note really is, and who makes it.

**Deep truth.** In the Comb Test, a slow stroke over 40 teeth gave 40 separate ticks. A quick stroke over the same teeth gave one steady note. Only the speed had changed. So a steady note is a row of separate pushes, arriving too quickly for the ear to hear apart.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat a tick and a note as two kinds of sound, one broken and one smooth. They are the same kind at different speeds. The pushes stay separate in the air. Above about 20 pushes a second, a human ear reports them as one note.  
*Evidence:* In the Comb Test, 5 and 10 ticks a second were heard one by one. About 20 gave a rattle, and 80 gave a low note. Robert Hooke's toothed wheel showed the same in 1681. A male cricket makes his song by drawing a scraper across a row of teeth on his wing.

- *who am i:* Your voice is your own body vibrating. Two small folds in your throat open and close about 250 times a second when you speak.
- *what is life:* Living beings make and catch vibrations all day. A male cricket draws one wing across the other. A spider reads her web through her feet. You do it with a throat and two eardrums.
- *what is this world:* Anything that is springy and has weight can vibrate: a string, the air, the ground, the Sun. The world goes on ringing for a while after every push.

In the Comb Test, 40 separate ticks became one note. Looked at closely, the one note is still many pushes. So 'one' and 'many' can be two true descriptions of the same event. Which one you get depends on how fast the pushes come, and on who is listening. Many sages taught that the many different things in the world are really one thing, which we meet only a part at a time. The Comb Test does not prove that. It does show, with a comb and a card, how many can be heard as one.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2, 'On Making Things Equal' (China, about 2,300 years ago):** “夫大塊噫氣，其名爲風。是唯无作，作則萬竅怒呺。 / 夫吹萬不同而使其自己也，咸其自取，怒者其誰邪？” A teacher describes the wind blowing through all the hollows of a forest. Each hollow gives its own sound: some roar, some whistle, some sigh. Then he asks who does the sounding. (This is an air column, described long ago. In the Palm Pipe, a long tube and a short tube gave different notes from the same slap. Each hollow gives the note that its own length sets. The book leaves its question open, and so do we.)
- **Sharngadeva, Sangita Ratnakara, chapter 1 (India, 13th century):** This book on music speaks of two kinds of sound. The struck sound is made when things meet, and it is the sound of all music. The unstruck sound is said to be heard only in deep stillness. (Every sound in this lesson is a struck sound. Something was plucked, hit, rubbed or blown, and then it vibrated. Science studies this kind. The unstruck sound is a teaching about the mind, and science does not measure it.)
- **Aristotle, On the Soul, book 2, chapter 8 (Greece, about 2,350 years ago):** “Γίνεται δ’ ὁ κατ’ ἐνέργειαν ψόφος ἀεί τινος πρός τι καὶ ἔν τινι· πληγὴ γάρ ἐστιν ἡ ποιοῦσα. / ἕτερον γὰρ τὸ τύπτον καὶ τὸ τυπτόμενον / Ψοφητικὸν μὲν οὖν τὸ κινητικὸν ἑνὸς ἀέρος συνεχείᾳ μέχρις ἀκοῆς” A sound needs three things: something that strikes, something that is struck, and a medium between them and the ear. The air must be moved as one mass, all the way to the hearing. (This matches the chain you built in the Spoon on a String: a source, a medium and an ear. Aristotle did not know about particles, but he saw that the air must carry the movement.)
- Science (settled): The particles of every solid vibrate all the time. This movement is what we measure as temperature. It never stops completely, even at the lowest temperature there can be.
- Science (settled): Our best tested theory, quantum field theory, describes every particle as a ripple in a field that fills space.
- Science (idea): String theory says every particle is a tiny vibrating string. No experiment has tested it.
- Other view, Many physicists, and people who study hearing: The ticks of the comb blend because of how human ears and brains work. That is a fact about ears, and it says little about the world. Vibration itself is ordinary. Anything that is pushed from rest and pulled back will vibrate, and forces and weight explain it fully. On this view, calling the world 'a vibration' borrows a word from music and stretches it too far.
- Practice, The bee hum (3 min): Sit up and close your lips. Breathe in, then hum one long, low note, like a bee. Rest your fingertips on your throat, then on your cheeks, then on the top of your head. Notice where you feel the hum most. Now stop humming and sit quite still. Notice the moment when the buzz in your body fades. Do this three times.
- Ask: In the Comb Test, 40 ticks became one note. Was the note made by the comb, by the air, or by your ear?
- Ask: Elephants and pigeons hear vibrations too slow for you to hear. Is a sound still a sound when you cannot hear it?
- Ask: When the plucked string is still again, where is your pluck now?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the Comb Test, 40 ticks became one note. Did the ticks join together in the air, or in you? (hint: A microphone would still record 40 separate pushes. Think about what your ear and brain add.)
- *deep:* A plucked string is moving fastest as it passes its rest position. So how does a vibrating thing ever come to rest? (hint: Think about the Slow String. Did each swing go as far as the one before?)
- *big:* An elephant hears vibrations too slow for you. A bat hears vibrations too fast for you. Whose list of sounds is the right one? (hint: Is there one list of sounds in the world, or one list for each kind of ear?)
- *deep:* When a string stops vibrating, where has the energy of your pluck gone? (hint: Some went into the air as sound. Where does sound go when it fades?)
- *big:* Atoms, the Earth and the Sun all vibrate. Does that make the universe a kind of music, or is that only a way of speaking? (hint: What would have to be true for it to be music? Does music need a listener?)

## Story: The String with a Coat
*Tresa, Lilly Ammayi and Joby are made up, and so is the shop. 'Ammayi' is a Malayalam word for aunt. These things are real: Fort Kochi is a harbour town in Kerala. The low strings of guitars, violins and pianos are a thin core string with fine wire wound round it. The wire adds weight, so the string vibrates slowly while staying tight. A springy plank bounces more slowly under a heavy load than under a light one. Winding a string by hand with a drill is possible, but it is slow and the result is rough. String makers use a winding machine.*

The last ferry had already gone when Joby came to the shop. Rain was falling hard on Fort Kochi, and the sea was the colour of a steel plate.

Tresa was ten. Every evening after school she helped her aunt, Lilly Ammayi, who mended instruments in a narrow shop behind the fish market. Violins hung from the ceiling. A harmonium lay open on the bench with its metal reeds in a row.

Joby was twelve, and he was soaked. He carried a guitar wrapped in a plastic sheet.

"It was my grandfather's," he said. "I am playing at the church feast tomorrow evening. Look."

The thickest string had snapped. It hung from the guitar in two curls.

Ammayi looked through her tin of strings. "Only thin ones," she said. "The thick ones come from Ernakulam, and no boat will cross tonight." She turned to Tresa. "You have until tomorrow noon. I have a harmonium to finish."

Then she went back to her bench. That was how Ammayi taught. She gave you the problem, and then she gave you room.

Tresa fitted a thin string where the thick one had been. She tightened it and plucked. The note was clear, and far too high.

"Loosen it," said Joby. "Loose strings sound lower."

He was right. She turned the peg back, and the note slid down, lower and lower. But the string grew slack. By the time the note was low enough, the string flopped against the wood and rattled. It hardly made a sound at all.

"Too loose to play," said Joby. "So a low string has to be a thick string. And we have no thick string."

Tresa went to the door to think. The rain had thinned. Down at the jetty, a plank lay from the stone edge to a fishing boat. A grey cat ran across, and the plank shivered quickly under her feet. Then a woman crossed with a full crate of sardines on her head. Under her, the same plank dipped and rose slowly, once for each step.

Tresa watched until the woman reached the boat.

It was the same plank, and just as springy as before. Only the weight on it had changed. With more weight to move, it bounced more slowly.

She ran back inside and picked up the two curls of the broken string. She had always taken it for one fat wire. Now she held the broken end under the lamp and bent it. Something was coming undone. A wire as fine as a hair was unwinding, round and round, from a plain thin string in the middle.

"Joby," she said. "It was never a thick string. It is a thin string wearing a coat."

"Why would a string need a coat?"

"To be heavy. A heavy string moves slowly, even when it is pulled tight. The string must be tight so that it rings. It must be heavy so that it moves slowly. And a string that moves slowly gives a low note."

Ammayi did not look up from the harmonium. "There is copper wire in the dead fan motor under the bench," she said.

It took them until late. They gripped one end of a thin string in the hand drill and tied the other end to the vice. Joby turned the handle slowly. Tresa fed the copper wire on, each turn tight against the last, like thread on a bobbin. Twice the coat came loose, and twice they began again.

At last she fitted the string to the guitar and turned the peg. The string tightened. She plucked it.

The note was low and steady, and it went on ringing. It was not quite the old sound. It was a little dull, as a first try often is. Joby played the first line of the feast hymn, and grinned.

Ammayi came over then. She plucked the string once and listened until it faded.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you can tell me why the thin strings wear no coat."

Tresa was already working it out.

**For the parent:** For the parent: how fast a string vibrates depends on two things. One is how hard it is pulled back, which is its tightness. The other is how much there is to move, which is its weight. Loosening a thin string lowers the note, but leaves the string too slack to ring. Winding wire round the string adds weight without loosening it. So the string vibrates slowly and still rings. Tresa saw the same rule in the plank, which bounced slowly under a heavy load.

- Ask: A loose string gave a low note. So why could Joby not just play on the loose string?
- Ask: The plank was the same under the cat and under the woman. What changed, and what did that do to the bounce?
- Ask: Why do the thin, high strings of a guitar need no wire wound round them?
- Ask: Tresa's string sounded a little dull. What might be different between her winding and one made by a machine?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Comb Test: when do ticks become a note? (35 min)
- **question:** How many ticks a second does it take before separate ticks sound like one steady note? What happens to the note when the ticks come faster still?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will draw a card along 40 teeth of a comb in 8 seconds. Then you will do it in 4, 2, 1 and half a second. Which strokes will sound like separate ticks, and which like a note? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same comb, the same 40 teeth and the same card for every stroke. Press just as lightly each time. Change only one thing: the time the stroke takes. That time is the independent variable. What you hear is the dependent variable. Do every stroke three times, because a hand cannot keep a perfectly steady speed.
- **record:** Draw a table with five rows, one for each stroke time: 8 seconds, 4 seconds, 2 seconds, 1 second and half a second. For each row, write the ticks each second and what you heard. Then write what you thought would happen, what you found, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Time for the stroke | Ticks each second (40 ÷ the time) | What we heard: ticks, rattle, buzz or note
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count 40 teeth along the comb. 2. Mark the first tooth and the fortieth with the sketch pen. 3. Hold the comb in one hand with the teeth pointing up. 4. Rest the short edge of the card between the teeth, about 3 millimetres deep.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to draw this card along exactly 40 teeth. Each tooth will bend the card and let it snap back. So one stroke gives 40 ticks.  
  *Child mode:* Count 40 teeth on the comb. Mark the first and the last. Rest the edge of the card between the teeth.
  - Note: If the comb has fine teeth at one end and wide teeth at the other, mark 40 of one kind. Keep the other kind for a later step. A thin, bendy card gives a weak sound. A visiting card or a playing card works well.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child the five stroke times: 8 seconds, 4 seconds, 2 seconds, 1 second and half a second. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* A slow stroke will take 8 seconds, and the fastest will take half a second. Which strokes will sound like separate ticks? Which will sound like a note? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which strokes will give separate ticks and which will give a note.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: A fast stroke makes the ticks come too close together to hear apart.; A zip makes a note when you pull it fast.; I think it will always be ticks, only quicker.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. One person watches the stopwatch and counts the seconds aloud. 2. The other draws the card from the first mark to the second in 8 seconds. 3. Do it three times. 4. Then do the same in 4 seconds, three times. 5. Write what you heard, and work out 40 ÷ 8 and 40 ÷ 4.  
  *Parent says:* I will count the seconds aloud. Move the card steadily, so that you reach the second mark as I say eight. What do you hear? Can you count the ticks?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the card along the 40 teeth in 8 seconds. Listen. Then do it in 4 seconds. Write what you hear.
  - Note: 40 teeth in 8 seconds is 5 ticks each second. 40 teeth in 4 seconds is 10 ticks each second. Most people hear every tick at both speeds. The first tries are often jerky. That is why each stroke is done three times.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the card along the 40 teeth in 2 seconds, three times. 2. Then in 1 second, three times. 3. Then in half a second, three times. 4. After each one, write what you heard. 5. Work out 40 ÷ 2, 40 ÷ 1 and 40 ÷ a half.  
  *Parent says:* Now faster. Two seconds first, then one second, then half a second. Half a second is about as long as saying the word 'one'. Listen each time. Is it ticks, a rattle, a buzz or a note?  
  *Child mode:* Now do it in 2 seconds. Then 1 second. Then half a second. Is it ticks, a rattle, a buzz or a note?
  - Note: The numbers are 20, 40 and 80 ticks each second. Half a second cannot be timed exactly by hand, and it does not need to be. One quick, smooth stroke is enough.
  - Check: In the Comb Test, the card crosses 40 teeth in half a second. How many ticks is that each second? → **80**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. If the comb has wide teeth and fine teeth, stroke 5 centimetres of each kind in one second. 2. If it has one kind only, stroke 20 teeth in one second, and then 40 teeth in one second. 3. Ask which sounded higher.  
  *Parent says:* Here is one more test. Keep the stroke time at one second, and change the number of teeth the card crosses. Which gives the higher note? Why do you think that is?  
  *Child mode:* Keep the time at 1 second. Cross few teeth, then many teeth. Which note is higher?
  - Ask: Which gave the higher note, and why? (good: The one with more teeth, because more ticks reached my ear each second.; The fine teeth, because they are closer together.)
  - Note: More teeth in the same time means more ticks each second, and a higher note. This shows that the number of ticks each second sets the note.
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they heard, and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Say what you thought would happen, what you heard, and why you think it happened. At about how many ticks a second did the ticks turn into a note?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you heard. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: What was the only thing that changed between the ticks and the note? (good: The time the stroke took.; How many ticks came each second.; The comb, the card and the teeth were the same.)
  - Ask: A plucked string gives a note. What does that tell you about how the string is moving? (good: It must be pushing the air many times each second.; It moves back and forth too fast to see, like fast ticks.)
  - Note: Most people hear separate ticks at 5 and 10 a second, and a rattle at about 20. At about 40 they hear a rough buzz, and at 80 a low note. Your results may differ a little. The reason: each tooth bends the card and lets it snap back, so each tooth gives the air one push. Above about 20 pushes a second, human ears stop hearing the pushes one by one. We hear how fast they come, as a note. More pushes each second give a higher note. Robert Hooke showed this in London in 1681, with a card held against a turning toothed wheel.
  - Think: A mosquito's wings beat several hundred times a second. What does the comb tell you about the mosquito's whine?

### a2 · The Slow String: watch one vibration (15 min)
- **record:** Tell your grown-up where the peg was moving fastest and where it stopped for a moment. You can also take a photo of the peg at the far side and another as it passes the middle.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Loop six rubber bands into a chain. 2. Tie one end to a chair. 3. Hold the other end so that the chain is just straight and hardly stretched. 4. Clip the peg to the middle. 5. Pull the peg a hand-width to one side and let go.  
  *Parent says:* This chain is a string, but a slow one. Pull the peg to the side and let go. Watch one full trip. Where does the peg stop for a moment? Where is it moving fastest?  
  *Child mode:* Clip the peg to the middle of the chain. Pull it to the side. Let go and watch one full swing.
  - Ask: Where is the peg moving fastest, and where does it stop for a moment? (good: It is fastest in the middle, at the rest position.; It stops for a moment at each side, then turns back.)
  - Note: With the peg on, the chain swings about 3 times a second. If it is too quick to follow, hold the chain a little looser or add a second peg.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the question below before you explain anything. 2. Let your child watch several swings. 3. Then read them the explanation in the note.  
  *Parent says:* The chain pulls the peg back towards the middle. So why does the peg not stop when it gets to the middle? Watch again, and tell me what you think.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the peg swing several times. Think about why it keeps going past the middle.
  - Ask: Why does the peg not stop in the middle? (good: It is moving fastest there, so it carries on.; Nothing stops it in the middle. The pull only starts again once it has gone past.)
  - Note: The stretched chain pulls the peg back towards its rest position. The peg speeds up all the way there, so it arrives at full speed and goes past. On the other side the chain pulls it back again. Each swing is a little smaller, because some energy leaves each time.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the peg off and pluck the chain. 2. Your child takes one step back to tighten the chain, and plucks. 3. They take one more step back, and pluck again.  
  *Parent says:* Now take the peg off. Is the swing faster or slower? Why? Now step back to pull the chain tighter. What happens to the swing?  
  *Child mode:* Take the peg off. Pluck. Is it faster? Step back and pluck again. What changes?
  - Note: Without the peg, the chain flutters too fast to count. Pulled tight, it becomes a blur, widest in the middle, and you may hear a faint low hum. A tighter chain pulls back harder, so it vibrates faster.
  - Check: Taking the peg off the Slow String made it swing faster. Why? → **There was less weight to move**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep the chain tight. 2. Pinch it with two fingers about 10 cm from your child's hand. 3. Your child plucks the short part and listens. 4. Ask what they thought would happen, what happened and why.  
  *Parent says:* Now I will pinch the chain, so that only a short part can move. Pluck the short part. What do you hear? Say what you thought would happen, what happened, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Pluck the short part of the chain. What do you hear? Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: The same chain gave a slow swing, then a blur, then a note. What changed each time? (good: First the weight, when the peg came off.; Then the tightness, when I stepped back.; Then the length, when it was pinched short.)
  - Note: The short part gives a clear twang. A short string has less to move, so it vibrates faster. A slow vibration that you can watch and a fast vibration that you can hear are the same kind of movement. Only the frequency is different.
  - Think: A veena player presses a string against a fret to play a higher note. Which of your three changes is that?

  *Child mode:* Slap the long tube. Slap the short tube. Which is higher? Say why you think it happened.
  - Note: The short tube gives the higher note. With the tube at your ear you hear a faint steady hum, picked out of the noise of the room. It drops lower when a palm closes the far end. This last part is faint, so try it in a room with a fan running.
  - Check: A 30 cm tube and a 15 cm tube are each slapped on one end. Which gives the higher note? → **The 15 cm tube**

### p1 · The Dancing Light Spot: a skin that shows your voice (40 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the cup and the balloon. Do not use a laser pointer: it can harm eyes. Never shine the torch or the sunlight into anyone's eyes.

  *Parent says:* This balloon is our skin. The mirror will ride on it. If the skin moves, the mirror tilts. Why might a mirror be a good way to see a very small movement?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts the cup and the balloon. Stretch the balloon over the cup. Glue the mirror on, a little off the centre.
  - Note: The skin should be tight and flat. The mirror goes off the centre because the skin tilts most there. At the exact centre the mirror only moves in and out, and the spot hardly shifts.

  *Child mode:* Shine the torch on the mirror. Find the spot on the wall. Clap near the cup. Does the spot move?
  - Note: The spot from a torch is a fuzzy patch a few centimetres wide. That is fine. Sunlight from a window gives a sharper spot. The further away the wall is, the more the spot moves.
  - Wait: Carry on the next day

  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a guess first: will a loud 'aaa' move the spot more than a soft one? Why? 2. Your child speaks into the open end of the cup: a soft 'aaa', then a loud 'aaa'. 3.   - Note: A louder sound gives a longer streak, because the skin has a bigger amplitude. Low and high notes often give different shapes. Some notes move the skin much more than others. If the spot does not move, bring the mouth closer, and check that the skin is tight.

  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why you think it happened.
  - Note: The voice pushes the air, the air pushes the skin, and the mirror shows the movement. A microphone works in the same way: a thin skin moves with the sound, and the movement is turned into an electric signal.
  - Check: A louder voice made a longer streak of light. What does that show about the skin? → **It vibrated with a bigger amplitude**

### c1 · Quiet the Buzz: a stand for a buzzing phone (25 min)
- **problem:** A phone set to vibrate lies on an upturned steel plate. When it buzzes, the rattle can be heard across the flat. Build a stand for the phone that sits on the same plate. The buzz must be as quiet as you can make it. The phone must lie on top where you can see the whole screen.

You may not wrap it or cover it.; The phone must stay on the stand while it buzzes for 10 seconds.; Use only things from home, such as cloth, sponge, erasers, rubber bands, bottle caps, card and rice.

  - Ask: The phone is the same. Why is it so much louder on the plate than in a hand? (good: The plate vibrates too, and it is big, so it pushes more air.; The hand is soft and takes the vibration.)
  - Note: A phone buzzes because a tiny motor inside spins a weight that is off its centre. The phone is small, so it pushes little air. On the plate, the whole plate is made to vibrate and acts as a soundboard.

## Worksheets
### What vibrates, and how the sound reaches you (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a vibration?  
   Options: A movement back and forth, again and again / A movement in one direction only / A loud sound / A kind of air  
   **Answer:** A movement back and forth, again and again. *Why:* A vibrating thing moves to one side of its rest position and back through it to the other side. It does this over and over. Choosing 'a loud sound' mixes up the sound with the movement that makes it.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a thing can make a sound without any part of it vibrating.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every sound source vibrates. In the Blur and the Pinch, the tumbler went silent the moment its vibration was stopped.
3. (fill) The number of vibrations in one second is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['frequency']. *Why:* Frequency is counted in hertz. One hertz is one vibration each second.
4. (match) Match each sound maker to the part that vibrates.  
   **Answer:** Veena → A tight string; Mridangam → A tight skin; Bansuri → The air inside a tube; Your voice → Two folds in the throat. *Why:* Strings, skins and air columns are the three main vibrating things in instruments. A voice starts with the vocal folds in the voice box.
5. (mcq) In the Comb Test, why did a quick stroke give a note and not ticks?  
   Options: The ticks came too fast to hear apart / The comb grew warmer / The card changed shape / The teeth moved closer together  
   **Answer:** The ticks came too fast to hear apart. *Why:* The comb, the card and the teeth were the same. Only the number of ticks each second changed. Above about 20 a second, the ear hears them as one note.
6. (sort) Sort these instruments by what vibrates: a string, a skin or an air column.  
   **Answer:** sitar → String; violin → String; tabla → Skin; chenda → Skin; nadaswaram → Air column; conch → Air column. *Why:* A sitar and a violin have strings. A tabla and a chenda have skins. In a nadaswaram and a conch, the air inside vibrates.
7. (order) A veena is plucked. Put these in the order the vibration passes through them.  
   **Answer:** ['The string', 'The wooden body', 'The air in the room', 'The eardrum', 'The three tiny ear bones']. *Why:* The string makes the body vibrate, the body pushes the air, and the air makes the eardrum vibrate. The ear bones pass the vibration to the inner ear.
8. (truefalse) True or false: sound can travel through a solid, a liquid and a gas.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In the Spoon on a String, the ring crossed air, ran along a string and passed through water. Each one is made of particles that pass the push on.
9. (count) A card crosses 40 teeth of a comb in 2 seconds. How many ticks is that each second?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* 40 ticks shared over 2 seconds is 40 ÷ 2, which is 20 ticks each second.
10. (mcq) In the Thali and the Skin, what did you change on purpose?  
   Options: The distance from the plate to the bowl / The number of grains / The size of the bowl / The kind of spoon  
   **Answer:** The distance from the plate to the bowl. *Why:* The distance was the independent variable, the one thing changed on purpose. The grains, the bowl and the spoon were control variables, kept the same.
11. (write) You pinch the rim of a ringing steel tumbler, and the sound stops. Explain why, in two sentences.  
   **Answer:** The sound comes from the tumbler vibrating. The pinch stops the vibration, so the tumbler no longer pushes the air, and the sound stops.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the sound lasts only as long as the vibration. It also says that fingers take the energy of the vibration.
12. (write) In the Spoon on a String, the spoon sounded far louder with your fingers at your ears. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** With my fingers at my ears, the vibration ran along the solid string. It passed into my fingers and the bones of my head. It did not spread out in all directions, as it does in air. So much more of it reached my inner ear.. *Why:* A strong answer names the path, which is string, fingers and bone. It also says that sound in the air spreads out, so less of it arrives.
13. (draw) Draw a plucked string as it looks while it hums. Mark its rest position, and mark where it moves most.  
   **Answer:** A blur shaped like a thin lens between two fixed ends. A straight line through the middle of the blur is the rest position. The widest part of the blur, halfway along, is where the string moves most.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the ends still and the middle moving most. The rest position is a straight line between the ends.
14. (perspective) Which matters more for the sound of a veena: the string or the hollow body?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that the note changes when the string is tightened or shortened. Another is that a buzzing phone was much louder on a steel plate, which acted as a soundboard.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses evidence. The Slow String shows what sets the note. Quiet the Buzz shows what a wide surface does.
15. (mcq) Why can sound not cross empty space?  
   Options: No particles are there to pass the push / Space is too cold / Space is too far away / Sound is too slow  
   **Answer:** No particles are there to pass the push. *Why:* A sound travels when each particle pushes the next. Empty space has no particles, so the vibration has nothing to travel through.
### Why a vibration is fast or slow (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Where is a vibrating string moving fastest?  
   Options: As it passes its rest position / At the far edge of its swing / At its two fixed ends / It moves at one speed everywhere  
   **Answer:** As it passes its rest position. *Why:* The string speeds up all the way back to its rest position and slows down after it. At the far edge it stops for an instant. The fixed ends do not move at all.
2. (mcq) In the Slow String, a clothes peg on the chain made it swing more slowly. Why?  
   Options: There was more weight to move / The chain was pulled back harder / The chain became shorter / The peg blocked the air  
   **Answer:** There was more weight to move. *Why:* The chain pulled back just as hard as before. It had more weight to move, so each trip took longer.
3. (truefalse) True or false: plucking a string harder makes it vibrate more times each second.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A harder pluck gives a bigger amplitude and a louder sound. The frequency, and so the note, stays the same.
4. (mcq) The lowest string of a guitar has fine wire wound round it. What is the wire for?  
   Options: To add weight, so it vibrates slowly / To make the string tighter / To make the string shine / To stop the string vibrating  
   **Answer:** To add weight, so it vibrates slowly. *Why:* More weight to move gives a slower vibration and a lower note. The string can then stay tight enough to ring well.
5. (odd) Three of these make a string vibrate faster. Which one makes it slower?  
   Options: Tightening it / Pressing it to make it shorter / Changing it for a lighter string / Winding wire round it  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Winding wire round a string adds weight, so it vibrates more slowly. A tighter, shorter or lighter string vibrates faster.
6. (count) A honeybee's wings beat about 230 times a second. How many times do they beat in 2 seconds?  
   **Answer:** 460. *Why:* About 230 beats each second for 2 seconds is 230 × 2, which is about 460 beats.
7. (sort) Does each change make the vibration faster or slower?  
   **Answer:** tightening a drum skin → Faster; pressing wet paste onto a drum skin → Slower; covering more holes on a flute → Slower; shortening a string → Faster; cutting a pipe in half → Faster. *Why:* A harder pull back, or less to move, gives a faster vibration. More weight, or a longer air column, gives a slower one.
8. (fill) The distance a vibrating thing moves from its rest position is called its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['amplitude']. *Why:* Amplitude is the size of a vibration. A bigger amplitude gives a louder sound.
9. (order) A card crosses 40 comb teeth. Put the strokes in order, from the lowest sound to the highest.  
   **Answer:** ['A stroke of 4 seconds', 'A stroke of 2 seconds', 'A stroke of 1 second', 'A stroke of half a second']. *Why:* The strokes give 10, 20, 40 and 80 ticks each second. More ticks each second give a higher sound.
10. (write) In the Palm Pipe, the short tube gave a higher note than the long tube. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The note came from the air inside the tube vibrating back and forth. The short tube holds less air, so there is less to move. The air column vibrates faster and gives a higher note.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the air column vibrates. It links a shorter column to less air to move and a faster vibration.
11. (write) A phone buzzes on a steel plate, then on a folded towel. Predict which is louder, and explain.  
   **Answer:** The phone is louder on the steel plate. The plate is hard, so the phone makes the whole plate vibrate. The wide plate pushes a lot of air. The soft towel takes the energy of the vibration, so only the small phone pushes the air.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the plate acts as a soundboard and that the towel damps the vibration. It may use the step counts from Quiet the Buzz.
12. (write) Plan a fair test: do rava or rice grains jump more on a skin? Name your three kinds of variable.  
   **Answer:** I change the kind of grain: that is the independent variable. I measure how much the grains move, with a score of 2, 1 or 0: that is the dependent variable. I keep the same bowl, film, plate, spoon, strike and distance: those are the control variables. I do three strikes for each grain.. *Why:* A strong plan changes one thing only, and says what is measured and how. It lists what stays the same, and repeats each test.
13. (perspective) Are vibrations slower than 20 a second, which humans cannot hear, still sound?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that the Comb Test shows the air carrying pushes whether or not they blend into a note. Another is that different animals hear different ranges, so one animal's silence is another's sound.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement about what the word 'sound' should mean. A strong answer uses a fact from the unit and treats another view fairly.
14. (label) Label this picture of a sound reaching an ear: source, medium, eardrum, ear bones, inner ear.  
   **Answer:** ['Source: the drum, which vibrates', 'Medium: the air between the drum and the ear', 'Eardrum: the small tight skin at the end of the ear hole', 'Ear bones: the three tiny bones behind the eardrum', 'Inner ear: the coiled part filled with liquid']. *Why:* The vibration starts at the source, crosses the medium, and makes the eardrum vibrate. The bones pass it to the liquid of the inner ear.
15. (truefalse) True or false: a plucked string moves most at its two ends.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The ends are held still. The string moves most in the middle, where the blur is widest.
16. (sort) Sort each sound by what makes it: a living body, or things knocking and rubbing together.  
   **Answer:** a bird's song → A living body; a rustle of dry leaves in the wind → Things knocking or rubbing; a dog's bark → A living body; a loose tin sheet clanging in the wind → Things knocking or rubbing; a cricket's chirp → A living body; a gate creaking as it swings → Things knocking or rubbing. *Why:* A bird, a dog and a cricket make sound with their own bodies. The leaves, the tin and the gate knock or rub against each other.
### Testing ideas about vibration (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A bell rings inside a jar while the air is pumped out. What happens?  
   Options: The bell still moves, but its sound fades / The bell stops moving / The sound grows louder / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** The bell still moves, but its sound fades. *Why:* The bell still vibrates, but with fewer and fewer air particles there is less to carry the pushes. Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke showed this about 1660.
2. (mcq) The quartz crystal in a watch vibrates 32,768 times a second. What best explains such a high frequency?  
   Options: It is very stiff and very small / It is very soft and very heavy / It is kept very cold / It is hit very hard  
   **Answer:** It is very stiff and very small. *Why:* A stiff thing is pulled back hard, and a tiny thing has little to move. Both make a vibration fast. Hitting harder changes only the amplitude.
3. (truefalse) True or false: after a very large earthquake, the whole Earth can ring for weeks.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Earth is springy and has weight, so it vibrates when struck. After the Sumatra earthquake of 2004 it rang for weeks, far too slowly to hear.
4. (truefalse) True or false: the air next to a drum travels across the room to your ear.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each air particle moves a tiny way and comes back. It pushes the next particle, and the push travels across the room.
5. (match) Match each wrong idea to the evidence against it.  
   **Answer:** Sound is tiny particles thrown from the source → A bell in an airless jar goes quiet; The voice is a flute, and no solid part vibrates → Holding the vocal folds still stops the sound; A note is a different kind of sound from a tick → Fast ticks on a comb become a note; A harder pluck gives a higher note → The note stays the same when you pluck harder. *Why:* Each wrong idea was reasonable until someone tested it. The test in each pair is one that the idea cannot explain.
6. (count) A card crosses 80 teeth in half a second. How many ticks is that each second?  
   **Answer:** 160. *Why:* 80 ticks in half a second means twice as many in a whole second. 80 × 2 is 160 ticks each second.
7. (fill) A wide surface that a string makes vibrate, so that more air is pushed, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['soundboard']. *Why:* A thin string pushes very little air. The soundboard is wide, so it pushes much more, and the instrument is louder.
8. (mcq) In the Spoon on a String, the spoon sounded deeper through the string than through the air. Why?  
   Options: Its slow vibrations hardly push the air / The string slowed the spoon down / Fingers make every sound deeper / The air removed the high notes  
   **Answer:** Its slow vibrations hardly push the air. *Why:* A spoon vibrates in fast ways and slow ways at once. A small spoon pushes the air poorly with its slow vibrations, but they travel well along the string. So the deep part of the ring is heard only through the string.
9. (write) Someone says, 'Heavy things always make low notes.' Give one example that fits and one that does not.  
   **Answer:** It fits a big bell, which rings lower than a small bell of the same metal. It does not fit a heavy string that is pulled very tight. That string can sound higher than a light, slack one. The note depends on two things together: the weight to move and how hard it is pulled back.. *Why:* A strong answer shows that weight is only one of the two things that set the frequency. The other is the strength of the pull back.
10. (write) One strike of a plate 80 cm away made the grains jump. Give two possible reasons, and a check.  
   **Answer:** Someone may have bumped the table, or that strike may have been much harder than the others. To check, strike again several times at 80 cm with nobody touching the table. If the grains stay still, the one jump was an accident.. *Why:* A strong answer treats one odd result as something to test again. It names causes other than the sound, and says how to rule them out.
11. (write) Antoine Ferrein blew air through a voice box and tightened the bands inside it. The note rose. What did that show?  
   **Answer:** It showed that the bands must vibrate for there to be a voice. So the voice does not come only from air in the windpipe, as in a flute. It did not show how the bands move in a living person, which nobody had yet seen.. *Why:* A strong answer says what the test proves, that the bands are the source. It also says what the test leaves open.
12. (write) Two astronauts on the Moon touch helmets, and then they can hear each other speak. Explain how.  
   **Answer:** The Moon has no air, so there are no particles between the astronauts to carry a sound. When the helmets touch, a voice makes the air in one helmet vibrate. That makes the helmet vibrate, and the vibration passes through the solid helmets into the air of the other.. *Why:* A strong answer says that sound needs a medium, and traces the path: air, solid helmet, solid helmet, air.
13. (perspective) Do plants hear?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer notes that a sound is a vibration and that a leaf can be shaken by it. It also says what would count as evidence, such as other teams finding the same result.. *Why:* Plants sense vibrations and respond to them. Whether to call that hearing is argued. A strong answer keeps the finding and the word apart.
14. (draw) Draw air particles carrying one push from a drum skin to an ear. Show where the particles are crowded.  
   **Answer:** A row of dots from the drum to the ear. In one band the dots are crowded together, and on each side of it they are spread out as usual. An arrow shows the crowded band moving towards the ear. Small two-way arrows on single dots show that each particle only moves a little and comes back.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the crowded band travelling while the particles themselves stay in their own places.
15. (order) Put these vibrations in order, from the slowest to the fastest.  
   **Answer:** ['The whole Earth ringing: one vibration in 54 minutes', "A cat's purr: about 25 each second", "A honeybee's wings: about 230 each second", 'A quartz watch crystal: 32,768 each second']. *Why:* Big, heavy things vibrate slowly, and small, stiff things vibrate fast. The Earth is the biggest and the crystal is the smallest and stiffest.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which words describe one full vibration of a string?  
   Options: To one side, across to the other, and back / From one end of the string to the other / One pluck by a finger / Once round in a circle  
   **Answer:** To one side, across to the other, and back. *Why:* One vibration is a full trip: away from the rest position, back through it, out to the other side and back again.
2. (mcq) A string vibrates 440 times each second. What is its frequency?  
   Options: 440 hertz / 440 metres / 440 seconds / 44 hertz  
   **Answer:** 440 hertz. *Why:* Frequency is the number of vibrations each second, and its unit is the hertz. Metres measure length and seconds measure time.
3. (mcq) Why does a plucked string go past its rest position, and not stop there?  
   Options: It is moving fastest there / The air pushes it across / The finger is still pulling it / Strings cannot stop  
   **Answer:** It is moving fastest there. *Why:* The string is pulled back towards its rest position and speeds up all the way. It arrives at full speed, so it carries on to the other side.
4. (mcq) Which change makes a string vibrate faster?  
   Options: Pulling it tighter / Winding wire round it / Making it longer / Plucking it harder  
   **Answer:** Pulling it tighter. *Why:* A tighter string is pulled back harder, so it vibrates faster. Wire and extra length add more to move, which slows it. A harder pluck changes only the loudness.
5. (mcq) A card gives 5 ticks a second on a comb, then 80. What do you hear?  
   Options: Separate ticks, then a low note / A note, then separate ticks / Separate ticks both times / Silence both times  
   **Answer:** Separate ticks, then a low note. *Why:* At 5 a second each tick is heard one by one. At 80 a second they come too fast to hear apart, so the ear hears a note.
6. (mcq) A male cricket draws one wing across a row of teeth on the other. Which test is this like?  
   Options: The card drawn along a comb / The spoon hung on a string / The phone on a sponge / The bottle squeezed with its cap on  
   **Answer:** The card drawn along a comb. *Why:* Each tooth gives one push, as each comb tooth did. The teeth pass so quickly that the pushes blend into a note, which is the cricket's song.
7. (mcq) What vibrates first when someone plays a nadaswaram?  
   Options: A cane reed, then the air column / A stretched skin / A tight string / The player's fingers  
   **Answer:** A cane reed, then the air column. *Why:* The player's breath makes the cane reed flap, and the reed sets the air inside the long pipe vibrating. A nadaswaram has no skin and no string.
8. (truefalse) True or false: your vocal folds can go on vibrating when no breath is flowing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Breath blows the folds apart and they spring shut, again and again. In Sss and Zzz, a hum stopped when the nose and the lips were both shut.
9. (mcq) How does the sound of a struck steel plate reach a skin 10 cm away?  
   Options: Air particles pass the pushes along / Bits of steel fly across / The table carries it / Light carries it  
   **Answer:** Air particles pass the pushes along. *Why:* The plate pushes the air next to it. Each air particle pushes the next, until the pushes reach the skin. In the Thali and the Skin, nothing but air was in between.
10. (mcq) A test changes the kind of string and counts seconds of ringing. Which is the dependent variable?  
   Options: The seconds of ringing / The kind of string / The spoon / The length of the string  
   **Answer:** The seconds of ringing. *Why:* The dependent variable is what you measure. The kind of string is the independent variable, changed on purpose. The spoon and the length are control variables.
11. (mcq) A plucked string slowly goes quiet. Where has the energy of the pluck gone?  
   Options: Into the air as sound, and into warmth / Back into the finger / Nowhere: it was destroyed / Into the string's weight  
   **Answer:** Into the air as sound, and into warmth. *Why:* Each vibration passes a little energy to the air as sound. A little more warms the string and what it touches. Energy is never destroyed.
12. (mcq) Why is a veena string much louder on the veena than stretched between two hands?  
   Options: The wide wooden body pushes more air / The wood makes the string tighter / The wood vibrates faster than the string / Hands make strings heavier  
   **Answer:** The wide wooden body pushes more air. *Why:* A thin string pushes very little air. On the veena, the string makes the wide hollow body vibrate, and that large surface pushes much more air.
13. (mcq) With your eyes shut, you hear a soft rustle from a tree on a windy day. What is most likely making it?  
   Options: Many leaves knocking and rubbing against each other in the wind / A note sung by the trunk of the tree / A bird with two vocal folds, singing from inside the leaves / A tabla skin under the branches  
   **Answer:** Many leaves knocking and rubbing against each other in the wind. *Why:* Wind moves the leaves, and they knock and rub against each other. A rustle has no clear note. Trees do not sing, and birds sing with the syrinx, not with vocal folds.

## Applications
- Makers of veenas, violins and guitars choose the wood, the thickness and the shape of the body with care. A good body vibrates well and pushes the air strongly.
- A loudspeaker is a skin, called a cone, pushed back and forth by a magnet. A microphone is a skin that sound pushes, joined to something that turns the movement into an electric signal.
- A quartz watch keeps time by counting the vibrations of a tiny crystal, 32,768 each second.
- Engineers put rubber pads under engines, washing machines and mixer-grinders. Then less vibration passes into the floor and the walls.
- Factory engineers listen to machines with vibration meters. A worn bearing changes the vibration long before the machine breaks.
- A doctor's ultrasound scanner sends vibrations too fast to hear into the body, and builds a picture from the echoes.
- A phone buzzes because a tiny motor spins a weight that is off its centre. Each turn gives the phone one push.

## Spotted around us
- A temple bell after it is rung. Rest a fingertip on it, and you feel the buzz as the sound fades.
- A mridangam player at a concert, pressing a hand on the skin to stop a note short.
- Crickets at dusk. Each singer is a male, drawing one wing across a row of tiny teeth on the other.
- The whine of a mosquito near your ear. It is the beat of her wings, several hundred times a second.
- The side mirror of an auto-rickshaw while the engine idles. The picture in the mirror turns to a blur.
- A kite string humming in the wind at Uttarayan or at Pongal.
- The nadaswaram and the thavil at a wedding: one is an air column, the other has two skins.
- Steel tumblers that rattle on the kitchen counter when the mixer-grinder runs.

## Open questions
- **How does the brain turn a row of pushes into the feeling of one note?** Scientists know which parts of the inner ear and the brain respond to each frequency. They still argue about how timing and place are combined into the note we hear.
- **Why do cats purr, and how do they keep the purr so low?** A 2023 study found pads inside cats' vocal folds that may slow the vibration. How a living cat controls the purr, and all that a purr is for, are still not settled.
- **Do plants make sounds for anyone to hear?** In 2023 a team recorded dry and cut plants giving off clicks too high for humans to hear. Nobody knows yet whether insects, other animals or other plants listen to them.
- **Is everything, at the smallest scale, a vibration?** String theory says so, but no experiment has been able to test it. It may stay untested for a long time.
- **What is it like to hear a sound too low or too high for humans?** We can measure what an elephant's or a bat's ears respond to. We have no way yet to know what the hearing is like for them.

## Connected maths
- In the Comb Test, a card crosses 40 teeth in 2 seconds, and then in half a second. How many ticks each second is each stroke? **20 ticks each second, and then 80 ticks each second.**. 40 ticks shared over 2 seconds is 40 ÷ 2 = 20 each second. In half a second there are 40 ticks, so a whole second would have twice as many: 40 × 2 = 80. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- In the Comb Test, the strokes gave 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 ticks each second. What is the pattern? What stroke time would give the next number? **Each number is double the one before. The next is 160 ticks each second, from a stroke of a quarter of a second.**. 5 × 2 = 10, 10 × 2 = 20, and so on, so the next is 80 × 2 = 160. The stroke times halve each time: 8, 4, 2, 1, a half, and then a quarter of a second. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A mosquito's wings beat about 500 times each second. A honeybee's beat about 230 times. Whose hum is higher? How many times do the mosquito's wings beat in one minute? **The mosquito's hum is higher. Her wings beat about 30,000 times in one minute.**. More pushes each second give a higher note, and 500 is more than 230. One minute is 60 seconds, and 500 × 60 = 30,000. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- In the Spoon on a String, the ring lasted 7, 8 and 9 seconds through the string. Through the air it lasted 1, 2 and 1 seconds. About how many times as long did it last through the string? **About 6 times as long.**. The string total is 7 + 8 + 9 = 24 seconds. The air total is 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 seconds. 24 ÷ 4 = 6. Both totals are for three taps, so they can be compared directly. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the Thali and the Skin, the total scores were 6 at 10 cm and 5 at 20 cm. They were 2 at 40 cm and 0 at 80 cm. A friend then gets 4 at 80 cm. Is that likely to be right? Explain. **It is not likely. It breaks the pattern, so it should be tested again.**. The scores fall as the distance grows: 6, 5, 2, 0. A score of 4 at 80 cm is higher than the score at 40 cm, which does not fit. A bumped table or a much harder strike could explain it. Repeating the strikes at 80 cm will show. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- A young person hears from about 20 hertz up to about 20,000 hertz. How many times as high is the top of that range as the bottom? **1,000 times as high.**. 20,000 ÷ 20 = 1,000. The fastest vibration a young ear can hear is a thousand times as fast as the slowest. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
