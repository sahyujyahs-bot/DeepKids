# Stargazing: the Moon, bright stars and planets, and why the Moon seems to follow you
`sci.sky.stargazing.L1`

**Summary.** Ride in a bus at night, and the Moon seems to come along with you. The trees rush past, but the Moon keeps up. In this unit your child walks past a window and finds out why: the Moon is so far away that a few steps hardly change where it is. Your child also learns to tell the Moon, stars, planets and aeroplanes apart. Stars are faraway suns that twinkle, because moving air shakes their light. Planets shine with sunlight, usually without twinkling.

**Intro.** *Have you ever looked at the Moon from a moving bus, car or auto? What did the Moon do?* Most children say the Moon followed them. The trees and lamp posts rushed past, but the Moon stayed beside the window. Is the Moon really following you? In this unit you test it at your own window. You also find out which bright dots in the night sky are stars, which are planets, and which are aeroplanes.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can find the Moon and bright stars in the night sky, and tell them from an aeroplane.
1. By the end I can show that the Moon seems to follow me because it is very far away.
2. By the end I can say that the Moon and the planets shine with light from the Sun, and that stars make their own light.
3. By the end I can show, with the hot tawa test, that moving air makes a small light twinkle.
4. By the end I can say that stars usually twinkle and planets usually shine steadily, and that planets wander slowly among the stars.

## Terms
- **Moon**: The Moon is a big ball of rock that goes round the Earth. It makes no light of its own. It shines because sunlight falls on it. *e.g.* The Moon takes about a month to go once round the Earth.
- **Star**: A star is a huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. The Sun is our nearest star. *e.g.* The stars at night are suns too. They look tiny because they are very, very far away.
- **Planet**: A planet is a big ball that goes round the Sun. The Earth is a planet. Other planets shine in our sky with the Sun's light. *e.g.* Venus is a planet. It is the brightest planet in our sky, brighter than any star.
- **Twinkle**: A star twinkles when its light seems to flicker and shiver. The moving air above us wobbles its light. *e.g.* Stars low down near the rooftops twinkle the most. Planets usually shine more steadily.

## Explanations
### The Moon seems to follow you because it is very far away · *defined*
**Definition:** When you move, near things seem to slide past you, and far things hardly seem to move. The Moon is so far away that it seems to stay in the same place, so it looks as if it comes along with you.

In the window strip test, the paper strips slid past the Moon when you walked. A far building slid a little, and the Moon stayed in the same place. The Moon is about 3,84,000 kilometres away, so a few steps make almost no difference to where you see it.
- e.g. In the window strip test, the paper strips slid past, and the Moon stayed put.
- e.g. From a moving train, the poles by the track flash past, far hills move slowly, and the Moon seems to keep up.
- e.g. In the tumbler test, the near tumbler jumped across the far wall when you took two steps.
### The Moon and the planets shine with sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon and the planets make no light of their own. We see them because sunlight falls on them and bounces off into our eyes.

The Moon is a ball of grey rock. At night the Sun is shining on the other side of the Earth, but its light still reaches the Moon and bounces off it to us. Venus, Jupiter, Mars and Saturn shine in the same way.
- e.g. The bright side of the Moon always faces the Sun.
- e.g. Venus shines brightly just after sunset, lit by the Sun that has just gone down.
- e.g. A steel plate in a dark room cannot be seen, but shines when a torch falls on it. The Moon is like the plate, and the Sun is like the torch.
### The stars are faraway suns, in different colours · *defined*
**Definition:** Every star is a sun, a huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. Stars look like tiny dots because they are very far away.

The Sun is our nearest star. The other stars are so far away that their light takes years to reach us. Bright stars have colours: reddish stars are the coolest, and bluish white stars are the hottest.
- e.g. Thiruvathirai, also called Ardra, is a bright reddish orange star in the winter evening sky.
- e.g. Sirius, the brightest star at night, looks bluish white and often twinkles in many colours when low.
- e.g. A gas flame is blue where it is hottest, and a dying coal glows red.
### Stars twinkle, and planets usually shine steadily · *defined*
**Definition:** Starlight flickers because the moving air above us bends it this way and that. Planets usually shine more steadily.

Starlight passes through a thick blanket of moving air before it reaches us, and the air wobbles it. A star is so far away that its light is one tiny point, so the wobble makes it flicker. A planet shows a tiny round patch, so the wobbles mostly cancel out. Planets also wander slowly among the stars from week to week.
- e.g. Stars low down near the rooftops twinkle more, because their light passes through more air.
- e.g. Jupiter usually shines steadily, like a calm lamp.
- e.g. Astronauts in space see the stars shine steadily, with no twinkle.

## Mostly agreed
- The Moon makes no light of its own. It shines with sunlight. *How we know:* The bright side of the Moon always faces the Sun. Spacecraft such as India's Chandrayaan have photographed the Moon up close: grey rock and dust, with no fire or lamp.
- Stars are suns, very far away. *How we know:* The light from stars, split into its colours, looks like the Sun's light. Astronomers have measured how far away many stars are. Even the nearest one after the Sun is so far that its light takes over four years to reach us.
- Planets move slowly among the stars. *How we know:* Anyone can watch a planet for a few weeks and see it change its place among the stars. People in India, China, Babylon and Greece recorded this thousands of years ago. The Greek word planet means wanderer.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, a Greek thinker; Isaac Newton, an English scientist, About 350 BCE to 1704.** About 2,300 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle noticed something about the night sky. The stars twinkle, but the planets mostly shine steadily. He thought he knew why. The planets are nearer, he said. The stars are very far, so our sight gets tired and trembles on the long way to them, and that makes them seem to twinkle. He was right that the planets are much nearer than the stars. But the trembling is not in our sight. In 1704, the English scientist Isaac Newton wrote that twinkling comes from the air. The air above us is always moving, and it shakes the light passing through it. He said telescopes would work best on the tops of high mountains, where the air is calm. Today India has a telescope at Hanle in Ladakh, about 4,500 metres up. And telescopes sent into space, above all the air, see stars that do not twinkle at all.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle thought stars twinkle because our sight trembles on its long trip to them. Newton wrote that the trembling is in the moving air, and telescopes in space, above the air, see stars shine without twinkling.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Moon follows me wherever I go.** (A common idea among young children, and a feeling many grown-ups still have; *rejected*)  
  For: From a moving bus or car, the Moon really does seem to keep up with the window.  
  Against: In the window strip test, near things slid past and the Moon stayed put. Someone in another city sees the same Moon at the same moment (try the One Moon, Two Windows project). The Moon is so far that it seems to keep up with everyone at once.
- **Stars twinkle because they blink on and off.** (A common idea among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: A twinkling star really does seem to flash and flicker.  
  Against: In the hot tawa test, the torch made a steady light, but it shivered when seen through moving warm air. Stars shine steadily too. Astronauts above the air see them without any twinkle.
- **Every bright dot in the night sky is a star.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Venus, Jupiter and an aeroplane can all look like bright stars.  
  Against: Some bright dots are planets that shine with sunlight and hardly twinkle. Some move steadily and blink red and green: those are aeroplanes.

## Roots
We have walked past a window and watched the Moon stay put while the paper strips slid by. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** When you walked past the window, the paper strips slid past, but the Moon stayed put. Near things change place when you move. The Moon is so far that a few steps hardly change where you see it. So the Moon seems to follow everyone who looks at it, all at once.  
*Habit it breaks:* We feel that a thing which keeps up with us must be near us, or following us. The window test shows the opposite: the Moon keeps up because it is so very far away.  
*Evidence:* In the window strip test, the near strips slid past fast, a far building slid a little, and the Moon did not move at all. In the One Moon, Two Windows project, someone in another town draws the same Moon at the same time.

- *who am i:* You are one of millions of people looking up at the same Moon tonight. Where you stand changes what is near you, but not the Moon.
- *what is life:* Many living beings use the night sky. A dung beetle rolls straight by the Milky Way, birds fly by the stars at night, and you find the Moon from your window.
- *what is this world:* The world is much bigger than it looks. The Moon seems to sit just above the rooftops, but it is much farther than any building or hill.

There is one Moon. Tonight it shines in the window of every home that faces it, from Kochi to Kohima. Each child sees it from their own place, and each thinks it came along with them. Everything is aware in its own way, the Moon too. Many scientists disagree: they say only living beings with nerves are aware.

- **Brahmabindu Upanishad 12:** There is one Self in all beings. It is seen as one and as many, like the one Moon reflected in many pots of water. (The Upanishad uses the one Moon seen in many places as a picture of one Self in many beings. Your test showed why one Moon can be seen from so many places at once: it is very far away. The teaching is a picture, not a proof.)
- **Su Shi, 'Shui Diao Ge Tou' (China, Mid-Autumn night, 1076):** The poet Su Shi missed his brother, who lived far away. He wished that they could both live long, and share the beauty of the same Moon, though a thousand miles apart. (Su Shi knew that two people far apart see the same Moon at the same time, as you do when you phone someone in another town.)
- Science (settled): Near things seem to shift more than far things when you move. Astronomers call this parallax, and they use it to measure how far away the nearer stars are.
- Science (settled): The Moon is about 3,84,000 kilometres away. Its light reaches us in just over one second.
- Other view, Scientists who study how we see: They say each person's eyes and brain build their own picture of the Moon. So in one way there are as many Moons as there are people looking, even though there is one Moon in the sky.
- Practice, One Moon, many windows (3 min): Stand at the window and find the Moon. If it is not up, look at the brightest star. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out slowly, five times. Now think of someone far away. They might be looking at the same Moon right now. Send them a kind thought.
- Ask: The Moon came along with you, and with someone far away, at the same time. Whose Moon is it?
- Ask: The Moon's light started at the Sun. When you see the Moon, are you also seeing the Sun?
- Ask: If nobody in the whole world looked at the Moon tonight, would it still be shining?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When you walked past the window, the strips slid and the Moon stayed. How could you use this to tell which of two lights is further away? (hint: Walk a few steps and watch which light slides more against the sky.)
- *deep:* In the hot tawa test, the light never changed, but it looked as if it did. When a star twinkles, is the star changing, or is it the air, or is it you? (hint: Think about what changed when the stove went off.)
- *big:* A dung beetle, some birds and people all use the night sky to find their way. What do you think the sky looks like to them? (hint: Each animal has different eyes. A beetle may see the glow of the Milky Way as one bright stripe.)
- *deep:* A planet moves slowly among the stars from week to week. How could you check that a bright dot is a planet, with no gadget? (hint: Mark where the dot is next to a rooftop or a star, and look again after a week.)

## Story: The Moon on the Night Bus
*Nila is a Tamil word for the Moon. Paati means grandmother in Tamil. Nila, Paati, Appa and Meher are made up. Guwahati is in Assam, far away in the northeast of India, but its sky has the same Moon.*

Nila was six. She lived in a flat in Chennai with her Appa and her grandmother, Paati.

One night they were coming home on the bus from a wedding in Tambaram. Nila had the window seat. She pressed her nose to the glass.

Outside, everything was rushing past. Street lamps went whoosh, whoosh, whoosh. A tea shop flashed by, then a tall tree, then a petrol pump with bright lights.

But up above the rooftops, there was the Moon. She was half round, like a broken appalam, and she was not rushing at all. She kept pace with the bus, right beside Nila's window.

"Paati," whispered Nila. "The Moon is following us."

Paati leaned over and looked. "Is she? Keep watching. Tell me what she does when the bus turns."

The bus turned left at a big junction. The Moon slid round to the other window. The bus turned again. The Moon came back. When the bus stopped at a signal, the Moon stopped too.

"She waits for us!" said Nila. "She must like us."

Paati smiled. "Maybe she likes everybody," she said. "Tomorrow, let's find out."

The next evening, Paati called Nila to the window of their flat. She had stuck three strips of paper on the glass, a hand-width apart. She had put one slipper on the floor at each end of the window.

"Stand at the first slipper," said Paati. "Close one eye. Where is the Moon?"

"Just next to the first strip," said Nila.

"Now walk slowly to the other slipper. Keep watching."

Nila walked. The strips slid across the Moon, one, two, three. The water tank on the next building slid a little. But the Moon stayed exactly where she was, high over the rooftops.

"The strips moved and the Moon didn't!" said Nila. "But the strips are stuck on the glass. They can't move."

"That's right," said Paati. "Only you moved. Near things look as if they slide past when you move. Far things hardly change. The Moon is very, very far away. If we could drive there without stopping, it would take more than six months."

Just then Paati's phone rang. It was Nila's cousin Meher, calling from Guwahati, a long, long way away.

"Meher," said Paati, "go to your window. Can you see the Moon?"

Meher ran to look. She held up the phone. There was a dark sky, a bamboo pole, and a half Moon, exactly like Nila's.

"It's the same Moon!" said Nila. "She's at your window and at my window, at the same time!"

"She is," said Paati. "She is not following you, and not following Meher. She is so far away that she looks the same to everyone. So everyone thinks she came along with them."

Nila thought about it. Then she frowned. "But she was so bright last night. Is she a big lamp?"

"The Moon is made of grey rock," said Paati. "She has no light of her own. The Sun shines on her, even at night, from the other side of the Earth. We see the sunlight bouncing off her."

Later, Appa came home from work. He found Nila waiting at the door.

"Appa, come to the window," she said. "I'll show you why the Moon follows the bus."

She made him stand at the first slipper and close one eye. She made him walk slowly to the other slipper.

"The strips slide," said Appa, surprised. "The Moon stays."

"Because she's very, very far," said Nila. "So she goes with everyone at once. Even with Meher in Guwahati."

Appa looked at the Moon for a long time. "When I was small, in my village, I thought she followed only me," he said.

"She seemed to," said Nila. "And she seemed to follow everyone else too."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Nila finds that the paper strips on the glass slide past the Moon when she walks, while the Moon stays where she is. Near things change place when we move, and the Moon is so far away that it hardly changes at all. So the Moon seems to follow everyone at once, as she sees when her cousin in Guwahati shows the same Moon on the phone. Nila then teaches her Appa.

- Ask: Why did the Moon seem to follow the bus, while the street lamps rushed past?
- Ask: What did the paper strips do when Nila walked past the window? What did the Moon do?
- Ask: How could Nila and Meher both see the Moon at their own windows at the same time?
- Ask: Where does the Moon's light come from?

### g1 · Moon, Star, Planet or Plane? A sorting card game (10 min)
- **rules:** Mix the 16 cards and put them in a pile, face down.; Lay four papers on the floor: MOON, STAR, PLANET and OTHER. Draw a Moon, a twinkling star, a steady dot and an aeroplane on them.; Players take turns. On your turn, a grown-up reads the front of the top card.; Put the card on the paper you think is right, and say why.; Turn the card over and read the back. If the paper is right, keep the card. If not, put it to one side.; When the pile is finished, count your cards. With two or more children, the most cards wins, and a tie is a shared win. With one child, the goal is 12 cards or more.
- **Printable cards: 16 sky cards:** A big round light that changes shape over the month (back: MOON paper. It shines with sunlight.); A dot that twinkles and shivers (back: STAR paper. Stars make their own light.); A very bright dot, steady, low in the west just after sunset (back: PLANET paper. Probably Venus, shining with sunlight.); A light that moves across the sky and blinks red and green (back: OTHER paper. It is an aeroplane, with its own lamps.); A thin curved shape like a fingernail, just after sunset (back: MOON paper. A thin crescent Moon.); A bright steady dot that slowly changes its place among the stars over many weeks (back: PLANET paper. Planets wander among the stars.); Seven stars in the shape of a ladle (back: STAR paper. These stars are the Saptarishi.); The biggest, brightest light, seen only in the day (back: STAR paper. The Sun is our nearest star. Never look straight at it.); A pale, rocky ball you can sometimes see in the daytime sky (back: MOON paper. The Moon is often up in the day too.); A steady dot that glides smoothly across the sky in a few minutes, with no blinking (back: OTHER paper. It is the space station, with people living in it. It shines with sunlight, like a planet.); A tiny dot that looks reddish and steady (back: PLANET paper. It may be Mars.); A streak of light that flashes across the sky in one second (back: OTHER paper. A shooting star is really a tiny grain of space dust burning up in the air. It is not a star.); A bright dot that twinkles in many colours, very low near the rooftops (back: STAR paper. Low stars twinkle the most.); A round, lit face with grey patches on it (back: MOON paper. You can see these grey patches with your eyes alone.); A very bright, steady dot high up at midnight (back: PLANET paper. Maybe Jupiter, Saturn or Mars.   *Parent does:* Lay out the four papers. Read the rules together.  
  *Parent says:* Each card tells you about a light in the sky. You say if it is the Moon, a star, a planet or something else.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the card. Is it the Moon, a star, a planet, or other?
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play through all 16 cards, taking turns. Read each front, wait for the choice and reason, then read the back.  
  *Parent says:* A dot that twinkles and shivers. Moon, star, planet or other? Why? Let's turn it over.  
  *Child mode:* Listen. Which paper? Why? Turn it over and check.
  - Note: The tricky cards are the steady dots, the space station and the shooting star. Ask: does it twinkle? Does it move? Does it make its own light?
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the red tower lamp card and read it again.  
  *Parent says:* A red light blinks on a tall tower, but it never moves. Is it a star in the sky?  
  *Child mode:* A red light blinks on a tower and never moves. Is it a star?
  - Check: A red light blinks on top of a tall tower and never moves. Is it a star? → **No, it is a lamp on the tower**
### g2 · Twinkle, Steady, Fly! A moving game (8 min)
- **rules:** One player is the caller. The caller holds the call cards.; At the start, the caller reads the back of each card aloud once, so everyone knows the moves.; The caller reads the front of a card out loud. Everyone else does the move, as fast as they can.; Anyone who does the wrong move, such as twinkling for PLANET, becomes the next caller. The caller also changes after every four calls.; If the cards run out, shuffle them and go on. Play until everyone has been the caller once. There is no winner: it is a game to move and laugh.

  - Ask: What does the Moon player standing still remind you of? (good: The Moon seems to stay in the same place when we move.; The Moon looks the same to everyone.)

## Experiments
### e1 · Does the Moon Follow You? The window strip test (22 min)
- **question:** When you walk, do near things and the faraway Moon seem to move in the same way?
- **predict:** When you walk past the window, will the Moon slide away like the paper strips, or stay with you? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We walk the same path each time, between two slippers. We keep our head at the same height, and close the same eye, so only our steps change what we see.
- **safety:** On a balcony, stay well back from the railing. Never climb on a chair or a ledge to see the Moon. A grown-up stays beside the child.
- **record:** Draw the window, the three strips and the Moon. Draw an arrow on each thing that slid. A grown-up writes 'near' or 'far' beside each one.
- **results table:** What I watched | Did it slide when I walked? | Near or far?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a steel tumbler on a stool in the middle of a room. 2. Your child stands three big steps away. 3. The tumbler should be in front of something on the far wall, such as a calendar.  
  *Parent says:* Close one eye. Is the tumbler in front of the calendar? Now take two big steps to the side. Where is the tumbler now?  
  *Child mode:* Close one eye. Look at the tumbler and the calendar. Take two big steps sideways. Look again.
  - Ask: What seemed to move? (good: The tumbler moved away from the calendar.; The near tumbler slid, and the far wall stayed.)
  - Note: Nothing moved except your child. The near tumbler seems to jump across the far wall. Don't explain yet. Let your child notice it and say it in their own words.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child about tonight's test. Ask for a guess and a reason. A grown-up writes both down in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Tonight we will walk past the window and watch the Moon. Will the Moon slide away, like the tumbler? Or will it come with you? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Will the Moon slide away, or come with you? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What will the Moon do, and why? (good: It will come with me, because it follows our car.; It will slide, like the tumbler did.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Most children of six say the Moon will come with them. Don't say what will happen.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After dark, find a window or balcony where you can see the Moon. 2. Stick three paper strips on the glass, a hand-width apart, near the Moon. 3. Put a slipper at each end of a three-step path.  
  *Parent says:* Stand at this slipper. Close one eye. Is the Moon to the left or the right of the first strip?  
  *Child mode:* Stand at the slipper. Close one eye. Find the Moon. Where is it next to the strips?
  - Note: Pick an evening when the Moon is up (see the first activity). A calendar that shows Purnima helps. If you cannot see the Moon, use the brightest dot in the sky. It works the same way. On a balcony, you can watch the grill bars instead of paper strips.
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child walks slowly to the other slipper, one eye closed, watching the Moon. 2. Then they walk back. 3. Ask them to watch a far building or water tank too.  
  *Parent says:* Walk slowly to the other slipper. Keep watching the Moon. What do the strips do? What does the Moon do? What does that far building do?  
  *Child mode:* Walk slowly. Watch the Moon and the strips. What slides? What stays?
  - Ask: What happened? (good: The strips slid past the Moon.; The far building slid a little.; The Moon stayed in the same place.)
  - Note: Look for three things. The strips slide past fast, because they are near. A far building slides a little. The Moon does not seem to move at all. If the strips seem to jump about, your child may be opening both eyes. Try again with one eye covered by a hand.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they thought, what they saw, and why. 2. Then explain, using what they saw.  
  *Parent says:* Was your guess right? What did the strips do? Why do you think the Moon stayed? Near things slide past when we move. The Moon is so far away that our steps make no difference.  
  *Child mode:* Was your guess right? Near things slide past when you move. The Moon is very far. So it stays.
  - Note: Now link it to the bus. From a bus, the lamp posts are near, so they rush past. The Moon is so far that the bus ride hardly changes where it is. So it seems to come along.
  - Check: When you walked past the window, what slid past the most? → **The paper strips on the glass**
### e2 · Why Do Stars Twinkle? The hot tawa test (14 min)
- **question:** Does a small, steady light shiver when we look at it through moving warm air?
- **predict:** We will look at a small light across a hot tawa. Will the light stay steady, or will it shiver? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We look at the same small light, from the same place, with our eyes at the height of the tawa. We look once with the tawa cold and once with it hot, so only the air above it changes.
- **safety:** Only a grown-up lights the stove and handles the tawa. The child stands back, at least two big steps from the stove, and never touches the tawa. Keep dupattas, sleeves and paper away from the flame. Switch the stove off as soon as the test is done.
- **record:** Draw the small light with straight lines when the tawa was cold, and with wiggly lines when it was hot.
- **results table:** When I looked | Did the small light stay steady or shiver?
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the phone on the steel box, a forearm's length behind the tawa, with its torch on. 2. Your child stands two big steps back. 3. They crouch so their eyes are at tawa height.  
  *Parent says:* Look across the tawa at the little light. Is it steady, or does it shiver?  
  *Child mode:* Crouch down. Look across the tawa at the little light. Is it steady?
  - Note: The tawa is still cold. The light should look steady. Point the torch at the ceiling or the wall, not into your child's eyes.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason before you heat the tawa. A grown-up writes it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* I will heat the tawa. The light will stay just the same. Will it still look steady, or will it shiver? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The tawa will get hot. Will the light shiver? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What will happen, and why? (good: It will stay steady, because the light is the same.; It will shiver, because of the heat.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Don't say what will happen.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Heat the tawa on a medium flame for about two minutes, as you would before making dosa. 2. Your child looks across it again, from the same place.  
  *Parent says:* Look across the hot tawa at the little light. What is the light doing now?  
  *Child mode:* Stay back. Look across the hot tawa. What does the light do?
  - Ask: What do you see? (good: The light is shivering.; It is wobbling and dancing.; The things behind the tawa are wobbly.)
  - Note: Warm air rises from the tawa in moving swirls. Light bends a little as it passes through warm and cool air, so the small light seems to shiver and dance. The shiver is small and quick, but you can see it. If you cannot, heat the tawa a minute longer and crouch lower, so your eyes are level with the tawa.
- **s4 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off the stove. 2. Look again after one or two minutes, while the tawa cools. Do not touch it.  
  *Parent says:* The stove is off now. Look again. Is the light still shivering?  
  *Child mode:* The stove is off. Look again. Is the light still shivering?
  - Note: As the tawa cools, the shivering gets weaker. A hot tawa stays hot for a long time, so keep everyone's hands away.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what your child thought, what they saw, and why. Then link it to the stars.  
  *Parent says:* Did the light change? Or did the air change? The air above us moves all the time, like the air over the tawa. It makes starlight shiver. That is twinkling.  
  *Child mode:* The light stayed the same. The moving warm air made it shiver. Moving air makes stars twinkle too.
  - Check: In the hot tawa test, what made the little light shiver? → **The moving warm air above the tawa**

## Worksheets
### Moon, Stars and Planets (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (sort) A grown-up reads each one. Does it make its own light, or shine with sunlight? Colour the ones that make their own light yellow.  
   **Answer:** The Sun → Makes its own light; A twinkling star → Makes its own light; The Moon → Shines with sunlight; Venus → Shines with sunlight; Jupiter → Shines with sunlight. *Why:* The Sun and the other stars make their own light. The Moon and the planets, such as Venus and Jupiter, only shine because sunlight falls on them.
2. (mcq) Divya, a girl in Class 1, walks past her window and watches the Moon and the paper strips on the glass. What slides past?  
   Options: The paper strips / The Moon / Both, just the same  
   **Answer:** The paper strips. *Why:* The strips are near, so they slide past when she walks. The Moon is very far, so it stays in the same place.
3. (truefalse) Stars twinkle because they blink on and off by themselves.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A steady light shivers when it shines through moving warm air, as the hot tawa test shows. The moving air above us makes starlight shiver in the same way.
4. (draw) Draw the Moon as you saw it from your window. Shade in its dark patches.  
   **Answer:** The Moon's shape that night, with grey patches shaded in.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the shape the child really saw, with the dark patches shaded.
5. (match) Draw a line from what you see to what it is.  
   **Answer:** A dot that twinkles → A star; A bright dot that shines steadily → A planet; A light that moves and blinks red and green → An aeroplane; A big round light with grey patches → The Moon. *Why:* Stars usually twinkle. Planets usually shine steadily. Aeroplanes move and blink. The Moon is big and round, with grey patches of rock.
6. (draw) Draw the window, one paper strip on the glass, and the Moon. Then draw where the strip looks to be after you walk to the other slipper. Draw an arrow for the way it slid.  
   **Answer:** The strip drawn in two places with an arrow between them, and the Moon in the same place both times.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the near strip changing place and the far Moon staying where it is.
7. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: a train rushes past. The poles by the track, the far hills and the Moon are all outside the window. Which one seems to move fastest, and which hardly moves? Why?  
   **Answer:** The poles seem to rush past, the far hills move slowly, and the Moon hardly moves. Near things change place the most, and the Moon is the farthest.. *Why:* A strong answer puts the poles, hills and Moon in order of how near they are, and says near things seem to move more.
8. (perspective) Three people are looking at the Moon. They have different ideas about whether the Moon is a light.  
   **Answer:** Any choice with a good reason. Farhana is right that moonlight helps us see. Meenakshi is right that the Moon makes no light of its own. Ammamma joins both ideas.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason, and fairly says what is good about another view. Moonlight is real light that helps us see, and it all started at the Sun.
9. (label) Look at this night sky. A grown-up points to each thing. Say what it is, and the grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** ['the Moon', 'a star', 'a planet', 'an aeroplane']. *Why:* The big round light is the Moon. A twinkling dot is a star. A steady bright dot is probably a planet. A light that moves and blinks is an aeroplane.
10. (count) Look at this night sky picture. Count the dots that twinkle. Then count the dots that shine steadily.  
   **Answer:** 5 twinkle and 2 are steady. The steady ones are probably planets.. *Why:* Stars usually twinkle, and planets usually shine steadily. So the 2 steady dots are probably planets.
### Moon, Stars and Planets (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (order) Number these from 1 to 4, in the order you see them on a clear evening.  
   **Answer:** ['The Sun is still up', 'The Sun goes down', 'The first bright dot appears, often a planet', 'Many stars appear']. *Why:* As the sky darkens after sunset, the brightest lights show first. Venus or Jupiter often appears before any star. The fainter stars appear when the sky is darker.
2. (mcq) Nila, a girl in Class 1, walks past her window, and the paper strips slide past the Moon. Then she walks across the road to a far building and looks again. Where is the Moon?  
   Options: Still in the same place in the sky / Left behind at the window / Much bigger  
   **Answer:** Still in the same place in the sky. *Why:* The Moon is so far that walking to the next building hardly changes where it is. That is why it seems to follow you everywhere.
3. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: there is almost no air on the Moon. If you stood there, would the stars twinkle? Why?  
   **Answer:** No. Twinkling comes from moving air, and the Moon has almost no air, so the stars would shine steadily.. *Why:* A strong answer says twinkling comes from moving air, and so a place with almost no air has no twinkling.
4. (draw) Draw the Sun, the Moon and you. Draw arrows to show how sunlight travels until you see the Moon.  
   **Answer:** An arrow from the Sun to the Moon, and an arrow from the Moon to the child's eyes.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the light starting at the Sun, landing on the Moon and bouncing to the child.
5. (write) Your friend says, 'The stars are tiny, as small as glitter.' Say out loud what you would tell her, and a grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** The stars only look tiny because they are very far away. Each one is a sun, and some are much bigger than our Sun.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the stars look small because they are far, and that they are really suns.
6. (mcq) In the hot tawa test, the stove was switched off and the tawa cooled. What happened to the shivering?  
   Options: It got weaker / It got stronger / The torch went off  
   **Answer:** It got weaker. *Why:* As the tawa cooled, less warm air rose from it, so the light shivered less. Without moving warm air, the light looks steady.
7. (odd) Three of these usually twinkle. Which one usually does not? Circle it.  
   Options: A star low over the rooftops / A star high overhead / Venus / A bright star such as Sirius  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Venus is a planet and usually shines steadily. Stars twinkle because moving air shakes their light.

9. (write) A friend says, 'Every bright dot in the night sky is a star.' Say out loud what you would tell her, and a grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** Some bright dots are planets, and they shine steadily. Some are aeroplanes that move and blink red and green. Stars twinkle.. *Why:* A strong answer names at least one other kind of bright dot and says how to tell it from a star.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) From a moving bus, the street lamps rush past. What does the Moon seem to do?  
   Options: Come along with the bus / Rush past like the lamps / Go behind the bus  
   **Answer:** Come along with the bus. *Why:* The Moon is so far away that the bus ride hardly changes where you see it, so it seems to come along.
2. (mcq) You take two steps sideways. A steel tumbler on a stool seems to jump across the far wall. Why?  
   Options: The tumbler is near you, so a few steps change where you see it / The tumbler jumped by itself / The wall moved  
   **Answer:** The tumbler is near you, so a few steps change where you see it. *Why:* Near things seem to move a lot when you move, and far things hardly move at all. Only you moved.
3. (mcq) Two cousins, Nila in Chennai and Meher in Guwahati, look up at the same time. What do they see?  
   Options: The same Moon / Two different Moons / Only Meher can see the Moon  
   **Answer:** The same Moon. *Why:* There is one Moon. It is so far away that people all over India see her at the same time.
4. (mcq) Where does the Moon's light come from?  
   Options: The Sun / A fire inside the Moon / The stars  
   **Answer:** The Sun. *Why:* The Moon is grey rock with no light of its own. Sunlight falls on it and bounces to us.
5. (mcq) Which of these makes its own light?  
   Options: A star / Venus / The Moon  
   **Answer:** A star. *Why:* A star is a sun. Venus is a planet, and Venus and the Moon shine only with sunlight.
6. (mcq) Why do stars close to the rooftops twinkle more than stars high up?  
   Options: Their light goes through more air / They are nearer to us / They are hotter  
   **Answer:** Their light goes through more air. *Why:* The light of a low star passes through more of the moving air before it reaches us, so it shivers more.
7. (mcq) Where would a star look steady, with no twinkle at all?  
   Options: From a spacecraft above the air / From a balcony on a windy night / From a bus window  
   **Answer:** From a spacecraft above the air. *Why:* Twinkling comes from the moving air. Above the air, in space, stars shine steadily.
8. (mcq) Kavya, a girl in Class 1, sees a bright dot that does not twinkle at all. What could it be?  
   Options: A planet / A star / A shooting star  
   **Answer:** A planet. *Why:* Stars usually twinkle. Planets usually shine steadily, because their light comes from a tiny round patch, not a single point.
9. (mcq) Why do stars twinkle?  
   Options: Moving air wobbles their light / They switch on and off / They are spinning fast  
   **Answer:** Moving air wobbles their light. *Why:* The air above us is always moving. It bends starlight this way and that, so the star seems to flicker.
10. (mcq) A light moves across the night sky and blinks red and green. What is it?  
   Options: An aeroplane / A planet / The Moon  
   **Answer:** An aeroplane. *Why:* Aeroplanes have blinking red and green lamps, and they move across the sky quickly. Planets and the Moon don't blink.

## Applications
- Sailors and fishing families on the coasts of India have used the stars and the Moon to find their way and to know the tides.
- Many Indian calendars count months by the Moon, so festivals like Diwali and Eid are set by its shape.
- Astronomers use the 'near things slide more' rule, called parallax, to measure how far away stars are.
- Pilots and sailors learn to tell planets, stars and aircraft lights apart at night.
- Dark sky places such as Hanle in Ladakh keep lights low so that telescopes and people can see the stars.

## Spotted around us
- On every full Moon night, thousands of people walk round the Arunachala hill at Tiruvannamalai, in Tamil Nadu, by moonlight.
- Tamil children sing 'Nila nila odi vaa', asking the Moon to come running. In Kerala, the Moon is called Ambili Ammavan, Uncle Moon.
- Telugu families sing 'Chandamama raave' to little children, calling the Moon to come and play.
- On Kojagiri Purnima in Maharashtra, families stay up to see the full Moon and drink warm masala milk.
- In Bengal, on the full Moon night after Durga Puja, families keep Kojagori Lakshmi Puja.
- At the end of Ramzan, people all over India look for the thin new crescent Moon before Eid begins.
- At Hanle in Ladakh, India's first Dark Sky Reserve, the sky is so dark that the Milky Way shines over the hills.

## Open questions
- **How was the Moon made?** Most scientists think a planet-sized rock crashed into the young Earth, and the Moon formed from the pieces. They still argue about how big the crash was and what the pieces were made of.
- **Why does the Moon look so big when it is low near the rooftops?** Photos show that the rising Moon is the same size as the Moon high up. The bigger look happens in our seeing. Scientists still argue about exactly why.
- **How do animals use the night sky?** Dung beetles use the glow of the Milky Way, and some birds learn the stars to fly at night. Scientists are still finding out which animals use the sky, and what each one sees in it.

- Average Earth to Moon distance: about 384,400 km. At 80 km per hour without stopping, that is about 4,800 hours, or about 200 days.
- M. Dacke, E. Baird, M. Byrne, C. H. Scholtz and E. J. Warrant, 'Dung beetles use the Milky Way for orientation', Current Biology 23 (2013): beetles rolled straight under a starry sky and a planetarium Milky Way, and lost their way when the sky was hidden.
- Aristotle, On the Heavens, Book 2, chapter 8: the planets do not twinkle because they are near, and the stars twinkle because our sight trembles over the great distance. Isaac Newton, Opticks (1704), Book 1: twinkling comes from the trembling of the air, and telescopes work best in the still air of high mountains.
