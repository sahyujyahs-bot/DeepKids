# Food webs: what happens when one link disappears
`sci.ecology.food-chains.L5`

**Summary.** When hunters killed the sea otters of the North Pacific, the tall seaweed forests there vanished too, though nobody cut any seaweed. How? In this lesson you will turn food chains into a web, test which kind of web loses more when beings go, and find what keeps numbers steady.

**Intro.** *A farmer finds a rat snake in her paddy field. Her neighbours tell her to kill him. The snake has never eaten one grain of paddy. Would her harvest be bigger or smaller without him?* A rat snake eats rats, and rats eat paddy. So the snake and the paddy are joined, though the snake never touches a grain. A food web is a map of all such links in one place. In this lesson you learn to read that map. You find out what happens to a web when one being disappears, and why some webs cope better than others. You also find out what keeps the numbers of plants and animals steady from year to year.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can draw a food web for a real place, count the links and say what the arrows mean.
1. By the end I can sort animals into herbivores, carnivores, omnivores and scavengers, and say what decomposers do.
2. By the end I can follow a change through a web step by step, and tell a direct effect from an indirect effect.
3. By the end I can explain, with my Two Webs Test results, why eaters with many foods cope better with a loss.
4. By the end I can explain natural balance as births and deaths that match, using the Leaky Bottle.
5. By the end I can explain why prey animals have many young, and why the numbers of eaters follow their food late.
6. By the end I can give real examples of what happened when people removed or added a being, and say what scientists still argue about.

## Terms
- **Food web**: A map of who eats whom in one place. It is made of many food chains that cross, because most animals eat more than one food. *e.g.* In a forest web, a leopard eats chital, hares, langurs and peafowl. Four chains meet at the leopard.
- **Link**: One arrow in a food web. It joins one food to one eater, and points from the food to the eater. *e.g.* Grass → hare is one link. It shows that food passes from the grass to the hare.
- **Producer**: A living being who makes their own food from sunlight, air and water. Green plants and algae are producers. Every food web starts with them. *e.g.* Paddy, grass, a fig tree and the green algae in a pond are all producers.
- **Consumer**: A living being who cannot make their own food and must eat other living beings. All animals are consumers, including humans. *e.g.* A grasshopper, a frog, a rat snake and a child eating idli are all consumers.
- **Herbivore**: An animal who eats only plants. *e.g.* A chital deer, an elephant, a hare and a grasshopper are herbivores.
- **Carnivore**: An animal who eats only other animals. *e.g.* A leopard, a frog, a spider and a rat snake are carnivores. A frog counts, because insects are animals.
- **Omnivore**: An animal who eats both plants and animals. *e.g.* A crow, a bear, a peafowl and a human are omnivores.
- **Scavenger**: An animal who eats animals that are already dead. *e.g.* A vulture feeds on dead cattle. A crow or a jackal will scavenge too, though they also hunt.
- **Decomposer**: A living being who breaks dead plants and animals down into simple matter that goes back to the soil. Most decomposers are fungi and bacteria. *e.g.* The white mould on an old chapati and the mushrooms on a wet log are decomposers at work.
- **Predator and prey**: A predator is an animal who hunts and eats other animals. The animals they hunt are their prey. *e.g.* An owl is a predator and a rat is her prey. A frog is a predator of flies and the prey of snakes.
- **Population**: All the living beings of one kind in one place. *e.g.* All the chital in Mudumalai forest are one population. All the rats in one village are another.
- **Specialist and generalist**: A specialist eats one food, or very few. A generalist eats many kinds of food. *e.g.* A koala eats almost nothing but eucalyptus leaves, so koalas are specialists. A crow eats nearly anything, so crows are generalists.
- **Direct and indirect effect**: A direct effect passes along one link, from an eater to their food or from a food to their eater. An indirect effect passes through a third being in between. *e.g.* A rat snake eats rats. That is a direct effect on the rats. Fewer rats means more paddy. That is the snake's indirect effect on the paddy.
- **Trophic cascade**: A change at the top of a food chain that passes down, step by step, to the plants. 'Trophic' means 'to do with feeding'. *e.g.* Fewer sea otters led to more sea urchins, and more sea urchins led to less seaweed.
- **Keystone species**: A kind of living being whose loss changes the whole web far more than their small numbers would suggest. A species is one kind of living being. *e.g.* On one rocky shore, taking away one kind of starfish cut the kinds of animals from 15 to 8.
- **Natural balance**: The way numbers in nature stay within a range from year to year. It happens when births and deaths roughly match. *e.g.* A forest may hold about the same number of chital for many years. Yet chital are born and die every day.
- **Feedback**: When a change causes something that pushes back against that change. Feedback is what brings a number back towards its usual level. *e.g.* When there are more deer, each deer finds less food and predators find them more easily. So more deer die, and the number comes down again.
- **Invasive species**: A kind of living being whom people bring to a new place. There they spread so fast that others are harmed. *e.g.* Lantana, a garden shrub brought to India about 200 years ago, now covers the ground in many forests.
- **Model**: A simple copy of a real thing. We make one to test ideas that are too big or too slow to test for real. A model always leaves some things out. *e.g.* In the Two Webs Test, cards and arrows on paper stand for a whole forest.

## Explanations
### From chains to webs: counting the links · *defined*
**Definition:** A food chain is one line of who eats whom, starting with a producer. A food web is all the chains of one place drawn together. Each arrow is one link, and points from the food to the eater.

A chain such as grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle has 5 beings and 4 links. But a frog also eats flies, and a snake also eats rats. When you add what each animal really eats, the chain becomes a web with many more links. Scientists measure a web by counting. They count the beings, count the links, and divide to find the links for each being. A web with more links for each being has more paths for food to travel along. Every path still starts at a producer, because only producers make food from sunlight.
- e.g. In Chain to Web, a chain with 4 links grew into a web with about 12 links.
- e.g. In the Two Webs Test, the narrow web has 10 beings and 7 links. The wide web has the same 10 beings and 18 links.
- e.g. A real web is far bigger. Scientists who mapped one small pond in England found nearly 200 links among 25 kinds of animal.
### Five ways to get food · *defined*
**Definition:** Herbivores eat plants. Carnivores eat other animals. Omnivores eat both. Scavengers eat animals that are already dead. Decomposers, mostly fungi and bacteria, break down whatever is left and return it to the soil.

These five words describe what a being eats, and many beings fit more than one. A jackal hunts and also scavenges. A bear eats fruit, honey, termites and fish. A crow does almost everything. So the groups are useful, but real animals do not always fit one group. Decomposers finish every chain. They break dead leaves, droppings and bodies into simple matter. Plants take that matter up through their roots and build new leaves with it. So matter goes round the web again and again, while each meal's energy is used and leaves as heat.
- e.g. Herbivores include the horse, the buffalo, the elephant and the chital. Carnivores include the tiger, the leopard, the frog and the cat.
- e.g. Omnivores include humans, crows, bears and peafowl. A dog eats many foods too.
- e.g. Scavengers include vultures and hyenas. A kite and a jackal scavenge and also hunt.
- e.g. Decomposers include the mould on old bread, mushrooms, and soil bacteria too small to see.
### When a link is lost, the change travels both ways · *defined*
**Definition:** A change to one being in a web passes to others along the links. It travels up, to those who eat that being. It also travels down, to those whom that being eats.

Two rules help you follow a change. Rule one: when a food becomes scarce, their eaters go hungry and become fewer. Rule two: when an eater becomes scarce, their food is eaten less and becomes more plentiful. A change that passes along one link is a direct effect. A change that passes on through a third being is an indirect effect. If people kill the rat snakes in a field, the rats increase. That is direct. Then the paddy decreases, though no snake ever ate paddy. That is indirect. In a web, two paths can push the same being in opposite ways. Then nobody can tell the result without counting in a real field.
- e.g. In Take the Snakes Away, removing the snakes pushed the paddy down through the rats. It also pushed the paddy up, through the frogs and grasshoppers.
- e.g. India stopped the export of frogs' legs in 1987. One reason was that frogs eat insects who damage crops.
- e.g. Insect poison sprayed on paddy also kills spiders. In many Asian countries, plant-sucking insects called brown planthoppers then multiplied, because their hunters were gone.
### Trophic cascades and keystone species · *defined*
**Definition:** A trophic cascade is a change at the top of a food chain that passes down to the plants. A keystone species is one whose loss changes the whole web far more than their numbers would suggest.

Sea otters were hunted for their fur until very few were left. In 1974, James Estes and John Palmisano compared islands in Alaska. Around the islands with otters, tall seaweed called kelp grew thick, and fish lived among the kelp. Around the islands without otters, big sea urchins covered the rock and had eaten the kelp down. The change had passed down three steps, from otter to urchin to kelp. A keystone species does not have to be a predator. Fig trees fruit when few other trees do, so many birds and monkeys depend on them. Elephants open paths and spread seeds. What marks a keystone is how much changes when they go.
- e.g. Robert Paine removed one kind of starfish from a rocky shore. Mussels took over, and the kinds of animals there fell from 15 to 8.
- e.g. When most of India's vultures died, stray dogs are thought to have grown in number.
- e.g. A fig tree in fruit in a dry month feeds barbets, mynas, hornbills, langurs and bats.
### Specialists and generalists: why a wide web loses fewer · *defined*
**Definition:** A specialist depends on one food. A generalist can switch to another food when one is lost. A web in which eaters have many foods loses fewer beings when some disappear.

In the Two Webs Test, you took the same three beings out of two paper webs. In the narrow web, each eater had only one food. When that food went, the eater starved, and then whoever ate that eater starved too. In the wide web, most eaters had two, three or four foods, so most of them lived. In 2002, Jennifer Dunne and her team did the same test on a computer with 16 real food webs. Webs with more links lost fewer extra beings. The model leaves something out. It follows hunger only. It does not show what happens when a predator is lost and their prey multiply.
- e.g. In the narrow web, losing the grass alone also starves the grasshopper, the hare, the peafowl and the dhole. Five beings are lost.
- e.g. In the wide web, losing any one being starves nobody else.
- e.g. The giant panda eats almost only bamboo. When bamboo flowers and dies over a whole hillside, pandas there have gone hungry.
### Natural balance: births and deaths that match · *defined*
**Definition:** A population stays about the same size when births and deaths roughly match. Natural balance is a steady level kept up by beings arriving and leaving all the time.

In the Leaky Bottle, water ran in from the tap and out through a hole the whole time. Yet the level stayed at one mark. When you held the hole shut for a while, the level rose. When you let go, the deeper water pushed out faster, and the level came back down. A population works in a similar way. When there are more deer, each one finds less food, illness spreads more easily and predators catch them more often. So more deer die, and the number comes back down. This pushing back is called feedback. When you opened a second hole, the level settled at a new, lower mark. A lasting change, such as a new predator or a lost forest, moves the balance to a new level.
- e.g. In the Leaky Bottle, the level settled at one mark with one hole. With two holes it settled at a much lower mark.
- e.g. With no hole at all the bottle overflows. Deer with no predators can multiply until they eat down their own food.
- e.g. Numbers in nature are never perfectly still. They rise and fall within a range.
### Why prey animals have many young · *defined*
**Definition:** Think of a population that stays the same size. On average, each pair of parents is replaced by just two young who live to become parents. All the other young die first, and most of them are eaten.

A female Indian bullfrog lays thousands of eggs in one season. If all of them lived, ponds would overflow with frogs within a few years. They do not. So nearly all the eggs and tadpoles must be dying, most of them as food for fish, birds and snakes. An animal who is eaten often needs many young, so that two are still left. An animal who is seldom eaten, such as a tiger or an elephant, has few young. The mother looks after each one for years. So the number of young shows how dangerous life is for the young of that animal. It also shows how much of the web that animal feeds.
- e.g. A female rat can have 6 to 8 young at a time, several times a year. A tigress has 2 to 4 cubs every two or three years.
- e.g. An elephant has one calf every few years, and the herd guards the calf.
- e.g. In Two Out of Thousands, you coloured 2 squares for the frogs who live, out of 4,000 eggs.
### Booms and crashes: eaters follow their food late · *defined*
**Definition:** When food increases, the number of eaters rises some time afterwards. When food runs short, the number of eaters falls some time afterwards. The delay makes numbers swing up and down.

In Ants and the Sugar Drop, the ants kept coming for several minutes after the sugar was gone. Populations show the same delay, over years. In northern Canada, fur traders kept records for about 200 years. They counted snowshoe hares and the lynx who hunt them. Hare numbers rise and crash about every ten years, and lynx numbers follow a year or two later. A sudden flood of food can also break a balance. In Mizoram, one kind of bamboo flowers and fruits all together about every 48 years. Rats feed on the fruit and multiply far faster than owls and snakes can. When the fruit is finished, the rats turn to the rice fields. People there call this mautam.
- e.g. In the Ten-Year Wave, the lynx line reached its top two years after the hare line.
- e.g. The bamboo fruited in Mizoram in 1959, and again from 2006 to 2008. Both times, rats destroyed much of the rice crop.
- e.g. Charles Elton and Mary Nicholson described the ten-year lynx cycle from the fur records in 1942.
### New arrivals: when a link is added · *defined*
**Definition:** An invasive species is a living being whom people bring to a new place. There they spread fast and harm the beings who already live there.

A web can be changed by adding a being as well as by removing one. In their old home, a plant or animal has eaters and illnesses that hold their numbers down. In a new place, those may be missing, so nothing pushes back. Lantana is a shrub from the Americas that was brought to India as a garden plant about 200 years ago. Deer and cattle avoid eating lantana, so lantana spreads and shades out the grass. Water hyacinth, from South America, covers ponds and lakes and blocks the light from the plants below. Most plants and animals that people carry to new places do not spread like this. Chilli, tomato and potato also came from the Americas, and they stay in the fields.
- e.g. In A New Arrival, adding lantana left less grass for the hare and the chital. That left less food for the dhole and the leopard.
- e.g. A 2020 study found lantana in more than 40 of every 100 parts of India's tiger forests.
- e.g. The Nile perch, a large hunting fish, was put into Lake Victoria in Africa in the 1950s. About 200 kinds of small fish found nowhere else then died out.
### People in the web: hunting, cutting forests and protecting · *defined*
**Definition:** People are part of food webs. We change them by hunting, by cutting forests, by using poisons, and by protecting places and animals.

When people hunt out the carnivores, herbivores multiply and eat the plants down. Then the herbivores starve, and the whole web suffers. When a forest is cut, the producers go first, so every animal above them loses food and shelter. Bare ground also holds less rain, so floods and droughts become worse. India protects whole webs by law. The Wildlife (Protection) Act of 1972 bans the hunting of protected animals. Project Tiger began in 1973. A tiger needs a large forest with many deer, so saving tigers saves everything below them. A national park has the strictest protection. A wildlife sanctuary also protects animals, but may allow some use, such as grazing. Long before these laws, the Bishnoi people of Rajasthan stood in front of trees to save them. So did the women of the Chipko movement.
- e.g. Mudumalai in Tamil Nadu, Periyar in Kerala, Kaziranga in Assam and Gir in Gujarat are protected forests.
- e.g. Farmers in Tamil Nadu put up T-shaped perches in paddy fields. Owls sit on them and hunt rats at night.
- e.g. India banned the cattle medicine diclofenac in 2006, because it was killing the vultures who ate dead cattle.
### Does a web with more links stay steadier? · *debated*
For a long time, ecologists thought that a web with many kinds and many links must be steadier. In 1972, Robert May tested this with mathematics and found the opposite for webs joined at random. Since then, scientists have looked for what makes real webs different from random ones. The answer seems to depend on what 'steady' means, and on how strong the links are.
- e.g. In the Two Webs Test, the wide web lost fewer beings. That test measures only one kind of steadiness: how many starve.
- e.g. A field of one crop can be wiped out by one pest. A mixed forest seldom loses everything at once.
- **Charles Elton (1958) and Robert MacArthur (1955):** More kinds and more links make a web steadier. If one food fails, eaters switch to another. Simple webs, such as islands and single-crop fields, suffer the worst outbreaks of pests.
- **Robert May (1972):** In a mathematical web joined at random, more kinds and more links make the numbers swing more, not less. A disturbance has more paths to spread along. So links alone cannot be the reason real webs last.
- **Kevin McCann and others (1998), Jennifer Dunne and others (2002):** Real webs are not joined at random. Most of their links are weak, because most eaters take only a little of each food. Many weak links damp down the swings, and webs with more links lose fewer beings to hunger.
### Why does the bamboo wait 48 years? · *idea*
The bamboo called mautak covers the hills of Mizoram. Almost all the plants flower in the same few years, drop their fruit and die. Then the hills stay without bamboo fruit for about 48 years. In 1976 the ecologist Daniel Janzen suggested a reason. If a few plants fruited every year, rats and birds would eat nearly every seed. When all the plants fruit together, there is far more fruit than the eaters can finish. So many seeds are left to grow. The long wait keeps the numbers of seed eaters low in between. This idea fits what people see, but it is hard to test, because one test takes 48 years.
- e.g. Mango trees also fruit heavily in some years and lightly in others.
- e.g. After the Mizoram bamboo fruited from 2006 to 2008, young bamboo came up thickly across the hills.

## Mostly agreed
- Every food web starts with producers, and most start with green plants or algae using sunlight. *How we know:* No animal can make food from air and water. Trace any meal back, and the path ends at a plant or an alga. A few webs in the deep sea start with microbes that use chemicals instead of light.
- Removing one kind of living being can change the numbers of many others, including beings they never eat. *How we know:* Robert Paine removed starfish from a shore from the early 1960s and counted the animals for years. James Estes and John Palmisano compared islands with and without sea otters in 1974.
- A population stays steady only when births and deaths roughly match. *How we know:* Counts of animals over many years show numbers rising and falling within a range. When predators or food change, the range shifts.
- Eaters with many foods cope with the loss of one food better than eaters with a single food. *How we know:* In 2002, Jennifer Dunne and her team tested computer copies of real food webs. Webs with more links lost fewer further beings.
- Living beings brought to new places by people can spread and harm the beings already there. *How we know:* Records show the same pattern for the Nile perch in Lake Victoria, and for water hyacinth and lantana in India.

## How we found out
**Robert Paine, an American ecologist, The test began in the early 1960s. The results were published in 1966, and the name 'keystone species' in 1969..** In the 1950s, many ecologists thought that weather and food decided how many kinds of beings a place could hold. Predators, they thought, only trimmed the numbers a little. In the early 1960s a young American scientist, Robert Paine, decided to test this. He chose a rocky shore at Mukkaw Bay, in Washington State. The shore was covered with mussels, barnacles, limpets and seaweeds, and a purple starfish hunted there. Paine chose one stretch of rock and pulled every starfish off with a crowbar. He threw them far out into the sea. He kept doing this for years, and left the next stretch of rock alone to compare. On the untouched rock nothing changed. On the cleared rock, mussels spread and pushed the other animals out. In a few years the number of kinds fell from 15 to 8. The starfish had been eating mussels and keeping space open for everyone else. In 1969 Paine named such an animal a keystone species. Removing things on purpose, with a second place left alone, became a standard way to study food webs.  
*What people believed before:* That predators only trim the numbers of their prey. People thought predators did not decide which kinds of beings could live in a place. Paine showed that removing one predator changed the whole community.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Killing the snakes makes a village safer.** (A common belief in many villages and towns; *rejected*)  
  For: A few kinds of Indian snake have venom that can kill a person. Snakebite is a real danger for people who work in fields.  
  Against: Most snakes near homes and fields are harmless rat snakes. Snakes eat rats, and rats eat stored grain and standing crops. Where snakes are killed, rats multiply. Knowing the few dangerous kinds and wearing shoes in the field protects people better.
- **Nature stays in perfect balance if people leave her alone.** (A very old idea, found in many cultures; *debated*)  
  For: Forests and ponds can look the same for many years. Feedback does bring numbers back after small changes.  
  Against: Counts over many years show numbers swinging widely even where no people live. Hares and lynx in Canada rise and crash about every ten years. Fires, floods and droughts also change webs.
- **A keystone species is always a big, fierce predator.** (A common belief, because the best-known examples are wolves, tigers and sharks; *rejected*)  
  For: The first keystone species to be named, a starfish, was a predator. Sea otters are predators too.  
  Against: Fig trees, bees who pollinate, termites who build mounds and elephants who spread seeds can all be keystones. What counts is how much the web changes when they go.
- **Poison is the best way to get rid of rats in a field.** (Many farmers and shopkeepers; *debated*)  
  For: Poison kills many rats quickly, and it is cheap.  
  Against: Owls, snakes and cats who eat poisoned rats can die too. Rats breed much faster than their hunters, so the rats return first, with fewer hunters left. Traps, owl perches and clean grain stores work for longer.
- **More links always make a food web safer.** (Charles Elton and many ecologists before 1972; *debated*)  
  For: In the Two Webs Test and in computer tests on real webs, eaters with more foods starve less often.  
  Against: Robert May showed in 1972 that in webs joined at random, more links make numbers swing more. Links also carry harm. A poison or a disease can spread along the same links as food.

## Roots
We have learned how food webs join living beings, and what happens when one link is lost. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Two Webs Test, the same three beings were taken from both webs. The narrow web then lost more beings to hunger than the wide web did. An eater with one food starved when that food went, and an eater with three foods lived. So the harm done by one loss depends on how many links the rest of the web has.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call one living being important and another unimportant, or one useful and another a pest. We speak as if that were a fact about that being alone. The test shows that the same loss does little harm in one web and great harm in another. How much a being matters depends on the web around them.  
*Evidence:* In the Two Webs Test, losing the grass from the narrow web also starved four animals. Losing the grass from the wide web starved nobody. On Robert Paine's shore, removing one kind of starfish cut the kinds of animals from 15 to 8. On that shore the starfish was the main hunter of mussels, so nobody else could do the same work.

- *who am i:* You eat many kinds of food, so you are an eater with many links. Each food on your plate joins you to a field, a river or a sea. It joins you to everyone who lives there.
- *what is life:* No animal feeds alone. Each one lives on those they eat, and feeds those who eat them. Even the young who die early feed someone.
- *what is this world:* The world is a web. A change in one place arrives in another place later, often where nobody expected it.

All the food in a web begins as sunlight caught by producers. That one supply of food passes into grass, hare, dhole and leopard. All the food in a web begins as sunlight caught by producers. That one supply of food passes into grass, hare, dhole and leopard. Each of them is built from the same food, in a different body. So the many beings of a forest all live on one shared supply. This is why none of them can be understood alone. To know what one of them does, you have to look at the others they are joined to.

- **Samyutta Nikaya 12.61, the Buddha's teaching of dependent arising:** “iti imasmiṁ sati idaṁ hoti, imassuppādā idaṁ uppajjati; / imasmiṁ asati idaṁ na hoti, imassa nirodhā idaṁ nirujjhati” When this is, that is. When this arises, that arises. When this is not, that is not. When this stops, that stops. (This is the rule you used in Take the Snakes Away. When the snakes are gone, something else rises and something else falls. Nothing in a web changes alone.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra 5.21, a Jain text by Umasvati:** “परस्परोपग्रहो जीवानाम्” Living beings support one another. In Sanskrit: parasparopagraho jivanam. (A food web shows this support in one form. Every animal is held up by the plants and animals they eat, and holds up those who eat them.)
- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** A net stretches in every direction, with a jewel at every knot. Each jewel reflects all the other jewels. (In a food web, each being is a knot where links meet. The Two Webs Test showed that what happens at one knot depends on the links around it.)
- Science (settled): Removing one kind of living being can change many others who never meet them, through indirect effects.
- Science (debated): Whether webs with more links are steadier in every way is still argued about.
- Science (open): Nobody can yet predict exactly what the loss of one being will do to a real web.
- Other view, Henry Gleason, an American plant scientist, in 1926, and many ecologists since: A forest is not one tightly joined whole. Each kind of plant and animal lives wherever conditions suit them, and they happen to overlap. Most links are weak, and most losses change little. The web is real, but it is loose.
- Practice, Trace one bite back (3 min): Before you eat your next bite, hold the bite still for a moment. Name the plant this food came from. Now name one animal who helped that plant, such as a bee or an earthworm. Then name one animal who ate a pest of that plant, such as a frog or a spider. Take three slow breaths. Then eat the bite slowly.
- Ask: In the Two Webs Test, the eaters with many foods lived. Does having many links also help people? How?
- Ask: Is any living being truly unimportant? How could you find out?
- Ask: Only two of a frog's thousands of young become parents. What happened to the food and the life in all the others?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* A leopard never eats grass, yet leopards change how much grass grows. Who else changes your life without ever meeting you? (hint: Think of who grew, carried and cooked your last meal, and who ate the pests on that farm.)
- *deep:* If numbers in nature keep rising and falling, is there such a thing as a balance of nature? (hint: Think of the Leaky Bottle. The level was steady, but the water in the bottle was never the same water.)
- *deep:* A rat is a pest in a granary and food for an owl. Is being a pest a fact about the rat, or does it depend on who is looking? (hint: Try describing the same rat as the owl would, then as the farmer would.)
- *big:* Only two of a frog's thousands of young live to be parents. Were the other lives wasted? (hint: Follow where the other tadpoles went. Who lived because of them?)
- *deep:* We can test a paper web in an hour, but a real forest takes many years. How much should we trust a model? (hint: List what the Two Webs Test left out, and what it still got right.)
- *big:* People have removed some beings from webs and added others. Should people now try to manage every web, or leave webs alone? (hint: Think about the otters, the lantana and the owl perches. What did people know, and not know, each time?)

## Story: The Year the Bamboo Flowered
*Mautam is real. The bamboo of the Mizoram hills flowers and fruits all together about every 48 years. Rats multiplied and destroyed rice crops in 1959 and again from 2006 to 2008. The government did pay for rats' tails, and farmers were advised to grow ginger and turmeric. Zuali, Pi Thangi, Sanga and what their family did are made up. 'Pi' is a Mizo title of respect for an older woman.*

In the spring of 2007, the bamboo on the hills around Zuali's village began to flower. Zuali was ten, and she had never seen bamboo flowers before. Nobody in her class had. Small pale tassels hung from every stem, on every slope, as far as she could see.

Her grandmother, Pi Thangi, looked at the hills for a long time. "Mautam," she said. "I was eleven the last time. It was 1959."

"What happened in 1959?" Zuali asked.

"The bamboo flowered. Then the rats came. Then there was no rice." Pi Thangi did not say more that day.

By the rains, the flowers had turned into fruit. They were green and as big as small pears, and they fell from the bamboo in thousands. Zuali's cousin Sanga, who was fourteen, cut one open. It was soft and pale inside. "Rats love these," he said. "Look how many there are already."

He was right. There were rats on the path, rats under the house, rats in the firewood. The government had promised a small reward for every rat's tail, and Sanga set traps each evening. At the end of the first week he had forty tails drying on a string. "Forty," he said. "At this speed I will finish them before the rice is ripe."

That night Zuali heard the barn owl who lived in the dead tree beside their field. She had raised three chicks that year. A rat snake lived under the granary too, and he was as long as Sanga was tall. Sanga had plans for both of them. The dead tree would make good firewood. And the snake, he said, frightened the hens.

Zuali sat on the bamboo floor with a piece of charcoal and began to draw. She drew a bamboo fruit and a rat, with an arrow from the fruit to the rat. She drew the owl and the snake, with arrows from the rat to each of them.

"Pi," she said, "how many babies does a rat have?"

"Six or seven at a time," said her grandmother. "When food is plenty, a mother has another family a few weeks later. And her daughters have their own before the season ends."

Zuali looked at her drawing. The owl had three chicks in one year. One rat mother might have twenty young before the rains ended, and those twenty would have young of their own. She looked at Sanga's string of forty tails. Then she looked out at the hills, where more fruit lay on the ground than all the rats in Mizoram could eat.

"Sanga," she said, "you cannot win. The fruit is making rats faster than you can trap them. Faster than the owl and the snake can eat them, too."

"Then I will trap harder," said Sanga.

Zuali did not answer. She was looking at the arrow from the fruit to the rat. Zuali did not answer. She was looking at the arrow from the fruit to the rat, and it worried her. The fruit would not lie there for ever. Already some of the fruits on the path had split, and a white root was pushing out of each one into the soil. Already some of the fruits on the path had split, and a white root was pushing out of each one into the soil.

"Pi," she said slowly, "when the fruit has all sprouted, what will the rats eat?"

Her grandmother put down her work.

Zuali drew a rice plant on the floor. She drew an arrow from the rice to the rat. "They are not here for our rice," she said. "They came for the fruit. But the fruit will be finished just when the rice is ripe. Then all of them will come to the fields at once."

"That is how it was," said Pi Thangi quietly. "They came in the night, like water running downhill. In the morning the field was stalks."

Nobody spoke. Then Zuali asked the question that mattered. "What will rats not eat?"

"Ginger," said her grandmother. "Turmeric. They leave those alone in the ground. The officer who came to the village said the same."

So that year the family did three things. They planted ginger and turmeric on half of their slope, and rice only on the other half. They packed last year's paddy into tin drums with tight lids. And when Sanga picked up his axe to cut the dead tree, Zuali stood in front of the trunk. "The owl catches rats every night for nothing," she said. "So does the snake. The fruit will finish one day, and then we will need every rat-catcher we have." Sanga looked at the charcoal arrows on the floor for a while. He put the axe away.

In October the rats came to the fields, just as the rice turned gold. The family banged tins and lit fires, but it made little difference. They saved three baskets of rice from half a slope. Many families saved less.

But the ginger and the turmeric lay safe under the soil, and the drums of old paddy stayed shut. The family sold ginger in the market at Aizawl and bought rice with the money. It was a hard year, and nobody in the house went hungry.

By the next spring there were few rats to be seen. Their food was gone, and most of them had starved. The old bamboo stood dry and dead on the hills, and beneath the dead stems the ground was green with young bamboo, thick as grass.

Zuali never rubbed out the drawing on the floor. Under the bamboo fruit she wrote a number in charcoal: 2055. "That is when it will flower again," she told Sanga. "I will be fifty-eight. Somebody will have to tell them what is coming."

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn comes when Zuali adds a second arrow to her drawing. The rats and the rice were joined through the bamboo fruit. A flood of food at the bottom of the web made rats faster than any hunter could eat them. A rat has many young quickly, and an owl has few. When that food ended, the rats moved along another link, to the rice. Trapping could not undo this. Reading the web told the family what was coming and what to plant.

- Ask: Why could Sanga's traps not keep up with the rats?
- Ask: The rats came for the bamboo fruit. Why did they end up in the rice fields?
- Ask: Why did Zuali stop Sanga from cutting the owl's tree?
- Ask: Why were there so few rats the next spring?
- Ask: The bamboo will flower again in about 48 years. What would you tell the people living there then?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Two Webs Test: which web loses more when three beings go? (44 min)
- **question:** Two webs have the same 10 beings. When the same 3 beings disappear from both, which web has more beings left: the one with 7 links or the one with 18 links?
- **predict:** Guess first. Three beings will be taken from both webs. Will the narrow web or the wide web have more beings left, or will they be the same? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Both webs have the same 10 beings. In each round, the same 3 beings are taken from both webs. Only the number of arrows is different. Play 6 rounds, because one draw of cards can be a fluke.
- **record:** Fill in the table for 6 rounds. Find the average number left in each web, and show them as two coin towers. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Round | The 3 beings drawn | Narrow web: beings left | Wide web: beings left
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On both sheets, write the same 10 names in the same places. Bottom row: Grass, Shrubs, Fig tree. Second row: Grasshopper, Hare, Chital, Langur. Third row: Peafowl. Top row: Dhole, Leopard. 2. On sheet 1, write NARROW WEB. Here each animal eats one food. The grasshopper and the hare eat grass. The chital eats shrubs. The langur eats figs. The peafowl eats grasshoppers. The dhole eats hares. The leopard eats chital. 3. On sheet 2, write WIDE WEB. The grasshopper and the hare eat grass and shrubs. The chital eats grass, shrubs and figs. The langur eats shrubs and figs. The peafowl eats grass seeds, figs and grasshoppers. The dhole eats hares and chital. The leopard eats hares, chital, langurs and peafowl. 4. Draw an arrow from each food to the eater. Count the arrows.  
  *Parent says:* These are beings of the Mudumalai forest in Tamil Nadu. A chital is a spotted deer, and a dhole is a wild dog. Let's draw the same forest twice. In one web each animal eats one food. In the other, most animals eat several.  
  *Child mode:* Write the same 10 names on two sheets. On sheet 1, each animal eats one food. On sheet 2, most animals eat several foods. Draw an arrow from each food to the eater. Count the arrows on each sheet.
  - Note: Sheet 1 should have 7 arrows and sheet 2 should have 18. The wide web is close to what these animals really eat. The narrow web is made up for the test. No real leopard eats only chital.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one token on every name on both sheets. 2. Write the 10 names on 10 slips of paper and fold them. 3. Explain that 3 beings will be taken from both webs. 4. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will take the same three beings away from both webs. Which web will have more beings left afterwards? Or will they be the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put a token on every name. Three beings will be taken from both webs. Guess which web will have more left.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: In the wide web an animal can eat something else if one food goes.; They will be the same, because both lose 3 beings. (A fair guess. The test will show.))
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Teach the hunger rule on the narrow web. Take the token off Hare. 2. Ask who eats only hares in this web. Take the token off Dhole too. 3. Say the rule: an animal with no food left in the web is lost. 4. Put both tokens back.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the hunger rule. When a being goes, look at everyone who eats them. If an animal has no food left, that animal is lost too. Then check again, because the loss can pass further up.  
  *Child mode:* Take the token off Hare on the narrow web. Who eats only hares? Take that token off too. An animal with no food left is lost. Put the tokens back.
  - Note: Plants need no food from the web, so a plant is lost only when their own card is drawn. Keep checking until nobody else is without food.
- **s4 (18 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shake the 10 name cards in the bowl and draw 3. 2. Take the tokens off those 3 beings on both sheets. 3. On each sheet, use the hunger rule until nobody else is lost. 4. Count the tokens left on each sheet and write both numbers in the table. 5. Put all the tokens and cards back. 6. Play 6 rounds.  
  *Parent says:* Draw three cards. Those three beings are gone from both forests. Now check each animal on each sheet. Does she still have a food with a token on it?  
  *Child mode:* Shake the cards and draw 3. Take those tokens off both sheets. Take off any animal with no food left. Check again. Count the tokens left on each sheet. Write the numbers. Play 6 rounds.
  - Ask: Which card did the most harm in the narrow web when it was drawn? (good: Grass, because the grasshopper, hare, peafowl and dhole all depended on grass.; A plant card, because everything above the plant went too.)
  - Note: The wide web can never have fewer left than the narrow web. It has every arrow of the narrow web, and more. Most rounds leave 6 or 7 in the wide web and 3 to 7 in the narrow web. In about one round in six they tie. If all three plant cards come out together, both webs lose everyone.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add up the 6 numbers for the narrow web and divide by 6. Do the same for the wide web. 2. Stack coins into two towers, as tall as the two averages (round to a whole number). 3. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 4. Ask what the paper model leaves out.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Find the average for each web. Stack two coin towers. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: Why did the wide web lose fewer beings? (good: Most animals there had another food left when one food went.; In the narrow web each animal had only one food, so one loss passed up the chain.)
  - Ask: Our model follows hunger only. What could happen in a real forest that the model leaves out? (good: If the leopard and dhole go, the chital could multiply and eat down the plants.; Animals can move away or find new foods.; Some links are strong and some are weak.)
  - Check: In the Two Webs Test, why were more beings left in the wide web? → **Most eaters there had another food to turn to**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to add just one new arrow to the narrow web, where they think it will help most. 2. Ask why they chose that place. 3. Play 3 more rounds on the narrow web and see if the average rises.  
  *Parent says:* Now you change the test. You may add one arrow to the narrow web. Where will one new link help the most? Why there?  
  *Child mode:* Add one new arrow to the narrow web. Choose where it helps most. Say why. Play 3 more rounds.
  - Note: A strong choice gives a second food to an animal near the bottom, such as shrubs to the hare. Then the loss of grass no longer starves so many. Three rounds are too few to be sure, and that is worth saying aloud.
### e2 · The Leaky Bottle: what keeps a level steady? (34 min)
- **question:** Water runs into a bottle and out through a hole at the same time. Does the level keep rising, keep falling, or stop at one height? What does a second hole do?
- **predict:** Guess first. When water runs in at the top and out at the hole, what will the level do? Then guess what will happen to the level if you open a second hole of the same size. Why do you think so? Write both guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** Use the same bottle and the same tap all through. Once the stream is set, do not touch the tap again. Only one thing changes at a time: first a finger over the hole for a count of 30, then a second hole.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the holes. A heated nail is very hot, so hold it with pliers and keep children back. Do the test in a bathroom or at a sink, because wet floors are slippery.
- **record:** Write the level every minute in the table. Draw a line graph of level against minutes, or just read the numbers aloud and say where the level stopped changing. Mark where you covered the hole and where you opened the second hole. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Minute | Water level above the holes (cm) | What we changed
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up makes two holes in the bottle, each about 3 mm wide, as wide as a matchstick. Both are 3 cm above the base, on opposite sides. 2. Dry the bottle. Press tape firmly over one hole. 3. With the ruler and marker, draw a line every 1 cm up the side. Start with 0 level with the holes and go up to 18.  
  *Parent says:* This bottle is our model. Water coming in will stand for animals being born. Water going out will stand for animals dying. The level will stand for how many animals there are.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes two small holes near the base. Tape one hole shut. Draw a line every centimetre up the side. Start at the holes.
  - Note: A nail heated on the gas flame for a few seconds and held with pliers melts a neat hole. Make both holes the same size. The tape must go on while the bottle is dry, or it will not hold.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show how the test will work, without turning on the tap. 2. Ask what the level will do when water runs in and out together. 3. Ask what a second hole will do to the level. 4. Write down both guesses and the reasons.  
  *Parent says:* Water will run in at the top and out at the hole. What will the level do? Later we will open a second hole. What will the level do then? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what the level will do. Then guess what a second hole will do. Write your guesses.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: It will stop where the water going out equals the water coming in.; With two holes the level will be half as high. (A fair guess. The test will show.))
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the bottle in the bucket under the tap. 2. Open the tap to a thin, steady stream that goes into the mouth of the bottle. 3. Read the level every minute for 8 minutes and write it down. 4. When the level stays the same for 2 minutes, mark it with the pen.  
  *Parent says:* Read the level each minute. Is it still rising? Is it rising as fast as before?  
  *Child mode:* Stand the bottle under the tap. Let a thin stream run in. Read the level every minute. Mark where it stops rising.
  - Ask: The level has stopped, but water is still running in. Where is that water going? (good: Out of the hole, just as fast as it comes in.)
  - Note: You want the level to settle between 8 and 16 cm. If the water reaches the shoulder of the bottle, turn the tap down a little and start the readings again. If it settles below 6 cm, turn the tap up a little. After that, do not touch the tap. The test uses about one bucket of water, so keep it for mopping or for plants.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press a finger firmly over the open hole for a slow count of 30. 2. Let go, and read the level at once. 3. Read the level every minute for 4 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Now nothing can leave for a short while. What will the level do? And what will it do when you let go?  
  *Child mode:* Press a finger on the hole. Count slowly to 30. Let go. Read the level every minute for 4 minutes.
  - Ask: The level came back to the old mark. Look at the jet. What was different while the level was high? (good: The jet shot out farther and faster.; Deeper water pushes out faster, so the extra water drained away.)
  - Note: The level should rise 3 to 4 cm and then sink back to the pen mark within about 4 minutes. This return is the feedback. Do not explain it yet. Ask what your child noticed about the jet.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Peel the tape off the second hole. 2. Read the level every minute for 5 minutes. 3. Mark the new steady level with the pen. 4. Measure both pen marks from the holes.  
  *Parent says:* Now water can leave by two holes. Watch the level. Where does it stop this time?  
  *Child mode:* Peel the tape off the second hole. Read the level every minute. Mark where it stops. Measure both marks.
  - Note: With two equal holes the level usually settles at about a quarter of the first height, not a half. A jet from deep water is faster than a jet from shallow water, but not by much. So the level has to fall a long way before two slow jets carry what one fast jet did. If your result is nearer a third, the holes are probably not quite equal.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn off the tap. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 3. Then match the model to a herd of deer. Ask what the tap, the holes and the level stand for.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then tell me what the tap, the hole and the level would be in a herd of deer.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then say what the tap, the hole and the level stand for.
  - Ask: In a herd of deer, what is like the second hole? (good: A new predator, a disease or hunters: one more way for deer to die.)
  - Ask: With no hole at all, the bottle would overflow. What is that like in a forest? (good: Deer with no predators multiply until they eat down their own food.)
  - Check: In the Leaky Bottle, why did the level stay at one mark? → **Water left as fast as it arrived**
### e3 · Ants and the Sugar Drop: how fast do eaters follow their food? (40 min)
- **question:** When food appears, how quickly does the number of ants rise? When the food is taken away, how quickly does the number fall?
- **predict:** Guess first. How many ants will you count after 16 minutes? Will the number rise steadily, or slowly at first? Then guess how many minutes the ants will stay after the sugar is taken away. Why do you think so? Write your guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** Count only the ants inside the circle. Count every 2 minutes by the clock. Do not move the paper, blow on the ants or add more sugar during the test.
- **record:** Write the count every 2 minutes in the table. Draw a line graph of ants against minutes, and mark the minute when you took the sugar away. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Minutes since the first ant arrived | Ants inside the circle | Is the sugar there?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a place where ants walk, such as a window sill, a balcony floor or a kitchen corner. 2. Lay the sheet of paper there, with the circle facing up. 3. Mix the sugar with the drops of water on the small square. 4. Put the square in the middle of the circle.  
  *Parent says:* We are putting out a small feast. Let's see who finds it, and how fast the news spreads.  
  *Child mode:* Find where ants walk. Lay the paper there. Wet the sugar on the small square. Put it in the middle of the circle.
  - Note: The first ant may take 5 to 30 minutes to find the sugar. Do something else nearby while you wait. If no ant has come in 30 minutes, move the paper close to where you last saw ants. Or try again in the evening. Small black or red house ants work well. Do not use big red tree ants, who bite hard.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. While you wait for the first ant, ask for a guess. 2. Ask your child to guess how many ants there will be after 16 minutes. Will the number rise steadily, or slowly at first? 3. Ask how long the ants will stay once the sugar is gone, and why.  
  *Parent says:* How do you think the number of ants will change? Will it rise steadily, or slowly at first? When we take the sugar away, how soon will they leave? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many ants will come. When the sugar goes, how soon will they leave?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: One ant finds the sugar first and the others follow her, so the number rises slowly at first.; They will leave at once because there is nothing to eat. (A fair guess. The test will show.))
- **s3 (16 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When the first ant reaches the sugar, start the timer. Write 1 ant at 0 minutes. 2. Every 2 minutes, count the ants inside the circle and write the number. 3. Go on for 16 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* The first ant is here. Start the clock. Every two minutes, count everyone inside the circle.  
  *Child mode:* Start the timer when the first ant arrives. Count the ants in the circle every 2 minutes. Write each number. Go on for 16 minutes.
  - Ask: How do the later ants know where to come? (good: The first ants lay a scent trail on the way home, and the others follow the scent.)
  - Note: If there are too many to count one by one, count one half of the circle and double it. The numbers usually rise slowly for a few minutes and then quickly.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift the small square with the sugar off the paper. Tap it gently so the ants on it drop back into the circle. 2. Put the square far away, in another room. 3. Go on counting the ants inside the circle every 2 minutes for 10 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* The food is gone now. Keep counting. Do the ants know yet?  
  *Child mode:* Lift the sugar square away. Tap the ants off gently. Count the ants in the circle every 2 minutes. Go on for 10 minutes.
  - Note: New ants usually keep arriving for several minutes, because the scent trail is still there. The count falls slowly, not all at once. Afterwards, put the sugar square outside for the ants.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a line graph of ants against minutes, or read the counts aloud and say where the jump was biggest. 2. Mark the minute when the sugar was taken away. 3. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your graph. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your line graph (or say where the biggest jump was). Mark when the sugar went. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: Owls and snakes eat rats. If rats suddenly had far more food, would the owls and snakes multiply at once? (good: No. Their numbers would rise later, because raising young takes time.; The eaters follow the food late, as the ants did.)
  - Check: In Ants and the Sugar Drop, what happened after the sugar was taken away? → **Ants kept coming for some minutes**

### a2 · Take the Snakes Away: follow a change through a web (20 min)
- **record:** Write the steps as a chain of changes, such as: snakes gone → rats more → paddy less.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write six names: Paddy, Grasshopper, Rat, Frog, Rat snake, Owl. 2. Draw six arrows. Paddy goes to the grasshopper and to the rat. The grasshopper goes to the frog. The frog and the rat go to the rat snake. The rat also goes to the owl. 3. Teach the two rules.  
  *Parent says:* Here are two rules. When an eater becomes fewer, their food becomes more. When a food becomes fewer, their eaters become fewer too.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the six beings and six arrows. Learn two rules. Fewer eaters means more of their food. Less food means fewer eaters.
  - Note: The rules also work the other way round. More eaters means less of their food, and more food means more eaters.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cross out the rat snake. Ask who is changed first, and how. Put a coin heads up for 'more' and tails up for 'fewer'. 2. Ask who is changed next. 3. Go on until every being has a coin. 4. Ask what happens to the paddy.  
  *Parent says:* People have killed the rat snakes. Who feels it first? Will there be more of them, or fewer? Why? Who feels it next?  
  *Child mode:* Cross out the snake. Who changes first? Heads means more. Tails means fewer. Go on until every being has a coin.
  - Ask: What happens to the paddy? (good: More rats eat more paddy, so the paddy goes down.; More frogs eat more grasshoppers, so the paddy is eaten less by grasshoppers. The two paths push opposite ways.)
  - Note: Step one: rats become more and frogs become more. Step two: more rats eat more paddy, more frogs eat more grasshoppers, and more rats can feed more owls later. Step three: fewer grasshoppers eat less paddy. So the paddy is pushed down by one path and up by another. Farmers' experience is that the rats win, and the harvest falls.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take the coins off and bring the snake back. 2. Ask your child to choose a different change, such as catching all the frogs or putting up an owl perch. 3. Ask them to follow it through with the coins. 4. Ask which effects are direct and which are indirect.  
  *Parent says:* Now you choose the change. Follow it through the web. Which effect is direct, along one arrow? Which is indirect, through someone in between?  
  *Child mode:* Bring the snake back. Choose a new change. Follow it with the coins. Which effects are direct? Which are indirect?
  - Check: People kill the rat snakes in a paddy field. Which is a direct effect? → **The rats become more**
### a3 · Two Out of Thousands: why prey animals have many young (15 min)
- **record:** Keep the page. Under each drawing write the share who live, such as '2 in 4,000'.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child that a pair of bullfrogs may leave about 4,000 eggs in their lives. 2. Say that the number of frogs in their pond stays about the same from year to year. 3. Ask how many of the 4,000 can live to be parents.  
  *Parent says:* A pair of frogs leaves about 4,000 eggs. The pond has about the same number of frogs every year. So how many of those 4,000 grow up to be parents? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Two frogs leave 4,000 eggs. The pond keeps the same number of frogs. How many of the 4,000 become parents? Say why.
  - Note: The answer is about 2. Two parents are replaced by two young. If 3 lived, the frogs would grow by half in every generation. Let your child reason it out. Do not give the answer yet.
- **s2 (7 min)**

## Challenges
### c1 · A Web That Holds: 8 beings, 12 arrows (30 min)
- **problem:** Design a food web with 2 producers and 6 animals, joined by exactly 12 arrows. When any 2 beings are taken away, as few others as possible should be lost to hunger.
- **your turn:** Now design your own web. Draw it, test it with 5 draws and write the average number of beings left. Then move one arrow and test again. Did it improve?
- **constraints:** Every animal must have at least one food, and every arrow goes from a food to an eater.; At least 2 of the animals must eat other animals.; Test with 5 draws of 2 name cards, using the hunger rule: an animal with no food left is lost.
- **general:** Give every animal exactly two foods.; Let both producers feed several herbivores.; Give the top predator two different prey.
- **creative:** Add an omnivore who eats one plant and one animal.; Give three foods to the animal that most others depend on.; Make sure no animal depends only on another animal with one food.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose 2 producers and 6 animals. They can be real or made up. 2. Draw them with producers at the bottom. 3. Add exactly 12 arrows from foods to eaters. 4. Write the 8 names on 8 cards.  
  *Parent says:* You have only 12 arrows. Where will each one do the most good? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Choose 2 plants and 6 animals. Draw them. Add exactly 12 arrows. Write the 8 names on cards.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a coin on every being. 2. Shake the cards and draw 2. Remove those coins. 3. Use the hunger rule (an animal with no food left is lost) until nobody else is lost. 4. Count who is left and write it down. 5. Do this 5 times and find the average.  
  *Parent says:* Two beings are gone. Does anyone have no food left? Check again after each loss.  
  *Child mode:* Put a coin on every being. Draw 2 cards. Remove those coins. Remove any animal with no food left. Count who is left. Do it 5 times.
  - Note: The best possible score is 6 left. A web where every animal has two different foods scores 5 or 6 in nearly every draw.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find the animal who was lost most often. 2. Move one arrow to help that animal. 3. Test 5 more times. 4. Compare the two averages.  
  *Parent says:* Who was lost most often? Why? Which arrow would you move to help?  
  *Child mode:* Move one arrow to help that animal. Test 5 more times. Compare your two averages.
  - Check: You have a fixed number of arrows. Which design loses the fewest animals to hunger? → **Every animal gets at least two foods**

## Worksheets
### Reading a Food Web (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In a food web, what does an arrow from grass to hare mean?  
   Options: The grass eats the hare / The hare hides in the grass / The grass grows near hares / The hare eats the grass  
   **Answer:** The hare eats the grass. *Why:* An arrow points from the food to the eater, the way the food goes. Reading it backwards is a common slip, because we think of the hunter going towards the food.
2. (truefalse) True or false: every food chain in a web starts with a producer.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Only producers make food from sunlight, air and water. A strong answer adds that every animal eats plants, or eats animals who ate plants.
3. (fill) An animal who eats both plants and animals is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['omnivore']. *Why:* 'Omni' means 'all'. Crows, bears and humans are omnivores.
4. (sort) Sort these animals by what they eat.  
   **Answer:** chital → Herbivore; frog → Carnivore; crow → Omnivore; vulture → Scavenger; elephant → Herbivore; leopard → Carnivore; bear → Omnivore; hyena → Scavenger. *Why:* Chital and elephants eat only plants. Frogs and leopards eat only animals. Crows and bears eat both. Vultures and hyenas eat animals who are already dead.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Producer → makes food from sunlight, air and water; Predator → hunts and eats other animals; Prey → is hunted and eaten; Scavenger → eats animals who are already dead; Decomposer → breaks dead things down into the soil. *Why:* These words are needed to talk about any food web. A strong answer could give an example of each from a paddy field.
6. (mcq) Sea otters eat sea urchins, and urchins eat kelp. Hunters kill most of the otters. What happens to the kelp?  
   Options: The kelp is eaten down / The kelp grows thicker / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** The kelp is eaten down. *Why:* With few otters, the urchins multiply and eat the kelp down. Choosing 'grows thicker' means following only one step. The change passes down two links.
7. (write) In the Two Webs Test, the wide web had more beings left. Explain why, using the word 'food'.  
   **Answer:** In the wide web, most animals had more than one food. When one food was taken away, they still had another to eat. In the narrow web each animal had only one food, so they starved when that food went.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the number of foods each eater has made the difference. It uses a result from the test.
8. (draw) Draw a food web with 2 plants and 4 animals. Draw every arrow from the food to the eater.  
   **Answer:** A web with two producers at the bottom and four animals above. Each animal has at least one arrow coming in from a food.. *Why:* A strong drawing has every arrow pointing from the food to the eater. Every path starts at a plant, and at least one animal has two foods.
9. (odd) Three of these are consumers. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Frog / Paddy plant / Owl / Rat  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* A paddy plant is a producer, who makes their own food. The frog, the owl and the rat must all eat other living beings.
10. (truefalse) True or false: a frog is a herbivore, because frogs are small.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A frog eats insects, and insects are animals, so a frog is a carnivore. The group does not depend on size.
11. (order) A forest loses all its leopards and dholes. Put what follows in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The chital multiply', 'The chital eat down the grass and shrubs', 'The chital run short of food', 'Many chital starve']. *Why:* With no predators, the herbivores increase until they eat down the plants. Then they starve. Removing the top of a web harms the bottom, and then the middle.
12. (fill) A change that reaches a being through a third being in between is called an ____ effect.  
   **Answer:** ['indirect']. *Why:* A direct effect passes along one link. An indirect effect passes through someone in between, as from a snake through the rats to the paddy.
13. (perspective) A rat snake is found in a paddy field. Two neighbours disagree about what to do.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the web: the snake eats rats, and rats eat paddy, so killing the snake costs grain. It also takes the fear seriously: some snakes are dangerous, so people should learn to tell them apart and wear shoes in the field.. *Why:* Both neighbours care about the village. The web shows what killing the snake would cost, and the fear shows what people need to feel safe.
14. (mcq) Why does a frog lay thousands of eggs, while a tigress has only two to four cubs?  
   Options: Most frog eggs and tadpoles are eaten / Frogs live much longer than tigers / Tigers have less food than frogs  
   **Answer:** Most frog eggs and tadpoles are eaten. *Why:* Most of a frog's young become food for others, so she needs thousands for a few to be left. A tigress's cubs are seldom eaten, and she guards each one.
15. (write) In the Leaky Bottle, the level stayed still while water ran through. How is a deer herd like that?  
   **Answer:** Fawns are born and deer die all the time, like water running in and out. If births and deaths match, the number of deer stays about the same, like the water level.. *Why:* A strong answer matches the tap to births, the hole to deaths and the level to the number of deer.
### Following a Change (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Owls eat rats, and rats eat paddy. All the owls leave a field. Which is the indirect effect?  
   Options: There is less paddy / There are more rats / There are fewer owl nests  
   **Answer:** There is less paddy. *Why:* More rats is the direct effect, along one link. Less paddy is indirect, because it reaches the paddy through the rats.
2. (write) Insect poison sprayed on paddy also kills the spiders. Explain why the plant-sucking insects may then become more, not fewer.  
   **Answer:** Spiders eat the plant-sucking insects. The poison kills the spiders as well as some of the insects. The insects who are left breed quickly, and now few spiders are there to eat them.. *Why:* A strong answer names the link from the insect to the spider. It says that prey usually multiply faster than their predators.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a keystone species is always the most common animal in a place.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A keystone species is often few in number. What marks them is how much the web changes when they are lost.
4. (fill) A specialist eats one food. An eater of many kinds of food is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['generalist']. *Why:* A generalist can turn to another food when one is lost. Crows, rats and humans are generalists.
5. (sort) Specialist or generalist? Sort these eaters.  
   **Answer:** koala → Specialist; crow → Generalist; giant panda → Specialist; house rat → Generalist; silkworm → Specialist; human → Generalist. *Why:* A koala eats eucalyptus leaves, a giant panda eats bamboo and a silkworm eats mulberry leaves. Crows, house rats and humans eat many kinds of food.
6. (mcq) Hares peak in year 4. The lynx who eat them peak in year 6. Why are the lynx late?  
   Options: Lynx sleep for two years / Raising more young takes time / Hares hide for two years  
   **Answer:** Raising more young takes time. *Why:* More food lets more lynx kittens live, but kittens take time to be born and grow. So the number of eaters follows the food late.
7. (draw) Sketch a line graph of ants at a sugar drop. The sugar is removed at 16 minutes. Mark that point.  
   **Answer:** A line that rises slowly, then quickly, and levels off. After the mark at 16 minutes the line falls slowly, not straight down.. *Why:* A strong sketch shows the slow start and the slow fall after the food is gone. Both show that eaters follow their food late.
8. (perspective) Rats are eating the paddy in a village. Three people suggest what to do.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that poison works fast but can kill the owls and snakes. Then the rats return with fewer hunters. Owls and snakes work every night, but they cannot stop a sudden flood of rats alone. It chooses a plan for one season and a plan for ten years, and says why they differ.. *Why:* There is a real trade-off between a quick fix and keeping the web's own rat-catchers alive.
9. (order) The bamboo in Mizoram fruits all at once. Put what follows in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Bamboo fruit covers the ground', 'Rats feed and multiply fast', 'The bamboo fruit is finished', 'The rats move into the rice fields', 'The rats starve and become few']. *Why:* A flood of food at the bottom raises the eaters. When that food ends, the eaters move along another link, and then crash.
10. (write) A second hole lowered the level in the Leaky Bottle. What change in a forest is like a second hole?  
   **Answer:** A new cause of death that stays, such as a new predator, a disease or hunting. The population settles at a new, lower number.. *Why:* A strong answer names a lasting change. It says the number settles at a new level and does not return to the old one.
11. (match) Match each change that people made to what followed.  
   **Answer:** Sea otters were hunted → Urchins ate down the kelp forests; Starfish were removed from a shore → Mussels crowded out other animals; A cattle medicine poisoned vultures → Stray dogs fed on the dead cattle; Nile perch were put in a lake → Many kinds of small fish died out; Lantana was planted in gardens → Forest grass was shaded out. *Why:* Each of these really happened. In every case the change passed on to beings that nobody had meant to harm.
12. (mcq) A pond's frogs stay the same in number. One pair leaves 4,000 eggs. About how many become parents?  
   Options: 4,000 / 2,000 / 400 / 2  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Two parents are replaced by about two young. If more lived, the number of frogs would grow every year. Choosing 2,000 means halving the eggs, but nearly all the eggs are eaten.
13. (odd) Three of these were brought to India by people and then spread widely. Which one was not?  
   Options: Lantana / Water hyacinth / Neem / Giant African snail  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Neem is native to India. Lantana and water hyacinth came from the Americas, and the giant African snail from Africa.
14. (label) Hunters kill most sea otters. Label each being 'more' or 'fewer': kelp → sea urchin → sea otter.  
   **Answer:** ['Sea otter: fewer', 'Sea urchin: more', 'Kelp: fewer']. *Why:* Each step down the chain flips. Fewer otters means more urchins, and more urchins means less kelp.
15. (write) The Two Webs Test followed hunger only. Name one thing in a real forest that the test left out.  
   **Answer:** When predators are lost, their prey can multiply and eat down the plants. Animals can also move away, find new foods or die of disease.. *Why:* A strong answer names a real effect the paper model cannot show, such as prey multiplying when a predator goes.
### How Ecologists Test Their Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) A scientist removes starfish from one stretch of shore and leaves the next untouched. Why is the untouched stretch needed?  
   **Answer:** Without that stretch, nobody could tell what caused the change. It might be the missing starfish, or something else, such as the weather that year. The untouched stretch shows what happens when nothing is removed.. *Why:* A strong answer says the two stretches differ in only one thing, so that one thing must be the cause.
2. (perspective) Does a food web with more links stay steadier? Two scientists disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the Two Webs Test supports the forest ecologist, because the wide web lost fewer beings. It then says the test measured only hunger after a loss. It did not measure how much numbers swing, or how a disease or poison spreads along links.. *Why:* Both are partly right. The answer depends on what 'steady' means and on how strong the links are.
3. (mcq) Which test would best show whether owl perches reduce rats in paddy fields?  
   Options: Ask one farmer what she thinks / Count the rats in one field once / Compare similar fields with and without perches / Count the owls in a zoo  
   **Answer:** Compare similar fields with and without perches. *Why:* Comparing similar fields that differ only in the perches is a fair test. One farmer's opinion or one count cannot separate the perches from the weather or the season.
4. (write) Losing the snakes pushes the paddy down by one path and up by another. How could you find the stronger path?  
   **Answer:** Compare real fields with snakes and without snakes. Measure the harvest and count the rats and grasshoppers in each, for a few seasons.. *Why:* A strong answer says a drawing cannot settle it, and proposes measuring real fields that differ only in the snakes.
5. (truefalse) True or false: if a population stays the same size, no animals in that population are dying.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Animals are born and die all the time. The number stays the same when births and deaths match, as the level did in the Leaky Bottle.
6. (mcq) A second equal hole dropped the Leaky Bottle's level from 12 cm to 3 cm. Why not to 6 cm?  
   Options: The second hole was bigger / The tap slowed down / Shallow water leaves each hole more slowly  
   **Answer:** Shallow water leaves each hole more slowly. *Why:* At 6 cm deep, each jet is only a little slower than at 12 cm. So two holes still let out more than the tap brings in. The level keeps falling until two slow jets together match the tap.
7. (write) Mizoram's bamboo fruits all together, once in about 48 years. Give one way this could help the bamboo's seeds.  
   **Answer:** There is far more fruit at once than the rats and birds can eat. So many seeds are left to grow. In the long gap between, there is no fruit, so the seed eaters stay few.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a flood of food swamps the eaters. It may add that this is an idea that is hard to test.
8. (fill) When a change causes something that pushes back against that change, this is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['feedback']. *Why:* More deer leads to more deaths, which brings the number of deer back down. That push back is feedback.
9. (sort) Does each change start at the top of a web or at the bottom? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** Otters are hunted → Starts at the top; Bamboo fruits all at once → Starts at the bottom; A drought dries the grass → Starts at the bottom; Snakes are killed → Starts at the top; A forest is cut → Starts at the bottom; Owls are poisoned → Starts at the top. *Why:* A change to an eater starts at the top and passes down. A change to a producer or a food starts at the bottom and passes up.
10. (mcq) Chilli, tomato and lantana all came to India from the Americas. Why is only lantana called invasive?  
   Options: Lantana arrived most recently / Lantana spreads unaided and harms other beings / Lantana has no flowers  
   **Answer:** Lantana spreads unaided and harms other beings. *Why:* Invasive does not mean foreign. Chilli and tomato stay where farmers plant them. Lantana spreads through forests without help and shades out the plants already there.
11. (order) Put these steps of a removal test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Choose two similar places', 'Count the living beings in both', 'Remove one kind from one place only', 'Count again for several years', 'Compare the two places']. *Why:* First find two matching places and count. Then change one thing in one place. Then count for long enough to see slow effects, and compare.
12. (write) Some people say the world would be better with no rats at all. Use a food web to answer them.  
   **Answer:** Owls, snakes, cats and many other animals eat rats, so they would lose food. Rats also eat insects and spread seeds. Rats become a problem where their predators are missing or where there is a flood of food.. *Why:* A strong answer shows who depends on rats. It says that 'pest' describes a place and a number, as well as an animal.
13. (match) Match each way of studying a food web to what it can show.  
   **Answer:** Remove one kind and keep a second place untouched → what that kind does in the web; Count animals every year for many years → waves and delays in numbers; Compare islands with and without a predator → a cascade, without removing anyone; Test a web on paper or on a computer → what could happen, quickly; Study droppings and stomach contents → who eats whom. *Why:* Each method answers a different question. Ecologists use several together, because each one misses something.
14. (odd) Three of these changes add a being to a web. Which one removes a being?  
   Options: Water hyacinth reaches a lake / Nile perch are put into a lake / Vultures die from a cattle medicine / Lantana is planted in a garden  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The vultures were lost from the web. The other three are new arrivals. Webs can be changed in both ways.
15. (label) The Leaky Bottle is a model of a deer herd. Label what the tap, hole, level and second hole stand for.  
   **Answer:** ['Tap: births', 'Hole: deaths', 'Water level: the number of deer', 'Second hole: a new cause of death']. *Why:* A model is useful only when each part stands for something real. A strong answer can also name one thing the bottle leaves out.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A food web has 10 beings and 18 arrows. What does each arrow show?  
   Options: One animal chasing another / One food passing to one eater / Two animals living together / Which animal is bigger  
   **Answer:** One food passing to one eater. *Why:* Each arrow is one link. It points from a food to an eater of that food.
2. (mcq) A jackal hunts hares, eats fruit and feeds on dead animals. Which groups fit a jackal?  
   Options: Herbivore only / Decomposer / Omnivore and scavenger / Producer  
   **Answer:** Omnivore and scavenger. *Why:* Hares and fruit make a jackal an omnivore. Feeding on dead animals makes a jackal a scavenger too. Many animals fit more than one group.
3. (mcq) What do decomposers do in a food web?  
   Options: Hunt the top predators / Make food from sunlight / Return dead matter to the soil / Eat only living plants  
   **Answer:** Return dead matter to the soil. *Why:* Fungi and bacteria break dead plants and animals down into simple matter. Plants take that matter up again through their roots.
4. (mcq) Owls eat rats, and rats eat paddy. More owls come to a field. What is the indirect effect?  
   Options: Fewer rats / More rats / More paddy / Fewer owls  
   **Answer:** More paddy. *Why:* Fewer rats is the direct effect. More paddy is the indirect effect, because it reaches the paddy through the rats.
5. (truefalse) True or false: a leopard can change how much grass grows, without ever eating grass.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Leopards eat chital, and chital eat grass. Fewer leopards can mean more chital and less grass. This is an indirect effect.
6. (mcq) The grass disappears from two webs. In which web do more animals starve?  
   Options: Where each animal eats several foods / The same number in both / Where each animal eats one food  
   **Answer:** Where each animal eats one food. *Why:* An animal with one food starves when that food goes. An animal with several foods can turn to another. That is what the Two Webs Test showed.
7. (mcq) A forest has about 500 chital year after year. What must be true?  
   Options: No chital are born / No chital die / The chital never move / Births and deaths roughly match  
   **Answer:** Births and deaths roughly match. *Why:* Chital are born and die all the time. The number stays steady because the two roughly match, as the water level did in the Leaky Bottle.
8. (mcq) Why can rats multiply much faster than the owls who eat them?  
   Options: Rats have many young, several times a year / Rats live much longer than owls / Owls eat only one rat a year  
   **Answer:** Rats have many young, several times a year. *Why:* A rat has 6 to 8 young at a time, several times a year. An owl raises a few chicks a year. Prey usually breed faster than their predators.
9. (mcq) Hares suddenly have much more food. Hares suddenly get much more food. When will the number of lynx who eat hares rise?  
   Options: A year or two later / In the same week / Before the hares do / Never  
   **Answer:** A year or two later. *Why:* First the hares increase. Then more lynx kittens live and grow. The eaters follow their food late.
10. (mcq) Why has lantana spread so widely through India's forests?  
   Options: Lantana is native to India / Few animals here eat lantana / Lantana needs no light  
   **Answer:** Few animals here eat lantana. *Why:* Lantana came from the Americas. Deer and cattle here avoid the leaves, so little holds lantana back.
11. (mcq) One kind of starfish is removed from a rocky shore for some years. What follows?  
   Options: Mussels take over and other kinds are lost / Nothing changes / More kinds of animals move in  
   **Answer:** Mussels take over and other kinds are lost. *Why:* The starfish ate mussels and kept space open on the rock. Without them, mussels crowded the others out. One predator was holding the whole community together.

## Applications
- Farmers in Tamil Nadu and other states put up T-shaped perches in paddy fields. Owls sit on them at night and hunt rats, so less poison is needed.
- Forest teams in several Indian tiger reserves pull out lantana by the root, so that grass can return for deer and elephants.
- Before bringing back a lost animal or moving one to a new place, wildlife scientists map the food web to see who will be affected.
- Rice farmers in many Asian countries now spray less insect poison, so that spiders and other hunters of crop pests stay alive.
- Fishery scientists set limits on catches, because taking too many of one kind of fish changes the numbers of the others in the web.
- Doctors and health workers use the same thinking for diseases. When India's vultures were lost, stray dogs are thought to have increased, and so did the danger of dog bites.
- Park managers count animals every few years. A steady count tells them that births and deaths are roughly matching.

## Open questions
- **Can anyone predict what the loss of one kind of living being will do to a real web?** Real webs have thousands of links, strong and weak. Small tests on shores and ponds work, but whole forests and seas still surprise scientists.
- **Are food webs with more links steadier?** Mathematics and field studies have given different answers since 1972. The answer seems to depend on what 'steady' means and on how strong the links are.
- **Why do the hares and lynx of Canada rise and fall about every ten years?** Predators and food both play a part, and scientists still argue about how much each one matters.
- **Why does the bamboo of Mizoram wait about 48 years to flower, and how do the plants all keep time?** Swamping the seed eaters is the leading idea, but a test takes 48 years. Nobody knows how a bamboo counts the years.
- **When a web has been badly changed, can it return to what it was?** Some webs recover when the lost animal comes back. Others settle into a new state and stay there, and nobody can yet tell in advance which will happen.
- **How many links are there in the food web of one Indian forest?** Nobody has mapped all the insects, fungi and microbes of any forest, so every published web is incomplete.

## Connected maths
- In six rounds of the Two Webs Test, the narrow web had 5, 6, 3, 5, 5 and 6 left. What is the average? **5 beings.**. Add the six rounds: 5 + 6 + 3 + 5 + 5 + 6 = 30. Divide by the number of rounds: 30 ÷ 6 = 5. So on average 5 of the 10 beings were left. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- The narrow web has 10 beings and 7 links. The wide web has 10 beings and 18 links. How many links for each being does each web have? **0.7 in the narrow web and 1.8 in the wide web.**. Divide the links by the beings. Narrow web: 7 ÷ 10 = 0.7. Wide web: 18 ÷ 10 = 1.8. The wide web has more than twice as many links for each being. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Leaky Bottle, the level settled at 12 cm with one hole. With two equal holes it settled at 3 cm. If the pattern continues, where would it settle with three holes? **At about 1.3 cm.**. With 1 hole the level is 12. With 2 holes it is 12 ÷ 4 = 3, and 4 is 2 × 2. So with 3 holes, divide by 3 × 3 = 9. 12 ÷ 9 is about 1.3 cm. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- Suppose the rats in a village double every month while bamboo fruit lasts. There are 100 rats at the start. How many are there after 6 months? **6,400 rats.**. Double six times: 100, 200, 400, 800, 1,600, 3,200, 6,400. That is 100 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 100 × 64 = 6,400. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- A pair of frogs leaves 4,000 eggs, and 2 of the young live to be parents. What fraction live? What percentage is that? **1 in 2,000, which is 0.05%.**. 2 out of 4,000 is the same as 1 out of 2,000. As a percentage: 2 ÷ 4,000 = 0.0005, and 0.0005 × 100 = 0.05%. That is one twentieth of one percent. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- On the Ten-Year Wave graph, the hares peak in years 4 and 14. The lynx peak in years 6 and 16. The hares peak again in year 24. When should the lynx peak next? **In year 26.**. The lynx peak comes 2 years after each hare peak: 4 + 2 = 6 and 14 + 2 = 16. So after a hare peak in year 24, the lynx peak should come in year 24 + 2 = 26. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
