# Exploring space: why rockets and astronauts must carry their own air
`sci.sky.exploring-space.L3`

**Summary.** A rocket's engine is a fire, and a fire needs oxygen from the air. But the air ends a short way above our heads, and space beyond it is almost empty. So a rocket carries its own oxygen as well as its fuel, and astronauts carry their own air. Telescopes let us explore from the ground, because they collect far more light than our eyes can. India explores space too: its rockets launch from Sriharikota, Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole in 2023, and Indian astronauts have flown to space.

**Intro.** *A rocket's engine burns fuel, and burning needs air. There is no air in space. So how does a rocket's fire keep burning up there?* In this unit you cover a candle with jars of different sizes and time how long the flame lasts. You make a lime-sized Earth and find how close space really is. You cover half a magnifying glass to find out what a big telescope is for. And you meet the people and machines that India has sent beyond the air: rockets from Thumba and Sriharikota, Chandrayaan on the Moon, Mangalyaan at Mars, and Indian astronauts.

## Learning outcomes

1. I can explain, from my own candle test, why a flame needs air, and why a rocket must carry its own oxygen.
2. I can say how a rocket moves upward, and why it drops its empty parts on the way.
3. I can explain that a telescope's main job is to collect light, and why bigger telescopes see fainter things.
4. I can say what an astronaut needs to stay alive in space, and where each thing comes from.
5. I can name some of India's space missions, including Chandrayaan-3 and Mangalyaan, and say what each one did.
6. I can tell how Chandrayaan-1 helped show that the Moon is not completely dry.

## Terms
- **Space**: Everything beyond the Earth's air. It is almost empty: no air to breathe, and no air to carry sound. *e.g.* The Moon, the Sun and the stars are all out in space.
- **Atmosphere**: The layer of air around the Earth. It gets thinner and thinner the higher you go. *e.g.* Climbers on very high mountains carry oxygen tanks, because the air up there is thin.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air. People and animals breathe it in, and fires need it to burn. About one fifth of the air is oxygen. *e.g.* A diya goes out if you cover it with a tumbler, because the flame soon runs short of oxygen.
- **Fuel**: Something that burns to give heat and light, or to push an engine. *e.g.* Cooking gas is a fuel for the stove. Rockets burn fuels such as kerosene or liquid hydrogen.
- **Rocket**: A machine that moves by pushing hot gas out of its back very fast. The gas pushes the rocket the other way. *e.g.* India's LVM3 rocket carried Chandrayaan-3 into space in 2023.
- **Launch**: Sending a rocket up from the ground into space. *e.g.* Indian rockets are launched from Sriharikota, an island on the coast of Andhra Pradesh.
- **Astronaut**: A person trained to travel and work in space. *e.g.* Rakesh Sharma was the first Indian astronaut, in 1984.
- **Spacesuit**: A suit that holds air around an astronaut's body and keeps them at a safe temperature outside the spacecraft. *e.g.* An astronaut walking outside the space station wears a spacesuit with an oxygen pack on the back.
- **Telescope**: A tool that collects light from faraway things with a big lens or a curved mirror, so we can see them more clearly. *e.g.* Through a small telescope you can see craters on the Moon.
- **Lens**: A curved piece of glass or clear plastic that bends light to make a picture. *e.g.* A magnifying glass is a lens. So is the clear front part of your eye.
- **Satellite**: Something that goes round and round a planet. The Moon is a natural satellite. People also build satellites and send them up on rockets. *e.g.* Weather satellites take pictures of clouds over India so we know when a cyclone is coming.
- **Orbit**: The curved path of something going round a planet, a moon or a star. *e.g.* The International Space Station orbits the Earth about 16 times a day.
- **Lander and rover**: A lander is a spacecraft that comes down gently on to a moon or planet. A rover is a robot car that drives about on it. *e.g.* Chandrayaan-3's lander was called Vikram, and its rover was called Pragyan.
- **Mission**: A planned journey or job in space, with a team of people on the ground who run it. *e.g.* Mangalyaan was India's mission to Mars.

## Explanations
### Where space begins · *defined*
**Definition:** Space is what lies beyond the Earth's atmosphere. Many scientists use a line 100 kilometres up as the edge of space.

The air is thickest at the ground. Every step higher, there is less of it. On top of Mount Everest, nearly 9 kilometres up, each breath holds only about a third as much air as at the beach. Planes fly about 10 to 12 kilometres up. At 100 kilometres there is so little air that a plane's wings cannot hold it up. Above that is space. It is not that the air stops at a wall. It just thins out until there is almost none. 100 kilometres is a little less than the distance from Bengaluru to Mysuru. Space is that close above your head.
- e.g. On a lime-sized Earth, the edge of space is less than half a millimetre above the skin.
- e.g. The International Space Station flies about 400 kilometres up.
- e.g. Climbers on Mount Everest often carry oxygen tanks because the air is so thin.
### Why a flame needs air · *defined*
**Definition:** Burning is fuel joining with oxygen. It gives out heat and light. When the oxygen around a flame runs low, the flame goes out.

A candle flame burns wax gas, and it uses oxygen from the air around it. In the jar test, the flame could use only the air inside the jar. A small glass held only a little air, so the flame soon ran short and went out. A big jar held more air, so the flame lasted longer. The flame does not use up every bit of oxygen. It stops once there is too little left near the wick. Fresh air rushing in is why blowing gently on coals makes them glow brighter.
- e.g. A diya covered by a steel tumbler goes out in a few seconds.
- e.g. Fanning a coal stove makes it burn hotter, because it brings more oxygen.
- e.g. A fire blanket puts out a pan fire by keeping the air away.
### Why a rocket carries its own oxygen · *defined*
**Definition:** A rocket engine burns fuel with oxygen it carries inside the rocket, so it can work where there is no air.

A car or a plane engine takes oxygen from the air around it. A rocket cannot do that, because it flies into space, where there is no air. So a rocket carries two things: a fuel, and an oxidiser. An oxidiser is oxygen, or a chemical full of oxygen, that lets the fuel burn. Some rockets carry liquid oxygen. Oxygen turns liquid only when it is colder than minus 183 degrees Celsius, so these tanks must be kept very cold. Other rockets burn a rubbery solid fuel that has the oxidiser mixed into it. India's PSLV uses both kinds in its different parts.
- e.g. India's LVM3 rocket has an engine that burns liquid hydrogen with liquid oxygen.
- e.g. The big side boosters on many rockets burn a solid fuel with the oxidiser already mixed in.
- e.g. A plane cannot fly into space, because its engines need air.
### How a rocket moves up · *defined*
**Definition:** A rocket pushes hot gas out of its engine downwards, very fast. The gas pushes back on the rocket and drives it upwards.

Burning makes a huge amount of hot gas inside the engine. The gas rushes out of the bottom of the rocket. As the rocket pushes the gas down, the gas pushes the rocket up, just as hard. This works in space too, because the rocket is pushing on its own gas, not on the air. Most of a rocket's weight at launch is fuel and oxygen. As they burn away, the rocket gets lighter and goes faster. Rockets are built in parts called stages. When a stage's tanks are empty, it drops off, so the rocket does not carry dead weight. PSLV has four stages.
- e.g. When you let go of a blown-up balloon, the air rushes out one way and the balloon flies the other way.
- e.g. A rocket's first stage falls into the sea after a few minutes.
- e.g. Diwali rockets work the same way, pushing hot gas down out of their tail.
### What a telescope is really for · *defined*
**Definition:** A telescope collects light from faraway things with a large lens or a curved mirror, and brings it together to make a picture.

The black circle in the middle of your eye, the pupil, is at most about 7 millimetres across in the dark. Only the light that falls into that small hole reaches you. A telescope's lens or mirror is far bigger, so it catches far more light. The light from many faint stars is too weak for our eyes. A big telescope gathers enough of it to see them. That is why the biggest telescopes are built with mirrors many metres across. In your half-lens test, covering half the lens did not cut off half the picture. The whole window stayed, only dimmer. Every part of a lens collects light from every part of the view, so a bigger lens gives a brighter picture.
- e.g. Galileo's small telescope in 1610 showed him many more stars than anyone had ever seen.
- e.g. The Devasthal telescope near Nainital has a mirror 3.6 metres wide.
- e.g. The Vainu Bappu Observatory at Kavalur in Tamil Nadu has a telescope with a mirror 2.3 metres wide.
### Why some telescopes go into space · *defined*
**Definition:** Space telescopes orbit above the air, so the air cannot blur their pictures or block some kinds of light.

When you look at stars from the ground, they twinkle. Twinkling is the moving air bending their light this way and that. The air also blocks some kinds of light, such as X-rays and much of the ultraviolet, before it reaches the ground. A telescope above the air sees sharp, steady stars, and catches light that never reaches us. The Hubble Space Telescope has orbited the Earth since 1990. The James Webb Space Telescope was launched in 2021. India launched its own space telescope, AstroSat, in 2015. In 2023 India sent Aditya-L1 to watch the Sun from far out in space, about 1.5 million kilometres away.
- e.g. Stars twinkle, but a space telescope sees them as steady points.
- e.g. AstroSat studies light from stars and galaxies, including ultraviolet and X-rays.
- e.g. Aditya-L1 watches the Sun all the time, with no night and no clouds in the way.
### Staying alive as an astronaut · *defined*
**Definition:** Astronauts need air to breathe, water, food, a safe temperature, and protection from the Sun's harsh light. All of these must be brought from Earth or made from things brought from Earth.

A spacecraft is a sealed bubble of air. Fans keep the air moving, because in orbit warm air does not rise, so breathed-out air could stay around an astronaut's face. On the International Space Station, machines make oxygen by splitting water with electricity. That water came up from Earth on rockets. Most of the station's water is cleaned and used again, even the water from sweat and urine. Food comes up from Earth in packets. Outside, an astronaut wears a spacesuit, which is like a small spacecraft shaped like a person. The astronauts on the station float because they and the station are falling round the Earth together, not because there is no gravity there.
- e.g. One person breathes in a little less than 1 kilogram of oxygen in a day.
- e.g. On the space station, the water from yesterday's sweat can be today's drinking water, after it is cleaned.
- e.g. Astronauts strap themselves into sleeping bags so they do not float about while they sleep.
### India in space · *defined*
**Definition:** India's space agency is ISRO, the Indian Space Research Organisation. It was set up in 1969, and it builds rockets, satellites and missions to the Moon, Mars and the Sun.

India's first rocket went up from Thumba, near Thiruvananthapuram, on 21 November 1963. Vikram Sarabhai led this work. Thumba was chosen because the Earth's magnetic equator passes close by, which matters for studying the high air. India's first satellite, Aryabhata, was launched in 1975. In 1980, India's own rocket, SLV-3, put the Rohini satellite into orbit. Today ISRO launches from Sriharikota in Andhra Pradesh. Chandrayaan-1 went round the Moon in 2008. Mangalyaan reached Mars orbit on 24 September 2014, on India's first try. Chandrayaan-3 landed near the Moon's south pole on 23 August 2023. No country had landed there before.
- e.g. India's weather satellites track cyclones in the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea.
- e.g. In 2017, one PSLV rocket carried 104 satellites into space at once.
- e.g. Many women led these missions. Ritu Karidhal and Nandini Harinath worked on Mangalyaan, and M. Vanitha was project director of Chandrayaan-2.
### Indian astronauts · *defined*
**Definition:** An Indian astronaut is an Indian citizen who has travelled into space.

Rakesh Sharma, a pilot in the Indian Air Force, was the first Indian in space. On 3 April 1984 he flew on a Soviet rocket and spent about eight days on the Salyut 7 space station. In 2025, Shubhanshu Shukla, also an Air Force pilot, flew to the International Space Station. He was the first Indian to go there. Two women of Indian origin, Kalpana Chawla and Sunita Williams, flew as American astronauts. Kalpana Chawla was born in Karnal, in Haryana. She died in 2003, when the space shuttle Columbia broke up on its way home. ISRO is building Gaganyaan, a spacecraft to carry Indian astronauts on an Indian rocket.
- e.g. Prasanth Balakrishnan Nair, from Palakkad in Kerala, is one of the astronauts chosen for Gaganyaan.
- e.g. Sunita Williams has spent many months on the International Space Station.
- e.g. Rakesh Sharma did yoga in space, to see how it helped his body there.
### Should people go to space, or only robots? · *debated*
Robots and telescopes have done most of our exploring. A robot needs no air, food or water, and if it breaks, no one is hurt. People are far more costly and risky to send. But a person can see something odd and change plans at once, while a robot waits for orders from Earth. People disagree about which is better, and many say we need both.
- e.g. Pragyan, a robot, drove about 100 metres on the Moon in 2023.
- e.g. Twelve astronauts walked on the Moon between 1969 and 1972, and they brought back hundreds of kilograms of rock.
- **People who favour robots:** Robots are cheaper and safer. They can work for years on Mars, in heat and cold that would kill a person. The money saved can send many more missions.
- **People who favour astronauts:** A trained person notices things a robot misses and can fix problems on the spot. Some places, like the Moon, may one day be homes, and only people can learn how to live there.
- **Many space scientists:** We need both. Robots go first, to the most dangerous places. People follow where their skill matters most.

## Mostly agreed
- The air around the Earth gets thinner with height, and above about 100 kilometres there is almost none. *How we know:* Balloons, planes and rockets have carried instruments up and measured the air at every height.
- Burning needs oxygen. A flame cut off from fresh air goes out. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with a candle and a jar. Bigger jars hold more air and keep the flame alight longer.
- A rocket can move in space because it pushes its own hot gas out behind it. *How we know:* Thousands of rockets and spacecraft have fired their engines in empty space and changed their speed.
- Bigger telescopes collect more light and show fainter things. *How we know:* Each time a larger telescope has been built, it has found stars and galaxies too faint for the ones before.
- There is water on the Moon, mostly as ice in dark craters near the poles and as traces in the dust. *How we know:* Chandrayaan-1, two other spacecraft and new tests on old Apollo rocks all found signs of it.

## How we found out
**ISRO's Chandrayaan-1 team, and Carle Pieters, an American scientist who led NASA's Moon Mineralogy Mapper on board, 1969 to 1972 (Moon rocks brought back), 2008 (Chandrayaan-1 launched), 2009 (water traces reported) and 2018 (polar ice found in its data).** Between 1969 and 1972, astronauts brought back about 380 kilograms of rock and dust from the Moon. Scientists tested it for water and found almost none. The few traces they did find, they blamed on damp air from Earth getting into the boxes. So for nearly 40 years, most scientists believed the Moon was bone dry. On 22 October 2008, India launched its first Moon mission, Chandrayaan-1, from Sriharikota. It carried an instrument from NASA called the Moon Mineralogy Mapper, led by the American scientist Carle Pieters. It measured the sunlight bouncing off the Moon. Water soaks up some kinds of light, which our eyes cannot see, and leaves a gap in the reflected light. The Mapper found that gap over large parts of the Moon, strongest near the poles. In September 2009, Pieters and her team reported thin traces of water in the Moon's dust. Two other spacecraft had seen the same. In 2008, scientists also found tiny amounts of water inside glass beads in the old Apollo rocks. In 2018, a study of Chandrayaan-1's data found ice in dark craters at both poles. The dry Moon idea was wrong.  
*What people believed before:* Most scientists thought the Moon was completely dry, because the Apollo rocks seemed to have no water. Chandrayaan-1 measured the light reflected by the Moon and found the gap that water leaves, over much of the surface. Later its data showed ice in the dark polar craters.

## Challenging perspectives
- **There is no gravity in space. That is why astronauts float.** (A very common idea, even among grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Astronauts on the space station float, and so does everything around them.  
  Against: At the height of the space station, the Earth's gravity is still about nine tenths as strong as on the ground. The station and the astronauts float because they are falling round the Earth together, all the time.
- **A telescope's main job is to make things look bigger.** (Most people, before they use one; *rejected*)  
  For: A telescope does make the Moon look bigger.  
  Against: Stars stay as points even in huge telescopes, because they are so far away. What a big telescope really gives is more light, so it can show faint things that our eyes cannot see.
- **The Moon is completely dry.** (Most scientists, from the 1970s until about 2009; *rejected*)  
  For: The rocks that astronauts brought back seemed to have no water at all.  
  Against: Chandrayaan-1 found signs of water spread thinly over the Moon in 2009. Later studies found ice in dark craters at the poles.
- **Spending money on space is a waste when people on Earth need help.** (Some people in India and around the world; *debated*)  
  For: Rockets and missions cost a great deal of money that could be spent on food, schools or hospitals.  
  Against: Satellites warn of cyclones, guide fishing boats and carry phone and TV signals, which save lives and money. Space spending is a small share of what most countries spend.
- **People will soon live on Mars.** (Some space companies and their founders; *debated*)  
  For: Rockets are getting cheaper, and robots have shown that Mars has ice that could give water and oxygen.  
  Against: Mars has very thin air with almost no oxygen, deadly cold and harmful radiation. Nobody yet knows how to keep people healthy there for years.

## Roots
We have timed a candle under jars, found how thin the air is, and followed rockets and astronauts beyond it. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Under the bigger jar, your candle burned longer, because it had more air to use. A flame goes out when the oxygen around it runs low. Space has no air, so rockets carry their own oxygen and astronauts carry their own air. Leaving the Earth means taking some of the Earth's air along.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture space as more sky, the same air going up and up. But the air is a thin layer close to the ground, with almost nothing above it. We also think of going into space as leaving the Earth behind. No one has gone without bringing the Earth's air, or water to make it.  
*Evidence:* In The Jar and the Flame, the tealight went out in a few seconds under a small glass, and lasted several times longer under a big jar. In How Far Is Space?, the edge of space was less than half a millimetre above a lime-sized Earth.

- *who am i:* You are a body that needs fresh air every few seconds, like the candle needed it under the jar.
- *what is life:* Plants and algae put oxygen into the air, and you and the candle use it up. Every breath you take was made by living things.
- *what is this world:* The Earth is wrapped in a thin skin of air, thinner on a lime than four sheets of paper, and beyond it space is almost empty.

All the oxygen in the Earth's air was put there by living things, mostly tiny sea microbes, algae and plants, over a very long time. You breathe it, a candle burns it, and a rocket carries it into space as liquid oxygen. An astronaut's breath on the space station comes from water that was carried up from Earth. So one thin layer of air, made by countless living beings, is shared by every flame and every breath, even the ones beyond the sky.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.1:** The senses argued about which of them mattered most. Speech left the body for a year, then sight, then hearing, then the mind. Each time, the body lived on. Then breath began to leave, and all the others were pulled out with it, like pegs pulled up by a horse. So they agreed that breath was the greatest. (An astronaut can go without many comforts for a while. But without air for a few minutes, nothing else matters. That is why every spacecraft and spacesuit is built first of all to hold air.)
- **Carl Sagan, Pale Blue Dot, 1994:** In 1990, the Voyager 1 spacecraft looked back from about 6 billion kilometres away and photographed the Earth as a tiny bright dot. Sagan wrote about that dot: 'That's here. That's home. That's us.' Everyone who has ever lived, lived there. (From far enough away, the whole Earth and its thin air are one small dot. Every astronaut carries a little of that dot's air with them, because it is the only air we have.)
- Science (settled): Almost all the oxygen in the air was made by living things, mostly algae, bacteria and plants, using sunlight.
- Science (idea): Moon rock is nearly half oxygen by weight, locked in minerals. Scientists are testing ways to free it, so future astronauts might not need to carry all their air from Earth.
- Other view, Many space engineers: Air in space is an engineering problem, and engineering can solve it. One day oxygen may be made from Moon rock or from ice on Mars, so people would no longer depend on air from Earth. Calling it a piece of the Earth is a nice thought, but oxygen is oxygen, wherever it comes from.
- Practice, Where does this breath come from? (3 min): Sit near an open window. Put one hand on your tummy. Breathe in slowly through your nose, and feel the cool air come in. Breathe out slowly, and feel the warm air go out. Do it three more times. Now think: this air came in through the window. Plants and tiny sea algae made its oxygen. An astronaut has to bring every breath from here. Take one more slow breath, and open your eyes.
- Ask: If an astronaut breathes air made from water carried up from Earth, have they really left the Earth?
- Ask: The oxygen you breathe was made by plants and algae. Who else's work is inside your breath?
- Ask: One day people may make air from Moon rock. Would that air be any different from the air here?

## Story: The Orange Cloud over Thumba
*Kavya, Arjun and their grandmother are made up. The history is true: India's first rocket went up from Thumba, near Thiruvananthapuram, on 21 November 1963. Rocket parts were carried on bicycles, the old church became the first office and is now a space museum, and that first rocket made a glowing cloud of sodium high in the evening sky so scientists could watch the winds up there.*

The sea at Thumba was turning from blue to grey. Kavya was eight, and she sat on the warm sand with her grandmother. Her little brother Arjun ran up and down, chasing the foam. Far above them, a plane was drawing a thin white line across the sky.

"Ammamma," Arjun shouted, "can that plane fly to the Moon?"

"No, kanna," said Ammamma. "Come and sit, and I will tell you why. I will tell you about the night I saw a rocket go up from this very beach."

Arjun flopped down on the sand. Kavya moved closer.

"I was ten," said Ammamma. "My family lived in a fishing village just here. One day, some scientists came. Their leader was a man called Vikram Sarabhai. They wanted to send rockets up from Thumba, because the Earth's magnetic equator passes right over this place. That made it a special spot to study the air high above us."

"Where did they work?" asked Kavya.

"In our church," said Ammamma. "The bishop and the people of the village agreed to give the land. The church became the scientists' office. I used to peep in at the windows. And do you know how they carried the rocket parts? On bicycles! I saw a young man pedalling slowly down the sandy road with a shining metal cone tied on the back."

Arjun laughed. "A rocket on a cycle!"

"It was a small rocket," said Ammamma. "About as long as two cars. On the evening of 21 November 1963, everyone in the village came out to watch. The sun had just set, like now. There was a roar, and a line of fire went straight up into the sky. Then it was gone."

"Gone where?" asked Arjun.

"Up past where any plane can fly," said Ammamma. "Then, very high up, it let out a puff of a metal called sodium. And a cloud appeared in the sky. A glowing orange cloud, like a great lamp. Down here on the beach it was almost dark. But up there, the cloud was still in sunlight, so it shone."

Kavya looked up at the darkening sky and tried to imagine it.

"Why did they make a cloud?" she asked.

"To see the wind," said Ammamma. "You cannot see wind. But you can watch a cloud move. The scientists photographed the orange cloud from far apart. They saw it stretch and drift, and so they learned how the winds blow up there, where the air is very thin."

Arjun was frowning. "But why can't a plane go there?"

"A plane needs air for two things," said Ammamma. She held up one finger. "Its wings push on the air to hold it up. Where the air is thin, there is too little to push on." She held up a second finger. "And its engines burn fuel. Burning needs oxygen, which is a gas in the air. High up, there is not enough oxygen to keep the fire going."

"Then how did your rocket keep burning?" asked Kavya.

Ammamma smiled. It was the question she had been waiting for.

"When I grew up, I became a chemist," she said. "And I worked at Thumba. My job was to help make the fuel for rockets. A rocket does not take oxygen from the air. It carries its own. Our fuel was a thick, rubbery solid, with the oxygen-giving part mixed right into it. So it could burn even where there is no air at all."

"Like carrying your own lunch," said Kavya slowly, "because there will be no shops where you are going."

"Exactly like that," said Ammamma. "And the people who go up in rockets must carry their own air too. Every breath an astronaut takes, they brought from here."

Arjun took a big breath of sea air and held it, his cheeks puffed out. Then he let it go with a whoosh. "I would need a very big lunch box for my air," he said.

Kavya laughed. "Can we see the church, Ammamma?"

"We can go tomorrow," said Ammamma. "It is a space museum now."

The next morning they walked through the doors of the old church. Inside, under its high roof, stood rockets and models of satellites. There was a photograph of a young man wheeling a bicycle with a rocket part tied on. Arjun pressed his nose to the glass.

Kavya found a small model of Chandrayaan-3's lander. A card beside it said that it had landed near the Moon's south pole in 2023, sixty years after that first rocket from the beach.

"From a bicycle to the Moon," she said.

"From a bicycle to the Moon," said Ammamma. "And every one of those rockets carried its own oxygen."

That evening, back on the sand, Kavya watched the sky turn orange and then grey. She thought about the air around her, the air she could not see. It felt thick and soft here by the sea. But it went up only a little way, and then it thinned to nothing. Beyond it, the stars were coming out.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a plane needs the air twice, once for its wings to push on and once for the oxygen its engines burn. High up there is too little of both. A rocket works where there is no air because it carries its own oxygen and pushes on its own hot gas. Astronauts must carry their air for the same reason. The orange cloud is true history: sodium glows in sunlight, and at dusk the cloud high above was still in sunshine while the beach was dark.

- Ask: Why can a plane not fly to the Moon? Give both of Ammamma's reasons.
- Ask: How did Ammamma's rocket fuel burn where there is no air?
- Ask: Why was the orange cloud still shining when the beach was almost dark?
- Ask: Why did the scientists want to see a cloud move?
- Ask: Kavya said a rocket carrying oxygen is like carrying your own lunch. Can you think of another way to say it?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Jar and the Flame: how long can a candle burn on the air it has? (30 min)
- **question:** If a candle can use only the air trapped inside a jar, does a bigger jar keep it burning longer?
- **predict:** We will cover a lit candle with a small glass, a big glass and a big jar. Under which one will the flame last longest? Why do you think so? Do you think the biggest jar will keep it burning for ever?
- **fair test:** Use the same candle, the same plate and the same person on the timer every time. Only the size of the glass or jar changes. Start the timer the moment the rim touches the plate. Lift the jar and wave it in the air for half a minute between tries, so it is full of fresh air. Do three tries with each one, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights the candle and stays at the table all the time. Tie back long hair and keep sleeves and paper away from the flame. Put the plate on a steady table, away from curtains and fans. The glass may get warm at the top, so the grown-up lifts it by its lower part. Blow the candle out when you finish.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw three bars, one for each container, as tall as its middle time. Write one sentence under the bars that says what the pattern is.
- **results table:** Glass or jar | How many small tumblers of water it holds | My guess | Try 1 (seconds) | Try 2 (seconds) | Try 3 (seconds) | Middle number
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill each container with water, using the small steel tumbler, and count the tumblers. 2. Write the numbers in the table. 3. Pour the water out and dry each container well.  
  *Parent says:* Let's find out how much air each container can hold. We can't measure air easily, but we can measure water. A jar that holds more water holds more air.  
  *Child mode:* Fill each jar with water, one small tumbler at a time. Count the tumblers. Then dry the jars.
  - Note: A small glass might hold 1 tumbler, a big glass 2, and a big jar 5 or more. The numbers will be different in your home. The containers must be completely dry, or drops of water can put the flame out.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the three dry containers and the unlit candle. 2. Ask your child to guess how long the flame will last under each, and why. 3. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* I will cover the candle with each of these, one at a time. How long do you think the flame will keep burning under each one? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many seconds the flame will last under each jar. Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the flame will last longer under that one? (good: Because it has more air inside.; Because the fire needs air, and the big jar has more.)
  - Note: Do not explain yet. Some children think the flame will go out at once under every jar. Some think it will burn for ever under a big jar. Both are good guesses to test.
- **s3 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the plate on a steady table. 2. Put the candle in the middle. 3. Light it, and wait until the flame is its normal size.  
  *Parent says:* I will light the candle. We wait until the flame stands tall and steady before we start.  
  *Child mode:* Fetch a grown-up to light the candle. Wait for a tall, steady flame.
  - Note: Keep the matches with you. Make sure the flame is well inside the smallest container's rim, with space above it.
- **s4 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the small glass upside down just above the flame. 2. Say 'go' and lower it flat on the plate. 3. Your child times until the flame disappears. 4. Lift the glass, wave it for half a minute, and relight the candle if needed. 5. Do three tries for each container.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? Go! Watch the flame closely. Say 'stop' the moment it disappears.  
  *Child mode:* Say go when the glass touches the plate. Watch the flame. Say stop when it vanishes.
  - Note: You will see the flame shrink and turn dim before it goes out, and a thin line of smoke from the wick. With a tealight, a small glass often gives about 5 to 10 seconds, and a 1 litre jar about 25 to 45 seconds. Your times will be different. What matters is the order. If one try is very different from the other two, do a fourth. If the candle will not relight, the wick may be damp: wait a minute.
  - Think: Why did the flame go out, when there was still wax left?
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find the middle number of the three tries for each container. 2. Put the times next to the tumbler counts.  
  *Parent says:* Look at our numbers. The jar that held the most water kept the flame burning longest. Does the time grow when the jar gets bigger?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your numbers. Which jar kept the flame longest? How much air did it hold?
  - Check: The flame went out under every jar, even though wax was left. Why? → **The flame used up the oxygen in the jar, and too little was left to keep it burning**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think, what I saw, and why. 2. Then ask the rocket question.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why? Now, a rocket's engine is a fire. In space there is no air at all. What must a rocket take with it?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why. A rocket's engine is a fire. What must it carry into space?
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: what I think: a flame needs air, and more air keeps it burning longer. What I saw: under the small glass the flame lasted about 8 seconds, and under the big jar about 35 seconds. Why: the flame uses up the oxygen in the air, and a big jar holds more. So a rocket in space must carry its own oxygen, because there is no air there.
  - Check: In space there is no air. What does a rocket carry so that its fuel can burn? → **Its own oxygen, or a chemical full of oxygen**
### e2 · Half a Lens: does covering half a magnifying glass cut the picture in half? (25 min)
- **question:** A magnifying glass can make a picture of a window on paper. If you cover half of the lens, what happens to the picture?
- **predict:** You will make a picture of a window with a magnifying glass. Then you will cover the left half of the lens with a card. What will happen to the picture? Will half of it vanish? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Keep the lens, the paper and the window in the same places all the time. Only change how much of the lens is covered. Look at the same part of the picture each time, such as the window bars. Try each change twice.
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun through the lens, or point the lens at the Sun. Never leave a magnifying glass lying in sunshine, because it can focus sunlight and start a fire. A grown-up cuts the hole in the card.
- **record:** Draw the picture three times: with the whole lens open, with half covered, and with only the small hole. Shade each drawing to show how bright it was.
- **results table:** How much of the lens is open | My guess | Is the whole window still in the picture? | Bright, dimmer or very dim?
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Darken the room, but leave one window open to daylight. 2. Tape the paper to the wall opposite the window, at least 3 metres away. 3. Hold the lens near the paper and slowly move it towards the window until a sharp picture appears.  
  *Parent says:* Let's catch the window on paper. Move the lens slowly, closer to the paper and further away, until the picture is sharp.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the lens near the paper. Move it slowly. Stop when the window looks sharp.
  - Note: A common magnifying glass makes a sharp picture about 10 to 20 cm from the paper. The picture is small and upside down, with the sky at the bottom. Resting your elbow on a chair or a pile of books keeps the lens steady.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the plain card. 2. Ask what will happen to the picture when the card covers the left half of the lens, and why.  
  *Parent says:* I am going to cover the left half of the lens with this card. What will happen to the picture? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The card will cover half the lens. What will the picture do? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen to the picture? (good: Half of it will disappear.; It will get darker.; It will stay the same.)
  - Note: Most children, and many grown-ups, think half the picture will vanish. Do not say what happens. Any guess with a reason is a good guess.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep the lens still. 2. Slide the card slowly across the left half of the lens. 3. Watch the picture. 4. Do it again with the right half.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the picture while I slide the card across. Keep your eyes on the window bars. Is any part missing?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the picture as the card covers half the lens. Is any part missing?
  - Note: The whole window stays in the picture, but it becomes dimmer. Nothing is cut off. If you see a dark edge, the card is too far from the lens: hold it flat against the glass.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now hold the card with the small hole flat against the middle of the lens. 2. Look at the picture.  
  *Parent says:* Now only a tiny part of the lens is open. Is the whole window still there?  
  *Child mode:* Now only a small hole of the lens is open. What does the picture look like?
  - Note: The whole window is still there, but it is very dim. Every part of a lens collects light from every part of the window. So a smaller opening gives the same picture with less light.
  - Check: When you covered half of the lens, what happened to the picture of the window? → **The whole window stayed, but it was dimmer**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for what I think, what I saw and why. 2. Then talk about telescopes.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you think, what you saw, and why. Now think about a telescope. Why do you think the biggest telescopes in the world have mirrors many metres wide?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why. Why are big telescopes so big?
  - Note: A good answer: what I think: a bigger lens collects more light. What I saw: with half the lens covered, the whole window was still there, only dimmer. With a small hole it was very dim. Why: each part of the lens catches light from the whole window. So a giant telescope makes bright pictures of faint stars, because it catches so much of their light.
  - Check: Why are the biggest telescopes built with huge mirrors? → **To collect more light, so faint stars and galaxies show up**
### e3 · Ice in the Crater: why the Moon's ice lasts in the shadows (25 min)
- **question:** Chandrayaan-1's data showed ice in dark craters near the Moon's poles. Does ice last longer in shadow than in sunlight, when the air around is the same?
- **predict:** We will put one ice cube in sunlight and one in the shadow of a book, side by side. Which one will melt first? How much longer do you think the other will last, and why?
- **fair test:** Use ice cubes from the same tray, so they are the same size. Put them on the same kind of plate, a hand-span apart, in the same room. Only the sunlight changes. Check both at the same times.
- **safety:** Do not look at the Sun. Wipe up melted water so no one slips.
- **record:** Draw both cubes at each check, in two rows. Write the time under each drawing.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Cube in sunlight | Cube in shadow
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a patch of sunlight. 2. Stand the book up at the edge of the patch so it casts a shadow beside it. 3. Put one plate in the sun and one in the book's shadow, close together. 4. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* The Moon's poles have craters so deep that sunlight never reaches their floors. Our book is a crater wall. Which cube do you think will melt first? Why?  
  *Child mode:* One cube in sun. One in the book's shadow. Which melts first? Why?
  - Note: Both plates must be in the same air, close together. Only the sunlight is different. If there is no sunshine today, try another day.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put an ice cube on each plate at the same moment. 2. Every 5 minutes, look at both and draw them. 3. Move the book if the shadow moves off the plate.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look again. Which cube is smaller? Is the shadow still on its plate?  
  *Child mode:* Look every 5 minutes. Draw both cubes. Which is smaller?
  - Note: In strong sunlight, the sunny cube shrinks fast and sits in a puddle. The cube in shadow melts too, because the room air is warm, but more slowly. The Sun moves across the sky, so the shadow moves: check it each time.
  - Wait: Check every 5 minutes for about 15 to 20 minutes.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Talk about what you saw. 2. Then talk about the Moon.  
  *Parent says:* Which cube lasted longer, and why? The Moon has almost no air to warm things. So what happens to ice on a crater floor where the sunlight never reaches?  
  *Child mode:* Which lasted longer? On the Moon, where could ice stay for ages?
  - Note: A good answer: the cube in shadow lasted longer, because sunlight carries energy that warms and melts ice. On the Moon there is almost no air to carry warmth into a dark crater, so its floor stays colder than minus 160 degrees Celsius, and ice can stay there for a very long time. That is why Chandrayaan-3 was sent to land near the south pole.
  - Check: Why can ice last for a very long time on the floor of a dark crater near the Moon's pole? → **Sunlight never reaches the crater floor, and there is almost no air to bring warmth in**

## Applications
- Weather satellites track cyclones over the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea, so people can be moved to safety.
- Satellites carry TV signals to dish antennas on rooftops across India.
- Phone maps find where you are using signals from navigation satellites. India has its own system, called NavIC.
- Fishing boats in Kerala and Tamil Nadu get advice on where fish are likely to be, made using satellite pictures of the sea.
- Satellite pictures help farmers and the government watch crops, floods and forest fires.
- Hospitals use oxygen cylinders, like the oxygen tanks that rockets and astronauts carry.
- Ideas made for space, such as water cleaners and light, strong materials, are used on Earth too.
- Telescopes on Earth and in space find asteroids that come close to the Earth, so we know where they are going.

## Open questions
- **How much water is there on the Moon, and could people use it?** We know there is ice in dark polar craters, but not how much or how easy it would be to dig out.
- **Can people stay healthy on a trip to Mars and back?** A trip would take more than a year and a half. Nobody yet knows how to keep bones, muscles and eyes healthy, and protect against radiation, for that long.
- **Is there life anywhere else, and could a telescope find it?** New telescopes can study the air of planets around other stars. Nobody knows yet whether any of them have signs of life.
- **Could people make oxygen from Moon rock?** Moon rock is nearly half oxygen by weight, locked in minerals. Small tests work in laboratories, but nobody has yet done it on the Moon.

## Connected maths
- In the jar test, Deepa's small glass held 1 tumbler of water and kept the flame alight for 7 seconds. Her pickle jar held 4 tumblers. If the time grows in step with the air, how long should the flame last under the pickle jar? **About 28 seconds.**. The pickle jar holds 4 times as much air. So the flame should last about 4 times as long. 4 times 7 is 28. Real results will be close to this, not exactly on it. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Deepa's results were: small glass 7 seconds, big glass 13 seconds, pickle jar 30 seconds. Her friend says the big glass should have given about 14 seconds, because it held 2 tumblers. Was Deepa's result close enough? Explain. **Yes. 13 is only 1 second away from 14.**. 2 times 7 is 14. Deepa got 13. A difference of 1 second is small, and could come from starting the timer a moment late. So the pattern holds: twice the air, about twice the time. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L3`)
- The Moon is about 30 Earths away. On your lime-sized Earth, 5 cm wide, how far away should the grape Moon go? **150 cm.**. 30 lots of 5 cm. 30 times 5 is 150. So the grape goes 150 cm, about one and a half metres, from the lime. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- A message from Earth takes 12 minutes to reach a rover on Mars. The team sends 'Stop!' Then they wait to see a picture from the rover showing that it stopped. How long after sending do they see that picture? **24 minutes.**. The order takes 12 minutes to get there. The picture takes another 12 minutes to come back. 12 plus 12 is 24 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- India's first satellite, Aryabhata, was launched in 1975. Chandrayaan-3 landed on the Moon in 2023. How many years apart were they? The first rocket from Thumba was in 1963. How many years before Aryabhata was that? **48 years, and 12 years.**. 2023 take away 1975: from 1975 to 2000 is 25 years, and from 2000 to 2023 is 23 more. 25 plus 23 is 48. Then 1975 take away 1963 is 12. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
