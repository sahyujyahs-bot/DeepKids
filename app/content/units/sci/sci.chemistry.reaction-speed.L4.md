# Speeding up and slowing down a change: warmth, stirring, powder, crowds and helpers
`sci.chemistry.reaction-speed.L4`

**Summary.** A sachet of fruit salt fizzes for about half a minute in a glass of water. In a glass of fridge-cold water, the same powder takes much longer. The powder and the water are the same, so what changed? Your child times the fizz in cold, room-temperature and warm water, then speeds up and slows down the browning of an apple. They find that every way of speeding up a change works by making particles meet more often or more strongly. They also meet catalysts, helpers that are not used up, from enzymes in food to the metal in a car's exhaust. They hear how a Scottish chemist, Elizabeth Fulhame, found a helper hiding in plain water in 1794. Then they ask why the Delhi Iron Pillar has stood for about 1,600 years.

**Intro.** *Open one sachet of fruit salt. Drop half a teaspoon into cold water from the fridge, and the same amount into warm water. The warm glass finishes fizzing long before the cold one. Why does warmth make the same powder hurry?* The powder never changes. The water never changes. Only the warmth does, and the change speeds up. Reactions are changes that make new substances, and you met fast and slow ones in Class 3. Now you find out how to steer the speed. You will time the fizz, race browning apples, and test what stirring, crowding and powder do. Then you meet helpers called catalysts, which speed up a change and come out unchanged.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that a reaction happens when particles of different substances meet, and that its speed is how much new substance forms in each second.
1. I can name four ways to speed up a reaction (warmth, stirring, smaller pieces and more crowding) and explain each one as more or harder meetings of particles.
2. I can run a fair test with cups at three temperatures, time each one, and say what I think, what I saw and why.
3. I can explain that a catalyst speeds up one kind of reaction and is not used up, and name catalysts in food, in cars and in my body.
4. I can explain how cold, a barrier, lime juice or a coating slows a change, and why too much heat stops an enzyme.
5. I can read a graph of a reaction over time and explain why it starts fast and then slows.
6. I can tell how Elizabeth Fulhame and the Delhi Iron Pillar show that a change depends on what can touch what.

## Terms
- **Reaction rate**: How fast a reaction goes. It is how much new substance forms in each second, minute or year. *e.g.* Fruit salt in warm water makes its bubbles in about 15 seconds. In cold water the same bubbles take much longer, so the rate is lower.
- **Reactant**: A substance that goes into a reaction and is used up as it changes. *e.g.* In fruit salt, the baking soda and the citric acid are the reactants. They meet in water and are used up.
- **Product**: A new substance that a reaction makes. *e.g.* The bubbles in a fizzing glass are carbon dioxide gas, a product of baking soda meeting citric acid.
- **Particles**: The tiny bits that every substance is made of. They are far too small to see, and they are always moving. *e.g.* In warm water the particles move faster than in cold water, so a warm cup of tea cools as they slow down.
- **Collision**: A bump between two particles. A reaction can only happen when particles collide. *e.g.* Billions of water particles bump into a sugar lump every second, and a few bumps are enough to pull sugar particles off it.
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold something is. It tells us how fast its particles are moving. *e.g.* A thermometer in warm water reads about 50 degrees. In fridge water it reads about 5 degrees.
- **Surface area**: The size of the outside of a thing, where it can touch whatever is around it. Cutting a thing into small pieces gives it more surface area. *e.g.* A grated apple has far more surface area than the same apple in one piece, so more of it touches the air.
- **Concentration**: How crowded the particles of a substance are in a space. Many particles in a small space means a high concentration. *e.g.* Lime juice squeezed into two spoons of water is more concentrated than the same juice in twelve spoons of water.
- **Stirring**: Moving a liquid so that its particles mix and meet new neighbours. *e.g.* A spoon in a cup of tea carries sugar particles away from the lump and brings in fresh water.
- **Catalyst**: A substance that makes one kind of reaction go faster and is not used up. It comes out of the reaction the same as it went in. *e.g.* The metal inside a car's exhaust pipe helps poisonous gas turn into safer gas, and the metal stays in the pipe for years.
- **Enzyme**: A catalyst made by a living being. Each enzyme helps only one job. *e.g.* Catalase in a potato breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. An enzyme in an apple turns a cut surface brown.
- **Preservative**: Something that slows the changes that spoil food. *e.g.* Salt, oil, chilli and lime in a mango pickle slow down the changes that would make the mangoes rot.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. *e.g.* To test warmth, you use the same amount of fruit salt and the same amount of water in every cup.
- **Control**: The cup or sample where you change nothing, so you can compare the others with it. *e.g.* In the apple test, the plain chunk left on a plate is the control. You compare the lime chunk with it.
- **Rust**: The orange-brown flaky substance that forms when iron slowly reacts with oxygen and water. *e.g.* A balcony grill in Chennai, near the sea, rusts faster than one in a dry town.

## Explanations
### A reaction is a meeting of particles · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical reaction happens when particles of different substances meet and rearrange into new substances.

Dry fruit salt can sit in a sachet for months. Its two powders, baking soda and citric acid, are side by side but locked in dry grains. Add water, and the powders dissolve. Their particles are now free to move and meet. When a baking soda particle meets a citric acid particle in the right way, they rearrange into new particles. One of the new substances is carbon dioxide gas, and that is the fizz. So a reaction is something that happens between substances, not inside one of them. If the particles never meet, nothing happens. This is why 'how fast' always means 'how often and how well do the particles meet?'
- e.g. Dry fruit salt in a sachet does nothing. In water, it fizzes within seconds.
- e.g. A steel grill in a dry cupboard rusts very slowly. In a wet balcony it rusts faster, because water and oxygen reach the iron.
- e.g. Dry baking soda and dry lime powder can sit together in a jar. Add a few drops of water and they fizz.
### Reaction rate: how fast a change goes · *defined*
**Definition:** The rate of a reaction is how much new substance it makes, or how much old substance it uses up, in a given time.

Chemists measure rate with a clock. They might time how long bubbles last, how much gas fills a balloon in a minute, or how quickly a colour fades. A reaction that makes a lot of product in a short time has a high rate. A reaction that takes a year to make a little rust has a low rate. Rates cover an enormous range. A spark in a cracker finishes in a thousandth of a second. A diamond turning into graphite is so slow that nobody has ever seen it happen. The same ideas work all the way along this range.
- e.g. Fruit salt in warm water: a high rate, over in about 15 seconds.
- e.g. An iron gate rusting: a low rate, visible only after months.
- e.g. A banana skin spotting: a middle rate, over a few days.
### A reaction starts fast and then slows down · *defined*
**Definition:** In most reactions, the rate is highest at the start and falls steadily as the reactants get used up.

Watch a glass of fruit salt. At first, the bubbles race up, because there are many fresh particles of both powders to meet. After some seconds, many have already reacted. There are fewer particles left to meet, so fewer bubbles rise each second. In the end, one of the reactants runs out and the fizz stops. If you draw the amount of product against time, you get a curve. It climbs steeply at the start, then bends over and flattens. A flat curve does not mean the reaction is boring. It means one of the reactants is used up.
- e.g. The bubbles in a glass of fruit salt are fiercest in the first few seconds.
- e.g. A tea bag colours the water fast at first, and then more slowly.
- e.g. A burning agarbatti burns steadily, but a heap of crackers is done in a flash because everything is lit at once.
### Warmth speeds up almost every reaction · *defined*
**Definition:** Raising the temperature makes particles move faster, so they meet more often and more forcefully, and the reaction goes faster.

Temperature is a measure of how fast the particles in a thing are moving. In warm water they zoom around faster than in cold water. Faster particles bump into each other more often. They also bump harder, and a hard bump is more likely to rearrange them into something new. For many reactions near room temperature, a rise of about 10 degrees roughly doubles the rate. That is a rule of thumb, not an exact law. It is why milk goes off faster on a hot day, why a pressure cooker cooks dal quickly, and why a fridge keeps food fresh longer. Warmth is not magic. It just means more meetings, and stronger ones. Dissolving sugar is not a reaction, because no new substance forms, but the same idea of meetings helps explain why hot tea dissolves it faster.
- e.g. A pressure cooker raises the cooking temperature above that of an open pot, so dal cooks in a fraction of the time.
- e.g. Milk turns sour much faster on a hot afternoon than in the fridge.
- e.g. Sugar dissolves faster in hot tea than in cold water.
### Stirring and moving: bringing fresh particles together · *defined*
**Definition:** Stirring makes a reaction faster because it keeps carrying used-up particles away and bringing fresh ones to meet.

In a still glass, the particles at the bottom of a lump of fruit salt meet only the water that happens to be next to them. After they react, that nearby water is full of the new substance, and new water is slow to arrive. A spoon swirls the water around, so fresh water keeps reaching the powder. Stirring does not make particles move faster. It brings new partners to the meeting. That is a different way to get the same result as warmth, which is more meetings per second.
- e.g. Stirring the sugar in a tumbler of tea dissolves it faster.
- e.g. Swirling a dal pot stops the bottom from burning, because fresh dal keeps moving to the hot floor of the pot.
- e.g. A washing machine keeps tossing clothes so that fresh detergent water reaches the dirt.
### Smaller pieces mean more surface and faster reactions · *defined*
**Definition:** A solid can only react where it touches the other substance. Cutting it into small pieces gives it more surface area, so it reacts faster.

A cube with sides of 2 centimetres has six faces, each 4 square centimetres, so its surface area is 24 square centimetres. Cut it into eight small cubes with sides of 1 centimetre, and each has a surface area of 6 square centimetres. Altogether they have 48 square centimetres, double the surface, from the same amount of stuff. More of the solid can now touch the air or the liquid. That is why grated apple browns faster than a chunk, why powdered medicine dissolves faster than a pill, and why a heap of fine flour dust in a mill can explode when a lump of flour cannot. Flour dust has so much surface area that the whole cloud of it burns at once.
- e.g. Grated apple browns in minutes. A whole apple takes hours.
- e.g. A lump of jaggery dissolves slowly in water. Powdered jaggery dissolves fast.
- e.g. Flour mills and grain stores have a risk of dust explosions, because fine dust burns almost instantly in the air.
### Crowding: more particles in the same space · *defined*
**Definition:** A higher concentration means more particles in each bit of space, so they meet more often and the reaction goes faster.

Imagine a hall with six people walking about. They hardly ever bump into each other. Now put sixty people into the same hall. They bump all the time. It is the same with particles. Lime juice in two spoons of water has many acid particles near each baking soda particle. Lime juice in twelve spoons of water has the same acid particles spread out, so meetings are rarer. Concentration also works for gases. In pure oxygen, a glowing splint bursts into flame, because oxygen particles are five times more crowded than in air, where only about one particle in five is oxygen. This is why hospitals keep flames far from oxygen cylinders.
- e.g. Strong vinegar on baking soda fizzes harder than weak vinegar.
- e.g. A glowing splinter bursts into flame in pure oxygen, but only glows in air.
- e.g. Deep inside the Sun, hydrogen particles are squashed so tightly that they sometimes join. Even there, an average particle waits several billion years for its turn. This is a different kind of change, but speed still depends on how often particles meet.
### A catalyst makes one reaction faster and is not used up · *defined*
**Definition:** A catalyst is a substance that lowers the effort a reaction needs, so it goes faster, and the catalyst comes out unchanged.

Think of a mountain pass. Walkers can climb over the high mountain, which is hard and slow. A catalyst is like a lower pass cut through the same mountain. Many more walkers get across in the same time, and the pass is still there afterwards. In a reaction, the particles need a good bump, and in some cases a lot of energy to start changing. A catalyst gives them an easier path. It does not change which substances you start with or which ones you end up with, and it does not change how much product you finally get. It only changes how fast. Most catalysts also help only one kind of reaction, like a key made for one lock.
- e.g. The potato's catalase makes hydrogen peroxide break down in seconds instead of months.
- e.g. In a car's exhaust, a honeycomb coated with the metals platinum, palladium and rhodium changes poisonous carbon monoxide into carbon dioxide. The metals are not used up.
- e.g. Iron helps in factories to join nitrogen from the air with hydrogen to make ammonia for fertiliser. About four in every ten people alive eat food grown with fertiliser made this way.
### Enzymes: living catalysts that work at body warmth · *defined*
**Definition:** An enzyme is a catalyst made by a living being. Each enzyme speeds up one job, and most work best in a narrow range of warmth.

Your body cannot cook its food at a high temperature. It must break down food at about 37 degrees. Enzymes make that possible. One enzyme can speed up a reaction by a huge amount. A famous one, studied by Richard Wolfenden and his team, turns a job that would take about 78 million years into a fraction of a second. Because enzymes are folded chains with a special shape, heat can ruin them. A hot dip of just-boiled water ruins the enzyme that turns apple brown. That is why dipped apple stays pale. So heat speeds up reactions but also breaks enzymes, and living beings keep their warmth in a narrow, safe range.
- e.g. An enzyme in a cut apple helps oxygen turn some of the apple's own chemicals brown.
- e.g. Saliva has an enzyme that begins to break down starch in rice.
- e.g. Many washing powders contain enzymes that break down stains of food and sweat. Check the label.
### Slowing a change down on purpose · *defined*
**Definition:** To slow a reaction, do the opposite of speeding it up: cool it, keep the reactants apart, make them less crowded, or block the helper.

People have been slowing reactions for thousands of years, long before they knew why. A fridge cools food, so changes that spoil it go slowly. A sealed jar keeps out oxygen. Salt, oil and chilli in pickle make it hard for spoiling microbes to live. A coat of paint on an iron gate keeps water and oxygen away from the iron. Lime juice on cut apple is acidic and slows the enzyme that turns it brown. In each case, the aim is the same: fewer, weaker meetings. Some medicines are coated so that they dissolve slowly in the stomach and work for hours.
- e.g. Cut apple dipped in lime juice stays pale for much longer than plain cut apple.
- e.g. A painted iron gate lasts for years, but a bare one is quickly covered in rust.
- e.g. Hydrogen peroxide is sold in brown bottles, because light speeds up its breakdown.
### Some changes are so slow that we never see them · *defined*
**Definition:** Every reaction has a rate, but some rates are so low that nothing visible happens in a human lifetime.

Diamond is made only of carbon. Graphite, the black stuff in a pencil, is also only carbon. At normal air pressure, graphite is the steadier form. So diamond is always very slowly turning into graphite. But the rate is so low that a diamond ring will not change in a million lifetimes. Slow does not mean stopped. It means rare meetings and a very hard job for each one. The Delhi Iron Pillar shows another way to slow things down. Iron really does keep trying to rust. But a thin skin on the pillar keeps most of the water and air away from the iron below, so the rate is very low.
- e.g. Diamond turning into graphite is too slow to see.
- e.g. The Delhi Iron Pillar has rusted only a little in about 1,600 years.
- e.g. Old paper turns yellow over decades.
### Not every bump makes a reaction · *idea*
**Definition:** Most bumps between particles change nothing. A reaction needs the particles to bump hard enough, and in the right way.

At room temperature, one particle in the air bumps into others billions of times in a second. If every bump led to a reaction, everything around us would change in a flash. Most bumps are like two people brushing shoulders in a crowd. Only a rare bump has enough energy, hitting the right way round, to break old links and make new ones. Scientists call the energy needed the activation energy. You can picture it as a hill that particles must get over. Warmth gives more particles enough speed to get over the hill. A catalyst lowers the hill. You will study this picture in more detail in Class 5.
- e.g. A bump between two baking soda and citric acid particles sometimes does nothing.
- e.g. Heating a gas makes more of its particles fast enough to get over the hill.
- e.g. An enzyme lowers the hill, so many more bumps work at body warmth.

## Mostly agreed
- A reaction needs its particles to meet, and anything that makes meetings more frequent or more forceful speeds it up. *How we know:* Warm water, stirring, powder and a higher concentration each speed up a reaction in the lab and in the kitchen. The particle picture explains all four.
- For many reactions near room temperature, a rise of about 10 degrees roughly doubles the rate. *How we know:* Measurements of many reactions show this pattern, but not all, and the exact number differs. It is a rule of thumb, first noticed by Jacobus van 't Hoff in the 1880s.
- A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being used up, and does not change the final amount of product. *How we know:* The same potato or the same metal can be used again and again. Chemists weigh a catalyst before and after and find the same mass.
- Enzymes are catalysts made by living beings, and heat above a certain point destroys them. *How we know:* A fruit dipped in just-boiled water stops browning, because the enzyme has been ruined. Raw potato makes peroxide foam, but boiled potato hardly does.
- Slow reactions do not stop. They only happen so rarely that we cannot see the change. *How we know:* Very sensitive instruments can detect the slow rusting of an iron pillar and the slow breakdown of old paper.
- A thin skin that sticks tightly to a metal can slow its rusting a great deal. *How we know:* Scientists at the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur found a thin protective skin on the Delhi Iron Pillar, and showed that it shields the iron below.

## How we found out
**Elizabeth Fulhame, a Scottish chemist, and later Jons Jacob Berzelius, 1794 and 1835.** In the 1790s, most chemists thought a metal needed only air or a hot flame to change. Water was just a bystander. A Scottish chemist, Elizabeth Fulhame, tested this. She was soaking cloth in solutions of gold and silver and watching the metals appear as the cloth was exposed to hydrogen and other chemicals. When the cloth was bone dry, nothing happened. When it was damp, the metal appeared. She decided that water took part, but did not seem to be used up. In her view it was made again at the end, ready to work once more. She published all this in a book in 1794. Forty years later, the Swedish chemist Jons Jacob Berzelius gave this kind of helper a name, catalysis. Fulhame's book was read in Britain and America, but she was rarely given credit for the idea of catalysis until chemists looked again at her work in the 1990s.  
*What people believed before:* Most chemists believed that water was just a bystander in such changes. Fulhame showed that dry cloth did not react at all and damp cloth did, so water was taking part, and coming out ready to work again.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A catalyst can make a reaction happen that would never happen on its own.** (Some textbooks and many students.; *rejected*)  
  For: A catalyst can turn a change that takes millions of years into one that takes a second, so it can look like magic.  
  Against: A catalyst only speeds up a reaction that was already possible. It does not change the products or the final amount. Peroxide breaks down on its own, just very slowly.
- **If you heat something more, it always reacts faster.** (Many people, and some old cookery advice.; *debated*)  
  For: For most simple reactions, the rate does rise with temperature, as in the fruit salt test.  
  Against: Enzymes fall apart in too much heat, so heat can stop the reaction. A dipped apple does not brown because the heat ruined its enzyme. A pan of milk heated too hard catches and burns instead of cooking.
- **Old window glass has flowed downwards over the centuries, which is why it is thicker at the bottom.** (A popular story, repeated in many books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many old panes really are thicker at the bottom.  
  Against: Calculations by Edgar Zanotto in 1998 showed that glass at room temperature would need far longer than the age of the universe to flow that much. Old window panes were made by spinning molten glass into a disc, which made them uneven from the start. Workers often fitted the heavy edge at the bottom.
- **Salty air makes iron rust faster.** (Painters and engineers in coastal cities such as Chennai, Kochi and Mumbai.; *accepted*)  
  For: Railings and grills near the sea need painting more often. Salt dissolved in a film of water helps electric charge move, which speeds the rusting.  
  Against: Very few scientists disagree. The size of the effect depends on wind, wet weather and the kind of iron.
- **Cold stops microbes and chemical changes in food completely.** (Some people who leave food in the fridge for weeks.; *rejected*)  
  For: A fridge keeps milk fresh for days longer than a shelf does.  
  Against: A fridge only slows the changes. Even in a freezer, some reactions go on very slowly, which is why frozen food still has a limit.

## Roots
You timed fizzing cups, raced apples and met helpers called catalysts. Now we ask what the speed of a change tells us about how things happen at all.

**Deep truth.** A change is not something one substance does alone. It happens when things meet. Warmth, stirring, crowding and powder do not push a change. They only make meetings more often or harder, so slowing a change means keeping things apart.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'Eno fizzes' or 'iron rusts' as if the change lived inside one substance, like its colour. But dry Eno does not fizz, and a painted gate does not rust. The change belongs to the meeting.  
*Evidence:* Dry fruit salt sat still. In water, it fizzed, and it fizzed faster in warm water, stirred water and a more crowded mix. The Delhi Iron Pillar has the same iron as a rusting gate, but a tight skin keeps it from meeting water and air.

- *who am i:* Your body runs thousands of reactions every second at 37 degrees, each at the right speed. You are a set of meetings, kept in balance.
- *what is life:* A living being is a place where particles meet at just the right speed. Enzymes make the useful meetings happen fast, and warmth, food and water keep them going.
- *what is this world:* From a lit match to a rusting pillar to a slowly shining star, everything changes through meetings. Fast and slow are what we call the meetings, depending on how long we can watch.

Fruit salt, a pickle jar, an apple and an iron pillar seem to have nothing in common. In each, particles meet and change into something new, and the speed depends on how often and how hard they meet. A flame and a rusting gate are the same kind of change, at very different speeds. Nothing is ever fully still. Things we call stable are only changing very slowly. The world is a vast number of meetings, some quick and some slow, all part of one whole.

- **Heraclitus, as reported by Plato in Cratylus 402a:** Heraclitus, a Greek thinker, is said to have taught that everything flows, and that you cannot step into the same river twice. The water keeps moving, so the river is never the same. (A rusting nail, a ripening banana and a fizzing glass are all flowing in a way. They look still, but their particles are always meeting and changing.)
- **Dhammapada 277:** The Buddha is recorded as teaching that all made things are impermanent. Nothing made of parts stays the same. (The Delhi Iron Pillar looks as if it will last forever. But a very slow reaction is working on it all the time. Slow is not the same as still.)
- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika, chapter 1:** Nagarjuna, a Buddhist teacher in India about 1,800 years ago, argued that nothing happens by itself. Every event depends on causes and conditions meeting. (Fizz needs dry powders, water and the right warmth to meet. Take away one, and nothing happens.)
- Science (settled): Temperature measures how fast the particles in a thing are moving. Reactions slow in the cold, but they do not stop, even in the deepest freezer.
- Science (debated): Some scientists think that in certain enzymes, tiny hydrogen particles can pass through the energy hill instead of climbing over it, a quantum effect called tunnelling. How much this speeds up enzymes is still argued.
- Other view, Parmenides, a Greek philosopher from about 500 BCE: Parmenides argued that change is an illusion. What truly exists simply is, and never comes to be or passes away. He thought that our senses fool us when we think things change.
- Practice, Feel a slow burning (3 min): Rub your hands together for five seconds. Now hold one palm against your cheek and breathe slowly. The warmth you feel is partly slow burning, as your cells use oxygen to release energy from food. It is happening in every cell now, with the help of enzymes. Breathe in three times and notice the warmth.
- Ask: If dry Eno never fizzes, is the fizz in the powder, in the water, or in the meeting?
- Ask: If a thing only looks still because its change is slow, what else around you might be changing too slowly to see?
- Ask: Is the pillar's skin part of the pillar, or part of the air around it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A mayfly lives for a day, and an iron pillar stands for 1,600 years. To the mayfly, the pillar never changes at all. Is 'slow' a property of the change, or of the one who is watching it? (hint: Think about what 'slow' would mean to a mayfly, to you, and to a mountain.)
- *deep:* Are we seeing the same change differently, or are we seeing different changes? A rusting nail, a flame and the Sun are all particles meeting, but they look nothing alike. (hint: Think about how slow, fast and hot hide the fact that they are the same kind of change.)
- *big:* Which changes in your life do you want to speed up, and which do you want to slow down? Who decides? (hint: Think about food, learning, healing and friendships.)
- *big:* Your body runs thousands of reactions at 37 degrees, each at just the right speed. What is keeping them in balance, since no one is in charge? (hint: Think about enzymes, and about what happens when you have a fever.)
- *deep:* A catalyst takes part in a change and comes out exactly the same. Does it count as part of the change, or only a bystander? (hint: Think about the potato in the peroxide, and the walker's low pass in the mountain.)
- *deep:* If nothing in the universe is ever really still, only slow, what does it mean to call something 'stable'? (hint: Think about diamonds, iron and glass.)
- *big:* Some of the fastest changes, like a flame, and some of the slowest, like a rusting pillar, are the same kind of event. What does that tell you about how you divide the world into 'fast' and 'slow'? (hint: Think about how scientists measure both with the same idea.)
- *big:* Many things that keep us alive, like digestion, depend on slowing a change down just enough. What would a world with only fast changes be like, and what would a world with only slow changes be like? (hint: Think of a world where food burned like a firework, and a world where it never changed.)

## Story: Sixteen Hundred Summers
*A made-up story around a real place. The Iron Pillar stands in the Qutb complex in Mehrauli, Delhi. It is about 1,600 years old, and it has rusted very little. Scientists at the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur found a thin, tightly stuck protective skin on it. Gayathri, Thatha, Rashmi Madam and the talk between them are imagined.*

In May, the heat in Delhi pressed down like a hand. Gayathri was in Class 4, and she had come from Coimbatore with her grandfather, Thatha. The thermometer on the bus said forty-one degrees.

Thatha had spent forty years as an engineer on the railways. He walked slowly now, with a cap on his head and a cloth over his neck. He stopped talking at once when they came round a corner in the Qutb complex and saw the pillar.

It stood alone in a courtyard. It was a dark, smooth column of iron, taller than three grown men standing on each other's shoulders. A low fence kept visitors back.

'Sixteen hundred years old,' said the guide, a young woman called Rashmi Madam, with a cloth bag on her shoulder. 'Older than the fort. Older than the tower beside it.'

Thatha said nothing for a long time. Then he said, very quietly, 'It is impossible.'

'Why?' asked Gayathri.

'Iron rusts, Gayathri. I spent forty years painting iron bridges so they would not fall into the river. Every five years, new paint. Each time, I scraped off rust as thick as a biscuit. Iron wants to rust. It always has. And this one has stood in the sun and the monsoon for sixteen hundred summers.' He shook his head. 'It must be special iron that does not want to rust.'

Gayathri was thinking about her nails. Before the trip, she had put five iron nails in five jars on the balcony. One had dry air. One had plain water. One had salty water. One had oil. One had vinegar. In three days the salty nail was covered with orange flakes, and the bottom of the jar had a heap of orange powder. The dry nail had only a thin dark skin. The nail in oil was still bright.

'Thatha,' she said slowly, 'my salty nail made a lot of rust. But the rust did not stay on. It fell off in flakes. And then the nail was bare again, and it rusted some more.'

'Yes,' said Thatha. 'Flaking rust is the engineer's enemy. It peels, and fresh iron is open to the damp again.'

'But the dry nail had a thin dark skin, and the skin stayed stuck,' said Gayathri. 'That nail did not rust any more. So maybe the iron is the same, Thatha. Maybe the pillar is not special iron. Maybe it has a skin that does not peel.'

Thatha turned and looked at her as if she had said something in a new language.

Rashmi Madam smiled. 'May I tell you what the scientists found?' she said. 'They looked at the pillar's surface with strong instruments. They found a very thin skin, much thinner than paper, made of iron joined with oxygen, hydrogen and phosphorus. It sticks tightly. The iron of the pillar also has a lot of phosphorus in it, more than the iron on your bridges. The skin has slowly built up over many years. It keeps most of the water and air from touching the iron below.'

'So the iron still wants to rust,' said Thatha.

'It still tries,' said Rashmi Madam. 'But it hardly ever gets the chance. Every year, a tiny bit more rust forms, so the pillar is not perfect. It is just very, very slow.'

Gayathri looked at the fence. 'Did people touch it a lot?'

'Oh yes. For a long time, visitors stood with their backs to it and tried to reach their arms around it. They said a wish would come true. That hand-oil and sweat wore on the skin. That is why the fence is here now.'

'It is hot today,' Gayathri said. 'Heat speeds up almost everything. At forty-one degrees, why is the pillar still slow?'

'Because the skin is winning,' said Rashmi Madam. 'Heat helps the iron and the air meet. The skin keeps them apart. And the skin is stronger than the heat.'

Thatha took off his cap and stared at the pillar in the sun. He had known for forty years that rust was a slow fire. But he had thought of it as a thing about the iron, a stubborn fact like a river flowing downhill. He had never thought of it as a meeting that could be stopped from happening.

'Forty years of paint,' he said, 'and a girl in Class 4 tells me the iron was never the problem. It was the meeting.'

'You did the same thing,' said Gayathri. 'Paint is a skin too. It keeps iron and water apart.'

Thatha laughed so loudly that a family nearby turned round. 'My bridges would have lasted longer,' he said, 'if I had known to ask for that.'

That evening on the train, Gayathri drew the pillar in her notebook. She drew the iron inside it, and she drew the thin skin round it. She drew water and air outside the skin, with little arrows that could not get through.

Under the drawing she wrote one line. 'Slow does not mean the iron does not try. It means the iron hardly ever gets to meet the air.'

Thatha read it twice, and then tucked the notebook gently into his shirt pocket, as though it were a ticket.

**For the parent:** The pillar is slow because of what stands between the iron and the air. The iron itself is not special.

- Ask: Gayathri and Thatha explained the pillar in different ways. Which explanation do you find more convincing, and why?
- Ask: Paint, oil and a stuck-on skin all slow rusting. What do they have in common?
- Ask: How could you test whether the skin, and not the iron, is why the pillar rusts so slowly?

  - Think: A catalyst helps a meeting work. How could you add a catalyst to this game?

## Experiments
### e1 · Three Cups: does warmth hurry the fizz? (32 min)
- **question:** Does the same amount of fruit salt fizz faster in cold water, room-temperature water or warm water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which cup will stop fizzing first, and which last? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Every cup is the same size and has 150 millilitres of water and half a level teaspoon of fruit salt. Nobody stirs. The only thing that changes is the temperature of the water, so any difference in the fizzing time comes from the temperature. We do each cup twice, because one try can be a fluke. The room-temperature cup is the control.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the warm water and checks that it is not too hot to hold. Do not drink the water from the test glasses. Wipe up spills so nobody slips.
- **record:** Write the seconds for each try in the table. Work out the average for each cup. Then draw a bar for each cup, with the cup names along the bottom and seconds up the side.
- **results table:** Cup | Water | Try 1 (seconds) | Try 2 (seconds) | Average (seconds)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three glasses and the fruit salt. Say what changes and what stays the same. Write down their guess and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will fizz the same powder in three glasses of water. One is cold, one is room-temperature and one is warm. Which will stop fizzing first? Which will be last? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three glasses of water. One cold, one room, one warm. Which stops fizzing first? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that glass will finish first? (good: Warm water makes things go faster.; The cold glass slows it down.; The powder dissolves quicker in warm water.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write the guesses at the top of the results table.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up makes the three kinds of water. Cold water: add ice for 5 minutes, then take the ice out. Room water: use tap water. Warm water: mix hot and cold until you can hold the glass for 10 seconds. Put 150 millilitres in each glass.  
  *Parent says:* Cold water first. Chill it with ice for five minutes, then take the ice out. The room glass is plain tap water. For the warm glass, mix hot and cold water until the glass is comfortable to hold. Pour 150 millilitres into each.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up helps with the warm water. Pour 150 millilitres into each glass. Label them cold, room and warm.
  - Note: If you have a thermometer, cold is about 5 to 10 degrees, room is about 25 to 30, and warm is about 45 to 50. Without a thermometer, use your hand. Cold should ache a little. Warm should feel like a warm bath. It must not be hot.
  - Check: Why do we use exactly 150 millilitres of water in every glass? → **So that only the temperature is different**
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start with the room glass. Tip half a level teaspoon of fruit salt into the water. Start the stopwatch at once. Do not stir. Stop it when no bubbles rise. Write down the seconds. Then do the cold glass, and then the warm glass.  
  *Parent says:* Tip in half a teaspoon of fruit salt, and start the stopwatch at the same moment. Do not stir. Watch the bubbles rise from the powder at the bottom. Stop the clock when they stop. Write the seconds in the table.  
  *Child mode:* Tip in the powder. Start the stopwatch. Do not stir. Stop it when bubbles stop.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the bubbles in the first few seconds compared with the last few? (good: They come fast at the start.; They slow down near the end.; There are fewer bubbles at the end.)
  - Note: Use fresh water for the second try. Pour out the old water, rinse the glass, and use new water at the same temperature. Warm water cools quickly, so for the second try make it warm again. Tiny bubbles on the side of the glass do not count. Stop the clock when no bubbles rise from the powder.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Check the table. Work out the average for each glass by adding the two tries and dividing by two. Draw a bar for each glass.  
  *Parent says:* Add the two tries for each glass and divide by two. That gives the average. Now draw one bar for each glass. Which bar is shortest?  
  *Child mode:* Add two tries. Divide by two. Draw a bar for each glass.
  - Check: The warm glass finished in 14 seconds and the cold glass in 56 seconds. How many times faster was the warm glass? → **4 times**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why they think it happened. Then explain.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Note: A strong answer says the warm glass finished first and the cold glass last. After they say what they saw, explain: in warm water the particles move faster, so they meet more often and harder. The powder particles meet the water faster too. If the order was not warm, room, cold, say that is also a result. Check the water amounts and whether anyone stirred, and try again.
  - Think: The powder and the water were the same in every glass. So what was different about the water particles in the warm glass?
### e2 · Apple Race: speed up and slow down browning (42 min)
- **question:** Which browns an apple fastest: a plain chunk, grated apple, a chunk dipped in lime juice, a chunk in the fridge, or a chunk dipped in hot water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which plate will turn brown first, and which will stay palest? Say why you think so.

  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write the guesses at the top of the table.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**

  - Note: The cut faces must all be fresh and start at the same time. Put plates A, B, C and E in the same spot, away from a fan. Do not wipe the pieces. If you have no fridge shelf, use a bowl of ice-cold water under a cover.
  - Check: Why is plate A, the plain chunk, kept in the test? → **So that we can compare the other plates with it**
- **s3 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At 10 minutes, look at the cut face of each plate. Give each a score from 0 to 4 and write it in the table. Do the same at 20 and 30 minutes. Take plate D out of the fridge for a moment each time.  
  *Parent says:* At ten minutes, look at the cut faces. Give each plate a score from nought to four. Write it down. Do the same at twenty and thirty minutes. Which plate changes first?  
  *Child mode:* Look closely at each plate. Score the colour from 0 to 4. Write it in the table.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the grated apple compared with the chunk? (good: It goes brown faster.; It is darker already.; More of it touches the air.)
  - Note: If your apple is slow to brown, wait up to an hour and keep scoring. If nothing browns, try a banana. Write what you see, even if it is not what you guessed. The cold plate may only be a little paler than the plain one in 30 minutes. That is also a result.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a line graph. Put minutes along the bottom and colour score up the side. Draw one line for each plate, in a different colour.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a line graph. Minutes go along the bottom and the colour score goes up the side. One line for each plate. Which line climbs fastest?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line for each plate. Which line climbs fastest?
  - Check: Plate B, the grated apple, is darker than plate A, the chunk, at 10 minutes. What best explains this? → **Grated apple has much more surface touching the air**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then explain.  
  *Parent says:* What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think each plate changed as it did?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the grated apple browned fastest because of its large surface, and that cold, lime and heat each slowed it. After they say what they saw, explain: a cut apple has an enzyme that helps oxygen turn some of the apple's own chemicals brown. Small pieces mean more surface. Cold slows the particles. Lime juice is acidic, and the enzyme works poorly in acid. The hot dip breaks the enzyme, so it cannot help any more.

### e3 · Same Lime, More Water: does a crowd speed up the fizz? (28 min)

## Worksheets
### Fast, Slow and the Four Speed-Ups (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What happens in a chemical reaction?  
   Options: Particles of different substances meet and rearrange into new substances / A substance only changes its shape / A solid always turns into a liquid  
   **Answer:** Particles of different substances meet and rearrange into new substances. *Why:* A reaction makes new substances, because particles meet and rearrange. Changing shape or state, like ice melting, makes no new substance. A wrong pick often means a change of state is being mixed up with a reaction.
2. (truefalse) A sachet of dry fruit salt fizzes by itself while it sits closed on a shelf.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The powders are dry, so their particles cannot move and meet. Water lets them dissolve and mix, and then they react.
3. (mcq) Fruit salt fizzes in water. What are the bubbles?  
   Options: Carbon dioxide gas, a new substance / Air that was trapped in the powder / Steam from the water  
   **Answer:** Carbon dioxide gas, a new substance. *Why:* Baking soda and citric acid react and make carbon dioxide. Trapped air would not keep coming for half a minute, and the water is not hot enough to make steam.
4. (sort) Sort these changes into fast (seconds or minutes) and slow (days or longer).  
   **Answer:** a cracker banging → Fast; an iron gate rusting → Slow; fruit salt fizzing in warm water → Fast; old paper turning yellow → Slow; sugar dissolving in hot tea → Fast; a pickle jar changing flavour → Slow. *Why:* A cracker, fizz and hot tea finish in seconds. Rust, yellow paper and pickles change over days, months or years. A strong answer says each one is a reaction, just at a different rate.
5. (match) Match each speed-up to an example.  
   **Answer:** Warmth → Sugar dissolving faster in hot tea; Stirring → A spoon swirling a glass of fizz; Smaller pieces → Powdered jaggery dissolving fast; More crowded → Strong vinegar fizzing harder than weak vinegar. *Why:* Warmth means faster particles. Stirring brings fresh partners. Smaller pieces give more surface. A crowd means more particles per space. All four make meetings more frequent or stronger.
6. (fill) When water gets warmer, its particles move ____.  
   **Answer:** ['faster']. *Why:* Temperature measures how fast particles move. In warm water they move faster, so they meet more often and harder.
7. (mcq) In the chart, which glass finished fizzing first?  
   Options: The warm glass / The cold glass / The room glass  
   **Answer:** The warm glass. *Why:* The warm glass has the shortest bar, 15 seconds. A shorter time means a faster reaction. A wrong pick often means a tall bar was read as faster.
8. (count) Kavya, a girl in Hyderabad, times fruit salt in two glasses. The cold glass takes 90 seconds. The warm glass takes 30 seconds. How many times faster is the warm glass?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 90 divided by 30 is 3. The warm glass finished 3 times faster. Subtracting would only tell you how many seconds apart they were.
9. (truefalse) Stirring makes the particles in a liquid move faster.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Stirring does not speed up particles. It brings fresh partners to a meeting and carries used-up liquid away.
10. (write) Zoya says a heap of fine powder reacts faster than one big lump of the same stuff. Explain why she is right, using the word surface.  
   **Answer:** A lump only reacts where its surface touches the other substance. Powder has many tiny pieces, so its total surface is much larger. More of the stuff can meet the other substance at once, so the reaction is faster.. *Why:* A strong answer names the surface, says more of it touches the other substance, and links that to faster reactions. It may also mention the grated apple.
11. (draw) Draw two glasses of water side by side, one cold and one warm. Draw dots for particles and arrows to show how fast they move.  
   **Answer:** Two glasses with dots in each. Short arrows on the dots in the cold glass and long arrows on the dots in the warm glass.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows longer arrows in the warm glass, because its particles move faster. Some also mark more bumps in the warm glass.
12. (mcq) What is special about a catalyst?  
   Options: It speeds up a reaction and is not used up / It is the ingredient that makes the most product / It slows a reaction down  
   **Answer:** It speeds up a reaction and is not used up. *Why:* A catalyst comes out the same as it went in, so it can work again. It is not an ingredient. A pick of slows down means it was mixed up with the idea of a slow-down, such as a fridge.
13. (odd) Three of these slow a change down. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: A fridge / A coat of paint / Stirring / Lime juice on cut apple  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A fridge cools, paint blocks the meeting, and lime juice slows the browning enzyme. Stirring brings fresh partners together, so it speeds a reaction up.
14. (order) Put these changes in order from the fastest to the slowest.  
   **Answer:** ['a cracker banging', 'fruit salt fizzing in warm water', 'a cut apple browning', 'an iron gate rusting']. *Why:* A cracker bangs in a flash. The warm fizz takes about 15 seconds. An apple browns in minutes to hours. A gate rusts over months. All four are reactions at different rates.
15. (perspective) A family is arguing about how to keep sliced apple pale in a tiffin box.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses an idea from the unit, such as that lime juice makes the enzyme work poorly, or that an uncut apple has less surface meeting air. It also names one good point from another view.. *Why:* All three ideas keep oxygen, the enzyme and the cut surface apart or slow. A strong answer gives a reason, not just a choice.
### Curves, Catalysts and Slow-Downs (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A graph of fizz against time rises steeply at first and then flattens. What does the flat part mean?  
   Options: One of the reactants has been used up, so no more gas is made / The reaction has become boring / The water has become cold  
   **Answer:** One of the reactants has been used up, so no more gas is made. *Why:* At the start there are many fresh particles to meet, so the rate is high. As they are used up, fewer meetings happen. When one reactant runs out, the curve goes flat.
2. (label) Here is a curve of gas made against time. Label the three parts: 'steep: many fresh particles', 'bending over: fewer left' and 'flat: one reactant used up'.  
   **Answer:** ['The first part of the curve is steep', 'the middle bends over', 'the end is flat']. *Why:* The steep start is the highest rate. As reactants are used up, the curve bends over. When one runs out, it goes flat.
3. (count) A cube of jaggery has sides of 3 centimetres. Each face is 9 square centimetres, and a cube has 6 faces. What is its total surface area in square centimetres?  
   **Answer:** 54. *Why:* 6 faces times 9 square centimetres is 54. Cut into smaller cubes, the same jaggery would have much more surface area.
4. (count) A reaction takes 40 minutes at 20 degrees. For each 10 degrees warmer it takes half the time. How many minutes will it take at 40 degrees?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* At 30 degrees it takes half of 40, which is 20 minutes. At 40 degrees it takes half of 20, which is 10 minutes. This is a rule of thumb, not an exact law.
5. (fill) A catalyst is ____ used up by the reaction it speeds up.  
   **Answer:** ['not', 'never']. *Why:* A catalyst comes out unchanged, so the same potato, or the same exhaust metal, can help again and again.
6. (truefalse) A catalyst changes how much product you finally get from the same amount of reactants.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A catalyst only changes how fast the product forms. The final amount depends on how much reactant you started with.
7. (mcq) Why can a washing powder with enzymes lift food stains even in cool water?  
   Options: Enzymes are catalysts that work without needing high heat / Enzymes heat the water / Enzymes are scrubbed away with the stain  
   **Answer:** Enzymes are catalysts that work without needing high heat. *Why:* Enzymes are catalysts. They lower the effort the reaction needs, so the stain breaks down quickly without hot water. A pick of heating the water confuses a catalyst with a heater.
8. (sort) Sort each of these by what it does to a reaction.  
   **Answer:** stirring → Speeds up; a fridge → Slows down; lime juice on cut apple → Slows down; powdering a solid → Speeds up; a pressure cooker → Speeds up; paint on a gate → Slows down. *Why:* Stirring, powder and the hotter cooker make meetings more frequent or stronger. A fridge, acid and paint make them rarer or weaker.
9. (match) Match each way of slowing a change to an example.  
   **Answer:** Cooling → Milk kept in the fridge; A barrier → A painted iron gate; Acid → Lime juice on cut apple; Breaking the enzyme → A hot dip for an apple chunk. *Why:* Each one slows the change by a different route. Cold slows the particles, paint keeps the iron from water and air, acid makes the enzyme work poorly, and the hot dip destroys the enzyme.
10. (mcq) A hot dip of just-boiled water keeps a cut apple pale. Why?  
   Options: The heat breaks the browning enzyme, so it cannot help any more / The heat makes the apple wet / The heat cools the apple  
   **Answer:** The heat breaks the browning enzyme, so it cannot help any more. *Why:* Enzymes are folded chains that fall apart in strong heat. Without the enzyme, the browning reaction is far slower. A wrong pick often means heat is thought to always speed things up.
11. (write) Salty water makes a nail rust faster than plain water. Explain why salt might help, and say how you could test it with three glasses.  
   **Answer:** Salt dissolved in the water helps electric charge move, which speeds up rusting. To test it, put the same kind of nail in plain water, in salty water and in dry air, then score the rust each day.. *Why:* A strong answer says salt speeds rusting, gives a reason or says it helps charge move, and describes a fair test with one change and a control.
12. (draw) Draw a mountain with a high path over the top and a low tunnel through it. Label the high path 'no catalyst' and the tunnel 'with catalyst', and draw walkers coming out the other end.  
   **Answer:** A mountain with a path over the top and a tunnel through it. Labels on each. Walkers coming out at the far end of both.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the tunnel as an easier route to the same place. The walkers and the destination are the same with and without the tunnel.
13. (truefalse) Old glass windows have flowed downwards over the centuries, which is why they are thicker at the bottom.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What has to happen before two substances can react?  
   Options: Their particles must meet / They must be the same colour / One must be a liquid  
   **Answer:** Their particles must meet. *Why:* A reaction is a meeting. Dry fruit salt does nothing until water lets its particles move and meet. A wrong pick often means a property of the substance is mistaken for the cause.
2. (mcq) Why does the same fruit salt finish fizzing sooner in warm water?  
   Options: Warm water has faster particles that meet more often and harder / Warm water has more fruit salt in it / Warm water makes the powder lighter  
   **Answer:** Warm water has faster particles that meet more often and harder. *Why:* Temperature measures how fast particles move. Faster particles meet more often and more forcefully. The amount of powder is the same in every glass.
3. (mcq) Why does grated apple turn brown faster than one big chunk?  
   Options: More of the apple is touching the air / Grated apple is sweeter / Grated apple is warmer  
   **Answer:** More of the apple is touching the air. *Why:* Browning happens where cut apple meets oxygen. Grating makes many tiny pieces, so far more surface meets the air.
4. (mcq) Which of these is a catalyst?  
   Options: Catalase in a potato, helping hydrogen peroxide break down / Baking soda poured on vinegar / Lime juice on a cut apple  
   **Answer:** Catalase in a potato, helping hydrogen peroxide break down. *Why:* Catalase speeds up the breakdown of peroxide and is not used up. Baking soda is used up in the fizz. Lime juice slows a reaction down.
5. (mcq) What is the best way to slow down the rusting of an iron gate?  
   Options: Cover it with paint, so water and air cannot reach it / Leave it in salty air / Wash it with vinegar  
   **Answer:** Cover it with paint, so water and air cannot reach it. *Why:* Rusting needs iron to meet water and oxygen. Paint keeps them apart. Salty air and vinegar speed rusting up.
6. (mcq) The same lime juice is mixed with more water. What happens to the speed of its fizz on baking soda?  
   Options: It slows down, because the acid is less crowded / It speeds up, because there is more liquid / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It slows down, because the acid is less crowded. *Why:* The same acid spread through more water is less crowded, so meetings with baking soda are rarer. The final amount of gas stays the same.
7. (mcq) A graph of gas made against time rises steeply and then flattens. What does the steep part show?  
   Options: The reaction is fastest at the start / The reaction has stopped / A catalyst was added  
   **Answer:** The reaction is fastest at the start. *Why:* At the start there are plenty of fresh particles to meet, so the rate is highest. It slows as they are used up.
8. (fill) A cut apple dipped in just-boiled water stays pale, because the heat breaks the ____ that helps it turn brown.  
   **Answer:** ['enzyme']. *Why:* Enzymes are folded chains that fall apart in strong heat. With the enzyme broken, browning is far slower.
9. (mcq) The Delhi Iron Pillar has rusted very little in about 1,600 years. What best explains this?  
   Options: A thin, tightly stuck skin keeps water and air from the iron below / Iron never rusts in Delhi / The pillar is made of a metal that cannot react  
   **Answer:** A thin, tightly stuck skin keeps water and air from the iron below. *Why:* Scientists found a thin protective skin on the pillar. The iron itself can react, but it rarely meets water and air. A wrong pick treats the iron as magic instead of looking at the meeting.
10. (truefalse) Putting food in a fridge stops every change in it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Cold only slows the particles, so meetings are rarer and weaker. The changes continue, but slowly.
11. (count) Pooja, a girl in Kochi, finds a fizz takes 48 seconds in cold water and 12 seconds in warm water. How many times faster is the warm fizz?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 48 divided by 12 is 4. The warm fizz is 4 times faster. Subtracting would give how many seconds apart they are.
12. (mcq) Stirring a glass of fizz makes it finish sooner. What does stirring do?  
   Options: It brings fresh water to the powder / It makes the particles hotter / It makes the powder bigger  
   **Answer:** It brings fresh water to the powder. *Why:* Stirring does not change the temperature or the size of the powder. It carries used-up liquid away and brings fresh partners to the meeting.

## Applications
- Pressure cookers cook dal and rice faster by raising the temperature of the water.
- Fridges and freezers slow down the changes that spoil food.
- Catalytic converters in petrol cars and CNG autos change harmful exhaust gases into safer ones.
- Washing powders with enzymes break down food stains in cool water.
- Paint, oil and galvanising (a coat of zinc) slow the rusting of iron.
- Slow-release medicines are coated so that they dissolve over hours.
- Factories use an iron catalyst to make ammonia for fertiliser from nitrogen and hydrogen.
- Mills and grain stores control dust, because fine dust burns almost all at once.
- Pickles use salt, oil and chilli to slow spoiling.
- Chemists stir, heat or powder their materials to make a lab reaction finish in time.

## Open questions
- **How exactly do enzymes make reactions up to a million billion billion times faster?** Scientists can describe how enzymes hold particles in place, but they still argue about every part of the story. Some think quantum tunnelling of hydrogen has a role in some enzymes.
- **How did the very first catalysts on Earth begin?** Before there were living cells, something had to speed up chemistry. Some think rocks, metals or small molecules did it. Nobody has shown which came first.
- **Could we design catalysts as good as enzymes for any reaction we want?** Chemists have won prizes for making catalysts and for evolving enzymes in the lab. But for many important reactions, we still have no good catalyst.
- **Why does the Delhi Iron Pillar's skin form the way it does, and can we copy it?** Scientists have found out a lot about the skin, but making new iron that protects itself as well and for as long is still hard.
- **Can a single reaction be filmed as it happens?** Using very fast flashes of light, scientists have filmed bonds breaking and forming, but only for a few simple reactions so far.
- **Are there reactions in space that take longer than the age of the universe?** Some changes, such as the slow decay of certain particles, may be that slow. Scientists are still searching for them, and may never see one finish.

## Connected maths
- In the Three Cups test, Meenakshi, a girl in Madurai, finds the warm glass fizzes for 14 seconds and the cold glass for 56 seconds. How many times faster is the warm glass? **4 times.**. 56 divided by 14 is 4. The warm glass is 4 times faster. A fair comparison divides the times, because we want to know how many times, not how many seconds apart. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Many reactions go about twice as fast for each 10 degrees of warmth. A reaction takes 40 minutes at 20 degrees. How long should it take at 30 degrees, at 40 degrees and at 50 degrees? **20 minutes, 10 minutes and 5 minutes.**. Each 10 degrees halves the time. Half of 40 is 20. Half of 20 is 10. Half of 10 is 5. This is a rule of thumb and not an exact law, but it is a good guess for many reactions. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A jaggery cube has sides of 2 centimetres. Each face is 4 square centimetres, and a cube has 6 faces. Cut into 8 small cubes with sides of 1 centimetre, each small cube has a surface area of 6 square centimetres. By how many square centimetres does the total surface grow? **24 square centimetres more.**. The big cube has 6 times 4, which is 24 square centimetres. The 8 small cubes have 8 times 6, which is 48. The surface grows by 48 minus 24, which is 24. The same jaggery now has double the surface. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
- In the Apple Race, a plain chunk scores 1 at 10 minutes, 2 at 20 minutes and 4 at 30 minutes. The chunk in lime juice scores 0, 0 and 1. Between which two times did the plain chunk change the most? **Between 20 and 30 minutes, when it rose by 2.**. From 10 to 20 minutes it rose from 1 to 2, which is 1. From 20 to 30 minutes it rose from 2 to 4, which is 2. The line is steepest between 20 and 30 minutes. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- Lakshmi, a girl in Mysuru, hears that dal takes 45 minutes in an open pot but 15 minutes in a pressure cooker. She puts dal on at 6:10 in each. When does each one finish? How many times faster is the cooker? **The open pot finishes at 6:55, and the cooker at 6:25. The cooker is 3 times faster.**. 6:10 plus 45 minutes is 6:55. 6:10 plus 15 minutes is 6:25. 45 divided by 15 is 3, so the cooker is 3 times faster. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- In the Same Lime test, each cup has 2 teaspoons of lime juice. Cup A has no water, cup B has 4 teaspoons of water and cup C has 12 teaspoons of water. What fraction of each cup is lime juice? **A is 2 out of 2, which is the whole. B is 2 out of 6, which is one third. C is 2 out of 14, which is one seventh.**. The total liquid is 2, 6 and 14 teaspoons. Lime juice is always 2 teaspoons. The fractions are 2/2, 2/6 and 2/14, which simplify to 1, one third and one seventh. The smaller the fraction, the less crowded the acid. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- The Delhi Iron Pillar is about 1,600 years old. A railway engineer paints an iron bridge every 5 years to stop rust. How many times would the bridge be painted in 1,600 years? **320 times.**. 1,600 divided by 5 is 320. Painting is a barrier that has to be renewed. The pillar's skin has done the same job without anyone repainting it. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- In Meeting Party, four particles in the big square make 6 meetings in 20 seconds. In the small square, the same four make 18 meetings. The small square is a quarter of the area of the big one. How many times more meetings are there in the small square? **3 times more.**. 18 divided by 6 is 3. The same particles in a quarter of the space meet 3 times as often. In a real reaction, a bigger crowd in a smaller space also speeds the reaction up. The numbers are a made-up example. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
