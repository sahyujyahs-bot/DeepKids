# Falling: why a falling thing speeds up, and why it stops speeding up
`sci.gravity.falling.L4`

**Summary.** A raindrop falls from a cloud two kilometres up and only taps your head. A stone dropped from a first-floor balcony would sting your toe. Your child finds out why, by dropping paper cups, a ball of dough while walking, and a marble into soft dough from three heights. They learn that a falling thing gains the same speed every second, until the push of the air matches the pull of the Earth. They see that sideways speed and falling do not disturb each other, why a mouse can fall safely and a horse cannot, and how Galileo's first idea about falling turned out to be wrong.

**Intro.** *A raindrop falls from a cloud about 2 kilometres up. A pebble dropped from a first-floor balcony, only 3 metres up, would sting your toe. How can a raindrop that falls so much farther be so gentle?* If nothing slowed it, the raindrop would hit the ground at about 700 kilometres an hour. That is almost as fast as an aeroplane. But something does slow it. In this unit you find out what, by dropping paper cups and a ball of dough. You will also find out why a ball that you drop while walking does not land at your feet.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that a falling thing gains the same extra speed every second, about 10 metres a second more, and use this to find its speed after 1, 2 and 3 seconds.
1. I can explain why a falling thing in air stops speeding up: the push of the air grows with speed until it equals the pull of gravity. I can name this top speed.
2. I can run a fair test that shows a heavier thing of the same shape reaches a higher top speed, and explain why.
3. I can show that sideways speed and falling are separate, and explain why a ball tossed up in a moving train comes back to my hand.
4. I can show that a thing falling twice as fast makes a dent four times as deep, and say that movement energy grows with speed times speed.
5. I can explain why a mouse can survive a fall that would break a horse, using weight and the area the air pushes on.
6. I can tell how Galileo's first idea about falling was wrong and how it was shown wrong. I can name something about falling that nobody can yet predict.

## Terms
- **Speed**: How far something moves in a certain time, such as in one second or one hour. *e.g.* A child walking at an easy pace moves about 1 metre every second.
- **Acceleration**: How fast the speed of something is changing. Speeding up is a kind of acceleration. *e.g.* A falling stone gains about 10 metres a second of speed every second.
- **Air resistance**: The push of the air against anything that moves through it. It gets bigger as the thing goes faster. *e.g.* You feel air resistance as a strong push on your hand when you hold it out of a moving bus.
- **Terminal speed**: The top speed of a falling thing in air. At this speed the push of the air up equals the pull of gravity down, so the thing stops speeding up. *e.g.* A big raindrop has a terminal speed of about 30 kilometres an hour.
- **Sideways speed**: How fast a thing moves along, not up or down. *e.g.* A bus moving along the road has sideways speed, and so do the people inside it.
- **Inertia**: The way a thing keeps moving as it is moving, or keeps still, unless something pushes or pulls it. *e.g.* You lurch forward when a bus brakes, because your body keeps moving forward.
- **Path**: The line a thing follows as it moves through the air. The path of a thrown ball is a curve called a parabola. *e.g.* A ball thrown to a friend follows a curved path, up and then down.
- **Movement energy**: The energy a thing has because it is moving. It grows with speed times speed. Scientists also call it kinetic energy. *e.g.* A cricket ball at twice the speed has four times the movement energy, so it can dig a much deeper dent.
- **Stored energy**: The energy a thing has because it is held high up. It turns into movement energy as the thing falls. *e.g.* A coconut high in a palm has stored energy. When it falls, this becomes movement energy.
- **Surface area**: The amount of outside that a thing has. The air can only push on the outside. *e.g.* A wide umbrella has a bigger surface area than a closed one, so the air pushes on it more.
- **Square of a number**: A number multiplied by itself. The square of 3 is 3 times 3, which is 9. *e.g.* Twice the speed gives 2 times 2, which is 4 times the movement energy.

## Explanations
### A falling thing gains the same speed every second · *defined*
**Definition:** Without air, a falling thing gains about 10 metres a second of speed every second. The steady pull of the Earth gives a steady speeding up.

Drop a ball. At the start its speed is zero. After 1 second it moves at about 10 metres a second. After 2 seconds it moves at about 20. After 3 seconds it moves at about 30. Each second adds the same 10. The Earth pulls the ball with the same pull all the way down, and a steady pull adds speed at a steady rate. The exact number is 9.8 metres a second every second, and we round it to 10 to make the sums easy. A speed of 10 metres a second is 36 kilometres an hour. So after 3 seconds the ball is going 108 kilometres an hour. How far the ball has fallen grows even faster. It is about 5 metres after 1 second, 20 metres after 2 seconds and 45 metres after 3 seconds.
- e.g. A coconut that falls for 1 second hits the ground at about 36 kilometres an hour, if the air is not in the way.
- e.g. A cliff diver who falls for 2 seconds is going about 72 kilometres an hour when she hits the water.
- e.g. A dough ball dropped from table height, 1 metre, is moving at about 16 kilometres an hour when it lands.
### Terminal speed: the air pushes back harder the faster you fall · *defined*
**Definition:** Terminal speed is the top speed of a falling thing in air. It is the speed at which the push of the air up equals the pull of gravity down.

A falling thing has to push the air out of its way. The faster it goes, the more air particles it hits each second, and the harder it hits them. So the push of the air grows as the speed grows. A rough rule is that twice the speed gives four times the push. At the start the push of the air is tiny, and the pull of gravity wins, so the thing speeds up. At some speed the push of the air grows as big as the pull. The two are now equal, so nothing is left to speed the thing up. It falls at a steady speed. Gravity has not stopped pulling. The air is pushing back just as hard.
- e.g. A big raindrop reaches about 30 kilometres an hour within a few metres of falling, and then falls at that steady speed all the way down.
- e.g. A skydiver lying flat falls at about 200 kilometres an hour. If she pulls in her arms and legs and dives head first, she meets less air and falls faster, at about 300.
- e.g. A peregrine falcon that folds her wings and dives passes 200 kilometres an hour.
### A heavier thing of the same shape has a higher top speed · *defined*
**Definition:** If two things have the same shape and size, the heavier one has the higher terminal speed. Four times the weight gives about twice the terminal speed.

In a vacuum, heavy and light things fall together. In air, the shape and size also matter. Take two things of the same shape. The heavier one is pulled harder by the Earth. So the air has to push harder before the two forces are equal. The air pushes harder only when the thing goes faster. So the heavier thing must go faster before it stops speeding up. Because the push of the air grows as speed times speed, four times the push needs only twice the speed. That is why a stack of four paper cups falls much faster than one cup, but not four times faster.
- e.g. A cricket ball and a table-tennis ball are the same size. In air the cricket ball falls faster, because it is heavier.
- e.g. A stack of four cupcake liners reaches the floor before one cupcake liner.
- e.g. A big hailstone falls faster than a small one, because it has more weight for the same shape.
### Sideways speed and falling are separate · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing that moves sideways and falls does both at once. Gravity changes only the falling part. The sideways speed stays the same unless the air or something else pushes on it.

Drop a ball while you walk. It does not fall straight down to the floor beneath your hand. It keeps moving forward at your walking speed while it falls. So it lands ahead of the spot where you let go. How far ahead? The time it takes to fall, times your walking speed. A fall from the height of your waist takes about half a second. At a walking speed of 1 metre a second, the ball lands about half a metre ahead. A ball rolled off a table and a ball dropped from the same height land at the same moment, because gravity pulls both of them down in the same way. Galileo showed in 1638 that the path of a thrown thing is a curve, and the curve is a sideways movement and a falling movement added together.
- e.g. A ball rolled off a table and a ball dropped from the table's edge land at the same moment.
- e.g. A cricket ball hit for a six rises and falls along a curve. The falling part follows the same rule as a dropped ball.
- e.g. Water from a tap that points sideways falls in a curve. Water that runs faster lands farther away, but it takes the same time to fall.
### Why a ball tossed up in a moving train comes back to your hand · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing tossed up inside a smoothly moving train already shares the train's sideways speed. It keeps that speed while it rises and falls, so it comes back to your hand.

A ball tossed up in a train that moves at 80 kilometres an hour is also moving forward at 80 kilometres an hour. Gravity does not take that sideways speed away. The air in the carriage moves with the train too. So while the ball is up, it moves forward exactly as far as you do, and it drops back into your hand. This means that you cannot tell from this test whether the train is moving. Galileo wrote about this in 1632. He said that nothing you do below decks in a smoothly moving ship shows whether the ship is moving. The Earth is a very big ship. It turns once a day and travels around the Sun. You and everything near you share its sideways speed, and so a coconut falls to the foot of the palm and not far to the west.
- e.g. A pen tossed up in a smoothly moving bus lands back in your hand.
- e.g. A coconut falls straight down beside the palm even though the Earth turns at hundreds of metres a second beneath it.
- e.g. A cup of tea on a train table stays where it is, until the train brakes or turns.
### The old argument about a turning Earth · *debated*
In 499 CE, the Indian astronomer Aryabhata wrote in his book the Aryabhatiya that the Earth turns once a day, and that the stars only seem to move west. He gave a picture: a person in a boat moving forward sees still things on the bank slide backward. Later astronomers disagreed. Varahamihira and Brahmagupta argued that if the Earth turned, things thrown into the air, and birds that left their nests, would be left behind. This was a sensible argument. What they did not have was the idea that a thing in the air keeps the sideways speed it had. Galileo gave this idea in the 1600s. A ball tossed up in a moving train shows it. Today nobody doubts that the Earth turns.
- e.g. A bird that leaves a nest in a tree on the turning Earth still shares the Earth's sideways speed, so she can fly back to the same tree.
- e.g. A stone thrown straight up on the turning Earth lands back in your hand.
- **Aryabhata, about 499 CE:** The Earth turns, and the stars only seem to move. He compared it to a person in a moving boat who sees things on the bank move backward.
- **Varahamihira and Brahmagupta, about 505 CE and 628 CE:** The Earth cannot be turning. If it were, a stone thrown up or a bird that left her nest would be left behind. They had no idea yet that a thing in the air keeps its sideways speed.
### Movement energy: twice the speed, four times the dent · *defined*
**Definition:** The movement energy of a thing grows with speed times speed. Twice the speed gives four times the movement energy, and three times the speed gives nine times.

A ball held high has stored energy. As it falls, the stored energy turns into movement energy. When the ball hits something soft, the movement energy goes into making a dent. A little of it also becomes sound and a tiny bit of heat. If you drop a ball from 4 times the height, it hits with twice the speed, when the air is not in the way. In the 1720s, a Dutch scientist named Willem 's Gravesande dropped brass balls into soft clay. A ball with twice the speed dug a dent about 4 times as deep. A French scientist named Emilie du Chatelet used his results in her book of 1740 to argue that the strength of a moving thing depends on speed times speed, and not on speed alone. This is also why a car going twice as fast needs about four times the distance to stop.
- e.g. A car going 60 kilometres an hour needs about 4 times the distance to stop of a car going 30 kilometres an hour.
- e.g. A marble dropped from 40 centimetres lands with twice the speed of a marble dropped from 10 centimetres.
- e.g. Water at the bottom of a high waterfall hits the pool far harder than water from a low step.
### Why a mouse can fall and a horse cannot · *defined*
**Definition:** When an animal gets bigger, her weight grows faster than the area of her body that the air pushes on. So a bigger animal has a higher terminal speed and hits the ground harder.

Take a cube that is 1 centimetre wide. Now make a cube that is twice as wide, 2 centimetres. It weighs 8 times as much, because 2 times 2 times 2 is 8. But its outside is only 4 times as big, because 2 times 2 is 4. The air can only push on the outside. So the bigger cube has more weight for each bit of outside. It must fall faster before the air pushes back enough. Animals work the same way. In 1926, the scientist J. B. S. Haldane wrote that a mouse dropped down a deep mine shaft gets only a slight shock and walks away, while a rat is killed, a man is broken and a horse splashes. A small animal has little weight for her size, and an ant is hardly hurt by any fall.
- e.g. An ant dropped from a roof walks away, because her top speed is low.
- e.g. The winged seeds of the sal tree spin down slowly, because they have a large wing for a tiny weight.
- e.g. A flying squirrel spreads out skin flaps to make a bigger area, so the air pushes on her more.
### Falling stars: a fall from space · *defined*
**Definition:** A meteor is a speck of rock or metal from space that falls into the Earth's air so fast that the air in front of it glows. The bright streak is called a shooting star.

Most meteors are no bigger than a grain of sand or a small pebble. They hit the Earth's air at between 11 and 72 kilometres every second. At that speed the air cannot get out of the way. It is squeezed in front of the speck and gets very hot, and the heat makes the speck glow and boil away. Most meteors glow about 80 to 100 kilometres above the ground, and nothing reaches the ground. A larger piece that does survive, called a meteorite, is slowed by the air to its terminal speed in the last few kilometres. By then it is falling like a stone from a tall building, not like a shooting star.
- e.g. The Perseid meteors that you can see each August are tiny specks left by a comet.
- e.g. A meteorite that lands is often cool to touch, because it has fallen slowly for the last part of its journey.
### Falling on other worlds · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon, Mars and Jupiter pull things down by different amounts, so a falling thing gains speed at a different rate on each.

On Earth, a falling thing gains about 10 metres a second of speed every second. On the Moon it gains only about 1.6 metres a second every second, six times less, because the Moon is smaller and pulls less. On Mars it gains about 3.7. On Jupiter, at the top of the clouds, it gains about 25. A ball dropped from the height of a table, 1 metre, takes about 0.45 seconds to land on Earth. It takes 1.1 seconds on the Moon, 0.7 seconds on Mars and 0.3 seconds on Jupiter. The rule stays the same on every world. The speed grows by the same amount each second. Only the size of the amount changes.
- e.g. The astronauts who jumped on the Moon seemed to fall in slow motion, because they gained speed six times more slowly.
- e.g. A ball dropped on Mars takes about 1.6 times as long to land as on Earth.

## Mostly agreed
- Without air, everything near the Earth gains the same speed each second, about 9.8 metres a second. *How we know:* The Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott let a hammer and a feather fall on the Moon in 1971, and they landed together. In 2022, a French satellite named MICROSCOPE showed that two different metals fall alike, to about 1 part in 10 to the power of 15.
- In air, a falling thing reaches a top speed when the push of the air equals its weight. *How we know:* Scientists measured the speeds of falling water drops of different sizes in still air in 1949, and found that each size reaches a steady speed. Skydivers' recorded speeds show the same thing.
- A thing that moves sideways and falls does both at once, and the path is a curve. *How we know:* Galileo described this in 1638. Photographs of two balls, one dropped and one thrown sideways at the same moment, show them at the same height at every flash.
- Movement energy grows with speed times speed. *How we know:* 's Gravesande's clay-dent tests in the 1720s, and modern measurements of car stopping distances, both show that twice the speed gives four times the effect.
- Bigger animals reach higher terminal speeds than smaller ones, so they are hurt more by a fall. *How we know:* Weight grows with the cube of an animal's length and area grows with the square, so the air pushes on a big animal less for her weight. J. B. S. Haldane described this in 1926.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist (1564 to 1642), 1604 (the letter to Paolo Sarpi) and 1638 (the book Two New Sciences).** In 1604, the Italian scientist Galileo Galilei wrote a letter to his friend Paolo Sarpi. He had found a rule for falling things and he was sure of it. He thought that the speed of a falling thing grows in proportion to the distance it has fallen. Later he saw that this could not be true. If it were, a stone would take the same time to fall the first 4 feet as to fall 8 feet, because after 4 feet it would be going twice as fast as after 2. Equal times for different distances would mean the fall happened in no time at all, and that is impossible. So the speed must grow with the time of the fall. In 1638, in his book Two New Sciences, Galileo gave the right rule and tested it with balls rolling down slopes. Historians think the argument against the first rule is Galileo correcting his own early mistake.  
*What people believed before:* The speed of a falling thing grows in proportion to the distance it has fallen. Galileo showed it wrong by reasoning: it would make falling 4 feet and 8 feet take the same time, which is impossible. The right rule is that speed grows with time.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A falling thing keeps speeding up until it lands.** (Many people, and many textbook pictures that leave out the air.; *rejected*)  
  For: For short falls, such as a dropped pen, the air hardly matters, and the thing really does speed up all the way. On the Moon, with no air, it is true for every fall.  
  Against: A raindrop from a high cloud and a skydiver both stop speeding up. The push of the air grows until it equals the pull of the Earth.
- **A coin dropped from a skyscraper could kill a person on the ground.** (A story many people have heard.; *rejected*)  
  For: A coin has fallen a very long way, so it seems it should be going extremely fast.  
  Against: A coin tumbles in the air and reaches a terminal speed of only about 50 to 80 kilometres an hour. It can sting, but it does not have enough movement energy to be deadly.
- **A ball thrown hard sideways stays in the air longer than a ball that is dropped.** (A common idea, because the thrown ball travels farther.; *rejected*)  
  For: A thrown ball goes a long way, so it seems that it must stay up for a long time.  
  Against: Gravity pulls down the same way on both balls. A ball rolled off a table and a ball dropped from its edge land at the same moment.
- **The speed of a falling thing grows in proportion to the distance it has fallen.** (Galileo Galilei himself, in a letter to Paolo Sarpi in 1604.; *rejected*)  
  For: It seems natural, because a thing that has fallen farther has been pulled for longer.  
  Against: Galileo later showed it cannot be true, because falling 4 feet and 8 feet would then take the same time. The speed grows with the time of the fall.
- **Cats can fall from any height and walk away, because they land on their feet.** (A popular belief, partly supported by a study of cats brought to a vet in New York in 1987.; *debated*)  
  For: In that study, cats that fell from more than seven storeys had fewer injuries on average than cats that fell from fewer. Some scientists think the cats reach their top speed and relax.  
  Against: Only cats that survive are taken to the vet, so we do not see the ones that did not. A long fall can still kill a cat.

## Roots
We have dropped cups and dough and learned how falling things speed up. Now we ask what a lime tossed on a train tells us about being still and being in motion.

**Deep truth.** When you toss a lime up on a moving train, it falls back into your hand. The lime keeps the train's sideways speed all the way. So being at rest does not mean having no speed. It means moving along together with everything around you.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call a thing 'still' or 'moving' as if that were a fact about the thing alone. But still always means still compared with something. Seen from the Sun, the room you sit in is moving at about 30 kilometres every second.  
*Evidence:* In Toss on the Move, the ball went up and came down into the walker's hand while the walker kept moving. In the Walking Drop, the dough ball landed ahead of the drop mark by about the walking speed times half a second.

- *who am i:* You are moving right now. You travel around the Earth's axis at hundreds of metres a second and around the Sun at about 30 kilometres a second, and you feel nothing.
- *what is life:* A falcon folds her wings and dives at more than 200 kilometres an hour. A seed spins down slowly. Each living body meets the same pull and the same air in its own way.
- *what is this world:* Dropping, throwing, tossing and walking all follow one rule. Gravity pulls down, and the sideways speed carries on.

Every falling thing near the Earth feels the same pull. A stone, a raindrop, a seed and a mouse all gain speed at the same rate to begin with. The many different kinds of fall come from what the pull meets: the air, the shape, the size and the sideways speed. The air itself is made of the same kind of particles as our bodies. So the fall of a raindrop is a meeting between the Earth, the air and the drop, and none of them is the whole story.

- **Aryabhatiya, Golapada 9 (Aryabhata, 499 CE):** A person in a boat moving forward sees still things on the bank moving backward. In the same way, the still stars seem to move west to people on the Earth. (Aryabhata saw that a still thing and a moving thing depend on where you stand. The tossed lime shows the same idea inside a train.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 5:** It moves, and it does not move. It is far, and it is near. It is inside all this, and it is outside all this. (In the train, the lime is moving at 80 kilometres an hour and is also at rest in your hand. Both are true, depending on what you compare it with.)
- Science (settled): Galileo wrote in 1632 that nothing you do in a smoothly moving ship's cabin shows whether the ship is moving. Physicists call this the relativity of motion. It is settled science.
- Science (debated): Measurements of the glow left over from the early universe show that our Solar System moves at about 370 kilometres a second compared with that glow. Whether this counts as a true rest, or only as a handy way to measure, is still debated.
- Other view, Isaac Newton: Newton thought that there is a true, fixed space and a true rest, even though we can never find it by experiments inside a ship or a train. He argued that spinning, which we can feel, shows a real kind of motion. Many physicists since have disagreed with him, and say that all motion is measured against something else.
- Practice, Still, and moving (3 min): Sit very still, with your eyes closed. Feel your feet on the floor and your breath in your belly. Notice that nothing in you feels like it is moving. Now remember that you are being carried around the Sun at about 30 kilometres every second. Breathe in slowly three times, and notice how quiet that fast movement feels.
- Ask: If nothing in a smooth ride shows that you are moving, what does it mean to say that you are still?
- Ask: Is there anything in the universe that is truly at rest, or is rest always rest compared with something?
- Ask: You feel a bump when a train brakes or turns, but not when it moves at a steady speed. What is the difference?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* At top speed, gravity is still pulling a raindrop, yet the raindrop is not speeding up. How can a thing be pulled and not change speed? (hint: Think about the push of the air as well as the pull of the Earth.)
- *deep:* A ball moves sideways and falls at the same time, and each movement ignores the other. How can one thing do two separate movements at once? (hint: Think about the walking drop, and what the ball was doing before you let go.)
- *deep:* A ball dropped from 4 times higher hits with 4 times as much movement energy. Where did the extra energy come from? (hint: Think about the stored energy of a thing held high, and what pulls it down for longer.)
- *deep:* Air pushes back harder as a thing goes faster, until the two forces match exactly. Why does nature find this balance every time, and by itself? (hint: Think about what happens when the push of the air is a little too small, and when it is a little too big.)
- *big:* You are moving around the Sun at about 30 kilometres every second, and you feel nothing. What else might we be moving along with, without feeling it? (hint: Think about the train and the tossed lime.)
- *big:* A small animal is safe from a fall and a big animal is not. Is being safe a fixed trait, or does it depend on size? (hint: Think about the two cubes, and what changed when one became twice as wide.)
- *big:* Aryabhata's critics had a sensible reason, yet they were wrong. How can a good argument be wrong, and how do we find out? (hint: Think about what idea they did not have yet.)
- *big:* Galileo made a mistake about falling, then found it by thinking. Is thinking enough to find a mistake, or do we need to test as well? (hint: Think about how he showed that his first idea could not be true.)

## Story: The Lime on the Night Train
*A made-up story. Kavya, Raman Mama and Paati are imagined. The science and history in it are real. Aryabhata did write about a person in a moving boat in the Aryabhatiya in 499 CE. Later astronomers such as Varahamihira and Brahmagupta did argue against a turning Earth, in words much like Raman Mama's. A turning Earth carries a person in Chennai along at about 450 metres every second.*

Kavya was ten years old, and she was going to Chennai on the night train from Madurai with her uncle. Raman Mama had been a schoolmaster for forty years. He had an opinion about everything, and he liked it best when someone argued with him.

The train clattered through the dark. The lower berth smelled of murukku and of the steel tiffin box that Paati had packed. Raman Mama had an old book open on his knee and his glasses halfway down his nose.

'Listen to this,' he said. 'About fifteen hundred years ago, an astronomer called Aryabhata wrote that the Earth turns round once every day. It is not the stars that move, he said. It only seems so. It is like a man in a boat, who sees the trees on the bank slide backward.'

'That sounds right to me,' said Kavya.

'It sounded wrong to a great many clever people,' said Raman Mama. 'Varahamihira was one. Brahmagupta was another. They asked a simple question. If the Earth turns, and the ground slides away to the east, why is a stone thrown straight up not left behind? Why does it not fall far to the west? And why does a bird that leaves her nest ever find the nest again? Answer that, Kavya, and I will believe you.'

Kavya thought hard. She did not know. Her mother always said that a girl who does not know should say so, and then go and find out.

'I don't know yet,' she said. 'But I think I can find out right now.'

She reached into Paati's bag and took out a lime. Paati had packed it for the long ride, in case anyone felt sick. Kavya stood in the narrow aisle between the berths, holding the bunk rail with one hand. The train swayed.

'We are going about eighty kilometres an hour,' she said. 'The ground outside is sliding backward, like Aryabhata's trees. If your argument is right, Mama, then a thing thrown up gets left behind. The lime should fly to the back of the carriage.'

She tossed the lime straight up, just above her head.

It rose. It stopped. It fell. It landed in her palm with a tiny slap, in exactly the place where she was standing. She tossed it again, and again. Each time the train rushed forward through the dark, and each time the lime came back to her hand.

'Hm,' said Raman Mama. 'The air in this carriage is moving with us. The roof is over us. That is cheating.'

'Then it is cheating for the bird as well,' said Kavya. 'The air goes round with the Earth, right up high. And the tree, and the nest, and the bird were all going round before she ever left. Look. The lime was already going forward at eighty before I threw it. My throw only added an up. Nothing took the forward away.'

She tossed it once more to show him. 'Up and down is one movement. Forward is another. The Earth pulls only on the up and down. It does not touch the forward. So when the lime comes down, the train has gone forward, and so has the lime, and so have I. We all went the same distance. That is why I am still under it.'

Raman Mama said nothing for a long time. The wheels sang on the rails. Then he took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes.

'Varahamihira asked a very good question,' he said slowly. 'And he did not have a lime.'

'He did not have a train,' said Kavya.

Raman Mama began to laugh, and he laughed until the man in the next berth turned over in his sleep. 'Forty years,' he said. 'Forty years I have taught that argument as a puzzle with no answer. And a girl answered it in the aisle of a train, with a lime from her grandmother's bag.'

'Tell your old class it was Paati's lime,' said Kavya. She sat down on the berth, because the grown-ups had said never to stand for long when the train was moving, and she held the lime in her lap.

At dawn, the train slowed beside a grey sea. A platform slid past the window, with chai sellers and a crow on a pillar. Kavya looked out. The whole of Chennai was being carried round by the Earth at about four hundred and fifty metres every second. Nothing in her cup of tea shook. Nothing in her felt it at all.

She squeezed the lime, and smelled it, and smiled.

**For the parent:** A thing that is moving keeps its sideways speed while it falls. So a tossed lime comes back to your hand, even on a moving train, and a bird can fly back to her nest on a turning Earth.

- Ask: Why did the lime come back to Kavya's hand, even though the train was moving?
- Ask: A boy throws a ball straight up inside a bus that is slowing down. Would it still land in his hand? What do you think, and why?
- Ask: Raman Mama's argument was sensible, yet it was wrong. What idea was missing from it?

A player scores one more point for a good reason.; The reader decides about the reason, using the back of the card. A reason that says the same thing as the back is good.; After each card, the job of reader passes to the player on the left.; With two players, the parent and the child swap the reader's job after every card.; The game ends when all 20 cards have been read. ## Experiments
### e1 · Liner Stacks: does a heavier stack fall faster in air? (35 min)
- **question:** Four paper cupcake liners fit inside each other as one stack. Does a stack of four liners fall faster than one single liner, or do they land together?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the stack of four land first, last or together with the single liner? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every liner is the same. Every drop starts from the same height, marked with tape on the wall. Every liner is held with its opening facing up and is let go, not thrown. Only the number of liners changes. Each stack is dropped three times, because one drop can be a fluke, and a drop where the liner tumbles over is repeated.
- **safety:** The grown-up drops the liners while standing on the floor. Nobody stands on a chair or a stool. Keep the small liners away from very young children, who might put them in their mouths.
- **record:** For each stack, write the three times and the middle time. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each stack, using the middle time.
- **results table:** Number of liners | Drop 1 (seconds) | Drop 2 (seconds) | Drop 3 (seconds) | Middle time (seconds)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child one liner and a stack of four. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write both at the top of the results table.  
  *Parent says:* I will drop one liner and a stack of four liners. Which will land first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* One liner or a stack of four? Which lands first? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will land first? (good: The stack is heavier.; They are the same shape, so they land together.; The air will hold up the single one.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say they will land together, because heavy and light things fall together. That is a good reason to test it.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Check that all four liners are exactly alike. Stick a strip of tape on the wall at the height of the grown-up's raised hand. Always drop from this height. No liners? Cut four circles from newspaper, 12 centimetres wide, using a katori as a guide. Cut a slit to the middle of each and overlap the cut edges by 1 centimetre. Tape them into four shallow cones.  
  *Parent says:* All the liners must be the same. The drop height must be the same too. We will mark it on the wall.  
  *Child mode:* Check the four liners are the same. Put tape on the wall to mark the drop height.
  - Note: The grown-up should do every drop, holding the liners at the tape with a straight arm. A child on tiptoe changes the height each time. Press nested liners together so they act as one thing.
  - Check: Why do we mark the drop height on the wall? → **So every drop starts from the same height**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold one liner in the left hand and a stack of four in the right hand. Hold both at the tape mark, with the openings facing up. Let go of both at the same moment. A helper watches from the side and says which landed first. Do this three times.  
  *Parent says:* Ready, steady, go. Let go of both together. Which one landed first? By a lot or by a little?  
  *Child mode:* Hold one liner and a stack of four at the mark. Let go together. Which lands first?
  - Ask: Did the same one land first every time? (good: Yes, the stack landed first each time.; Yes, and the single liner wobbled more.; Mostly, but one drop was a wobble.)
  - Note: A liner that tumbles over is a wobble. Call it a wobble and repeat that drop. The stack of four should land first by a clear gap. If the single liner wins, check that the stack is pressed together and that the openings face up.
  - Check: The stack of four landed first. In the Drop Race, a heavy bottle and a light bottle landed together. What is different with the liners? → **The liners are wide and light, so the air pushes on them a lot**
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Drop one liner from the mark. The helper starts the phone stopwatch as it is let go and stops it when it touches the floor. Write the time. Do this three times. Then do the same for stacks of two, three and four liners.  
  *Parent says:* Start the stopwatch when I let go. Stop it when the liner touches the floor. Write down the time.  
  *Child mode:* Drop the liners. Time each fall. Write the time in the table.
  - Note: The times are rough, because a stopwatch is started a fraction of a second late. That is why each stack is dropped three times, and we use the middle time. Roughly, one liner takes 1.5 to 2 seconds and a stack of four takes about 1 second. Yours may differ. A wobble does not count.
  - Check: Three drops of a stack took 1.6, 1.9 and 1.7 seconds. Which is the middle time? → **1.7 seconds**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar for each stack, using the middle times. Then ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each stack. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: Listen first. Then say: the air pushes harder the faster a thing falls. A heavier stack must go faster before the push of the air matches its weight. So it has a higher top speed. Four times the weight gives only about twice the top speed. That is why the stack of four falls faster, but not four times faster.
  - Think: Would a stack of a hundred liners fall a hundred times faster?
### e2 · The Walking Drop: where does a ball land if you drop it while walking? (25 min)
- **question:** If you let go of a ball while you walk, where does it land: behind the spot below your hand, right under it, or ahead of it? Does it matter how fast you walk?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the ball land behind the mark, under it, or ahead of it? Will walking faster change where it lands? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The ball, the drop height and the mark stay the same every time. You let go as your hand passes the mark and you do not throw. Only your walking speed changes. You do three drops at each pace, because one drop can be a fluke, and the helper marks the first place the ball touches the floor.
- **safety:** Walk, never run. Wear shoes on a dry floor. Clear toys and mats from the path.
- **record:** Write the distance from the mark to the landing note for each drop. For each pace, write the middle of the three distances.
- **results table:** Pace | Drop 1 (cm ahead of the mark) | Drop 2 (cm ahead) | Drop 3 (cm ahead) | Middle distance (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the dough ball and the drop mark. Ask your child to draw three dots on paper. They show where the ball may land: behind, under or ahead of the mark. Ask her to say why.  
  *Parent says:* You will walk past the mark and let go of the ball as your hand passes it. Where will it land? Behind the mark, under it or ahead of it? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Walk past the mark and let go. Where will the ball land? Behind, under or ahead? Why?
  - Ask: Will walking faster change where the ball lands? (good: Yes, it will land farther ahead.; No, it will always drop straight down.; It will land behind, because the ball is slow.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say the ball falls straight down under the hand. Write it down so they can compare later.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stick the drop mark on the floor. Measure the height of your child's shoulder and write it down. Stand still with your hand above the mark and let go of the ball. The helper puts a sticky note where it lands. Measure from the mark to the note.  
  *Parent says:* First we stand still. Let go of the ball above the mark. It should land right under your hand. This is our check.  
  *Child mode:* Stand still over the mark. Let go of the ball. Where does it land?
  - Ask: Why did we drop the ball while standing still first? (good: To see what happens with no walking.; So we have something to compare with.; To check our measuring works.)
  - Note: Hold the ball at shoulder height, which is about 1 metre for most children of this age. The standing drop should land within a couple of centimetres of the mark. If it does not, check that the hand really is above the mark.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Walk along the tape line at a slow pace. Let go of the ball as your hand passes the mark. The helper puts a note at the first place the ball touches. Do this three times. Then do three drops at an easy pace and three at a brisk pace. Do not run.  
  *Parent says:* Slow pace first. Let go as your hand passes the mark. Helper, mark the first touch.  
  *Child mode:* Walk past the mark. Let go as your hand passes it. Slow, then easy, then brisk.
  - Note: Open the hand to let go. Do not throw or flick. Dough does not bounce, so the first touch is easy to see. If one drop lands far from the other two, repeat it. Most odd drops come from letting go too early or too late.
  - Check: Meera's ball landed 50 centimetres ahead of the mark when she walked at an easy pace. About where will it land if she walks twice as fast? → **About 100 centimetres ahead**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure each landing from the mark and write it in the table. Find the middle of the three for each pace. Ask your child what pattern she sees.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the three middle numbers. What happens to the landing spot when you walk faster?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your numbers. What happens when you walk faster?
  - Ask: Did the ball ever land behind the mark? (good: No, it always landed ahead.; No, it kept moving forward with me.; Only when I let go too late.)
  - Note: Expect about 20 to 30 centimetres for a slow pace, about 50 for an easy pace and 70 to 90 for a brisk pace. Your numbers may be different. What matters is that faster walking gave a longer distance.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then return the dough and tidy the notes.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: Listen first. Then say: the ball was moving forward with your hand before you let go. Letting go took away the hold of your hand, but not the forward speed. The fall from shoulder height takes about half a second whatever your pace. So the ball lands about half your walking speed, in metres, ahead of the mark.
  - Think: If you could walk twice as fast, where would the ball land?
### e3 · Dents in Dough: does a faster hit dig deeper? (30 min)
- **question:** A marble is dropped into soft dough from 10, 20 and 40 centimetres. How much deeper is the dent from the higher drops? Is it twice as deep, or more?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which height will dig the deepest dent? Will the dent from 40 centimetres be 2 times as deep as the one from 20 centimetres, or more? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The marble, the dough and the way you let go stay the same. Each drop lands on a fresh patch of smooth dough. Only the drop height changes. Each height is tried three times, because one drop can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Keep the marble away from children under five, who might put it in their mouths.
- **record:** Write the depth of each dent in millimetres. For each height, write the middle of the three depths. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each height.
- **results table:** Drop height (cm) | Dent 1 depth (mm) | Dent 2 depth (mm) | Dent 3 depth (mm) | Middle depth (mm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the marble and explain that it will be dropped from 10, 20 and 40 centimetres. Ask your child to guess which dent will be deepest and how much deeper the dent from 40 centimetres will be. Ask for a reason.  
  *Parent says:* We drop a marble into soft dough from three heights. Which dent will be deepest? How much deeper will the 40 centimetre dent be than the 20 centimetre dent? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A marble drops into dough from three heights. Which dent is deepest? Why?
  - Ask: Will the dent from twice the height be twice as deep? (good: Yes, twice as deep.; No, it will be even deeper.; It will be about the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write it at the top of the table.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mix 2 cups of atta with water until the dough is as soft as an earlobe. Press it flat in the tray, about 3 centimetres thick. Smooth the top with the back of a spoon. Tape a ruler upright beside the tray.  
  *Parent says:* The dough must be soft, so the marble leaves a dent. We smooth it flat every time we start.  
  *Child mode:* Make soft dough. Press it flat in the tray. Smooth the top.
  - Note: If the marble bounces away, the dough is too stiff. Add a spoon of water and knead again. If the marble sinks in completely, add some dry atta.
  - Check: Why do we drop each marble on a fresh, smooth patch of dough? → **So every drop meets the same kind of surface**
- **s3 (11 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the marble with its bottom at the 10 centimetre mark. Let go. Do not push it. Leave the marble in its dent. Do this three times on three fresh patches, at least 8 centimetres apart. Then do three drops from 20 centimetres and three from 40 centimetres. Smooth the dough between rounds.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the marble at the mark. Let go. Do not push it. Leave it in its dent.  
  *Child mode:* Drop the marble from 10, 20 and 40 centimetres. Do three drops from each height.
  - Note: Keep the marble from rolling by letting go with a gentle opening of the fingers. Use the whole tray, a new patch each time. If the tray is full, smooth it flat again.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lift out each marble. Lay the ruler across the dent. Push a toothpick down to the bottom of the dent. Pinch it at the edge of the ruler, lift it out and measure it. Write the depth in millimetres. Find the middle of the three for each height.  
  *Parent says:* Lay the ruler across the dent. Push the toothpick to the bottom. Pinch it at the ruler. Then measure it.  
  *Child mode:* Measure how deep each dent is with a toothpick. Write it down.
  - Note: The numbers will be small, a few millimetres, so measure carefully. A dent from 40 centimetres is usually clearly deeper than one from 10 centimetres.
  - Check: You push a toothpick to the bottom of a dent, with a ruler lying across the top. Where do you pinch the toothpick? → **At the edge of the ruler**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar for each height, using the middle depths. Then ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each height. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: Listen first. Then say: a marble dropped from 4 times the height lands with twice the speed. In the 1720s, a scientist named Willem 's Gravesande dropped balls into clay, and found that twice the speed made a dent about 4 times as deep. Emilie du Chatelet used this to argue that the strength of a hit grows with speed times speed. Dough is softer and springs back a little, so your numbers will be less tidy. Look for the trend.
  - Think: Why does a car going fast need so much more room to stop?

### a1 · Toss on the Move: does a tossed ball come back to your hand? (15 min)

  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw and why.

  - Think: What would happen to the ball if the train suddenly braked while it was in the air?
### a2 · Cube Animals: a mouse, a cat and a horse made of cubes (20 min)

  - Ask: How many times more weight does the cat have than the mouse? (good: 8 times.; 8 cubes instead of 1.)
  - Check: The cat cube has 8 times the weight of the mouse cube. How many times more outside faces does it have? → **4 times, which is 24 faces instead of 6**

### a3 · Swings: does a heavy swing swing faster? (25 min)

  *Child mode:* Try a short string and a long string. Count the swings in 20 seconds.

- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Gravity pulls both balls down in the same way. The sideways speed changes only how far away the ball lands.
8. (mcq) Galileo's first idea was that the speed of a falling thing grows with the distance it has fallen. How did he show it was wrong?  
   Options: He saw that falling 4 feet and 8 feet would take the same time, which is impossible / He dropped two balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa / He measured the speed with a very fast clock  
   **Answer:** He saw that falling 4 feet and 8 feet would take the same time, which is impossible. *Why:* If speed grew with distance, the two falls would take equal times, which would mean no time at all. So the speed must grow with time.
9. (mcq) A ball is tossed straight up in a smoothly moving train, and it comes back to the thrower's hand. What does this show?  
   Options: The ball keeps the train's sideways speed while it goes up and down / The train must be standing still / Gravity does not work in trains  
   **Answer:** The ball keeps the train's sideways speed while it goes up and down. *Why:* The ball shared the sideways speed of the train before it was tossed, and it kept it. It moved forward as far as the thrower did.
10. (mcq) A mouse survives a fall that would hurt a horse. Which fact explains it best?  
   Options: A smaller animal has less weight for the area of her body that the air pushes on / Mice are always lucky / Mice fall at the speed of a feather  
   **Answer:** A smaller animal has less weight for the area of her body that the air pushes on. *Why:* Weight grows faster than outside area as an animal gets bigger. A mouse has a low top speed, and a horse has a high one.
11. (mcq) A car going 30 kilometres an hour needs 5 metres to stop. How far does it need at 60 kilometres an hour?  
   Options: About 20 metres / About 10 metres / About 5 metres  
   **Answer:** About 20 metres. *Why:* Twice the speed gives four times the movement energy, so about four times the distance. 4 times 5 is 20.
12. (mcq) What does a skydiver do to fall faster?  
   Options: Pulls in her arms and legs and dives head first, to meet less air / Spreads her arms and legs wide / Puts on a bigger coat  
   **Answer:** Pulls in her arms and legs and dives head first, to meet less air. *Why:* A smaller shape meets less air, so the push of the air matches her weight only at a higher speed.

## Applications
- Designing parachutes: a bigger canopy gives more push from the air, so a heavier load lands at a safe speed.
- Setting speed limits near schools, because twice the speed means four times the movement energy and about four times the distance to stop.
- Catching a ball in cricket: a fielder judges the curved path of the ball, which is a sideways movement and a fall added together.
- Heat shields on spacecraft that come back to Earth, which protect against the hot squeezed air in front.
- Choosing the right size of a seed or a drone for the job: a larger area for the weight means a slower fall.

## Spotted around us
- A stream of water from a tap gets thinner as it falls, because the water speeds up as it falls.
- A raindrop on a window or an umbrella tapping softly even when the cloud was very high.
- Winged sal seeds or other helicopter seeds spinning slowly down from a tree.
- A cricket ball hit for a six, rising and falling in a curve against the sky.
- A bus passenger's hanging strap swinging forward when the bus brakes, because the strap keeps moving forward.

## Open questions
- **How exactly does a falling leaf or sheet of paper flutter and tumble, and where will it land?** The air swirls around a falling flat thing in very complicated ways. Computers can model it, but scientists cannot yet predict exactly where one leaf will land.
- **Does antimatter fall in exactly the same way as ordinary matter?** In 2023, an experiment at CERN saw atoms of antihydrogen fall downward. The measurement is still rough, so scientists are building better tests.
- **Can anything be truly at rest?** Motion is always measured against something else. Scientists disagree about whether there is any special rest that all motion could be measured against.
- **Why do tests of falling find that everything falls alike, with no exceptions yet?** Tests have shown it to about 1 part in 10 to the power of 15. Einstein explained it with curved space and time, but nobody knows whether a deeper theory will show a tiny difference.
- **What is the quickest way an animal can fall without getting hurt?** Cats, flying squirrels, spiders and seeds all do it in different ways. Scientists are still working out how much of the saving comes from shape, from size and from the way the body bends on landing.

## Connected maths
- A mango falls from a tree for 3 seconds. With no air in the way, it gains 10 metres a second of speed every second. How fast is it going after 3 seconds, in metres a second and in kilometres an hour? (10 metres a second is 36 kilometres an hour.) **30 metres a second, which is 108 kilometres an hour.**. Each second adds 10, so 3 seconds give 3 times 10, which is 30 metres a second. In kilometres an hour it is 3 times 36, which is 108. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A stone falls 5 metres in the first second, 20 metres in the first 2 seconds and 45 metres in the first 3 seconds. How far does it fall in the second second alone, and in the third second alone? What pattern do you see? **15 metres in the second second and 25 metres in the third. The distances 5, 15, 25 go up by 10 each time.**. 20 take away 5 is 15. 45 take away 20 is 25. The gaps grow by 10 because the speed grows by 10 every second. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- In the Liner Stacks test, a family found a middle time of 1.8 seconds for one liner and 1.0 second for a stack of four. How much sooner did the stack land? Did four times the weight make the liners land in a quarter of the time? How do you know? **0.8 seconds sooner. No. The single liner took 1.8 times as long, not 4 times.**. 1.8 take away 1.0 is 0.8. 1.8 divided by 1.0 is 1.8, which is much less than 4. Four times the weight gives only about twice the top speed, so the stack is faster, but not four times faster. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- In the Walking Drop, a ball falls for about 0.5 seconds. Aarav walks at 1 metre a second, Meenakshi at 1.5 metres a second and Farhana at 2 metres a second. How far ahead of the mark does each ball land? **50 centimetres, 75 centimetres and 100 centimetres.**. The distance ahead is speed times 0.5 seconds. Half of 1 metre is 50 centimetres. Half of 1.5 metres is 75 centimetres. Half of 2 metres is 100 centimetres. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)
- In the dents test, a marble dropped from 10 centimetres makes a dent 3 millimetres deep. A drop from 40 centimetres is 4 times the height, so the marble hits with twice the speed, and twice the speed makes a dent 4 times as deep. How deep is the dent from 40 centimetres? What would you expect from 90 centimetres, which is 9 times the height and 3 times the speed? **12 millimetres from 40 centimetres. From 90 centimetres, 3 times the speed gives a dent 9 times as deep, which is 27 millimetres.**. 4 times 3 is 12. Three times the speed gives 3 times 3, which is 9 times the dent. 9 times 3 is 27. Real dough will not match exactly, because it springs back a little. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- Zoya builds a cube 4 cubes wide, 4 deep and 4 high. How many cubes does it weigh? Each side shows 4 times 4 faces. How many faces are on the outside? How many cubes of weight are there for each outside face? **64 cubes of weight and 96 outside faces. That is 64 divided by 96, which is two thirds, about 0.67.**. 4 times 4 times 4 is 64. One side has 16 faces and there are 6 sides, so 96. 64 over 96 is 2 over 3, about 0.67, which is more than the horse's 0.5. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
- A car at 20 kilometres an hour needs 2 metres to stop. Twice the speed needs 4 times the distance. How many metres does it need at 40 kilometres an hour, and at 80 kilometres an hour? **8 metres at 40 kilometres an hour and 32 metres at 80 kilometres an hour.**. At 40 the speed is twice as big, so the distance is 4 times 2, which is 8 metres. At 80 the speed has doubled again, so the distance is 4 times 8, which is 32 metres. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)

## Warm-up
- About how much speed does a falling thing gain each second, with no air? → About 10 metres a second.
- What is terminal speed? → The top speed of a falling thing in air, when the push of the air equals the pull of gravity.
- Why does a stack of four liners fall faster than one? → It is heavier, so it must go faster before the push of the air matches its weight.
- Where does a ball land if you let go of it while walking? → Ahead of the spot below your hand.
- A ball rolled off a table and a ball dropped from its edge. Which lands first? → They land together.
- Twice the speed gives how many times the movement energy? → Four times.
- Why can a mouse survive a fall that would break a horse? → She has less weight for the area the air pushes on.
- Why does a ball tossed up in a moving train land in your hand? → It keeps the train's sideways speed.
- What was Galileo's first wrong idea about falling? → That speed grows with the distance fallen. It grows with time.
