# Feedback loops: how bodies and machines sense, decide and correct
`sci.body.keeping-balance.L5`

**Summary.** You can balance a long stick on your palm for many seconds, but a pencil falls almost at once. Why is the short one harder? In this lesson you will test sticks of three lengths, measure what your pulse does when you stand up, and find the same three-part loop in your body, in an electric iron and in a flush tank.

**Intro.** *Stand a pencil upright on your open palm and let go with the other hand. Try to keep the pencil standing. How long did it stay up? Now guess: would a stick as long as your arm be easier or harder? Say why you think so.* Most people guess that the long stick is harder, because it is bigger and heavier. You will test that guess in the Stick Test. To keep anything steady, three jobs must be done again and again. Something must notice a change. Something must decide what to do. Something must act. Your body does these three jobs for your warmth, your blood pressure and your blood sugar. An iron, a fridge and a flush tank do them too. In this lesson you find the three jobs in each one, and you find out what happens when one job is missing or late.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can name the three jobs in a feedback loop, which are sensor, control centre and effector, and say what each one does.
1. By the end I can tell a closed loop from an open loop by asking whether news of the result comes back.
2. By the end I can explain, from my Stick Test, why a loop fails when its news is missing or arrives too late.
3. By the end I can trace the loop that keeps blood reaching my brain when I stand up.
4. By the end I can find the sensor, the control and the effector in an iron, a fridge and a flush tank.
5. By the end I can compare on-off control with how-much control, and say what each one is good for.
6. By the end I can give examples of feedback loops at many sizes: one cell, one body, one machine and perhaps the whole Earth.
7. By the end I can plan a fair test, repeat it, find a mean, and write my claim, evidence and reasoning.

## Terms
- **Homeostasis**: Homeostasis is the way a living body keeps its inside conditions within safe limits while the outside keeps changing. The word was made from Greek words meaning 'similar' and 'staying'. *e.g.* Your body stays close to 37 degrees Celsius in a cold cinema hall and on a hot May afternoon in Chennai.
- **Feedback**: Feedback is news about the result of an action that comes back and changes the next action. *e.g.* When you pour tea, you watch the level rise. That sight is feedback, and it tells your hand when to stop.
- **Feedback loop**: A feedback loop is a circle of cause and effect. A change is sensed, a response is made, the response alters the change, and the altered change is sensed again. *e.g.* Fridge warms, thermostat senses it, motor runs, fridge cools, thermostat senses that, motor stops.
- **Negative feedback**: Negative feedback is feedback that acts against a change, so the level comes back towards where it was. 'Negative' here means 'opposite', not 'bad'. *e.g.* You get hot, so you sweat, and the sweating cools you.
- **Sensor**: A sensor is a part that detects one kind of change and reports it. In the body, a sensor is also called a receptor. *e.g.* Warmth sensors in your skin report to your brain when your skin heats up.
- **Control centre**: A control centre is the part that receives the report, compares it with the level that is needed, and sends out an order. *e.g.* The brainstem compares your blood pressure with what is needed and orders your heart to speed up.
- **Effector**: An effector is a part that carries out the order and so changes the level. Muscles and glands are the body's effectors. *e.g.* Sweat glands are effectors. So is the heart muscle.
- **Response**: A response is what the effector does. *e.g.* Shivering is a response to cold. A faster pulse is a response to standing up.
- **Set point**: A set point is the level that a loop works to keep. In a machine it is set by a dial. In the body it is a useful idea, and scientists still argue about what makes it. *e.g.* An iron's dial at 'cotton' sets a higher set point than the dial at 'nylon'.
- **Error**: In a loop, the error is the gap between the level now and the set point. It does not mean a mistake. *e.g.* The room is at 29 degrees and the AC is set to 24 degrees. The error is 5 degrees.
- **Disturbance**: A disturbance is anything from outside the loop that pushes the level away from the set point. *e.g.* Opening the fridge door lets warm air in. That is a disturbance for the fridge.
- **Closed loop**: A closed loop is a system in which news of the result comes back and changes the action. *e.g.* A geyser with a thermostat stops heating when the water is hot enough.
- **Open loop**: An open loop is a system that acts without checking its own result. *e.g.* A mixer-grinder runs for as long as you hold the switch, whether the chutney is smooth or not.
- **Delay**: Delay is the time a loop takes to go once round, from sensing a change to finishing the response. *e.g.* It takes you about a fifth of a second to see a stick lean and start to move your hand.
- **On-off control**: On-off control is control with only two responses, fully on and fully off. *e.g.* An iron's heater is either on or off. The iron's temperature goes up and down a little round the set point.
- **How-much control**: How-much control makes the response bigger when the error is bigger and smaller when the error is smaller. Engineers call it proportional control. *e.g.* You sweat a little on a warm day and a lot after running in the sun.
- **Baroreceptor**: A baroreceptor is a sensor in the wall of a large artery that reports how much the blood is stretching the wall. An artery is a tube that carries blood away from the heart. *e.g.* Baroreceptors in the arteries of your neck report a drop in pressure when you stand up.
- **Brainstem**: The brainstem is the lowest part of the brain, where it joins the spinal cord. It holds the control centres for heartbeat, blood pressure and breathing. *e.g.* Your brainstem adjusts your heart while you sleep.
- **Hypothalamus**: The hypothalamus is a part of the brain about the size of an almond, deep in the middle. It holds control centres for warmth, thirst and hunger. *e.g.* When your blood becomes a little too salty, the hypothalamus makes you feel thirsty.
- **Thermostat**: A thermostat is a device that senses temperature and switches a heater or cooler on and off to keep the temperature near a set point. *e.g.* The thermostat in a fridge starts the motor when the inside warms up.
- **Bimetallic strip**: A bimetallic strip is two different metals joined along their length. One metal grows more than the other when heated, so the strip bends. *e.g.* Inside an iron, a bimetallic strip bends as it heats and pulls a switch open.
- **Float valve**: A float valve is a tap opened and closed by a float that rides on the water. *e.g.* In a flush tank, the float rises with the water and shuts the tap when the tank is full.
- **Governor**: A governor is a device that keeps the speed of an engine steady. *e.g.* On James Watt's steam engines, two spinning metal balls swung outwards as the engine sped up, and that closed the steam valve a little.
- **Cybernetics**: Cybernetics is the study of control and feedback in animals, including humans, and in machines. Norbert Wiener named it in 1948 from the Greek word for a person who steers a boat. *e.g.* A cybernetics question: what do a thermostat and the hypothalamus have in common?

## Explanations
### Every feedback loop has three jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** A feedback loop needs a sensor to detect a change, a control centre to compare and decide, and an effector to act. The result of the action is then sensed again.

Think of pouring water to a line. Your eyes detect the level. Your brain compares the level with the line and decides. Your hand tips the jug or stops. Then your eyes look again. These are the three jobs, and they repeat in a circle. Homeostasis works the same way. For your warmth, the sensors are nerve endings in the skin and in the brain that respond to temperature. The control centre is in the hypothalamus. The effectors are sweat glands, the muscles round the small blood vessels of your skin, and the big muscles that shiver. Why three jobs and not two? Without a sensor, nothing knows there is a change. Without a comparison, nothing knows which way to act, or when to stop. Without an effector, nothing changes. Take away any one, and the level drifts.
- e.g. Body warmth: temperature sensors, then the hypothalamus, then sweat glands and skin blood vessels.
- e.g. A fridge: the thermostat's sensing tube, then the thermostat's switch, then the motor that pumps heat out.
- e.g. A flush tank: the float, then the arm, then the inlet tap.
### Closed loop or open loop: does the news come back? · *defined*
**Definition:** A closed loop checks its own result and changes its action. An open loop acts without checking.

To tell them apart, ask one question. Does news of the result come back and change what the system does next? A fan regulator sets a speed, and the fan keeps that speed whether the room is hot or cold. That is an open loop. An air conditioner measures the room and works harder or stops. That is a closed loop. A sensor alone does not make a closed loop. A street light with a light sensor comes on at dusk. But the lamp does not change the daylight that the sensor measures. No news about its own result comes back, so it is an open loop with a sensor. Open loops are simple and cheap, and they work well when nothing unexpected happens. Closed loops cope with disturbances, which are pushes from outside that nobody planned for. Living bodies meet disturbances all day, so they are full of closed loops.
- e.g. Open: a toaster timer, a washing machine's wash timer, a fan regulator, a tap left running into a bucket.
- e.g. Closed: a fridge, an iron, a geyser with a thermostat, a flush tank, your pupils in bright light.
- e.g. Pouring with eyes shut is open loop. Pouring while watching the level is closed loop.
### Set point, error and negative feedback · *defined*
**Definition:** The set point is the level a loop keeps. The error is the gap between the level now and the set point. In negative feedback, the response always acts to make the error smaller.

A control centre does not need to know the level itself. It needs to know the error: how far the level is from the set point, and on which side. If the level is too high, the response pushes down. If it is too low, the response pushes up. The response is opposite to the error, which is why this is called negative feedback. Why does this make things steady? Because the response shrinks the error, and a smaller error then calls for a smaller response. The loop quietens itself. There is also positive feedback, where the response makes the change bigger. A microphone held near its loudspeaker squeals for this reason. The body uses positive feedback only for things that must finish quickly, such as a blood clot sealing a cut. For keeping a level steady, it uses negative feedback.
- e.g. An AC set to 24 degrees in a room at 29 degrees has an error of 5 degrees, so it cools.
- e.g. Blood too salty: you feel thirsty and your kidneys hold back water. Blood too watery: thirst stops and the kidneys let water go.
- e.g. A float in a flush tank is below its set height, so the tap is open. As the float rises, the error shrinks to nothing and the tap shuts.
### Delay: a loop must go round faster than the change it is correcting · *defined*
**Definition:** Every loop takes time to go round. If the change happens faster than the loop can go round, the correction arrives too late.

In the Stick Test, your eyes see the stick lean, your brain decides, and your arm moves the hand. One trip round takes about a fifth of a second. A pencil that starts to tip is far over in about a quarter of a second. So you get only one correction, and one is rarely enough. A stick one metre long takes more than twice as long to tip the same amount, so you can make several corrections in the time. That is why the long stick is easier, though it is bigger. Shut your eyes, and the best sensor is gone. The touch sensors in your palm still give some news, but it is rough and slow, and the stick falls. A late correction can even make things worse. If you answer where the stick was a moment ago, you may push the wrong way. Engineers call the back-and-forth swinging of a slow loop 'hunting'.
- e.g. A geyser takes minutes to heat, so its thermostat may be slow. A stick falls in under a second, so its loop must be very fast.
- e.g. Your pupils correct for bright light in about a second. Your kidneys correct your water over an hour or more.
- e.g. When the hot tap takes a few seconds to answer, people turn it too far, and then too far back.
### Standing up: the blood pressure loop · *defined*
**Definition:** When you stand, gravity pulls blood towards your legs. Sensors in your neck arteries detect the drop in pressure, and the brainstem speeds the heart and narrows blood vessels within a few seconds.

Your brain needs a steady flow of blood. Blood flows because the heart keeps it under pressure. When you lie down, your head, heart and legs are at one level. When you stand, gravity pulls blood down into the legs and belly. Less blood returns to the heart, so each beat sends out less, and the pressure in the arteries of the neck starts to fall. Baroreceptors are stretch sensors in the walls of those arteries and of the large artery leaving the heart. Lower pressure stretches the walls less, so the sensors send fewer signals. Control centres in the brainstem read the change. They order the heart to beat faster and the muscle in the walls of small arteries to tighten. Pressure comes back up. All of this is finished in a few seconds, without you choosing any of it. If the loop is a little slow, you feel dizzy or your sight goes dim for a moment. Standing still, the pull of gravity continues, so the pulse stays a little faster than it was lying down.
- e.g. In the Stand-Up Test, most people's pulse jumps in the first 15 seconds of standing and then settles a little.
- e.g. A giraffe's heart keeps the blood at about twice our pressure, because the brain is so far above the heart. Scientists are still working out how giraffes cope when they lower their heads to drink.
- e.g. Astronauts back from months in orbit often feel faint when they first stand, because their loop has had no practice against gravity.
### Control centres: the brain, and cells that are their own control · *defined*
**Definition:** Many of the body's control centres are in the brainstem and the hypothalamus. But some loops have no brain part at all: one cell senses, decides and responds.

The brainstem runs the loops for blood pressure, heartbeat and breathing. The hypothalamus runs loops for warmth, water and hunger. In 1951 the Indian doctor Bal Krishan Anand, working with John Brobeck at Yale University, found a small area at the side of the hypothalamus in rats. When it was damaged, the rats stopped eating. The two called it a feeding centre. Later work showed that hunger is controlled by several areas and signals together, so the picture today is more complicated. Not every loop passes through the brain. A beta cell in the pancreas senses the glucose in the blood that reaches it. Glucose is the sugar your blood carries. When there is more glucose, the same cell releases more insulin, and insulin makes other cells take glucose in. The sensor and the control are inside one cell. So the saying 'the brain controls the body' is only partly true. The brain runs many loops, and many others run without it.
- e.g. Brainstem: blood pressure, breathing, heartbeat.
- e.g. Hypothalamus: warmth, thirst, hunger.
- e.g. One cell as sensor and control: a beta cell in the pancreas for glucose.
### Machines with loops: the float, the bending strip and the spinning balls · *defined*
**Definition:** People have built feedback loops into machines for more than two thousand years. A float valve, a thermostat and a governor each sense, compare and act.

About 270 BCE in Alexandria, Ktesibios built a water clock that needed a steady water level. He used a float that rose and blocked the inlet, and sank and opened it. The flush tank in a bathroom and the float valve in an overhead tank work the same way today. In an electric iron, the sensor is a bimetallic strip: two metals joined together. When heated, one metal grows longer than the other, so the strip bends. At the set temperature the bend pulls two metal contacts apart and the heater goes off. The iron cools, the strip straightens, the contacts touch and the heater comes on. The dial moves the contact, and that changes the set point. In 1788 James Watt fitted a governor to his steam engines. Two heavy balls spun with the engine. If the engine sped up, the balls swung outwards and a lever closed the steam valve a little. In each machine you can find the same three jobs as in the body. In some, one part does two jobs.
- e.g. Flush tank: the float is the sensor, the arm is the control, and the inlet tap is the effector.
- e.g. Iron: the bimetallic strip senses, its contacts switch, and the heating coil acts.
- e.g. Voltage stabiliser beside a fridge or television: it senses the mains voltage and switches its coils to correct the voltage.
### On-off control and how-much control · *defined*
**Definition:** On-off control has two responses, full and none. How-much control makes the response match the size of the error.

An iron's heater is either on or off. So the iron heats past the set point, switches off, cools below the set point, and switches on. Its temperature moves up and down in a small zigzag. This is cheap and good enough for ironing. An older air conditioner works the same way: the compressor, which is its pump, runs fully or stops. An inverter air conditioner changes the speed of its compressor. When the room is far from the set point it runs fast, and when the room is close it runs slowly. The temperature stays smoother, and it wastes less electricity. The body mostly uses how-much control. You sweat a little when you are a little warm and a lot when you are very warm. Your heart can beat at any rate between resting and racing. But single nerve cells and muscle fibres are on-off: each one fires or does not. The body gets a how-much response by changing how many of them fire, and how often.
- e.g. In the Iron Light Test, the light goes on and off. The share of time it stays on is larger at a hotter setting.
- e.g. In the Blind Pencil, 'left' and 'right' calls give a zigzag line. 'A little left' gives a smoother one.
- e.g. Shivering can be a small tremble or a strong shake, depending on how cold you are.
### Loops inside one cell · *defined*
**Definition:** A single cell runs feedback loops with molecules instead of nerves, one more kind of hardware for the same job. Molecules do the sensing, the comparing and the acting.

The bacterium Escherichia coli lives in the gut of humans and other animals. They swim towards food such as sugar. To do this they compare the amount of sugar now with the amount a few seconds ago. If things are getting better, they keep swimming straight. If not, they tumble and try a new direction. To compare, they must reset their sensors to each new background level. In 2000, Tau-Mu Yi, Yun Huang, Melvin Simon and John Doyle showed that this resetting is a kind of feedback that engineers already knew and used in machines, called integral feedback. Cells also control their own genes by feedback. In 1961, François Jacob and Jacques Monod showed how E. coli switch on the genes for digesting milk sugar only when milk sugar is present. So homeostasis did not begin with brains. Cells were running loops for billions of years before any animal had a nerve.
- e.g. E. coli reset their sugar sensors again and again, so they can detect small changes at any background level.
- e.g. A cell that has made enough of a substance often uses that same substance to slow the enzyme that makes it.
- e.g. The guard cells round each tiny pore in a leaf close the pore when the plant is short of water.
### When a loop is broken, and how people mend it · *defined*
**Definition:** If the sensor, the control or the effector of a loop fails, the level drifts. Some broken loops can be closed again from outside.

In type 1 diabetes, the beta cells of the pancreas are lost, so the body's sensor and control for glucose are gone together. For a hundred years people have closed the loop by hand. They prick a finger to measure glucose, decide on a dose, and inject insulin. The person is the sensor and the control centre. Today some people wear a closed-loop system. A small sensor under the skin measures glucose every few minutes. A computer program compares the reading with a target. A pump gives more or less insulin. It is the same three jobs, done by a machine joined to a body. These systems still need the person's help at meal times, and a fully automatic one is not yet here. Other loops fail for a short time. In heatstroke the body can no longer lose heat fast enough, and the temperature climbs. A person who faints on standing has a blood pressure loop that answered too slowly. Lying flat brings the blood back to the brain.
- e.g. A finger-prick glucose test, a decision and an injection: a person closing a loop by hand.
- e.g. A glucose sensor patch on the arm that sends readings to an insulin pump.
- e.g. A helper who fans and cools a person with heatstroke is acting as that person's effector. Heatstroke needs a doctor quickly.
### Is a set point really written somewhere in the body? · *debated*
A thermostat has a dial, and the set point is a real position of that dial. Is there anything like a dial for 37 degrees in the brain? Scientists disagree. This matters because it changes how we draw the loop: one boss with a target, or many small loops with no boss.
- e.g. Fever: body temperature is held at a higher level for a while. One view says the dial was turned up. The other says the thresholds of the separate loops shifted.
- e.g. Skin blood vessels, sweating and shivering each begin at a slightly different body temperature.
- **The older textbook picture:** The hypothalamus holds one set point, compares body temperature with it, and commands all the effectors, as a thermostat does.
- **Andrej Romanovsky, physiologist (2007):** There is no single controller and no stored number. Sweating, shivering and skin blood flow are separate loops, each with its own sensors and its own switching temperature. They seem to agree only because they all respond to the same body temperature. The steady 37 degrees is what their combined action produces.
### Does a loop want something? · *debated*
A thermostat acts until the room reaches 24 degrees and then stops. A bacterium swims until they reach the sugar. Both act towards an end. In 1943 Arturo Rosenblueth, Norbert Wiener and Julian Bigelow wrote that this is what purpose is: behaviour steered by negative feedback from a goal. Philosophers have argued about that ever since. A bacterium who swims towards sugar wants the sugar: every living being senses, chooses and acts. Whether the same can be said of a thermostat is a question this lesson leaves open.
- e.g. A dog who is hot looks for shade. A fridge that is warm runs its motor.
- e.g. A float valve keeps a tank full with no wires, no brain and no program.
- **Arturo Rosenblueth, Norbert Wiener and Julian Bigelow (1943):** Purposeful behaviour is behaviour controlled by negative feedback from a goal. This is true of a cat chasing a mouse and of a machine that tracks a target.
- **Richard Taylor, philosopher (1950):** That definition is too wide. A machine with feedback only behaves as if it had a purpose. Purpose needs someone who wants or intends the goal.
- **Many biologists today:** A bacterium's swimming is chemistry that evolution has tuned. They call it goal-directed, and they avoid the word 'want' for a single cell.
### Does the Earth keep its own temperature steady? · *debated*
The Sun has grown brighter over billions of years, yet the Earth has had liquid water for most of that time. Something has acted against the change. One slow loop is widely accepted. When the Earth is warmer, rain breaks down rock faster, and that takes more carbon dioxide out of the air. Less carbon dioxide means less warming. James Walker, Paul Hays and James Kasting described this in 1981. It works over hundreds of thousands of years, far too slowly to undo the warming people are causing now. A bolder idea is that living beings themselves are part of the Earth's loops.
- e.g. Warm, wet weather wears rock away faster than cold, dry weather does.
- e.g. Daisyworld is a made-up planet in a computer. Dark daisies warm it and pale daisies cool it, and together they hold its temperature steady.
- **James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis (1974):** Life and the air, sea and rocks together form one system that keeps the planet fit for life. They named it the Gaia hypothesis. Lynn Margulis supplied the knowledge of microbes that the idea needed.
- **Many biologists and Earth scientists:** Living beings do change the air and the sea, and some loops are real. But nothing shows that the whole planet is regulated for the good of life. Some changes made by living beings seem to have harmed most other life.

## Mostly agreed
- Living bodies keep many inside conditions within narrow limits by negative feedback. *How we know:* Claude Bernard described the steady 'inner environment' of the body in the 1860s and 1870s. Walter Cannon named homeostasis in the 1920s. Each loop since has been traced by measuring what happens when one part is blocked.
- A feedback loop needs sensing, comparing and acting, and it fails if any of the three is missing. *How we know:* You can test it yourself. In the Stick Test, closing your eyes removes the main sensor, and the stick falls within a second or two.
- Stretch sensors in the neck arteries and the large artery from the heart report blood pressure to the brainstem. *How we know:* Heinrich Hering showed the reflex from the neck artery in 1923 and found the nerve that carries the signal. Corneille Heymans received the 1938 Nobel Prize for work on these sensors.
- A loop with a long delay tends to overshoot and swing. *How we know:* Early steam engine governors made engines speed up and slow down again and again. James Clerk Maxwell explained why with mathematics in 1868, in a paper called 'On Governors'.
- Single cells run feedback loops with molecules instead of nerves. *How we know:* The loop by which E. coli reset their sugar sensors has been worked out molecule by molecule and matched to an engineer's integral feedback (Yi, Huang, Simon and Doyle, 2000).
- The same rules of feedback describe bodies and machines. *How we know:* The mathematics Maxwell wrote for governors is used today for artificial pancreas systems, aircraft autopilots and models of the blood pressure loop.

## How we found out
**Johann Czermak, Heinrich Hering, Fernando de Castro and Corneille Heymans, The 1860s (Czermak), 1923 (Hering) and the 1938 Nobel Prize (Heymans).** In the 1860s the Czech scientist Johann Czermak found that pressing a finger on the side of the neck made the heart slow down. He explained it this way. A large nerve called the vagus runs down the neck to the heart, and it slows the heart. So the finger must be squeezing that nerve. Doctors accepted this for about sixty years and called it the vagus pressure test. In 1923 the German doctor Heinrich Hering tested the idea. He showed that the slowing did not come from squeezing the vagus nerve. It began at one small spot, a slight bulge where the neck artery divides in two. Pressing or stretching the wall of that bulge slowed the heart and lowered the blood pressure. He also found the thin nerve that carries the news from that spot to the brain. The bulge held sensors. The Spanish scientist Fernando de Castro drew its nerve endings under the microscope, and Corneille Heymans of Belgium showed how they work. Heymans received the 1938 Nobel Prize. De Castro did not share it. Never press on anyone's neck to try this.  
*What people believed before:* That pressing on the neck slows the heart because the finger squeezes the vagus nerve. Hering showed that the slowing starts from stretch sensors in the wall of the neck artery.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The brain controls everything the body does.** (A common saying; *rejected*)  
  For: The brainstem and hypothalamus do run the loops for blood pressure, breathing, warmth and thirst.  
  Against: A beta cell in the pancreas senses glucose and releases insulin with no order from the brain. Kidney cells, liver cells and single bacteria run their own loops too. The brain is one control centre among many.
- **The stronger the correction, the steadier the level.** (A natural first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: A weak response lets the level drift a long way before it comes back.  
  Against: A response that is too strong for the loop's delay overshoots, and the level swings from side to side. In the game Drift, Bang or Nudge, a fixed jump of 4 often lands on the far side of the target.
- **The hypothalamus holds a set point of 37 degrees, like the dial of a thermostat.** (Many school and medical textbooks; *debated*)  
  For: Body temperature behaves as if there were a dial, and fever looks like the dial being turned up.  
  Against: Nobody has found a stored number. Andrej Romanovsky and others argue that separate loops for sweating, shivering and skin blood flow together produce the steady level.
- **The body corrects a change only after the change has happened.** (The simplest picture of homeostasis; *debated*)  
  For: Many responses do follow a change. Your pulse rises after you stand, not before.  
  Against: The brain also acts ahead of time. Your mouth waters and insulin starts to rise at the sight and smell of food. Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer named this predicting 'allostasis' in 1988.
- **Living beings and the Earth together form one self-regulating system.** (James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis, 1974; *minority*)  
  For: Life has changed the air and the oceans, and some planet-wide negative feedback loops are real.  
  Against: Many scientists say there is no evidence that the loops act for the good of life as a whole. Some changes made by living beings have made the planet worse for most life.

## Roots
We have learned that a level is held steady by a loop of sensing, deciding and acting. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Stick Test, the stick fell within a second or two when you shut your eyes. Your brain and your hand were as able as before. What had stopped was the news going round. In a feedback loop, each part acts only on what the part before it sends. So a steady level is made by the whole circle, and no single part is in charge of it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We look for the one part that is in charge: the brain in a body, the thermostat in a fridge. That picture has orders going one way, from a boss to workers. In a loop, the worker's result comes back and changes the next order, so the causes run in a circle.  
*Evidence:* In the Stick Test, removing only the news from the eyes made the stick fall. In Pour to the Line, the same hand poured well or badly depending on whether news of the level came back. In the Bowl That Refills Itself, the level held with no control part at all. In the pancreas, one beta cell is sensor and control together.

- *who am i:* Hundreds of loops are holding you steady at this moment, and you started none of them on purpose. Part of what you call 'me' is this constant sensing and correcting.
- *what is life:* Every living being, from a bacterium to a banyan tree, keeps their inside steady while the outside changes. Staying alive means sensing and correcting, all the time.
- *what is this world:* The same loop turns up in a cell, a body, an iron and perhaps the whole Earth. In each one, an effect comes back and becomes a cause.

Without a loop, a level goes wherever it is pushed. Join a sensor, a comparison and a response in a circle, and something new appears: a level that holds itself. That one pattern is found in a bacterium seeking sugar, in your pulse when you stand, in a flush tank and in a steam engine. The parts are different each time. The circle is the same. One way of working has become many kinds of steady things.

- **Tao Te Ching 40:** Returning is the movement of the Way. (Feedback means the result of an action returning to change the next action. A flush tank, a pulse and a boat held on a line across a river all stay steady because something returns.)
- **Udana 1.1:** The Buddha, just after his awakening, said: when this is, that is. When this arises, that arises. (In a loop nothing acts by itself. The heart speeds up because the brainstem signalled, the brainstem signalled because the sensors reported, and the sensors reported because of what the heart had done.)
- **Thirukkural 948:** Find the illness, find its cause, find what will ease it, and then act in the way that fits. (Valluvar gives a healer three steps: notice, work out what is needed, and act. These are the three jobs of a loop, the sensor, the control and the effector, written down in Tamil long ago.)
- Science (settled): The same mathematics of feedback describes a bacterium resetting their sensors, the blood pressure loop of a human and the thermostat of a machine.
- Science (debated): Rosenblueth, Wiener and Bigelow proposed in 1943 that purpose is behaviour steered by negative feedback from a goal. Whether that is all purpose is has been argued over ever since.
- Other view, Many physiologists and engineers: Causes never truly run in a circle. Each event is caused by an earlier one: the lean of the stick now causes the hand's move a fifth of a second later. The circle is only in our drawing. And some parts do matter more than others. Damage to the brainstem stops many loops at once, and damage to one sweat gland stops none.
- Practice, Feel a loop between your breath and your heart (3 min): Sit comfortably. Find your pulse at your wrist with two fingers. Now breathe in slowly while I count to four. Breathe out slowly while I count to six. Keep going, and feel the beats. In many children the beats come a little closer together on the in-breath. They spread out a little on the out-breath. You may feel it, or you may not. Either way, your heart and your breathing are joined by loops in your brainstem. They are working now, and you did not start them.
- Ask: In the Stick Test your hand moved before you could say which way to move. So who balanced the stick?
- Ask: In a circle of causes, is there a first cause?
- Ask: A thermostat acts until the room is right. A bacterium swims until they reach the sugar. Is either of them wanting something? How could you tell?
- Ask: Hundreds of loops keep you steady without asking you. Are they you, or do they work for you?

## Story: The Post and the Cross
*A made-up story. Kainakary is a real village in Kuttanad, Kerala, where many families cross the water in small boats every day. The people and the ferry are imagined. Steering by keeping a near mark and a far mark in one line is a real method that boat people use all over the world. The word 'cybernetics', the study of feedback, comes from the Greek word for a person who steers a boat.*

Ammini had counted the river since she was seven. From their landing at Kainakary to the landing on the far bank it was forty strokes of the paddle. Her aunt Thankamma had taken the ferry canoe across more times than anyone could count. But Ammini counted. Forty strokes, with the nose of the boat pointed at the red post on the other side.

This year Thankamma let her paddle on calm mornings. Ammini sat at the back, where the steering is done, and her aunt sat in the middle with the passengers. Forty strokes. Every time, the nose of the boat touched the mud beside the red post. Ammini began to think there was nothing more to learn.

In the second week of June her aunt slipped on the wet steps and sprained her wrist. The health worker bound it with a crepe bandage and said, no paddling for ten days.

'Then who will take me across tomorrow?' the health worker asked. Her name was Sister Lissy, and three babies on the far bank were due their injections.

'Ammini will,' said Thankamma.

It rained all that night. It rained in the hills too, far to the east, where the river begins.

In the morning the water was the colour of tea with milk, and it was moving. Clumps of water hyacinth slid past the landing faster than a person walks. Sister Lissy climbed in with her blue box. Thankamma climbed in after her and sat in the middle, holding her bandaged wrist in her lap.

Ammini pointed the nose at the red post and began. One. Two. Three. She paddled well. She knew she was paddling well. The strokes were long and even, and the nose of the boat pointed at the red post the whole way.

At thirty she looked up properly, and her stomach turned over. The red post was not in front of her. It was far away to the left, and getting further. The nose still pointed towards it, but the whole boat had slid sideways down the river while she counted.

At forty the boat pushed into a mat of hyacinth below somebody's coconut grove, a long way from any landing. Sister Lissy had to climb out into mud up to her shins. She did not say anything unkind. She took off her sandals, picked up her blue box and walked up the bank.

Ammini's face was hot. 'I did forty,' she said. 'I pointed at the post. I did everything the same.'

'You did,' said her aunt. 'The river did not.'

They waited under the coconut palms. Thankamma did not explain. She watched the hyacinth go by.

When Sister Lissy came back, they dragged the boat along the bank until it was level with the far landing. Ammini's arms already ached. Across the water she could see their own landing, with its white post where the boats were tied. Behind it, quite far back, stood the church, with a small cross on the roof.

'Do you see our white post?' Thankamma asked. 'And the cross behind it?'

'Yes.'

'From here the post is right under the cross. Keep the post under the cross. That is all. Do not count.'

Ammini pushed off. She pointed the nose at the white post and paddled. After five strokes the post was no longer under the cross. It had slipped to the right of it. Only by the width of a finger, but it had moved.

She understood before she could have said it. The post had not moved. The cross had not moved. She had. The river was carrying her to one side, and the two marks were showing her so, at once, while it was still only a finger's width.

She turned the nose upstream, to the right, and pulled hard. The post crept back under the cross. She eased off. It began to slip again, so she turned the nose a little further. It was strange. The boat was not pointing at the landing at all now. It was pointing well upstream, at a clump of banana plants. But the post stayed under the cross, and the banana plants slid away to the side, and the landing grew.

In the middle of the river the current was stronger, and the post slipped faster. She gave the nose more. Near the bank the current was weak, and the post slipped the other way, to the left of the cross. She gave the nose less. She never once thought, now the current is stronger. She only watched the gap between the post and the cross, and answered it.

The boat touched the mud beside the white post. Sister Lissy stepped out with dry feet.

'How many strokes?' Thankamma asked.

Ammini opened her mouth and shut it again. She had no idea.

'Forty was right for a quiet river,' her aunt said. 'I counted it for you when you were small, on a quiet day. It was never the river's number.'

That evening the wind got up from the west and blew against the current. Ammini brought the schoolteacher home from the far bank. The post slipped to the left of the cross this time. She did not stop to wonder why. She turned the nose the other way, a little, and then a little less.

At the landing the teacher asked how long she had been steering.

'Since this morning,' Ammini said.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Ammini's count of forty strokes is a plan made in advance. It works only while nothing changes, and it has no way of noticing when something does. The post and the cross give her news of the result while she is still acting. The gap between them is the error. She turns the boat against the gap, more when the gap grows fast and less when it grows slowly. That is a feedback loop with a sensor, a comparison and a response. She does not need to know the cause of the drift, whether current or wind. She needs only to see the gap early and answer it.

- Ask: Why did counting forty strokes work on calm days and fail after the rain?
- Ask: What did the post and the cross tell Ammini that pointing the boat at the post did not?
- Ask: In the middle of the river she turned the boat more, and near the bank less. How did she know how much?
- Ask: In the evening the wind pushed the boat the other way. Why did she not need a new rule?
- Ask: Where in your own day do you follow a fixed plan, and where do you watch the result and correct?

### g2 · Drift, Bang or Nudge: which rule keeps the counter nearest to 10? (20 min)
- **rules:** You need one die and one counter, such as a button or a tamarind seed. Put the counter on square 10.; Each turn starts with a disturbance. Roll the die. ### e1 · The Stick Test: which stick is easiest to balance, and what does balancing need? (40 min)
- **question:** Which is easiest to keep upright on your palm: a pencil, a 30 centimetre ruler or a stick 1 metre long? And where do your eyes need to look?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put the pencil, the ruler and the long stick in order, from easiest to hardest to balance. Then guess which is best for the long stick: looking at its top, looking at your hand, or shutting your eyes. Write both guesses, and write the reason for each.

- **record:** Fill in the table with every try and every mean. Draw a bar chart of the three sticks, and a second bar chart for the three ways of looking. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.

  *Parent does:* 1. Clear a floor space and switch off the fan. 2. Show how to stand a stick upright on a flat, open palm. 3. Your child tries each stick twice for practice. 4. Agree when the timer starts and stops.  
  *Parent says:* Hold your palm flat. Stand the stick on it and let go with the other hand. You may move your hand and your feet.  
  *Child mode:* Clear a space. Stand a stick on your flat palm. Let go. Try each stick twice.

- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the three sticks in a row. 2. Ask your child to order them from easiest to hardest. 3. Ask for the reason. 4. Write the guess down.

  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children and grown-ups expect the pencil to be easiest.

  *Child mode:* Balance the pencil 5 times. Then the ruler. Then the long stick. Look at the top.

- **s4 (2 min)**

  *Child mode:* Guess where your eyes should look. Guess what shut eyes will do. Say why.
- **s5 (7 min)**

  - Note: Stand close during the eyes-shut tries and be ready to catch the stick. It usually falls within a second or two.

If your long stick did not win, check that the palm stayed open and the eyes stayed on the top, then repeat.
  - Check: With your eyes shut, the long stick fell quickly. Which job of the loop was missing? → **Sensing: news of the lean**
### e2 · The Stand-Up Test: what does your pulse do when you stand up? (25 min)

## Worksheets
### Feedback loops: sensor, control and effector (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which three jobs does every feedback loop need?  
   Options: Sensing, deciding and acting / Heating, cooling and resting / Starting, running and stopping  
   **Answer:** Sensing, deciding and acting. *Why:* A loop must detect the change, compare it with what is needed, and act. Take one away and the level drifts.
2. (fill) The part of a loop that detects a change is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['sensor', 'receptor']. *Why:* A sensor detects one kind of change and reports it. In the body a sensor is also called a receptor.
3. (match) Match each part of the body-warmth loop to its job.  
   **Answer:** Nerve endings in the skin → sensor; Hypothalamus → control centre; Sweat glands → effector; Sweating → response. *Why:* The nerve endings detect warmth. The hypothalamus compares and sends an order. The sweat glands carry it out, and what they do is the response.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a fan with a regulator slows down by itself when the room gets cold.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A fan regulator sets a speed and never checks the room. It is an open loop.
5. (sort) Sort each machine: closed loop or open loop?  
   **Answer:** fridge → Closed loop; mixer-grinder → Open loop; electric iron → Closed loop; tube light → Open loop; flush tank → Closed loop; toaster timer → Open loop. *Why:* A fridge, an iron and a flush tank each measure their own result and change what they do. The others run as set, whatever the result.
6. (mcq) You pour water with your eyes shut and it overflows. What was missing?  
   Options: A stronger hand / News of the water level / A bigger jug  
   **Answer:** News of the water level. *Why:* With eyes shut, no news of the level came back, so the hand could not stop in time. The loop was open.
7. (write) In the Stick Test, the pencil fell much sooner than the long stick. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** A short stick tips over faster than a long one. My eyes, brain and hand need about a fifth of a second for each correction. The pencil is too far over before the correction arrives. The long stick tips slowly, so there is time for several corrections.. *Why:* A strong answer compares two times: how fast the stick tips, and how long the loop takes to go round.
8. (mcq) With eyes shut, the long stick fell quickly. Which part of the loop had been removed?  
   Options: The control centre / The effector / The main sensor  
   **Answer:** The main sensor. *Why:* The eyes are the main sensor for the stick's lean. The brain and the arm were unchanged.
9. (order) Put these in order, for what happens when you stand up.  
   **Answer:** ['Gravity pulls blood towards your legs', 'Pressure in the neck arteries falls a little', 'Baroreceptors send fewer signals', 'The brainstem speeds the heart and tightens small arteries', 'The pressure comes back up']. *Why:* The fall in pressure is sensed in the neck arteries. The brainstem compares and orders the heart and arteries to act. The response undoes the fall.
10. (fill) Stretch sensors in the walls of the neck arteries are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['baroreceptors']. *Why:* Baroreceptors report how much the blood is stretching the artery wall, which shows the pressure.
11. (match) Match each part of a flush tank to its job in the loop.  
   **Answer:** Float → sensor; Arm joining float to tap → control; Inlet tap → effector; Water height → the level that is kept. *Why:* The float detects the height. The arm turns that height into more or less opening. The tap lets water in.
12. (mcq) What does the bimetallic strip in an iron do when it gets hot?  
   Options: It bends and opens a switch / It melts / It glows red  
   **Answer:** It bends and opens a switch. *Why:* The strip is two metals joined together. One grows more when heated, so the strip bends, and the bend pulls the switch open.
13. (draw) Draw the loop for a fridge as a circle. Label the sensor, the control, the effector and the level.  
   **Answer:** A circle of four parts with arrows: the temperature inside the fridge, the thermostat's sensor, the thermostat switch, and the compressor motor, which leads back to the temperature.. *Why:* A strong drawing is a closed circle. The arrow from the motor must come back to the temperature, because the motor's work changes what the sensor detects.
14. (truefalse) True or false: an iron's heater is on for a larger share of the time at a hotter setting.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A hotter iron loses heat to the room faster, so the heater must be on for more of the time to make up the loss.
15. (write) A street light has a light sensor and comes on at dusk. Is it a closed loop? Give your reason.  
   **Answer:** No. The sensor measures daylight, and the lamp does not change the daylight. No news about the lamp's own result comes back, so the loop is open.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a sensor alone is not enough. The result of the action must come back to the sensor.
### Error, delay and two kinds of control (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In a loop, what does 'error' mean?  
   Options: A mistake made by the sensor / The gap between level and set point / A broken effector  
   **Answer:** The gap between level and set point. *Why:* The error is how far the level is from the set point, and on which side. It is a measurement, not a mistake.
2. (fill) Feedback that acts against a change is called ____ feedback.  
   **Answer:** ['negative']. *Why:* 'Negative' here means opposite. The response pushes the level back the opposite way to the change.
3. (mcq) An AC is set to 24 degrees. The room is at 30 degrees. What is the error?  
   Options: 24 degrees / 54 degrees / 6 degrees too warm  
   **Answer:** 6 degrees too warm. *Why:* The error is the gap between now and the set point. 30 − 24 = 6, and the room is on the warm side.
4. (sort) Sort each control: on-off, or how-much?  
   **Answer:** an iron's heater → On-off control; an inverter air conditioner → How-much control; sweating → How-much control; a fridge motor that runs or stops → On-off control; your heart rate → How-much control; a geyser's heater → On-off control. *Why:* On-off parts have two states. How-much parts can give a small, medium or large response to match the error.
5. (write) In Drift, Bang or Nudge, the Bang rule always jumps 4 squares. Why does it overshoot near 10?  
   **Answer:** Near 10 the gap is only 1 or 2 squares. A jump of 4 is bigger than the gap, so the counter lands on the far side of 10. The response does not match the size of the error.. *Why:* A strong answer compares the size of the push with the size of the gap.
6. (mcq) A pencil tips in 0.25 seconds. Your loop takes 0.2 seconds. How many corrections can you make?  
   Options: About one / About five / About twenty  
   **Answer:** About one. *Why:* A fifth of a second is 0.2 seconds and a quarter is 0.25 seconds. Only one trip round the loop fits in that time, and one correction is rarely enough.
7. (truefalse) True or false: making a correction stronger always makes a level steadier.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A correction that is too strong for the loop's delay overshoots, and the level swings from side to side.
8. (order) Put these in order, for one on-off cycle of a hot iron.  
   **Answer:** ['The iron cools below the set temperature', 'The strip straightens and the switch closes', 'The heater warms the base plate', 'The strip bends and the switch opens']. *Why:* Cooling closes the switch, and heating opens it again. The temperature moves up and down round the set point.
9. (match) Match each loop to its control centre.  
   **Answer:** Blood pressure → brainstem; Body warmth → hypothalamus; Blood glucose → beta cells of the pancreas; Fridge temperature → thermostat switch. *Why:* The brainstem and hypothalamus are in the brain. Beta cells sense and decide for themselves. In a fridge, the thermostat switch decides.
10. (write) While you keep standing, your pulse stays a little faster than it was lying down. Why?  
   **Answer:** Gravity keeps pulling blood towards the legs for as long as I stand. The disturbance has not gone away, so the response must continue to hold the pressure up.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the disturbance continues, so the response continues.
11. (mcq) A beta cell senses glucose and releases insulin. Which jobs of the loop does this one cell do?  
   Options: Effector only / Sensor and control / Sensor only  
   **Answer:** Sensor and control. *Why:* The beta cell detects glucose and decides how much insulin to release. Liver, muscle and fat cells are the effectors.
12. (truefalse) True or false: a single bacterium can run a feedback loop.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. E. coli reset their sugar sensors by a feedback loop made of molecules. No nerves are needed.
13. (draw) Draw what happens to the temperature of an iron over ten minutes. Add a dotted line for the set point.  
   **Answer:** A zigzag line that rises above a dotted horizontal line, falls below it, and rises again, several times.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the temperature crossing the set point both ways. With an on-off heater it cannot sit exactly on the line.
14. (perspective) Two girls are talking about the bowl that refills itself from a bottle with a hole.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side and gives evidence. For Shalini: the level came back each time water was taken out. For Yamuna: there is no separate part that compares the level with a target.. *Why:* Both girls are using the science. The answer depends on whether a loop needs three separate parts or only needs the result to come back and change the action. Engineers accept the second.
15. (fill) The self-filling bowl keeps its level only while its store lasts. The store is the water in the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['bottle']. *Why:* The plate's level stays the same while the bottle's level falls. When the bottle is empty, the plate begins to dry.
### Loops in cells, machines and the Earth (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Design a loop that keeps a fish tank at 26 degrees. Name its sensor, its control and its effector.  
   **Answer:** Sensor: a thermometer part in the water that reports the temperature. Control: a switch that compares the temperature with 26 degrees. Effector: a small heater in the water, switched on when the water is below 26 and off when it is above.. *Why:* A strong answer names all three jobs and says which way the effector acts when the water is too cold.
2. (mcq) A loop's news arrives 5 seconds late, and the level can change a lot in 1 second. What is likely?  
   Options: The level stays perfectly flat / The loop stops for ever / The level swings back and forth  
   **Answer:** The level swings back and forth. *Why:* The loop answers where the level was 5 seconds ago. By then the level has moved on, so the correction is often wrong and the level swings.
3. (truefalse) True or false: every loop in the human body has its control centre in the brain.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Beta cells in the pancreas sense glucose and release insulin with no order from the brain. Many cells run their own loops.
4. (sort) Sort each event: disturbance, or response?  
   **Answer:** opening the fridge door → Disturbance; the fridge motor starting → Response; standing up after lying down → Disturbance; the heart beating faster → Response; running in the sun → Disturbance; sweating → Response. *Why:* A disturbance pushes the level away from the set point from outside the loop. A response is what the loop's effector does to bring it back.
5. (write) A glucose sensor, a computer program and an insulin pump work together. Which loop job does each one do?  
   **Answer:** The sensor under her skin detects glucose. The computer program compares the reading with a target and decides. The pump is the effector and gives insulin. Her own liver, muscle and fat cells then take glucose in.. *Why:* A strong answer matches sensor, control and effector to the three machine parts, and notices that her body's cells still do the final work.
6. (mcq) James Watt's governor had two spinning balls. What did the balls sense?  
   Options: The speed of the engine / The heat of the steam / The weight of the load  
   **Answer:** The speed of the engine. *Why:* The faster the engine turned, the further the balls swung out. Their height showed the speed, and a lever used it to move the steam valve.
7. (order) Put these in order, for how a bacterium uses feedback to find sugar.  
   **Answer:** ['The bacterium senses the sugar level now', 'They compare it with the level a few seconds ago', 'If it is rising, they keep swimming straight', 'They reset their sensors to the new level']. *Why:* The comparison with a moment ago tells the bacterium whether things are getting better. Resetting the sensors lets them detect the next small change.
8. (fill) An AC whose compressor changes speed instead of switching on and off is called an ____ AC.  
   **Answer:** ['inverter']. *Why:* An inverter air conditioner runs its compressor fast when the error is large and slowly when it is small. That is how-much control.
9. (write) Some scientists say the brain stores no set point for temperature. What, then, keeps the body near 37 degrees?  
   **Answer:** They say that sweating, shivering and skin blood flow are separate loops, each switching on at its own temperature. All of them respond to the same body temperature, and their combined action holds it near 37 degrees.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the steady level comes from several loops acting together, and not from one stored number.
10. (mcq) A pencil with forks hanging below it stays up with your eyes shut. What kind of steadiness is this?  
   Options: Steady by a fast feedback loop / Steady by its shape, with no loop / Steady by luck  
   **Answer:** Steady by its shape, with no loop. *Why:* With the weight below the support, a lean swings back by itself. No sensing or correcting is needed.
11. (truefalse) True or false: positive feedback makes a change smaller.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Positive feedback makes a change bigger. The squeal of a microphone held near its loudspeaker is positive feedback.
12. (draw) Draw the loop for standing up. Show the neck arteries, the brainstem and the heart, with arrows for the messages.  
   **Answer:** A body outline with sensors marked in the neck arteries, an arrow from them up to the brainstem, an arrow from the brainstem down to the heart and to small arteries, and an arrow from the heart back to the pressure in the neck arteries.. *Why:* A strong drawing closes the circle. The heart's faster beat raises the pressure, and the neck sensors detect that rise.
13. (perspective) A thermostat acts until the room reaches 24 degrees, and then stops. Two views on what that means.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason, such as the iron holding its temperature with no one watching, or the difference between a bacterium seeking sugar and a float valve.. *Why:* This question is not settled. What matters is that the reason uses evidence, and that the answer says what 'purpose' is taken to mean.
14. (match) Match each machine to the part that does its sensing.  
   **Answer:** Steam engine governor → two spinning balls; Electric iron → bimetallic strip; Flush tank → float; Closed-loop insulin pump → glucose sensor under the skin. *Why:* The balls swing out with speed, the strip bends with heat, the float rises with water, and the skin sensor reads glucose. Each turns one kind of change into a movement or a signal.
15. (mcq) Warm weather wears rock away faster, which removes carbon dioxide and cools the Earth. What is this?  
   Options: Positive feedback / An open loop / Negative feedback  
   **Answer:** Negative feedback. *Why:* The response acts against the warming, so it is negative feedback. It works over hundreds of thousands of years.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which part of a feedback loop compares the level with what is needed?  
   Options: The control centre / The sensor / The effector  
   **Answer:** The control centre. *Why:* The control centre receives the sensor's report, compares it with the level that is needed and sends an order. Choosing 'sensor' mixes up detecting with deciding.
2. (mcq) Sweat glands carry out an order from the brain. Which job in the loop is that?  
   Options: Sensor / Effector / Control centre  
   **Answer:** Effector. *Why:* An effector acts and so changes the level. Sweat glands, muscles and the heart are effectors.
3. (mcq) What makes a system a closed loop?  
   Options: It has a switch / It runs on electricity / News of its result changes its action  
   **Answer:** News of its result changes its action. *Why:* A closed loop measures its own result and adjusts. An open loop acts without checking.
4. (mcq) Which of these is an open loop?  
   Options: A mixer-grinder / A fridge / A flush tank  
   **Answer:** A mixer-grinder. *Why:* A mixer-grinder runs while the switch is held, whatever is in the jar. A fridge and a flush tank both check their own result.
5. (mcq) Why is a metre stick easier to balance on your palm than a pencil?  
   Options: It is heavier / It tips more slowly / It is easier to see  
   **Answer:** It tips more slowly. *Why:* A long stick takes longer to tip, so your eyes and hand have time for several corrections. Choosing 'heavier' is a common guess, but a heavy short stick is still hard.
6. (mcq) A loop's sensor stops working. What happens to the level?  
   Options: It stays steady / It doubles / It drifts  
   **Answer:** It drifts. *Why:* With no news of the change, nothing corrects it. In the Stick Test, the stick fell soon after the eyes were shut.
7. (mcq) When you stand up, what first detects that blood pressure has dropped?  
   Options: Stretch sensors in the neck arteries / The leg muscles / The lungs  
   **Answer:** Stretch sensors in the neck arteries. *Why:* Baroreceptors in the walls of the neck arteries and the large artery from the heart detect the drop and signal the brainstem.
8. (mcq) In a flush tank, which part is the sensor?  
   Options: The inlet tap / The float / The flush handle  
   **Answer:** The float. *Why:* The float rides on the water, so its height shows the water level. The tap is the effector. The handle only starts the flush.
9. (mcq) An iron's light goes on and off. What kind of control is this?  
   Options: How-much control / No control / On-off control  
   **Answer:** On-off control. *Why:* The heater is either fully on or fully off, and a switch moved by a bimetallic strip decides which.
10. (mcq) Which of these senses glucose and releases insulin with no order from the brain?  
   Options: A beta cell in the pancreas / A sweat gland / A baroreceptor  
   **Answer:** A beta cell in the pancreas. *Why:* A beta cell is a sensor and a control in one cell. It shows that not every loop passes through the brain.
11. (mcq) You timed a stick 5 times. Why find the mean and not use the best try?  
   Options: The mean is always bigger / One try can be lucky or unlucky / It is quicker  
   **Answer:** One try can be lucky or unlucky. *Why:* A single try can be unusually good or bad. The mean of several tries is a fairer picture of what usually happens.

## Applications
- A flush tank and the float valve in an overhead water tank shut off the water at a set level, so that tanks do not overflow.
- Fridges, irons, geysers, ovens and air conditioners all hold a temperature with a thermostat.
- A voltage stabiliser senses the mains voltage and corrects it, to protect a fridge or a television.
- Closed-loop insulin pumps join a glucose sensor to a pump, for people with type 1 diabetes.
- A pacemaker senses the heart's own beat and gives a small electric pulse only when a beat is missing.
- An aircraft's autopilot senses height and direction many times a second and moves the wing flaps and the rudder to correct them.
- Cruise control in a car senses the speed and gives more or less fuel to hold it, uphill and downhill.
- Doctors and nurses check pulse, temperature and blood pressure because a level that has drifted shows which loop is struggling.

## Open questions
- **Is there one set point for body temperature in the brain, or only many loops that add up to 37 degrees?** Nobody has found a stored number. Models with separate loops fit many measurements, but how the loops stay in step is still argued over.
- **What sets a person's blood pressure over years, and why do the neck sensors not correct high blood pressure?** The baroreceptors slowly get used to a new pressure and stop reporting it as high. Whether the kidneys or the brain set the long-term level is still debated.
- **How do giraffes keep blood reaching the brain when they raise their heads after drinking?** The pressure in a drinking giraffe's head has been measured in very few animals. Several explanations exist and none is settled.
- **Can a machine fully close the glucose loop for a person with diabetes, with no help at meal times?** Insulin put under the skin acts more slowly than insulin from the pancreas. That delay makes a fully automatic loop hard to keep steady.
- **Does life help to hold the Earth's temperature steady, or is the Earth's steadiness partly luck?** Some loops are known, such as the wearing away of rock. Whether they would have saved life every time is impossible to test on one planet.
- **How did the first feedback loop arise in the earliest cells?** Every cell alive today already has thousands of loops. No simpler stage survives to be studied, so scientists can only build models.

## Connected maths
- In the Stick Test, Kavya's five tries with the long stick lasted 6, 9, 12, 8 and 10 seconds. What is the mean? **9 seconds.**. Add the five tries: 6 + 9 + 12 + 8 + 10 = 45. Divide by the number of tries: 45 ÷ 5 = 9. So the mean is 9 seconds. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In the Stand-Up Test, a helper counted 19 beats in 15 seconds lying down, and 25 beats in the first 15 seconds of standing. How many beats a minute is each? How big was the rise? **76 beats a minute lying and 100 beats a minute standing. The rise was 24 beats a minute.**. 15 seconds is a quarter of a minute, so multiply each count by 4. 19 × 4 = 76. 25 × 4 = 100. The rise is 100 − 76 = 24 beats a minute. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L5`)
- In the Iron Light Test, at a low setting the light was on for 20 seconds and off for 100 seconds in each cycle. At a high setting it was on for 30 seconds and off for 60 seconds. For what fraction of the time was the heater on at each setting? How do the two compare? **One sixth of the time at the low setting, and one third at the high setting. The heater was on for twice as large a share of the time at the high setting.**. Low setting: one cycle lasts 20 + 100 = 120 seconds, and 20 out of 120 is 20/120 = 1/6. High setting: one cycle lasts 30 + 60 = 90 seconds, and 30 out of 90 is 30/90 = 1/3. One third is twice one sixth. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- Your eyes, brain and hand take about 0.2 seconds for one correction. A pencil tips too far in about 0.25 seconds. A metre stick tips too far in about 0.6 seconds. How many whole corrections fit in each time? What does that explain? **One correction for the pencil and three for the metre stick. It explains why the long stick is easier to balance.**. Pencil: 0.25 ÷ 0.2 = 1.25, so only 1 whole correction fits. Metre stick: 0.6 ÷ 0.2 = 3, so 3 corrections fit. One correction is rarely enough to save a stick. Three give you a good chance. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Bowl That Refills Itself, the water in the bottle stood 19 centimetres high on the first morning. It fell 3 centimetres each day. The hole is 1 centimetre above the base. On which day will the plate start to dry? **On day 7.**. The bottle can give water until its level reaches the hole at 1 centimetre. That is 19 − 1 = 18 centimetres of water. At 3 centimetres a day, 18 ÷ 3 = 6 days. So the bottle keeps the plate full for 6 whole days, and the plate starts to dry on day 7. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In Drift, Bang or Nudge, Meera's total gap was 14 in the Drift round and 19 in the Bang round. She played Drift three more times and scored 52, 38 and 47. What is her mean for the four Drift rounds? Was Drift really steadier than Bang? **The mean is 37.75. No. The first Drift round was a lucky one.**. Add the four Drift totals: 14 + 52 + 38 + 47 = 151. Divide by 4: 151 ÷ 4 = 37.75. That is about twice her Bang total of 19. One round of 12 dice rolls can come out unusually low by chance. Several rounds show what usually happens. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
