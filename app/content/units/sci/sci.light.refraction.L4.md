# Refraction: the rule of the bend, and how light gets trapped
`sci.light.refraction.L4`

**Summary.** Shine a torch at a slant into water and the beam turns toward an upright line. Shine it straight on and it does not turn at all. Your child measures the bend with a protractor and finds the pattern. The same rule explains why a diamond sparkles, why a fish sees the whole sky in one round window, and how hair-thin glass fibres carry the internet. It explains why a hot road looks wet, how two lenses make a telescope, and why spectacles were worn for hundreds of years before anyone knew how they work.

**Intro.** *A fish looks up at you from under the water. Above her, the whole sky, from one edge to the other, is squeezed into one round window. Outside that window she sees only a silver ceiling. Why?* You already know that light bends when it goes from air into water. Now we ask by how much, and what happens when you push the bend as far as it will go. You will measure the bend with a torch and a protractor. Then you will find out how the same rule makes a silver ceiling under the water, a glowing stream of water, a sparkling diamond and the cables that carry phone calls under the sea.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the parts of a bend diagram: the surface, the normal line, the angle in and the angle out.
1. I can say the rule of the bend: light bends toward the normal when it slows down, away from it when it speeds up, and not at all when it arrives straight on.
2. I can measure how much a beam turns in water with a protractor, say how sure I am of my numbers, and finish the test like a scientist: what I think, what I saw and why.
3. I can explain that a bigger refractive index means slower light and a bigger bend, and that this is not the same as being heavier.
4. I can explain total internal reflection: why light inside water or glass bounces back when it hits the surface at a shallow angle.
5. I can use total internal reflection to explain a fish's round window, a glowing water jet, a sparkling diamond and an optical fibre.
6. I can explain a hot-road mirage as light bending a little at a time through layers of air.
7. I can explain how hollow and bulging lenses fix short sight and long sight, and how two lenses make a telescope.

## Terms
- **Normal**: An imaginary straight line at a right angle to a surface, at the spot where light arrives. Scientists measure the bend of light from this line, not from the surface. *e.g.* Stand a pencil straight up on a flat table. The pencil shows the normal line for the table top.
- **Angle**: The amount of turn between two lines that meet at a point. A wide turn is a big angle and a small turn is a small angle. *e.g.* The corner of a book is an angle. The hands of a clock make a different angle every minute.
- **Degree**: The unit we use to measure an angle. A square corner is 90 degrees. A full turn all the way round is 360 degrees. *e.g.* A protractor is a ruler for angles. It has the degrees marked along its curved edge.
- **Ray**: A thin, straight path of light, drawn as a line with an arrow on it. A ray shows which way light is going. *e.g.* A torch with a very thin slit in front of it sends out something close to a single ray.
- **Angle in**: The angle between the ray arriving at a surface and the normal line. Scientists call it the angle of incidence. *e.g.* A torch pointed straight down at a table has an angle in of 0 degrees. A torch lying nearly flat has an angle in close to 90 degrees.
- **Angle out**: The angle between the ray after it has crossed the surface and the normal line. Scientists call it the angle of refraction. *e.g.* A beam that goes into water at 60 degrees comes out of the surface into the water at about 40 degrees from the normal. That 40 degrees is the angle out.
- **Refractive index**: A number that tells how many times slower light travels in a material than in empty space. A bigger number means slower light and a bigger bend. *e.g.* Water is about 1.33 and glass about 1.5. Diamond is about 2.4, so diamond slows light the most of these three.
- **Total internal reflection**: What happens when light inside water or glass reaches the surface at a very shallow angle. None of it gets out. All of it bounces back inside, like off a mirror. *e.g.* Under the water, look up at the surface from a low angle. It shines silver, like a mirror.
- **Critical angle**: The angle from the normal beyond which light inside a material is trapped. For water it is about 49 degrees. For glass it is about 42 degrees. For diamond it is about 24 degrees. *e.g.* A diamond has a small critical angle, so it traps light easily. That is part of why a diamond sparkles.
- **Optical fibre**: A strand of very clear glass or plastic about as thin as a hair. It carries light along its length, even round bends, by trapping the light inside. *e.g.* Internet cables under the sea are bundles of optical fibres. Doctors also use fibres to see inside the body.
- **Prism**: A block of glass or clear plastic with flat sides that are not parallel. A common prism is shaped like a triangle. It turns a beam of light. *e.g.* A triangle-shaped glass prism turns a beam of sunlight and spreads it into a band of colours.
- **Hollow lens**: A lens that is thinner in the middle than at its edges. It spreads light out. Scientists call it a concave lens. *e.g.* Spectacles for short sight use hollow lenses. Through them, the world looks a little smaller.
- **Focus point**: The spot where a bulging lens brings light from a faraway thing together. The distance from the lens to that spot is called its focal length. *e.g.* A magnifying glass held up to a window brings the window's light to a sharp picture on a card, at one distance only.
- **Dioptre**: The unit for the strength of a lens. A lens with a focal length of 1 metre has a power of 1 dioptre. A shorter focal length means more dioptres and a stronger lens. *e.g.* Reading spectacles marked plus 2 have a focal length of half a metre, which is 50 centimetres.
- **Short sight**: An eye problem where faraway things look blurry but close things look clear. The eye bends light a little too much, so the sharp picture forms in front of the back of the eye. Scientists call it myopia. *e.g.* A child who cannot read the board from the back of the class, but reads a book easily, may be short-sighted.
- **Long sight**: An eye problem where close things look blurry. The eye cannot bend light enough for near things, so the sharp picture would form behind the back of the eye. Many older people need reading spectacles for this. *e.g.* A grandparent who holds the newspaper at arm's length to read it may be long-sighted.
- **Retina**: The layer at the back of the eye where the picture forms. It turns light into signals that travel to the brain. *e.g.* When you look at a tree, a tiny upside-down picture of the tree forms on your retina.
- **Telescope**: A tube with lenses or mirrors that makes faraway things look closer and bigger. *e.g.* A telescope shows the craters on the Moon and the four biggest moons of Jupiter.
- **Mirage**: A picture of the sky, or of faraway things, that appears near the ground. It is made when light bends a little at a time through layers of hot and cool air. A mirage is real light. A camera can photograph it. *e.g.* On a hot day, a long straight road ahead can look wet and shiny. The shine is a picture of the sky.
- **Parallel**: Lines that run side by side and always stay the same distance apart, so they never meet. *e.g.* The two rails of a railway track are parallel.

## Explanations
### The rule of the bend: toward the normal, or away · *defined*
**Definition:** When light slows down as it crosses into a new material at a slant, it bends toward the normal line. When light speeds up as it crosses out, it bends away from the normal line. Light that arrives straight on, along the normal, does not bend at all.

Draw a line standing straight up from the surface, at the spot where the light arrives. This is the normal. Now measure the angle between the ray and the normal, in degrees. Going from air into water, the ray bends toward the normal, so the angle in the water is smaller than the angle in the air. Going from water back into air, the ray bends away from the normal, so the angle in the air is bigger. The bigger the slant, the bigger the bend. A beam at 60 degrees in the air goes on at about 41 degrees in the water, so it turns by about 19 degrees. A beam at 20 degrees turns by only about 5. A beam at 0 degrees does not turn at all. Glass bends light more than water does, and diamond bends it more still. But the rule is always the same: into a slower material, toward the normal. Out into a faster material, away from it. Light paths can also be run backwards. A ray that goes from air to water follows exactly the same path if it is sent back from the water to the air.
- e.g. A torch beam going into a tank of water at a slant turns toward the upright line, and the angle in the water is smaller than the angle in the air.
- e.g. A beam of sunlight going into a glass of water turns downward and steeper than it arrived.
- e.g. A beam that goes straight down into a pond, along the normal, goes on straight down.
### How much slower: the refractive index · *defined*
**Definition:** The refractive index of a material is a number that tells how many times slower light moves there than in empty space. The bigger the number, the slower the light and the bigger the bend.

In empty space, light travels about 300,000 kilometres every second. Air slows light by a tiny amount, so for air the number is almost exactly 1. Water has a refractive index of about 1.33. That means light in water moves 1.33 times slower than in empty space, which is about 225,000 kilometres a second. Cooking oil is about 1.47. Glass is about 1.5, though the exact number depends on the kind of glass. Diamond is about 2.4, so light in a diamond moves at less than half its speed in air. Notice something odd. Oil floats on water, so oil is lighter for its size, yet oil bends light more. So a bigger refractive index does not mean heavier. It depends on how the atoms of the material hold on to their electrons, and how the electrons answer the light passing by. This is why a clear glass marble almost vanishes in honey or oil, which have refractive indexes near that of glass. Where two see-through materials have the same number, light does not bend between them.
- e.g. Light in water travels at about three quarters of its speed in air, because 1 divided by 1.33 is about three quarters.
- e.g. Cooking oil bends light more than water, although oil floats on water.
- e.g. Glass wins over water and diamond wins over glass: the order from least to most bending is air, water, oil, glass, diamond.
### A glass block shifts a beam, and a prism turns it · *defined*
**Definition:** A block of glass with parallel sides bends a ray toward the normal on the way in and away from it by the same amount on the way out, so the ray leaves parallel to the way it arrived, but shifted sideways. A prism, whose sides are not parallel, turns the ray for good.

Send a ray at a slant into a thick glass slab. At the first surface it bends toward the normal. Inside the glass it goes straight. At the second surface, which is parallel to the first, the normals are parallel too. Now the ray bends away from the normal by exactly the same amount, so it comes out going in the same direction as before. But it has been moved sideways a little. That is why a thick window can make the view outside look slightly shifted when you look at a slant. A prism is different. Its two surfaces are tilted toward each other, so the second bend adds to the first instead of undoing it. The ray leaves turned toward the thick end of the prism. A bulging lens is like a set of prisms, thin at the top and bottom, and the prisms turn the rays toward each other. Isaac Newton used prisms in 1666 and showed that each colour in sunlight bends by a slightly different amount.
- e.g. Look at a pencil through the side of a thick, clean glass block at a slant. The pencil seems shifted sideways.
- e.g. A triangle-shaped prism turns a torch beam toward its thick end.
- e.g. Two prisms placed base to base act like a bulging lens, and bring two rays together.
### The fish's round window and the silver ceiling: total internal reflection · *defined*
**Definition:** When light inside water tries to leave at a slant steeper than about 49 degrees from the normal, none of it gets out. All of it bounces back down, like off a mirror. This is called total internal reflection.

Light going from water into air bends away from the normal. The bigger the angle in the water, the bigger the angle in the air, and the faster it grows. At an angle of about 49 degrees in the water, the ray in the air would have to lie flat along the surface, at 90 degrees. Beyond that angle, there is no way out. Scientists call 49 degrees the critical angle for water. Every bit of the light bounces back into the water, and the surface works like a perfect mirror. Now picture a fish looking up. Rays from the whole sky, from one horizon to the other, are bent down by the surface into a cone about 97 degrees wide. So the fish sees the entire sky squeezed into one round window straight above her. Outside the window, she is looking at the underside of the surface at shallow angles. There it is a mirror, and it shows the seabed. A diver sees the same. This only works when the light is going from a slower material to a faster one. Light going from air into water can never be trapped.
- e.g. Hold a glass of water above your eyes and look up through the side at the surface. At a slant, the surface turns silver.
- e.g. A diver looking up on a calm day sees a bright round window of sky with a silver ring around it.
- e.g. Light inside a diamond that hits the outside at a shallow angle bounces back inside, instead of escaping.
### Light trapped on purpose: water jets, diamonds and optical fibres · *defined*
**Definition:** Total internal reflection can trap light inside a clear material. It can then be guided along a curve, as in a stream of water or an optical fibre, or kept bouncing inside a gem so that it comes out sparkling.

In the 1840s, Jean-Daniel Colladon in Geneva showed that light shone into a jet of water from a tank follows the jet as it curves down. Each time the light reaches the edge of the jet, the angle is shallow, so it bounces back in. The jet glows like a bent pipe of light. An optical fibre does the same inside a strand of glass thinner than a pencil lead, about as thin as a human hair. The glass has a thin coat with a lower refractive index, so light bounces from wall to wall along the strand, even round bends. In 1966, Charles Kao and George Hockham argued that glass of that time dimmed light because of impurities, not because glass itself blocks light. Purer glass could carry light for kilometres. Kao shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2009. The Indian-born physicist Narinder Singh Kapany did early work on bundles of fibres in the 1950s and is often called the father of fibre optics. Today most of the data between continents crosses the oceans as flashes of light inside fibres on the sea floor. A diamond has a very small critical angle, about 24 degrees, so it traps light easily. A diamond cutter cuts the sides at angles chosen to send the light back out through the top, so the stone flashes.
- e.g. A glowing jet of water from a bottle in a dark room carries a torch beam along its curve.
- e.g. Doctors use bundles of optical fibres to look inside the body through a tiny cut.
- e.g. Cables under the sea carry phone calls and internet between countries as flashes of light.
### Mirages and shimmering roads: bending a little at a time · *defined*
**Definition:** Air that is hotter is thinner, and light moves slightly faster in it. On a hot day, light from the sky bends gradually as it passes down through layers of air that get hotter nearer the road, and curves back up to your eyes. You see a picture of the sky on the road, which looks like water.

Near a sun-baked road the air touching the ground is much hotter than the air higher up. Hot air is thinner, so light travels through it a tiny bit faster. A ray heading down toward the road meets thinner and thinner air. The lower side of the ray moves a little faster than the upper side, so the whole ray swings upward, a little at a time, until it curves up toward your eye. Your brain follows the light straight back and puts the sky down on the road. That is why the shiny patch looks like water. It is real light, and a camera can photograph it. If you walk toward the patch, it keeps moving away, because it is only visible from a shallow angle. The same bending also makes the air above a hot tava shimmer, and makes stars twinkle. Over very cold sea, the opposite happens. Light curves down, and a ship beyond the horizon can seem to float in the sky. Sailors have long reported these pictures, which are sometimes called the fata morgana.
- e.g. A long highway in the afternoon looks wet far ahead, and the wetness is a picture of the sky.
- e.g. In the Thar desert, travellers see shining lakes in the distance that vanish as they come closer.
- e.g. The air above a hot roof or a hot tava shimmers, and the view through it wobbles.
### Lenses in detail: bulging lenses, hollow lenses and the focus · *defined*
**Definition:** A bulging lens bends rays toward each other so that rays from a faraway thing meet at one spot, the focus point. A hollow lens bends rays away from each other. A fatter bulging lens has a shorter focal length.

Think of a lens as a set of prisms. In a bulging lens the prisms are thick at the middle and thin at the edge. Every ray bends toward the thick part, and rays that are further from the middle bend more. Because of this, parallel rays from a faraway thing all arrive at the same spot, which is the focus point. The distance from the lens to that spot is the focal length. A fatter, more curved lens bends rays more sharply, so its focus point is closer. Lens strength is measured in dioptres. One divided by the focal length in metres gives the power in dioptres. So a lens with a focal length of 50 centimetres has a power of 2 dioptres. A hollow lens is thin in the middle, so its prisms turn the rays away from each other. The rays never meet, so a hollow lens cannot throw a picture on a card. It makes things look smaller. You can tell which kind of lens you hold. Look at some print through it. If the print looks bigger, the lens is bulging. If it looks smaller, the lens is hollow.
- e.g. A magnifying glass has a short focal length. Reading spectacles for a grandparent have a longer one, such as 50 centimetres.
- e.g. Spectacles for a short-sighted child make the print look smaller, so they are hollow lenses.
- e.g. A peephole in a door has a hollow lens that shows a wide, small view of the person outside.
### The eye is a lens: short sight, long sight and spectacles · *defined*
**Definition:** Your eye bends light to make a sharp picture on the retina. In short sight the eye bends light too much, so the picture forms in front of the retina. In long sight it forms behind. Spectacles with a hollow or bulging lens move the picture to the right place.

Most of the bending in your eye is done at its clear front window, the cornea. The lens inside does the fine focusing. It gets fatter for near things and thinner for far things. For a sharp picture, the light must come to a point exactly on the retina. In short sight, the eyeball is a little too long, or the eye bends light too much. Rays from far things meet before the retina and spread out again, so the picture is blurry. A hollow lens in front of the eye spreads the rays a little first, which moves the focus back onto the retina. In long sight, the eyeball is a little too short, or the lens cannot get fat enough. Near things focus behind the retina. A bulging lens helps. Around the age of forty or fifty, the lens inside everyone's eyes gets stiffer, which makes close reading hard, and reading spectacles help. Studies suggest that children who spend more time outdoors in daylight are less likely to become short-sighted, and scientists are still working out why. Wearing the right spectacles does not make eyes weaker. Health organisations report that millions of people in India and across the world need spectacles and do not have them. When the lens inside the eye turns cloudy, which is called a cataract, a surgeon can replace it with a clear plastic lens. The Aravind Eye Hospital in Madurai, started in 1976, is known for doing this operation for very large numbers of people, many of them free of charge.
- e.g. A short-sighted child sees the board blurry but reads a book easily, and a hollow lens in spectacles makes the board clear.
- e.g. A grandparent holds the newspaper far away to read it until reading spectacles with a bulging lens bring the print back into focus.
- e.g. A cloudy eye lens can be replaced with a clear plastic one in a short operation.
### Two lenses make a telescope or a microscope · *defined*
**Definition:** A telescope has a large weak lens that makes a small picture of a faraway thing, and a small strong lens that magnifies that picture. The telescope magnifies as many times as the focal length of the first lens is longer than the second. A microscope uses two strong lenses in the same way for tiny things close by.

A single bulging lens makes a picture of the faraway thing at its focus point. The picture is small and upside down. Now put a second lens, a strong magnifier, so that you look at that small picture. The magnifier makes it look big. The first lens is the objective lens. The second is the eyepiece. If the objective has a focal length of 60 centimetres and the eyepiece has a focal length of 6 centimetres, the telescope magnifies 10 times, because 60 divided by 6 is 10. This kind of telescope shows everything upside down. Galileo built telescopes in 1609 and by 1610 had seen mountains on the Moon, many more stars and four moons circling Jupiter. A microscope is built the same way, but the first lens is a very strong one held close to a tiny thing, so that it makes a large picture, and the eyepiece magnifies that picture again. Robert Hooke used a microscope with two lenses, and in 1665 published drawings of what he saw, including the little boxes in cork that he called cells. Today the biggest telescopes use curved mirrors instead of lenses for the first step. Mirrors are easier to make large.
- e.g. A simple toy telescope with two lenses shows a far signboard upside down.
- e.g. A school microscope uses two lenses to make a pond-water creature look hundreds of times bigger.
- e.g. Binoculars have two telescopes, with prisms inside that turn the picture right way up.
### Rainbows: every raindrop is a tiny lens and mirror · *defined*
**Definition:** A rainbow is sunlight that has bent as it entered each raindrop, bounced off the back of the drop, and bent again on the way out. Each colour bends by a slightly different amount, so the colours leave the drop in slightly different directions.

A raindrop is a tiny round lens. Sunlight enters the drop and bends. Some of it bounces off the back of the drop, like off a weak mirror. This is not the silver-ceiling trick, because here only part of the light bounces back, which is why rainbows are fairly faint. Then it bends again as it leaves the drop. Red leaves at an angle of about 42 degrees from the line pointing away from the Sun. Violet leaves at about 40 degrees. Every drop at the same angle from that line sends the same colour to your eye. So the drops make a circle of each colour around the point opposite the Sun. You see the rainbow only with the Sun behind you. No two people see exactly the same rainbow, because each person's eyes are at a different place and so get light from different drops. In a second rainbow, which is higher and fainter, the light bounces twice inside each drop, and the colours are in the opposite order. From an aeroplane, a rainbow can be seen as a full circle. In the early 1300s, Theodoric of Freiberg in Germany and Kamal al-Din al-Farisi in Persia each worked out, by experiments with glass balls filled with water, that this is how rainbows are made.
- e.g. A garden sprinkler on a sunny day makes a small rainbow when you stand with the Sun behind you.
- e.g. The fainter second rainbow outside a bright one has its colours in the opposite order.
- e.g. A glass ball filled with water in a window throws bands of colour on the wall.
### Who invented the telescope? · *debated*
Telescopes appeared in the Netherlands in 1608, but nobody is sure who made the first one. The earliest records are patent applications, not proof of who thought of it first. Galileo did not invent the telescope, but he made the first well-known use of it for studying the sky.
- e.g. Hans Lipperhey, a spectacle maker in the town of Middelburg, asked for a patent for a telescope in October 1608.
- e.g. A few weeks later, Jacob Metius of Alkmaar also asked for a patent.
- e.g. Galileo heard of the new invention in 1609, made his own better one, and pointed it at the sky.
- **Hans Lipperhey, 1608:** His request for a patent in October 1608 is the first written record. The authorities did not grant it, because others seemed to know the secret too, but they paid him to make some telescopes.
- **Jacob Metius, 1608:** He asked for a patent a few weeks after Lipperhey and said he had been working on the idea for two years. He also did not get a patent.
- **Zacharias Janssen, Middelburg:** Later writers said that Janssen, another spectacle maker in the same town, had made the first one even earlier. The evidence is weak and historians doubt it.
- **Galileo Galilei, Padua, 1609:** He did not invent it, but he learned of it, built better ones by 1609, and was the first to use telescopes for careful study of the Moon, Jupiter and the stars. He made a telescope that magnified about 20 times.
### Slowing light to a walking pace · *defined*
**Definition:** Light slows down in every material, and in 1999 a team led by Lene Hau at Harvard University in the USA slowed light to about 17 metres per second in a cloud of very cold atoms. That is about the speed of a cyclist.

In glass, light moves at about two thirds of its speed in air. That is a large change, but it is still very fast. Lene Vestergaard Hau, a physicist born in Denmark, and her team wanted to see how slow they could make it. They cooled a cloud of sodium atoms to a temperature a tiny fraction of a degree above the coldest possible, and shone a special laser through it. In the cloud, light that normally covers 300,000 kilometres in a second slowed to about 17 metres in a second. In 2001, her team and another team in the USA went further. They stopped a pulse of light inside a cloud of atoms and let it go again, a bit like pressing pause. Nobody yet knows if this will lead to everyday machines. Some scientists hope it might help store and send information in future quantum computers, but this is still only an idea.
- e.g. In the 1999 experiment, light slowed to about the speed of a person on a bicycle.
- e.g. In 2001, a pulse of light was stopped inside a cloud of atoms and then released.
- e.g. In ordinary glass, light slows only to about two thirds of its speed in air.

## Mostly agreed
- Light bends toward the normal when it goes at a slant into a material where it moves more slowly, and away from the normal when it goes into a material where it moves faster. *How we know:* The angles have been measured for about a thousand years, and anyone can check them with a torch, water and a protractor. Léon Foucault and others measured the speed of light in water directly in 1850, and found it slower than in air.
- Light that arrives straight on, along the normal, does not bend. *How we know:* A beam shone straight down into a tank goes on straight down. Every careful test gives the same result.
- Light going from water into air at an angle bigger than about 49 degrees from the normal does not leave the water. It all bounces back. *How we know:* Look up at the underside of the surface of a glass of water. At a shallow angle it turns silver like a mirror. Divers and photographers see a round window of sky with a silver ring around it.
- Optical fibres carry light along their length by total internal reflection, and nearly all the data that crosses the oceans now travels this way. *How we know:* Fibres have been made, tested and laid on the sea floor for decades. Charles Kao shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2009 for his part in making this possible.
- A mirage on a hot road is real light that has been bent, and not a trick of the mind. *How we know:* Cameras photograph mirages. In pictures of a road mirage, the shiny patch really does show an upside-down image of the sky or of far objects.
- Two lenses together can magnify far more than one lens, and a telescope magnifies by the ratio of the two focal lengths. *How we know:* You can build a telescope with two lenses, measure each focal length and check how much bigger things look. Telescope makers have used this rule for four hundred years.

## How we found out
**Johannes Kepler (1604) and Christoph Scheiner (early 1600s), 1604 to the early 1600s.** Spectacles were first worn in Italy about 700 years ago. For more than 300 years, nobody could say why they helped. The old idea, taught since ancient Greece, was that the lens inside the eye is the part that sees, like a tiny screen. In 1604 the German astronomer Johannes Kepler worked out something different. The lens of the eye bends light to make a small upside-down picture on the back of the eye, the retina. If the eye bends light too much, the sharp picture forms in front of the retina, and the world looks blurry. A hollow lens in front of the eye can move the picture back. A bulging lens helps in the opposite case. A few years later Christoph Scheiner, a German astronomer, took the back coats from an ox eye. He looked through the thin back and saw the tiny upside-down picture glowing there. So the lens was not the screen. It was only one of the lenses.  
*What people believed before:* For centuries people believed that the lens inside the eye is the part that sees. Kepler showed that it bends light into a picture on the retina, and Scheiner showed that picture directly in an ox eye.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A denser material always bends light more.** (Many people, because both ideas feel like 'more stuff in the way'.; *rejected*)  
  For: Glass is heavier than water and bends light more than water does. Diamond is heavier still and bends light the most.  
  Against: Cooking oil floats on water, so it is lighter for its size, yet it bends light more than water does. What matters is how the atoms of the material answer to light, and not how heavy it is.
- **The silver ceiling under the water is a mirror coating that the water makes.** (Some children, because it looks like a mirror.; *rejected*)  
  For: It really does shine like a mirror, and you can see your finger in it.  
  Against: There is no coating. It happens only when light inside the water meets the surface at a shallow angle. Look up steeply and the same surface is a clear window. So the surface is a mirror or a window, depending on the angle.
- **A mirage is something the mind makes up, not something real.** (A common idea, because a mirage seems to disappear when you reach it.; *rejected*)  
  For: A traveller cannot walk to the 'water'. It always stays ahead.  
  Against: A camera can photograph a mirage. The picture of the sky on the road is made of real light that really has been bent. Our brain misreads where that light came from. It is the interpretation that is wrong, not the light.
- **All lenses make things look bigger.** (People who have only used magnifying glasses.; *rejected*)  
  For: Magnifying glasses, binoculars and microscopes all make things look bigger.  
  Against: A hollow lens spreads light out and makes things look smaller. Spectacles for short sight and the peephole in a door are hollow lenses.
- **Wearing spectacles makes your eyes weaker, so you should avoid them as long as you can.** (A belief heard in many families.; *rejected*)  
  For: People who get spectacles often seem to need stronger ones every few years.  
  Against: Studies do not show that the right spectacles weaken eyes. Short sight tends to change while a child grows, because the eyeball grows too. Not wearing spectacles can make children struggle at school and, in some cases, can stop the eyes developing properly.
- **Galileo invented the telescope.** (Many schoolbooks.; *rejected*)  
  For: He built and used telescopes, and made the most famous discoveries with them.

## Roots
We measured how light bends, and found the surface of water can be a window or a mirror. Now we ask what this tells us about things, about seeing, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** From under the water, one surface was a window at one angle and a mirror at another. The water had not changed. Only the angle of the light had. So what a surface does depends on how the light meets it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort things into windows and mirrors, as if each thing had one fixed nature. But a surface does one thing or the other depending on the angle of the light that meets it.  
*Evidence:* In the water-mirror test, the underside of the water let you see through when you looked up steeply, and turned to a silver mirror when you looked at a slant. The glass and the water did not change. Only the angle of your eye did. If your results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* What you see depends on where you stand. A fish and a diver look at the same sky and see it differently.
- *what is life:* Your own eyes bend light too. If the eye grows a little long, the world goes blurry, and a lens fixes it. Living bodies keep adjusting how they meet the light.
- *what is this world:* One rule, light slowing as it crosses an edge, makes a snapped pencil, a rainbow, a mirage, a sparkling diamond and a cable under the sea.

One rule does all of this work. Light moves at different speeds in different materials, so it bends where they meet. At a steep angle it passes through the edge. At a shallow angle it is turned back. Nothing new is added for the rainbow, the diamond or the cable. The same light simply meets different edges at different angles. What looks like many separate wonders is one thing, meeting the world in many ways.

- **Udana 6.4, the Buddhist story of the blind men and the elephant:** A king asks blind men to feel an elephant. One feels the head, one the tusk, one the leg, and each says what the elephant is like. They quarrel, because each is sure he is right. The Buddha says each has felt a part and taken it for the whole. (The surface of the water is a window to one eye and a mirror to another. Each is right about the side they meet, and wrong if they say it is only that.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 15:** The verse says that the face of truth is covered by a golden disc, and asks the Sun to uncover it so that the seeker can see. (A bright shining surface can hide as well as show. The silver ring shows the sea floor and hides the sky. You see more by moving to a new angle.)
- Science (settled): Total internal reflection is settled physics. It is the working principle of the optical fibres that carry most of the world's internet.
- Science (debated): Physicists describe why light slows down in materials with different pictures, some in terms of waves adding up and some in terms of photons. They agree on the measured bends, but not always on the best picture.
- Other view, Many physicists: A surface has fixed properties, such as its refractive index. Window and mirror are only two results of one rule, and the angle is just a number in it. Nothing about it depends on a point of view.
- Practice, One glass, two faces (3 min): Stand at a window at dusk. Switch on the room light and look at the glass. You see the room and your face. Now switch the room light off and wait. Look again. You see the world outside. Breathe in slowly three times. Ask yourself how one glass can show both.
- Ask: Is the surface of the water a window or a mirror, or is it neither until something looks at it?
- Ask: A fish and a diver see the sky differently. Which one sees it as it really is?
- Ask: If a thing can be two things depending on the angle, what other things might we have sorted into one box too soon?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Light slows down in glass and speeds up again when it comes out. Where does it get its speed back? (hint: Light slows down because the tiny electrons in the glass pass it on. What happens when the light leaves the glass and there are no electrons to pass it on?)
- *deep:* A glass marble nearly vanishes in honey, but a diamond would not. Why not? (hint: A diamond bends light much more than honey does. What happens at the edge of something that bends light differently?)
- *deep:* At the edge of a fish's round window, what is the light doing that comes in at the lowest angle? (hint: Think about the biggest angle that light in water can have and still get out into the air.)
- *deep:* Each colour bends by a slightly different amount. What does that tell you about white light? (hint: A prism and a raindrop both split white light. What must be hidden inside it?)
- *big:* If you were a fish, would you say the sky is a small round window or a huge dome? Which is the truth? (hint: Your eyes follow light straight back, but the light has been bent. Who is better placed to say, the fish or the diver?)
- *big:* A mirage can be photographed. If a camera can see it, is it real or not? (hint: The light is real. The place you think it came from is not.)
- *big:* Spectacles were worn for more than 300 years before anyone could explain how they worked. Does something have to be explained before it can be useful? (hint: Think of other things you use every day without knowing how they work.)
- *big:* Whose job is it to make sure that every child who needs spectacles can get them? (hint: Think of a child in a village far from an eye doctor, and what stands in the way: cost, distance, or not knowing.)

## Story: The Round Window
*The Gulf of Mannar, near Mandapam in Tamil Nadu, is a real place where women dive in shallow water for seaweed, which is dried and sold to make agar. Selvi, her grandmother Paati, her brother Murugan and everything that happens in the story are made up. The round window of sky and the silver ring seen from under the water are real, and they are seen best when the water is calm and clear.*

Selvi was ten years old, and this was the morning her grandmother let her open her eyes under the sea.

They lived in a fishing village near Mandapam, on the Gulf of Mannar, where the women dive for seaweed. The seaweed is dried on the sand and sold, and far away it becomes the jelly in shops. Selvi's grandmother, Paati, had dived for it for fifty years.

They went out in a small boat at dawn, when the tide was low. Selvi's uncle rowed. Her little brother Murugan sat in the middle holding the anchor rope with both hands, because he was six and did not like the sea at all. The boat rocked on a patch of green water that was only three metres deep.

Paati tied a net bag round her waist and handed Selvi an old pair of swimming goggles, scratched but tight. 'Listen carefully,' she said. 'When you are on the bottom, look up. The sea will show you two faces.'

'Two faces?' said Selvi.

'In the middle there is a round window. Everything bright and clear is in the window, and the boat is in the middle of it. Around the window there is a silver ring. The silver ring is a mirror. Never swim toward the silver. Swim toward the window.' Then Paati slipped over the side, as easily as a fish, and was gone.

Selvi took a huge breath and followed her.

The sea was cool and green. Pale sunbeams hung in the water like ropes. Below her the sand was rippled like a plate of sevai, and brown seaweed grew in tufts between the stones. Paati was already there, bent over, filling her bag, her white hair floating about her head like smoke.

Selvi picked a handful of seaweed. Then she remembered, and looked up.

It was the strangest sight of her life. Straight above her was a round window, bright and sharp. In it was the whole sky, from one edge to the other, and the dark shape of the boat in the middle like a leaf. All around the window was a ring of shimmering silver. And in the silver, she could see the sea floor. She could see the sand, the stones and the brown tufts. She could even see Paati's feet. It was as if there was a second sea bed on the ceiling.

Her chest went tight. Which way was up? The floor was below her, and the floor was above her too. A wave rolled across the surface, and the silver ring rippled and tore like cloth. Selvi dropped the seaweed. A string of bubbles rushed out of her mouth. Her arms began to flap, and she did not know which way they should pull.

A hand closed on her wrist. It was Paati. She did not pull Selvi anywhere. She only pointed one finger, up, at the very middle of the round window. Then she pressed the palm of her other hand against the air in front of her, as if it were a mirror, and she shook her head.

Selvi understood. 'The silver is a mirror. It is only showing me the floor.' Her fear went quiet. She stopped looking at the silver. She fixed her eyes on the boat in the middle of the window, and kicked.

As she rose, the window shrank. It closed around her gently, smaller and smaller, as if the sea were pulling a drawstring. At last her head broke through the very middle of it, into the air and the noise and the sun.

'You were down there for ages!' shouted Murugan, leaning over the side. His face was green with worry. His uncle laughed and said it had been less than half a minute.

Paati came up beside her, breathing hard and smiling. In the boat she explained. 'Light from the sky bends when it goes down into the sea. All the sky, from one side to the other, gets squeezed into that one round window. But light from the sea floor, trying to come up at a low slant, cannot get out. The top of the sea sends it back down. At a low slant the top of the sea is a mirror. At a steep one it is a window. Two faces.'

'You told me,' said Selvi, 'and my head forgot.'

'Every head forgets once,' said Paati. 'The second time it remembers.'

That evening, while the seaweed lay drying on the sand like brown lace, Murugan stood at the edge of the water, with his arms folded and his feet far from the waves. Selvi filled a clear plastic tumbler from the kitchen and held it above his head. 'Look up through the side,' she said. 'Look at the top of the water.'

Murugan looked. The surface of the water in the tumbler shone like a silver plate, and in it he saw the bottom of the tumbler and his own finger. 'It is a mirror!' he said. Selvi tipped the tumbler a little. He moved his eye. 'Now it is a window!'

'Same water,' said Selvi. 'Same top. It only depends how the light comes at it.'

Murugan looked at the sea. Then, very slowly, he put one foot in.

**For the parent:** For the parent: light from the sky bends toward the normal as it goes down into the water, so the whole sky, from horizon to horizon, is squeezed into a cone about 97 degrees wide. That is the round window. At shallower angles, light going up from the sea bed meets the surface beyond the critical angle of about 49 degrees, and all of it is reflected. That is the silver ring, a mirror made by the water surface itself. The same surface is a window at one angle and a mirror at another. The window also shrinks as the diver rises, because its width depends on the depth.

- Ask: Why does the water's surface look like a mirror when you look up at it from a low slant, but like a window when you look straight up?
- Ask: If the round window is made from the whole sky, what do you think is happening to the picture of the sky at the very edge of the window?
- Ask: Why would the window of sky get smaller as a diver swims closer to the surface?
- Ask: A diver looks up at the silver ring and sees the seabed there. Is the diver seeing a real place or a picture? What is the difference?
- Ask: How could you check the two faces of the surface for yourself at home, without a sea?

Light slows down in water, so it turns.; The bigger the slant, the bigger the turn. At 0 degrees there was no turn.; The angle in the water was always smaller than the angle in the air.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses their own numbers, says toward the normal, and mentions that light moves more slowly in water. If their numbers are different from the expected ones, do not worry. Ask what could make the beam hard to measure, such as a wide beam or a crooked box.
  - Think: If the beam went from the water back into the air, which way would it turn?
### e2 · Build an Eye That Needs Spectacles (40 min)
- **question:** A magnifying glass can make a sharp picture of a faraway window on a card, like the lens in an eye makes a picture on the retina. What happens when the card is a little too far back, like in a short-sighted eye, or too near, like in a long-sighted eye? Can a spectacle lens fix it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. If you move the card back a little, will the picture stay sharp? Will a lens from a short-sighted person's spectacles fix it, or a lens from reading spectacles? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The magnifying glass, the window and the room stay the same. You never change the magnifying glass in your model eye. Only the place of the card and the spectacle lens in front change. Each time, you slide the card slowly to find the sharpest picture, and you check it twice.
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun through the magnifying glass or the spectacle lenses, and never leave them where sunlight shines through them onto paper, cloth or skin, because they can start a fire or burn. Borrowed spectacles belong to someone, so hold them by the frame and give them back unscratched.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw your model eye from the side: the magnifying glass, the card and the window, with the light from the window bending in the lens to a point on the card. Draw it once for the sharp picture and once for the blurry picture. Last, write what you think, what you saw, and why.
- **results table:** The model eye | Distance from the magnifying glass to the card for the sharpest picture (centimetres) | Was the picture of the window sharp, blurry or too dim to tell? | What fixed it, if anything
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the magnifying glass, the card and the two pairs of spectacles. Check which pair makes print look smaller. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* A magnifying glass can make a small picture of a window on a card. That is a little like your eye. If we move the card back, what will happen to the picture? Can a spectacle lens fix it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A lens can make a picture of a window. What if the card is too far back? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think moving the card might change the picture? (good: The light comes together at one place only.; The picture is only sharp at the right distance.; It will stay the same, because the lens is the same.)
  - Note: With 2 to 4 children, one holds the magnifying glass steady in clay, one slides the card, one reads the ruler and one writes; swap for each step. Do not correct the guess. Let your child test each pair of spectacles by looking at print through the lens. The pair where print looks smaller has hollow lenses, which are for short sight. The pair where print looks bigger has bulging lenses.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the magnifying glass upright in clay, facing the window. Hold the card behind it and slide the card slowly toward and away from the lens. Stop where the picture of the window is sharpest. Measure the distance from the lens to the card in centimetres. Write it down. Check it a second time.  
  *Parent says:* Slide the card slowly. At one distance, a small, sharp picture of the window appears. It will be upside down. When it looks sharpest, measure the distance and write it down.  
  *Child mode:* Slide the card back and forth. Find where the window looks sharp. Measure the distance.
  - Note: Switch off the room light, if the window is not very bright, or stand in a shaded part of the room. The picture is upside down, as in a real eye. The sharp distance is the focal length of your magnifying glass. It is usually between 10 and 30 centimetres. Mark the place on the ruler, because you will want to come back to it.
  - Check: The picture of the window on the card is sharp at one distance only. What does that tell you about a lens? → **A lens brings light together to a sharp point at one distance behind it**
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Move the card 3 centimetres further back. The picture goes blurry. This is your short-sighted eye. Now hold the hollow spectacle lens right in front of the magnifying glass, touching it. Slide the card to find the new sharp place. Measure the distance. Write down what happened.  
  *Parent says:* Move the card back by a few centimetres. The picture goes blurry. This is like an eyeball that is a little too long. Now put the short-sighted spectacle lens in front. Slide the card again. What do you find?  
  *Child mode:* Move the card back 3 centimetres. It is blurry. Hold the hollow lens in front. Slide the card.
  - Ask: Where did the sharp picture move to when you added the hollow lens? (good: It moved further back, away from the magnifying glass.; The card had to be further back to get a sharp picture.; The hollow lens made the light come together later.)
  - Note: Typically the sharp place moves back by about 3 to 6 centimetres, but it depends on the strength of the two lenses, so your number will differ. A spectacle lens is smaller than the magnifying glass, so the picture will be dimmer; hold the lens flat against the middle of the magnifying glass and dim the room. If the picture never gets sharp, the lens is weak, or the card is not far enough back. Try moving the card back 6 centimetres. If nobody at home is short-sighted, skip this step and ask your child to draw what they think would happen.
  - Check: The hollow lens moved the sharp picture further back. Which eye problem does this fix? → **Short sight, where the picture forms in front of the retina**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the card back at the sharp place. Then move it 2 centimetres closer to the lens. The picture goes blurry. This is your long-sighted eye. Hold the reading spectacle lens right in front of the magnifying glass, touching it. Slide the card to find the new sharp place. Measure the distance. Write down what happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now move the card closer. The picture goes blurry. This is like an eyeball that is a little too short. Put the reading spectacle lens in front. Slide the card again. What do you find?  
  *Child mode:* Move the card 2 centimetres closer. It is blurry. Hold the reading lens in front. Slide the card.
  - Ask: Where did the sharp picture move to when you added the bulging lens? (good: It moved closer to the magnifying glass.; The card had to be nearer to get a sharp picture.; The bulging lens made the light come together sooner.)
  - Note: Typically the sharp place moves closer by about 2 to 4 centimetres, depending on the strength. Record how far it moved in centimetres, and compare it with the hollow lens: which moved it more? If the move is too small to see, try a stronger pair, or two pairs held together.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill in the table with your three sharp distances. Draw the model eye for the sharp picture and the blurry picture. Compare your results with the guess from the start.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. For the plain model eye, where was the sharp place? With the hollow lens, did it move back or forward? With the reading lens?  
  *Child mode:* Fill in the table. Draw the model eye. Was your guess right?
  - Note: A good table has three distances in centimetres. The hollow lens moves the sharp place back. The bulging lens moves it closer.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw and why. Write the answer under the drawing.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think at the start? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: What is your claim, your evidence and your reasoning? (good: A spectacle lens can move the sharp place of the picture. My hollow lens moved it back by about 5 centimetres. Hollow lenses spread the light, so it comes together later.; The picture is only sharp at one distance, so an eye that is too long or too short needs a lens to fix it.; The reading lens moved the sharp picture closer, because bulging lenses bend light more.)
  - Think: Spectacles move the sharp picture to where the back of the eye is. Why can they not shorten or lengthen the eye itself?

### a1 · The Water Mirror: when the window turns silver (20 min)

  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. With 2 to 4 children, take turns to look; one child at a time sits on the floor by the box while the others wait with the towel.
- **s2 (7 min)**

  *Child mode:* Hold the box above your eyes. Look up through the side. What does the top of the water look like?
  - Note: A flat-sided box shows the effect more clearly than a round glass, because round glass bends the view. If you see nothing special, look at a lower slant, with your eyes level with the bottom of the box.
  - Check: At a low slant, the underside of the water looked silver, like a mirror. What was it doing with the light? → **Bouncing all the light from below back down into the water**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now raise your eyes slowly, so you look more and more steeply up at the water. Notice the place where the silver sheet gives way to a clear window, and you see the ceiling. Move your head up and down a few times to find the place. A helper marks on the box, with a sticky note, where your eye was when it flipped. Then pour out half the water and test again. Does it flip at the same angle? Last, look straight up through the bottom of the box, if it is clear.  
  *Parent says:* Raise your eyes slowly. Where does the silver stop? What can you see in the clear part? Can you find the place where it changes?  
  *Child mode:* Look more steeply. Find where the silver turns clear. What do you see then?
  - Note: The change happens roughly where your line of sight makes an angle of about 40 degrees with the water's top, which is about 50 degrees from the normal. Your child may find that it is hard to find the exact place. That is fine. It is the idea that matters. The flip angle is the same with half the water, because it depends on the angle, not on how much water there is. If nothing silver shows, sit closer to the box, and use a box with clean, dry, scratch-free walls.
  - Think: The water did not change. Only your angle of looking did. So what decides whether the top of the water is a window or a mirror?
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the box from the side, with the eye in two places. For each place, draw the line of sight to the underside of the water. Draw one going through, and one bouncing back. Write one sentence on what changed.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the box from the side. Draw two sight lines to the top of the water. One goes through, and one bounces. Write one sentence about what changed.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the box. Draw two sight lines. One goes through. One bounces.
  - Ask: Why is the top of the water a window from one place and a mirror from another? (good: At a steep angle the light passes through. At a shallow angle it cannot get out, so it bounces back.; The angle of the light changes what the surface does.; Beyond about 49 degrees from the normal, all the light is bounced back.)

  *Parent says:* Look along the road, far into the distance. Do you see a shiny patch that looks like water? Zoom in with the phone and take a picture. What do you think it is?  
  *Child mode:* Look far along a hot road. Do you see a shiny patch? Take a picture.

  - Note: A mirage needs a hot, sunny day and a long, flat, dark surface seen from a low angle. It may not appear if it is cloudy, or if you are looking at a short road. (With a hair dryer, a grown-up blows hot air across the line of sight instead.)

  *Child mode:* Look at the stripes over the cold plate. A grown-up heats it. Do not touch it. Look again.

  *Child mode:* Draw the wobbling stripes. Write why they wobbled.
  - Ask: Why does the road look wet when it is dry? (good: Light from the sky bends in the hot air above the road, and curves up to your eyes. You see a picture of the sky on the road.; It is a picture of the sky made by bent light.; Hot air near the ground bends the light, so the sky looks like it is on the road.)

Do the pouring over the sink or the tray, and keep the phone or torch away from the water. Wipe up spills, because wet floors are slippery.

  *Child mode:* Draw the jet. Draw a zigzag of light inside it. Write why it stays in.

### p1 · Two-Lens Telescope: see the faraway upside down (60 min)
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun through the telescope or the lenses. It can blind. Keep lenses out of direct sunlight, because they can start fires. A grown-up helps with the scissors. Borrowed spectacles belong to someone: tape only the card ring, not the lens.

  *Child mode:* Tape the weak lens to the big tube. Tape the strong lens to the thin tube.

Expect a rough, dim, colour-fringed picture; it is a model that works, not a good telescope.
  - Check: Which lens goes at the front of the telescope, facing the faraway thing? → **The weak lens with the long focal length**

  *Parent says:* Look through the strong lens at a far signboard. Slide the tube in and out. When the picture is sharp, tell me what you see. Which way up is it?  
  *Child mode:* Look through the small lens at a far sign. Slide the tube. What do you see?
  - Ask: Which way up is the picture? Why do you think that is? (good: It is upside down, because the first lens crosses the rays over.; The lens makes an upside-down picture, and the eyepiece does not turn it back.; It is upside down, like the picture on the card.)
  - Note: The picture will look upside down. This is correct for this kind of telescope. If it is blurry, slide the tube further. If you see colour fringes, that is normal for a simple lens. Typical magnification is only about 4 to 8 times. Choose a far sign, a tree or a building to look at. Do not look at the Sun, ever.

Check by looking at a row of bricks or tiles with one eye through the telescope and the other eye bare. Count how many bare-eye bricks fit in one telescope brick.  
  *Parent says:* Divide the weak lens's focal length by the strong lens's focal length. That is how many times it magnifies. Then check with a wall of bricks.  
  *Child mode:* Divide the long focal length by the short one. That is the magnification.
  - Ask: Does the number you counted match the number you worked out? (good: It is close, but not exactly the same.; Yes. My telescope magnifies about 6 times.; No, because it is hard to count with one eye in each place.)
  - Note: A weak lens of 50 centimetres and a strong lens of 8 centimetres gives about 6 times. The bricks test is difficult, so a result within a factor of one and a half is good. If the numbers are far apart, check the two focal lengths again.

### Bending Light (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) In a drawing of light bending at a water surface, what is the normal?  
   Options: An imaginary line standing at a right angle to the surface, at the spot where the light arrives / The flat surface of the water / The path of the light in the water / The edge of the glass  
   **Answer:** An imaginary line standing at a right angle to the surface, at the spot where the light arrives. *Why:* Scientists measure every bend from the normal, the upright line at a right angle to the surface. Choosing the surface is a common mistake, and it gives the wrong angles. A beam along the normal has an angle of 0 degrees.
2. (fill) When light goes at a slant from air into water, it bends ____ the normal.  
   **Answer:** ['toward', 'towards']. *Why:* Light moves more slowly in water, so it bends toward the normal. The angle in the water is smaller than the angle in the air.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a torch beam that arrives along the normal into water bends a lot.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Light that arrives along the normal does not bend at all. The bend only happens when the light arrives at a slant.
4. (mcq) A torch beam goes into a tank of water at 50 degrees from the normal. Which angle in the water is the most likely?  
   Options: 20 degrees / 35 degrees / 50 degrees / 65 degrees  
   **Answer:** 35 degrees. *Why:* The water turns the beam toward the normal, so the angle in the water must be smaller than 50 degrees. It is about 35 degrees. Choosing 20 degrees bends the beam too much. Choosing 65 degrees bends it the wrong way.
5. (order) Put the steps of the measure-the-bend test in order, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]. *Why:* First draw the map with the normal, so that every angle has a place to be measured from. Then place the torch, find where the beam leaves, and measure. Without the normal drawn first, the angles have nothing to be measured from.
6. (sort) Sort these journeys of light. Does the light bend toward the normal, bend away from it, or not bend?  
   **Answer:** air into water at a slant → Bends toward the normal; water into air at a slant → Bends away from the normal; air into glass at a slant → Bends toward the normal; glass into air at a slant → Bends away from the normal; a beam along the normal into water → Does not bend; a beam across a room of still air → Does not bend. *Why:* Light bends toward the normal when it goes into a material that is slower, such as water or glass. It bends away when it goes into a faster material, such as air. Light that goes along the normal, or stays in one material, does not bend.
7. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** normal → the imaginary upright line at a right angle to a surface; refractive index → a number telling how many times slower light is in a material; critical angle → the angle beyond which light inside a material is trapped; optical fibre → a hair-thin glass strand that carries light by trapping it. *Why:* The normal is the line we measure from. The refractive index is a number for how much a material slows light. The critical angle tells us when light is trapped, and a fibre is the thing that uses that to carry light.
8. (mcq) Water, glass, air and diamond all slow light. Which slows it the most?  
   Options: Air / Water / Glass / Diamond  
   **Answer:** Diamond. *Why:* Diamond has a refractive index of about 2.4, the biggest of these. Light in diamond moves at less than half its speed in air. Glass is about 1.5, water about 1.33, and air is almost 1.
9. (truefalse) True or false: cooking oil floats on water, so it bends light less than water does.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Oil is lighter than water for its size, yet it bends light more. The bend depends on how the atoms of a material answer to light, and not on how heavy the material is.
10. (fill) The bigger the refractive index of a material, the ____ light moves through it.  
   **Answer:** ['slower', 'more slowly']. *Why:* The refractive index tells how many times slower light is in a material than in empty space. A bigger number means slower light and a bigger bend.
11. (mcq) When can light inside water be trapped by the surface?  
   Options: When it reaches the surface at a very shallow angle, beyond about 49 degrees from the normal / When it reaches the surface along the normal / When the water is very deep / When the water is cold  
   **Answer:** When it reaches the surface at a very shallow angle, beyond about 49 degrees from the normal. *Why:* Trapping, or total internal reflection, happens only when the angle from the normal is bigger than the critical angle, which is about 49 degrees for water. Light along the normal goes straight out. Depth and temperature do not matter much.
12. (write) The top of the water in a glass can look like a window from one place and a mirror from another. Explain how one surface can be both.  
   **Answer:** The water is the same, but the angle of the light is different. At a steep angle, light from below passes through the top, so it is a window. At a shallow angle, bigger than about 49 degrees from the normal, no light can get out, so it all bounces back, and the top is a mirror.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the angle decides, and mentions that beyond the critical angle all the light bounces back. It does not say the water changes.
13. (label) Here is a drawing of a ray going from air into water. Label the four parts.  
   **Answer:** ['the surface of the water: the flat line the ray crosses', 'the normal: the dotted line at a right angle to the surface', 'the angle in the air: between the ray in the air and the normal', 'the angle in the water: between the ray in the water and the normal, and smaller than the angle in the air']. *Why:* Every bend diagram has the same four parts. The two angles are both measured from the normal. In water, the angle is smaller because the ray bends toward the normal.
14. (draw) Draw a ray going at a slant into a glass block, through it, and out the other side. Draw the normal at both surfaces. Show how the ray bends.  
   **Answer:** The ray bends toward the normal going in, goes straight in the glass, and bends away from the normal coming out. It leaves parallel to the way it came in, but shifted sideways.. *Why:* A strong drawing has a normal at each surface, a bend toward the normal on the way in, and an equal bend away on the way out. The two outside rays are parallel.
15. (perspective) On a hot afternoon, three children look along a road and see a shiny patch that looks like water.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks Farhan, and says that a camera can photograph a mirage, and that the patch moves away as we walk toward it, so it is not real water. Hot air bends light, so a picture of the sky appears on the road. A good test is to take a photo.. *Why:* The mirage is made of real light that has been bent by layers of air. A camera sees it, so it is not only in the mind. It is not water, because it keeps moving away and does not wet the road.
16. (count) A lens has a focal length of 25 centimetres, which is a quarter of a metre. Its power in dioptres is 1 divided by the focal length in metres. What is its power?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* A quarter of a metre is 0.25 metres. 1 divided by 0.25 is 4. So the lens is 4 dioptres. A shorter focal length means a stronger lens.
### Why Light Bends and Gets Trapped (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)

   **Answer:** About 200,000 kilometres a second. *Why:* The refractive index says how many times slower light is. 300,000 divided by 1.5 is 200,000. Choosing 450,000 multiplies, which would make light faster in glass. Light never speeds up when it goes into glass.
2. (count) A beam goes from air into water at 60 degrees from the normal and bends to 41 degrees from the normal. By how many degrees has the water turned the beam?  
   **Answer:** 19. *Why:* The beam was at 60 degrees in the air and is at 41 degrees in the water. 60 take away 41 is 19. So the water turned the beam by 19 degrees, toward the normal.

   Options: Honey bends light almost as much as glass does, but much less than diamond does / Honey is heavier than diamond / Diamond is a different colour / Honey is darker than glass  
   **Answer:** Honey bends light almost as much as glass does, but much less than diamond does. *Why:* We see a clear thing only where light bends or bounces at its edge. Honey and glass slow light by almost the same amount, so there is hardly any bend and the marble nearly vanishes. Diamond slows light far more than honey, so its edge still bends the light a lot.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a block of glass with parallel sides turns a beam of light for good.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The ray bends toward the normal going in, and by the same amount away from it coming out, so it leaves going the same way as before, only shifted sideways. A prism, whose sides are not parallel, turns a beam for good.

   **Answer:** ['shifted', 'moved', 'displaced']. *Why:* The two bends cancel, so the direction is the same. But the ray travelled a slanted path inside the glass, so it comes out a little to one side.

   Options: Diamond, because it has the smaller critical angle / Glass, because it has the bigger critical angle / Both the same / Neither can trap light  
   **Answer:** Diamond, because it has the smaller critical angle. *Why:* Light is trapped whenever it meets the edge at an angle bigger than the critical angle. A smaller critical angle means that more of the angles trap light. That is part of why diamonds sparkle.
7. (sort) Light is going from one material to another at a very shallow angle. In which journeys can it be trapped?  
   **Answer:** from water into air → Can be trapped; from glass into air → Can be trapped; from diamond into air → Can be trapped; from air into water → Cannot be trapped; from air into glass → Cannot be trapped; from water into glass → Cannot be trapped. *Why:* Light can be trapped only when it is going from a slower material to a faster one, because only then does it bend away from the normal so much that it cannot leave. Going into a slower material, the light always bends toward the normal and gets in.
8. (order) Put the path of a ray of sunlight through a raindrop in order, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3]. *Why:* A rainbow is made by one bend on the way in, one bounce at the back and one bend on the way out. Each colour bends by a slightly different amount, so the colours leave in slightly different directions.

Outside that cone, the fish sees the underside of the surface at angles bigger than the critical angle of about 49 degrees, where it is a mirror.    **Answer:** 10 times. *Why:* A telescope magnifies by the focal length of the first lens divided by the focal length of the eyepiece. 40 divided by 4 is 10.
11. (mcq) Why can you see a rainbow only when the Sun is behind you?  
   Options: The drops send each colour back toward the Sun's side, at about 42 degrees from the line pointing away from the Sun / The Sun is too bright in front / Rain falls only behind you / Rainbows are made by the eyes  
   **Answer:** The drops send each colour back toward the Sun's side, at about 42 degrees from the line pointing away from the Sun. *Why:* Light enters a raindrop, bounces off the back and comes out going back toward where it came from, at about 42 degrees for red. So the drops that send light to your eyes lie in the direction opposite the Sun.
12. (mcq) A scientist takes the back coats off an ox eye and sees a tiny upside-down picture on the back wall of the eye. What does this show?  
   Options: The lens of the eye bends light to form a picture on the retina / The lens of the eye is the screen that sees / Ox eyes are special / Light comes out of the eye  
   **Answer:** The lens of the eye bends light to form a picture on the retina. *Why:* The picture appears on the retina, at the back, and not in the lens. So the lens is one of the lenses that bends light into a picture. The old idea, that the lens is where seeing happens, was wrong.

## Applications
- Optical fibre cables carry phone calls and the internet along the floor of the sea, as flashes of light.
- Doctors use bundles of optical fibres in thin tubes to look inside the body without a big operation.
- Spectacles and contact lenses use hollow and bulging lenses to put the picture on the retina.
- A cut diamond or a cut-glass bowl is shaped so that trapped light comes back out of the top and sparkles.
- Binoculars and periscopes use glass prisms that bounce light by total internal reflection to turn the picture right way up.
- Telescopes and microscopes use two or more lenses to make faraway or tiny things look big.
- Cameras and phones use several lenses together to make a sharp picture on a sensor.
- Eye surgeons replace a cloudy lens, a cataract, with a clear plastic lens.
- Lighthouses use a ring-shaped lens that bends the lamp's light into one strong beam.
- Bottle bulbs, bottles of water fixed through a roof, bring daylight into homes with no windows.

## Spotted around us
- The thin fibre cable that an internet company brings to a house or a building.
- The sparkle of a cut-glass bowl or a faceted glass bead in a shop window when you move your head.
- The silver underside of the water in a swimming pool, seen from underneath at a low slant.
- The wobbling air above a hot tava, a hot roof or a chimney, which makes the view behind it shake.
- A shiny, wet-looking patch on a long, hot road, or on the floor of a long corridor in the sun.
- The little lens in a door peephole, which shows a wide, small view of the person outside.
- Hollow and bulging lenses in spectacles at an optician's shop. Print looks smaller through one and bigger through the other.
- A rainbow in the spray of a sprinkler, a garden hose or a waterfall, with the Sun behind you.
- The big ring-shaped lens at the top of a lighthouse, if you visit one.

## Open questions
- **Which is the best picture of why light slows down in glass?** Physicists agree on the numbers for how much light slows, and on the bends it makes. They use more than one picture to explain what happens inside the material, and they do not always agree on which is best.
- **Can light be slowed or stopped and used to store information?** Light has been slowed to the speed of a bicycle and even stopped for a short time in a cloud of atoms. Nobody yet knows if this can be made small and cheap enough to be useful.
- **Why do more and more children become short-sighted?** Reports say that short sight in children is rising in many places. Studies link it to more time spent on near work and less time outdoors in daylight, but scientists do not yet agree on the whole cause.
- **Can a lens be as flat as a sheet of paper?** In 2016, scientists made flat lenses from tiny pillars of material, called metalenses. Early ones worked well for one colour only. Making them work for all colours, cheaply, is still being worked on.
- **How do hunting animals that work across the water's surface, such as fish and birds, cope with the bend?** Some animals seem to correct for the bend very well. Scientists are still studying how much is learned by practice and how much is born in them.
- **Who first used a curved piece of glass or crystal to look closely at things?** Ancient polished crystals have been found in several places, and some may have been used as lenses. Historians do not agree on which ones were, and written records are very scarce.

## Connected maths
- In your bend test, a beam goes into the water at 30 degrees from the normal and comes out at 22 degrees from the normal in the water. By how many degrees did the water turn the beam? **8 degrees.**. The beam was at 30 degrees in the air and at 22 degrees in the water. 30 take away 22 is 8. So the water turned the beam by 8 degrees, toward the normal. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- Light moves at about 300,000 kilometres a second in empty space and at about 225,000 kilometres a second in water. What fraction of its speed does light keep in water? **Three quarters.**. 225,000 out of 300,000 is the same as 225 out of 300. Divide both by 75 to get 3 out of 4. So light keeps three quarters of its speed in water, which is why water has a refractive index of about 1.33. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Seen from straight above, a temple tank looks 3 metres deep. Water looks three quarters as deep as it really is. How deep is the tank really? **4 metres.**. 3 metres is three quarters of the real depth. One quarter is 3 divided by 3, which is 1 metre. The whole depth is 4 quarters, which is 4 metres. So the tank is 4 metres deep. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Light moves at about 200,000 kilometres a second in glass. A fibre in a cable under the sea is 3,000 kilometres long. How many thousandths of a second does the light take to travel along it? **15 thousandths of a second.**. Time is distance divided by speed. 3,000 divided by 200,000 is 0.015 of a second. That is 15 thousandths of a second. So a flash of light crosses the whole cable in less than one fiftieth of a second. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A diver's round window of sky is about 9 metres wide for every 4 metres of depth. How wide is the window for a diver at 8 metres? And for a diver at 2 metres? **18 metres at 8 metres deep, and 4 and a half metres at 2 metres deep.**. 8 metres is two lots of 4 metres, so the window is two lots of 9 metres, which is 18 metres. 2 metres is half of 4 metres, so the window is half of 9 metres, which is 4 and a half metres. The deeper you go, the wider the window. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Reading spectacles marked plus 2 have a focal length of 50 centimetres. A hand magnifier has a focal length of 25 centimetres. Which is stronger, and how many times stronger? **The magnifier is stronger, twice as strong.**. A shorter focal length means a stronger lens. 25 centimetres is half of 50 centimetres. The magnifier is 4 dioptres, because 1 divided by a quarter of a metre is 4. The spectacles are 2 dioptres. 4 is twice 2. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- You want a telescope that magnifies 12 times. The front lens has a focal length of 60 centimetres. What focal length must the eyepiece have? **5 centimetres.**. Magnification is the front focal length divided by the eyepiece focal length. So the eyepiece focal length is 60 divided by 12, which is 5 centimetres. Check: 60 divided by 5 is 12. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- The critical angle is about 49 degrees for water, about 42 degrees for glass and about 24 degrees for diamond. Put them in order, from the one that traps light most easily to the one that traps it least easily. How many degrees apart are water and diamond? **Diamond, then glass, then water. Water and diamond are 25 degrees apart.**. A smaller critical angle means that light is trapped at more angles, so diamond traps most easily, then glass, then water. 49 take away 24 is 25 degrees. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
- A rainbow is a circle around the point exactly opposite the Sun, and the red arc is 42 degrees from that point. That point is as far below the horizon as the Sun is above it. The Sun is 20 degrees above the horizon. How high above the horizon is the top of the rainbow? **22 degrees.**. The centre of the circle is 20 degrees below the horizon. The top of the rainbow is 42 degrees above the centre. 42 take away 20 is 22 degrees above the horizon. If the Sun were higher than 42 degrees, the whole rainbow would be below the horizon, and we would not see it from the ground. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is the normal in a bend diagram? → An imaginary line at a right angle to the surface, where we measure every angle from.
- A beam goes from air into water at a slant. Which way does it bend? → Toward the normal, because light slows down in water.
- What does light do when it arrives along the normal? → It goes straight on without bending.
- What does a bigger refractive index mean? → Slower light in the material, and a bigger bend.
- What is total internal reflection? → Light inside a material that bounces back from the edge, because it meets it at a very shallow angle.
- Why does a fish see the whole sky in a round window? → The surface bends light from the whole sky into a cone, and beyond it the surface is a mirror.
- How does an optical fibre carry light round a bend? → The light bounces off the walls inside again and again.
- What is a hollow lens used for? → To fix short sight, because it spreads the light a little before it enters the eye.
- How do you find how much a telescope magnifies? → Divide the focal length of the front lens by the focal length of the eyepiece.
