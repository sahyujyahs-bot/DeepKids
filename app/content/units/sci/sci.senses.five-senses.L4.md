# Senses in action: how long a signal takes, and how the brain guesses
`sci.senses.five-senses.L4`

**Summary.** A friend drops a ruler between your fingers without warning. You grab it, but never before it has fallen a hand-length. Light from the falling ruler reached your eye almost at once, so where did the delay happen? Your child times a signal on its way from eye, ear and skin to the hand, then asks how big a change must be before a sense notices, why a smell fades while you stand in it, and why you cannot tickle yourself. Along the way you meet pain that arrives in two waves, a rubber hand that starts to feel like yours, and animals that sense magnetic fields and electricity. Nobody yet knows how a brain makes one steady 'now' out of signals that all arrive late.

**Intro.** *Ask a friend to hold a ruler upright, with its bottom between your finger and thumb. Do not move. Your friend lets go without any warning. Close your fingers as fast as you can. Look at the number where you caught it. Did your fingers close at once?* They did not. The ruler fell a hand-length or so before you caught it, even though you saw it start to fall. In this unit you measure that delay for the eyes, the ears and the skin, and you find out where the time goes. Then you test how small a change a sense can notice, how a smell fades while you stand in it, and how your brain guesses what is about to happen. You also meet animals that sense magnetism and electricity.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that every sense sends the brain tiny pulses, and that a stronger stimulus makes more pulses each second, not bigger pulses.
1. I can measure my reaction time with a falling ruler, compare sight, sound and touch, and explain where the time is spent between sensing and moving.
2. I can explain that nerve signals travel at different speeds, and use that to explain why a stubbed toe hurts in two waves.
3. I can explain what a threshold is, and use a fair test to show that the smallest change a sense notices depends on how much was there to start with.
4. I can explain why a steady smell, touch or sight fades from notice, and why that helps a body.
5. I can explain that the brain keeps a map of the body, that pain is made by the brain as a warning, and why people can feel a limb that is gone.
6. I can give evidence that the brain predicts what it is about to sense, and use it to explain why you cannot tickle yourself.
7. I can give examples of animals with senses that humans lack, and say which questions about the senses scientists still cannot answer.

## Terms
- **Stimulus**: Anything that a receptor can notice, such as light, a sound, a touch, a smell or a change in warmth. The plural is stimuli. *e.g.* The smell of tadka in hot oil is a stimulus for the smell receptors in your nose.
- **Neuron**: A nerve cell. It has branches that gather messages, a body, and a long thread that sends the message on. Nerves are bundles of these threads. *e.g.* Some neurons in a grown-up's leg are about one metre long, from the toe to the lower back.
- **Nerve pulse**: A tiny wave of electricity that travels along a neuron. Every pulse is the same size. A stronger stimulus makes more pulses each second, not bigger ones. *e.g.* A light touch might send 10 pulses each second. A hard press might send 100.
- **Myelin**: A fatty wrapping around some nerve threads, like the plastic around an electric wire. Pulses move much faster along wrapped threads. *e.g.* Touch and muscle signals travel along wrapped threads. Slow, dull pain travels along unwrapped ones.
- **Reaction time**: The time between a stimulus and your response to it. It includes the signal going in, a decision in the brain, and the signal going out to the muscles. *e.g.* When a ruler starts to fall, your reaction time is the time until your fingers close. For most people it is about one fifth of a second.
- **Threshold**: The smallest amount of a stimulus that a sense can notice. Anything smaller goes unnoticed. *e.g.* A very faint tick of a wall clock in a quiet room is at your threshold for hearing.
- **Just noticeable difference**: The smallest change in a stimulus that you can tell apart. Scientists often shorten it to JND. A small JND means a sharp sense. *e.g.* Two bags of rice weigh 500 grams and 540 grams. If you can just tell which is heavier, 40 grams is your JND at that weight.
- **Adaptation**: When a sense gets used to a stimulus that stays the same, and stops signalling it so strongly. Receptors do this on their own. *e.g.* When you walk into a kitchen full of frying onions, the smell is strong. After a few minutes you hardly notice it.
- **Pain**: A feeling the brain makes to warn you that something may be harming your body. Nerve endings in the skin and body send danger signals, and the brain decides how much it hurts. *e.g.* A thorn in your foot makes the foot hurt. The warning is made in your brain.
- **Body map**: Your brain has a strip on its surface where each part of your body has its own patch. Parts with many receptors, such as lips and fingertips, get big patches. *e.g.* On the brain's body map, your thumb takes more room than your whole back.
- **Phantom limb**: The feeling that an arm or a leg is still there after it has been lost. The brain's patch for that limb is still working. *e.g.* Many people who lose a hand can still feel their fingers itch.
- **Prediction**: A guess about what will happen next. Your brain makes predictions about what you are about to sense, so it can react faster and ignore what it expected. *e.g.* When you reach for a door you know, your brain predicts how heavy it feels. A door that is much lighter than expected makes you stumble.
- **Illusion**: Something that your senses and brain build wrongly, even though the signals from the senses were correct. You can know it is wrong and still sense it. *e.g.* In the rubber hand test, a glove on the table starts to feel like your own hand.
- **Cross-modal**: Linking one sense with another. People link sounds with shapes, or tastes with colours, in ways that are much the same across the world. *e.g.* Most people say the made-up word 'bouba' fits a round shape and 'kiki' fits a spiky one.
- **Magnetoreception**: Sensing magnetic fields. Some birds, turtles, fish and insects can sense the direction of the Earth's magnetic field. *e.g.* A small bird flying south for the winter may use the Earth's magnetism to keep going the right way.
- **Electroreception**: Sensing weak electric fields. All living muscles make tiny electric fields, so a hunter with this sense can find hidden prey. *e.g.* A shark has jelly-filled pores on their snout that sense the weak electricity of a fish hidden in the sand.

## Explanations
### A signal is a pulse, and a strong signal is a fast drumbeat of pulses · *defined*
**Definition:** A nerve pulse is a tiny wave of electricity that runs along a neuron. All pulses are the same size. A stronger stimulus makes more pulses each second.

In the earlier unit on the senses you learned that every sense sends the brain nerve signals. Now look closer at one signal. A neuron is a nerve cell with a long thread. When the neuron is stirred, tiny gates open in the skin of the thread. Charged particles rush in, and that makes a small wave of electricity that runs down the thread. This wave is a nerve pulse. A pulse is like a spark that is either there or not there. It is never half a spark, and it is never a bigger spark. So how does the brain know that a touch is hard and not gentle? In 1926 two scientists, Edgar Adrian and Yngve Zotterman, listened to a single nerve thread from a frog's muscle. When they hung a heavier weight on the muscle, the pulses did not get bigger. They came faster. A light weight gave a few pulses each second. A heavy weight gave many. Edgar Adrian later shared the 1932 Nobel Prize for this kind of work. So the strength of a sense is written in the speed of the drumbeat. What the signal is about is written in which thread it travels along, as you learned before. Your brain holds about 86 billion neurons. Suzana Herculano-Houzel, a Brazilian scientist, counted them in 2009 by turning a brain into a soup of cell centres and counting those. Each eye sends about one million threads to the brain, and every thread sends its own drumbeat. Plants also use pulses. A Venus flytrap has trigger hairs inside her trap. One touch on a hair sends one pulse, and nothing happens. A second touch within about 20 seconds makes the trap snap shut. She counts.
- e.g. Press a fingertip on a table very lightly, then harder. The skin sends the same kind of pulse both times. The harder press only sends more of them each second.
- e.g. A Venus flytrap shuts only when two touches arrive within about 20 seconds. A raindrop that touches one hair once does not trigger her. She uses pulses to tell prey from rain.
- e.g. Doctors can record the pulses from a nerve and play them as clicks. A stronger touch sounds like faster clicking.
### Fast wires and slow wires: why signals take time · *defined*
**Definition:** Nerve pulses travel at different speeds. Threads wrapped in myelin carry pulses fastest, up to about 100 metres each second. Thin unwrapped threads carry pulses at about 1 metre each second.

Light from a falling ruler reaches your eye almost at once. But your finger does not close at once. The signal has to be made in the eye, travel to the brain, be sorted and decided on, and then travel down the arm to the muscles. How fast is a nerve signal? In the 1840s the German scientist Johannes Müller wrote that its speed would probably never be measured, because it must be too fast. In 1850 another German scientist, Hermann von Helmholtz, proved him wrong. He gave a frog's leg nerve a small shock in two places, one near the muscle and one further away. The muscle twitched a little later when the shock was further away. From the extra time and the extra distance, Helmholtz worked out the speed: about 30 metres each second. That is much slower than light, and slower than a car on a highway. Later scientists found that different threads have different speeds. The big threads wrapped in myelin, which carry touch and the position of muscles, run at 50 to 100 metres each second. Threads for sharp pain are thinner and run at about 5 to 30 metres each second. Threads for slow, dull pain are thin and unwrapped, and run at about 1 metre each second. This explains something you may have felt. Stub your toe, and first comes a quick sharp pain. About a second later comes a slow, deep ache. Two kinds of thread, two speeds. From toe to brain is about 1.5 metres for a grown-up. At 1 metre each second that is about a second and a half. At 15 metres each second it is only a tenth of a second. On top of travel time, the brain needs time to decide. In total, simple reaction times are about 0.15 to 0.25 seconds. Touch and hearing are usually a little faster than sight, because the eye does extra work before it sends its signal. In a race, sprinters wait for the sound of a gun, not for a flash. The rule says that if a runner starts less than 0.1 seconds after the gun, the start was too early. Nobody can truly react that fast, so the runner must have guessed.
- e.g. Stub your toe and count. The sharp pain comes first and the dull ache comes after, because the two kinds of pain travel along threads of different speeds.
- e.g. In 1796 an English astronomer, Nevil Maskelyne, dismissed his assistant David Kinnebrook, because Kinnebrook timed a star crossing about 0.8 seconds later than Maskelyne. Years later, the German astronomer Friedrich Bessel found that every astronomer has their own delay. It is called the personal equation.
- e.g. A sprinter's feet cannot leave the block less than 0.1 seconds after the gun without the start counting as a false start.
### The faintest thing a sense can notice: thresholds · *defined*
**Definition:** A threshold is the smallest amount of a stimulus that a sense can notice. Below it, you notice nothing. Above it, you notice. Thresholds change with the person, the place and how carefully they pay attention.

Every sense has a faintest stimulus that it can pick up. In 1942 Selig Hecht, a scientist in the USA, and his team asked people to sit in a completely dark room for about half an hour. This lets the eyes become as sensitive as they can. Then they showed very faint flashes of light. They worked out that a person can notice a flash when only a handful of light particles are caught by the light cells of the eye, fewer than 15. On a very dark clear night, scientists have estimated that your eye could notice a candle flame tens of kilometres away. The threshold for smell is also very low. Cooking gas has no smell of its own. Gas companies add a smelly chemical so that a leak is noticed even when there is very little in the air. Hearing tests in a clinic work by finding your threshold. The tester plays softer and softer beeps and finds the softest one you can hear. Here is a surprise. A threshold is not one fixed line. In the 1950s scientists such as Wilson Tanner and John Swets showed that a person's answer depends on two things. One is how well the sense works. The other is how ready the person is to say yes. A careful person who does not want to be fooled says no to faint signals. An eager person says yes more often. At the very faint end, the same person can answer differently on different days. A tired nose, a noisy room or a worried mind can all change a threshold. The best tests ask many times and then look for the stimulus that you notice about half of the time.
- e.g. A hearing test finds the softest beep each ear can notice.
- e.g. Cooking gas is given a strong smell on purpose, so even a tiny leak is above most people's threshold.
- e.g. In a noisy market you miss a soft sound that you would hear easily in a quiet room. The noise raises your threshold.
### Ratios, not amounts: how big a change must be to notice it · *defined*
**Definition:** The smallest change you can notice, called the just noticeable difference, is usually a fixed fraction of what was there to start with. This is called Weber's law.

Lift a light bag in one hand and a slightly heavier bag in the other. You can tell which is heavier. Now carry a very heavy sack in each hand, and make one a little heavier. You cannot tell, unless the difference is bigger. In the 1830s a German scientist, Ernst Weber, tested this with people lifting weights. He found that the change needed to notice a difference was not a fixed number of grams. It was a fixed fraction of the weight you started with. In careful lab tests, about 2 or 3 extra grams in every 100 grams could be noticed. At home with bottles, most people need a bigger step, often 10 in every 100 or more. The same idea works for other senses. A candle is easy to see in a dark room and impossible to see in bright sunshine, because the same candle is a tiny fraction of the sunlight. A whisper is clear in a quiet room and lost in a noisy market. In 1860 Gustav Fechner, another German scientist, turned Weber's finding into a rule about how sensations grow. When a stimulus doubles, a sensation grows by a small, equal step. This is why we measure many things on a scale where each step multiplies. Loudness is measured in decibels, and every 10 decibels means 10 times more sound energy, yet it sounds only about twice as loud. Astronomers grade the brightness of stars in magnitudes. The ancient Greek astronomer Hipparchus sorted stars into six classes. In 1856 Norman Pogson showed that one class step is about 2.5 times in brightness, so five steps is 100 times. Working with ratios lets the eye work from dim starlight to bright noon sunshine, a range of billions of times, without getting dazzled. Weber's law is only approximate. It works best in the middle of the range and fails near the threshold and near the top.
- e.g. If you can just tell 100 grams from 110 grams, then at 500 grams you might need about 550 grams to just tell the difference. The fraction stays about the same.
- e.g. A torch is easy to spot at night and almost invisible at noon. It is the same torch, but a tiny fraction of the daylight.
- e.g. A decibel scale makes a whisper (about 30 decibels) and a loud horn (about 110 decibels) fit on one short ruler.
### Tired receptors: why steady things fade · *defined*
**Definition:** Adaptation is when a sense reports a steady stimulus less and less. It saves effort, keeps the sense ready for change, and works at the receptor itself and also in the brain.

Walk into a kitchen where onions are frying and the smell is strong. After a few minutes you hardly notice it. Walk outside for a while and come back, and the smell is strong again. This is adaptation. Smell receptors send a burst of pulses at the start, then fewer and fewer if the smell stays the same. Vision adapts too. When you walk from sunshine into a dark cinema, you see nothing at first. The light cells in the eye need time to become sensitive again. Cones recover in a few minutes. Rods take about 20 to 30 minutes to become fully ready. Then a torch in the same hall can look blinding. Even a steady picture fades. Your eyes make tiny jumps several times each second, even when you think you are staring. In 1953 Lorrin Riggs and his team fixed a tiny mirror to a contact lens, so that a picture moved exactly with the eye and stayed in the same place on the retina. The picture faded to nothing within seconds. Scientists now think that the eyes must keep moving for us to keep seeing. Adaptation is not a fault. It means that your senses spend their effort on what changes, and a change is the thing that matters most. A new smell in the house, a new sound in the night, a new touch on your arm may all mean something. But not all senses adapt equally. Pain adapts much less than smell does. A warning that faded away while you were still being hurt would not be much use. This is also why a smell that you cannot notice may still be there. A gas leak or a slowly rotting food may have been in the room for hours. A person who has just arrived can smell it at once.
- e.g. You stop noticing your own house's smell, but a visitor notices it as soon as they walk in.
- e.g. Walking into a dark cinema hall, you cannot see the seats. After 10 minutes you can see the whole room, because your rods have become ready.
- e.g. A watch on your wrist feels like nothing after a minute, but you notice at once if you take it off.
### The body map in the brain, and pain that the brain makes · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain has a strip on its surface with a patch for each part of the body. Pain is a warning that the brain makes from danger signals. It is not a direct reading of damage.

In the 1930s and 1940s, a Canadian brain surgeon, Wilder Penfield, operated on awake patients who had a serious illness called epilepsy. The brain itself has no pain nerves, so patients can stay awake while the surgeon works, with just the scalp numbed. When Penfield touched a spot on the strip of brain with a tiny electric probe, the patient said that they felt a touch on a body part, for example a thumb or a lip. He joined the spots into a map of the body on the brain. The map is strange. The hands, the lips and the tongue take up huge areas, and the back and legs take up small ones. Parts with many receptors get the most room. This fits the two-point test you may have done earlier. Pain also comes from nerve endings that warn of danger, called nociceptors. But the feeling of hurting is made in the brain. A person born with a broken gene for a certain sodium gate in the pain nerves feels no pain at all. In 2006 a team led by James Cox and Geoffrey Woods found this gene in families in Pakistan. These people hurt themselves badly without knowing, because they never get the warning. The map also explains phantom limbs. In the 1870s an American doctor, Silas Weir Mitchell, described soldiers who still felt a limb that had been lost. Most people who lose a limb feel it. The brain's patch for the hand is still there and still active. In the 1990s V. S. Ramachandran, a neuroscientist who grew up in India, and Diane Rogers-Ramachandran reported that some patients felt touches on their face as touches on the missing hand, because the face patch lies next to the hand patch. They also built a mirror box that makes it look as if the missing hand is back. For some patients, this eased their phantom pain. Heart trouble can show up as pain in the left arm. The nerves of the heart and the arm share paths into the brain, which can mix up the message.
- e.g. A map of the body drawn the size of the brain's patches has giant lips, hands and tongue and a tiny back.
- e.g. A person who has lost a hand may feel a touch on their cheek as a touch on the missing fingers.
- e.g. A person who feels no pain can break a bone and walk on it for days without knowing.
### The brain guesses ahead: why you cannot tickle yourself · *defined*
**Definition:** The brain predicts what you are about to sense, especially from your own movements. It treats what it predicted as less important. That is why your own touch does not tickle.

Try to tickle your own palm. It does not work. Someone else can tickle you easily. In 1998 Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, Daniel Wolpert and Chris Frith tested this with a machine. People held a soft piece of foam that was moved by a robot arm. When they moved a handle to make the foam stroke their own palm, the touch felt less ticklish than when the machine did the same stroke without their control. When the scientists added a small delay between the movement and the touch, the touch tickled more. So the brain predicts the result of your own movement, and turns down the feeling that it expected. This is a general trick. Your eyes jump from point to point about three times each second. The picture on the retina smears each time, but you never see the smear, because the brain turns off the signal during each jump. A trick shows this. Look in a mirror at your left eye, and then at your right eye. You never see your eyes move. The brain also guesses what things should look like. In 2001 a team found that when you glance at a clock with a second hand, the first second seems to last longer than the others. The brain fills in the gap during the eye jump with what it sees next. Predictions can also lead you wrong. In 1998 Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen showed that, if you cannot see your real hand, and a rubber hand on a table is stroked in time with your hidden hand, you start to feel the rubber hand as yours. Your brain finds the best match for the signals and decides that the hand you can see is your hand. Many scientists now think that perception is mostly prediction, checked and corrected by signals from the senses. How far this idea goes is still being tested.
- e.g. You cannot tickle yourself, but a friend can. Your brain expects your own touch.
- e.g. Walk down a step that is not there in the dark. You stumble, because your brain had predicted the step and your foot did not find it.
- e.g. In the rubber hand test, brushes on a hidden hand and a rubber hand, in time with each other, make many people feel the rubber hand as part of them.
### Rewired and cross-wired: senses are less separate than they seem · *defined*
**Definition:** Parts of the brain are not fixed to one sense. They can learn to handle signals from another sense, and the senses link up in surprising ways.

In the earlier unit you learned that signals from the eye and the ear look much alike. If so, can the brain use a part of itself for a different sense? Since the 1980s, Mriganka Sur, an Indian-born brain scientist in the USA, and his team rerouted the eye nerves of newborn ferrets so they reached the part of the brain that normally listens to sound. In the experiments reported in 2000 by Laurie von Melchner, Sarah Pallas and Mriganka Sur, the ferrets could still respond to light with that rewired part. The part of the brain that usually hears had learned to work with signals from the eye. In the 1960s Paul Bach-y-Rita, a doctor in the USA, built a chair with 400 small vibrating points against the back. A camera sent a picture to the points. After practice, blind people learned to tell shapes and even to catch a rolling ball. Later versions put the pattern on the tongue. The senses also link up in ways that happen on their own. In 1929 the German psychologist Wolfgang Köhler showed people two shapes, one round and one spiky. He asked which one is called 'takete' and which is called 'baluba'. Most chose round for baluba. In 2001 V. S. Ramachandran and Edward Hubbard repeated this with the words 'bouba' and 'kiki', in the USA and with Tamil speakers in India, and found the same pattern in both groups. Tests across many languages find that most people match the sounds to the shapes in the same way. Babies only a few months old do it too. About 1 person in 25 has synaesthesia, which means that one sense always triggers another. For example, they may always see the letter A as red. Scientists do not yet know why the links exist, but one idea is that the brain finds patterns that are shared between senses.
- e.g. A blind person using a tongue display can learn to tell the shape of a cup without touching it.
- e.g. Most people choose 'bouba' for the round shape and 'kiki' for the spiky one, whichever language they speak.
- e.g. A person with synaesthesia may taste a flavour when they hear a certain word.
### Senses we do not have: magnetism, electricity and heat · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals have receptors for things humans have no receptor for, such as the Earth's magnetic field, weak electric fields and infrared heat.

The Earth is a giant magnet, and its field is always around us. In the 1960s two German scientists, Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko, showed that European robins kept in cages tried to head in the direction of their migration, but only when the magnetic field around them matched the real one. When they turned the field with coils, the robins turned too. Scientists think that some birds sense the field through a special molecule in their eyes, and that the field changes how a chemical reaction in that molecule goes. This idea is still being tested. Sea turtles also seem to use the field. Olive ridley turtles nest on the beaches of Odisha and Tamil Nadu. Scientists think that they, like other sea turtles, may use the field to find their way back across the sea, but this has been tested more in other kinds of sea turtle. Electric fields are even more surprising. Every living muscle makes a tiny electric field when it moves. A shark has jelly-filled pores on her snout, called the ampullae of Lorenzini, after the Italian who described them in 1678. They can feel the field of a fish buried in the sand. The platypus has thousands of electric sensors in her bill, and hunts with her eyes shut. In 2013 a team led by Daniel Clarke and Heather Whitney found that bumblebees can sense the weak electric field around a flower. A flower that has just been visited has a changed field, which may tell a bee that the nectar is gone. Pit vipers sense infrared, or heat, with small pits between their eyes and nostrils. Some other animals have extra kinds of light receptor. The mantis shrimp has 12 to 16 kinds, compared with our three. But in tests, she was not better at telling similar colours apart, so more kinds of receptor do not always mean finer sight. Each animal's senses are built for the life that animal lives.
- e.g. A shark can sense the weak electricity of a flatfish hidden under sand.
- e.g. Bumblebees can sense the electric field of a flower before they land on it.
- e.g. A mantis shrimp has up to 16 kinds of light receptor, but was not better than other animals at telling colours apart.
### Is there a real world behind the signals? · *debated*
Each sense gives the brain a stream of pulses. The brain turns them into a world of colours, sounds, smells and a steady 'now'. The signals arrive late, are different from animal to animal, and are filled in by guesses. So scientists and philosophers ask: how much of what you experience is the world, and how much is built by you? Everyone agrees that there is a world, that light really reaches your eye and that molecules really reach your nose. The argument is about what the experience is, and whether the world itself looks anything like it.
- e.g. The same fruit is red to a child and appears dark to a bee in ordinary light. Each is right about what their eyes detect.
- e.g. A person who has lost a hand feels the hand, even though nothing is there. The feeling was real. The hand was not.
- **Direct realists, many philosophers and ordinary people:** When you look at a mango, you see the mango itself. The delay of a fifth of a second is small and does not change what you see. Signals are the means by which we meet the world, not a curtain in front of it.
- **Anil Seth, a neuroscientist in England, and others who study the predicting brain:** The brain never meets the world directly. It makes a best guess about the causes of its signals and corrects the guess. Seth calls what we experience a 'controlled hallucination', controlled because the senses keep checking it.
- **Donald Hoffman, a scientist in the USA, in 2015:** Evolution shaped our senses to help us survive, not to show us the truth. Like icons on a phone screen, our colours and shapes hide what is really there. Many scientists think this goes too far, because accurate senses usually help survival.
- **Advaita Vedanta, an Indian school of thought, and Adi Shankara of about the 8th century:** The world of shapes and colours that the senses show is appearance. What is real beneath it is one awareness. The science of the senses does not prove this, but it shows how much of our world is built by us.

## Mostly agreed
- Every nerve pulse is the same size. The strength of a stimulus is written in how many pulses arrive each second. *How we know:* Since the 1920s, scientists have recorded single nerve threads in frogs, cats and people. A harder press, a brighter light or a louder sound always gives faster pulses, never bigger ones.
- Nerve signals take time. Simple reaction times are about 0.15 to 0.25 seconds, and nerve pulses travel at speeds from about 1 to about 100 metres each second. *How we know:* Hermann von Helmholtz measured the speed of a frog nerve in 1850. Reaction times have been timed in millions of people since, with the same range of answers.
- The smallest change you can notice is roughly a fixed fraction of what was there before, for weight, loudness and brightness. This is called Weber's law, and it works best in the middle of the range. *How we know:* Ernst Weber tested people lifting weights in the 1830s. Later tests with sound and light found much the same pattern, with some exceptions near the faintest and strongest stimuli.
- A steady stimulus is reported less and less. Receptors adapt, and so does the brain, which saves effort and keeps the senses ready for change. *How we know:* Recordings from smell, touch and light receptors show their pulses falling away when the stimulus stays the same. A picture that is held perfectly still on the retina fades within seconds.
- The brain keeps a map of the body, and pain is made by the brain as a warning. It is not a direct reading of damage. *How we know:* Touching spots on an awake patient's brain made them feel touches on parts of their body. People with a broken pain-nerve gene feel no pain. People who have lost a limb often feel the limb for years.
- The brain predicts what its own movements will cause, and turns down those expected signals. That is why your own touch does not tickle. *How we know:* In 1998 Sarah-Jayne Blakemore and her team found that a touch you make yourself feels less ticklish than the same touch made by a machine, and more ticklish if a small delay is added.

## How we found out
**Hermann von Helmholtz, a German scientist (1821 to 1894), 1850.** For centuries, people thought that a signal in a nerve must be as quick as thought, perhaps as quick as light. In the 1840s the famous German scientist Johannes Müller wrote that its speed would probably never be measured. In 1850 another German scientist, Hermann von Helmholtz, tried anyway. He took the leg nerve of a frog, with the calf muscle still attached. He gave the nerve a small electric shock at two spots, one near the muscle and one further away. Then he timed, with a fast device he built himself, how long the muscle took to twitch. The twitch came slightly later when the shock was further from the muscle. From the extra distance and the extra time, he worked out the speed. It was about 30 metres each second, much slower than light. Helmholtz soon tried it on people, who were asked to press a button when they felt a touch. The results varied more, but they showed the same thing. A signal takes time, and the body is always a little behind the world.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists, including Johannes Müller, thought a nerve signal was too fast to measure, perhaps as fast as light. Helmholtz showed it is slow enough to time with a frog's leg.

## Challenging perspectives
- **You see and hear things at the very moment they happen.** (Most people in daily life, and many old books about the mind; *rejected*)  
  For: Everything seems to happen at once. When a ball hits a wall, the sight and the thump seem to come together.  
  Against: Every signal needs time to travel along nerves, and the brain needs more time to sort it. Reaction times show a delay of about a fifth of a second. The brain also adjusts the timing so that sight and sound seem to match.
- **Pain is a direct measure of how much your body is damaged.** (A common belief, held by many people and in many old books; *rejected*)  
  For: A big cut usually hurts more than a small scratch, so pain and damage seem to go together.  
  Against: Soldiers with bad wounds have sometimes felt almost nothing in the moment. A paper cut can hurt a lot. People with phantom limbs feel pain from a limb that is gone. The brain makes pain from danger signals, memory and the situation.
- **You are born with the senses you have, and they cannot get much better.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Receptors are made by the body, and nobody can add a new kind of receptor by practice.  
  Against: Practice changes the brain's use of the signals. Tea tasters in Assam, Darjeeling and the Nilgiris train for years to tell tiny differences between leaves. Perfumers and musicians do the same in their own senses. The receptors are the same, but the brain's reading gets finer.
- **Humans have no sense of the Earth's magnetic field.** (Most scientists today, and many books; *debated*)  
  For: No one has found a human magnetic organ. No person has been shown to use the field to find their way.  
  Against: In 2019 a team led by Connie Wang and Joseph Kirschvink reported that some people's brain waves changed when the magnetic field around them was turned in a certain way. The people did not notice anything, and other scientists want the test repeated.
- **An animal with more kinds of colour receptor sees finer colours.** (A common assumption after the mantis shrimp's receptors were counted; *rejected*)  
  For: The mantis shrimp has 12 to 16 kinds of receptor, and we have three. It seems that more should mean more colours.  
  Against: In 2014 a team led by Hanne Thoen tested mantis shrimps and found that they could not tell close colours apart as well as people can. Maybe they read colour in a quick, simple way.

## Roots
We have followed the senses from stimulus to nerve pulse to brain, and found that everything arrives late, and arrives as pulses. Now we ask what this says about who is sensing, and about the world.

**Deep truth.** Your fingers closed on the ruler about a fifth of a second after it began to fall. The light reached your eye almost at once. The delay happened inside you. So everything you sense has already happened, and your 'now' is built from slightly old signals.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think we meet the world as it is at this very moment. Every sense needs time to turn the world into pulses, carry them and sort them, so we live a little behind it.  
*Evidence:* In the Ruler Drop test, the ruler fell about 15 to 20 centimetres before your fingers closed. At the speed a ruler falls, that is about 0.17 to 0.2 seconds.

- *who am i:* The 'you' who is aware of this moment is always a fraction of a second behind the world, and you still feel that you are right here.
- *what is life:* A Venus flytrap counts two touches before she shuts. A robin senses the Earth's magnetism. Every living being turns the world into pulses, then acts on them.
- *what is this world:* A light, a sound and a smell are very different in the world. Inside a body, all three become the same kind of pulse. The world we know is made where the world meets a body.

Light, sound, smell and pressure are very different things out in the world. Once they pass through a sense they are all the same: pulses on a thread. A bee, a shark and a child each turn the same world into different pulses, and each is right about what they detect. The world and the body that senses it are not two separate things. Each experience is made from both together, and made a moment late. If everything is one awareness, then the sensing is that awareness meeting itself, slowly, through many different bodies.

- **Kena Upanishad 1.2:** The Upanishad asks what is behind the senses. It speaks of the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind and the eye of the eye. Something hears through the ear and sees through the eye, and cannot itself be heard or seen. (Science finds that the ear and the eye only send pulses to the brain. The question of who is aware of those pulses is the same question the Upanishad asks.)
- **Sabba Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 35.23:** The Buddha taught that what we can know as 'the all' is the eye and what it sees, the ear and what it hears, the nose, the tongue, the body and the mind, each with what it meets. (This matches what the science of the senses finds. All we know of the world has come through the senses and the brain's work on their signals.)
- **Plato, Republic, Book 7 (the cave):** Plato, a Greek thinker, told of prisoners who see only shadows on a wall and take them for the whole of reality. One prisoner turns round and learns that the shadows were made by something else. (Our brain also meets the world through signals and builds a picture. The picture is useful and real as an experience, and it is not the thing itself.)
- Science (settled): Every sense turns the world into nerve pulses of the same size, and every signal takes time to arrive.
- Science (debated): Perception is mostly the brain's prediction, corrected by the senses. Scientists argue about how far this idea goes.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India, and many modern philosophers called direct realists: Perception is the surest way of knowing. What you see and touch is simply there, and talk of a hidden world behind the senses is not needed. A delay of a fifth of a second does not stop you meeting the real mango.
- Practice, Catch the sound that has already happened (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Breathe in slowly three times. Now listen. Every time you notice a sound, whisper the word 'now'. Then ask yourself something. When you whispered, was the sound already finished? Keep listening for one minute, and notice how each sound arrives just after it was made.
- Ask: If your 'now' is always a little late, what do you mean when you say 'right now'?
- Ask: Who is the one that notices the pulses? Is it the eye, the brain, or something else?
- Ask: If a bee, a shark and a child each meet the same fruit with different pulses, is there a fruit that is none of theirs?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your 'now' is built from signals that are already about a fifth of a second old. Do you ever live in the present? (hint: Think of the falling ruler. Then think of starlight that left a star long ago.)
- *big:* Your senses are best at noticing change. What would the world feel like if nothing ever changed? (hint: Think of a smell that fades, and a watch you forget you are wearing.)
- *deep:* Pain is made in the brain, but it feels like it is in your toe. So where is the pain? (hint: Think of a person who feels an itch in a hand that is gone.)
- *deep:* If the brain is always guessing, how can it tell when a guess is wrong? (hint: Think of stumbling on a step that was not there, or a rubber hand that stops feeling like yours.)
- *big:* Should a sense show what is true, or what is useful? A bee's colours help her find flowers. Are they true? (hint: Think of a fruit that is red to you and dark to a bee. Which one is the real fruit?)
- *big:* A shark feels the electricity of a hidden fish. Is electricity part of the shark's world only, or is it in our world too, where we cannot notice it? (hint: Think of what a robin does with the Earth's magnetism, which is all around you right now.)
- *big:* Scientists tried a belt that buzzes towards north. After weeks, wearers said their sense of space had changed. If you gained a new sense, would you be the same person? (hint: Think about how a sense becomes part of how you think, not only part of what you detect.)
- *big:* Maskelyne sent away an assistant for timing a star 0.8 seconds later than him. Who was right, and how could they have found out? (hint: Think of how each person's reaction time is different, and how you could measure it.)

## Story: The Honest First Minute
*Devika, Saraswathi Ammamma, Mr. Balaji and the warehouse are made up. Mattancherry in Kochi really is an old spice-trading quarter. The science in the story is real. A smell that stays the same fades from notice, and the nose recovers after a few minutes in fresh air. Damp black pepper really can grow mould.*

Devika went to her grandmother's spice warehouse every day after school. It stood in Mattancherry, near the old harbour in Kochi, in a lane where the air always smelled of pepper, dried ginger and sea salt.

Her grandmother, Saraswathi Ammamma, had run the warehouse for forty years. People said she had the best nose in Kochi. She could sniff a handful of cardamom and tell you which hill it came from. Traders drove all the way from Coimbatore just to ask her opinion.

On this Thursday a lorry stood outside the door. Forty sacks of black pepper had to be loaded by six o'clock for a buyer from Coimbatore, Mr. Balaji. He was a large, impatient man who kept tapping his watch.

'Everything is checked,' said Ammamma, wiping her hands on her sari. She had been inside since six that morning, weighing sacks, sniffing samples and scolding the boys who carried them. 'Load them.'

Devika dropped her school bag by the door. At once, her nose wrinkled.

'Ammamma,' she said, 'something smells wrong in here.'

'It smells of pepper,' said Ammamma. 'This is a pepper warehouse.'

'Not only pepper. There is another smell underneath. It is like a wet towel that someone forgot in a bag.'

Mr. Balaji snorted. 'A child's imagination, madam. I am a busy man.' Ammamma lifted her chin and breathed in, long and slow. She frowned. She smelled pepper, and nothing else. 'My nose is never wrong,' she said, a little sharply.

Devika's heart thumped. She did not want to say that her grandmother's nose was wrong. But she remembered something from science class that week. 'Ammamma, when I come out of the kitchen after Amma has fried onions, I cannot smell the onions any more. My teacher says the nose stops reporting a smell that stays the same. It only wakes up when something changes.'

'You have been standing in this smell since six o'clock this morning,' she went on, very quietly. 'I have been in it for one minute. My nose is awake. Yours is tired.'

Nobody in forty years had dared to say such a thing to Saraswathi Ammamma. Mr. Balaji began to laugh, and then stopped, because Ammamma did not. She was thinking. 'Come,' she said to Devika at last. 'We will test it. Mr. Balaji, you will have tea, and it is my treat.'

They walked out to the tea stall by the water. The breeze smelled of fish, diesel, hot tea and the jasmine in a passing woman's hair. Ammamma drank slowly, with her eyes half closed. Devika watched the clock on her wrist. Ten whole minutes passed.

Then they walked back to the warehouse. At the door, Ammamma stopped. Her eyes opened wide. 'Ah,' she whispered. Under the sharp, hot smell of pepper, there was a thin, cold, musty smell. It had been there all afternoon. She had simply stopped hearing it.

She followed it to the back corner by the wall. That morning's monsoon shower had leaked through a crack in the roof. Three sacks at the bottom were wet through. Pepper that stays damp grows mould within days, and mould spreads. A whole lorryload could have been ruined before it reached Coimbatore.

Mr. Balaji went pale. 'Those three would have gone on my lorry,' he said slowly. The boys carried the wet sacks into the sun to dry. The other thirty-seven were loaded, and the lorry left at half past six, with Mr. Balaji waving from the window.

That evening Ammamma sat on a stool and looked at Devika for a long time. 'Why did your nose find it, and mine did not?' she asked. 'Mine did not stop working. It was only tired of the same smell,' she added to herself, as if she were learning it by heart.

'From today,' she said, 'there are two rules in this warehouse. Before any lorry leaves, a nose that has just come in from outside sniffs the sacks first. And whoever has been inside all day goes out to the tea stall for ten minutes before deciding anything.'

'And who is the fresh nose?' asked Devika, trying not to smile. 'I come at four every day.' Ammamma laughed for the first time that evening. 'Then you are hired. Your wages are one packet of banana chips.' And that is how a girl of nine became the Honest First Minute of the best spice warehouse in Kochi.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a smell that stays the same fades from notice. Smell receptors send a burst of pulses at the start, then fewer and fewer. Ammamma's nose was not broken. It was tired of the pepper, which had been constant since morning. A change, like a new damp smell, is exactly what a nose reports, but only a nose that has been away or just arrived. The ten minutes at the tea stall gave her receptors time to recover, so the first sniff back was honest. Devika's fresh nose was the first honest nose of the day.

- Ask: Why could Devika smell the damp when her grandmother could not?
- Ask: Why did going to the tea stall help Ammamma's nose?
- Ask: Name something in your own home that you can no longer notice, but a visitor could.
- Ask: Why might a nose that fades on steady smells still be good to have?
- Ask: How could you test whether a nose has become tired of a smell?

### e1 · Ruler Drop: how long does a signal take? (45 min)
- **question:** When a friend drops a ruler, how far does it fall before you catch it? Is the distance the same when you see it start to fall, hear a word, or feel a tap?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will you catch the ruler soonest by seeing it, hearing a word or feeling a tap? Which will be slowest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same ruler, the same catcher and the same hand every time. The catcher's thumb and finger start at the 0 centimetre end, with the ruler hanging between them. The dropper waits a different time before each drop, so the catcher cannot guess. Each cue is tried 3 times as practice and then 7 times for the record. The rounds are done in one order, and then in the opposite order after the two people swap roles, so that tiredness or practice does not favour one cue. Only the cue changes, so any difference comes from the cue. With 2 to 4 children, give three jobs: catcher, dropper and recorder. Rotate so each child is the catcher once, and give each catcher their own table. Two catchers fit in 45 minutes, so let the others catch in a second sitting.
- **safety:** Use a light ruler, so a miss does not hurt anyone's foot. The catcher sits down. Do not throw the ruler. Take a short break if your eyes feel tired. Do not use a steel ruler.
- **record:** For each cue, write the seven counted catch distances. Cross out the three practice tries. Then find the middle value of the seven. Draw a bar for each cue using its middle value.
- **results table:** Try (counted) | Seeing: centimetres caught | Hearing: centimetres caught | Touch: centimetres caught

  *Parent does:* Show your child the ruler. Explain the three rounds. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both at the top of the results table.

### e2 · The Bottle Lift: how big must a change be to feel it? (35 min)
- **question:** How much heavier must a bottle be before you can tell which is heavier? Is the answer the same for a light bottle and for a heavy one?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will you need the same extra water for a heavy bottle as for a light bottle, or more, or less? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the bottles are the same kind, closed, and with the water hidden. The lifter is blindfolded, and lifts one bottle in each hand. The helper puts the heavier bottle in a random hand each time. Each comparison is tried 4 times. Only the weight of the standard changes, so any difference comes from that. With 2 to 4 children, one is the lifter, one fills and keeps the key, and one writes right or wrong. Swap jobs for each set. Each lifter has their own results table, because every hand is different.
- **safety:** Fill the bottles at the sink and wipe up any spills at once, because a wet floor is slippery. The lifter sits down while blindfolded.
- **record:** For each set, write the smallest extra water that the lifter always got right. Then work out the extra as a share of the standard, out of 100. Compare the two shares.
- **results table:** Set | Standard bottle (millilitres) | Smallest extra that I always got right (millilitres) | That extra as a share of the standard (out of 100)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bottles and the jug. Say that you will find the smallest extra weight they can feel, for a light bottle and for a heavy one. Write their guess and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* I will put some water in six bottles. We will find how much extra water a bottle needs before you can tell it is heavier. We will do it for a light bottle, and then for a heavy one. Will the heavy one need the same extra, more or less? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* How much extra water can you feel? Is it the same for a heavy bottle? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think a heavy bottle needs a bigger or smaller extra? (good: A heavy bottle needs more extra, because a small bit gets lost in a big weight.; It will be the same, because my hand is the same.; I think it needs less, because my arm works harder.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write it at the top of the table.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the first bottle with 100 millilitres of water. Fill five more with 110, 120, 130, 140 and 160 millilitres. Put a secret letter on each base. Write which letter is which on the helper's paper. Close all the caps tightly.  
  *Parent says:* Bottle A has 100 millilitres of water. This is the standard. The other five have a little more, from 110 up to 160. Put a secret letter on each base, so the lifter cannot tell. Close the caps tight.  
  *Child mode:* Fill bottle A with 100 millilitres. Fill the others a little more. Close the caps tight.
  - Note: The bottles must look the same and feel the same on the outside. The helper keeps the key and does not tell the lifter. If you have fewer than six bottles, use two or three and add water between rounds with the jug.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Blindfold the lifter and sit them down. Give them the standard in one hand and the bottle with 110 millilitres in the other. Ask which is heavier. Swap hands at random, and repeat 4 times. Then move on to 120, 130, 140 and 160.  
  *Parent says:* Hold this bottle in one hand and this one in the other. Which is heavier? Tell me the hand. Do not lift them high, just a little. Now again with the hands swapped, and another bottle.  
  *Child mode:* Hold two bottles. Say which is heavier. We swap hands and try again.
  - Note: Put the heavier bottle in the right hand on some tries and the left on others, in a mixed order, so a lifter cannot just guess the same hand. Write R for right and W for wrong. The smallest extra that the lifter always got right, together with the next one up, is the answer. If they get 110 right every time, try 105 and 102 as well.
  - Check: Why does the helper put the heavier bottle in a different hand each time? → **So the lifter cannot get it right by always picking the same hand**
- **s4 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Empty the bottles. Fill the standard with 400 millilitres. Fill the others with 440, 480, 520, 560 and 640 millilitres. Put new secret letters. Repeat the lifting test in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now the heavy set. The standard has 400 millilitres. The others have 40, 80, 120, 160 and 240 more. These steps are four times bigger than before, so we can compare fairly. Lift and tell me which is heavier.  
  *Child mode:* Fill A with 400 millilitres. Fill the others with more. Do the same test.
  - Note: The extras are four times the light-set extras, so each is the same share of its standard. The standard has four times as much water. The empty bottle also has a small weight, so the shares will not match exactly. Wipe any spilled water. If the lifter always gets 440 right, try 420 and 410 as well. All the numbers here are typical, not promised, and a few lifters will feel much smaller or larger steps.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each set, find the smallest extra that was always right. Divide it by the standard and multiply by 100 to get the share. Ask your child for their claim, their evidence and their reasoning before you explain anything.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the smallest extra you always got right in each set. Divide that extra by the standard bottle. Multiply by 100 to get a share out of 100. What do you notice? What is your claim? What is your evidence? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Find the smallest extra you got right each time. Compare the two sets.
  - Ask: Was the extra the same in both sets? Was the share the same? (good: The extra in millilitres was bigger for the heavy set, but the share was about the same.; The heavy set needed 4 times as much water.; The share out of 100 was about 10 to 20 in both.)
  - Note: Typical result: the lifter needs an extra of about 10 to 20 millilitres for the light set and about 40 to 80 for the heavy set. The share is about 10 to 20 out of 100 for both. If your results differ, write them as they are and, if you can, test again. Do not explain Weber's law yet. Let the child say what they found.
  - Check: A lifter needs an extra of 20 millilitres on a 100 millilitre bottle. About how much extra would they need on a 400 millilitre bottle if the share stays the same? → **About 80 millilitres**

### a1 · The Tired Nose: how fast does a smell fade, and how fast does the nose wake up? (25 min)

Keep the katori away from small brothers and sisters, who may put it in their mouths.

  *Parent does:* Show your child the katori of spices and the orange peel. Ask for a guess and a reason about how long the smell will last. Write the guess on the table.

  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A common idea is that the smell runs out. The test can show that it does not.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the katori about 10 centimetres from your child's nose and start the timer. Your child breathes normally and raises a hand when the smell is almost gone. Write the number of seconds.

  - Check: The smell has almost gone. The cloves are still in the katori. What has changed? → **The nose is reporting the smell less**
- **s3 (8 min)**

  *Child mode:* Rest at the window. Smell the spice again after a while. Give it a score.
  - Note: Typical, not promised: the smell is weak after 30 seconds, stronger after 1 minute and nearly full after 3 minutes. The nose recovers in a few minutes. The numbers may vary, so write what you find.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the first smell fade again. Right after it fades, hold the orange peel to the nose and score the orange smell from 0 to 5.

  *Child mode:* Fade the spice again. Then smell the orange. How strong is it?
  - Note: Expected: the orange smells strong straight away. The nose adapts to one smell, not to every smell. If the orange also seems weak, the child may simply be tired. Try again after a rest.
  - Check: The orange smells strong right after the spice smell faded. What does that suggest? → **Smell receptors adapt to one smell at a time**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning. Then ask them where in their home they have stopped noticing a smell, and who could be a fresh nose.  
  *Parent says:* What is your claim about a smell that stays the same? What did you measure? Why do you think it happened? Where at home might you have stopped noticing a smell?  
  *Child mode:* What happened to the smell? What did you measure? Why did it happen?
  - Ask: Why might it be useful for the nose to fade a steady smell? (good: It lets the nose notice new smells.; It saves effort.; I would not want to smell my own clothes all day.)
### a2 · The Rubber Hand: can your eyes fool your skin? (25 min)
- **safety:** Only soft brushes and a rubber glove are used. If anyone has a latex allergy, use a cloth or non-latex glove. The grown-up must not hit the glove hard. Only a gentle tap with a pencil, if the child agrees, is allowed. Stop if the child feels uneasy. The feeling is harmless and goes away within a few seconds. With several children, only one child sits behind the screen at a time.
- **record:** Write a score from 0 to 5 for 'how much the rubber hand feels like mine' after the matched round and after the out-of-time round. Write what you felt.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the glove, the screen and the brushes. Explain that you will stroke a hidden hand and a rubber hand. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Your real hand will be behind this screen. You will look at this rubber hand while I stroke both with a brush. What do you think you will feel? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will look at a rubber hand. A brush strokes it and your real hand. What will you feel?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen to the feeling of the stroke on your hidden hand? (good: I will feel it in my real hand only.; I might feel it in the rubber hand.; I do not know.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children say they will feel only their real hand. It is fine to be surprised.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit your child at the table. Rest their left hand and forearm behind the screen, out of sight. Put the glove about 15 centimetres to the side of where the hidden hand is. Cover the glove's wrist and the child's sleeve with the cloth so it looks joined.  
  *Parent says:* Rest your left hand behind the screen. Keep looking at the rubber hand in front. Try not to look at anything else. I will cover the wrist so the hand looks joined to your arm.  
  *Child mode:* Put your hand behind the screen. Look at the rubber hand. Keep your eyes on it.
  - Note: The child's real hand and the glove must be in the same position, both with palms down. The child must not see their own hand. Put the glove close enough that it looks like the child's own.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take a brush in each hand. Stroke the hidden index finger and the same glove finger at the same moment, once each second, for two minutes. Ask 'where do you feel the touch?' at 30 seconds and 2 minutes. Score from 0 to 5.  
  *Parent says:* Keep looking at the rubber hand. I will stroke your hidden finger and the rubber finger together. Where do you feel the touch? How much does the rubber hand feel like yours, from nothing to very much?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the rubber hand. Say where you feel the touch. Give a score.
  - Note: The two brushes must move in step, at the same speed and the same place. Most people, but not all, start to feel the touch in the rubber hand within about a minute. If your child feels nothing, that is a normal result, so write down 0. About 6 to 8 in 10 people feel it, and it may take longer than a minute. With 2 to 4 children, one is the subject, two stroke (one brush each, practising to stay in step), and one keeps the clock and scores. Rotate. For a measurement, before and after each round, the subject shuts their eyes and slides the other index finger along the table edge to touch their hidden finger. Mark the spot with tape and measure how far it moved toward the rubber hand.
  - Check: Your child says the rubber hand 'feels like mine'. What is the best explanation? → **The brain matched what it saw with what it felt, and guessed that the rubber hand is part of the body**
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Rest for a minute. Now stroke the hidden finger and the glove's finger at different times, for two minutes. Score again. Then, if your child agrees, tap the glove gently with a pencil and watch for a flinch.  
  *Parent says:* Now I will stroke them out of step. Keep looking at the rubber hand. What do you feel this time? Give a score. At the end, I may tap the glove once. Tell me if you would rather I did not.  
  *Child mode:* Now the strokes are out of step. Watch the glove. Give a new score.
  - Note: Out-of-time strokes usually give a much lower score, which shows that the matching in time is what makes the feeling. If the child jumps when the glove is tapped, the brain treated the glove as a part of the body. Never hit the glove hard.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning. Then ask how this connects to a person who has lost a hand and still feels it.  
  *Parent says:* What is your claim? What did you feel in each round? Why do you think matched strokes made the difference? What does this tell you about how the brain decides which hand is yours?  
  *Child mode:* Which round felt most like your hand? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What does this tell you about how the brain decides which body parts belong to you? (good: It uses what it sees and what it feels together.; It makes a best guess.; The feeling of owning a hand can be changed.)
### a3 · Bouba or Kiki: do sounds have shapes? (20 min)
- **record:** Tally how many people said 'bouba' is the round shape and how many said 'bouba' is the spiky shape. Work out how many in every 10 chose round.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child that two made-up words, 'bouba' and 'kiki', are names for two shapes. Ask for a guess and a reason about which word goes with which shape.

  *Child mode:* Two made-up words. Bouba and kiki. Which shape is bouba? Why?
  - Ask: What makes you pick that word for that shape? (good: Bouba sounds soft and round.; Kiki sounds sharp and pointy.; I say it with my mouth and it feels that way.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children say that 'bouba' is the round one. Do not tell the child that this is what most people say until the test is over.

  *Child mode:* Draw a soft round shape. Draw a spiky shape. Make them the same size.
  - Note: Keep the shapes clear and simple. If one is much bigger, people may pick by size.
- **s3 (8 min)**

If you cannot find 10 people, use a phone call or ask over two days, because fewer than 10 tell very little.

  *Parent does:* Add up the tally. Work out how many people in every 10 said that 'bouba' is the round shape. Compare with your guess.

  *Child mode:* Count the ticks. How many said bouba is round? Did most agree?
  - Ask: What would it mean if most people picked round for 'bouba'? (good: Sounds and shapes seem to match for many people.; It is not random.; Different senses are linked in the brain.)
  - Note: Expected: most people, in many languages, say bouba is round. A few will pick the other way, and that is fine. Scientists found much the same in the USA and in India in 2001. No one knows exactly why. One idea is that the mouth shape for 'bouba' is round and soft, and for 'kiki' is sharp and tight.

### p1 · The Reaction Ruler: build a ruler that reads time, and hold a three-day family league (55 min, 3 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the cardboard strip with scissors. The catcher sits down and the ruler is light, so a miss hurts no one.
- **record:** A league table with each person's middle value for each of the three tests, in centimetres and in seconds. A short note on what you found.
- **schedule:** day 1: Make the Reaction Ruler. Test each family member in the morning, 7 catches each. (25 min); day 2: Test the same people after lunch or in the evening. Do 7 catches each. (12 min); day 3: Test again while each person counts backwards from 100 in sevens. Draw the league table and write your findings. (18 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the cardboard strip. Explain that it will show reaction time in seconds, after the distance guide. Ask for a guess about what changes the time.  
  *Parent says:* We will make a ruler that shows your reaction time directly. Then we will test the whole family. Do you think a person is faster in the morning or in the evening? Is a person slower when their mind is busy? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We make a ruler that shows time. Who is fastest? Does time of day matter? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think might make a person faster or slower? (good: Being sleepy makes you slower.; Being busy thinking about something else makes you slower.; Practice makes you faster.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mark the cardboard strip at the bottom as 0. Use the ruler to make marks every 5 centimetres. Write the times beside the marks. 5 cm is 0.10 seconds. 10 cm is 0.14. 15 cm is 0.17. 20 cm is 0.20. 25 cm is 0.23. 30 cm is 0.25.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a line at the bottom for zero. Mark every 5 centimetres going up. Write the time beside each mark. A thing that falls a short way took a short time. A thing that falls further took longer.  
  *Child mode:* Mark the strip every 5 centimetres. Write the time beside each mark.
  - Note: These times come from the way things fall: the time for a fall of d metres is the square root of 2d divided by 9.8, rounded. You do not need to explain this yet. The time gaps between the marks get smaller further up the strip, which shows that the time grows more slowly than the distance.
  - Check: On the strip, the gap between 0.10 and 0.14 seconds is 5 centimetres, but the gap between 0.23 and 0.25 seconds is also 5 centimetres. What does that tell you? → **A falling ruler speeds up, so each extra centimetre takes less time further down**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 1, test each family member in the morning. The dropper holds the strip at the top. The catcher keeps thumb and finger open at the 0 end. Do 3 practice tries and then 7 counted tries. Write the middle value.  
  *Parent says:* Rest your arm on the table. Watch the strip. I will drop it without warning. Catch it as fast as you can. Seven counted tries. We will take the middle one.  
  *Child mode:* Rest your arm. Watch the strip. Catch it when it drops.
  - Note: Test everyone at about the same time of day. Use the same method as in the Ruler Drop test. Write each person's middle value in centimetres and then read the time off the strip. If the cardboard strip flutters, fold it along its length or tape a second strip behind it. With 2 to 4 children, one drops, one writes and the rest catch in turn.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, test everyone again, but at a different time of day, for example after lunch or in the evening. Use the same method. Write the middle values.  
  *Parent says:* Today we test again at a different time of day. Same method, same rules. Are you faster or slower than yesterday? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Test again at a new time of day. Are you faster or slower?
  - Note: Results may be close, or noisy. If the numbers are about the same, that is a real result. Tiredness, hunger and mood can change reaction time, but only by a small amount.
  - Wait: Do this on day 2, at a different time of day from day 1.
- **s5 (18 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3, test everyone while they count backwards from 100 in sevens. Then draw the league table and ask for the claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Today you must catch the strip while counting backwards from 100 in sevens, out loud. What do you think will happen to your time? Then we will look at all three tests together and see who was fastest, and what changed.  
  *Child mode:* Count backwards from 100 in sevens. Catch the strip too. Is it harder?
  - Ask: Why might a busy mind slow down a reaction? (good: The brain has to do two jobs at once.; The decision to move waits for the counting.; Attention is shared and not all of it goes to the strip.)
  - Note: Expected: most people are slower when distracted, often by a few centimetres. Draw the league table with a row for each person and a column for each test. Do not turn it into a contest about who is best, because reaction times change with the day. If counting back in sevens is too hard for your child, use threes. It should be hard but possible.
  - Check: A person catches the strip at 14 cm normally and 19 cm while counting backwards. About how much longer was the second time? → **About 0.03 seconds longer, from about 0.17 to 0.20 seconds**
  - Wait: Do this on day 3.

### c1 · Pulse Talk: send a message using only touch (40 min)
- **problem:** Send the five-word message 'PLEASE BRING ME A BOOK' from one person to another using only taps on the back of the hand. The receiver has their eyes shut and their ears covered. Get all the letters right in under six minutes.
- **safety:** Do not push cotton wool or earplugs deep into the ear. Hold soft ear covers or earmuffs over the ears instead. Taps stay gentle.
- **your turn:** Design your own code on paper. Test it on a short word like 'TEA'. Change one thing, such as how long you pause. Then send the whole message and time it. Which design was fastest with no mistakes? Why do you think it worked?
- **constraints:** No speaking, no humming, no sounds and no signs the receiver could see.; Only taps, pressure, or strokes on the back of the receiver's hand may be used.; The receiver must write down every letter they think they received.; You may agree on your code beforehand, but you cannot change it once the message has started.; All taps must be gentle, so nobody is hurt.
- **general:** A tap code that counts taps for each letter, one tap for A, two for B, and so on. It is simple and slow.; A grid code: write the alphabet in a 5 by 5 grid, and tap the row number, pause, then the column number for each letter.; Short and long taps like Morse code, with a pause between letters and a longer pause between words.; A code that only taps for the most common letters, and spells the rest out.
- **creative:** Use six fingers or six spots on the hand as the six dots of a Braille cell, and tap the dots that make up each letter.; Use how hard the tap is as part of the code, soft and hard, so each tap carries two kinds of information.; Agree on short signs for common words like 'please', 'a' and 'me', to cut the time down.; Use pairs of taps in a rhythm, so the receiver recognises rhythms and not only counts taps.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Look at the constraints. Ask your child to draw two or three different code ideas on paper.  
  *Parent says:* You have to send a whole message with only taps on a hand. How could you turn letters into taps? Draw some ideas before you try them.  
  *Child mode:* Send letters with taps only. Draw two ideas for a code.
  - Ask: What can a tap change to carry more information? (good: How many taps.; How long the pause is.; How hard the tap is.; Which finger or spot is tapped.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose one code. Write it on paper. Practise sending the word 'TEA' and the word 'BOOK'. Swap roles and practise again.  
  *Parent says:* Choose the code you want to try. Write it down so you both know it. Then practise on the words 'TEA' and 'BOOK'. Swap roles so both of you can send and receive.  
  *Child mode:* Pick one code. Write it down. Practise a short word. Then swap roles.
  - Note: Practise with the receiver's eyes shut and ears covered, so the practice is as hard as the real test. If the first code feels too slow, do not give up. Slowness is a clue about what to change.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Send the full message. Start the timer. The receiver writes every letter. When it ends, check the letters and the time.  
  *Parent says:* Now send PLEASE BRING ME A BOOK. The receiver writes down every letter. Keep your taps gentle and steady. When you finish, we will check the time and the mistakes.  
  *Child mode:* Send the whole message. Receiver, write each letter. Check the time.
  - Note: Count the number of wrong letters as well as the time. A fast message with many mistakes is not a success. Try to find where the mistakes started: was it a pause, a missed tap or a mix-up in the code?
  - Check: Why does a code that uses only taps still work, even though a tap carries so little information? → **Patterns of taps in time and place can stand for many different letters**
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Change one thing in your code. Send the message again, or a new five-word message, and compare the time and the number of mistakes.  
  *Parent says:* Pick one thing to change. Maybe the length of the pause, or the order of the code. Send again. Is it faster or slower? Are there more or fewer mistakes?  
  *Child mode:* Change one thing. Send again. Was it faster? Were there fewer mistakes?
  - Ask: What did you change, and why did it help or not help? (good: A shorter pause made it faster but caused more mistakes.; Using the grid code made fewer mistakes.; The fastest code was not the clearest one.)
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child which code worked best. Then ask how it is like the way senses send pulses to the brain.  
  *Parent says:* Which code was the best? How is sending a message with taps like the way your skin sends signals to your brain? What is the same? What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Which code worked best? How is it like a nerve sending pulses?
  - Ask: How is a tap code like the way nerves send messages? (good: Both use many pulses of the same size.; Both use timing and place to carry meaning.; A receiver has to decode the pulses.)

## Worksheets
### Signals, Delays and Tired Senses (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A harder press on your skin makes a touch nerve do what?  
   Options: Send bigger pulses / Send more pulses each second / Send pulses that travel faster / Send a different kind of pulse  
   **Answer:** Send more pulses each second. *Why:* All nerve pulses are the same size. A stronger stimulus makes more of them each second. Picking 'bigger pulses' is a common idea, because we imagine a signal like a loud sound, but a pulse is either there or not there.
2. (fill) The smallest amount of a stimulus that a sense can notice is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['threshold']. *Why:* A threshold is the faintest amount that a sense can pick up. Anything below it goes unnoticed.
3. (truefalse) Light travels so fast that you can catch a dropped ruler with no delay inside your body. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The light reaches your eye almost at once, but the signal has to travel to the brain, a decision has to be made, and a signal has to travel to your hand. That takes about a fifth of a second.
4. (mcq) Which steps are part of your reaction time when you catch a falling ruler?  
   Options: Only the time the ruler takes to fall / The signal going to the brain, a decision, and the signal going out to the hand / Only the time your hand takes to close / Only the time light takes to reach your eye  
   **Answer:** The signal going to the brain, a decision, and the signal going out to the hand. *Why:* Your reaction time is the whole chain from the stimulus to the movement. A child who picks 'only the hand' forgets the signal travelling in and the deciding.

## Applications
- Hearing tests that find the softest sound each ear can notice, so that hearing problems are found early.
- Setting safe following distances for vehicles, because every driver has a reaction time during which the vehicle keeps moving.

- Adding a strong smell to cooking gas so that a leak is noticed even at very low amounts.

- Screens that change their brightness to the room's light, so that the eye's changes are comfortable.
- Tea tasting, spice grading and perfume making, where years of practice train the brain to read fine differences.
- Eye tests and hearing tests that measure thresholds with repeated questions and a middle value.

## Open questions
- **How does the brain make one steady 'now' from signals that arrive at different times?** Sight, sound and touch take different times to arrive and be sorted. Scientists have clues, such as the brain waiting briefly for the slowest signal, but nobody has a full account.
- **Why do some people feel the rubber hand as their own, and others do not?** The test works on most people but not all. Scientists are still studying what is different about the people it does not work on.
- **Can people sense the Earth's magnetic field without knowing it?** A 2019 test saw changes in brain waves when the field turned. Other scientists want it repeated, and no one knows if it changes what we do.
- **How exactly do birds sense magnetism?** One idea is a special molecule in the eye. Another is tiny iron crystals in the beak. Both have some evidence and neither is settled.
- **What is it like to feel the electric field of a hidden fish, as a shark does?** We can measure what the receptors detect. We cannot measure what the shark experiences, and nobody has a way yet.
- **How does a pattern of pulses in the brain become the feeling of pain, or the taste of a mango?** Scientists can follow the pulses a long way. Why they feel like anything is still an open question, sometimes called the hard problem.

About 10 centimetres is 0.14 seconds and 20 centimetres is 0.20 seconds. Is Farah right? **No. Her time was about 0.14 seconds and Meena's was about 0.20, so hers was not half as long.**. Half of 0.20 is 0.10. Farah's time was 0.14, which is more than 0.10. The distance was half, but the time was not, because a falling ruler speeds up. So equal steps in distance are not equal steps in time. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
- A touch signal goes from a toe to the brain, 1.2 metres, at 60 metres each second. A slow pain signal goes the same way at 1 metre each second. How long does each take? How many times as long is the slow one? **The touch signal takes 0.02 seconds. The slow pain signal takes 1.2 seconds. The slow one takes 60 times as long.**. Time is distance divided by speed. 1.2 divided by 60 is 0.02. 1.2 divided by 1 is 1.2. 1.2 divided by 0.02 is 60, which matches the 60 times difference in speed. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A person can just feel an extra 15 millilitres of water on a 100 millilitre bottle. If the share stays the same, how much extra will they need on a 400 millilitre bottle? What about a 1 litre bottle? **60 millilitres on the 400 millilitre bottle, and 150 millilitres on the 1 litre bottle.**. 15 out of 100 is the share. 400 is 4 times 100, so the extra is 4 times 15, which is 60. 1 litre is 1000 millilitres, which is 10 times 100, so the extra is 10 times 15, which is 150. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In a Bottle Lift test, a lifter needed an extra of 20 millilitres on a 100 millilitre bottle and an extra of 60 millilitres on a 400 millilitre bottle. Which extra was the bigger share of its standard? What does that suggest? **The light bottle needed 20 out of every 100 and the heavy bottle needed 15 out of every 100. The light bottle needed the bigger share, so this lifter was a little better with the heavy bottle.**. 20 divided by 100 is 0.20, or 20 out of every 100. 60 divided by 400 is 0.15, or 15 out of every 100. The shares are close, but not equal. Weber's law is only approximate, so small differences like this are normal. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- An auto goes at 36 kilometres each hour, which is 10 metres each second. The driver's reaction time is 0.25 seconds. How far does the auto go before the driver starts to brake? What if the auto goes twice as fast? **2.5 metres at 10 metres each second, and 5 metres at 20 metres each second.**. Distance is speed times time. 10 times 0.25 is 2.5. At twice the speed, 20 times 0.25 is 5. Twice as fast means twice as far before the driver even starts to brake. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L4`)
- Meena scored the smell of a spice from 0 to 5. Right after it faded, the score was 0. After resting 30 seconds the score was 1, after 60 seconds it was 2 and after 180 seconds it was 4. Her brother says the score goes up by the same amount each minute. Is he right? **No. In the first minute the score rose by 2. In the next two minutes it rose by only 2 in total, which is 1 each minute. The nose wakes up quickly at first and then more slowly.**. From 0 to 60 seconds the score went from 0 to 2, a rise of 2 in one minute. From 60 to 180 seconds it went from 2 to 4, a rise of only 1 each minute. So the recovery is faster at first and slower later. A line graph of these scores would bend and not be straight. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In a Bouba or Kiki test, 17 out of 20 people said that 'bouba' is the round shape. If people were just guessing, about how many out of 20 would you expect to say round? How many more than that said round? How many out of every 100 is that? **About 10 out of 20. 7 more than guessing. 17 out of 20 is 85 out of every 100.**. A random guess between two shapes would give about half, or 10 out of 20. 17 minus 10 is 7. 17 out of 20 is the same as 85 out of 100, because 20 times 5 is 100 and 17 times 5 is 85. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- Each eye sends about 1 000 000 nerve threads to the brain. A signal from the eye needs about 0.1 seconds to reach the brain. How many threads do two eyes send? If the brain gets 10 pulses each second from every thread, how many pulses arrive each second? **Two eyes send 2 000 000 threads. At 10 pulses each second, that is 20 000 000 pulses each second.**. 1 000 000 times 2 is 2 000 000. 2 000 000 times 10 is 20 000 000. The 0.1 second time is not needed for this answer. It is extra information. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)

## Warm-up
- Does a stronger touch make a nerve send bigger pulses or more pulses? → More pulses each second. All pulses are the same size.
- Roughly how long is the delay when you catch a falling ruler? → About a fifth of a second.
- Which nerve threads are faster: wrapped ones or unwrapped ones? → Wrapped ones, because of the myelin.
- What is a threshold? → The smallest amount of a stimulus that a sense can notice.
- If you can just feel 10 grams extra on a 100 gram bag, about how much on a 500 gram bag? → About 50 grams, the same share.
- Why does the smell of your own house fade? → Your smell receptors adapt to a steady smell.
- Does the brain have pain receptors in its own tissue? → No. The brain makes pain from danger signals from the body.
- Why can you not tickle yourself? → Your brain predicts your own touch and turns the feeling down.
- Name an animal sense that humans do not have. → A shark senses electric fields. A robin senses magnetism. A pit viper senses heat.
