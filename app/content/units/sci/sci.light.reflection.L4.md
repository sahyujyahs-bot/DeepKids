# Reflection: shiny and dull surfaces, and seeing round corners with mirrors
`sci.light.reflection.L4`

**Summary.** A white wall and a mirror both bounce back the light that lands on them, yet you see your face in only one of them. Breathe on a steel tumbler and your face vanishes at once, then returns as the fog clears. A polished plate can flash a spot of sunlight across a bay, while a dull pot cannot. In this unit your child maps where light goes after it hits a mirror, foil, paper and cloth. They find out how to turn a dull surface into a shiny one, and why a reflective strip on a school bag glows back at a torch. They build a periscope that sees over a wall, and learn that a wall and a mirror obey exactly the same rule.

**Intro.** *Hold up a steel tumbler and look at your face in the shiny side. Breathe on it. Your face vanishes into fog. Wait a few seconds. Your face comes back. The steel did not change. What did the fog do to the light?* Every surface sends back some of the light that lands on it. What is different is how tidily it does it. A mirror sends light back in neat order, so you see a picture. A wall sends it back in every direction, so you see the wall itself, from anywhere in the room. In this unit you find out why, and how polish, water and tape turn a dull surface into a shiny one. You also learn how two mirrors can send light round two corners, which is how a periscope lets you see over a wall.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that every surface reflects some light, and that a smooth surface sends it back in order while a rough surface scatters it in every direction.
1. I can map where light goes after it hits a mirror, foil, paper and cloth, and say which surfaces I could see the lit spot on from many places.
2. I can explain that a rough surface still obeys the rule of reflection at each of its tiny bumps.
3. I can turn a dull surface into a shiny one, and explain how polish, wax, water and tape smooth the bumps.
4. I can explain why every surface turns mirror-like when I look at it from a very low angle, and why wet roads glare at night.
5. I can build a periscope with two mirrors, and explain why it shows the picture upright and not turned round.
6. I can explain why two or three mirrors at right angles send light straight back, as in a cat's eye on a road.
7. I can say how a curved mirror gathers or spreads light, and where people use them.

## Terms
- **Reflection**: Reflection is light bouncing off a surface. Every surface reflects some of the light that lands on it. *e.g.* You see a book because light reflects off the book into your eyes.
- **Mirror-like reflection**: Mirror-like reflection is when a very smooth surface sends light back in neat order, all the rays going the same way together. It makes a clear image. *e.g.* A bathroom mirror and still water in a bowl give mirror-like reflection.
- **Scattered reflection**: Scattered reflection is when a rough surface sends light back in every direction. It makes no clear image, but lets you see the surface from anywhere. *e.g.* A white wall scatters light, so you can see the wall from any corner of the room.
- **Image**: An image is a picture that light makes of something, such as your face in a mirror. In a mirror, the image seems to be behind the glass. *e.g.* Your face in a mirror is an image made by light that bounced off you and then off the mirror.
- **Ray**: A ray is a thin line that shows the path of light. Light travels in straight lines until it hits something. *e.g.* A ray of sunlight through a gap in a curtain looks like a straight bright line in dusty air.
- **Surface**: A surface is the outside of a thing, where it meets the air or another thing. Reflection happens at the surface. *e.g.* The surface of a steel tumbler is smooth. The surface of a coconut shell is rough.
- **Matt**: A matt surface looks dull and does not show a clear picture of anything. Its tiny bumps scatter the light. *e.g.* Chalk, plain cloth and unpolished wood are matt.
- **Gloss**: A glossy surface is shiny. It sends back part of the light in an orderly way, so you can see bright reflections in it. *e.g.* A glossy magazine page shows a bright reflection of a lamp.
- **Polish**: To polish a surface is to rub it until the tiny bumps are smoothed down. A polished surface reflects more neatly and looks shinier. *e.g.* Polishing a brass lamp makes it shine.
- **Glare**: Glare is a very bright reflection that is hard to look at. It happens when light bounces off a smooth surface straight into your eyes. *e.g.* The Sun's glare off a wet road or a lake makes you squint.
- **Periscope**: A periscope is a tube with two mirrors that lets you see round a corner or over something. Light bounces off the top mirror, then off the lower mirror, into your eye. *e.g.* A submarine has a periscope to look above the water while the submarine stays below.
- **Retroreflector**: A retroreflector is a set of mirror surfaces at right angles that sends light straight back to where it came from. *e.g.* A bicycle reflector is a retroreflector. It glows back when car headlights hit it.
- **Concave mirror**: A concave mirror curves inward, like the inside of a spoon. It gathers light to a small bright spot. *e.g.* A solar cooker dish is a concave mirror that gathers sunlight onto a pot.
- **Convex mirror**: A convex mirror curves outward, like the back of a spoon. It spreads light out and shows a wide view, in a smaller size. *e.g.* A shop's corner mirror is convex, so the shopkeeper can see the whole shop.
- **Focus**: The focus of a mirror is the spot where it gathers rays together. A focus is very bright because the light is packed into a small space. *e.g.* A curved steel katori held in a torch beam gathers light into a bright spot on a card.
- **Optical fibre**: An optical fibre is a thin strand of glass that carries light along inside it, even round bends. The light bounces along the inside walls. *e.g.* Internet messages travel as flashes of light along optical fibres under the sea.
- **Reflecting telescope**: A reflecting telescope uses a big curved mirror, instead of a big glass lens, to gather the light from faraway things. *e.g.* Isaac Newton built a small reflecting telescope in 1668.
- **Beam**: A beam is a narrow stream of light going in one direction. A torch with a slit, or a laser, makes a beam. *e.g.* A torch through a card with a thin slit makes a narrow beam you can follow across a table.
- **Albedo**: Albedo is the share of the light that lands on a surface that the surface sends back. A mirror has a high albedo, and black cloth has a low one. *e.g.* Fresh snow has a high albedo. Dark tar has a low one.
- **Solar cooker**: A solar cooker uses mirrors to gather sunlight onto a pot, to heat food without fuel. *e.g.* A box cooker with a shiny lid can bake rice on a sunny day in Bengaluru.

## Explanations
### Every surface reflects some light · *defined*
**Definition:** When light lands on any surface, some of it bounces off. The bounced light is what lets you see the surface.

A mirror is not special because it reflects. Everything reflects. A wall, a banana, a school bag and your own hand all send some of the light that lands on them into the room. That is the only reason you can see them. They make no light of their own. A thing that reflected nothing at all would be invisible, and you would see only what is behind it. So the real question about a surface is not whether it reflects, but how it does it. Some surfaces send the light back in neat order. Others scatter it in every direction.
- e.g. A dark cloth still reflects a little light, which is why you can see its folds.
- e.g. The Moon makes no light. It shines because it reflects sunlight.
- e.g. You see a friend across the room because light reflected off your friend reaches your eyes.
### Smooth surfaces and rough surfaces · *defined*
**Definition:** A very smooth surface sends light back in neat order and makes an image. A rough surface scatters light in all directions and makes no image, but can be seen from anywhere.

Picture a bundle of parallel rays landing on a mirror. They all meet a flat surface, so they all bounce off together, in the same new direction. Your eye, in the right place, collects the whole bundle and sees a picture. Now picture the same rays landing on white paper. Under a microscope, paper is a tangle of tiny fibres, with bumps and slopes facing every way. Each ray meets a differently tilted bump, so each bounces off in a different direction. The light is scattered. No picture forms, but light leaves the paper in every direction, so you can see the paper from any side of the room.
- e.g. A bathroom mirror and still water give a clear image of your face.
- e.g. A white wall scatters light, and looks the same from every corner of a room.
- e.g. A glossy magazine page does a bit of both: you can see a bright patch of lamp in it, and you can also read the page from any angle.
### The same rule works on every tiny bump · *defined*
**Definition:** Light leaves a flat surface at the same angle that it arrives. A rough surface is made of tiny flat patches tilted every way, and each patch follows the rule.

In Class 3, you learned that a ray leaves a flat mirror at the same angle that it arrives. This is the rule of reflection. A rough surface does not break the rule. Look at one tiny patch of a rough surface, so small that it is flat. On that patch, the ray leaves at the same angle it arrived, measured against that patch. But the next patch is tilted differently, so its ray goes off somewhere else. A wall is a huge number of tiny mirrors tilted every which way. A mirror is a huge number of tiny patches all lying flat together. The rule is the same. The only difference is how well the patches line up.
- e.g. Crumpled foil sparkles in every direction, because each little flat fold sends light its own way.
- e.g. A sheet of paper looks the same brightness from every side, because its bumps face every way.
- e.g. A smooth steel tumbler shows a clear face, because its surface is a long, even curve.
### How big a bump can be before it spoils the mirror · *defined*
**Definition:** A surface acts like a mirror when its bumps are much smaller than the wavelength of light, about half a thousandth of a millimetre.

Light is a wave. A wave cannot 'feel' a bump that is far smaller than the length of the wave itself. Visible light has a wavelength of about 0.0005 millimetres. If a surface's bumps are much smaller than that, the light sees the surface as flat, and the surface works like a mirror. If the bumps are bigger, light sees every bump and scatters. So the same material can be a mirror or a wall, depending on how finely it is polished. A mirror-makers' polish is smooth to a tiny fraction of a wavelength. Ordinary paper has bumps many times bigger than that, so it scatters.
- e.g. Rubbing a dull metal tray with fine polish makes it shine, because it wears down the bumps.
- e.g. The same sheet of glass can be clear and shiny, or frosted and dull if its surface is roughened.
- e.g. A very fine mirror for a telescope is polished so smooth that its bumps are tiny compared with a wavelength of light.
### How polish, wax, water and tape make a surface shine · *defined*
**Definition:** Polish, wax, water and clear tape all fill or flatten the tiny bumps on a surface, so the surface reflects in order and looks shinier.

Shoe polish and car wax fill the tiny dips in a surface with a smooth layer, and rubbing flattens the top. A thin film of water or oil does a similar thing, by filling the dips with a layer that is smooth on top. Clear tape lays a very flat skin over everything. In every case, the bumps are hidden under a smooth top, so the light meets a flat surface. This is why a wet pebble looks brighter and deeper in colour than a dry one, why a shoeshine man's work makes leather shine, and why fog on a mirror spoils it. Fog is thousands of tiny drops, each one a bump that scatters the light.
- e.g. A shoeshine man's polish makes a dull black shoe gleam.
- e.g. A wet stone looks shinier than a dry stone, because the water smooths the surface.
- e.g. Fog on a bathroom mirror hides your face, because the droplets scatter the light.
### Every surface turns into a mirror at a low enough angle · *defined*
**Definition:** At a very low, glancing angle, even a dull surface reflects most of the light in an orderly way and shows a mirror-like image.

Try this with a plain page. Hold it level, with a lamp on the far side of the room. Look straight down, and the page looks matt. Now lower your head until your eye is almost level with the page and look across it. The page now shows a blurry bright image of the lamp. At a very shallow angle, the light skims across the tiny bumps instead of hitting them, so they behave like a smooth surface. This is why wet roads glare at night. Water on the road smooths it, and the street lights and the car headlights hit it at a glancing angle. A calm lake looks like a mirror at sunset for the same reason. Polarising sunglasses cut down this glare.
- e.g. A wet road at night shows long streaks of street lights.
- e.g. A calm lake at sunrise shows the sky almost perfectly, though it is a poor mirror at noon.
- e.g. A tiled floor shows the ceiling light when you look across it from low down.
### How much light a surface sends back · *defined*
**Definition:** Every surface sends back only a share of the light that lands on it. The share is called its albedo. The rest is absorbed and turned into heat, or goes through.

A good mirror sends back about nine tenths of the light. Fresh white paper sends back most of it, about four fifths. Black cloth sends back only a small share, which is why it looks black and gets warm. The Moon is a good example. Its rock and dust are dark grey, and it sends back only about one eighth of the sunlight that lands on it. Yet that is enough to light up a whole night, because the Moon is huge and the light is scattered all round. Fresh snow has a high albedo, and so cools the ground below it. Dark forests and roads have a low albedo. The shares are rough numbers, because they change with the colour and the angle.
- e.g. White paper reflects most of the light and looks bright.
- e.g. Black cloth reflects very little, looks dark and gets warm in the sun.
- e.g. The Moon sends back only about one eighth of the sunlight that lands on it.
### Two bounces: the periscope · *defined*
**Definition:** A periscope uses two mirrors, each tilted at 45 degrees, to send light round two right-angle turns, so you can see over or round something.

A mirror tilted at 45 degrees turns a ray through a right angle. In a periscope, light from a scene goes into the top window, hits the top mirror and turns down the tube. At the bottom, it hits the second mirror and turns again, out through the viewing window to your eye. You see the scene as if you were looking from the top of the tube. Two mirrors turn the picture twice. The first turn flips the picture, and the second flips it back. So the picture is upright and the right way round. Submarines used periscopes to look above the waves while the boat stayed hidden. Soldiers in trenches used them to look over the top without raising their heads.
- e.g. A submarine periscope lets the crew see ships on the surface.
- e.g. A child with a cardboard periscope can see over a garden wall.
- e.g. A periscope in a long hall lets a person see round a pillar.
### Corner mirrors that send light straight back · *defined*
**Definition:** Two mirrors set at a right angle, or three mirrors set like the corner of a box, send light back parallel to the way it came in. Such a device is called a retroreflector.

A single mirror sends a beam off at an angle. Two mirrors at a right angle make the beam bounce twice, and it comes out parallel to its path in, going back toward the torch. Three mirrors at right angles, like the inside corner of a cardboard box, do this for a beam from any direction. That is why a bicycle reflector, a cat's eye on a road and the reflective strip on a school bag glow when a headlight shines on them, as seen from near the headlight. Left on the Moon by astronauts in 1969, a set of corner reflectors still sends laser flashes back to Earth. India's Chandrayaan-3 lander, in 2023, carried one made by NASA.
- e.g. A cat's eye in the middle of a highway glows back at the driver.
- e.g. A reflective strip on a school bag glows brightly in a torch beam, and can look dark in daylight.
- e.g. Astronauts left corner reflectors on the Moon, and scientists still bounce lasers off them to measure the distance to the Moon.
### Curved mirrors gather or spread light · *defined*
**Definition:** A concave mirror, curved inward, gathers parallel rays to a bright spot. A convex mirror, curved outward, spreads them and shows a wide view.

Every tiny patch of a curved mirror follows the rule of reflection, but the patches are tilted differently. On a concave mirror, the patches tilt so that the reflected rays cross at one spot, the focus. The light there is packed tight and bright. Solar cookers, torch reflectors and telescope mirrors use this. On a convex mirror, the patches tilt away from each other, so the rays spread out. You see a wide view, but small. Shop corner mirrors and the side mirrors of cars are convex. Never use a curved mirror to focus sunlight at your hand, a person or a pet. The bright spot can burn skin and start fires.
- e.g. The inside of a steel katori gathers a torch beam to a small spot, and the back spreads it out.
- e.g. Car headlights have a curved mirror behind the bulb, so most of the light goes forward in a beam.
- e.g. A convex shop mirror shows the whole aisle at once.
### Light trapped inside glass: optical fibres · *defined*
**Definition:** Light travelling along inside a thin glass strand bounces off the inside walls and stays in, even round bends. This is how an optical fibre carries a message.

When light inside glass hits the wall at a shallow angle, it is all reflected back in, and none escapes. This is called total internal reflection. Inside a thin strand of glass, the light keeps hitting the wall at shallow angles, so it zigzags along the whole length, bends and all. Narinder Singh Kapany, a physicist born in Punjab, did early work in the 1950s that helped to show that light could be carried this way, and he is remembered for naming the field fibre optics. Today, internet messages travel as flashes of light along fibres on the sea floor. Doctors use bundles of fibres to look inside the body.
- e.g. Internet cables under the sea are optical fibres, carrying flashes of light.
- e.g. Doctors can look inside the stomach with a thin tube of optical fibres.
- e.g. A light-up toy with glowing threads at the ends is made of optical fibres.
### Mirrors in living bodies · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings have mirrors in their bodies, to see, to hide or to shine.

A scallop has dozens of tiny eyes along the edge of their shell. In 1965, Michael Land, a British scientist, showed that each eye has a curved mirror at the back that gathers light to form an image. Many silvery fish, such as sardines, have tiny crystals in their scales that act as mirrors. In open water, these scales reflect the light around the fish, which makes the fish hard for hunters to see. Cats have a mirror layer behind the retina, which sends light back through the eye for a second chance to be caught. That is why a cat's eyes shine in a torch beam. Some beetles and butterflies have shining wings that are not coloured by paint. Their surface structure reflects only certain colours.
- e.g. A scallop's eyes use a curved mirror to make an image.
- e.g. A sardine's silver sides reflect the water around them.
- e.g. A cat's eyes glow green or yellow in a torch beam at night.
### Should we reflect sunlight away to cool the Earth? · *debated*
The Earth is warming because we are adding gases such as carbon dioxide that hold in heat. Some scientists have asked a bold question: could we send some sunlight back into space before it warms us? Ideas include painting roofs white, putting tiny shiny particles in the high air, and even huge mirrors in space. Each idea reflects some sunlight, so each would cool the Earth a little. But no one has tested the big ideas, and they carry risks nobody fully understands. People disagree about whether to even try them. Painting roofs and pavements white and light is the least risky, and it is already being done in some hot cities.
- e.g. Painting roofs white in a hot city can lower the temperature inside buildings.
- e.g. A volcano that throws dust high into the air can cool the Earth for a year or two, because the dust reflects sunlight.
- e.g. Fresh snow reflects most sunlight, so melting snow makes the Earth absorb more.
- **Scientists who want it studied:** The warming is serious and the cuts to carbon dioxide are slow. We should study reflecting some sunlight carefully, so that people have the facts if they ever need them.
- **Scientists who warn against it:** Reflecting sunlight would not remove the carbon dioxide. It might change rainfall, including the monsoon, in ways we cannot predict. And if it stopped suddenly, the Earth could warm quickly.
- **Many climate scientists:** The first job is to cut the gases that cause the warming. Reflecting sunlight, at the most, should come after that and be done only with every country having a say.

## Mostly agreed
- Every surface reflects some of the light that lands on it, and that reflected light is what lets us see things that make no light of their own. *How we know:* You can see unlit objects in a room with a lamp, and cameras record them. In a room with no light at all, nothing is seen.
- A smooth surface sends light back in order and makes an image. A rough surface scatters light in all directions. *How we know:* A mirror shows a clear image of a torch only from one place. White paper, lit by the same torch, can be seen from any place around it.
- Light follows the same rule of reflection on every flat patch of a surface, rough or smooth. *How we know:* Looking at a surface under a microscope, each tiny patch bounces a thin beam at the matching angle. Laser tests on rough and smooth surfaces agree with this.
- Polishing, waxing or wetting a surface can make it shinier, because it smooths the bumps. *How we know:* Measurements of surfaces before and after polishing show smoother surfaces, and the reflected light becomes more orderly.
- At a very low angle, even rough surfaces reflect most of the light in an orderly way. *How we know:* Wet roads, lakes and glossy paper all show mirror-like reflections when seen at a shallow angle, and the effect is measured in labs.
- Two mirrors at 45 degrees make a periscope that shows an upright picture, and corner mirrors at right angles send light straight back. *How we know:* Submarines, bicycle reflectors, cat's eyes and the reflectors left on the Moon all work this way, and anyone can test them with a torch.
- A concave mirror gathers light to a focus, and a convex mirror spreads it out. *How we know:* Telescopes with curved mirrors, solar cookers, car headlights and shop mirrors all work, and a katori and a torch show it at home.

## How we found out
**Isaac Newton, 1666 to 1671.** In the 1660s, telescopes used glass lenses. They had a problem. Stars and planets looked blurry, with coloured fringes. Many thinkers, including Descartes, believed this came from lenses not being ground to quite the right shape, and that better grinding would cure it. In 1666, Isaac Newton let a thin beam of sunlight through a glass prism. He found that white light is a mixture of colours, and that glass bends each colour by a different amount. So no lens shape could bring all the colours to one point. But a mirror bounces every colour at the same angle. In 1668 Newton built a small telescope with a curved metal mirror in place of the big lens. It showed no coloured fringes. He showed it to the Royal Society in London in 1671. Almost all the largest telescopes built since then use mirrors.  
*What people believed before:* Many thinkers believed that the coloured blur in telescopes came from badly shaped lenses, and that perfect grinding would cure it. Newton showed that glass bends colours by different amounts, so no shape can fix it, but a mirror treats all colours alike.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Mirrors reflect light, but walls and paper do not.** (Many people, because mirrors show pictures and walls do not.; *rejected*)  
  For: You see your face in a mirror but not in a white wall, so it feels as if only the mirror bounces light.  
  Against: You can see a white wall only because it reflects light into your eyes. A wall scatters the light in every direction instead of in one tidy direction. It reflects, but it does not make an image.
- **A rough surface breaks the rule that light bounces off at the same angle it arrives.** (Many students, who see light scatter off paper and think the rule has failed.; *rejected*)  
  For: On a rough surface the rays go off in all directions, not just one.  
  Against: The rule holds on every tiny flat patch. The patches face different ways, so the rays go in different directions. Nothing is broken. The surface is simply a huge number of tiny mirrors tilted every way.
- **A black thing reflects no light at all.** (Many people, because black things look so dark.; *rejected*)  
  For: A black cloth or black paint looks dark, so it seems to send nothing back.  
  Against: Black cloth still sends back a small share, which is why you can see its folds. Some modern black materials take in more than 99.9 parts in 100 of the light, but nothing takes in absolutely all of it.
- **A periscope turns the picture upside down or back to front.** (Some people, because they have seen pictures turned round in a spoon or a single mirror.; *rejected*)  
  For: A single mirror turns a picture back to front, and a lens can turn it upside down.  
  Against: A periscope uses two mirrors. The first mirror flips the picture and the second flips it back. The two flips cancel, so the picture comes out upright and the right way round.
- **Archimedes burned the Roman fleet at Syracuse using a ring of mirrors or shiny shields.** (Writers who lived hundreds of years after Archimedes, such as Lucian, wrote about it, and many people since have repeated it.; *debated*)  
  For: A big curved mirror can focus sunlight to a hot spot. Some modern tests have lit wood at short range after several minutes.  
  Against: The earliest writers who describe the siege do not mention it. Ships move, the Sun is not a point and the spot is hard to hold in place. Most historians doubt the story, though it remains a famous idea.
- **Telescopes with a big glass lens are the best way to see faint stars.** (Most astronomers before 1668, and many popular books since.; *rejected*)  
  For: The first telescopes used lenses, and lenses are easy to understand.  
  Against: A big lens bends different colours by different amounts, so images get coloured fringes. A mirror bounces all colours the same way. Almost every big telescope today is a reflecting telescope with a huge curved mirror.

## Roots
We found out that every surface reflects, and that the only difference is how tidily it does it. Now we ask what that tells us about seeing the world and ourselves.

**Deep truth.** A white wall and a mirror both bounce light off at the same angle. The wall is made of tiny bumps facing every way. So it sends light everywhere, and you see the wall itself.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'mirrors reflect' as if walls, paper and people did not. We only notice reflection when it makes a picture.  
*Evidence:* In the Where Does the Bounce Go test, the mirror showed its lit spot from one place only, and the white paper showed its spot from every place. Both followed the rule. If your results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* You see your friends only because light scatters off them into your eyes. A mirror is the only time you see your own face with tidy light.
- *what is life:* A scallop sees with tiny curved mirrors at the back of their eyes. A sardine hides by reflecting the water around her. Many living beings use reflection to see or to hide.
- *what is this world:* Almost everything you have ever seen was scattered light. The Moon, the page of a book and a friend's face are all lit by light that bounced off them.

A mirror and a wall do the same thing. Each tiny patch of a surface bounces light by the same rule. The patches of a mirror line up. The patches of a wall face every way. So a mirror shows one picture, and a wall shows itself to the whole room. Almost all that we see of the world is light scattered by rough surfaces. The tidy picture in a mirror is the rare case.

- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the net of Indra:** This Buddhist teaching describes a net hung over the world, with a bright jewel at each knot. Each jewel reflects every other jewel in the net, and each of those jewels reflects all the others again, without end. (Two mirrors facing each other make endless copies, and every bumpy patch of a wall bounces a little of the whole room. Each place holds a trace of all the others.)
- **Rumi, Masnavi, Book 1 (the Greek and the Chinese painters):** In a story told by Rumi, a Persian poet, painters from Greece and from China each decorated one side of a room. The Chinese painters used many colours. The Greek painters only polished their wall until it shone like a mirror. When the curtain between them was drawn back, the polished wall showed the Chinese painting, even more beautiful than before. (The Greek painters did not paint anything. They polished the bumps away until the wall could show everything in front of it. Polishing turned a wall that only scattered light into a surface that could hold a clear picture.)
- Science (settled): Light follows the same rule of reflection at every tiny patch of every surface. Smooth surfaces send the light back in order, and rough surfaces scatter it.
- Science (settled): A surface acts as a mirror when its bumps are much smaller than the wavelength of light, about half a thousandth of a millimetre.
- Science (debated): Whether we should reflect sunlight away from the Earth to cool it is debated, and no one has tested the big ideas.
- Other view, Many physicists: A mirror and a wall differ only in the size of their bumps. The tale of 'tidy light' and 'scattered light' is a way of talking about bump size. Nothing deeper about seeing or about oneself needs to be read into it. This is an honest view.
- Practice, Fog and clear (3 min): Take a clean steel tumbler, or a small mirror. Look at your eyes in it. Breathe in slowly. Breathe out onto the steel, and watch your face vanish into fog. Breathe in slowly again. Wait, and watch the fog fade. Your face comes back. Breathe out and in a few more times. Think about how the light was always there. Only the bumps changed.
- Ask: A white wall reflects as much light as a mirror, yet it shows no picture. Is that because the wall is hiding something, or because it shows everything at once?
- Ask: When you see your face in a mirror, who is looking at whom?
- Ask: If everything we see is scattered light, do we ever see a thing as it is, or only what it does to light?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A white wall reflects the light of a lamp, but it does not show your face. Where did the picture of your face go? (hint: Think about what happens to a bundle of rays that lands on a bumpy surface. Does it stay in order?)
- *deep:* The Moon reflects only about one eighth of the sunlight that lands on it, yet it lights up the night. If the Moon were a perfect mirror ball, would the night be brighter or darker? (hint: A mirror ball sends each ray in one direction. Imagine standing on the Earth and looking for the bright spot.)
- *deep:* You see your friends only by the light that scatters off them, and you see yourself in a mirror by tidy light. Is the face you see in a mirror the same face your friends see? (hint: A mirror turns front to back. Think about the photographs you have seen of yourself, and which feels more like you.)
- *deep:* If you could make a surface perfectly smooth, down to single atoms, would it look like anything at all? (hint: A perfect mirror shows only the things in front of it. Can you see the mirror itself?)
- *deep:* Light carries a little news from every surface it bounces off. Is the light in a room a kind of record of everything it has touched? (hint: The colour of a red apple is in the light that reaches your eye. What else is in it?)
- *big:* James Gregory designed a reflecting telescope in 1663, but could not make it work. Isaac Newton built one in 1668. Who invented the reflecting telescope? (hint: Think about the difference between an idea, a design and a working machine. Who should get the credit for each?)
- *big:* Reflectors left on the Moon in 1969 still send laser flashes back to us. Which of the things people make today might still be working in a thousand years? (hint: Think about what the reflectors have in common. They have no batteries, no moving parts and nothing to wear out.)

## Story: The Plate That Called the Shore
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. A smooth, polished surface sends a flash of sunlight in one direction, and a dull, scratched one scatters it. People at sea have long used mirrors to flash sunlight as a signal, and three short flashes, three long and three short is the well-known call for help. The Gulf of Mannar and Rameswaram are real places. Selvi, her family and everything they say and do are made up.*

Kalaivani Amma was one of the few women in Rameswaram who took her own boat out to sea. In the school holidays, she took her daughter Selvi, who was ten, and her son Murugan, who was six.

One afternoon in the Gulf of Mannar, the engine coughed twice and died. Kalaivani Amma pulled the starter cord until her arm ached. It would not start again. The sea was calm, but a slow current was pushing the boat away from the land.

Her phone was dead. She had forgotten to charge it. Selvi's torch was at home in her school bag. The shore was a thin brown line. The lighthouse was a pale pencil mark. The Sun was low and off to one side, turning the water orange.

'Can we shout?' asked Murugan.

'The shore is much too far,' said his mother. 'Nobody would hear us.'

Selvi looked at what they had. There was a coil of rope. There was a fish basket. There was an aluminium pot for making tea. There was a steel plate from lunch. She thought of her teacher, Miss Fernandez, who had once shown the class how a small mirror could throw a bright spot of sunlight across the playground. 'Light bounces off a smooth surface like a ball off a wall,' Miss Fernandez had said. 'It leaves at the same angle it arrives. So you can aim it.'

Selvi picked up the aluminium pot first. It was big, and she thought it would catch more sun. She turned it toward the Sun. The pot glowed a soft orange all over. She tilted it this way and that. The glow stayed soft and spread out. It lit up her own face, and the side of the boat. That was all.

'It shines nicely,' said her mother. 'But nobody on shore would notice it.'

Selvi turned the pot over and looked at the outside. It was scratched all over by years of use. There were hundreds of tiny dents and lines. 'Every dent bounces the light somewhere different,' she said slowly. 'So it sends light everywhere. And that means it sends it nowhere in particular.'

She put down the pot and picked up the steel plate. Kalaivani Amma scrubbed it every day with ash and a bit of tamarind, until it was smooth and bright. Selvi held it out toward the Sun. A round coin of dazzling white light leapt onto the wooden side of the boat. She tipped the plate a tiny bit. The spot of light jumped all the way across the boat and onto the water.

'It moves a lot when I move the plate a little,' she said. 'That is how a ball bounces. A tiny turn makes a big change.'

Now she had to aim. She remembered the trick Miss Fernandez had taught them. She held out her other hand with two fingers in a V, in front of her. She tilted the plate until the bright spot sat on the tip of one finger. Then she moved her whole hand until the V pointed straight at the white lighthouse on the shore. She slid the spot of light along her fingers, and then off the tip, and out along the line toward the land.

She could not see where the spot landed, a long way off. She only knew that it was going in the right direction. Murugan stood beside her and counted. She covered the plate with her palm and uncovered it, to make short flashes and long flashes. She made three short, then three long, then three short. It was the old call for help that Miss Fernandez had told them sailors used.

The Sun slid lower. Her arm ached. The flashes got fainter, because the Sun was low and the light that fell on the plate was thinner. Her mother took the plate and held it while Selvi rested her arm, and then Selvi took it back. They kept it going, three short, three long, three short.

Far to the north, a bigger fishing boat was heading home. At the front stood Mary Akka, a friend of Kalaivani Amma, in a red scarf. She saw something strange. It was a small white star, blinking low over the sea, at a time of day when no stars were out. Three short blinks, three long blinks, three short blinks. She stared at it for a moment. Then she shouted to her crew, and the big boat turned.

That evening, Kalaivani Amma and the children were towed back into Rameswaram harbour with the lamps coming on all along the shore.

The next day Miss Fernandez asked Selvi what she had done. 'I used the plate,' said Selvi. 'The plate is smooth. The pot is covered in tiny dents. A smooth surface bounces all the light the same way, so it can be aimed in one beam. A rough surface bounces it every which way, so it just glows.'

'And why does the same rule work on both?' asked Miss Fernandez.

Selvi thought about it. 'Because each dent is a tiny mirror too,' she said. 'It just faces the wrong way.'

**For the parent:** A smooth plate sends sunlight off in one direction, so it can be aimed far away. A scratched pot scatters the same sunlight everywhere, so it only glows.

- Ask: Why can a polished plate send a spot of sunlight far away, when a scratched pot cannot?
- Ask: Why does the spot of light move a long way when you tilt a mirror by a tiny amount?
- Ask: What would you check if your signal flashes seemed to be going the wrong way?
- Ask: Why would a dull, scratched pot still be useful for something else on the boat?

The glossy page shows an even glow from all marks, plus an extra bright flash at mark 4. If your results differ, write them down as they are. If the black cloth shows nothing from any mark, write 0 and say so. A brighter torch, used for every surface, helps. Do not change the distance for one surface only.
  - Check: You can see the lit spot on the white paper from all five marks, but on the mirror from only one. What is the best reason? → **Paper scatters the light in every direction, and the mirror sends it in one direction**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at your table. Sort the surfaces by how many marks saw a lit spot. Ask your child what the surfaces with the most marks have in common, and what the mirror does differently.  
  *Parent says:* The mirror showed the spot from one mark. The paper showed it from five. The same torch, the same beam. What is different about the surfaces?  
  *Child mode:* Sort the surfaces by how many marks saw the spot. What is different about the mirror?
  - Ask: Did the white paper bounce the light, or soak it up? (good: It bounced it, in every direction.; It scattered it.; It bounced it, because we could see the spot.)
  - Note: The key idea is that the paper reflects too. We see the lit spot on the paper only because paper reflects light into our eyes, from every direction. The mirror sends all of its light in one direction. They follow the same rule, but the bumps in paper face every way.
### e2 · Make a Dull Thing Shine: how does polish work? (30 min)
- **question:** Four strips of the same dull black paper are treated in different ways: left alone, wiped with a damp cloth, rubbed with wax and polished, and covered with clear tape. Which one shows the sharpest reflection of a torch, and which shows the fuzziest?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which strip will show the sharpest reflection of the torch, and which the fuzziest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The four strips are cut from the same sheet of black paper. The torch, the distance and the height of your eye stay the same. You look at each strip in the same way, from the same place. Only the treatment changes. The untouched strip is the control.
- **safety:** Do not light the candle. Use it only as a block of wax. A grown-up supervises the water and the scissors.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the four strips in a row, with a picture of the torch's reflection on each. Write which treatment made the strip shine most.
- **results table:** Strip | Reflection of the torch when I look from a low angle: sharp, fuzzy or none | Reflection when I look from a high angle: sharp, fuzzy or none
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the four strips and what will be done to each. Ask which will shine most and which least. Write the guesses and reasons. With 2 to 4 children, each makes and judges their own set of four strips, then you compare scores. With one child, the parent is the second judge.  
  *Parent says:* Strip A we leave alone. Strip B gets wiped with a wet cloth. Strip C gets rubbed with wax and polished. Strip D gets covered with clear tape. Which will show the best reflection of a torch? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Four strips of dull paper, four treatments. Which will shine best? Why?
  - Ask: Why might wax or tape make paper shine? (good: They make the paper smoother.; They fill the tiny bumps.; They make a shiny layer on top.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children think water will be best because wet things look shiny. Test it. Wet paper wrinkles, and the water soaks in, so it may help less than they expect.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Label the strips A, B, C and D. Leave A alone. Wipe B once with a damp cloth that is not dripping. Rub C hard with the wax block in one direction for one minute. Then polish C with a dry cloth. Smooth a strip of clear tape over D, with no bubbles.  
  *Parent says:* A is our control. We do nothing to it. B gets one wipe of damp cloth. C gets a minute of rubbing with wax, and then polishing. D gets clear tape, pressed down smooth.  
  *Child mode:* Leave A alone. Wipe B. Rub C with wax and polish. Cover D with tape.
  - Note: Press the tape down from one end to the other with your thumb so that no bubbles are trapped. Rub the wax in one direction only. The wax leaves a thin film that you polish with the cloth. Test strip B quickly, before it dries or wrinkles.
  - Check: Why do we leave strip A alone? → **So we can compare the treated strips with an untreated one**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Dim the room. Stand the torch on the table 40 centimetres from a strip, shining along the table. Lay strip A flat in front of you. Put your chin on the table and look across the strip toward the torch. Sit on the far side of the strip so the torch is straight ahead beyond it. Score each strip. Give 0 for none, 1 for a faint smear, 2 for fuzzy but clear, and 3 for a sharp torch shape. Write what the torch's reflection looks like. Repeat for B, C and D.  
  *Parent says:* Chin on the table, looking across the strip at the torch. Can you see the torch's reflection in the strip? Is it sharp, or is it a fuzzy smear? Write it down. Then the next strip.  
  *Child mode:* Put your chin on the table. Look across each strip at the torch. Is the reflection sharp or fuzzy?
  - Note: Expected: A shows a faint fuzzy smear. D, the taped strip, shows the sharpest reflection of the torch. C is usually better than A. B varies, and may be no better than A. If B looks as good as D, write that down. Do not look directly at the torch. If every strip looks the same, darken the room and lower your chin until it touches the table. The effect needs a very low angle.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now raise your head until you are looking down at each strip from about 30 centimetres above it. Look at each one again. Write whether the torch's reflection is sharp, fuzzy or gone. Then explain what the low angle did.  
  *Parent says:* Now look down at the strips from above. Which strips still show the torch? Which have lost it? What did lowering your head do?  
  *Child mode:* Raise your head. Look down at each strip. Which still show the torch?
  - Check: Strip A showed a faint reflection from a low angle and none from a high angle. What does this suggest? → **Even a dull surface acts more like a mirror when you look at it from a low angle**
### e3 · Send It Straight Back: two mirrors at a right angle (35 min)
- **question:** A thin beam of light hits two mirrors set at a right angle. Where does it come out, and does the answer change when the beam comes in at a different angle?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After bouncing off both mirrors, will the beam come out going the same way it went in, the opposite way, or somewhere else? Will it change when the beam arrives at a different angle? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The two mirrors stay fixed in the same place at a right angle. The slit torch lies flat on the paper each time. Only the angle of the beam changes. You try three angles, and then repeat with one mirror alone for comparison.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the slit and tapes the edges of the mirrors. Never shine a torch into anyone's eyes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the two mirrors and your three beams on the paper, with arrows to show the way in and the way out. Colour each in and out pair with the same colour.
- **results table:** Beam angle (about) | With two mirrors: did the beam leave parallel to the way it came in? | With one mirror alone: did the beam leave parallel to the way it came in?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the two mirrors, the slit torch and the reflective strip. Ask where the beam will go after bouncing off both mirrors. Write the guess and reason. Roles: one child holds the torch, one holds the mirrors steady, one marks the dots. With one child, the parent holds the mirrors and the child marks the dots. With 4 children, two pairs can each build a corner and compare.  
  *Parent says:* Two mirrors make a corner, like the inside of an open book. A thin beam goes into the corner. It bounces off one mirror, and then the other. Which way will it come out? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Two mirrors make a corner. A beam goes in. Which way will it come out? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the beam will come out that way? (good: It bounces off each mirror once.; It will go straight back.; It will go off to the side.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. The beam bounces twice, once on each mirror, and each bounce follows the rule that the angle out equals the angle in.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a line on the paper. Stand the two mirrors upright with clay, so they meet at a corner on the line, at a right angle. Use the corner of a book to check the angle. The shiny sides face each other.  
  *Parent says:* Like an open book standing up, with the shiny sides facing in. The corner of the book checks that the angle between the mirrors is a right angle.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the mirrors like an open book. Check the corner with a book. Shiny sides in.
  - Note: Press the clay firmly. The mirrors must not wobble. If the room is bright, close the curtains. The thin line of light is faint and needs a dark room. If the line is too faint, make the slit a little wider. To make the thin line: tape the slit card flat over the torch lamp. For a sharper line, stand a second slit card on the paper about 5 centimetres in front of the torch, and let the beam skim along the paper.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the torch flat on the paper, aimed at the corner at about 20 degrees to the first mirror. Mark two dots on the thin line going in. Mark two dots on the line coming out. Join the dots with a ruler. Repeat at about 45 degrees and 70 degrees. Then remove one mirror and try again with one mirror alone. Use the protractor to measure the angle between each pair of lines. It should be close to 0 degrees.  
  *Parent says:* Lay the torch down. Mark two dots on the beam going in. Mark two dots on the beam coming out. Join each pair with a ruler. How do the two lines compare? Do the same for a different angle.  
  *Child mode:* Mark the beam going in. Mark the beam coming out. Join the dots. How do the lines compare?
  - Note: Expected: with two mirrors at a right angle, the line out is parallel to the line in, and goes back the way it came, at all three angles. With one mirror alone, the beam goes off in a different direction every time. Tiny errors are normal. Check that the lines look parallel, not that they match exactly.
  - Check: With two mirrors at a right angle, the beam came out parallel to the way it went in, at all three angles. What does this show? → **Two right-angle mirrors send light back the way it came, whatever the angle**
- **s4 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put the reflective strip on a wall or bag. Make the room dark. Shine the torch at it from different angles. Hold the torch right beside your eye and look at the strip. Then step to the side, away from the torch, and look again. Try the same with a plain white paper.  
  *Parent says:* Shine the torch on the reflective strip. Hold it right beside your eye. What do you see? Now step aside and look from there. What changes? Try plain white paper too.  
  *Child mode:* Shine a torch at the strip. Look from beside the torch. Then step aside. What changes?
  - Note: Expected: the reflective strip glows brightly when you look from beside the torch, from almost any angle, and looks much dimmer from the side. White paper looks equally lit from both places. The strip is built from many tiny corner mirrors, or from tiny glass beads, which send light back toward the torch.

## Activities
### a1 · Shiny or Dull Hunt: twelve things and the window test (25 min)
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun, or at its reflection in any shiny thing. Test with the window behind you, in daylight that is not direct sun, or use a lamp.
- **record:** A table with twelve things, your guess for each, what you saw in each one (clear window, fuzzy window or none), and a final sort into three piles.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Line up the twelve things on a table near the window. Ask your child to guess for each one whether it will show the window clearly, fuzzily or not at all. Write the guesses in a table. With 2 to 4 children, share the twelve things out. Each child guesses and tests their own. Then check each other's results.  
  *Parent says:* Look at each thing. If we hold it up to the window, will we see the window in it? Clearly, fuzzily or not at all? Write down your guess for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Look at twelve things. Guess which show the window. Write your guesses.
  - Ask: Which is the hardest to guess? (good: The glossy magazine page.; The crumpled foil.; The wooden table.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Any guess is fine. The foil, tile and stone are good surprises.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test each thing in turn. Hold it up, with the window or lamp behind you, and look for the window shape in it. Write down clear, fuzzy or none. Look at the steel tumbler, spoon and foil from different angles.  
  *Parent says:* Stand with the window behind you. Hold up each thing and look for the shape of the window in it. Can you see the panes and the frame? Clearly, fuzzily or not at all?  
  *Child mode:* Hold each thing up. Look for the window in it. Clear, fuzzy or none?
  - Note: Expected: the tumbler, spoon, flat foil and tile show the window clearly or almost. The coin, brass lamp, magazine page and wood show a fuzzy or partial one. White paper, black cloth and crumpled foil show nothing clear. Stone depends on how smooth it is. Write what you see.
  - Check: Crumpled foil is shiny, but shows no clear window. Why? → **Its many little folds face every way and scatter the light**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put your chin on the table beside the wooden table, the tile and the paper. Look across each one toward the window or lamp. Write whether you see a bright reflection of it at this low angle.  
  *Parent says:* Put your chin down. Look across the table toward the window. Can you see a bright patch where the window is reflected? Now the paper. Now the tile.  
  *Child mode:* Put your chin on the table. Look across the wood, the tile and the paper. Do you see the window?
  - Note: At a low angle, even dull things show a bright reflection. Wood, paper and tile are good ones to try. This is why a wet road glares at night. The light skims over the bumps.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sort the twelve things into three piles: clear, fuzzy and none. Ask what the things in the clear pile have in common.  
  *Parent says:* Make three piles. What do the things in the clear pile have in common? What about the none pile?  
  *Child mode:* Sort into three piles. What do the clear ones have in common?
### a2 · Colour Bounce: a white page catches the colour next to it (20 min)
- **record:** Draw the two cards side by side, and colour the white card where it picked up a tint. Write what you saw with red, green and no coloured card.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the red card and the white card. Ask what will happen to the white card if you shine a torch on the red card only. Ask for a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* We stand a red card next to a white card. We shine the torch only on the red card. Will the white card change? How? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A red card next to a white card. Light only the red one. Will the white card change? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the white card might change colour? (good: The red light might bounce onto it.; It might not change at all.; Some red might spread across.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the room dim. Fold the white card and the red card into a V, like a half-open book, with about a right angle between them. Stand them on the table. Shine the torch at the red card, so the beam hits only the red. Look at the white card near the fold. Shine the torch from about 15 centimetres, at a slant along the red card. No direct light may fall on the white card. Hold the plain white paper well away from the red and compare its colour with the white card near the fold. Give the tint a score from 0 (none) to 3 (clear) at the fold, 5 centimetres from it and 10 centimetres from it. With 2 to 4 children, one holds the torch, one holds the cards, and the others score.  
  *Parent says:* The beam lands only on the red card. Look at the white card, close to the fold. Do you see any colour on it? Move your eyes further from the fold. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Light only the red card. Look at the white card near the fold. Do you see any colour?
  - Note: Expected: a faint pink glow on the white card near the fold, that fades further away. The effect is subtle. A very dim room and a bright torch help. If you cannot see it, hold a clean sheet of white paper beside the white card for comparison. It will look plainer. The most reliable way is a sunny window: lay the red card in bright daylight beside the white card, with no direct sun on the white. Never look into the sun.
  - Check: The torch light only landed on the red card, yet the white card looked slightly pink near the fold. Why? → **Light bounced off the red card onto the white card, carrying red with it**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap the red for the green card, and shine the torch on the green only. Look at the white card. Then take the coloured card away and shine the torch at the white one alone. Compare.  
  *Parent says:* Now the green card. What colour does the white card take? Now take the green away. What does the white card look like by itself?  
  *Child mode:* Swap in the green card. Light it. What colour does the white card take?
  - Note: The white card should pick up a green tint near the fold, and none when the coloured card is removed. This is the same reason a person standing beside a bright red wall looks slightly flushed.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about where you see this at home. Sunlight on a green wall can turn a white ceiling slightly green. A yellow wall can make a room glow warm.  
  *Parent says:* Where might this happen at home? A bright curtain in the sun, a coloured wall, a red cloth near a white sheet. Have you ever seen a white thing look tinted?  
  *Child mode:* Where could a bright wall tint a white thing at home?
### a3 · Steel Katori Beam: a bowl that gathers light and a back that spreads it (22 min)
- **safety:** Use a torch only, never sunlight. A curved mirror can focus sunlight into a spot hot enough to burn skin or start a fire, and can harm eyes. Never look into the bright reflection.
- **record:** Draw the torch, the katori and the card. Show where the bright spot was smallest. Write what the back of the katori did to the beam.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the katori. Ask what a torch beam will do after bouncing off the inside of the bowl, and off the outside. Ask for guesses and reasons. Write them.  
  *Parent says:* The inside of the katori curves in. The back curves out. If we shine a torch on each, will the light look different on a card? How? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A torch shines on the inside of a bowl and on its back. Will the light look different? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the bowl will change the beam? (good: It curves, so it bends the light.; It will gather the light.; It will spread the light.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (9 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Make the room dark. Prop the torch on books about 2 metres away, aimed at you. Hold the katori with its inside facing the torch, tilted a little so the reflected light goes off to one side. Hold the white card in that reflected light, a few centimetres from the bowl. Move it back and forth until the spot is smallest and brightest. Measure the distance from the bowl to the card.  
  *Parent says:* Aim the torch into the bowl. Hold the card to catch the light that comes back. Slide the card nearer and further. Where is the spot smallest and brightest?  
  *Child mode:* Aim the torch into the bowl. Catch the light on the card. Find the smallest spot.
  - Note: With one child, the parent props the torch and the child holds the katori and card. With 2 to 4 children, one holds the katori, one holds the card, one measures, then swap. Do not look into the bowl at the beam. The bright spot forms a few centimetres in front of a small katori, at the focus. A katori is deep, so the spot is a soft blob, not a point. If you cannot find it, tilt the katori more and move the card slowly.
  - Check: The inside of the katori made the light into a small, bright spot. What does a concave mirror do? → **It gathers light to a point**
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the katori round, so that its back faces the torch. Catch the light on the card and move the card as before. Compare it with the inside. Then light the room. Look at your face in the inside of a large steel spoon, at arm's length. Look in the back too.  
  *Parent says:* Now the back of the katori. Where does the light go? Is it small and bright, or large and dim? What is different from before?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the katori round. Shine the torch on its back. Is the spot big or small?
  - Note: The back spreads the beam into a wide, dim patch with no sharp spot. That is why convex mirrors, which curve outward, are used on cars and in shop corners: they show a wide view. This is a good moment to name the two kinds, concave and convex. Expected: the back never makes a small bright spot, only a dim wide patch. In the inside of the spoon your face is usually upside down; in the back it is upright and small.
### a4 · Dusk Reflector Hunt: what glows back at a torch? (25 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up must come. Stay on the pavement or in a safe space, away from traffic. Never shine a torch into a driver's eyes or at people.
- **record:** A list of at least six things that glowed back at the torch, and three that did not, with a note on where you were standing when each glowed.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Before you go out, ask your child to guess what kinds of things will glow back at a torch. Write the guesses. Check the torch works. With 2 to 4 children, give each their own torch and a job: spotter, recorder, side-stepper. One grown-up can look after up to four children on a quiet path.  
  *Parent says:* We will walk at dusk with a torch. What kinds of things do you think will glow back at us when we shine the torch on them? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Walk at dusk with a torch. What kinds of things will glow back? Why?
  - Ask: What do you expect to glow back? (good: Mirrors.; Bicycle reflectors.; Shiny things.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children expect shiny things such as mirrors, and are surprised by the reflective patches on signs, bags and clothes.
- **s2 (14 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Walk with a grown-up. Hold the torch beside your eye. Shine it at bicycle reflectors, the red patches on autos and trucks, and road signs. Try number plates, school bag stripes and cat's eyes in the road. Write down each thing that glows. Try a painted wall and a plain sign for comparison.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the torch right beside your eye. Shine it at a reflector. Does it glow? Now try a painted wall. Does it glow? Write down what you find.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the torch beside your eye. Shine it at reflectors. Write what glows back.
  - Note: Expected: bicycle reflectors, signs, number plates, cat's eyes and reflective stripes glow brightly. Painted walls and plain signs do not. Do not shine the torch at drivers. If there is nothing to see, look at the reflectors on your own bicycle or bag.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose one glowing reflector. Stand in front of it with the torch beside your eye and note the glow. Then step to the side, keep the torch pointing at it, and look from where you stand. Compare the two. Then step back a few paces at a time and count how many paces away you can still see the glow.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the reflector with the torch beside your eye. Now step to the side but keep the torch pointed at it. Does it glow as brightly from here?  
  *Child mode:* Look from beside the torch. Then step to the side. Does the glow stay?
  - Check: A bicycle reflector glows brightly when a car's headlight lights it, and the driver sees the glow. Why is the glow so good for the driver? → **It sends the light straight back toward the headlight, near the driver's eyes**

### p1 · Milk-Carton Periscope: see over the wall (75 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the cutting with the craft knife and scissors. Tape over the edges of the mirrors, because the edges can be sharp. Glass mirrors break, so keep them away from small children and handle them over a table.

- **schedule:** day 1: Prepare the carton, cut the windows, make the two wedges and stick the mirrors on them. (45 min); day 2: Fit the wedges, test the periscope, fix any problems and use it to see over a wall. (30 min)

  *Parent does:* Show your child the carton and mirrors. Ask what they will see if they look into the lower window while holding the periscope above a wall. Ask if the picture will be the right way up. Write the guesses. With 2 to 4 children, work in pairs. One pair builds the carton and the other makes the wedges. Then join them. With one child, the parent does the cutting and the child folds, tapes and tests.  
  *Parent says:* A periscope uses two mirrors to look over a wall. What will you see through the lower window? Will the picture be upside down, back to front or the right way up? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A periscope has two mirrors. Will the picture be the right way up? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the picture will come out that way? (good: One mirror flips it, and the other flips it back.; It will be upside down.; It will be the right way up.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect the picture to be flipped or upside down. Test it.
- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts off the top and bottom of the carton. Cut a window 5 centimetres wide and 4 centimetres tall in the front face, starting 1 centimetre below the top edge. Turn the carton round. Cut a window of the same size in the back face, starting 1 centimetre above the bottom edge. Colour the inside black.  
  *Parent says:* The two windows must be on opposite faces. One is near the top on the front. One is near the bottom on the back. Make sure they face opposite ways.  
  *Child mode:* Cut the ends off. Cut a window at the top front. Cut one at the bottom back.
  - Note: Check that the carton is clean and dry. The windows must be on opposite faces, one high and one low. Colouring the inside black cuts down stray reflections and makes the picture clearer.
- **s3 (25 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Take a card strip. Fold it at 7 centimetres and at 14 centimetres, so the three sides are 7, 7 and about 10 centimetres. Check that the corner between the two 7 centimetre sides is a right angle. Tape the ends together to make a triangle tube. Tape a mirror onto the long side, shiny side out. Make the second wedge the same way.  
  *Parent says:* We make two wedges, each a triangle with two short sides and one long side. The mirror goes on the long side, shiny side out. Use the corner of a book to check the right angle.  
  *Child mode:* Fold a card strip into a triangle. Tape a mirror on the long side. Make two.
  - Note: The triangle with sides 7, 7 and 10 centimetres has a right angle between its two short sides and a 45 degree slope on its long side. That slope is what turns the light through a right angle. If you have no mirrors, glue smooth kitchen foil flat onto the card. The picture will be wobbly but visible. Try the wedge in the carton before taping. Cartons are a little narrower than 7 centimetres inside, so if a wedge is too tight, trim 2 or 3 millimetres off its sides.
  - Check: Why is the long side of each wedge set at 45 degrees? → **A mirror at 45 degrees turns a ray through a right angle**
- **s4 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, slide one wedge into the top of the carton. Its top leg lies along the top edge. Its flat leg lies against the back wall. Its mirror faces the front window. Slide the second wedge into the bottom. Its flat leg lies against the front wall, and its mirror faces the back window. Tape both in place. Look into the lower window.  
  *Parent says:* Top wedge: the mirror faces down and toward the front window. Bottom wedge: the mirror faces up and toward the back window. Both mirrors lean the same way, with their shiny sides facing each other.  
  *Child mode:* Fit the top wedge. Fit the bottom wedge. The mirrors face each other. Look in the lower window.
  - Note: If you see only black, check that the mirrors face each other and that the windows are on opposite faces. If you see a tilted picture, one wedge is crooked. Press the wedges to sit flat against the walls. The picture should come out upright and the right way round.
  - Wait: Day 2
- **s5 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the periscope up beside a wall, a sofa or a door, with the top window facing the far side. Look into the lower window. Write what you see. Test whether writing, such as a word on a page, looks the right way round. Measure how much higher the top window is than the lower one. Find something that height to see over.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the periscope above the wall with the top window facing the far side. Look in the lower window. What do you see? Is the picture the right way up? Can you read a word held up in front of the top window?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the periscope up. Look in the lower window. What do you see? Is it the right way up?
  - Ask: Why does the picture come out the right way up? (good: The first mirror flips it and the second flips it back.; The two mirrors cancel each other.; Two bounces turn it back.)

### Smooth, Rough and Mirrors (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A white wall and a mirror both reflect light. Why do you see a clear picture of your face in the mirror but not in the wall?  
   Options: The mirror is smooth and sends light back in order, but the wall scatters it in every direction / The mirror makes its own light / The wall takes in all the light / The wall is too big  
   **Answer:** The mirror is smooth and sends light back in order, but the wall scatters it in every direction. *Why:* Both bounce light. The mirror is smooth, so the rays leave together in order and your eye collects a picture. The wall is made of tiny bumps facing every way, so the rays leave in every direction. Picking 'the wall takes in all the light' is a common idea, but you can see the wall only because it reflects.
2. (truefalse) A white wall reflects no light, which is why it shows no picture of you. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. You can see the wall only because it reflects light into your eyes. It reflects in every direction, so there is no single picture. A strong answer says the wall reflects but scatters.
3. (fill) When a rough surface sends light off in every direction, this is called ____ reflection.  
   **Answer:** ['scattered', 'diffuse']. *Why:* Scattered reflection happens when the bumps face every way. The rays go off in many directions, and you can see the surface from anywhere.
4. (match) Match each surface to what you see in it.  
   **Answer:** a clean bathroom mirror → a clear picture of your face; a glossy magazine page → a bright patch of the lamp and a soft picture; a white wall → no picture, but the wall looks the same from everywhere; a mirror covered in fog → no picture, because tiny drops scatter the light. *Why:* Smooth surfaces make clear pictures. 5. (sort) Sort these. Does the surface show a clear picture, or a blurry picture or none?

Cloth, clay and chalk are rough, and scatter the light.

   **Answer:** It filled and flattened the tiny bumps, so light reflects in order. *Why:* Polish fills the tiny dips and rubbing makes the top smooth. A smoother surface reflects in order and shows bright reflections. Picking 'it added light' mixes up shining with making light.
7. (truefalse) A rough surface breaks the rule that light leaves at the same angle at which it arrived. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The rule holds on every tiny flat patch of the surface. A rough surface is made of patches tilted every way, so the rays go in different directions. Nothing is broken.
8. (write) Fog covers a bathroom mirror. Explain why you cannot see your face in it, even though the mirror is still there.  
   **Answer:** The fog is made of thousands of tiny drops of water. Each drop scatters light in a different direction. When the fog clears, the picture returns.. *Why:* A strong answer says the drops scatter the light, and that the mirror itself has not changed.
9. (mcq) Which tool uses two mirrors so that you can see over a wall?  
   Options: A periscope / A magnifying glass / A pair of spectacles / A camera with a lens  
   **Answer:** A periscope. *Why:* A periscope has a mirror at each end of a tube. Light from the scene goes down the tube by two bounces to your eye.
10. (order) Put these steps in order, to follow a ray of light through a periscope.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3]. *Why:* The ray turns twice, once at each mirror. Each mirror is tilted at 45 degrees, so each turns the ray through a right angle.
11. (fill) A mirror tilted at ____ degrees turns a ray of light through a right angle.  
   **Answer:** ['45', 'forty-five', 'forty five']. *Why:* At 45 degrees, the ray meets the mirror at 45 degrees and leaves at 45 degrees on the other side. The two angles together make a right angle.
12. (draw) Draw a side view of a periscope. Show the two mirrors, the two windows and the path of a ray of light from the top window to the eye.  
   **Answer:** A tall tube with a window near the top on one face and a window near the bottom on the opposite face. A mirror sloping at 45 degrees behind each window, with shiny sides facing each other. A zigzag ray enters the top window, turns down, turns again and leaves the lower window.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the mirrors parallel to each other, the windows on opposite faces, and the ray turning through a right angle at each mirror.
13. (mcq) A bicycle reflector glows brightly when a car's headlight shines on it. What is the best reason?

   **Answer:** It sends the light back toward the headlight with many tiny corner mirrors. *Why:* A reflector is made of many tiny corner mirrors at right angles. They send light straight back the way it came, so the driver, near the headlight, sees a bright glow. Picking 'it makes its own light' is a common idea, but a reflector is dark if no light shines on it.
14. (perspective) On a wet road at night, the street lights make long bright streaks. Three people explain why.

Water does not make light.. *Why:* A strong answer picks one view with a reason from smoothness or angle, and credits a point from another view.
15. (truefalse) If you look across a plain white page from a very low angle, you can see a bright reflection of a lamp in it. True or false?  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. At a very low angle, light skims over the tiny bumps instead of hitting them, so even a dull surface reflects in order. This is why a book page or a tiled floor can show a lamp when seen from low down.

### Mirrors That Turn, Gather and Return Light (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)

   **Answer:** The Sun is low, so we see the water at a low angle, and calm water is smooth. *Why:* A low angle makes any surface reflect more in order, and calm water is smooth already. Together they make a clear picture. Seen from high above at noon, the same lake reflects less of the sky.
5. (mcq) How many mirrors does a simple periscope have, and how are they set?  
   Options: Two, each tilted at 45 degrees, with their shiny sides facing each other / One, tilted at 90 degrees / Three, each pointing at the viewer / Two, one flat on the floor and one on the ceiling  
   **Answer:** Two, each tilted at 45 degrees, with their shiny sides facing each other. *Why:* A mirror tilted at 45 degrees turns a ray through a right angle. Two of them, one at each end of a tube, turn the ray twice, so you can look over a wall.
6. (mcq) A periscope with just one mirror, instead of two, would show a picture that is  
   Options: Reversed, because one flip is not cancelled / Upright, the same as with two mirrors / Upside down / Twice as bright  
   **Answer:** Reversed, because one flip is not cancelled. *Why:* Each flat mirror flips the picture front to back once. With two mirrors, the two flips cancel. With one mirror, the flip stays and the picture is reversed.
7. (mcq) A cat's eye in the road and a bicycle reflector both glow in a car's headlights. What do they have in common?  
   Options: Both have tiny corner mirrors that send light straight back toward the light / Both make their own light / Both are made of the same metal / Both only work in daylight  
   **Answer:** Both have tiny corner mirrors that send light straight back toward the light. *Why:* Corner mirrors at right angles send light back the way it came. The driver's eyes are near the headlights, so the driver sees the glow.
8. (mcq) A shopkeeper wants one mirror that shows the whole shop. Which kind is best?  
   Options: A convex mirror, which curves outward / A concave mirror, which curves inward / A flat mirror / No mirror shows more than a flat mirror  
   **Answer:** A convex mirror, which curves outward. *Why:* A convex mirror collects light from a wide range of directions, so it shows a wide view, in a smaller size. A concave mirror held far from the face would turn the picture upside down, and shows a narrower view.
9. (mcq) A solar cooker has a curved shiny dish. What does the dish do?  
   Options: It gathers sunlight onto the pot / It makes new sunlight / It spreads sunlight over the whole kitchen / It turns sunlight into sound  
   **Answer:** It gathers sunlight onto the pot. *Why:* A concave dish tilts each patch so that the reflected rays cross at the pot. The light is packed into a small area, which heats the pot. Dangerous for eyes and skin, so never aim one at a person.
10. (mcq) Isaac Newton built a telescope with a curved mirror instead of a big lens. What problem did this avoid?  
   Options: Coloured fringes, because a mirror bounces every colour at the same angle / The telescope being too heavy / The stars being too bright / The Moon being too far away  
   **Answer:** Coloured fringes, because a mirror bounces every colour at the same angle. *Why:* Glass bends colours by different amounts, so lenses give coloured fringes. A mirror reflects all colours alike. Newton showed this about 1666 with a prism.
11. (mcq) A torch lights only a red card. A white card next to it looks slightly pink near the fold. What does this show?  
   Options: Light can bounce twice, and it carries colour from the first surface to the second / White paper turns pink in the dark / The torch is red / Red cards make their own light  
   **Answer:** Light can bounce twice, and it carries colour from the first surface to the second. *Why:* The red card scatters red light onto the white card, which scatters it again into your eyes. Every surface reflects, so light can bounce from one surface to another.
12. (mcq) A friend says, 'A wall does not reflect light, because it shows no picture of me.' What is the best reply?  
   Options: You can see the wall only because it reflects light into your eyes. It scatters the light, so there is no picture / You are right, a wall takes in all the light / A wall makes its own light / Walls only reflect in the dark  
   **Answer:** You can see the wall only because it reflects light into your eyes. It scatters the light, so there is no picture. *Why:* A wall reflects light in every direction, so it is visible from anywhere. What it lacks is the tidy order that makes a picture.

## Applications
- Periscopes in submarines let the crew look above the waves.
- Bicycle reflectors, cat's eyes and reflective road signs send light back to drivers.
- Reflective stripes on school bags, jackets and trucks make them visible at night.
- Telescopes with huge curved mirrors collect light from faint, distant stars.
- Car headlights use a curved mirror behind the bulb to send light forward in a beam.
- Solar cookers use shiny dishes to gather sunlight onto a pot.
- Shop corner mirrors and car side mirrors are convex, to show a wide view.
- Optical fibres carry internet messages as flashes of light under the sea.
- Polish, wax and varnish make shoes, cars and furniture shine.
- A signal mirror can flash sunlight across a long distance to call for help.

## Open questions
- **Could we build a mirror so smooth and so large that it makes a telescope as sharp as we dream of?** Telescope mirrors are getting bigger, but big mirrors bend under their own weight and the air above them blurs the view. Engineers are still finding ways round these limits.
- **Should the world try to reflect sunlight away to cool the Earth?** The ideas have not been tested at scale, and no one knows what they would do to the monsoon or to the world's weather. Scientists and countries disagree.
- **Why do only some animals recognise their own face in a mirror?** A few kinds of animals pass the mark test. Scientists disagree about whether failing it means an animal has no sense of self, or only that the test does not suit them.
- **Can we make a surface that reflects no light at all?** The blackest materials take in more than 99.9 parts in 100 of the light, but none is perfect. Scientists do not agree on whether a perfect one can exist.
- **Could a cloak bend light round a person to make them invisible?** Special materials can bend some kinds of light round small objects in a laboratory. Nobody has made a cloak for a person that works for all the colours.
- **What is the best shape for a mirror that gathers sunlight over a whole day without moving?** The Sun moves across the sky, so most solar mirrors need to be turned. Engineers keep searching for cheaper shapes that follow the Sun without moving parts.

## Connected maths
- A mirror reflects 9 out of every 10 parts of the light that lands on it. White paper reflects 8 out of every 10 parts. Black cloth reflects 1 out of every 20 parts. If 400 units of light land on each, how many units does each one reflect? **The mirror reflects 360 units, the paper 320 units and the black cloth 20 units.**. For the mirror, a tenth of 400 is 40, and 9 times 40 is 360. For the paper, 8 times 40 is 320. For the cloth, a twentieth of 400 is 20. So the mirror reflects the most and the cloth the least. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A beam of light lands on a flat mirror and makes an angle of 30 degrees with the surface. It leaves at the same angle on the other side. What is the angle between the beam going in and the beam going out? **120 degrees.**. Along the surface of the mirror there is a straight line of 180 degrees. The beam going in uses 30 degrees at one end, and the beam going out uses 30 degrees at the other. 180 take away 30 take away 30 is 120. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- A mirror at 45 degrees turns a beam through 90 degrees. A beam passes through four such mirrors in a row, and each one turns it 90 degrees in the same direction. How many degrees does the beam turn in total, and which way is it going at the end? **360 degrees in total. It is going the same way it started.**. 4 times 90 is 360. A full turn is 360 degrees. So after four turns in the same direction, the beam is back to its starting direction. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- A butterfly shape has one line of symmetry down the middle. If you stand a mirror along that line, the left wing and its reflection make the whole butterfly. The left wing is 6 centimetres wide at its widest. How wide is the whole butterfly? **12 centimetres.**. A mirror along the line of symmetry shows a reflection of the left wing that is the same width. 6 plus 6 is 12. So the whole butterfly is 12 centimetres wide. (queues `math.symmetry.line-symmetry.L4`)
- Each of the two card wedges for the Milk-Carton Periscope is a strip 7 centimetres wide and 24 centimetres long. How much card do you need for one wedge, and for both? **168 square centimetres for one wedge, and 336 square centimetres for both.**. 7 times 24 is 7 times 20, which is 140, plus 7 times 4, which is 28. 140 plus 28 is 168. Two wedges need 168 times 2, which is 336. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
- The Moon sends back about 12 parts in every 100 of the sunlight that lands on it. White paper sends back about 80 parts in every 100. About how many times as much light does white paper send back as the Moon, for the same light landing? **About 7 times as much.**. 80 divided by 12 is a little more than 6 and a half. 12 times 6 is 72, and 12 times 7 is 84. So 80 is a bit less than 7 times 12. We say about 7 times. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In Where Does the Bounce Go, six surfaces were tested. The mirror was seen from 1 of the 5 marks, the smooth foil 1, the crumpled foil 5, the white paper 5, the black cloth 2 and the glossy page 5. What fraction of the surfaces could be seen from all 5 marks? **One half, which is 3 of the 6 surfaces.**. The crumpled foil, the white paper and the glossy page were seen from all 5 marks. That is 3 surfaces out of 6. 3 out of 6 is the same as one half. These are made-up results. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A laser flash goes to the reflectors on the Moon and back in about 2 and a half seconds. So the flash takes 1 and a quarter seconds each way. Light goes about 3 lakh kilometres in one second. About how far away is the Moon? **About 3 lakh 75 thousand kilometres.**. 1 and a quarter seconds is 1 second plus a quarter second. In 1 second light goes 3,00,000 kilometres. A quarter of 3,00,000 is 75,000. 3,00,000 plus 75,000 is 3,75,000 kilometres. The real distance changes a little as the Moon moves. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
