# Clouds and rain: how tiny drops in a cloud become rain
`sci.weather.clouds-and-rain.L2`

**Summary.** A cloud is made of millions of tiny drops of water, each one thinner than a hair. They are so small and light that they float in the air. Rain falls only when many tiny drops join into one bigger, heavier drop. Your child tests this with a spray bottle and a steel plate: the fine mist clings to the plate, and only after many sprays does a drop grow big enough to run down. Then they find out where a cloud's water comes from, and why grey clouds are the ones that bring rain.

**Intro.** *Spray one puff of mist from a spray bottle into the air, in a beam of torchlight. Then let one drop of water fall from a spoon. Which one reaches the floor first?* The drop from the spoon falls straight down in a blink. The tiny drops of the mist sparkle in the light and drift slowly, and some seem to hang in the air. A cloud is made of drops even smaller than these. So how does a cloud ever make rain? In this unit you test it yourself, with a spray bottle and a steel plate.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that a cloud is made of millions of tiny drops of water floating in the air.
1. By the end I can say that tiny drops fall very slowly and float, and big drops fall fast.
2. By the end I can explain that rain falls when many tiny drops join into bigger, heavier drops.
3. By the end I can say where the water in a cloud comes from: water evaporates from seas, rivers and wet things, and cools high up into tiny drops.
4. By the end I can say that thick grey clouds are more likely to bring rain than thin white ones, and why.
5. By the end I can guess first, give a reason, test it, and say what my test showed.

## Terms
- **Cloud**: A cloud is a huge crowd of tiny drops of water, or tiny bits of ice, floating high in the air. *e.g.* The white puffy shapes in the sky on a sunny afternoon, and the dark sky before a monsoon storm, are both clouds.
- **Cloud drop**: One of the tiny drops a cloud is made of. It is far thinner than a hair, too small to see on its own. *e.g.* A cloud drop is about as wide as a hair split into five.
- **Raindrop**: A drop of water big and heavy enough to fall from a cloud to the ground. *e.g.* One raindrop holds about as much water as a million cloud drops.
- **Water vapour**: Water as a gas. It is mixed into the air, and you cannot see it. *e.g.* The air in your kitchen holds water vapour, even when it looks clear and empty.
- **Evaporate**: When water evaporates, it slowly turns from a liquid into water vapour and goes into the air. *e.g.* Wet clothes on a line dry because their water evaporates.
- **Condense**: When water vapour condenses, it cools and turns back into tiny drops of liquid water. *e.g.* High in the sky, the air is cold. There, water vapour condenses into the tiny drops that make a cloud.
- **Monsoon**: The season of strong winds from the sea that bring most of India's rain. *e.g.* In Kerala, the monsoon rain usually begins in early June. In Chennai, most rain comes from October to December.

## Explanations
### A cloud is a crowd of tiny drops · *defined*
**Definition:** A cloud is made of millions and millions of tiny drops of water, or tiny bits of ice, floating in the air.

From far away, a cloud looks soft and solid, like cotton. Up close, it is wet mist. People who walk on hills in Munnar or Shillong sometimes walk right through a cloud. It feels cool and damp, and tiny drops collect on their hair. Each cloud drop is far thinner than a hair. There are so many of them that, all together, they hide the sky behind them.
- e.g. Walking through a cloud on a hill road feels like cool, wet mist.
- e.g. Tiny drops collect on your hair and clothes inside a cloud.
- e.g. The mist from a spray bottle is a little like a cloud, but its drops are bigger.
### Tiny drops float, big drops fall · *defined*
**Definition:** Every drop falls, but a tiny drop falls very slowly. The air pushes against it as it falls, and for a tiny drop that push matters a lot.

In the torch beam, the drop from the spoon fell in a blink. The tiny drops of mist drifted, and some floated for many seconds. A cloud drop is even smaller than a mist drop. It falls less than one centimetre each second. Air in the sky is often rising, and rising air easily carries such tiny drops up. That is why a cloud can float, even though it is made of water. A big raindrop falls several metres each second, about as fast as a person cycling. Clouds are heavy. Peggy LeMone, a cloud scientist in the United States, worked out that one small puffy cloud holds about 500 tonnes of water. That is about as heavy as 100 elephants. Yet the cloud floats, because that water is split into tiny, slow drops.
- e.g. The spoon drop fell straight to the floor.
- e.g. The mist drifted slowly in the torch beam.
- e.g. Dust floats in a sunbeam for the same reason: each speck is tiny and light.
### Rain falls when tiny drops join · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside a cloud, tiny drops bump into each other and join. When a drop has grown big and heavy enough, it falls as rain.

On your steel plate, the first sprays left tiny drops that stayed still. As you sprayed more, the drops grew, touched and joined. Only a big drop ran down. Inside a cloud, the same kind of joining happens again and again. One raindrop holds about as much water as a million cloud drops. In very tall clouds, the top is freezing cold. There, tiny bits of ice grow, join and fall. They melt into raindrops on the way down.
- e.g. On the plate, many tiny drops joined into one drop that ran down.
- e.g. On a window in the rain, a big drop races down and swallows the small drops in its way.
- e.g. Tall monsoon clouds have ice at the top, even on a hot day.
### Where a cloud's water comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun warms water in seas, rivers, ponds and wet things, and the water evaporates into the air as invisible water vapour. High in the sky the air is cold, and the vapour condenses into tiny drops. That is a cloud.

Water is always evaporating, from the Bay of Bengal, from the Kaveri, from a puddle and from the wet clothes on your balcony. The water vapour mixes into the air, and you cannot see it. Warm air rises. The higher it goes, the colder it gets. When the air is cold enough, the vapour condenses into tiny drops, each one round a speck of dust or sea salt too small to see. Millions of drops together make a cloud. When the drops join and fall as rain, the water comes back down to the land and the sea.
- e.g. Wet clothes dry because their water evaporates into the air.
- e.g. Monsoon winds carry damp air from the sea over Kerala and the Western Ghats.
- e.g. Rain that falls in Chennai may have evaporated from the Bay of Bengal a few days before.
### Why rain clouds look grey · *defined*
**Definition:** A thin cloud lets plenty of sunlight through, so it looks white. A very thick cloud stops most of the light before it reaches the bottom, so its underside looks grey or nearly black.

All clouds are made of the same clear water drops. A white cloud and a grey cloud are not different kinds of water. A thick cloud has many more drops, piled up high. The light gets spread out by so many drops that little comes out of the bottom. A thick cloud also has more water, and its drops have more chances to join. That is why dark grey clouds are the ones that usually bring rain. Joanne Simpson, the first woman to earn a doctorate in weather science, spent her life studying tall rain clouds over warm seas, like the clouds of India's monsoon.
- e.g. Thin white clouds on a sunny morning rarely rain.
- e.g. Before a monsoon storm, the sky turns dark grey.
- e.g. The top of a tall storm cloud, lit by the Sun, can look bright white while its bottom is dark.

## Mostly agreed
- Clouds are made of tiny drops of liquid water, or tiny bits of ice, floating in the air. *How we know:* People in planes and on mountains have caught cloud drops and looked at them under a microscope. Walking through a cloud on a hill leaves tiny drops on your hair.
- Rain falls when tiny cloud drops grow and join into drops heavy enough to fall. *How we know:* Scientists have flown special planes through clouds to measure the drops. Clouds that are raining hold bigger drops among the tiny ones. Clouds that are not raining hold almost only tiny drops.
- The water in clouds comes from water that evaporated from seas, rivers, lakes, soil, plants and wet things. *How we know:* The water that evaporates and the water that falls as rain balance out over the whole Earth, year after year.

## How we found out
**Horace-Bénédict de Saussure, a Swiss scientist who believed cloud specks were bubbles, and Augustus Waller, a doctor in London who looked at them with a microscope, The 1700s (the bubble idea) and 1846 (Waller's microscope study, printed in 1847).** If clouds are made of water, why do they float? Water is much heavier than air. In the 1700s, many scientists in Europe had an answer. They said the specks in a cloud are tiny hollow bubbles, like soap bubbles, filled with air. A hollow bubble is very light, so it floats. Some even said they had seen rainbow colours on these bubbles. Horace-Bénédict de Saussure, a Swiss scientist who climbed the Alps to study the air, believed in the bubbles too. Then, in 1846, Augustus Waller, a young doctor in London, looked at the mist above hot water through a microscope. He saw solid round drops, not hollow bubbles. He sent his findings to the Royal Society, a famous science society in London, and they were printed in 1847. Other scientists agreed with him in the years after. Today we know a cloud is made of solid water drops. They float because they are so tiny that they fall very slowly, and rising air carries them up.  
*What people believed before:* That cloud specks are tiny hollow bubbles filled with air, which is why they float. Waller's microscope showed solid round drops instead.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Clouds are made of smoke, or of something like cotton.** (A common guess by young children; *rejected*)  
  For: From far away, clouds look soft and fluffy, and some look like smoke from a chimney.  
  Against: Walk through a cloud on a hill, and you come out with damp hair. Cloud drops have been caught and looked at under microscopes: they are water.
- **The specks in a cloud are tiny hollow bubbles, filled with air, like soap bubbles.** (Many scientists in Europe in the 1700s, such as Horace-Bénédict de Saussure; *rejected*)  
  For: A hollow bubble would be very light, so it would explain why a cloud floats.  
  Against: In 1846, Augustus Waller looked at mist through a microscope and saw solid round drops, not bubbles. Today we know cloud drops float because they are tiny, and rising air holds them up.
- **Raindrops are shaped like tears, with a pointed top.** (What most drawings show; *rejected*)  
  For: A drop hanging from a tap, just before it falls, has a pointed top.  
  Against: Photos taken with very fast cameras show falling raindrops are round when small. Big ones get squashed flat at the bottom by the air, a little like a burger bun.
- **People can make clouds rain by 'seeding' them: spraying tiny specks of salt or other powders into them from planes.** (Scientists who test cloud seeding, in India and other countries; *debated*)  
  For: Tests show the right clouds sometimes give more rain after seeding. Scientists from the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology in Pune have tested it over Solapur in Maharashtra.  
  Against: Seeding only works on clouds that already hold plenty of water. It cannot make rain from a clear sky. It is also hard to know how much rain would have fallen anyway.

## Roots
We have sprayed mist on a plate, watched drops race down a window and found out what a cloud is made of. Now we ask what a cloud and its rain tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** On your plate, the tiny drops from the spray clung on and did not fall. Only after many sprays did they join into a big drop that slid off. A cloud holds its water in drops too small to fall. Rain begins when many of them join into heavier drops.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a cloud as a bag or tank of rain that bursts open, and we say 'the sky opened' or 'a cloudburst'. The plate test showed that rain is made, drop by drop, by tiny drops joining.  
*Evidence:* In the spray plate test, one spray left tiny drops that stayed still. A drop ran down only after many sprays, once tiny drops had joined into one big drop.

- *who am i:* Some of the water in your body once fell as rain. It was millions of cloud drops before that.
- *what is life:* Every plant and animal, including us, lives on water that tiny drops brought down as rain. Plants also give water back to the air from their leaves.
- *what is this world:* The same water keeps going round: from the sea into the air, into a cloud, down as rain, into a river and back to the sea.

A cloud has one name, but it is millions of drops, each floating on its own. Rain begins when many of them join into one heavier drop. The raindrop lands, and later its water evaporates and spreads out into the air again. So water keeps moving between being many tiny drops and being one big drop. Everything is conscious, down to the water in a cloud, and each drop meets its neighbours as it joins them. Many scientists describe this joining with forces alone.

- **Tirukkural, chapter 2, 'The Excellence of Rain', couplet 20:** Thiruvalluvar, a Tamil poet and thinker, wrote that the world cannot go on without water, and that water cannot flow without rain. (Every river and tap is filled, in the end, by rain. And rain only comes when tiny cloud drops join, as they did on your plate.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, No Death, No Fear (2002):** Thich Nhat Hanh, a Vietnamese Buddhist teacher, taught that a cloud never becomes nothing. It changes into rain, snow or ice, and goes on in a new form. (When a cloud rains itself away, its drops have joined and fallen. The water is still all there, in the rain, the rivers and the air.)
- **Kalidasa, Meghaduta (The Cloud Messenger), about the 5th century:** In this Sanskrit poem, a yaksha far from home asks a monsoon cloud to carry a message of love to his wife. He talks to the cloud as someone who can travel, listen and help. (Kalidasa watched the monsoon clouds travel north across India. He spoke to a cloud as a someone, not a something.)
- Science (settled): One raindrop holds about as much water as a million cloud drops. Cloud drops join by bumping into each other, and in tall clouds ice helps too.
- Science (open): Scientists still study why drops in some warm clouds join into rain so quickly. Swirling air probably helps, but how much is not yet known.
- Other view, Many physicists: They say a water drop has no awareness at all. They explain how drops join with forces between water particles, and they see no need for a mind to explain it.
- Practice, Wait for the drop (3 min): Dip one finger in a cup of water. Hold it up, pointing down, over the cup. Keep very still. Watch the water slide to your fingertip and gather there. Breathe in and out slowly while the drop grows. When it is heavy enough, it lets go and falls. Do it three times, and breathe slowly each time.
- Ask: On your fingertip, the drop waited until it was heavy enough. What do you think it is like to be a tiny drop in a cloud, waiting?
- Ask: Is a cloud one thing or millions of things? Does it matter which we say?
- Ask: When a cloud has rained itself away, is the cloud gone, or is it somewhere else now?

## Story: The Bag in the Sky
*Meenakshi, Karthik, Amma and Paati are made up. Amma works as a weather scientist. The northeast monsoon really does bring Chennai most of its rain between October and December.*

It was November in Chennai, and the sky over the flats had been grey all morning. Meenakshi was seven. Her little brother Karthik was five. They sat at the window and watched the clouds.

"When is the rain coming?" asked Karthik. "The clouds are full. Why don't they burst?"

"Burst?" said Meenakshi.

"Like a bag of water," said Karthik. "A cloud is a big bag, full of rain. When it gets too full, it bursts. Paati said the sky will open today."

Paati laughed from the kitchen. "That is only a way of saying it, kanna. Ask your Amma. Clouds are her work."

Amma worked at the weather office in Chennai. She studied clouds and rain all day, and she was home early because of the storm warning. She came over with a spray bottle, the one Paati used for ironing clothes, and a steel plate.

"Let us find out what is inside a cloud," Amma said. "Meenakshi, you can be the scientist. Karthik, you can be the counter."

Meenakshi stood the plate on its edge on a tray, leaning against the wall. Amma turned the nozzle of the bottle until it made the finest mist.

"A cloud is made of tiny drops of water," said Amma. "Millions of them. Each one is thinner than a hair. This mist is a little like a cloud. Its drops are tiny too, though a cloud's are tinier still. Now, Meenakshi, if you spray the plate, what will the water do?"

"It will run down," said Meenakshi. "Water always runs down."

"Let us see. One spray at a time."

Meenakshi sprayed once. The shiny plate went dull, as if someone had breathed on it. She bent close. The plate was covered in tiny drops, like grains of rava. Not one of them moved.

"They are stuck," said Karthik.

"Spray again," said Amma. "Karthik, count."

Two. Three. Four. Five. The tiny drops grew fatter. Here and there, two drops touched and became one. Six. Seven. Eight. Now the drops were as big as mustard seeds. Nine. Ten.

At the eleventh spray, one drop near the top touched its neighbour, then another. Suddenly it was too big to hold on. It slid down the plate, fast, and on the way it swallowed every little drop in its path. It left a clean, shining line behind it.

"It fell!" shouted Karthik. "Eleven!"

"Why did that drop fall, and the others did not?" asked Amma.

Meenakshi thought. "The small ones were too small. They just stayed. When lots of them joined together, the drop got big and heavy. Then it fell."

"That is what a scientist would say," said Amma. "Let us try again, to check it was not luck." Meenakshi wiped the plate dry and sprayed again. This time the first drop ran down at fourteen sprays. The third time, at twelve.

"So the cloud is not a bag?" asked Karthik.

"No bag," said Meenakshi. Now she was the one explaining. "A cloud is all tiny drops, floating. They are too small to fall. Inside the cloud they bump and join, like on the plate. When a drop is big enough, it falls. That is a raindrop."

Karthik frowned. "But how many little drops make one raindrop?"

"About a million," said Amma. "Far more than you could ever count."

Just then there was a soft tapping on the window. Then the tapping became a roar. The rain had come.

The two children pressed their noses to the glass. On the outside of the window, small raindrops sat still, as the mist had on the plate. Then a big drop came racing down from the top. It ran into a small drop and swallowed it, and ran faster. It swallowed another, and another.

"Look, Karthik," said Meenakshi. "The window is doing our plate test."

Karthik watched for a long time. Then he said, "The cloud did not burst. It just let go of its big drops, one by one."

Paati brought them hot sundal in steel bowls. "So the sky did not open after all?" she asked, smiling.

"No, Paati," said Karthik, with his mouth full. "The drops joined."

**For the parent:** A cloud is not a bag of rain. It is millions of tiny drops, too small to fall. Rain falls when many tiny drops join into one drop heavy enough to fall.

- Ask: What did Karthik think a cloud was, at the start?
- Ask: On the plate, why did the tiny drops stay still at first?
- Ask: Why did Amma ask Meenakshi to do the test three times?
- Ask: What did the big drop on the window do to the small drops in its way?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Spray Plate: how many sprays until a drop runs down? (25 min)
- **question:** When tiny drops of mist land on a plate, do they run down straight away? How many sprays does it take before a drop runs down?
- **predict:** When I spray the plate once, will the water run down straight away? How many sprays do you think it will take before a drop runs down? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We stand the plate the same way each time, and spray from the same distance, one ruler length away. We spray once at a time and count every spray. We wipe the plate dry before each try. We do three tries, because one try can be a fluke.
- **record:** Write your guess. Make one tally mark for each spray. Circle the spray when the first drop ran down. Draw the plate after 2 sprays, and just before the drop ran.
- **results table:** Try | My guess: how many sprays? | Sprays until the first drop ran down | What the drops looked like before that
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the plate on its edge on the tray, leaning against a wall. 2. Turn the nozzle to its finest mist. 3. Show your child the plate and the bottle.  
  *Parent says:* I am going to spray a fine mist onto this plate, one spray at a time. Will the water run down straight away? How many sprays will it take? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the plate and the spray bottle. Will the water run down at once? Guess how many sprays. Say why.
  - Ask: How many sprays will it take? Why? (good: One, because water always runs down.; Lots, because the mist is tiny.; A few, because the drops have to get bigger.)
  - Note: Don't say if the guess is right. Many children say one spray, because water always runs down. Write the guess down.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds the bottle one ruler length from the plate. 2. They spray once. 3. You both look closely at the plate from the side.  
  *Parent says:* Spray once. Now look very closely. What is on the plate? Is anything moving?  
  *Child mode:* Spray once. Look very closely at the plate. What do you see? Does anything move?
  - Ask: What do you see on the plate? (good: Tiny dots of water.; The plate looks dull, like when you breathe on it.; Nothing is running down.)
  - Note: After one spray, the plate goes dull and is covered in tiny drops. None of them move. If the bottle shoots a jet, not a mist, turn the nozzle or stand further back.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sprays one spray at a time, with a short wait between. 2. You make one tally mark for each spray. 3. Stop when the first drop runs down. 4. Count the marks together.  
  *Parent says:* Keep spraying, one at a time. I will make a mark for each spray. Watch the drops. Shout stop when one runs down.  
  *Child mode:* Spray again, one at a time. Watch the drops grow. Shout stop when one runs down.
  - Ask: What happened to the tiny drops as you kept spraying? (good: They got bigger.; Two drops touched and became one.; The big drop ran down and took the small ones with it.)
  - Note: Most bottles take somewhere between 5 and 30 sprays, depending on the bottle and the plate. Any number is a real result. Look for drops touching and joining.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wipe the plate completely dry. 2. Do the test again, from the same distance, counting every spray. 3. Dry it and do it a third time. 4. Write all three numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let us do it twice more, the same way, in case the first try was a fluke. Did you get the same number each time?  
  *Child mode:* Wipe the plate dry. Do it again. Then once more. Write your three numbers.
  - Ask: Were the three numbers the same? (good: Close, but not exactly the same.; It always took many sprays, never just one.)
  - Note: The numbers will be close but not the same. That is normal. What matters is that it always takes many sprays before a drop runs down.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and a reason. 2. Then tell them about clouds. 3. Drip one spoonful drop onto the dry plate and watch it run at once.  
  *Parent says:* Why did the first sprays not run down? What had to happen first? Now watch one big drop from a spoon. A cloud is made of drops even tinier than the mist.  
  *Child mode:* Why did the tiny drops stay still? What made one run down? Now watch a big spoon drop.
  - Ask: What has to happen in a cloud before rain falls? (good: The tiny drops have to join into a big drop.; The drop has to get heavy enough to fall.)
  - Note: Wait for your child's reason before you explain. The spoon drop runs down at once, because it is already big and heavy. Tiny drops cling on until many have joined.
  - Check: Why did the tiny drops from the first spray stay on the plate? → **They were too small and light to run down**
### e2 · Raindrops in Atta: are all raindrops the same size? (20 min)
- **question:** Are all raindrops the same size, or are some big and some small?
- **predict:** When we catch raindrops in flour, will all the dough balls be the same size? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We hold the plate out for just 3 seconds, so the drops don't land on top of each other. We use a smooth, flat layer of flour. We sort the balls by size only after they have dried a little.
- **safety:** A grown-up holds the plate out, and the child stays inside the balcony railing or behind the window grill. Don't do this in a thunderstorm with lightning.
- **record:** Draw the three circles. Put the dough balls in the right circle and count them. Write the numbers under each circle.
- **results table:** Size of dough ball | My guess: how many? | How many we found
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the plate with flour, about one finger deep. 2. Smooth it flat with the back of a spoon. 3. Draw three circles on paper: small, medium, big.  
  *Parent says:* Each raindrop that lands in the flour will make a little ball of dough. Will all the balls be the same size? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Each raindrop will make a dough ball. Will they all be the same size? Say why.
  - Ask: Will all the raindrops be the same size? (good: Yes, because they all come from the same cloud.; No, because some drops joined more than others.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is good. Write it down.
- **s2 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the plate out in the rain, flat. 2. Your child counts to three slowly. 3. Bring it in at once.  
  *Parent says:* Count to three with me, slowly. One, two, three. In it comes!  
  *Child mode:* Count to three slowly while the grown-up holds the plate out.
  - Note: In heavy rain, 1 or 2 seconds is enough. In light drizzle, 5 seconds. You want separate dots, not a soggy mess.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave the plate for 20 minutes, so the dough balls firm up. 2. Gently sieve the flour with a tea strainer. 3. Your child sorts the balls onto the three circles and counts them.  
  *Parent says:* Let us sieve out the balls. Put each one in the right circle. Which circle has the most?  
  *Child mode:* Sieve out the dough balls. Put each one in a circle. Count them.
  - Ask: Which size did you find most of? (good: Small ones.; There were many sizes, not just one.)
  - Wait: Leave the plate for at least 20 minutes before sieving.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child why the raindrops were different sizes.  
  *Parent says:* Why were some raindrops big and some small? Think about the spray plate.  
  *Child mode:* Why were some drops big and some small? Think about the plate.
  - Ask: Why are raindrops different sizes? (good: Some drops joined with more drops than others.; A big drop swallowed lots of little ones on the way down.)
  - Note: Raindrops grow by joining, and each drop meets a different number of others, so sizes vary. If it is not raining today, save this for the next rainy day.
  - Check: Why were the raindrops in the flour different sizes? → **Each drop had joined a different number of tiny drops**

1. (match) Draw a line from each word to what it means.

   **Answer:** First picture: many tiny dots. Second picture: fewer, bigger drops, with one starting to run.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the drops getting bigger and fewer as they join.

## Applications
- Weather forecasters watch clouds from satellites to warn people when heavy rain is coming.
- Rainwater harvesting: buildings collect rain from their roofs and send it into the ground or into tanks. Tamil Nadu made this compulsory for buildings in 2003.
- Pilots fly around tall storm clouds, because the air inside them moves up and down very strongly.
- Cloud seeding: scientists test spraying tiny specks into clouds to help their drops join and fall as rain.
- Farmers plan when to sow their seeds by the arrival of the monsoon rains.

- Clouds sitting on the hills of the Western Ghats during the monsoon, in places like Munnar and Mahabaleshwar.
- Mawsynram and Sohra (Cherrapunji) in Meghalaya, two of the rainiest places on Earth. Meghalaya means 'the home of the clouds'.
- Big drops racing down the windows of a bus or an auto in the rain, swallowing smaller drops on the way.
- Dark grey clouds over the Marina beach in Chennai in November, before a northeast monsoon storm.
- Fog on a winter morning in Delhi or Kolkata. Fog is a cloud that sits on the ground.

## Open questions
- **Why do the tiny drops in some warm clouds join so quickly into rain?** Scientists can see that it happens faster than simple bumping would explain. Swirling air inside clouds probably helps, but nobody knows exactly how much.
- **How exactly does lightning start inside a storm cloud?** We know ice bits and drops bump and build up electric charge, but scientists still argue about how the first spark begins.
- **How will the monsoon rains change as the Earth gets warmer?** Warmer air can hold more water vapour, so very heavy rains may become more common. But the monsoon depends on many things, and scientists are still working out what will happen.

## Connected maths
- Revathi did the spray plate test three times. The first drop ran down at 8, 11 and 9 sprays. Which try took the most sprays? Which took the fewest? **The second try took the most, 11 sprays. The first try took the fewest, 8 sprays.**. 11 is bigger than 9, and 9 is bigger than 8. The numbers are close, so every try agrees: it takes many sprays, never just one. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In the card game, Fathima has drop cards of 3 and 4. How many more drops does she need to make a raindrop of 10? **3 more drops.**. 3 and 4 make 7. From 7, count on 8, 9, 10. That is 3 more. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.number-facts.L2`)
- Lakshmi kept a sky book for 4 weeks. Grey sky: 10 days, and it rained on 7 of them. White or clear sky: 18 days, and it rained on 2 of them. On which kind of day was rain more likely? Draw tallies to show it. **Grey days. Rain came on 7 out of 10 grey days, but only 2 out of 18 white or clear days.**. 7 out of 10 is most of the grey days. 2 out of 18 is very few of the white days. So grey skies make rain much more likely, though not certain: 3 grey days stayed dry. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L2`)
- In the flour test, the small dough balls were 2 mm across and the big ones were 6 mm across. How many small balls would fit in a row across one big ball? **3 small balls.**. 2 and 2 and 2 make 6. So 3 small balls in a row are as wide as one big ball. But the big ball holds far more water than 3 small ones, because it is bigger every way: wide, tall and deep. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)

## Warm-up
- What is a cloud made of? → Millions of tiny drops of water, or tiny bits of ice.
- Do tiny drops fall fast or slowly? → Very slowly. That is why clouds float.
- What has to happen before rain falls? → Tiny drops join into drops heavy enough to fall.
- Where does a cloud's water come from? → From water that evaporated from seas, rivers and wet things.
- Which clouds usually bring rain: white or dark grey? → Dark grey ones, because they are thick and hold more water.
- Is a raindrop shaped like a tear? → No. Small raindrops are round. Big ones are flattened like a bun.
