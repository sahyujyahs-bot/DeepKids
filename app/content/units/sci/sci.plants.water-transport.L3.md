# How water climbs a plant, from the roots to the leaves
`sci.plants.water-transport.L3`

**Summary.** A tree has no heart and no pump, yet water reaches the leaves at the very top. Water enters a plant through the roots and climbs the stem in tiny tubes called xylem. Most of it then evaporates out of tiny holes in the leaves. As water leaves the top, it pulls up the water below it, because water molecules attract each other. The Sun's heat makes the water evaporate, so the energy for the lift comes from the Sun. Your child stands a pale cabbage leaf or a white flower in coloured water, and within a day finds the colour in its veins. They also meet Jagadish Chandra Bose, who thought living cells pumped the water up, and the evidence that it is pulled instead.

**Intro.** *Look at a tall coconut palm or a neem tree. Their top leaves need water, but the water is in the soil, far below. Nobody carries it up, and a tree has no heart and no pump. So how does the water get to the top?* Try this today. Stir lots of food colour into half a glass of water. Stand a pale inner leaf of a cabbage in it, thick white rib down, or a white carnation. Look again after a few hours, and the next day. If thin coloured lines appear in the leaf or the petals, the coloured water has climbed up inside. Water makes this journey in every plant, from the roots to the very top leaf.

## Terms
- **Root**: The part of a plant that grows into the soil. It holds the plant firm and soaks up water. *e.g.* Pull up a weed gently. The white threads at the bottom are the weed's roots.
- **Root hair**: A very thin, fuzzy hair on a young root. Each one is a single long cell. Root hairs push between grains of soil and soak up water. *e.g.* Sprout a moong seed on a wet cloth. The white fuzz on their root is root hairs.
- **Absorb**: To soak something up, the way a sponge soaks up water. *e.g.* Roots absorb water from wet soil.
- **Stem**: The part that holds a plant up. It carries water up from the roots to the leaves and flowers. A tree's trunk is a big, woody stem. *e.g.* The long stalk of a rajnigandha flower, or of a balsam plant, is the stem.
- **Transport**: To carry something from one place to another. *e.g.* A truck transports vegetables to the market. A stem transports water to the leaves.
- **Xylem (say it: ZY-lum)**: The tiny water tubes inside a plant. They run from the roots, up the stem, and into every leaf. Most are thinner than a hair. *e.g.* The tough strings in a stick of sugarcane or a banana stem are bundles that hold xylem tubes.
- **Vein**: A line you can see on a leaf or petal. Veins hold the water tubes that bring water to every part of the leaf. *e.g.* Hold a peepal leaf up to the light. The lines are its veins.
- **Evaporate**: When a liquid turns into a gas. Water that evaporates becomes invisible water vapour and mixes into the air. *e.g.* Wet clothes on a line dry because the water evaporates.
- **Water vapour**: Water as an invisible gas in the air. When it cools, it turns back into drops. *e.g.* A cold steel tumbler gets drops on the outside. They come from water vapour in the air.
- **Transpiration (say it: tran-spi-RAY-shun)**: Water leaving a plant's leaves as invisible vapour. As water leaves the top of the plant, it pulls more water up the stem. *e.g.* Tie a clear bag over a leafy branch. After an hour, the inside is misty. That is transpiration.
- **Stoma, stomata (say it: STOH-ma, STOH-ma-ta)**: A tiny hole in a leaf, far too small to see without a microscope. One hole is a stoma. Many are stomata. Water vapour goes out of them, and air comes in. *e.g.* Most of a mango leaf's stomata are on its underside.
- **Guard cells**: Two curved cells around each leaf hole, like two lips. They open the hole or close it. *e.g.* On a very hot, dry afternoon, guard cells often close the holes so the plant loses less water.
- **Wilt**: When a plant goes floppy and droops because they are losing water faster than they can drink. *e.g.* Coriander left out of the fridge on a hot day wilts.
- **Turgid (say it: TUR-jid)**: Firm and stiff because the cells are full of water. Full cells push against each other like water balloons packed in a bag, so stems and leaves stand up. *e.g.* Fresh palak leaves are crisp and turgid. Old ones go limp.
- **Molecule**: The smallest piece of something that is still that thing. A water molecule is made of three atoms: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. *e.g.* Scientists estimate that one drop of water has more molecules than there are grains of sand on all the beaches on Earth.
- **Cohesion (say it: co-HEE-zhun)**: Water molecules attracting each other, so that water holds together. It is why water forms round drops. *e.g.* A drop of water on a leaf stays round, because its molecules hold on to each other.
- **Capillary action (say it: ka-PIL-a-ree)**: Water creeping up into very thin gaps by itself, because it sticks to the sides of the gap. *e.g.* Dip the corner of a tissue in tea. The tea climbs up the tissue.
- **Root pressure**: A small push from the roots that squeezes water a little way up the stem, mostly at night. *e.g.* On some mornings, grass tips have tiny beads of water squeezed out of them.
- **Phloem (say it: FLOW-um)**: The plant's second set of tubes. They carry sugary food made in the leaves to the rest of the plant. This unit is about the water tubes, the xylem. *e.g.* Sugarcane juice is sweet because the cane stores food carried in its phloem.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing, and keep everything else the same. Then you know what caused the difference. *e.g.* Two mint stems in the same bottles of water, one with leaves and one without. Only the leaves are different.
- **Evidence**: What you saw or measured that shows your idea is true, or not true. *e.g.* Blue lines in a white petal are evidence that coloured water climbed up the stem.

## Explanations
### The path of water: root, stem, leaf, air · *defined*
**Definition:** Water enters a plant through the roots. It travels up the stem in tiny tubes. It spreads through the veins to every leaf and flower. Most of it then leaves through tiny holes in the leaves, as invisible vapour.

The path starts in the soil, where root hairs soak up water. The water moves into the root and then up the stem, inside bundles of very fine tubes. In each leaf, the tubes branch out along the veins. From the veins, water soaks into the leaf's cells. It evaporates into tiny air spaces inside the leaf, and the vapour escapes through tiny holes in the leaf's skin. Some water stays behind, to keep the plant firm and to help the leaves make food. The photosynthesis unit tells that part of the story.
- e.g. Water a tulsi pot. The water goes into the roots, not the leaves.
- e.g. A banana leaf's veins run from its thick middle rib out to the edges, carrying water across the leaf.
- e.g. On a hot day a neem tree sends water from deep soil to leaves higher than a house.
### The tubes inside a stem: xylem · *defined*
**Definition:** Xylem is the set of tiny tubes that carry water from the roots to the leaves. They run the whole length of the plant, and into every vein.

A stem is not a solid stick. Inside, it is packed with bundles of tubes. Most of them are thinner than a hair. Each tube is made of cells that have died and lost their insides. Their end walls opened up, so the cells join into long hollow pipes, like cups with no bottoms stacked one on top of another. When you stand a white flower or a cabbage leaf in coloured water and then cut it across, each coloured dot is one bundle of tubes. In a tree, the xylem is the wood itself. In places with a cold or a dry season, the tree adds a new ring of water tubes every year.
- e.g. The tough strings in a stick of sugarcane or a banana stem are bundles of tubes.
- e.g. The rings in a cut tree trunk are layers of old water tubes, one for each year.
- e.g. The lines on a white carnation's petals fill with colour when its stem stands in coloured water for a day.
### Leaves let water out: transpiration · *defined*
**Definition:** Transpiration is water leaving a plant through tiny holes in their leaves, called stomata, as invisible water vapour.

Each leaf is covered in tiny holes, far too small to see. A patch of leaf the size of your little fingernail can have thousands of them. Each hole has two curved guard cells around it, which open and close it. When the holes open, air comes in. The leaf needs a gas from the air, called carbon dioxide, to make its food. But while the holes are open, water vapour escapes too. The plant cannot let carbon dioxide in without letting water out. That is why most of the water a plant takes up goes back into the air.
- e.g. A clear bag tied over a leafy branch turns misty inside within an hour in the sun.
- e.g. A potted plant covered with a clear jar in the sun gets drops on the inside of the jar.
- e.g. A cactus has very few holes, and opens them mostly at night, when the air is cool.
### Pulled up from the top · *defined*
**Definition:** Water is pulled up a plant from the top. As water evaporates from the leaves, it tugs on the water behind it. Because water molecules hold on to each other, the whole thread of water in each tube moves up.

Imagine a long line of children holding hands, standing up a staircase. The child at the top lets go and runs off. Everyone else steps up one stair to close the gap. A new child joins at the bottom. That is how water climbs a plant. Each water tube holds one unbroken thread of water, from the roots to a leaf. When a bit of water evaporates from the leaf, the thread is pulled up a little. New water is soaked up at the roots to fill the bottom. Scientists call this the cohesion-tension idea. Cohesion means water sticking to itself. Tension means a pull, like a stretched rope.
- e.g. A leafy stem pulls water up faster than a stem with its leaves taken off.
- e.g. Trees drink most in the hot, sunny middle of the day, when their leaves lose the most water.
- e.g. Drinking through a straw looks similar, but there the air outside pushes the drink up. In a tree, the water really is pulled from the top.
### An old argument: push, pump or pull? · *debated*
For more than 150 years, scientists argued about how water reaches the top of a tree. Some thought the roots pushed it. Some thought living cells pumped it. Some thought the leaves pulled it. Today, almost all plant scientists agree that the pull from the leaves does most of the work. Sometimes the roots give a small push too. The argument was settled by experiments, not by who was most famous.
- e.g. Cut a grapevine stem in spring and sap drips out of the stump. That drip is a push from the roots.
- e.g. Coloured water still climbs up a stem whose living cells have been killed. So living pumps are not needed.
- **Stephen Hales, an English clergyman and scientist, in his book Vegetable Staticks, 1727:** He weighed potted plants and measured how much water they lost through their leaves each day. He also fixed glass tubes to cut vine stems and saw sap pushed up the tubes. He showed that roots can push, and that leaves lose a lot of water.
- **Eduard Strasburger, a German botanist, in 1891:** He killed the living cells in long stems with poison and heat. Coloured liquid still climbed up them. So whatever lifts the water does not need living cells along the way.
- **Henry Dixon and John Joly, two scientists in Dublin, Ireland, in 1894:** Water is pulled up from the top. Leaves let water evaporate, and that tugs on unbroken threads of water in the tubes. Water molecules cling together so strongly that the threads do not snap. This is the idea accepted today.
- **Jagadish Chandra Bose, an Indian scientist in Kolkata, in his book The Physiology of the Ascent of Sap, 1923:** A layer of living cells in the stem squeezes in a rhythm, like tiny hearts, and pumps the water upwards. He built clever instruments to show plants pulsing. Most scientists were not convinced, and this idea is now rejected. Bose is still honoured for showing that plants sense and respond.
### Why plants wilt, and spring back · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant wilts when they lose water from their leaves faster than their roots can take water in. Their cells go soft, like half-empty water balloons, and the plant droops.

Most living plant cells have a stretchy bag inside, called a vacuole, that fills with water. When all the cells are full, they press hard against each other and against their walls. That pressing makes leaves and soft stems stand up firm. When water runs short, the cells go soft and floppy. Then the stem bends and the leaves hang down. Give a wilted plant water soon, and the cells fill up again. In an hour they can stand tall. But if they stay dry too long, the cells are damaged and they may not recover.
- e.g. Wilted coriander stood in a glass of water perks up within an hour or two.
- e.g. Garden plants often droop in the hot afternoon, then stand up again in the cool evening.
- e.g. A plant pulled up roughly wilts, because their root hairs are torn off and they cannot drink.
### What makes a plant drink faster · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant loses water faster, and so drinks faster, when it is sunny, warm, windy and dry. A plant loses water more slowly when it is dark, cool, still or damp.

Think about drying clothes. They dry fastest on a hot, sunny, windy day, and slowest on a damp, still night. Leaves lose water in the same way. Sunlight also makes most plants open their leaf holes, to let air in for making food. More leaves mean more holes, so a leafy stem drinks faster than a bare one. The chart shows made-up results from the leafy and bare stem test in this unit.
- e.g. A plant on a sunny balcony needs watering more often than one in a shady room.
- e.g. Desert plants like cactus have few leaves, or none, so they lose less water.
- e.g. Farmers in hot places often water in the early morning or evening, when less is lost to the air.
### Why water holds together: cohesion · *defined*
**Definition:** Water molecules attract each other. This is called cohesion. They also cling to the walls of thin tubes. This is called adhesion. Both come from tiny electric forces between molecules.

A water molecule is one oxygen atom with two hydrogen atoms stuck on. It looks a bit like a head with two ears. The oxygen end has a tiny bit of negative electric charge. The hydrogen ends have a tiny bit of positive charge. Opposite charges attract. So the hydrogen end of one molecule attracts the oxygen end of the next. Each molecule holds a few neighbours like this, again and again, all through the water. Scientists call these hydrogen bonds. They are why a drop is round, and why a water strider can walk on a pond. They are also why a thread of water in a tree can be pulled up without snapping.
- e.g. A paperclip laid gently on still water can float, held up by the water's skin.
- e.g. Fill a steel tumbler to the brim, then add drops one by one. The water bulges above the rim before it spills.
- e.g. Water creeps up a tissue because it clings to the paper and to itself.
### The Sun does the lifting · *defined*
**Definition:** The energy that lifts water up a plant comes from the Sun. Sunlight warms the leaves and makes water evaporate. The plant does not spend their own food energy to lift the water.

Lifting water is work. Ask anyone who has carried a bucket up the stairs. Your heart uses energy from your food every second to push your blood round. A tree has no heart. Instead, sunlight warms the wet inside of each leaf, and water evaporates out of the holes. Turning water into vapour takes a lot of energy, and most of it comes from sunlight. The plant does not spend their own food on the lift. Evaporation also cools the leaves, the way sweat drying on your skin cools you. That is part of why the shade of a big tree feels so cool.
- e.g. Sweat drying on your arm cools you. Water leaving a leaf cools the leaf.
- e.g. On a hot day, the air under a big neem or banyan tree feels cooler than the air in a car park.
### Why a straw cannot do what a tree does · *defined*
**Definition:** When you suck on a straw, the air pushing on the drink outside pushes it up the straw. That push can only lift water about 10 metres, even with a perfect pump. Tall trees lift water much higher, so they must work in a different way.

Sucking does not really pull the drink. It takes air out of the straw. Then the air pressing on the drink in the glass pushes it up into the straw. But the air can only push water up about 10 metres, the height of a three-storey building. No suction pump can lift water higher than that. Yet the tallest tree alive, a coast redwood called Hyperion in California, is about 116 metres tall. Their water threads are not pushed up by air. They are pulled from the top and hang together by cohesion, like a rope being pulled up a well. Scientists say the water in a tall tree is under tension, a stretching pull.
- e.g. A simple hand pump at the top of a well can lift water from only about 7 or 8 metres down. Deeper wells need a pump down at the water.
- e.g. A coconut palm can be 25 metres tall. That is already more than twice the straw limit.
### Bubbles in the tubes, and plants that click · *debated*
The water threads in a tree are stretched tight. If the plant gets very thirsty, a thread can snap and a tiny air bubble can form in the tube. Scientists call this an embolism. A tube with a bubble cannot carry water. Flower sellers know about this. They cut flower stems again under water, so no air gets into the fresh tube ends. Snapping threads also make tiny clicks. In 1966, John Milburn and Robert Johnson listened to thirsty castor plants with sensitive sound detectors and heard the clicks. In 2023, a team at Tel Aviv University in Israel, led by Lilach Hadany, recorded thirsty tomato and tobacco plants clicking. The sounds were too high for people to hear. The team recorded them with microphones 10 centimetres from the plants.
- e.g. Cut roses last longer when their stems are trimmed under water.
- e.g. A thirsty tomato plant in the Tel Aviv study clicked dozens of times an hour. A well-watered one hardly clicked at all.
- **Most plant scientists:** The clicks most likely come from water threads snapping and bubbles forming in thirsty tubes.
- **Lilach Hadany's team and others:** Moths, mice or other plants might be able to hear these clicks and learn that a plant is thirsty. Nobody has shown this yet.
- **Other scientists:** The clicks are most likely a side effect of bubbles forming, like the creak of a floor, and not a message. The question is still open.
### Pipes let plants grow tall · *idea*
The first plants on land were small and flat, like mosses today. They had no stiff, strong water tubes. So they had to stay low, close to damp ground, and soak water up through their whole body. Fossils show that by about 430 million years ago some small plants had simple water tubes. Later, plants used a tough material called lignin to make their tubes and stems stiff. Lignin is what makes wood hard. With strong pipes, plants could grow upwards and reach the light above their neighbours. By about 385 million years ago, the first forests of tall trees had grown. Scientists agree on this broad story from the fossils. The exact dates and order keep changing as new fossils are found.
- e.g. Moss on a wall in the monsoon grows only a few centimetres tall.
- e.g. A fern has water tubes, and grows much taller than moss.
- e.g. A banyan tree's hanging roots grow down to the soil and become new pillars that carry water too.
### Forests make rain · *debated*
All the water leaving leaves goes up into the air. Over a forest, it can form clouds and fall again as rain, further along. Scientists have measured this in the Amazon rainforest in South America. The forest sends so much water into the air that some call it a flying river. Plants are a big part of the water cycle. That is the endless trip of water from land and sea, up to the sky, and back. But how big a part? Scientists still argue about that.
- e.g. A big tree on a hot day can send hundreds of litres of water into the air.
- e.g. Cutting down large forests can make the land around them drier.
- **Scott Jasechko and colleagues, in the journal Nature, 2013:** They estimated that plants send up most of the water that rises from land: about 80 to 90 out of every 100 drops. The rest evaporates from soil, lakes and rivers.
- **William Schlesinger and Scott Jasechko, in 2014, after comparing many studies:** After more studies, they gave a lower estimate: about 60 out of every 100 drops. Different methods give different answers.
- **What most agree on:** Plants send a very large share of the land's water back into the sky. The exact amount is still being measured.
### How tall can a tree grow? · *idea*
The higher water has to climb, the harder the pull on the thread. And the harder the pull, the more likely the thread is to snap. In 2004, George Koch and his team climbed the tallest redwood trees in California and studied their top leaves. The top leaves were small and thirsty, as if the water barely reached them. From this they estimated that no redwood could grow much taller than about 122 to 130 metres. The tallest known tree, Hyperion, is about 116 metres. Other scientists think other limits, like wind and getting enough food to the top, also matter. Nobody knows for sure.
- e.g. The tallest kinds of tree, coast redwoods and mountain ash trees from Australia, can grow taller than 90 metres.
- e.g. A redwood's top leaves are much smaller than their lower leaves.
### Plants and bodies: a pull, not a pump · *defined*
**Definition:** Your blood is pushed round your body in a loop by your heart. A plant's water goes one way, from root to leaf, pulled from the top by evaporation. Most of the water tubes are dead cells.

You and a plant both need to move water to every part. Your body uses a living pump, your heart, and living pipes, your blood vessels. The blood goes round and round. A plant uses dead, hollow tubes, and the pull of water evaporating in the Sun. The water goes one way only, up and out. A plant does have a second set of living tubes, the phloem, that carry sugary food both up and down. Both of you cool off in the same way, though. You sweat. A leaf lets water evaporate.
- e.g. When you run, your heart beats faster to push more blood. On a hot day, a plant pulls more water.
- e.g. Blood keeps going round your body. Water leaves the plant and goes into the sky.
### Plants in space, and the water's long journey · *defined*
**Definition:** In space, water does not drip downwards. So astronauts on the International Space Station grow plants in soft pillows with wicks. The wicks feed water to the roots by capillary action.

On Earth, gravity pulls water down into the soil and around the roots. On the space station, everything floats. Water clumps into wobbly balls and can drown roots or float away. NASA's Veggie garden grows lettuce in small pillows of soil that soak up water through wicks, like a cotton thread. The water in your plant also has a very long story. Its hydrogen was made soon after the universe began, about 13.8 billion years ago. Its oxygen atoms were made inside stars that exploded long before the Sun was born. The same water has been through clouds, seas, dinosaurs and trees, over and over.
- e.g. Astronauts on the space station have grown and eaten lettuce they grew there.
- e.g. The water in your glass may once have been inside a dinosaur or a tree in an ancient forest.

## Mostly agreed
- Water travels into a plant through the roots. It goes up the stem in tiny tubes called xylem, and out to the leaves and flowers. *How we know:* Stand a white flower in coloured water. The colour reaches the petals, and a cut stem shows coloured dots where the tubes are. Scientists also see the tubes under microscopes.
- Most of the water a plant takes up leaves through tiny holes in their leaves, as vapour. *How we know:* A bag tied over a leafy branch fills with mist and drops. Stephen Hales weighed potted plants in 1727 and found they lost a lot of water each day. Plant scientists estimate that more than 90 out of every 100 drops a plant takes up go back into the air.
- Water is pulled up from the top, as it evaporates from the leaves. Water molecules hold together in unbroken threads as they rise. *How we know:* Dixon and Joly proposed this in 1894. Since then, scientists have measured the stretching pull inside the water tubes of many plants. Leafy stems pull water up faster than bare ones.
- A plant wilts when they lose water faster than they can take it in. *How we know:* A wilted plant given water stands up again within hours. Plants droop most on hot, dry afternoons.
- Sun, warmth, wind and dry air make plants lose water faster. *How we know:* In the Sweating Branch test, the branch in the sun gives off more water than the one in the shade. Gardeners see it every summer: plants in hot sun and wind need watering more often.

## How we found out
**Stephen Hales, an English clergyman; Eduard Strasburger, a German botanist; Henry Dixon and John Joly, scientists in Dublin, 1727 (Hales), 1891 (Strasburger) and 1894 (Dixon and Joly).** For a long time, people thought water must be pushed up a plant from below. In 1727, Stephen Hales, an English clergyman, showed that roots really can push. He fixed a tall glass tube to a cut grapevine stump, and sap rose up the tube. Many scientists then thought the roots, or living cells in the stem working like tiny pumps, did all the lifting. In 1891, Eduard Strasburger, a German botanist, tested the pump idea. He stood long cut stems in poison, which climbed up them and killed their living cells. Then he stood the dead stems in coloured liquid. The colour still climbed. So no living pump was needed. In 1894, Henry Dixon and John Joly, in Dublin, explained what does the lifting. Water evaporating from the leaves pulls on the water below it, and the water holds together as one unbroken thread. Root pressure is real, but it is far too weak to lift water to the top of a tall tree.  
*What people believed before:* That roots, or living cells in the stem, pump water all the way up a plant. Strasburger killed the cells in long stems, and coloured liquid still climbed them. Root pressure exists, but it is far too weak for a tall tree.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Plants drink mainly through their leaves, so we should pour water over the leaves.** (A common idea, especially when people water plants from above; *rejected*)  
  For: Leaves do get wet in the rain, and a very little water can soak in through leaves.  
  Against: Almost all the water a plant uses comes in through their roots. Water poured on the leaves mostly runs off or evaporates. Leaves that stay wet overnight can even help some plant diseases spread. Water the soil, near the roots.
- **Living cells in the stem pump water up, like tiny hearts.** (Jagadish Chandra Bose, the great Indian scientist, in 1923; *rejected*)  
  For: Bose's very sensitive instruments recorded tiny rhythmic electrical pulses in the living cells of stems. He believed these pulses were the pump.  
  Against: In 1891, Eduard Strasburger killed the living cells in long stems, and liquid still climbed up them. The main water tubes are dead cells anyway. Today almost all plant scientists accept the pulling idea of Dixon and Joly instead. Bose was wrong about this, but right about many other things, like plants sensing touch and injury.
- **The roots push water all the way to the top of a tree.** (An early idea, from the work of Stephen Hales and others; *rejected*)  
  For: Roots really do push. A cut vine drips sap, and beads of water appear on grass tips in the morning.  
  Against: Root pressure is small. It can push water up a few metres, and many trees show none at all in summer, when they need the most water. It cannot lift water 100 metres up a redwood. The pull from the leaves does most of the work.
- **Water climbs a stem the way tea climbs a tissue, by capillary action alone.** (Many children, and some older books; *rejected*)  
  For: Water does creep up thin gaps by itself, and plant tubes are very thin.  
  Against: Capillary action alone can only lift water a short way in tubes as wide as a plant's, much less than the height of a tall tree. It helps water cling to the tube walls, but the big lift comes from the pull of evaporation at the leaves.
- **Plants waste water by letting most of it escape from their leaves.** (A common way of seeing it, especially in dry places; *rejected*)  
  For: Most of the water a plant takes up does go straight back into the air.  
  Against: The plant must open their leaf holes to let in air for making food, and water escapes whenever they are open. The escaping water also cools the leaves, pulls minerals up from the soil, and returns water to the sky as rain. Scientists see it as a price the plant pays, not a waste.
- **Thirsty plants click, and animals or other plants can hear it and learn something.** (Lilach Hadany's team in Tel Aviv, as an idea to test, after their 2023 study; *debated*)  
  For: The clicks are real and were recorded from about 10 centimetres away. Some moths and mice hear sounds this high.  
  Against: Nobody has yet shown any animal or plant listening to these clicks or acting on them. They may just be a side effect of air bubbles forming.

## Roots
We have been following one drop of water from a plant's root to a leaf, and into the air. Now we ask what that journey tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A cut flower has no roots and no pump, yet coloured water still climbs to its petals. Water evaporating from the leaves pulls up the water below, each particle tugging the next. The Sun's warmth pays for this lift, and the plant controls it by opening and closing tiny leaf holes. So a big tree can lift hundreds of litres of water a day without spending their own food.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say a plant 'drinks', as if they sucked water up the way we sip through a straw. But a plant spends none of their food on lifting water. The Sun supplies the energy, and the plant keeps the path open and controls the flow.  
*Evidence:* In the Blue Climb, a cut stem with no roots carried coloured water up to its petals overnight. In Leafy or Bare, the stem with leaves used much more water than the bare one. In the Sweating Branch, the plant in the sun gave off more water than the plant in the shade.

- *who am i:* Sweat cools you as it evaporates from your skin. A leaf is cooled the same way, as water evaporates from it.
- *what is life:* A tree has no heart. They use the Sun's heat to lift their water, and they control the flow with their leaf holes.
- *what is this world:* The water a tree lets out joins the air, and can fall as rain on a farm far away.

A drop of water climbs a tree, leaves as vapour, joins a cloud and falls as rain. Then it may climb another tree, or be drunk by a child. The water does not stay with the tree or with the child. It passes through each of them for a while. The tree, the cloud and the child are less separate than they look, because the same water keeps passing through all three.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.10:** Rivers flow east and west and reach the sea. In the sea, no river can say, 'I am this river.' They are all the sea. (Water vapour from a neem tree and from a mango tree mixes in the same cloud. When it rains, nobody can say which tree each drop came from.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see a cloud in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, without rain no tree, and without the tree no paper. He called this interbeing. (A tree is built with water their leaves pulled up from the soil. So in a leaf you can see the rain that fed it, and the cloud its vapour will help to make.)
- Science (settled): Water is pulled up a plant from the top. As it evaporates from the leaves, it pulls up the water below it, because water molecules attract each other.
- Science (open): How plants repair water tubes that have been blocked by air bubbles is still argued about.
- Other view, The Charvaka thinkers of ancient India, and many scientists today: Water moving from a tree to a cloud to you is just matter moving from place to place. It does not make the tree, the cloud and you any less separate.
- Practice, Breathe like a leaf (2 min): Put a steel tumbler in the fridge for ten minutes. Now hold it near your mouth. Breathe out slowly onto it. Do you see the mist? That is water leaving your body as vapour, like water leaving a leaf. Now sit still and take three slow breaths. Where will that water go next?

- Ask: A tree lets almost all their water go into the air. Is that water lost, or is it going somewhere?
- Ask: If the Sun gives the energy and the plant gives the path, who is really lifting the water?

## Story: Blue Before Morning
*Mullick Ghat flower market under Howrah Bridge in Kolkata is real. Florists in many countries colour white flowers by standing their stems in coloured water. Cutting stems again under water is a real florists' trick to keep air out of the tubes. Rumki, her mother, Rahima Khala and the wedding order are made up.*

Under the great iron bridge in Kolkata, where the river slides past brown and slow, there is a flower market that never sleeps. Marigolds lie in orange hills. Roses come in wet sacks. And at the far end, where the steps go down to the water, Rumki's mother had a stall no wider than a bed.

Rumki was ten. She could tie a marigold garland faster than she could say her eight times table. But on the evening of this story, there was no time for garlands.

A man in a shiny shirt had come to the stall. 'My daughter is getting married tomorrow,' he said. 'She wants blue flowers. Blue like the sky over her grandmother's village. Three hundred stems of rajnigandha, blue, by six in the morning.' He put down more money than Rumki had ever seen her mother hold. 'Can you do it?'

Rajnigandha is white. Their flowers are small and waxy and smell of night. Nobody grows a blue one.

'I can do it,' said Rumki's mother.

Rumki stared at her. Her mother only winked.

That evening they bought three hundred stems from a farmer whose cart had stood in the sun all afternoon. The stems were a little tired. Then Rumki ran to Bhola-da's sweet shop and came back with four small bottles of blue food colour, the kind he used for his sugar birds. They filled two plastic buckets with water, poured in the colour until it was dark as ink, and stood the stems inside. Then they carried the buckets into the little back room behind the stall, where it was warm and quiet, and shut the door so that nobody would knock them over.

'Now the flowers will drink,' said her mother. 'By morning, they will drink the blue right up into their petals.'

Rumki fell asleep on a pile of empty sacks.

When she woke, it was midnight. The market was humming. Her mother was holding a lantern over the buckets, and she looked worried.

The flowers were white. Not a single petal had a speck of blue.

'They are not drinking,' her mother whispered. 'I have done this a hundred times. Why aren't they drinking?'

Rumki looked at the stems standing in the dark blue water. They looked as if they were waiting for something. She thought of the farmer's cart, and the stems lying in a heap in the hot afternoon sun, their cut ends open to the air.

And she thought of Rahima Khala.

Rahima Khala had the stall next to theirs. She made the long white garlands for weddings, and she was very old and very slow, and she did one thing that had always made Rumki laugh. Before she put any flower into water, she held its stem under the water in her big tin tub. Then she cut the end off, there, under the water, with her little curved knife. Never in the air. Always under the water.

'Why do you do that, Khala?' she had asked her once.

'So the stem doesn't swallow air,' she had said. 'A stem is a bundle of tiny pipes, beti, finer than your hair. Inside each pipe is one long thread of water, all the way up to the flower. If air gets in at the bottom, the thread breaks. Then the flower can pull and pull, and nothing comes.'

Rumki had thought Khala was telling her a story. Now she looked at the white flowers, and her heart started to race.

'Ma,' she said. 'Bring the knife.'

So that is what they did, in the middle of the night. Rumki held each stem down under the blue water, and her mother cut a finger's width off the bottom with the kitchen knife. Snip, under the water. Three hundred times. Their arms went blue to the elbow.

But when they had finished, the room was still hot and still, and the air was thick and wet, like the inside of a cooking pot with the lid on. Rumki thought of the washing on the roof. Her mother always said clothes dried fastest in the wind off the river, and slowest on a still, wet day. If the flowers had to let water go, to pull more water up behind it, then they needed somewhere to let it go to.

'The roof,' she said. 'Ma, they need the wind.'

They carried the heavy buckets up the narrow stairs, one by one, slopping blue onto the steps. On the roof, the river wind came off the water, cool and steady, and it moved through the white flowers like a hand through hair. Rumki sat beside the buckets with her knees under her chin. She watched the lights of the bridge. She did not sleep.

At about four o'clock, when the sky was the colour of a pigeon's wing, she saw it. At the very edge of one petal, a thin blue line, as fine as a thread. Then another. Then, as she looked closer, she saw that each petal had tiny blue roads running through it, branching and branching, like the map of a city. The blue was climbing the pipes. It was following the water to the place where the water left, into the wind.

By six o'clock, three hundred rajnigandha stood in the buckets, white at the centre, blue at every edge and every vein, as if someone had drawn them with a pen made of sky.

The man in the shiny shirt came. He looked at the flowers for a long time and said nothing. Then he paid, and he gave Rumki an extra ten rupees, and he carried the buckets to his car very carefully, the way people carry babies.

When he had gone, Rahima Khala shuffled over to look at the blue drips on the stairs. 'Under the water?' she asked.

'Under the water,' said Rumki. 'And then the wind.'

The old woman nodded, as if this was the most ordinary thing in the world. Then she went back to her tub, held a stem under the water, and cut.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the flowers do not take up the colour just because they are standing in it. They take it up because water evaporates from the petals and leaves, and that pulls more water up the stem. Two things stopped that pull in the story. Air got into the cut ends while the stems lay in the sun, and broke the threads of water in the tubes. And the hot, still, damp back room gave the water nowhere to evaporate. Cutting off the ends under water gives each tube a fresh end full of water. The river wind lets the water evaporate. Then the blue climbs, and shows the tubes inside every petal. The story also notices the quiet expert at the next stall, whose habit everyone laughed at.

- Ask: The flowers stood in blue water for hours and stayed white. Why do you think they were not drinking?
- Ask: Why did Rahima Khala always cut stems under the water, never in the air?
- Ask: Why did Rumki carry the buckets up to the windy roof?
- Ask: What did the thin blue lines in the petals show Rumki?
- Ask: Rumki's mother had coloured flowers a hundred times. What was different about these stems?

### a2 · The Water Chain (act it out) (15 min)
- **safety:** Walk slowly on the stairs and hold the railing. Do not pull hard on anyone's arms.
- **record:** Draw the chain of water people on the stairs, with the top one floating off as vapour.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Everyone stands in a line up the stairs, holding hands. The bottom person is at the root. The top person is at the leaf hole.  
  *Parent says:* We are water molecules inside one tiny tube in a stem. We hold hands, because water sticks to itself. The bottom of the stairs is the root. The top is a leaf.  
  *Child mode:* Stand in a line up the stairs. Hold hands. You are water inside a stem. The top of the stairs is the leaf.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The top person lets go and walks away, waving: they have evaporated into the air. 2. Everyone steps up one stair. 3. A new person, or a toy, joins at the bottom. 4. Repeat a few times.  
  *Parent says:* The sun warms the leaf. The top molecule evaporates and floats away. Everyone steps up. A new molecule comes in at the root. That's how water climbs!  
  *Child mode:* The top person waves and floats away. Everyone steps up one stair. Someone new joins at the bottom. Again!
  - Ask: Who pulled everyone up? (good: The one who left at the top.; The sun, by making the top one evaporate.)
  - Note: This is the cohesion-tension idea. Evaporation at the leaf pulls on the whole chain. The chain holds because water molecules cling to each other.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now one person in the middle lets go of hands: they are an air bubble. The top person leaves again. Can the people below the gap step up?  
  *Parent says:* Oh no, an air bubble! Someone in the middle let go. Now when the top drop leaves, can the bottom of the chain still be pulled up?  
  *Child mode:* An air bubble! Someone in the middle lets go. Now the top one leaves. Can the bottom of the chain still move up?
  - Check: In the Water Chain, someone in the middle let go of hands, like an air bubble. What happens to the people below the gap? → **They get stuck, because nothing is pulling them any more**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about how a plant copes with bubbles.  
  *Parent says:* A plant has thousands of tubes side by side. If one gets a bubble, the water can go round it through the others.  
  *Child mode:* A plant has thousands of tubes. If one gets a bubble, water uses the others.

### Follow the Drop (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (order) Put the path of water through a plant in order, from start to end: stem, air, root hairs, leaf, root.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Root hairs soak up the water, and it moves into the root. It climbs the stem and spreads through the leaf. Then it leaves into the air as vapour.

   **Answer:** Thousands of thin hairs touch much more soil, so they soak up more water. *Why:* Water gets in wherever a root touches wet soil. Thousands of thin hairs squeeze between the soil grains and touch far more soil than a few fat roots could. Picking 'stronger' mixes up the job of soaking with the job of holding on.
3. (truefalse) If you pull a seedling out roughly and plant them again, they may wilt, even though their main roots are still there.

5. (truefalse) Most of the water a plant takes in leaves again through tiny holes in their leaves.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. More than 90 out of every 100 drops go back into the air as vapour. This is called transpiration.

The escaping water also cools the leaves. And the holes must be open to let air in, so the leaf can make food.. *Why:* A strong answer says what transpiration does for the plant: it pulls water and minerals up, and cools the leaves. Losing water is the cost of keeping the holes open for air.

   **Answer:** 6 times. *Why:* 18 divided by 3 is 6. So the leafy stem drank 6 times as much. Picking 15 means taking away instead of dividing. 18 take away 3 is 15 mm more, which is also true, but it does not say how many times.
4. (mcq) Fill a steel tumbler to the brim. Then add a few more drops, gently. The water bulges above the rim without spilling. Which idea from this unit does this show?

   **Answer:** Water molecules hold on to each other. *Why:* The water at the top holds on to the water around it, so it bulges instead of spilling. This holding on is called cohesion. It is the same pull that keeps a thread of water in a tree from snapping. Picking 'leaves pull water up' names a true idea, but there are no leaves here.
5. (mcq) Plants need minerals, tiny bits from the soil like nitrogen, to grow. The minerals are dissolved in the soil water. How do they reach the leaves?

   **Answer:** They travel up with the water in the xylem tubes. *Why:* Dissolved minerals ride along with the water, like sugar dissolved in tea. When water is pulled up the xylem, the minerals come too. Picking 'from the air' mixes up minerals with the carbon dioxide that leaves take in.
6. (write) A grown-up splits the bottom of a white flower's stem into two halves. One half stands in blue water and the other in red. Predict what the flower will look like the next day, and explain why.  
   **Answer:** One side of the flower will go blue and the other side red. Each half of the stem has its own tubes, and each tube carries water to its own part of the flower. The tubes do not mix, so the colours stay apart.. *Why:* A strong answer makes a clear guess and gives the reason: the stem is a bundle of separate tubes. Many people first guess purple, because the stem looks like one pipe.
7. (match) Match each person to their idea about how water climbs.  
   **Answer:** Stephen Hales, 1727 → weighed plants and showed leaves lose lots of water; Eduard Strasburger, 1891 → showed water climbs even when stem cells are killed; Dixon and Joly, 1894 → water is pulled up from the top and holds together; J. C. Bose, 1923 → living cells pump water up, like tiny hearts. *Why:* These four scientists each added a piece to the puzzle. Today, the pulling idea of Dixon and Joly is accepted.

Dew and drops on a cold tumbler form from water vapour in the air.
9. (fill) Water creeping up a paper towel or a thin cloth wick is called ____ action.  
   **Answer:** ['capillary']. *Why:* In capillary action, water clings to the thin fibres and to itself, so it creeps up the narrow gaps. It lifts water only a few centimetres, far less than a tree does.
10. (mcq) In the Water Chain play, one person in the middle let go of hands. Why did the people below them stop moving up?  
   Options: They were tired / Nothing was pulling them any more, like a broken thread of water / The roots stopped pushing / The sun went down  
   **Answer:** Nothing was pulling them any more, like a broken thread of water. *Why:* The pull from the top only travels along joined hands. An air bubble breaks a real water thread in the same way. Picking 'the roots stopped pushing' means thinking roots do the main work.
11. (truefalse) Mosses stay small partly because they have no stiff, strong water tubes like a tree's.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Without strong tubes to carry water up, mosses stay low, close to damp ground.
12. (mcq) In Be Stephen Hales, you wrap the pot and soil in a plastic bag before you weigh the plant each day. Why?  
   Options: So water can leave only through the leaves, and the weight loss shows what the leaves let out / To keep the plant warm / So the plant weighs more / To stop insects eating the soil  
   **Answer:** So water can leave only through the leaves, and the weight loss shows what the leaves let out. *Why:* Water can dry off wet soil too. With the soil sealed, any weight the plant loses must be water that went out through the leaves. Stephen Hales did this in the 1720s. Picking 'keep the plant warm' misses that the bag is there to make the test fair.
13. (write) A cut flower in a vase has no roots at all. Yet the water in the vase goes down day by day. How does the flower drink without roots?  
   **Answer:** The water is pulled up from the top. Water evaporates from the leaves and petals, and the water in the tubes is pulled up behind it, because water molecules hold on to each other. Roots are not needed for this pull.. *Why:* This tests the common idea that roots pump water up. A strong answer says the pull comes from water leaving the top, which a cut stem can still do.
14. (perspective) It is a hot, dry afternoon. Two friends argue about what a mango leaf should do with their tiny leaf holes, the stomata.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees the trade-off. Open holes let in the carbon dioxide the leaf needs to make food, and the evaporating water cools the leaf. But open holes also let water escape, and a plant short of water can wilt. Many plants close their holes in the hottest, driest part of the day and open them again when it is cooler. A strong answer gives the other friend's point too.. *Why:* Both friends are partly right. A plant cannot get air in without letting water out. Plants manage this by opening and closing their stomata as the day changes.
15. (write) Imagine a plant with no leaves at all. Would water still climb up their stem? Explain using the Leafy or Bare test.  
   **Answer:** Only a little. In the Leafy or Bare test, the bare stem drank only 3 mm, but the leafy stem drank 18 mm. Without leaves, there is almost nowhere for the water to leave, so almost nothing pulls it up.. *Why:* A good answer uses the evidence from the test and says the leaves do the pulling.
16. (truefalse) Water moves up a plant only in the daytime.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. At night the leaf holes are mostly closed and it is cool, so water moves up much more slowly. But it does not stop completely. Roots can even push a little water up at night, which is why you may find drops at the tips of grass blades at dawn.
### Puzzles of the Climbing Water (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) In 1891, Eduard Strasburger killed all the living cells in long stems, and coloured water still climbed up them. Which idea did this show to be wrong?

   **Answer:** Living cells must pump water up the stem. *Why:* If living pumps were needed, a dead stem could not lift water. But it did. So living pumps are not needed. Picking 'pulled by evaporation' misses that a dead stem can still let water evaporate from its top.

   Options: The Sun / The soil / The tree's food  
   **Answer:** The Sun. *Why:* Sunlight makes water evaporate from the leaves, and that does the pulling. The tree does not spend their own food energy to lift water. Picking 'the soil' mixes up where the water comes from with what lifts it.

## Applications
- Flower sellers and florists cut stems under water and keep them in buckets, so cut flowers keep drinking.
- Some coloured flowers sold for weddings and festivals are white flowers whose stems stood in coloured water.
- Farmers water in the early morning or evening, when less water escapes into the air.
- Drip irrigation sends water straight to the roots, so less is wasted.
- Buried clay pots and cotton wicks water plants slowly in dry places.
- Gardeners trim some leaves off a plant when moving them, so the plant loses less water while their roots settle.
- Greenhouses and plastic tunnels keep the air damp, so young plants lose less water.
- Trees planted along streets cool cities, partly by letting water evaporate from their leaves.
- Keeping forests standing helps keep rain falling on farms downwind of them.
- Weather forecasters and climate scientists include water from plants in their models of rain.
- Scientists measure the sap flow in trees with tiny heat sensors, to see how thirsty a forest is.
- Farmers and scientists breed crops that close their leaf holes sooner, to survive dry spells.
- Astronauts grow food on the space station using wicks, because water does not drip down in space.
- Wood is mostly old water tubes and fibres. The grain that carpenters follow runs along them.
- Sugarcane juice is water and sugar stored in the cane's stem.

## Open questions
- **How do trees fix their water tubes after an air bubble blocks them?** Some studies suggest plants can refill blocked tubes, even while the water is under tension. Others think the measurements were fooled by the way the stems were cut. Scientists still argue.
- **How tall could a tree ever grow?** Water sets one limit, estimated at about 122 to 130 metres for redwoods. Wind, weight and getting food to the top may set others. Nobody can test it by waiting a thousand years.
- **Does anything listen to the clicks of thirsty plants?** The clicks were recorded in 2023. Whether moths, mice or other plants use them is unknown.
- **Exactly how much of the rain over land comes from water that plants send up?** Different ways of measuring give answers from about 60 to 90 out of every 100 drops rising from land.
- **How does a plant decide when to close their leaf holes?** Signals come from the leaves, the roots and the water in the tubes. How a plant weighs them all together is still being studied.
- **Do living cells next to the water tubes help the water move, as Bose once thought, even a little?** The main pull is from the leaves. But living cells next to the tubes may help refill them or steer water, and that is still being studied.
- **How did the very first water tubes evolve in plants, more than 400 million years ago?** Early fossils are tiny and rare. New finds keep changing the story.
- **How will hotter, drier weather change the world's forests?** In droughts, trees can get so many air bubbles in their tubes that they die. Scientists are trying to predict which forests are most at risk.

## Connected maths
- In the Blue Climb, the colour climbed 3 cm up a balsam stem in the first hour. If it kept climbing at the same speed, how high would it be after 4 hours? Look at the line chart. **12 cm.**. Each hour adds 3 cm. After 4 hours that is 4 lots of 3 cm. 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12, or 4 × 3 = 12. So the colour would be 12 cm up the stem. Real stems may speed up or slow down, which is why we measure instead of only working it out. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Leafy or Bare test, the leafy stem's water was at 95 mm on the ruler at the start. Two days later it was at 77 mm. The bare stem's went from 95 mm to 92 mm. How much did each one drop? How much more did the leafy stem drink? **The leafy stem's water dropped 18 mm and the bare stem's 3 mm. The leafy stem drank 15 mm more.**. For the leafy stem, 95 take away 77 is 18 mm. For the bare stem, 95 take away 92 is 3 mm. Then 18 take away 3 is 15. So the leafy stem's water went down 15 mm more. 18 divided by 3 is 6, so it drank 6 times as much. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)
- Look at the ruler in the picture. It measures the water level in the Leafy or Bare bottle. The big numbers are centimetres. The small marks between them are millimetres. What is the reading, in millimetres? **77 millimetres, which is 7 centimetres and 7 millimetres.**. The water is past the 7 cm mark, and 7 small marks further on. Each centimetre has 10 millimetres, so 7 cm is 70 mm. 70 + 7 = 77 mm. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- In Be Stephen Hales, a plant was weighed each day. The plant weighed 512 g on Monday, 480 g on Tuesday, 466 g on Wednesday and 428 g on Thursday. How much water did they let out each day? Draw a bar chart. Which day did the plant lose the most? **32 g, then 14 g, then 38 g. They lost the most from Wednesday to Thursday.**. Take each day's weight from the day before. 512 take away 480 is 32. 480 take away 466 is 14. 466 take away 428 is 38. Draw a bar for each. The tallest bar, 38 g, is Wednesday to Thursday. In the made-up example, that was the windy day. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- A big tree lets out about 200 litres of water on a hot day. A bucket holds 10 litres. How many buckets is that? How many buckets in one week of hot days? **20 buckets in a day, and 140 buckets in a week.**. 200 divided by 10 is 20, so 20 buckets in a day. A week has 7 days. 20 × 7 = 140. So 140 buckets in a week, all lifted by the Sun, with no pump and no heart. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)

## Warm-up
- Which part of a plant soaks up water? → The roots, with their tiny root hairs.
- What are the water tubes in a stem called? → Xylem.
- A white flower stands in blue water for a day. What happens? → Its petals get blue edges and lines, because the water climbs up the stem.
- Water leaving the leaves as vapour is called ____. → Transpiration.
- Does a leafy stem or a bare stem drink more? → The leafy stem.
- Sun or shade: where does a branch give off more water? → In the sun.
- Why do flower sellers cut stems under water? → To stop air bubbles blocking the tubes.
- Why does a plant wilt? → They lose water faster than they can drink.
- What holds a thread of water together in a tube? → Water molecules holding on to each other, called cohesion.
- How high can sucking lift water, at most? → About 10 metres. Trees lift it much higher by pulling from the top.
- Which Indian scientist thought living cells pump water up stems? → Jagadish Chandra Bose. Most scientists now think water is pulled up from the leaves instead.
