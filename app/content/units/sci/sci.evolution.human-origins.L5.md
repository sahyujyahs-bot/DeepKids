# Where humans came from: many kinds of humans, and what old bones and DNA tell us
`sci.evolution.human-origins.L5`

**Summary.** Today there is only one kind of human on Earth. A hundred thousand years ago there were at least five, and they lived at the same time. Where did the others go? In this lesson you will feel four clues on your own skull, and follow one ancestor's DNA down the generations with squared paper, a coin and number chits. You will see why most people alive today carry small pieces of Neanderthal DNA, and why your pieces differ from your neighbour's. You will also weigh a question that scientists still argue about: what, if anything, makes our kind different?

**Intro.** *If you had walked across Asia 100,000 years ago, you could have met at least five different kinds of humans. Today you would meet only one kind, us. What happened to the other four?* For a long time the answer seemed simple: they died out, and nothing of them was left. Then scientists learned to read DNA from bones tens of thousands of years old. The DNA showed something nobody could have seen in the bones alone. Our ancestors met some of these other humans and had children with them, and pieces of their DNA are still inside most people alive today. In this lesson you will find out who the other humans were, how a bone or a speck of bone powder can tell us about them, and what is still unknown. India has a part in this story too, and a big gap in it.

1. By the end I can say what a fossil bone or tooth can tell us about a person, and what it cannot.

## Terms
- **Homo sapiens**: Homo sapiens is the scientific name of our own kind of human. Every person alive today is Homo sapiens. *e.g.* The oldest known fossils on our line come from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco. They are about 315,000 years old.
- **Neanderthal**: Neanderthals were a kind of human who lived in Europe and western and central Asia. They were gone as a separate people by about 40,000 years ago. *e.g.* A Neanderthal had a long, low skull, a thick ridge of bone over the eyes and no chin. Their brain was as big as ours.
- **Denisovan**: Denisovans were a kind of human who lived in Asia. They were first recognised from the DNA in one finger bone, not from the shape of any bone. *e.g.* The finger bone was found in 2008 in Denisova Cave in Siberia. That cave gave the Denisovans their name.
- **Species**: A species is one kind of living being. One common test is that members of a species can have young together, and those young can have young too. *e.g.* Tigers are one species and lions are another. The test does not always give a clear answer, as the Neanderthals show.
- **Fossil**: A fossil is a remain or a trace of a living being from long ago, kept in rock or soil. A bone, a tooth and a footprint can all be fossils. *e.g.* The Narmada skull, found at Hathnora in Madhya Pradesh in 1982, is a fossil of an ancient human.
- **DNA**: DNA is the long molecule in every cell that carries the instructions for building and running a body. The instructions are written in four chemical letters: A, T, C and G. *e.g.* Your DNA has about 3 billion letters. You got one set from your mother and one set from your father.
- **Genome**: A genome is the complete set of DNA instructions of one living being. *e.g.* The first rough copy of a Neanderthal genome was read in 2010 from three bones found in Vindija Cave in Croatia.
- **Ancient DNA**: Ancient DNA is DNA taken from the remains of a being who died long ago. It is broken into very short pieces and mixed with the DNA of microbes. *e.g.* The oldest DNA read from any human relative comes from bones in a Spanish cave. They are about 430,000 years old.
- **Chromosome**: A chromosome is one long thread of DNA. Human cells have 46 chromosomes, in 23 pairs. One of each pair came from the mother and one from the father. *e.g.* In the Shrinking Pieces Test, each row of 20 squares stands for one chromosome.
- **Crossing over**: Crossing over happens when a body makes an egg cell or a sperm cell. The two chromosomes of a pair swap parts, so the child gets a chromosome that is part one grandparent's and part the other's. *e.g.* Because of crossing over, a long piece of an ancestor's DNA is cut shorter in each generation.
- **Mitochondrial DNA**: Mitochondrial DNA is a small separate loop of DNA in the mitochondria, the parts of a cell that release energy. A child gets it only from the mother. *e.g.* Denny's mitochondrial DNA was the Neanderthal kind. That told scientists that her mother was a Neanderthal.
- **Interbreeding**: Interbreeding means that members of two different groups have children together. *e.g.* About 47,000 years ago, our ancestors and Neanderthals interbred. Their children were part of our ancestors' families.
- **Contamination**: Contamination means that something from outside has got into a sample. In ancient DNA work, it means DNA from microbes or from living people has got mixed in. *e.g.* A person who picks up an old bone with bare hands leaves skin cells on the bone. Those cells carry today's human DNA.
- **Collagen**: Collagen is the main protein in bone. Proteins are long chains built by cells, and they last longer in old bones than DNA does. *e.g.* The collagen of a human bone differs a little from the collagen of a hyena bone. A machine can tell them apart from a tiny chip.
- **Extinct**: A kind of living being is extinct when no members are left alive. *e.g.* Homo floresiensis, a small human from the island of Flores in Indonesia, is extinct. No one has found their DNA in living people.
- **Hybrid**: A hybrid is a child whose parents came from two different kinds or groups. *e.g.* Denny, who lived about 90,000 years ago, had a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father.
- **Cumulative culture**: Cumulative culture means that each generation learns what the last one knew and then adds to it. Skills build up over time. *e.g.* Nobody could invent a bicycle alone from nothing. The wheel, the chain and the rubber tyre were each worked out by different people long ago.

## Explanations
### Several kinds of humans lived at the same time · *defined*
**Definition:** For most of the last 300,000 years, our species shared the Earth with other kinds of humans. We have been the only kind for only the last 40,000 years or so.

People often draw human evolution as one line: an ape, then a bent figure, then an upright one, then us. That picture is wrong. The human family was a branching bush, and many branches were alive at once. Our own species, Homo sapiens, began in Africa. The oldest known fossils on our line are about 315,000 years old. At that time Neanderthals lived in Europe and western Asia, and Denisovans lived further east. A small-brained human called Homo naledi lived in southern Africa. On the island of Java, Homo erectus lived on until about 110,000 years ago. On two other islands lived two very small kinds of humans, Homo floresiensis and Homo luzonensis. Why does this matter? Because it changes the question. We should not ask how one line led to us. We should ask why, out of so many kinds, only one is left. Nobody has a full answer yet.
- e.g. About 100,000 years ago there were at least five kinds: Homo sapiens, Neanderthals, Denisovans, Homo floresiensis and Homo luzonensis.
- e.g. The last Homo erectus known, from Ngandong in Java, lived between 117,000 and 108,000 years ago.
- e.g. In the activity Who Lived When, you draw each kind as a bar on a time line and count how many bars overlap.
### Neanderthals · *defined*
**Definition:** Neanderthals were a kind of human who lived across Europe and as far east as Siberia. They made fire and tools, cared for their injured, and were gone as a separate people by about 40,000 years ago.

The first Neanderthal bones to be recognised were found in 1856 in the Neander Valley in Germany. A schoolteacher, Johann Carl Fuhlrott, saw that they were not from a person of today. A Neanderthal skull is long and low, with a thick ridge of bone over the eyes and a bulge at the back. The jaw has no chin. The body was short, broad and very strong. For many years people pictured Neanderthals as dull brutes. The evidence does not support that picture. Their brains were as big as ours, and on average a little bigger. They made fine stone tools, kept fires and probably wore coverings of animal skin. Most scientists accept that they buried at least some of their dead. One man from Shanidar Cave in Iraq had a crushed eye socket, a withered right arm and damaged legs. His bones show that he lived for years after these injuries. The scientists who studied him think he could not have done that alone. Other Neanderthals must have fed and protected him. Why were they gone by 40,000 years ago? Scientists have several ideas, and you will meet them in another card.
- e.g. Neanderthal skulls held brains of about 1,460 to 1,640 cubic centimetres. Brains of people today are about 1,300 cubic centimetres on average.
- e.g. The Shanidar man lived with a useless arm and one damaged eye. His injuries had healed, so they happened long before he died.
- e.g. Red marks in three Spanish caves were dated to more than 64,000 years ago and credited to Neanderthals. Other scientists doubt the dating.
### Denisovans: a people first known from DNA · *defined*
**Definition:** Denisovans were a kind of human who lived in Asia. Scientists found out that they existed by reading the DNA in a finger bone, before anyone knew what a Denisovan looked like.

In 2008, Russian archaeologists found a small piece of a girl's finger bone in Denisova Cave, in the Altai Mountains of Siberia. Nobody could tell from the shape whose it was. In 2010 Svante Pääbo's team in Leipzig, Germany, read the DNA. The DNA was not that of our species, and not Neanderthal. The girl belonged to a group nobody had known about. This was the first time a kind of human was discovered from DNA alone. For years the only Denisovan remains were that finger bone and a few teeth. Then other fossils were matched to them, mostly by proteins. Proteins last longer than DNA. A jaw from a cave about 3,300 metres up on the Tibetan Plateau was matched in 2019. A jaw dredged from the sea floor near Taiwan was matched in 2025. In the same year a nearly complete skull from Harbin in China was matched too, using proteins and DNA from the hardened plaque on a tooth. So Denisovans finally have a face: a large, long skull with a heavy brow ridge. They lived from cold Siberia to the high plateau and probably far into Southeast Asia. Scientists have still not agreed on a species name for them.
- e.g. The Xiahe jaw from the Tibetan Plateau is at least 160,000 years old. A monk found it in 1980, and nobody knew what it was for almost 40 years.
- e.g. Many Tibetan people carry a version of a gene called EPAS1 that helps the body cope with thin mountain air. The version came from Denisovans.
- e.g. The Ayta Magbukon people of the Philippines carry about 5 percent Denisovan DNA, the highest share known.
### The small humans of the islands, and Homo naledi · *idea*
**Definition:** Homo floresiensis and Homo luzonensis were very small humans who lived on islands in Southeast Asia. Homo naledi was a small-brained human from southern Africa. Where each came from is still argued about.

In 2003, a team led by Thomas Sutikna and Mike Morwood dug up a skeleton in Liang Bua cave on the island of Flores in Indonesia. She was an adult woman only about 1.06 metres tall, with a brain about the size of a chimpanzee's. Her kind was named Homo floresiensis. They made stone tools and lived on Flores until about 50,000 years ago. How did they come to be so small? Most scientists think their ancestors were larger humans who reached the island more than 700,000 years ago. On small islands with little food, large animals often become smaller over many generations, because a small body needs less food. Some scientists think instead that they came from an older, smaller kind of human. In 2019 another small human, Homo luzonensis, was named from Callao Cave on Luzon in the Philippines. Homo naledi was found in 2013 deep inside the Rising Star cave in South Africa. Six women scientists who could squeeze through the narrow passage brought out more than 1,550 pieces of bone. Homo naledi had a brain less than half the size of ours and lived only about 300,000 years ago. So big brains were not the only way to be human at that time.
- e.g. The elephant relatives called Stegodon also became small on Flores. Island life changed them and the humans in the same direction.
- e.g. Homo luzonensis had small teeth and curved finger and toe bones. At least three individuals are known.
- e.g. Lee Berger's team says Homo naledi buried their dead in the cave. Most other scientists who reviewed the evidence were not convinced.
### What a fossil can tell us, and what it cannot · *defined*
**Definition:** A fossil bone or tooth can show a person's size, age, injuries and way of moving. It cannot show their skin, hair, words or thoughts.

A fossil is a small part of a body, and scientists read it closely. The length of a thigh bone gives the height. Teeth show the age, because teeth grow in a fixed order and lay down thin growth lines. Healed breaks show injuries that the person lived through. The shape of the hip and the place where the spine joins the skull show how the person walked. But soft parts rot, so a fossil is silent about skin, hair, voice and thoughts. Fossils are also very rare. A body becomes a fossil only if it is buried quickly and the minerals in the ground are right. This is where India's gap comes in. India has stone tools that are more than a million years old. Those at Attirampakkam near Chennai, studied by Shanti Pappu and Kumar Akhilesh, are about 1.5 million years old. So ancient humans certainly lived here. Yet almost none of their bones have been found. The main one is the Narmada skull, found by the geologist Arun Sonakia on 5 December 1982. She was probably a woman in her thirties. Scientists still argue about how long ago she lived and about which kind of human she was. No DNA has been read from her, so that question stays open.
- e.g. Feel your own chin. A chin that sticks out is found only in our species, so a jaw with a chin tells scientists the jaw is Homo sapiens.
- e.g. Scratches on teeth and chemicals in bone show what a person ate. Neanderthals in cold places ate a lot of meat.
- e.g. The tool makers of Attirampakkam left thousands of hand axes. Nobody knows which kind of human they were, because no bones were found with the tools.
### How DNA is read from old bones · *defined*
**Definition:** After death, DNA breaks into short pieces and slowly falls apart. Scientists collect the short pieces from bone powder, read them, and fit them together by where they overlap.

In a living cell, each chromosome is one unbroken thread of DNA, millions of letters long. Living cells repair their DNA all the time. After death the repair stops. Water reacts with the DNA and cuts the thread, again and again. After thousands of years the pieces are often shorter than 50 letters. Why do cold places matter? These cutting reactions are chemical reactions, and chemical reactions go faster when it is warm. So DNA lasts longest in cold, dry caves and frozen ground. That is one reason the richest finds come from Siberia and Europe, and why no ancient human DNA has yet been read from India's oldest fossils. There is a second problem. Most of the DNA in an old bone is not from the person. It comes from microbes that lived in the bone after death, and from every person who touched the bone. So the work is done in clean rooms, by people in suits, masks and gloves. The pieces are read by machines. A computer then lines them up against a known human genome and uses overlaps to put them in order. Each place is read many times, so that one damaged piece cannot mislead. Where no DNA is left, proteins such as collagen can still help, because they last longer.
- e.g. In the activity The Shredded Message, you rebuild a sentence from short overlapping pieces, with pieces of a different sentence mixed in.
- e.g. In the activity Warm or Cold, the same powder fizzes away faster in warm water than in cold. DNA also breaks down faster in warmth.
- e.g. The oldest DNA from a human relative is about 430,000 years old. It comes from a cave in Spain called Sima de los Huesos.
### What the old DNA showed: the kinds of humans mixed · *defined*
**Definition:** DNA from old bones shows that our ancestors had children with Neanderthals and with Denisovans, and that Neanderthals and Denisovans had children with each other.

In 2010, Pääbo's team compared the Neanderthal genome with the genomes of people living today. People whose ancestors lived outside Africa shared a little more DNA with Neanderthals than people from Africa south of the Sahara did. The best explanation is that Neanderthals and our ancestors had children together after some of our ancestors left Africa. Today about 2 percent of the DNA of people outside Africa came from Neanderthals. Later studies found that people across Africa also carry a little, about 0.3 percent. It was probably carried back by people who returned to Africa. Denisovan DNA is found too. It makes up about 5 percent in some peoples of the Philippines, New Guinea and Australia, and a small trace across South and East Asia. A study of 2,762 people in India, published in 2025, found that 1 to 2 percent of their DNA came from Neanderthals and Denisovans. The clearest proof of mixing is a girl nicknamed Denny, who lived about 90,000 years ago. Half of her DNA was Neanderthal and half was Denisovan. Her mother was a Neanderthal and her father a Denisovan. Why is this certain? At place after place in her DNA, one copy was the Neanderthal kind and the other was the Denisovan kind. That pattern appears only in a child of the first generation.
- e.g. A man who lived about 40,000 years ago in what is now Romania had 6 to 9 percent Neanderthal DNA. He had a Neanderthal ancestor only four to six generations before him.
- e.g. Any two people today are about 99.9 percent the same in their DNA. A person today and a Neanderthal are about 99.7 percent the same.
- e.g. In the activity Whose Child, you read pairs of DNA markers to work out who a mystery child's parents were.
### Why the Neanderthal pieces are short, and different in each person · *defined*
**Definition:** Crossing over cuts an ancestor's DNA into shorter pieces in every generation, and chance decides which pieces are passed on. So each person keeps only a few small pieces, and different people keep different ones.

Think back to the Shrinking Pieces Test. The first child had a whole red chromosome from the red ancestor. In each new generation, crossing over swapped part of that chromosome for a white part. The red piece got shorter, and in many family lines the red was lost completely. But different lines kept different pieces. The same has happened to Neanderthal DNA for about 47,000 years. That is why no person today has a whole Neanderthal chromosome. Each person outside Africa has thousands of short pieces, adding up to about 2 percent. Your pieces are not the same as your neighbour's. When scientists add up the different pieces carried by many people, a large part of a Neanderthal genome appears. A study of people in Iceland recovered about 41 percent. The 2025 study in India recovered about half. The length of the pieces is also a clock. Long pieces mean the mixing was recent, and short pieces mean it was long ago. That is how scientists worked out that most of the mixing happened about 47,000 years ago, over about 7,000 years. The paper test is simplified in one way. In the test the share halved every generation, because every partner was all white. In real life, after the mixing, nearly everyone in the group carried some pieces. So the share stopped falling quickly and stayed near 2 percent, while the pieces kept getting shorter.
- e.g. Some Neanderthal pieces helped. Several affect how the body fights germs, and they became common.
- e.g. Some pieces seem to have done harm and were lost. Long stretches of our DNA, including much of the X chromosome, have almost no Neanderthal pieces.
- e.g. The man from Romania had very long Neanderthal pieces. That is how scientists knew his Neanderthal ancestor lived only a few generations before him.
### Were Neanderthals a different species? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists do not agree on whether Neanderthals and our ancestors were two species or two groups within one species.

A common test of a species is that members can have children together, and those children can have children too. Neanderthals and our ancestors did that, and so did Denisovans. By that test, all three were one species. Yet their skulls and bodies were clearly different, and the groups had lived apart for more than 500,000 years. There is also a sign that the mixing did not always go well, because some parts of our DNA hold almost no Neanderthal pieces. Why can't the question be settled? Because a species is a way of sorting that people made. Living beings change slowly, and two groups that are drifting apart pass through a long time when they are neither fully one nor fully two. Neanderthals and our ancestors met during that in-between time.
- e.g. Lions and tigers can have cubs together in zoos, yet everyone counts them as two species.
- e.g. Some scientists write Homo neanderthalensis, a separate species. Others write Homo sapiens neanderthalensis, a group within our species.
- e.g. Denisovans have no agreed scientific name at all.
- **Scientists who study the shapes of bones, such as Ian Tattersall and Chris Stringer:** Neanderthals were a separate species, Homo neanderthalensis. Their skulls and skeletons differ from ours as clearly as those of other close species. A little mixing does not erase that.
- **Scientists who use the test of having children together:** Neanderthals, Denisovans and our ancestors had healthy children whose descendants are alive today. So they were groups within one species.
- **Svante Pääbo, who read the Neanderthal genome:** The argument cannot be settled, because there is no single definition of a species that fits every case. It is more useful to describe what happened than to argue about the label.
### What makes us human? · *debated*
**Definition:** There is no agreed answer to what sets our kind of human apart. Almost every ability once called 'only human' has been found in other humans or in other animals.

People have offered many answers: making tools, using fire, speaking, making art, burying the dead, caring for the weak. The evidence has taken away most of them. Chimpanzees and crows make tools. Neanderthals used fire, made glue from birch bark and cared for injured friends. So scientists now look for differences of amount, not of kind. One strong idea is cumulative culture. We teach, the learner improves the skill a little, and teaches it again. In a real experiment in 2015, Thomas Morgan and his team asked 184 people to learn to make simple stone tools. Some could only look at finished tools. Some could watch a maker. Some were taught with words. The skill passed along a chain of learners well only when there was teaching, and best of all with words. Another idea is that our ancestors lived in larger groups and kept in touch with friends far away, so good ideas were not lost. A third idea looks at DNA. A small number of DNA changes are shared by nearly everyone today and missing in Neanderthals and Denisovans. Scientists are testing what those changes do. Nobody knows yet whether any of them matters much. The honest answer is that we differ from other humans and other animals in degree, and nobody knows whether any single thing explains it.
- e.g. In the Teaching Test, you compare how well a paper fold is learned by watching alone and by watching with words.
- e.g. Neanderthals carried the same version of FOXP2, a gene needed for speech, as people today. Whether they spoke as we do is not known.
- e.g. The one clear feature of the skeleton that only our species has is the chin. Nobody is sure what the chin is for.
- **Charles Darwin, in The Descent of Man (1871):** The difference in mind between humans and other animals is great, but it is a difference of degree and not of kind.
- **Scientists who study culture, such as Kevin Laland and Thomas Morgan:** What sets us apart is teaching and language working together. They let skills build up over generations until no one person could invent them alone.
- **Scientists who stress symbols, such as Ian Tattersall:** Our species thinks in symbols, such as words, pictures and numbers. This way of thinking is new and special to us, and it appeared late and quite suddenly.
- **Scientists who study Neanderthal sites, such as Francesco d'Errico and João Zilhão:** Neanderthals used colours and ornaments and may have made marks on cave walls. Symbols were not ours alone.
### Why are we the only humans left? · *debated*
**Definition:** Nobody knows for certain why the other kinds of humans disappeared. The main ideas are changing climate, small numbers, competition with our ancestors, and being absorbed by mixing.

Neanderthals had lived through many ice ages before our ancestors arrived in Europe. Within about ten thousand years of that arrival, they were gone as a separate people. What happened? The first thing to know is that there were never many Neanderthals. Their DNA shows little variety, which means their groups were small and thinly spread. A small group can die out by bad luck alone: a few hard winters, a sickness, too few babies. Our ancestors came in larger numbers. They may have taken the best hunting grounds without any fighting. The climate was also swinging between cold and mild at that time. And some Neanderthals joined our ancestors' families, so part of their people was absorbed. Most scientists think several of these causes worked together. For the Denisovans and the small island humans, there is even less evidence.
- e.g. Studies of Neanderthal DNA suggest only a few thousand breeding adults at a time across all of Europe and western Asia.
- e.g. Homo floresiensis disappears from Liang Bua about 50,000 years ago, close to when our species reached that region. Nobody knows whether the two met.
- e.g. Toss 4 coins, and all 4 land tails about once in 16 tries. A group of four families can have that kind of bad luck. A group of four hundred almost never does.
- **Scientists who stress numbers, such as Krist Vaesen:** Neanderthal groups were so small that chance, with no help from us, could have ended them.
- **Scientists who stress competition, such as Chris Stringer:** Our ancestors arrived in larger, better connected groups and slowly took over the food and the land.
- **Scientists who stress mixing:** Neanderthals did not only die out. Many were absorbed into our ancestors' much larger population, and their DNA lives on.

## Mostly agreed
- Our species, Homo sapiens, began in Africa. *How we know:* The oldest fossils on our line are African, such as those from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, about 315,000 years old. People in Africa also have the most variety in their DNA, which is what an oldest home looks like.
- Several kinds of humans lived at the same time as our species. *How we know:* Dated fossils of Neanderthals, Denisovans, Homo naledi, Homo floresiensis, Homo luzonensis and late Homo erectus all fall within the last 335,000 years.
- Our ancestors had children with Neanderthals and with Denisovans. *How we know:* Genomes read from old bones match short pieces of DNA carried by living people. People outside Africa carry about 2 percent Neanderthal DNA.
- Neanderthals and Denisovans had children with each other. *How we know:* The genome of Denny, read in 2018 from a bone 2.5 centimetres long, is half Neanderthal and half Denisovan.
- All people alive today are one species, and very alike. *How we know:* Any two people differ in about one DNA letter in a thousand. That is less variety than is found within chimpanzees.
- DNA breaks down with time, and faster in warm places. *How we know:* The oldest genomes come from frozen ground and cold caves. Very little old DNA has been recovered from hot, wet regions.

## How we found out
**Matthias Krings, Svante Pääbo, Richard Green and their team at the Max Planck Institute in Leipzig, Germany, 1997 to 2010.** In 1997, Matthias Krings and Svante Pääbo read the first Neanderthal DNA. It came from the arm bone of the first Neanderthal ever recognised, found in Germany in 1856. They could read only a small piece of mitochondrial DNA, the loop that passes from mother to child. It was unlike the mitochondrial DNA of any living person. Many scientists concluded that Neanderthals had died out without leaving descendants. But mitochondrial DNA is a tiny part of a genome, and a family loses it whenever a mother has no daughters. So Pääbo's team spent years learning to read the whole genome from old bones. Most of the DNA in the bones came from microbes, and the human pieces were short and damaged. In 2010, Richard Green and the team published a first rough Neanderthal genome, read mostly from three bones from Vindija Cave in Croatia. People outside Africa shared more of it than people in Africa south of the Sahara did. Neanderthals and our ancestors had mixed. Pääbo received a Nobel Prize in 2022.  
*What people believed before:* That Neanderthals died out without leaving any descendants. Reading the whole genome showed that people outside Africa carry between 1 and 4 percent Neanderthal DNA. Later work put the figure at about 2 percent.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Neanderthals were dull, stooping brutes.** (Marcellin Boule, a French scientist, who described a Neanderthal skeleton from 1908 onwards; *rejected*)  
  For: The skeleton he studied was bent, and the heavy brow and jaw looked rough to people of his time.  
  Against: The man had been old and ill, which bent his bones. Neanderthals stood as upright as we do, had large brains, made fire and tools, and cared for their injured.
- **Neanderthals died out without leaving any descendants.** (Many scientists before 2010, after the first small piece of Neanderthal DNA was read in 1997; *rejected*)  
  For: The first DNA read was mitochondrial DNA, and no living person has the Neanderthal kind.  
  Against: The whole genome, read in 2010, showed Neanderthal pieces in people outside Africa. Mitochondrial DNA is one tiny part, passed on only by mothers, and can be lost by chance.
- **Neanderthals painted on cave walls.** (Dirk Hoffmann, Alistair Pike and others, in 2018; *debated*)  
  For: Thin mineral crusts over red marks in three Spanish caves were dated to more than 64,000 years. That is before our species is known to have reached Spain.  
  Against: Other scientists say the crust that was dated may not lie directly over the paint, so the paint could be much younger.
- **Homo naledi buried their dead and scratched marks on the cave wall.** (Lee Berger and his team, in 2023; *minority*)  
  For: Bodies lay in hollows deep in a cave that is very hard to reach, and there are crossed lines on a wall nearby.  
  Against: The scientists who reviewed the reports said the evidence did not show that the hollows were dug or that the marks were made by Homo naledi.
- **Modern humans arose in many regions at once, from the older humans living in each region.** (Milford Wolpoff, Alan Thorne and Wu Xinzhi, from 1984; *rejected*)  
  For: Some features of old skulls in Asia look a little like those of people living there now.  
  Against: DNA shows that almost all the ancestry of every living person goes back to Africa within the last 300,000 years. Older regional humans added only a few percent.

## Roots
We have learned that several kinds of humans once lived together, and that some of their DNA is still in living people. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Shrinking Pieces Test, most family lines lost every red square, yet different lines kept different red pieces. Neanderthals ended as a separate people about 40,000 years ago. But most people alive carry small pieces of their DNA, and together the pieces make up about half a Neanderthal genome. So a kind of human can end as a group and still go on, in pieces, inside another.  
*Habit it breaks:* We use the word 'extinct' to mean completely gone, and we speak of 'them' and 'us' as two separate peoples. The DNA shows that the Neanderthals' ending and our own history overlap. Some Neanderthals were among our ancestors' mothers and fathers.  
*Evidence:* In the Shrinking Pieces Test, no single line kept much red, but the lines together kept more. In 2010 the Neanderthal genome showed that people outside Africa carry Neanderthal pieces. In 2025, scientists joined the pieces carried by 2,762 people in India and recovered about half of a Neanderthal genome.

- *what is life:* Life does not keep its kinds in sealed boxes. Two groups drift apart slowly, and for a very long time they can still meet and become one family again.
- *what is this world:* For most of our species' time, the world held several kinds of humans at once. A world with only one kind of human is new and unusual.

Every kind of human came from one earlier people in Africa. That one people became many: Neanderthals in the west, Denisovans in the east, small humans on islands, and our own ancestors in Africa. Each kind met the world with their own bodies and their own minds, and each of them thought, felt and decided. Then some of the many met, and their children joined them into one family again. Both the parting and the joining are written in your cells.

## Story: One Bone in Two Thousand
*This is a true story. The cave, the dates, the people, the number of bone pieces, the collagen test, the result and the checks are all real. Viviane Slon has said that her first thought was that she had made a mistake, and Svante Pääbo has said he first thought the laboratory had slipped up. The order in which the bone pieces were tested is not known, so the count before the find is imagined. What Samantha Brown and Viviane Slon were thinking and feeling while they worked is imagined, and so are the words in quotation marks.*

In 2012, archaeologists digging in Denisova Cave, in the mountains of Siberia, lifted a splinter of bone out of the earth. It was about as long as the top joint of your thumb. It had no shape that anyone could name. It went into a bag with other splinters like it.

The cave had given up thousands of such splinters. Most were bits of animals that had lived and died around the cave. A few, perhaps, were human. But a splinter of a deer and a splinter of a person look the same to the eye. So the bags were labelled 'unidentifiable' and put away.

In 2015, some of those bags travelled to the University of Oxford in England. Two scientists there, Katerina Douka and Tom Higham, had a plan. A student named Samantha Brown took it on as the project for her master's degree.

The plan used collagen, the main protein in bone. Collagen is a long chain, and the chain is a little different in each kind of animal. A machine can weigh the broken bits of the chain very exactly. The pattern of weights works like a fingerprint. A hyena gives one pattern, a horse gives another, and a human gives a third.

So Samantha could find a human bone without knowing its shape. She only had to take a tiny chip from every splinter, treat it, and send it through the machine at the University of Manchester. There were 2,315 splinters.

She began. Chip, label, test. The first pattern was an animal. So was the second. So was the hundredth.

Weeks went by. Five hundred splinters. A thousand. Every one an animal. A project like this can end with nothing at all, and a student has only so much time. 'Maybe there is no human in these bags,' she thought. She picked up the next splinter anyway.

Then one pattern was different. It was the pattern of the human family. The splinter was number DC1227. It was 2.5 centimetres long and weighed less than two grams. It was the only human bone among the 2,315.

The collagen test could say 'human'. It could not say which kind of human. For that, the splinter had to go to Leipzig in Germany, to the laboratory that had read the first Neanderthal genome.

There the scientists drilled out a little bone powder in a clean room. First they read the mitochondrial DNA. This is the small loop of DNA that a child gets only from the mother. It was the Neanderthal kind. So the bone seemed to belong to a Neanderthal.

A young scientist named Viviane Slon went on to read the rest of the DNA, the part that comes from both parents. The result made no sense. About half of the pieces matched Neanderthals. The other half matched Denisovans, the people known from a finger bone in the same cave.

Viviane's first thought was not 'what a discovery'. It was 'what did I do wrong?' Had powder from two bones been mixed? Had something got into the sample? In this kind of work, a strange result is usually a mistake.

So she tested the result before she trusted it. She took bone powder again, six samples in all, and prepared each one separately. All six gave the same answer: half and half.

Then she looked at how the two halves were arranged. Remember that a child has two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent. If this person's family had been mixed long ago, the two kinds of DNA would lie in patches. Some places would have two Neanderthal copies, and some would have two Denisovan copies.

That was not the pattern. At place after place, all along the chromosomes, there was one Neanderthal copy and one Denisovan copy. Only one kind of family gives that pattern. One parent was a Neanderthal, and the other parent was a Denisovan.

The mitochondrial DNA had already said which was which. It comes only from the mother, and it was Neanderthal. So the mother was a Neanderthal, and the father was a Denisovan.

The bone could tell a little more. Its thickness showed that the person was at least 13 years old. The DNA showed that she was a girl. She had lived about 90,000 years ago. The team gave her a nickname, Denny.

Her DNA held two more surprises. Her mother was more closely related to Neanderthals who lived far to the west, in what is now Croatia, than to an earlier Neanderthal from her own cave. So Neanderthals had travelled thousands of kilometres across Europe and Asia. And her Denisovan father had a Neanderthal ancestor of his own, hundreds of generations before him. So Denny's parents were not the first such pair.

When Svante Pääbo, the head of the laboratory, first saw the result, he also thought someone had made a mistake. Then he saw the checks. The team published the genome in 2018.

Nobody knows what Denny's real name was, or what words she heard from her mother and her father. Everything known about her came from a splinter that had been put away as unidentifiable. It was found because one student tested every piece.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn in this story is the question 'mistake, or real?' Half Neanderthal and half Denisovan DNA could come from a mix-up, from a long-mixed family, or from a child with one parent of each kind. Viviane Slon separated these by repeating the test six times and then by looking at how the DNA was arranged. In a first-generation child, every pair of chromosomes has one copy from each parent, all the way along. This is the same idea as the Shrinking Pieces Test: unbroken stretches mean the mixing was very recent. The story also shows two habits of good science. Samantha Brown tested all 2,315 pieces when most would have stopped. Viviane Slon doubted her own surprising result first.

- Ask: A splinter of deer bone and a splinter of human bone look the same. How can collagen tell them apart?
- Ask: Why did the mitochondrial DNA, taken alone, give only half of the answer?
- Ask: Viviane Slon's first thought was that she had made a mistake. Why is that a good first thought for a scientist?
- Ask: How does the pattern 'one copy of each kind, all the way along' show that the parents were of two different kinds?
- Ask: Denny's father also had a Neanderthal ancestor. What does that tell you about how often these peoples met?

### e1 · The Shrinking Pieces Test: what happens to one ancestor's DNA over the generations? (35 min)

  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the three ways of passing on the skill: looking only, watching, and watching with words. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* One learner only looks at your finished cup. One watches you fold in silence. One watches while you explain. Who will do best? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three learners, three ways. Guess who will make the best cup. Say why.
  - Ask: What might a learner miss if nobody says anything? (good: That the flap's top edge must be level.; Where exactly the corner should touch.; Which way to fold the last flaps.)
  - Note: With only two learners, leave out 'looking only'. Compare watching in silence with watching with words.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Learner 1 looks at the finished cup for one minute. They may turn it over but not unfold it. 2.   *Parent says:* Each learner comes in alone, so nobody learns from watching the others. You show the fold only once. Then start the timer.  
  *Child mode:* Bring in one learner at a time. Show the fold once, in that learner's way. Time them. Test the cup with 15 channa.
  - Note: Keep the other learners in another room. Staying silent is hard, so remind your child before learner 2 comes in. Write down which fold went wrong, because that is the most useful result.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why. 2. Have them write the claim, the evidence and the reasoning. 3. Only then read the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me your claim. Which results support it? Why do you think that way of passing on the skill worked best?  
  *Child mode:* Write your claim. Write what each learner managed. Write why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Which fold went wrong most often? Could it be learned by watching alone? (good: The second fold. You can see it, but not what matters about it.; Words can say 'keep this edge level', and watching cannot.)
  - Ask: Is one learner for each way enough to be sure? (good: No. One person may just be good at folding.; We should test more people, or swap the learners with a new fold.)
  - Note: After your child has answered, explain. In 2015, Thomas Morgan and his team taught 184 people to chip simple stone tools. The skill was passed down chains of learners. Report what you found.
  - Check: What can spoken teaching pass on that silent watching cannot? → **Which details matter and why**

## Worksheets
### Many Kinds of Humans (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) About how many kinds of humans were alive 100,000 years ago?  
   Options: Only one / At least five / Exactly two / None yet  
   **Answer:** At least five. *Why:* Our species, Neanderthals, Denisovans, Homo floresiensis and Homo luzonensis were all alive then. Choosing 'only one' follows the old picture of a single line.
2. (fill) The kind of human who lived in Europe until about 40,000 years ago is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['Neanderthal', 'Neanderthals']. *Why:* Neanderthals lived across Europe and western and central Asia. They are named after the Neander Valley in Germany.
3. (truefalse) True or false: the kinds of humans came one after another in a single line, never two at once.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dated fossils show several kinds alive at the same time. The family is a branching bush.
4. (match) Match each kind of human to where their fossils were found.  
   **Answer:** Neanderthals → Europe and western Asia; Denisovans → Siberia, Tibet and eastern Asia; Homo floresiensis → Flores, in Indonesia; Homo naledi → South Africa; Homo luzonensis → Luzon, in the Philippines. *Why:* Each kind is known from a different part of the world. Only our species spread to every continent.
5. (mcq) Which feature of the skeleton is found only in our species?  
   Options: A large brain / A chin that sticks out / Legs for upright walking / A thumb  
   **Answer:** A chin that sticks out. *Why:* Only Homo sapiens has a chin that sticks out. Neanderthals had brains as large as ours, and all these humans walked upright and had thumbs.
6. (sort) Sort each one: can a fossil bone or tooth show it?  
   **Answer:** How tall the person was → A bone or tooth can show it; How old the person was → A bone or tooth can show it; An injury that healed → A bone or tooth can show it; The person's favourite song → A bone or tooth cannot show it; The language the person spoke → A bone or tooth cannot show it; The person's skin colour → A bone or tooth cannot show it. *Why:* Bones and teeth record size, age and injuries. Soft parts, words and thoughts leave nothing in bone.
7. (truefalse) True or false: Neanderthals had smaller brains than people today.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Neanderthal brains were as large as ours, and a little larger on average.
8. (fill) The complete set of DNA instructions of one living being is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['genome']. *Why:* A human genome has about 3 billion DNA letters. The first rough Neanderthal genome was read in 2010.
9. (write) In the Shrinking Pieces Test, the red piece got shorter down each block. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Each new row was made by crossing over. One side was copied from the row above, and the other side came from a white partner. So part of the red was swapped for white in each generation, and it could never grow back.. *Why:* A strong answer names crossing over, and says that only one side is copied from the family's chromosome.
10. (mcq) Why is ancient DNA found mostly in cold places?  
   Options: Ancient humans lived only in cold places / DNA breaks down more slowly in the cold / Cold makes new DNA / Hot places have no caves  
   **Answer:** DNA breaks down more slowly in the cold. *Why:* The reactions that cut DNA go more slowly in the cold. Ancient humans lived in hot places too, as India's stone tools show, but their DNA has not lasted.
11. (order) Put these in order, from longest ago to most recent.  
   **Answer:** ['The oldest known fossils on our line', 'Denny, the girl with parents of two kinds, lives', 'Most of the mixing between our ancestors and Neanderthals', 'Neanderthals are gone as a separate people', 'The first Neanderthal genome is read']. *Why:* The fossils are about 315,000 years old. Denny lived about 90,000 years ago, the mixing was about 47,000 years ago, Neanderthals were gone by about 40,000, and the genome was read in 2010.
12. (draw) Draw a Neanderthal skull and a skull of our species from the side. Label two differences.  
   **Answer:** The Neanderthal skull is long and low, with a thick brow ridge, a sloping forehead and no chin. Ours is tall and round, with a smooth brow, an upright forehead and a chin.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows at least two of the four clues: chin, brow, forehead and the shape of the skull.
13. (mcq) About how much of the DNA of people outside Africa came from Neanderthals?  
   Options: About 2 percent / About 50 percent / None at all / All of it  
   **Answer:** About 2 percent. *Why:* It is about 2 percent, in thousands of short pieces. Choosing 50 percent mixes up people today with a child of the first generation.
14. (truefalse) True or false: a child gets mitochondrial DNA only from the mother.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Mitochondria are passed on in the egg cell. That is why this DNA can show who the mother's family was.
15. (write) India has stone tools more than a million years old. Why do we not know who made them?  
   **Answer:** Stone lasts, but bones rarely become fossils, and almost no bones have been found with the tools. India is also warm, so no ancient DNA has been recovered. Without bones or DNA, the tools cannot be matched to a kind of human.. *Why:* A strong answer says that tools show someone was there, and that only bones or DNA can say who.
### Reading Old DNA (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)

   **Answer:** One Neanderthal and one Denisovan. *Why:* Each parent gave one copy at every place. One could give only the Neanderthal kind and the other only the Denisovan kind.

   **Answer:** Contamination → DNA from outside mixed into a sample; Collagen → the main protein in bone; Hybrid → a child of parents from two kinds; Extinct → no members left alive; Interbreeding → two groups having children together. *Why:* These are the main words for reading old DNA. A strong answer can also give an example of each.
7. (sort) Sort each finding: was it learned from the shape of bones, or from DNA?  
   **Answer:** Neanderthals had thick brow ridges → From the shape of bones; Denny's mother was a Neanderthal → From DNA; Homo floresiensis was about a metre tall → From the shape of bones; People outside Africa carry Neanderthal pieces → From DNA; A Neanderthal man lived on after bad injuries → From the shape of bones; Denisovans were a group of their own → From DNA. *Why:* Bones show bodies and injuries. DNA shows who was related to whom. The full story needs both.
8. (odd) Three of these make ancient DNA hard to read. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: It is in very short pieces / Microbe DNA is mixed in / People who touched the bone left DNA / It has more than four letters  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Ancient DNA has the same four letters as all DNA. The other three are the real difficulties.
9. (write) Why do scientists who read ancient DNA wear suits, masks and gloves?  
   **Answer:** Their own skin, hair and breath carry human DNA. If it reached the bone powder, it would be read along with the ancient DNA and could be mistaken for it. The suit, mask and gloves keep it out.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the danger is the scientist's own DNA, and that it looks very like the DNA being studied.
10. (order) Put the steps of reading ancient DNA in order, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['Drill a little powder from inside the bone', 'Collect the short DNA pieces from the powder', 'Read the letters of each piece by machine', 'Line the pieces up where they overlap', 'Check each place against many pieces']. *Why:* The sample is taken first, then the pieces are read, then a computer puts them in order. Checking comes last, because one damaged piece could mislead.
11. (truefalse) True or false: people in Africa carry no Neanderthal DNA at all.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A 2020 study found a little, about 0.3 percent. It was probably carried back by people who returned to Africa.
12. (perspective) Two children argue about Neanderthals.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side, or says the question cannot be settled, and gives at least one piece of evidence.    Options: DNA from a finger bone / A complete skeleton / Paintings in a cave / A set of stone tools  
   **Answer:** DNA from a finger bone. *Why:* The DNA in a finger bone matched no known kind of human. No skull was linked to Denisovans until years later.
14. (label) Label a drawing of two chromosomes crossing over.  
   **Answer:** ['Chromosome from the grandmother', 'Chromosome from the grandfather', 'The place where they cross', 'The new mixed chromosome']. *Why:* The new chromosome is part one grandparent's and part the other's. The crossing place is different every time.

   **Answer:** Proteins last longer than DNA. *Why:* Proteins such as collagen break down more slowly than DNA. They do break down in the end, so 'never' is wrong.
### Evidence, Doubt and What Makes Us Human (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Scientists knew Denisovans existed before they had a skull. What could DNA tell them, and what could it not?  
   **Answer:** DNA could tell them that Denisovans were a separate group, who their nearest relatives were, and that they had children with Neanderthals and with our ancestors. It could not show their faces, their height, their tools or how they lived.. *Why:* A strong answer gives at least one thing on each side.
2. (mcq) In the paper test the red share kept halving. Why does the real Neanderthal share stay near 2 percent?  
   Options: Neanderthal DNA copies itself faster / Later partners carried pieces too / People still meet Neanderthals / The paper test was wrong in every way  
   **Answer:** Later partners carried pieces too. *Why:* In the paper test every partner was all white. In real life, after the mixing, most people in the group carried some pieces, so the average stopped falling quickly.
3. (perspective) Three children argue about what makes our kind of human different.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one view, or combines two, and gives evidence. It also names one thing that does not fit the view chosen.. *Why:* Scientists hold all three views. Language helps teaching, but nobody knows whether Neanderthals spoke. Big groups keep ideas alive. And nearly every single ability has been found elsewhere.
4. (truefalse) True or false: making tools is something only our species does.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Chimpanzees, crows and several earlier kinds of humans make tools.

6. (write) A new fossil jaw is found in India. Name three tests scientists could do, and what each could show.  
   **Answer:** They could study its shape, for example whether it has a chin, to judge the kind of human. They could date the layer it lay in to find its age. They could test its proteins, and look for DNA, to see who its relatives were.. *Why:* A strong answer names shape, dating and proteins or DNA, and says that DNA may not have lasted in the heat.
7. (odd) Three of these are ideas about why Neanderthals disappeared. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Their groups were very small / The climate kept changing / Our ancestors came in larger numbers / Their brains were too small  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Neanderthal brains were as large as ours. The other three are ideas that scientists take seriously.
8. (mcq) A fossil's mitochondrial DNA is the Neanderthal kind. What does that prove, taken alone?  
   Options: That both parents were Neanderthals / That the father was a Neanderthal / Only that the mother's line was Neanderthal / That the fossil is our species  
   **Answer:** Only that the mother's line was Neanderthal. *Why:* Mitochondrial DNA comes only from the mother. Denny's was Neanderthal, yet her father was a Denisovan. The rest of the genome is needed for the father's side.

One arrow from Neanderthals to our branch and one from Denisovans to our branch, or one between Neanderthals and Denisovans.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows branches that overlap in time, and arrows that cross between them.

   **Answer:** ['cumulative']. *Why:* Cumulative means building up. It is one leading idea about what helped our kind of human.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which statement about the human family is correct?  
   Options: Each kind turned into the next / Several kinds lived at the same time / Only one kind ever existed / Humans came from chimpanzees  
   **Answer:** Several kinds lived at the same time. *Why:* Fossils show several kinds alive together. Humans and chimpanzees share an ancestor, and neither came from the other.
2. (mcq) On which continent did Denisovans live?  
   Options: Asia / South America / Antarctica / Australia  
   **Answer:** Asia. *Why:* Denisovan fossils come from Siberia, the Tibetan Plateau and eastern Asia. No other kind of human reached the Americas or Antarctica.
3. (mcq) What can the teeth in a fossil jaw tell a scientist?  
   Options: What language they spoke / The person's name / Their favourite food by taste / About how old the person was  
   **Answer:** About how old the person was. *Why:* Teeth come through in a fixed order and have growth lines, so they show age. Words and names leave no trace in bone.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a fossil skull can show what a person thought about.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A skull shows the size and shape of the brain's space, not the thoughts. Thoughts leave no fossil.
5. (mcq) What happens to DNA after a living being dies?  
   Options: It slowly breaks into short pieces / It grows longer / It turns into bone / It stays whole for ever  
   **Answer:** It slowly breaks into short pieces. *Why:* Repair stops at death, and water slowly cuts the DNA. After thousands of years the pieces are very short.
6. (mcq) Where does most of the DNA in an old bone usually come from?  
   Options: Microbes that lived in the bone / The person who died / Plants growing nearby / The rock around the bone  
   **Answer:** Microbes that lived in the bone. *Why:* Microbes move into a bone after death and leave their DNA. The person's own DNA is often a small part, which is why the work is hard.
7. (mcq) In the Shrinking Pieces Test, what made the red piece shorter in each new row?  
   Options: The red colour fading / Crossing over with a white chromosome / The paper wearing out / A counting mistake  
   **Answer:** Crossing over with a white chromosome. *Why:* Each new row copied only one side from the row above. The other side came from a white partner, so part of the red was swapped away.
8. (mcq) Two people each carry about 2 percent Neanderthal DNA. How do their pieces compare?  
   Options: They are exactly the same pieces / One of them must have none / Both have a whole Neanderthal chromosome / They are mostly different pieces  
   **Answer:** They are mostly different pieces. *Why:* Chance decided which pieces each family line kept. That is why the pieces of many people together cover so much more of the genome.
9. (mcq) Which evidence shows that our ancestors had children with Neanderthals?  
   Options: Neanderthal tools in museums / Neanderthal DNA pieces in living people / Old stories about giants / Neanderthal bones in Europe  
   **Answer:** Neanderthal DNA pieces in living people. *Why:* Tools and bones show that Neanderthals existed. Only shared DNA shows that children were born between the two groups.
10. (mcq) A bone's DNA has one Neanderthal and one Denisovan copy all along every chromosome. What does that show?  
   Options: One parent of each kind / A mistake in the laboratory, always / Two Neanderthal parents / A long-mixed family  
   **Answer:** One parent of each kind. *Why:* Each parent gives one copy of every chromosome. Unbroken copies of two kinds mean the parents themselves were of the two kinds. A long-mixed family would show patches.
11. (mcq) Which of these is found only in our species, as far as we know?  
   Options: Using fire / Making tools / Caring for the injured / Writing  
   **Answer:** Writing. *Why:* Neanderthals used fire, made tools and cared for injured friends. Writing is only about 5,000 years old.
12. (mcq) Which of these is still not known?  
   Options: Whether Neanderthals existed / Whether children get DNA from parents / Which kind of human made India's oldest stone tools / Whether our species began in Africa  
   **Answer:** Which kind of human made India's oldest stone tools. *Why:* India's oldest tools have no bones with them and no DNA has lasted, so the makers are unknown. The other three are well supported by evidence.

## Applications
- Doctors study the Neanderthal and Denisovan pieces in people's DNA. Some pieces change how the body fights infections, so they help explain why people respond differently to the same illness.
- The 2025 study of 2,762 people in India mapped these old pieces along with newer changes. Maps like this help doctors look for the causes of diseases that are common in Indian families.
- Police laboratories identify people from tiny, damaged traces of DNA. Many of their methods for short and damaged DNA were first worked out on ancient bones.
- Clean rooms and blank samples, which you tried in The Clean Sample, are used wherever a tiny trace matters: in hospital test laboratories, in forensic laboratories and in making computer chips.
- The collagen test that found Denny's bone is used by archaeologists to name thousands of bone scraps quickly. It shows which animals people hunted and herded.
- Scientists who protect endangered animals read DNA from old museum skins and bones. It shows how much variety a species has lost.
- Doctors who treat mountain sickness study the EPAS1 gene, which many Tibetan people got from Denisovans, to understand how a body copes with thin air.

## Spotted around us
- Look in a mirror and press the bump of your chin. You are looking at the one bony feature that no other kind of human had.
- In a museum, look for stone hand axes made long ago.
- A stone hand axe in a museum picture. People made hand axes about 1.5 million years ago. No bones were found with them, so nobody knows which kind of human made them.
- The top of an ancient human skull, found in 1982 on a river bank, in a museum picture.
- Paintings of animals and dancers on rock walls, painted by people of our own species thousands of years ago.
- Food left out in a summer kitchen spoils in hours, and the same food lasts for days in the fridge. Cold slows chemical reactions, and that is also why DNA lasts in cold caves.
- Watch a grandmother teach a kolam, or a mother teach how to pleat a sari. Notice how much is passed on by words, not only by showing.
- Tibetan families living in the mountains. Many Tibetan people carry a gene version for thin mountain air that came from Denisovans.
- In a family photograph, look for a feature that skipped a child. Each child gets a different half of each parent's DNA, which is the same chance you saw in the Shrinking Pieces Test.

## Open questions
- **Why is our species the only kind of human left?** Small numbers, changing climate, competition and mixing may all have played a part. The evidence cannot yet say how much each one mattered.
- **Which kinds of humans lived in India before our species, and who made the oldest tools here?** India has many very old stone tools and almost no old human bones. No ancient human DNA has lasted in the heat.
- **Could Neanderthals and Denisovans speak as we do?** Speech leaves no fossil. They had the same version of one speech gene, but many genes and much learning are needed for language.
- **Where did the small island humans, Homo floresiensis and Homo luzonensis, come from?** No DNA or protein has been recovered from them. From bones alone, scientists cannot agree which older humans were their ancestors.
- **Do the few DNA changes that only people today share make any difference to how we think?** The list of changes is known. What each change does in a growing brain is only beginning to be tested.
- **Were there other kinds of humans whom nobody has found yet?** The DNA of living people in Africa and Asia holds pieces that match no known fossil. Some scientists read these as traces of unknown groups.

## Connected maths
- In one family line of the Shrinking Pieces Test, the red squares in rows 1, 2 and 3 were 20, 8 and 3 out of 20. Write each as a fraction of the chromosome and as a percentage. **Row 1 is 20 out of 20, which is 100 percent. Row 2 is 8 out of 20, which is two fifths, or 40 percent. Row 3 is 3 out of 20, or 15 percent.**. Each square is one twentieth of the chromosome, and one twentieth is 5 percent. So 8 squares are 8 × 5 = 40 percent, and 3 squares are 3 × 5 = 15 percent. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- Suppose an ancestor's share of DNA exactly halved in every generation, starting from 100 percent. After how many halvings would it first fall below 2 percent? The mixing with Neanderthals was more than 1,500 generations ago. Why is the real share not close to zero? **After 6 halvings the share is about 1.6 percent, which is below 2 percent. The real share is not close to zero because the halving stopped once nearly everyone in the group carried some pieces.**. 100, 50, 25, 12.5, 6.25, 3.125, 1.5625. That is 6 halvings. Halving happens only when the other parent has none of the ancestor's DNA. After a few generations of mixing, both parents carried pieces, so a child lost some and gained some. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A human genome has about 3 billion DNA letters. About 2 percent of the genome of a person outside Africa came from Neanderthals. About how many letters is that? **About 60 million letters.**. 1 percent of 3,000,000,000 is 30,000,000. So 2 percent is 2 × 30,000,000 = 60,000,000, which is 60 million. A small percentage of a very large number is still a large number. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- On the Who Lived When time line, 1 centimetre is 10,000 years. The bar for our species is 31.5 centimetres long. The Neanderthal bar ends 4 centimetres from today. For how many years did both kinds live at the same time? About what fraction of our species' time is that? **275,000 years. That is about seven eighths of our species' time.**. 31.5 − 4 = 27.5 centimetres, and 27.5 × 10,000 = 275,000 years. As a fraction, 27.5 out of 31.5 is about 0.87, which is close to seven eighths. So we shared the Earth with Neanderthals for most of our history. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- Most of the mixing between our ancestors and Neanderthals happened about 47,000 years ago. If one generation is about 25 years, how many generations ago was that? In the Shrinking Pieces Test you did 4 generations of crossing over. How many times more is the real number? **About 1,880 generations ago. That is 470 times as many as in the test.**. 47,000 ÷ 25 = 1,880. Then 1,880 ÷ 4 = 470. After so many rounds of crossing over, the Neanderthal pieces in people today are very short. (queues `math.division.L5`)
- In row 5 of the Shrinking Pieces Test, line A kept squares 3, 4 and 5. Line E kept squares 12 and 13. The other four lines kept nothing. What fraction of the chromosome is red in the All Together row? Could the All Together row ever have less red than the best single line? **5 out of 20, which is one quarter. No, the All Together row can never have less red than the best single line.**. 3 + 2 = 5 squares, because the two pieces do not overlap, and 5 out of 20 is one quarter. Every red square in any line is also red in the All Together row. So it has at least as much red as the best line, and more whenever the pieces sit in different places. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
