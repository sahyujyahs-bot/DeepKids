# Questioning the evidence: says who, and how do they know?
`sci.how-we-know.questioning-evidence.L3`

**Summary.** People can feel very sure and still be wrong. You will test this at home: ask your family questions about things in your own flat, ask how sure they are, and then go and check. Then you will learn three questions to ask about anything you hear: Says who? How do they know? Who is missing? They help you tell a claim that was checked from a story that only sounds true.

**Intro.** *Kavya's aunt says, "Our wall clock has all twelve numbers on it. I see it every day." Kavya's uncle says, "No, it only has 12, 3, 6 and 9." Both of them are completely sure. They cannot both be right.* Feeling sure did not settle it. Looking at the clock did. In this unit you find out how often people are sure and wrong, and how to check a claim before you believe it.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a claim is, and what evidence is.
1. I can ask three questions about any claim: Says who? How do they know? Who is missing?
2. I can explain, using my own test, why being sure is not the same as being right.
3. I can tell a claim that someone checked from a story that only sounds true.
4. I can spot when evidence leaves people or things out, and say how that could change the answer.
5. I can explain why people remember the times a saying came true and forget the times it did not.
6. I can tell how Robert Wood showed in 1904 that the N-rays many scientists saw were not there.

## Terms
- **Claim**: A sentence that says something is true. A claim can be right or wrong. *e.g.* "Our wall clock has all twelve numbers" is a claim. You can check it by looking.
- **Evidence**: Something you can see, hear, count or measure that shows whether a claim is true. *e.g.* Looking at the clock and counting the numbers is evidence. Remembering the clock is not as good.
- **Source**: Where a claim comes from: the person, book, message or place that said it. *e.g.* If a message says 'NASA says', the source should be NASA. You can check NASA's own website.
- **Check**: To look at the real thing, or ask someone who really knows, to see if a claim is true. *e.g.* Meenakshi checked how many steps go up to her floor by counting them as she climbed.
- **Sure**: How certain a person feels about an answer. Being sure is a feeling. It is not evidence. *e.g.* Appa was very sure the gas stove had three burners. When he looked, it had four.
- **Sample**: The part of something that you look at, to learn about the whole thing. *e.g.* One spoonful from the top of a dal jar is a sample of the jar.
- **Who is missing**: The people or things that were left out of the evidence. Leaving them out can change the answer. *e.g.* If you ask only grown-ups whether the rasam is too spicy, the children's answer is missing.
- **Myth**: A story that many people repeat and believe, but that checking has shown to be wrong. *e.g.* Many people say a goldfish forgets everything in three seconds. Tests show goldfish remember things for months.

## Explanations
### Claims and evidence · *defined*
**Definition:** A claim says that something is true. Evidence is what you can see, count or measure that shows whether the claim is right.

Every day we hear claims. "It will rain today." "This soap kills germs." "The shop opens at nine." A claim on its own is just words. To know if it is true, we need evidence. The best evidence is something anyone can look at for themselves. If you say the shop opens at nine, the evidence is the board on its door, or going there at nine.
- e.g. Claim: our flat has seven windows. Evidence: walking round and counting them.
- e.g. Claim: the milk has gone sour. Evidence: it smells sour and forms lumps when heated.
- e.g. Claim: the tulsi plant leans towards the window. Evidence: a photo each day for a week.
### Being sure is not the same as being right · *defined*
**Definition:** How sure a person feels is a feeling inside them. It does not show whether their answer is true. Only checking shows that.

When we see something every day, we feel we know it well. But our memory keeps only the parts we need. In 2015, scientists in California asked 85 students to draw the Apple logo from memory, a picture they saw all the time. Only one student drew it with no mistakes. Most had felt sure they could do it. People who are very sure are often right, but not always. So when two sure people disagree, the way to settle it is to go and look.
- e.g. In the Sure or Right? test, someone may be very sure about the clock and still be wrong.
- e.g. Two people can both be very sure and give different answers. At least one of them is wrong.
- e.g. A person who says 'I'm not sure' and then checks often ends up knowing more than a person who is sure.
### Says who? Where a claim comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** The source of a claim is who said it, and how they came to say it. A good source is someone who looked, measured or tested for themselves.

Many claims reach us second-hand. A friend heard it from a cousin, who saw it in a message. Each time a claim is passed on, it can change, and the person passing it on usually has not checked it. So the first question is: says who? Then: did that person really say it? A message once went round the world saying that NASA had announced six days of darkness. NASA had said no such thing. Anyone could have checked NASA's own website.
- e.g. The weather office measures air, wind and clouds across India, so it is a good source for a rain forecast.
- e.g. A doctor who has examined you is a better source about your cough than a message on a phone.
- e.g. A famous name at the bottom of a message does not show that person wrote it.
### How do they know? Checked, or only sounds true · *defined*
**Definition:** A checked claim is one that someone tested, in a way others can repeat. A claim that only sounds true is repeated often, but nobody has shown it is right.

Some claims sound true because we hear them so often. "Lightning never strikes the same place twice." "A camel stores water in their hump." Both are wrong. Tall buildings are struck by lightning many times a year. A camel's hump is mostly fat. To ask 'how do they know?' is to ask what was done to find out. Did someone count, measure or test it? Could other people do the same and get the same answer? If nobody can say how it was found out, it is a story, even if it sounds true.
- e.g. Checked: an octopus has three hearts. Scientists have looked inside octopuses and seen them.
- e.g. Only sounds true: we use only a tenth of our brain. Brain scans show every part of the brain is used during a day.
- e.g. Checked at home: water from a tumbler left in the sun gets warm. You can feel it yourself.
### Who is missing? Evidence that leaves things out · *defined*
**Definition:** If evidence comes from only some people or some parts of a thing, the answer may be wrong for the rest. Asking 'who is missing?' finds the gaps.

Suppose you want to know if the rasam is too spicy. You ask Amma and Appa, and they say no. But nobody asked your little brother. His answer is missing, and it might be different. Scientists make this mistake too. For many years, most crash tests for cars used a dummy built to the size of an average man. Studies later found that women were more likely to be badly hurt in similar crashes. Women's bodies had been missing from the tests. Now some tests use dummies of other sizes too.
- e.g. In the Top-Spoon Test, a spoonful from the top of the jar left out the moong at the bottom.
- e.g. A survey of only the children in a building cannot say what the grandparents think.
- e.g. For many years, many new medicines were tested mostly on men, so less was known about how they worked for women.
### Why we remember the hits and forget the misses · *defined*
**Definition:** People tend to notice and remember the times a saying came true, and forget the times it did not. Scientists call this confirmation bias.

A day when a guest arrives is easy to remember. A day when nothing happens is easy to forget. So if you believe that a cawing crow brings a guest, you remember every time it worked. The many mornings when the crow cawed and nobody came slip away. The saying seems to come true again and again. The fix is to write down every time, the hits and the misses, and then count both.
- e.g. Meenakshi's Paati remembered four guests on crow days, and forgot nine crow days with no guest.
- e.g. Someone who thinks Fridays are unlucky remembers the bad Fridays and forgets the good ones.
- e.g. A tally kept every day remembers the misses for you.
### What makes a claim scientific? · *debated*
Thinkers have argued for a long time about what makes something science and not just a story. Most agree that science needs evidence that others can check. They disagree about what matters most.
- e.g. "A crow cawing brings a guest" can be tested with a tally. If guests came no more often on crow days, the claim fails.
- e.g. "Everything happens for a reason" is hard to test, because any result could fit it.
- **Karl Popper, a philosopher (1902 to 1994):** A claim is scientific if a test could show it is wrong. 'The crow brings guests' is scientific, because a tally could show it fails. A claim that fits every possible result tells us nothing about the world.
- **Thomas Kuhn, a historian of science (1922 to 1996):** Scientists work inside a shared way of seeing. They do not drop an idea after one failed test. They often keep it until a better idea comes along.
- **Many working scientists:** What matters most is that a claim is checked again and again, by different people in different places, and that they share how they did it so others can try.
### We cannot check everything ourselves · *idea*
Nobody can test every claim. You have not been to the Moon, and you have not looked inside an octopus. So we all trust other people. 'Says who?' does not mean 'trust nobody'. It means trust people because of how they know, not because of how sure they sound. A good source can say how they found out, lets others check, and changes their mind when new evidence comes. People still argue about how much to trust experts, and when.
- e.g. You trust the train timetable because the railway sets the times and you can see trains leave on time.
- e.g. You trust that the Earth goes round the Sun because many people, over hundreds of years, measured it in ways anyone can repeat.

## Mostly agreed
- Being sure about an answer does not make it right. Checking is what shows whether it is right. *How we know:* In many studies, people who felt sure about everyday things they had seen many times, such as a logo or a coin, still made mistakes when they drew or picked them.
- People remember the times a belief came true more than the times it did not. *How we know:* Psychologists have shown this many times, by asking people to recall events and then comparing their memory with a written record.
- A claim is more trustworthy when other people can repeat the test and get the same result. *How we know:* N-rays were seen in one lab and its followers, but careful tests elsewhere found nothing. Real discoveries, like X-rays, were seen by everyone who repeated the test.
- Evidence that leaves out a group can give the wrong answer for that group. *How we know:* Crash tests built around an average man's body missed injuries that women were more likely to get.

## How we found out
**Prosper-René Blondlot, a French physicist, and Robert Wood, an American physicist who tested his claim, 1903 (N-rays announced), 1904 (Wood's visit to Nancy and his report in Nature).** In 1903, Prosper-René Blondlot, a respected physicist at the University of Nancy in France, announced a new kind of invisible ray. He called them N-rays, after his city. He said they made a tiny electric spark glow a little brighter. Dozens of other scientists, mostly in France, soon reported seeing N-rays too. But scientists in other countries tried and saw nothing. In 1904, the American physicist Robert Wood visited Blondlot's lab. Blondlot used a prism, a block that bends rays, to spread the N-rays out, and he read off where each one fell. The room was dark. While Blondlot worked, Wood quietly took the prism away. Blondlot kept reading out the same numbers, as if nothing had changed. The rays he saw were not there. He had seen what he expected to see. Wood wrote up what happened in the journal Nature that September, and within a few years almost nobody spoke of N-rays again.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists believed N-rays were real, because a respected physicist and many others said they saw them. Wood's test, removing the prism without telling anyone, showed that the rays were seen even when they could not be there.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If very many people believe something, it must be true.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Many things that most people believe are true, such as the Earth being round.  
  Against: Many people once believed in N-rays, and many still believe that lightning never strikes twice. The number of believers is not evidence. What counts is whether the claim was checked.
- **Old sayings and home remedies are just stories.** (Some people who trust only modern science; *rejected*)  
  For: Many old sayings, like the crow and the guest, fail when they are tested.  
  Against: Some old knowledge passes the test. Tu Youyou, a scientist in China, found a malaria medicine in sweet wormwood after reading a treatment in a book about 1,700 years old. The fair answer is to test each one.
- **Scientists are never fooled.** (Some people; *rejected*)  
  For: Science has ways to catch mistakes, such as repeating tests.  
  Against: In 1903 and 1904, many scientists in France reported N-rays, which did not exist. They saw what they expected to see. Science caught the mistake only when someone tested it in a way that could fail.
- **We should always trust experts.** (A common view; *debated*)  
  For: Experts have studied a subject for years, and are right far more often than people who have not.  
  Against: Experts can be wrong, and they sometimes disagree. Most people say we should trust an expert who can explain how they know and who changes their mind when the evidence changes.

## Roots
We have tested how often people are sure and wrong, and learned to ask: says who, how do they know, and who is missing? Now we ask what this tells us about knowing anything at all.

**Deep truth.** Some answers your family gave as 'very sure' were wrong when you checked the real thing. Being sure is a feeling inside the person. The clock on the wall stays the same, however sure anyone feels. So how sure someone sounds tells you about them, and only checking tells you about the clock.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat a sure voice as if it were proof, our own most of all. But the feeling of being sure comes from memory and habit, which can drift away from the thing itself. The test showed the two can come apart.  
*Evidence:* In Sure or Right?, every person answered from memory and said how sure they were. Then the family looked at the clock, the windows and the tap. Some very sure answers did not match what was there.

- *who am i:* You are someone who can feel sure and be wrong. You are also someone who can notice it and check.
- *what is life:* Every living thing acts on what they sense and remember. A crow comes back each morning to the balcony where rice was put out before, and looks to see if there is more.
- *what is this world:* The world is the same whatever anyone believes about it. That is why two people who check carefully end up agreeing.

Each person in your home carries their own picture of the flat in their memory. The pictures differ a little, and each one feels sure. But there is only one flat, and when everyone goes and looks, the pictures come together. Many views, one world: checking is how the many come back to the one.

- **Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65:** The Buddha told the Kalama people not to accept a teaching only because it is repeated, or written in a book, or because the teacher is respected. When you know for yourselves that it is true and good, then accept it. (Paati was respected and the crow saying was old and often repeated. Meenakshi did what the Buddha advised: she found out for herself, with a tally.)
- **Jain anekantavada, many-sidedness, and the story of the blind men and the elephant:** Several blind men touch one elephant. One feels the trunk and says it is a snake. One feels a leg and says it is a pillar. Each is sure, and each has only a part. (In the Top-Spoon Test, the top spoonful was sure the jar was all chana. It had only a part. Asking 'who is missing?' is asking what part of the elephant we have not touched.)
- Science (settled): Memories are rebuilt each time we recall them, so a memory can change and still feel sure.
- Science (settled): Scientists hide which sample is which from the people measuring, because expectations can change what people see, as in the N-rays story.
- Other view, Philosophers who study trust, such as many in the tradition of Thomas Reid: Most of what anyone knows comes from what others told us, and we could not live if we checked it all. Trusting what people say is the normal way of knowing, and doubting should be kept for when there is a reason to doubt.
- Practice, Sure, then look (3 min): Close your eyes. Think of the door of this room. Is the handle on the left or the right? Say how sure you are. Now open your eyes and look. Were you right? Close your eyes again. Feel the floor under you. You don't need to remember the floor. It is right here. Open your eyes slowly.
- Ask: If you are sure about something and wrong, what was your sureness made of?
- Ask: Can two people who are both sure ever both be right?
- Ask: Is there anything you know without anyone telling you?
- Ask: What do you believe that you have never checked?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your family member was very sure, and wrong. What was the feeling of being sure made of, if it was not made of knowing? (hint: Think about where the answer came from: the clock, or their memory of the clock.)
- *deep:* Is there anything you can be completely sure about without checking? (hint: What about the fact that you are thinking right now?)
- *big:* Most of what you know, you were told. How much of it have you checked yourself? (hint: Try a few: the Earth is round, your birthday, the name of your great-grandmother.)
- *big:* When a whole group is missing from the evidence, who decides that they should be included? (hint: Think of the crash-test dummy. Who noticed women were missing?)
- *big:* If a saying is not true, why do people keep passing it on? (hint: Paati kept feeding the crows. Does a saying do other things for people, apart from being true?)

## Story: Paati's Crow
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: people remember the times a saying came true and forget the times it did not, and a written tally of every day shows both. House crows are found in almost every Indian city, and they call to each other often, most of all where food is put out.*

Every morning in Madurai, Paati put a small heap of cooked rice on the balcony rail. Every morning, a crow came. Often two or three came. They hopped along the rail, pecked at the rice and called out loudly, kaa, kaa.

One Saturday a crow called for a long time. Paati looked up from her coffee and smiled. "Someone is coming today," she said. "When the crow calls like that, a guest always comes."

Meenakshi was nine. She put down her dosa. "Always, Paati?"

"Always," said Paati. "Last month the crow called, and your Chitti came from Trichy with a box of sweets. Before that, the crow called, and Mrs. Iyer from the third floor came to borrow curd."

That evening, Meenakshi's school friend Revathi came over with her mother. Paati gave Meenakshi a look that said, you see?

Meenakshi thought about it in bed. Paati remembered the days a guest came. But did she remember the other days? Meenakshi could not remember them herself. A day with no guest was just an ordinary day. Nobody talks about an ordinary day.

The next morning she found an old notebook. She drew a table with three columns: the date, "Did the crow call?" and "Did a guest come?" Her brother Arun, who was six, wanted to help. "I'll be the crow watcher," he said. "You be the guest watcher."

"We have to write every day," said Meenakshi. "Even the boring days. Especially the boring days."

"Why the boring days?" asked Arun.

"Because those are the days everyone forgets."

Paati heard them and laughed. "Write all you like," she said. "The crow knows what they are doing."

For two weeks they wrote. Arun stood at the balcony door every morning while Paati put out the rice. He wrote a big red tick if a crow called. A crow called on Monday, and on Tuesday, and on every day that week. On the second Tuesday it rained hard, and no crow came. Meenakshi wrote a cross. Every other morning, the crows came and called.

Meenakshi watched the door. A guest counted only if they were not family who lived in the flat. The milkman did not count, because he came every day. On Wednesday, Mrs. Iyer came for curd again. On Friday, Revathi came back to do homework with Meenakshi. On the first Saturday, Chitti phoned, but did not come. The phone call did not count either. On the second Sunday, two cousins came for lunch. On the second Thursday, a neighbour came with a wedding invitation.

At the end of the two weeks, Meenakshi and Arun sat on the floor with Paati and the notebook. They counted together.

The crow had called on 13 mornings. A guest had come on 4 of those days.

"Four!" said Paati, pleased. "Four guests, all on crow days. What did I tell you?"

"But Paati," said Meenakshi, "look at the other line. On 9 days the crow called and nobody came."

Paati put on her glasses and looked. She looked for a long time. "Nine," she said. "I don't remember those days at all."

"That's the point," said Meenakshi. "Nobody remembers them. You remembered the four days a guest came, because a guest is special. The nine ordinary days just went away."

Arun had a question. "What about the day with no crow? Did a guest come then?"

"No," said Meenakshi. "But that was only one day, and it was raining hard. Who visits in heavy rain? One day isn't enough to tell us anything."

Paati was quiet. Then she said something Meenakshi had not thought of. "Do you know why the crows call so much on our balcony?"

Meenakshi shook her head.

"Because I put out rice," said Paati. "When one crow finds food, they call, and the others come. I make them call, every single morning. Of course the crow calls on guest days. The crow calls on every day."

Meenakshi stared at her. Paati had found the biggest problem with the saying herself. If the crow called every morning, then the crow could never tell them which mornings were special.

"So the saying is wrong?" asked Arun, a little sad.

"The saying is wrong," said Paati. "Your sister checked it, and the notebook shows it." She stood up and fetched the rice pot. "But I will still feed the crows. They are my friends, not my postmen."

The next morning the crow called on the balcony rail, kaa, kaa. Arun looked at Meenakshi. Meenakshi looked at Paati. Paati smiled and said, "Someone may come today, and someone may not. Let's write it down."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Paati remembered the hits (crow days with a guest) and forgot the misses (crow days with no guest). That is confirmation bias. Writing down every day, the boring ones too, put the misses back. Paati then found a second problem: the crow called every day, because she fed them, so a crow call could not pick out guest days. A saying can only be tested if there are days when the sign is missing, to compare with.

- Ask: Why was Paati so sure the crow brought guests?
- Ask: Why did Meenakshi say they must write down the boring days too?
- Ask: On how many crow days did nobody come? Why had Paati forgotten them?
- Ask: Why was one rainy day with no crow not enough to tell them anything?
- Ask: What did Paati notice about why the crows called every morning?

## Games
### g1 · Checked or Only Sounds True? a card game (15 min)
- **rules:** Each player has a CHECKED card and an ONLY SOUNDS TRUE card. Shuffle the 16 claim cards and put them face down.; One player reads the top claim card aloud, without reading the answer in brackets.; Everyone holds up CHECKED or ONLY SOUNDS TRUE at the same time.; One point for the right card.; One more point for saying how someone could find out, such as 'look inside an octopus' or 'count your pulse'.; Take turns to read. The game ends when all the cards are read. Most points wins.; Playing alone: read each card, decide, then check the answer in brackets. Keep your own score out of 16.
- **Printable cards: 16 claim cards:** A goldfish forgets everything after three seconds. (Only sounds true. Scientists have trained goldfish to come to a sound at feeding time, and they remembered it for months.); An octopus has three hearts. (Checked. Scientists have looked inside octopuses: two hearts pump blood to the gills and one to the rest of the body.); Lightning never strikes the same place twice. (Only sounds true. Very tall buildings are struck by lightning many times every year.); A camel stores water in their hump. (Only sounds true. The hump is mostly fat. A camel uses it when food is short.); Your heart beats faster when you run. (Checked. Count your pulse for 15 seconds before and after running on the spot, and see.); Bulls charge at a red cloth because they hate red. (Only sounds true. Bulls cannot tell red from green well. In tests, they charged at a moving cloth whatever its colour.); An ostrich buries their head in the sand to hide. (Only sounds true. Ostriches lower their heads to turn their eggs in a nest in the ground. From far away, it can look like they are hiding.); We use only one tenth of our brain. (Only sounds true. Brain scans show that every part of the brain is busy at some time during a day.); The Moon makes its own light. (Only sounds true. The Moon shines with sunlight bouncing off it. That is why we see only the lit part.); A crow can remember a person's face. (Checked. In the USA, scientists wore a mask while they caught and ringed crows. For years after, crows scolded anyone wearing that mask.); Plants grow towards light. (Checked. Put a potted plant by a window for a week. The shoots lean towards the window.); Swallowed chewing gum stays in your stomach for seven years. (Only sounds true. It passes through the body in a few days, like other things the body does not digest.); Bats are blind. (Only sounds true. Bats can see. Many kinds also find their way in the dark by listening to echoes.); Water boils at a lower temperature high up on a mountain. (Checked. Climbers have measured it with thermometers many times.); Wild snakes drink milk. (Only sounds true. Wild snakes eat animals such as rats, frogs and eggs, and drink water.); Sound cannot travel through empty space. (Checked. A bell ringing inside a jar goes quiet when the air is pumped out of the jar.)
- **Printable cards: One CHECKED card and one SOUNDS TRUE card for each player:** CHECKED; ONLY SOUNDS TRUE
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player name; Points: 1 for the right card, 1 more for saying how someone could find out
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the cards. 2. Give each player a CHECKED and an ONLY SOUNDS TRUE card. 3. Shuffle the claim cards.  
  *Parent says:* Each card has a claim. Some were checked and found true. Some only sound true. You decide which.  
  *Child mode:* Each card has a claim. Was it checked, or does it only sound true?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the goldfish card as a practice round.  
  *Parent says:* A goldfish forgets everything after three seconds. Checked, or only sounds true?  
  *Child mode:* A goldfish forgets everything in three seconds. Checked or only sounds true?
  - Check: Many people say a goldfish forgets everything after three seconds. Scientists trained goldfish to come to a sound at feeding time. Months later, the fish still came. What does this show? → **Goldfish remember for much longer than three seconds**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the rest of the cards. Take turns to read. Give points as in the rules.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me how someone could find out. That's where the extra point is.  
  *Child mode:* Hold up your card. Then say how someone could find out.
  - Ask: How could someone find out if bats are blind? (good: Test whether bats react to light or to things they can see.; Watch bats with a camera in the dark and in the light.)
  - Note: The 'how could we find out' part matters more than the card. Some claims sound strange and are true, like the octopus. Some sound sensible and are wrong, like the camel.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the points. Ask which card surprised your child most.  
  *Parent says:* Which claim surprised you most? Had you believed it before today?  
  *Child mode:* Which card surprised you most? Did you believe it before?
### g2 · Who Would Know? a source-picking game (10 min)
- **rules:** Turn over a source card and read the claim and the two sources.; Pick the better source.; Say why it is better: did they see it, measure it, or write it down at the time?; One point for the source, one for the reason.
- **Printable cards: 8 source cards:** 'It will rain in Chennai tomorrow.' Who would know better: a neighbour whose knee aches before rain, or the weather office? (The weather office. It measures air, wind and clouds across the country every day.); 'The train to Madurai leaves at 6:15.' Who would know better: a friend who went last year, or the railway timetable for today? (The timetable. Times can change, and the railway sets them.); 'Paati's favourite festival is Pongal.' Who would know better: a cousin, or Paati? (Paati. Only she knows what she likes best.); 'This cut needs stitches.' Who would know better: a message on a phone, or a doctor who looks at the cut? (The doctor. She has seen the cut and is trained to judge it.); 'Our flat has seven windows.' Who would know better: Appa's memory, or counting them now? (Counting them now. Memory can be wrong, as the Sure or Right? test showed.); 'The match yesterday ended in a draw.' Who would know better: a friend who heard it from a friend, or the scorecard? (The scorecard. It was written as the match was played.); 'This medicine is safe for children.' Who would know better: an advert, or a doctor and the tests the medicine went through? (The doctor and the tests. - **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the first card, the rain in Chennai, aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Who would know better if it will rain tomorrow: a neighbour whose knee aches, or the weather office? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Who would know better about rain: the knee, or the weather office?
  - Check: Who would know better whether it will rain in Chennai tomorrow? → **The weather office, which measures air, wind and clouds every day**
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Go through the other cards. Let your child pick and give a reason before you read the answer.  
  *Parent says:* Which is the better source? Did they see it, measure it, or write it down at the time?  
  *Child mode:* Pick the better source. Did they see it, or measure it?
  - Ask: Why is Paati the best source about her favourite festival? (good: Because only Paati knows what she likes.; Because it is about her own feelings.)

### e1 · Sure or Right? The Family Check (35 min)
- **question:** When people are very sure about something in their own home, are they always right?
- **predict:** Think of the grown-ups in your home. When they say 'I'm very sure', will they always be right? Will a 'just guessing' answer ever be right? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Everyone gets the same questions, in the same words, read the same way. Nobody may look at the thing or go to it before answering. Each person answers alone, out of earshot of the others, so nobody copies. Each person says how sure they are before anyone checks. We check every answer by looking at the real thing, in the same way for everyone.
- **safety:** Count the stairs only with a grown-up, and walk, don't run. Don't open the fridge or the gas stove to check. Look from where you stand.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then count three numbers for each colour: how many answers were given, how many were right, how many were wrong. Write them in a small box at the bottom of the page.
- **results table:** Question | Person | Their answer | How sure (very sure, a bit sure, just guessing) | Right or wrong, after checking
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read out the list of question ideas in the note below. 2. Your child picks 8 that can be checked in your home. 3. Write them down the side of the page.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to ask the family questions about our own home. Pick questions we can check by looking.  
  *Child mode:* Pick 8 questions about your home. Each one must be easy to check.
  - Note: Question ideas: How many numbers are written on our wall clock? How many windows does our flat have? How many burners does our gas stove have? Which way does the kitchen tap turn to open, like a clock's hands or the other way? What colour is the cap of the toothpaste? How many steps go up from the ground to our floor? Which hand does Amma wear her watch on? What is the first word printed on the milk packet? Mix a few easy ones with a few hard ones.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw the results table with a ruler. Then ask for a prediction and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Before we ask anyone: do you think the very sure answers will all be right? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Will the very sure answers all be right? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Will the very sure answers all be right? Why? (good: Yes, because people know their own home.; No, because people don't look closely at things they see every day.; Most will be right, but maybe one will be wrong.)
  - Note: Write the guess and the reason at the top of the page. Do not say what you expect. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child asks each person the 8 questions, one person at a time, in another room from the others. 2. After each answer, your child asks: very sure, a bit sure, or just guessing? 3. Your child writes the answer and colours the sure box.  
  *Parent says:* Ask each question in the same words. After each answer, ask how sure they are. Don't tell them if they are right.  
  *Child mode:* Ask each person alone. Say each question the same way. Then ask: how sure are you? Colour green, yellow or red.
  - Note: Answer the questions yourself too, honestly, without looking. Nobody may walk to the clock or the tap before answering. If only one grown-up is at home, phone a relative and ask about their own home, and they check while you wait on the phone.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now go round the home together and check every answer. Count the windows, look at the clock, turn the tap. Mark each answer right or wrong.  
  *Parent says:* Now we find out. Let's go and look at each thing. Was the answer right or wrong?  
  *Child mode:* Now go and look at each thing. Was each answer right or wrong?
  - Check: Appa was very sure the clock has all 12 numbers. How do we find out if he is right? → **Look at the clock**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the green answers, and how many of them were wrong. Do the same for yellow and red. 2. Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and a reason. 3. Then read the card 'Being sure is not the same as being right'.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find out? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Count each colour. How many were wrong? What did you find out?
  - Ask: Were all the very sure answers right? (good: No. Two green answers were wrong.; Most green answers were right, but Paati was very sure about the windows and got it wrong.)
  - Ask: Why might someone be sure and wrong about their own home? (good: They see it every day but don't look closely.; They remember it wrong.; They remember an old clock, not this one.)
  - Note: Most families find at least one very sure answer that is wrong. Often the very sure answers are right more often than the guesses, and that is worth noticing too. If every green answer was right, ask: did we pick only easy questions? Try three harder ones, such as the steps or the tap.
### e2 · The Top-Spoon Test: does one spoonful tell you about the whole jar? (20 min)
- **question:** Does a spoonful from the top of a jar show what the whole jar holds?
- **predict:** If you take one spoonful from the top of the jar, will it show both kinds of dal? What about after the jar is shaken? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The thing we change: where the sample comes from, and whether the jar was mixed. The things we keep the same: the same jar, the same spoon, a level spoonful each time. What we count: how many grains of each kind are in each spoonful.
- **safety:** Do this away from small children who might put grains in their mouth or nose. Put the grains back into their tins afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the jar as it looked before mixing and after mixing.
- **results table:** Sample | Where it came from | Chana grains | Moong grains | What this sample says the jar holds
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Out of your child's sight, pour the moong into the jar first, then the chana on top. 2. Wrap the cloth round the jar so the sides can't be seen. 3. Put the jar on the table.  
  *Parent says:* This jar has dal in it. You can only see the top. Take one spoonful and tell me what's in the jar.  
  *Child mode:* You can only see the top of this jar. Take one spoonful.
  - Note: Pour gently so the layers stay separate. A layer of at least 3 centimetres of each works well.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child takes one level spoonful from the top, tips it on the plate and counts each kind. Write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Count the grains of each kind. What does this spoonful say is in the jar?  
  *Child mode:* Count each kind. What does the spoonful say is in the jar?
  - Ask: What is in the jar, going by this spoonful? (good: Only chana.; All chana, no moong.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take off the cloth. Look at the jar from the side together.  
  *Parent says:* Now look at the whole jar. Was the spoonful right about the jar?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the jar from the side. Was the spoonful right?
  - Check: The top spoonful had only chana. Why was it wrong about the jar? → **The moong at the bottom was missing from the sample**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tip everything onto a big plate, mix it well with clean hands, and pour it back into the jar. 2. Take three spoonfuls from three places: the top, the middle and near the bottom. 3. Count each one and write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Now the dal is mixed. Take three spoonfuls from three places. Do they agree with each other now?  
  *Child mode:* Mix the dal. Take three spoonfuls from three places. Count each.
  - Ask: What do the three spoonfuls say now? (good: They all have both kinds.; They have some of each, but not exactly the same numbers.)
  - Note: Mixing by hand works better than shaking. When a jar is shaken, big grains tend to rise to the top again. If your three samples disagree, that is worth talking about. It shows why scientists take samples from many places.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a claim, the evidence and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find out about taking one spoonful from the top?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find out? What did you see?
  - Think: Where else might someone take a sample from only the top, and get the wrong answer?

## Worksheets
### Says Who? Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (fill) A sentence that says something is true is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['claim']. *Why:* A claim can be right or wrong. Evidence shows which.
2. (fill) Something you can see, count or measure that shows whether a claim is true is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['evidence']. *Why:* Counting the windows is evidence. Remembering them is weaker evidence.
3. (truefalse) Divya says, 'If someone is very sure, they must be right.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the Sure or Right? test, some very sure answers were wrong. Being sure is a feeling, not evidence.
4. (mcq) Revathi wants to know how many steps go up to her floor. Which is the best evidence?  
   Options: Her brother's guess / Counting the steps as she climbs / What she remembers from last year / Asking the oldest neighbour  
   **Answer:** Counting the steps as she climbs. *Why:* Counting the steps now is evidence anyone could repeat. Guesses and memories can be wrong, even old people's.
5. (match) Match each question to what it asks.  
   **Answer:** Says who? → Who said this claim first?; How do they know? → What did they do to find out?; Who is missing? → Who or what was left out of the evidence?. *Why:* These are the three questions to ask about any claim.
6. (sort) Sort each claim: has it been checked and found true, or does it only sound true?  
   **Answer:** An octopus has three hearts. → Checked; Lightning never strikes the same place twice. → Only sounds true; Plants grow towards light. → Checked; We use only one tenth of our brain. → Only sounds true; Your heart beats faster when you run. → Checked; Bats are blind. → Only sounds true. *Why:* The checked claims have been tested. The others are repeated a lot but have been shown wrong.
7. (write) In the Sure or Right? test, what did you find out about the answers people were very sure about? Use your own results.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer gives numbers, such as 'There were 10 very sure answers and 2 were wrong', and says what that shows.. *Why:* The answer uses the child's own evidence, not a guess.
8. (mcq) Simran asked only the grown-ups in her home if the TV was too loud. They all said no. What is missing from her evidence?  
   Options: The TV's brand / The children's and grandparents' answers / The time of day / Nothing  
   **Answer:** The children's and grandparents' answers. *Why:* Only some people were asked. Others might find the TV too loud. Who is missing can change the answer.
9. (draw) Draw the jar from the Top-Spoon Test before it was mixed. Show where the spoonful came from, and label the part the spoonful missed.  
   **Answer:** A jar with two layers, a spoon at the top in the chana, and a label on the moong at the bottom saying 'missed'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the sample came only from the top.
10. (truefalse) Arun says, 'A whispered message stays the same however many people pass it on.' Is he right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the Passing Message, the words changed as they went along. Each person can add, drop or change a little.
11. (odd) Three of these are good evidence. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Counting the windows yourself / A photo of the plant taken every day / A message that says 'everyone knows this' / A table written during the test  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 'Everyone knows this' gives no source and no evidence. The others can be checked.
12. (write) Why did Meenakshi say they must write down the boring days, when no guest came?  
   **Answer:** Because the boring days are the ones people forget. Without them, every guest seems to come on a crow day. Writing every day shows the misses as well as the hits.. *Why:* A strong answer says that people remember hits and forget misses.
13. (perspective) Two sisters in Kochi argue about the old saying that a crow calling means a guest is coming.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that a tally of every day is better evidence than memory. It also gives Lakshmi her due: Paati has watched crows for years, and she noticed something true, that crows call a lot.. *Why:* Pick-a-side questions have no single right answer. Marks go for using evidence and stating the other view fairly.
14. (order) Put the steps of checking a claim in order.

### Hits, Misses and Missing People (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)

   **Answer:** The crow claim is, because a tally could show guests come no more often on crow days. 'Everything happens for a reason' is hard to test, because any result could be said to fit it.. *Why:* A strong answer says what result would show each claim wrong, or says that none could.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is evidence?  
   Options: Something a famous person says / Something you can see, count or measure that shows whether a claim is true / A claim that many people repeat / How sure someone feels  
   **Answer:** Something you can see, count or measure that shows whether a claim is true. *Why:* Evidence is what you can look at or measure for yourself. A famous name, many voices or a sure feeling are not evidence.
2. (mcq) Anjali's grandfather is very sure their flat has six windows. Her mother is very sure it has eight. What is the best way to find out?  
   Options: Believe the oldest person / Believe whoever sounds most sure / Walk round the flat and count the windows / Take a vote  
   **Answer:** Walk round the flat and count the windows. *Why:* Both of them are sure, so being sure cannot settle it. Counting the windows is evidence anyone can check.
3. (mcq) In the Sure or Right? test, Kavya found that 3 of her family's 'very sure' answers were wrong. What does this show?  
   Options: Her family is careless / Feeling very sure does not always mean being right / Very sure answers are always wrong / The questions were unfair  
   **Answer:** Feeling very sure does not always mean being right. *Why:* Most very sure answers may be right, but not all. Feeling sure is a feeling. Only checking shows whether an answer is right.
4. (mcq) A message says, 'NASA says the Earth will go dark for six days. Forward to everyone!' Which question should you ask first?  
   Options: How many people have forwarded it? / Says who? Did NASA really say it? / Is the message long? / Is it in English?  
   **Answer:** Says who? Did NASA really say it?. *Why:* The message names NASA, so check NASA's own website. A message like this once went round the world, and NASA had never said it.
5. (mcq) Farhan asked three friends if they like cricket. All three said yes. He says, 'All children love cricket.' What is the problem?  
   Options: Cricket is a boring game / Three friends is too few, and children who don't like cricket are missing / He should have asked grown-ups / Nothing, three is enough  
   **Answer:** Three friends is too few, and children who don't like cricket are missing. *Why:* He asked only three friends, who may all like the same things. The children he did not ask are missing from his evidence.
6. (mcq) Meenakshi's Paati said a calling crow brings a guest. Why did she feel the saying always came true?  
   Options: Because crows can see guests coming / Because she remembered the days a guest came and forgot the days nobody came / Because guests only come when crows call / Because she counted every day  
   **Answer:** Because she remembered the days a guest came and forgot the days nobody came. *Why:* A guest is easy to remember, and an ordinary day is easy to forget. Meenakshi's tally of every day showed 9 crow days with no guest.
7. (mcq) In the Top-Spoon Test, a spoonful from the top of the jar had only chana in it. But the jar also held moong at the bottom. Why was the spoonful wrong about the jar?  
   Options: The spoon was dirty / The sample came only from the top, so the moong was missing / Chana and moong look the same / One spoonful is always right  
   **Answer:** The sample came only from the top, so the moong was missing. *Why:* A sample tells you only about the part it came from. To learn about the whole jar, take samples from many places, or mix it first.
8. (mcq) Which of these claims has been checked and found true?  
   Options: A goldfish forgets everything after three seconds / Lightning never strikes the same place twice / An octopus has three hearts / A camel stores water in their hump  
   **Answer:** An octopus has three hearts. *Why:* Scientists have looked inside octopuses and found three hearts. The other three are repeated often, but checking has shown them wrong.
9. (mcq) In 1904, Robert Wood secretly took away the prism while Prosper-René Blondlot was looking for N-rays. Blondlot kept reading out the same numbers. What did this show?  
   Options: N-rays can pass through anything / Blondlot was seeing what he expected, not real rays / The prism was broken / Wood was not a real scientist  
   **Answer:** Blondlot was seeing what he expected, not real rays. *Why:* Without the prism, the rays could not have fallen where Blondlot said. He kept seeing them because he expected to. Wood's test could have failed, and it did not.
10. (mcq) Why were many scientists fooled by N-rays?  
   Options: They expected to see them, and the signs were faint and hard to judge / N-rays were real but disappeared later / They did not have eyes / Wood paid them  
   **Answer:** They expected to see them, and the signs were faint and hard to judge. *Why:* The rays were said to make a spark a tiny bit brighter, in a dark room. A change that small is easy to imagine when you expect it.
11. (mcq) Who is usually the best source about whether it will rain in Madurai tomorrow?  
   Options: A message forwarded many times / The weather office, which measures air and clouds every day / Someone who is very sure / A crow on the balcony  
   **Answer:** The weather office, which measures air and clouds every day. *Why:* The weather office measures the air across India and checks how its forecasts turned out. That is how they know.
12. (mcq) Priya says, 'If a claim sounds true and lots of people say it, it must be true.' What is wrong with this?  
   Options: Nothing, she is right / Many claims that sound true and are repeated often turn out wrong when checked / Claims that sound true are always false / Only children repeat claims  
   **Answer:** Many claims that sound true and are repeated often turn out wrong when checked. *Why:* The goldfish and lightning stories are repeated by many people and are wrong. What makes a claim trustworthy is that someone checked it, in a way others can repeat.

## Applications
- Doctors ask who was in a medicine's tests before they give it to a child, a pregnant woman or an old person.
- Journalists check a story with a second source before they print it.
- Fact-checkers trace a forwarded message back to where it started.
- Judges and police know that a witness can be sure and still wrong, so they look for other evidence too.
- Car makers now test seats with dummies of different sizes.
- Scientists hide which sample is which from the person measuring, so expectations cannot change what they see.
- Survey makers try to ask people from every group, not only the people easiest to reach.

## Spotted around us
- A forwarded message on the family phone with 'Forward to everyone!' at the end.
- An advert on a toothpaste tube or a soap wrapper that says 'Doctors recommend' without saying which doctors.
- Crows calling on the balcony rail of a city flat in the morning.
- The weather forecast on the TV news, made from measurements by the weather office.
- A newspaper correction box, where the paper says it got something wrong.
- A grandparent's saying at the dinner table, like 'Don't cut your nails at night'.

## Open questions
- **Why do people feel sure about some memories that are wrong?** Scientists know that memories are rebuilt each time we recall them, and can change. Nobody fully knows why a wrong memory can feel just as sure as a right one.
- **How much should we trust experts when they disagree?** Philosophers and scientists still argue about this. There is no simple rule that works every time.
- **Can any test be completely free of what the tester expects?** Hiding information from testers, as Wood did, helps a lot. But scientists still argue about how far expectations creep into what we choose to measure.
- **Why do false stories often spread faster than true ones?** Some studies of social media found that false news spread further and faster than true news. Researchers are still working out why, and whether it is true everywhere.

## Connected maths
- In Kavya's Sure or Right? test, her family gave 12 'very sure' answers. 3 were wrong. How many were right? **9 were right.**. 12 take away 3 is 9. So 9 of the 12 very sure answers were right, and 3 were wrong. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Kavya's family gave 10 'just guessing' answers, and 5 were right. They gave 12 'very sure' answers, and 9 were right. Which kind of answer was right more often? **The very sure answers. 9 out of 12 is more than half. 5 out of 10 is exactly half.**. Half of 12 is 6, and 9 is more than 6. Half of 10 is 5, so the guesses were right exactly half the time. Very sure answers did better, but not perfectly. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- In the story Paati's Crow, a crow called on 13 mornings. A guest came on 4 of those days. On how many crow days did nobody come? **On 9 crow days nobody came.**. 13 take away 4 is 9. Paati remembered the 4 days and forgot the 9. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- Divya plays One True, One Made Up with 10 pairs of claims. If she only guesses, about how many will she get right? **About 5.**. Each pair has two choices, so a guess is right about half the time. Half of 10 is 5. If she gets 9 or 10 right, she probably knows something. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L3`)
- In the Top-Spoon Test after mixing, three spoonfuls had 20, 23 and 18 moong grains. What is the total? What is the difference between the biggest and the smallest? **The total is 61. The difference is 5.**. 20 + 23 + 18 = 61. The biggest is 23 and the smallest is 18. 23 take away 18 is 5. The spoonfuls are close, but not the same, so one spoonful alone could mislead a little. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
