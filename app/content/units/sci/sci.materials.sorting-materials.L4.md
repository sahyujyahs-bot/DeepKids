# Sorting materials: where to draw the line, and what a map of two numbers can show
`sci.materials.sorting-materials.L4`

**Summary.** Weigh one katori of each of ten kitchen foods, and they line up in a ladder with no clear gap in the middle. So which foods are heavy and which are light? Anyone can draw the line anywhere. Then measure a second thing, how tall each food piles up, and put both numbers on a map. Sometimes the foods fall into clumps with empty space between them. Your child sorts dal by eye, in a hurry and with care, and counts the two kinds of mistake. They learn why a sorter can swap its mistakes but not remove them. They meet the astronomers who found whole families of stars by putting two numbers side by side. They also see why the groups we find depend on what we measure.

**Intro.** *Ten kitchen foods stand in a row: salt, sugar, rice, toor dal, mustard seeds, atta, maida, poha, ragi flour and sooji. Weigh one katori of each, and the weights make a ladder from the lightest food to the heaviest. There is no clear gap in the middle of it. Now, which of these foods are heavy foods, and which are light? Where does the line go?* Nobody can say, because the ladder has no gap to show where to cut. You have to choose a line, and a friend can choose a different one. In this unit you find out how a line changes the groups, how a second number can show clumps that one number hides, and what happens when a sorter is hurried or strict. You also meet the astronomers who found the families of stars by doing exactly this.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can put things in order by a number, draw a cut-off to make two groups, and say that the cut-off was my choice.
1. I can show that moving the cut-off moves some things into the other group while others never move.
2. I can plot two measurements for each thing on a map, and say whether the things fall into clumps with gaps.
3. I can explain that a gap on a map belongs to the numbers I chose as well as to the things, and that other numbers can give other groups.
4. I can count the mistakes a sorter makes, name the two kinds, and explain why moving the cut-off swaps one for the other.
5. I can sort at more than one level, lumping or splitting, and say which level suits which job.
6. I can give real examples of sorting by numbers, such as the star map, the plastic codes and rice grading.
7. I can say what computers can do with clumps in many numbers, and what is still argued about.

## Terms
- **Ladder**: Things lined up in order by a number, from the smallest to the biggest. *e.g.* Ten kitchen foods lined up from the lightest to the heaviest katori make a ladder.
- **Cut-off**: A number you choose to split a ladder into two groups. Things at or above it go in one group, and things below it go in the other. *e.g.* A college may say its cut-off is 90 marks. A student with 90 gets in, and a student with 89 does not.
- **Measurement**: A number that says how much of something there is, with a unit such as grams or millimetres. *e.g.* One katori of rice has a mass of about 120 grams on a kitchen scale.
- **Map of numbers**: A picture that places each thing as a dot, using two numbers. Scientists call it a scatter plot. *e.g.* On the Katori Map, each food is a dot placed by its mass across and its heap height up.
- **Axis**: One of the two lines along the edges of a map. Each axis shows one measurement with a scale. *e.g.* On the Katori Map, the line along the bottom is the mass axis, and the line up the side is the heap height axis.
- **Clump**: A group of dots lying close together on a map. Scientists also call it a cluster. *e.g.* On the Katori Map, atta, maida and ragi flour may sit together in one clump.
- **Gap**: An empty stretch on a map or a ladder with no dots in it. *e.g.* On a map of stars, there is a gap between the faint hot white dwarfs and the bright hot main sequence stars.
- **Outlier**: A dot that sits far from all the others. It does not belong to any clump. *e.g.* Poha, light and flaky, may sit far from the other foods on the Katori Map.
- **Wrongly kept back**: A mistake made by a sorter that holds back a thing that belongs in the group. It is also called a missed one. *e.g.* A good grain of toor dal that was left in the pile because the sorter was not sure of it.
- **Wrongly let through**: A mistake made by a sorter that lets in a thing that does not belong to the group. It is also called a false alarm. *e.g.* A piece of chana dal that landed in the bowl for toor dal.
- **Trade-off**: A change that makes one thing better and another thing worse at the same time. Moving the cut-off of a sorter is a trade-off between the two kinds of mistake. *e.g.* A stricter dal sorter lets fewer stones through but also throws away more good dal.
- **Sieve**: A flat tool with holes of one size. Small things pass through and big things stay on top. *e.g.* A chalni sorts fine flour from lumps in the kitchen.
- **Level**: A step in sorting into groups inside groups. A coarse level has few big groups, and a fine level has many small groups. *e.g.* Metal is a coarse level, steel is a finer level, and stainless steel is finer still.
- **Lumping and splitting**: Lumping means putting things into a few big groups. Splitting means putting them into many small groups. *e.g.* A spice shop might lump all seeds together, or split them into mustard, cumin, fennel and coriander.
- **Grade**: A name for a group in a sorting by quality, such as grade A and grade B. *e.g.* Tea is sorted into grades by the size of the leaf pieces.
- **Sample**: A small part taken from a big heap to find out about the whole heap. *e.g.* A trader takes one handful from a sack of rice to check how many grains are broken.
- **Angle of repose**: The steepest slope that a heap of grains or powder can hold without sliding. A steeper heap has a bigger angle. *e.g.* A heap of mustard seeds is flatter than a heap of flour, so mustard has a smaller angle of repose.
- **Resin code**: A number from 1 to 7 inside a triangle of arrows that is stamped on many plastic things. It tells which kind of plastic the thing is made of. *e.g.* A clear drinking water bottle often has a 1 in the triangle, which means a plastic called PET.
- **Main sequence**: The long band of dots on a star map where most stars lie. The stars on it are all burning hydrogen, and the hotter ones are brighter. *e.g.* The Sun is on the main sequence, with a yellow colour and middling brightness.
- **White dwarf**: A small, hot, faint star. It is what is left when an ordinary star has used up its fuel and cast off its outer layers. *e.g.* Sirius B is a white dwarf that circles the bright star Sirius.

## Explanations
### Sorting by a number: the ladder and the cut-off · *defined*
**Definition:** A ladder puts things in order by a number. A cut-off is a number chosen to split the ladder into two groups.

In the earlier unit on sorting, you sorted things by yes or no questions, such as hard or not hard. Many properties are not yes or no. How heavy is a katori of rice? It is a number, such as 120 grams. When you weigh ten foods, you can line them up from the lightest to the heaviest. That line is a ladder. To make two groups, you choose a cut-off. For example, you could say that any food at 100 grams or more is a heavy food. Everything on the ladder at 100 grams or more goes into one group. Everything below goes in the other. The ladder came from the foods. The cut-off came from you. A friend can choose 110 grams, and her groups will be different. Neither of you is wrong. Both are saying what you mean by heavy.
- e.g. A cut-off of 100 grams puts rice and dal in the heavy group, and a cut-off of 130 grams puts them in the light group.
- e.g. A school may set a pass mark of 35 out of 100. A child with 35 passes, and a child with 34 does not.
- e.g. A tea trader sorts leaf pieces into grades by size, and the lines between grades are chosen by the trade.
### Moving the cut-off: who moves and who never does · *defined*
**Definition:** When a cut-off moves, only the things close to it change group. Things far away from it stay in the same group, whatever sensible cut-off you pick.

Imagine a ladder of ten foods. In most kitchens, salt is the heaviest and poha is the lightest. If you draw the line anywhere in the middle, salt stays in the heavy group and poha stays in the light group. They are far from every sensible line. But a food in the middle, such as sooji, moves from one group to the other each time you slide the line past it. These are the borderline cases. They are the ones people argue about. You can measure how borderline a thing is. Ask ten people to draw their line, and count for each food how many put it in the heavy group. Salt will get ten out of ten and poha will get zero. Sooji might get five out of ten. A number from zero to ten tells you how sure the group is. This is better than just saying tricky. It also shows that the argument is about the line and not about the food.
- e.g. Salt and poha stay put whatever cut-off you choose, and foods in the middle of the ladder swap groups.
- e.g. A student who scores 34 and a student who scores 36 are almost the same, but a pass mark of 35 puts them in different groups.
- e.g. Five voters may put a rubber eraser in the soft group or the hard group, but all five put a steel katori in the hard group.
### Two numbers make a map · *defined*
**Definition:** A map of numbers, also called a scatter plot, shows each thing as a dot. One number decides how far across the dot is, and the other decides how far up.

A ladder uses one number. A map uses two. To make one, you measure two things about each food. You might measure the mass of a katori and the height of the heap it makes. Draw two lines, one along the bottom and one up the side. These are the axes. Put the mass along the bottom and the height up the side. Each food becomes one dot. Say sooji measures 100 grams and 33 millimetres. Its dot goes 100 grams across and 33 millimetres up. A map shows something that no ladder can. Two foods can be close on one number but far apart on the other. On the mass ladder, sugar and rice might sit side by side. On the map, they may be far apart if rice makes a flatter heap. So a map shows how things are alike in two ways at once.
- e.g. Each food is one dot placed by its mass and its heap height.
- e.g. A map of children could place each child by height across and age up.
- e.g. A map of stars places each star by colour across and brightness up.
### Clumps and gaps: groups that show themselves · *defined*
**Definition:** A clump is a set of dots lying close together on a map. A gap is an empty stretch between clumps. Scientists call this finding clusters.

When you put many things on a map, the dots do not always spread evenly. Sometimes they gather in clumps with empty space between them. Nobody drew those clumps. The numbers made them. On the Katori Map, the fine flours may all sit together, light and steep. The smooth round seeds may sit together, middling and flatter. Salt and sugar may form a third clump. Then you can draw a ring around each clump, and name the group. This is a very different way of making groups. On a ladder, you choose a line and the line makes the groups. On a map, the dots may show you the groups first, and then you draw the ring round what you see. Some dots stay alone. These are outliers. An outlier is not a mistake. It may be the most interesting dot on the map.
- e.g. Atta, maida and ragi flour may sit close together on the Katori Map.
- e.g. Salt, sugar and rice may form a group of heavy, smooth crystals and grains.
- e.g. Poha, light and flaky, may sit alone as an outlier.
### Do the groups exist before we look for them? · *debated*
Gaps on a map seem to show that nature has groups of its own. But the gap you see depends on the two numbers you chose. Measure other things about the same foods, and the dots may fall in different places, with different clumps and different gaps. So are the groups out there, or do we make them by choosing what to measure? Scientists and philosophers still argue about this. Some say that certain groups are real. For example, all gold atoms are exactly alike, and no gold atom sits half way to silver. Others say that most groups, such as a weed, a vegetable or a plastic, are made by us for our own purposes, and nature has no such boxes. Most people agree on a middle path. Nature has patterns, such as the gap between a faint white dwarf and a bright star. Which patterns we notice, and what we call them, depends on the questions we ask.
- e.g. All gold atoms are alike, so the group gold seems real.
- e.g. A tomato is a fruit to a botanist and a vegetable to a cook.
- e.g. A food can be in the clump of flours on one map and not on another.
- **Scientists who think many groups are real:** Some groups are found in nature whatever we measure. All the atoms of one element are exactly alike, so the group is there before we look. Gaps that show up on many different maps are probably real.
- **Scientists who think most groups are made by us:** Most groups depend on the numbers we pick and the lines we draw. A different question gives different groups. Nature has patterns, but the boxes are ours.
### The star map: Hertzsprung, Russell and the families of stars · *defined*
**Definition:** A star map, called the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, places each star by its colour across and its brightness up. Most stars fall on a band called the main sequence, with giants above it and white dwarfs below it.

About 1900, astronomers at Harvard sorted thousands of stars by the dark lines in their light. Annie Jump Cannon led the sorting, and Antonia Maury added careful notes on how sharp the lines were. Their types were really a sorting by colour and surface heat. In the early 1900s, a Danish astronomer named Ejnar Hertzsprung noticed that among the red stars, some were enormously bright and others very faint, with few in between. In 1913, the American astronomer Henry Norris Russell showed a map that put brightness against type for many stars. The stars were not scattered everywhere. Most lay along a long band from hot and bright to cool and faint. Above the band sat bright cool giants, and below it sat faint hot white dwarfs. The map showed families of stars before anybody knew what made them different. Later, physics showed that the band is made of stars of different masses, all burning hydrogen. Cecilia Payne, in 1925, had shown that the types were really a ladder of temperature.
- e.g. The Sun sits in the middle of the main sequence.
- e.g. Betelgeuse is a red giant, cool and very bright.
- e.g. Sirius B is a white dwarf, hot and faint.
### The map of materials: families that appear by themselves · *defined*
**Definition:** A materials chart, such as the Ashby chart, places materials by two properties, for example how heavy they are for their size and how stiff they are. Families of materials show up as clumps.

Starting around 1990, an engineer at Cambridge named Michael Ashby made charts for choosing materials. He plotted thousands of materials by two properties at a time, such as stiffness against density. On many of these charts, the materials fall into families. Metals sit in one clump, heavy and stiff. Woods and foams sit in another, light and less stiff. Plastics and rubbers sit in a third, light and soft. Glass and ceramics sit in a fourth, hard and brittle. Nobody told the dots where to go. The families are the ones people already knew from names like metal, plastic and wood, and the chart shows that those names mean something about the numbers. An engineer who needs something light and stiff finds that spot on the chart and reads off the materials near it. The empty parts of a chart are interesting too. They show what no known material does well, and they are where designers aim new materials.
- e.g. Steel and copper sit in the heavy, stiff clump.
- e.g. Pine and bamboo sit near each other in the light clump.
- e.g. Carbon-fibre materials sit in a place that is light and very stiff, where few older materials did.
### Counting mistakes: two ways a sorter can be wrong · *defined*
**Definition:** A sorter can make two kinds of mistake. It can wrongly keep back a thing that belongs in the group, or it can wrongly let through a thing that does not belong.

Suppose you sort toor dal from a mix that also holds chana dal. Your toor dal bowl should hold only toor dal. If a piece of toor dal is left in the mixed heap, you have wrongly kept it back. If a piece of chana dal ends up in the toor bowl, you have wrongly let it through. These are two different mistakes, and you can count each one. Doctors do the same with a test for an illness. A test can miss a sick person, or it can say that a healthy person is sick. The two mistakes are counted separately because they cost different things. A rice mill that throws out good rice loses money. A rice mill that lets a stone through can break a customer's tooth. The mill must decide which mistake is worse, and set its sorter accordingly. Counting both kinds is the first step in judging any sorter.
- e.g. A piece of toor dal left in the mixed heap is wrongly kept back.
- e.g. A piece of chana dal in the toor bowl is wrongly let through.
- e.g. A smoke alarm that rings for toast is a false alarm. One that stays silent in a fire has missed the fire.
### The trade-off: no cut-off removes both mistakes · *defined*
**Definition:** When two groups overlap on one number, moving the cut-off swaps one kind of mistake for the other. Only a better measurement can reduce both.

In the Strict and Relaxed test, a strict sorter takes only pieces she is sure of. She lets almost no wrong pieces in, but she leaves many good ones behind. A relaxed sorter takes everything that might be right. She leaves almost none behind, but she lets in many wrong pieces. Moving from strict to relaxed does not remove mistakes. It swaps one kind for the other. This happens whenever the two groups overlap on the thing you measure. A small chana dal may look just like a big toor dal. No cut-off on size can split them perfectly. To do better, you need a second number that separates them, such as colour or weight. With two numbers, the overlap may disappear. This is why real mills pass grain through several machines, each sorting by a different number. When one number leaves the groups overlapping, a cleverer cut-off will not fix it. A new number might.
- e.g. A strict sorter lets nothing wrong in, and wrongly keeps back many good pieces.
- e.g. A relaxed sorter wrongly keeps back almost none, and lets many wrong pieces in.
- e.g. A rice mill uses one machine for size, one for weight and one with a camera for colour.
### Fast and careful: how sorters check their work · *defined*
**Definition:** Hurrying makes more mistakes, so careful sorters check a sample and sort in more than one stage.

A quick sort and a careful sort give different results. When you hurry, you make more mistakes of both kinds, because you glance at each piece for less time. Real sorting systems know this. A buyer does not look at every grain in a sack. She takes a sample, a small handful, and counts the broken or wrong grains in it. If the share of wrong grains in the sample is small, she assumes the sack is good. Mills also sort in stages. A fast, cheap first stage removes most of the rubbish. A slower, more careful stage works on what is left. This is cheaper than one slow stage for everything. A bigger sample gives a better guess, but it takes longer. So sorting is always a balance between speed, cost and the number of mistakes you can live with.
- e.g. A trader takes a handful from a sack to judge the whole sack.
- e.g. A mill removes dust and big stones first, then uses a camera sorter on the cleaner rice.
- e.g. A doctor may use a quick test first, and a slower, more exact test if the first one says maybe.
### Levels: lumping and splitting · *defined*
**Definition:** Sorting can be done at different levels. Lumping uses a few big groups. Splitting uses many small groups, often inside the big ones.

You can sort the same spice shelf at several levels. At the coarsest level, you have one group, spices. One level finer, you can have seeds, powders and leaves. Finer still, you can have mustard, cumin, fennel and coriander seeds. A sorter who makes many small groups is called a splitter, and one who makes a few big groups is called a lumper. Charles Darwin wrote in a letter to a friend that it is good to have both hair-splitters and lumpers. Which level is right depends on the job. If you only need to know which jars go in the kitchen, seeds and powders is enough. If you need to find cumin in the dark, you need the finer level. Scientists use levels everywhere. Steel is a kind of metal, and stainless steel is a kind of steel. Linnaeus, a Swedish scientist, sorted living things into groups inside groups in the 1700s.
- e.g. Spices, then seeds, then mustard seeds, are three levels of the same shelf.
- e.g. Metal, steel, and stainless steel are three levels of the same family.
- e.g. Plastic, then the plastic called PET, is a finer level than just plastic.
### Sorting plastics: the number in the triangle · *defined*
**Definition:** A resin code is a number from 1 to 7 stamped inside a triangle of arrows on plastic things. It names the family of plastic the thing is made from.

Turn over a plastic bottle, a curd cup or a tiffin box. Many have a small triangle of arrows with a number inside. This is a resin code. It started in the United States in 1988, to help sort plastics for recycling. A 1 means PET, the plastic of most clear drink bottles. A 2 means HDPE, used for milk cans and many caps. A 5 means polypropylene, used for many tiffin boxes and curd cups. A 7 means other, which is a box for everything that does not fit the first six. That makes it the tricky middle again. Recycling plants use the differences between plastics to sort them. For example, many of them put chopped plastic in a tank of water. Some plastics, such as HDPE and polypropylene, float, and others, such as PET, sink. The code tells you what the plastic is made of. It does not tell you whether your town can recycle it.
- e.g. A clear water bottle often has a 1 in the triangle.
- e.g. A milk can or shampoo bottle often has a 2.
- e.g. A box marked 7 may hold many different kinds of plastic.
### Sorting by computers: clumps in many numbers · *idea*
A human can draw a map of two numbers. A computer can find clumps among hundreds. In 1936, a British statistician named Ronald Fisher studied 150 iris flowers that an American botanist, Edgar Anderson, had measured four ways each. When he looked at the numbers, one kind of iris was cleanly separate. The other two kinds overlapped, so no cut-off on those four numbers could sort them without some mistakes. Today, computer programs sort photographs, voices and shopping habits in the same way. They look for clumps, or they learn a rule from examples. But they still need a person to decide which numbers to give them, and what the clumps mean. And they can make mistakes unevenly. In 2018, two researchers, Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru, reported a problem. Several commercial computer systems tried to tell men from women in photographs. They made far more mistakes on darker-skinned women than on lighter-skinned men. Counting both kinds of mistake, for every group of people, is how such a problem is found.
- e.g. Fisher found that one kind of iris was cleanly separate and two overlapped.
- e.g. A computer can find clumps among hundreds of numbers at once.
- e.g. A sorter that works for most people can still make many more mistakes for some people.

## Mostly agreed
- When things are sorted by one number, the cut-off is a choice. Things near the cut-off change group when it moves, and things far from it never do. *How we know:* Ten foods sorted by the mass of a katori gave the same two groups at the ends of the ladder whatever cut-off was used, and different groups in the middle.
- A map of two numbers can show clumps and gaps that a ladder of one number hides. *How we know:* On the star map, stars fall into a main sequence, giants and white dwarfs, though they form no clear groups on a ladder of brightness alone.
- A sorter that uses one measurement on groups that overlap has to trade one kind of mistake for the other. *How we know:* In the Strict and Relaxed test, the strict round left more good pieces behind and the relaxed round let in more wrong pieces.
- The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram shows families of stars: the main sequence, giants and white dwarfs. *How we know:* It has been drawn for many thousands of stars, using the measured brightness and colour of each.
- Families of materials, such as metals, woods, plastics and ceramics, appear as clumps on charts that plot two properties. *How we know:* Ashby's charts, built from the measured properties of thousands of materials.
- Computers can find clumps in many numbers at once, but people choose the numbers and decide what the clumps mean. *How we know:* Fisher's iris study in 1936, and tests of face-sorting systems that counted mistakes for each group of people.

## How we found out
**Ejnar Hertzsprung (Danish) and Henry Norris Russell (American), using the sorting by Annie Jump Cannon and Antonia Maury, About 1905 to 1914.** About 1900, astronomers at Harvard College Observatory sorted thousands of stars by the dark lines in their light. Annie Jump Cannon led the sorting, and Antonia Maury added careful notes on how sharp the lines were. These were two ladders: a type, and a brightness. Around 1905, a Danish astronomer named Ejnar Hertzsprung noticed that among the red stars, some were enormously bright and others very faint, with few in between. In 1913, the American astronomer Henry Norris Russell showed brightness against type for many stars on one map. The stars were not scattered everywhere. Most lay along a long band, with bright giants above it and faint white dwarfs below. For a while, many thought the map showed the path that every star travels as it ages. That turned out to be wrong. Later, physics showed that the band is made of stars of different masses, all burning hydrogen. One map had shown the families before anybody knew why they existed.  
*What people believed before:* Many thought that the band of stars on the map was a path that every star follows as it ages, from giant to dwarf. Later work showed that stars of different masses sit at different places on the band, and that each star stays near one place for most of its life.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A group is found in nature. We do not make it.** (Some scientists, and many people who learned sorting from a book.; *debated*)  
  For: All atoms of gold are alike, and stars fall in clear families on a map.  
  Against: The gap you see depends on the numbers you measure. Another pair of numbers can give another set of groups. A group such as vegetable or weed is made by us for our own purposes.
- **A careful enough sorter can have no mistakes.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: A slow, careful sorter makes fewer mistakes, so it seems that enough care would remove them all.  
  Against: If two groups overlap on the number you measure, no cut-off can separate them. Care moves the mistakes between the two kinds but does not remove them. Only a better measurement can.
- **Things in the same clump are the same kind of thing.** (Many people, and some early scientists.; *rejected*)  
  For: Dots in a clump look alike, and the clump seems to be a natural kind.  
  Against: A clump is alike only in the numbers that were used. Two foods in one clump on the Katori Map can differ in colour, taste and what they are used for. A different map would put them apart.
- **A sorter that makes more mistakes is a worse sorter.** (Many people.; *debated*)  
  For: Fewer mistakes seems always better.  
  Against: A strict sorter and a relaxed sorter can make the same total number of mistakes. They make different kinds, and one kind may cost far more. A smoke alarm should rather ring for toast than miss a fire.
- **There is one right number of groups for any set of things.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: A set of things seems to have natural boundaries.  
  Against: Lumpers and splitters sort the same things into fewer or more groups, and each can be useful. The right level depends on the job.
- **A computer sorts without bias because it only uses numbers.** (Some people who trust computers.; *rejected*)  
  For: A computer follows fixed rules, so it seems fair.  
  Against: Researchers have shown that some computer systems made far more mistakes on some groups of people than on others. The numbers chosen and the examples used to train the system can carry a bias.

## Roots
We have sorted things by lines and by maps. Now we ask what sorting tells us about the world, and about the one who sorts.

**Deep truth.** Laid out by one number, the foods made a ladder with no gaps. Every line we drew on it was our choice. Laid out by two numbers, some foods fell into clumps with empty space between them. A gap belongs to the numbers we chose, as well as to the foods.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say that these foods are one kind, as if the group sat inside the foods. But the same foods can fall into other groups when we measure other numbers, so a group is partly a fact about our measuring.  
*Evidence:* On the Katori Map, which foods formed a clump depended on the two numbers we plotted. On the mass ladder alone, there was no clear gap, so every cut-off was a choice. If your map shows no clear clumps, that tells you something too: those two numbers do not split these foods.

- *who am i:* You are a dot on many maps at once, such as your height, your age and your marks. On each map you sit in a different clump, and no clump is the whole of you.
- *what is life:* Your body sorts all the time. Your tongue sorts sweet from bitter, and your gut sorts what to keep from what to pass on. Every sorter in a body makes some mistakes, and the body lives with them.
- *what is this world:* Nature has real patterns, such as the gap between white dwarfs and bright stars. The boxes and the names are ours, and a new number can show a new pattern.

Start with one heap of foods. One number puts the heap in a line. Two numbers spread the heap into a flat picture, where clumps may show. More numbers can show more. At every step the heap is the same heap. The groups come from looking, and the looking is done by someone who is part of the same world as the heap.

- **Udana 6.4:** In this Buddhist text, blind men each touch a different part of an elephant and say what an elephant is. One touches the head, one the tail, one the leg. They argue, and each is right about the part they touched. (One number is like one part of the elephant. A second number shows more, and neither one alone tells you what the whole heap is.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2:** Zhuangzi says that 'this' and 'that' give birth to each other. Without a 'that' there is no 'this'. (When you draw a cut-off, 'heavy' and 'light' are born at the same moment. Neither group exists before the line.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.10.1-2:** Rivers that flow east and rivers that flow west all go to the sea and become the sea. Once they are in the sea, they do not know 'I am this river, I am that river'. (The names we give to groups are like the names of rivers. They are useful on the way, and they all belong to one heap.)
- Science (settled): On a map of brightness against colour, stars fall into families: the main sequence, the giants and the white dwarfs.
- Science (debated): Whether the groups that scientists find are in nature, or are partly made by the numbers they choose to measure, is argued by philosophers of science.
- Other view, Many scientists and philosophers who think some groups are real: Some groups do not depend on our numbers. Every atom of gold is exactly like every other, and no atom sits half way to silver. An element is not a line that we drew. Where we find groups like that, the clumps were there before we measured.
- Practice, Sort the sounds (3 min): Close your eyes. Breathe in slowly three times. Listen to the sounds around you. Put each sound into near or far. Now put the same sounds into loud or soft. Notice how one sound moves between groups. Then stop sorting, and listen to everything as one.
- Ask: If you could measure only one thing about a friend, which would you choose, and what would you miss?
- Ask: Is there a group you belong to that you did not choose? Is there one that you did choose?
- Ask: When you stop sorting and just look, what is left?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The ladder of foods had no gap, yet people drew lines anyway. Is a line something nature gives us, or something we make? (hint: Think about the pass mark in a test and about the exact moment a child becomes an adult.)
- *deep:* Two numbers showed clumps that one number hid. What might a third number show? (hint: Think about the foods and what else you could measure about them, such as how they taste or how they cook.)
- *big:* A smoke alarm that never misses a fire will sometimes ring for toast. Which mistake would you rather it make, and who should decide? (hint: Count the cost of each mistake, not just how many there are.)
- *big:* A pass mark of 35 means that 34 fails and 35 passes. What is the real difference between those two children? (hint: Think about how a line on a ladder treats people who are almost the same.)
- *big:* A computer that tried to tell men from women in photographs made more mistakes on some groups of people than on others. Where could those mistakes come from, and how would you find them? (hint: Think about the examples it learned from, and about counting mistakes for each group.)
- *deep:* Annie Jump Cannon sorted stars by their light, and Hertzsprung and Russell put brightness next to that sort. Why do new answers so often come from putting two old sorts side by side? (hint: Think of what a map shows that a ladder cannot.)
- *big:* Should a sorter ever be allowed to say that it is not sure? What would we need to change if it could? (hint: Think of the unsure bowl in a sorting test and what happens to the pieces in it.)
- *deep:* On a map, the dots made clumps that we did not draw. Does that mean the groups were already in the foods, or did our two numbers make them? (hint: Think about what would happen on a map made from two different numbers.)
- *big:* Many languages have fewer or more words for colours than others, and draw the line between blue and green at different places. Is the colour line in the world, or in the people? (hint: Think about a ladder of colours with no gaps and about who needs the line.)

## Story: The Dial on the Machine
*A made-up story. Rice mills in the Thanjavur region, machines with cameras and puffs of air that push out bad grains, and machines that remove stones by weight are all real. The mill, the people and the map in the story are imagined.*

Kamala Athai's rice mill stood at the end of a long road through the paddy fields near Thanjavur. In the week before Pongal, it never stopped. Lorries came in with sacks of rice. Lorries went out with sacks of rice. Meenakshi, who was ten, spent her holidays there, sitting on an upturned bucket and watching.

This year there was something new in the mill. It was a tall grey machine with a camera inside and a small dial on the front. Rice poured in at the top. The camera looked at every grain as it fell. If a grain looked wrong, a tiny puff of air blew it into a side bin. Good rice fell into the front bin.

'What is the dial for?' asked Meenakshi. 'It says how strict the machine is,' said her cousin Karthik. He was fourteen, and he had been trusted to run it. 'Turn it up, and the machine throws out more.'

The trouble began on the second day. A customer in Kumbakonam telephoned. She had bitten on a small stone in her rice, and it had hurt her tooth. Kamala Athai frowned. 'Make the machine stricter,' she said. Karthik turned the dial up.

On the third day, the side bin was full to the brim. Meenakshi looked into it. Most of what lay there was good white rice, with a chalky spot or a slightly different shade. Kamala Athai looked at the bin, and then at her account book. 'At this rate,' she said, 'we throw away more than we sell.' She told Karthik to turn the dial down.

On the fourth day, a stone got through again. Kamala Athai said nothing for a long time. She had turned the dial up, and she had turned it down, and each time she had swapped one trouble for the other.

Meenakshi had learned at school that week how to count the mistakes of a sorter. She asked the kitchen for two cups. She took a handful from the side bin and a handful from the front bin. Then she sat on her bucket and counted. In the front bin, a few stones. In the side bin, many good grains. She wrote two columns in her notebook.

'The dial does not take the mistakes away,' she said slowly. 'It only moves them. Strict has fewer stones and more thrown-away rice. Loose has more stones and less thrown-away rice. It is a seesaw.' Karthik rolled his eyes. 'Then how do you fix a seesaw?' 'You don't push it,' said Meenakshi. 'You look at something else.'

She laid thirty things on a newspaper: ten stones from the bin, ten chalky grains and ten good grains. She looked at them very hard. By colour, the stones and the chalky grains looked almost the same, pale and dull. That was why the camera could not tell them apart. But when she held a stone in one hand and a chalky grain in the other, one felt like nothing, and the other felt like a tiny weight.

She borrowed the little scale from the tea shop and weighed the things in tens. Then she drew a map. Across the page went how pale a thing was. Up the page went how heavy it was. She put a dot for every one of the thirty things.

The dots fell into three small clumps. The good grains sat low down on one side. The chalky grains sat low down on the other. The stones sat high above both, with a clear gap of empty paper between. 'Look,' said Meenakshi. 'The camera cannot find the stones by colour. A scale finds them at once.'

Kamala Athai looked at the map for a long time. 'There is an old machine in the back shed,' she said. 'It shakes the rice on a sloping tray with a little wind. The heavy things stay on the tray, and the light rice floats off. We never used it, because the new machine had a camera.' Karthik fetched it. They put it first in the line, and the camera second. Meenakshi turned the dial of the camera back to its middle.

By Pongal morning, the stones were gone from the front bin, and the side bin was nearly empty. The camera now threw out only the chalky grains that it was meant to find. Kamala Athai tore the page from Meenakshi's notebook and pinned it beside the machine.

'Why did it work?' asked Karthik. 'One number could not split them,' said Meenakshi. 'Two numbers could.' She looked at the pinned page. Three clumps and a gap. The grains had never been the problem. The problem was the way they had been looked at.

Outside, the first pots of Pongal were beginning to boil over in the courtyards of the village. Not one of them held a stone.

**For the parent:** A dial can only swap one kind of mistake for the other. When two groups look alike on one number, a second number can pull them apart into clumps with a gap between.

- Ask: Why did turning the dial up, and then down, not solve the trouble at the mill?
- Ask: Why could the camera not tell the stones from the chalky grains, when a scale could?
- Ask: Can you think of another job where one measurement is not enough, and a second one would help?

### e1 · The Katori Map: two numbers for ten kitchen foods (40 min)
- **question:** If you sort ten kitchen foods by the mass of one katori, is there a gap in the ladder? And if you also measure how tall each food piles up, do the foods fall into clumps?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which food will be the heaviest in a katori, and which the lightest? Which will make the tallest heap? Will any foods sit close together on both counts? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** You use the same katori, filled level to the top, for every food. You pour each food from the same height onto the same plate, slowly. You measure each heap in the same way. Only the food changes, so any difference in mass or in heap height comes from the food.

### e2 · Strict or Relaxed? Two ways to sort, two kinds of mistake (35 min)
- **question:** When you sort a mix of two look-alike dals under a time limit, what happens to the mistakes if you are strict, and if you are relaxed?

  *Child mode:* Guess first. Which way makes fewer mistakes? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that way will make fewer mistakes? (good: If I am careful I will be right more.; If I take everything I will miss nothing.; Each way will make a different kind of mistake.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.

  *Parent says:* Look at the two dals side by side. What is different? What is alike? Now we mix them into one heap.  
  *Child mode:* Look at both dals. How are they different? Now mix them up.
  - Ask: What is different about the two dals? (good: One is a little bigger.; One is a deeper yellow.; They look almost the same.)
  - Note: Chana dal pieces are usually a little larger and a deeper yellow than toor dal, but brands differ. They look much alike, and that is the point. If yours look very different, use two dals that look more alike, such as toor dal and split moong dal with its skin removed. If your child makes no mistakes in either round, that is a real result. One child with a parent: the parent keeps time and counts. Two to four children: each child sorts their own 20 and 20 on a tray, while another child times and a third counts. Rotate the jobs and give each sorter a row in the table. Compare the rows.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set the timer for 30 seconds. Say go. Your child moves into the toor bowl only the pieces they are very sure are toor dal. Stop when the timer ends. Then count the pieces in the bowl, and count carefully how many of them are chana dal.  
  *Parent says:* Strict round. Take only the pieces you are very sure are toor dal. You have thirty seconds. Ready, go. Now stop, and count.  
  *Child mode:* Strict round. Take only pieces you are sure of. Thirty seconds. Go.
  - Note: Count slowly with no time limit, because the aim now is to find every mistake. Toor dal not in the bowl is 20 minus the toor dal that is in the bowl. A strict round often leaves many good pieces behind and has almost no chana dal in the bowl.
  - Check: In a strict round, the toor bowl had 9 pieces and all were toor dal. 11 toor dal were left in the heap. What kind of mistake were the 11? → **Wrongly kept back**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Mix all 40 pieces into one heap again. Set the timer for 30 seconds. This time, your child moves into the toor bowl every piece that might be toor dal. Stop when the timer ends. Count the pieces in the bowl, and the chana dal among them.  
  *Parent says:* Relaxed round. Take every piece that might be toor dal. You have thirty seconds. Ready, go. Now stop, and count.  
  *Child mode:* Relaxed round. Take every piece that might be toor dal. Thirty seconds. Go.
  - Note: A relaxed round often has more chana dal in the bowl and fewer toor dal left behind. If you did not get this pattern, write down what you saw. Some people are careful whatever the rule.
  - Check: In a relaxed round, the toor bowl had 30 pieces and 11 of them were chana dal. What kind of mistake were those 11? → **Wrongly let through**

  - Ask: What happened to the two kinds of mistake when you changed from strict to relaxed? (good: One kind went down and the other went up.; The mistakes just swapped.; Neither round was perfect.)

### e3 · Soft or Hard? Five voters, one ladder (35 min)
- **question:** Do people agree on where soft ends and hard begins? Which things do all voters put in the same group, and which split the vote? Does a test make the votes agree more?

  - Note: Press with a steady push. About three seconds is enough. A few things, such as the candle and the eraser, may still split the vote. Write what you see.   - Check: Round 2 gives every voter the same test. What do you expect to happen to the votes? → **The voters will agree more, because the test is clearer than a feeling**

  *Child mode:* Count the votes. Put the things in order. Mark the ones that split.
  - Ask: Which things did every voter agree on, in both rounds? (good: The cotton wool and the sponge.; The steel katori and the ceramic cup.; The things at the two ends of the ladder.)
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened. Ask which round had more agreement.  
  *Parent says:* Write three sentences. What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Which round had more agreement?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three sentences. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good reason says that the things in the middle of the ladder split the vote. A clear test makes people agree more, but it cannot remove the middle. Let your child try first. Then read the Moving the Cut-off card together.

## Worksheets
### Ladders, lines and maps (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Ten foods are lined up by the mass of one katori, with no clear gap in the middle of the ladder. Someone says that heavy foods start at 100 grams. Which statement is true?  
   Options: The ladder itself shows where the line must go / The line at 100 grams is a choice, and another person could choose another line / The foods decide where the line goes / There is only one correct line  
   **Answer:** The line at 100 grams is a choice, and another person could choose another line. *Why:* A ladder with no gap gives no sign of where to cut. The cut-off comes from the person who chooses it. Another sensible person could choose 110 grams, and get different groups.
2. (fill) A number chosen to split a ladder of things into two groups is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['cut-off']. *Why:* A cut-off is chosen by the sorter. Things at or above it go in one group, and things below it go in the other.
3. (truefalse) If you move a cut-off a little, every thing on the ladder changes group.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Only the things close to the cut-off change group. Things far from it stay in the same group, whatever sensible cut-off you pick.
4. (order) Put these steps for making a map of numbers in the right order.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* You need the measurements first. Then you need the axes to place them on. Then each thing becomes a dot, and only then can you look for clumps and gaps.
5. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Ladder → Things in order by a number; Cut-off → A number chosen to split a ladder in two; Clump → Dots lying close together on a map; Gap → An empty stretch with no dots; Outlier → A dot far from all the others. *Why:* These five words are the vocabulary of sorting by numbers. A ladder uses one number, and a map of numbers uses two, so clumps, gaps and outliers appear only on a map.
6. (sort) Which of these are mistakes that wrongly keep back, and which wrongly let through? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** A good toor dal piece left in the mixed heap → Wrongly kept back; Good rice thrown into the reject bin → Wrongly kept back; A chana dal piece in the toor bowl → Wrongly let through; A pebble in the rice bag after sorting → Wrongly let through. *Why:* Wrongly kept back means a thing that belongs in the good group was held out of it. Wrongly let through means a thing that does not belong got in. The two kinds cost different things, so we count them separately.
7. (mcq) A strict sorter takes only the pieces she is very sure about. Which kind of mistake will she make most of?  
   Options: Wrongly keeping back good pieces / Both kinds equally / Neither kind / Wrongly letting in pieces that do not belong

## Open questions
- **Are the groups we find on maps in nature, or are they made by the numbers we choose?** Some groups, such as all the atoms of one element, do not depend on what we measure. For many others, such as star types, species or kinds of plastic, scientists and philosophers still argue about how much of the group belongs to the world and how much belongs to the way we look.
- **Can a computer find groups that no person has ever thought of, and will we understand them?** Computers already find clumps in huge piles of numbers, such as weather, galaxies and shopping. Whether the groups they find always mean something we can explain is not settled.
- **Can a sorter ever make no mistakes at all?** When the two groups overlap on every number we can measure, no sorter can be perfect. Whether new measurements can always pull groups apart is not known.
- **Are there kinds of material that nobody has made, in the empty parts of the materials chart?** Some empty parts of the chart are empty because no material can do that. Others are empty only because nobody has found a way yet. It is hard to tell which is which.
- **Why do languages draw the lines between colours at different places?** Some researchers think the colour words come from how our eyes work, and others think they come from culture and what people need to say. Studies in different peoples' languages have found some of each.
- **How should a sorter choose between a mistake that costs a little and one that costs a lot?** The cost of each kind of mistake is a choice about values, not a fact of science. People disagree about it for fire alarms, medical tests and exams.

Which round made fewer mistakes in total, if the strict round also let 0 chana through and the relaxed round also kept 1 toor dal back? **55 percent each. The strict round made fewer mistakes: 11 compared with 12.**. 11 out of 20 is 55 out of 100, which is 55 percent. The strict round had 11 plus 0, which is 11 mistakes. The relaxed round had 11 plus 1, which is 12 mistakes. So the strict round made 1 fewer. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A rice mill counts a stone let through as 10 points of cost, and a good grain thrown out as 1 point. In every 1000 grains, the strict setting throws out 30 good grains and lets 0 stones through. The loose setting throws out 2 good grains and lets 1 stone through. What is the cost of each setting for 5000 grains? **The strict setting costs 150 points. The loose setting costs 60 points.**. For 1000 grains, strict costs 30 times 1, which is 30 points. Loose costs 2 times 1 plus 1 times 10, which is 12 points. For 5000 grains, multiply by 5. Strict is 30 times 5, which is 150 points. Loose is 12 times 5, which is 60 points. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- On your map, flour is at 80 grams and 48 millimetres, maida is at 80 grams and 51 millimetres, rice is at 120 grams and 31 millimetres, and poha is at 60 grams and 40 millimetres. Which food sits closest to flour? How many millimetres lower is the rice heap than the flour heap? **Maida sits closest. The rice heap is 17 millimetres lower.**. Maida has the same mass as flour, 80 grams, and is only 3 millimetres higher. The others differ in both numbers. 48 take away 31 is 17. So the rice heap is 17 millimetres lower than the flour heap. (queues `math.position.directions-and-grids.L4`)
- Five toor dal pieces are 4, 5, 5, 6 and 6 millimetres wide. Five chana dal pieces are 5, 6, 7, 7 and 8 millimetres wide. A slot lets a piece fall through if the piece is narrower than the slot. For slots of 4.5, 5.5, 6.5 and 7.5 millimetres, count the mistakes in each. A mistake is a toor dal that does not fall through, or a chana dal that does. Which slot is best? **4.5 gives 4 mistakes, 5.5 gives 3, 6.5 gives 2 and 7.5 gives 4. The 6.5 millimetre slot is best.**. At 4.5, one toor dal falls through and 4 are kept back, and no chana falls, so 4 mistakes. At 5.5, three toor dal fall through, 2 are kept back, and 1 chana falls, so 3 mistakes. At 6.5, all 5 toor dal fall, and 2 chana fall, so 2 mistakes. At 7.5, all toor fall, and 4 chana fall, so 4 mistakes. The best slot makes 2 mistakes. No slot makes none, because the sizes overlap. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A spice shop has 24 jars. The owner splits them into 2 groups, then splits each of those into 3 groups, then splits each of those into 2 groups. How many small groups are there at the end? How many jars are in each, if the groups are the same size? **12 small groups, with 2 jars in each.**. 2 times 3 is 6, and 6 times 2 is 12. So there are 12 small groups. 24 jars shared among 12 groups is 24 divided by 12, which is 2 jars each. (queues `math.multiplication.properties.L3`)
- After a relaxed round, there are 30 pieces in the toor bowl and 11 of them are chana dal. If you pick one piece from the bowl with your eyes closed, what is the chance that it is chana dal? Is that more or less than half? **11 out of 30. That is less than half.**. There are 30 pieces, and 11 of them are chana dal, so the chance is 11 out of 30. Half of 30 is 15. 11 is less than 15, so the chance is less than half. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
- In the Star Map, 16 stars are plotted. Four of them are giants and 1 is a white dwarf. The rest lie on the main sequence. How many stars are on the main sequence? What fraction of all the stars is that? **11 stars, which is 11 out of 16.**. 4 giants plus 1 white dwarf is 5 stars. 16 take away 5 is 11. So 11 of the 16 stars lie on the main sequence. That is more than half, because half of 16 is 8. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Five voters draw the line between soft and hard at rungs 4, 5, 5, 6 and 8 of a ladder of ten things. Find the average rung. Which rung is in the middle when they are in order? Which of the two is a better description of where most voters put the line, and why? **The average is 5.6 and the middle rung is 5. The middle rung describes most voters better.**. 4 plus 5 plus 5 plus 6 plus 8 is 28, and 28 divided by 5 is 5.6. In order, the rungs are 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, and the middle one is 5. The voter at 8 pulls the average up, but most voters are at 5 or 6, so the middle rung is closer to what most voters did. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a cut-off? → A number you choose to split a ladder into two groups.
- Which things change group when you move a cut-off? → Only the ones close to it.
- What do two numbers make that one number cannot? → A map, which can show clumps and gaps.
- What is an outlier? → A dot far away from all the others.
- What does wrongly kept back mean? → A thing that belongs was left out.
- What does wrongly let through mean? → A thing that does not belong got in.
- What happens to the mistakes when you move from strict to relaxed? → One kind goes down and the other goes up.
- What is the long band of stars on a star map called? → The main sequence.
- What does the number in a triangle on plastic tell you? → What family of plastic it is made of.
