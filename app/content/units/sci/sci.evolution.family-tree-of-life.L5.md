# The family tree of life: how scientists work out who is related to whom, and how long ago their lines split
`sci.evolution.family-tree-of-life.L5`

**Summary.** A dolphin looks like a shark, yet the dolphin's closest relatives are hippos and cows. How can anyone know that, when nobody was there to watch the family lines split? In this lesson you build trees from bodies and from DNA, and you choose between trees by counting changes. Then you roll a die to find out whether chance copying mistakes can tell time. You use your own dice clock to date a split that a grown-up keeps secret.

**Intro.** *A hippo stands in an African river. A blue whale swims in the open sea. Scientists say these two are closer relatives than a hippo and a pig. Nobody saw their family lines split. So how do scientists know, and how do they know when it happened?* Nobody has a birth record for a whale. What we have are bodies, fossils and DNA. DNA is the long molecule that carries the instructions for building a body, written in four chemical letters. The cells of every living being hold DNA. In this lesson you first guess who is whose cousin from looks alone, and keep your guesses. You count the changes that three different trees would need, and pick the tree that needs the fewest. You roll a die seventy times to see whether chance mistakes add up steadily enough to work as a clock. Then you follow the case of the whale, where teeth, DNA and ankle bones each pointed somewhere. You also meet a purple frog from Kerala whose nearest relatives live on islands far across the Indian Ocean.

## Learning outcomes

3. By the end I can explain how chance copying mistakes can keep time, using my results from the Dice Clock Test.

## Terms
- **Phylogeny**: A phylogeny is the family history of a group of living beings. It shows which family lines split from which, and in what order. *e.g.* The phylogeny of the big cats shows that lions and leopards share a more recent ancestor than lions and house cats do.
- **Common ancestor**: A common ancestor is a parent population that two or more living groups both descend from. *e.g.* You and your cousin have a common ancestor in your grandmother. A crow and a crocodile have a common ancestor who lived about 243 million years ago.
- **Fork**: A fork is the point on a family tree where one line splits into two. Each fork stands for a common ancestor. Scientists also call a fork a node. *e.g.* On a tree of apes, the fork that joins the human line and the chimpanzee line is about 6.5 million years old.
- **Sister groups**: Sister groups are the two branches that come out of one fork. Each is the other's closest relative. *e.g.* Whales and hippos are sister groups. Crocodiles and birds are sister groups.
- **Clade**: A clade is one ancestor together with all of the descendants. You can cut a clade off a tree with a single snip. *e.g.* Birds are a clade. 'Animals that fly' is no clade, because bats, birds and butterflies got their wings separately.
- **Shared new feature**: A shared new feature is a feature that first appeared in one ancestor and was passed to all the descendants. Scientists call this a shared derived feature. *e.g.* Milk is a shared new feature of mammals. A dolphin mother and a cow mother both feed milk, because both inherited it from the first mammals.
- **Lookalike feature**: A lookalike feature is a feature that two groups evolved separately, because they live in the same way. Scientists call this convergent evolution. *e.g.* A shark and a dolphin both have a smooth shape and fins. The shark's tail fin stands upright. The dolphin's tail fin lies flat.
- **Outsider**: An outsider is a living being known to be a more distant relative than all the others on your tree. Comparing with the outsider shows which features are old and which are new. Scientists call this an outgroup. *e.g.* To build a tree of a shark, a dolphin and a cow, you can use an earthworm as the outsider.
- **Fewest changes**: When several trees are possible, scientists first try the tree that needs the fewest changes. A change is a feature appearing or being lost. The proper name is parsimony. *e.g.* If dolphins were sisters of sharks, then lungs, milk and hair would each have to appear twice. If dolphins are sisters of cows, each appears once.
- **DNA sequence**: A DNA sequence is the order of the four chemical letters, A, C, G and T, along a piece of DNA. *e.g.* The first letters of one human gene read ATGGTGCAC. The same gene in a chimpanzee starts with the same letters.
- **Mutation**: A mutation is a change in a DNA sequence. Most mutations are copying mistakes made when a cell copies DNA. *e.g.* A cell copies three billion letters and gets a few of them wrong. Most of those changes make no difference to the body.
- **Molecular clock**: A molecular clock is a way of telling how long ago two family lines split. It counts the DNA differences between them, and uses the average rate at which mutations pile up. *e.g.* Human and chimpanzee DNA differ in about 1.2 letters in every 100. The clock puts the split at about 6.5 million years ago.
- **Rate**: A rate says how much of something happens in a set time. *e.g.* If 6 squares change in 20 rounds, the rate is 6 divided by 20, which is 0.3 squares in each round.
- **Calibration**: Calibration means setting a measuring tool against something already known. A molecular clock is calibrated with a split whose date is known from fossils or rocks. *e.g.* A shopkeeper checks a weighing scale with a one-kilogram weight. A scientist checks a DNA clock with a dated fossil.
- **Time tree**: A time tree is a family tree drawn to scale, so the place of each fork shows how long ago that split happened. *e.g.* On a time tree where 1 centimetre is 10 million years, the fork between humans and rats is 8.4 centimetres back from today.
- **Hidden change**: A hidden change is a second mutation at a place that has already changed. It cannot be seen by comparing two sequences, so very old splits can look younger than they are. Scientists call this problem saturation. *e.g.* In the Dice Clock Test, a square that is hit twice is still only one coloured square.
- **Bracket tree**: A bracket tree is a family tree written in one line. Each pair of brackets holds one fork, with the two sister branches inside. *e.g.* ((crow, crocodile), lizard) means the crow and the crocodile are sisters, and the lizard is the next closest relative of both.

## Explanations
### A family tree is read by its forks · *defined*
**Definition:** A family tree of life, or phylogeny, is a diagram of which family lines split from which. Each fork is a common ancestor, and each tip is a group living today or a fossil group.

Start with your own family. You and your sister share parents. You and your cousin share grandparents. Your sister is the closer relative, because the ancestors you share with her lived more recently. A tree of life works in the same way. To find how closely two tips are related, trace back from each until the lines meet. The more recent that fork, the closer the two are. The order of the tips along the top means nothing. Any fork can be turned round like a mobile on a thread, and the tree stays the same tree. Three mistakes are common. The first is to read the tips from left to right as a ladder from 'simple' to 'advanced'. Every living tip has been evolving for exactly the same length of time. The second is to think one living tip is the ancestor of another. Humans did not come from chimpanzees. Both came from an ancestor who was neither, and who lived about 6.5 million years ago. The third is to judge by how near two tips are drawn.
- e.g. On a tree of apes, the gorilla sits beside the chimpanzee in many drawings. Yet a gorilla is exactly as closely related to you as to a chimpanzee. The gorilla line split off before the human and chimpanzee lines parted.
- e.g. ((crow, crocodile), lizard) and (lizard, (crocodile, crow)) are the same tree. In both, the crow and the crocodile share a fork that the lizard is outside.
- e.g. A rohu fish alive today is no more 'ancient' than you are. Your line and the rohu's line parted about 435 million years ago, and both have changed ever since.
### Building a tree from bodies: look for shared new features · *defined*
**Definition:** A shared new feature is one that appeared in a single ancestor and was passed on. Groups that share new features are placed together on the tree.

Not every likeness helps. A dolphin, a cow and a shark all have a backbone. That does not tell you which two are closer, because the backbone is old. All three inherited it from an ancestor far back. What helps is a feature that is newer than the group as a whole, and that only some members have. A dolphin and a cow both have lungs, milk, hair at some time in life and three tiny bones in each middle ear. A shark has none of these. So the dolphin and the cow share an ancestor that the shark's line had already left. The German insect scientist Willi Hennig set out this rule in a book in 1950. It was little noticed until an English version came out in 1966. Why does the rule work? A new feature appears once, in one population. Everyone descended from that population can inherit it. So a shared new feature is a mark left by one particular ancestor. Lookalike features are the trap. A way of life can shape unrelated bodies alike, and then the likeness says how they live, not who their parents were.
- e.g. Feathers are a shared new feature of birds and of some fossil dinosaurs. That is one reason scientists place birds inside the dinosaur branch.
- e.g. A flying fox and a crow both have wings. Look inside and the wings differ. The bat's wing is skin stretched over long finger bones. The crow's wing is feathers on an arm with joined finger bones. The wings are lookalike features.
- e.g. Dolphin calves are born with a few whiskers on the snout, which soon fall out. Even this small trace of hair marks the dolphin as a mammal.
### Choosing between trees: count the changes · *defined*
**Definition:** Parsimony is the rule of trying first the tree that needs the fewest changes. A change is a feature appearing once or being lost once.

For three living groups and an outsider, three trees are possible. Any two of the three might be the sisters. Scientists test each tree against the features. Take one feature that two groups have. If those two are sisters on the tree, the feature needed to appear only once, in their shared ancestor. If they are not sisters, it needed two changes. Either it appeared twice, or it appeared once and was lost once. Add up the changes for every feature. The tree with the smallest total is the first choice. Why prefer fewer changes? A new feature such as milk needs many genes to change together over a long time. For that to happen twice in the same way is much less likely than once. The rule is a starting point and not a proof. When many features are lookalikes, the count can point to the wrong tree. So scientists use as many features as they can, and today most of the features are DNA letters.
- e.g. With eight features of a shark, a dolphin and a cow, the tree with the dolphin and the cow as sisters needs 10 changes. The tree with the dolphin and the shark as sisters needs 14.
- e.g. With only the two features 'fish shape' and 'lives in the sea', the count would favour the dolphin and the shark. Two features are too few.
- e.g. A computer can count changes on millions of possible trees. With 10 groups there are more than 34 million possible trees.
### Building a tree from DNA · *defined*
**Definition:** A DNA tree is built by lining up the same stretch of DNA from several living beings and comparing the letters, place by place.

DNA gives far more features than bodies do. A body offers some hundreds of features to compare. Two DNA sequences offer millions of places, and each place is a small feature of its own. Scientists first line the sequences up, so that each letter sits above the matching letter in the other species. Then a computer searches for the tree that explains the pattern best. Proteins were used before DNA could be read. In 1966 the American scientist Margaret Dayhoff, with Richard Eck, used a computer to build family trees from protein sequences. It was the first time anyone had done so. Some DNA features are especially trustworthy. One kind is a 'jumping gene'. This is a short piece of DNA that sometimes copies itself into a new place among the three billion letters. The chance of the same piece landing in exactly the same place twice is tiny. So if two species have the same jumping gene at the same place, they got it from one ancestor. Shared mistakes are strong evidence for the same reason. A correct letter could be correct in anyone. A rare mistake shared by two species is best explained by one ancestor who made it.
- e.g. In 1997 Mitsuru Shimamura, Norihiro Okada and their team in Japan found jumping genes at the same places in whales, hippos and cattle. Camels and pigs did not have them.
- e.g. Humans, chimpanzees and gorillas all carry the same broken gene for making vitamin C. A shared broken gene is hard to explain without a shared ancestor.
- e.g. During the COVID-19 years, scientists read the virus's sequence from patients all over the world. They drew its family tree week by week, and so could see where each new variant had come from.
### The molecular clock: chance mistakes that keep time · *defined*
**Definition:** A molecular clock uses the number of DNA differences between two family lines to work out how long ago the lines split.

Each time DNA is copied, a few letters are copied wrongly. Nobody can say which letter will change next, or when. Each mistake is a chance event. But chance events in large numbers are steady. You cannot say which roll of a die will be a six. You can be nearly sure that 600 rolls will give close to 100 sixes. Most mutations fall in stretches of DNA where they make no difference to the body. Natural selection has nothing to act on there, so these changes simply pile up, at about the rate at which the mistakes are made. The Japanese scientist Motoo Kimura argued this in 1968. After two lines split, each line collects changes of their own. So the number of differences between them keeps growing, roughly in step with time. Count the differences, divide by the rate, and you have a time. Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling first proposed this in the 1960s. They had compared a blood protein, haemoglobin, across mammals. In 1965 they wrote that there may be 'a molecular evolutionary clock'.
- e.g. In the Dice Clock Test, a strip rolled for 40 rounds ends with about 11 coloured squares, and a strip rolled for 10 rounds ends with about 3. No single roll could have been predicted.
- e.g. Human and chimpanzee DNA differ at about 1.2 places in every 100, in the parts that can be lined up. Human and macaque DNA differ at several times as many.
- e.g. A new baby has some dozens of new mutations that neither parent had. One study of families in Iceland, published in 2012, counted them directly.
### Setting the clock: a known date, and an honest range · *defined*
**Definition:** To calibrate a molecular clock is to work out its rate from a split whose date is already known.

Counting differences gives a number, such as 12 changes in every 1,000 letters. That is not yet a date. To turn it into years you need the rate, and the rate has to be measured against something known. Usually the known thing is a fossil. Suppose the oldest fossil that clearly belongs to one branch lies in rock 50 million years old. Then that branch had split off by 50 million years ago at the latest. The rock itself is dated from radioactive atoms. Sometimes the known thing is the Earth. When a piece of land breaks away and drifts off, the animals who cannot cross the sea are split at about that time. With one or more known dates, the scientist works out the rate. Then the same rate dates other splits that have no fossils. Every step has some error. The fossil gives only the latest possible date. The mistakes arrive by chance, so the count wobbles. So an honest clock date is always a range, such as '131 million years ago, and somewhere between 93 and 177'. Using many genes and many fossils narrows the range.
- e.g. In the Dice Clock Test you work out the rate from three strips whose rounds you know. Then you date a mystery strip. Three times out of four the answer lands within 10 rounds of the truth.
- e.g. The website TimeTree gathers thousands of published clock studies. For the split between the human line and the hen's line it gives about 319 million years ago.
- e.g. The purple frog of the Western Ghats and the tiny frogs of the Seychelles islands split about 131 million years ago, by the clock. India and the Seychelles broke apart about 65 million years ago. So the two frog families were already separate, and both rode the drifting land.
### Fast genes, slow genes and clocks that run at different speeds · *debated*
**Definition:** Different stretches of DNA change at different rates, and the rate can differ between kinds of living beings.

Some genes can hardly change at all, because nearly every change breaks something that every cell needs. These genes are slow. Other stretches of DNA can change freely, and they are fast. A fast stretch is good for recent splits, because it has had time to collect enough differences to count. Over a long time a fast stretch fills up. The same places get hit again and again, and the later hits are hidden changes. The count stops growing, so a very old split looks younger than it is. A slow gene is the opposite. It shows almost nothing for recent splits, and keeps counting steadily over hundreds of millions of years. So scientists choose the gene to suit the question. The rate also differs between family lines. Mice and rats collect changes faster each year than humans or elephants do. One likely reason is that their generations are short, so their DNA is copied more often each century. Modern methods let the rate vary from branch to branch. How far such 'relaxed' clocks can be trusted for the oldest splits is still argued about.
- e.g. In Fast Gene, Slow Gene, the fast strip tells 6 rounds from 12 rounds easily. The same strip is nearly full by 24 rounds, and can hardly tell 24 from 48.
- e.g. The DNA in mitochondria changes fast in animals. It is used to compare populations of one species, and species that split recently.
- e.g. The oldest clear fossils of animals are less than 600 million years old. Many molecular clocks put the first animals more than 100 million years earlier than that.
- **Many scientists who build molecular clocks:** The first animals were small and soft and left almost no fossils. The clock sees a history that the rocks did not keep.
- **Many scientists who study fossils:** For the oldest splits, hidden changes and changing rates make the clock stretch. The true dates may be nearer to the first fossils.
- **A middle view:** Both are partly right. The clock gives a wide range for such old dates, and the range should be stated every time.
### Surprising cousins · *defined*
**Definition:** A surprising cousin is a close relative who looks or lives very differently, and whose kinship was found from DNA, fossils or both.

Bodies change fast when a way of life changes. So some of the closest relatives look nothing alike, and some lookalikes are far apart. Whales and dolphins are sisters of hippos. The dugong, the gentle sea cow of Palk Bay in Tamil Nadu, swims beside dolphins. Yet her nearer relatives are elephants and the small furry hyraxes of Africa. In 1998 DNA showed something larger. Elephants, sea cows, hyraxes, the aardvark, tenrecs, golden moles and elephant shrews form one old African branch of mammals, now called Afrotheria. No one had grouped them from their bodies alone. A mugger crocodile is a closer relative of a crow than of a garden lizard. Bats are closer to dogs and cows than to rats. In South India, the black Nilgiri langur was long grouped with the leaf monkeys of South-East Asia, whom they resemble. In 2008 Praveen Karanth, now at the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru, and his co-workers compared DNA. They found that the Nilgiri langur's closest relatives are the grey Hanuman langurs living in the plains below.
- e.g. Hippo and whale: their lines split about 53 million years ago. The hippo's line and the pig's line split earlier, about 59 million years ago.
- e.g. Dugong and elephant: about 61 million years ago. Dugong and dolphin: about 97 million years ago.
- e.g. Crocodile and hen: about 243 million years ago. Garden lizard and hen: about 281 million years ago.
- e.g. In your kitchen, the tomato's closest relative is the potato. Brinjal comes next and chilli after that. All four belong to one plant family.
### When genes disagree with each other · *defined*
**Definition:** A gene tree is the family tree of one stretch of DNA. It usually matches the tree of the species, and sometimes does not.

Humans and chimpanzees are each other's closest living relatives, and gorillas come next. Yet in 2012, when the gorilla's DNA was read in full, scientists found something odd. In about 30 letters out of every 100, either the human or the chimpanzee sequence is closer to the gorilla's than to the other's. How can that be? The ancestor of all three was a large population, and that population held several versions of many stretches of DNA. The gorilla line split away first. Not long after, the human and chimpanzee lines parted. The time between the two splits was short. So by chance, the human line sometimes kept the same old version as the gorilla line, while the chimpanzee line kept another. For that stretch of DNA, the tree puts humans with gorillas. Nothing is wrong with either tree. A species tree is the tree that most of the DNA agrees on. This is why scientists no longer trust a tree built from one gene. They read thousands of stretches and report how many agree.
- e.g. Think of three sisters and their grandmother's bangles. The two younger sisters are closest in age, yet by chance the eldest and the youngest may have got the matching pair.
- e.g. When splits come quickly one after another, as in the first birds after the dinosaurs died out, many gene trees disagree and the order of the splits is hard to settle.
- e.g. When two splits are far apart in time, nearly every gene gives the same tree.
### Trees of anything that is copied · *defined*
**Definition:** Any set of things made by copying, with small mistakes passed on, has a family tree that can be rebuilt from the shared mistakes.

The method does not need DNA. It needs three things. Something is copied. The copies are sometimes wrong. A wrong copy passes the mistake on. Before printing, every book was copied by hand, and each copyist made slips. A copy made from a slipped copy carried the slip onward. Scholars of old books learned to group hand-written copies by their shared mistakes, long before anyone read DNA. In 1998 a team in Britain joined the two fields. They fed 58 hand-written copies of part of Geoffrey Chaucer's Canterbury Tales into a computer program made for DNA. The program drew a family tree of the copies. Languages work in a similar way. Tamil, Malayalam, Kannada and Telugu share old words because they descend from one earlier language. So when you rebuild a family tree from DNA, you use a kind of reasoning that people have long used for books and words.
- e.g. Two recipe notebooks that both number two recipes '17' and none '18' were almost surely copied from one notebook with that slip.
- e.g. Tamil 'kan', Malayalam 'kannu', Kannada 'kannu' and Telugu 'kannu' all mean eye. The likeness comes from one parent language.
- e.g. A message passed along a line of whispering children changes a little at each child. The last versions on two branches of the line share the early changes.
### Two clocks made of chance: DNA and radioactive atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** A clock can be made from any change that happens by chance at a steady average rate. DNA mutations are one such change. The breakdown of radioactive atoms is another.

The fossil dates that set the molecular clock come from a second clock, inside rocks. Some kinds of atom are radioactive. This means the centre of the atom is unstable, and at some moment it breaks down and becomes a different kind of atom. Nobody can say when one such atom will break down. It may be in the next second or in a billion years. Yet in a lump of rock there are huge numbers of these atoms, and the fraction that breaks down each year is steady. Volcanic ash holds a radioactive kind of potassium. Half of it turns into other atoms in about 1.25 billion years. By measuring how much has turned, scientists date the ash, and so date the fossils lying between two ash layers. So the date of a split in the tree of life rests on two counts of chance events. One count is of changed letters in cells. The other is of changed atoms in stone. Why do the copying mistakes happen at all? The molecules that copy DNA are jostled all the time by heat. Ultraviolet light and natural radiation also damage letters now and then. A cell repairs nearly all of this, and a few changes slip through.
- e.g. The rocks in Kashmir that held the whale relative Indohyus are about 48 million years old.
- e.g. The Deccan lava flows of Maharashtra are dated to about 66 million years ago by the radioactive clock.
- e.g. A tossed coin is chance each time. A thousand tossed coins give very nearly 500 heads.
### The frontier: the first fork of the animals, and the root of the whole tree · *debated*
**Definition:** The deepest forks of the tree of life are the hardest to place, and some are not settled.

The branches near the tips are well known. The deepest forks are harder, because so much time has passed and so many changes are hidden. One open case is the very first fork among the animals. For a long time most scientists thought sponges split off first. A sponge has no nerves and no muscles, so a sponge-like first animal seemed likely. From 2008 onward, some large DNA studies pointed instead to comb jellies. A comb jelly is a clear sea animal who swims with rows of tiny beating hairs, and who has nerves and muscles. In 2023 a team led by Darrin Schultz compared how genes are arranged along whole chromosomes. A chromosome is one long thread of DNA. They found that comb jellies still have an old arrangement that sponges and all other animals have lost. That points to comb jellies as the first to branch. If so, either nerves evolved twice, or sponges once had them and lost them. Many scientists accept the new result, and some still question it. At the very base of the tree, among bacteria and archaea, genes pass sideways between unrelated cells so often that some scientists draw a net there and not a tree.
- e.g. The same DNA data have given sponges first with one method of analysis and comb jellies first with another.
- e.g. An arrangement of genes along a chromosome, once scrambled, almost never returns to the old order. That is why it is strong evidence.
- e.g. For the split between the human line and the honeybee's line, the clock gives about 720 million years ago, with a range of about 650 to 800.
- **Scientists who place comb jellies first:** The old arrangement of genes on comb jelly chromosomes is hard to explain in any other way. Bodies can become simpler, so the sponge's simple body proves little.
- **Scientists who place sponges first:** Comb jelly DNA has changed very fast, and fast-changing branches are easily misplaced by tree-building methods. The result should be repeated with more species.

## Mostly agreed
- All living beings on Earth are related, and their history has the shape of a branching tree for almost all animals, plants and fungi. *How we know:* Every cell uses the same four DNA letters and nearly the same code for reading them. Trees built from bones, from proteins and from different genes agree with each other far more often than chance would allow.
- How closely two living beings are related is decided by how recently their family lines split, not by how alike they look. *How we know:* Sharks and dolphins look alike and split about 460 million years ago. Dolphins and cows look unlike and split about 57 million years ago. DNA, milk, lungs and ear bones all agree on this.
- Whales descend from four-legged land mammals, and hippos are their closest living relatives. *How we know:* DNA and jumping genes place whales beside hippos. In 2001 two teams found early whales with legs in Pakistan. Their ankle bones have the double-pulley shape of the hoofed group that includes hippos, cattle and deer.
- DNA differences pile up roughly in step with time, so they can be used as a clock once the rate is set against known dates. *How we know:* Clock dates set with one fossil have often been confirmed by fossils found later. The human and chimpanzee split of about 5 to 7 million years ago was first proposed from molecules in 1967, and later fossils fit it.
- A clock date is a range, and the range is wider for older splits. *How we know:* For the human and chimpanzee split, published studies give about 6.2 to 6.7 million years. For the human and honeybee split they give about 650 to 800 million years.
- A tree built from one gene can differ from the tree of the species, so many genes are needed. *How we know:* In the full gorilla DNA sequence published in 2012, about 30 letters in every 100 put the gorilla closer to humans or to chimpanzees than those two are to each other.

## How we found out
**Vincent Sarich and Allan Wilson at the University of California, Berkeley. David Pilbeam, who first supported the old idea, described the fossil that overturned it., 1967 for the protein clock, and 1982 for the fossil face..** In the 1930s a fossil hunter named Edward Lewis found pieces of an ape's jaw in the Siwalik Hills of India. He named the ape Ramapithecus. In the 1960s several experts decided that the jaw was shaped like a human's. The rocks were many millions of years old. So they said the human line had split from the other apes at least 15 million years ago. In 1967 two scientists in California, Vincent Sarich and Allan Wilson, tried a different kind of evidence. They compared a blood protein called albumin in humans, apes and monkeys. They reasoned that the protein changes at a steady rate, and they set the rate using a split that fossils had dated. Their answer was that humans and chimpanzees parted only about 5 million years ago. Most fossil experts rejected it. Then in 1982 David Pilbeam described a fossil face from Pakistan belonging to the same group as Ramapithecus. The face looked like an orangutan's. The old jaws had belonged to relatives of orangutans, and the molecules had been right.  
*What people believed before:* That the human line split from the other apes 15 million years ago or more, and that Ramapithecus was an early human ancestor. A protein clock, and then a better fossil, showed the split was far more recent.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Whales descend from mesonychids, a group of extinct meat-eating hoofed mammals.** (Leigh Van Valen, an American scientist, from 1966, and most fossil experts until 2001; *rejected*)  
  For: The teeth of the earliest fossil whales look very much like mesonychid teeth. For thirty years teeth were the best evidence anyone had.  
  Against: DNA placed whales next to hippos. In 2001, ankle bones of early whales showed the double-pulley shape that hippos, cattle and deer have and mesonychids lack. The teeth were lookalike features.
- **The human line split from the other apes more than 15 million years ago.** (Many fossil experts in the 1960s, including Elwyn Simons and, at first, David Pilbeam; *rejected*)  
  For: Jaw pieces of a fossil ape called Ramapithecus, from the Siwalik Hills of India and Pakistan, seemed to have a human-like shape. They were many millions of years old.  
  Against: In 1967 a protein clock gave about 5 million years. In 1982 a fossil face found in Pakistan showed that these apes were relatives of the orangutan.
- **The molecular clock ticks at one fixed rate in every living being and every gene.** (An early, simple form of the clock idea in the 1960s; *rejected*)  
  For: For one protein compared across similar animals, the number of differences does rise evenly with time.  
  Against: Rates differ between genes, and between family lines. Rats and mice collect changes faster each year than humans do. Clocks today let the rate vary.
- **Comb jellies, and not sponges, were the first animals to branch off from all the others.** (Casey Dunn and others in 2008, and Darrin Schultz and others in 2023; *debated*)  
  For: Several large DNA studies, and the arrangement of genes on whole chromosomes, point to comb jellies.  
  Against: Comb jelly DNA changes fast, which can mislead tree-building methods. Sponges have the simpler body, with no nerves or muscles.
- **When DNA and bodies disagree, DNA is always right.** (A common view among people who work only with DNA; *debated*)  
  For: DNA gives millions of features, and it settled the whale, the dugong and the langur cases.  
  Against: Almost no fossil has DNA left, so the place of extinct groups rests on bones. DNA trees can also be wrong for fast-changing branches. The surest trees use both.

## Roots
We have learned how family trees are built, and how chance changes in DNA can tell time. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Dice Clock Test, nobody could say which roll would bring the next change. Yet over seventy rolls the changes piled up at a steady rate, and you told time with them. Copying mistakes in DNA arrive by chance in the same way. So the differences between your DNA and a crow's are a rough count of years. They record how long your two family lines have been apart.  
*Habit it breaks:* We set chance against order, as if what happens by chance could never be relied on. We also treat a mistake as damage and nothing more. In this lesson, each change is a chance mistake, and a large number of them is steady enough to measure time with. The steadiness belongs to the large number, not to any single event.  
*Evidence:* Strips rolled for 10, 20 and 40 rounds ended with about 3, 6 and 11 coloured squares, though no single roll could be predicted. Human and chimpanzee DNA differ at about 1.2 places in every 100, and the clock built on such counts gave a date that later fossils fitted.

- *who am i:* You are a cousin of every living being you will ever meet. The crow at the window is a cousin about 319 million years removed, and the banyan a much more distant one. Your cells carry the count.
- *what is life:* Life is one family that has kept copying itself for about four billion years. No copy is perfect. The small mistakes are how life changes, and they are also how the family's history can be read.
- *what is this world:* The world keeps records of its own past without anyone writing them down. Changed letters in cells and changed atoms in rocks both hold a count of time, for anyone who learns to read it.

All living beings descend from one population of early cells. Each fork on the tree is a moment when one family line became two. Followed forward, the forks turn one kind of life into millions of kinds. Followed backward, any two living beings, however unlike, meet at a single ancestor. So the many kinds of life are one family seen at different distances, and the DNA in each cell still carries the measure of those distances.

- **Isha Upanishad 6:** “यस्तु सर्वाणि भूतान्यात्मन्येवानुपश्यति / सर्वभूतेषु चात्मानं ततो न विजुगुप्सते” The one who sees all beings in the self, and the self in all beings, does not turn away from anyone. (The Upanishad speaks of seeing, and gives no evidence of the kind a scientist uses. The tree of life gives a plain fact that sits beside it: every being you meet shares an ancestor with you, and the shared history is written in the cells of both.)
- **Samyutta Nikaya 15.14 (the Mata Sutta), a discourse of the Buddha:** A being who has not been your mother at one time in the past is not easy to find. (The Buddha was speaking of a long round of rebirths. He was not speaking of ancestry. Science reaches a nearby thought by another road: no living being can be found who is not your relative.)
- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), chapter 4, closing paragraph:** Darwin compares all life to a great tree. The green buds are the living species. The older branches are their ancestors, and the dead and broken branches lie buried in the Earth's crust as fossils. (Darwin drew the tree from bodies and fossils alone. He had no way to date the forks. A hundred years later the molecular clock began to do that.)
- Science (settled): All living beings on Earth share one ancestry, and any two of them meet at a common ancestor.
- Science (settled): DNA differences can date the splits between family lines, with a range that widens for older splits.
- Science (debated): Whether the deepest part of life's history is a tree or a net of branches that join.
- Other view, The Jain tradition, and many working biologists: Jain teachers hold that every living being is a separate soul with no beginning, and that one soul never comes out of another. On this view two beings may share a bodily ancestor and still be two, for ever. Many biologists would add something plainer. Being related is a fact about ancestors. It tells you where two beings came from, and by itself it says nothing about whether they are one.
- Practice, Counting back with a cousin (3 min): Sit by a window and find one living being outside: a crow, a tree, an ant. Look at them quietly. Breathe in, and think of your mother. Breathe out, and think of theirs. With each breath, go back one more generation on both sides. Somewhere far back, the two lines of mothers become one line. You do not need to reach it. Just notice that both lines are unbroken, and that they meet. Now look again at the one outside, and say the word 'cousin' quietly.

- Ask: A shared copying mistake can show that two notebooks, or two species, are family. Is a mistake always a bad thing?
- Ask: The banyan tree is your relative. What, if anything, do you owe a relative so distant?

## Story: Two Seventeens
*Valli, her Aachi, the aunts, Dr Kamala Devi and the notebooks are made up. These things are real. Nattukottai Chettiar families of Karaikudi and the towns around it traded for generations in Burma, Malaya and Ceylon, and Chettiar families still live in Penang in Malaysia. Kavuni arisi is a Chettinad sweet made from black sticky rice, sugar or jaggery, ghee and coconut, and the rice is said to have first come home with traders from Burma. 'Aachi' is the Chettinad word for an elder woman or grandmother. Scholars really do work out the family tree of hand-copied books from the mistakes the copies share.*

The letter from Penang came on a Tuesday, and Aachi read it standing up, which she never did.

"A Dr Kamala Devi," she said. "She teaches at a university in Malaysia. Her grandmother was called Unnamalai and came from a town in India beginning with K. She has found an old notebook of recipes in a trunk. She asks whether any family in Karaikudi knows the handwriting." Aachi folded the page. "There are a hundred towns beginning with K. And a thousand Unnamalais."

Valli was not really listening. She had her own trouble with notebooks. Aachi would be eighty in two weeks, and Valli had decided to make kavuni arisi for her, exactly as Aachi's own grandmother Meenakshi had made it. Meenakshi's recipe book had been eaten by white ants long ago. But her daughters had copied it by hand, and their daughters had copied the copies. Three copies were left. Valli had all three open on the swing in the hall.

They did not agree.

Aachi's copy said: cardamom, six pods. Periamma's copy, sent from Madurai, said sixty. So did Chitti's copy from Coimbatore.

"Sixty!" said Aachi. "You could clear a blocked nose from the next street."

"But two notebooks say sixty and only one says six," said Valli. "Two against one."

She did not like her own answer. That night she went through the copies page by page with a pencil. Periamma's copy spelt 'jaggery' with one g, every time. So did Chitti's. Periamma's copy had left out the brinjal pickle altogether. So had Chitti's. And Chitti's had a few slips of her own on top. In the pepper rasam, the pepper was missing.

Valli sat back. Chitti had not copied Meenakshi's book. She had copied Periamma's copy, mistakes and all, and added her own. Her 'sixty' was only Periamma's 'sixty' written out again. It was not two against one. It was one against one.

"So I still don't know," she told Aachi in the morning.

"You know more than you did," said Aachi. "Yesterday you were sure, and wrong."

Valli turned the pages of all three, looking for anything that would break the tie. That was when she noticed the numbers. In Aachi's copy the recipes were numbered down the margin. Fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, seventeen, nineteen. There were two seventeens, lime rice and tamarind rice, and no eighteen at all. She pulled Periamma's copy over. It had two seventeens. So did Chitti's.

"Aachi, did you know your notebook can't count?"

Aachi laughed. "That was my grandmother. She wrote the tamarind rice in late, between two others, and never fixed the numbers. My mother copied it just as it stood. She said it was not her place to correct her mother's book."

Valli looked at the two seventeens for a while. Then she looked at the letter on the shelf.

"Aachi. Write to the doctor in Penang. Ask her one thing. Ask her what number comes after seventeen."

The answer came in nine days, with photographs. Dr Kamala Devi wrote that she had never noticed until she was asked. In her grandmother's notebook, the lime rice was seventeen. The tamarind rice was seventeen. Then came nineteen.

Aachi sat down on the swing.

"Anyone can write 'six pods of cardamom'," said Valli. She was talking fast now. "That proves nothing. It is just correct. But nobody numbers two recipes seventeen by accident in the same place, in two countries. She has the mistake. So her notebook came from the same book as ours."

"Unnamalai," said Aachi slowly. "My grandmother had a younger daughter who sailed with her husband before the war. My mother spoke of a sister. Then letters stopped. I thought it was a story."

Valli was already bent over the photographs. The Penang notebook had none of Periamma's slips. Jaggery had two g's. The brinjal pickle was there. Unnamalai must have copied straight from her mother's book before she sailed. That made her copy a separate line from all three in India.

"Kavuni arisi," Valli read out. "Cardamom." She held her breath. "Six pods."

"Six," said Aachi. "I told you."

"You told me. But you could have been the one who was wrong. Now two lines that never met each other both say six." Valli read on down the page and stopped. "There is a line here that none of ours has. After the ghee. 'A pinch of salt, or the sweetness has no edge.'"

Aachi closed her eyes. "My mother must have skipped it," she said. "Sixty years I have been making it without the salt."

On the birthday, Valli soaked the black rice overnight and cooked it soft. She added six pods of cardamom, counted twice, and a pinch of salt. Aachi tasted it and said nothing for some time.

Then she asked for the telephone number in Penang.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the recipe copies are a family tree, and Valli rebuilds it the way scientists rebuild the tree of life. Two copies agreeing on 'sixty' looked like strong evidence. But one was copied from the other, so they counted as one voice. Shared mistakes showed that. The two seventeens are a mistake in the first book, so every true descendant carries it. That is how Valli knew the Penang notebook belonged to the family. A correct line could come from anywhere, and a rare shared mistake points to one source. Last, the Penang copy was a separate early branch. Where two separate branches agree, the reading most likely goes back to the first book. Scientists use shared rare changes in DNA in the same three ways.

- Ask: Two notebooks said sixty and one said six. Why was that not 'two against one'?
- Ask: Why did the two seventeens prove more than a correctly copied line could?
- Ask: Draw the family tree of the five notebooks: Meenakshi's, Aachi's, Periamma's, Chitti's and Unnamalai's.
- Ask: The Penang notebook had a line about salt that the Indian copies lacked. On which branch of your tree was that line lost?
- Ask: Where else could you rebuild a family tree from copies with mistakes?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Dice Clock Test: can chance mistakes tell time? (40 min)
- **question:** A strip of 30 squares stands for a gene. In each round a die decides, by chance, whether one square changes. If you roll for 10, 20 and 40 rounds, do the changes add up steadily enough to work as a clock? And can you use them to find out how many rounds a secret strip was rolled for?
- **predict:** Guess first. Strip C is rolled for four times as many rounds as strip A. Will strip C end with about four times as many coloured squares, or will chance make the counts come out anyhow? Why do you think so? Then guess how close you will get to the secret number of rounds: within 5, within 10 or further off. Give your reason.
- **fair test:** Every strip has 30 squares. The same die is used, and a change happens only on a 5 or a 6. The chit always goes back into the tumbler, so every square has the same chance each time. Only one thing differs between the strips: the number of rounds.
- **record:** Draw the results table with four rows, for strips A, B, C and Mystery. For each strip write the rounds rolled, the number of coloured squares and the number of dots. Below the table write the rate you worked out and your estimate for the Mystery strip.
- **results table:** Strip | Rounds rolled | Coloured squares | Dots (hidden changes)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child cuts 30 small chits and numbers them 1 to 30. 2. They put the chits in the tumbler. 3. They rule four strips, each 30 squares long. 4. They number the squares 1 to 30 and label the strips A, B, C and Mystery.  
  *Parent says:* Each strip is one gene, and each square is one DNA letter. A coloured square will mean that letter has changed from the ancestor's letter.  
  *Child mode:* Cut 30 chits and number them. Rule four strips of 30 squares. Label them A, B, C and Mystery.
  - Note: Squares about 1 centimetre wide are easy to colour. If you have no squared paper, rule lines 1 centimetre apart.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the rule of one round, given in the note below. 2. Ask for both guesses and the reasons. 3. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* One round is one roll. On a 5 or a 6, one square changes. Strip C gets four times as many rounds as strip A. Will C have about four times as many coloured squares? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess: will strip C have four times as many coloured squares as A? Say why. Write your guess.
  - Ask: Each roll is pure chance. Do you think the totals will be regular or anyhow? Why? (good: Chance is random, so the totals could be anything.; Over many rolls the 5s and 6s should even out, so more rounds should give more changes.)
  - Note: The rule of one round: roll the die. On 1, 2, 3 or 4, nothing happens. On 5 or 6, draw a chit, colour that square and put the chit back. Do not say which guess is right. Many children expect chance to give no pattern at all.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rolls 10 rounds for strip A, making a tally mark for each round. 2. On a 5 or a 6 they draw a chit and colour that square on strip A. 3. The chit goes back in the tumbler each time. 4. If the square is already coloured, they put a small dot in it.  
  *Parent says:* Ten rounds for strip A. Keep a tally so you do not lose count. If a chit comes up twice, put a dot in the square. That is a second change at the same place.  
  *Child mode:* Roll 10 rounds for strip A. On a 5 or 6, draw a chit. Colour that square. Put the chit back.
  - Note: Strip A usually ends with 2 to 4 coloured squares. It can end with 1, or with 5 or 6. Any of these is a real result.
  - Check: In the Dice Clock Test, what does a roll of 5 or 6 stand for? → **A copying mistake in the DNA**

  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rolls 20 rounds for strip B in the same way. 2. They roll 40 rounds for strip C. 3. They count the coloured squares and the dots on each strip. 4. They write all the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now twenty rounds for strip B and forty for strip C. Count only the coloured squares. A scientist comparing two sequences cannot see the dots.

  - Note: For example, 9 coloured squares ÷ 0.3 = 30 rounds. The ready-made strip was rolled for 34 rounds. About three times in four, the estimate lands within 10 rounds of the truth. A miss by more than 10 is also a real result. Ask what could make the estimate better.
- **s8 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 2. Ask the questions below. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Could you have predicted any single roll?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Your estimate was not exactly right. What would make a clock like this more exact? (good: More rounds and more squares, so chance evens out more.; Using several strips and taking the average.; Setting the rate from more strips.)
  - Ask: Why does a scientist give a range, such as '25 to 35', and not one number? (good: Because the changes come by chance, so the count wobbles.; Because the rate is only an average.)
  - Note: The explanation: no single roll can be predicted, but about one roll in three gives a 5 or a 6. Over many rolls the changes pile up at close to that average rate. So the count of changes rises roughly in step with the rounds, and can be read backwards as a time. DNA works in the same way, with copying mistakes in place of rolls. Three things are simplified here. You compared each strip with the starting strip. Scientists never have the ancestor's DNA, so they compare two living species, and the changes in both lines add up. Real DNA has four letters, so a place can change back. And real rates are far lower: most letters do not change in a million years.
  - Think: If you rolled a strip for 500 rounds, nearly every square would be coloured. What could your clock tell you then?
### e2 · Fast Gene, Slow Gene: is a faster clock always a better clock? (30 min)
- **question:** Two strips of 12 squares stand for two genes. The fast gene changes on a roll of 3, 4, 5 or 6. The slow gene changes only on a 6. Which gene is the better clock for a short time, and which for a long time?
- **predict:** Guess first. After 6 rounds, which strip will tell you more? After 48 rounds, which strip will tell you more? Write both guesses, and the reason for each.
- **fair test:** Both strips have 12 squares and use the same die and the same 12 chits. Both are rolled for the same rounds, and counted at rounds 6, 12, 24 and 48. Only one thing differs: how often a change happens.
- **record:** Draw the results table with four rows, for rounds 6, 12, 24 and 48. At each stop, write the number of coloured squares on the fast strip and on the slow strip. Then draw both as lines on one chart.
- **results table:** Rounds so far | Fast strip: coloured squares | Slow strip: coloured squares
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rules two strips of 12 squares and numbers the squares. 2. They label one Fast and one Slow. 3. They put chits 1 to 12 in the tumbler. 4. They draw the results table.  
  *Parent says:* The fast gene can change freely. The slow gene does a job that almost any change would spoil, so changes rarely last.  
  *Child mode:* Rule two strips of 12 squares. Label them Fast and Slow. Put chits 1 to 12 in the tumbler.
  - Note: One round is two rolls: one for the fast strip and one for the slow strip. Use a different colour for each strip.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask which strip will tell more after 6 rounds, and why. 2. Ask which will tell more after 48 rounds, and why. 3. Write both guesses.  
  *Parent says:* A fast gene collects changes quickly. Is a faster clock always a better clock? What do you think, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Which strip will tell you more after 6 rounds? After 48 rounds? Say why.
  - Ask: What happens to a strip of 12 squares when it is hit many, many times? (good: It fills up.; New hits land on squares that are already coloured.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say the fast strip is better every time, because it gives more to count.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rolls once for the fast strip. On 3, 4, 5 or 6 they draw a chit and colour that square. 2. They roll once for the slow strip. On a 6 they draw a chit and colour that square. 3. The chit goes back each time. 4. They stop after rounds 6 and 12 and write both counts.  
  *Parent says:* One roll for the fast strip, one roll for the slow strip. That is one round. Stop at round 6 and at round 12 to count.  
  *Child mode:* Roll once for Fast and once for Slow. Colour the squares that change. Count at rounds 6 and 12.
  - Note: After 6 rounds the fast strip usually has 3 or 4 coloured squares, and the slow strip 0, 1 or 2. Keep a tally of rounds.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child carries on with the same two strips up to round 24, and writes both counts. 2. They carry on to round 48 and write both counts. 3. Take turns with the rolling.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going on the same strips. Count again at round 24 and at round 48.  
  *Child mode:* Keep rolling on the same strips. Count at round 24. Count again at round 48.
  - Note: By round 24 the fast strip usually has 8 to 10 coloured squares. By round 48 it has 11 or 12. The slow strip usually has 2 to 4 at round 24, and 5 to 7 at round 48.
  - Check: A strip of 12 squares has 11 coloured. Why will the count now rise very slowly? → **Most new hits land on coloured squares**
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws a line chart with rounds along the bottom and coloured squares up the side. 2. They plot one line for each strip. 3. Ask what they thought, what they saw and why. 4. After they answer, read the explanation in the note.  
  *Parent says:* Draw both lines on one chart. Which line is still climbing at the end? Which strip could tell round 6 from round 12? Which could tell round 24 from round 48?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line for each strip. Which line is still climbing at the end? Say why.
  - Ask: You want to date a split that happened very long ago. Which kind of gene do you choose, and why? (good: A slow gene, because it has not filled up.; A fast gene would be full of hidden changes.)
  - Ask: You want to tell apart two groups that split only recently. Which kind of gene, and why? (good: A fast gene, because the slow one has hardly changed yet.)
  - Note: The explanation: the fast strip separates short times well, because it soon has several changes to count. But it fills up, and after that it hardly moves. The slow strip shows almost nothing at first, and keeps climbing steadily over the long run. Real scientists choose in the same way. They use fast-changing DNA for recent splits, and slow genes, such as the genes for ribosome parts, for the oldest splits.
  - Think: Is there one gene that would be a good clock for every question?

### a2 · Fewest Changes: three possible trees for a shark, a dolphin and a cow (20 min)
- **record:** Draw the feature table. Draw the three trees. Under each tree write the number of changes it needs, and circle the smallest.

  *Parent says:* On each tree, two of the animals share a fork and the third joins lower down. Draw all three trees. Which do you think is right, and why?

- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give the counting rule from the note below. 2. Your child takes Tree 1 and goes down the table, one feature at a time. 3. They write 1 or 2 beside each feature and add up.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the rule. If the two animals with a feature are sisters on this tree, the feature appeared once. That is 1 change. If they are not sisters, that is 2 changes.  
  *Child mode:* Take Tree 1. For each feature write 1 if the two owners are sisters. Write 2 if not. Add up.
  - Note: Why 2 changes? Either the feature appeared twice, or it appeared once lower down and was then lost once. Both ways cost two changes. Tree 1: features 1 to 6 cost 1 each, and features 7 and 8 cost 2 each. 6 + 4 = 10.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child counts Tree 2 and Tree 3 in the same way. 2. They circle the tree with the smallest total. 3. Ask what the winning tree says about the fish shape.  
  *Parent says:* Count the other two trees. Which tree needs the fewest changes? On that tree, how many times did the fish shape appear?  
  *Child mode:* Count Tree 2 and Tree 3. Circle the smallest total. How many times did the fish shape appear?
  - Note: Tree 2: features 7 and 8 cost 1 each, and features 1 to 6 cost 2 each. 2 + 12 = 14. Tree 3: all eight cost 2 each, which is 16. Tree 1 wins with 10. On Tree 1 the fish shape appeared twice, once in sharks and once in dolphins. It is a lookalike feature.
- **s5 (3 min)**

  - Note: With only features 7 and 8, Tree 2 wins, and the dolphin goes with the shark. That is the mistake people made for centuries. More features make a lookalike easier to catch.
  - Check: Two possible trees need 10 changes and 14 changes. Why do scientists try the 10-change tree first? → **A big feature rarely evolves twice**
  - Think: Your eyes and an octopus's eyes work in a similar way. Would you count that as a shared new feature or a lookalike? How could you find out?

### Reading and building family trees (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What decides how closely two living beings are related?  
   Options: How alike they look / Whether they live in the same place / Whether they eat the same food / How recently their family lines split  
   **Answer:** How recently their family lines split. *Why:* Relatives are close when their shared ancestor lived recently. Looks and homes can match in distant relatives. Choosing 'how alike they look' is the natural first guess, and the dolphin and the shark show why it fails.
2. (fill) On a family tree, each fork stands for a common ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ancestor']. *Why:* A fork is the point where one family line became two. The population living at that point is the ancestor of everything above the fork.
3. (truefalse) True or false: humans descended from chimpanzees.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Humans and chimpanzees both descended from an ancestor who was neither. That ancestor lived about 6.5 million years ago, and both lines have changed since.
4. (match) Match each animal or plant to their closest relative on the list.  
   **Answer:** Blue whale → Hippo; Dugong → Elephant; Mugger crocodile → Crow; Tomato → Potato; Flying fox bat → Dog. *Why:* Each pair was found from DNA, and fossils or body features agree. None of the pairs look alike at first sight.
5. (sort) Sort each likeness: a shared new feature, or a lookalike feature?  
   **Answer:** Milk in a cow and a dolphin → Shared new feature; Wings of a bat and a crow → Lookalike feature; Hair on a dog and a whale calf → Shared new feature; Fish shape of a shark and a dolphin → Lookalike feature; Three ear bones in a rat and an elephant → Shared new feature; Flippers of a dugong and a turtle → Lookalike feature. *Why:* Milk, hair and the three ear bones were each inherited from one mammal ancestor. Wings and the fish shape evolved separately in groups that live in the same way.
6. (mcq) Two hand-copied notebooks are found. Which likeness best shows that one source lies behind both?  
   Options: The same rare mistake in the same place / The same correct spelling of 'rice' / The same number of pages / The same colour of ink  
   **Answer:** The same rare mistake in the same place. *Why:* A correct spelling could come from anywhere. A rare mistake in the same place is very unlikely to happen twice by chance, so one source is the best explanation. Scientists use shared rare changes in DNA in the same way.
7. (draw) Draw the bracket tree ((crow, crocodile), lizard) as a fork diagram. Mark the ancestor that the crow and the crocodile share.  
   **Answer:** A tree with two forks. The crow and the crocodile meet at the upper fork, which is marked. The lizard's line joins at the lower fork.. *Why:* The inner brackets are the upper fork. A strong drawing marks that fork, and not the lower one, as the shared ancestor of the crow and the crocodile.
8. (truefalse) True or false: flipping a fork on a family tree changes who is related to whom.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A flipped fork holds the same members. Only the order of the tips on the page changes, and that order carries no meaning.
9. (write) In the Dice Clock Test, no roll could be predicted. Explain how the strips could still tell time.  
   **Answer:** About one roll in three gives a 5 or a 6. Over many rolls the changes add up at close to that rate. So the number of coloured squares rises roughly in step with the number of rounds, and can be read back as a time.. *Why:* A strong answer separates one roll, which is chance, from many rolls, which have a steady average.
10. (mcq) A strip gains 6 coloured squares in 20 rounds. What is the rate?  
   Options: 3 squares in each round / 0.3 squares in each round / 26 squares in each round / 120 squares in each round  
   **Answer:** 0.3 squares in each round. *Why:* A rate is the amount divided by the time. 6 ÷ 20 = 0.3. Choosing 120 means multiplying where dividing was needed. Choosing 26 means adding.
11. (order) Put the steps of using a molecular clock in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Count the DNA differences between two species', 'Find a split whose date is known from fossils', 'Work out the rate from that known split', 'Divide the differences by the rate to date the new split']. *Why:* The count comes first, and it is not yet a date. The rate has to be set against something known before the count can be turned into years.
12. (odd) Three of these are shared new features of mammals. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Milk / Hair / A backbone / Three bones in the middle ear  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A backbone is much older than the mammals. Fish, frogs and birds have one too, so it cannot show which animals are mammals. The other three first appeared in the mammal line.
13. (fill) Setting a clock or a scale against something already known is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['calibration', 'calibrating']. *Why:* A molecular clock is calibrated with a split whose date is known. Without that step, a count of differences cannot be turned into years.
14. (label) Label a family tree of three animals: tip, fork, branch, and the oldest fork.  
   **Answer:** ['Tip: the end of a line, a living group', 'Fork: where one line splits into two', 'Branch: the line between two forks, or from a fork to a tip', 'Oldest fork: the lowest one, shared by all three']. *Why:* The tips are the living groups. Every fork is a common ancestor, and the lowest fork is the ancestor of everyone on the tree.
15. (write) Name one surprising pair of close relatives. Say what evidence shows they are close.  
   **Answer:** For example: whales and hippos. DNA puts them together, and they share jumping genes at the same places. Early fossil whales have the same double-pulley ankle bone as hippos, cattle and deer.. *Why:* A strong answer names the pair and gives at least one piece of evidence that is not about looks.
16. (mcq) Why do scientists give the date of a split as a range?  
   Options: They have not tried hard enough / Dates are a matter of opinion / The fossils keep moving / The changes arrive by chance, so counts wobble  
   **Answer:** The changes arrive by chance, so counts wobble. *Why:* A count of chance events is never exactly the average, and the rate itself is an estimate. A range is the honest way to say how far off the date could be. Choosing 'a matter of opinion' misses that the range is worked out from the data.
### Fewest changes, fast and slow genes, and setting the clock (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) On a possible tree, the two animals that share a feature are sisters. How many changes does that feature cost?  
   Options: One / Two / Three / None  
   **Answer:** One. *Why:* The feature needed to appear only once, in the ancestor the two sisters share. If the two were not sisters, it would cost two changes. Choosing 'none' forgets that the feature had to appear somewhere.
2. (write) Two possible trees need 10 changes and 14 changes. Which do scientists try first, and why?  
   **Answer:** The tree with 10 changes. Each change is a real event, such as milk or lungs appearing. A big feature appearing twice in the same way is much less likely than appearing once. So the tree with fewer repeats is more likely.. *Why:* A strong answer says what a change is, and why repeats are unlikely. It may add that the rule is a first choice and can be wrong when there are many lookalikes.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a dugong is a closer relative of a dolphin than of an elephant.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The dugong's line and the elephant's line split about 61 million years ago. The dugong's line and the dolphin's line split about 97 million years ago. The shared shape is a lookalike feature.
4. (mcq) A fast-changing strip of 12 squares is nearly full. What can it no longer tell you?  
   Options: Whether any time has passed / A long time from a very long time / Which die was used / How many squares it has  
   **Answer:** A long time from a very long time. *Why:* Once nearly every square has changed, more rounds add almost nothing to the count. So 24 rounds and 48 rounds look alike. The strip still shows that plenty of time has passed.
5. (match) Match each kind of evidence to what it is best used for.  
   **Answer:** Fast-changing DNA → Telling apart groups that split recently; A slow-changing gene → Dating a very old split; The same jumping gene at the same place → Showing two species share an ancestor; A fossil in dated rock → Setting the rate of the clock. *Why:* Each tool suits a job. Fast DNA has enough changes to separate recent splits. Slow genes have not filled up over long times. A jumping gene at one place is a rare shared event. A dated fossil turns counts into years.
6. (fill) A second change at a place that has already changed is called a ____ change.  
   **Answer:** ['hidden']. *Why:* Comparing two sequences shows only that the place differs. It cannot show how many times the place changed. Hidden changes make old splits look younger than they are.
7. (perspective) It is 1997. Two scientists argue about the whale's closest relatives.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason. For example: the DNA scientist has the stronger case, because a jumping gene at the same place is very unlikely twice. It also says what the other has right: no fossil then showed a whale with a hoofed animal's bones, so asking for one was fair.. *Why:* In 2001 the ankle bones were found and agreed with the DNA. Both scientists were reasoning well in 1997. The case was settled by a third kind of evidence.
8. (order) Put these relatives of a hen in order, from closest to most distant.  
   **Answer:** ['Crocodile', 'Garden lizard', 'Human', 'Frog', 'Rohu fish']. *Why:* The hen's line split from the crocodile's about 243 million years ago, the lizard's about 281, the mammals' about 319, the frog's about 352 and the rohu's about 435.
9. (draw) Draw the tree (((tomato, potato), brinjal), chilli). Circle the fork where the brinjal's line joins.  
   **Answer:** A tree with three forks, one above another. Tomato and potato meet at the top fork. The brinjal joins at the middle fork, which is circled. The chilli joins at the lowest fork.. *Why:* The circled fork is the ancestor that the brinjal shares equally with the tomato and with the potato.
10. (sort) Sort each group: a clade, or not a clade?  
   **Answer:** Birds → A clade; Animals that fly → Not a clade; Mammals → A clade; Animals that live in the sea → Not a clade; Whales and hippos, with their shared ancestor → A clade; Fish, without the land animals → Not a clade. *Why:* A clade is one ancestor with all the descendants. Birds, mammals, and whales with hippos each pass that test. Flyers and sea animals come from many separate branches. 'Fish' leaves out the land animals, who descend from fish.
11. (mcq) Two frog families split 131 million years ago. Their lands broke apart 65 million years ago. What follows?  
   Options: The land split caused the families to split / The families were separate before the land split / The frogs swam across the sea / The clock must be wrong  
   **Answer:** The families were separate before the land split. *Why:* 131 is older than 65. So the two families already existed while the land was one piece. When the land broke up, each piece carried one family away. Choosing 'the land split caused it' would need the two dates to match.
12. (write) The oldest fossil of a branch is 50 million years old. Why might the branch be older than that?  
   **Answer:** Fossils form rarely, and few are found. The first members of the branch probably lived before the oldest fossil we happen to have. So the fossil shows the branch is at least 50 million years old, and it may be older.. *Why:* A strong answer says the fossil gives the latest date by which the branch existed, not the date it began.
13. (truefalse) True or false: the oldest fossil of a branch shows the branch is at least that old.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. If a member of the branch was alive then, the branch had already begun. The branch may be older, because earlier members may have left no fossils.
14. (odd) Three of these are on one branch of mammals that DNA revealed. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Elephant / Dugong / Hyrax / Dolphin  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Elephants, dugongs and hyraxes belong to an old African branch called Afrotheria. The dolphin belongs with hippos, cattle and deer, on a different branch.
15. (mcq) Two species differ in 2 letters per 100 and split 10 million years ago. When did a pair that differs in 6 split?  
   Options: About 12 million years ago / About 30 million years ago / About 60 million years ago / About 3 million years ago  
   **Answer:** About 30 million years ago. *Why:* If the rate is the same, three times the differences means three times the time. 6 ÷ 2 = 3, and 3 × 10 = 30. Choosing 12 means adding 2 to 10 where multiplying was needed. These numbers are made up.
16. (write) A rohu and a shark are both called fish. Explain why 'fish' is not one branch of the tree.  
   **Answer:** The rohu's line is closer to the line of frogs, birds and humans than to the shark's line. Any branch that holds both the rohu and the shark must also hold the land animals. Leaving them out makes 'fish' a group by way of life, not a whole branch.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea of a clade: one ancestor with all the descendants.
### When genes disagree, and what the clock cannot tell (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) Chimpanzees are our closest relatives. Explain how one stretch of our DNA can still be closer to a gorilla's.  
   **Answer:** The ancestor population of all three had several versions of that stretch. The gorilla line split first, and soon after, the human and chimpanzee lines split. By chance the human line kept the same old version as the gorilla line, and the chimpanzee line kept a different one.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the old population had variety, and that the two splits came close together, so chance decided which line kept which version.
2. (mcq) Different genes give different trees for three species. What do scientists report as the species tree?  
   Options: The tree from the longest gene / The tree most of the DNA supports / The tree found first / The tree that matches looks  
   **Answer:** The tree most of the DNA supports. *Why:* Each stretch of DNA has a history of its own. The species tree is the one that the large majority of stretches agree on. Scientists also report how many disagree, because that shows how quickly the splits followed each other.
3. (perspective) Two scientists argue about the first fork in the tree of the animals.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives the evidence. For comb jellies first: the old arrangement of genes on their chromosomes. For sponges first: comb jelly DNA changes fast and may be misplaced, and a sponge has the simpler body. It also says what would settle it, such as more species or a new kind of evidence.. *Why:* This question is not settled. A simple body proves little, because bodies can lose parts. A fast-changing branch is a known source of error. Both points are fair.
4. (truefalse) True or false: if a clock date and the oldest fossil disagree, one of them must be useless.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The fossil gives the latest date by which a group existed. The clock gives a range for when the line split. A clock date older than the first fossil is expected, because the earliest members rarely leave fossils.
5. (write) Two notebooks say '60' and one says '6'. One '60' was copied from the other. Why is this not two against one?  
   **Answer:** The copy only repeats what its source said. So the two notebooks give one piece of evidence, not two. It is one against one, and another notebook from a separate line of copying is needed to decide.. *Why:* Evidence counts as separate only when it comes by separate routes. Scientists apply the same care to genes that are inherited together.
6. (mcq) Rats collect DNA changes faster each year than elephants do. What is one likely reason?  
   Options: Rats are less careful animals / Rat DNA uses different letters / Elephant DNA never changes / Rat generations are short, so DNA is copied more often  
   **Answer:** Rat generations are short, so DNA is copied more often. *Why:* Many mutations happen when DNA is copied for the next generation. A rat can be a parent within months, and an elephant takes more than ten years. So a century holds far more rat generations. Scientists think other causes add to this too.
7. (draw) Sketch the fast strip and the slow strip as two lines over 48 rounds. Label where the fast line flattens.  
   **Answer:** The fast line climbs steeply and then bends flat near 12 squares. The slow line climbs gently and is still rising at round 48. The label points to the bend in the fast line.. *Why:* The bend is where hidden changes take over. After it, the fast strip can no longer separate one long time from another.
8. (fill) One radioactive atom breaks down by ____, yet a huge number break down at a steady rate.  
   **Answer:** ['chance']. *Why:* Nobody can say when one atom will break down. In a rock there are so many atoms that the fraction breaking down each year is steady. That is why rocks, like DNA, can be used as clocks.
9. (match) Match each clock to what it counts.  
   **Answer:** A molecular clock → Changed DNA letters; A rock clock → Atoms that have broken down; The Dice Clock Test → Coloured squares; A tree's rings → One ring for each year of growth. *Why:* Every clock counts some change that happens at a known average rate. The first three count chance events, and need large numbers to be steady.
10. (order) Order these splits from the narrowest date range to the widest.  
   **Answer:** ['Human and chimpanzee', 'Human and hen', 'Human and honeybee', 'Human and banyan tree']. *Why:* The older the split, the more hidden changes there are and the fewer fossils to set the rate. The ranges are about half a million years, 4 million, 150 million and 450 million.
11. (write) There is no fossil for a certain split. Suggest two other ways to check a clock date for it.  
   **Answer:** Use a date from the Earth, such as when an island or a continent broke away. Or date the split again with different genes, or with a rate set from a different fossil, and see whether the answers agree.. *Why:* A strong answer looks for a second route to the same date. A result reached by two separate routes can be trusted more.
12. (mcq) Tamil and Malayalam share many old words. What does a family tree of languages say about this?  
   Options: Each borrowed every word from the other / All languages have the same words / It is chance / Both descend from one earlier language  
   **Answer:** Both descend from one earlier language. *Why:* Languages are passed on by copying, with small changes kept. Two languages that share many old words, changed in regular ways, split from one parent language. The reasoning is the same as for shared changes in DNA.
13. (truefalse) True or false: a tree built from a single gene always matches the tree of the species.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A single stretch of DNA can have a history that differs from the species' history, most often when two splits came close together. That is why many genes are used.
14. (label) Label three points on a time line: today, India and the Seychelles break apart, the two frog families split.  
   **Answer:** ['Today: at the right-hand end', 'India and the Seychelles break apart: about 65 million years ago', "The purple frog's family and the Seychelles frogs split: about 131 million years ago, further left"]. *Why:* The frog split is about twice as far back as the break-up of the land. So the two families were already separate when the land divided.
15. (mcq) Two living species differ in 12 squares. Each line changes 0.3 squares a round. How many rounds since they split?  
   Options: 20 rounds / 40 rounds / 36 rounds / 4 rounds  
   **Answer:** 20 rounds. *Why:* Both lines have been changing since the split, so the differences grow at 0.3 + 0.3 = 0.6 squares a round. 12 ÷ 0.6 = 20. Choosing 40 means dividing by 0.3, which forgets that two lines are changing.
16. (odd) Three of these are passed on by copying, with small changes kept. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: DNA / Hand-copied books / Languages / Pebbles in a river  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* DNA, books copied by hand and languages are each copied from an earlier version, so each has a family tree. A pebble is shaped by water and is not a copy of an earlier pebble.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) On a family tree, how do you find out how closely two tips are related?  
   Options: See how near each other they are drawn / Count the tips between them / Trace back to the fork where their lines meet / Check which is nearer the top  
   **Answer:** Trace back to the fork where their lines meet. *Why:* The fork where two lines meet is their last shared ancestor. The more recent that fork, the closer the relatives. How near two tips are drawn means nothing, because any fork can be flipped.
2. (mcq) A tree is drawn with a fish at the left and a human at the right. What does that order show?  
   Options: Fish are less evolved than humans / Humans came last / Nothing: the order of the tips has no meaning / Fish are the ancestors of humans  
   **Answer:** Nothing: the order of the tips has no meaning. *Why:* The same tree can be drawn with the tips in many orders. Every living tip has been evolving for the same length of time. Choosing 'less evolved' reads the tree as a ladder, which a tree is not.
3. (mcq) A bat and a crow both have wings. Why is that weak evidence that they are close relatives?  
   Options: Wings are too small to study / Wings evolved separately in each, and are built differently / Bats fly only at night / All animals once had wings  
   **Answer:** Wings evolved separately in each, and are built differently. *Why:* A bat's wing is skin over long finger bones, and a crow's is feathers on an arm. The two evolved separately, for the same job. A lookalike feature tells you how animals live, not who their ancestors were.
4. (mcq) Which feature best shows that a dolphin is closer to a cow than to a shark?  
   Options: The dolphin lives in the sea / The dolphin has a backbone / The mother feeds her young on milk / The dolphin has a smooth shape  
   **Answer:** The mother feeds her young on milk. *Why:* Milk is a new feature that appeared once, in the first mammals, so sharing it marks a shared ancestor. All three have a backbone, so it cannot separate them. The smooth shape is a lookalike shared with the shark.
5. (mcq) Scientists first try the tree that needs the fewest changes. What counts as a change?  
   Options: A new fossil being found / A feature appearing or being lost / A scientist changing her mind / An animal moving to a new place  
   **Answer:** A feature appearing or being lost. *Why:* Each change is an event in a family line: a feature appears, or is lost. A tree that makes milk or lungs appear twice needs more such events, so it is less likely.
6. (mcq) Why can copying mistakes in DNA be used as a clock?  
   Options: Each mistake happens on a fixed date / Cells count the years / Mistakes happen only once a year / Over long times they add up at a steady average rate  
   **Answer:** Over long times they add up at a steady average rate. *Why:* Each mistake is chance, but a large number of chance events has a steady average. So the count of differences grows roughly in step with time. No cell counts years, and no mistake has a fixed date.
7. (truefalse) True or false: a scientist can predict which DNA letter will change next.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each mutation is a chance event, like one roll of a die. Only the average rate over very many letters and many generations can be measured.
8. (mcq) What is a dated fossil used for in a molecular clock?  
   Options: To supply the DNA / To replace the counting of differences / To decide which genes to read / To set the rate of the clock  
   **Answer:** To set the rate of the clock. *Why:* The fossil gives a known date for one split. Dividing the DNA differences for that split by the date gives the rate. Almost no fossil has DNA left, so the DNA comes from living relatives.
9. (mcq) A study dates a split to '131 million years ago, between 93 and 177'. What does the range tell you?  
   Options: That the scientists disagree with each other / How far from 131 the true date might be / That there were two splits / That the study failed  
   **Answer:** How far from 131 the true date might be. *Why:* The count of chance changes wobbles, and the rate is an estimate. The range shows the dates that fit the evidence. A study that gave no range would be hiding its uncertainty.
10. (mcq) Which pair are each other's closest living relatives?  
   Options: A shark and a dolphin / A bat and a crow / A whale and a hippo / A dugong and a dolphin  
   **Answer:** A whale and a hippo. *Why:* DNA, jumping genes and fossil ankle bones all place whales beside hippos. The other three pairs only look alike, because they swim or fly in the same way.
11. (mcq) Fossil whales with legs had a double-pulley ankle bone. What did that show?  
   Options: Whales once climbed trees / The fossils were not whales / Ankles are lookalike features / Whales belong with hippos, cattle and deer  
   **Answer:** Whales belong with hippos, cattle and deer. *Why:* Only the even-toed hoofed mammals have that ankle. Finding it in early whales agreed with what DNA had already shown. An odd bone shape with no link to diet or swimming is unlikely to be a lookalike.
12. (mcq) You want to date a split from 500 million years ago. Which gene do you choose?  
   Options: A slow-changing gene / A fast-changing gene / Any gene, since all change alike / The shortest gene  
   **Answer:** A slow-changing gene. *Why:* Over 500 million years a fast gene fills up with hidden changes, and the count stops growing. A slow gene is still collecting changes steadily. Fast genes suit recent splits.
13. (mcq) Why do scientists build a species tree from thousands of genes and not one?  
   Options: A single gene can have a different history / One gene is too short to read / Genes are all identical / It makes the tree look bigger  
   **Answer:** A single gene can have a different history. *Why:* When two splits come close together, chance can leave one gene with a tree that differs from the species tree. With thousands of genes, the tree that most agree on stands out.

## Applications
- Doctors and health workers build the family tree of a virus from samples taken in different places. The tree shows where an outbreak started and how it is spreading.
- Scientists who protect wildlife use DNA trees to find out whether two populations are one species or two. The purple frog of the Western Ghats was recognised as a family of their own in this way.
- Forest officers test seized ivory, skins and timber. The DNA shows which species, and sometimes which forest, the material came from.
- Plant breeders look for the wild relatives of crops such as rice, brinjal and tomato. A close wild cousin may carry genes for resisting a disease.
- Food inspectors read a short stretch of DNA to check that fish sold under one name is not a cheaper species.
- People who study old books and palm-leaf manuscripts group the copies by shared mistakes, to work out what the first version said.
- Scientists who study languages build family trees of words, to learn how peoples moved and met long ago.

## Open questions
- **Which animals branched off first: sponges or comb jellies?** DNA letters have given both answers, depending on the method. A 2023 study of whole chromosomes points to comb jellies, and not every scientist is convinced yet.
- **How long before the first animal fossils did animals really begin?** Molecular clocks put the first animals well before the oldest clear fossils. The earliest animals were probably tiny and soft, and may have left nothing in the rocks to check the clock against.
- **Why does the molecular clock run at different speeds in different family lines?** Short generations explain part of it. Body temperature, body size and how well cells repair DNA may matter too. Nobody can yet predict a line's rate from how the animal lives.
- **Is the deepest part of the tree of life a tree at all?** Bacteria and archaea pass genes sideways to unrelated cells. Some scientists think the base of the tree is better drawn as a net, and they disagree about how much of a tree is left.
- **How many branches of life have never been seen by anyone?** Most microbes cannot yet be grown in a laboratory. They are known only from scraps of DNA in soil and sea water, and new large branches are still being found.

## Connected maths
- In the Dice Clock Test, strips A, B and C had 3, 7 and 11 coloured squares after 10, 20 and 40 rounds. What is the rate of this clock, in coloured squares for each round? **0.3 coloured squares in each round.**. Add the squares: 3 + 7 + 11 = 21. Add the rounds: 10 + 20 + 40 = 70. Divide: 21 ÷ 70 = 0.3. So on average, three squares change in every ten rounds. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- The Mystery strip has 9 coloured squares, and your clock's rate is 0.3 squares in each round. Estimate the rounds. The secret number was 34. By what fraction of 34 were you off? **The estimate is 30 rounds. It is off by 4 rounds, which is 2 parts in 17, or about 12 per cent.**. 9 ÷ 0.3 = 30 rounds. 34 − 30 = 4. As a fraction of the true number, 4 ÷ 34 = 2 ÷ 17, which is about 0.12, or 12 per cent. An answer within 12 per cent is good for a clock made of chance. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Strip C was rolled for 4 times as many rounds as strip A. Strip A had 3 coloured squares, so you might expect 12 on C. It had 11, and it also had 2 dots. Explain the 11. **There were 13 changes on strip C, but 2 of them landed on squares that were already coloured. 13 − 2 = 11.**. A dot marks a second change on one square. So the real number of changes was 11 + 2 = 13, a little more than the 12 expected. Only 11 can be seen. The longer a strip is rolled, the more changes hide like this. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L5`)
- Two kinds of bird differ in 2 DNA letters in every 100. Fossils show their lines split 10 million years ago. Two other birds differ in 5 letters in every 100. If the rate is the same, when did the second pair split? **About 25 million years ago.**. 2 letters in 100 took 10 million years, so 1 letter in 100 takes 5 million years. 5 letters in 100 take 5 × 5 = 25 million years. These numbers are made up, to show the method. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In Fewest Changes, Tree 1 costs 1 change for each of 6 features and 2 changes for each of 2 features. Tree 2 costs 2 changes for each of 6 features and 1 for each of 2. How many more changes does Tree 2 need? **Tree 2 needs 4 more changes: 14 against 10.**. Tree 1: 6 × 1 + 2 × 2 = 6 + 4 = 10. Tree 2: 6 × 2 + 2 × 1 = 12 + 2 = 14. 14 − 10 = 4. Tree 1, with the dolphin and the cow as sisters, is the first choice. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- In Fast Gene, Slow Gene, the fast strip had 4, 6, 9 and 11 coloured squares at rounds 6, 12, 24 and 48. The rounds doubled each time. Did the squares double? What pattern do you see, and why? **No. The squares rose by 2, then 3, then 2. The rounds added went up from 6 to 12 to 24, but the strip has only 12 squares and was filling up.**. From round 6 to 12 the strip gained 2 squares in 6 rounds. From 12 to 24 it gained 3 squares in 12 rounds. From 24 to 48 it gained only 2 squares in 24 rounds. Each stretch of time added less for each round, because more and more hits landed on coloured squares. The count can never pass 12. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)

## Warm-up
- On a family tree, what does a fork stand for? → A common ancestor, where one family line split into two.
- Who is the whale's closest living relative? → The hippo.
- What is a lookalike feature? → A feature that two groups evolved separately because they live in the same way.
- What does a molecular clock count? → The DNA differences between two family lines.
- What is a dated fossil used for in a molecular clock? → To set the rate, by giving a known date for one split.
- Why is the date of a split given as a range? → Because the changes come by chance, so the count wobbles.
- Is a crocodile closer to a lizard or to a crow? → To a crow. Crocodiles and birds are sister groups.
