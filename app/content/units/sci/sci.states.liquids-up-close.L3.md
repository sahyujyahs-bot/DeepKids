# Thick and runny liquids, and the skin on water
`sci.states.liquids-up-close.L3`

**Summary.** Tip a spoon of water and a spoon of honey together, and the water is gone while the honey crawls. How easily a liquid flows is called its viscosity. Honey has a high viscosity because its particles hold on to each other and slide past each other slowly. Warm honey runs much faster than cool honey, because warmth makes its particles move faster. Water is runny, but its particles attract each other too, and at the top this makes a stretchy skin called surface tension. Water striders, insects with long, hairy, waterproof legs, live on that skin and hunt insects that fall onto ponds.

**Intro.** *Hold a spoon of water and a spoon of honey over a plate, and tip both at the same moment. The water is gone in a blink. The honey stretches into a slow, shiny rope. Both are liquids. So why does one pour so much more slowly than the other?* In this unit you race kitchen liquids down a slope and time each one. You find out what makes a liquid thick or runny, and what warming does to it. Then you look closely at the top of water. There, water acts like a thin stretchy skin. It is strong enough to hold a dome of water on a coin, and to hold up a living insect.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what viscosity means, and put kitchen liquids in order from runny to thick.
1. I can run a fair race of liquids down a slope, time each one, and show the results in a table.
2. I can explain that warming makes most liquids runnier, because their particles move faster and slip past each other more easily.
3. I can explain surface tension: water particles attract each other, so the top of the water acts like a stretchy skin, and soap makes that skin weaker.
4. I can explain how a water strider lives on the water's skin: the problem they face, and how their legs and senses help.
5. I can tell the story of the pitch drop experiment, and explain why something that shatters like a solid can still flow.

## Terms
- **Liquid**: A liquid flows and takes the shape of whatever holds it, but keeps the same amount. *e.g.* Water poured from a jug into a tumbler takes the tumbler's shape.
- **Flow**: To flow is to move along in a stream, the way a liquid does when it is poured or runs downhill. *e.g.* Rain water flows down the slope of a road into the drain.
- **Viscosity**: Viscosity is how much a liquid resists flowing. A liquid with high viscosity flows slowly. A liquid with low viscosity flows fast. *e.g.* Honey has a high viscosity. Water has a low viscosity.
- **Viscous**: Viscous means thick and slow to flow. It is the scientist's word for a thick liquid. *e.g.* Dosa batter is more viscous than buttermilk.
- **Runny**: Runny means thin and quick to flow, with low viscosity. *e.g.* Rasam is runny. Sambar with lots of dal is thicker.
- **Particle**: A particle is a tiny piece of a substance, far too small to see. Every solid, liquid and gas is made of particles. *e.g.* A drop of water has more water particles than there are grains of sand on many beaches.
- **Attract**: To attract is to pull something closer. Particles of a liquid attract each other. *e.g.* Water particles attract each other, so a drop holds together in a round shape.
- **Surface**: The surface is the outside or top layer of something. The surface of water is where the water meets the air. *e.g.* A dry leaf floats on the surface of a bucket of water.
- **Surface tension**: Surface tension is the pull between particles at the surface of a liquid. It makes the surface act like a thin stretchy skin. *e.g.* A one-rupee coin can hold a dome of water drops higher than its edge, because of surface tension.
- **Water strider**: A water strider, also called a pond skater, is an insect with very long legs who lives on the surface of ponds and slow streams. *e.g.* Water striders skate across temple tanks and paddy fields in India.
- **Pitch**: Pitch is a black, tar-like material made from coal tar or crude oil. It feels hard, but it flows very slowly. *e.g.* Pitch was once used to make boats waterproof.
- **Fair test**: A fair test changes only one thing and keeps everything else the same, so you know what caused the result. *e.g.* In the syrup race, every liquid uses the same spoon, the same slope and the same finish line.

## Explanations
### Viscosity: how easily a liquid flows · *defined*
**Definition:** Viscosity is how much a liquid resists flowing. Thick liquids, like honey, have a high viscosity. Runny liquids, like water, have a low viscosity.

All liquids flow, but not at the same speed. Pour water, oil, shampoo and honey down the same slope, and they arrive in that order. Scientists measure viscosity by timing how long a liquid takes to flow through a narrow tube, or how fast a ball sinks through it. Honey at room warmth has a viscosity thousands of times higher than water. Viscosity is about flowing, not about weight. Coconut oil is more viscous than water, yet it is lighter, so it floats on top of water.
- e.g. Water runs off a plate at once. Honey takes minutes to slide down the same plate.
- e.g. Coconut oil pours more slowly than water, but floats on water.
- e.g. Dosa batter is viscous enough to stay in a round shape on the tawa for a moment.
### Why honey is thick and water is runny · *defined*
**Definition:** The particles in a liquid hold on to each other, but they can slide past each other. When they hold on strongly, or are long and tangled, the liquid flows slowly.

In a liquid, the particles touch, but they are not locked in place as in a solid. They keep sliding past each other, and that is why a liquid flows. Water particles are small and slide past each other easily. Honey is about four parts sugar to one part water. Its sugar particles are much bigger than water particles, and they cling tightly to the water particles and to each other. So they slide past each other slowly. Oil particles are long and much bigger than water particles. Shampoo has long, chain-like particles that tangle like cooked noodles.
- e.g. Add sugar to water bit by bit, and the water gets thicker: that is how sugar syrup is made.
- e.g. Kanji, the water rice is cooked in, is thick because of long starch particles from the rice.
- e.g. Shampoo is made thick on purpose, so it stays in your hand.
### Warming makes liquids runnier · *defined*
**Definition:** When most liquids are warmed, their viscosity goes down, so they flow faster. Warmth makes the particles move faster, so they slip past each other more easily.

The particles in a liquid are always jiggling. Warm the liquid, and they jiggle harder. Then they break away from their neighbours more often, and slide past them more easily. Honey shows this very clearly. Warm a spoon of honey in a bowl of warm water for five minutes, and it runs down a plate many times faster. Cooking oil gets runnier in a hot kadai. A halwai warms thick jaggery syrup so it can be poured. If some liquids get cold enough, they stop flowing and turn solid. Coconut oil turns white and hard in a cool room, below about 24 degrees Celsius.
- e.g. Warm honey runs fast. Honey from a cold room crawls.
- e.g. Ghee is soft and thick on a cool morning, and pours easily when warmed.
- e.g. Coconut oil in a bottle goes white and hard in a cold winter, and melts again in warm water.
### Liquids that change when you stir or shake them · *defined*
**Definition:** Some liquids get runnier when they are stirred, shaken or squeezed. A few do the opposite and get thicker.

Ketchup is the best known example. Turn a full bottle upside down, and the ketchup often stays put. Shake it or tap the bottle hard, and it suddenly runs. Shaking pulls apart the tiny bits that were holding it in shape. Set curd does something similar. Stir it with a spoon, and it becomes runnier. Paint is made this way on purpose, so it spreads under a brush but does not drip off the wall. A thick paste of cornflour and water does the opposite. It flows when you move it slowly, but turns hard when you punch it. The unit Sorting materials has a test with it.
- e.g. Ketchup stays in the bottle, then runs when you tap the bottle.
- e.g. Set curd turns runnier when you stir it well.
- e.g. Wall paint spreads easily under the brush, then stays where it is put.
### The slowest liquids: pitch and ice · *defined*
**Definition:** Some materials feel solid but still flow, so slowly that it takes years to notice. Pitch is the most famous example.

Pitch is a black material made from tar. Hit it with a hammer at room temperature and it shatters like glass. Yet it flows. At the University of Queensland in Australia, pitch has been dripping out of a glass funnel since 1930. Only nine drops have fallen so far. The university says pitch is about 100 billion times more viscous than water. Ice also flows when there is a lot of it. A glacier is a huge river of ice, such as the Gangotri glacier in the Himalayas. Its own weight makes it creep slowly downhill. Even the hot rock deep inside the Earth moves very slowly, a few centimetres a year.
- e.g. In the pitch drop experiment, a drop has fallen about every seven to fourteen years.
- e.g. A glacier creeps downhill, carrying rocks along with it.
- e.g. A thick wax candle left in a hot car can sag and bend over hours.
### Surface tension: the skin on water · *defined*
**Definition:** Surface tension is the pull between particles at the surface of a liquid. It makes the surface act like a thin stretchy skin.

Water particles attract each other. Deep inside the water, each particle is pulled equally from every side. At the surface there is only air above, and air pulls very little. So the surface particles are pulled down and sideways, towards their neighbours. They hold together like a stretched sheet. That is why a drop is round, and why a coin can hold a dome of water above its edge. Soap makes the skin much weaker. Oil has a weaker skin than water, so oil drops spread out flatter. The units Biomimicry and How water climbs a plant say more about water's pull.
- e.g. Drops of water pile up on a coin into a dome before they spill over.
- e.g. A drop of soap breaks the skin, and the dome spills at once.
- e.g. A loop of thread on water snaps into a circle when soap touches inside it.
### Water striders: insects who live on the water's skin · *defined*
**Definition:** A water strider is an insect who lives on the surface of still or slow water. Their legs are long, and covered in tiny waxy hairs that keep water off, so the water's skin holds them up.

Many small insects fall onto ponds by accident: ants, flies, small moths. They struggle and cannot get off. That is food for anyone who can reach them, but reaching them means standing on water without sinking. A water strider does this. Their legs are covered in thousands of tiny hairs with a waxy coat, and air is trapped between the hairs. Water cannot wet the legs, so each foot only presses a small dent in the water's skin. Their long legs spread their weight over a wide area. Water striders feel ripples through their legs. A struggling insect sends out ripples, and the strider turns and skates to it, then grabs the prey with their short front legs. Many water striders also send ripple signals to each other by tapping the water.
- e.g. Water striders rush to an ant that has fallen into a temple tank.
- e.g. Under a water strider, you can see small round dents in the water, and their shadows on the bottom.
- e.g. Sea skaters, a kind of water strider, live on the open ocean, far from land.
### How a water strider rows · *defined*
**Definition:** A water strider moves by sweeping their middle legs backwards like oars. The legs push the water back, and the water pushes the strider forward.

Each middle leg presses a dent in the water's skin and sweeps it backwards fast. This pushes water backwards in small swirls. The water pushes back on the leg, so the strider shoots forward. The back legs steer, like a rudder. For a long time, some scientists thought striders moved mainly by making ripples. But baby water striders move their legs too slowly to make those ripples, and they still move well. In 2003, scientists in the United States added dye to the water and filmed striders at high speed. They saw the swirls pushed backwards, and showed that this is how striders move. They also built a little robot strider that rowed the same way.
- e.g. A water strider can move many times their own body length in one stroke.
- e.g. Watch a strider in sunlight, and you may see the shadows of the dents move as they row.
### Other lives that use the water's skin · *defined*
**Definition:** Many small living things use the surface of water: some stand on it, some hang from it, and some use it to escape.

Mosquito larvae, the young of mosquitoes, live in still water. They hang just under the surface and breathe through a short tube that pokes through the skin. Health workers sometimes spread a thin film of special oil on stagnant water. The oil weakens the skin, so the larvae cannot hang on and breathe. Some small beetles, called rove beetles, live at the water's edge. If one falls in, they let out a soap-like liquid from their back end. It weakens the skin behind them, and the stronger skin in front pulls them forward fast. Some small water bugs escape in the same way, using their spit.
- e.g. Mosquito larvae hang under the surface of water left standing in a pot or a tyre.
- e.g. Emptying standing water every week stops mosquitoes from growing there.
- e.g. A rove beetle can shoot across water by letting out a soap-like liquid.
### Is glass a very slow liquid? · *debated*
Glass is made by melting sand and cooling it fast. Its particles stay in a jumble, like a liquid, instead of lining up in neat rows like a crystal. So people often ask if glass is a liquid that is too slow to see. Scientists agree that ordinary glass does not flow in any way we can measure at room temperature. They still argue about what exactly happens to the particles as a hot runny liquid turns into hard glass.
- e.g. Window glass hundreds of years old has not flowed in any way anyone can measure.
- e.g. Hot glass in a glass factory flows like very thick honey.
- **Most materials scientists:** Glass is a solid. Its particles are jumbled like a liquid's, but they are stuck in place. At room temperature it would take far longer than people have existed to sag at all.
- **Some physicists who study glass:** Glass is a liquid that has slowed down so much that it seems frozen. How a liquid slows into a glass is still one of the big unsolved puzzles of physics.

## Mostly agreed
- Every liquid has a viscosity, and different liquids flow at very different speeds. *How we know:* Scientists time liquids flowing through narrow tubes, and record their viscosity in tables. Anyone can see the difference by racing liquids down a slope.
- Warming makes almost every liquid less viscous, so it flows faster. *How we know:* Measurements of honey, oils and syrups at different temperatures all show this. Honey can flow ten times faster when it is warm.
- Water has a strong surface tension, because water particles attract each other strongly. Soap makes it much weaker. *How we know:* The pull of the surface can be measured with a ring or a plate pulled up from water. Agnes Pockels measured how soap and oil change it in her kitchen, in the 1880s.
- Water striders stand on water because their waxy, hairy legs repel water, and surface tension holds up the small dents they make. *How we know:* Scientists have looked at the legs under powerful microscopes and measured how much weight one leg can hold up. In 2004, a team in China found that one leg can hold about 15 times the insect's weight.

## How we found out
**Thomas Parnell, the first physics professor at the University of Queensland, and John Mainstone, who looked after the experiment after him, 1927 (the pitch was poured), 1930 (the funnel was opened) and 1938 (the first drop fell).** Pitch is a black material made from tar, once used to keep boats from leaking. At room temperature it feels hard. Hit it with a hammer, and it shatters into sharp pieces, like glass. So most people thought pitch was a solid. In 1927, Thomas Parnell, a physics professor in Brisbane, Australia, wanted to show his students that it was not so simple. He heated some pitch until it was soft and poured it into a glass funnel with its stem sealed. He let it settle for three years. In 1930 he cut off the end of the stem, and waited. Nothing seemed to happen. Slowly, a drop began to grow under the funnel. The first drop fell in 1938, eight years later. Parnell died before he saw one fall. John Mainstone looked after the funnel for 52 years and also never saw a drop fall, though he came close. By 2014, nine drops had fallen. The pitch has been flowing all along, about 100 billion times more slowly than water.  
*What people believed before:* People thought pitch was a solid, because it feels hard and shatters into sharp pieces when hit with a hammer. The dripping funnel showed that it flows, only extremely slowly.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Thick liquids are heavier than runny ones.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Honey is thick, and a jar of honey is heavier than the same jar of water.  
  Against: Coconut oil is more viscous than water, but it is lighter. Pour it on water, and it floats. Thickness is about how a liquid flows, not how heavy it is.
- **Old church windows are thicker at the bottom because the glass has slowly flowed down.** (A story often told in books and by tour guides; *rejected*)  
  For: Some very old window panes in Europe really are thicker at one edge.  
  Against: Old glass was made by spinning hot glass into a disc, so panes came out uneven. Builders often put the thick edge at the bottom, but sometimes at the top or sides. Scientists have worked out that glass at room temperature would take far longer than people have existed to sag in any way we could see.
- **Pitch, which shatters under a hammer, is really a liquid.** (Thomas Parnell, a physics professor in Australia, in 1927; *accepted*)  
  For: Pitch has been dripping out of a funnel at the University of Queensland since 1930. Nine drops have fallen.  
  Against: Pitch breaks into sharp pieces when hit, like a solid. Some people say it is fairer to call it a solid that flows.
- **A water strider stays up only because they are very light.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Water striders are small and light. A heavy insect, like a beetle, would sink.  
  Against: Add a drop of soap to the water, and a water strider sinks, though their weight has not changed. Their waxy, hairy legs matter as much as their weight. The legs keep the water from wetting them, so the skin holds.
- **Water striders move by making ripples that push them along.** (Some scientists, until about 2003; *rejected*)  
  For: A rowing strider does make ripples, and ripples carry energy.  
  Against: Baby water striders move their legs too slowly to make those ripples, yet they move well. In 2003, filming showed that striders move mainly by pushing swirls of water backwards with their legs.

## Roots
We have raced liquids and watched the water's skin hold up a dome of drops. Now we ask what thick and runny liquids tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The same honey crawled down your plate at room warmth and ran fast once warmed. Warmth makes a liquid's particles jiggle harder, so they slip past each other more easily. So a liquid's thickness depends on how warm it is, as well as on what it is made of.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'honey is thick' and 'water is runny' as if thickness were a fixed part of each, like a name. But thickness is how easily particles slide past each other, and that changes with warmth. The same honey can crawl or run.  
*Evidence:* In the syrup race, two spoons from the same honey jar raced side by side. The only difference was five minutes in warm water. The warmed honey reached the finish line several times faster.

- *who am i:* Your blood is a liquid too. Its viscosity matters: your heart pumps it through tubes thinner than a hair.
- *what is life:* A water strider lives their whole life on the water's skin. They find food and send signals through ripples on it.
- *what is this world:* Even things that look solid flow, given time: pitch in a funnel, the ice of a glacier, the hot rock deep under the ground.

Thick and runny, skin and no skin, all come from one thing: particles that attract each other and keep moving. In honey, they cling and slide slowly. At the top of water, the same kind of pull makes a skin a strider can stand on. Warmth shakes the particles harder and loosens their hold. So the flowing honey, the dome on the coin and the skating strider are one set of particles, holding on and letting go.

- **Heraclitus, as reported by Plato in the Cratylus (402a):** Heraclitus says somewhere that all things move and nothing stays still. (Pitch looks still, yet it has dripped from a funnel since 1930. Even a glacier creeps downhill. Things we call still may be moving too slowly for us to notice.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 78 (a common English translation):** Nothing in the world is softer or weaker than water. Yet for attacking what is hard and strong, nothing can surpass it. (Water feels like nothing to a finger. Yet its skin holds up a dome on a coin and a living insect. Soft things can hold more than they seem to.)
- Science (settled): Warming lowers the viscosity of almost every liquid, because faster-moving particles slide past each other more easily.
- Science (settled): Surface tension comes from the attraction between particles of a liquid. Soap weakens it.
- Science (settled): Pitch flows, about 100 billion times more slowly than water.
- Science (open): What exactly happens to particles when a liquid slows into a glass has no agreed explanation yet.
- Other view, Many physicists: Viscosity is a number we measure, and the particle picture is a useful model of it. Honey flowing faster when warm is a fact about honey. It carries no lesson about stillness or change, and reading one into it says more about us than about honey.
- Practice, Slow as honey (3 min): Let's watch one slow spoon of honey pour onto a plate. As the honey stretches down, breathe in slowly. As it coils on the plate, breathe out slowly. Let's do it three times. Notice how long one slow pour really takes. Then say one thing around you that might be moving too slowly to see.
- Ask: If the same honey can crawl or run, what is honey really like?
- Ask: Pitch looks still but flows. What else around you might be moving too slowly to notice?
- Ask: A water strider lives on a skin we cannot even see. What other worlds might be right in front of us that we never notice?

- *deep:* Pitch shatters like a solid but drips like a liquid. Is it a solid or a liquid? Who gets to decide? (hint: Think about how long you watch. In one second, pitch acts like a solid. In ten years, it acts like a liquid.)
- *deep:* Honey is thick, and warm honey is runny. If the same honey can be both, is 'thick' really part of what honey is? (hint: Remember the syrup race. What changed between the two spoons of honey, and what stayed the same?)
- *big:* Water feels like nothing when you put your finger in it. Yet its skin holds up a living insect. What else might be stronger than it feels? (hint: Think about the dome of water on the coin, and how many drops it took to break it.)
- *big:* A water strider feels ripples with their legs and knows where food is. What would the world feel like if you lived on top of a pond? (hint: Close your eyes and rest your fingertips on water while someone drops a grain of rice. What do you notice?)

## Story: The Skaters on Paati's Tank
*Nila, her cousin Arjun, Paati and the town are made up. Water striders really do live on temple tanks and ponds across India, and soap really does make them sink.*

Every May, Nila stayed with her Paati in a small town near Kumbakonam. Paati means grandmother in Tamil. Behind Paati's house was an old temple tank, a square pond with stone steps going down to the water on all four sides. Nila was nine. This year her cousin Arjun had come too. He was seven, and he had never seen a temple tank before.

On the first morning, Nila took Arjun down the steps to the water. It was early and still cool. The water was flat and green, and lotus leaves floated in one corner.

'Look,' said Nila, and she pointed. 'Skaters.'

Near the bottom step, a dozen thin brown insects stood on the water. They had long legs spread wide, like the legs of a stool. They did not swim. They stood on top of the water, as if it were a floor. Then one of them swept their middle legs back, and shot forward the length of Arjun's hand, and stopped.

Arjun crouched down. 'Why don't they fall in?'

'Look at their feet,' said Nila.

The sun was low and shone through the water onto the stone step below. Under each skater, on the stone, there were six little round shadows. They looked like dark coins with bright edges.

'Those are dents,' said Nila. 'Each foot pushes a small dent into the top of the water, like your finger pushing into a balloon. But the foot does not go through.'

'Why not?'

'Paati showed me last year. Come on.'

In the kitchen, Paati was making coffee. Nila took a one-rupee coin from the shelf and a steel tumbler of water. She dipped a straw into the water, put her finger over the top, and let drops fall onto the coin, one at a time. Arjun counted. At ten drops the water was a little hill. At twenty it was a tall shiny dome, higher than the edge of the coin. At twenty-six it spilled.

'See?' said Nila. 'The water holds on to itself. The top acts like a skin. Paati says scientists call it surface tension. The skaters stand on the skin.'

Paati smiled over her coffee. 'And their legs are covered in tiny hairs, with a kind of wax on them. Water cannot wet those legs. So the skin stays whole under their feet.'

For the next three days, Arjun went down to the tank every morning. He learned to sit very still on the bottom step. He saw that the skaters did not just stand about. When a small ant fell off a lotus leaf and kicked on the water, rings spread out around the ant. Within a moment, two skaters had turned and raced towards it. The first one grabbed the ant with their short front legs.

'They felt the ripples,' Arjun said at lunch. 'Like a phone buzzing.'

On the fourth morning, a man came down the far steps with a bucket and a bar of soap. He soaped his scooter's mudguards on the bottom step and splashed them clean. White foam slid across the water into the corner where the skaters were.

Nila and Arjun watched. One skater skated into the foam. Their feet slipped through. Their legs sank, and then their body tipped, and they were in the water, kicking and pulling, unable to climb back up.

'It's drowning,' said Arjun.

'They,' said Nila. 'Not it. And they're not drowning yet. Quick, get me a lotus leaf.'

Arjun pulled a small leaf from the edge. Nila slid it under the struggling skater and lifted them out. She carried the leaf to the other side of the tank, where the water was clear and calm, and tipped it gently. The skater sat still for a long moment. Then they rubbed their legs with their feet, one by one. Then they stepped onto the water, and stood, and skated away.

Arjun let out his breath. 'Why did the soap make them sink?'

That night, Nila showed him. She dropped water on the coin again, and Arjun counted to twenty-four before it spilled. Then she dried the coin and used water with one drop of soap stirred in. This time the water made a flat puddle, and spilled at eight.

'Soap breaks the skin,' said Nila. 'It makes the water hold on to itself less. Then the skaters' feet go through, and the water wets their legs.'

Arjun looked at the coin for a long time. 'So for us, soap is for washing. For them, it's like a hole in the floor.'

The next morning, Arjun pulled Nila across to the man with the scooter. Arjun was seven and very serious. He pointed at the corner, and at the skaters, and at the soap. Nila explained how soap breaks the water's skin. The man listened, scratched his head, and laughed. Then he carried his bucket up the steps and washed his scooter on the road instead.

**For the parent:** The top of water is a skin that holds itself together. Water striders live on that skin, and soap, which seems so harmless to us, breaks it under their feet.

- Ask: How could Nila and Arjun tell that each skater's foot made a dent in the water?
- Ask: Why did the dome on the coin spill so much sooner when Nila added soap?
- Ask: How did the skaters know where the fallen ant was?
- Ask: Why did the skater rub their legs with their feet after Nila rescued them?
- Ask: Arjun said soap is like a hole in the floor for a skater. Is that a good way to explain it? Why?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Syrup Race: which liquid is slowest, and does warmth change it? (40 min)
- **question:** Which kitchen liquid flows slowest down a slope? And does the same honey flow faster when it is warm?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put water, coconut oil, shampoo and honey in order, from fastest to slowest. Then guess: will warm honey be faster, slower or the same as honey at room warmth? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every liquid gets the same amount, half a level teaspoon, dropped on the same start line. Every lane is on the same tray, at the same slope, with the same finish line. Each lane is used once, so no liquid runs over another's track. In the honey race, the only thing that changes is how warm the honey is.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the warm water. It should feel comfortably warm on the inside of your wrist, like bath water, never hot from the stove. Do not taste the shampoo. Wash the tray with soap afterwards, and keep honey drips away from the floor, or ants will come.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then draw the tray with each liquid's lane and how far it went.
- **results table:** Liquid | My guess (1st to 5th) | Time to the finish line (seconds) | Real place (1st to 5th)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread newspaper on the floor or table. 2. Put the tray on it. 3. Mark a start line 3 centimetres from the top edge, with tape on both sides. 4. Mark a finish line 15 centimetres below it. 5. Put the stack of books under the top end.  
  *Parent says:* This tray is our race track. Every liquid starts here and must reach the finish line down there.  
  *Child mode:* Put tape on both edges at the top. That is the start line. Measure 15 centimetres down. Tape again. That is the finish line.
  - Note: The marks only need to be on the two side edges. Your child can look across from one mark to the other. A slope of about 8 centimetres on a 30 centimetre tray works well. Too steep, and everything is too fast to time.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the four liquids. 2. Let your child tip each bottle slowly and look. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Look at how each one moves in its bottle. Which will be fastest down the tray? Which will be slowest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Tip each bottle slowly. Watch it move. Which will win? Which will come last? Why?
  - Ask: Put them in order from fastest to slowest. What is your reason? (good: Water first, because it splashes and pours fast.; Honey last, because it is thick and sticky.; Oil before shampoo, because shampoo is thicker.)
  - Note: Write the guesses in the 'My guess' column. Any order with a reason is a good guess. Don't say what will happen.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the tray flat for a moment. 2. Put half a level teaspoon of water on the start line, in the left lane. 3. Lift the top onto the books and start the stopwatch. 4. Stop it when the front of the liquid crosses the finish line. 5. Do the same for oil and shampoo, each in their own lane.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? I lift, you start the stopwatch. Stop it the moment the front of the liquid crosses the finish line.  
  *Child mode:* Lift the tray onto the books. Start the stopwatch. Stop it at the finish line. Write the time.
  - Note: Lift the tray onto the books smoothly, not with a bang. Water will probably finish in about one or two seconds, which is hard to time. Writing 'under 2 seconds' is fine. Oil often takes a few seconds, and shampoo much longer. If your coconut oil has gone white and hard in a cool room, stand the bottle in warm water first. If a liquid has not finished after 5 minutes, write how far it got with the ruler.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the tray flat again. 2. Put half a teaspoon of honey on the start line, in a new lane. 3. Lift, start the stopwatch, and watch until it crosses the line, or until 5 minutes have passed.  
  *Parent says:* Now the honey. Watch it carefully. How is it moving compared with the water?  
  *Child mode:* Now the honey. Start the stopwatch. How is it moving? Wait for the finish line.
  - Note: Honey at room warmth often takes one to four minutes to go 15 centimetres. It depends on your honey and your room. While you wait, ask your child what they notice about the front of the honey. It often stays round and fat.
- **s5 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put half a teaspoon of honey in each small bowl. 2. Stand one bowl in the big bowl of warm water. 3. Leave the other on the table. 4. Wait 5 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Both bowls have the same honey. One sits in warm water and one stays on the table. Will the warm honey be faster, slower, or the same? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Same honey in two bowls. One sits in warm water. Will warm honey be faster? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What do you think the warmth will do, and why? (good: Faster, because warm things melt and get runny.; Slower, because warm honey is stickier.; The same, because it is still honey.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is good. A grown-up mixes the warm water from the tap or a kettle with some cold water. It should feel like bath water.
  - Wait: Wait 5 minutes while the honey warms. Look back at the table of results.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the tray flat. 2. Scoop the warm honey onto the start line in a new lane. 3. At the same moment, put the room-warm honey in the next lane. 4. Lift the tray and start the stopwatch. 5. Time each one to the finish line.  
  *Parent says:* Two honeys, side by side. Lift, start, and watch which one wins.  
  *Child mode:* Put both honeys on the start line together. Lift the tray. Which one wins?
  - Note: Warm honey usually runs several times faster. If your tray has no clean lanes left, wipe two lanes with a damp cloth and dry them well. If the difference is small, the warm water may have cooled. Make it warm again and try once more.
  - Check: The warm honey ran down much faster than the honey at room warmth. Why? → **Warmth made the honey's particles move faster, so they slipped past each other more easily**
- **s7 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill in the 'Real place' column. 2. Compare it with the guesses. 3. Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Was your guess right? Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Fill in the real places. Was your guess right? Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: honey has the highest viscosity, and warmth lowers it. What I saw: water finished in about a second and honey took minutes. Warm honey beat the same honey at room warmth. Why: honey's particles cling together and slide slowly, and warmth helps them slip past each other.
  - Think: If you put the honey in the fridge for an hour, what do you think would happen in the race?
### e2 · How Many Drops Fit on a Coin? (25 min)
- **question:** How many drops of water can sit on a coin before they spill? Does soapy water or oil hold as many?
- **predict:** Guess first. How many drops of plain water will fit on a one-rupee coin before it spills? Will soapy water fit more drops, fewer, or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same coin is used every time, wiped clean and dry between tries. The drops are all made the same way, with the same dropper or straw, from the same height. Each liquid is tried three times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** None needed. Wipe up oil spills so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the coin from the side, with the water dome just before it spilled.
- **results table:** Liquid | Try 1 (drops) | Try 2 (drops) | Try 3 (drops) | Middle number
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the dry coin on the steel plate. 2. Show how to use the dropper. With a straw, dip it in water, press a finger on the top end, and lift. Lift the finger a little to let one drop fall.  
  *Parent says:* We will drop water onto this coin, one drop at a time. We count every drop until the water spills over the edge.  
  *Child mode:* Put the coin on the plate. Practise making one drop at a time.
  - Note: A straw dropper takes a little practice. Let your child make a few drops on the plate first. Hold the dropper just above the coin, about one centimetre, and let each drop fall gently.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* How many drops of water do you think will fit on the coin? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* How many drops will fit before it spills? Why?
  - Ask: How many drops will fit, and why? (good: Three, because the coin is small and flat.; Ten, because drops are small.; Lots, because the water can pile up.)
  - Note: Most children guess between 2 and 10. Write the guess down.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Drop plain water onto the middle of the coin, one drop at a time. 2. Make a tally mark for every drop. 3. Stop when the water spills over the edge. 4. Dry the coin and repeat twice more.  
  *Parent says:* Count out loud. Look at the coin from the side. What shape is the water making?  
  *Child mode:* Drop, count, drop, count. Look from the side. What shape is the water?
  - Ask: What do you notice about the water as you add drops? (good: It makes a hill.; It goes higher than the edge of the coin.; It wobbles but does not spill.)
  - Note: A one-rupee coin usually holds somewhere between 15 and 40 drops, depending on the coin and the drop size. The water makes a dome that rises well above the edge. That surprises most children.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dry the coin well. 2. Do the same with the soapy water, three times. 3. Then do it with the oil, three times, wiping the coin with a tissue between tries.  
  *Parent says:* Now soapy water. Will it make the same tall dome? Then oil.  
  *Child mode:* Now try soapy water three times. Then oil three times. Count each drop.
  - Note: Soapy water usually holds far fewer drops, and makes a low, flat puddle that spills early. Oil also spreads flatter than water. Dry the coin very well after the soapy water. Any soap left on it will spoil the next plain water try.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each liquid, put the three numbers in order. 2. Circle the middle one. 3. Write it in the 'Middle number' column.  
  *Parent says:* Put your three numbers in order, and pick the middle one. That is a fair number for each liquid.  
  *Child mode:* Put the three numbers in order. Circle the middle one.
  - Check: Plain water made a tall dome on the coin. Soapy water spilled after far fewer drops. Why? → **Soap weakens the pull between water particles at the surface, so the skin breaks sooner**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? Why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: water has a strong skin, and soap makes it weaker. What I saw: water piled up to 28 drops in a tall dome, soapy water spilled at 9. Why: water particles pull on each other at the surface, and soap gets in the way.
  - Think: A water strider stands on the water's skin. What do you think would happen to a strider if soapy water ran into their pond?

## Applications
- Cooks thicken sambar, gravies and payasam to the right viscosity with dal, coconut or rice flour.
- Engine oil must stay runny enough on a cold morning and thick enough when the engine is hot.
- Paint and ketchup are made to flow when pushed and stay put when left alone.
- Cough syrup is made thick so it coats the throat.
- Soaps and detergents weaken water's skin, so water can soak into cloth and lift out dirt.
- Doctors know that thicker blood flows more slowly, which matters for the heart.
- Road builders heat tar until it is runny enough to spread, and it hardens as it cools.
- Health workers use thin oil films on stagnant water to stop mosquito larvae breathing.
- Engineers have built small robots that skate on water like water striders.
- Ink pens and printers depend on ink with just the right viscosity and surface tension.

## Open questions
- **What exactly happens to the particles when a hot liquid slows down and becomes glass?** Physicists can measure it very carefully, but there is still no agreed theory that explains it fully. It is often called one of the big unsolved problems of physics.
- **Why do some liquids, like ketchup, get runnier when shaken, while a cornflour paste gets thicker?** Scientists understand the main idea, but working out exactly how the tiny bits arrange themselves in each mixture is still being studied.
- **How do water striders tell apart the ripples from food, from a mate and from danger?** Scientists know they use ripple signals, and have recorded some of them. How their legs and nerves sort one ripple pattern from another is not fully known.
- **When will the next drop fall in the pitch drop experiment?** The flow speed changes with the weather, so nobody can say exactly. The university expected the tenth drop sometime in the 2020s.
- **Can we predict the viscosity of a new liquid from its particles alone?** For simple liquids, yes, roughly. For mixtures like honey or blood, scientists still have to measure it.

## Connected maths
- Honey at room warmth took 150 seconds to reach the finish line. Warmed honey took 30 seconds. How many seconds faster was the warm honey? **120 seconds faster.**. 150 minus 30 is 120. So the warm honey arrived 120 seconds sooner. That is 2 whole minutes, because 60 + 60 = 120. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Honey at room warmth took 150 seconds. Warmed honey took 30 seconds. How many times as fast was the warm honey? **5 times as fast.**. 150 divided by 30 is 5. Check: 30 + 30 + 30 + 30 + 30 = 150. So the warm honey covered the track 5 times in the time the room-warm honey covered it once. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
- Nila counted 26, 31 and 28 drops of plain water on her coin. Which is the middle number? Why is it a good number to use? **28.**. In order, the numbers are 26, 28, 31. The middle one is 28. One try can be a fluke, for example a drop that splashed. The middle number is not pulled up or down by one odd try. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- Honey took 150 seconds to flow 15 centimetres. If it keeps the same speed, how long would it take to flow down a tray 30 centimetres long? **300 seconds, which is 5 minutes.**. 30 centimetres is 2 lots of 15 centimetres. So it takes 2 lots of 150 seconds: 150 + 150 = 300 seconds. 300 seconds is 5 minutes, because 5 × 60 = 300. A real honey blob may slow down as it spreads thinner, so test it. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Thomas Parnell poured the pitch in 1927. He opened the funnel in 1930. The first drop fell in 1938. How many years after the pitch was poured did the first drop fall? **11 years.**. From 1927 to 1930 is 3 years. From 1930 to 1938 is 8 years. 3 + 8 = 11 years. You can also do 1938 − 1927 = 11. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)

## Things to buy
- [A small bottle of honey, in a clear bottle](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=honey+small+bottle) (material): The slowest liquid in the syrup race, and the bubble race uses the clear bottle. Most homes have some. A small bottle from any kirana is enough. Used in e1, a1, a4, c1. 
- [A plastic medicine dropper](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=plastic+dropper+pipette) (tool): Optional. It makes even drops for the coin test. A drinking straw with a finger over the top works too. Chemists sell droppers cheaply. Used in e2. Useful again in units on dissolving, plants and colour mixing.

## Warm-up
- Which is more viscous, water or honey? → Honey.
- Does warming make honey thicker or runnier? → Runnier.
- What is the skin on top of water called? → Surface tension.
- Does soap make the water's skin stronger or weaker? → Weaker.
- Name an insect who stands on water. → A water strider.
- Is coconut oil heavier or lighter than water? → Lighter. It floats.
- Which black material drips from a funnel about once every ten years? → Pitch.
- How does a water strider find a fallen ant? → They feel the ant's ripples through their legs.
