# Antibodies, allergies and HIV: how the defence remembers, and three ways it goes wrong
`sci.body.immune-system.L5`

**Summary.** A doctor can take one drop of your blood and tell which germs you met years ago. How can blood hold a record? In this lesson you will run a colour test on three pretend patients to measure how much antibody each one has, and find out why a test can miss a new infection. You will stroke your arm to wake the cells that cause allergies, count who lived and died in Boston in 1721, and model what HIV does to helper T cells. You will also learn why the defence sometimes attacks harmless food, or the body itself.

**Intro.** *A mosquito bites you and flies away. Ten minutes later a bump rises and itches. The mosquito has gone, so who made the bump?* Your own body made the bump. A mosquito leaves a little saliva in your skin. Your immune system has met that saliva before, and it has made antibodies against it. Antibodies are tiny Y-shaped proteins that fit one target and nothing else. The bump and the itch are your body answering the saliva. A baby who is bitten for the very first time usually gets no bump at all, because the baby's body has no record of mosquito saliva yet. So a bump is a sign that your body remembers. In this lesson you will find out how the body keeps that record, how doctors read it, and what happens when the defence answers the wrong thing.

## Terms
- **Antigen**: An antigen is any molecule that the immune system can recognise by its shape. Most antigens are proteins or sugars on the outside of a germ. *e.g.* The spike on the outside of a measles virus is an antigen. So is a protein in mosquito saliva.
- **Antibody**: An antibody is a Y-shaped protein made by the immune system. The two tips of the Y are the same, and they fit one antigen. *e.g.* An antibody against measles sticks to a measles virus. The same antibody does not stick to a chickenpox virus.
- **Immunoglobulin**: Immunoglobulin is the scientists' name for an antibody. It is shortened to Ig. Humans make five classes: IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE and IgD. *e.g.* IgG is the main antibody in blood. IgA is the main antibody in tears, spit and mother's milk.
- **B cell**: A B cell is a white blood cell that carries one kind of antibody on their surface. When that antibody meets a matching antigen, the B cell divides into many cells. *e.g.* A B cell whose antibody fits tetanus toxin rests in a lymph node until that toxin arrives.
- **Plasma cell**: A plasma cell is a B cell who has changed into an antibody factory. One plasma cell can release thousands of antibodies every second. *e.g.* Plasma cells living in your bone marrow still release measles antibodies years after your measles vaccine.
- **Memory cell**: A memory cell is a B cell or T cell who stays alive for years after an infection. Memory cells divide fast if the same antigen comes back. *e.g.* People who lived through the flu of 1918 still had memory B cells against that virus ninety years later.
- **Clumping**: Clumping is what happens when antibodies join many germs into one lump. It works because each antibody has two arms and can hold two germs. Scientists call it agglutination. *e.g.* Germs in a clump cannot spread out, and one phagocyte can swallow the whole clump.
- **Titre**: A titre is a measure of how much antibody a sample holds. You dilute the sample step by step. The titre is the weakest dilution that still tests positive. *e.g.* If a blood sample still tests positive when it is 64 times weaker, but not at 256 times weaker, the titre is 64.
- **Window period**: The window period is the time just after an infection when a test still says no. The germ is in the body, but there is not yet enough antibody for the test to find. *e.g.* A person tested for HIV too soon after catching the virus can get a negative result, so the test is repeated later.
- **Allergy**: An allergy is an immune response against something harmless, such as pollen, dust mites or a food. The response itself causes the trouble. *e.g.* A child with a peanut allergy may get itchy lips and a rash within minutes of eating one peanut.
- **Allergen**: An allergen is the harmless thing that sets off an allergy. It is an antigen that the body answers with IgE antibodies. *e.g.* Common allergens in India are house dust mites, pollen, cockroach droppings, milk, egg, peanuts and prawns.
- **IgE**: IgE is the class of antibody that causes allergies. IgE antibodies sit on mast cells and wait. Blood holds less IgE than any other class of antibody. *e.g.* A person who is allergic to prawns has IgE antibodies that fit a prawn protein.
- **Mast cell**: A mast cell is an immune cell who lives in the skin, the nose, the lungs and the gut. Mast cells are packed with tiny bags of histamine. *e.g.* Mast cells in your skin release histamine around a mosquito bite, and a bump rises.
- **Histamine**: Histamine is a chemical that mast cells release. It widens small blood vessels and makes them leaky, and it makes nerve endings itch. *e.g.* Medicines for hay fever are called antihistamines, because they stop histamine from acting on cells.
- **Anaphylaxis**: Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction all over the body. Histamine is released in many places at once. Breathing becomes hard and blood pressure falls. It is an emergency. *e.g.* A person in anaphylaxis needs an injection of adrenaline and a hospital straight away.
- **Thymus**: The thymus is a soft organ behind the breastbone, above the heart. Young T cells are tested there. The T cells who would attack the body's own cells are removed. *e.g.* A child's thymus is large and busy. A grown-up's thymus has shrunk and is mostly fat.
- **Tolerance**: Tolerance means that the immune system does not attack a certain antigen. The body is tolerant of its own cells, of food and of friendly microbes. *e.g.* You eat rice every day and your immune system does not attack rice proteins. That is tolerance.
- **Autoimmune disease**: An autoimmune disease is an illness in which the immune system attacks some of the body's own cells. 'Auto' means self. *e.g.* In type 1 diabetes, T cells destroy the cells in the pancreas who make insulin.
- **Helper T cell**: A helper T cell is a white blood cell who switches other immune cells on. Helper T cells tell B cells to make antibodies. They are also called CD4 cells. *e.g.* Without a signal from a helper T cell, most B cells do not become plasma cells.
- **HIV**: HIV is a virus who infects helper T cells and destroys them over years. The letters stand for human immunodeficiency virus. *e.g.* HIV passes from person to person in blood, in sex between grown-ups, and from a mother to her baby.
- **AIDS**: AIDS is the illness that follows when HIV has destroyed most of a person's helper T cells. Germs that a healthy body controls easily can then cause serious disease. *e.g.* A person with HIV who takes medicine every day may never get AIDS.
- **Antiretroviral medicine**: Antiretroviral medicines stop HIV from copying themselves inside cells. A person takes them every day for life. The treatment is shortened to ART. *e.g.* In India, government ART centres give these medicines free.
- **Inoculation**: Inoculation was an old way of protecting people from smallpox. A little matter from a smallpox blister was put into a scratch on a healthy person. It is also called variolation. *e.g.* Onesimus was inoculated as a boy in West Africa. In Bengal, people called tikadars did the same work.

## Explanations
### An antibody is a Y-shaped protein with two arms · *defined*
**Definition:** An antibody is a protein shaped like the letter Y. The two tips of the Y are exactly alike, and each tip fits one antigen.

An antibody is built from four chains of protein, two long and two short, held together in a Y. It is about ten nanometres long. A million antibodies laid end to end would be about one centimetre. The stem of the Y is nearly the same in every antibody of one class. The tips differ from one B cell's antibody to the next, and the tips are where the antibody fits its antigen. Why two tips and not one? With two tips, one antibody can hold two germs at once, or hold one germ in two places. Holding in two places is much firmer than holding in one. Nobody shapes a tip to suit a germ. The tips are made before any germ arrives. A tip fits an antigen because their shapes and electric charges match closely, the way your hand fits a glove made for it. Rodney Porter in England and Gerald Edelman in the United States worked out this structure around 1960. They shared the Nobel Prize for it in 1972.
- e.g. An antibody against tetanus toxin holds a toxin molecule at each tip.
- e.g. An antibody against a measles virus does not stick to a chickenpox virus, because the shapes do not match.
- e.g. IgM, the first antibody made in an infection, is five Y shapes joined in a ring, so it has ten tips.
### What antibodies do to a germ: block, clump and flag · *defined*
**Definition:** Antibodies do not kill a germ by themselves. They block the germ, join germs into clumps, and flag them so that other parts of the defence destroy them.

Many people picture antibodies as tiny weapons. That is not correct. An antibody is a sticker with two sticky tips. It helps in three main ways. First, blocking. A virus gets into a cell by fitting part of their surface to a part of the cell. If antibodies cover that part, the virus cannot get in. A toxin, which is a poison made by a germ, is blocked in the same way. Second, clumping. Each antibody can hold two germs, so many antibodies tie many germs into one lump. A lump cannot spread through the body, and one phagocyte can swallow the whole of it. A phagocyte is a white blood cell who swallows germs. Third, flagging. Phagocytes have grips that fit the stem of the Y. A germ coated in antibodies is caught and swallowed far more quickly than a bare germ. Antibodies on a bacterium also switch on complement. Complement is a set of blood proteins that make holes in the outer skin of bacteria.
- e.g. Antibodies against the diphtheria toxin block the toxin before it reaches your cells.
- e.g. When a doctor checks a blood group, antibodies clump red blood cells of the wrong group, and the clumps are easy to see.
- e.g. A bacterium coated in antibodies is swallowed by a phagocyte within minutes.
### Plasma cells and memory cells: how the record is kept · *defined*
**Definition:** After an infection, some B cells become plasma cells who go on releasing antibodies, and others become memory cells who wait. Together they are the body's record of that germ.

When a B cell's antibody fits an antigen, and a helper T cell gives the signal, the B cell divides. Most of the new cells become plasma cells. A plasma cell is a factory that releases thousands of antibodies every second. Most plasma cells die within days or weeks. Some travel to the bone marrow, the soft inside of your bones, and live there for years. Here is a puzzle. One IgG antibody lasts only about three weeks in the blood before the body breaks it down. Yet people still have measles antibodies fifty years after measles. How? The long-lived plasma cells in the bone marrow keep making fresh ones, year after year. In 2007, scientists in the United States reported on blood samples saved from 45 people over many years. The level of measles antibody had hardly fallen. Tetanus antibody fell faster, by half in about eleven years, which is one reason tetanus needs boosters. Memory cells are the second half of the record. They make no antibody while they wait. If the germ returns, they divide at once into new plasma cells.
- e.g. Old people who lived through the 1918 flu still had memory B cells against that virus about ninety years later.
- e.g. A tetanus booster is given because the level of tetanus antibody slowly falls.
- e.g. Your bone marrow holds plasma cells for many different germs and vaccines at the same time.
### Reading the record: how an antibody test works, and when it misses · *defined*
**Definition:** An antibody test does not look for the germ. It looks for antibodies against the germ in a person's blood. It can miss an infection that is only a few weeks old.

Many blood tests for infections look for antibodies. The test has pieces of the germ fixed to a plate or a strip. A drop of the person's blood serum is added. Serum is the clear yellowish liquid part of blood. If the serum holds antibodies that fit, they stick. A second chemical then makes a colour appear. Colour means that antibodies are there. To measure how much antibody there is, the serum is diluted step by step, and each step is tested. The weakest dilution that still gives colour is called the titre. A high titre means a lot of antibody. This kind of test has a weakness. After a first infection, the body needs time to make antibodies. For one to three weeks there are too few to find. With HIV, most tests cannot find antibodies until some weeks after infection, and a few people take up to three months. This gap is called the window period. A person in the window period has the virus and can pass them on, but the test says no. That is why a test is repeated later. A test that looks for the germ's own genes can find an infection sooner.
- e.g. The ELISA test that found the first HIV cases in India in 1986 showed a yellow colour where antibodies were present.
- e.g. Before a child gets some vaccines again, a doctor can measure the titre to see whether the first dose still protects.
- e.g. Blood banks in India test every lesson of donated blood for HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C.
### Five classes of antibody for five kinds of job · *defined*
**Definition:** Humans make five classes of antibody, called IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE and IgD. The tips can be the same while the stem differs, and the stem decides where the antibody goes and what happens next.

Why does one body need five classes? The tips of the Y decide what the antibody sticks to. The stem decides what happens after that. So a B cell can keep the same tips and change the stem, to send the same fit to a different place. IgM comes first in a new infection. It is five Y shapes joined in a ring. Each tip of a new antibody holds weakly, so ten tips together make up for that. IgG comes next. It is the main antibody in blood and it lasts longest. In a pregnant mother, IgG passes across the placenta into the baby's blood. So a newborn has a copy of the mother's record for the first months. IgA is released into tears, spit, the lining of the nose and gut, and mother's milk. It meets germs before they get inside. IgE sits on mast cells and is the antibody of allergies. IgD is found on the surface of young B cells. Scientists are still not sure of all that IgD does.
- e.g. A baby drinking mother's milk swallows IgA, which coats the inside of the baby's gut.
- e.g. A test that finds IgM against dengue virus points to a recent infection. IgG alone points to an older one.
- e.g. A person with hay fever has IgE that fits a pollen protein.
### Allergy: a full alarm for a harmless thing · *defined*
**Definition:** In an allergy, the body makes IgE antibodies against something harmless. When that thing comes again, mast cells release histamine, and the histamine causes the itching, swelling and sneezing.

An allergy needs two meetings. At the first meeting nothing seems to happen. But some B cells make IgE that fits the allergen, perhaps a protein in peanut. The IgE antibodies fix their stems to mast cells in the skin, nose, lungs and gut, with the tips pointing outward. Now the person is ready to react. At the next meeting, one allergen molecule touches the tips of two IgE antibodies on the same mast cell and links them. Within a minute or two the mast cell releases bags of histamine. Histamine widens small blood vessels and makes them leaky, so fluid moves out into the skin or the lining of the nose. That gives a rash, a runny nose, watery eyes and swelling. Histamine also makes nerve endings itch. In the lungs it tightens the airways. A mosquito bump is a small, safe allergic reaction to mosquito saliva. A severe reaction all over the body is called anaphylaxis. The throat can swell, breathing becomes hard and blood pressure falls. It needs an injection of adrenaline and an ambulance at once. In India the ambulance number is 108, and the emergency number is 112.
- e.g. A baby bitten by a mosquito for the first time usually gets no bump, because the baby has no IgE against the saliva yet.
- e.g. A child allergic to house dust mites sneezes when old bedding is shaken.
- e.g. Antihistamine tablets work by stopping histamine from acting on cells.
### Why do allergies exist at all? · *debated*
IgE and mast cells cost the body energy to make, and they can kill. So why do we have them? Scientists have several ideas, and each has some evidence. Nobody has settled the question. Allergies have also become more common in many countries in the last sixty years, in rich countries first and now in many cities of Asia. Human genes cannot change that fast, so something in the way people live must have changed. The ideas below are not rivals in every way. More than one may be true.
- e.g. In 2013, two teams found that mice who had met bee venom once made IgE against the venom. Those mice survived a larger dose later, and mice with no IgE did not.
- e.g. Later mouse studies found the same for the venom of Russell's viper, a snake found across India.
- e.g. In a British study published in 2015, babies at high risk of peanut allergy either ate peanut foods from their first year or avoided peanut. By age five, about 17 in 100 of the avoiders were allergic, and about 3 in 100 of the eaters.
- **The worm idea:** IgE and mast cells fight parasitic worms, who are too big for a phagocyte to swallow. Itching, sneezing, mucus and a rushing gut help to push a worm out. Where worms are rare, the same system fires at pollen and food instead.
- **The toxin idea, from Margie Profet in 1991:** Allergic reactions are a defence against poisons such as venoms and harmful plant chemicals. Sneezing, vomiting and scratching get a poison out of the body fast. The mouse studies of 2013 support this for bee and snake venom.
- **The old friends idea:** A young immune system needs to meet many harmless microbes from soil, animals and other children. They teach it what to ignore. With fewer of these meetings, it learns less well. David Strachan first suggested something like this in 1989.
- **The early food idea:** The gut teaches tolerance. A baby who eats a food early learns to tolerate it. A baby who first meets the food through sore, broken skin may make IgE against that food. The 2015 peanut study supports this.
### Telling self from not-self: the test in the thymus · *defined*
**Definition:** Tolerance means that the immune system does not attack the body's own cells. Much of it is learned in the thymus, where T cells who would attack the body are removed before they are let out.

B cells and T cells get their receptors by a shuffle of genes, with no plan. A receptor is the part of a cell that fits an antigen. A random shuffle must also make some receptors that fit the body's own molecules. Those cells would be dangerous. So young T cells are tested in the thymus, a soft organ behind the breastbone. Cells of the thymus show the young T cells samples of the body's own proteins, even proteins that belong to the eye or the pancreas. A T cell whose receptor grips one of these strongly is given a signal to die. A T cell whose receptor is of no use at all dies too. In mice, about 95 out of every 100 young T cells die in the thymus and never leave. Jacques Miller found out what the thymus is for in 1961. He removed the thymus from newborn mice, and they grew up with almost no defence. The test is not perfect. So a second guard exists outside the thymus. Regulatory T cells are T cells who calm other immune cells down. Shimon Sakaguchi found them in 1995, and Mary Brunkow and Fred Ramsdell found the gene they depend on. The three shared the Nobel Prize in 2025.
- e.g. Your immune system leaves your own red blood cells alone but attacks red blood cells of the wrong blood group.
- e.g. A child's thymus is large. It shrinks from the teenage years, because most of the testing is done early in life.
- e.g. Babies born without a working thymus have very few T cells and catch many infections.
### Autoimmune disease: the defence attacks the body · *defined*
**Definition:** An autoimmune disease is an illness in which a person's own immune system attacks some of their own cells. Tolerance has failed for one part of the body.

In each autoimmune disease, the target is different. In type 1 diabetes, T cells destroy the cells of the pancreas who make insulin. The child must then take insulin every day. In Hashimoto's disease, antibodies and T cells attack the thyroid gland in the neck. In rheumatoid arthritis the joints are attacked, and they swell and hurt. In coeliac disease, gluten, a protein in wheat, sets off an attack on the person's own gut lining. In vitiligo, patches of skin turn white. Most scientists think this is the immune system destroying the cells who make skin colour. Why does tolerance fail? Genes matter. So do triggers such as certain infections. Sometimes a germ's antigen looks so much like a body molecule that the antibodies made against the germ fit the body too. In rheumatic fever, antibodies made against a throat bacterium also stick to the heart. About four out of five people with autoimmune diseases are women, and nobody is sure why. Autoimmune diseases are not catching. They are treated with medicines that replace what was lost, or that calm the immune system. Most cannot yet be cured.
- e.g. A child with type 1 diabetes checks blood sugar and takes insulin before meals.
- e.g. A person with coeliac disease stays well by eating no wheat, barley or rye. Rice, ragi, jowar and bajra are safe.
- e.g. Vitiligo is not catching. You cannot get it by touching someone.
### HIV: a virus who infects the helper T cells · *defined*
**Definition:** HIV is a virus who enters helper T cells, copies themselves there and destroys those cells over years. With too few helper T cells, the rest of the defence does not get switched on.

A virus must get inside a cell to copy themselves. Each kind of virus fits certain cells. HIV fits a molecule called CD4, which sits on helper T cells. That is the worst possible cell to lose. Helper T cells give the signal that lets B cells make antibodies and lets killer T cells work. HIV carries their genes as RNA. Inside the cell, an enzyme copies the RNA into DNA, and the DNA is joined into the cell's own DNA. From then on the cell makes new viruses. This copying makes many mistakes, so HIV changes very fast. Antibodies made against last month's virus fit this month's virus poorly. A healthy person has roughly 500 to 1,500 helper T cells in a cubic millimetre of blood, a drop the size of a pinhead. Without treatment the number falls slowly for about ten years on average. The person feels well for most of that time. Below 200, germs that a healthy body controls easily cause serious illness. That stage is called AIDS. Tuberculosis is the most common killer of people with AIDS. Françoise Barré-Sinoussi and Luc Montagnier in Paris found the virus in 1983.
- e.g. A person can carry HIV for years, look healthy and not know, which is why testing matters.
- e.g. The fungus that causes thrush is kept in check in a healthy mouth. In a person with AIDS the fungus can spread down the throat.
- e.g. The body does make antibodies against HIV. That is how the test works. But the virus changes too fast for them to clear the infection.
### Living with HIV: medicines, and how HIV does and does not spread · *defined*
**Definition:** Antiretroviral medicines stop HIV from copying. A person who takes them every day can live a long and healthy life, and when the virus in their blood is too low to measure they do not pass HIV on through sex.

HIV passes from one person to another in only a few ways. The virus travels in blood, in the body fluids shared in sex between grown-ups, and from a mother to her baby during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. HIV does not pass through hugging, shaking hands, sharing a plate or a tumbler, coughing, toilets, swimming pools or mosquitoes. You can play, eat and study with a person who has HIV with no risk at all. Since 1996, doctors have given three antiretroviral medicines together. Why three? HIV changes so fast that a virus able to resist one medicine soon appears. A virus who can resist three at once is very unlikely. The medicines do not remove the virus. HIV DNA stays hidden inside resting cells, and the virus returns if the medicines stop. So they are taken for life. In 2001 these medicines cost more than 10,000 dollars a year for one person. That year Yusuf Hamied of the Indian company Cipla offered them at 350 dollars a year, less than a dollar a day. Millions of people in Africa and Asia could then be treated. India has given the medicines free since 2004. A mother on treatment has a very small chance of passing HIV to her baby.
- e.g. In 2023, about 25 lakh people in India were living with HIV.
- e.g. Hospitals use a new sterile needle for every injection, so that no blood passes between patients.
- e.g. A pregnant woman in India is offered an HIV test, so that treatment can protect her baby.
### Onesimus, the tikadars and Jenner: protection before anyone knew of antibodies · *defined*
**Definition:** Inoculation meant putting a little matter from a smallpox blister into a scratch on a healthy person. The person usually had a mild illness and was then protected for life. People in Africa, India, China and Turkey did this long before Europe did.

Smallpox once killed about three in every ten people who caught the disease. People noticed that nobody caught smallpox twice. So in several parts of the world, healers gave people a small dose on purpose. In Bengal, travelling inoculators did this work. They were later known as tikadars, from tika, a mark. A British doctor, John Holwell, described their method in a report in 1767. Historians still argue about how old the practice was in India. In West Africa the practice was common too. A West African man was enslaved and taken to Boston, in America, where he was given the name Onesimus. Around 1716 he told the man who held him, a minister called Cotton Mather, that he had been inoculated as a boy and showed the scar. When smallpox struck Boston in 1721, Mather urged the town's doctors to try it. Only one, Zabdiel Boylston, agreed. He counted his results, and far fewer of the inoculated died. But inoculation used real smallpox. A few people died of it, and they could infect others. In 1796 the English doctor Edward Jenner tested a safer way, using cowpox, a mild disease caught from cows. That was the first vaccine. In 1980 the World Health Organization declared that smallpox was gone from the Earth. Nobody in this story knew that antibodies and memory cells existed. They knew what worked.
- e.g. Lady Mary Wortley Montagu saw inoculation in Turkey and had her daughter inoculated in London in 1721.
- e.g. Jenner's word 'vaccine' comes from vacca, the Latin word for cow.
- e.g. We do not know what name Onesimus was given by his parents, because nobody wrote it down.
### Down to the atoms: why an antibody sticks · *defined*
**Definition:** An antibody holds an antigen by many weak attractions between atoms at the same time. No strong chemical bond is made, so the hold depends on a close fit.

Zoom in on one tip of an antibody. It is a small pocket or surface made of a few dozen amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. Some of their atoms carry a little positive electric charge, and some a little negative charge. Some parts mix well with water, and some avoid it. An antigen sticks when its bumps sit in the hollows of the tip, and its positive spots lie against negative spots. Each single attraction is very weak. The moving water around them could break it at once. But when many weak attractions act together, the antigen stays. This is why shape matters so much. A molecule that is slightly the wrong shape touches at only a few points and drifts away. It is also why an antibody can let go. Heat, acid or strong salt loosens the hold. Weak attractions of this kind also hold a gecko's foot to a wall. So the whole of antibody recognition comes down to atoms attracting and repelling each other by their charges.
- e.g. During an infection, B cells whose antibodies hold more firmly are chosen again and again, so later antibodies fit better than early ones.
- e.g. A penicillin molecule is too small to act as an antigen alone. Joined to a body protein it can become one, and a few people become allergic.
- e.g. Scientists now design antibodies as medicines. Their names often end in 'mab'.
### What nobody knows yet · *idea*
Much about this defence is still unknown. Nobody has made a vaccine that protects well against HIV, after more than forty years of trying. The virus changes too fast, and hides inside the DNA of resting cells. A very small number of people have been cured of HIV. The first was Timothy Ray Brown, who was treated in Berlin in 2007. He had a blood cancer, and he was given new bone marrow from a donor whose cells lack a doorway that HIV uses. This treatment is far too dangerous to give to people who are not already very ill. Nobody knows why most people with autoimmune diseases are women. Nobody knows exactly why allergies have become more common, or why one child outgrows a milk allergy and another does not. Nobody knows how some plasma cells stay alive for a lifetime while others die in a week. These are open questions that scientists are working on now.
- e.g. A few people control HIV for many years without medicine. Scientists study them to learn how.
- e.g. Children often outgrow allergies to milk and egg, but much less often an allergy to peanuts.
- e.g. New medicines for some autoimmune diseases calm one part of the immune system and leave the rest working.
- **Scientists working on an HIV vaccine:** Some think a vaccine must teach the body to make rare antibodies that fit the parts of HIV that cannot change. Others think it must train T cells instead. Neither has worked well enough yet.

## Mostly agreed
- An antibody is a Y-shaped protein with two matching tips, and each tip fits one antigen. *How we know:* Rodney Porter cut antibodies into three pieces with an enzyme in 1959. Two pieces still held the antigen and one did not. Later, pictures made with X-rays and electron microscopes showed the Y shape directly.
- Antibodies do not kill germs alone. They block them, clump them and flag them for other parts of the defence. *How we know:* Under a microscope, bacteria mixed with matching antibodies gather into clumps but stay alive. Add phagocytes or complement from fresh blood, and the bacteria are destroyed.
- The body keeps making antibodies against a germ for many years after the germ has gone. *How we know:* In a 2007 study, blood saved from 45 people over about fifteen years showed that the level of measles antibody hardly fell. One antibody molecule lasts only weeks, so cells must keep making new ones.
- An allergic reaction is caused by IgE antibodies on mast cells, which release histamine when the allergen links two IgE antibodies. *How we know:* Teruko and Kimishige Ishizaka found IgE in 1966, and Gunnar Johansson and Hans Bennich found it separately in Sweden. Serum from an allergic person, put into the skin of a person who is not allergic, makes that patch of skin react to the allergen.
- The immune system can attack the body's own cells, and this causes autoimmune diseases. *How we know:* In 1956, Noel Rose and Ernest Witebsky made rabbits attack their own thyroid glands. In the same year Deborah Doniach, Ivan Roitt and Peter Campbell found antibodies against the thyroid in the blood of patients with Hashimoto's disease.
- HIV causes AIDS by destroying helper T cells, and antiretroviral medicines stop the virus from copying. *How we know:* The virus was found in 1983 in patients' lymph nodes. The number of helper T cells in the blood falls as the amount of virus rises. When people take the medicines, the virus in the blood falls and the helper T cells rise again.
- A person with HIV whose virus is too low to measure does not pass HIV on through sex. *How we know:* Large studies followed thousands of couples in which one partner had HIV and took medicine, and the other did not have HIV. Over several years, not one partner caught HIV from a person whose virus was too low to measure.

## How we found out
**Paul Ehrlich, who said the body never attacks itself. Noel Rose and Ernest Witebsky in Buffalo, and Deborah Doniach, Ivan Roitt and Peter Campbell in London, who showed that it can., About 1900 (Ehrlich's idea) and 1956 (the rabbit experiments and the patients with Hashimoto's disease).** Around 1900 the German scientist Paul Ehrlich injected goats with blood from other goats. The goats made antibodies against it. When he injected a goat with her own blood, she made none. Ehrlich decided that a body would never attack itself. He gave the idea a Latin name, horror autotoxicus. For fifty years most doctors believed him, and few looked for diseases of self-attack. In the 1950s, in Buffalo in the United States, a young researcher called Noel Rose injected rabbits with material from their own thyroid glands, mixed with a substance that alerts the immune system. The rabbits made antibodies against their own thyroids, and the glands were damaged. His professor, Ernest Witebsky, had been taught in Ehrlich's tradition and did not believe it at first. They repeated the work and published it in 1956. In London that same year, Deborah Doniach, Ivan Roitt and Peter Campbell tested blood from patients with Hashimoto's disease, in which the thyroid slowly fails. The blood held antibodies against the patients' own thyroid. The body can attack itself.  
*What people believed before:* That a body's immune system will never attack the body's own cells. In 1956, rabbits and human patients were both shown to make antibodies against their own thyroid glands.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Antibodies kill germs.** (Many school books and news reports; *rejected*)  
  For: People with plenty of antibody against a germ usually do not fall ill from that germ. So the antibodies seem to kill.  
  Against: An antibody is only a protein that sticks. Bacteria coated in antibody stay alive in a dish. They die when a phagocyte swallows them or complement makes holes in them. Antibodies block, clump and flag.
- **The body will never attack itself.** (Paul Ehrlich, about 1900; *rejected*)  
  For: Ehrlich injected goats with the blood of other goats, and they made antibodies. Injected with their own blood, they made none. He called the idea of self-attack 'horror autotoxicus', the dread of poisoning oneself.  
  Against: In 1956, rabbits were made to attack their own thyroid, and patients with Hashimoto's disease were found to carry antibodies against their own thyroid. Today more than eighty autoimmune diseases are known.
- **A negative test means the person is not infected.** (Common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Modern tests are very good. Once antibodies have appeared, an HIV antibody test finds almost every infected person.  
  Against: An antibody test looks for the body's answer, and the answer takes weeks to appear. In the window period the virus is present and the test says no. A second test some weeks later settles it.
- **Edward Jenner was the first person to protect people from smallpox.** (Many history books; *debated*)  
  For: Jenner tested cowpox in 1796, published his results and made a much safer method that the whole world took up.  
  Against: People in West Africa, India, China and Turkey had inoculated against smallpox for a long time before him. Onesimus brought the knowledge to Boston about eighty years before Jenner's test. Jenner himself was inoculated as a boy.
- **An allergy is nothing but a useless mistake of the immune system.** (The usual view in medicine for most of the last century; *debated*)  
  For: Pollen and peanuts do no harm, yet the reaction to them can kill. Nothing seems to be gained.  
  Against: Mice who had met bee venom once made IgE against it and survived a bigger dose later. So the allergic reaction may be a real defence against venoms and poisons, which fires at the wrong targets today.
- **You can catch HIV from a hug, a shared plate or a mosquito bite.** (A fear held by many people in every country; *rejected*)  
  For: Many illnesses do spread by touch, food or mosquitoes, so the fear seems sensible.  
  Against: HIV breaks down quickly outside the body and does not pass through skin. A mosquito injects saliva and not the last person's blood, and HIV cannot copy inside a mosquito. Forty years of records show no case from hugging, sharing food or mosquitoes.
- **The stronger the immune system, the better.** (Advertisements for health drinks and pills; *rejected*)  
  For: People whose defence is too weak, as in AIDS, fall ill from germs that others control easily.  
  Against: In an allergy and in an autoimmune disease the defence is too active, or aimed at the wrong thing. Doctors treat these by calming it. A healthy immune system is well aimed and well controlled, which is different from strong.

## Roots
We have learned that blood holds antibodies against germs met long ago, and that a test can read them. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Titre Test, the turmeric never showed you a germ. It showed the washing powder, which stood for antibodies. Many real blood tests work the same way, and the germ may have gone years ago. So your blood holds a record of the germs you have met, and a stranger can read it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think that the past is over, and that only a brain can keep a memory. But a body keeps what has happened to them in plasma cells and antibodies, for decades. A brain is one kind of hardware for memory, and cells keep memory too.  
*Evidence:* In the Titre Test you measured how much was in each sample without ever seeing it, only by a colour. In 1986, six women in Madras looked well, and their blood showed antibodies against HIV. People who lived through the flu of 1918 still carried B cells against that virus ninety years later.

- *who am i:* Your blood holds answers to germs you met before you could talk. Part of who you are is everything your body has met and answered.
- *what is life:* Every living being keeps some record of what has happened to them. A tree keeps rings, a bacterium keeps pieces of the genes of viruses who attacked, and you keep memory cells.
- *what is this world:* Things that have gone leave marks that stay. A test, a scar or a layer of rock lets us read what is no longer here.

A germ and a body look like two separate things. After they meet, the shape of the germ is kept inside the body as a matching antibody, and the body is changed for life. Part of your record came from your mother, whose antibodies crossed into you before you were born. A vaccine adds what people learned from illnesses long ago. So one drop of your blood holds marks left by many lives meeting.

## Story: Six Yellow Wells
*For parents. Real: Sellappan Nirmala, a 32-year-old microbiology student in Madras (now Chennai). Her teacher, Dr Suniti Solomon. Her husband, Veerappan Ramamoorthy, and his scooter. More than 80 blood samples collected over about three months, with no gloves. The serum kept in the fridge at home. The test done at the Christian Medical College in Vellore, 200 kilometres away, in 1986. Six samples turning yellow. The promise of secrecy. The second test in the United States. The health minister's announcement in the Tamil Nadu Assembly in May 1986, with both women in the visitors' gallery, and the disbelief that followed. Imagined: every conversation, Nirmala's thoughts, how the couple travelled to Vellore, the box of ice and the details of the laboratory. The women who gave blood had been arrested for sex work, which means being paid for sex. They were held in a government home called the Vigilance Home. The story says only that the police had arrested them. Tell your child as much as suits them.*

Nirmala did not want this project. She was thirty-two, a student of microbiology at the medical college in Madras, and she needed a subject for her research. She had studied a disease that people catch from rats and dogs. That was work she understood.

"Look for AIDS," said her teacher, Dr Suniti Solomon.

"There is no AIDS in India, madam. Everybody says so. They tested hundreds of blood samples from Bombay and found nothing."

"Everybody says so," Dr Solomon agreed. "Nobody has looked here. That is a different thing."

So, at the end of 1985, Nirmala began to look. The illness was new to the world. Doctors knew that a virus caused it and that the virus passed from person to person in blood. They knew that people could carry the virus for years and feel perfectly well.

That was the part that troubled her. She said so one evening, while Dr Solomon was setting up a small laboratory with machines borrowed from her husband, who was a heart surgeon.

"Madam, if I take blood from a woman who feels well and looks well, what can the blood possibly show? You cannot see a virus. And if she had a fever from the virus, it would have gone long ago."

Dr Solomon was wiping a glass tube. "You are right. We will not see the virus, and we will not look for the virus. We will look for what her body has done about the virus."

"Antibodies."

"Antibodies. If the virus has ever been in her blood, her body has answered. It goes on answering every day, whether she feels anything or not. Her face can tell you nothing. Her blood keeps a record."

Every morning before her classes, Nirmala's husband, Ramamoorthy, drove her on his scooter to a government home. The police sent women there after arresting them. The women had not been asked whether they wanted to live there. Nirmala sat with them and talked. Some laughed at her needle. Some held out an arm without a word. She had no gloves. Nobody had told her she might need them.

She did not tell the women what she was testing for. In those days almost nobody asked a patient's permission for a test. Today the law says they must be asked, and that is right.

In three months she had more than eighty small tubes of blood. In the little laboratory she spun each tube until the red cells sank and the clear, straw-coloured serum stood on top. She drew off the serum. There was no proper cold store, so she took the tubes home and put them in her own fridge, beside the milk and the curry leaves.

Madras had no machine for the test. The nearest was in Vellore, two hundred kilometres away, at the Christian Medical College. So one night in 1986 Nirmala and Ramamoorthy packed the tubes in a box of ice and took the train.

In the Vellore laboratory there was a flat plastic plate with rows of small wells, each no bigger than a drop of rain. A little of the virus's own protein was stuck to the bottom of every well. Into each well went serum from one woman.

"If her serum has antibodies that fit, they will stick to the protein," the doctor running the test told her. "Then we wash the plate. Everything that has not stuck goes down the sink. Last, we add a chemical that turns yellow wherever an antibody is still holding on."

"And if there are no antibodies?"

"Then the well stays clear as water."

They filled the wells and they waited. Nirmala watched the plate. She thought of the scooter rides in the early light. She thought of eighty arms held out to her.

The doctor added the last chemical. For a while nothing happened. Then, in one well, the faintest colour came, like a drop of weak tea. Then in another. The colour deepened. She counted, and counted again.

Six wells were yellow.

Nobody spoke. Nirmala knew six numbers, and each number was a woman. She had seen all six of them laugh and scold and comb their hair. None of them had looked ill. Their bodies had known for months what no person in India knew, and had written it down where only this plate could read it.

"You will tell no one," the doctor said. "Not until we are certain."

Blood was then tested again in the United States, in another way. The answer was the same. In May, the health minister of Tamil Nadu stood up in the Assembly and announced that the virus had reached India. Nirmala and Dr Solomon sat in the visitors' gallery and listened.

Many people refused to believe it. Some said the test was wrong. Some said the two women had made a mistake.

They had not. Because the country now knew, the country could act. Blood for transfusion began to be tested. Clinics opened. Dr Solomon spent the rest of her life caring for people with HIV in Chennai, at a time when many hospitals turned them away.

Nirmala finished her research and handed it in the next year. She went to work at the King Institute in Chennai, and she retired quietly in 2010. For thirty years almost nobody knew her name.

In 2016 a reporter came to her home and wrote her story down at last. Nirmala had looked into the women's faces every morning for three months and learned nothing. Then she had looked at a plate of plastic wells for an hour, and their blood had told her.

**For the parent:** The women looked and felt well, so nobody could tell who carried the virus. The test did not look for the virus at all. It looked for antibodies, which a body goes on making for as long as the virus is there. Six wells turned yellow because six women's blood held antibodies that fit the virus's protein. A person's face showed nothing, and their blood held a record that a test could read.

- Ask: Why could nobody tell which women carried the virus just by looking at them?
- Ask: The test did not look for the virus. What did it look for, and why does that work?
- Ask: If one of the women had caught the virus only a week before, what might her well have shown? Why?
- Ask: The women were not asked before they were tested. Why does the law now say that people must be asked?
- Ask: Many people would not believe the result. What would you want to see before you believed it?

### e1 · The Titre Test: how much can you weaken a sample before the test misses it? (40 min)
- **question:** A sample turns turmeric red. If you make the sample 4 times weaker again and again, at which step does the red stop? Is the step the same for all three patients?
- **predict:** Guess first. The three samples look alike. Will they all stop turning red at the same step? At which step do you think the red will stop: 4 times weaker, 16, 64 or 256? Write your guess, and write the reason for it.
- **fair test:** Change only one thing: how weak the sample is. Use the same spoon to measure every time, and rinse it in clean water after every katori, so that no strong liquid is carried into a weak one. Add the same amount of turmeric water to every katori. Stir the turmeric water before each spoonful, because the powder sinks. Judge every colour beside the plain-water katori, in the same light, one minute after adding the turmeric.
- **safety:** Washing powder stings the eyes, so wash hands after the test and do not rub your eyes. Nobody tastes anything. Turmeric stains cloth and stone, so work on a steel plate or old newspaper.
- **record:** Fill in the table with the colour of every katori: red, orange or yellow. Circle the weakest step that is still red or orange for each patient. That number is the titre. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Patient | Full strength | 4 times weaker | 16 times weaker | 64 times weaker | 256 times weaker | Titre (the weakest step still red or orange)
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do this part where your child cannot see. 2. Stir one pinch of washing powder into a tumbler of water. 3. Put 1 tablespoon of that liquid into a second tumbler and fill it with plain water. 4. Fill a third tumbler with plain water only. 5. Label them 1, 2 and 3 in any order, and write the order down secretly.  
  *Parent says:* These three tumblers are blood samples from three patients. One patient has a lot of antibody against a germ. One has a little. One has none. They look the same. Your test will tell them apart.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to make three mystery samples. They are pretend blood from three patients. You must not see which is which.
  - Note: Check your powder once before you start. Put a spoon of the first tumbler's liquid in a katori and add a little turmeric water. It must turn clearly red. If it turns only orange, add another pinch of powder to the tumbler.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir half a teaspoon of turmeric into half a tumbler of water. 2. Put 4 tablespoons of plain water in one katori and add 1 teaspoon of turmeric water. 3. Stand this katori to one side. 4. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This yellow katori has no washing powder. It shows what 'no' looks like. Now guess. At which step will the red stop? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make the turmeric water. Make one katori with plain water and turmeric. That yellow is your 'no' colour. Now write your guess and your reason.
  - Note: Accept any guess that comes with a reason. Do not say whether it is right. Turmeric does not dissolve in water, so the test liquid is cloudy. That is fine.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand five katoris in a row on white paper. 2. Put 4 tablespoons of Patient 1's sample in the first. 3. Put 3 tablespoons of plain water in each of the other four. 4. Move 1 tablespoon from the first katori to the second and stir. 5. Rinse the spoon. 6. Move 1 tablespoon from the second to the third, and so on to the fifth.  
  *Parent says:* One spoon of sample and three spoons of water make four spoons. So the second katori is 4 times weaker than the first. How many times weaker is the third?  
  *Child mode:* Make a row of five katoris. Move one spoon along each time. Stir and rinse the spoon every time. Each katori is 4 times weaker.
  - Ask: The second katori is 4 times weaker. How many times weaker is the third, and the fourth? (good: 16 times, because 4 times 4 is 16; then 64, then 256; it multiplies by 4 each step)
  - Note: Rinsing the spoon matters. A wet spoon from a strong katori carries enough powder to change a weak one.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir the turmeric water. 2. Add 1 teaspoon of it to each of the five katoris and stir each. 3. Wait one minute. 4. Hold each katori beside the plain yellow one. 5. Write red, orange or yellow for each in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at each katori beside the plain yellow one. Is it redder? Which is the weakest step that still shows any red or orange?  
  *Child mode:* Add one teaspoon of turmeric water to each katori. Wait one minute. Compare each with the plain yellow katori. Write the colour of each one.
  - Note: The change from red to yellow often passes through orange over one or two steps. Count orange as a weak yes. If all five are red, the sample was too strong. Make it again with half a pinch.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty and rinse the five katoris. 2. Repeat the whole row for Patient 2. 3. Write the colours. 4. Rinse again and repeat for Patient 3. 5. Circle each patient's titre in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now do the same for the other two patients. Keep everything the same. Only the sample changes.  
  *Child mode:* Rinse the katoris. Do the same for Patient 2. Then for Patient 3. Circle the weakest red step for each patient.
  - Note: Keep the plain yellow katori through all three rows as the comparison. One patient's row should stay yellow all the way along.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Point to the weakest katori of the strongest patient, which stayed yellow. 2. Ask whether it has any washing powder in it. 3. Wait for an answer and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This katori stayed yellow. The test says no. Think about how we made it. Is there any washing powder in it at all?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the last yellow katori of the strongest patient. The test says no. Is there really no washing powder in it? Think how you made it.
  - Note: This is the main point of the test. A 'no' from a test means 'not enough to find', which is different from 'none'.
  - Check: A katori was made by weakening a red sample 256 times. It stays yellow. What does that show? → **It holds some, but too little for the test**
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to say which patient had the most and how they know. 2. Show your secret note of which tumbler was which. 3. Only now explain what the model stands for. 4. Your child writes the three sentences.  
  *Parent says:* Which patient had the most, and what is your evidence? Now I will show you what I made. In a real laboratory, the washing powder would be antibodies and the turmeric would be the test chemicals.  
  *Child mode:* Say which patient had the most. Say how you know. Check with the grown-up's secret note. Then write what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Ask: Patient 3's row stayed yellow all along. Does that prove Patient 3 never met the germ? (good: No. They may have met it only days ago, in the window period; It shows there is not enough antibody to find; You would test again in a few weeks)
  - Note: The titre of the first tumbler should be about two steps higher than the titre of the weak one, because you made the weak one about 16 times weaker. Tell your child this is a model. Washing powder is not an antibody. What is real is the method: laboratories do weaken blood serum step by step and report the titre.
### e2 · The Red Line Test: what does your skin do after one firm stroke? (25 min)
- **question:** After a light stroke and a firm stroke with a blunt tool, does the skin change colour or rise? How many seconds does the change take to appear, and how many minutes to fade?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the light stroke and the firm stroke leave the same mark? How long will a mark take to appear, and how long to go? Write your guesses, and write the reason for each.
- **fair test:** Change only one thing: how firmly you press. Use the same blunt tool, the same arm and lines of the same length, about 5 centimetres. Draw the two lines three finger-widths apart so that they do not join. Look at the same times after each stroke. Then repeat on a second person, because skins differ.
- **safety:** Use only a blunt, smooth tool. Never use a fingernail, a pin, a compass point or anything sharp, and never press hard enough to hurt. Do not test on skin that has a rash, a cut, sunburn or eczema. In a few people the firm line rises into an itchy ridge. This is harmless and goes in about half an hour. Do not scratch it.
- **record:** Fill in the table at each time. Draw a line graph for the firm stroke, with the time along the bottom and the width of the mark up the side. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Time after the stroke | Light stroke: colour | Light stroke: raised? (yes or no) | Firm stroke: colour | Firm stroke: width of the mark (mm) | Firm stroke: raised? (yes or no) | Itchy? (yes or no)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rests one forearm on the table, palm up. 2. Show the blunt tool and say what you will do. 3. Ask for the guesses and the reasons. 4. Your child writes them down.  
  *Parent says:* I will draw one light line and one firm line on your arm with this spoon handle. It will not hurt. What will your skin do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Rest your arm palm up. You will draw two lines with a blunt spoon handle. Guess what your skin will do. Write your reason.
  - Note: Check the skin first. If your child has eczema or a rash on the forearm, be the test person yourself and let your child do the stroking and timing.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mark two dots 5 centimetres apart. 2. Draw the tool lightly from dot to dot, as lightly as a feather. 3. Start the timer. 4. Look at 15 seconds, 30 seconds, 1 minute and 2 minutes. 5. Write the colour each time, and whether the line is raised.  
  *Parent says:* That was the light stroke. Watch the line. Tell me what you see at each time. Run a fingertip over it. Is it raised?  
  *Child mode:* Draw one very light line. Start the timer. Look at 15 seconds, 30 seconds, 1 minute and 2 minutes. Write what you see.
  - Note: A light stroke often leaves a pale line for a minute or two, or nothing at all. On brown skin a mark may look darker or lighter and not red. Light from the side helps.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Three finger-widths from the first line, draw a firm line of the same length. 2. Press about as hard as you press to rub out pencil. 3. Start the timer. 4. Look at 15 and 30 seconds, then at 1, 2, 3, 5 and 10 minutes. 5. Each time, measure the width of the mark and feel whether it is raised.  
  *Parent says:* That was the firm stroke. It should not hurt. Now watch. When does the colour come? How wide does it get? When does it start to fade?  
  *Child mode:* Draw one firm line. It must not hurt. Start the timer. Measure the mark at each time. Feel if it is raised. Write it all down.
  - Note: In most people a line of colour shows within half a minute, spreads a little wider than the stroke, and fades in five to fifteen minutes. In some people the line also rises a little after one to three minutes. If nothing shows, try once more a little more firmly on the upper arm. If still nothing shows, that is a real result. Write it down.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. While the first arm fades, do the firm stroke on a second person. 2. Use the same tool and the same pressure. 3. Record at 30 seconds, 1 minute and 3 minutes. 4. Compare the two people.  
  *Parent says:* Now we test my arm in the same way. Do you think my skin will do the same as yours? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Do the firm stroke on a second person. Use the same tool. Record at 30 seconds, 1 minute and 3 minutes. Compare.
  - Note: People differ a great deal. About one person in twenty to fifty has skin that rises into a clear ridge. Doctors call this skin writing, or dermatographism. It is harmless.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what your child saw, and what they think made the mark. 2. Listen to the whole answer. 3. Then give the scientists' explanation. 4. Your child writes the three sentences.  
  *Parent says:* Nothing went into your skin, and the spoon has gone. So what made the mark come later, and spread? Tell me your idea first.  
  *Child mode:* The spoon touched you for one second. The mark came later. What do you think made it? Say your idea. Then write three sentences.
  - Ask: The mark came after the spoon had gone, and it spread wider than the stroke. What does that tell you about who made it? (good: My skin made it, the spoon did not; Something spread out from where I pressed; It is the body answering, like a mosquito bump)
  - Note: The explanation, to give only after your child's own idea: the skin holds mast cells packed with histamine. Firm pressure makes some of them release a little. Histamine widens small blood vessels, which gives the colour, and makes them leaky, which gives the swelling. The doctor Thomas Lewis studied this in London in the 1920s. An allergy uses the same cells and the same chemical. The difference is the trigger, which in an allergy is IgE meeting an allergen.
  - Check: After a firm stroke, a mark appears later and spreads wider than the stroke. What does this show? → **The skin answered with chemicals that spread**

### a3 · Boylston's Count: was inoculation safer than catching smallpox? (20 min)
- **record:** Draw two grids of 100 squares. On one, shade the number who died out of every 100 people who caught smallpox the usual way. On the other, shade the number who died out of every 100 who were inoculated. Write one sentence that says what the grids show, and one question you would still ask.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the numbers aloud. 2. About 5,759 people caught smallpox the usual way, and 844 of them died. 3. Boylston inoculated 247 people, and 6 of them died. 4. Ask the question below and wait.  
  *Parent says:* Six inoculated people died, and 844 other people died. Does that prove inoculation was safer? What else do you need to look at? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Read the four numbers. Six is much smaller than 844. Is that enough to say inoculation was safer? Say why or why not.
  - Ask: Why is it unfair to compare 6 deaths with 844 deaths? (good: The groups are different sizes; Only 247 people were inoculated but thousands caught it the usual way; We need to know how many died out of the same number)
  - Note: The point to reach is that the groups are of very different sizes. The count of deaths must be compared with the size of each group.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each group, divide the number who died by the number in the group. 2. Multiply by 100. 3. Round to the nearest whole number. 4. Shade that many squares on a grid of 100.  
  *Parent says:* Let's find how many died out of every hundred. 844 divided by 5,759, times 100. Then 6 divided by 247, times 100. Guess each answer before you work it out.  
  *Child mode:* Divide 844 by 5,759. Multiply by 100. Then divide 6 by 247. Multiply by 100. Shade that many squares on each grid.
  - Note: 844 ÷ 5,759 × 100 is about 14.7, so shade 15 squares. 6 ÷ 247 × 100 is about 2.4, so shade 2 squares. To estimate without a calculator: 5,759 is nearly 5,800, and one hundredth of that is 58. 844 ÷ 58 is between 14 and 15.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the two grids. 2. Ask what they show. 3. Ask whether the comparison is fully fair. 4. Tell the story of Onesimus from the parent note. 5. Your child writes a sentence and a question.  
  *Parent says:* What do the two grids show? Now be a careful scientist. Could anything other than inoculation explain the difference? What would you want to check?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your two grids. Say what they show. Think of one thing that could make the comparison unfair. Write a sentence and a question.
  - Ask: Could anything else explain why fewer inoculated people died? (good: Maybe healthier or richer people chose inoculation; Maybe they were better looked after; The inoculated group is small, so chance matters more; Inoculated people could pass smallpox to others, and those deaths were not counted)
  - Note: About 15 in 100 died after catching smallpox the usual way, and about 2 in 100 after inoculation. That is about six times safer. Boylston's count was one of the first times anyone used numbers to judge a medical treatment. The knowledge came from Onesimus, a West African man enslaved in the house of the minister Cotton Mather. He told Mather he had been inoculated as a boy. Most Boston doctors refused to try it, and one reason was that the knowledge came from Africans.

### p1 · Hugs Are Safe: a true-or-false poster about how HIV spreads (50 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** Ask only grown-ups you know well. Nobody has to say whether they know a person with HIV, and you should not ask.
- **record:** Keep the tally table of answers in your notebook. Write which statement the most people got wrong. Take a photograph of the finished poster.
- **schedule:** day 1: Write six true-or-false statements about HIV. Ask three grown-ups, and tally their answers. (20 min); day 2: Make a poster that gives the right answer and the reason for each statement. Put it up at home and show it to the people you asked. (30 min)
- **s1 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child copies the six statements from the parent note. 2. Your child asks three grown-ups to answer true or false to each. 3. Tally the answers in a table. 4. Do not tell anyone the right answers yet.  
  *Parent says:* Ask each person all six. Write a tick for true and a cross for false. Do not tell them the answers yet. Which one do you think most people will get wrong? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Copy the six statements. Ask three grown-ups: true or false? Tally their answers. Do not tell them the answers yet.
  - Note: The six statements, with answers. 1. You can catch HIV by sharing a plate or a tumbler. False. 2. A mosquito can carry HIV from one person to another. False. 3. You can catch HIV by hugging or shaking hands. False. 4. HIV can pass through blood, for example on a reused needle. True. 5. A mother with HIV can pass the virus to her baby if she gets no treatment. True. 6. A person with HIV who takes medicine every day can live a long and healthy life. True.
- **s2 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the tally together and find the statement most people got wrong. 2. Your child plans the poster in pencil. 3. One side shows how HIV does not spread. 4. The other side shows how it does. 5. Add one reason beside each.  
  *Parent says:* Which statement fooled the most people? Put that one biggest on your poster. For each one, write the reason, so that people can check it and need not just believe you.  
  *Child mode:* Find the statement most people got wrong. Plan your poster. Show how HIV does not spread, and how HIV does. Write a reason for each.
  - Note: Reasons a child can write: HIV breaks down quickly outside the body. HIV does not pass through healthy skin. A mosquito injects saliva, not the last person's blood. Hospitals use a new needle each time. Medicine for the mother protects the baby. HIV also passes in sex between grown-ups, and your child can write that plainly.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the poster up at home. 2. Your child shows it to each person who answered. 3. Ask each person whether anything surprised them. 4. Your child writes down what they said.  
  *Parent says:* Show your poster to everyone you asked. Did anything surprise them? Did anyone change their mind? What changed it?  
  *Child mode:* Put up your poster. Show it to the people you asked. Ask what surprised them. Write down what they say.
  - Think: If nobody has ever caught HIV from a hug, where does the fear of a hug come from?

## Challenges
### c1 · The Allergy-Safe Party: one table that six friends can all eat from (30 min)
- **problem:** Plan five snacks and one drink for six children. One child is allergic to peanuts, one to milk and one to egg. One has coeliac disease and cannot eat wheat, barley or rye. Every child must be able to eat at least four of the six things.
- **your turn:** Now write your own menu. Make a table with the six things down the side and the six children along the top, and tick who can eat what. Does every child have at least four ticks?
- **constraints:** Every child must be able to eat at least four of the six things on the table.; At least two things must be safe for all six children.; Each dish needs a small card that says what it contains.; Use foods you can make or buy near your home.
- **general:** Choose dishes that are free of all four foods to begin with, such as idli with coconut chutney, sundal, fruit and lemon juice.; Check every dish for hidden ingredients by asking the cook and reading the packets.; Give each dish its own spoon, so that no food is carried from one dish to another.
- **creative:** Hunt for hidden wheat and milk. Rava is wheat. Ghee, paneer, curd and khoa are milk. Powdered hing is often mixed with wheat flour, so read the packet.; Put a small coloured flag in each dish, with one colour for each of the four foods, so that a child can see at a glance what to avoid.; Ask the four friends or their parents before the party what they like to eat. They know their own allergy best.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the problem aloud. 2. Your child lists ten party snacks they like. 3. For each, ask a cook or read a packet to find out whether it holds peanuts, milk, egg or wheat. 4. Cross out or mark each one.  
  *Parent says:* First list the snacks you would love at a party. Then we become detectives. What is really inside each one? How can we find out?  
  *Child mode:* List ten party snacks. Find out what is in each one. Ask a cook or read the packet. Mark peanuts, milk, egg and wheat.
  - Note: Common surprises: kesari and upma are made of rava, which is wheat. Payasam, laddus made with ghee and most chocolates hold milk. Cake holds wheat, milk and egg. Many namkeen mixtures hold peanuts.
- **s2 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child chooses five snacks and one drink. 2. Draw the table of dishes and children. 3. Tick who can eat what. 4. Check the two rules: four ticks for every child, and two dishes safe for all. 5. Change the menu until both rules are met.  
  *Parent says:* Choose your six things and fill in the table. Does every child have four ticks? Are two dishes safe for everyone? If not, what will you swap?  
  *Child mode:* Choose five snacks and one drink. Draw the table. Tick who can eat what. Check both rules. Swap dishes until the rules are met.
  - Note: A menu that works: idli with coconut chutney, chana sundal, cut fruit, rice murukku, banana chips and lemon juice. Ask what oil the murukku and chips were fried in, because some families with a peanut allergy also avoid groundnut oil.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child writes a label card for each dish. 2. Ask how to stop food moving from one dish to another. 3. Ask the question below. 4. Your child improves the plan once more.  
  *Parent says:* Your menu is safe on paper. What could still go wrong at the real table? How will you stop it? And how will the four friends feel at your party?  
  *Child mode:* Write a label for each dish. Think what could go wrong at the table. Fix your plan. How will your friends feel?
  - Think: A child with an allergy reads labels and asks questions at every meal. What could the rest of us do to make that easier?
### c2 · Fewer Tests: find one positive sample among eight (30 min)
- **problem:** Eight cups of water look the same. One holds a pinch of washing powder. Find which one, using as few turmeric tests as you can. Eight tests is easy. Can you do it in four, or in three?
- **your turn:** Now ask the grown-up to hide the powder in one of sixteen cups, or draw sixteen cups on paper. How many tests do you need? What is the pattern as the number of cups doubles?
- **constraints:** One test means one katori with one teaspoon of turmeric water added.; You may put spoonfuls from several cups into the same katori before you test it.; Never pour anything back into a cup, and rinse the spoon after every cup.; Write down your plan before you test.
- **general:** Test the cups one at a time. This always works, and it can take up to eight tests.; Mix a spoonful from each of cups 1 to 4 in one katori and test it. One test tells you which half holds the powder.; Keep halving: test two of the four, then one of the two. That finds the cup in three tests.
- **creative:** Plan three mixtures before you test anything, so that every cup is in a different set of mixtures. Then the pattern of red and yellow names the cup, with all three tests run side by side.; Ask what happens if two cups hold powder. Work out a plan that still finds both.; Mixing makes each sample weaker. Use the Titre Test to check how many cups you can mix before the turmeric stops turning red.
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Number eight cups and fill each with the same amount of water. 2. Send your child out, and stir a good pinch of washing powder into one cup. 3. Read the problem and the rules aloud. 4. Ask for a plan on paper.  
  *Parent says:* One of these eight cups holds washing powder. You could test all eight. Can you think of a way to learn about several cups with one test? Write your plan.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to hide washing powder in one of eight cups. Read the rules. Write a plan to find it with few tests.
  - Note: Use a bigger pinch than in the Titre Test, because mixing with three other cups makes the sample four times weaker. Check that a spoonful from the powder cup, mixed with three spoonfuls of plain water, still turns turmeric red.
- **s2 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child carries out the plan. 2. Count every test. 3. Rinse the spoon after every cup. 4. When the cup is found, check it with one last test.  
  *Parent says:* Carry out your plan and count your tests. Before each test, tell me what a red result would mean and what a yellow result would mean.  
  *Child mode:* Follow your plan. Count each test. Rinse the spoon every time. Before each test, say what red and yellow would mean.
  - Note: Halving finds the cup in three tests. If your child tests one cup at a time, let them finish, then ask whether one test could tell them about four cups at once.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the number of tests with eight. 2. Ask how many tests sixteen cups would need. 3. Tell the true story in the parent note. 4. Your child writes the pattern.  
  *Parent says:* You found one cup in eight with a few tests. How many would you need for sixteen cups? For thirty-two? What is the pattern?  
  *Child mode:* How many tests did you use? Work out how many you would need for 16 cups. Then for 32. Write the pattern.
  - Note: Each time the number of cups doubles, halving needs only one more test: 8 cups need 3 tests, 16 need 4 and 32 need 5. The economist Robert Dorfman suggested mixing blood samples in 1943, to test American soldiers for an infection with fewer tests. In 2020, laboratories in India mixed five samples at a time to test for the coronavirus when test kits were short. It works well only when few people are infected.

## Worksheets
### Antibodies, the body's record and the defence gone wrong (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What shape is an antibody?  
   Options: Like the letter Y / Like a ball / Like a long thread  
   **Answer:** Like the letter Y. *Why:* An antibody is a Y-shaped protein. The two tips of the Y fit the antigen, and the stem tells other cells what to do.
2. (fill) The two tips of an antibody fit one ____, which is usually a molecule on the outside of a germ.  
   **Answer:** ['antigen']. *Why:* An antigen is any molecule the immune system can recognise by its shape. Each antibody fits one antigen.
3. (mcq) What do antibodies do to a germ?  
   Options: Burn the germ with acid / Swallow the germ / Stick to germs and flag them  
   **Answer:** Stick to germs and flag them. *Why:* Antibodies stick. They block germs, clump them and flag them for phagocytes. Choosing 'swallow' mixes up antibodies with phagocytes, who are whole cells.
4. (truefalse) True or false: antibodies kill germs all by themselves.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. An antibody only holds on. Phagocytes and complement proteins destroy the germs that antibodies have flagged.
5. (match) Match each cell to the job that cell does.  
   **Answer:** Plasma cell → releases antibodies; Memory cell → waits for the germ to return; Phagocyte → swallows flagged germs; Mast cell → releases histamine; Helper T cell → switches other immune cells on. *Why:* Each kind of immune cell has a different job, and they work together.
6. (order) Put these in order, for a first infection.  
   **Answer:** ['A germ gets into the body', "A B cell's antibody fits the germ's antigen", 'The B cell divides into many cells', 'Plasma cells release antibodies', 'Memory cells stay for years']. *Why:* The fit comes first. Then the chosen B cell multiplies, and the new cells become antibody factories and memory cells.
7. (mcq) A blood sample still tests positive when 64 times weaker, but not at 256. What is its titre?  
   Options: 256 / 4 / 64  
   **Answer:** 64. *Why:* The titre is the weakest dilution that still tests positive, so it is 64. Choosing 256 picks the first step where the test failed.
8. (fill) The time when a new infection is not yet found by an antibody test is called the ____ period.  
   **Answer:** ['window']. *Why:* In the window period the germ is in the body, but the body has not yet made enough antibody for the test to find.
9. (truefalse) True or false: an allergy is the immune system answering something harmless.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Pollen, dust mites and peanut protein do no harm themselves. The body's answer causes the trouble.
10. (sort) Is each of these an allergy or an autoimmune disease?  
   **Answer:** hay fever → Allergy; type 1 diabetes → Autoimmune disease; a reaction to peanuts → Allergy; coeliac disease → Autoimmune disease; a reaction to dust mites → Allergy; rheumatoid arthritis → Autoimmune disease. *Why:* In an allergy the target is a harmless thing from outside. In an autoimmune disease the target is part of the person's own body.
11. (mcq) Which cells does HIV infect and destroy?  
   Options: Red blood cells / Helper T cells / Skin cells  
   **Answer:** Helper T cells. *Why:* HIV fits a molecule called CD4 on helper T cells. These cells switch the rest of the defence on, so losing them weakens everything.
12. (mcq) Which of these can pass HIV from one person to another?  
   Options: A mosquito bite / Sharing a tumbler / A reused injection needle  
   **Answer:** A reused injection needle. *Why:* HIV passes in blood, so a reused needle can carry the virus. HIV does not pass through shared tumblers or mosquitoes.
13. (write) One antibody lasts only weeks. Explain how a person still has measles antibodies fifty years after measles.  
   **Answer:** Some plasma cells live in the bone marrow for many years. They keep making fresh measles antibodies all the time, to replace the ones that are broken down.. *Why:* A strong answer says that long-lived plasma cells keep making new antibodies. The antibodies themselves are not fifty years old.
14. (draw) Draw one antibody holding two germs. Label the two tips and the stem.  
   **Answer:** A Y shape with one germ at each tip. The tips are labelled as the parts that fit the antigen. The stem is labelled as the part that phagocytes grip.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that both tips are the same and that one antibody can join two germs.
15. (write) After a bee sting, a friend's throat swells and she struggles to breathe. What must happen at once?  
   **Answer:** This is anaphylaxis, which is an emergency. A grown-up must be told at once. She needs an adrenaline injection if she carries one, and an ambulance to hospital. In India, call 108 or 112.. *Why:* A strong answer names it as an emergency and says adrenaline and hospital. Waiting to see if it passes is dangerous.
### Classes of antibody, the thymus and HIV (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does an antibody have two matching tips?  
   Options: To fit two different kinds of germ / To cut a germ in half / To hold two germs, or one germ twice  
   **Answer:** To hold two germs, or one germ twice. *Why:* Two matching tips let one antibody join two germs into a clump, or grip one germ firmly in two places. The tips are the same, so they fit the same antigen.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a B cell can keep the same antibody tips and change the stem.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The tips decide what the antibody fits. The stem decides where it goes and what happens next. Changing the stem turns IgM into IgG, IgA or IgE with the same fit.
3. (match) Match each class of antibody to where it is found or when it is made.  
   **Answer:** IgM → made first in a new infection; IgG → the main antibody in blood; IgA → in tears, spit and mother's milk; IgE → sitting on mast cells. *Why:* The stem of each class sends the antibody to a different place and gives it a different job.
4. (order) Put the steps of an allergy in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The body meets the allergen for the first time', 'B cells make IgE that fits the allergen', 'The IgE settles on mast cells', 'The allergen comes again and links two IgE antibodies', 'The mast cell releases histamine']. *Why:* An allergy needs two meetings. The first prepares the mast cells. The second sets them off.
5. (mcq) What does histamine do to small blood vessels?  
   Options: Makes them wider and leaky / Makes them narrower / Blocks them completely  
   **Answer:** Makes them wider and leaky. *Why:* Histamine widens small blood vessels and makes them leaky, so fluid moves into the skin or the nose lining. That gives redness, swelling and a runny nose.
6. (fill) Young T cells are tested in the ____, a soft organ behind the breastbone.  
   **Answer:** ['thymus']. *Why:* The thymus shows young T cells samples of the body's own proteins and removes the T cells who would attack them.
7. (mcq) What happens in the thymus to a young T cell whose receptor fits a body protein strongly?  
   Options: That T cell becomes a B cell / That T cell is removed / That T cell is copied many times  
   **Answer:** That T cell is removed. *Why:* A T cell who fits the body would attack the body, so the thymus gives that cell a signal to die. Choosing 'copied' is what happens later to a T cell who fits a germ.
8. (sort) Can HIV pass from one person to another in this way?  
   **Answer:** blood on a reused needle → Yes; from a mother to her baby, with no treatment → Yes; hugging → No; sharing food → No; a mosquito bite → No; coughing → No. *Why:* HIV passes in blood, in sex between grown-ups, and from mother to baby. The virus does not pass through skin, food, air or mosquitoes.
9. (write) A person's HIV antibody test is negative. Give one reason why the person could still have HIV.  
   **Answer:** The person may have caught HIV only a few weeks ago. The body has not yet made enough antibodies for the test to find. This is the window period, so the test is done again later.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the test looks for antibodies and that antibodies take weeks to appear.
10. (write) In Boston, 6 inoculated people died and 844 others died. Why is that comparison unfair as it stands?  
   **Answer:** The two groups were very different in size. Only 247 people were inoculated, and 5,759 caught smallpox the usual way. We must compare how many died out of every 100 in each group.. *Why:* A strong answer says that counts from groups of different sizes cannot be compared directly, and that a rate is needed.
11. (truefalse) True or false: HIV medicines remove every virus from the body.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The medicines stop HIV from copying. HIV DNA stays hidden inside resting cells, so the virus returns if the medicines are stopped.
12. (mcq) Why are three HIV medicines given together?  
   Options: Each one treats a different illness / One tablet is too small to work / A virus resisting all three is very unlikely  
   **Answer:** A virus resisting all three is very unlikely. *Why:* HIV changes fast, so a virus who resists one medicine soon appears. A virus who resists three different medicines at once is very unlikely.
13. (draw) Sketch a line graph of helper T cells over ten years of HIV. Add a second line with medicine.  
   **Answer:** The first line falls slowly from a high start towards a low level over about ten years. The second line falls at first, then rises again from the year the medicine starts.. *Why:* A strong sketch shows a slow fall over years, not a sudden drop, and shows that medicine lets the count recover.
14. (perspective) Two views about what an allergy is.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side and gives evidence. For the scientist: the mouse studies, and the way sneezing and vomiting push things out of the body. For the doctor: most allergens are not poisons, and the reaction can kill.. *Why:* Both can be true together. A defence that once protected against venoms can still be an error when it fires at peanuts.
15. (mcq) Why was cowpox a safer way to protect people than smallpox inoculation?  
   Options: Cowpox is a mild disease in humans / Cowpox viruses have no antigens / Cows never fall ill  
   **Answer:** Cowpox is a mild disease in humans. *Why:* Cowpox virus is closely related to smallpox virus, so antibodies against one fit the other. But cowpox is mild in humans, so nobody died of the protection. Inoculation used real smallpox, and a few people died of it.
### Atoms, evidence and open arguments (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) How does the tip of an antibody hold an antigen?  
   Options: By tiny hooks that pierce it / By many weak attractions between atoms / By one strong chemical bond  
   **Answer:** By many weak attractions between atoms. *Why:* Many weak attractions act together where the shapes and electric charges match. No strong bond forms, which is why a close fit matters and why the antibody can let go.
2. (write) Explain why a molecule of slightly the wrong shape does not stay on an antibody's tip.  
   **Answer:** The wrong shape touches the tip at only a few points. A few weak attractions are not enough to hold against the moving water around them, so the molecule drifts away.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the hold comes from many weak attractions together, and that a poor fit gives too few of them.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a high titre means that a sample holds a lot of antibody.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A sample with a lot of antibody can be made much weaker and still test positive, so its titre is a large number.
4. (order) Put these five samples in order, from the most antibody to the least.  
   **Answer:** ['Titre 256', 'Titre 64', 'Titre 16', 'Titre 4', 'No colour at any step']. *Why:* The larger the titre, the more times the sample was weakened before the test stopped seeing it, so the more antibody it held.
5. (mcq) One test looks for a germ's genes, not for antibodies. Why can it find a new infection sooner?  
   Options: Genes are bigger than antibodies / Antibodies hide from tests / The germ is there before the antibodies  
   **Answer:** The germ is there before the antibodies. *Why:* The germ is in the body from the first day. Antibodies appear only after the body has had weeks to answer. A test for the germ's genes has a shorter window period.
6. (write) The thymus removes T cells who fit the body. Give one reason why autoimmune disease still happens.  
   **Answer:** The test in the thymus is not perfect, and some T cells who fit the body slip through. Regulatory T cells usually calm them, but this can fail. Also, a germ's antigen can look like a body protein, so the answer to the germ hits the body too.. *Why:* A strong answer gives one of these: an imperfect test, a failure of regulatory T cells, or a germ that resembles a body part.
7. (sort) Which idea about allergies does each finding support?  
   **Answer:** mice with IgE against bee venom survived a bigger dose → The toxin idea; babies who ate peanut early had fewer peanut allergies → The early food idea; sneezing and vomiting push a poison out of the body → The toxin idea; babies with sore, broken skin more often become allergic to foods → The early food idea. *Why:* Evidence about venoms and getting poisons out supports the toxin idea. Evidence about how a baby first meets a food supports the early food idea. Both ideas can be true.
8. (mcq) After a throat infection, antibodies against the bacterium also damage the heart. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Part of the bacterium resembles a heart protein / The bacterium is living in the heart / The antibodies have grown too old  
   **Answer:** Part of the bacterium resembles a heart protein. *Why:* An antibody sticks to whatever its tips fit. If a heart protein has nearly the same shape as a bacterial antigen, the same antibody sticks to both. This happens in rheumatic fever.
9. (write) HIV copies their genes with many mistakes. Explain how this helps the virus escape antibodies.  
   **Answer:** The mistakes change the shape of the virus's antigens a little each time. Antibodies made against the old shape fit the new shape poorly. By the time the body makes new antibodies, the virus has changed again.. *Why:* A strong answer links copying mistakes to changed shapes, and changed shapes to antibodies that no longer fit.
10. (perspective) Two views about who should get the credit for stopping smallpox.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side and gives evidence. For the historian: inoculation was used in Boston in 1721, and Jenner himself was inoculated as a boy. For the book: cowpox was far safer, and Jenner tested it and wrote it down so that others could check.. *Why:* Both did something real. The first group found that protection is possible. Jenner found a much safer way and tested it openly.
11. (fill) T cells who calm other immune cells down are called ____ T cells.  
   **Answer:** ['regulatory']. *Why:* Regulatory T cells are a second guard outside the thymus. They hold back T cells who would attack the body.
12. (write) 15 of 100 untreated people died, and 2 of 100 treated people. What must you check before saying the treatment worked?  
   **Answer:** Check whether the two groups were alike to begin with. The treated people may have been healthier, richer or better cared for. Also check that the treated group was large enough for the result not to be chance.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the groups must be alike in every way except the treatment before the difference can be put down to the treatment.
13. (draw) Draw a mast cell before and after an allergen links two IgE antibodies. Label IgE, allergen and histamine.  
   **Answer:** Before: a round cell full of dots, with Y shapes standing on the surface. After: one allergen touching the tips of two Y shapes, and dots leaving the cell. The Y shapes are labelled IgE and the dots are labelled histamine.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows one allergen joining two IgE antibodies, because that link is what sets the mast cell off.
14. (mcq) One of eight samples is positive. You mix four and test the mixture. What does the result tell you?  
   Options: Nothing useful at all / Which half holds the positive sample / Exactly which sample is positive  
   **Answer:** Which half holds the positive sample. *Why:* If the mixture is positive, the positive sample is one of those four. If it is negative, it is one of the other four. One test has halved the search.
15. (truefalse) True or false: a person whose HIV is too low to measure does not pass HIV on through sex.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Studies of thousands of couples found no case of HIV passing from a partner whose virus was too low to measure. People say 'undetectable equals untransmittable'.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which part of an antibody fits the antigen?  
   Options: The stem of the Y / The whole surface / The two tips of the Y  
   **Answer:** The two tips of the Y. *Why:* The two tips fit the antigen. The stem is the part that phagocytes and mast cells hold. Choosing 'the stem' mixes up the two jobs.
2. (mcq) Antibodies tie many germs into one lump. Why does that help the body?  
   Options: A lump cannot spread, and is swallowed whole / A lump is squeezed until the germs die / A lump turns into more antibodies  
   **Answer:** A lump cannot spread, and is swallowed whole. *Why:* Clumped germs cannot spread through the body, and one phagocyte can swallow the whole clump. Antibodies do not squeeze or kill.
3. (mcq) Which cells keep releasing antibodies for years after an infection is over?  
   Options: Mast cells in the skin / Plasma cells in the bone marrow / Red blood cells  
   **Answer:** Plasma cells in the bone marrow. *Why:* Long-lived plasma cells in the bone marrow keep making fresh antibodies. Mast cells hold IgE on their surface but do not make antibodies.
4. (mcq) What does an antibody test look for in a person's blood?  
   Options: The living germ / Damaged red blood cells / Antibodies the body made against the germ  
   **Answer:** Antibodies the body made against the germ. *Why:* The test finds the body's answer to the germ. That is why it can show an infection from long ago, and why it can miss one from last week.
5. (truefalse) True or false: a negative antibody test always means that the person has no infection.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the first weeks the body has not yet made enough antibody to find. This is the window period, so the test is repeated later.
6. (mcq) Which class of antibody sits on mast cells and causes allergies?  
   Options: IgE / IgG / IgA  
   **Answer:** IgE. *Why:* IgE fixes its stem to mast cells. IgG is the main antibody in blood, and IgA is in tears, spit and milk.
7. (mcq) Why does a baby's first mosquito bite usually leave no bump?  
   Options: Babies have no mast cells / The baby has no IgE against saliva yet / Mosquito saliva cannot enter a baby's skin  
   **Answer:** The baby has no IgE against saliva yet. *Why:* A bump is the body's answer to mosquito saliva, and it needs IgE made after an earlier bite. At the first bite there is none yet.
8. (mcq) In an autoimmune disease, what does the immune system attack?  
   Options: A harmless food or pollen / A virus hiding in the body / Part of the person's own body  
   **Answer:** Part of the person's own body. *Why:* 'Auto' means self. The defence attacks the person's own cells, such as the insulin-making cells in type 1 diabetes. Choosing 'a harmless food' describes an allergy.
9. (mcq) How do antiretroviral medicines help a person with HIV?  
   Options: They stop the virus from copying / They remove every virus in a week / They are helper T cells in a tablet  
   **Answer:** They stop the virus from copying. *Why:* The medicines stop HIV from copying, so helper T cells are no longer destroyed and the body makes more. The virus stays hidden, so the medicines are taken for life.
10. (truefalse) True or false: you can catch HIV by sharing a tiffin box with a friend who has HIV.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. HIV passes in blood, in sex between grown-ups and from a mother to her baby. The virus does not pass through food, plates, touch or air.
11. (mcq) Why did a small dose of smallpox in a scratch protect a person for life?  
   Options: The dose used up all the smallpox nearby / The body kept memory cells and antibodies / The scratch let the illness out  
   **Answer:** The body kept memory cells and antibodies. *Why:* The body answered the small dose and kept plasma cells and memory cells against smallpox. The healers who did this did not know about these cells, but they knew that it worked.
12. (mcq) 844 of 5,759 people died in one group, and 6 of 247 in another. What is the fair comparison?  
   Options: The two numbers of deaths / The two sizes of the groups / Deaths out of every 100 in each group  
   **Answer:** Deaths out of every 100 in each group. *Why:* The groups are very different in size, so the counts cannot be compared directly. Working out deaths in every 100 gives about 15 and about 2.

## Applications
- Blood banks in India test every lesson of donated blood for HIV, hepatitis B and hepatitis C before anyone receives it.
- Rapid test strips for dengue, malaria and pregnancy have antibodies fixed to the paper. A line appears where the antibodies catch what the test is looking for.
- Before a blood transfusion, a laboratory mixes a drop of blood with antibodies. If the red cells clump, the blood group is known.
- Snake antivenom is made of antibodies. Horses are given tiny, safe doses of venom, and the antibodies from their blood are given to people who have been bitten. Irula snake catchers near Chennai collect much of the venom used in India.
- Many new medicines for cancers and autoimmune diseases are antibodies made in factories. Their names often end in 'mab'.
- A person who has a new kidney or heart takes medicines that calm T cells, so that the body does not attack the new organ.
- People with severe allergies carry an adrenaline injector pen, and food packets print the allergens they contain.
- A pregnant woman with HIV takes antiretroviral medicines, and her baby is very likely to be born without the virus.

## Open questions
- **Can anyone make a vaccine that protects against HIV?** HIV changes shape faster than any vaccine made so far can follow, and hides inside cells. Large trials over forty years have not given good protection. Scientists are now trying to guide the body, step by step, towards rare antibodies that fit the parts of HIV that cannot change.
- **Why are about four out of five people with autoimmune diseases women?** Hormones, genes on the X chromosome and pregnancy have all been suggested, and each has some evidence. Nobody knows how much each one matters.
- **Why have allergies become so much more common in the last sixty years?** Genes cannot change that fast. Fewer microbes in early life, changes in food, cleaner water, more time indoors and air pollution are all being studied. No single cause explains the rise everywhere.
- **How do some plasma cells stay alive for a whole human lifetime?** Most cells who work that hard die within weeks. Long-lived plasma cells sit in special places in the bone marrow and get signals from the cells around them. What exactly keeps them alive is not yet clear.
- **Can a body that has started attacking itself be taught tolerance again?** Doctors can calm the whole immune system, but that leaves a person open to infections. Scientists are testing ways to switch off the attack on one target only, for example by growing more regulatory T cells. It works in some animals and is only beginning in people.
- **Can HIV be cured in everyone who has it?** A few people have been cured after a risky bone marrow transplant for cancer. The virus's DNA hides in resting cells where no medicine reaches. Nobody yet knows a safe way to find and clear every one of those cells.

## Connected maths
- In the Titre Test, each katori is 4 times weaker than the one before. How many times weaker than the first katori is the fifth? What fraction of the first katori's strength is that? **256 times weaker. That is one part in 256 of the first katori's strength.**. There are four steps from the first katori to the fifth, and each step makes the liquid 4 times weaker. 4 × 4 = 16. 16 × 4 = 64. 64 × 4 = 256. So the fifth katori has one 256th of the strength. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- In Boston in 1721, 844 of the 5,759 people who caught smallpox died. Of the 247 people who were inoculated, 6 died. About how many died out of every 100 in each group? About how many times safer was inoculation? **About 15 in every 100, and about 2 in every 100. Inoculation was about 6 times safer.**. 844 ÷ 5,759 = 0.147, which is about 15 in 100. 6 ÷ 247 = 0.024, which is about 2 in 100. Using the unrounded numbers, 14.7 ÷ 2.4 is about 6. The two counts of deaths, 844 and 6, cannot be compared directly because the groups are of very different sizes. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- Half of the IgG antibodies in blood are broken down every 3 weeks. If no new ones were made, what fraction would be left after 12 weeks? A woman still has plenty of measles antibody 50 years after measles. What does that tell you? **One sixteenth would be left after 12 weeks. So her body must be making new measles antibodies all the time.**. 12 weeks is 4 lots of 3 weeks, so the amount halves 4 times. 1 → one half → one quarter → one eighth → one sixteenth. After a year almost nothing would be left. Since she still has plenty after 50 years, plasma cells must be making fresh antibodies every day. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In Fewer Tests, halving finds one positive cup among 8 in 3 tests, and among 16 in 4 tests. How many tests are needed for 64 cups? Describe the pattern. **6 tests. Each time the number of cups doubles, you need only one more test.**. Each test cuts the cups left in half. 64 → 32 → 16 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1. That is 6 halvings, so 6 tests. Testing the cups one by one could take up to 64 tests. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L5`)
- In the Helper Count, a die takes out 3 and a half beans a year on average, and the body adds 2. You start with 20 beans. After about how many years will 4 beans be left? Why might your own run be different? **After about 11 years. Your run can be shorter or longer because each roll of the die is chance.**. On average the katori loses 3.5 − 2 = 1.5 beans a year. To go from 20 beans to 4 beans it must lose 16. 16 ÷ 1.5 is a little under 11. A run of high rolls reaches 4 sooner, and a run of low rolls takes longer. (queues `math.big-ideas.functions-and-models.L5`)
- Look at the made-up graph from the Red Line Test. At what time was the mark widest? How many minutes after the stroke had it shrunk to less than half of that width? **The mark was widest, at 9 mm, at 2 and 3 minutes. By 10 minutes it had shrunk to 1 mm, which is less than half of 9 mm.**. Read the highest point of the line: 9 mm at 2 minutes and at 3 minutes. Half of 9 mm is 4.5 mm. At 5 minutes the mark is 6 mm, which is still more than half. At 10 minutes it is 1 mm. So it passed below half some time between 5 and 10 minutes. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What shape is an antibody, and which part fits the antigen? → It is shaped like a Y. The two tips fit the antigen.
- Name three things antibodies do to germs. → They block germs, clump germs together and flag germs for phagocytes.
- Which cells are the body's antibody factories? → Plasma cells. Some live in the bone marrow for many years.
- What is the window period? → The first weeks of an infection, when there is not yet enough antibody for a test to find.
- Which chemical causes the itch and swelling of an allergy, and which cells release it? → Histamine. Mast cells release it.
- What is an autoimmune disease? Name one. → An illness in which the immune system attacks the person's own body. Type 1 diabetes and coeliac disease are two.
- Which cells does HIV destroy? → Helper T cells, who switch the rest of the defence on.
- Name two ways HIV does not spread. → Any two of these: hugging, sharing food, coughing, toilets and mosquito bites.
- Where are young T cells tested? → In the thymus, behind the breastbone.
