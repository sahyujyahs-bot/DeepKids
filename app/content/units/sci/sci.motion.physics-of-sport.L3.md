# The physics of sport: how the ground, a throw and a kick change how things move
`sci.motion.physics-of-sport.L3`

**Summary.** Drop a ball on a stone floor and it bounces back up. Drop the same ball on a pillow and it hardly moves again. A bounce needs something to squash and spring back fast. On a hard floor the ball does that. On a pillow, the pillow squashes instead and springs back too slowly to push the ball. The same is true for a runner, so running on soft sand is tiring work. A thrown ball keeps moving forward while gravity pulls it down, so it flies in a curve. A kick off the middle of a ball makes it spin, and a spinning ball curves in the air. In every sport, the player, the ball and the ground push on each other.

**Intro.** *Try this. Hold a ball at the height of your chin and let it fall onto the floor. Catch it. Now let it fall onto a pillow. It is the same ball, falling from the same height. Why does it bounce back up from the floor, but hardly at all from the pillow?* Every game you play is full of questions like this. Why is it so tiring to run on the beach? Why does a ball you throw fly in a curve, and not in a straight line? How do footballers make a ball bend in the air? In this unit you test bounces on different floors in your own home. You find out what the ground does to a ball, and to your feet. Then you look at throwing, kicking, jumping and catching.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull. A force can start something moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down, turn it or change its shape. *e.g.* When you kick a ball, your foot pushes it. That push is a force.
- **Bounce**: When a moving thing hits a surface and moves back off it. *e.g.* A tennis ball dropped on a tiled floor bounces back up about half the height it fell from.
- **Surface**: The top or outside layer of something, the part that is touched. *e.g.* A tiled floor, a doormat and a pillow are three different surfaces.
- **Elastic**: Able to spring back to its old shape after it is squashed or stretched. *e.g.* A rubber ball is elastic. Squeeze it and let go, and it is round again at once.
- **Energy**: What something needs to move, warm up or make a sound. Energy is never used up. It changes from one kind to another. *e.g.* A falling ball has energy of movement. When it hits a pillow, most of that energy turns into a little warmth and sound.
- **Gravity**: The pull of the Earth on everything. It pulls things down towards the ground. *e.g.* Gravity pulls a thrown ball down, so it always comes back to the ground.
- **Friction**: The grip between two things that touch. It acts against sliding. *e.g.* Friction between your feet and the floor lets you push off when you run.
- **Rebound height**: How high a ball goes back up after it bounces. *e.g.* Kavya dropped her ball from 100 cm. It went back up to 55 cm, so the rebound height was 55 cm.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. *e.g.* To test floors, use the same ball and drop it from the same height each time. Change only the floor.
- **Curved path**: The line a thrown thing follows through the air. It goes up, bends over, and comes down. Scientists call this curve a parabola. *e.g.* A cricket ball thrown from the boundary flies in a curved path to the wicket keeper.
- **Angle**: How much something is turned or tilted. Angles are measured in degrees. *e.g.* Throwing a ball straight up is an angle of 90 degrees. Throwing it flat along the ground is 0 degrees.
- **Spin**: Turning round and round while moving. *e.g.* A kick on the side of a football makes it spin as it flies.
- **Tendon**: A strong, stretchy cord that joins a muscle to a bone. Tendons stretch and spring back like rubber bands. *e.g.* The Achilles tendon at the back of your heel stretches each time your foot lands when you run.
- **Air resistance**: The push of the air against something moving through it. It slows things down. *e.g.* A crumpled paper ball and a flat sheet of paper fall differently because of air resistance.

## Explanations
### How a ball bounces · *defined*
**Definition:** A ball bounces when it squashes against a surface and then springs back to its round shape. As it springs back, it pushes on the surface, and the surface pushes it back up.

When a falling ball hits a hard floor, it stops for a tiny moment. In that moment it squashes flat at the bottom. A rubber ball is elastic, so it springs back to round almost at once. That springing back pushes on the floor, and the floor pushes the ball up. All this happens in a few thousandths of a second, far too fast to see. A slow-motion video shows the squash. A ball that does not spring back, like a ball of wet clay, does not bounce at all.
- e.g. A rubber ball dropped on the floor squashes, springs back and bounces up.
- e.g. A ball of wet atta dough dropped on the floor goes flat and stays there.
- e.g. A tennis ball rule says that dropped from 254 cm onto concrete, a good ball must bounce back to between 135 and 147 cm.
### Why the surface matters · *defined*
**Definition:** A hard surface lets the ball do the squashing and springing back, so the ball bounces high. A soft surface squashes instead of the ball. It springs back too slowly to push the ball, so the ball bounces low.

In the bounce test, the floor hardly gave way at all. So the ball squashed, sprang back and bounced high. A pillow is soft. When the ball hits it, the pillow squashes instead of the ball. The pillow's cotton or foam rubs on itself as it squashes. That rubbing turns the ball's movement into a little warmth and sound. By the time the pillow comes back up, the ball has stopped. So soft surfaces take the bounce away. This is why a cricket ball bounces higher on a hard, dry pitch than on a soft, damp one.
- e.g. A ball bounces high off a tiled floor, lower off a doormat, and hardly at all off a pillow.
- e.g. A cricket ball bounces higher on a hard, dry pitch than on a soft, damp one.
- e.g. A tennis ball bounces more slowly and higher on a clay court than on a grass court, so players need to play differently.
### Where a bounce goes · *defined*
**Definition:** No bounce gives back all the energy of the fall. Some of it always turns into warmth and sound, so each bounce is lower than the one before.

Energy is never used up. It only changes from one kind to another. A falling ball has energy of movement. When it bounces, some of that energy turns into warmth in the ball and the floor, and some into the sound of the bounce. Only what is left lifts the ball back up. So a ball bounces lower and lower, and then rolls and stops. Squash players hit their ball against a wall many times before a game. This warms the ball up, and a warm squash ball bounces higher.
- e.g. A ball left to bounce on its own gets lower each time and then stops.
- e.g. A squash ball feels warm after a few minutes of play.
- e.g. You hear a thud with every bounce. That sound is some of the ball's energy, travelling away through the air.
### Running and the ground · *defined*
**Definition:** When you run, your foot pushes back and down on the ground, and the ground pushes you forward and up. Your legs also squash and spring back with every step, like a ball.

Each time your foot lands, your knee bends a little and the tendons in your leg stretch. Then they spring back and help lift you into the next step. On a hard road, the ground does not give way, so your legs do the springing. On soft sand, your foot sinks in. The sand moves and does not spring back, so some of your push is lost in it. Your muscles must do more work for every step. Scientists in Belgium measured people walking and running on sand in 1998. Both took more energy than on a hard surface. Grip matters too. Friction between your foot and the ground stops your foot sliding back as you push.
- e.g. Running on Marina Beach in Chennai is much more tiring than running on the road beside it.
- e.g. A kangaroo stretches the long tendons in their legs with every hop. The tendons spring back and help lift the kangaroo again, so they can travel far without tiring.
- e.g. On a smooth wet floor your foot slides back when you try to run, because there is little friction.
### Why a thrown ball flies in a curve · *defined*
**Definition:** A thrown ball keeps moving forward because of the throw, while gravity pulls it down all the time. Together, these make a curved path called a parabola.

After the ball leaves your hand, nothing pushes it forward any more. It keeps going forward on its own, slowed a little by the air. At the same time, gravity pulls it down, from the very first moment. So the ball's path bends down more and more, in a smooth curve. A faster throw goes further forward before it reaches the ground. The angle matters too. Thrown flat, the ball soon hits the ground. Thrown straight up, it comes back to you. Without air, a throw halfway between flat and straight up, at 45 degrees, goes furthest. In real sport, with air and the thrower's height, the best angle is usually a little lower.
- e.g. A faster marble rolled off a table lands further from the table.
- e.g. Water squirting from a hose or a squeezed bottle makes the same curve you can see.
- e.g. A fielder throwing from the boundary throws the ball up a little, not flat, to reach the wicket keeper.
### Kicking: where you hit the ball · *defined*
**Definition:** A kick through the middle of a ball sends it straight without spin. A kick off the middle makes it spin as well as move.

A kick is a short, hard push. If your foot pushes straight through the centre of the ball, the ball goes straight and does not spin. If your foot hits the ball to one side of the centre, part of the push turns the ball, so it spins. A kick under the ball makes it lift into the air, and it often spins backwards too. A harder kick, or a heavier foot moving fast, gives the ball more speed. Footballers use the side of the foot to make the ball spin on purpose.
- e.g. Flick a light ball at its middle and it rolls straight. Flick it at its side and it spins and turns.
- e.g. A footballer taking a corner kicks the side of the ball so that it spins and bends towards the goal.
- e.g. A kick under a ball lifts it high into the air.
### Why a spinning ball curves in the air · *debated*
**Definition:** A spinning ball pushes the air around it to one side, and the air pushes the ball the other way. This is called the Magnus effect, after Heinrich Gustav Magnus, who studied it in 1852.

That a spinning ball curves is measured and agreed. Isaac Newton noticed a spinning tennis ball curving in 1672. Scientists explain why in two ways. Both describe the same thing, but teachers argue about which is clearer. A good way to settle it is to watch a spinning ball yourself, and see which way it curves.
- e.g. A footballer kicks the side of the ball, and it bends round a wall of players.
- e.g. In cricket, a spin bowler makes the ball turn sideways when it bounces, and drift a little in the air.
- e.g. A table tennis ball hit with topspin dips down fast onto the table.
- **The 'air pushed aside' explanation:** The spinning surface drags the air near it round with it. So air leaving the back of the ball is thrown to one side. The ball pushes the air one way, so the air pushes the ball the other way.
- **The 'air pressure' explanation:** On one side, the ball's surface turns the same way the air flows past, and the air moves faster there. On the other side it turns against the air and slows it. Faster-moving air presses less, so the ball is pushed towards the faster side.
### Jumping and catching · *defined*
**Definition:** To jump, you push down and back on the ground, and the ground pushes you up and forward. To catch softly, you let your hands move back with the ball, so it slows down over a longer time.

When you swing your arms forward and up as you jump, your arms gain forward speed. Your body carries that speed with it into the air. The swing also keeps your legs pushing on the ground for a little longer. So you go further. Catching works the other way round. A ball must be slowed from fast to still. If your hands stay stiff, it stops in a very short time, and the force on your hands is big. If your hands move back with the ball, it takes longer to stop. Then the force is smaller, so the catch stings less and the ball is less likely to bounce out.
- e.g. A long jumper swings both arms forward as they take off.
- e.g. A cricketer catching a hard ball pulls their hands back towards their body.
- e.g. A water balloon caught with stiff hands bursts. Caught with hands that move back, it often stays whole.

## Mostly agreed
- A ball bounces when it squashes and springs back. Hard surfaces give a higher bounce than soft ones. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with one ball and a few surfaces at home. Sports bodies test balls and pitches this way, by dropping balls from a set height and measuring the bounce.
- No bounce gives back all the energy of the fall. Some always turns into warmth and sound. *How we know:* No dropped ball ever bounces back to the height it fell from. Careful measurements show the ball and the floor warm up very slightly.
- A thrown ball, without air, follows a curve called a parabola. *How we know:* Galileo showed it by experiment and with geometry in 1638. Slow-motion videos and photos taken with flashing lights show the same curve.
- Running on soft sand takes more energy than running on a hard surface. *How we know:* Scientists measured the oxygen people breathed while walking and running on sand and on a hard floor. On sand, they used more.
- A spinning ball curves in the air. *How we know:* It has been measured in wind tunnels and filmed in slow motion. Newton noticed it in 1672, and Magnus measured it in 1852.

## How we found out
**Niccolò Tartaglia, an Italian mathematician, and Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, 1537 (Tartaglia's first book on cannonballs) to 1638 (Galileo's book Two New Sciences).** For hundreds of years, many scholars in Europe taught that a thrown ball or a cannonball moves in a straight line until the force of the throw runs out. Then it falls straight down. Books showed cannonballs flying along a straight line and then dropping, like a hook. Niccolò Tartaglia, an Italian mathematician, disagreed. As a boy he had been badly wounded by a soldier's sword, and it left him with a stammer. His nickname, Tartaglia, means 'the stammerer'. His family was too poor to keep him at school, so he taught himself. In 1537 he wrote a book about the paths of cannonballs. He showed that aiming halfway up, at 45 degrees, sends a cannonball furthest. He also argued that no part of the path is really straight, because the ball's weight pulls on it all the time. About 70 years later, Galileo Galilei tested this. He rolled balls down a ramp and off a table, and marked where they landed. Faster balls landed further away, and the numbers fitted a curve. In 1638 he showed that, without air, a thrown thing follows a smooth curve called a parabola.  
*What people believed before:* People thought a thrown ball flies straight until the push of the throw is used up, and then drops straight down. Tartaglia argued that the path curves all the way. Galileo's experiments with balls rolled off a table showed it, and he proved the curve is a parabola.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A heavier ball always bounces higher.** (A common guess among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: A heavy cricket ball feels powerful, and a light balloon hardly bounces at all.  
  Against: How high a ball bounces depends on how elastic it is and on the surface, not on how heavy it is. A light rubber ball bounces far higher than a heavy cricket ball or a steel ball on a tiled floor.
- **A thrown ball goes straight until its push runs out, then drops straight down.** (Many scholars in Europe before about 1540, and many children today; *rejected*)  
  For: A hard throw looks almost straight at the start.  
  Against: Gravity pulls the ball down from the first moment. In the 1500s, Tartaglia argued that the path curves all the way. Galileo showed with experiments that it is a smooth curve, a parabola.
- **Training on sand makes you a faster runner on a hard track.** (Some coaches and athletes; *debated*)  
  For: Sand makes leg muscles work harder, and it is gentler on the joints when you land.  
  Against: Running on sand is slower and feels different, so it may not train the exact movements of a fast run. Studies of sand training are small, and they do not agree.
- **Running barefoot is better for your feet than running in shoes.** (Some runners and some scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Barefoot runners often land on the front of the foot, which can be gentler. People ran without shoes for most of human history.  
  Against: Shoes protect feet from glass, thorns and very hot roads. There is not enough evidence that barefoot running prevents injuries.

## Roots
We have learned how a ball bounces, and how the ground changes a bounce and a run. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your ball bounced high off the hard floor, but hardly at all off a pillow. On the floor, the ball squashed and sprang back up. On the pillow, the pillow squashed instead, and sprang back too slowly to push. Soft sand does the same to a runner's feet, so every step on sand takes more work.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of the ground as something that just lies there while the ball or the runner does all the work. But a bounce and a stride are made by two things together. What the ground does when it is squashed decides how much of the push comes back.  
*Evidence:* One ball, dropped from the same height, bounced to about 55 cm off the floor and about 5 cm off a pillow. Only the surface changed. Jogging on a mattress felt harder than on the floor, for the same reason.

- *who am i:* You are a springy body. Your tendons stretch and spring back every time you land.
- *what is life:* Kangaroos, camels and runners have bodies shaped for the ground they move on.
- *what is this world:* The ground is never just still. Every floor squashes a tiny bit and pushes back on you.

A ball on its own cannot bounce, and a runner on their own cannot run. Each bounce and each step is made between two things that push on each other. A hard floor and a soft pillow meet the same ball, and two different bounces come out. So a game is not something one player does alone. It happens between the player, the ball and the ground, all pushing at once.

- **Samyutta Nikaya 12.67, the simile of the two sheaves of reeds:** Two bundles of reeds can stand up by leaning on each other. Take one away, and the other falls. (A bounce needs the ball and the ground together. Take away a firm ground, as on a pillow or soft sand, and the bounce falls flat.)
- **Jain anekantavada, the story of the blind men and the elephant:** Each person who touches one part of the elephant describes a different animal. Each is partly right. (The same ball seems bouncy on the floor and dead on the pillow. To understand a bounce, you have to look at the ball and the ground together.)
- Science (settled): Every push comes with an equal push back. When your foot pushes the ground, the ground pushes your foot just as hard. This is Newton's third law.
- Science (settled): Tendons in the legs store energy as they stretch when a foot lands, and return much of it in the next step. This is part of why running on firm ground costs less energy than on sand.
- Other view, Many physicists and coaches: A bounce is just the ball's own elasticity meeting a floor. The ground is a surface, and the skill is in the player. Talking about the ground 'taking part' adds nothing to the physics.
- Practice, Feel the ground push back (3 min): Let's stand with bare feet on the floor. Close your eyes. Rise slowly onto your toes, then come down. Do it five times. Feel the floor pressing up on the soles of your feet. Now stand still and breathe slowly. The floor is pushing up on you right now, just as hard as you push down on it.
- Ask: When you jump, who pushes you up: you, or the floor?
- Ask: The pillow and the floor met the same ball. Was the bounce in the ball, in the floor, or between them?
- Ask: If the ground pushes back on every step you take, is walking something you do alone?

## Story: Nila and the Beach Race
*Elliot's Beach is a real beach in Besant Nagar, Chennai, and beach cricket on wet sand is common there. Nila, Arjun, their mother and the race are made up.*

Sports Day was in two weeks, and Arjun wanted to win the 100 metre race. He was eight, and he had come second last year. 'This year I will train every evening,' he told his sister Nila. 'On the beach, like the big runners in films.'

Nila was ten. She liked to watch things carefully before she believed them. So every evening she walked with Arjun and Amma to Elliot's Beach, carrying her notebook and an old tennis ball.

Arjun ran on the soft, dry sand near the road, from the lifeguard tower to the fishing boats and back. After four runs his face was red and his legs were shaking. 'The sand is too hot,' he said. Next evening it was cooler, and he was just as tired. 'My legs are getting weaker,' he said on the third day, and his lip wobbled.

On Saturday, the school let the children practise on the track. Arjun ran 100 metres while Amma timed him on her phone. He was faster than he had ever been. 'Maybe the beach is working,' Amma said. But Arjun shook his head. 'On the beach I feel slow and heavy,' he said. 'Here I feel like a ball.'

Nila wrote that down. Like a ball. Then she had an idea.

That evening, on the beach, Nila stood on the cement path at the edge of the sand. She held the tennis ball at the height of her nose and let it drop. It bounced back up to her waist. She stepped onto the soft, dry sand and dropped it again from the same height. The ball landed with a soft thump, made a little hollow in the sand and stayed there.

'Arjun, come and see,' she called. She did it three more times on the path and three more times on the sand. On the path, the ball always came back to her waist. On the dry sand, it never came back up at all.

'The sand ate the bounce,' said Arjun.

'It's not eating it,' said Nila. 'Look at the hollow. When the ball lands on the path, the path doesn't move. So the ball squashes and springs back up. When the ball lands on the sand, the sand moves instead. It slides out of the way and doesn't spring back. So nothing pushes the ball up again.'

She walked down to the dark, wet sand near the waves, where some boys were playing cricket. The wet sand was firm and packed down. Nila dropped the ball there. It bounced, not as high as on the path, but much higher than on the dry sand.

'That's why they play cricket down here,' said Amma, who had come to watch. 'On the dry sand the ball would just stop.'

Nila turned to her brother. 'When you run on the track, you said you feel like a ball. When your foot lands, your legs bend a little and then spring back. The track doesn't move, so all of your push goes into the next step. On the dry sand, your foot sinks and the sand slides away. Some of your push goes into the sand, not into you. So every step takes more work.'

Arjun looked at the deep footprints he had left near the tower. Each one had a little heap of sand kicked up behind it.

'So my legs aren't getting weaker?' he asked.

'Your legs are doing more work than on the track,' said Nila. 'The sand makes every step harder. That doesn't mean you are slow.'

Arjun thought about this. 'Then is running on sand good or bad?'

Nila opened her notebook. 'I don't know,' she said. 'It makes your legs work hard. But it's not the same as running on a track. Maybe you should do both.'

So for the next ten days, Arjun ran twice on the dry sand and twice on the firm, wet sand by the waves, where his feet hardly sank. Then on Saturdays he ran on the track. Nila timed him and kept a table in her notebook, with a column for each kind of ground.

On Sports Day, Arjun came first by the length of one long stride. When he got his medal, he ran straight to Nila. 'I didn't feel heavy at all,' he said. 'I felt like your tennis ball on the path.'

Nila smiled and wrote one more line in her notebook: 'The ground runs with you. Hard ground gives your push back. Soft ground keeps some of it.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: a bounce needs something to squash and spring back fast. On the hard path, the ball squashed and sprang back, and the path hardly moved. On dry sand, the sand moved instead: the grains slid apart and did not spring back, so the ball's movement was spent shifting sand and was not returned. Firm wet sand, where water holds the grains together, gave a middling bounce. A runner's legs work like the ball: tendons and muscles stretch and spring back with each step. On soft sand, part of each push goes into moving sand, so running takes more energy. Whether sand training makes a runner faster on a track is still argued about.

- Ask: Why did the tennis ball bounce on the path but not on the dry sand?
- Ask: Why do people play beach cricket on the wet sand near the waves?
- Ask: Arjun said his legs were getting weaker. What was really happening?
- Ask: How did Nila find out, instead of just guessing?
- Ask: Nila said 'I don't know' when Arjun asked if sand training was good. Was that a good answer? Why?

- **Printable cards: 12 sport cards to cut out:** Running a race (Feet push back on the ground, the ground pushes you forward); Long jump take-off (Feet push down and back, arms swing forward); Throwing a ball (Hand pushes the ball forward); Catching a ball (Hands move back to slow the ball); Kicking a football (Foot pushes the ball); Kabaddi raider turning fast (Foot pushes sideways on the mat); Bowling in cricket (Arm swings, hand lets go of the ball); Skipping with a rope (Legs push on the floor, then land and spring); Swimming (Arms push water back, water pushes you forward); Lagori: throwing the ball at the stones (Hand pushes the ball, the ball pushes the stones); Tug of war (Hands pull the rope, feet push on the ground); Running on sand (Feet push, sand slides back, so the push is weaker)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the cards and shuffle them. Clear a space in the room.  
  *Parent says:* We will act out sports, then freeze, and say what is pushing what.  
  *Child mode:* Pick a card. Act it out. Freeze when you hear 'Freeze!'
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Act out 'Running a race' yourself, freeze, and explain the forces.  
  *Parent says:* I'm running. Freeze! My foot pushes back on the floor. The floor pushes me forward. That's what moves me.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the grown-up run and freeze. What pushes them forward?
  - Check: When you run, which way does your foot push on the ground? → **Backward**
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns to act out the cards and freeze. Guess, then name the forces.  
  *Parent says:* Freeze! Who can guess the sport? What is pushing what?  
  *Child mode:* Act out your card. Freeze! Say one push or pull.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the points.  
  *Parent says:* In which sport was the ground most important?  
  *Child mode:* Count the points. Which sport needed the ground most?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bounce Test: which floor gives the ball back? (30 min)
- **question:** If we drop the same ball from the same height onto different surfaces, which one gives the highest bounce?
- **predict:** Guess first. Put the five surfaces in order, from the one you think gives the highest bounce to the lowest. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same ball every time, and drop it from the same height, 100 cm. Let go without pushing it down. Change only the surface. Drop the ball 3 times on each surface, because one try can be a fluke, and use the middle number.
- **safety:** Do this on a dry floor with space around you. Sweep up any rice that spills, so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar for each surface, as tall as its middle number.
- **results table:** Surface | Try 1 (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Middle number (cm)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the paper strip up a wall, starting at the floor. 2. Use the measuring tape to mark it every 10 cm, up to 100 cm. 3. Write the numbers beside the marks.  
  *Parent says:* We need to measure how high the ball bounces. So let's make a tall paper ruler on the wall first.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the paper up the wall from the floor. Mark it every 10 cm. Write the numbers. Stop at 100.
  - Note: A door or a cupboard side works too, if your walls cannot take tape. Check that the floor below the strip is hard and flat, like tiles, stone or cement.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the five surfaces in a row near the wall. 2. Show your child each one, and ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* Here are five surfaces: the bare floor, a doormat, a folded bedsheet, a pillow and a plate of rice. Which will give the highest bounce? Which the lowest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the five surfaces. Which gives the highest bounce? Which the lowest? Why? Write your order.
  - Ask: Why do you think that surface will give the highest bounce? (good: Because it is hard.; Because it does not squash.; Because the ball bounces well on the floor when I play.)
  - Note: Wait for a guess and a reason. Don't say whether it is right. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the ball so its bottom is level with the 100 cm mark. 2. Open your fingers and let it fall, without pushing it down. 3. Your child crouches with their eyes at about the 50 cm mark. 4. They read the mark at the bottom of the ball, at the top of its bounce. 5. Do this 3 times on the bare floor.  
  *Parent says:* I will just open my hand. If I push the ball down, it would not be a fair test. You watch the bottom of the ball at the top of its bounce.  
  *Child mode:* Crouch down by the wall. Watch the bottom of the ball. Where does it stop going up? Read the mark.
  - Note: Reading a bounce by eye is hard. If two of you disagree, take the number in between. A slow-motion phone video, held level with the bounce, makes reading easy. Pause it at the top of the bounce. Write each try in the table.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the doormat against the wall, under the paper ruler. 2. Drop the ball 3 times and read each bounce. 3. Do the same with the folded bedsheet, the pillow and the plate of rice.  
  *Parent says:* Same ball, same height, same way of letting go. Only the surface changes. Let's see what each one does.  
  *Child mode:* Now the doormat. Drop it 3 times. Then the bedsheet, the pillow and the rice. Write each bounce.
  - Note: Measure from the top of each surface, not from the floor. A folded sheet is about 2 to 3 cm thick, so take that off the reading, or hold the ball 100 cm above the top of the sheet. On the pillow and the rice, the ball may hardly lift at all. That is a real result: write 0 or a small number.
  - Check: Lakshmi drops her ball from 100 cm on the floor, but from 50 cm on the pillow. Is that a fair test? → **No, because the height changed as well as the surface**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each surface, find the middle number of the three tries. 2. Draw a bar chart, with one bar for each surface.  
  *Parent says:* Let's find the middle number for each surface. If the tries were 50, 55 and 60, the middle one is 55.  
  *Child mode:* Find the middle number for each surface. Draw a bar for each. Which bar is tallest?
  - Note: The middle number, called the median, is less upset by one odd try than the average is.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Drop the ball onto the pillow 10 times quickly. 2. Your child presses the pillow and listens to each landing.  
  *Parent says:* The ball fell 100 cm onto the pillow and hardly came back up. Where did its movement go? Listen and feel.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the ball land on the pillow. Feel the dent. Where did the bounce go?
  - Ask: The ball fell fast. On the pillow it stopped and stayed. What happened to the pillow? (good: It squashed.; It made a dent.; It took the bounce.; It went down and came back up slowly.)
  - Note: The warmth is far too small to feel. The dent and the soft thud are what your child will notice. Both are signs of the pillow squashing and rubbing on itself.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: hard surfaces give a high bounce and soft ones give a low bounce. What I saw: on the floor it bounced to about 55 cm, and on the pillow to about 5 cm. Why: on the floor the ball squashed and sprang back up. On the pillow, the pillow squashed instead and did not push the ball back up.
### e2 · Galileo's Table: does a faster ball land further? (25 min)
- **question:** A marble rolls off the edge of a table. If it is moving faster, does it land further from the table?
- **predict:** Guess first. We will roll a marble from three heights on a ramp, so it leaves the table slowly, faster and fastest. Will it land in the same place each time, or further each time? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same marble, the same ramp and the same table each time. Change only how high up the ramp you let go. Let go without pushing. Do each height 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Pick up the marble after each try. A marble on the floor can make someone slip, and small children may put it in their mouth.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the table from the side, with three curved paths, one for each height.
- **results table:** Start height on the ramp (cm) | Try 1 (cm from the table) | Try 2 | Try 3 | Middle number
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the ruler on the table with its low end about 10 cm from the edge. 2. Lift the other end on books. 3. Hang the key on its thread from the table edge, so it just touches the floor. 4. Mark that spot on the floor with a pencil or a bit of tape.  
  *Parent says:* The marble will roll down the ramp, run across the flat bit of table, and fly off the edge. We need to know where 'right below the edge' is, so we can measure from there.  
  *Child mode:* Set the ramp up. Hang the key from the edge. Mark the spot under it.
  - Note: The flat gap of table between the ramp and the edge matters. It makes the marble leave the edge moving flat, not downwards, so the three tries are fair.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mark three start points on the ramp, 5 cm, 10 cm and 20 cm above the table. 2. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* From higher up the ramp, the marble will be going faster when it leaves the table. Will it land in the same place, or further away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Higher up the ramp means faster. Where will it land each time? Why?
  - Ask: Where do you think the fastest marble will land? (good: Further from the table.; In the same place, because it falls the same height.)
  - Note: Measure the start heights straight up from the table top with the ruler, not along the ramp. Both guesses are common and sensible. Let the test decide.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the plate of rava on the floor in front of the table. 2. Roll the marble once from 10 cm to see where it lands. 3. Move the plate so that spot is in the middle.  
  *Parent says:* This first roll is a practice, to find where to put the plate.  
  *Child mode:* Practice roll first. Move the plate to where the marble lands.
  - Note: Smooth the rava flat with your hand after each try, so each new dent is easy to see.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let go of the marble from the 5 cm mark, 3 times. 2. Each time, measure from the floor mark to the dent. 3. Do the same from 10 cm and from 20 cm, moving the plate if needed.  
  *Parent says:* Just open your fingers, don't push. Watch the path of the marble as it falls. Is it straight down, or a curve?  
  *Child mode:* Let go from 5 cm. Measure to the dent. Then 10 cm. Then 20 cm.
  - Note: The marble flies fast. Your child may only see a blur. A slow-motion phone video, taken from the side, shows the curve clearly.
  - Check: As the marble flies off the table, what path does it follow? → **A curve that bends down more and more**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to draw the table from the side, with three curved paths ending at the three dents.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the three paths. Do they all start in the same place? Where do they end?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the table from the side. Draw three paths. Where does each end?
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a faster ball goes further before it lands. What I saw: from 5 cm the marble landed about 30 cm from the table, and from 20 cm about 58 cm. Why: the marble keeps moving forward while gravity pulls it down. The faster one went further forward in the same short time it took to fall.
### e3 · Arms or No Arms: does swinging your arms help you jump further? (20 min)
- **question:** Do you jump further from standing if you swing your arms, or if you keep them still behind your back?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will swinging your arms make your jump longer, shorter, or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same child jumps from the same line, on the same floor, both ways. Change only what the arms do. Jump 3 times each way, and rest a little between jumps, so tiredness does not spoil the test.
- **safety:** Jump barefoot on a dry floor, never in socks on tiles. Move rugs, toys and furniture away from the landing place. A grown-up watches every jump.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw yourself jumping both ways.
- **results table:** How I jumped | Jump 1 (cm) | Jump 2 (cm) | Jump 3 (cm) | Middle number (cm)
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stick a tape line across the floor. 2. Clear 2 metres of floor in front of it. 3. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* You will jump from this line with both feet together. First with your hands held behind your back. Then swinging your arms. Will your arms make any difference? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Arms behind you, or arms swinging. Which jump will be longer? Why?
  - Note: Check the floor is dry and your child is barefoot. Socks on tiles are slippery.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds their hands together behind their back. 2. They bend their knees and jump forward with both feet. 3. Put the coin at the back of the heel nearest the line. 4. Measure from the line to the coin. 5. Do 3 jumps, resting a little between them.  
  *Parent says:* Hands behind your back. Bend your knees. Jump! Stay where you land, so I can mark your heel.  
  *Child mode:* Hands behind your back. Bend your knees. Jump with both feet. Stay still!
  - Note: Measure to the heel that lands closest to the line. If your child falls back onto their hands, measure to the hand, as long jump judges do, or do the jump again.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now your child swings both arms back, then forward and up as they jump. 2. Mark and measure in the same way. 3. Do 3 jumps.  
  *Parent says:* This time, swing both arms back as you bend, and throw them forward and up as you jump.  
  *Child mode:* Swing your arms back. Bend. Throw your arms forward and up as you jump!
  - Check: Meenakshi jumped 3 times with arms swinging and got 110, 115 and 112 cm. What is her middle number? → **112 cm**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. Any honest answer with all three parts is good. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: swinging my arms helps me jump further. What I saw: with my arms behind my back I jumped about 95 cm, and with them swinging about 112 cm. Why: my arms swung forward fast, and my body carried that speed with it into the air. Most children jump further with their arms, often by 10 to 20 cm. If your child did not, they may not yet time the swing with the jump. That is a good thing to practise.

## Challenges
### c1 · The Bounciest Floor: make a mini trampoline (30 min)

### c2 · Feet for Sand: how not to sink (25 min)
- **problem:** Soft sand is hard to walk and run on, because feet sink in. Camels walk across deserts of soft sand every day. Find a way for a 'foot' to sink less into soft ground.

## Applications
- Tennis, cricket and football balls are tested by dropping them from a set height and measuring the bounce.
- Cricket groundsmen roll and water the pitch to make it harder or softer, which changes how high the ball bounces.
- Running tracks are made of firm rubber that gives a runner's push back and grips their shoes.
- Gym mats and the sand pit in long jump are soft, so a landing slows down gently and does not hurt.
- Running shoes have soft, springy soles that squash and spring back.
- Cricket helmets and pads have soft foam that squashes and slows a hit ball over a longer time.
- Footballers and spin bowlers make the ball spin so that it curves or turns.
- Wide tyres on sand buggies and tractors spread the weight so they sink less in soft ground.

## Open questions
- **Does training on sand make a runner faster on a track?** Sand training clearly works the legs harder. But studies have been small and do not agree on whether it improves speed on a hard track.
- **What is the best floor for a running track?** Tracks that are a little springy seem to help runners, but how springy is best for speed, and for avoiding injuries, is still studied.
- **Is barefoot running safer than running in shoes?** Barefoot runners land differently, which some think is gentler. There is not yet enough evidence either way about injuries.
- **Exactly how much do the tendons and the muscles each do in a running stride?** Scientists can measure the forces, but it is hard to see inside a living leg while it runs. The share changes with speed and with the ground.

## Connected maths
- In the bounce test, Kavya's ball bounced to 55 cm on the floor, 40 cm on the doormat, 20 cm on the folded bedsheet, 5 cm on the pillow and 0 cm on the rice. Draw a bar chart. Which surface gave the highest bounce, and how much higher was it than the doormat? **The floor gave the highest bounce. It was 15 cm higher than the doormat.**. The floor's bar is tallest, at 55 cm. The doormat's is 40 cm. 55 minus 40 is 15. So the floor bounce was 15 cm higher. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Kavya dropped the ball from 100 cm and it bounced to 55 cm on the floor. Then she dropped it from 50 cm. Predict how high it will bounce, and say why. **About 27 or 28 cm, roughly half of 55.**. From 100 cm it came back about 55 cm, just over half. On the same floor, the ball gives back about the same share of the fall. Half of 100 is 50, so half of 55 is about 27 or 28 cm. A real ball may differ a little, so test it. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- Meenakshi throws a ball three times at the same speed: at an angle of 15 degrees, 45 degrees and 75 degrees. Without air, the 15 and 75 degree throws land in the same place. Which throw lands furthest? What do 15 and 75 add up to? **The 45 degree throw lands furthest. 15 and 75 add up to 90.**. Without air, the throw halfway up, at 45 degrees, goes furthest. Two angles that add up to 90 degrees, such as 15 and 75, land in the same place: the low one goes fast and flat, and the high one goes up and comes down. 15 + 75 = 90. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L3`)
- In the arm swing test, Nithya jumped 92, 98 and 95 cm with her arms behind her back, and 110, 116 and 112 cm with her arms swinging. Find each middle number. How much further did swinging her arms take her? **95 cm and 112 cm. Swinging her arms took her 17 cm further.**. In order, 92, 95, 98 gives a middle of 95. In order, 110, 112, 116 gives a middle of 112. 112 minus 95 is 17. So she jumped 17 cm further with her arms. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
