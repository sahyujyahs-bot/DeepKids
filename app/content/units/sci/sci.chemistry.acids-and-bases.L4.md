# The pH scale, stomach acid and antacids: why does the burning go away?
`sci.chemistry.acids-and-bases.L4`

**Summary.** After a heavy feast, some grown-ups feel a burning in the chest. They chew a chalky tablet, and the burning fades in minutes. The tablet is a base, and it is fighting an acid that the stomach makes on purpose. Your child races different powders against a glass of acid, counts how many scoops each one needs, and learns what the numbers on the pH scale really mean. They find out why every step on the scale is ten times bigger than the last, and why the stomach does not burn itself. They also meet the two doctors who proved that a bacterium can live in stomach acid.

**Intro.** *After a big wedding feast, a grown-up chews a white tablet, and the burning in their chest goes away within minutes. The tablet never touched the burn. So what did it do?* A burning in the chest after a meal is called heartburn. It has nothing to do with the heart. Acid from the stomach has splashed up into the food pipe, and the food pipe has no shield against it. A tablet that stops the burning must change that acid. In this unit you make a glass of 'stomach acid' with vinegar and cabbage water. Then you race different powders to see which one beats it. You also learn to read the pH scale with real numbers, and you find out why the stomach does not burn itself.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can read the pH scale from 0 to 14 and say whether a number means an acid, neutral or a base.
1. I can explain that each step on the pH scale is ten times bigger than the last, and work out how many times more acidic one liquid is than another.
2. I can use an indicator, such as cabbage water or pH paper, to place a liquid on the pH scale, and say how rough or exact the reading is.
3. I can explain why the stomach makes a strong acid, and how its lining keeps itself safe from it.
4. I can explain how an antacid works, and run a fair test that counts how much of each powder it takes to neutralise the same acid.
5. I can explain why too much base is as wrong as too much acid, and why the body keeps different places at different pH.
6. I can tell how Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed that a bacterium lives in the stomach and causes most ulcers.

## Terms
- **pH**: A number that says how acidic or basic a watery liquid is. A low number means a strong acid. A high number means a strong base. Seven is neutral. *e.g.* Lemon juice is about pH 2. Pure water is pH 7. Soapy water is about pH 9.
- **Hydrogen ion**: A tiny hydrogen particle that carries an electric charge and floats free in a watery liquid. The more hydrogen ions a liquid has, the more acidic it is. *e.g.* Lemon juice holds about 100,000 times more hydrogen ions than pure water.
- **Tenfold**: Ten times as much. A tenfold step multiplies a number by ten. *e.g.* A drop of dye in ten spoons of water is a tenfold dilution. The colour is ten times weaker.
- **Indicator**: A dye that changes colour to show how acidic or basic a liquid is. *e.g.* Red cabbage water is pink in acid and green in a strong base.
- **Universal indicator**: A mixture of several dyes that gives a different colour at almost every pH number. It is sold as a liquid or as pH paper. *e.g.* A pH paper strip turns red in lemon juice, green in water and blue in soapy water.
- **Neutral**: Neither acidic nor basic. A neutral liquid has equal amounts of acid-type and base-type particles. *e.g.* Pure water is neutral, at pH 7.
- **Stomach acid**: A strong acid, called hydrochloric acid, that cells in the stomach wall make on purpose. It starts the digestion of protein and kills many germs that arrive with food. *e.g.* The acid in an empty stomach is about pH 1 to 2, strong enough to sting a cut.
- **Enzyme**: A natural helper made by living cells that speeds up one kind of change, such as breaking food into smaller pieces. Each enzyme works best at its own pH. *e.g.* The enzyme pepsin, in the stomach, breaks the protein in dal, egg and paneer into smaller pieces.
- **Mucus**: A thick, slippery, wet coating made by cells. Mucus protects the soft inside of the stomach, the nose and the throat. *e.g.* The mucus in the stomach holds a little base, so the stomach wall stays close to neutral even when the middle of the stomach is full of acid.
- **Heartburn**: A burning feeling in the chest that happens when stomach acid splashes up into the food pipe, which has no mucus shield. *e.g.* A very big, oily meal can make acid splash up when you lie down straight after eating.
- **Antacid**: A medicine or powder that is a base. It reacts with stomach acid and uses some of it up. *e.g.* Milk of magnesia, cooking soda and many chewable tablets work as antacids.
- **Neutralise**: To use up acid by adding a base, or use up a base by adding acid, until the mixture moves towards neutral. *e.g.* Cooking soda neutralises vinegar and makes fizzing carbon dioxide gas.
- **Bicarbonate**: A gentle base. Cooking soda is sodium bicarbonate. Your own body also makes bicarbonate to protect the stomach wall and to neutralise acid after the stomach. *e.g.* Fruit salt such as Eno holds bicarbonate and a dry acid. It fizzes in water, because the two meet and react.
- **Helicobacter pylori**: A spiral-shaped bacterium that lives in the stomachs of about half of all people. Most of the time it does no harm. Sometimes it causes sore patches, called ulcers. *e.g.* Barry Marshall and Robin Warren showed in the 1980s that this bacterium causes most stomach ulcers.
- **Ulcer**: A sore patch in the stomach wall or the first part of the small intestine, where the protective layer has worn away. *e.g.* Most ulcers are caused by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori or by some painkillers.

## Explanations
### pH counts hydrogen ions · *defined*
**Definition:** pH is a number that tells how many free hydrogen ions a watery liquid holds. The more hydrogen ions, the lower the pH.

Last year you met the idea that acids give out tiny charged particles of hydrogen when they dissolve in water. Those are hydrogen ions, and pH counts them. Even pure water has a very few of them. About one water particle in every 500 million splits in two. It makes one hydrogen ion and one partner particle called a hydroxide ion, always in equal numbers. So pure water is balanced, and its pH is 7. An acid adds hydrogen ions, so the pH falls below 7. A base takes hydrogen ions out of the water or adds hydroxide ions, so the pH rises above 7. Most everyday liquids lie between 0 and 14. Very strong acids go below 0, and very strong bases go above 14.
- e.g. Lemon juice has a pH of about 2. It holds a great many hydrogen ions.
- e.g. Pure water has a pH of 7. Its hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions are equal in number.
- e.g. Soapy water has a pH of about 9 or 10. It holds very few hydrogen ions and many hydroxide ions.
### Every step on the pH scale is ten times bigger · *defined*
**Definition:** A drop of one pH number means ten times as many hydrogen ions. A rise of one pH number means ten times fewer.

On a ruler, every step is the same size. The pH scale is different. Each step multiplies by ten. Vinegar at pH 3 has ten times as many hydrogen ions as a drink at pH 4. Lemon juice at pH 2 has ten times as many as vinegar, and a hundred times as many as the drink at pH 4. From lemon juice at pH 2 up to pure water at pH 7 is five steps. That is ten times ten times ten times ten times ten, which is 100,000. So lemon juice has 100,000 times as many hydrogen ions as pure water. Scientists use scales like this when numbers swing very wide. The small numbers on the pH scale hide huge differences. Earthquake magnitudes work in a similar way, because each whole number is about ten times more shaking.
- e.g. pH 3 holds 100 times as many hydrogen ions as pH 5.
- e.g. The acid in an empty stomach, at pH 2, holds about 250,000 times as many hydrogen ions as blood, at pH 7.4.
- e.g. A tiny drop of strong acid in a glass changes the pH of plain water by many steps at once.
### Indicators: dyes that show hydrogen ions · *defined*
**Definition:** An indicator is a dye whose colour changes with the number of hydrogen ions around it.

A dye particle is made of atoms joined in a shape. When hydrogen ions attach to it, or let go of it, the shape changes a little. A different shape soaks up different colours of light, so we see a different colour. Red cabbage holds a dye called anthocyanin. It is red at pH 1 to 3, purple near pH 6 to 7, blue near pH 8 to 9, green near pH 10 to 12, and yellow above that. Litmus, a dye from lichens, is red in acids and blue in bases. Turmeric is yellow in acids and turns red in bases. That is why kumkum, which is made from turmeric and lime, is red. Universal indicator is a mixture of several dyes, so it gives a different colour at almost every number. Cabbage colours are only rough. They change with the cabbage, the water and the light.
- e.g. Red cabbage water turns pink in vinegar and green in washing powder water.
- e.g. Turmeric water turns red when a pinch of cooking soda is stirred in.
- e.g. Butterfly pea flower tea is blue, turns purple in lemon juice and turns green in soapy water.
### pH paper and pH meters: how exact is a reading? · *defined*
**Definition:** A pH strip gives a number by matching its colour to a chart. A pH meter gives a number by measuring a tiny electric voltage.

pH paper is a strip soaked in several indicators. You dip it, wait a few seconds, and match its colour to the chart on the pack. Most strips read whole numbers, so they are good to about one step. A step is ten times, so even that is a very useful reading. A pH meter has a thin glass bulb on a probe. The glass makes a tiny voltage that changes with the number of hydrogen ions on the other side. The meter turns the voltage into a number, and a good one reads to one hundredth of a step. A dark or coloured liquid can hide a strip's colour, and a dirty probe gives a wrong number. So scientists measure again, rinse between samples, and say how sure they are.
- e.g. A strip dipped in lemon juice turns red-orange and reads about 2.
- e.g. A school pH meter reads a glass of plain water at 7.1 and a glass of left-open water at 6.4.
- e.g. Cabbage water can tell pink from purple from green, but it cannot tell pH 4 from pH 5.
### Stomach acid: an acid made on purpose · *defined*
**Definition:** Stomach acid is hydrochloric acid, which cells in the stomach wall make and pour into the stomach.

Cells in the wall of your stomach pump hydrogen ions and chloride ions into the stomach, so it holds hydrochloric acid. On an empty stomach the pH is about 1 to 2. While food is inside, the pH rises for a while, and then the stomach makes more. The acid does three jobs. It kills many germs that arrive on food. It unfolds the long protein chains in dal, egg and paneer, so they can be cut. And it wakes up pepsin, an enzyme that the stomach makes in a sleeping form. Pepsin cuts protein into smaller pieces. The stomach can start making acid before the food even arrives. The sight and smell of a hot sambar are enough.
- e.g. Pepsin works best at about pH 2. In plain water, it hardly works at all.
- e.g. People with too little stomach acid get some infections of the gut more easily.
- e.g. The sight and smell of food can start the stomach making acid, a few minutes before the first bite.
### Why the stomach does not burn itself · *defined*
**Definition:** The stomach wall is protected by a coat of mucus that holds a base, and by cells that are replaced every few days.

The stomach wall has three defences. First, a thick coat of mucus covers the wall, like a wet gel. Second, the mucus traps bicarbonate, which is a base. The middle of the stomach is at about pH 2, but the layer touching the wall is close to pH 7. The acid is neutralised on its way through the coat. Third, the cells of the wall live only a few days. New cells keep replacing the worn ones. So the stomach is not safe because it is tough. It is safe because it is being made again, all the time. If the coat thins or the cells are damaged, acid reaches them, and a sore patch called an ulcer can form.
- e.g. Painkillers such as aspirin can thin the mucus, which is why a doctor says to take them after food.
- e.g. A small crack in the mucus lets acid reach the cells, and an ulcer can start there.
- e.g. The mucus coat is a layer a few tenths of a millimetre thick, about as thick as a sheet of card.
### Heartburn and antacids · *defined*
**Definition:** An antacid is a base. It reacts with acid and uses some of it up, so the pH rises.

At the top of your stomach, a ring of muscle works like a door. It lets food in and keeps acid down. Sometimes it opens when it should not, and a little acid splashes up into the food pipe. The food pipe has no mucus shield, so it burns. That is heartburn. An antacid is a base such as sodium bicarbonate, calcium carbonate, magnesium hydroxide or aluminium hydroxide. It reacts with the acid and makes a salt and water. The carbonates also make carbon dioxide gas, which may bring a burp. Antacids do not stop the stomach making acid. They only use up what is already there, so the relief lasts from less than an hour to a few hours. Heartburn that comes often needs a doctor, because it can hide other problems. Children should not be given medicines without asking a doctor.
- e.g. Cooking soda in water, milk of magnesia and chewable tablets all work as antacids.
- e.g. Eno fruit salt holds bicarbonate and a dry acid. In water, it fizzes, and the liquid left is usually a little basic.
- e.g. Lying down straight after a big meal makes it easier for acid to splash up.
### The body keeps different places at different pH · *defined*
**Definition:** Different parts of the body are held at different pH, because each part does a job that needs its own pH.

Saliva in your mouth is close to neutral. The stomach is a pool of acid, at pH 1 to 3. Just after the stomach, the pancreas and the gut wall pour out bicarbonate. It neutralises the acid, so the small intestine is near neutral or slightly basic. The enzymes of the intestine work best there. They would stop working in the stomach's acid, and pepsin stops working in the intestine. Your blood is held between pH 7.35 and 7.45. That is a very narrow range, and the lungs, the kidneys and chemicals called buffers all work to keep it there. The skin is a little acidic, at about pH 5, and that keeps many germs down. So the body is not one steady condition. It is a row of rooms, each held at the pH its job needs.
- e.g. Pepsin is at work in the stomach at pH 2 and stops working in the intestine at about pH 7.
- e.g. A person whose blood pH falls to 7.0 or rises to 7.8 is seriously ill.
- e.g. The pancreas releases bicarbonate into the first part of the small intestine. It is the body's own antacid.
### A bacterium with an antacid of her own · *defined*
**Definition:** Helicobacter pylori is a spiral-shaped bacterium that lives in the mucus of the stomach and makes a base to protect herself from the acid.

Helicobacter pylori swims through the mucus until she reaches the layer near the wall, where the pH is close to neutral. On the way, she makes an enzyme called urease. Urease breaks down urea, a chemical that is always present in the stomach, into ammonia. Ammonia is a base. It makes a little cloud of neutral liquid around her. About half of all people carry this bacterium, and most people notice nothing. Sometimes she irritates the wall, and the damage can become an ulcer. Other living beings also thrive in acid. The bacteria that make curd work at pH 4. Some tiny microbes live in volcanic hot springs at a pH close to 0.
- e.g. The urea breath test finds H. pylori by catching the gas from her urease.
- e.g. Lactic acid bacteria in curd grow at pH 4, and their own acid slows them down.
- e.g. Picrophilus, a microbe from hot sulphur springs in Japan, grows at a pH of about 0.7.
### Why is human stomach acid so strong? · *idea*
**Definition:** Nobody knows for certain why human stomachs are so acidic. One recent idea is that our ancestors often ate old meat that held many microbes.

In 2015, a group of scientists led by Daniel Beasley compared the stomach acidity of many animals. They found that humans are more acidic than most animals that eat meat. Our stomachs are more like those of scavengers such as vultures, which eat old meat. The scientists suggested that strong acid protects a scavenger from the germs in food that has been lying around. They think our ancestors may have eaten carrion often enough that the acid was worth having. But this is an idea and not a settled fact. Other scientists say the strong acid may also help us get the most from protein, or protect against the huge number of microbes in all food.
- e.g. A vulture can eat old meat that would make most animals ill.
- e.g. Humans, vultures and some other scavengers all have very acidic stomachs.
- e.g. Animals that mostly eat leaves tend to have milder stomach acid.
- **Beasley and colleagues, 2015:** Strong acid evolved because our ancestors ate carrion, and it cleared out the germs.
- **Other scientists:** The acid may be more about digesting protein and protecting against the many microbes on all food, and not about carrion.
### Hydrogen ions power every living cell · *defined*
**Definition:** Living cells keep a difference in hydrogen ions across a thin wall, and use it like a battery.

Inside every cell of your body, tiny parts called mitochondria pump hydrogen ions across a thin wall. There are more hydrogen ions on one side than on the other, like water held behind a dam. The ions can flow back only through a tiny machine in the wall, called ATP synthase. As they pour through, they turn the machine like a mill wheel, and it makes ATP. ATP is the small packet of energy that every cell spends. Peter Mitchell worked out this idea in 1961, and he won the Nobel Prize in 1978. Your stomach cells use a similar pump to make stomach acid. Some scientists think the first living cells may have used a difference in pH at warm vents on the sea floor. That idea is not settled.
- e.g. Every breath you take feeds a hydrogen ion pump in each of your cells.
- e.g. The pump in your stomach wall, which makes acid, is the target of some heartburn medicines.
- e.g. Plants use a hydrogen ion difference in their leaves to turn sunlight into stored energy.
### Acids and bases in soil and in dye vats · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil and dye vats each have a pH, and the plants and microbes that live in them do best in a certain range.

Tea plants grow best in acidic soil, at about pH 4.5 to 5.5, and the hills of the Nilgiris and Assam have soil like that. Many vegetables do better in soil closer to pH 6 to 7. A farmer can spread lime on soil that is too acidic, and the lime acts as an antacid for the soil. The hydrangea flowers of Ooty and Kodaikanal are blue in acidic soil and pink in less acidic soil, because soil acidity changes how much aluminium the roots can take up. A traditional indigo dye vat is kept basic, at about pH 10 to 11, and the dye stops working if the vat turns sour. Dyers add lime to keep it right.
- e.g. Tea in the Nilgiris grows in acidic soil, at about pH 5.
- e.g. Blue hydrangeas grow where the soil is acidic. Pink ones grow where it is less acidic.
- e.g. A dyer adds lime to an indigo vat when its pH has fallen.

## Mostly agreed
- pH measures the number of free hydrogen ions in a watery liquid, and each step on the scale is a tenfold change. *How we know:* The scale was set out by Søren Sørensen in 1909. Instruments that count hydrogen ions agree with it, and pH meters read it to a hundredth of a step.
- Stomach acid is hydrochloric acid, with a pH of about 1 to 2 in an empty stomach. *How we know:* Doctors have measured the pH inside living stomachs with a thin probe passed down the food pipe, and the numbers are the same in many studies.
- The stomach protects itself with a mucus coat that traps bicarbonate, and with cells that are replaced every few days. *How we know:* Probes that measure pH layer by layer have shown a smooth change, from about pH 2 in the middle of the stomach to about pH 7 at the wall.
- Antacids are bases. They use up stomach acid, and the relief lasts only until the stomach makes more. *How we know:* Studies that measure stomach pH after an antacid show the pH rising within minutes and falling back within an hour or two.
- Most stomach ulcers are caused by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori or by some painkillers, and not by stress or spicy food. *How we know:* In 1984 Barry Marshall drank the bacterium and developed gastritis. Large trials later showed that killing the bacterium with antibiotics cured most ulcers for good.
- Blood is held in a very narrow range, between pH 7.35 and 7.45, whatever a person eats. *How we know:* Blood tests from large numbers of healthy people show this range. Breathing, the kidneys and chemical buffers keep it steady.

## How we found out
**Barry Marshall and Robin Warren, doctors in Perth, Australia, 1979 to 1984, and the Nobel Prize in 2005.** For many decades, doctors believed that no bacterium could live in the strong acid of the stomach. They also believed that ulcers, the sore patches in the stomach wall, came from stress, spicy food and too much acid. Patients were told to rest and take antacids. The ulcers kept coming back. In 1979, Robin Warren, a pathologist in Perth, Australia, noticed curved bacteria in stomach samples. In 1981 a young doctor, Barry Marshall, began working with him. In 1982 a dish of stomach samples was left in the warm cupboard over a long Easter weekend, and the slow-growing bacteria finally showed up. Many doctors still did not believe them. So in 1984, Marshall drank a dish of the bacteria. Within days he felt ill, and a camera tube showed that his stomach wall was inflamed. Antibiotics cured him, and later cured many patients. Marshall and Warren shared the Nobel Prize in 2005.  
*What people believed before:* Doctors believed that stomach acid kills all bacteria, so ulcers had to come from stress, spicy food and too much acid. Marshall and Warren showed that a bacterium lives in the stomach and causes most ulcers, and that antibiotics cure them.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Stomach ulcers are caused by stress, spicy food and too much acid.** (Most doctors until the 1980s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Ulcers do happen more in stressed people, and an ulcer hurts more after spicy food. Acid-blocking medicines did ease the pain.  
  Against: Marshall and Warren found a bacterium in the stomachs of people with ulcers. Marshall drank the bacterium and got sick. Antibiotics cured ulcers for good, while acid-blockers alone let them return.
- **A pH of 3 is only a little more acidic than a pH of 4.** (Many people who look at the numbers.; *rejected*)  
  For: The numbers are next to each other on the scale, and they look close.  
  Against: Each step on the pH scale is ten times. pH 3 holds ten times as many hydrogen ions as pH 4, and pH 2 holds a hundred times as many.
- **Cabbage water tells you the exact pH of a liquid.** (Some activity books.; *rejected*)  
  For: The colour does change in a steady way from red through purple to green.  
  Against: Cabbage colours change with the cabbage, the water and the light, and a colour can only separate pH values that are a few steps apart. A pH meter reads far more exactly.
- **It is harmless to take an antacid after every meal.** (Some advertisements for antacids.; *debated*)  
  For: Antacids are cheap and easy to buy, and an occasional tablet is fine for most adults.  
  Against: Heartburn that comes often can be a sign of another problem, and a doctor should look at it. Some antacids also cause stomach upset, and strong long-term acid-reducing medicines have risks that doctors are still studying.
- **No living being can survive in strong acid.** (Doctors before 1982, and many people today.; *rejected*)  
  For: Strong acid burns skin and kills most bacteria on contact.  
  Against: Helicobacter pylori lives in the stomach. Curd bacteria grow in acid at pH 4. Some microbes in volcanic hot springs grow at a pH close to 0.

## Roots
We have measured acids and bases with numbers and raced antacids against a glass of acid. Now we ask what this tells us about the body we live in and the world it is part of.

**Deep truth.** Your stomach holds a strong acid on purpose, and the pancreas pours out a base right after it. Your blood is kept almost exactly neutral. Acid is not bad and base is not good. A living body holds each place at the pH its job needs.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call acids harsh and bases gentle, and we hope for a neutral middle. But the body needs strong acid in the stomach and a base just after it. The right place on the scale depends on the job.  
*Evidence:* In the Antacid Showdown, each powder moved the same glass of acid along the scale. Too few scoops left it sour, and too many pushed it past purple. The pH Ladder cards show the stomach, the blood and the skin sitting at three different numbers. If your own results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* You are not one steady thing. Your stomach is acid and your blood is nearly neutral, and your body keeps both going every moment.
- *what is life:* Every living cell uses a difference in hydrogen ions like a battery. Life is held up by differences that are kept, and not by everything being the same.
- *what is this world:* Even pure water is a balance of two kinds of particles in equal numbers. Everything sour or soapy is that balance tipped one way or the other.

Pure water is a perfect balance. A very few water particles split into a hydrogen ion and a hydroxide ion, always in equal numbers, so the two cancel exactly. Acid and base are what we get when the balance tips. The stomach tips it one way, the blood holds it level, and the dye vat tips it the other way, all from the same water. In this picture, the one balance shows up as many different places, each with its own job. Nothing is the opposite of anything else. They are two sides of one balance.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.5.1:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu that food we eat divides into three. The coarsest part leaves the body, the middle part becomes flesh, and the finest part becomes the mind. (Digestion begins in the acid of the stomach. The acid and the enzymes cut food into pieces small enough to build a body. Dal and rice become you.)
- **Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II, chapter 6:** Aristotle taught that the right amount is a middle between too much and too little, and that the middle is different for different people. A trained wrestler needs more food than a beginner. (The right pH is also a middle that depends on the place. Pepsin needs pH 2, and blood needs pH 7.4. The same number would be wrong in the other place.)
- **Tao Te Ching 42:** All things carry the dark and embrace the light, and by blending their breath they reach harmony. (Acid and base are opposites, and pure water holds them in equal balance. The body uses both to stay in harmony.)
- Science (settled): Blood is held between pH 7.35 and 7.45 by the lungs, the kidneys and chemical buffers, even though the stomach beside it sits at pH 1 to 2.
- Science (idea): Strong stomach acid may have evolved because our ancestors often ate old meat, as a vulture does. Daniel Beasley and colleagues suggested this in 2015.
- Other view, Many chemists and doctors: The stomach pumps out hydrogen ions and a bacterium makes ammonia because of chemistry. They would say that chemistry alone holds each pH and makes the ammonia, and that words like keep and choose only describe automatic processes.
- Practice, Smell and wait (3 min): Sit with a banana, a piece of jaggery or a hot idli. Close your eyes and smell it for one slow breath. Do not eat yet. Notice whether your mouth fills with saliva. Your stomach is getting ready too, with acid and pepsin, before the first bite. Breathe out slowly. Then take one slow bite and chew it twenty times.
- Ask: Is the acid in your stomach part of you, or something you make and keep apart from you?
- Ask: If pure water holds acid and base in equal balance, what does it mean to call water neutral?
- Ask: Is a balance one steady place, or the work of always bringing things back?

## Story: The Vat That Was Sulking
*A made-up story. The village and the people are imagined. The science in it is real. A traditional indigo vat is kept basic, usually at about pH 10 to 11, and it stops working when it turns sour. Dyers differ in how they judge a vat and which ingredients they add with the lime. Avuri is the Tamil name for the indigo plant. Lime water is strongly basic and burns skin, so only a grown-up should handle it.*

Every summer, Vimala spent two weeks in a village in Tirunelveli district with her great-aunt, Valli Paati. Paati dyed cloth blue. Behind her house stood a low shed, and in the shed sat a clay vat as wide as a bullock-cart wheel, full of dark water that smelled of wet leaves.

The dye came from avuri, the indigo plant. Paati's family had soaked avuri leaves in that vat for as long as anyone remembered, with a little lime to keep the water right. Vimala loved what happened next. Paati dipped a white cotton towel into the dark water and counted to sixty. When she lifted it out, the towel dripped yellow-green, the colour of a lemon leaf. Then, in the open air, it turned blue before Vimala's eyes, a little more with every breath, until it was the blue of the sky just after sunset.

This summer, the magic did not come. Paati dipped a towel, counted to sixty, and lifted it out. It was a pale, streaky grey. She dipped it again, and again. It stayed grey.

Paati bent over the vat and sniffed. She shook her head. 'The vat is sulking,' she said. 'She has gone sour, and I do not know why.'

Her grandson Murali, who was twelve, had come for the day with a sack of lime on his shoulder. 'Easy,' he said. 'Lime fixes everything. Tip in the whole sack.'

'Too little and she sulks,' said Paati. 'Too much and she burns, and the tiny living beings in her stop their work. My mother knew the right amount by the feel of the water and a touch of it on her tongue. I know it by my nose. But my nose is old this year.' She looked at Vimala. 'And you must never taste a vat. Never.'

Vimala thought of the pencil box in her bag. Her Class 4 teacher, Kalpana Miss, had given every child a small roll of pH paper. Vimala unrolled a strip and rinsed a steel cup. 'May I try something, Paati?'

Paati nodded and ladled out a cup of the dark water. Vimala dipped a strip for one second, as Kalpana Miss had taught her. The tip came out stained, but the clean middle of the strip matched the number 8 on the chart.

Then she ran next door, where Selvi Akka's vat worked beautifully, and asked for a cup of her water. The strip matched 10.

'Two steps apart,' said Vimala. 'Every step on the chart is ten times. So Selvi Akka's vat has a hundred times more of the base particles than yours.'

'A hundred times!' said Murali. 'Then I was right. Tip in the sack!'

'No,' said Vimala. 'The chart does not say how much lime a big vat needs. And Paati said too much is wrong as well. We must test it in small cups first.'

Murali opened his mouth, and closed it again.

Vimala set five steel cups on the step, each holding the same ladle of Paati's vat water. Only Paati was allowed to touch the clear lime water. She added it with a spoon: none to the first cup, one spoon to the second, then two, then three, then four. Vimala stirred each cup, dipped a fresh strip, and wrote the number in her notebook. Eight. Nine. Nine. Ten. Eleven.

'Three spoons matches Selvi Akka,' said Vimala. 'Four is past it.'

'But four spoons is stronger,' said Murali. 'So it must be better.'

'Stronger is not better,' said Paati slowly. 'Past the right place, the little ones in her stop. By tomorrow the vat would be as dead as that grey cloth.' She cut a scrap from the towel and dipped it in the first cup. It stayed grey.

'Now try a scrap in the cup with three spoons,' said Vimala. The scrap came out yellow-green. In the air, it turned a deep, clean blue. Even Murali clapped.

A big vat holds hundreds of cups. Paati did not pour in hundreds of spoons at once. She added the lime water in four portions through the day, stirring with her long wooden paddle, and each time Vimala dipped a strip. By evening, the strip matched 10.

The next morning, Paati dipped a white towel, counted to sixty, and lifted it out. It dripped yellow-green. Murali and Vimala held their breath. In the air, it turned blue, and then a deeper blue. 'She is happy,' said Paati. She put the towel in Vimala's hands.

'Your strip told me what my mother's tongue told her,' Paati said. 'And my nose tells me this morning that the vat has stopped sulking. I will keep the nose and the strip both.'

'So lime fixes everything,' said Murali, still thinking.

'Lime fixed this vat, because this vat was too sour,' said Vimala. 'If it had been too strong already, more lime would have hurt it. It is not about more. It is about the right place.'

Murali looked at the roll of strips. 'Can I have one? I want to test the tea.'

Vimala tore him a strip. That evening, she dipped one into the glass of buttermilk Paati gave her. It matched 4. Even a glass of buttermilk, she thought, had its own place on the line.

**For the parent:** The vat did not need more lime. It needed to be in the right place on the pH scale.

- Ask: Why did Vimala test lime water in small cups before putting any in the big vat?
- Ask: The strip said the vat was two steps lower than Selvi Akka's. Why did that not mean the vat needed exactly twice as much lime?
- Ask: If a vat can be too sour and too strong, what does that tell you about the words 'more' and 'better'?

### g1 · The pH Ladder: where does it belong? (20 min)

## Experiments
### e1 · Antacid Showdown: which powder beats the acid with the fewest scoops? (42 min)
- **question:** Cooking soda, a crushed antacid tablet, eggshell powder and salt: how many scoops of each does it take to turn a glass of pretend stomach acid from pink to purple?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which powder will need the fewest scoops? Will the salt change the colour at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every glass gets the same 2 tablespoons of vinegar and 4 tablespoons of cabbage water, the same size of scoop and the same 20 seconds of stirring. Only the powder is different. Glass A has cooking soda, B has antacid powder, C has eggshell powder and D has salt. Salt is not a base, so glass D is the control. A plain purple glass T is the target to match.
- **safety:** Vinegar is mild, but keep it away from eyes. Do not taste anything in the glasses. Antacid tablets are medicine, so a grown-up crushes them and puts the rest back in the packet, out of reach of small children. Wash your hands afterwards and pour everything down the sink.
- **record:** Write the number of scoops each powder needed and what the colour did after two extra scoops. Then draw a bar for each powder.
- **results table:** Glass | Powder | Scoops to match the target | Fizz (none, little or lots) | Colour after 2 extra scoops
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the four powders in four bowls. Read out their names. Write down their guess and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* These are four powders. Cooking soda, crushed antacid tablet, eggshell powder and salt. Which will beat the acid? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Here are four powders. Which one beats the acid? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Which powder will need the fewest scoops, and why? (good: The antacid, because it is made for stomach acid.; Cooking soda, because it fizzed with lemon last year.; Not the salt, because it is not a base.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write the guesses at the top of the table.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the cabbage water and strain it. Fill glass T with 4 tablespoons of cabbage water and 2 tablespoons of plain water.  
  *Parent says:* This purple glass is our target. We want every acid glass to match it. Purple means the acid is used up.  
  *Child mode:* Make the cabbage water. Fill glass T. This purple is the target.
  - Note: If you use butterfly pea flowers, the target is blue-violet and the acid glasses are pink-purple. Match the target as best you can. Keep the glass T next to the others the whole time.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 2 tablespoons of vinegar and 4 tablespoons of cabbage water into each of glasses A, B, C and D. Stir.  
  *Parent says:* Each glass now holds the same pretend stomach acid. It is pink. Only the powder will be different.  
  *Child mode:* Put vinegar and cabbage water in glasses A to D. Stir. They turn pink.
  - Check: Why does every glass get exactly the same vinegar and cabbage water? → **So that only the powder is different, and any difference comes from the powder**
- **s4 (17 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Glass A gets cooking soda, B gets antacid powder, C gets eggshell powder and D gets salt. Add one level scoop to A and stir for 20 seconds. Look at the colour. Add another scoop. Stop when A matches glass T, or after 8 scoops. Do B, C and D the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Add one scoop, stir for 20 seconds, and look. Is it still pink? Add another. Keep counting until it matches the target. Write down the number of scoops.  
  *Child mode:* Add one scoop. Stir. Look at the colour. Add another. Count the scoops. Stop at purple.
  - Ask: What do you see on the top of glass A after the first scoop? (good: Bubbles.; Fizz.; The colour starts to change.)
  - Note: Eggshell powder reacts slowly, so stir it for a full minute. Antacid powder may stay cloudy, so judge the colour of the liquid. If a glass has not matched after 8 scoops, write 'more than 8'. Salt should do nothing at all.
  - Check: Salt is added to glass D and the colour stays pink. What does this show? → **The colour change in the other glasses comes from the powder being a base, and not just from stirring in a powder**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take the first glass that matched the target. Add two more scoops of the same powder. Look at the colour and write it in the last column.  
  *Parent says:* Now we go past the target. Add two more scoops. What colour is it now?  
  *Child mode:* Add two more scoops to the purple glass. What colour is it now?
  - Note: A glass that was purple often turns blue after two more scoops. That means the mixture is now a base, and not an acid. If nothing changes, write that down. Cooking soda and eggshell can only push a glass a little way.
  - Think: Is a base that is too strong better than one that is just right?
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar for each powder, showing its scoops. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why. Pour everything down the sink.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each powder. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer names the powder that needed the fewest scoops, says that salt did nothing, and says that extra scoops pushed the colour past purple. After they speak, explain: the powders that worked are bases. A base uses up acid until the mixture is neutral. Past that, more base makes the mixture basic. Scoop counts depend on how fine the powder is, so your numbers may differ from someone else's. That is also a result.
  - Think: A person takes two tablets for heartburn. Another takes ten. Why might ten be worse than two?
### e2 · The Ten-Times Ladder: how far can a colour be made weaker? (32 min)
- **question:** If you make a colour ten times weaker, again and again, after how many steps can you no longer see it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After how many cups will the colour be gone? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every cup gets exactly 9 teaspoons of plain water and exactly 1 teaspoon from the cup before. The spoon is rinsed between cups, and the same white paper stands behind every cup. Only the number of steps changes.
- **safety:** Food colour stains clothes and hands, so wear an old shirt and wipe up drips. Do not drink anything in the cups.
- **record:** Write a number from 5 to 0 for the colour in each cup. Then write the cup number where the colour was last easy to see, and work out how many times weaker it is than cup 0.
- **results table:** Cup | Parts of dye water in the cup | Colour you can see (5 is dark, 0 is none)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the seven cups in a row. Explain that each cup will hold ten times less colour than the one before. Write down their guess and reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will make a colour ten times weaker, again and again. In which cup will you stop seeing it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Each cup gets ten times less colour. In which cup will you stop seeing it? Why?
  - Ask: In which cup do you think the colour will disappear, and why? (good: Cup 3, because 3 steps is a lot.; Cup 6, because ten times weaker still leaves something.; Cup 2, because it will go quickly.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write it above the table.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 10 teaspoons of water into cup 0 and add 3 drops of food colour. Stir. Put 9 teaspoons of plain water into each of cups 1 to 6.  
  *Parent says:* Cup 0 gets the colour. Cups 1 to 6 get nine teaspoons of plain water each. Count each spoon with me.  
  *Child mode:* Cup 0 gets colour. Put nine spoons of plain water in cups 1 to 6.
  - Note: Count the spoons out loud. Use level teaspoons and keep the number the same each time. If you have no teaspoon, use a small medicine cap and keep that size for every step.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take 1 teaspoon from cup 0 and stir it into cup 1. Rinse the spoon. Take 1 teaspoon from cup 1 and stir it into cup 2. Carry on to cup 6.  
  *Parent says:* One spoon from cup 0 goes into cup 1. Stir. Rinse. One spoon from cup 1 goes into cup 2. Keep going to cup 6.  
  *Child mode:* Move one spoon from cup 0 to cup 1. Stir. Rinse. Carry on to cup 6.
  - Check: Cup 1 holds 1 teaspoon of colour water and 9 teaspoons of plain water. How does cup 1 compare with cup 0? → **Its colour is 10 times weaker**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold white paper behind the row. Give each cup a number from 5 to 0 for its colour. Mark the last cup where you can still see colour.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the cups against the white paper. Give each one a number. Five is dark. Zero is no colour. Where do you stop seeing it?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the row. Give each cup a number from 5 to 0. Where does the colour stop?
  - Ask: What do you notice about how the colour changes from cup to cup? (good: It gets paler each time.; The first steps change the most.; Cup 6 looks like plain water.)
  - Check: Cup 5 looks like plain water. Is the dye gone? → **No. It is only too little to see, about 1 part in 100,000**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the steps from cup 0 to the last cup where you saw colour. Work out how many times weaker that cup is than cup 0. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Count the steps to the last cup you can see. How many times weaker is it?
  - Note: If the colour was last visible in cup 4, that is 4 steps, so 10 x 10 x 10 x 10 = 10,000 times weaker. After they speak, explain: pH works like this. Each step is ten times. Lemon juice to water is 5 steps, so 100,000 times. The dye is a stand-in for hydrogen ions. Your cups may fade sooner or later, depending on the dye. That is also a result.
  - Think: Lemon juice is pH 2 and pure water is pH 7. How many cups of this ladder would that be?

9. (mcq) Blood stays near pH 7.4 while the stomach is at pH 2. What does this show?  
   Options: Different parts of the body are held at different pH for their jobs / The body made a mistake / Blood is more acidic than the stomach  
   **Answer:** Different parts of the body are held at different pH for their jobs. *Why:* Each part of the body needs its own pH. The stomach's acid does a digesting job, and the blood's narrow range keeps cells working.
10. (mcq) Doctors believed no bacterium could live in stomach acid. Which finding showed they were wrong?  
   Options: Helicobacter pylori was found living in the stomach, and antibiotics cured ulcers / Antacids cured every ulcer / Taking away spicy food made every ulcer vanish  
   **Answer:** Helicobacter pylori was found living in the stomach, and antibiotics cured ulcers. *Why:* A bacterium was found living in the stomach, and killing it with antibiotics cured ulcers for good. Antacids and plain food changes did not.
11. (truefalse) A change from pH 6 to pH 5 means ten times as many hydrogen ions.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. One step down the scale is ten times as many hydrogen ions. A small change in the number is a large change in the liquid.
12. (fill) An antacid is a ____ (acid or base).  
   **Answer:** ['base']. *Why:* An antacid is a base. It reacts with stomach acid and uses some of it up.

## Applications
- Doctors use antacids to give quick relief from heartburn, and use antibiotics to cure ulcers caused by Helicobacter pylori.
- Farmers test soil with pH paper and spread lime on soil that is too acidic.
- Swimming pool workers test the water's pH and keep it near neutral, so that eyes do not sting.
- Bakers and cooks use cooking soda, a base, to cancel the sourness in batters.
- Dyers keep indigo vats at the right pH so that the dye works.
- Toothpaste holds a base that helps to cancel the acid made by bacteria on teeth.
- Doctors test blood and urine pH to find out whether the lungs and kidneys are working.
- Scientists check the pH of rivers and the sea to see whether the water is changing.

## Open questions
- **Why do most people who carry Helicobacter pylori never get an ulcer?** About half of all people carry the bacterium, but only one in ten or fewer gets an ulcer. Scientists are comparing the bacterium's strains, people's genes and diets, and have no full answer.
- **Why are human stomachs as acidic as a vulture's?** One idea says our ancestors ate old meat. Others say the acid helps digestion or guards against the many microbes on all food. No one has found a way to test the ideas on ancestors who lived long ago.
- **How safe is it to take strong acid-reducing medicines for many years?** These medicines help many people. Some studies link long use to other problems, such as weaker bones, but it is hard to tell whether the medicine or the illness caused the problem.
- **Did the first living cells begin where a difference in pH already existed, such as at warm vents on the sea floor?** Some scientists think a pH difference across thin rock walls could have powered the first cells. Others think life began in other places. The vents have been studied, but no one has made life from them.
- **How exactly does the gut keep its acid and base rooms in the right places?** Scientists know the pumps, hormones and nerves that control the stomach and the pancreas, but they are still working out how the whole system is tuned, and why it fails in some people.

## Connected maths
- Lemon juice has a pH of 2 and a drink has a pH of 5. How many times as many hydrogen ions does the lemon juice have? **1000 times.**. From pH 5 down to pH 2 is three steps. Each step is ten times, so 10 times 10 times 10 is 1,000. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- In a dye ladder, each cup holds ten times less colour than the one before. The colour in cup 6 is how many times weaker than in cup 0? **1,000,000 times.**. Six steps of ten is 10 times 10 times 10 times 10 times 10 times 10. That is one million. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- Ritu, a girl in Class 4 in Pune, needs 3 scoops of cooking soda for a glass of acid. A strip of 10 antacid tablets costs 30 rupees, and she needs 4 tablets for the same glass. A scoop of cooking soda costs about 20 paise. Which is cheaper for the same glass, and by how many times? (These prices are made up.) **Cooking soda, about 20 times cheaper.**. 3 scoops of soda cost 3 times 20 paise, which is 60 paise. 4 tablets cost 4 times 3 rupees, which is 12 rupees, or 1,200 paise. 1,200 divided by 60 is 20. The tablets are made to be safe and handy, which is part of what you pay for. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Mira makes curd from warm milk. The pH is 6.6 at the start, 6.0 after 2 hours, 5.2 after 4 hours, 4.6 after 6 hours and 4.4 after 8 hours. In which 2-hour stretch did the pH fall the most? What does it say about the bacteria? (These numbers are made up from typical results.) **Between 2 and 4 hours, when it fell by 0.8. The falls were 0.6, 0.8, 0.6 and 0.2.**. The falls are 6.6 minus 6.0 is 0.6, 6.0 minus 5.2 is 0.8, 5.2 minus 4.6 is 0.6 and 4.6 minus 4.4 is 0.2. The bacteria make acid, and as the acid builds up the bacteria slow down, so the fall gets smaller at the end. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- Suppose the cells of the stomach lining are replaced about every 4 days. About how many times are they replaced in a year of 365 days? **About 90 times.**. 365 is close to 360. 360 divided by 4 is 90. So the lining is made new about 90 times a year. A good estimate is enough here. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- Put these liquids in order, from the most acidic to the least acidic: curd 4.4, tomato juice 4.2, coffee 5.0, lemon juice 2.2, milk 6.6. **Lemon juice 2.2, tomato juice 4.2, curd 4.4, coffee 5.0, milk 6.6.**. The lowest pH is the most acidic. 2.2 is the smallest, then 4.2, then 4.4, then 5.0, and 6.6 is the largest. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
- A dye ladder has 10,000 parts of colour in cup 0. Each cup after it holds a tenth of the one before. Write the number of parts in cups 0 to 4. In which cup does it first fall below 1 part? **10,000, 1,000, 100, 10 and 1. It falls below 1 part in cup 5.**. Dividing by 10 each time gives 10,000, then 1,000, then 100, then 10, then 1 in cup 4. In cup 5 there is only one tenth of a part. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
