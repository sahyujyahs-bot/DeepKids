# Solids, liquids and gases: why a gas has mass and fills any container
`sci.states.solids-liquids-gases.L5`

**Summary.** A full cooking gas cylinder is 14.2 kilograms heavier than an empty one. Yet the gas that comes out of it cannot be seen. A gas is a real substance. It takes up space, it has mass, and it spreads out to fill whatever holds it. Air is a mixture of gases, mostly nitrogen and oxygen. Your child makes carbon dioxide and hangs a balloon of it beside a balloon of air. One gas turns out to be heavier than the other. They also compare solids, liquids and gases using particles, and count the dust that floats in the air at home.

**Intro.** *A full cooking gas cylinder is 14.2 kilograms heavier than an empty one. You cannot see the gas that burns at the stove. So how can something you cannot see have so much mass?* The gas is a real substance, made of particles, just as water and steel are. Its particles are far apart and fly about freely, so we see straight through it. In the cylinder the gas is squeezed until its particles touch, and it becomes a liquid. Air is a gas too, and air has mass. A bedroom holds about 40 kilograms of it. In this unit you catch a gas, pour it, make a new one, and hang two gases on a balance. You also find out why air seems to weigh nothing at all.

## Terms
- **State of matter**: One of the ways a substance can be: solid, liquid or gas. The particles are the same in each state. Only their spacing and movement change. *e.g.* Ice, water and water vapour are three states of the same substance.
- **Solid**: A state in which particles touch and are held in place. A solid keeps its own shape and its own volume. *e.g.* A steel spoon stays the same shape in a tumbler, a bowl or your hand.
- **Liquid**: A state in which particles touch but slide past each other. A liquid keeps its volume but takes the shape of its container. *e.g.* One tumbler of buttermilk is still one tumbler when you pour it into a wide bowl.
- **Gas**: A state in which particles are far apart and fly about freely. A gas has no shape or volume of its own. It spreads out to fill its container. *e.g.* The air in a room and the carbon dioxide in a fizzy drink are gases. So is the steam just above a kettle spout.
- **Mass**: How much matter a thing is made of. We measure mass in grams and kilograms. *e.g.* One litre of water has a mass of 1 kilogram. One litre of air has a mass of about 1.2 grams.
- **Volume**: How much space a thing takes up. We measure volume in millilitres and litres. *e.g.* A deep breath of a ten-year-old child takes up about one and a half litres.
- **Particle model**: The idea that every solid, liquid and gas is made of tiny moving particles. How close they are and how they move explains how each state behaves. *e.g.* The model explains why you can squash air in a capped bottle but cannot squash water.
- **Compress**: To squash something into a smaller space. Gases can be compressed, because their particles have empty space between them. *e.g.* A cycle pump compresses air into a tyre until the tyre feels hard.
- **Diffusion**: The slow spreading of particles of one substance among the particles of another. It happens because particles move all the time. *e.g.* The smell of jasmine in one corner slowly reaches every part of a closed room.
- **Mixture**: Two or more substances together, each keeping its own particles. *e.g.* Air is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen and small amounts of other gases.
- **Nitrogen**: The gas that makes up about 78 of every 100 particles of air. It has no colour or smell, and our bodies breathe it in and out unchanged. *e.g.* Packets of chips are filled with nitrogen, so the chips stay crisp.
- **Oxygen**: The gas that makes up about 21 of every 100 particles of air. Humans and other animals need it to get energy from food, and fires need it to burn. *e.g.* Hospitals keep oxygen squeezed into strong steel cylinders.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas made when things burn, when living beings breathe out, and when vinegar meets baking soda. It is about one and a half times as heavy as air. *e.g.* The bubbles in soda water are carbon dioxide.
- **Water vapour**: Water as a gas. It is invisible, and the amount in the air changes from day to day. *e.g.* On a humid monsoon day in Kochi, up to 4 of every 100 particles of air are water vapour.
- **Atmosphere**: The layer of air around the Earth. It is thickest near the ground and thins out slowly, with no sharp top. *e.g.* Half of all the air lies below about five and a half kilometres, lower than many Himalayan peaks.
- **Density**: How much mass is packed into a certain space. A dense gas is heavy for its size. *e.g.* Carbon dioxide is denser than air, so a balloon of it falls faster than a balloon of air.
- **Upthrust**: The upward push of a liquid or a gas on anything inside it. The push equals the weight of the liquid or gas that the thing pushes aside. *e.g.* The upthrust of air holds up a helium balloon, as the upthrust of water holds up a boat.
- **Air pollution**: Harmful gases and tiny solid or liquid bits that people add to the air. *e.g.* Smoke from traffic, burning rubbish and wood fires pollutes the air of many Indian cities.
- **PM2.5**: Tiny solid and liquid bits floating in air, each smaller than 2.5 thousandths of a millimetre. They are small enough to go deep into the lungs. *e.g.* A bit of PM2.5 is about thirty times thinner than a human hair.
- **Air Quality Index (AQI)**: A number that tells how polluted the air is. In India it runs from 0 to 500. Up to 50 is good, and over 300 is very poor. *e.g.* A weather app may show an AQI of 60 in Thiruvananthapuram and 350 in Delhi on the same winter day.

## Explanations
### A gas is a substance: it takes up space and has mass · *defined*
**Definition:** A gas is matter, just like a solid or a liquid. It is made of particles, it takes up space, and it has mass.

We cannot see most gases, so they are easy to mistake for nothing. Two tests show that a gas is something. First, a gas takes up space. Push a tumbler mouth-down into water, and the air inside keeps the water out. Second, a gas has mass. One litre of air has a mass of about 1.2 grams. That is small, but air adds up. Take a bedroom 4 metres long, 3 metres wide and 3 metres high. The air in it has a mass of about 43 kilograms. When vinegar and baking soda fizz in an open tumbler, the tumbler gets lighter. The lost grams left as carbon dioxide gas. Cooking gas is even sold by mass. A home cylinder holds 14.2 kilograms of it.
- e.g. In Pour Air Upwards, air moved from one tumbler to another under water. It pushed the water out of the second tumbler.
- e.g. In Weigh the Fizz, the open tumbler lost about 3 grams as the gas left. The closed bottle lost nothing until its cap was opened.
- e.g. An auto-rickshaw driver in Bengaluru buys compressed natural gas by the kilogram.
### The three states compared · *defined*
**Definition:** A solid keeps its shape and its volume. A liquid keeps its volume but takes the shape of its container. A gas keeps neither: it fills all the space it is given.

All three states are made of particles. In a solid the particles touch and are held in place, so they only shake. In a liquid the particles still touch, but they slide past each other, so a liquid flows. In a gas the particles are far apart and fly freely. In the air around you, each particle is about ten of its own widths from the next. That is why a gas can be compressed and the other two cannot. Solids, liquids and gases all have mass. Liquids and gases both flow, so scientists call them both fluids. When water boils, its particles do not grow. They move about 12 widths apart. So the steam takes about 1,700 times as much space as the water did.
- e.g. Shape: a steel spoon keeps its shape. Water takes the shape of the tumbler. Air takes the shape of the whole room.
- e.g. Volume: a litre of milk stays a litre in any vessel. A litre of air let out of a balloon spreads through the room.
- e.g. Squashing: in The Compare Table, the capped bottle of air squeezed in, and the capped bottle of water did not.
- e.g. One teaspoon of water, 5 millilitres, boils into about 8.5 litres of steam.
### Why a gas fills its container · *defined*
**Definition:** Gas particles fly in straight lines until they hit something, and nothing holds them together. So they keep moving until they are spread through all the space they can reach.

Nothing pushes a gas into the corners of a room. Each particle only flies, hits another particle or a wall, and bounces off in a new direction. With so many particles moving by chance, the spread-out arrangement is far more likely than any other. In the Bean Gas game, six beans moved by the roll of a die. They spread over the whole board and stayed spread. The chance of all six being back in the starting corner at once is 1 in 4,096. The number of particles in a room is 27 digits long, so its air never gathers in one corner. Gas particles in air fly at about 500 metres a second. Yet a smell takes a minute or more to cross a still room. Each smell particle hits air particles billions of times a second, so its path zigzags. In a real room, slow air currents carry smells much faster than diffusion alone.
- e.g. In Smoke in Two Jars, the smoke from one jar spread into both jars and looked thinner in each.
- e.g. In Still Air or Fan, the smell reached the far chair much sooner with the fan on. Moving air carried it.
- e.g. Open a bottle of perfume in Mysuru, and by evening some of its particles are streets away.
### Compressing a gas, and turning it into a liquid · *defined*
**Definition:** A gas can be compressed because there is empty space between its particles. If a gas is compressed and cooled enough, its particles touch, and it becomes a liquid.

Squeeze a capped bottle of air, and its sides go in. The particles have moved closer together. Squeeze a capped bottle full of water, and nothing moves, because its particles already touch. The space between gas particles is truly empty. It is not filled with air, because air is the particles. Cooking gas, called LPG, is compressed until it becomes a liquid. That is how 14.2 kilograms fit in one home cylinder. Let out as a gas at room pressure, it would fill about 7,000 litres. That is as much as 350 buckets of 20 litres. The natural gas in buses and autos, called CNG, is squeezed to about 200 times the pressure of the air. It stays a gas. It would become a liquid only if it were also made very cold.
- e.g. Rock a full LPG cylinder gently, and you can feel liquid slosh inside. An empty one does not slosh.
- e.g. A clear plastic lighter shows liquid butane inside. The butane leaves the nozzle as a gas.
- e.g. A cycle tyre holds air squeezed to between three and five times the pressure of the air outside.
### One gas can be heavier than another · *defined*
**Definition:** At the same temperature and pressure, a litre of any gas holds about the same number of particles. Gases with heavier particles are therefore heavier, litre for litre.

A particle of carbon dioxide has about one and a half times the mass of an average particle of air. So a litre of carbon dioxide has a mass of about 1.8 grams. A litre of air has about 1.2 grams. That is why the carbon dioxide balloon sank lower in Two Balloons on a Hanger. Helium particles are very light. A litre of helium has only about one-seventh of the mass of a litre of air. So a helium balloon rises. A heavy gas can lie at the bottom of a pit or a jug for a while. It sits there like water in a bowl. But its particles keep moving, so in time it mixes with the air above. Leaked LPG is heavier than air and collects near the floor. Piped natural gas is lighter than air and collects near the ceiling.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide: about 1.8 grams a litre. Air: about 1.2 grams a litre. Helium: about 0.17 grams a litre.
- e.g. A balloon blown up by mouth falls slowly. A balloon filled with carbon dioxide falls faster.
- e.g. Weather balloons sent up every day from Chennai and Thiruvananthapuram are filled with hydrogen, the lightest gas.
### Why air seems to weigh nothing · *defined*
**Definition:** Anything in air is pushed up by the air around it. The push, called upthrust, equals the weight of the air that the thing pushes aside.

A bag of water held under water feels as if it weighs almost nothing. The water around it holds it up. A balloon of air in a room is the same. The air inside it has a mass of 3 grams or so. But the air around it pushes up with nearly the same force. So a balance in a room shows almost none of it. A balance in air shows only how much heavier a thing is than the air it pushes aside. That is why the carbon dioxide balloon showed only its extra mass, about 1 to 2 grams. To weigh air itself, people squeeze extra air into a closed container, or pump the air out of one. The air's weight on us also goes unnoticed, because air pushes on us equally from every side.
- e.g. In Two Balloons on a Hanger, the air balloon barely moved its end. Yet it held about 3 grams of air.
- e.g. A football has more mass after pumping, because extra air has been squeezed into the same space.
- e.g. About 10 tonnes of air rest on every square metre of the ground at sea level.
### What air is made of · *defined*
**Definition:** Dry air is a mixture of gases. In every 100 particles, about 78 are nitrogen, about 21 are oxygen and about 1 is argon. Carbon dioxide is about 4 particles in every 10,000.

Air also holds water vapour. Desert air has almost none. On a humid day, about 4 in every 100 particles are water vapour. It carries dust, smoke, pollen and spores as well, but these are tiny solids and liquids floating in the gas. Winds stir the atmosphere all the time, and gas particles spread among each other. So the mix of nitrogen and oxygen is almost the same in Chennai, in Shimla and over the sea. What changes with height is how much air there is. At the top of Mount Everest, each breath holds about one-third as many particles as at the seashore. The oxygen in the air was made by living beings. Tiny water-living bacteria began making oxygen more than two thousand million years ago. Plants and algae keep making oxygen now.
- e.g. In 100 Grains of Air, 78 grains of rice stood for nitrogen and 21 grains of dal for oxygen. One peppercorn stood for argon.
- e.g. We breathe in air with about 21 parts of oxygen in 100, and breathe out air with about 16.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide is rare in air, yet plants build their bodies from this gas.
### Air pollution: what we add to the air · *defined*
**Definition:** Air pollution is harmful gases and tiny solid or liquid bits that people add to the air. The most harmful bits are the smallest, called PM2.5.

Engines, power stations, factories, burning rubbish, burning crop stubble, firecrackers and building sites all add to it. Indoors, smoke from cooking on wood or dung, mosquito coils and agarbatti add to it too. Because a gas spreads to fill the space it reaches, smoke does not stay where it is made. Large specks of dust are caught by the hairs and wet lining of the nose. PM2.5 is small enough to pass deep into the lungs and even into the blood. Breathing it for years raises the risk of lung and heart disease. In India the Air Quality Index gives one number for the day's air, from 0 to 500. Wind and rain clear the air, and still, cold winter air traps pollution near the ground.
- e.g. In Five Sticky Squares, the square by the road or the stove usually catches the most specks.
- e.g. In many villages, the women who cook on wood fires breathe the most smoke in the family. So do the small children near them.
- e.g. After a heavy monsoon shower in Mumbai, the AQI often falls, because rain washes dust out of the air.
### How many states of matter are there? · *debated*
School books name three states, and three are enough for most things at home. But some things do not fit neatly. Glass is hard like a solid, yet its particles are jumbled like those of a liquid. Toothpaste keeps its shape until you squeeze it, and then it flows. A flame and a lightning flash are plasma, a gas so hot that its particles have broken into charged pieces. Scientists do not agree on one count.
- e.g. Plasma: lightning, the Sun, and the glow inside a tube light.
- e.g. Glass: a solid whose particles never lined up in rows.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide above 31 degrees Celsius and squeezed 73 times is neither a liquid nor a gas. Factories use it to take the caffeine out of coffee.
- **Most school science books:** There are three states: solid, liquid and gas. They explain nearly everything a child meets, and plasma can be added as a fourth.
- **Many physicists:** There are many more states. Plasma, liquid crystals in phone screens and several very cold states each follow their own rules. So each counts as a state.
- **Some materials scientists:** The states are boxes that people drew. Glass, gels, foams and pastes sit between the boxes, so asking for one exact number is not useful.

## Mostly agreed
- Gases have mass. *How we know:* A closed container gains mass when more gas is pumped in and loses mass when gas is let out. A litre of air at sea level has a mass of about 1.2 grams.
- Dry air is about 78 parts nitrogen, 21 parts oxygen and 1 part argon in every 100. *How we know:* Chemists have separated air into its gases and measured each one since the 1770s. Argon was found in 1894, when nitrogen taken from air turned out to be slightly too heavy.
- Gas particles are far apart and move fast in all directions. *How we know:* This picture gives the correct sizes for gas pressure, for how gases compress and for how fast they spread. Liquid air takes about 700 times less space than the same air as a gas.
- Different gases have different densities. *How we know:* Equal volumes of hydrogen, helium, air and carbon dioxide have been weighed many times. Each gives its own mass, always in the same order.
- Tiny bits in polluted air harm the lungs and the heart. *How we know:* Studies have followed many thousands of people for years, in many countries. They find more lung and heart disease where PM2.5 is higher.
- The amount of carbon dioxide in the air is rising. *How we know:* It has been measured without a break since 1958 at Mauna Loa in Hawaii. It has risen from about 315 to more than 420 particles in every million.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei in Italy, Jan Baptist van Helmont in Brussels, Joseph Black in Scotland, Henry Cavendish, Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay in England, Galileo's weighing was printed in 1638 and van Helmont's book in 1648. Black worked in the 1750s and Cavendish in 1766. Argon was found in 1894..** For about two thousand years, most scholars in Europe and West Asia held that air was one simple thing. They called it one of the four elements, with earth, water and fire. In 1638 Galileo Galilei pumped extra air into a glass flask and weighed it. The flask was heavier, so air has weight. Around the same time, Jan Baptist van Helmont, a doctor in Brussels, burned 62 pounds of charcoal. Only 1 pound of ash was left. He said the rest had left as something like air, and he gave it a new name, gas. In the 1750s Joseph Black in Scotland heated a white powder. It lost more than half its mass as a gas. Flames went out in this gas, so it was not ordinary air. In 1766 Henry Cavendish weighed another gas and found it many times lighter than air. In 1894 Lord Rayleigh weighed nitrogen taken from air and nitrogen made from chemicals. The nitrogen from air was heavier, by 1 part in 200. He and William Ramsay hunted for the reason. They found a gas nobody had noticed, argon.  
*What people believed before:* That air is one simple element, and that every kind of 'air' is the same thing. Black showed that the gas from his powder put out flames. Cavendish and Rayleigh showed, by weighing, that different gases have different masses.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Air is nothing, so an empty tumbler is empty.** (A common belief, and what our eyes tell us; *rejected*)  
  For: Air has no colour, smell or taste. A hand moves through it easily, and a tumbler of air pours out nothing we can see.  
  Against: Air keeps water out of an upturned tumbler. It can be poured upwards under water, squeezed, and weighed when extra air is pumped into a ball.
- **All gases are light, so every gas rises.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Steam, smoke from a fire and helium balloons all go up. So it seems that going up is what gases do.  
  Against: Smoke rises because it is hot, and helium because its particles are light. Cold carbon dioxide and leaked LPG are heavier than air and sink to the floor.
- **Carbon dioxide in the air traps the Sun's heat, so more of it would warm the Earth.** (Eunice Newton Foote, an American scientist, in 1856; *accepted*)  
  For: She filled glass cylinders with different gases and stood them in sunshine. The cylinder of carbon dioxide grew hottest and stayed warm longest.  
  Against: Her paper was read out by a man at a science meeting and then forgotten for about 150 years. Later measurements by many scientists showed she was right.
- **Glass is a very slow liquid, which is why old window panes are thicker at the bottom.** (A popular belief, repeated in many books; *rejected*)  
  For: The particles of glass are jumbled like those of a liquid. Some very old window panes really are thicker at the bottom.  
  Against: In 1998 the physicist Edgar Zanotto did the sums. Window glass would need far longer than the age of the universe to flow that much. Old panes were uneven when they were made.
- **Space begins 100 kilometres above the ground.** (The world body that keeps flight records; *debated*)  
  For: Above about 100 kilometres the air is too thin for an aeroplane's wings to hold it up. A line is useful for records and for law.  
  Against: The air has no top edge. It thins out slowly for hundreds of kilometres. Some scientists and the United States use 80 kilometres instead.

## Roots
We have learned that a gas is a substance with mass, and that it fills its container. We have also learned that air is a mixture of gases. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In Two Balloons on a Hanger, a balloon full of air hardly moved the hanger. Yet it held about 3 grams of air. The air around it pushed it up by almost as much. So a balance in a room shows only how much heavier a thing is than the air around it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'as light as air', and we call a tumbler of air empty. We do so because air shows no weight in our hands. Air seems weightless only because we weigh everything while standing inside air. The weight is there, and the surrounding air hides it.  
*Evidence:* In Two Balloons on a Hanger, the air balloon against a flat balloon left the hanger almost level. The carbon dioxide balloon showed only its extra mass, 1 to 2 grams more than the air balloon. In Weigh the Fizz, a closed bottle kept its mass until the gas was let out. A bag of water held under water shows the same thing: the water around it holds it up.

- *who am i:* You take in about 6 litres of air every minute, and about 10 kilograms of it in a day. That is more than three times the mass of all your food and drink.
- *what is life:* A tree builds their trunk mostly from a gas. The carbon in wood came out of the carbon dioxide in the air, one particle at a time.
- *what is this world:* The space between things is full of gas. About 10 tonnes of air rest on every square metre of the ground. We live at the bottom of it.

Ice, water and steam look like three different things, and they are one kind of particle moving in three ways. Air looks like nothing, and it is several gases mixed evenly around the whole Earth. The same particles of oxygen and carbon dioxide pass through leaves, lungs, fires and seas, again and again. A gas spreads into all the space it can reach. So no room and no country has air of its own. One layer of air is shared by every being that breathes.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 4.3.1 to 4.3.2, the teaching of Raikva:** Raikva calls air the one that takes things in. When a fire goes out, it goes into the air. When water dries up, it goes into the air. (This matches what the science finds for these two cases. Water that dries up does go into the air, as water vapour. What a fire makes also goes into the air, as carbon dioxide and water vapour.)
- **Anaximenes of Miletus, about 550 BCE, as reported by later Greek writers:** Anaximenes taught that everything is made of air. Thinned out, air becomes fire. Packed closer, it becomes wind, then cloud, then water, then earth and stone. (He was wrong that everything is air. But he saw that one substance can be thin or thick, flowing or hard. It depends on how closely it is packed. That is close to how particles explain gas, liquid and solid.)
- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.7.2, Yajnavalkya answering Uddalaka:** Yajnavalkya says that air is the thread. By air, as by a thread, this world and all beings are strung together. (The science does not prove this teaching. It does show one plain thing: every breathing being takes from, and gives to, the same mixed air.)
- Science (settled): Gases have mass. The atmosphere presses on the ground with the weight of about 10 tonnes on each square metre.
- Science (settled): Living beings made the oxygen in the air. The carbon in living bodies came from the carbon dioxide in the air.
- Science (debated): How many states of matter there are depends on how they are counted. Scientists do not agree on one number.
- Other view, Materialists, such as the Charvaka school of ancient India and many scientists today: Only matter is real, and air is matter moving by chance. It joins beings together no more than a road joins the people who walk on it. Sharing air is a fact about gases, and it carries no deeper meaning.
- Practice, Feel one breath arrive and leave (3 min): Let's sit still and hold one hand just below the nose. Breathe in slowly. Feel the cool air come in. Breathe out slowly. Feel the warm air on your hand. Each breath is about a third of a litre of gas. Now let's take six slow breaths. With each one, notice where the air goes after it leaves you.
- Ask: The air you breathed out a minute ago is spreading through this room. When does it stop being your breath?
- Ask: A fish lives inside water all the time. We live inside air all the time. What else might we be living inside without noticing?
- Ask: Air belongs to nobody, and everybody needs it. Who should look after it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A balloon of air on a balance in a room shows almost no weight. So how can we be sure that air has weight? (hint: Think about what changes when extra air is squeezed into a football.)
- *deep:* Nobody tells the particles of a gas where to go. Why do they always end up spread through the whole room? (hint: Think about the Bean Gas game. How many ways are there to be spread out, and how many to be in one corner?)
- *deep:* Ice, water and steam are made of the same particles. Is 'solid' what a thing is, or what its particles are doing just now? (hint: What would you have to change to turn one into another?)
- *big:* You breathe out carbon dioxide, and a tulsi plant takes that gas in. The plant gives out oxygen, and you breathe that oxygen in. Whose air is it? (hint: Follow one particle of carbon dioxide for a day. Where could it go?)
- *big:* Smoke made in one street spreads to the next street, and to the next town. Who should decide what may be put into the air? (hint: Think about who makes the smoke and who breathes it. Are they the same people?)
- *big:* The air has no sharp top edge. So where does the Earth end? (hint: Is the air part of the Earth? Is a satellite inside the Earth's air or outside it?)

## Story: The Light Cylinder
*Thenmozhi, Kathir, Selvi athai, Fathima akka, the delivery man and the idli stall are made up. These things are real. A home cooking gas cylinder in India holds 14.2 kilograms of gas. The mass of the empty cylinder is painted on it, and differs a little from one cylinder to the next. The gas is a liquid inside and sloshes when a full cylinder is rocked. A strong smell is added to the gas so that leaks can be noticed. A customer may ask for a cylinder to be weighed when it is delivered. Children should never move or open a gas cylinder.*

Selvi athai's idli stall stood at the corner of a lane near the Meenakshi temple in Madurai. Every morning before six she lit two big burners under two big steamers. In the school holidays her niece Thenmozhi, who was ten, came to wrap parcels in banana leaf. Thenmozhi's brother Kathir, who was seven, came to eat.

On Thursday the flame under the second steamer shrank to a thin blue ring and went out. Athai stared at it. "Finished? I joined this cylinder on Saturday. A cylinder lasts me twelve days. This one has lasted five."

She shut the knob, took off the regulator and rocked the red cylinder on its base. Nothing moved inside it. "Empty," she said, and rolled the spare cylinder into its place.

"Maybe it leaked," said Kathir.

Athai shook her head. "A leak stinks. They add a smell to the gas so that we can tell. I smelt nothing all week."

"Then maybe they gave you less," said Thenmozhi.

Kathir laughed. "Less gas? How would anyone know? Gas is like air. You can't count air."

Thenmozhi did not answer. She was rocking the spare cylinder, the way Athai had rocked the old one. Something heavy swung inside and slapped the steel, like water in a pot carried on the hip. She rocked the old one. Nothing. "Athai, there is a liquid in the full one."

"Of course there is. They squeeze the gas so hard that it turns into a liquid. It turns back into gas on its way out of the pipe."

"So when the liquid is gone, the cylinder is empty."

"When the liquid is gone, my idlis stop," said Athai, and she went back to her customers.

Thenmozhi crouched beside the old cylinder. Inside the ring at its top, someone had painted a number in white: 15.8. On the spare one the paint said 16.1. "Kathir, why would they paint a number on each cylinder? And why are the two numbers different?"

"Roll numbers," said Kathir, with his mouth full.

"Roll numbers don't have points in them." She looked at the bill clipped to the wall. Net weight: 14.2 kg. She read it twice. "Kathir. They sell the gas by the kilo. Like rice."

"You can't weigh gas."

"They do. Fourteen point two kilos. So the number on the ring must be the cylinder alone, with nothing in it. No two cylinders are made exactly alike, so each has its own number." She stood up. "Then we can find out what is inside this spare one without opening it."

Fathima akka's rice shop next door had a platform scale for sacks. Akka laughed at the idea, but she helped them roll the old cylinder onto the scale first. The red numbers settled at 15.9.

"Wrong," said Kathir. "The paint says fifteen point eight."

Thenmozhi frowned at the 15.9 for a long moment. "It isn't wrong. The liquid is gone, but the cylinder is still full of gas. It is gas that isn't squeezed any more. It fills the whole inside, and it weighs a hundred grams."

"A hundred grams of nothing," said Kathir, but he said it quietly.

Then they rolled the spare cylinder on. Athai had come to the door, wiping her hands on her sari. Thenmozhi did the sum aloud before she looked. "Sixteen point one for the steel. Fourteen point two for the gas. It should say thirty point three."

The scale said 25.1.

Nobody spoke. Akka bent down to check that nothing was touching the platform. Thenmozhi wrote the numbers on the back of a parcel wrapper. "Twenty-five point one, take away sixteen point one. Nine kilos of gas. They sold you fourteen point two, Athai. Five point two kilos are missing."

"And the old one finished in five days," Athai said slowly. "In eleven years I never once asked them to weigh a cylinder. The delivery men are supposed to carry a scale. I thought, who weighs gas?"

When the delivery man came, he did not argue. He hung the spare cylinder from his own spring scale and read the same number. He said a half-used cylinder that someone had sent back must have been loaded with the full ones. He brought a new cylinder from the lorry and weighed it where everyone could see. The paint said 15.5. The scale said 29.7.

"Fifteen point five and fourteen point two," said Kathir, before anyone else could. "Twenty-nine point seven. This one is honest."

After the lorry had gone, Kathir climbed onto Akka's scale himself. Twenty-two kilos. He filled his cheeks and blew a long breath down at the platform. The numbers did not move. "My breath weighs nothing," he said. "So I was right about that."

"Your breath didn't stay on the scale," said Thenmozhi. "It went off into the lane the moment it left your mouth. Gas goes everywhere it can. If you want to weigh it, you have to catch it in something closed first."

Kathir looked at the red cylinder, and then down the lane, where his breath had gone. "That's why they need all that steel," he said.

Athai lifted the lid of the steamer, and a white cloud rolled up into the morning. She put a hot idli on a leaf for each of them. "Eat," she said. "These were cooked on gas that my niece weighed."

**For the parent:** For the parent: a gas has mass, so cooking gas is sold by the kilogram. A home cylinder holds 14.2 kilograms, and the mass of the empty steel cylinder is painted on it. So the gas inside can be found by weighing the cylinder and taking away the painted number. In the cylinder the gas is squeezed into a liquid. When the liquid is used up, the cylinder is still full of gas at low pressure. That gas has a mass of about 100 grams. A gas let loose spreads out at once. So it must be caught in a closed container before it can be weighed.

- Ask: Kathir said nobody can count or weigh gas. What on the bill showed that gas is sold by mass?
- Ask: Why is a number painted on every cylinder, and why does it differ from one cylinder to the next?
- Ask: The old cylinder had no liquid left, yet the scale showed 100 grams more than the painted number. What was still inside?
- Ask: Why did Kathir's breath not change the number on the scale?
- Ask: What would you weigh, and what sum would you do, to check a cylinder at your home?

### e1 · Two Balloons on a Hanger: is one gas heavier than another? (40 min)
- **question:** Does a balloon full of air pull a balance down? Two balloons are the same size. One is full of air and one is full of carbon dioxide. Which is heavier?
- **predict:** Guess twice, and write both guesses. First: a balloon blown up with air hangs opposite a flat balloon. Which end of the hanger goes down, or does it stay level? Second: a balloon of air hangs opposite a balloon of carbon dioxide of the same size. Which end goes down? Why do you think so, each time?
- **fair test:** Use two balloons from the same packet. Make them the same size by measuring round each one with a thread. Hang them the same distance from the middle of the hanger. Switch off the fan and close the windows, because moving air pushes balloons about. Then swap the two balloons and test again, in case one end of the hanger is heavier.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps to fit the balloon on the bottle. Keep faces away from the bottle mouth, and rinse with water if vinegar gets in an eye. Never put a balloon of this gas to your mouth. Keep balloons away from children under three.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the hanger with the two balloons and show which end was lower. Then write what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** What hung on the two ends | Which end went lower? | How many centimetres lower? | Paper clips needed to level it
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hang the coat hanger on a door handle or hook, away from the fan. 2. Tie a 30 cm thread to each end of the hanger. 3. Tie one flat balloon to each thread by its neck. 4. If one end is lower, stick small bits of tape on the higher end until the hanger is level. 5. Tape a sheet of paper behind one end and mark where that end sits.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to weigh gases. First we need a balance. This hanger must sit level with a flat balloon on each end.  
  *Child mode:* Hang the hanger on a door handle. Tie a flat balloon to each end. Is it level? Add tape to the higher end. Mark where one end sits.
  - Note: Switch off the fan and close the windows now. Even a small draught swings the balloons. Tie the threads with bows, because the balloons come off and go back on.
- **s2 (5 min)**

  - Ask: The two balloons were the same size. Why was one heavier? (good: Carbon dioxide particles are heavier than the particles of air.; The same space holds about the same number of particles, so heavier particles make a heavier balloon.)
  - Ask: The air balloon held about 3 grams of air. Why did the hanger not show it? (good: The balloon was hanging in air, and the air around it held it up.; A bag of water under water feels light for the same reason.)
  - Note: Both gases have mass. A litre of air has a mass of about 1.2 grams. A litre of carbon dioxide has about 1.8 grams, because its particles are heavier. The air balloon held about 3 grams of air. But the air around it pushed it up by nearly the same amount, so the hanger hardly showed it. The hanger showed only how much heavier the carbon dioxide is than the air it pushed aside. That is 1 to 2 grams.

## Worksheets
### Gases Are Something (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is a gas at room temperature?  
   Options: Oxygen / Coconut oil / Salt / Honey  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* Oxygen is a gas: it has no shape or volume of its own. Coconut oil and honey are liquids, and salt is a solid.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a tumbler with nothing poured into it is empty.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The tumbler is full of air, which is a mixture of gases. A strong answer gives evidence, such as an upturned tumbler keeping water out.
3. (fill) A gas spreads out to fill all the space in its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['container']. *Why:* A gas has no volume of its own. Its particles fly freely until walls stop them, so it fills whatever holds it.
4. (sort) Sort these into solid, liquid or gas.  
   **Answer:** steel spoon → Solid; water vapour → Gas; petrol → Liquid; ice cube → Solid; carbon dioxide → Gas; buttermilk → Liquid; nitrogen → Gas; a grain of sugar → Solid. *Why:* Solids keep their shape. Liquids flow but keep their volume. Gases spread to fill their container. A strong answer can give the property used for each.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Compress → to squash into a smaller space; Atmosphere → the layer of air around the Earth; Mixture → two or more substances together; Mass → how much matter a thing is made of; Volume → how much space a thing takes up. *Why:* These words come up all through the unit. A strong answer could give a home example of each.
6. (write) Two balloons are the same size. The carbon dioxide balloon sinks lower on a balance than the air balloon. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Both balloons held the same volume of gas. Carbon dioxide particles are heavier than the particles of air. So the same volume of carbon dioxide has more mass, and its end of the hanger went down.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the sizes were the same, so the difference must come from the gas. It also says that both gases have mass.
7. (mcq) Baking soda is added to vinegar, and the mix fizzes. What state is the new substance in the bubbles?  
   Options: A gas / A liquid / A solid / No new substance is made  
   **Answer:** A gas. *Why:* The bubbles are carbon dioxide, a gas made when the solid and the liquid meet. Choosing 'no new substance' treats the fizz as air that was hiding in the vinegar.
8. (draw) Draw the particles of a solid, a liquid and a gas in three boxes of the same size.  
   **Answer:** Solid: many particles touching, in neat rows. Liquid: about as many particles, touching but jumbled. Gas: only one or two particles, far apart, with arrows to show them flying.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps the particles the same size in all three boxes, and leaves the gas box almost empty.
9. (odd) Three of these are gases in the air. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Nitrogen / Oxygen / Salt / Argon  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Nitrogen, oxygen and argon are the three main gases of dry air. Salt is a solid.
10. (truefalse) True or false: a gas has mass.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A fizzing mix gets lighter as its gas leaves, and a ball gets heavier when air is pumped in. A litre of air has a mass of about 1.2 grams.
11. (order) These are the steps for making a gas and catching it in a balloon. Put them in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Pour vinegar into a bottle', 'Put baking soda into a flat balloon', 'Fit the balloon over the bottle mouth', 'Tip the baking soda into the vinegar', 'Tie the balloon when the fizzing stops']. *Why:* The solid and the liquid must meet only after the balloon is fitted. Otherwise the gas escapes before it can be caught.
12. (label) Label a drawing of an upside-down tumbler pushed under water: trapped air, water inside the tumbler, water outside.  
   **Answer:** ['Trapped air at the top of the tumbler', 'Water inside the tumbler, only a little way up', 'Water outside, much higher']. *Why:* The air takes up space, so the water can rise only a little way inside the tumbler.
13. (perspective) Two children look at a bottle with nothing poured into it. They disagree about it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer agrees that the bottle is full of air. It gives evidence from a test, such as trapped air keeping water out. It also sees that the first child uses 'empty' the everyday way. To that child, it means that nothing we put there is inside.. *Why:* In science the second child is right. But the everyday word 'empty' is useful too, and the two meanings need not fight.
14. (mcq) About how much of the air is nitrogen?  
   Options: About 78 particles in every 100 / About 21 particles in every 100 / About 1 particle in every 100 / Almost none  
   **Answer:** About 78 particles in every 100. *Why:* About 78 in 100 are nitrogen, 21 are oxygen and 1 is argon. Choosing 21 mixes nitrogen up with oxygen.
15. (write) Name two things in your home that hold a gas. Say what each gas is used for.  
   **Answer:** For example: a cooking gas cylinder holds LPG, which burns to cook food. A cycle tyre holds compressed air, which makes the ride soft. ### Why Gases Behave As They Do (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does a gas spread out to fill a whole room?  
   Options: Its particles fly freely in all directions / Its particles grow bigger / The walls pull the gas towards them / Air pushes it into the corners  
   **Answer:** Its particles fly freely in all directions. *Why:* Gas particles are not held together. Each flies until it hits something, so in time they are spread through all the space. The particles do not grow, and nothing pulls or pushes them into place.
2. (write) A capped bottle of air squeezes in. A capped bottle full of water does not. Explain why, using particles.  
   **Answer:** In air the particles are far apart, with empty space between them, so squeezing pushes them closer. In water the particles already touch, so they have nowhere to go.. *Why:* A strong answer talks about the empty space between gas particles, not about the particles being soft or small.
3. (truefalse) True or false: the space between the particles of a gas is filled with air.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Between the particles there is empty space. Air is itself made of particles, so there is no 'air' left over to fill the gaps.
4. (fill) Cooking gas is compressed in its cylinder until it becomes a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['liquid']. *Why:* Compressing pushes the particles together until they touch. Then the gas has become a liquid, which takes far less space.
5. (sort) Sort these gases: is each one lighter than air, or heavier than air?  
   **Answer:** helium → Lighter than air; carbon dioxide → Heavier than air; hydrogen → Lighter than air; cooking gas (LPG) → Heavier than air; piped natural gas → Lighter than air. *Why:* Helium, hydrogen and natural gas have light particles, so they rise in air. Carbon dioxide and cooking gas have heavy particles, so they sink.
6. (draw) A box holds 36 particles of liquid water. Draw the same box holding steam. How many particles belong in it?  
   **Answer:** At most one particle, and often none. In steam each particle is about 12 widths from the next. So a box 6 particles wide has room for less than one.. *Why:* A strong drawing is almost empty. Most drawings of gases show far too many particles.
7. (perspective) A family wants a gas-leak alarm in the kitchen. Two fitters give opposite advice.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says both can be right, because it depends on the gas. Cooking gas from a cylinder is heavier than air and collects low down. Piped natural gas is lighter than air and collects high up. So the family must first find out which gas they use.. *Why:* Different gases have different densities, so no single rule fits every gas.
8. (mcq) A balloon of air hangs from a balance in a room. Why does the air inside add almost nothing?  
   Options: The air around the balloon holds it up / Air has no mass / The rubber is too thin to hold mass / The air inside has leaked out  
   **Answer:** The air around the balloon holds it up. *Why:* The air inside has mass, but the air outside pushes the balloon up by nearly the same amount. Choosing 'air has no mass' forgets that a pumped ball gets heavier.
9. (order) Put these in order, from particles closest together to particles farthest apart.  
   **Answer:** ['Water in a tumbler', 'Air pumped into a cycle tyre', 'Air in a room', 'Air at the top of Mount Everest']. *Why:* Liquid particles touch. Tyre air is compressed, so its particles are closer than in room air. High on a mountain the air is thin, so its particles are farthest apart.
10. (match) Match each gas to a place where you meet it.  
   **Answer:** Nitrogen → inside a packet of chips; Carbon dioxide → the bubbles in soda water; Helium → a balloon that floats up; Oxygen → a cylinder beside a hospital bed; LPG → the red cylinder in a kitchen. *Why:* Each gas is chosen for what it does: nitrogen does not spoil food, helium is light, and oxygen keeps us alive.
11. (odd) Three of these are two states mixed together. Which one is a single state?  
   Options: Fog / Smoke / Coffee foam / Nitrogen  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Fog is liquid drops in a gas, and smoke is solid bits in a gas. Foam is gas bubbles in a liquid. Nitrogen is only a gas.
12. (write) A smell crosses a room faster when the fan is on. What does that tell you about how smells travel?  
   **Answer:** Smell particles are mixed into the air, so when the air moves, they move with it. On their own they spread slowly, because they keep bumping into air particles. The fan carries them along quickly.. *Why:* A strong answer separates two things: particles spreading by themselves, and moving air carrying them.
13. (label) Label a bottle with a balloon on top: vinegar, baking soda, carbon dioxide. Write the state beside each.  
   **Answer:** ['Vinegar: liquid, in the bottle', 'Baking soda: solid, falling in from the balloon', 'Carbon dioxide: gas, filling the balloon']. *Why:* A liquid and a solid meet and make a new substance, which is a gas.
14. (truefalse) True or false: the white cloud above a boiling kettle is water as a gas.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Water as a gas is invisible. The white cloud is tiny drops of liquid water, formed where the hot vapour cools in the air.
15. (mcq) A deep breath pushes 1,800 ml of water out of a bottle. About what is the mass of that air?  
   Options: About 2 grams / About 2 kilograms / About 1,800 grams / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** About 2 grams. *Why:* A litre of air has a mass of about 1.2 grams. 1.8 litres times 1.2 is about 2.2 grams. Choosing 1,800 grams mixes up millilitres of air with grams of water.
### Weighing What You Cannot See (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (perspective) In 1894, nitrogen taken from air weighed slightly more than nitrogen made from chemicals. Two scientists disagree about it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that a small difference which comes back every time is evidence, not a slip. It suggests a test, such as removing everything known from air and seeing whether any gas is left. Lord Rayleigh and William Ramsay did this and found argon.. *Why:* Both views are reasonable at first. Repeating the measurement is what separates a mistake from a discovery.
2. (write) A closed bottle of fizzing vinegar and soda keeps its mass. Opened, it loses 3 grams. Explain both results.  
   **Answer:** The fizz makes carbon dioxide gas, which has mass. While the bottle is closed, the gas stays inside, so the mass stays the same. When the cap opens, gas rushes out, and its mass leaves with it.. *Why:* A strong answer uses one idea, that a gas has mass, to explain both results.
3. (mcq) One litre of carbon dioxide is 0.6 g heavier than one litre of air. How much heavier are 2 litres?  
   Options: 1.2 g / 0.3 g / 2.6 g / 0.6 g  
   **Answer:** 1.2 g. *Why:* Twice the volume holds twice the extra mass. 0.6 times 2 is 1.2 grams.
4. (truefalse) True or false: cooking gas that leaks from a cylinder collects near the ceiling.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Cooking gas from a cylinder is heavier than air, so it sinks and collects near the floor. That is why people open the doors and windows and do not touch any switch.
5. (fill) The upward push of the air on a balloon is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['upthrust', 'buoyancy']. *Why:* Upthrust is the upward push of a gas or a liquid on anything inside it. It equals the weight of the gas or liquid pushed aside.
6. (draw) Draw the Earth with its layer of air. Show where the air is thickest and how it changes higher up.  
   **Answer:** A thin layer round the Earth, shaded darkest at the ground and fading upwards, with no sharp top line.. *Why:* A strong drawing makes the layer very thin compared with the Earth, and lets it fade out without an edge.
7. (sort) Sort these: is it only a gas, or tiny solids and liquids floating in a gas?  
   **Answer:** oxygen → Only a gas; smoke → Bits floating in a gas; fog → Bits floating in a gas; nitrogen → Only a gas; dust haze → Bits floating in a gas; water vapour → Only a gas; carbon dioxide → Only a gas; mist from a spray bottle → Bits floating in a gas. *Why:* Oxygen, nitrogen, water vapour and carbon dioxide are gases, and all are invisible. Smoke, fog, haze and spray can be seen because they hold tiny solid or liquid bits.
8. (mcq) A sticky square by the road catches 40 specks. One in a bedroom catches 5. What is the fairest conclusion?  
   Options: More dust floats in air by the road / The bedroom air has no dust at all / Road air holds 40 kinds of gas / The bedroom square was not sticky  
   **Answer:** More dust floats in air by the road. *Why:* The squares compare the dust that floats past. Five specks is still some dust, and the smallest bits do not show at all.
9. (order) A litre of each is weighed. Put them in order, from the smallest mass to the largest.  
   **Answer:** ['Hydrogen', 'Helium', 'Air', 'Carbon dioxide', 'Water']. *Why:* Hydrogen has the lightest particles, then helium. Air is heavier, and carbon dioxide heavier still. Water is a liquid, with its particles touching, so a litre of it has a mass of a whole kilogram.
10. (write) A friend says, 'Gases always rise.' Describe one test or example that shows this is not always true.  
   **Answer:** For example: a balloon filled with carbon dioxide falls faster than a balloon of air, and sinks on a balance. Leaked cooking gas collects near the floor. A gas rises only if it is lighter than the air around it.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a real case and then states the rule that replaces 'always'.
11. (match) Match each idea about gases to the evidence for it.  
   **Answer:** A gas takes up space → trapped air keeps water out of an upturned tumbler; A gas has mass → an open tumbler of fizz gets lighter; A gas fills its container → smoke spreads from one jar into two; A gas can be compressed → a capped bottle of air squeezes in; Gases differ in density → a balloon of carbon dioxide sinks below a balloon of air. *Why:* Each idea in science stands on evidence. A strong answer could describe a second piece of evidence for one of them.
12. (label) Label a pie chart of dry air with three slices: nitrogen, oxygen, and argon with other gases.  
   **Answer:** ['Largest slice: nitrogen, about 78 in 100', 'Smaller slice: oxygen, about 21 in 100', 'Thin sliver: argon and other gases, about 1 in 100']. *Why:* Nitrogen is nearly four-fifths of the air and oxygen about one-fifth.
13. (mcq) Why is the mix of gases in air almost the same in Chennai and in Shimla?  
   Options: Winds stir the air, and gases mix / Each city makes its own air / The heavy gases all stay in Chennai / Mountain air has no nitrogen  
   **Answer:** Winds stir the air, and gases mix. *Why:* Gases spread to fill the space they reach, and winds mix the whole atmosphere. Shimla has less air in each breath than Chennai, but the same mix.
14. (odd) Three of these lower air pollution. Which one adds to it?  
   Options: Many people sharing one bus / Burning dry leaves in a heap / Covering a building site / Cooking on gas, not smoky wood  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* Burning leaves puts smoke straight into the air. Shared buses, covered building sites and cleaner cooking fuel all put less smoke and dust into it.
15. (write) Glass keeps its shape, but its particles are jumbled like a liquid's. Is glass a solid? Give your reasons.  
   **Answer:** For example: glass is a solid, because it keeps its own shape and volume and does not flow. But it is an unusual solid, because its particles are not in neat rows. This shows that the three states are useful boxes, and some things sit near the edge of a box.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the properties of states to decide, and admits that the fit is not perfect.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A tumbler is pushed mouth-down into water, and the inside stays mostly dry. Why?  
   Options: The air inside takes up the space / Glass pushes water away / The water is too heavy to rise / The tumbler is too small  
   **Answer:** The air inside takes up the space. *Why:* Air is a substance that takes up space, so the water has no room to rise. The glass and the size of the tumbler make no difference.
2. (mcq) Which test best shows that a gas has mass?  
   Options: A fizzing mix gets lighter as gas leaves / A balloon feels smooth / Smoke looks grey / Wind moves the leaves  
   **Answer:** A fizzing mix gets lighter as gas leaves. *Why:* If mass leaves the scale when only gas leaves, the gas must have mass. Wind shows that air can push, but not that it has mass.
3. (mcq) Perfume is opened in one corner of a closed room. An hour later, where is the smell?  
   Options: Everywhere in the room / Only in that corner / Only near the floor / Only near the ceiling  
   **Answer:** Everywhere in the room. *Why:* The particles of a gas keep moving until they are spread through all the space they can reach.
4. (mcq) In which state are the particles far apart and flying freely in all directions?  
   Options: Gas / Liquid / Solid / All three states  
   **Answer:** Gas. *Why:* In a solid and a liquid the particles touch. Only in a gas are they far apart and free.
5. (mcq) Which property do liquids and gases share, but solids do not have?  
   Options: They flow and take their container's shape / They keep one fixed shape / They can be squashed easily / They have no mass  
   **Answer:** They flow and take their container's shape. *Why:* Liquids and gases both flow. Only gases can be squashed easily, and all three states have mass.
6. (mcq) A litre of milk is poured from a jug into a wide pan. What stays the same?  
   Options: Its volume / Its shape / Its height in the vessel / Nothing stays the same  
   **Answer:** Its volume. *Why:* A liquid takes the shape of its container but keeps its volume. A litre is still a litre in any vessel.
7. (mcq) Which gas makes up about one-fifth of the air, and is the gas our bodies use?  
   Options: Oxygen / Nitrogen / Carbon dioxide / Argon  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* About 21 in every 100 particles of air are oxygen. Nitrogen is nearly four-fifths, but our bodies breathe it in and out unchanged.
8. (mcq) Vinegar is a liquid and baking soda is a solid. They fizz together. What is made?  
   Options: A new gas, carbon dioxide / More vinegar / Only a new solid / Nothing new  
   **Answer:** A new gas, carbon dioxide. *Why:* The fizz is bubbles of carbon dioxide, a new substance in a third state. Choosing 'nothing new' treats the bubbles as air that was already there.
9. (mcq) Two balloons are the same size. One holds carbon dioxide and one holds air. Why is the first heavier?  
   Options: Its particles are heavier, and just as many / It holds a liquid / It is colder / It was blown up harder  
   **Answer:** Its particles are heavier, and just as many. *Why:* The same space holds about the same number of gas particles. Carbon dioxide particles have about one and a half times the mass of air particles.
10. (mcq) A bag of water feels almost weightless while it is under water. What is the same idea for air?  
   Options: A balloon of air seems weightless in air / Air has no mass / Water has no mass / Bags always float  
   **Answer:** A balloon of air seems weightless in air. *Why:* The water around the bag holds it up. The air around a balloon of air holds it up in the same way. Both the water and the air have mass.
11. (mcq) What is PM2.5?  
   Options: Tiny solid and liquid bits floating in air / A gas that plants make / A kind of oxygen / The name of a weather app  
   **Answer:** Tiny solid and liquid bits floating in air. *Why:* PM2.5 is not a gas. It is bits smaller than 2.5 thousandths of a millimetre, small enough to reach deep into the lungs.
12. (mcq) Two balloons of different gases are compared, but one balloon is bigger. Why is the test not fair?  
   Options: Two things differ, so the cause is unclear / Bigger balloons always win / A fair test needs three balloons / The balloons must be the same colour  
   **Answer:** Two things differ, so the cause is unclear. *Why:* In a fair test only one thing changes. If the gas and the size both differ, either could explain the result.

## Applications
- Cooking gas is compressed into a liquid, so that 14.2 kilograms fit in one home cylinder. Buses and autos carry natural gas compressed to about 200 times the pressure of the air.
- Packets of chips are filled with nitrogen. It keeps out the oxygen that would turn the oil stale, and it cushions the chips.
- Hospitals store oxygen in cylinders and tanks, and give it to people who cannot take in enough from the air.
- Some fire extinguishers pour out carbon dioxide. It is heavier than air, so it settles over the fire and keeps oxygen away.
- Weather offices in India send up hydrogen-filled balloons every day to measure the air high above the ground.
- Anna Mani, a physicist from Kerala, built instruments for India's weather service. One of them measures ozone gas high in the atmosphere.
- The Air Quality Index lets a city warn people on days when they should not exercise outdoors.
- Divers and firefighters carry compressed air in tanks, so that a small tank holds enough for many breaths.

## Open questions
- **How many states of matter are there?** Physicists keep finding new ones in very cold or very squeezed matter. They do not agree on how to count them.
- **What exactly is glass?** Glass is a liquid that cooled into a solid without its particles lining up in rows. Why it stops flowing is still not fully explained. Some physicists call it one of the hardest unsolved problems about solids.
- **Where does the Earth's air end?** The air thins out slowly for hundreds of kilometres and has no edge. Countries and scientists do not agree on a single height for the start of space.
- **Is any amount of PM2.5 safe to breathe?** In 2021 the World Health Organization lowered its guideline, because harm was found at lower levels than before. Nobody has yet found a level with no harm at all.
- **How will the mix of gases in the air change over the next hundred years?** It depends on how much carbon dioxide people add, and on how much the seas and forests take up. Both are hard to predict.

## Connected maths
- In Two Balloons on a Hanger, the gas balloon held 2.5 litres. A litre of carbon dioxide has a mass of 1.8 g and a litre of air 1.2 g. How much heavier was the gas balloon than an air balloon of the same size? **1.5 grams heavier.**. Each litre of carbon dioxide is 1.8 − 1.2 = 0.6 g heavier than a litre of air. For 2.5 litres, 0.6 × 2.5 = 1.5 g. That is about 3 small paper clips. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A cylinder has 15.3 kg painted inside its top ring. A full one holds 14.2 kg of gas. On a scale it shows 22.4 kg. How much gas is left, and is the cylinder more or less than half full? **7.1 kg of gas is left. That is exactly half.**. Gas left = 22.4 − 15.3 = 7.1 kg. Half of 14.2 kg is 7.1 kg. So the cylinder is exactly half full. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L5`)
- You breathe about 6 litres of air every minute. A litre of air has a mass of 1.2 g. About what mass of air do you breathe in one day? **About 10 kilograms.**. A day has 24 × 60 = 1,440 minutes. 6 × 1,440 = 8,640 litres. 8,640 × 1.2 g = 10,368 g, which is about 10 kg. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- In Bean Gas, one bean has 1 chance in 4 of being in the START quarter. For two beans to be there together, the chance is 1 in 16. For three, it is 1 in 64. What is it for all six? **1 chance in 4,096.**. Each extra bean multiplies the number by 4. So: 4, 16, 64, 256, 1,024, 4,096. With six beans, they are all in the START quarter once in about 4,096 looks. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In Coins on the Floor, each steam particle sat 12 coin-widths from the next. Real particles spread in three directions: along, across and up. How many times as much space does steam take as water? How much steam comes from 5 ml of water? **About 1,700 times. So 5 ml of water makes about 8.5 litres of steam.**. 12 × 12 × 12 = 1,728, which is about 1,700. Then 5 ml × 1,700 = 8,500 ml, or 8.5 litres. (queues `math.area-perimeter.volume.L5`)
- In 100 Grains of Air, 4 particles in every 10,000 are carbon dioxide. How many particles of air come with each one particle of carbon dioxide? About what fraction of the air is oxygen, at 21 in 100? **2,500 particles for each one of carbon dioxide. Oxygen is about one-fifth of the air.**. 10,000 ÷ 4 = 2,500. For oxygen, 21 in 100 is close to 20 in 100, and 20 in 100 is one-fifth. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
