# Magnets: how they attract iron without touching it
`sci.magnets.magnets-and-materials.L3`

**Summary.** A magnet attracts iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. It does not attract wood, plastic, paper or most other metals, such as copper and aluminium. A magnet does not need to touch iron to attract it. Every magnet has a magnetic field around it. This is the space where the magnet's force acts. Two magnets can also push each other away. This is called repelling. Inside iron, each atom acts like a tiny magnet. In a magnet, these tiny magnets all point the same way, so their forces add up. In an ordinary nail, they point in different directions, so their forces cancel out. The Earth is a giant magnet too. A compass needle lines up with the Earth's magnetic field. Long ago, sailors in China, India, Arabia and Europe used this to find their way at sea. Turtles and birds can sense the Earth's magnetic field and use it to travel.

**Intro.** *Try this. Hold a magnet near a steel safety pin and move it closer very slowly. Stop just before they touch. Did the pin jump to the magnet? The magnet never touched the pin. So how did it move it?* People have wondered about this for thousands of years. In Sanskrit, a magnet is called chumbaka, which means 'the one that kisses'. An old Chinese book from over 2,000 years ago calls it the 'loving stone'. In this unit you find the real answer. A magnet has an invisible magnetic field around it, and its force acts through that field, even across air. You also find out what happens inside the iron, and why the whole Earth is a magnet too.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the materials a magnet attracts: iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. I can name metals it does not attract, such as copper, brass and aluminium.
1. I can magnetise a needle by stroking it with a magnet, use it as a compass, and explain why it points north and south.
2. I can run a fair test to compare how strong magnets are, and show my results in a table and a bar chart.
3. I can explain that every electron is a tiny magnet, and that iron becomes a magnet when the tiny magnets in its domains point the same way.
4. I can tell the story of the magnet stone, lodestone, in India, China, Greece and the Islamic world.
5. I can explain that the Earth is a giant magnet, made by liquid iron moving deep inside it, and that its magnetic field moves and sometimes flips.

## Terms
- **Magnet**: An object that attracts iron without touching it. Two magnets can attract each other or repel each other. *e.g.* A fridge magnet holds a list on the fridge door, because the door has iron in it.
- **Attract**: To draw something closer. A magnet attracts iron: the iron moves towards the magnet and sticks to it. *e.g.* Hold a magnet a little above a steel pin. The magnet attracts the pin, and the pin jumps up to it.
- **Repel**: To push something away. Two magnets can repel each other. If two magnets attract each other, turn one of them round. Now they push apart instead. *e.g.* Slide two ring magnets onto a pencil. If they snap together, take one off and turn it over. Now it floats above the other, because they repel.
- **Magnetic force**: A force is a push or a pull. A magnetic force is the force a magnet puts on iron, or on another magnet. It can attract or repel, it acts without touching, and it gets weaker quickly with distance. *e.g.* A fridge magnet's force is strong enough to hold a sheet of paper on the fridge door.
- **Magnetic material**: A material that a magnet attracts. The main ones are iron, most kinds of steel, nickel and cobalt. *e.g.* A steel nail and a steel safety pin are magnetic materials.
- **Non-magnetic material**: A material that a magnet does not attract, as far as we can feel. *e.g.* Wood, plastic, glass, paper, copper, brass and aluminium are non-magnetic.
- **Steel**: Iron mixed with a small amount of other substances, such as carbon, to make it stronger. Most steel is magnetic. Some stainless steel, used for kitchen vessels, is not. *e.g.* A steel almirah holds a magnet. Some steel tumblers do not.
- **Pole**: One of the two ends of a magnet, where its magnetic force is strongest. One is called the north pole and the other the south pole. A north pole and a south pole attract each other. Two north poles, or two south poles, repel each other. *e.g.* Dip a bar magnet in paper clips. Most clips cling to the two ends.
- **Compass**: A small magnet, usually a needle, that can turn freely. The Earth's magnetic field turns it until one end points north. *e.g.* Sailors and hikers use a compass to find their way when they cannot see the Sun or the stars.
- **Magnetise**: To turn a piece of iron or steel into a magnet. One way is to stroke it with a magnet, many times, always in the same direction. *e.g.* Stroke a sewing needle 50 times with a magnet. Now the needle can pick up a pin.
- **Lodestone**: A natural magnet. It is a piece of a dark mineral called magnetite, which has iron in it. Most magnetite is not a magnet, but a lodestone is already a magnet when it comes out of the ground. *e.g.* Long ago, people in China, India and Greece found lodestones that picked up iron.
- **Magnetite**: A black mineral made of iron and oxygen. A magnet attracts it. Tiny grains of it are in most sand and soil. *e.g.* Drag a magnet through dry sand. The black grains that cling to it are magnetite.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* A steel nail, your hand and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Electron**: A particle far smaller than an atom, in the outer part of every atom. Every electron is also a tiny magnet, with its own north pole and south pole. *e.g.* Every atom in your body has electrons. In you, the electrons are in pairs whose tiny magnets point opposite ways and cancel out.
- **Spin**: A property of every electron that makes it a tiny magnet. Electrons are not really spinning like tops. Spin is simply something every electron has, just as it has electric charge. *e.g.* When two electrons in an atom form a pair, their spins point opposite ways, so their tiny magnets cancel.
- **Domain**: A tiny region inside iron where the tiny magnets of billions of atoms all point the same way. *e.g.* In a plain nail, the domains point in different directions, so their magnetic forces cancel out. In a magnet, most domains point the same way.
- **Magnetic field**: The space around a magnet where its magnetic force acts. We cannot see it, but iron grains and compasses show its shape. *e.g.* Sprinkle black sand on paper over a magnet and tap it. The grains line up in curved lines.
- **Temporary magnet**: A piece of iron that acts as a magnet only while a magnet is near it. *e.g.* A paper clip hanging from a magnet can hold another clip. Take the magnet away and the clips drop.
- **Permanent magnet**: A magnet that stays magnetised by itself, for a long time. *e.g.* A fridge magnet, a bar magnet and a lodestone are permanent magnets.
- **Curie point**: The temperature at which a magnet stops being a magnet, because the heat shakes its tiny magnets out of line. For iron it is about 770 degrees Celsius, when iron glows red. *e.g.* A magnetised needle held in a gas flame until it glows red is no longer a magnet once it cools.
- **Earth's core**: The centre of the Earth. It starts about 2,900 kilometres under our feet. It is mostly iron and nickel. Its outer part is liquid metal and its inner part is solid. *e.g.* Moving liquid iron in the outer core makes the Earth into a giant magnet.
- **Aurora**: Coloured lights in the night sky, mostly near the North and South Poles. They glow when tiny charged particles from the Sun hit the air high up. The Earth's magnetic field guides these particles towards the poles. *e.g.* In May 2024, red auroras were photographed from Hanle in Ladakh.
- **Magnetic sense**: The ability of some living things to sense the Earth's magnetic field and use it to find their way. Scientists call it magnetoreception. *e.g.* Young sea turtles use it to find their way across the ocean.

## Explanations
### What a magnet attracts · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnet attracts iron, most kinds of steel, nickel and cobalt. These are called magnetic materials. A magnet does not attract wood, plastic, glass, paper, cloth or most other metals.

Many people think a magnet attracts every metal. It does not. Copper, brass, aluminium, gold and silver are all metals, and a magnet does not attract any of them. Steel is mostly iron, so a magnet attracts most steel. Some stainless steel, used for kitchen vessels, has its iron atoms packed in a different pattern. That pattern makes it almost non-magnetic. The magnetic force also acts through materials that are not magnetic. A magnet can attract a pin through paper, cloth, wood or water. The force gets weaker very quickly as the pin moves further away from the magnet.
- e.g. A magnet sticks to a steel almirah but not to a brass diya.
- e.g. Put a paper clip on a sheet of cardboard. Move a magnet underneath the cardboard, and the clip follows the magnet.
- e.g. A one-rupee coin may or may not stick to a magnet, depending on which metal it is made of. Test yours.
### Every electron is a tiny magnet · *defined*
**Definition:** Every atom has tiny particles on its outside called electrons. Every electron is itself a tiny magnet, with a north and a south. Its magnetism comes from a property scientists call spin.

So why isn't everything a magnet? In most atoms, the electrons are in pairs. In each pair, the two tiny magnets point opposite ways, so they cancel each other out. That is what happens in wood, plastic, water, copper and your body. In iron, some electrons have no partner. Their tiny magnets are not cancelled. So each iron atom is a tiny magnet.
- e.g. In a copper atom, all the electrons but one are in pairs, and that last one is shared with the other copper atoms. So copper is not magnetic.
- e.g. An iron atom has four electrons without partners. So every iron atom is a tiny magnet.
### Domains: where iron's tiny magnets line up · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside iron, each atom's tiny magnet points the same way as its neighbours'. Together they form small regions called domains. In one domain, the tiny magnets of billions of atoms all point the same way.

A plain steel nail has many domains, and they point in different directions. Their magnetic forces cancel out, so the nail is not a magnet. Bring a magnet near, and the domains that point along its magnetic field grow, while the others shrink. Now the nail is a magnet too, for as long as the magnet is near. That is why one paper clip hanging from a magnet can hold up another clip. In a permanent magnet, most domains stay lined up by themselves. Why does each iron atom point the same way as its neighbours? The electrons of neighbouring iron atoms affect each other, and they settle with their tiny magnets pointing the same way. Scientists explain this with quantum physics, the science of the very tiniest things. The French scientist Pierre Weiss said in 1907 that domains must exist. In 1931, the American scientist Francis Bitter first saw their edges. He spread a liquid with very fine magnetic powder in it over a piece of iron, and looked through a microscope.
- e.g. A chain of paper clips hangs from a magnet. Each clip becomes a temporary magnet and holds up the next one.
- e.g. Stroke a needle with a magnet and its domains line up. Now the needle is a magnet.
### Making a magnet, and breaking one · *defined*
**Definition:** You can make a magnet by lining up the domains in iron or steel. You can break a magnet by shaking them out of line again, with heat or hard knocks.

Stroke a steel needle with a magnet 50 times, always in the same direction. Each stroke turns more domains to point the same way, and the needle becomes a magnet. Nothing is added to the needle and nothing is taken away. Only the direction of its tiny magnets changes. Heat does the opposite. When iron gets hotter, its atoms jiggle faster. At about 770 degrees Celsius, when iron glows red, the atoms jiggle so hard that their tiny magnets cannot stay lined up. The iron is no longer a magnet. This temperature is called the Curie point, after Pierre Curie, who measured it in 1895. Marie Curie's first research, which she began in 1894, was also about magnets. She measured how well different kinds of steel stay magnetised. Later she discovered radium.
- e.g. Dropping or hammering a magnet many times can weaken it, because the knocks shake its domains out of line.
- e.g. A plain steel nail loses most of its magnetism quickly. Hard steel, like a needle, stays magnetised for days or weeks.
### What is an electron's spin, really? · *debated*
Scientists agree that every electron has a tiny magnet built in, called spin. They can measure it very exactly. But what is it, underneath? People have given different answers, and the question is still argued about.
- e.g. In 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent silver atoms flying between the poles of a magnet. The atoms split into just two streams, not a smear. This showed that something inside each atom could point only 'up' or 'down'.
- e.g. Nobody has ever seen an electron, or watched it turn.
- **George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit, two young scientists in the Netherlands, in 1925:** The electron is a tiny ball of charge spinning like a top. A spinning charge makes a magnet. That is how spin got its name.
- **Most physicists today:** The electron cannot really be a spinning ball. Its surface would have to move faster than light. Spin is just a basic property of every electron, like its charge. Nothing deeper is needed to explain it.
- **The physicist Richard Feynman, in a 1983 BBC interview:** When we ask 'why do magnets attract and repel?', each answer leads to another 'why'. At some point we can only say: this is how the world behaves. Some physicists and philosophers think that is not a full answer, and keep looking.
### Magnetism and electricity are one force · *defined*
**Definition:** Magnetism and electricity are two sides of one force, which scientists call the electromagnetic force. Moving electric charge makes a magnetic field, and a moving magnet can make electricity flow in a wire.

In 1820, a Danish scientist, Hans Christian Oersted, noticed that a compass needle moved when electricity flowed in a wire nearby. The electric current was making a magnetic field. Michael Faraday, who started work at 13 as an errand boy for a bookseller, found the reverse in 1831. Moving a magnet in and out of a coil of wire makes electricity flow. In 1865, James Clerk Maxwell wrote the rules of electricity and magnetism as one set of equations. They showed that light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields. In 1905, Albert Einstein showed that whether a force looks electric or magnetic depends on how you are moving. The same electromagnetic force also holds atoms together and causes friction.
- e.g. A doorbell, a fan and a mixer all have electromagnets inside. An electromagnet is a coil of wire that becomes a magnet when electricity flows through it.
- e.g. Most power stations spin magnets near coils of wire to make electricity.
- e.g. Light from the Sun is a wave of electric and magnetic fields.
### Lodestone: the stone that finds iron · *defined*
**Definition:** A lodestone is a natural magnet. It is a piece of a black mineral called magnetite, which is made of iron and oxygen. Many cultures found lodestones long ago.

In Greece, about 2,600 years ago, the thinker Thales said that a lodestone has a soul, because it can move iron. We know this from Aristotle, who wrote about it later. In China, a book from about 240 BCE, the Lüshi Chunqiu, says 'the loving stone calls the iron to it'. In India, the magnet was called ayaskanta, which means 'beloved of iron', and chumbaka, which means 'the one that kisses'. The Sushruta Samhita, an old Indian book of surgery, describes using a magnet to draw a smooth iron arrowhead out of a wound. Later, people learned to find directions with magnets. A Chinese military book from 1044, the Wujing Zongyao, describes an iron 'south-pointing fish' floated in water. The fish was made into a magnet by heating it red-hot and letting it cool while it pointed north and south. In 1088, the Chinese scholar Shen Kuo described a needle made into a magnet by rubbing it on a lodestone. He also noticed that it does not point exactly north. Around 1232, Muhammad al-Awfi, a writer at the court of Delhi, described sailors who floated an iron fish rubbed with a magnet to find their way. In Europe, Petrus Peregrinus described a magnet's two poles in 1269.
- e.g. Lodestones were set in brass or silver caps, so people could carry them and rub needles with them.
- e.g. Iron fish compasses floated in a bowl of water, like the needle compass in this unit's experiment.
### How did lodestones become magnets? · *debated*
Most magnetite is not a magnet. Only a few pieces are lodestones. The Earth's magnetic field is far too weak to make such strong magnets. So how did lodestones become magnets? Scientists have different ideas.
- e.g. Lodestones are usually found near the surface of the ground, not deep inside it.
- e.g. A lightning strike carries a huge electric current, and an electric current makes a magnetic field.
- **Peter Wasilewski and Gunther Kletetschka, scientists in the USA, in 1999:** Lightning did it. When lightning strikes the ground, its huge current makes a very strong magnetic field for a moment. That field lines up the domains in the magnetite nearby, and they stay lined up.
- **Other scientists:** Lightning explains some lodestones, but maybe not all. The kind of magnetite matters too. Some magnetite keeps its domains lined up much better than other magnetite. The full answer is still being studied.
### The Earth is a giant magnet · *defined*
**Definition:** The whole Earth is a magnet. Its magnetic field is made by liquid iron moving in the Earth's outer core, which starts about 2,900 kilometres under our feet.

In 1600, an English doctor, William Gilbert, carved a ball out of lodestone. He called it a 'little Earth'. He moved a tiny compass over it, and the compass behaved just as compasses do on the real Earth. So he said the Earth itself is a magnet. A compass needle is a small magnet, so the Earth's magnetic field turns it to point north and south. Today we know more. The Earth's core is mostly iron and nickel. Its outer part is liquid metal, and its centre is about as hot as the surface of the Sun. The liquid iron churns slowly, as heat escapes from the core. Electric currents flow in the moving iron, and electric currents make a magnetic field. In 1936, a Danish scientist, Inge Lehmann, studied earthquake waves that had passed through the Earth. She found that the very centre is a solid ball inside the liquid. A compass does not point exactly at the North Pole. It lines up with the Earth's magnetic field, which points roughly towards the magnetic north pole. That is some distance away from the North Pole.
- e.g. Hang a magnetised needle on a thread, far from any iron. It turns until it points north and south.
- e.g. In most of India, a compass points only a degree or two away from true north. In Canada, it can point far off.
### The Earth's magnetic field moves and flips · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's magnetic field changes. Its magnetic north pole moves every year. Every so often, usually hundreds of thousands of years apart, the whole field flips. After a flip, a compass would point south.

When hot lava cools into rock, tiny magnetite grains in it line up with the Earth's magnetic field. Then they are fixed in place. So old rocks keep a record of which way the field pointed when they formed. In 1906, a French scientist, Bernard Brunhes, found lava rocks magnetised in the opposite direction to today's field. In 1929, a Japanese scientist, Motonori Matuyama, studied old lava rocks from Japan, Korea and China. He showed that the Earth's magnetic field has flipped many times. The last flip was about 780,000 years ago. Today the magnetic north pole is moving from Canada towards Siberia, by tens of kilometres every year. India has measured the Earth's magnetic field for a long time. An observatory at Colaba in Bombay began measuring it in 1841. In 1904 it moved to Alibag, because the electric currents of Bombay's new electric trams would have disturbed the measurements. Its records are still kept.
- e.g. The Colaba observatory recorded a huge magnetic storm in 1859, when auroras were seen over much of the world.
- e.g. The rock of the ocean floor lies in long stripes. One stripe is magnetised one way, the next stripe the other way. Each stripe formed at a different time, so the stripes record the flips.
### The Earth's magnetic field shields us, and makes auroras · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's magnetic field reaches far out into space. It turns aside most of the tiny charged particles that stream out from the Sun. This stream is called the solar wind.

The Sun blows out a steady stream of tiny charged particles, called the solar wind. A magnetic field pushes moving charged particles sideways, so they cannot travel straight through it. So the Earth's magnetic field steers most of the solar wind around our planet. Some particles are guided along the field down towards the North and South Poles. There they hit the air high above the ground and make it glow. These glowing curtains of light are auroras. When the Sun has a big storm, auroras can be seen much further from the poles. In May 2024, cameras at the Indian Astronomical Observatory in Hanle, Ladakh, photographed a red aurora. Mars has almost no magnetic field around the whole planet today. Many scientists think that, without one, the solar wind slowly stripped away much of Mars's air.
- e.g. Big solar storms can disturb compasses, radio signals, satellites and power lines.
- e.g. Auroras glow green, red and sometimes purple, depending on which gas in the air is glowing, and how high up it is.
### Living things that sense the Earth's magnetic field · *debated*
Many animals use the Earth's magnetic field to find their way. For some of them, scientists have strong evidence. How they sense the field is still argued about.
- e.g. Some bacteria, living things far too small to see, build a chain of tiny magnetite crystals inside their bodies. The chain works like a tiny compass needle. The Earth's magnetic field turns the chain, and the bacterium swims in the direction the chain points. Salvatore Bellini saw them in Italy in 1963, but his notes were not published. Richard Blakemore described them in the USA in 1975.
- e.g. Kenneth and Catherine Lohmann have shown that young loggerhead sea turtles can tell where they are in the ocean from the Earth's magnetic field. They sense how strong it is, and the angle at which it dips into the Earth. Olive ridley turtles nest on Odisha's beaches and may do the same, but nobody has tested them as carefully.
- e.g. In 1972, Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko showed that European robins, small birds, choose their migration direction using the Earth's magnetic field. When the scientists changed the magnetic field around the birds' cage, the birds changed direction.
- **Many scientists studying birds:** Birds may sense the field with their eyes. When light enters the eye, it strikes a protein called cryptochrome. This makes pairs of electrons whose spins are affected by the Earth's magnetic field. In 2021, a team led by Jingjing Xu and Henrik Mouritsen showed that robin cryptochrome responds to magnetic fields in the lab.
- **Other scientists:** Some animals may carry tiny magnetite crystals, like the bacteria do. For a while, iron-rich cells in pigeon beaks looked like the answer. In 2012, careful tests showed that those cells were immune cells that clean up the body, not magnetic sensors. The search goes on.
- **About people:** In 2019, Connie Wang, Joseph Kirschvink and others reported that some people's brainwaves changed when the magnetic field around them was rotated. The people did not feel anything. Other scientists say this needs to be tested again before anyone can be sure.
### Every material responds to a magnet, a little · *defined*
**Definition:** Even materials we call non-magnetic respond to a very strong magnet. Some are attracted very slightly, and some are repelled very slightly.

Water, wood, plastic and your body are very slightly repelled by a magnet. The force is far too weak to feel with a home magnet. In a giant lab magnet, it is strong enough to lift a live frog into the air. Andre Geim did this in the Netherlands in 1997, and the frog was unharmed. He worked out the explanation with Michael Berry. Some materials are very slightly attracted. Liquid oxygen, which is extremely cold, clings between the poles of a strong magnet. So 'magnetic' and 'non-magnetic' are not two separate kinds of material. Every material responds to a magnet. Iron responds strongly, and most others respond very weakly.
- e.g. Fix a grape on each end of a drinking straw, and balance the straw on a pin. Bring a strong neodymium magnet close to one grape. The grape slowly moves away, because the water in it is repelled.
- e.g. Liquid oxygen hangs between the poles of a strong magnet, like a little bridge.

## Mostly agreed
- Magnets attract iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. They do not noticeably attract most other materials, including most metals. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with a magnet at home. Scientists have tested thousands of materials in the lab.
- A magnet's force comes from its electrons, which are each a tiny magnet. In iron, the electrons' tiny magnets line up in regions called domains. *How we know:* Scientists can measure the tiny magnet of a single electron. They can also see domains under special microscopes and watch their edges move as a magnet comes near.
- Iron and steel can be magnetised, and magnets can be weakened by heat and hard knocks. *How we know:* Stroke a needle and it picks up a pin. Heat it red-hot and it stops. Pierre Curie measured the exact temperatures in 1895.
- The Earth is a magnet. Its magnetic field is made by liquid iron moving deep inside it, and the field has flipped many times. *How we know:* Compasses line up north and south almost everywhere on Earth. Earthquake waves show that the outer core is liquid. Old lava rocks are magnetised in both directions.
- Some living things use the Earth's magnetic field to find their way. *How we know:* Magnetic bacteria swim along the field. Young sea turtles and robins change direction when scientists change the magnetic field around them.

## How we found out
**Robert Norman, a London compass maker, and William Gilbert, an English doctor, 1581 (Norman's dipping needle) and 1600 (Gilbert's book De Magnete).** For hundreds of years, people knew that a compass points north, but not why. Some said the Pole Star attracted the needle. Others said there was a giant magnetic mountain far in the north. In 1581, Robert Norman, a compass maker in London, noticed something new. He balanced a magnetised needle so it could tilt up and down. It did not lie flat. Its north end dipped down towards the ground. Then William Gilbert, an English doctor, tested the idea that the Earth is a magnet. He carved a ball out of lodestone and called it a terrella, which means 'little Earth'. He moved a tiny needle over it. Near the middle of the ball, the needle lay flat. Near the poles of the ball, it tipped down, just as Norman's needle did in London. In 1600 Gilbert wrote a book, De Magnete, saying that the Earth itself is a giant magnet. That is why a compass points north. Gilbert thought the inside of the Earth was solid lodestone. That part was wrong. The core is far too hot to stay a magnet. Today we know the Earth's magnetic field is made by moving liquid iron.  
*What people believed before:* People thought a compass was attracted by the Pole Star, or by a magnetic mountain in the far north. Gilbert's lodestone ball showed that the Earth itself is a magnet. Gilbert in turn thought the Earth's inside was solid lodestone. That was wrong too: the core is too hot, and the field comes from moving liquid iron.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A magnet attracts all metals.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Most things a magnet sticks to at home are metal: the fridge door, the almirah and pins.  
  Against: Test a brass key, a copper vessel, aluminium foil or a gold ring. A magnet does not attract any of them. It attracts only metals with iron, nickel or cobalt in them.
- **A compass points to the North Pole, or to the Pole Star.** (Many people, and many old books; *rejected*)  
  For: A compass points roughly north, and the Pole Star is roughly north too.  
  Against: A compass lines up with the Earth's magnetic field. The field points roughly towards the magnetic north pole, which is a different place from the North Pole and moves every year. Shen Kuo noticed in China in 1088 that a compass does not point exactly north. The Pole Star has no effect on a compass.
- **Magnets were discovered by a shepherd called Magnes, whose iron-nailed sandals stuck to a rock.** (A legend told by the Roman writer Pliny the Elder, about 1,950 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: It is a good story, and the word 'magnet' does sound like 'Magnes'.  
  Against: There is no evidence the shepherd was real. Most experts think the word comes from Magnesia, a region in Greece and Turkey where lodestones were found. And people in China and India knew lodestones too.
- **Indian sailors invented the compass, using an iron fish floating in oil called a matsya yantra.** (Some writers in the 1900s, and many websites; *minority*)  
  For: Indian Ocean sailors did use floating iron fish compasses. Al-Awfi, writing at the Delhi court around 1232, describes one.  
  Against: No old Indian text has been found that describes a matsya yantra compass. The oldest clear written descriptions of magnetic compasses are Chinese, from 1044 and 1088. Ideas probably travelled along the trade routes.
- **Cows and deer face north or south as they graze, because they sense the Earth's magnetic field.** (Sabine Begall and other scientists, studying satellite pictures, in 2008; *debated*)  
  For: In thousands of satellite pictures, grazing cattle seemed to face north or south more often than other ways.  
  Against: In 2011, another team looked at new pictures and found no such pattern. Wind, sunlight and slopes also change which way cows face.
- **Magnet bracelets and magnet mats cure pain and illness.** (Many sellers, in India and around the world; *rejected*)  
  For: Some people say they feel better when they wear them.  
  Against: Careful tests compare real magnets with fake ones that look the same. People feel about as much better with the fake ones. In 2007, a review of many such tests found no good evidence that magnets help pain. Feeling better because we expect to is real, but the magnet is not what causes it.
- **People can sense the Earth's magnetic field.** (Connie Wang, Joseph Kirschvink and others, in 2019; *debated*)  
  For: In a quiet test room, some people's brainwaves changed when the magnetic field around them was rotated in one particular direction.  
  Against: Nobody in the test felt anything. Only some people showed it, and the result has not yet been repeated by other teams.

## Roots
We have learned how magnets attract iron, and about the tiny electron magnets inside iron. Now we ask what magnets tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A steel needle can be a magnet one minute and not the next. Stroke it with a magnet, and its tiny magnets line up. Heat it red-hot, and they fall out of line. So the same steel can be a magnet or not, depending only on which way its tiny magnets point.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort objects into 'magnets' and 'not magnets', as if a magnet were made of a special material. But a needle becomes a magnet and stops being one while staying the same steel. What changes is the direction its tiny magnets point.  
*Evidence:* Stroke a needle and it picks up a pin. Heat it red-hot and it stops. Stroke it again and it picks up the pin again. Nothing was added and nothing was taken away. Only the direction of its tiny magnets changed.

- *who am i:* Your body is full of electrons, and each one is a tiny magnet. In you, they are in pairs that cancel out.
- *what is life:* Turtles, robins and tiny bacteria sense the Earth's magnetic field and use it to find their way.
- *what is this world:* The whole Earth is a magnet. Its magnetic field is made by moving liquid iron far beneath your feet.

Inside a plain nail, countless tiny magnets point in every direction. Their forces add up to zero, a perfect balance. Turn them to point the same way, and a magnetic force appears out of that zero. Nothing new was made. That one force then acts on many things: a pin, a ship's compass, a turtle's journey and the lights in the sky. This is a small picture of the One becoming the many: what is already there, arranged in a new way.

## Story: The Iron Fish
*Floating iron fish compasses rubbed with a lodestone are real: Muhammad al-Awfi, writing at the Delhi court around 1232, describes sailors using one, and ships really sailed between Khambhat in Gujarat and Aden. Amina, her mother, the pilot and this voyage are made up.*

This happened about 800 years ago. The ship was called the Swift Gazelle, and it smelled of cloves, tar and the sea. It had left Khambhat, in Gujarat, nine days ago. It was carrying cotton cloth and indigo to Aden, far across the Arabian Sea. Amina was ten. She was the only child on board, because her mother was the ship's cook.

Amina's favourite person on the ship, after her mother, was the pilot, old Mariam. She knew the stars the way Amina knew the path to her grandmother's well. At night she sat with her chin up and her eyes half closed, reading the sky. She told the helmsman where to steer.

But Mariam had a second way of finding the way. It lived in a wooden box near the back of the ship. Inside the box was a small brass bowl. Inside a silk bag was a lumpy black stone. And inside a fold of cloth was a fish, no longer than Amina's finger, cut from a thin sheet of iron.

On the fourth night, Mariam had let her watch. She poured water into the brass bowl. She took out the black stone and rubbed it along the iron fish, from tail to nose. She lifted it away, and rubbed again, from tail to nose. Never back and forth. Always the same way. She counted under her breath, all the way to fifty. Then she laid the fish gently on the water, where it floated like a leaf.

The fish shivered. It turned slowly, as if it were sniffing the air, and then it stopped. Its nose pointed to the south. Amina turned the bowl. The fish turned back. She turned the bowl the other way. The fish turned back again, to the south, every time.

'The stone has made the fish a magnet,' said Mariam. 'Now it will always turn to the south. You will not need it often. But when the clouds come, the fish is the only one on this ship who still knows the way.'

Amina also learned what else the black stone could do. In the galley, the ship's tiny kitchen, she held it near her mother's iron ladle. The ladle leapt to the stone with a click. The stone did nothing to the brass cups, the clay pots or the wooden spoons. It attracted only iron.

On the eleventh day, the clouds came.

They came low and grey, and then black. The wind rose and the rain hit the deck like thrown rice. That night there were no stars at all. The ship rolled so hard that her mother tied the big iron cooking pot, the deg, to the back wall with a rope, right beside Mariam's wooden box, so it could not slide about and break someone's leg.

Mariam brought out the brass bowl. She floated the iron fish. Its nose swung round and settled. She called her direction to the helmsman, and the ship turned.

For a long time, nobody spoke. Amina sat wrapped in a blanket, watching the fish by the light of a little oil lamp. Something was wrong, and it took her a while to see what it was. The fish was not pointing where it had pointed on the fourth night. It was pointing at the back wall of the ship. It was pointing at the deg.

Her heart thumped. She thought of the ladle leaping to the stone. The stone attracted iron. The fish had been rubbed with the stone. Did the fish attract iron too? She took the lamp and crept to the back wall. The big iron pot was tied right beside the box. Then she held her mother's iron ladle near the bowl. The fish swung towards the ladle as if it had been called.

'Mariam,' she whispered. Then louder, 'Mariam! The fish is looking at the pot!'

The pilot frowned. She looked at the bowl, at the pot, at the ladle in Amina's hand. Then she said a word Amina's mother would not have liked, and shouted for two sailors to untie the deg and carry it to the front of the ship.

When the pot was gone, the fish shivered. It turned, slowly, slowly, nearly a quarter of the way round. And there it stopped, pointing to the south.

Mariam looked at it for a long time. 'We have been steering towards a cooking pot,' she said at last. 'For half the night.' She called the new direction to the helmsman. The Swift Gazelle leaned, creaked and turned.

But now the fish turned slowly, and it wandered. For hours the storm had thrown the box about, and the knocks had weakened the fish's magnet. Mariam's hands were shaking with cold. So Amina opened the silk bag and took out the black stone. From tail to nose. Lift away. From tail to nose. Never back and forth. She counted to fifty, as she had heard Mariam count. When she floated the fish again, it swung round at once, quick and sure.

In the grey morning, the rain stopped. The sailors leaned over the rail and pointed. Beside the ship, in the dark water, swam three sea turtles, heading the same way the ship was going. They had no bowl, no stone and no iron fish. They swam steadily on, as if they had never been lost at all.

'How do they know the way?' Amina asked.

Mariam watched them go. 'I have sailed for forty years,' she said, 'and I have never once known.'

Two days later they saw the brown mountains behind Aden. After that, on every voyage, the big iron deg travelled at the front of the ship. And every night, in the fold of cloth beside the brass bowl, the black stone and the iron fish lay waiting for a girl who knew the way to rub them.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the iron fish is a magnet, and a magnet and iron attract each other. So the big iron cooking pot turned the fish's nose towards it, and the ship steered towards the pot instead of south. Once the pot was moved away, the Earth's magnetic field could turn the fish to point south again. The knocks of the storm had weakened the fish's magnetism. Stroking it with the lodestone, always in one direction, lined up the tiny magnets inside the iron again, so it turned quickly and surely. The turtles at the end need no iron fish, because their own bodies sense the Earth's magnetic field. Nobody yet fully knows how.

- Ask: Why did the iron fish point the wrong way during the storm?
- Ask: How did Amina work out what was wrong? What had she seen in the galley?
- Ask: Why did Mariam rub the fish from tail to nose, and never back and forth?
- Ask: The turtles found the way without a fish. How do you think they did it?
- Ask: Amina was the cook's daughter, not a sailor, and she noticed what the pilot missed. Why do you think the person who notices is not always the person in charge?

## Worksheets
### Magnet Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (sort) Kavya, a girl in Class 3 in Madurai, empties her pencil box and her mother's sewing tin onto the table. Sort each thing: will a magnet attract it?  
   **Answer:** a steel safety pin → Attracted; a brass key → Not attracted; aluminium foil → Not attracted; an iron nail → Attracted; a copper wire → Not attracted; a steel paper clip → Attracted; a rubber eraser → Not attracted; a steel sewing needle → Attracted. *Why:* The pin, nail, clip and needle are attracted, because they are iron or steel, and steel is mostly iron. Brass, aluminium and copper are metals too, but they have no iron, nickel or cobalt in them. Rubber is not a metal at all. A strong answer sorts by what the thing is made of, not by whether it is shiny or hard.
2. (truefalse) Meenakshi says, 'Every metal sticks to a magnet.' Is she right?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Copper, brass, aluminium, gold and silver are all metals, and a magnet does not attract any of them. A magnet attracts iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. A strong answer names one metal that does not stick, such as a brass key.
3. (mcq) Lakshmi puts a steel pin on the table and lays a page of her notebook over it. She slides a magnet around on top of the page. What will the pin do?  
   Options: Follow the magnet around under the page / Stay still, because the paper blocks the magnet / Stick to the paper and stay there / Jump off the table  
   **Answer:** Follow the magnet around under the page. *Why:* The magnet's force acts through paper, cloth, wood and even your hand. So the pin follows the magnet under the page. Many people guess the paper blocks it. Try it and see.
4. (write) A magnet can move a pin without touching it. Explain how. Use the words 'magnetic field' in your answer.  
   **Answer:** Every magnet has a magnetic field around it. This is the space where its force acts. When the pin is inside the magnetic field, the magnet attracts it, even across a gap.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the force acts across the space around the magnet, the magnetic field, so nothing has to touch.
5. (draw) Draw a bar magnet that has been rolled in a heap of paper clips. Show where the clips cling. Label the north pole and the south pole.  
   **Answer:** Bunches of clips at the two ends, labelled north and south, and few or none in the middle.. *Why:* A magnet's force is strongest at its two ends, the poles. A strong drawing shows that clearly, with the middle almost bare.
6. (mcq) Anjali slides two ring magnets onto a pencil. They snap together. She takes the top one off, turns it over and slides it back on. What happens now?  
   Options: They snap together even harder / The top magnet floats above the bottom one / Both magnets stop being magnets / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** The top magnet floats above the bottom one. *Why:* Turning the top magnet over puts the same kind of pole face to face, for example north facing north. Poles that are the same repel each other, so the top magnet floats. A strong answer says which poles now face each other.
7. (fill) When two magnets push each other away, we say they ____ each other.  
   **Answer:** ['repel']. *Why:* Repel means push away. Two north poles, or two south poles, repel each other. A north pole and a south pole attract each other.
8. (mcq) Revathi strokes a sewing needle 50 times with a magnet, always the same way. Now the needle picks up a pin. What changed inside the needle?  
   Options: Some iron moved from the magnet into the needle / The needle became heavier / The tiny magnets inside the needle now point the same way / The needle became hotter  
   **Answer:** The tiny magnets inside the needle now point the same way. *Why:* Nothing passes from the magnet into the needle. The stroking turns the needle's own tiny magnets to point the same way, so their forces add up. A strong answer uses the words 'tiny magnets' and 'line up'.

## Applications
- Compasses for sailors, hikers, pilots and soldiers.
- Fridge doors and cupboard catches stay shut with magnets.
- Scrap yards and recycling plants lift iron and steel out of mixed waste with giant magnets.
- Every electric fan, mixer, washing machine and car motor has magnets inside.
- Most power stations spin magnets near coils of wire to make electricity.
- Speakers, earphones and phone vibrators use magnets to make sound and buzzing.
- Hospitals use MRI scanners, with huge magnets, to see inside the body without cutting.
- Maglev trains, like the one in Shanghai, float above the track, held up by magnetic force.
- Hard disks and the magnetic strips on old bank cards store information as tiny lined-up magnets.
- Small, very strong neodymium magnets, invented in the 1980s, are used in wind turbines and electric cars.
- Doctors once used magnets to draw iron splinters out of eyes and wounds, as the Sushruta Samhita describes.
- Geologists use magnetometers, instruments that measure magnetic fields, to find iron ore and to read the history of the Earth's magnetic field in old rocks.
- Scientists use the magnetic bacteria's tiny crystals to study how living things build minerals.
- Magnetic observatories, like the one at Alibag, help warn about solar storms that can disturb power lines and satellites.

## Open questions
- **What is an electron's spin, really?** Scientists can measure it very exactly and use it every day, but cannot say what it is underneath. Some think it needs no further explanation.
- **Exactly how do birds sense the Earth's magnetic field?** The leading idea is cryptochrome, a protein in their eyes that works through electron spins. It has been shown in the lab but not yet fully proven in living birds.
- **Can people sense the Earth's magnetic field, even without knowing it?** One 2019 study found brainwave changes in some people. It has not yet been repeated by other teams.
- **When will the Earth's magnetic field flip next?** Flips don't come on a schedule. The field has weakened a little over the last 200 years, but that may or may not mean a flip is coming. Nobody can predict it yet.
- **Why does the magnetic north pole move so fast, and why did it speed up and then slow down?** It depends on how the liquid iron moves deep in the core, which nobody can see directly. Scientists use computer models and argue about them.
- **Do magnetic monopoles, magnets with only one pole, exist anywhere?** Some theories say they should have been made just after the universe began. Physicists have searched for nearly a hundred years and found none.
- **How exactly did lodestones become magnets?** Lightning explains some, but maybe not all. Scientists are still studying which kinds of magnetite hold their magnet best.
- **How does the hot liquid core keep making a magnetic field for billions of years?** Scientists call the moving iron in the core the geodynamo. It is so complicated that computers can only copy it roughly. How the Earth's magnetic field first started, and how it has lasted, are still argued about.

## Connected maths
- In the paper clip chain test, the neodymium magnet held 12 clips. The ceramic magnet held 4 clips. How many times as many clips did the neodymium magnet hold? **3 times as many.**. The neodymium magnet held 12 clips and the ceramic magnet held 4. 12 divided by 4 is 3. So it held 3 times as many. Check: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- To make a needle into a magnet, you stroke it in 5 rounds of 10 strokes. How many strokes is that altogether? **50 strokes.**. Each round is 10 strokes. There are 5 rounds. 5 groups of 10 is 50. You can count in tens: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- The Chinese book Wujing Zongyao described an iron fish compass in 1044. William Gilbert said the Earth is a magnet in 1600. How many years apart are they? **556 years.**. Take the earlier year from the later year. 1600 take away 1044. 1600 take away 1000 is 600. 600 take away 44 is 556. So they are 556 years apart. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Look at the map of your home. You stand at the front door facing north. You turn to face east, then turn again to face south. How many quarter turns did you make, and which way did you turn? **2 quarter turns, both to the right. That is a half turn.**. North to east is one quarter turn to the right. East to south is another quarter turn to the right. 1 + 1 = 2 quarter turns. Two quarter turns make a half turn, so now you face the opposite way from north. (queues `math.position.directions-and-grids.L3`)
- Look at the scale. It shows how far a compass was from a steel almirah when the almirah stopped turning its needle. Each big mark is 10 cm. Each small mark is 5 cm. What is the reading? **75 centimetres.**. The pointer is past the 70 mark but before 80. It sits on the small mark halfway between them. Each small mark is 5 cm. 70 + 5 = 75. So the compass had to be 75 cm away. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)

- Which way does a compass point? → North and south.
