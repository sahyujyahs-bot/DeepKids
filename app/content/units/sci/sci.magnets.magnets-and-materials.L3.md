# Magnets: why a pin jumps to a magnet
`sci.magnets.magnets-and-materials.L3`

**Summary.** A magnet pulls iron without touching it. It also pulls most steel, nickel and cobalt. It does not pull wood, plastic, paper or most other metals. The pull comes from inside the iron. Each tiny bit of iron is a tiny magnet. In a magnet, these tiny magnets all point the same way, so their pulls add up. In an ordinary nail, they point in different directions, so their pulls cancel out. Long ago, people in China, India, Greece and the Islamic world used magnet stones to steer ships. The whole Earth is a giant magnet too. Turtles, birds and even some bacteria can feel it. Nobody knows yet exactly how some animals feel it.

**Intro.** *Try this. Hold a magnet near a steel safety pin. Move it closer very slowly, and stop just before they touch. Did the pin jump? Nothing touched it. So what reached across that gap?* People have asked that question for thousands of years. In Sanskrit, a magnet is called chumbaka, which means 'the kisser'. An old Chinese book from about 2,250 years ago calls it the 'loving stone' that calls iron to come. Both names tell the same story. A magnet seems to want iron. In this unit we find out what is really happening. We go deep inside the iron, down to its atoms. Then we go out to the whole Earth, which is a magnet too.

## Terms
- **Magnet**: A thing that pulls iron towards it, without touching it. A magnet can also push or pull another magnet. *e.g.* A fridge magnet holds a list on the fridge door, because the door has iron in it.
- **Attract**: To pull something towards you. Magnets attract iron. *e.g.* A magnet attracts a steel pin across a small gap.
- **Magnetic material**: A material that a magnet pulls. The main ones are iron, most kinds of steel, nickel and cobalt. *e.g.* An iron nail and a steel safety pin are magnetic materials.
- **Non-magnetic material**: A material that a magnet does not pull, as far as we can feel. *e.g.* Wood, plastic, glass, paper, copper, brass and aluminium are non-magnetic.
- **Steel**: Iron mixed with a little of other things, to make it stronger. Most steel is magnetic. Some stainless steel, used for kitchen vessels, is not. *e.g.* A steel almirah holds a magnet. Some steel tumblers do not.
- **Pole**: One of the two ends of a magnet, where the pull is strongest. One end is called the north pole and the other the south pole. *e.g.* Dip a bar magnet in paper clips. Most clips cling to the two ends.
- **Compass**: A small magnet that can turn freely. It turns until one end points north. *e.g.* Sailors and hikers use a compass to find their way when they cannot see the Sun or the stars.
- **Magnetise**: To turn a piece of iron or steel into a magnet. One way is to stroke it with a magnet, many times, always in the same direction. *e.g.* Stroke a sewing needle 50 times with a magnet. Now the needle can pick up a pin.
- **Lodestone**: A natural magnet. It is a dark rock made of a mineral called magnetite, which has iron in it. Some pieces of it are magnets, straight out of the ground. *e.g.* Long ago, people in China, India and Greece found lodestones that picked up iron.
- **Magnetite**: A black mineral made of iron and oxygen. A magnet pulls it. Tiny grains of it are in most sand and soil. *e.g.* Drag a magnet through dry sand. The black grains that cling to it are magnetite.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* An iron nail, your hand and the air are all made of atoms.
- **Electron**: An even tinier bit on the outside of every atom. Every electron is also a tiny magnet, with its own north and south. *e.g.* Every atom in your body has electrons. In you, their tiny magnets point every which way and cancel out.
- **Spin**: The name scientists give to the tiny magnet built into every electron. Electrons are not really spinning like tops. Spin is just something every electron has, like it has electric charge. *e.g.* Two electrons that share a home in an atom usually point their spins opposite ways, so their tiny magnets cancel.
- **Domain**: A tiny patch inside iron where millions of atoms all point their tiny magnets the same way. Each domain is a team. *e.g.* In a plain nail, the teams point in different directions and cancel out. In a magnet, most teams point the same way.
- **Magnetic field**: The space around a magnet where its pull can be felt. We cannot see it, but iron grains and compasses show its shape. *e.g.* Sprinkle black sand on paper over a magnet and tap it. The grains line up in curved lines.
- **Temporary magnet**: A piece of iron that acts as a magnet only while a magnet is near it. *e.g.* A paper clip hanging from a magnet can hold another clip. Take the magnet away and the clips drop.
- **Permanent magnet**: A magnet that keeps its pull for a long time, by itself. *e.g.* A fridge magnet, a bar magnet and a lodestone are permanent magnets.
- **Curie point**: The temperature at which a magnet stops being a magnet, because the heat shakes its tiny magnets out of line. For iron it is about 770 degrees Celsius, when iron glows red. *e.g.* A magnetised needle held in a gas flame until it glows red is no longer a magnet once it cools.
- **Earth's core**: The centre of the Earth, about 3,000 kilometres under our feet. It is mostly iron and nickel. The outer part is liquid metal and the inner part is solid. *e.g.* Moving liquid iron in the outer core makes the Earth into a giant magnet.
- **Aurora**: Coloured lights in the night sky, mostly near the North and South Poles. They glow when bits from the Sun crash into the air, guided by the Earth's magnet. *e.g.* In May 2024, red auroras were photographed from Hanle in Ladakh.
- **Magnetic sense**: The ability of some living things to feel the Earth's magnet and use it to find their way. Scientists call it magnetoreception. *e.g.* Young sea turtles use it to find their way across the ocean.

## Explanations
### What a magnet pulls · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnet pulls iron, most kinds of steel, nickel and cobalt. These are called magnetic materials. A magnet does not pull wood, plastic, glass, paper or cloth, or most other metals.

Many people think a magnet pulls every metal. It does not. Copper, brass, aluminium, gold and silver are all metals, and a magnet ignores them. Steel is mostly iron, so most steel is pulled. Some stainless steel, used for kitchen vessels, is made in a special way and is hardly pulled at all. A magnet's pull also goes through things. It can pull a pin through paper, cloth, wood or water. And the pull gets weaker very fast as you move further away.
- e.g. A magnet sticks to a steel almirah but not to a brass diya.
- e.g. A magnet can drag a paper clip along the underside of a table, through the wood.
- e.g. A one-rupee coin may or may not stick, depending on what metal it is made of. Test yours.
### Every electron is a tiny magnet · *defined*
**Definition:** Every atom has tiny bits on its outside called electrons. Every electron is itself a tiny magnet, with a north and a south. Scientists call this tiny magnet the electron's spin.

So why isn't everything a magnet? Because electrons in an atom usually live in pairs. In each pair, one electron points its tiny magnet one way, and the other points the opposite way. The two cancel out, like two children pulling a rope equally hard in opposite directions. Nothing moves. That is what happens in wood, plastic, water, copper and you. In a few kinds of atoms, like iron, some electrons have no partner. Their tiny magnets are not cancelled. So each iron atom is a little magnet.
- e.g. In a copper atom, all the electrons but one are in pairs, and that last one is shared with the other copper atoms. So copper is not magnetic.
- e.g. An iron atom has four electrons without partners. So every iron atom is a tiny magnet.
### Teams of tiny magnets: domains · *defined*
**Definition:** Inside iron, the tiny atom-magnets line up with their neighbours in patches called domains. Each domain is a team of millions of atoms all pointing the same way.

In a plain iron nail, there are many domains. They point in different directions, so their pulls cancel out. The nail is not a magnet. Bring a magnet near, and the domains turn to line up with it. Now the nail is a magnet too, for as long as the magnet is near. That is why one paper clip hanging from a magnet can hold another clip. In a permanent magnet, most domains stay lined up by themselves. The French scientist Pierre Weiss guessed that domains exist in 1907. In 1931 the American scientist Francis Bitter first showed their edges, using very fine iron powder in oil under a microscope. Why do iron's tiny magnets want to point like their neighbours? The answer comes from quantum physics, the science of the very tiniest things.
- e.g. A chain of paper clips hangs from a magnet. Each clip becomes a temporary magnet that holds the next one.
- e.g. Stroke a needle with a magnet and its domains line up. Now the needle is a magnet.
### Making a magnet, and breaking one · *defined*
**Definition:** You can make a magnet by lining up the domains in iron or steel. You can break a magnet by shaking them out of line again, with heat or hard knocks.

Stroke a steel needle with a magnet, 50 times, always the same way. Each stroke tugs more domains into line, and the needle becomes a magnet. Nothing was added to the needle. Nothing was taken away. Only the line-up changed. Heat does the opposite. Heat is really atoms jiggling. Heat iron until it glows red, at about 770 degrees Celsius, and the atoms jiggle so hard that they cannot stay lined up. The magnet is gone. This temperature is called the Curie point, after Pierre Curie, who studied it in 1895. Marie Curie's very first research, in 1897, was on magnets too. She measured how well many kinds of steel could hold their magnetism, before she went on to discover radium.
- e.g. Dropping or hammering a magnet many times can weaken it, because the knocks shake the domains.
- e.g. Old soft-iron nails lose their magnetism quickly. Hard steel, like a needle, keeps it for days or weeks.
### What is an electron's spin, really? · *debated*
Scientists agree that every electron has a tiny magnet built in, called spin. They can measure it very exactly. But what is it, underneath? People have given different answers, and the question is still argued about.
- e.g. In 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent silver atoms flying between magnets. The atoms split into just two streams, not a smear. Something inside each atom could only point 'up' or 'down'.
- e.g. Nobody has ever seen an electron, or watched it turn.
- **George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit, two young scientists in the Netherlands, in 1925:** The electron is a tiny ball of charge spinning like a top. A spinning charge makes a magnet. That is how spin got its name.
- **Most physicists today:** The electron cannot really be a spinning ball. Its surface would have to move faster than light. Spin is just a basic property of every electron, like its charge. It does not need anything under it.
- **The physicist Richard Feynman, in a 1983 BBC interview:** When we ask 'why do magnets pull?', each answer leads to another 'why'. At some point we can only say: this is how the world behaves. Some physicists and philosophers think that is not a real answer, and keep looking.
### Magnetism and electricity are one force · *defined*
**Definition:** Magnetism and electricity are two sides of one force, which scientists call the electromagnetic force. Moving electric charge makes a magnet, and a moving magnet makes electricity.

In 1820, a Danish scientist, Hans Christian Oersted, noticed that a compass needle twitched when electricity flowed in a wire nearby. The electric current was making a magnet. Michael Faraday, who had left school at 13, then found the reverse in 1831. Moving a magnet in a coil of wire makes electricity. In 1865, James Clerk Maxwell wrote down the rules of both in one set of equations. He found that light itself is a wave of electricity and magnetism. Later, in 1905, Albert Einstein showed that magnetism is what electric pushes and pulls look like when charges move. The same force also holds atoms together and makes friction.
- e.g. A doorbell, a fan and a mixer all have electric magnets inside.
- e.g. Every power station spins magnets near coils of wire to make electricity.
- e.g. Light from the Sun is a wave of electricity and magnetism.
### Lodestone: the stone that finds iron · *defined*
**Definition:** A lodestone is a natural magnet. It is a piece of a black mineral called magnetite, which is made of iron and oxygen. Many cultures found lodestones long ago.

In Greece, about 2,600 years ago, the thinker Thales is said to have wondered whether a lodestone has a soul, because it moves iron. In China, a book from about 240 BCE, the Lüshi Chunqiu, says the loving stone calls iron to come. In India, the magnet was called ayaskanta, which means 'beloved of iron', and chumbaka, 'the kisser'. The Sushruta Samhita, an old Indian book of surgery, describes using a magnet to pull a smooth iron arrowhead out of a wound. Later, people learned to steer by magnets. A Chinese military book from 1044, the Wujing Zongyao, describes an iron 'south-pointing fish' floated in water. In 1088, the Chinese scholar Shen Kuo described a magnetised needle. He also noticed that it does not point exactly north. Around 1232, Muhammad al-Awfi, a writer at the court of Delhi, described sailors floating an iron fish rubbed with a magnet to find their way. In Europe, Petrus Peregrinus described a magnet's two poles in 1269.
- e.g. Lodestones were set in brass or silver caps, so people could carry them and rub needles with them.
- e.g. Iron fish compasses floated in a bowl of water, like the needle compass in this unit's experiment.
### How did lodestones become magnets? · *debated*
Most magnetite is not a magnet. Only a few pieces are lodestones. The Earth's own magnet is far too weak to make such a strong magnet. So where did lodestones get their pull? Scientists have different ideas.
- e.g. Lodestones are usually found near the surface of the ground, not deep inside it.
- e.g. A lightning strike carries a huge electric current, and electric currents make magnets.
- **Peter Wasilewski and Gunther Kletetschka, scientists in the USA, in 1999:** Lightning did it. When lightning strikes the ground, its huge current makes a strong magnet for a moment. That can line up the domains in magnetite nearby.
- **Other scientists:** Lightning explains some lodestones, but maybe not all. The kind of magnetite matters too. Some magnetite holds a line-up much better than others. The full answer is still being studied.
### The Earth is a giant magnet · *defined*
**Definition:** The whole Earth is a magnet. Its magnet is made by liquid iron moving in the Earth's outer core, about 3,000 kilometres under our feet.

In 1600, an English doctor, William Gilbert, made a ball out of lodestone. He called it a 'little Earth'. He moved a tiny compass over it, and the compass behaved just as compasses do on the real Earth. So he said the Earth itself is a magnet. That is why a compass points north. Today we know more. The Earth's centre is mostly iron and nickel. The outer part of the core is liquid metal, hotter than the surface of the Sun. It swirls slowly as the Earth spins. Moving metal carries electric currents, and electric currents make magnets. In 1936, a Danish scientist, Inge Lehmann, studied earthquake waves passing through the Earth. She found that the very centre is solid, a ball inside the liquid. A compass does not point exactly at the North Pole. It points at the magnetic north pole, which is some distance away.
- e.g. Hang a magnetised needle on a thread, far from any iron. It turns until it points north and south.
- e.g. In most of India, a compass points only a degree or two away from true north. In Canada, it can point far off.
### The Earth's magnet wanders and flips · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's magnet is not fixed. Its magnetic north pole moves every year. Every so often, over hundreds of thousands of years, the whole magnet flips, so that a compass would point south.

When hot lava cools into rock, tiny magnetite grains in it line up with the Earth's magnet, then freeze in place. So old rocks are a record of where the Earth's magnet pointed. In 1906, a French scientist, Bernard Brunhes, found rocks magnetised the 'wrong' way. In 1929, a Japanese scientist, Motonori Matuyama, showed from rocks in Japan and Korea that the Earth's magnet has flipped many times. The last flip was about 780,000 years ago. Today the magnetic north pole is moving from Canada towards Siberia, tens of kilometres every year. India has watched the Earth's magnet for a long time. An observatory at Colaba in Bombay began measuring it in 1841. In 1904 it moved to Alibag, because Bombay's new electric trams would have disturbed the measurements. Its records are still kept.
- e.g. The Colaba observatory recorded a huge magnetic storm in 1859, when auroras were seen over much of the world.
- e.g. Stripes of rock on the ocean floor are magnetised one way, then the other, like a barcode of flips.
### The Earth's magnet shields us, and makes auroras · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's magnet reaches far out into space. It turns aside most of the fast, tiny bits that stream out from the Sun, called the solar wind.

The Sun blows out a steady wind of tiny charged bits. Charged bits are steered by magnets. So the Earth's magnet bends most of the solar wind around our planet. Some bits are led down towards the North and South Poles. There they crash into the air high above us and make it glow. These glowing curtains of light are auroras. When the Sun has a big storm, auroras can be seen much further from the poles. In May 2024, cameras at the Indian Astronomical Observatory in Hanle, Ladakh, photographed a red aurora. Mars has almost no magnet around the whole planet today. Many scientists think that, without one, the solar wind slowly stripped away much of Mars's air.
- e.g. Big solar storms can disturb compasses, radio and satellites.
- e.g. Auroras are green, red and sometimes purple, depending on which gas in the air glows.
### Living things that feel the Earth's magnet · *debated*
Many animals seem to use the Earth's magnet to find their way. For some, scientists have strong proof. How they feel it is still argued about.
- e.g. Some bacteria, living things far too small to see, grow a chain of tiny magnetite crystals inside them. The chain works like a compass needle, so they swim along the Earth's magnetic lines. Salvatore Bellini saw them in Italy in 1963, but his notes were not published. Richard Blakemore described them in the USA in 1975.
- e.g. Kenneth and Catherine Lohmann have shown that young loggerhead sea turtles can tell where they are in the ocean from the strength and slant of the Earth's magnet. Olive ridley turtles nest on Odisha's beaches, and may do the same, but nobody has tested them as carefully.
- e.g. In 1972, Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko showed that European robins, small birds, still pick the right way to migrate inside a closed room, using only the Earth's magnet.
- **Many scientists studying birds:** Birds see the magnet with their eyes. Light hits a special substance in the eye, called cryptochrome, and makes pairs of electrons whose spins are affected by the Earth's magnet. In 2021, a team led by Jingjing Xu and Henrik Mouritsen showed that robin cryptochrome responds to magnets in the lab.
- **Other scientists:** Some animals may carry tiny magnetite crystals, like the bacteria do. For a while, iron-rich cells in pigeon beaks looked like the answer. In 2012, careful tests showed those cells were blood cleaner cells, not magnet sensors. The search goes on.
- **About people:** In 2019, Connie Wang, Joseph Kirschvink and others reported that some people's brainwaves changed when a magnet was turned around them. They did not feel anything. Other scientists say this needs to be tested again before we believe it.
### Everything answers a magnet, a little · *defined*
**Definition:** Even 'non-magnetic' things react to a very strong magnet. Some are pulled a tiny bit, and some are pushed away a tiny bit.

Water, wood, plastic and you are pushed away from a magnet. The push is far too weak to feel with a home magnet. In a giant lab magnet, it is strong enough to make a live frog float in the air. Andre Geim and Michael Berry did this in the Netherlands in 1997. The frog was unharmed. Some things are pulled a little, too. Liquid oxygen, very cold, clings between the ends of a strong magnet. So 'magnetic' and 'non-magnetic' are not two separate kinds of stuff. They are big and tiny amounts of the same thing.
- e.g. A strong magnet held near a floating grape on a needle can make it turn a little away. This needs a very strong magnet.
- e.g. Liquid oxygen hangs between the ends of a strong magnet like a little bridge.

## Mostly agreed
- Magnets pull iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. They do not pull most other materials, including most metals. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with a magnet at home. Scientists have tested thousands of materials in the lab.
- A magnet's pull comes from electrons, which are each a tiny magnet. In iron, they line up in teams called domains. *How we know:* Scientists can measure the tiny magnet of a single electron. They can also see domains under special microscopes and watch their edges move as a magnet comes near.
- Iron and steel can be made into magnets, and magnets can be weakened by heat and knocks. *How we know:* Stroke a needle and it picks up a pin. Heat it red-hot and it stops. Pierre Curie measured the exact temperatures in 1895.
- The Earth is a magnet, made by moving liquid iron deep inside it. Its magnet has flipped many times. *How we know:* Compasses point north everywhere on Earth. Earthquake waves show a liquid metal core. Old lava rocks are magnetised in both directions.
- Some living things use the Earth's magnet to find their way. *How we know:* Magnetic bacteria swim along magnetic lines. Young sea turtles and robins change direction when scientists change the magnet around them.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A magnet pulls all metals.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Most things a magnet sticks to around the house are metal, like the fridge, the almirah and pins.  
  Against: Test a brass key, a copper vessel, aluminium foil or a gold ring. A magnet ignores them all. Only metals with iron, nickel or cobalt are pulled.
- **A compass points to the North Pole, or to the Pole Star.** (Many people, and many old books; *rejected*)  
  For: A compass points roughly north, and the Pole Star is roughly north too.  
  Against: A compass points to the magnetic north pole, which is not the same place as the North Pole, and which moves every year. Shen Kuo noticed the difference in China in 1088. The Pole Star has nothing to do with it.
- **Magnets were discovered by a shepherd called Magnes, whose iron-nailed sandals stuck to a rock.** (A legend told by the Roman writer Pliny the Elder, about 1,950 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: It is a good story, and the word 'magnet' does sound like 'Magnes'.  
  Against: There is no evidence the shepherd was real. Most experts think the word comes from Magnesia, a region in Greece and Turkey where lodestones were found. And people in China and India knew lodestones too.
- **Indian sailors invented the compass, using an iron fish floating in oil called a matsya yantra.** (Some writers in the 1900s, and many websites; *minority*)  
  For: Indian Ocean sailors did use floating iron fish compasses. Al-Awfi, writing at the Delhi court around 1232, describes one.  
  Against: No old Indian text has been found that describes a matsya yantra compass. The oldest clear written descriptions of magnetic compasses are Chinese, from 1044 and 1088. Ideas probably travelled along the trade routes.
- **Cows and deer line up north and south as they graze, because they feel the Earth's magnet.** (Sabine Begall and other scientists, studying satellite pictures, in 2008; *debated*)  
  For: In thousands of satellite pictures, grazing cattle seemed to face north or south more often than other ways.  
  Against: In 2011, another team looked at new pictures and found no such pattern. Wind, sunlight and slopes also change which way cows face.
- **Magnet bracelets and magnet mats cure pain and illness.** (Many sellers, in India and around the world; *rejected*)  
  For: Some people say they feel better when they wear them.  
  Against: Careful tests compare real magnets with fake ones that look the same. People feel about as much better with the fake ones. In 2007, a review of many such tests found no good evidence that magnets help pain. Feeling better because we expect to is real, but it is not the magnet.
- **People can sense the Earth's magnet.** (Connie Wang, Joseph Kirschvink and others, in 2019; *debated*)  
  For: In a quiet test room, some people's brainwaves changed when a magnet was turned around them in one particular way.  
  Against: Nobody in the test felt anything. Only some people showed it, and the result has not yet been repeated by other teams.

## Roots
We have been learning about magnets, and the tiny electron magnets inside iron. Now we ask what magnets tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A magnet is not a different kind of stuff. It is ordinary iron whose tiny parts all point the same way.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort things into 'magnets' and 'not magnets', as if the power lived in a special stone. But every electron is already a tiny magnet. A nail and a magnet are the same iron, arranged differently.  
*Evidence:* Stroke a needle and it becomes a magnet. Heat it red-hot and it stops. Stroke it again and it is a magnet again. Nothing was added or taken away. Only the line-up changed.

- *who am i:* You are full of tiny electron magnets too. In you, they point every which way and cancel out.
- *what is life:* Turtles, robins and tiny bacteria feel the Earth's magnet and find their way by it, each living its own life.
- *what is this world:* The whole Earth is a magnet, made by a sea of moving iron far beneath your feet.

Inside a plain nail, countless tiny magnets point every way. Their pulls add up to zero, a perfect balance. Nothing seems to be there. Line them up, and one pull appears out of that zero. That one pull then reaches out to many things: a pin, a needle, a ship's course, a turtle's journey and the lights in the sky. DeepKids sees in this a small picture of the One becoming the many, where nothing new is made, only arranged.

- **Rig Veda 10.191.2, the hymn of coming together (Samjnana Sukta):** Come together. Speak together. Let your minds be in agreement. (Tiny magnets that each point their own way cancel out. When they point together, they make a pull that none of them has alone.)
- **Tao Te Ching 42:** All things carry yin on their backs and hold yang in their arms. By blending the two, they reach harmony. (Every magnet has two ends, north and south, and you can never have one without the other. In a nail, the pulls balance perfectly.)
- **Empedocles, fragment 17 (Diels-Kranz numbering), about 450 BCE:** Sometimes all things come together into one through Love. Sometimes they are carried apart by Strife. (Magnets pull together and push apart. The old names for magnets, 'the kisser' and 'the loving stone', saw the same thing.)
- Science (settled): Every electron is a tiny magnet. In most atoms they pair up and cancel. In iron, they line up in domains.
- Science (settled): Electricity, magnetism and light are one force, electromagnetism. Maxwell joined them in 1865.
- Science (open): What an electron's spin is, underneath, has no agreed answer.
- Other view, Many physicists: Lining up is simply how electrons in iron behave. It is a beautiful fact, but it is not a message about agreeing, or about oneness. Reading lessons into iron says more about us than about iron. That is an honest view.
- Practice, Be the needle (3 min): Let's stand up and close our eyes. Without peeking, point to where you think north is. Now open your eyes and check with the compass. Now let's all turn to face north together. Stand still and take six slow breaths. We're like the tiny magnets in a needle, all facing the same way. Notice: are you still you?
- Ask: When everyone in your family faces the same way, are you still you?
- Ask: In a nail, the tiny magnets cancel to zero. Is the pull gone, or only hidden?
- Ask: A turtle finds its beach without a map. Does it know something we don't?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Every electron in your body is a tiny magnet. So why don't you stick to the fridge? (hint: What happens when two children pull a rope equally hard in opposite directions?)
- *deep:* A magnet pulls a pin through paper, through wood, even through your hand. What is in the gap that does the pulling? (hint: Scientists call it a field. Is an invisible field a 'thing'? Is empty space really empty?)
- *deep:* When you stroke a needle and it becomes a magnet, nothing is added and nothing is taken away. So what changed? (hint: Think about a crowd of people facing every way, and then all facing the same way.)
- *deep:* The Earth's magnet has flipped many times. What might happen to birds and turtles during a flip? (hint: Flips take thousands of years. Animals have lived through many of them.)
- *big:* A turtle can find the beach where it hatched, across a whole ocean, without a map. What does a turtle know that we don't? (hint: Do we sometimes think people are the cleverest animals only because we test the things we are good at?)
- *big:* Tiny magnets that each point their own way cancel out. When they all point together, they make a pull. When does pulling together make a family, a class or a town stronger? When could it be a bad thing? (hint: Think of a crowd that all runs one way. Is that always wise?)
- *big:* Old names for the magnet mean 'the kisser', 'beloved of iron' and 'the loving stone'. Why do you think so many people thought of magnets as loving? (hint: Is there a pull in your life that you can't see, but can feel?)
- *big:* Marie Curie's first research was on magnets in steel. Many books mention Pierre Curie's magnet work, but not hers. Why might that be? (hint: Who usually gets written down in history books? Who gets left out?)
- *big:* The Earth's magnet shields us from the Sun's wind, and we never feel it. What else protects us that we never notice? (hint: Think of the air, the ozone, the people who keep water clean.)
- *deep:* Scientists can measure an electron's spin very exactly, but can't say what it 'is'. Is it enough to know how something behaves, or do we need to know what it is? (hint: Can you describe your best friend without knowing what they are made of?)

## Story: The Iron Fish
*Floating iron fish compasses rubbed with a lodestone are real: Muhammad al-Awfi, writing at the Delhi court around 1232, describes sailors using one, and ships really sailed between Khambhat in Gujarat and Aden. Amina, her mother, the pilot and this voyage are made up.*

The ship was called the Swift Gazelle, and it smelled of cloves, tar and the sea. It had left Khambhat, in Gujarat, nine days ago. It was carrying cotton cloth and indigo to Aden, far across the Arabian Sea. Amina was ten. She was the only child on board, because her mother was the ship's cook.

Amina's favourite person on the ship, after her mother, was the pilot, old Yusuf. He knew the stars the way Amina knew the path to her grandmother's well. At night he sat with his chin up and his eyes half closed, reading the sky. He told the helmsman where to steer.

But Yusuf had a second way of finding the way. It lived in a wooden box near the back of the ship. Inside the box was a small brass bowl. Inside a silk bag was a lumpy black stone. And inside a fold of cloth was a fish, no longer than Amina's finger, cut from a thin sheet of iron.

On the fourth night, Yusuf had let her watch. He poured water into the brass bowl. He took out the black stone and rubbed it along the iron fish, from tail to nose. He lifted it away, and rubbed again, from tail to nose. Never back and forth. Always the same way. He counted under his breath, all the way to fifty. Then he laid the fish gently on the water, where it floated like a leaf.

The fish shivered. It turned slowly, as if it were sniffing the air, and then it stopped. Its nose pointed to the south. Amina turned the bowl. The fish turned back. She turned the bowl the other way. The fish turned back again, to the south, every time.

'It is hungry for the south,' said Yusuf. 'The stone teaches it. You will not need it often. But when the clouds come, the fish is the only one on this ship who still knows the way.'

Amina also learned what else the black stone could do. In the galley, the ship's tiny kitchen, she held it near her mother's iron ladle. The ladle leapt to the stone with a click. The stone ignored the brass cups, the clay pots and the wooden spoons. It wanted only iron.

On the eleventh day, the clouds came.

They came low and grey, and then black. The wind rose and the rain hit the deck like thrown rice. That night there were no stars at all. The ship rolled so hard that her mother tied the big iron cooking pot, the deg, to the back wall with a rope, right beside Yusuf's wooden box, so it could not slide about and break someone's leg.

Yusuf brought out the brass bowl. He floated the iron fish. Its nose swung round and settled. He called his direction to the helmsman, and the ship turned.

For a long time, nobody spoke. Amina sat wrapped in a blanket, watching the fish by the light of a little oil lamp. Something was wrong, and it took her a while to see what it was. The fish was not pointing where it had pointed on the fourth night. It was pointing at the back wall of the ship. It was pointing at the deg.

Her heart thumped. She thought of the ladle leaping to the stone. If the stone wanted iron, did the fish want iron too? She took the lamp, crept to the back wall, and moved the lamp past the big pot. Then she held her mother's iron ladle near the bowl. The fish swung towards the ladle as if it had been called.

'Yusuf,' she whispered. Then louder, 'Yusuf! The fish is looking at the pot!'

The pilot frowned. He looked at the bowl, at the pot, at the ladle in her hand. Then he said a word Amina's mother would not have liked, and shouted for two sailors to untie the deg and carry it to the front of the ship.

When the pot was gone, the fish shivered. It turned, slowly, slowly, nearly a quarter of the way round. And there it stopped, pointing to the south.

Yusuf looked at it for a long time. 'We have been steering towards a cooking pot,' he said at last. 'For half the night.' He called the new direction to the helmsman. The Swift Gazelle leaned, creaked and turned.

But the fish seemed lazy now, slow to turn and quick to wander. Yusuf's hands were shaking with cold. So Amina opened the silk bag and took out the black stone. From tail to nose. Lift away. From tail to nose. Never back and forth. She counted to fifty, as she had heard him count. When she floated the fish again, it swung round at once, quick and sure.

In the grey morning, the rain stopped. The sailors leaned over the rail and pointed. Beside the ship, in the dark water, swam three sea turtles, heading the same way the ship was going. They had no bowl, no stone and no iron fish. They swam steadily on, as if they had never been lost at all.

'How do they know the way?' Amina asked.

Yusuf watched them go. 'I have sailed for forty years,' he said, 'and I have never once known.'

Two days later they saw the brown mountains behind Aden. After that, on every voyage, the big iron deg travelled at the front of the ship. And every night, in the fold of cloth beside the brass bowl, the black stone and the iron fish lay waiting for a girl who knew the way to rub them.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the fish is a magnet, and a magnet pulls iron. So the big iron cooking pot pulled the fish's nose towards it, and the ship steered towards the pot instead of south. Moving the iron away let the fish line up with the Earth's magnet again. Stroking it with the lodestone, always in one direction, lined up the tiny magnets inside the iron, so its pull grew strong again. The turtles at the end know the way without any iron at all, because their own bodies sense the Earth's magnet. Nobody yet fully knows how.

- Ask: Why did the iron fish point the wrong way during the storm?
- Ask: How did Amina work out what was wrong? What had she seen in the galley?
- Ask: Why did Yusuf rub the fish from tail to nose, and never back and forth?
- Ask: The turtles found the way without a fish. How do you think they did it?
- Ask: Amina was the cook's daughter, not a sailor, and she noticed what the pilot missed. Why do you think the person who notices is not always the person in charge?

### e1 · The Floating Needle Compass (30 min)
- **question:** Can we turn a sewing needle into a magnet, and will it point north?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will a plain needle, floated on water, point any special way? And what about the same needle after you stroke it with a magnet?
- **fair test:** Float the needle before stroking it, and after. Use the same bowl, the same water and the same place both times. Only the stroking changes. Turn the bowl 3 times each time, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Needles are sharp. A grown-up hands over the needle and puts it away afterwards. Never put magnets in your mouth.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the bowl from above, with an arrow for where the needle pointed, and write N where north is.

- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the bowl with water. 2. Put it on a table at least one metre away from the gas stove, the fridge, a steel almirah, phones and other magnets. 3. Lay the plain needle on the paper square and float it in the middle.  
  *Parent says:* Let's see where a plain needle points. Iron things and phones nearby could fool it, so we keep it far away from them.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the bowl. Put it far from the fridge, the stove and phones. Lay the needle on the paper. Float it in the middle. Where does it point?

  *Child mode:* Touch the needle to the pin again. Does the pin stick now?
  - Note: If the pin does not stick, your magnet is probably weak. Try 50 more strokes, or a stronger magnet. A small staple or a short piece of thin wire is lighter than a pin and easier to lift.
  - Check: After 50 strokes, the needle picks up a pin. What changed inside the needle? → **The tiny magnets inside the needle now point the same way**

### Magnet Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these will a magnet pull?  
   Options: A wooden pencil / An iron nail / A plastic spoon / A glass bangle  
   **Answer:** An iron nail. *Why:* Iron is magnetic, so a magnet pulls a nail. Wood, plastic and glass are not magnetic. Picking the bangle sometimes comes from thinking shiny things are metal.
2. (sort) Sort these. Will a magnet pull them?  
   **Answer:** a steel safety pin → Magnetic; a brass key → Not magnetic; an iron nail → Magnetic; aluminium foil → Not magnetic; a copper wire → Not magnetic; a steel paper clip → Magnetic; a rubber eraser → Not magnetic; a nickel wire → Magnetic. *Why:* Things with iron, nickel or cobalt are magnetic. Brass, aluminium, copper and rubber are not.
3. (truefalse) All metals are magnetic.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Only metals with iron, nickel or cobalt in them are pulled. Copper, brass, aluminium, gold and silver are not.
4. (truefalse) A magnet's pull can go through paper.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A magnet can pull a pin through paper, cloth, wood and even your hand.
5. (fill) A natural magnet stone, made of magnetite, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lodestone']. *Why:* Lodestones are natural magnets. People in Greece, China and India found them long ago.
6. (fill) A small magnet that can turn freely and points north is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['compass']. *Why:* A compass is a magnet that turns until it lines up with the Earth's magnet.
7. (mcq) You stroke a needle with a magnet 50 times, always the same way. What happens?  
   Options: The needle becomes a magnet / The needle melts / The magnet becomes a needle / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** The needle becomes a magnet. *Why:* Stroking lines up the tiny magnets inside the needle, so it becomes a magnet. Picking 'nothing' can happen if the magnet used was a weak fridge magnet. Try a stronger one.
8. (mcq) Why should you float the needle compass in a plastic or glass bowl, not a steel one?  
   Options: Steel bowls are too heavy / Some steel bowls are magnetic and would pull the needle to the side / Water goes bad in steel / Plastic is prettier  
   **Answer:** Some steel bowls are magnetic and would pull the needle to the side. *Why:* The needle is a magnet, and a magnet pulls iron and many kinds of steel. A magnetic steel bowl would pull the needle to its edge and fool it.
9. (order) Put the steps for making a needle compass in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Stroke the needle with a magnet 50 times, always one way', 'Check that the needle picks up a pin', 'Float it on paper in a bowl of water', 'Wait for it to stop turning, and check north']. *Why:* First make the magnet, then check it worked, then float it, then read it.
10. (mcq) In the paper clip chain test, why does each clip hold the next one?  
   Options: The clips are sticky / Each clip becomes a temporary magnet while it touches the chain / The clips are tied together / The table pushes them up  
   **Answer:** Each clip becomes a temporary magnet while it touches the chain. *Why:* Near a magnet, a clip's tiny magnets line up, so it becomes a magnet too and can hold the next clip. Take the magnet away and the chain falls apart.
11. (mcq) Where is a magnet's pull strongest?  
   Options: In the middle / At its two ends, the poles / It is the same everywhere / Only on the side with writing  
   **Answer:** At its two ends, the poles. *Why:* A magnet's pull is strongest at its two ends, called poles. That is where the black grains crowd and where most paper clips cling.
12. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Magnet → pulls iron without touching it; Lodestone → a natural magnet stone; Compass → a magnet that points north; Pole → an end of a magnet, where the pull is strongest. *Why:* These four words come up all through the unit.
13. (mcq) In the story 'The Iron Fish', the compass pointed the wrong way in the storm. Why?  
   Options: The fish was tired / A big iron cooking pot was tied right next to it / The rain washed its magnet away / The stars were hidden  
   **Answer:** A big iron cooking pot was tied right next to it. *Why:* The iron fish was a magnet, and a magnet is pulled towards iron. The big iron pot pulled its nose. When the pot was moved away, the fish pointed south again. Hidden stars made the compass needed, but did not fool it.
14. (fill) The black grains that stick to a magnet dragged through sand are a mineral called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['magnetite']. *Why:* Magnetite is made of iron and oxygen. Lodestones are made of it too.
15. (write) Why does a compass point north? Use the words 'magnet' and 'Earth' in your answer.  
   **Answer:** A compass needle is a magnet. The Earth is a giant magnet too. The Earth's magnet turns the needle until it lines up north and south.. *Why:* A full answer says that both the needle and the Earth are magnets.
16. (draw) Draw a bar magnet with paper clips hanging from it. Show where most of the clips cling.  
   **Answer:** Most clips cling at the two ends, with few or none in the middle.. *Why:* A magnet's pull is strongest at its two poles, the ends.
### Inside the Iron (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Every electron is a tiny magnet. Why is copper not magnetic?  
   Options: Copper has no electrons / In copper, the electrons' tiny magnets cancel each other out / Copper is too heavy / Copper is too shiny  
   **Answer:** In copper, the electrons' tiny magnets cancel each other out. *Why:* Copper's electrons are in pairs, or shared, so their tiny magnets cancel. Iron has electrons with no partner, so its tiny magnets don't cancel. Picking 'no electrons' misses that every atom has electrons.
2. (mcq) A plain iron nail has millions of tiny atom-magnets. Why is it not a magnet?  
   Options: Its domains point in different directions and cancel out / Its atoms are not iron / It is too thin / Nails are made of wood inside  
   **Answer:** Its domains point in different directions and cancel out. *Why:* The nail's teams of tiny magnets, its domains, point every which way, so their pulls cancel. Bring a magnet near and they line up.
3. (truefalse) When you stroke a needle and it becomes a magnet, the magnet you stroked it with gets weaker.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Nothing passes from the magnet to the needle. The needle's own tiny magnets are lined up. You can magnetise hundreds of needles with one magnet.
4. (mcq) Which would most likely take away a magnet's magnetism?  
   Options: Keeping it in a drawer / Heating it until it glows red / Washing it in cold water / Painting it  
   **Answer:** Heating it until it glows red. *Why:* Heat makes atoms jiggle. At about 770 degrees Celsius, iron's tiny magnets cannot stay lined up. Cold water and paint do not shake them.
5. (fill) The temperature at which a magnet stops being a magnet is called the ____ point.  
   **Answer:** ['Curie']. *Why:* It is named after Pierre Curie, who measured it in 1895. Marie Curie's first research was on magnets in steel too.
6. (mcq) Which of these has a magnetic sense, shown by experiments?  
   Options: European robins / Mango trees / Copper wire / Clouds  
   **Answer:** European robins. *Why:* Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko showed in 1972 that robins pick their migration direction using the Earth's magnet.
7. (match) Match each person to what they found.  
   **Answer:** William Gilbert, 1600 → the Earth is a magnet; Shen Kuo, 1088 → a compass does not point exactly north; Inge Lehmann, 1936 → the Earth's centre is solid; Motonori Matuyama, 1929 → the Earth's magnet has flipped. *Why:* People from many countries pieced together the story of the Earth's magnet.

   **Answer:** ["The Chinese book calls the magnet a 'loving stone'", 'Shen Kuo describes a magnet needle', 'Gilbert says the Earth is a magnet', 'Marie Curie studies magnets in steel']. *Why:* About 240 BCE, then 1088, then 1600, then 1897.

   Options: Fridge magnet / Ceramic magnet / Bar magnet  
   **Answer:** Ceramic magnet. *Why:* In order, 1, 4, 6. The ceramic magnet, at 4, is in the middle.
10. (mcq) Why do auroras happen mostly near the poles?  
   Options: The Earth's magnet steers the Sun's tiny charged bits there / Ice reflects the Moon / Penguins make them / It is always night there  
   **Answer:** The Earth's magnet steers the Sun's tiny charged bits there. *Why:* The Earth's magnetic lines dip into the Earth near the poles, guiding the Sun's charged bits into the air there. They make the air glow.
11. (truefalse) A compass points exactly at the North Pole.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It points at the magnetic north pole, which is a different place and moves every year. Shen Kuo noticed the difference in 1088.
12. (write) In the story 'The Iron Fish', Amina rubbed the fish with the stone 'from tail to nose, never back and forth'. Explain why, using the words 'tiny magnets'.  
   **Answer:** Each stroke one way pulls more of the tiny magnets inside the iron to point the same way. Rubbing back and forth would undo each stroke, so they would stay jumbled.. *Why:* A good answer says that the strokes line the tiny magnets up, and back-and-forth undoes it.
13. (write) Black sand from a building site gave lots of magnetite. Pot plant soil gave almost none. Give one possible reason.  
   **Answer:** River sand used for building often comes from rocks rich in magnetite. Pot plant soil is mostly old leaves and compost, with less rock in it.. *Why:* Any reason about where the sand or soil came from, and what rocks it was made from, is good.
14. (mcq) Some people sell magnet bracelets that they say cure pain. What have careful tests found?  
   Options: They cure pain every time / People feel about as much better with fake magnets as with real ones / They make pain worse / Nobody has ever tested them  
   **Answer:** People feel about as much better with fake magnets as with real ones. *Why:* When people don't know whether their bracelet is real or fake, both groups feel about the same. So the magnet itself is not doing it. A 2007 review of many tests found no good evidence.
15. (draw) Draw two squares of iron from very close up. In one, draw the tiny magnets as arrows for a plain nail. In the other, for a magnet.  
   **Answer:** Plain nail: patches of arrows pointing in different directions. Magnet: nearly all the arrows pointing the same way.. *Why:* This shows domains: jumbled in a nail, lined up in a magnet.
### Magnet Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) The Earth's core is far hotter than 770 degrees Celsius, the temperature where iron stops being a magnet. So how can the Earth be a magnet?  
   **Answer:** The Earth is not a magnet because of lined-up domains. It is a magnet because liquid iron moves in the core. Moving metal carries electric currents, and electric currents make magnets.. *Why:* This joins two ideas from the unit: heat stops lined-up magnets, but moving electricity makes magnets.
2. (write) Some old books say Indian sailors invented a fish compass called a 'matsya yantra'. What evidence would you need to decide whether that is true?  
   **Answer:** I would need an old Indian text or object that describes or shows the compass, with a date. Then I could compare that date with the Chinese texts from 1044 and 1088.. *Why:* Good answers ask for a dated source, and compare it with other evidence.
3. (mcq) A scientist sees that a type of bacteria always swims towards the north in a dish. She turns a magnet around the dish, and the bacteria turn too. What is the best conclusion?  
   Options: The bacteria can sense magnets / The bacteria like the scientist / The dish is tilted / Bacteria can see the Pole Star  
   **Answer:** The bacteria can sense magnets. *Why:* Changing only the magnet changed what the bacteria did. That is good evidence they sense magnets. These are magnetic bacteria, with tiny magnetite chains inside them.
4. (write) Cows in satellite pictures seemed to face north and south. A later study found no pattern. If you were a scientist, what would you do next?  
   **Answer:** I would watch cows myself, in many places, and record which way they face. I would also record the wind and the Sun, because those could change which way they face.. *Why:* Good answers repeat the study and think about other causes.
5. (mcq) Salvatore Bellini saw magnetic bacteria in 1963, but his notes were not published. Richard Blakemore described them in 1975. Who should get credit?  
   Options: Only Blakemore, because he published / Only Bellini, because he was first / Both, and we should say what each did / Neither  
   **Answer:** Both, and we should say what each did. *Why:* Science usually credits whoever publishes first. But being honest means saying who saw it first, too. Bellini's notes were published in English in 2009.
6. (write) Andre Geim made a live frog float in a giant magnet. The frog has no iron in it. Explain how this can be.  
   **Answer:** Everything answers a magnet a little. Water, which frogs are mostly made of, is pushed away very slightly. In a giant magnet, the push is strong enough to hold the frog up.. *Why:* This shows that 'non-magnetic' really means 'very, very slightly magnetic'.
7. (mcq) Why do scientists think Mars lost much of its air?  
   Options: Without a strong magnet around the planet, the solar wind stripped it away / Martians breathed it all / It froze into ice caps / The Moon pulled it off  
   **Answer:** Without a strong magnet around the planet, the solar wind stripped it away. *Why:* Mars has almost no magnet around the whole planet today. Many scientists think the solar wind slowly stripped its air away. Some was frozen into ice caps, but not most of it.
8. (write) A robin's magnetic sense may depend on the spins of pairs of electrons in its eye. Why is that surprising?  
   **Answer:** Electron spin is part of quantum physics, the science of the very tiniest things. People thought quantum effects were too delicate to matter in a warm, living body.. *Why:* Good answers mention that quantum physics is usually about tiny, cold, still things, not living birds.
9. (mcq) Why did the Colaba magnetic observatory move from Bombay to Alibag in 1904?  
   Options: Electric trams were coming, and their currents would disturb the measurements / The building fell down / Alibag had better food / The sea was too loud  
   **Answer:** Electric trams were coming, and their currents would disturb the measurements. *Why:* Electric currents make magnets. Bombay's new electric trams would have disturbed the delicate measurements of the Earth's magnet.
10. (write) Marie Curie's first research was on magnets in steel. Find out one thing she discovered later. Why do you think her magnet work is less well known than Pierre Curie's?  
   **Answer:** She later discovered radium and polonium. Her magnet work may be less known because books often credited men's work first, and because her later work was so famous.. *Why:* Any honest answer that names a later discovery and thinks about why credit is uneven is good.
11. (truefalse) Nobody has ever found a magnet with only one pole.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Cut a magnet in half and you get two magnets, each with two poles. Physicists have searched for single-pole magnets, called monopoles, for nearly a hundred years, and have never found one.
12. (write) Design an experiment to test whether ants can sense a magnet. Say what you would change, what you would keep the same, and what you would watch.  
   **Answer:** I would put ants in a round dish and watch which way they walk. Then I would put a strong magnet on one side, and later on the other side. I would keep the light, food and dish the same. I would count how many ants walk towards the magnet each time.. *Why:* A good design changes only the magnet, keeps everything else the same, and counts what happens.
13. (mcq) The Earth's magnetic north pole moves about 40 kilometres a year. If a map's compass note is 10 years old, how far might the pole have moved?  
   Options: 4 kilometres / 40 kilometres / 400 kilometres / 4,000 kilometres  
   **Answer:** 400 kilometres. *Why:* 40 kilometres each year, for 10 years, is 40 times 10, which is 400 kilometres. That is why compass maps are updated.
14. (write) Many old names for magnets mean 'loving' or 'kissing'. Scientists say it is just electrons lining up. Can both be true in their own way? Explain what you think.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer. For example: the science explains how the pull works. The old names describe how it looks and feels to us. Both say that the magnet and the iron seem drawn together.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. A good answer keeps the science and the feeling clear and separate.
15. (draw) Draw the Earth cut in half. Label the crust, the liquid outer core and the solid inner core. Draw curved magnetic lines around the outside.  
   **Answer:** A thin crust, a thick rocky middle, a liquid outer core and a solid inner core, with curved lines looping from one pole round to the other.. *Why:* This shows where the Earth's magnet is made, and its shape.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these will a magnet pull?  
   Options: A brass key / A steel safety pin / Aluminium foil / A copper wire  
   **Answer:** A steel safety pin. *Why:* Steel is mostly iron, so a magnet pulls it. Brass, aluminium and copper have no iron, nickel or cobalt, so a magnet ignores them. Picking any of the others usually means thinking all metals are magnetic.
2. (mcq) Which material is NOT magnetic?  
   Options: Iron / Nickel / Copper / Cobalt  
   **Answer:** Copper. *Why:* Copper is a metal, but a magnet does not pull it. Iron, nickel and cobalt are the main magnetic metals.
3. (mcq) To make a needle into a magnet, how should you stroke it?  
   Options: Back and forth, fast / Always in the same direction, lifting the magnet away each time / In circles / Just once, very hard  
   **Answer:** Always in the same direction, lifting the magnet away each time. *Why:* Each stroke in the same direction tugs more of the needle's tiny magnets into line. Stroking back and forth undoes each stroke. Picking 'just once, very hard' usually means thinking force matters more than repeating the line-up.
4. (mcq) A floating magnetised needle always turns to point north and south. Why?  
   Options: The Pole Star pulls it / The Earth is a giant magnet that lines it up / Water flows towards the north / The bowl is magnetic  
   **Answer:** The Earth is a giant magnet that lines it up. *Why:* The needle is a magnet, and the Earth's magnet turns it to line up north and south. Picking the Pole Star comes from an old idea. Stars do not pull compasses, and a compass works in a closed room.
5. (mcq) In the paper clip chain test, a tiny magnet held 12 clips and a big fridge magnet held 1. What can you say?  
   Options: The tiny magnet is stronger / The big magnet is stronger, because it is bigger / They are the same / The test was not fair, because the magnets were different sizes  
   **Answer:** The tiny magnet is stronger. *Why:* More clips in the chain means a stronger magnet. Size does not decide strength. What the magnet is made of does. Picking 'not fair' is a thoughtful idea, but here the size is part of what we are comparing, not something we must keep the same.
6. (mcq) In a fair test of which magnet is strongest, what should stay the same?  
   Options: The magnet / The kind and size of paper clips / The number of clips each magnet holds / Nothing needs to stay the same  
   **Answer:** The kind and size of paper clips. *Why:* Everything except the magnet should stay the same, including the clips. If one magnet got smaller clips, it could hold more, and the test would not be fair. The number of clips is what we measure, so it changes.
7. (mcq) Every electron is a tiny magnet. Why aren't you magnetic?  
   Options: People have no electrons / Your electrons are in pairs pointing opposite ways, so they cancel / Your electrons are too small / You are magnetic, but only on Sundays  
   **Answer:** Your electrons are in pairs pointing opposite ways, so they cancel. *Why:* In most atoms, electrons live in pairs, and the two in each pair point their tiny magnets opposite ways. So they cancel out. Picking 'too small' sounds sensible, but iron's electrons are just as small. The difference is whether they cancel.
8. (mcq) What are domains?  
   Options: The two ends of a magnet / Teams of atoms inside iron whose tiny magnets point the same way / A kind of magnet stone / The lines around the Earth  
   **Answer:** Teams of atoms inside iron whose tiny magnets point the same way. *Why:* Domains are tiny patches inside iron, where millions of atoms point their tiny magnets the same way. In a plain nail they point different ways. In a magnet they mostly line up. Picking 'the two ends' mixes domains up with poles.
9. (mcq) A magnetised needle is heated until it glows red. When it cools, it no longer picks up a pin. Why?  
   Options: The heat burned the iron away / The heat shook its tiny magnets out of line / Hot things are never magnetic, even after they cool / The pin got heavier  
   **Answer:** The heat shook its tiny magnets out of line. *Why:* Heat is atoms jiggling. At about 770 degrees Celsius, iron's tiny magnets jiggle out of line, and they stay jumbled after it cools. Stroking it again can line them up again. Picking 'burned away' is natural, but the needle is still there, still steel.
10. (mcq) What is a lodestone?  
   Options: A stone that floats / A natural magnet made of the mineral magnetite / A heavy iron weight on a ship / A magnet made in a factory  
   **Answer:** A natural magnet made of the mineral magnetite. *Why:* A lodestone is a piece of magnetite, a black mineral with iron in it, that is a magnet straight from the ground. People in Greece, China and India found them long ago.
11. (mcq) In the old Indian surgery book, the Sushruta Samhita, what is a magnet used for?  
   Options: Finding north / Pulling a smooth iron arrowhead out of a wound / Making music / Cooking  
   **Answer:** Pulling a smooth iron arrowhead out of a wound. *Why:* The Sushruta Samhita describes using a magnet, called ayaskanta, to draw out iron arrowheads without barbs. A magnet pulls iron, but not skin. Picking 'finding north' mixes this up with the compass, which came into use later.
12. (mcq) What makes the Earth a magnet?  
   Options: A giant bar magnet buried at the North Pole / Liquid iron moving in the Earth's outer core / The Moon pulling on the Earth / Lodestones in the mountains  
   **Answer:** Liquid iron moving in the Earth's outer core. *Why:* Liquid iron swirls slowly in the outer core, about 3,000 kilometres down. Moving metal carries electric currents, and currents make a magnet. Picking 'a giant bar magnet' is a common picture, but the core is far too hot for a solid magnet. Above 770 degrees, iron cannot stay a magnet.
13. (truefalse) The Earth's magnet has flipped many times, so that a compass would have pointed south.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Old lava rocks are magnetised in both directions. The last flip was about 780,000 years ago. Motonori Matuyama showed this from rocks in Japan and Korea in 1929.
14. (mcq) Which of these living things has been shown to use the Earth's magnet to find its way?  
   Options: Young loggerhead sea turtles / Mango trees / Rocks / Nobody: only compasses can  
   **Answer:** Young loggerhead sea turtles. *Why:* Experiments by Kenneth and Catherine Lohmann show young loggerhead turtles change direction when the magnet around them is changed. Robins and some bacteria use it too. Picking 'only compasses can' puts our tools at the centre. Many animals had a magnetic sense long before people made compasses.
15. (mcq) Do scientists know exactly how birds feel the Earth's magnet?  
   Options: Yes, it is fully solved / No. The leading idea is a substance in their eyes, but it is still being tested / Birds don't feel it at all / Yes, they have a tiny compass needle in their beaks  
   **Answer:** No. The leading idea is a substance in their eyes, but it is still being tested. *Why:* The leading idea is cryptochrome, a substance in birds' eyes affected by the Earth's magnet. It is not yet fully proven. The beak idea was tested in 2012, and the iron cells there turned out to be blood cleaner cells, not magnet sensors.

## Applications
- Compasses for sailors, hikers, pilots and soldiers.
- Fridge doors and cupboard catches stay shut with magnets.
- Scrap yards and recycling plants lift iron and steel out of mixed waste with giant magnets.
- Every electric fan, mixer, washing machine and car motor has magnets inside.
- Power stations spin magnets near coils of wire to make electricity.
- Speakers, earphones and phone vibrators use magnets to make sound and buzzing.
- Hospitals use MRI scanners, with huge magnets, to see inside the body without cutting.
- Maglev trains, like the one in Shanghai, float above the track on magnets.
- Hard disks and the magnetic strips on old bank cards store information as tiny lined-up magnets.
- Small, very strong neodymium magnets, invented in the 1980s, are used in wind turbines and electric cars.
- Doctors once used magnets to draw iron splinters out of eyes and wounds, as the Sushruta Samhita describes.
- Geologists use magnets to find iron ore, and to read the history of the Earth's magnet in old rocks.
- Scientists use the magnetic bacteria's tiny crystals to study how living things build minerals.
- Magnetic observatories, like the one at Alibag, help warn about solar storms that can disturb power lines and satellites.

## Open questions
- **What is an electron's spin, really?** Scientists can measure it very exactly and use it every day, but cannot say what it is underneath. Some think it needs no further explanation.
- **Exactly how do birds feel the Earth's magnet?** The leading idea is cryptochrome in their eyes, which works through electron spins. It has been shown in the lab but not yet fully proven in living birds.
- **Can people sense the Earth's magnet, even without knowing it?** One 2019 study found brainwave changes in some people. It has not yet been repeated by other teams.
- **When will the Earth's magnet flip next?** Flips don't come on a schedule. The magnet has weakened a little over the last 200 years, but that may or may not mean a flip is coming. Nobody can predict it yet.
- **Why does the magnetic north pole move so fast, and why did it speed up and then slow down?** It depends on how the liquid iron moves deep in the core, which nobody can see directly. Scientists use computer models and argue about them.
- **Do magnetic monopoles, magnets with only one pole, exist anywhere?** Some theories say they should have been made just after the universe began. Physicists have searched for nearly a hundred years and found none.
- **How exactly did lodestones become magnets?** Lightning explains some, but maybe not all. Scientists are still studying which kinds of magnetite hold their magnet best.
- **Why does a hot liquid core keep making a magnet for billions of years?** The geodynamo is so complicated that computers can only copy it roughly. How the Earth's magnet first started, and how it has lasted, are still argued about.

## Connected maths
- In the paper clip chain test, the neodymium magnet held 12 clips. The ceramic magnet held 4 clips. How many times as many clips did the neodymium magnet hold? **3 times as many.**. The neodymium magnet held 12 clips and the ceramic magnet held 4. 12 divided by 4 is 3. So it held 3 times as many. Check: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- To make a needle into a magnet, you stroke it in 5 rounds of 10 strokes. How many strokes is that altogether? **50 strokes.**. Each round is 10 strokes. There are 5 rounds. 5 groups of 10 is 50. You can count in tens: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- The Chinese book Wujing Zongyao described an iron fish compass in 1044. William Gilbert said the Earth is a magnet in 1600. How many years apart are they? **556 years.**. Take the earlier year from the later year. 1600 take away 1044. 1600 take away 1000 is 600. 600 take away 44 is 556. So they are 556 years apart. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Look at the map of your home. You stand at the front door facing north. You turn to face east, then turn again to face south. How many quarter turns did you make, and which way did you turn? **2 quarter turns, both to the right. That is a half turn.**. North to east is one quarter turn to the right. East to south is another quarter turn to the right. 1 + 1 = 2 quarter turns. Two quarter turns make a half turn, so now you face the opposite way from north. (queues `math.position.directions-and-grids.L3`)
- Look at the scale. It shows how far a compass was from a steel almirah when it stopped being fooled. Each big mark is 10 cm. Each small mark is 5 cm. What is the reading? **75 centimetres.**. The pointer is past the 70 mark but before 80. It sits on the small mark halfway between them. Each small mark is 5 cm. 70 + 5 = 75. So the compass had to be 75 cm away. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
