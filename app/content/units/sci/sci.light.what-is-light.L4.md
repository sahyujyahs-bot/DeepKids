# What is light? Energy that spreads out, warms things and has a family you cannot see
`sci.light.what-is-light.L4`

**Summary.** A tar road is too hot to walk on at noon, while a whitewashed wall beside it stays much cooler, yet the same sunlight falls on both. A lamp across the street looks dim, although the lamp has not changed at all. A TV remote sends out light that your eyes cannot see, but a phone camera can. In this unit your child measures how a torch beam thins out as it travels, races ice cubes on black and white paper, and finds the invisible light of a remote control. They learn that light is energy, that it spreads out and turns into heat when a surface takes it in, and that the light we see is one thin slice of a long family of waves.

**Intro.** *Stand barefoot on a tar road at noon, then step onto the whitewashed wall's cool shade. Same Sun, same sunlight. Why is the road so hot? Where did the light go when it landed on the road?* Light does not just let us see. It carries energy, and when a surface takes the light in, that energy turns into heat. A lamp far away looks dim for a different reason. Its light spreads out over a wider and wider space, so each patch of your eye gets less. In this unit you will measure how fast a torch beam thins out, race ice cubes on black paper and white paper, and use a phone camera to catch light your eyes cannot see. You will also meet the long family of waves that light belongs to, from radio to X-rays.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that light is energy, and that the same light gets thinner as it spreads out in all directions.
1. I can show with a torch and squared paper that going twice as far makes the light cover four times the area.
2. I can explain that a surface takes in light and turns it into heat, and why a dark surface warms faster than a white one.
3. I can name the members of the light family, from radio waves to gamma rays, and say they are the same kind of wave with different wavelengths.
4. I can find the invisible infrared light of a TV remote with a phone camera, and say what that shows about the limits of my eyes.
5. I can explain that light carries a tiny push, and how that push bends the tails of comets and could drive a solar sail.
6. I can follow the energy in my food back to the Sun.
7. I can tell how William Herschel found invisible light in 1800, and say what scientists agree on and what is still not known.

## Terms
- **Light**: Light is energy that travels in straight lines from a source and lets us see. Some of it can warm things up. *e.g.* Sunlight warms a steel tumbler left on a windowsill.
- **Energy**: Energy is what makes things happen. It can move things, warm things and light things up. It is never used up. It only changes from one kind to another. *e.g.* A lit diya turns the energy stored in oil into light and heat.
- **Spread out**: To spread out means to cover a bigger and bigger space as you go further from the start. Light from a lamp spreads out in all directions. *e.g.* A drop of ink on wet blotting paper spreads out in a circle that keeps growing.
- **Brightness**: Brightness is how much light lands on a patch of a surface, or on your eye. A patch that gets more light looks brighter. *e.g.* A page near a lamp is brighter than the same page at the far end of the room.
- **Absorb**: To absorb light is to take it in instead of sending it back. The light's energy turns into heat inside the thing. *e.g.* A black umbrella absorbs most of the sunlight that falls on it, so the cloth gets warm.
- **Reflect**: To reflect light is to bounce it back instead of taking it in. A surface that reflects most light stays cooler. *e.g.* A white wall reflects most sunlight, which is why white walls feel cooler.
- **Heat**: Heat is energy that moves from a warmer thing to a cooler thing, and makes the cooler thing warmer. It is the jiggling of the tiny particles inside a thing. *e.g.* Heat flows from a hot tumbler of tea into your cold hands.
- **Visible light**: Visible light is the kind of light our eyes can see. It runs from violet to red, the colours of a rainbow. *e.g.* The coloured band that a glass of water throws on a wall is made of visible light.
- **Infrared**: Infrared is light that is just beyond red. Our eyes cannot see it, but our skin feels it as warmth. Every warm thing gives it out. *e.g.* A TV remote sends an infrared signal to the TV.
- **Ultraviolet**: Ultraviolet is light that is just beyond violet. Our eyes cannot see it. It carries more energy than visible light and can burn skin. *e.g.* Sunburn on a long day at the beach comes from ultraviolet light.
- **Radio waves**: Radio waves are the longest waves in the light family. Our eyes cannot see them. They carry radio, TV and phone signals. *e.g.* The song on an FM radio reaches you on radio waves about 3 metres long.
- **Microwaves**: Microwaves are waves a little shorter than radio waves. They carry Wi-Fi and phone signals, and they heat food in a microwave oven. *e.g.* A Wi-Fi router sends microwaves about 12 centimetres long through your home.
- **X-rays**: X-rays are waves much shorter than visible light. They carry a lot of energy. They pass through soft parts of the body but are stopped by bone. *e.g.* A hospital X-ray photo shows the bones of a broken arm.
- **Gamma rays**: Gamma rays are the shortest waves in the light family and carry the most energy. They come from exploding stars and from some atoms. *e.g.* Doctors use carefully aimed gamma rays to treat some cancers.
- **Wavelength**: Wavelength is the distance from the top of one wave to the top of the next. The members of the light family differ in wavelength. *e.g.* The wavelength of red light is about 100 times smaller than the width of a hair.
- **Electromagnetic spectrum**: The electromagnetic spectrum is the whole family of light waves, lined up from the longest wavelength to the shortest. Visible light is one small part of it. *e.g.* Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all in the electromagnetic spectrum.
- **Prism**: A prism is a block of clear glass or plastic, often shaped like a triangle. It splits white light into a band of colours. *e.g.* Sunlight through a glass prism makes a rainbow on the wall.
- **Thermometer**: A thermometer is a tool that measures how hot or cold something is. Most have a thin tube with liquid that rises when it gets warmer. *e.g.* A doctor puts a thermometer under your arm to check for fever.
- **Control**: A control in a test is a thing you leave alone, so you can compare it with the things you changed. It shows what happens when nothing special is done. *e.g.* In the ice test, one ice cube sits on a plain plate as the control.
- **Radiation pressure**: Radiation pressure is the tiny push that light gives to anything it lands on. It is very weak, but it is real. *e.g.* Sunlight pushes on a spacecraft, with a force smaller than the weight of a grain of sugar on each square metre.
- **Solar sail**: A solar sail is a huge, thin, shiny sheet on a spacecraft. Sunlight pushes it, so the spacecraft needs no fuel. *e.g.* A Japanese spacecraft called IKAROS unfolded a solar sail in space in 2010.
- **Comet**: A comet is a ball of ice and dust that goes round the Sun. When it gets near the Sun, it grows a glowing tail that points away from the Sun. *e.g.* A comet's tail can be millions of kilometres long.
- **Photosynthesis**: Photosynthesis is how plants use sunlight to make food from air and water. The energy of the light is stored in the food. *e.g.* A rice plant in a paddy field uses photosynthesis to make the grains you eat.

## Explanations
### Light is energy on the move · *defined*
**Definition:** Light is a kind of energy that travels. When it lands on something, it can warm it, make a chemical change in it, or push electrons around in it.

Light leaves a lamp, a fire or the Sun and carries energy away with it. That energy does not vanish when the light lands. It turns into something else. Sunlight on a steel tumbler turns into heat. Sunlight on a leaf turns into the stored energy of sugar. Sunlight on a solar panel pushes electrons along a wire and makes electricity. In every case, the light is gone and the same amount of energy is still there, in a new form.
- e.g. A solar water heater on a roof in Chennai turns sunlight into hot water for a whole family.
- e.g. A leaf turns sunlight into sugar, which the plant uses to grow.
- e.g. A solar calculator keeps working with no battery as long as light falls on its small panel.
### Light spreads out and gets thinner · *defined*
**Definition:** Light from a small source spreads out in all directions. Twice as far from the source, the same light covers four times the area, so each patch gets one quarter as much.

A torch sends its light out in a cone. Close to the torch, the cone is narrow and the light is packed tight. Further away, the cone is wider, and the same light is spread over a bigger patch. If you go twice as far, the patch is twice as wide and twice as tall. Two times two is four, so the patch is four times bigger. The same light is shared by four times the area, so each part of the patch is four times dimmer. At three times the distance, the area is nine times bigger and the light is nine times thinner. Scientists call this the inverse square law. Johannes Kepler wrote about it in the early 1600s.
- e.g. A lamp 2 metres away gives a page one quarter of the light it gives at 1 metre.
- e.g. Jupiter is about 5 times further from the Sun than the Earth. Each square metre there gets about 25 times less sunlight.
- e.g. A street light at the end of a long road looks dim, because its light has spread over a huge area by the time it reaches you.
### Dim does not mean used up · *defined*
**Definition:** In clear space, light is not used up as it travels. It only spreads over a bigger area, so each part of the area gets less.

Many people think light gets tired and weaker as it goes. In clear space, that is not what happens. Think of a bucket of water poured onto a floor. The same water makes a thick puddle if the floor is small and a thin film if the floor is huge. The amount of water has not changed. Light is like that. The total light going through a bigger and bigger sphere around a lamp stays the same. Only the light on each small patch gets less. Air with dust, smoke or fog does take in or scatter some light, so a lamp seen through fog looks dimmer still. Most of the dimming of a faraway star, however, is just spreading.
- e.g. A candle can be seen from a long way off on a dark night, because the light that reaches your eye is small but your eye still catches it.
- e.g. Starlight crosses space for thousands of years and arrives with its energy intact. It is thin only because it has spread.
- e.g. A street light looks dimmer in fog than on a clear night, because fog scatters some of its light away.
### What a surface does with light: reflect, absorb or let it through · *defined*
**Definition:** When light lands on a surface, some is reflected, some is absorbed and some passes through. The three always add up to all of the light that arrived.

Think of three things that can happen to a cricket ball thrown at a wall, a net or an open door. It can bounce back, get caught, or go through. Light has the same three choices. A mirror reflects almost all of it. A black cloth absorbs almost all of it. Clear glass lets most of it through. Most things do a mix. White paper reflects most of the light but absorbs some. Thin paper reflects some, absorbs some and lets some through. What the surface is made of and how smooth it is decide the mix. For every surface, the three parts add up to all the light that landed.
- e.g. A polished steel plate reflects most of the light that lands on it.
- e.g. Black cloth absorbs most of the light that lands on it.
- e.g. A window pane lets most of the light through, and reflects and absorbs a little.
### When light is absorbed, it becomes heat · *defined*
**Definition:** When a surface absorbs light, the energy of the light turns into heat. The surface warms up.

Light that is absorbed is not lost. Its energy goes into the tiny particles that the surface is made of, and makes them jiggle faster. Faster jiggling is what we feel as heat. A black surface absorbs most of the sunlight that lands on it, so it warms up quickly. A white surface reflects most of it, so it warms up slowly. This is why people in hot places often wear white clothes and paint their roofs white, and why tar roads burn the soles of bare feet. A shiny sheet of aluminium foil reflects almost everything, so it stays cool even in the Sun. The energy is never lost. It changes from light to heat.
- e.g. A black plastic water tank on a roof gets hot much faster than a white one.
- e.g. A tar road is far hotter than a whitewashed wall at noon.
- e.g. A black umbrella warms the air under it more than a white one does.
### Where the Sun's light goes when it reaches the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** The sunlight that reaches the Earth warms the land, sea and air, drives the weather and the water cycle, and is captured by plants, which feed almost all other life.

The Sun sends out light in every direction. The Earth catches only about one part in two billion of it. Even so, this is huge. The sunlight that falls on the Earth in one hour holds about as much energy as all of humanity uses in a year. Some of it warms the sea. The warm sea water evaporates, makes monsoon clouds and later falls as rain. Some of it warms the air unevenly and makes wind. A small part is caught by leaves and stored in food. The fuels we burn, such as coal, oil and gas, are the sunlight stored by plants and tiny sea creatures long ago.
- e.g. The south-west monsoon is driven by the Sun heating the land faster than the sea.
- e.g. An idli is rice, and rice grew in sunlight. So the energy you use to run and think was sunlight, a short while ago.
- e.g. Wind that turns the turbines in Tamil Nadu is air moved because the Sun heated some places more than others.
### Light in, heat out: why the Earth stays at one temperature · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth gives out as much infrared energy as it takes in as sunlight. The balance decides how warm the Earth is.

The Earth takes in sunlight all day. If nothing took the energy away, the Earth would keep getting hotter. But everything warm gives out infrared. The warm ground, sea and air send infrared back out into space. The Earth's temperature settles where the energy going out matches the energy coming in. Gases such as carbon dioxide and water vapour catch some of the outgoing infrared and slow its escape. They act like a blanket. This is called the greenhouse effect. It keeps the Earth warm enough for life. Adding more carbon dioxide to the air makes the blanket thicker, so the Earth warms. Scientists across the world agree on this.
- e.g. A closed car left in the Sun gets very hot, because light gets in through the glass but the heat cannot escape easily.
- e.g. Desert nights are cold, because the dry air holds little heat in. Cloudy nights are warmer, because clouds slow the escape of infrared.
- e.g. The Moon has no air to act as a blanket, so its sunlit ground gets very hot and its night side gets very cold.
### One family of waves: the electromagnetic spectrum · *defined*
**Definition:** Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all the same kind of wave. They differ only in wavelength.

Visible light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields. In 1865 James Clerk Maxwell worked out that such waves could be longer or shorter than light. In 1887 Heinrich Hertz made the longer ones in a lab and showed they behave just like light. The whole family is called the electromagnetic spectrum. The members from longest to shortest are radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. All of them travel at the speed of light in empty space. The shorter the wavelength, the more energy each photon carries. That is why a blue photon carries more than a red one, and an X-ray photon carries thousands of times more.
- e.g. FM radio waves are about 3 metres long. Wi-Fi waves are about 12 centimetres long.
- e.g. Red light has a wavelength about 100 times smaller than the width of a hair.
- e.g. X-rays can be as short as the width of an atom.
### Radio waves and microwaves: the long waves that carry messages · *defined*
**Definition:** Radio waves and microwaves are the longest waves in the light family. They pass through walls and carry radio, TV, Wi-Fi and phone signals.

Long waves are not stopped by most walls, so they are good for sending messages. A radio station turns a voice or a song into a pattern of changes in radio waves. Your radio turns the pattern back into sound. Mobile phones and Wi-Fi use microwaves, a bit shorter. A microwave oven uses microwaves of the same family, but strong ones, to shake the water in food and warm it. Jagadish Chandra Bose, a scientist in Kolkata, showed around 1895 that waves a few millimetres long can pass through walls and ring a bell in another room. The Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope near Pune uses 30 dishes to catch radio waves from far out in space.
- e.g. A phone call to a relative in Madurai travels as microwaves to a tower, then on through cables and more waves.
- e.g. Radio telescopes found pulsars, which are spinning dead stars that blink many times a second.
- e.g. Wi-Fi at home can get weaker as it passes through thick walls, because the walls absorb some of the waves.
### Infrared: the warmth you can feel but not see · *defined*
**Definition:** Infrared is light just beyond red. Every warm thing gives it out, and our skin feels it as warmth.

The bigger the temperature of a thing, the more infrared it sends out. Your body gives out infrared, and so do a hot tava, a fire and a warm stone. You feel the heat of a fire on your face from a metre away because infrared from the fire lands on your skin. A thermal camera sees infrared and turns it into a coloured picture, so it can see warm bodies in the dark. Some snakes, such as pit vipers, have tiny pits on their faces that sense the infrared from a warm mouse. A TV remote uses infrared too. It sends a quick pattern of flickers, and the TV reads the pattern. The remote's light is invisible, but a phone camera can catch it.
- e.g. Sitting near a lit hearth, you feel its warmth long before you touch anything hot.
- e.g. Thermal cameras at airports check whether travellers have a fever.
- e.g. A pit viper senses a warm mouse in total darkness.
### Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays: short waves with big energy · *defined*
**Definition:** Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays have very short wavelengths. Each photon carries a lot of energy, enough to damage living cells.

A photon of ultraviolet light carries more energy than a photon of blue light. It can burn skin, which is why we wear hats and sunscreen at the beach. Bees, and many birds, can see some ultraviolet. X-rays carry even more energy. They pass through soft parts of the body but are stopped by bone, so doctors use them to see inside us. Rosalind Franklin used X-rays in 1952 to take the photograph that helped show the shape of DNA. Marie Curie built mobile X-ray units in the First World War to help doctors find bullets and broken bones. Because X-rays can harm cells, they are used carefully and only when needed. Gamma rays carry the most energy of all.
- e.g. A hospital X-ray photo of a broken arm shows white bone against darker soft flesh.
- e.g. A bee sees patterns on a flower that are invisible to us, because they are painted in ultraviolet.
- e.g. A dentist moves behind a shield while taking an X-ray picture, since the rays can harm cells in large amounts.
### Why our eyes see only a thin slice of the family · *idea*
Our eyes catch light with wavelengths from about 0.0004 to 0.0007 millimetres. That is a very thin slice of the whole family. There are several likely reasons. The Sun gives out most of its light in this slice. The air and water let this slice through, while they block much of the ultraviolet and some of the infrared. A cell that catches visible light needs a certain amount of energy, which is the amount a visible photon carries. Many scientists think animals ended up using this slice because it was bright, and because it could reach the ground. Other animals use different slices. Bees and many birds see some ultraviolet. Pit vipers sense infrared. The reasons are an idea, but they fit what we know.
- e.g. Bees see ultraviolet patterns on flowers that look plain to us.
- e.g. Pit vipers sense infrared heat from warm prey in darkness.
- e.g. Fish in the deep sea see only blue light, because almost nothing else reaches that deep.
### Light gives a tiny push · *defined*
**Definition:** Light carries a tiny push. When light lands on a surface, it pushes that surface a very little. This is called radiation pressure.

Light has energy, and it also carries a small push. James Clerk Maxwell worked out in 1873 that light should push on whatever it lands on. Around 1900, Pyotr Lebedev in Moscow, and Ernest Nichols and Gordon Hull in the United States, measured the push. It is very small. Sunlight pushing on a square metre of a shiny sheet gives a force about the weight of one grain of sugar. Nothing in daily life shows it. But in space, with no air and no friction, a tiny push that never stops can add up. Comets give a clue. The tail of a comet always points away from the Sun, because sunlight and the stream of particles from the Sun push the dust and gas outwards.
- e.g. A comet's glowing tail always points away from the Sun, even when the comet is heading away from it.
- e.g. Sunlight pushes slightly on every spacecraft, and engineers must allow for it when they plan its path.
- e.g. A powerful laser can move a tiny, light mirror in a lab, just by shining on it.
### Sailing on sunlight · *idea*
A solar sail is a huge, very thin, shiny sheet that catches the push of sunlight. A spacecraft carrying one needs no fuel. In 2010 a Japanese spacecraft called IKAROS unfolded a square sail in space and showed that sailing on sunlight works. In 2019 a small craft called LightSail 2, paid for by The Planetary Society, used sunlight to raise its orbit around the Earth. Some scientists dream of much bigger sails. Breakthrough Starshot, a plan announced in 2016, imagines tiny sail craft pushed by powerful lasers to a nearby star within a human lifetime. Nobody has built it. The engineering is very hard, and many scientists doubt it can be done. It is an idea, but one built on a real push.
- e.g. IKAROS in 2010 was the first craft to sail on sunlight between planets.
- e.g. LightSail 2 raised its own orbit around the Earth using only sunlight.
- e.g. Breakthrough Starshot is a plan to push tiny craft toward another star with lasers.
### The oldest light and what it says about the start of everything · *debated*
Telescopes can detect a faint glow of microwaves coming from every direction in the sky. It is the oldest light there is. It was set free about 380,000 years after the universe began, when the hot young universe cooled enough to let light travel. In the mid-1960s two radio engineers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, found it by accident. Their antenna in New Jersey kept picking up a steady hiss. They even cleaned out pigeon droppings from the antenna, but the hiss stayed. As the universe grew, the waves of that old light were stretched until they became microwaves. Scientists agree the glow is real and very smooth. They argue about why it is so smooth, and about what came before it.
- e.g. A small part of the hiss between channels on an old analogue TV came from this old light.
- e.g. The glow is a little bit warmer in some directions and cooler in others. Those tiny differences are the seeds of galaxies.
- e.g. The glow is about 3 degrees above absolute zero, the coldest anything can be.
- **Most cosmologists:** The universe began hot and dense and has been growing ever since. A very quick early growth called inflation would explain why the glow is so smooth. Inflation is an idea that fits the measurements well, but it has not been proved.
- **Some other scientists:** The smooth glow could be explained in other ways, for example by a universe that bounces and begins again. These ideas are less popular, but they are taken seriously by some, because inflation has not been tested directly.
- **Everyone honest about it:** Nobody knows yet what happened before the glow was set free, or whether the question 'before' even makes sense.

## Mostly agreed
- Light spreads out from a small source, so twice as far away it covers four times the area and each patch gets one quarter as much. *How we know:* Photometers, which are instruments for measuring light, have confirmed this again and again with lamps at measured distances, and sunlight arriving at the other planets matches the same rule.
- When a surface absorbs light, the light's energy turns into heat, and dark surfaces absorb more sunlight than light-coloured ones. *How we know:* Thermometers on black and white surfaces in the same sunshine show the black one warming faster, and satellites measure the same difference on whole deserts and snowfields.
- Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are the same kind of wave, with different wavelengths. *How we know:* All of them travel at the same speed in empty space, can be reflected, and can be made and measured with the same equations that Maxwell wrote in the 1860s. Hertz showed in 1887 that radio waves behave just like light.
- Our eyes see only a thin slice of the family of light waves, about 0.0004 to 0.0007 millimetres in wavelength. *How we know:* Instruments such as infrared cameras, radio telescopes and X-ray machines detect the other members, and the measurements agree with each other.
- Light carries a tiny push, called radiation pressure. *How we know:* Lebedev in 1900 and Nichols and Hull in 1901 measured it, and spacecraft such as IKAROS in 2010 have sailed on it.
- The Earth stays at a steady temperature because it gives out as much infrared as it takes in as sunlight, and gases such as carbon dioxide slow that infrared down. *How we know:* Satellites measure the sunlight coming in and the infrared going out. Measurements of the air and sea over many decades agree.
- A faint glow of microwaves, the oldest light there is, comes from every direction in the sky. *How we know:* It was found by Penzias and Wilson in the 1960s, and satellites since then have mapped it in great detail.

## How we found out
**William Herschel, then Johann Ritter, 1800 and 1801.** For most of history, people thought sunlight held only the rays their eyes could see, the colours of the rainbow. In 1800, William Herschel, an astronomer living in England, wanted to know if some colours carry more heat than others. He had noticed that coloured glasses on his telescope let through different amounts of warmth. He used a glass prism to spread sunlight into a band of colours on a table. He put a thermometer with a blackened bulb in each colour in turn, and kept two more in the shade nearby as controls. The temperature climbed from violet toward red. Then he moved a thermometer just past the red edge, where nothing could be seen. It got warmest of all. Something invisible was carrying heat. In 1801 Johann Ritter, a German scientist, found invisible rays beyond the violet end too, because they darkened a chemical called silver chloride. Later scientists showed these rays behave like light, and belong to one big family.  
*What people believed before:* People thought the rays in sunlight were only the ones the eye can see, and that the dark place beyond the red end was empty. Herschel's thermometer warmed up there, so something unseen was arriving.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Light is only what our eyes can see.** (Most people before 1800, who had no way to detect anything else.; *rejected*)  
  For: For all of history, dark meant no light. Nothing we could feel or see told us otherwise.  
  Against: In 1800 William Herschel's thermometer warmed up beyond the red end of the colours, where nothing could be seen. Today cameras, radios and X-ray machines detect the rest of the family.
- **Light gets tired and weaker the further it goes.** (Many children and many adults.; *rejected*)  
  For: A far lamp looks dim, and a faraway star looks small and faint.  
  Against: In clear space, no light is used up. It spreads over a bigger and bigger area, so each patch gets less. Starlight that has travelled for thousands of years still arrives with its energy intact.
- **Heat and light are completely different things that just happen to travel together.** (Many scientists in the 1700s, and Herschel himself at first. He called the invisible heating rays calorific rays.; *rejected*)  
  For: You can feel heat without seeing anything, and you can see light without feeling heat. They seem to be separate.  
  Against: In the 1830s Macedonio Melloni, an Italian scientist, showed that radiant heat can be reflected and bent just like light. Both are members of one wave family, and light turns into heat when it is absorbed.
- **Light is weightless, so it cannot push anything.** (Most people, since we do not notice sunlight pushing us.; *rejected*)  
  For: Standing in strong sunlight, you feel warmth but no shove. Light has no mass that you can weigh.  
  Against: Maxwell predicted in 1873 that light pushes, and Lebedev, Nichols and Hull measured the push around 1900. It is tiny, but it moves comet dust and spacecraft sails.
- **A Crookes radiometer, the little glass bulb with a spinning set of vanes, spins because light pushes on the vanes.** (Many people in the 1870s, and many science toy sellers even today.; *rejected*)  
  For: The vanes spin when light shines on them. It looks like a direct push of light.  
  Against: The real push of light is far too small to turn the vanes. The radiometer spins best with a little air left inside, and almost stops in a very good vacuum, where light's push would matter most. It spins because the air next to the warm black sides moves.
- **Dark surfaces always take in more sunlight than light ones.** (Most people, and most textbooks as a rule of thumb.; *accepted*)  
  For: A black road warms faster than a white wall, and black tanks heat water faster than white ones.  
  Against: The colour we see tells us only about visible light. Sunlight also has infrared that we cannot see. Some white paints take in a lot of infrared, and some dark 'cool roof' paints reflect it. So the rule is a good one, but not exact.

## Roots
We found that light spreads out, turns into heat, and belongs to a long family of waves. Now we ask what that tells us about what we can see, and what is there when we cannot see it.

**Deep truth.** The thermometer beyond the red end of the colours warmed up, though that place looked dark. Our eyes catch only a thin slice of one long family of waves. The rest still reaches us and still does things. So 'dark' tells us what our eyes miss, not what is there.  
*Habit it breaks:* We treat the edge of what we can sense as the edge of what exists, and call whatever lies past it nothing.  
*Evidence:* In the remote control test, your eyes saw nothing coming from the remote, but a phone camera showed it flickering. In 1800 Herschel's thermometer warmed in a place that looked dark. If your phone shows nothing, write that down honestly and try another camera.

- *who am i:* You are warm right now, so you are giving out infrared. A thermal camera would show you glowing, even in a dark room.
- *what is life:* A pit viper senses a warm mouse by the infrared the mouse gives out, in total darkness. A bee steers by ultraviolet patterns on flowers. Each living being senses their own slice of the family of light.
- *what is this world:* The room you sit in is full of radio waves, microwaves and infrared passing through it. Whether a place seems empty depends on what you are able to sense.

Radio waves, infrared, visible light and X-rays are one kind of wave in different sizes. What we call light is the slice our eyes happen to catch. Our skin catches the next slice as warmth, though we call that feeling and not seeing. Each animal has their own slice. So what any one of us sees is a small part of what reaches us, and the world holds more than any one creature can sense.

- **Plato, Republic, Book 7 (the cave):** Plato, an ancient Greek thinker, told of prisoners chained in a cave since birth, who see only shadows on the wall in front of them. They think the shadows are the whole world. A prisoner who climbs out is dazzled by the real things. (The prisoners treat the edge of what they can see as the edge of the world. The thermometer beyond the red end of the colours showed the same mistake about light.)

## Story: The Third Thermometer
*A made-up story built on real events. In 1800, William Herschel really did spread sunlight into colours with a prism and measure the heat of each colour with thermometers. He really did find that the thermometer just past the red end got warmest. His sister Caroline, a real astronomer who found comets, lived in his house. Nell Hart, her questions and her part in the discovery are imagined. The words they speak are made up.*

In the summer of 1800, in the town of Slough in England, there lived a famous astronomer named William Herschel. Years before, he had found a new planet. Now he lived in a house full of telescopes, brass tubes, mirrors and clocks.

Nell Hart was eleven. She came to the house every morning to help. She polished the brass and carried the tea. Best of all, when Mr Herschel's hands were busy, she wrote his numbers in a big book. Her handwriting was very neat.

One bright morning, Mr Herschel closed the shutters of the sunniest room, and left one thin slit. A bar of sunlight came through. He held a glass prism in it. On the table, the white light spread into a band of colours. Red at one end, then orange, yellow, green, blue and violet at the other. Nell had seen rainbows in the sky. She had never seen one lying on a table.

'Here is my puzzle,' said Mr Herschel. 'When I look at the Sun through my telescope, I put coloured glass in front of my eye. The red glass makes my hand warm. Some other glasses do not. Does each colour of sunlight carry its own amount of heat?'

'How will you find out?' asked Nell.

'With thermometers.'

He set out three. He had painted the glass bulb of each one black with ink, because black takes in light well. The first he would move through the colours, one by one. The second he put far off in the shade, and the third he put in the shade at the side. 'Those two are my controls,' he said. 'They tell us how warm the room is on its own. If the room warms, I must not think the colours did it.'

Nell nodded. It was like keeping one pot of milk off the fire, to see how warm milk gets all by itself.

For an hour they worked. Nell read the numbers and wrote them in the book. The first thermometer, in the violet, climbed a little. In the green it climbed more. In the red it climbed most of all. Everything fitted. Red light was the warmest light, and Mr Herschel nodded as if the puzzle were solved.

But Nell was a girl who liked to check things. She kept glancing at the controls. The one far off in the shade stayed steady, just as it should. But the one standing close to the red edge was not steady. It sat on a patch of table where there was no colour at all, only shadow. And the thin red line in its glass was creeping up.

'Mr Herschel,' she said, 'this thermometer is going wrong. It is in the dark, but it is getting warm.'

'It cannot be going wrong,' he said. 'It is a control. Nothing is shining on it.'

'Then why is it warm?'

Mr Herschel looked at the thermometer. He looked at the far edge of the red. 'The room is not warmer,' he said slowly. 'The other control has not moved.'

'A thermometer cannot warm up by itself,' said Nell. 'The milk only warms if the pot is on the fire. Something must be landing on this one.'

'Something we cannot see,' said Mr Herschel. He took the first thermometer out of the red, and set it down in the dark patch, a hand's width beyond the red edge. They both stared at the red line in the glass. It rose. It rose past the number for the red. It kept rising until it stood higher than it had stood in any colour at all.

'It is warmer than the red,' whispered Nell. 'And there is no light there.'

Mr Herschel put down his pen. 'There are rays coming from the Sun,' he said, 'that my eyes cannot see.'

Nell wrote the number in the book. Underneath it, in her best handwriting, she wrote: 'The dark place was not empty.'

That evening, walking home, she held out her palm in the last of the sunshine. The warmth lay on her hand like a coin. She wondered how many more rays were falling on it that she would never see. The sky, she thought, was full of messengers, and her eyes were listening to only a few of them.

Mr Herschel wrote up what he had found, and it was printed for other scientists to read. In the years after, others went looking in the dark places beyond the colours. Beyond the violet they found more invisible rays. Later people found radio waves, and then X-rays. Each time, someone had put a detector where the eye saw nothing.

**For the parent:** A thermometer in a place that looked dark got warm. So something unseen was arriving. Our eyes catch only a slice of the light that reaches us.

- Ask: Why did a thermometer in a dark patch of the table get warm?
- Ask: Why is it useful to keep a control thermometer that you leave alone?
- Ask: What else might be falling on you right now that your eyes cannot see?
- Ask: How would you check whether a surprising reading was a mistake or a real discovery?

  *Child mode:* Switch the torch on in the dark. Count the lit squares across and down. Write the numbers down.
  - Note: At 20 centimetres, the patch should be about 4 squares wide and 4 tall, so about 16 squares. The edges are a little blurry because the torch bulb is not a tiny dot. Count a square if it is more than half lit.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Move the paper to 30 centimetres. Count the lit squares. Write them down. Repeat once. Then move the paper to 40 centimetres, count, and repeat once. Compare the brightness each time.  
  *Parent says:* Move the paper further away. Count the squares again. Is the patch bigger? How much bigger? Is it as bright as before?  
  *Child mode:* Move the paper to 30 centimetres. Count the squares. Then move it to 40 centimetres. Count again.
  - Note: Expected: about 6 by 6 squares at 30 centimetres, which is 36, and about 8 by 8 squares at 40 centimetres, which is 64. Real counts are often a bit bigger because of the blur. If your numbers are different, write them down as they are. The patch at 40 centimetres should look dimmer than the one at 20.
  - Check: The patch at 20 centimetres covers about 16 squares. The paper moves to 40 centimetres, twice as far. About how many squares are lit? → **About 64 squares, four times as many**
- **s5 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the three patches. Ask how many times bigger the patch at 40 centimetres is than at 20. Ask how much of the torch's light each square gets at 40 centimetres. Draw the results.  
  *Parent says:* The same torch light went through the same hole. At 40 centimetres, it covered four times as many squares. So each square got how much of the light, compared with 20 centimetres?  
  *Child mode:* The same light covered more squares. Did each square get more light or less?
  - Ask: Is the light on the far paper used up, or just spread out? (good: It is spread out over more squares.; The same light is shared by more squares.; Each square gets a quarter as much.)
  - Ask: Where would the patch be nine times bigger? (good: At three times the distance.; At 60 centimetres from the torch.; Three times three is nine.)
  - Note: The key idea is that the amount of light going through the hole did not change. It was spread over four times the area, so each square got one quarter as much. This is why a far lamp looks dim even though it has not changed.
  - Check: At 40 centimetres the patch covers four times the area it covered at 20. What can you say about each square of the far patch? → **It gets about one quarter as much light**
### e2 · Ice Race: where does the light go? (30 min)
- **question:** Four ice cubes sit in the same sunshine on black paper, dark blue paper, yellow paper and white paper. Which melts first, and which melts last?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which ice cube will melt first and which last? Will the cube in the shade melt at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the cubes come from the same ice tray, so they are the same size. All the papers are the same size, and all sit on one tray in the same sunshine. Only the colour of the paper changes. A fifth cube sits on white paper in the shade as the control, so you know what the room alone does.
- **safety:** Never look straight at the Sun. If you use a lamp on a cloudy day, a grown-up must set it up, because the bulb gets hot.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each paper, showing how many minutes the cube lasted. Write which colour melted its cube first and which last.
- **results table:** Paper under the ice cube | In the sun or in the shade | Size of the cube after 5 minutes: whole, big, half, small or gone | Size after 10 minutes | Size after 15 minutes | Minutes until the cube was gone
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the five papers, the tray and the ice. Ask for a guess and a reason about which cube melts first and last. Write it at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will put ice cubes on black, blue, yellow and white paper in the sunshine. Which one will melt first? Which one will melt last? What about the cube in the shade? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Ice cubes on four colours in the sun. Which melts first? Which last? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that colour will melt its cube first? (good: Black gets hotter in the sun.; Dark colours soak up more sun.; Light colours bounce the sun away.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. The cube in the shade is the control. It shows how fast ice melts with no sunshine at all.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose a spot in direct sun. Lay the black, blue, yellow and white papers on the tray, with a gap between them. Put the tray in the sun. Lay the fifth white paper in the shade next to the tray.  
  *Parent says:* Sun on four papers, and shade on one. The tray must sit in the same sunshine, so no paper gets more sun than another.  
  *Child mode:* Put four papers on the tray in the sun. Put one white paper in the shade.
  - Note: Check that no shadow of a window frame falls across the tray. Direct sun at midday or in the early afternoon works best. If the sun is weak or the day is cloudy, wait for a sunnier hour. The test needs direct sunshine.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Bring the ice cubes out only now. Use a spoon to put one on each paper, in the middle. Start the timer as you put down the last one. Look at the cubes at 5 minutes. Write down their sizes.  
  *Parent says:* Ice on all five papers. The timer is running. Watch closely. At 5 minutes, which cube looks smallest? Which looks the biggest?  
  *Child mode:* Put one ice cube on each paper. Start the timer. Look again at 5 minutes.
  - Note: Use the same words every time for the sizes: whole, big, half, small, gone. If the cubes slide, set them back in the middle. Do not shake the tray.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look again at 10 minutes and at 15 minutes. Write down the sizes. Keep looking every few minutes until each cube is gone. Write the minute each cube disappeared.  
  *Parent says:* Which cube is gone first? Which is last? Write down the minute for each one. Is the cube in the shade melting at all?  
  *Child mode:* Look at 10 and 15 minutes. Write how big each cube is. Note when each one is gone.
  - Note: Expected: the cube on black melts first, the cubes on dark blue and yellow come in between, and white lasts longest. The shaded cube melts slowest of all. If all the cubes melt at nearly the same time, the sun was weak. Try again on a sunnier day.
  - Check: Why do we keep a cube on white paper in the shade? → **To see how fast ice melts without any sunshine**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the times. Draw the bar chart. Ask what the different colours do with the sunlight that falls on them.  
  *Parent says:* Black melted its ice first. White lasted longest. The same sunlight landed on every paper. So what must the colours have done with it?  
  *Child mode:* The same sunlight landed on every paper. What did black do with it? What did white do?
  - Ask: Where did the light that landed on the black paper go? (good: It was soaked up.; It turned into heat.; The paper took in the light and got warm.)
  - Note: The paper that absorbs more light turns more of it to heat, and the heat melts the ice. The white paper reflects much of the light, so less becomes heat. Energy is not lost. It changes from light to heat.
  - Check: The ice melted fastest on black paper. What does this tell you about black paper and sunlight? → **It takes in more of the light and turns it into heat**
### e3 · The Invisible Flicker: what does a TV remote send out? (25 min)
- **question:** A TV remote sends a signal to the TV, and your eyes see nothing. Can a phone camera see what the remote sends, and does that signal go through things and bounce off things like light?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. When you look at the remote through a phone camera, will you see anything? Will the signal go through a piece of paper? Will it bounce off a mirror? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same remote, the same button and the same distance from the camera, 20 centimetres, every time. Keep the room dim so any flicker is easy to see. Only the thing you put in the way changes. A test with nothing in the way is the control.
- **safety:** Do not open the remote. Keep batteries away from young children, who must not put them in their mouths. The invisible light from a remote is harmless to your eyes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the remote, the phone and a wavy line between them, and write what you saw on the screen.
- **results table:** What was in the way | Did the flicker show on the phone screen: yes, faint or no? | Did the TV still obey the remote: yes or no?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the remote, the phone and the mirror. Ask for guesses and reasons about what the camera will show. Write them in the table notes.  
  *Parent says:* A remote controls the TV from across the room, and you cannot see anything leaving it. Will the phone camera see anything? Will paper block it? Will a mirror bounce it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A remote sends a signal you cannot see. Will a phone camera see it? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think leaves the front of the remote? (good: Light that we cannot see.; A radio wave.; Some sort of invisible beam.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Remote controls use infrared, which is light just beyond red. Your child's eyes cannot see it. The test shows whether a camera can.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Dim the room. First look at the front of the remote with your own eyes, and press a button. Then switch on the phone's front camera. Hold the remote 20 centimetres from it. Press a button while looking at the screen.  
  *Parent says:* First, look at the front of the remote with just your eyes. Press a button. What do you see? Now look through the phone's camera. What do you see now?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the remote with your eyes. Press a button. Now look through the phone.
  - Note: Expected: nothing with the eyes, and a pale purple or white flicker on the screen. The front camera of most phones shows it. Some rear cameras have a filter that hides it. If you see nothing, try another phone, the front camera or a laptop webcam. If nothing works, write that down. The TV test in the last step still works.
  - Check: Your eyes saw nothing from the remote, but the phone camera showed a flicker. What is the best explanation? → **The remote sends light that our eyes cannot see, but the camera can**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep the remote 20 centimetres from the camera. Put the paper in front of the remote, then the card, then the steel plate, then the glass tumbler. Press a button each time and write down what the screen shows.  
  *Parent says:* Put the paper in the way. Does the flicker still show? Now try the thick card, then steel, then glass. Which ones let the invisible light through?  
  *Child mode:* Put things in front of the remote one by one. Does the flicker still show?
  - Note: Results vary with the remote and the room, so write what you see. Often thin paper lets some through and looks fainter, thick card and steel block most of it, and glass lets it through. Do not worry if yours differ.
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Face the TV and hold the mirror to one side. Aim the remote at the mirror. Tilt the mirror so that it points to the TV. Press the button and see if the TV obeys. Then try aiming at a white wall beside the TV.  
  *Parent says:* Can we bounce the remote's signal to the TV with a mirror? Aim at the mirror and tilt it toward the TV. Does the TV obey? Does a wall work too?  
  *Child mode:* Aim the remote at the mirror. Tilt it toward the TV. Does the TV obey?
  - Note: A tilted mirror often works, because the invisible light reflects off the mirror just like visible light. A pale wall near the TV may also work, because the wall scatters some of the light onto the TV. If the TV does not obey, try getting closer and aiming more carefully.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the results with what you guessed. Ask what the experiment shows about the light we can see.  
  *Parent says:* The remote sends light that we cannot see. It goes through some things, gets blocked by others, and bounces off a mirror. So what is it like?  
  *Child mode:* The remote sends light you cannot see. Does it act like light you can see?
  - Ask: What did the camera show that your eyes could not? (good: A flicker from the remote.; Invisible light.; That there is light beyond what we can see.)
  - Note: This is the same lesson as Herschel's thermometer. There is light that our eyes do not catch, and other instruments can show it.
  - Check: The remote's signal bounced off a mirror and was blocked by a steel plate. What does this suggest about it? → **It behaves like light, though we cannot see it**

### p1 · The Grease-Spot Light Meter: which torch is brighter, and by how much? (70 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** Never shine a torch into anyone's eyes. Keep the cooking oil away from the torches and wipe your hands afterwards.
- **record:** A table with the distance of each torch from the paper when the spot vanished, and the ratio of the squares of the distances. Draw the set-up and write what the spot looked like when it vanished.
- **schedule:** day 1: Make the oil spot paper. Compare the phone torch with one key-ring torch and work out how many times brighter the phone torch is. (40 min); day 2: Tape the two key-ring torches together and test whether they give twice the light of one. (30 min)
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the two torches. Ask which they think is brighter, and by how many times. Write the guess. Explain that the paper with the oil spot will be the judge.  
  *Parent says:* Which torch is brighter, the phone torch or the small key-ring torch? How many times brighter? We will build a meter to find out. Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which torch is brighter, the phone or the key-ring torch? How many times brighter? Why?
  - Ask: How could you tell exactly how many times brighter one torch is? (good: Count how far away I need to put each torch to make them match.; Compare the patches they make on a wall.; I do not know.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Children often guess that the phone torch is about twice as bright, and are surprised by the answer. A strong phone torch is often many times brighter than a key-ring torch.
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a circle the size of a coin in the middle of the paper with a pencil. Rub a few drops of cooking oil over it with cotton wool. Wait 2 minutes. Blot off extra oil. Hold the paper up to a window. The spot should glow more than the rest. Stand the paper upright in the middle of the table, held in clay.  
  *Parent says:* The oil makes the paper thinner for light. More light can pass through the spot. Hold it up to the window. Does the spot look different from the rest of the paper?  
  *Child mode:* Rub oil on a coin-sized circle. Hold it to the window. The spot looks different.
  - Note: If the spot is not clear, use a little more oil. Blot well, so that no oil runs. Mark the middle of the table. The paper stands there with the oil spot facing along the table.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make the room dark. Put the phone torch 50 centimetres from the paper, on one side, pointing at it. Put the key-ring torch on the other side, close to the paper. Look at the paper from beside the phone torch. Slide the key-ring torch away slowly until the spot almost vanishes. Measure both distances.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the spot. Is it darker or brighter than the paper around it? Slide the small torch slowly. When the spot vanishes, both sides are getting the same light. Stop and measure.  
  *Child mode:* Slide the small torch away slowly. Stop when the spot almost vanishes. Measure both distances.
  - Note: The spot looks dark when the near side is brighter, and bright when the far side is brighter. It nearly vanishes when the two sides get equal light. Look from one side, a little off the line of the torches, so you do not block the light. Take your time. The point of vanishing is a range of a few centimetres, so choose the middle. Repeat twice.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the two distances. Work out each one multiplied by itself. Divide the bigger by the smaller. This is how many times brighter the phone torch is.  
  *Parent says:* Light thins out with the square of the distance. So we multiply each distance by itself. Then we divide. For example, if the phone is at 50 and the key-ring is at 20, then 2500 divided by 400 is about 6.  
  *Child mode:* Multiply each distance by itself. Divide the bigger by the smaller. That is how many times brighter.
  - Check: The phone torch is 50 centimetres from the paper, and the key-ring torch is 25 centimetres away when the spot vanishes. How many times brighter is the phone torch? → **4 times brighter**
- **s5 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, tape the two key-ring torches together so that they point the same way. Repeat the test with the phone torch fixed at 50 centimetres. Find the new distance where the spot vanishes. Work out how many times brighter than the phone torch the pair is, and compare with the single torch.  
  *Parent says:* Now two torches give light on one side. Should the spot vanish at a different distance? Do the sum again. Is the pair about twice as bright as one?  
  *Child mode:* Tape the two small torches together. Find the new place where the spot vanishes. Do the sum again.
  - Note: The pair should balance at about 1.4 times the distance of a single torch, because 1.4 times 1.4 is about 2. That means the pair is about twice as bright. Real results will be rough. If your answer is between 1.5 and 2.5, the meter is working.
  - Wait: Day 2

### c1 · Ice Challenge: melt one cube first, and keep another one longest (40 min)
- **problem:** You have two ice cubes of the same size and one hour of sunshine. Using only things from the kitchen, make one cube melt as fast as you can, and keep the other cube from melting for as long as you can.

### Light Spreads, Warms and Has a Family (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A lamp lights a page 1 metre away. The page is moved to 2 metres away. What happens to the light that falls on one square centimetre of the page?  
   Options: It becomes one quarter as much / It becomes half as much / It stays the same / It becomes double  
   **Answer:** It becomes one quarter as much. *Why:* At twice the distance, the same light covers four times the area. So each square centimetre gets one quarter as much. Picking half is the common guess, because the distance doubled. The patch grows in two directions, wide and tall, so the area grows four times.
2. (truefalse) A star far away looks dim because its light gets used up on the journey through empty space. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In empty space, light is not used up. It spreads over a bigger and bigger area, so the light that reaches your eye is thin. A strong answer says the energy is not lost, only shared out.
3. (fill) If you go three times as far from a lamp, the same light covers ____ times the area.  
   **Answer:** ['9', 'nine']. *Why:* Three times as far makes the patch three times as wide and three times as tall. Three times three is nine. So each part of the patch gets one ninth as much light.
4. (match) Match each thing to what it does with most of the light that lands on it.  
   **Answer:** a polished steel plate → reflects most of the light; a black cloth → absorbs most of the light; a clear window pane → lets most of the light through; white paper → reflects most of the light and absorbs some. *Why:* A surface can reflect light, absorb it or let it through, and most do a mix. The three parts always add up to all the light that landed.
5. (mcq) A school bag left in the sun feels hotter when it is black than when it is white. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Black takes in more of the sunlight and turns it into heat / Black makes its own heat / White bags are thicker / The Sun shines harder on black things  
   **Answer:** Black takes in more of the sunlight and turns it into heat. *Why:* The sunlight is the same for both bags. Black absorbs most of it, so the energy of the light becomes heat. White reflects much of it away. Picking 'the Sun shines harder' is a common idea, but the sunlight on the two bags is the same.
6. (sort) Sort these. Does it mostly reflect sunlight or mostly absorb it?  
   **Answer:** a whitewashed wall → Mostly reflects sunlight; aluminium foil → Mostly reflects sunlight; a black tar road → Mostly absorbs sunlight; a black umbrella → Mostly absorbs sunlight; a white shirt → Mostly reflects sunlight; a dark blue school bag → Mostly absorbs sunlight. *Why:* Light colours and shiny surfaces send most sunlight back, so they stay cooler. Dark surfaces take it in and turn it to heat, so they warm up fast.
7. (truefalse) When a black road absorbs sunlight, the energy of the light is destroyed. True or false?  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Energy is never destroyed. When the road absorbs sunlight, the energy turns into heat, and the road gets warmer. A strong answer says where the energy went.
8. (write) Kavya says, 'The street light at the end of our road is dim, so it must be a weak lamp.' Another lamp of the same kind, right outside her window, looks very bright. What would you tell Kavya?  
   **Answer:** The two lamps could be the same. The far lamp's light has spread over a much bigger area by the time it reaches Kavya, so each part of her eye gets less. Brightness depends on distance as well as on the lamp.. *Why:* A strong answer says the lamp is not weaker, and gives distance and spreading as the reason.
9. (mcq) Which member of the light family can our eyes see?  
   Options: Visible light / Infrared from a TV remote / Radio waves from a tower / X-rays in a hospital  
   **Answer:** Visible light. *Why:* Our eyes catch only visible light, from violet to red. Infrared, radio waves and X-rays are real, but our eyes cannot see them.
10. (order) Put these members of the light family in order, from the longest wavelength to the shortest.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. *Why:* The family runs from radio waves, as long as a room, down to X-rays, as short as an atom. Visible light sits in the middle, closer to the short end.
11. (fill) The invisible light that a TV remote sends out is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['infrared']. *Why:* Infrared is light just beyond red. The remote sends a pattern of flickers of it, and the TV reads the pattern.
12. (draw) Draw a torch, a card with a small square hole in it, and a wall. Show the light spreading after the hole. Then show how the patch on a wall twice as far away would look.  
   **Answer:** A torch on the left, the card in the middle, and two walls on the right, one near and one far. Straight lines spread out from the hole. The patch on the far wall is much bigger, about four times the area, and fainter.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the lines spreading out in a cone and the far patch covering more area with less light on each part.
13. (mcq) In 1800, William Herschel found that a thermometer placed just past the red end of the colours got warm. What did this show?  
   Options: Sunlight has rays that our eyes cannot see / Red light is hotter than the Sun / The thermometer was broken / Dark places are always warm  
   **Answer:** Sunlight has rays that our eyes cannot see. *Why:* Nothing could be seen in that spot, yet the thermometer warmed. So something unseen, now called infrared, was arriving from the Sun. Picking 'the thermometer was broken' is the first thing many people think. Herschel's controls in the shade did not warm, so it was not the room.
14. (perspective) Three children look at the dark patch just beyond the red end of a rainbow made by a prism. Each says something different.  
   **Answer:** Arjun's view fits, since Herschel's thermometer warmed in the dark patch. The aunt is right that we need an instrument, which is what Herschel used. Meenakshi's idea was what most people thought, because dark seems to mean no light.. *Why:* A strong answer picks one view with evidence from the thermometer and credits a point from another.
15. (truefalse) Radio waves and X-rays are the same kind of wave as visible light, only with a different wavelength. True or false?  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. All the members of the family are waves of electric and magnetic fields that travel at the speed of light in empty space. They differ in wavelength, and so in the energy of each photon.
16. (count) A torch makes a lit square patch 5 squares wide and 5 squares tall when the paper is 20 centimetres away. How many squares would the patch cover at 40 centimetres?

   **Answer:** About one twenty-fifth. *Why:* Five times as far means the light is spread over 5 times 5, which is 25 times the area. So each square metre gets about one twenty-fifth as much. Picking one fifth is the common slip, as it forgets that the area grows in two directions.

   **Answer:** Invisible rays from the Sun, which we now call infrared, fell on the thermometer's black bulb. The bulb absorbed them, and their energy turned into heat. So the thermometer warmed even though no light could be seen.. *Why:* A strong answer says something unseen carried energy to the thermometer and that absorbed energy becomes heat.
11. (mcq) The Earth stays at a fairly steady temperature over the years. What is the best reason?

8. (mcq) Why can our eyes not see infrared?  
   Options: The light-catching cells in our eyes respond only to a thin slice of wavelengths / Infrared is not really light / Infrared is too bright for our eyes / Infrared stops at the eyelid  
   **Answer:** The light-catching cells in our eyes respond only to a thin slice of wavelengths. *Why:* Our eyes are built to catch wavelengths from about 0.0004 to 0.0007 millimetres. Infrared is a little longer, and our eye cells do not respond to it. It is a real member of the light family, and a phone camera can catch it.
9. (mcq) Which carries more energy in each photon, ultraviolet or infrared?  
   Options: Ultraviolet / Infrared / They carry the same / It depends on the colour of the sky  
   **Answer:** Ultraviolet. *Why:* The shorter the wavelength, the more energy each photon carries. Ultraviolet is shorter than visible light, and infrared is longer. That is why ultraviolet can burn skin, and infrared only warms it.
10. (mcq) Why does the tail of a comet point away from the Sun?  
   Options: Sunlight and the stream of particles from the Sun push the dust and gas outward / The comet is falling into the Sun / The tail is left behind as the comet moves / The Sun's gravity pulls the tail  
   **Answer:** Sunlight and the stream of particles from the Sun push the dust and gas outward. *Why:* Light carries a small push, and the Sun also sends out a stream of particles. Together these push the comet's dust and gas away from the Sun. Picking 'left behind' is a common idea, because a kite's tail trails behind it.
11. (mcq) How is the energy in a rice grain connected to the Sun?  
   Options: The rice plant caught sunlight and stored the energy in the grain / The grain was warmed by the Sun while drying / The grain makes its own energy from nothing / The soil gives the grain its energy  
   **Answer:** The rice plant caught sunlight and stored the energy in the grain. *Why:* Plants use sunlight to make sugar from water and air, and store the energy in their grains. Warming in the Sun does not add the energy we get from food.
12. (mcq) Gases such as carbon dioxide in the air slow the escape of infrared from the Earth. What happens to the Earth's temperature when there is more of them?  
   Options: The Earth warms up / The Earth cools down / Nothing changes / The Earth gets brighter at night  
   **Answer:** The Earth warms up. *Why:* The Earth settles where the energy going out equals the energy coming in. If the gases slow the way out, the Earth must get warmer before the balance is reached again.

## Applications
- Solar water heaters on rooftops turn sunlight into heat for baths and kitchens.
- Solar panels turn light into electricity for homes, farms, lamps and water pumps.
- White roof paint and lime-washed walls reflect sunlight and keep rooms cooler.
- Thermal cameras show warm bodies in the dark and spot fevers at airports.
- TV, air conditioner and set-top box remotes send invisible infrared signals.
- Radio, TV, mobile phones and Wi-Fi all use long waves from the light family.
- Hospitals use X-rays to see bones and carefully aimed beams to treat some illnesses.
- Sunscreen, hats and umbrellas protect skin from ultraviolet light.
- Radio telescopes such as the GMRT near Pune listen to waves from far galaxies.
- Solar sails may one day move spacecraft with the push of sunlight and no fuel.

## Open questions
- **What happened before the oldest light in the sky was set free?** The oldest light shows us the universe about 380,000 years after it began. Before that, the universe was too hot and dense for light to travel, so we cannot see further back with light.
- **Why is the glow of the oldest light so smooth in every direction?** Inflation, a very fast early growth, is the favourite explanation. It has not been proved, and some scientists suggest other ideas.
- **Could a fleet of tiny sail craft pushed by lasers really reach another star?** The idea is built on a real push of light, but the engineering is very hard. Nobody has tested a craft at that scale.
- **Is there light we cannot yet detect, or waves of another kind that we have not found?** Every few decades, scientists have found new ways to see. Nobody can be sure there is nothing left to find.
- **What does it feel like to a pit viper or a bee to sense a slice of the family that we cannot?** We can measure what animals respond to, but nobody knows what their experience is like from inside.
- **Why do the amounts of energy come in packets, called photons, at all?** Quantum physics predicts how photons behave very well. Why light is built this way is still argued about.

## Connected maths
- A torch patch is 3 squares wide and 3 squares tall when the paper is 10 centimetres away. The paper moves to 30 centimetres, three times as far. How many squares does the patch cover now, and how many times as many as before? **81 squares, which is 9 times as many.**. At 3 times the distance, the patch is 3 times wider and 3 times taller. That is 9 squares by 9 squares. 9 times 9 is 81. At first it was 3 times 3, which is 9. 81 divided by 9 is 9. So the patch covers 9 times as many squares. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- One lantern gives 144 units of light at 1 step. At 4 steps, the light is spread over 4 times 4 times the area. How many units does the lantern give at 4 steps? **9 units.**. 4 times 4 is 16. The same 144 units are shared by 16 times the area. 144 divided by 16 is 9. Check: 9 times 16 is 144. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In an Ice Race, the cube on black paper melted in 12 minutes, and the cube on white paper melted in 30 minutes. The cube on white paper lasted how many minutes longer, and about how many times as long? **18 minutes longer, and about 2 and a half times as long.**. 30 take away 12 is 18. For the times: 12 times 2 is 24, and 12 times 2 and a half is 30. So white lasted 2 and a half times as long as black. A made-up example. Your own numbers will differ. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Jupiter is about 5 times further from the Sun than the Earth is. Saturn is about 10 times further. How many times thinner is sunlight on Jupiter, and how many times thinner on Saturn, compared with the Earth? **About 25 times thinner on Jupiter, and about 100 times thinner on Saturn.**. Light thins by the square of the distance. For Jupiter, 5 times 5 is 25. For Saturn, 10 times 10 is 100. So sunlight on Saturn is about 100 times thinner than on Earth. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- The sunlight that falls on the Earth in one hour holds about as much energy as all of humanity uses in a year. There are 24 hours in a day and 365 days in a year. About how many times more energy does the Sun send to the Earth in a year than humanity uses? **About 8,760 times more.**. 24 times 365 is 24 times 300, which is 7,200, plus 24 times 65, which is 1,560. 7,200 plus 1,560 is 8,760. So one year holds 8,760 hours. Each hour matches one year of use. So the Sun sends about 8,760 times as much as we use. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- About 100 red light waves fit across the width of a hair. A hair is about 0.07 millimetres wide. About how wide is one red light wave in millimetres? **About 0.0007 millimetres.**. Divide the width of the hair by 100. 0.07 divided by 100 is 0.0007. Dividing by 100 moves the digits two places to the right. So one red wave is about 0.0007 millimetres. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In the Grease-Spot Light Meter, the phone torch was 50 centimetres from the paper and the key-ring torch was 20 centimetres away when the spot vanished. About how many times brighter is the phone torch? **About 6 times brighter.**. Multiply each distance by itself. 50 times 50 is 2,500. 20 times 20 is 400. 2,500 divided by 400 is 6 and a quarter, so about 6 times brighter. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- Imagine a white wall reflects 8 out of every 10 parts of the sunlight that land on it, and absorbs the rest. If 500 units of sunlight land on the wall, how many units become heat? These numbers are made up. **100 units.**. 8 out of 10 are reflected, so 2 out of 10 are absorbed. 2 tenths of 500 is 500 divided by 10, which is 50, times 2, which is 100. So 100 units become heat. A black wall might absorb 9 out of 10, which is 450 units. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- You tape two key-ring torches together. Each gives 1 unit of light at a certain distance. At twice that distance, the light is spread over 4 times the area. How many units does the pair give at twice the distance? **Half a unit.**. Two torches give 2 units. At twice the distance, the light is 4 times thinner. 2 divided by 4 is one half. So the pair at twice the distance gives only half a unit, which is less than one torch gave at the start. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
