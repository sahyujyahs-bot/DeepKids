# Plants of India: how a plant's own protection became our medicine
`sci.plants.plant-variety.L4`

**Summary.** A clove is a dried flower bud, and the strong-smelling chemical inside it keeps moulds and insects off the bud. Neem leaves, haldi, chilli and tulsi carry chemicals like this too. Plants make them to protect themselves, and people have borrowed them as medicines, spices and colours for thousands of years. Your child runs a fair test to see whether haldi and clove really stop mould, with a results table and a line graph. They also meet trees and flowers that grow naturally only in India. They learn how scientists test an old remedy and who should own that knowledge. Last, they learn to care for a pot plant and grow a new one from a cutting.

**Intro.** *Open your spice box. Pick up one clove and look at it closely. Roll it between your fingers, then smell your fingers. That smell is strong. Who is the smell for?* A clove is not a seed or a stick. It is a flower bud, picked before it opened and dried in the sun. The bud is soft and full of food, so moulds and insects would love to eat it. Yet a clove bud keeps for months in a jar without going mouldy. Many grandmothers put a few cloves or dry neem leaves in the rice and dal tins. Is that only an old habit, or does it really work? In this unit you test it fairly, with numbers. Then you find out why so many medicines began inside plants.

## Terms
- **Medicinal plant**: A plant with a part that people use to treat an illness or to stay well. *e.g.* Tulsi leaves in kadha for a cough, and ajwain seeds for a stomach ache.
- **Defence chemical**: A chemical a plant makes to protect themselves from animals, insects, moulds or germs. Many are bitter, smelly, hot or sticky. *e.g.* Neem leaves taste bitter because of their defence chemicals.
- **Traditional knowledge**: What a community has learned over many generations, and passes on by teaching, stories and practice. *e.g.* A grandmother in Thanjavur knows that dry neem leaves in the paddy store keep insects away.
- **Mould**: A fungus that grows as fuzzy threads on damp food. Moulds digest the food and use it to grow. *e.g.* Green or white fuzz on an old piece of bread or a forgotten lemon is mould.
- **Spore**: A tiny cell that a mould or fungus sends out to start a new mould somewhere else. Spores float in the air everywhere. *e.g.* A puff of green dust from an old orange is millions of mould spores.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same, so you know what caused the result. *e.g.* To test haldi on potato, every potato square is the same size, in the same kind of box, in the same cupboard.
- **Control**: The part of a test where you add nothing new, so you can compare. It shows what happens anyway. *e.g.* In the Spice Guard test, the plain potato square is the control.
- **Placebo**: Something that looks like a medicine but has nothing in it that should work. People in a trial get it so they cannot tell which group they are in. *e.g.* A sugar pill that looks just like a real tablet is a placebo.
- **Dose**: How much of a medicine someone takes, and how often. The right dose helps. Too much can harm. *e.g.* A doctor writes 5 ml twice a day on a bottle of cough syrup. That is the dose.
- **Endemic**: Found naturally in only one place on Earth, and nowhere else in the wild. *e.g.* The pitcher plant Nepenthes khasiana is endemic to Meghalaya.
- **Native plant**: A plant that grew in a place on their own, before people brought plants from other lands. *e.g.* Neem and peepal are native to India. Gulmohar came from Madagascar.
- **Invasive plant**: A plant brought from another land who spreads so fast that they crowd out the native plants. *e.g.* Lantana came from Central and South America and now covers forest floors across India.
- **Sacred grove**: A patch of forest that a community protects for their gods or ancestors. Trees there are not cut. *e.g.* A kavu in Kerala and a devrai in Maharashtra are sacred groves.
- **Node**: The place on a stem where a leaf grows out. A node can grow new roots, and it holds a bud for a new shoot. *e.g.* On a money plant, each node has a leaf and a little brown bump that can become a root.
- **Cutting**: A piece cut from a plant that can grow roots and become a whole new plant. *e.g.* A money plant cutting in a glass of water grows new roots in about two weeks.
- **Drainage**: The way extra water flows out of the soil. A pot needs holes at the bottom for drainage. *e.g.* Water dripping out of the holes under a pot after watering shows good drainage.
- **Patent**: A legal right that lets only the inventor make or sell a new invention for some years. *e.g.* In 1995 a patent office in America gave a patent for using turmeric on wounds. India showed it was not new.

## Explanations
### Why plants make strong chemicals · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants cannot run away from an insect, a goat or a mould. So many plants make chemicals that are bitter, smelly, hot or sticky. These defence chemicals keep many attackers away.

An animal that is being chased can run. A plant stays where their seed landed. So plants protect themselves in other ways, and chemistry is one of the biggest. Many plants keep defence chemicals in their leaves, bark, buds, seeds or roots all the time. Many plants also sense when they are being chewed and make more defence chemicals within hours. A tomato plant bitten by a caterpillar sends signals to their other leaves, and those leaves get ready too. Some plants even give off a smell that nearby plants pick up. Plants sense, remember and decide. Some scientists call these automatic responses, built up over millions of years of evolution. Scientists have found many thousands of these chemicals, and new ones are found every year.
- e.g. Neem leaves and seeds hold azadirachtin. Insects who eat it stop feeding, and young insects cannot grow into adults properly.
- e.g. A clove bud is packed with eugenol, which has a strong smell and stops many moulds growing.
- e.g. Crushed garlic makes allicin in a few seconds. It smells sharp and harms many germs.
### A plant's shield, borrowed by us · *defined*
**Definition:** Many medicines, spices and colours that people use are a plant's own defence chemicals, taken out and used in a new way.

When people found that a bitter leaf or a strong-smelling bud did something useful, they kept using it and passed the knowledge on. Clove oil has been dabbed on aching teeth for centuries, and dentists still use eugenol from cloves in some fillings. Neem leaves have been kept in grain stores to keep insects off. Today neem extracts are used in sprays that farmers put on crops. The heat in chilli comes from capsaicin, a defence chemical, and creams made from capsaicin are used to ease some kinds of pain. Even the yellow of haldi is a chemical called curcumin, which the turmeric plant keeps in their underground stem. So the plant's protection is the reason the plant is useful to us.
- e.g. Eugenol from cloves is used in some dental cements and in clove oil for toothache.
- e.g. Neem extracts are used in sprays that keep insects off crops.
- e.g. India grows most of the world's turmeric. The yellow curcumin is only a few parts in every hundred of the powder.
### Not every shield is a chemical · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants also protect themselves with thorns, hairs, sticky sap, tough leaves and even movement.

Run a finger gently along a bhindi leaf and you feel tiny hairs. Small insects find it hard to walk and feed on a hairy leaf. The bael tree has long sharp thorns, which keep goats from eating the branches. A rubber tree is full of milky sap called latex. When an insect bites, the latex flows out and becomes sticky, and it can glue the insect's mouth shut. The touch-me-not plant folds their leaves within a second or two when touched. Many scientists think this startles leaf-eaters, or makes the plant look less tasty. That idea is still being tested.
- e.g. Bael has sharp thorns along their branches.
- e.g. Papaya and rubber trees leak sticky white sap when cut.
- e.g. The touch-me-not folds their leaves when a finger, or an insect, touches them.
### The chilli and the birds · *defined*
**Definition:** Capsaicin, the chemical that makes chilli hot, burns the mouths of mammals like us. Birds feel no burn at all.

A chilli plant's seeds grow best when they are carried far away. A mouse or a squirrel chews chilli seeds and crushes them, so those seeds never grow. A bird swallows the seeds whole, flies off and drops them somewhere new, still able to sprout. In 2001, two American scientists, Joshua Tewksbury and Gary Nabhan, showed that wild chillies are eaten by birds but avoided by rodents. So capsaicin works like a door that is shut to seed-crushers and open to seed-carriers. Chillies first grew in the Americas. Portuguese traders brought them to India about 500 years ago. Before that, Indian cooks made food hot with black pepper and long pepper, which are native to India.
- e.g. A bulbul or a myna can peck at a ripe red chilli without any trouble.
- e.g. Before chillies came, food in Kerala and Tamil Nadu was made hot with black pepper.
- e.g. Capsaicin creams for some kinds of pain use the same chemical that burns your tongue.
### Medicines that came from Indian plants · *defined*
**Definition:** Several medicines used around the world began as plants used in India's healing traditions or by village healers.

India's healing traditions, such as Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani and Sowa-Rigpa, use thousands of kinds of plants. Village and Adivasi healers know many more that are written in no book. Scientists have tested some of these plants and found real medicines inside. Sarpagandha root led to reserpine, one of the first medicines for high blood pressure. Asima Chatterjee, a chemist in Kolkata, spent her life studying the chemicals in Indian plants. She helped make Ayush-56, a medicine for epilepsy, from a small water fern called Marsilea minuta. In Bengal this fern is eaten as a leafy vegetable called sushni shak. In 1987, Kani guides in the forests of Kerala showed scientists a fruit called arogyapaacha, which they ate to stay strong on long walks. Scientists at a Kerala research garden used the plant to make a tonic called Jeevani.
- e.g. Sarpagandha root gave reserpine, a medicine for high blood pressure.
- e.g. Asima Chatterjee was the first woman to earn a Doctor of Science degree from an Indian university, in 1944.
- e.g. The Kani people received a share of the money from Jeevani. This was one of the first times a company paid a forest community for their knowledge.
### How scientists test an old remedy · *defined*
**Definition:** To find out whether a remedy really works, scientists compare two groups that are the same in every way except the remedy. One group gets the remedy and the other, the control group, does not.

Many illnesses, like a cold, get better on their own. So if you drink kadha and feel better in five days, you cannot tell whether the kadha helped. A fair trial needs two groups of people, as alike as possible. One group gets the remedy. The control group gets a placebo, something that looks and tastes the same but should do nothing. Nobody in the trial knows which group they are in, and often the doctors do not know either. This is called a blind trial. The trial needs many people, because one person's result can be luck. Scientists first test a plant in the laboratory, as you did with the potato squares. Only if it looks useful and safe do they test it in people. One of the first fair trials was in 1747, when a Scottish ship's doctor, James Lind, gave 12 sailors with scurvy six different remedies. The two who ate oranges and lemons got better fastest.
- e.g. Spice Guard is a laboratory test. The test shows a chemical can stop mould on food, not that the chemical cures anyone.
- e.g. Many trials compare the remedy with a placebo, because people often feel better just from being cared for.
- e.g. James Lind's trial had only 12 sailors. Today a good trial may have hundreds or thousands of people.
### The dose decides: help or harm · *defined*
**Definition:** The same plant chemical can help in a small amount and harm in a large one. How much, how often and in which form all matter.

Defence chemicals are strong on purpose: they are meant to hurt attackers. That is why they can help us and also why they can harm. A drop of clove oil on cotton, held on an aching tooth, can numb it a little. But a small child who drinks a spoonful of clove oil can become seriously ill. Neem oil swallowed by small children has also caused serious poisoning. Even haldi, which is safe in food, can upset the stomach in very large amounts. This is why a medicine has a dose written on it, and why doctors test how much is safe. A remedy being natural does not make it gentle.
- e.g. A drop of clove oil can ease a toothache, but swallowing clove oil can harm a child badly.
- e.g. Neem leaves in a rice tin are safe. Neem oil must never be given to a child to drink.
- e.g. Keep all oils and medicines out of reach of small children, natural or not.
### Does haldi work as a medicine? · *debated*
Many Indian families use haldi in milk for a cold and on small cuts. Thousands of laboratory studies have tested curcumin, the yellow chemical in haldi. In test tubes it does many things. In 2017, a team of American chemists led by Kathryn Nelson looked at these studies closely. They argued that curcumin is a poor medicine. The body absorbs very little of it, it breaks down quickly, and it can give false results in laboratory tests. Other scientists point out that black pepper helps the body absorb more curcumin. They also say whole turmeric holds other chemicals too, and that some small trials look hopeful. Nobody has yet shown in a large, fair trial that haldi cures any illness. Scientists are still arguing, and the trials go on.
- e.g. In 1998, scientists in Bengaluru found that piperine from black pepper raised the amount of curcumin in people's blood many times over.
- e.g. Haldi in food is safe for almost everyone. Whether haldi doodh shortens a cold has not been settled by a large fair trial.
- e.g. Haldi turns cloth and steel vessels yellow, which shows how strongly curcumin sticks to things.
- **Kathryn Nelson and her team, chemists in America:** Curcumin breaks down fast, hardly gets into the blood, and fools many laboratory tests. Thousands of studies have not given one proven medicine, so we should be careful about the claims.
- **Scientists who still study turmeric:** Whole turmeric is more than curcumin, and taking it with black pepper helps the body take it in. Some small trials look hopeful, so larger fair trials are worth doing.
- **Many Indian grandmothers:** Our families have used haldi for generations. It is safe in food, and it makes people feel cared for. Even if science has not proved it yet, it does no harm in the kitchen.
### Who owns traditional knowledge? · *debated*
In 1995, a patent office in America gave two researchers a patent for using turmeric powder to heal wounds. That meant others could not sell it for this use without permission. Scientists at India's CSIR, led by R. A. Mashelkar, showed old Indian texts that described this use long before. In 1997 the patent was cancelled. In 1994, the European Patent Office gave a patent on a way to use neem oil to kill moulds on crops. Vandana Shiva from India, Magda Aelvoet, a Belgian politician, and Linda Bullard of an organic farming group fought it. They showed that Indian farmers had used neem this way for generations. The patent was cancelled in 2000. India then built the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library, so patent offices can check old Indian knowledge before they give a patent. But there is a harder question. When a company does make a new medicine from a community's plant, who should be paid?
- e.g. The Kani people of Kerala received half the licence fee for Jeevani, through a trust set up in 1997.
- e.g. The Traditional Knowledge Digital Library was set up in 2001 by CSIR and the government department for Ayurveda and other traditional systems.
- e.g. A patent lasts about 20 years. After that, anyone can use the invention.
- **Many forest and village communities:** We found these plants and kept the knowledge alive for generations. If a company earns money from it, we should agree first and share the money.
- **Many companies and some scientists:** Turning a leaf into a safe, tested medicine takes years of work and a lot of money. A patent on the new medicine pays for that work. Without it, nobody would do the testing.
- **People who want knowledge to be free:** Knowledge about plants should belong to everyone, like a public library. No company and no community should be able to lock it away.
### Plants found only in India · *defined*
**Definition:** An endemic plant grows naturally in only one place on Earth. India has thousands of endemic plants, many of them in the Western Ghats, the north-east, the Himalayas and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

India has more than 18,000 kinds of flowering plants. Thousands of them grow wild nowhere else on Earth. Hills and islands are full of endemic plants, because their plants were cut off from other places for a very long time. The red sanders tree, whose deep red wood is very valuable, grows wild only in the southern Eastern Ghats of Andhra Pradesh and a few places close by. Smugglers cut so many that the tree is now endangered. India's only native pitcher plant, Nepenthes khasiana, grows only in Meghalaya. They catch insects in a jug-shaped leaf full of liquid. The neelakurinji shrub covers the high grassy hills of the Nilgiris, Munnar and Kodaikanal. Each plant blooms once in 12 years, and then dies.
- e.g. Red sanders grows wild in the Seshachalam hills near Tirupati.
- e.g. Nepenthes khasiana grows in the Khasi, Jaintia and Garo hills of Meghalaya.
- e.g. Neelakurinji turned the hills of Munnar blue in 2018. The next big bloom there is expected in 2030.
### Plants that wait years to flower · *defined*
**Definition:** Some plants grow for many years without flowering. Then all the plants of that kind flower together, make seeds, and die.

Neelakurinji plants on a hillside all flower in the same year, about every 12 years. The muli bamboo of Mizoram, Melocanna baccifera, flowers about every 48 years. When they flower, they make huge numbers of seeds. Rats eat the seeds, multiply fast, and then eat the farmers' rice. The Mizo people call this time mautam. Mautam came in 1959 and again from 2006 to 2008. The talipot palm of Kerala and Sri Lanka grows for 30 to 80 years. Then the palm makes one of the biggest flower clusters of any plant, and dies. How these plants keep count of the years, and how all of them flower together, is still not known.
- e.g. The 1959 mautam caused a famine in Mizoram.
- e.g. A talipot palm's flower cluster can be several metres tall, on top of the palm.
- e.g. Strobilanthes kunthiana is the scientific name of neelakurinji.
### Native plants and visitors · *defined*
**Definition:** Many common plants in India came from other continents, brought by traders, rulers and gardeners. A few of them spread so fast that they harm native plants.

Chilli, potato, tomato, papaya and cashew all came from the Americas within the last 500 years. The gulmohar, with their red summer flowers, came from Madagascar. Eucalyptus came from Australia. Even the tamarind in your rasam first grew in Africa, and reached India long ago. Most visitors live quietly alongside native plants. But some become invasive. Lantana, from Central and South America, was brought to India as a garden plant about 200 years ago. Today lantana covers huge areas of forest, and few native plants can grow under it. Water hyacinth from South America chokes lakes and rivers. Vilayati babul, called seemai karuvelam in Tamil Nadu, came from Mexico and Central America. They spread over dry land and take a lot of water from the ground.
- e.g. Amaltas, the golden shower tree, is native to India. Kerala calls them kanikonna and uses the flowers at Vishu.
- e.g. Gulmohar is from Madagascar, and jacaranda is from South America.
- e.g. The money plant in many Indian flats first came from islands in the Pacific Ocean.
### Caring for a pot plant · *defined*
**Definition:** A plant's roots need both water and air. Soil that is always soaked has no air in it, and the roots can rot.

Soil is full of tiny gaps between its grains. After watering, water fills some gaps and air fills the others. Roots take in water and also need the oxygen in that air. If a pot has no hole, or is watered again and again, the water fills every gap. The roots cannot get air, and moulds that cause root rot spread. Gardeners say that more pot plants die from too much water than from too little. So the best care is to check before you water. Push a finger about 2 cm into the soil. If it feels dry, water slowly until water comes out of the hole underneath. If it feels damp, wait. A plant also needs the right light. A money plant grows well in a bright room, and tulsi needs some direct sunlight.
- e.g. A pot plant with yellow, drooping leaves and soggy soil is often being given too much water.
- e.g. A clay pot dries out faster than a plastic pot, because water slowly escapes through the clay.
- e.g. In the monsoon, many balcony plants need much less water than in May.
### A new plant from a node · *defined*
**Definition:** A node is the place on a stem where a leaf grows. A node can grow new roots, and it holds a bud for a new shoot. So a cutting with a node can become a whole new plant.

Look closely at a money plant stem. Where each leaf joins the stem, there is a small brown bump. That bump can grow into a root. If you cut a piece of stem with a node and put the bottom in water, roots usually appear in one to two weeks. A piece of stem with no node usually grows no roots. Farmers use nodes all the time. Sugarcane is planted from pieces of cane, each with one or more nodes. Mint from the market will grow roots from the nodes in a glass of water. This is one of the easiest ways to make more plants and share them with friends.
- e.g. Sugarcane farmers plant short pieces of cane with buds at the nodes.
- e.g. A mint stem from the vegetable shop grows white roots from the nodes within about a week in water.
- e.g. Tulsi and hibiscus can also grow from cuttings with nodes.
### Sacred groves: caring for a forest together · *defined*
**Definition:** A sacred grove is a patch of forest that a village protects for their gods or ancestors. Often nobody may cut a tree or take more than a little fallen wood.

India has many thousands of sacred groves, with different names across the country. In Kerala they are kavu. In Maharashtra they are devrai. In Rajasthan they are oran, in Jharkhand sarna, and in Meghalaya law kyntang. Some are a few trees around a shrine. The Mawphlang sacred forest in Meghalaya covers many hectares. In the 1970s, the ecologist Madhav Gadgil and the botanist V. D. Vartak walked through the Western Ghats and studied these groves. They found that many groves still held old trees and rare plants that had vanished from the land around them. Because nobody had cut them, the groves showed what the forest had once looked like. Today scientists see sacred groves as seed banks that communities kept alive.
- e.g. In 1975, Gadgil and Vartak published a paper asking for sacred groves to be protected.
- e.g. Some sacred groves in Kerala have ponds and springs that never dry up, because the old trees hold the soil and water.
- e.g. In Mawphlang, people say that nothing may be taken out of the forest, not even a fallen leaf.
### Medicine plants in danger · *idea*
Most medicine plants sold in India are still collected from the wild, not grown on farms. When a plant becomes famous, collectors may dig up more than can grow back. Sarpagandha became so scarce in the wild that trade in this plant across borders is now controlled by an international agreement. Red sanders and many Himalayan herbs are endangered too. Nobody knows exactly how many of India's medicine plants are in danger, because many have never been counted. Some groups are now teaching farmers to grow medicine plants, so wild plants are left alone. Robin Wall Kimmerer, a botanist from the Potawatomi Nation in America, writes about the 'honourable harvest': take only what you need, and never take the last one.
- e.g. Sarpagandha is grown on farms now, so less needs to be dug from forests.
- e.g. When a root is the part used, collecting the medicine kills the plant.
- e.g. Seed banks and medicine plant gardens keep seeds safe for the future.

## Mostly agreed
- Plants make many chemicals that protect them from animals, insects, moulds and germs. *How we know:* Chemists can take these chemicals out of plants and test them. Insects fed on leaves with azadirachtin stop eating, and moulds grow poorly near eugenol from cloves.
- Many plants make more defence chemicals when they are being eaten. *How we know:* When scientists let caterpillars chew a tomato leaf, they can measure the defence chemicals rising, even in leaves the caterpillar never touched.
- Several important medicines first came from plants used by traditional healers. *How we know:* Reserpine came from sarpagandha root, which healers in India had used for centuries. Many such medicines were then tested in fair trials on patients.
- A remedy must be tested in a fair trial with a control group before we can say it works. *How we know:* Many illnesses get better on their own, and people feel better when they are cared for. Only a comparison with a control group can separate the remedy from these effects.
- India has thousands of endemic plants, and many are endangered. *How we know:* The Botanical Survey of India has listed plants from every region. Repeated surveys show some, like red sanders, disappearing from places where they once grew.
- Pot plants need roots with both water and air, and cuttings with a node can grow into new plants. *How we know:* Gardeners and scientists have seen roots rot in waterlogged soil many times. Cuttings with a node grow roots, while pieces without a node do not.

## How we found out
**G. Sen and K. C. Bose, doctors in Kolkata; Salimuzzaman Siddiqui and Rafat Husain Siddiqui, chemists; Rustom Jal Vakil, a heart doctor in Mumbai; chemists at a Swiss company who named reserpine, 1931 (first reports), 1949 (Vakil's study), 1952 (reserpine taken out of the root).** For centuries, healers in India used the root of sarpagandha, Indian snakeroot, to calm people who were very anxious or unwell in their minds, and for fevers and snakebite. Doctors trained in Europe mostly treated this as village folklore. In 1931, two doctors in Kolkata, G. Sen and K. C. Bose, reported that the root lowered blood pressure. The same year, the chemists Salimuzzaman Siddiqui and Rafat Husain Siddiqui took several new chemicals out of the root. Few doctors abroad paid attention. In 1949, the Mumbai heart doctor Rustom Jal Vakil published a careful study of patients in the British Heart Journal. Now the world listened. In 1952, chemists at a Swiss company took out the root's most powerful chemical and named it reserpine. Within a few years, reserpine was used around the world for high blood pressure. Later, doctors found it could cause sadness and tiredness in some people, so newer medicines replaced it. But it showed that a village remedy could be tested fairly, and be right.  
*What people believed before:* That village plant remedies from India were folklore not worth testing. Careful studies by Indian doctors and chemists showed that sarpagandha really lowers blood pressure.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Natural remedies have no side effects.** (Many people, and many advertisements.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some plant remedies have been used safely in food and homes for centuries.  
  Against: Plant defence chemicals are made to hurt attackers. Clove oil and neem oil have badly poisoned small children. The dose and the form decide whether a plant helps or harms.
- **Traditional knowledge is just superstition.** (Some people who trust only modern medicine.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some old remedies have been tested and did not work. Some old beliefs about plants were simply wrong.  
  Against: Reserpine, Ayush-56 and Jeevani all came from traditional and village knowledge. Healers had tested plants for generations by watching what happened. The right step is to test each claim fairly.
- **If a remedy is very old, it must work.** (Many people, out of respect for their elders.; *rejected*)  
  For: A remedy that has been used for centuries is at least unlikely to be very harmful in normal amounts.  
  Against: Many old remedies have never been tested fairly. People may have kept using them because the illness went away by itself. Age shows that people trusted a remedy, not that it works.
- **Traditional plant knowledge should be free for everyone, including companies.** (People who believe knowledge belongs to all, and some companies.; *debated*)  
  For: Knowledge spreads and helps more people when it is free, like a public library.  
  Against: Communities kept this knowledge alive and protected the plants. If a company makes money from it without asking or sharing, that is unfair to the people who found it.
- **A plant responds to a bite only where they are bitten.** (A common idea, because plants have no nerves like ours.; *rejected*)  
  For: The leaf that is bitten does change first and most.  
  Against: Scientists have measured signals travelling from a bitten leaf to the plant's other leaves within minutes. Some plants also give off smells that warn nearby plants. A plant responds as a whole.

## Roots
We have tested whether haldi and clove stop mould, and learned how plants protect themselves. Now we ask what this tells us about plants, medicines and ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the Spice Guard test, mould spread over the plain potato but stayed off the clove-covered piece. The rice flour did not protect the potato, so a powder alone was not the shield. A clove is a flower bud, and the clove's strong chemical guards the bud from moulds and insects. So many plant medicines are a plant's own defence, which people learned to borrow.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of medicines and spices as things made for people. But the clove's smell, the neem's bitterness and the chilli's heat were the plants' own protection, long before anyone cooked or healed with them.  
*Evidence:* In Spice Guard, the plain potato square and the rice flour square grew mould within a few days. The clove square stayed clean or nearly clean. The plain rice flour was the same kind of dry powder, so the protection came from the clove's chemical, eugenol. If your own squares gave different numbers, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Your body makes its own defences too: tears, stomach acid and white blood cells fight germs. You and the clove both protect yourselves with chemistry.
- *what is life:* Every living being looks after themselves. A plant who cannot run still defends their body, in their own way.
- *what is this world:* Moulds, insects and plants are always testing each other's defences. Our medicines come from that old contest.

Plants, moulds, insects and people all come from shared ancestors. Over millions of years, each plant family made their own set of chemicals to protect themselves: eugenol in cloves and tulsi, azadirachtin in neem, capsaicin in chilli. People who lived among these plants learned which ones help, and passed that knowledge on. So a plant's own protection ends up in a grandmother's rice tin and in a dentist's filling.

- **Charaka Samhita, Sutrasthana 26.12:** There is no substance in the world that cannot be a medicine, if it is used in the right way and for the right purpose. (In Spice Guard, clove powder stopped mould on food. A drop of clove oil can ease a toothache. But the same oil swallowed by a child can harm badly. Use and dose decide.)
- **Acharanga Sutra, Book 1, Lecture 1, Lesson 5 (Jain):** As a person is born, a plant is born. As a person grows, a plant grows. As a person has life, a plant has life. As a person is hurt when cut, a plant is hurt too. (A plant who is bitten senses the bite and makes more defence chemicals in their other leaves. Jain teachers saw plants as living beings who feel, more than 2,000 years before scientists measured these signals.)
- **Robin Wall Kimmerer, Braiding Sweetgrass (2013):** Kimmerer, a botanist from the Potawatomi Nation, writes about the honourable harvest. Take only what you need. Never take the first one you see, or the last one. Give something back. (Most medicine plants in India are still dug from the wild. Harvesting with care, as sacred groves do, keeps the plants and their protection alive for the next people.)
- Science (settled): Eugenol, the main chemical in clove oil, stops many kinds of mould and bacteria from growing.
- Science (settled): Plants sense a bite and send signals to their other leaves, which then make more defence chemicals. Some plants also respond to smells given off by bitten neighbours.
- Other view, Many plant scientists: Defence chemicals came from evolution. Over millions of years, plants that happened to make bitter or smelly chemicals were eaten less and left more seeds. On this view, the response to a bite is an automatic process, like our skin healing a cut, not a choice the plant makes.

## Story: The Leaves Nobody Ate
*A made-up story. The facts in it are real. Neem and tulsi leaves carry defence chemicals that many insects avoid. Plants make more of these chemicals when they are bitten. Roots in soaked soil with no drainage can rot.*

That summer, Meenakshi's family lived on the fourth floor of a building in Madurai. The balcony was small, but Paati had filled it with pots. There was a red hibiscus, a tulsi, a curry leaf plant and a little neem. Nobody had planted the neem. A bird had dropped a seed into the hibiscus pot, and one day there was a neem seedling. Paati moved them into a pot of their own.

Meenakshi was in Class 4. Her brother Kavin was six. In the holidays, Paati gave each of them a job. Meenakshi had to look after the hibiscus. Kavin had to look after the tulsi.

One morning Meenakshi came out with her tumbler of milk and stopped. The hibiscus leaves were full of holes. Some looked like lace. On the underside of one leaf sat a fat green caterpillar, chewing steadily.

'Paati! Something is eating my plant!'

Paati came out, drying her hands on her sari. She bent close and looked. 'Yes. One caterpillar, maybe two. They are hungry, and your hibiscus is soft and tasty.'

'Then why aren't they eating the neem? The neem is right next to the hibiscus.'

Paati smiled. 'That is a good question. Do not ask me. Find out.'

So Meenakshi found out the way her science book said. She took her notebook and counted. She looked at 20 leaves on the hibiscus. 14 had holes. She looked at 20 leaves on the neem. Only 1 had a small bite at the edge. On the tulsi, 2 leaves had bites. She wrote the numbers in a table and drew three bars.

'The neem is hardly eaten,' she said. 'And the tulsi only a little. Why?'

Paati picked a neem leaf and gave Meenakshi half. 'Touch it to your tongue. Only the tip. Then spit.'

Meenakshi did, and pulled a face. 'Chhi! It's so bitter!'

'That bitterness is the neem's armour,' said Paati. 'A caterpillar who eats neem leaves stops wanting to eat. If they eat more, they cannot grow up into a butterfly or moth properly. So most caterpillars stay away.'

Then Paati crushed a tulsi leaf between her fingers and held it under Meenakshi's nose. The smell was sharp and warm, a little like cloves. 'Tulsi has armour too. Not bitter, but strong-smelling. Many insects do not like it.'

Meenakshi thought about this. 'Paati, when you were small, your Amma put neem leaves in the rice, didn't she? You told me.'

'In Thanjavur, yes. Every year, after the harvest, my mother dried neem leaves in the sun. She put them between the sacks of paddy and inside the rice tins. The weevils stayed away.'

'So the neem grew bitter to help your Amma.'

Paati laughed so loudly that the crow on the railing flew away. 'No, kanna. The neem was bitter long before anyone in Thanjavur grew rice. The bitterness is for the neem. My mother only learned to borrow it.'

Meenakshi looked at the little neem in their pot. It seemed strange to think of such a small plant carrying armour. 'Does the neem know when a caterpillar is biting?'

'Plants feel a bite,' said Paati. 'Scientists have found that when a caterpillar chews one leaf, the plant sends signals to their other leaves. Those leaves make more of their bitter chemicals, before the caterpillar even reaches them. Some scientists say this is automatic, like a machine. I say it is how a plant looks after themselves.'

Just then they heard Kavin wailing from the other end of the balcony. 'Paati! My tulsi is dying! I gave them so much water!'

They went to look. The tulsi's leaves hung down, soft and yellow at the edges. Kavin held a full steel tumbler. Under the pot, the saucer was full of water, and the soil was dark and soggy.

'How many times did you water them?' asked Meenakshi.

'In the morning, in the afternoon and at night. Three times! Because I love them.'

Paati knelt down beside him. She lifted the pot and turned it over. There was no hole at the bottom. 'Kavin, look. Water came in, but it could not go out. Roots need water, but they need air too. In soil that is always soaked, there is no air left for the roots. So the roots begin to rot, and the leaves droop, as if they were thirsty.'

Kavin's lip shook. 'So I gave them too much love?'

'You gave them too much water,' said Meenakshi. She had read about this. 'Caring is not giving more. Caring is finding out what they need.' She showed him how to push his finger into the soil, up to the first line of his finger. 'If it feels wet, wait. If it feels dry, then water, slowly, until a little comes out at the bottom.'

That evening Paati made a hole in a new pot with a hot nail, and they moved the tulsi into it with fresh, dry soil. Kavin checked the soil with his finger every morning after that. He watered only twice that week. By the next Sunday, the tulsi had three new pairs of leaves at the top.

Meenakshi kept counting the leaves on the balcony. The caterpillar on the hibiscus turned into a pupa, and one morning there was a small brown moth on the wall. The hibiscus grew new leaves, and the neem grew taller. Nobody ate the neem.

On the last day of the holidays, she wrote at the bottom of her table: 'Every plant on our balcony protects themselves. The neem uses bitterness. The tulsi uses smell. The hibiscus has little armour against this caterpillar, so they grow new leaves instead. When Paati puts neem leaves in the rice, she is borrowing the neem's armour. When Kavin drinks tulsi kadha, he is borrowing the tulsi's.'

Then she added one more line, smaller. 'I wonder what the neem thinks of us.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: the neem's bitterness and the tulsi's smell are defence chemicals that the plants make for themselves. People learned to borrow them, for grain stores, medicines and kadha. Caring for a plant means finding out what that plant needs, like air for the roots, not giving more of everything.

- Ask: Meenakshi counted 20 leaves on each plant. Why did she count the same number on each one?
- Ask: Who is the neem's bitterness really for?
- Ask: Why did Kavin's tulsi droop, even though they had plenty of water?
- Ask: Paati says plants feel a bite. Some scientists say it is automatic. What do you think, and why?
- Ask: Meenakshi wonders what the neem thinks of us. What might a neem think?

### e1 · Spice Guard: do haldi and clove stop mould on potato? (54 min, 7 days)

### How Plants Protect Themselves (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why do many plants make bitter, smelly or hot chemicals?  
   Options: To protect themselves from insects, animals and moulds / To make food taste nice for people / Because they are old and going bad  
   **Answer:** To protect themselves from insects, animals and moulds. *Why:* A plant cannot run away, so many plants protect themselves with chemistry. People found these chemicals useful later.    Options: A dried flower bud / A seed / A piece of root  
   **Answer:** A dried flower bud. *Why:* Cloves are flower buds picked before they open and dried in the sun. Look at the little ball on top: that is the unopened flower.
3. (truefalse) In Spice Guard, the four potato squares are left open for 30 minutes so that mould spores from the air can land on every square.

## Open questions
- **How do neelakurinji and muli bamboo count the years before they flower?** Nobody has found the clock inside these plants, or how all the plants of one kind flower together.
- **Does haldi, taken as food or medicine, really help any illness in people?** Thousands of laboratory studies exist, but no large fair trial has yet settled it. Scientists still argue about how much curcumin the body takes in.
- **How many of India's plants hold medicines that nobody has found yet?** Only a small part of India's plants has been tested carefully. Many healers' knowledge has never been written down.
- **Who should own traditional plant knowledge, and how should the money be shared?** Countries, communities and companies still argue about this. India's laws and international agreements try to answer it, and cases are still being decided.
- **What do plants experience when they are bitten?** We can measure the signals a plant sends. What the plant feels, and how to study that, is still argued about.
- **How many of India's endemic plants are in danger?** Many plants have never been counted properly in the wild, so scientists do not know how many are disappearing.

In which years after that would you expect the next two big blooms? **About 2030 and 2042.**. 2018 + 12 is 2030. 2030 + 12 is 2042. The pattern adds 12 each time. Real blooms can be a year early or late. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- Muli bamboo in Mizoram flowered in 1959 and again from 2006 to 2008, about every 48 years. Starting from 1959, add 48 years. Is the answer inside 2006 to 2008? Then predict the next flowering. **1959 + 48 is 2007, which is inside 2006 to 2008. The next flowering would be around 2055.**. 1959 + 40 is 1999. 1999 + 8 is 2007. 2007 is between 2006 and 2008, so the pattern fits. 2007 + 48: 2007 + 40 is 2047, and 2047 + 8 is 2055. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- In Ask the Elders, Sunita, a girl in Class 4, collected 10 family plant remedies. 2 had been tested in fair trials and found to work. 1 had been tested and did not work. The rest had not been tested fairly yet. How many had not been tested? What fraction of all the remedies is that? **7 had not been tested. That is 7 out of 10, or seven tenths.**. 2 + 1 is 3 tested. 10 take away 3 is 7 not tested. 7 out of 10 is 7/10. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In a trial of a cold remedy, 14 of 20 people who took the remedy were better in 5 days. 13 of 20 people who took a placebo were better in 5 days. Mohan, a boy in Class 4, says the remedy works because 14 is more than 13. What would you tell him? **The difference is only 1 person out of 20. That could easily be luck. The trial does not show that the remedy works.**. 14 out of 20 and 13 out of 20 are very close: 70 out of every 100 and 65 out of every 100. If the trial were done again, the numbers could easily swap. A real effect would need a bigger, clearer difference, or many more people. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
