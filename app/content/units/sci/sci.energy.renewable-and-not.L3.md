# Renewable and non-renewable energy: what runs out, what comes back, and who decides
`sci.energy.renewable-and-not.L3`

**Summary.** A lump of coal and a gust of wind can both light the same bulb. So why do people say that one will run out and the other will not? In this lesson you sort energy sources by how fast they come back after we use them. You find that coal, oil and gas are sunlight caught long ago by living beings, and that wind, rain and firewood are sunlight caught this year. Then you test, with two bowls of beans, how a source that comes back can still run out when we take too fast.

**Intro.** *Look at a bulb, a fan or a phone charger in your home. The electricity in the wire looks the same every day. Can you tell, by looking, if it came from burning coal or from the wind?* You cannot tell. Electricity from a coal power station and electricity from a wind turbine are exactly alike when they reach your wall. The difference lies far away, at the place where the energy was collected. Some of those places are being emptied. Others are filled again every morning. Today you will find out which is which, and why the answer is less simple than two boxes.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what an energy source is and name six that my family uses.
1. I can sort energy sources into renewable and non-renewable, and give the reason for each.
2. I can explain why coal, oil and gas are called fossil fuels and why they cannot be made again in our lifetimes.
3. I can show, with a model, that a renewable source runs out if we take from it faster than it comes back.
4. I can trace wind, rain, firewood and coal back to the Sun.
5. I can give one good thing and one problem for coal, for a big dam, for solar panels and for nuclear power.
6. I can name three ways my family can use less energy, and say why using less matters in India.

## Terms
- **Energy source**: A place we collect energy from, to make things move, heat up, light up or grow. *e.g.* Rice is the energy source for your morning run, and sunlight is the energy source for a paddy field.
- **Fuel**: A material that holds stored energy and gives it out as heat and light when it burns. *e.g.* Cooking gas in a cylinder is the fuel that heats the dosa tawa.
- **Burn**: To join with oxygen from the air so quickly that heat and light come out. *e.g.* A candle burns, and the wax gets shorter because the wax is what is burning.
- **Renewable**: A kind of energy source that nature fills up again soon, in days, seasons or a few years. *e.g.* The monsoon fills the river again every year, so water for a small dam is renewable.
- **Non-renewable**: A kind of energy source that forms so slowly that, once used, it will not come back for millions of years. *e.g.* A coal seam at Talcher that is dug out stays empty. No mine has ever filled up again.
- **Coal**: A black rock made from the buried bodies of plants that lived millions of years ago. It burns. *e.g.* Coal from Odisha and Jharkhand is carried by train to power stations.
- **Oil**: A thick dark liquid under the ground and under the sea. Petrol and diesel are made from it. *e.g.* Oil is pumped from under the sea at Bombay High, off Mumbai.
- **Natural gas**: A gas found underground, often with oil. It burns with a clean flame. *e.g.* The cooking gas in a cylinder is separated out from natural gas and from oil at a refinery.
- **Fossil**: A trace of an ancient living being that has been kept in rock. *e.g.* A leaf print pressed into a slab of coal is a fossil.
- **Fossil fuel**: A fuel formed from the buried remains of plants and tiny sea beings that lived millions of years ago. *e.g.* Coal, oil and natural gas are the three fossil fuels.
- **Molecule**: A tiny group of atoms joined together. Water, sugar and the oils in living beings are all made of molecules. *e.g.* One drop of water holds more water molecules than anyone could count.
- **Lignite**: A soft brown coal that is younger than black coal and still looks a little like wood. *e.g.* At Neyveli in Tamil Nadu, lignite is dug from open pits.
- **Swamp**: Wet land where water stays all year and plants grow thickly. *e.g.* Coal began in warm swamps where tall plants fell into the water and were buried.
- **Pressure**: A push spread over a surface. Deep underground, the weight of rock above presses very hard on what lies below. *e.g.* A pressure cooker keeps steam in, so the push of the steam rises and food cooks faster.
- **Reserve**: The amount of a fuel that people have found and can dig up at a price worth paying. *e.g.* A coal reserve is always smaller than the coal in the ground, because some is too deep or too costly to reach.
- **Peat**: Half-rotted swamp plants that people dig up and burn. It forms very slowly, about one millimetre a year. *e.g.* In Scotland and Ireland, families have cut peat from wet ground to burn in stoves.
- **Sunlight**: The light and warmth that reach the Earth from the Sun. *e.g.* A black car seat left in sunlight gets too hot to touch.
- **Watt**: A measure of how fast energy is used or arrives. One watt is a small amount of energy every second. *e.g.* An LED bulb uses about 9 watts, and a ceiling fan uses about 50 to 75 watts.
- **Watt-hour**: The energy used when one watt runs for one hour. *e.g.* A 9 watt LED bulb that is on for 5 hours uses 9 times 5, which is 45 watt-hours.
- **Unit of electricity**: The amount of electricity that an electricity bill counts. One unit is 1,000 watt-hours. *e.g.* A 1,000 watt iron that runs for one hour uses one unit.
- **Solar cooker**: A box or a curved mirror that catches sunlight as heat and cooks food. *e.g.* A box cooker with a black pot and a glass lid can cook rice on a sunny day.
- **Solar cell**: A thin slice of silicon that makes electricity when light falls on it. *e.g.* The small panel on a calculator is a row of solar cells.
- **Solar panel**: Many solar cells joined together under glass in a flat frame. *e.g.* Rows of solar panels cover the airport roofs at Cochin in Kerala.
- **Silicon**: A grey material that comes from sand and is used to make solar cells and computer chips. *e.g.* Ordinary beach sand is mostly silicon joined with oxygen.
- **Wind**: Air that is moving, made when the Sun warms some places more than others. *e.g.* On a hot afternoon, a sea breeze blows from the cooler sea towards the warm land.
- **Turbine**: A wheel with blades that is spun by moving air or falling water. *e.g.* The tall white turbines at Muppandal spin in the wind.
- **Generator**: A machine that makes electricity when it is spun. *e.g.* A cycle dynamo is a small generator. It lights the lamp while the wheel turns.
- **Dam**: A strong wall built across a river to hold back water. *e.g.* The Mettur dam on the Kaveri holds water behind a long wall.
- **Hydropower**: Electricity made by water falling through pipes and spinning turbines. *e.g.* Water from the Shivanasamudra falls on the Kaveri has made electricity since 1902.
- **Biomass**: Material from plants and other living beings that lived recently, such as wood, straw and dung. *e.g.* Coconut shells and husks in Kerala are biomass, and so are dried dung cakes.
- **Biogas**: A fuel gas made when microbes break down biomass with no air. *e.g.* Gobar gas from a village tank burns with a blue flame on a stove.
- **Microbe**: A living being too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* Microbes in a closed tank eat cow dung and give off biogas.
- **Manure**: Rotted dung and plant waste that is put on fields to help plants grow. *e.g.* What is left in a biogas tank goes to the fields as manure.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas with no colour and no smell. It is given off when anything containing carbon burns, and it holds back heat in the air. *e.g.* Burning one litre of petrol puts about 2.3 kilograms of carbon dioxide into the air.
- **Methane**: A gas that burns, found in natural gas and in biogas. If it escapes unburnt, it holds back much more heat than carbon dioxide. *e.g.* Natural gas is mostly methane, and a leaking pipe lets it into the air.
- **Particle**: A very small piece of something. Atoms and the pieces inside them are particles. *e.g.* A grain of salt holds more particles than there are people in the world.
- **Atom**: A tiny piece of matter. Everything is made of atoms, and they are far too small to see. *e.g.* A single drop of water holds more atoms than there are grains of sand on every beach.
- **Uranium**: A very heavy metal dug from mines. Its atoms can be split to give heat. *e.g.* India's uranium is dug in Jharkhand and used in nuclear power stations.
- **Nuclear energy**: Heat released when the centres of very heavy atoms, such as uranium, are split. *e.g.* At Kudankulam, the heat of split uranium boils water to turn turbines.
- **Thorium**: A heavy metal found in beach sands of Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Odisha. India plans to use it as nuclear fuel one day. *e.g.* The dark sand on some Kerala beaches holds thorium.
- **Power station**: A place where energy from a fuel, falling water, wind or sunlight is turned into electricity. *e.g.* The power station at Thoothukudi burns coal brought by ship.
- **Electricity**: Energy that flows along wires and can be turned into light, heat or movement far away. *e.g.* Electricity from the power station reaches your fan through wires.
- **Store of energy**: Anything that keeps energy until we need it. *e.g.* A charged battery, a tank of water high on a hill and a heap of coal are all stores of energy.
- **Battery**: A store that keeps energy in chemicals and gives it out as electricity. *e.g.* A home inverter battery keeps the lights on during a power cut.
- **Pumped storage**: A pair of lakes, one high and one low. Spare electricity pumps water up, and the water falls back through turbines later. *e.g.* The Kadamparai station near Coimbatore pumps water uphill at quiet hours and lets it down at busy hours.
- **LED bulb**: A bulb that gives the same light as an old bulb for far less energy. *e.g.* A 9 watt LED bulb gives as much light as an old 60 watt bulb.
- **Saving energy**: Getting the same light, cooking or travel from less energy. *e.g.* A lid on a pot of water brings it to the boil sooner and saves gas.
- **Sacred grove**: A patch of forest that a community protects, where nobody cuts or hunts. *e.g.* Kavu in Kerala and devarakadu in Karnataka are sacred groves.
- **Tonne**: A mass of 1,000 kilograms. *e.g.* A small car weighs about one tonne.
- **Billion**: A thousand million. It is written 1,000,000,000, and it is 100 crore. *e.g.* The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old, which is 460 crore years.

## Explanations
### An energy source is a place we collect energy from · *idea*
**Definition:** An energy source is anything we can collect energy from to make things move, heat up, light up or grow.

Energy is what it takes to make something happen: to move, to warm, to light, to grow. We cannot make energy. We can only collect it from somewhere and pass it on. The place we collect it from is called an energy source. Rice is an energy source for your body. Cooking gas is an energy source for the stove. Sunlight is an energy source for a paddy field. Scientists can count energy and follow it from place to place. But nobody can say what energy finally is. The physicist Richard Feynman wrote that physics has no knowledge of what energy is. We know how to use the word, and the rest is an open question.
- e.g. Idli and sambar are the energy source for your morning run.
- e.g. A cylinder of cooking gas is the energy source for the dosa tawa.
- e.g. Diesel is the energy source for a bus, and electricity is the energy source for a metro train.
- e.g. Sunlight is the energy source for a neem tree, who builds leaves, wood and seeds from the light.
- **Physicists, following Richard Feynman in his Lectures on Physics, volume 1, chapter 4:** Energy is a number we can calculate. When things change, the number stays the same. That rule has never failed. It does not tell us what energy is made of.
- **Engineers who run power stations:** A source matters only if energy can be collected from it at a cost people can pay. Lightning holds a lot of energy, but nobody can catch it usefully, so engineers do not count it as a source.
- **Yoga and Ayurveda teachers in India:** They speak of prana, the life-force carried by breath and food. This is an older idea than the physicist's energy and it is not measured in the same way. The two words should not be mixed up.
### A fuel is a store of energy that we burn · *defined*
**Definition:** A fuel is a material that holds stored energy and gives it out as heat and light when it burns.

Many energy sources are fuels. A fuel is something we burn. Burning lets out the energy that was stored inside the fuel. Firewood, dried cow dung cakes, coal, kerosene, petrol, diesel and cooking gas are all fuels. After a fuel burns, the fuel is gone. You are left with ash, smoke and gases. The energy has not vanished. It has spread out into the room as warmth. But you cannot gather that warmth up and burn it again. So a fuel can be used only once.
- e.g. One cooking gas cylinder holds about 14 kilograms of gas. A family of four uses it up in about a month or two.
- e.g. A candle is a fuel. The wax gets shorter as it burns, because the wax is what is burning.
- e.g. A wood fire leaves a small heap of ash. Most of the wood has gone into the air as gases you cannot see.
- e.g. Sunlight and wind are energy sources, but they are not fuels, because nothing is burnt.
### A renewable source comes back about as fast as we use it · *defined*
**Definition:** A renewable energy source is one that nature replaces within days, seasons or a few years, so it can be used again and again.

Some sources fill up again soon after we take from them. The Sun shines again tomorrow. The wind blows again next week. Rain fills the river again next monsoon. A field that gave rice this year can give rice next year. Sources like these are called renewable. The word means 'able to be made new again'. The important part is the time. A renewable source comes back in a time that people can wait for: a day, a season, a few years.
- e.g. Sunlight comes back every morning.
- e.g. A river in Kerala runs low in April and fills again by July.
- e.g. A bamboo clump cut this year can be cut again in about three to five years.
- e.g. Wind at Muppandal in Tamil Nadu blows hardest from May to September, every year.
### A non-renewable source takes millions of years to form · *defined*
**Definition:** A non-renewable energy source is one that forms so slowly that, once we use it, it will not come back for millions of years.

Coal, oil and natural gas are non-renewable. They do form in nature, but very slowly. Most of India's coal took shape from forests that grew roughly 250 to 300 million years ago. People started burning coal in large amounts only about 250 years ago. So we are using in a few hundred years what took millions of years to make. That is like filling a bucket one drop each year and then emptying it with a mug. New coal is still forming in swamps today. It will be ready long after anyone we can imagine.
- e.g. Coal is dug at Jharia in Jharkhand, Talcher in Odisha, Singareni in Telangana and Neyveli in Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. Oil is pumped from under the sea at Bombay High, off Mumbai, and from the ground at Digboi in Assam, where oil was struck in 1889.
- e.g. Petrol, diesel, kerosene and most cooking gas are all made from oil and natural gas.
- e.g. A coal mine that is emptied stays empty. No mine has ever filled up again.
### Coal, oil and gas are the remains of ancient living beings · *defined*
**Definition:** A fossil fuel is a fuel formed from the buried remains of plants and tiny sea beings that lived millions of years ago.

Coal began as living plants. Long ago, tall plants grew in warm swamps. When they died, they fell into the water and were covered by mud before they could rot. More mud piled on top. Over millions of years the weight and the heat of the Earth pressed the plants into black rock, which is coal. Oil and gas began in the sea. Tiny beings floated near the surface, as they still do today. When they died they sank and were buried in mud. Heat and pressure slowly turned them into oil and gas. A fossil is a trace of ancient life kept in rock. So these three are called fossil fuels.
- e.g. Miners sometimes find the print of a leaf or a piece of tree bark pressed into a slab of coal.
- e.g. Oil did not come from dinosaurs. Most of it came from sea beings too small to see without a microscope.
- e.g. At Neyveli in Tamil Nadu the fuel is lignite. This soft brown coal is much younger than black coal and still looks a little like wood.
- **Geologists and chemists, since the 1800s:** Coal holds leaf prints and plant cells. Oil holds molecules that only living beings make. So both came from life.
- **Thomas Gold, an astronomer, in the 1970s to 1990s, and some Russian and Ukrainian geologists before him:** They proposed that some oil and gas rise from deep inside the Earth and never were alive. A deep hole drilled in Sweden in the 1980s to test this found almost no oil. Nearly all geologists rejected the idea, though small amounts of gas made without life are known.
### Renewable is about speed: a forest can run out too · *defined*
**Definition:** A source stays renewable only while we take from it more slowly than it comes back.

Firewood is on every list of renewable sources, because trees grow again. But suppose a village cuts ten trees a year and only four grow back. Each year the forest is smaller. One day the forest is gone. The wood did not change. The speed of cutting was greater than the speed of growing. So renewable is not a kind of thing. It is a match between two speeds: how fast we take and how fast nature gives back. Take more slowly than nature gives, and the source lasts. Take faster, and even a renewable source runs out.
- e.g. A well is refilled by rain soaking into the ground. Pump out more than the rain puts in, and the well goes dry. This has happened in parts of Punjab, Rajasthan and Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. A lake stocked with fish gives fish for ever if people catch fewer than are born each year.
- e.g. Peat is half-rotted swamp plants. Peat does grow back, about one millimetre a year, so people argue about which box it belongs in.
- **School textbooks and most government lists:** They sort each source into one of two boxes by its kind. Wood, water, wind and Sun go in one box. Coal, oil and gas go in the other. This is quick to learn and mostly right.
- **Elinor Ostrom, who won the Nobel Prize in economics in 2009:** She studied forests, fishing grounds and water channels that villages share, in Nepal and many other places. She found that many villages keep a shared source alive for hundreds of years. They do it by making their own rules about how much each family may take.
- **Forest communities of India, such as the Bishnoi of Rajasthan:** Rules given by Guru Jambheshwar in 1485 CE forbid cutting green trees. People collect only dead wood. By such rules a dry land has kept its khejri trees for more than 500 years.
### Nearly all our energy is sunlight, fresh or stored · *defined*
**Definition:** Wind, rain, plants and fossil fuels all get their energy from the Sun. Only a few sources, such as the Earth's inner heat, the tides and nuclear fuel, do not.

Follow almost any source back and you reach the Sun. Plants catch sunlight in their leaves and store it as food and wood. The Sun warms land and sea unevenly, and that makes the air move, which is wind. The Sun lifts water from the sea as vapour, the water falls as rain on the hills, and it runs down as rivers. Coal, oil and gas are sunlight that living beings caught long ago. So the real difference between coal and wind is the age of the sunlight. Wind is this week's sunlight. Coal is sunlight from before the dinosaurs.
- e.g. Your breakfast is sunlight caught by a rice plant a few months ago.
- e.g. The wood in a bullock cart is sunlight caught over about thirty years.
- e.g. Three sources do not come from the Sun. Hot springs, such as those in the Puga valley in Ladakh, are heated by the inside of the Earth. Tides are raised mostly by the pull of the Moon. Nuclear fuel was made in exploding and colliding stars long before the Sun was born.
- **Rig Veda 1.115.1, a hymn to Surya, composed more than 3,000 years ago:** The hymn calls the Sun the self of all that moves and all that stands still. The poets were praising, and they were not measuring. Still, they placed the Sun at the root of every living and moving thing.
- **Plant scientists, since Jan Ingenhousz in 1779:** Ingenhousz showed that plants need light to do their work on the air. Later scientists measured that all the food and wood on Earth is built using energy from light.
- **Deep-sea biologists, since 1977:** Scientists in the research submarine Alvin found communities of living beings at hot vents on the dark sea floor. Bacteria there make food using chemicals from inside the Earth. So a small part of life runs without sunlight.
### How much sunlight reaches us: about 1,000 watts on each square metre · *defined*
**Definition:** On a clear day at noon, sunlight brings about 1,000 watts of power to each square metre of ground that faces the Sun.

A watt measures how fast energy arrives. An LED bulb uses about 9 watts. At noon on a clear day, a patch of ground one metre long and one metre wide receives about 1,000 watts of sunlight. That is the power of more than 100 LED bulbs falling on a space the size of a small table. Now think of the whole Earth. The sunlight that arrives in one to two hours holds about as much energy as all the people of the world use in a whole year. So there is plenty of sunlight. The hard part is catching it, because it is spread thinly and stops at night.
- e.g. A black car seat left in the sun becomes too hot to touch. That heat is sunlight collected by one small surface.
- e.g. Clothes on a line in Chennai dry in about two hours on a sunny day, with no fuel at all.
- e.g. Much of India gets clear sunshine on roughly 250 to 300 days a year.
### Electricity is counted in units: one unit is 1,000 watt-hours · *defined*
**Definition:** A unit of electricity is 1,000 watt-hours, which is the energy that a 1,000 watt machine uses in one hour.

A watt tells how fast energy is used. To know how much energy is used, we also need the time. A 9 watt bulb that is on for 5 hours uses 9 times 5, which is 45 watt-hours. A watt-hour is one watt used for one hour. Your electricity bill does not count watt-hours. It counts units, and one unit is 1,000 watt-hours. A 1,000 watt iron that runs for one hour uses one unit. So does a 9 watt LED bulb that stays on for about 110 hours.
- e.g. A 60 watt fan that runs for 10 hours uses 600 watt-hours, which is 0.6 of a unit.
- e.g. A bill of 150 units for a month is 5 units a day. That is the same as a 1,000 watt iron running for 5 hours every day.
- e.g. The meter at your home counts units. Its numbers go up faster when a hungrier machine is running.
### Two ways to catch sunlight: as heat and as electricity · *defined*
**Definition:** A solar cooker or solar water heater catches sunlight as heat. A solar cell turns sunlight straight into electricity.

The first way is to catch warmth. A black surface soaks up sunlight and gets hot. Put it in a box under clear plastic, and the heat stays in. That is a solar cooker. The second way is a solar cell. A solar cell is a thin slice made mostly of silicon, which comes from sand. When light falls on the slice, electricity flows. Nothing burns and nothing moves. Many cells joined together make a solar panel. A good panel today turns about 20 parts of every 100 parts of sunlight into electricity. The rest becomes warmth.
- e.g. Cochin International Airport in Kerala has run fully on its own solar panels since 2015. It was the first airport in the world to do so.
- e.g. Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan covers more than 50 square kilometres of desert and is one of the largest in the world.
- e.g. Solar water heaters sit on many roofs in Bengaluru and Pune.
- e.g. Large kitchens at Shirdi in Maharashtra use curved mirrors to make steam and cook food for thousands of people each day.
- **Mária Telkes, a Hungarian-born scientist, with the architect Eleanor Raymond, in 1948:** They built a house in the United States that was heated only by stored sunlight. Telkes also designed solar cookers and a solar still that made drinking water from sea water. She showed that sunlight could do daily household work.
- **Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller and Gerald Pearson, at Bell Laboratories, in 1954:** They made the first silicon solar cell that gave a useful amount of electricity. It turned about 6 parts in every 100 of the light into electricity.
- **The women trained at the Barefoot College in Tilonia, Rajasthan:** Since the 1990s the college has taught village women, many of whom never went to school, to build and repair solar lamps and panels. They show that this work does not need a degree.
### Wind and falling water turn machines that make electricity · *defined*
**Definition:** A turbine is a wheel with blades that is spun by moving air or falling water. A generator joined to the turbine makes electricity as it spins.

Moving air and moving water carry energy. People have used them for a very long time. Sails pushed boats across the Indian Ocean. Water wheels called gharats still grind grain in the hills of Uttarakhand. Today the same push turns a turbine. The spinning turbine turns a generator, a machine that makes electricity when it spins. A dam holds river water high up. When the water is let fall through pipes, it spins the turbines at the bottom. This is called hydropower.
- e.g. The wind farms at Muppandal in Kanyakumari district are among the largest in India.
- e.g. The power station at Shivanasamudra falls on the Kaveri in Karnataka opened in 1902. It sent electricity about 150 kilometres to the Kolar gold mines.
- e.g. Windmills with upright sails were grinding grain in Sistan, in what is now Iran and Afghanistan, by about the 9th century CE. Some at Nashtifan in Iran still turn.
- e.g. One blade of a large modern wind turbine is longer than five buses parked end to end.
### Biomass and biogas: energy from beings that lived recently · *defined*
**Definition:** Biomass is material from plants and other living beings that lived recently, such as wood, straw and dung. Biogas is a fuel gas made when microbes break down biomass without air.

Wood, crop stalks, coconut shells and dried dung cakes are called biomass. They are fuels made by beings who lived recently, so new ones keep coming. There is also a cleaner way to use them. Put cow dung and kitchen scraps with water in a closed tank with no air. Tiny living beings called microbes eat the waste. As they eat, they give off a gas that burns with a blue flame. This is biogas, which many people in India call gobar gas. The gas goes to the stove. What is left in the tank goes to the fields as manure.
- e.g. A family with three or four cattle can cook most of its meals on gobar gas.
- e.g. Sugar mills in Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh burn bagasse, the crushed stalks left after the juice is pressed out, to make their own electricity.
- e.g. In Kerala, coconut shells and husks are burnt to dry copra, the white flesh of the coconut.
- **Engineers who promote biomass:** The gas given off in burning was taken from the air by the plant only a year or two ago. A new plant takes that gas back from the air. So the amount in the air stays about the same.
- **Doctors and the World Health Organization:** Smoke from wood and dung fires inside a home harms the lungs of whoever cooks, and of small children nearby. Renewable is not the same as healthy.
### Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide to the air, and that warms the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbon dioxide is a gas given off when anything containing carbon burns. In the air it lets sunlight in and slows the escape of heat.

Ancient plants took a gas called carbon dioxide out of the air and built their bodies from it. When coal, oil or gas burns, that carbon dioxide goes back into the air, all at once. Carbon dioxide has no colour and no smell. It lets sunlight pass down to the ground. It holds back some of the heat that the warm ground gives off. So more carbon dioxide means a warmer Earth. Before factories, roughly 280 of every million parts of air were carbon dioxide. Today the number is above 420. The Earth is now a little more than 1 degree Celsius warmer than it was 150 years ago.
- e.g. One degree sounds small. It is enough to melt glaciers in the Himalayas faster, raise the sea and make heat waves stronger.
- e.g. Burning one litre of petrol puts about 2.3 kilograms of carbon dioxide into the air. That is more than the petrol weighed, because the carbon joins with oxygen from the air.
- e.g. Coal smoke also carries soot and other gases that make city air unhealthy to breathe.
- **Eunice Foote, an American scientist, in 1856:** She filled glass cylinders with different gases and stood them in the Sun. The cylinder of carbon dioxide grew warmest and stayed warm longest. She wrote that air with more of this gas would give the Earth a higher temperature.
- **Climate scientists of the world, reporting together since 1990:** Thousands of scientists check each other's measurements and write joint reports. They agree that the warming since the 1800s is caused mainly by people burning fossil fuels.
- **A few scientists, such as Richard Lindzen:** He accepts that carbon dioxide warms the Earth, but has argued that clouds will cancel much of the warming. Measurements since then have not supported this, and nearly all climate scientists disagree with him.
### Renewable does not mean harmless · *debated*
**Definition:** Every way of collecting energy changes the land, the water or the lives of beings nearby. Renewable sources do this too.

A big dam makes clean electricity for a hundred years. It also drowns the valley behind it: forests, fields, temples, villages. Fish who swim upriver to lay eggs find a wall. Wind turbines and solar parks need a lot of land, and the wires that carry their electricity cross the paths of birds. Solar panels and batteries need metals that are dug from mines. None of this means renewable energy is bad. It means every source has a cost that someone pays. Often the ones who pay are forest people, farmers and wild beings who do not get the electricity.
- e.g. The Sardar Sarovar dam on the Narmada moved about 40,000 families by official counts. Some other counts are higher. The Narmada Bachao Andolan, led by Medha Patkar, argued for those families for decades.
- e.g. Fewer than 200 great Indian bustards are left, mostly in Rajasthan and Gujarat. These large birds fly low and die when they hit power lines. In 2021 the Supreme Court of India ordered some lines to be put underground.
- e.g. A worn-out solar panel holds glass, aluminium and silicon that can be used again, but only if someone collects it.
- **Governments and power engineers:** A warming Earth harms everyone, the poorest most. Dams, wind and solar parks must be built quickly and on a large scale. Some harm to some places is the price of avoiding a greater harm to all.
- **Medha Patkar and the Narmada Bachao Andolan, from 1985:** The people who lose their land and river to a dam are rarely asked and rarely given land of equal worth. A project that is clean for the city can be ruin for a village.
- **Wildlife scientists at the Wildlife Institute of India:** Clean energy and wild beings can both be served. Wires can be buried or marked where rare birds fly, and parks can be built on land that is already damaged.
### Nuclear energy: non-renewable, but it adds almost no carbon dioxide · *debated*
**Definition:** Nuclear energy is heat released when the centres of very heavy atoms, such as uranium, are split. The heat boils water, and the steam turns a turbine.

Everything is made of tiny particles called atoms. The atoms of a metal called uranium are very heavy. When the centre of a uranium atom is split, a great deal of heat comes out. A nuclear power station uses this heat to boil water and turn turbines. Uranium is dug from mines and is not replaced, so it is non-renewable. But nothing is burnt, so almost no carbon dioxide goes into the air. A small amount of fuel gives a very large amount of energy. The used fuel stays dangerous for thousands of years and must be kept safely away from all living beings.
- e.g. India has nuclear power stations at Kudankulam and Kalpakkam in Tamil Nadu, Kaiga in Karnataka, Tarapur in Maharashtra and other places.
- e.g. The beach sands of Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Odisha hold a metal called thorium. In the 1950s Homi Bhabha planned a way for India to use thorium as nuclear fuel in three stages. The third stage is not yet working.
- e.g. The Sun itself runs on a different nuclear process, in which very light atoms join together.
- **Supporters, such as many engineers of India's Department of Atomic Energy:** Nuclear stations run day and night in any weather and add almost no carbon dioxide. For the same amount of electricity made, nuclear power has harmed far fewer people than coal smoke has.
- **Opponents, such as the fishing communities who protested at Kudankulam in 2011 and 2012:** Accidents are rare but terrible, as at Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011. Nobody has yet shown a store for used fuel that has stayed safe for thousands of years. The people living nearest carry the risk.
### When will coal and oil run out? Nobody can give a date · *debated*
**Definition:** A reserve is the amount of a fuel that people have found and can dig up at a price worth paying. It is always smaller than the amount in the ground.

People have been predicting the end of oil for a hundred years, and the date keeps moving. Why? Because the amount we can use depends on two changing things: the tools for getting it and the price people will pay. New tools reach oil that old tools could not. A higher price makes a difficult mine worth opening. So the last tonne of coal will probably never be dug. Long before then, coal will cost more than the other choices. Many scientists now say the real limit is the air. Most of the known coal must stay in the ground if the Earth is not to warm much more.
- e.g. In 1956 the geologist M. King Hubbert predicted that oil from the United States would reach its highest level around 1970 and then fall. It did fall after 1970. Then new drilling methods pushed it above the 1970 level by 2018.
- e.g. Sheikh Ahmed Zaki Yamani was once the oil minister of Saudi Arabia. He is often said to have remarked that the Stone Age did not end because the world ran out of stone. People found better tools while there was still plenty of stone.
- **Geologists who follow M. King Hubbert:** Every oil field gives more each year until about half is gone, and then less. Add up all the fields and the world must reach a peak. The easy fuel goes first, and what is left is deeper and dearer to reach.
- **Economists such as Morris Adelman:** Reserves are not a fixed tank. They grow when prices rise and tools improve. Warnings of the last barrel have been wrong many times.
- **Christophe McGlade and Paul Ekins, in the journal Nature in 2015:** They calculated how much coal can be burnt if warming is to stay under 2 degrees Celsius. More than 80 parts in every 100 of the world's known coal should not be burnt before 2050. On this view the fuel will not run out. We must choose to leave it.
### Where India's electricity comes from today · *defined*
**Definition:** In about 2024, around 7 of every 10 units of electricity made in India came from burning coal. The rest came mostly from water, Sun, wind and nuclear fuel. These shares change every year.

India has a great deal of coal and few oil fields. So coal power stations made most of the country's electricity in about 2024, around 7 units in every 10. Looking at how much they can make at their best, about half of India's power stations used sources other than fossil fuels by about 2025. But they do not make half the electricity. A coal station can run all day and night. A solar park works only in daylight, and a wind farm only when the wind blows. India has promised to add no more carbon dioxide to the air, on balance, by the year 2070. These figures are from about 2024 and 2025. They change every year, so look up the latest ones.
- e.g. The power station at Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu burns coal brought by ship.
- e.g. Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Rajasthan and Karnataka are among the states that make the most wind and solar electricity.
- e.g. Most of the oil India uses, more than 8 parts in every 10 in about 2024, is bought from other countries.
- e.g. An electric scooter charged in India today still runs partly on coal, because the charging electricity comes mostly from coal.
### The Sun sets and the wind drops, so energy must be stored · *defined*
**Definition:** Storing energy means putting it into a form that keeps, such as a charged battery or water pumped uphill, so it can be used later.

Coal has one great advantage. It waits. A heap of coal sits beside the power station until it is needed. Sunlight and wind do not wait. People want the most electricity in the evening, just when solar panels stop. So renewable electricity needs a store. A battery is one kind of store. Another is a pair of lakes, one high and one low. At noon, spare solar electricity pumps water up to the high lake. At night the water runs back down through turbines. Building enough stores, cheaply, is one of the biggest jobs engineers have today.
- e.g. A home inverter is a small energy store. It fills a battery while the power is on and gives the energy back during a power cut.
- e.g. A solar lantern stores the day's sunlight in a battery and gives it out at night.
- e.g. The Kadamparai station in the hills near Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, pumps water uphill at quiet hours and lets it down at busy hours.
- e.g. Plants were the first to solve this. A potato and a grain of rice are sunlight stored for a dark season.
### Using less is the quickest way to make a source last · *defined*
**Definition:** Saving energy means getting the same light, cooking or travel from less energy.

There are two ways to make a bowl of beans last. Add beans more quickly, or take them more slowly. The second way is open to every family today. An LED bulb of 9 watts gives as much light as an old glass bulb of 60 watts. So six or seven LED bulbs can shine on the energy that one old bulb used. Since 2015, more than 36 crore LED bulbs have been sold through one Indian government programme. Most of India's electricity still comes from coal, so electricity that is not used is coal that stays in the ground.
- e.g. A pressure cooker cooks dal with about half the gas that an open pot needs, because the steam is kept in.
- e.g. A lid on a pot of water brings it to the boil sooner.
- e.g. A full bus carries fifty people on the fuel of only a few cars.
- e.g. A fan uses about 50 to 75 watts. An air conditioner uses 1,000 watts or more.
- **Energy engineers, such as Amory Lovins since 1976:** The cheapest and cleanest unit of energy is the one you never needed. Better bulbs, motors and buildings can do the same work with far less.
- **William Stanley Jevons, an English economist, in 1865:** He noticed that when steam engines became better at using coal, England burnt more coal, not less, because engines became worth using for many more jobs. When something gets cheaper to run, people often use more of it.
### Who used the old sunlight, and who should stop first? · *debated*
**Definition:** Countries have burnt very different amounts of fossil fuel. People disagree about how the remaining share should be divided.

The carbon dioxide in the air today was put there over 250 years, mostly by countries that grew rich first. In about 2022, each person in India added about 2 tonnes of carbon dioxide a year. The average for the world was nearly 5 tonnes. For each person in the United States it was about 15. These numbers change from year to year. But India has very many people, so India's total is the third largest in the world. Crores of Indian families still need more electricity, not less. So the question is hard. Should every country cut at the same speed? Or should the ones who burnt most cut first and help the others build clean power?
- e.g. A family that cooks on a wood fire and owns one bulb has added almost nothing to the problem, and will feel the heat waves most.
- e.g. At world climate meetings, India argues for 'common but differentiated responsibilities', which means: everyone must act, but not equally.
- **Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain, of the Centre for Science and Environment in Delhi, in 1991:** In a report called 'Global Warming in an Unequal World' they argued that the air belongs equally to every person. They separated 'survival' use, such as a poor farmer's rice field, from 'luxury' use, such as a second car. Counting per person is the fair way.
- **Governments of the United States and the European Union, at world climate meetings:** The air does not care who burns the coal. The countries whose use is growing fastest today must cut too, or no cut elsewhere will be enough.
- **Leaders of small island countries, such as the Maldives and Tuvalu:** Their land is only a metre or two above the sea. They ask all large countries, rich and poor, to cut quickly, because their homes will go under water first.
### Old teachings on taking from the Earth: take what can grow back · *idea*
**Definition:** Many old traditions hold that people should take from land, forest and water only at a speed at which these can recover.

The idea of a source that must be allowed to renew is far older than the word renewable. People who lived close to forests and rivers learnt it by living it. Many wrote it into verses, rules and customs. These teachings do not prove anything about coal or carbon dioxide. They show that people long ago had already noticed the two speeds, taking and giving back.
- e.g. Sacred groves are patches of forest where nobody cuts or hunts. They are called kavu in Kerala, devarakadu in Karnataka and sarna in Jharkhand. Many shelter plants and animals that are now rare elsewhere.
- e.g. At Khejarli in Rajasthan in 1730, Amrita Devi Bishnoi and 362 other Bishnois gave their lives to stop the king's men from felling khejri trees.
- **Atharva Veda 12.1.35, in the Hymn to the Earth:** The poet says to the Earth: whatever I dig from you, may it quickly grow again. May I not pierce your vital parts or your heart.
- **The Haudenosaunee peoples of North America:** Their teaching, often called the seventh generation principle, asks leaders to weigh each decision by its effect on people who will be born seven generations later.
- **Many economists, from the 1700s onward:** They describe land, coal and rivers as resources, meaning stores of wealth for people to use. This view helped build railways, hospitals and schools. It also left other living beings and future people out of the sums.
### Even the Sun will end: renewable means renewable for us · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun is using up its own fuel. It is expected to shine steadily for about 5 billion more years.

Is anything renewable for ever? The Sun is a star. It shines by joining very light atoms together deep inside, and this uses them up. The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old. Astronomers work out that it will shine much as it does now for about 5 billion years more. A billion is 100 crore. So the Sun is non-renewable too, on a very long clock. When we call sunlight renewable, we mean it will last far longer than any time people can plan for. Renewable and non-renewable are words about our own clock.
- e.g. For a mayfly who lives one day as an adult, a pond that dries every summer would seem to last for ever.
- e.g. For a mountain, a forest that takes 500 years to grow back comes and goes quickly.

## Mostly agreed
- The extra carbon dioxide in the air comes from burning fossil fuels, and not from volcanoes or the sea. *How we know:* Carbon from living plants carries a small amount of a slowly fading kind of carbon called carbon-14. Fossil carbon is so old that all of this has faded away. In 1955 the chemist Hans Suess found something in the air. Its carbon was losing its share of carbon-14, year by year. That is exactly what happens when very old carbon is added.
- Smoke from cooking fires inside homes is one of the largest causes of illness in the world. *How we know:* The World Health Organization estimates that about 3 million people die early each year from breathing smoke inside their homes. The smoke comes from wood, dung or coal fires. In 2016 India began a scheme to give families cooking gas connections. By about 2024 it had reached more than 10 crore families.
- Making electricity from sunlight now costs far less than it did in 2010. *How we know:* The International Renewable Energy Agency compared big solar parks around the world. From 2010 to 2020, the price of their electricity dropped by more than four-fifths. In many places a new solar park now makes cheaper electricity than a new coal station.
- Coal seams can catch fire underground and burn for a very long time. *How we know:* Fires in the coal seams under Jharia in Jharkhand were first recorded in 1916 and are still burning. They waste coal, crack the ground and send smoke into nearby homes.

## How we found out
**Eunice Foote, John Tyndall, Svante Arrhenius, Roger Revelle, Hans Suess, Charles David Keeling, 1856 to 1958, and continuing.** For a long time people believed the air was far too large for chimney smoke to change it. This fitted what they saw: smoke rises, thins out and is gone. In 1856 Eunice Foote, in the United States, stood glass cylinders of different gases in the Sun with a thermometer in each. The cylinder of carbon dioxide grew hottest. She wrote that more of this gas in the air would warm the Earth. As a woman she was not invited to read her own paper at the science meeting, and a man read it for her. Her work was then forgotten until 2011. In 1859 John Tyndall in London measured, with better instruments, how carbon dioxide holds back heat. In 1896 the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius calculated by hand how much the Earth would warm if the gas doubled. Many scientists replied that the sea would soak up any extra. In 1957 Roger Revelle and Hans Suess showed that the sea takes it up too slowly. In 1958 Charles David Keeling began measuring the air on Mauna Loa, a mountain in Hawaii. His numbers have risen every year since, from roughly 315 parts in a million to more than 420.  
*What people believed before:* People held that the air and the sea are so vast that nothing people burn could change them. It was believable because smoke always seemed to vanish. Careful measuring of the air, year after year, showed that the gas does not vanish. It builds up.

## Challenging perspectives
- **We need not fear running out of any resource, because people always invent a replacement.** (Julian Simon, an economist, in his book 'The Ultimate Resource' of 1981; *debated*)  
  For: In 1980 Simon made a bet with the biologist Paul Ehrlich that five metals would be cheaper in 1990. They were, and Ehrlich paid. Whale oil for lamps was replaced by kerosene, and kerosene by electricity, before either ran out.  
  Against: A clever replacement does not bring back a drowned valley, a lost species or a stable climate. The air's ability to take up carbon dioxide has no replacement. Economists have also shown that Simon would have lost the same bet in many other ten-year periods.
- **Natural gas is a clean 'bridge' from coal to renewable energy.** (Many energy companies and some governments, since about 2005; *debated*)  
  For: Burning gas gives off about half as much carbon dioxide as burning coal for the same electricity, and far less soot. Gas stations can be switched on quickly when the wind drops.  
  Against: Natural gas is mostly methane, which holds back much more heat than carbon dioxide if it escapes unburnt. In 2011 Robert Howarth and his colleagues at Cornell University calculated that leaks from wells and pipes could cancel the advantage. How much really leaks is still being measured.
- **A whole country can run on wind, water and sunlight alone by 2050.** (Mark Jacobson of Stanford University and his colleagues, in 2015; *debated*)  
  For: They published a plan for the United States. It showed, hour by hour, how wind, water and Sun could meet all its energy needs. The plan used stores of heat and hydrogen gas to cover the gaps.  
  Against: In 2017 Christopher Clack and twenty other scientists replied that the plan assumed far more hydropower and storage than could be built. They argued that nuclear power or other steady sources would be needed as well.
- **A large dam should be counted as renewable energy.** (The Government of India, which began counting large hydropower as renewable in 2019; *debated*)  
  For: The rain refills the reservoir every year, nothing is burnt, and a dam can be turned up within minutes when the Sun sets.  
  Against: Plants drowned under a new reservoir rot and give off methane, sometimes for decades. The river, the fish and the villages do not come back. Reservoirs also fill slowly with mud, so a dam has a lifetime.
- **Oil and gas are made deep in the Earth without life, and are refilling from below.** (Thomas Gold, and before him Nikolai Kudryavtsev in the 1950s; *rejected*)  
  For: Methane exists on planets and moons that have no known life, so it can form without life.  
  Against: Oil carries the chemical marks of living beings, and oil fields lie where rocks rich in ancient life were buried and heated. Wells drilled to test the idea found almost nothing. No oil field has been seen to refill.

## Roots
You have sorted energy sources by how fast they come back. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves, about life and about this world.

**Deep truth.** In the two bowls test, the same beans lasted and lasted in one game and ran out in the next. The beans had not changed. What changed was how fast you took them, compared with how fast they came back. A forest, a well and a field behave in the same way. So whether a source lasts is decided by the giver and the taker together.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort sources into two boxes, renewable and non-renewable, as if the label were written inside the wood or the water. That habit hides our own part. It lets us think the forest 'ran out', when the forest and the cutting were always one story.  
*Evidence:* With 3 beans added and 2 taken each round, the bowl never emptied. With 3 added and 6 taken, the same bowl was empty in a few rounds. The bowl with no beans added emptied at every speed, only sooner or later.

- *who am i:* Your body runs on rice, dal and fruit, which are sunlight caught by plants a few months ago. The fan above you may run on sunlight caught before the dinosaurs. You are a meeting place for sunlight of many ages. You are also one of the takers whose speed decides what lasts.
- *what is life:* Living beings were the first to catch sunlight and keep it. Every fuel we burn, except nuclear fuel, was first made by plants or by tiny sea beings. Life is the great saver of sunlight, and every life also spends what other lives have saved.
- *what is this world:* The Sun, the air, the sea, the plants and the lamp in your room are links in one long passing-on of energy. Each link receives before it gives. When you look for where one link's energy 'really' belongs, you are led to the next link, and the next.

This lesson began with two boxes and ended with a chain. Coal turned out to be a forest, and the forest turned out to be the Sun. The wind was the Sun too, and so was your breakfast. When a child follows any energy back far enough, the separate 'sources' join up. Some teachers say that consciousness is the ground from which the Sun, the leaf and the one who eats all arise. In their view, the chain of energy is one way of seeing that they were never apart. Many scientists accept the chain and do not accept this last step. Their view is given below.

- **Isha Upanishad, verse 1:** All this, whatever moves in this moving world, is filled with the one Lord. Enjoy by letting go. Do not grab what belongs to another. (The verse does not speak of coal or rivers. It gives a way of using the world: take what you need and do not grasp. The bowls test showed in beans what grasping does to a shared source.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, 'The Heart of Understanding', 1988:** This Vietnamese Buddhist teacher wrote that if you look deeply into a sheet of paper you can see the cloud and the sunshine in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, without rain no tree, and without the tree no paper. He called this interbeing. (The lesson did the same looking with a lump of coal and a plate of rice, and found the Sun in both. His teaching is about how to see. The science gives the measured links.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 3.19.1:** The sage teaches: the Sun is Brahman. This is the instruction. Brahman is the Upanishads' word for the one ground of everything. (The sage points to the Sun as a way of pointing to the source of all things. Science finds that the Sun is the source of nearly all the energy we use. One is a teaching about the whole, the other a measurement of a part, and they should not be taken for the same claim.)
- Science (settled): Energy is never made or destroyed. It only passes from one thing to another and changes form. Every measurement ever made agrees with this.
- Science (debated): James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis proposed in the 1970s that living beings, air, sea and rocks together keep the Earth fit for life. They saw it as something like one body. They called this the Gaia hypothesis. Parts of it are accepted and the strong form is not.
- Other view, Physicalist scientists and the old Indian Charvaka school: The chain of energy is real and can be measured. It needs nothing behind it. The Sun is a ball of hot gas, a leaf is a chemical factory, and awareness is something that brains do. Seeing a oneness in the chain is a feeling people add, and the sums work the same without it.
- Practice, One mouthful, traced back (3 min): Take one mouthful of your next meal and chew it slowly. Before you swallow, tell me where its energy was yesterday. Now tell me where it was a month ago, in the shop or the field. Now six months ago, in a leaf, in the light. Close your eyes and sit for three breaths with the thought that this mouthful was once sunlight.
- Ask: In the bowls test, who made the beans run out: the bowl, the beans or the taker?
- Ask: If your body is built from sunlight that plants caught, where does the Sun stop and where do you begin?
- Ask: The swamp plants stored sunlight for 300 million years. Is anything you do today a store for someone far in the future?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Coal is sunlight caught by plants about 300 million years ago. When you switch on a light, whose work are you using? (hint: Follow the energy backwards one step at a time, and count how many living beings and how many years lie between the Sun and the bulb.)
- *deep:* If a forest is cut faster than it grows, is wood still a renewable source? (hint: Think about what the two bowls of beans did. Ask where the word 'renewable' lives: in the wood, or somewhere else.)
- *deep:* Energy is never destroyed. So what exactly do we lose when a fuel is 'used up'? (hint: Think about a hot cup of tea left on a table. Where has its warmth gone an hour later, and could you collect it again?)
- *deep:* The Sun will stop shining in about 5 billion years. Is there any energy source that is truly for ever? (hint: Try each source in turn, and ask what it depends on. Then ask what 'for ever' could mean for a being who lives eighty years.)
- *big:* A dam gives clean electricity to a city and drowns a village and a forest. Who should decide if it is built? (hint: List everyone who gains and everyone who loses, including the beings who cannot speak at a meeting. Then ask how each could be heard.)
- *big:* People who are not yet born will live with the air we leave. Do we owe them anything, when they can do nothing for us? (hint: Think of something useful that someone left for you before you were born: a tree, a well, a road. Did they owe it to you?)
- *big:* Rich countries burnt most of the coal so far. Should a poorer country be allowed to burn more now, to catch up? (hint: Try arguing it once as a family that has no electricity, and once as a family whose island is going under the sea.)
- *big:* A plant stores sunlight in a seed without being asked. Is a plant saving for the future? (hint: Think about what a seed is for, and who gains from the store inside. Then ask what you mean by 'saving'.)
- *deep:* If using less energy were the best answer, why do people who save money on fuel often end up using more? (hint: Imagine your bus fare became half. Would you take the same number of trips?)

## Story: How Old Is the Light in the Bulb?
*A made-up story set in Thoothukudi, Tamil Nadu. The family is imagined. What is real: Thoothukudi has a coal power station, salt pans and wind farms nearby. Salt is made there by letting the Sun and wind dry sea water in flat pans. Coal from Odisha formed more than 250 million years ago, before the first dinosaurs.*

The power went off in Thoothukudi at seven in the evening, just as Selvi, who was nine, reached the last sum on the page. Her brother Arul, who was six, cheered. A power cut meant the old kerosene lantern and no homework.

Their aunt Mariamma lit the lantern. Mariamma chithi climbed wind turbines for her living and mended them, and her hands still smelt of grease. Their uncle Murugan came in behind her. He drove a lorry that carried coal from the harbour to the power station.

'Your windmills have gone to sleep again,' said Murugan mama. 'No wind this evening. Good thing my coal is still burning, or the hospital would be dark too.'

'Your coal is why my eyes sting on the harbour road,' said Mariamma chithi. But she was smiling. They had this argument every week.

Selvi watched the lantern flame. 'Chithi, where does the light in a bulb come from?'

'Tonight, mostly from his coal. On a windy night, a good part from my turbines.'

'And where does the coal get its light from?'

Mariamma chithi sat down on the step. 'From a forest. The coal on your uncle's lorry was dug in Odisha. More than two hundred and fifty million years ago there were swamps there, full of tall plants. Each plant caught sunlight in their leaves and used it to build their body. The plants fell into the water and were buried, and then pressed under rock for longer than anyone can picture. The sunlight they had caught stayed locked inside. When coal burns, it comes out again.'

Arul stared at the dark bulb. 'So the bulb shines with old sunlight?'

'Very old. Older than the first dinosaur.'

'And the wind?' asked Selvi.

'The Sun warms the land faster than the sea. Warm air rises, cooler air slides in under it, and that is wind. The wind that turned my blades this morning was made by this week's sunlight.'

Their grandmother Pechiammal spoke from her cot in the corner. She had worked on the salt pans for forty years. 'You two talk as if you invented it. We let the sea into the pans, and the Sun and the wind carried the water away and left us the salt. They dried our fish and our clothes as well. Nobody sent us a bill, and nobody called it energy.'

Selvi began counting on her fingers. 'The rice I ate is sunlight from last season. The salt on it is sunlight from last month. The wind is from this week. And the coal is from before the dinosaurs.' She looked up. 'It is all the same sunlight. So what are you two fighting about?'

'About how fast it comes back,' said Mariamma chithi. 'Tomorrow the Sun will make new wind. In four months the field will make new rice. A new seam of coal needs a swamp left alone for millions of years. The forest took longer to save that sunlight than there have been people on the Earth. We are spending it in a few hundred years.'

'And about when it comes,' said Murugan mama. 'My coal waits in a heap until it is needed. Her wind comes when it pleases. The ice factory cannot ask the fish to wait for a breeze. And half this street eats because of that power station.'

'That is true,' said Mariamma chithi. 'Keeping the wind for later is the hardest part of my job.'

'What about the smoke?' asked Arul.

'When the old forest burns, everything the plants took out of the air goes back into the air, all in one go. That gas holds heat. The sea your Paatti worked beside is a little warmer and a little higher than when she was a girl.'

Paatti was quiet for a while. Then she said, 'Those plants saved their sunlight without knowing we would come. I wonder what we are saving, and for whom.'

Nobody answered. A breeze came through the window and the lantern flame leaned over. Far off, the turbines would be starting to turn.

Then the bulb came on. Arul groaned and reached for his school bag.

Selvi kept looking at the bulb. Was this light from the coal, or from the wind that had just come up? Was it a few minutes old, or older than the dinosaurs? She looked as hard as she could. The light looked exactly the same either way.

**For the parent:** The rice, the salt, the wind and the coal were all sunlight, caught at different times. What differs is how long each takes to come back. That difference cannot be seen in the bulb. It can only be known by asking where the light came from.

- Ask: Selvi could not tell by looking how old the light was. What would she have to find out to know?
- Ask: Murugan mama and Mariamma chithi were both telling the truth. Can two people both be right and still disagree about what to do?
- Ask: Paatti asked what we are saving, and for whom. What would your answer be?
- Ask: What in your home was made or dried by the Sun without anyone calling it energy?

Trees in the forest at the start, trees cut by each player, trees added by the weather, trees in the forest at the end.; Round 2. The same four columns.; Round 3. The same four columns.; Round 4. The same four columns.; Round 5. The same four columns.; Round 6. The same four columns.; Round 7. The same four columns.; Round 8. The same four columns, then each player's wood.
- **g1s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the rules together. Put 20 beans in the forest bowl. Give each player a set of cut cards and a small bowl. Shuffle the weather cards. Say that the forest is like a well or a wood that nature fills a little each year.  
  *Parent says:* We all share one forest. Each round, we each choose how many trees to cut. After we cut, the weather decides how many grow back. If the forest runs out, we all lose.  
  *Child mode:* Put 20 beans in the forest bowl. Take your cut cards. Read the rules.
  - Note: Cut the cards out and keep them in an envelope for next time. The beans can be any dried bean, button or paper square.
- **g1s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play 8 rounds. In each round, talk for a minute. Then put the cut cards down together and take the wood. Last, turn over a weather card and add trees. Write each round on the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* Talk first. What are you going to cut, and why? Then put down your card and turn them over together.  
  *Child mode:* Talk, then put down a cut card. Turn them over together. Take the wood.
  - Ask: Is it better to take a lot early or a little each round? (good: It depends. Taking a lot early can win the wood but can end the forest.; Taking a little keeps the forest alive, but others might get ahead.)
  - Ask: If you could change one rule, which would you change? (good: Maybe let players share the wood, or let the forest hold more.; Any rule with a reason is good.)
  - Note: Both choices can be reasonable. A group that talks usually keeps the forest alive. A group that does not talk often loses it. Do not tell the child which is better.
- **g1s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After round 8, or when the forest is lost, count the wood and the beans left in the forest. Ask the players why the forest did or did not last. Ask what they would change.  
  *Parent says:* Did the forest last? What did we do that helped or hurt it?  
  *Child mode:* Count your wood and the forest. Say why the forest lasted or did not.
  - Ask: What did you learn about taking and giving back? (good: If we all take too much too fast, the forest ends for everyone.; Talking and agreeing a limit helped the forest last.)
  - Think: Who decides how much a village may cut, when the forest belongs to everybody?

## Experiments
### e1 · The two bowls test: which store lasts? (35 min)
- **question:** Two bowls start with the same number of beans. Nothing is ever added to the first. Three beans are added to the second after every round. How fast can we take beans before each bowl is empty?
- **predict:** Before the first round, guess. Which bowl will last longer when we take 2 beans a round, and when we take 6 beans a round? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Both bowls start with 20 beans. The same number of beans is taken from each bowl in a round. The only thing that differs between the bowls is that Bowl B gets 3 beans after every round and Bowl A gets none. Between tests, only the number taken changes: 2, then 4, then 6. Each test stops at 15 rounds or when a bowl is empty. Counting carefully matters more than doing it fast, because one missed bean changes the result.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the three tests. Write one sentence for each of: what I think, what I saw and why. Then say what the test cannot tell us about a real forest.
- **results table:** Beans taken each round | Round in which Bowl A is empty | Beans in Bowl B after round 15, or the round it was empty
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 20 dried beans in each of two steel bowls and call them Bowl A and Bowl B. Put about 50 more beans in a third bowl and call it the sky bowl. Say aloud what each bowl stands for. Bowl A is a heap of coal that nobody ever adds to. Bowl B is a well, a field or a forest that nature fills a little each round. The sky bowl is the rain and the Sun that fill Bowl B. Beans you take out go into a pile on the table, and they are used again when you reset.  
  *Parent says:* Here are two stores. Bowl A is like a coal mine. Bowl B is like a well that the rain fills. What do you think is different about them?  
  *Child mode:* Count 20 beans into Bowl A and 20 into Bowl B. Put the rest in the sky bowl.
  - Note: Dried rajma, chana, moong or any dried bean is fine. Paper squares or buttons also work. Keep the beans in separate bowls, so the counts are easy to see. Take beans out of the sky bowl only when the rules say so.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the rules for one round. First, take beans out of each bowl. Then put 3 beans from the sky bowl into Bowl B only. Bowl A never gets any beans. If a bowl has fewer beans than you need to take, take them all. That bowl is empty and its test stops. Ask for a guess and a reason before the first round, and write both on the results page.  
  *Parent says:* In every round we take the same number of beans from both bowls. Then Bowl B gets 3 beans from the sky bowl and Bowl A gets none. What do you think will happen to each bowl? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what will happen to each bowl. Write your guess and your reason.
  - Ask: What do you think happens if we take 2 beans a round? And 6 beans a round? (good: Bowl A will be empty sooner when we take more.; Bowl B might last when we take a little and not when we take a lot.; Any guess with a reason is good. The test will answer it.)
  - Note: Do not say who is right. A guess that turns out wrong is a good result, because it shows what the test teaches.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test 1. Take 2 beans from each bowl, then add 3 beans to Bowl B. Count that as round 1. Go on for up to 15 rounds, or until a bowl is empty. After every round write how many beans are in each bowl. When you finish, put the beans back. Bowl A and Bowl B should have 20 each, and the sky bowl holds the rest.  
  *Parent says:* Take 2 from each bowl, then add 3 to Bowl B. Count the beans in each bowl. What do you see happening?  
  *Child mode:* Take 2 beans from each bowl. Put 3 in Bowl B. Count. Do this for 15 rounds or until a bowl is empty.
  - Ask: What is happening to Bowl B when you take 2 and add 3? (good: It is getting fuller by one bean every round.; Nature is giving back more than we take.)
  - Note: Expect Bowl A to be empty at the end of round 10. Bowl B grows by one bean a round and has 35 beans after round 15. If your child skips the adding, the counts will not match, so watch that step.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test 2. Reset both bowls to 20 beans. This time take 4 beans from each bowl every round, then add 3 to Bowl B. Go on for up to 15 rounds. Write the count after each round. At the end, say how many beans Bowl B has left after round 15.  
  *Parent says:* Now take 4 each round. Bowl B still gets 3. Is it going up, staying the same or going down? By how much each round?  
  *Child mode:* Reset. Take 4 beans from each bowl. Put 3 in Bowl B. Count after each round.
  - Ask: Bowl B still has beans at round 15. Is it safe? (good: No. The bowl goes down by one bean every round, so the beans will run out later.; It looks fine now, but the speed of taking is bigger than the speed of giving.)
  - Note: Bowl A is empty at the end of round 5. Bowl B goes down by one bean each round and has 5 beans left after round 15. If you went on, the bowl would be empty in round 17. That is a slow slide, and it is easy to miss if you stop early.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test 3. Reset both bowls to 20 beans. Take 6 beans from each bowl every round, then add 3 to Bowl B. Write the count after each round, and note the round in which each bowl is empty.  
  *Parent says:* Now take 6 each round. Bowl B still gets 3. What do you think will happen this time?  
  *Child mode:* Reset. Take 6 beans from each bowl. Put 3 in Bowl B. Count after each round.
  - Note: Bowl A is empty in round 4. Bowl B is empty in round 6, because 5 beans are left when 6 are needed. Bowl B lasts longer than Bowl A, so nature's giving did help. It could not keep up with the taking.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the results table together. Find one test in which Bowl B never ran out. Find one in which Bowl B ran out. Ask which number made the difference, and what that number was compared with. Then ask what Bowl A would need, to behave like Bowl B.  
  *Parent says:* In which test did Bowl B never run out? What was different about the taking and the giving back? What would Bowl A need so that it could last as well?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. When did Bowl B last? Compare the beans taken with the beans added.
  - Ask: What was the difference between the tests in which Bowl B lasted and the tests in which it ran out? (good: Taking 3 or fewer beans a round let Bowl B last, because 3 are added.; When we took more than we added, Bowl B shrank.; It was the speed of taking compared with the speed of giving back.)
  - Ask: Coal is made again in swamps, but only after millions of years. Which bowl is it like, and how many beans does the sky add? (good: It is like Bowl A, because the adding is far too slow to wait for.; The adding is so tiny that it is nearly nothing in a human life.)
  - Note: The answer to look for is that the same bowl can last or run out. It depends on how fast we take compared with how fast it fills. A source is renewable only while the taking is slower than the giving back.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Say what this test cannot tell us. Real forests and wells do not get exactly 3 beans every round. Rain is sometimes more and sometimes less. Ask what the child would change in the test to make it more like a real forest, and write one idea.  
  *Parent says:* In this game the sky gave exactly 3 beans every round. Is a real forest like that? What would you change so that the game is more like a real forest or a real well?  
  *Child mode:* Write one change that would make the game more like a real forest.
  - Ask: What would you change in the test to make it more like a real well? (good: Add a different number of beans in different rounds, because rain changes.; Add a bad year with no beans, to match a drought.; Make a bowl for each village and let them share.)
  - Think: If nature gives back a different amount every year, how would a village know how much it is safe to take?
### e2 · The sun-catcher test: does black catch more of the Sun? (25 min)
- **question:** Two tumblers of water sit in the same sunlight for half an hour. One is wrapped in black paper and one in shiny foil. Which water is warmer?
- **predict:** Before putting them in the Sun, guess which tumbler will be warmer, or if they will be the same. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Both tumblers are the same size, hold the same amount of water from the same jug and start at the same temperature. They sit side by side on the same sill for the same 30 minutes. The only thing that differs is the wrapping. If you can, do the test twice and swap the tumblers' places the second time, because one place on a sill can be sunnier than another.
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun. Tumblers left in strong sun can get hot, so touch one with a fingertip first and lift them with a cloth.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Write what you expected, what you found and the reason. Say how you know both tumblers had the same sunlight.
- **results table:** Tumbler | Starting temperature (degrees Celsius) | Temperature after 30 minutes (degrees Celsius) | Warmed by (degrees)
- **e2s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find a sunny window sill, balcony or terrace wall that gets direct sunlight at about 11 am to 1 pm. Wrap one steel tumbler in black paper or black cloth, held on with tape. Wrap the other in kitchen foil with the shiny side out. Put 150 millilitres of tap water from one jug into each. Take the temperature of each, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* We have two tumblers that look different. Which one will catch more of the Sun? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Wrap one tumbler in black paper and one in foil. Pour the same amount of water into each. Write the starting temperature.
  - Note: Use the same jug of water, so that both tumblers start at the same temperature. If you have no thermometer, dip a clean finger in each, and rank the tumblers by touch.
- **e2s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the prediction and the reason. Write both before the tumblers go into the Sun.  
  *Parent says:* Which tumbler will be warmer after half an hour in the Sun? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which tumbler will be warmer. Write why you think so.
  - Note: Do not say who is right. Children often guess both will be the same, which is a good thing to test.
- **e2s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put both tumblers side by side on the sunny sill, with the same side facing the window. Start a timer for 30 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Put them next to each other, so that both get the same sunlight. Now we wait for half an hour.  
  *Child mode:* Put both tumblers side by side in the Sun. Start the timer.
  - Wait: Leave the tumblers in the sun for 30 minutes
- **e2s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 30 minutes, take the temperature of each tumbler's water, stirring gently with a clean spoon first. Write both numbers. Work out how many degrees each has warmed. If you have no thermometer, dip a clean finger in each and say which feels warmer.  
  *Parent says:* Which tumbler is warmer? How many degrees did each one warm?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each tumbler's water. Which is warmer? By how many degrees?
  - Ask: Why was the black tumbler warmer, if both got the same sunlight? (good: Black takes in most of the sunlight and turns it into heat.; Shiny foil bounces much of the sunlight away.)
  - Note: On a clear day, expect the black tumbler to be about 3 to 8 degrees warmer than the foil one. On a hazy day the gap may be only 1 or 2 degrees. If so, try again on a clearer day. If both are the same, check that both were in direct sunlight and not partly in shade.
- **e2s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Say what this test cannot tell us. It shows that a black surface catches more of the Sun's warmth than a shiny one. It does not say how much sunlight reaches your city on other days. Ask how a solar water heater might use this idea.  
  *Parent says:* A solar water heater on a roof has a black surface. Why do you think that is?  
  *Child mode:* Why would a solar water heater be black inside?
  - Ask: Where in your neighbourhood have you seen black surfaces or shiny surfaces used for heat? (good: Black pipes and tanks on roofs catch heat.; Shiny foil in a lunch box helps to keep heat in or out.)
  - Think: If clouds covered the Sun for a week, which of your energy sources would you still have?

### a2 · A rope of time: how old is the sunlight in each source? (20 min)
- **record:** Draw the rope as a line on paper and write the marks along it with their ages. Write how long the 250 years would be if you drew it.
- **a2s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the string along a hall or across a room. Tape one end down and write 'today' on a note there. Explain that 1 centimetre on the string stands for 1 million years. So 3 metres stands for 300 million years. Mark the ends with a note each.  
  *Parent says:* This string is a rope of time. One centimetre means a million years. The whole string means 300 million years. Where would you put the dinosaurs?  
  *Child mode:* Lay the string out. One end is today. Each centimetre is a million years.
  - Note: If the hall is too short, fold the string back on itself like a snake, or use a longer wool ball and mark with ink. The sums are the same.
- **a2s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Place paper flags along the string. Put the end of the dinosaurs, about 66 million years ago, at 66 centimetres. Put the first dinosaurs, about 230 million years ago, at 230 centimetres. Put the coal swamps of India, roughly 250 to 300 million years ago, at the far end, between 250 and 300 centimetres. Put the first people of our own kind, roughly 300,000 years ago, at 3 millimetres from today.  
  *Parent says:* Where do you think the coal swamps go? Were they before or after the dinosaurs? Where are the first people?  
  *Child mode:* Put the flags in place. Where is the coal? Where are we?
  - Ask: Is the coal older than the first dinosaurs, or younger? (good: Older. The coal swamps are near the far end of the rope.; The coal is about as old as the first dinosaurs or a bit older.)
  - Note: Dates are rounded and different books give slightly different numbers. The point is the size of the gaps.
- **a2s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now ask the child to mark where the 250 years of burning coal would go. It starts at the today end. 250 years is 0.00025 centimetres. That is 2.5 micrometres, and a micrometre is a thousandth of a millimetre. Show a pencil dot. Even a fine pencil makes a dot about 0.3 millimetres wide, which is 300 micrometres. The dot is more than 100 times wider than the 250 years. Ask what that tells us.  
  *Parent says:* How long is 250 years on our rope? Try to make a mark that small. Is it bigger or smaller than your pencil dot?  
  *Child mode:* Try to mark 250 years on the rope. Is your mark too big?
  - Ask: What does it mean that we are burning coal in a blink on this rope? (good: We are using in a tiny moment what took a very long time to make.; The coal cannot be made again in any time that people can plan for.)
  - Note: Use a fine pencil and a ruler with millimetres to see that the dot is much wider than the 250 years. The maths: 250 divided by 1,000,000 is 0.00025 centimetres, and one centimetre is 10,000 micrometres, so 0.00025 times 10,000 is 2.5 micrometres.
- **a2s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to say what the rope shows about which sources are renewable and which are not. Then ask where rice, firewood and the wind would sit on the rope.  
  *Parent says:* Where would last season's rice go on the rope? And firewood from a thirty-year-old tree? And the wind that blew this morning?  
  *Child mode:* Where do rice, firewood and wind go on the rope?
  - Ask: Why do rice, firewood and wind all sit so close to today? (good: The sunlight in them is only months or years old.; Their sunlight came back very recently, so they can be made again soon.)
  - Think: If we call coal old sunlight, who first saved it?
### a3 · The watt hunt: which things in the house use the most energy? (20 min)
- **safety:** Read the labels only. Do not open any machine. Do not pull a plug with wet hands.
- **record:** A table with four columns: the thing, its watts, its hours in a day and watts times hours. Circle the biggest number.
- **a3s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find six things that use electricity. Read the watts on each, from a label on the machine or its box. Try an LED bulb, a ceiling fan, a mixie, an iron or a phone charger, a television and a fridge. If a label shows volts and amps, multiply them to get watts. If there is no label, use these numbers: bulb 9, fan 60, mixie 500, iron 1,000, charger 10, television 80, fridge 150.  
  *Parent says:* Which do you think uses the most energy in one day? A bulb that is on all evening, a fan on all night or an iron that is on for ten minutes?  
  *Child mode:* Find the watts on six things. Write them in a table.
  - Note: Watts say how fast a thing uses energy, not how much it uses in a day. A big number for a short time can be less than a small number for a long time. Do not give the answer yet.
- **a3s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the child to guess how many hours each thing is on in a day. Write the guess. Then work out watts times hours for each, and call the answer 'watt-hours'. For example, a 60 watt fan on for 10 hours uses 60 times 10, which is 600 watt-hours.  
  *Parent says:* A fan of 60 watts is on for 10 hours. Multiply 60 by 10. How many watt-hours is that?  
  *Child mode:* For each thing, multiply watts by hours. Write the answer.
  - Ask: Which thing uses the most energy in a day, and which uses the least? (good: The thing with the biggest watts times hours, which is often a fan or a fridge, not an iron.; A thing with large watts can use less in a day if it is on for a short time.)
  - Note: Typical results: bulb 9 watts for 5 hours is 45 watt-hours. Iron 1,000 watts for 10 minutes is roughly 170. Fan 60 watts for 10 hours is 600. Fridge 150 watts working about 7 hours in a day is about 1,050.
- **a3s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain that a unit on the electricity bill is 1,000 watt-hours. Work out how many days the whole list would take to make 1 unit. Then ask what the family could change to use less in a day, and how many watt-hours that would save.  
  *Parent says:* A unit on the bill is 1,000 watt-hours. If we switch off the fan for 2 hours a day, how many watt-hours do we save?  
  *Child mode:* A unit is 1,000 watt-hours. How many do you save by switching the fan off for 2 hours?
  - Ask: What small change would save the most watt-hours in your home? (good: Switching off a fan or lights in an empty room for several hours.; Using a lid or a pressure cooker, because it saves gas as well.)
  - Note: A 60 watt fan switched off for 2 hours saves 120 watt-hours. Over a month of 30 days that is 3,600 watt-hours, which is more than 3 units.
- **a3s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask whether the hungriest thing is also the one that matters most to the family. Listen for answers about fridges, fans in heat, and iron for school clothes. Say that using less is a choice about what is worth the energy, not only a sum.  
  *Parent says:* The fridge uses a lot, but it keeps the food from spoiling. Is it worth it? How would you decide?  
  *Child mode:* Which thing is worth the energy it uses? Why?
  - Think: Is there anything in your home that you could do without, and anything you could not?

### a5 · Lid on, lid off: does a lid save gas? (20 min)
- **safety:** An adult lights the stove and moves the pans. Keep the child at arm's length from the flame and steam. Open a window for fresh air. Lift the lid away from your face.
- **record:** Write the time at which each pan first boiled strongly. Write who won, and by how many seconds.
- **a5s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* An adult measures 500 millilitres of cold tap water into each of the two pans. Put the lid on one pan. Set both flames to the same medium level. Do not light them yet. Ask for the prediction and reason.  
  *Parent says:* Two pans have the same water. One has a lid, and one does not. Which will boil first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which pan will boil first. Write why.
  - Note: Choose pans of the same size and metal. If you have only one lid, that is enough. The lid pan is the one being tested.
- **a5s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* The adult lights both burners together. The child starts the stopwatch. Watch until bubbles rise all over the water in each pan, and note each time. Turn both flames off at once. Leave the pans where no one will touch them until they are cool.  
  *Parent says:* Press start when the flames light. Tell us when the water in each pan is bubbling all over.  
  *Child mode:* Start the stopwatch when the stove is lit. Write the time when each pan bubbles all over.
  - Note: Usually the pan with the lid boils about 1 to 2 minutes sooner, though the size of the gap depends on your stove. If the two times are within 20 seconds, the two burners may have different flames. Swap the lid to the other burner tomorrow and try again. If your stove has only one burner, test the two pans on two days, with the same flame and the same water. Never lift the lid with your face over the pan.
- **a5s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the two times. Ask why the lid made a difference, and where the heat goes in the pan with no lid. Ask the child how much fuel the family could save in a month if a lid were used every time.

9. (match) Match each real case to the bowl it behaves like.  
   **Answer:** a coal mine → Bowl A, where nothing is added; a well pumped less than the rain fills it → Bowl B, taking 2 beans a round; a forest cut faster than it grows → Bowl B, taking 6 beans a round; a bamboo clump cut once in 4 years → Bowl B, taking a small amount slowly. *Why:* The two bowls were models. A coal mine gets nothing back in a human lifetime, so it is like Bowl A. The other cases are like Bowl B, and the speed of taking decides if each lasts.
10. (odd) Three of these are ways to store energy for the evening. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A battery in an inverter / Water pumped up to a high lake / A heap of coal waiting for the power station / A solar panel on a roof  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A battery, a high lake and a heap of coal all keep energy until it is used. A solar panel makes electricity while the Sun shines and does not keep any for night.
11. (write) Choose one thing in your home that could use less energy without losing what your family needs. How would you do it?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer names a thing and gives a real change, such as a lid, an LED bulb or switching off in an empty room. It says roughly how much that would save.. *Why:* Saving energy means getting the same light, cooking or travel from less. The best changes cost little and do not make anyone's day harder.
12. (mcq) India made about 7 in 10 units from coal in about 2024, and it changes yearly. What is fair to say?  
   Options: India can switch off coal tomorrow / Solar panels will never help / More stores and steady sources are needed as solar grows  
   **Answer:** More stores and steady sources are needed as solar grows. *Why:* Solar works in daylight only. As it grows, India needs batteries, pumped water stores and other steady sources for evening and cloudy days. Coal cannot be switched off overnight, but the mix is changing.
13. (draw) Graph the beans in Bowl B for rounds 1 to 6, when 4 are taken and 3 are added each round.  
   **Answer:** Points at 19, 18, 17, 16, 15 and 14 beans, sliding down by one each round.. *Why:* Bowl B loses 4 and gains 3, so it falls by one each round. A gentle slope can still end in an empty bowl, which a short test might not show.
14. (label) Label a pumped storage station: high lake, low lake, turbine, water up at noon, water down at night.  
   **Answer:** A high lake and a low lake joined by a pipe with a turbine in it. An arrow up marked noon and an arrow down marked night.. *Why:* At noon, spare electricity pumps water up. At night the water runs down through the turbine and makes electricity again. This is how a lake stores the Sun's work.
15. (fill) The amount of a fuel that people have found and can dig at a price worth paying is called its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['reserve']. *Why:* A reserve is always smaller than what is in the ground. New tools and higher prices make it larger, which is why the date for running out keeps moving.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Cooking gas in a cylinder is a fuel, and sunlight is not. Why?  
   Options: Gas is burnt for its stored energy, and sunlight is not burnt / Gas costs money and sunlight is free / Gas has more energy than sunlight  
   **Answer:** Gas is burnt for its stored energy, and sunlight is not burnt. *Why:* A fuel is something we burn to let out the energy stored in it. Sunlight is an energy source that nothing burns. If you chose the price, that tells us about shops and not about energy. Gas does not hold more energy than sunlight, since the Sun sends far more than we use.
2. (mcq) A friend says power stations make energy. Which is a better way to say it?  
   Options: Power stations make energy out of nothing / Power stations only store energy for later / Power stations collect energy from a source and pass it on  
   **Answer:** Power stations collect energy from a source and pass it on. *Why:* Nobody makes energy out of nothing. A power station collects energy from coal, falling water, wind, sunlight or nuclear fuel and turns it into electricity. It is not mainly a store, because most of the energy leaves at once.
3. (mcq) A village cuts 10 trees a year and 4 grow back. Is their firewood renewable?  
   Options: Not at this speed, because cutting is faster than growing / Yes, trees always grow back / Yes, because wood is a fuel  
   **Answer:** Not at this speed, because cutting is faster than growing. *Why:* A source stays renewable only while we take from it more slowly than it comes back. This village takes 10 and gets back 4, so the forest shrinks each year. Trees do grow back, but not fast enough here. Being a fuel says nothing about coming back.
4. (mcq) Peat grows about 1 millimetre a year. Why is it hard to put in one box?  
   Options: It is not a fuel / It grows back, but far slower than people dig it / It comes from the Sun  
   **Answer:** It grows back, but far slower than people dig it. *Why:* Peat is half-rotted swamp plants. It does grow, but very slowly, so people argue about whether to call it renewable. Peat does burn, so it is a fuel, and almost everything on Earth gets its energy from the Sun, so that does not decide the box.
5. (mcq) Why is coal called a fossil fuel?  
   Options: It is dug from rocks under the ground / It is as old as the dinosaur bones found with it / It is made from the buried remains of ancient living beings  
   **Answer:** It is made from the buried remains of ancient living beings. *Why:* A fossil is a trace of ancient life kept in rock, and coal is made from the pressed bodies of swamp plants. Being dug from rock is true of many things that are not fossil fuels, such as iron ore. Coal is not tied to dinosaur bones, and much of it is older than the first dinosaurs.
6. (mcq) A friend says oil came from dinosaurs. Which is better?  
   Options: Most oil came from tiny sea beings / Oil is dinosaur bodies pressed in rock / Oil was always in rocks and was never alive  
   **Answer:** Most oil came from tiny sea beings. *Why:* Most oil began as tiny beings that floated near the sea surface, died and were buried in mud. Dinosaurs lived on land and in water, and their bodies are not the main source. Oil carries chemical marks of living beings, so it was not made without life.
7. (mcq) A bowl gets 3 beans put back every round, and a player takes beans out every round. Which way of taking lets the bowl last?  
   Options: Taking 5 a round / Taking 3 or fewer a round / Taking 4 a round, because beans are left after 15 rounds  
   **Answer:** Taking 3 or fewer a round. *Why:* With 3 or fewer taken, Bowl B never shrinks, since 3 come back. Taking 5 empties it in round 10. Taking 4 leaves 5 beans at round 15, but it falls by one a round and is empty in round 17, so it only looks safe.
8. (mcq) A bowl never gets any beans put back. If you take beans from it very slowly, what happens?  
   Options: It empties, only later / It never empties, because slow is safe / It fills up by itself  
   **Answer:** It empties, only later. *Why:* With nothing added, every bean taken is gone for good. Slower taking only makes the emptying later. Only a bowl that gets beans back can last. Coal is like Bowl A, because the adding is too slow for anyone to wait for.
9. (mcq) A forest is cut faster than it grows. What is true about it?  
   Options: It is still renewable, because trees grow / It can run out, because renewable depends on both speeds / It is safe, because a forest is very big  
   **Answer:** It can run out, because renewable depends on both speeds. *Why:* Renewable is about how fast we take compared with how fast nature gives back. In the beans test, the same Bowl B lasted at one speed and ran out at another. A forest is not a bowl, but the same two speeds decide how long it lasts. Being big only makes the ending later.
10. (mcq) What mainly makes the wind blow?  
   Options: The Moon pulling the air / The Earth's inner heat / The Sun warming land and sea unevenly  
   **Answer:** The Sun warming land and sea unevenly. *Why:* The Sun warms land faster than sea. Warm air rises and cooler air slides in under it, and that moving air is wind. The Moon pulls the sea into tides, and the Earth's inner heat is too small to drive the wind.
11. (mcq) Which of these did not get its energy from the Sun?  
   Options: Firewood / Coal / Uranium for nuclear power  
   **Answer:** Uranium for nuclear power. *Why:* Firewood is sunlight caught by trees recently. Coal is sunlight caught by swamp plants long ago. The uranium atoms were made in exploding and colliding stars before the Sun was born.
12. (mcq) A big dam gives clean electricity. Which is a real problem with it?  
   Options: It burns a lot of coal / It drowns a valley and blocks fish swimming upriver / It makes new coal under the water  
   **Answer:** It drowns a valley and blocks fish swimming upriver. *Why:* A dam can flood forests, fields and villages, and a wall stops fish that swim upriver to lay eggs. It does not burn coal. No new coal forms in a lake behind a dam, since coal needs millions of years.
13. (mcq) A solar panel alone cannot light a street at night. Why?  
   Options: Panels use up their own energy each day / Street lights only run on coal / Panels need light, so night needs a store such as a battery  
   **Answer:** Panels need light, so night needs a store such as a battery. *Why:* A panel makes electricity only while light falls on it. To light a street at night we need a store, such as a battery, that was charged in the day. Panels do not use themselves up, and street lights can run on any source.
14. (mcq) A 9 watt LED and a 60 watt bulb give equal light for 5 hours. How much more energy does the old one use?  
   Options: 255 watt-hours / 51 watt-hours / 300 watt-hours  
   **Answer:** 255 watt-hours. *Why:* The old bulb uses 60 times 5, which is 300 watt-hours. The LED uses 9 times 5, which is 45. The difference is 255. If you chose 51, you found the difference in watts and forgot the hours. If you chose 300, you gave the old bulb's total and did not subtract the LED's.
15. (mcq) A city swaps to LED bulbs and still uses more electricity the next year. What is one possible reason?  
   Options: LED bulbs make more energy than they use / Cheaper light may mean more lights left on, or more things bought / Old bulbs always used less than LED bulbs  
   **Answer:** Cheaper light may mean more lights left on, or more things bought. *Why:* When something becomes cheaper to use, people sometimes use more of it, and the saving gets spent somewhere else. The English economist William Jevons noticed this about coal in 1865. No bulb makes energy, and an LED uses less than an old bulb for the same light.

## Applications
- Your electricity bill shows units used. One unit is 1,000 watt-hours, which is a 1,000 watt iron on for an hour, or a 9 watt LED bulb on for about 110 hours. Find a bill, read the units, and ask which things at home used the most.
- A cooking gas cylinder is a store of fuel. Ask how long the family's cylinder lasts. A lid on the pot can make it last longer. So can a pressure cooker, which uses about half the gas. Then fewer cylinders have to be made and carried.
- A solar water heater on a roof catches sunlight as heat in a black tank. Look for one on a nearby building and notice the angle of the panel and which way it faces. In Bengaluru and Pune many roofs have one.
- Count the people in the next bus you ride, and then count the cars beside it on the road with only one person inside. Ask how many cars the bus is standing in for, and how the road would look if every rider drove alone.
- Think about a power cut at home. If you have an inverter, ask how many hours it keeps the lights on and which things you switch off to make it last. If you do not, ask what the neighbours use.
- A village biogas tank turns dung and kitchen scraps into cooking gas, and the left-over slurry goes to the fields as manure. In Gujarat, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu many families cook on gobar gas.

## Spotted around us
- Look at the back of a ceiling fan, a bulb and a mixie. Find the number marked W. That is how many watts it uses. Notice that a mixie is far hungrier than a bulb, even though it runs for only a few minutes.
- Look at a lorry or a train of black coal as it goes along a railway or a harbour road. Notice how heavy it looks. That is old swamp sunlight on its way to a power station, perhaps from Odisha, Jharkhand or Telangana.
- Look at an LPG cylinder in your kitchen. Notice the date stamp and how long it lasts. Ask how many times the family has bought one this year, and notice what makes it finish sooner, such as an open pot.
- Look out for white wind turbines on a hill or a drive. If you pass them, count how many are spinning and how many are still. Notice that on a windy day nearly all spin together, and on a calm day nearly all stand still.
- Look at a roof or a street light with a solar panel. Notice which way it faces and if anything shades it. In India the panels face south, so that they catch the most sunlight through the day.
- Look at clothes drying on a line, salt drying in pans or papad drying on a terrace. Notice that the Sun and the wind are doing work for free. Ask how much energy it would take to dry the same things on a stove.
- At a petrol pump, look at the screen as the tank fills. Notice how many litres go in and how much it costs. A litre of petrol puts about 2.3 kilograms of carbon dioxide into the air when burnt.
- In a power cut, notice what runs on a battery and what stops. Ask how long the battery would last and what you could turn off to make it last longer.

## Open questions
- **How can large amounts of energy be stored cheaply for weeks, and not only for hours?** Batteries today can cover an evening. A cloudy, windless fortnight needs far more. Engineers are testing pumped water, hot sand and salt, hydrogen gas and new kinds of battery. None is yet both cheap and large enough.
- **Exactly how much will the Earth warm for a given amount of carbon dioxide?** Scientists agree it will warm. For a doubling of the gas, the best estimate is about 3 degrees Celsius, but the likely range runs from about 2.5 to 4. The main uncertainty is how clouds will change.
- **Can people make electricity the way the Sun makes its heat, by joining light atoms?** In December 2022 a laboratory in the United States got more energy out of a tiny fuel pellet than its lasers put in. It lasted for a fraction of a second. A power station needs this to happen many times a second, all day. India is a partner in ITER, a large test machine being built in France. Nobody knows yet if it can be made to pay.
- **What should be done with nuclear fuel after it is used?** It must be kept away from living beings for thousands of years. Finland has built a store deep in solid rock. No store has yet been tested over anything like the time needed, and nobody knows how to warn people that far in the future.
- **What is energy?** Physicists can calculate energy and have never seen the total change. They have no agreed account of what it is beyond the number. Confusion about this is honest, and questions about it are welcome.

## Connected maths
- A well holds 200 buckets of water. Rain adds 30 buckets a day. A village takes 20, then 30, then 40 buckets a day. How many buckets are in the well after 10 days at each speed? How long until the 40 a day empties it? **After 10 days: 300 buckets at 20 a day, 200 at 30 a day and 100 at 40 a day. At 40 a day the well is empty in 20 days.**. At 20 a day the well gains 30 minus 20, which is 10 each day, so 200 plus 100 is 300. At 30 a day nothing changes. At 40 a day it loses 10 each day, so 200 minus 100 is 100 after 10 days. 200 divided by 10 is 20 days to empty. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- A 9 watt LED bulb and a 60 watt old bulb are each on for 5 hours a day. Work out the watt-hours for each in one day and in 30 days. How many units on the bill does the old bulb use more in 30 days? One unit is 1,000 watt-hours. **In one day, the LED uses 45 and the old bulb 300 watt-hours. In 30 days, 1,350 and 9,000. The old bulb uses 7.65 units more.**. 9 times 5 is 45, and 60 times 5 is 300. Times 30 days: 45 times 30 is 1,350 and 300 times 30 is 9,000. 9,000 minus 1,350 is 7,650 watt-hours, which is 7.65 units. (queues `math.multiplication.L4`)
- On a rope of time, 1 centimetre stands for 1 million years. The coal swamps were 280 million years ago. The first people of our kind were 300,000 years ago. How far along the rope is each, and how many times farther is the coal than the people? **The coal is at 280 centimetres and the first people at 0.3 centimetre, which is 3 millimetres. The coal is roughly 930 times farther.**. 280 million divided by 1 million is 280 centimetres. 300,000 divided by 1,000,000 is 0.3 centimetre. 280 divided by 0.3 is roughly 933. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L4`)

## Warm-up
- Which is a fuel, a lump of coal or a gust of wind? → A lump of coal. A fuel is something we burn to let out its stored energy.
- What does renewable mean? → Able to be made new again. A renewable source comes back in days, seasons or a few years.
- What is the Sun's job for the wind? → The Sun warms land and sea unequally. Warm air rises, cooler air moves in, and the moving air is wind.
- Which three fuels are fossil fuels? → Coal, oil and natural gas. They are made from the buried remains of ancient living beings.
- What is a unit on an electricity bill? → 1,000 watt-hours, such as a 1,000 watt iron on for an hour.
- Name one thing that can be renewable and still run out. → A forest, a well or a fishpond, if we take from it faster than it comes back.
