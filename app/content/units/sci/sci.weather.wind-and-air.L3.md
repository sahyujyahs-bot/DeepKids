# Wind: why air moves, and how it lifts kites and turns windmills
`sci.weather.wind-and-air.L3`

**Summary.** Open the fridge door and cold air pours out over your feet, though nobody is blowing. Cold air is heavier than the same amount of warm air, so it slides down and under the warm air. Wind starts the same way, outdoors. The Sun warms the land more than the sea, the warm air over the land rises, and cooler sea air slides in under it. That is the sea breeze. Moving air is real stuff with weight, so it pushes on whatever it meets. Its push holds up a kite and turns the blades of a windmill.

**Intro.** *Switch off the fan and shut the windows. The air in the room is still. Now hold a strip of tissue paper and walk fast across the room. The tissue streams out behind you. Where did that wind come from?* Wind is air moving past you. You can make it by moving through still air, or a fan can push the air past you. But outdoors, nobody is pushing. So what moves the air? In this unit you find out by opening a fridge and watching where its cold air goes. You find out why the sea breeze reaches coastal towns in the afternoon, how a kite stays up on a thread, and how a paper windmill can lift a load. You also meet the people who argued about why the great winds over the oceans blow.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that wind is moving air, and show that air is real stuff with weight that pushes when it moves.
1. I can explain why air moves by itself: cooler, heavier air slides in under warmer, lighter air. I can use my fridge test as evidence.
2. I can explain why a sea breeze blows from the sea in the daytime, and a land breeze blows from the land at night.
3. I can explain how moving air holds up a kite, and why a kite needs wind or a runner.
4. I can build a windmill that lifts a load, and explain how wind turbines make electricity.
5. I can tell how Galileo and Halley explained the great winds differently, and what Hadley added.
6. I can follow the safety rules for wind and kites: no kites near power lines, no glass-coated thread, and stay indoors in a cyclone.

## Terms
- **Air**: The mixture of gases all around us. It is mostly nitrogen and oxygen. You cannot see it, but it is real stuff that takes up space and has weight. *e.g.* A 1 litre water bottle that looks empty is full of air. That air weighs about 1 gram.
- **Wind**: Air that is moving past you or past something else. *e.g.* When the washing on the balcony line flaps, the wind is pushing it.
- **Breeze**: A gentle wind. *e.g.* In the evening, a cool breeze comes through the window and moves the curtain a little.
- **Dense**: Packed closely. A litre of dense air holds more air, so it weighs more than a litre of less dense air. *e.g.* A litre of cold air is denser than a litre of warm air, so it is a little heavier.
- **Expand**: To spread out and take up more space. Air expands when it is warmed. *e.g.* Put a balloon in the sun and it gets a little bigger, because the air inside expands.
- **Sea breeze**: A wind that blows from the sea onto the land in the daytime, when the land is warmer than the sea. *e.g.* In Chennai in summer, a cool sea breeze often arrives in the afternoon.
- **Land breeze**: A wind that blows from the land out to the sea at night, when the land has become cooler than the sea. *e.g.* Before dawn, fishers on the coast used the land breeze to sail their boats out to sea.
- **Monsoon**: A wind that changes direction with the seasons and brings a rainy season. India's summer monsoon blows from the sea in the southwest. *e.g.* In June the southwest monsoon reaches Kerala and the rains begin.
- **Trade winds**: Steady winds that blow towards the equator from the northeast and the southeast, over the oceans, for most of the year. *e.g.* Sailing ships used the trade winds to cross the Atlantic Ocean.
- **Kite**: A light frame covered with paper or cloth that flies on a string. Moving air pushes on its tilted face and holds it up. *e.g.* At Makar Sankranti, the sky over Ahmedabad and Hyderabad fills with kites.
- **Windmill**: A machine with blades or sails that the wind turns. The turning does a job, such as grinding grain or pumping water. *e.g.* Old windmills in the Netherlands pumped water out of low fields.
- **Wind turbine**: A modern windmill that makes electricity. The wind turns the blades, and the blades turn a generator. *e.g.* Hundreds of wind turbines stand near Muppandal in Kanyakumari district in Tamil Nadu.
- **Generator**: A machine that makes electricity when something turns it. *e.g.* A bicycle dynamo is a small generator. The turning wheel spins it and the cycle lamp lights.
- **Thermal**: A rising column of warm air. *e.g.* A black kite, a bird, circles with still wings over a city, rising in a thermal.
- **Cyclone**: A huge storm with very strong winds that spin round a calm centre, called the eye. Cyclones form over warm seas. *e.g.* Cyclones hit the coasts of Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha, often in October to December.
- **Manja**: Kite thread coated with glue and powdered glass, to cut other kites' threads. It also cuts people and birds badly. *e.g.* Glass-coated and nylon kite thread are banned in India. Use plain cotton thread.

## Explanations
### Air is real stuff, and wind is air moving · *defined*
**Definition:** Air is a mixture of gases. It takes up space and has weight. Wind is air moving past something.

Air is made of tiny particles, far too small to see. They fly about very fast and bump into everything. You do not feel the air when it is still, because it presses on you equally from every side. When the air moves, its particles hit the front of you harder than the back, and you feel a push. That push is the wind. Air is light, but it is not weightless. One litre of air weighs a little over 1 gram. A bedroom 4 metres long, 3 metres wide and 3 metres high holds about 36,000 litres of air. That air weighs about 43 kilograms, more than most children in Class 3.
- e.g. You feel a wind when you cycle on a still day, because you are moving through the air.
- e.g. A ceiling fan pushes air down, and you feel it as a breeze.
- e.g. A flat football gets a little heavier when it is pumped up, because more air has been squeezed in.
### Why warm air rises and cool air sinks · *defined*
**Definition:** When air is warmed it expands, so a litre of it weighs less. Cooler, denser air sinks under it and pushes it up.

In warm air, the particles move faster and push each other further apart. So warm air expands, and a litre of warm air holds fewer particles than a litre of cool air. That makes it lighter for its size. Cool air is denser. It sinks and slides underneath, the way water runs under oil. As the cool air slides in, it pushes the warm air up. This is why the cold air in a fridge pours out at the bottom when you open the door. It is why the air near the ceiling of a room is warmer than the air near the floor. And it is how a hot air balloon rises: the burner heats the air inside, and the cooler air outside pushes the balloon up.
- e.g. Smoke from a lit agarbatti rises, because the flame warms the air around it.
- e.g. A hot air balloon rises when its air is heated, and comes down as that air cools.
- e.g. On a hot afternoon, the top bunk or the loft of a house is the warmest place.
### What makes the wind blow · *defined*
**Definition:** Winds blow because the Sun warms some places more than others. Air flows from cooler, heavier places towards warmer, lighter ones.

The Sun does not warm the Earth evenly. A rocky hill heats up faster than a lake. A city of concrete heats up faster than a forest. The land near the equator gets more sunshine than the land near the poles. Wherever one place is warmer than the next, the air over the warm place expands and rises. Cooler, heavier air from the next place flows in under it to take its place. That flow is a wind. So a wind needs no one to blow it. It needs only a warm place next to a cool one. The spinning of the Earth then bends the bigger winds as they travel.
- e.g. The sea breeze, from the cool sea to the warm land.
- e.g. Breezes that flow down a hill at night, when the hillside cools.
- e.g. The great trade winds over the oceans, flowing towards the hot equator.
### Sea breeze by day, land breeze by night · *defined*
**Definition:** In the daytime the land gets warmer than the sea, and a sea breeze blows onto the land. At night the land gets cooler than the sea, and a land breeze blows out to sea.

Soil and rock heat up quickly in the sunshine. Water heats up slowly, because the Sun's warmth spreads down into deep water and the waves keep mixing it. So by late morning, the land on a coast is much warmer than the sea. The air over the land rises, and cool air from the sea flows in under it. This is the sea breeze. It can travel many kilometres inland. At night the land loses its heat quickly, but the sea stays warm. Now the air over the sea is the warmer air. It rises, and air from the cool land flows out to sea. This is the land breeze. On the Coromandel coast, fishers who sailed kattumarams, boats made of logs tied together, went out early with the land breeze and came home with the sea breeze.
- e.g. People in Chennai wait for the sea breeze on hot summer afternoons.
- e.g. Kochi and Visakhapatnam get sea breezes too.
- e.g. Inland cities such as Bengaluru and Hyderabad are too far from the sea for a sea breeze.
### The monsoon: the biggest wind in India's year · *debated*
In summer, India's land becomes very hot, and in June winds from the Indian Ocean in the southwest sweep over the country, carrying water vapour that falls as rain. In winter the winds turn round and blow from the northeast. Scientists agree that the Sun's uneven heating drives these winds. They have argued about the best way to picture it.
- e.g. The southwest monsoon usually reaches Kerala around the beginning of June.
- e.g. Tamil Nadu gets much of its rain from the northeast monsoon, in October to December.
- e.g. Ships sailing between India and Arabia timed their voyages by the monsoon winds for hundreds of years.
- **Edmond Halley, 1686, and many textbooks since:** The monsoon is a giant sea breeze. In summer the land of India and Asia gets much hotter than the ocean, so air rises over the land and moist sea air flows in. In winter the land is cooler, and the wind turns round.
- **Sulochana Gadgil, an Indian scientist at the Indian Institute of Science, Bengaluru, and others:** The sea breeze picture is too simple. Near the equator there is a long belt of rising air and rain clouds that circles the Earth. This belt moves north and south with the seasons, following the Sun. In summer it moves over India, and the monsoon winds and rain come with it.
### How moving air pushes · *defined*
**Definition:** Moving air pushes on whatever is in its way. The faster the wind, the harder it pushes.

Air has weight, so moving air carries a push, like moving water does, only much weaker. When wind meets a wall, a sail or a kite, the air particles hit it and are turned aside, and the thing feels a push. A bigger surface catches more air, so it gets a bigger push. Speed matters most. When the wind blows twice as fast, it pushes about four times as hard. That is why a gentle breeze only stirs leaves, but a cyclone's wind can break branches and tear off tin roofs.
- e.g. An open umbrella is pulled hard in a strong wind, but a closed one is not.
- e.g. A sailboat's sail catches the wind and the push moves the boat.
- e.g. Clothes dry faster in a breeze, because moving air carries the water vapour away.
### How a kite flies · *defined*
**Definition:** A kite flies because moving air pushes on its tilted face. The string holds it against the wind, so the push holds it up.

A kite is held at a tilt by its string, with its face leaning back into the wind. The moving air hits the face and is pushed downwards. The air pushes back on the kite, up and backwards. The string stops the kite from being blown away, so the upward part of the push holds it in the sky. If the wind drops, the push drops, and the kite sinks. That is why you run with a kite on a still day. Running pulls the kite through the air, and the moving air holds it up. A tail adds a little weight and drag at the bottom, so the kite points into the wind and does not spin. Kites were probably first made in China, more than 2000 years ago.
- e.g. A patang at Makar Sankranti flies on wind, with the flyer tugging the string to steer it.
- e.g. A kite climbs when you pull the string in, because it moves faster through the air.
- e.g. Kitesurfers ride on the sea, pulled by a big kite.
### Windmills and wind turbines · *defined*
**Definition:** A windmill's blades are tilted, so the wind's push turns them. A wind turbine uses the turning to spin a generator and make electricity.

If you hold a flat card face-on to the wind, the wind just pushes it back. Tilt it, and the wind pushes it sideways too. A windmill has several tilted blades round a centre, all tilted the same way. So the wind pushes each blade sideways in the same direction round the circle, and the whole wheel turns. The turning shaft can do work. More than a thousand years ago, windmills in Persia, now Iran, ground grain. Later, windmills in the Netherlands pumped water out of low fields. A wind turbine turns a generator inside the box at the top of its tower. Tamil Nadu has many wind farms, such as the one near Muppandal in Kanyakumari district, where strong winds blow through a gap in the hills. In a dangerous storm, turbines are stopped and their blades are turned edge-on, so the wind cannot damage them.
- e.g. A paper pinwheel turns because its four tilted blades are all pushed the same way round.
- e.g. Old windmills ground wheat into flour between two big stones.
- e.g. Electricity from wind farms in Tamil Nadu reaches homes through the same wires as other electricity.
### How living things use the wind · *defined*
**Definition:** Many plants and animals use moving air to travel, spread their seeds and pollen, or save energy.

A black kite, a bird of prey common over Indian cities, has a problem. They must search a wide area for food every day, and flapping big wings uses a lot of energy. Rising warm air, called a thermal, solves this. The bird spreads their long wings and circles inside the thermal, rising higher and higher without a flap. Then they glide off to the next one. Plants that cannot walk use the wind to send their children away. The seeds of the erukku plant, called crown flower or Calotropis, have silky white hairs, so a breeze carries them far from the parent plant, where they will not compete with the parent for light and water. Grasses, maize and many trees let the wind carry their pollen from one plant to the next. Spiderlings can float on silk threads in the air and travel long distances.
- e.g. Black kites and Brahminy kites circle high above cities on hot afternoons.
- e.g. Erukku seeds float away on their silky hairs when the pod splits open.
- e.g. Maize plants release clouds of yellow pollen that the wind carries.
### Staying safe with wind and kites · *defined*
**Definition:** Strong winds and kite strings can hurt people and animals. A few simple rules keep everyone safe.

Fly kites only from an open terrace with a wall or railing, never from a roof edge or a road. Never fly a kite near electric wires. If a kite gets stuck on a wire, leave it and tell a grown-up. Use plain cotton thread. Glass-coated manja and nylon thread cut fingers and necks, and they kill many birds every year. Thousands of birds, such as pigeons and black kites, are hurt by manja at kite festivals. A cyclone's winds can throw tin sheets, flowerpots and branches through the air. When a cyclone warning comes, bring loose things in from the balcony, stay indoors and keep away from windows.
- e.g. In Ahmedabad, volunteers run bird hospitals during the Uttarayan kite festival, to treat birds cut by kite string.
- e.g. Before a cyclone, families in coastal towns bring flowerpots in from their balconies.
- e.g. A grown-up checks that the terrace has a railing before a kite is flown.

## Mostly agreed
- Air is real stuff. It takes up space and has weight. A litre of air weighs a little over 1 gram. *How we know:* In 1643, Evangelista Torricelli showed that the weight of the air holds up a column of mercury in a tube. Scientists have weighed air in sealed flasks many times since.
- Warm air expands and is less dense than cool air, so cool air sinks under it and pushes it up. *How we know:* Hot air balloons rise when the air in them is heated. Anyone can see the cold air pour out of the bottom of an open fridge with a little smoke.
- Winds blow because the Sun warms some places more than others. *How we know:* Weather stations all over the world measure temperature, air pressure and wind together. Air flows from places of high pressure to places of low pressure, and uneven heating makes these differences.
- On coasts, the wind usually blows from the sea by day and from the land at night. *How we know:* Weather records from coastal cities, such as Chennai, show this pattern on most clear days, especially in summer.
- The spin of the Earth bends winds as they travel long distances. *How we know:* George Hadley explained this in 1735, and later scientists worked out the exact rule. Weather maps show winds curving round storms, and cyclones in the northern half of the Earth all spin the same way.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei and Johannes Kepler, who had the first idea, Edmond Halley, an English astronomer, and George Hadley, an English lawyer and scientist, Early 1600s (the spin idea), 1686 (Halley's map and paper) and 1735 (Hadley's paper).** For a long time, sailors knew the trade winds, steady winds that blow from the east over the warm seas near the equator. Galileo and Kepler, in the early 1600s, thought they knew why. The Earth spins towards the east, they said, and the air cannot keep up, so it is left behind as an east wind. In 1686, the English astronomer Edmond Halley collected sailors' reports of the winds from many oceans. He had felt the trade winds himself, on a voyage to the island of St Helena. He drew them on a map, one of the first weather maps ever made, and the map included the monsoons of the seas near India. The monsoon winds turn round with the seasons, but the Earth spins the same way all year. So the Earth's spin could not be the main cause. Halley said the Sun is the cause. Air over the hottest places is warmed and rises, and cooler air flows in to take its place. In 1735, George Hadley added the last piece. The spin does not make the winds, but it bends them once they move. That is why air flowing towards the equator arrives from the east.  
*What people believed before:* Galileo and Kepler thought the trade winds were air left behind by the spinning Earth. Halley's map showed that winds such as the monsoon change with the seasons, which the spin alone cannot do, and that the Sun's heating drives them. Hadley then showed that the spin is still part of the story: it bends the winds as they flow.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Trees make the wind by waving their branches.** (Many young children. The Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget wrote down this idea from children he talked to in the 1920s.; *rejected*)  
  For: When the branches wave, there is wind, and when they are still, there is none. The two always go together.  
  Against: The wind moves the branches, not the other way round. There is wind over the sea and over deserts, where there are no trees at all. Waving one branch by hand makes only a tiny puff.
- **Air weighs nothing.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: You cannot feel the weight of the air on you, and an 'empty' bottle feels no heavier than a squashed one.  
  Against: Air presses on you from all sides at once, so you do not feel it. But a litre of air weighs a little over 1 gram. A balloon full of squeezed air tips a balance, and a pumped-up football weighs more than a flat one.
- **The trade winds blow because the Earth spins and leaves the air behind.** (Galileo Galilei and Johannes Kepler, in the early 1600s; *rejected*)  
  For: The Earth spins towards the east, and the trade winds near the equator blow from the east, as if the air were lagging behind.  
  Against: Air is held by the Earth's gravity and spins along with the ground. Edmond Halley showed in 1686 that the Sun's heating drives the winds. The spin only bends the winds once they are moving, as George Hadley showed in 1735.
- **Hot air rises by itself.** (The way most people, and many books, say it; *accepted*)  
  For: Smoke and warm air do go up, and nobody seems to lift them.  
  Against: Warm air is lighter for its size, but it goes up because the heavier, cooler air around it sinks and pushes in underneath. In a place with no gravity, such as a space station, warm air does not rise at all. A candle flame there is round, not pointed.
- **The monsoon is just a giant sea breeze.** (Edmond Halley in 1686, and many school books; *debated*)  
  For: The land of India is much hotter than the sea in summer, and the monsoon winds blow from the sea onto the land, like a sea breeze.  
  Against: Sulochana Gadgil and other scientists point out that the monsoon behaves like a belt of rising air and rain clouds that moves north with the Sun. A sea breeze picture cannot explain why the rains sometimes stop for weeks in the middle of the season.

## Roots
We have watched cold air pour out of a fridge, felt the sea breeze in a story, and seen moving air lift kites and turn windmills. Now we ask what the wind tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** When you opened the fridge, nobody blew, yet cold air poured out over your feet. Cold air is heavier than the same amount of warm air, so it slid under the room air. The sea breeze starts the same way, as cool sea air slides in under warm land air. So a wind needs no blower, only a warm place next to a cool one.  
*Habit it breaks:* We speak of the wind as if something blows it, like breath from a mouth or air from a fan. But most winds have no blower. They are air flowing by itself, from where it is cooler and heavier to where it is warmer and lighter.  
*Evidence:* In the Open Fridge test, smoke near the floor flowed out and down as soon as the door opened, and bare feet felt the cold air first. Nothing in the fridge was pushing. When the door closed, the flow stopped.

- *who am i:* Every breath you take is a small wind. Your chest makes room, and the air outside flows in to fill it.
- *what is life:* A black kite, the bird, rises on warm air without a single flap. Seeds of the erukku plant travel on the breeze to find new ground.
- *what is this world:* The whole sky is always moving, because the Sun warms some places more than others, and the air keeps flowing to even it out.

If the Sun warmed every place the same, the air would rest. Because the Earth is uneven, with land and sea, hills and plains, day and night, the air never rests. From one simple rule, cooler air sliding under warmer air, come sea breezes, land breezes, monsoons and trade winds, all joined into one moving sky. The breeze at your window is part of that one sky.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 4.3:** In this teaching, the wind, Vayu, is called the gatherer. When a fire goes out, it goes into the wind. When the Sun sets, it goes into the wind. Within the body, the breath, prana, is the gatherer in the same way. (The wind really does gather. The air over the whole Earth is one body of air, and the wind carries warmth, water vapour, smoke and seeds from one place to another.)
- **Tao Te Ching 5:** The space between heaven and earth is like a bellows. It is empty, yet it never runs out. The more it moves, the more it brings forth. (A bellows blows air by making room. The wind, too, flows in wherever warm air has risen and made room for it.)
- Science (settled): Winds blow because the Sun heats the Earth unevenly. The flowing air carries heat from the hot tropics towards the cold poles.
- Science (settled): Air from one part of the world mixes all around the Earth within about a year.
- Science (open): Exactly how the monsoon will change as the Earth gets warmer is not yet known.
- Other view, Many physicists and weather scientists: The wind is the movement of gas from high pressure to low pressure, and nothing more. Calling the sky one body, or comparing the wind to breath, adds a feeling to the science but does not help anyone forecast tomorrow's weather.
- Practice, Feel the small wind of your breath (3 min): Sit comfortably and hold one hand in front of your mouth. Breathe in slowly through your nose. Now breathe out gently through your mouth onto your hand. Feel that little wind? Do it three more times. Each time you breathe in, your chest makes room, and air flows in by itself. Now sit still and listen for the wind outside.
- Ask: If nobody blows the wind, what is the wind doing?
- Ask: The air you breathe in has been to many other places. Where might it have been before?
- Ask: A kite needs wind, but it also needs a string holding it back. Can holding back help something rise?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Nobody blows the wind. So is the wind something that happens to the air, or something the air does? (hint: Think of the fridge test. Did anything push the cold air, or did it flow out because it was heavier?)
- *deep:* If the Sun warmed every part of the Earth exactly the same, would there be any wind at all? (hint: Think about what the sea breeze needs: a warm place next to a cool one.)
- *big:* The air you breathe out today could reach another country in a few weeks. Whose air is it? (hint: Think about how far the wind can carry smoke, dust and seeds.)
- *big:* A black kite rides a thermal without flapping. Is the bird flying, or is the air flying the bird? (hint: Think about who chooses where to circle, and what lifts the bird.)
- *big:* You can only see the wind by what it moves. Are there other real things we know only by what they do? (hint: Think of gravity, magnetism, or someone's kindness.)
- *deep:* A wind turbine takes energy from the wind. Is the wind behind a wind farm a little slower? (hint: Think about what happens to a ball's speed when it pushes something out of the way.)

## Story: The Kite That Waited for the Sea
*Sea breezes in the afternoon and land breezes before dawn are real on the coast of Visakhapatnam, as on most of India's coast, and fishers really used them to sail. Sravani, Kiran, Lakshmamma and their building are made up.*

It was the first week of the summer holidays in Visakhapatnam, and Sravani had a new kite. It was a red and yellow patang with a long paper tail, and her grandmother had bought it from the shop at the bottom of the road. Sravani was nine. Her brother Kiran was six, and he wanted the kite more than anything in the world.

Their building had five floors and a flat terrace on top, with a railing all round it. From the terrace you could see the roofs of the whole street, a water tank, two coconut trees, and far away to the east, a thin grey line that was the sea.

At eight in the morning, Sravani and Kiran ran up the stairs with the kite. Sravani held the reel. Kiran held the kite above his head and let go. The kite rose a little, wobbled, and slid down onto the terrace floor. They tried again. And again. Every time, the kite flopped like a tired fish.

'It's broken,' said Kiran.

'It isn't broken,' said Sravani. 'There's no wind.' She was right. The washing on the line hung straight down. The coconut leaves were still. The morning air was warm and thick and did not move at all.

They went down for breakfast. Their grandmother, Bamma, was making pesarattu, and the kitchen smelled of green gram and ginger. 'No wind in the morning,' said Bamma, without looking up. 'Go up after lunch. The sea wind comes after lunch.'

'Why after lunch?' asked Sravani. 'Does the sea wind eat lunch too?'

Bamma laughed. 'Ask Lakshmamma. She knows the sea better than I do.'

Lakshmamma lived on the ground floor. Every morning she bought fish at the harbour and sold them from a basket at the corner. She was sitting on her doorstep cleaning a basket when Sravani came down to ask.

'The sea wind?' said Lakshmamma. 'When I was small, my father had a kattumaram, a boat made of logs tied together, with one sail. He went out before the sun came up. At that time the wind blows from the land to the sea, and it carried his sail out. In the afternoon the wind turns round and blows from the sea to the land. So he came home with it. Every day, the wind took him out and brought him back.'

'But why does it turn round?' asked Sravani.

Lakshmamma shrugged and smiled. 'That I never asked. You find out, and tell me.'

At two o'clock, Sravani climbed back to the terrace. This time she did not take the kite. She took a long strip of tissue paper from Bamma's sweet box, and a pencil. She stood in the middle of the terrace and held up the strip.

It was beginning to move. It lifted and fluttered, and then it streamed out steadily, pointing west, away from the sea. The wind was coming from the sea. The washing on the line was swinging. The coconut leaves were tossing like hair.

The terrace floor was so hot she had to stand on her chappals. She bent and touched the railing, and pulled her hand away. Everything up here had been baking in the sun since morning. But the wind on her face was cool, and it smelled of salt.

Sravani thought about the fridge at home. When Amma opened it, cold air came out at the bottom and poured over your feet, all by itself. Nobody pushed it. Cold air was heavier, so it slid out and down, under the warm kitchen air.

She looked out at the grey line of the sea. The sea did not get hot like the terrace. When they went to the beach in the evening, the sand burned your feet, but the water was always cool. So all day, the land was getting hotter and hotter, and the sea was staying cool. The air over the hot land would be warm and light. The air over the cool sea would be cool and heavy. And the heavy air would slide in under the light air, like the fridge air over her feet.

'The sea isn't blowing,' Sravani said out loud. 'The land is warming up, and the sea air is sliding in to fill the place.'

She ran down for Kiran and the kite. This time, when Kiran let go, the patang leapt up as if someone had pulled it from above. Sravani let out the thread, and the kite climbed until it was a red dot, leaning out over the street towards the west. The thread hummed in her fingers.

'Why does it work now?' shouted Kiran.

'Because the terrace is hot,' said Sravani. She told him about the fridge, and the warm land, and the cool sea. Kiran did not understand all of it. But he put his bare hand on the hot railing, and then held it up in the cool wind, and nodded seriously.

That night, Sravani could not sleep. She went to the window with the tissue strip and held it out through the grill. Very gently, it pointed the other way, towards the sea. The land had cooled down, and the sea was the warm one now.

In the morning, she knocked on Lakshmamma's door. 'I found out,' she said. 'The wind goes to wherever is warmest. In the day the land is warmest, so the sea air comes in. At night the sea is warmest, so the land air goes out. Your father was riding the warm and cold.'

Lakshmamma looked at her for a long time. Then she said, 'My father would have liked you.' And she gave Sravani the biggest prawn in the basket, for Bamma's curry.

**For the parent:** The sea does not blow the sea breeze. The Sun warms the land more than the sea, and the cooler, heavier sea air slides in under the warm land air, just as cold air pours out of an open fridge.

- Ask: Why would the kite not fly at eight in the morning?
- Ask: Sravani thought of the fridge when she felt the cool sea wind. What was the same about the two?
- Ask: At night the tissue strip pointed towards the sea. Why had the wind turned round?
- Ask: Lakshmamma's father used the wind without knowing why it turned. Is it still knowledge if you cannot explain it?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Open Fridge: where does the air go? (25 min)
- **question:** When you open the fridge door, does air move? Which way does it go at the top of the door, and which way at the bottom?
- **predict:** Guess first. When the fridge door opens, will the air at the bottom move in, move out, or stay still? What about the air at the top? Draw an arrow for each, and say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Hold the smoke in the same three places every time: at the top edge of the open door, in the middle, and at the bottom edge. Hold it the same distance from the fridge, about a hand-span. Close the door and wait one minute between tries, so the fridge cools again. Try each place 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights the agarbatti, blows out the flame so it only smokes, and holds it the whole time. Keep it away from hair, curtains, paper and plastic. Open a window afterwards. If anyone in the family has asthma, or you have no agarbatti, use the tissue strip and your bare feet instead. Never keep the fridge door open for more than half a minute at a time, so the food stays cold.
- **record:** Draw the open fridge from the side. Draw an arrow for where the smoke went at the top, the middle and the bottom. Fill in the table.
- **results table:** Place | Fridge closed: which way does the smoke go? | Door open, try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand with your child in front of the closed fridge. 2. Give them paper and a pencil. 3. Ask them to draw the open fridge from the side. 4. Ask them to add an arrow at the top and the bottom for where the air will go.  
  *Parent says:* When I open the fridge, will any air move? Nobody will blow. Draw an arrow for what you think the air does at the top, and one for the bottom. Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the fridge with its door open. Will the air move? Draw an arrow at the top. Draw one at the bottom. Why did you draw them that way?
  - Ask: Why do you think the air will go that way? (good: Cold air comes out because it is cold inside.; The air stays still because nothing is blowing.; Warm air goes in because the fridge is open.)
  - Note: Do not give away the answer. Any guess with a reason is good. Many children think nothing will move because nothing is blowing, or that cold air will come out everywhere.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child takes off their socks and chappals and stands right in front of the fridge. 2. Open the door wide for 20 seconds. 3. Ask: where do you feel cold, on your face, your tummy or your feet? 4. Close the door.  
  *Parent says:* Stand barefoot right by the fridge. I'm opening the door. Where do you feel the cold first: your face, your tummy, or your feet?  
  *Child mode:* Take off your chappals. Stand by the fridge. When the door opens, where do you feel cold? Face, tummy or feet?
  - Note: Most children feel the cold on their feet and ankles first, within a few seconds, and hardly at all on their face. That is a real result. Write it down.
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up lights the agarbatti and blows out the flame, so a thin line of smoke rises. 2. With the fridge door closed, hold it a hand-span in front of the door, at the bottom. 3. Watch which way the smoke goes.  
  *Parent says:* First, the smoke with the door closed. Watch where it goes.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up holds the smoking agarbatti near the closed fridge. Where does the smoke go?
  - Note: The smoke should rise almost straight up, because the agarbatti warms the air around it. This is your 'before'. Hold the stick still. If the room fan is on, switch it off, or the smoke will blow about.
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the smoke a hand-span from the fridge, level with the bottom shelf. 2. Your child opens the door wide. 3. Watch the smoke for 10 seconds. 4. Close the door, wait one minute. 5. Repeat 3 times and record each try.  
  *Parent says:* Now the bottom. Open the door. Watch the smoke for ten seconds. Which way does it go now?  
  *Child mode:* Open the fridge door wide. Watch the smoke at the bottom. Does it go up, out, or in? Write it down. Do it three times.
  - Note: At the bottom, the smoke usually bends out of the fridge and down towards the floor, then spreads along the floor. This is the clearest result in the experiment.
  - Check: At the bottom of the open fridge, the smoke flowed out and down to the floor. Why? → **The cold air inside is heavier than the room air, so it pours out and sinks**
- **s5 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the smoke a hand-span from the fridge, level with the top shelf. 2. Open the door and watch for 10 seconds. 3. Close, wait one minute. 4. Do the same in the middle. 5. Record each try.  
  *Parent says:* Now the top, and then the middle. Watch closely. Does the smoke go in, out, or straight up?  
  *Child mode:* Now hold the smoke at the top. Open the door. Which way does it go? Then try the middle.
  - Note: At the top, the smoke often drifts gently into the fridge, as warm room air flows in to take the place of the cold air that poured out. This is weaker than the bottom result, so look closely. In the middle, the smoke may hardly bend. If you see little at the top, that is fine. The bottom is the main result.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: air moves by itself when cold air and warm air meet. What I saw: my feet felt cold first, and the smoke at the bottom flowed out and down every time. At the top it went in a little. Why: cold air is heavier, so it poured out under the warm room air, and warm air went in at the top.
  - Think: Outdoors there is no fridge. But is there anywhere that is cooler, next to somewhere warmer?
### e2 · The Windmill That Lifts: does a stronger wind lift the load faster? (35 min)
- **question:** Does the windmill lift a paper clip faster when it is close to the fan, where the wind is stronger?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the windmill lift the clip faster close to the fan, far from the fan, or at the same speed? How many times faster? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Keep everything the same except the distance from the fan: the same windmill, the same clip, the same length of thread, the same fan speed. Time each distance 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the sharp tip off the skewer. Keep fingers, hair and the paper away from the fan blades and the fan grill.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Circle the fastest time at each distance. Draw your windmill and label the blades, the axle and the load.
- **results table:** Distance from the fan | Seconds to lift the clip, try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3 | Fastest try
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the paper square corner to corner both ways and open it. 2. Cut along each fold from the corners, stopping 2 cm from the middle. 3. Bend every other point to the middle and tape them, to make a pinwheel.  
  *Parent says:* First we make the blades. Each blade must be tilted, all the same way round.  
  *Child mode:* Fold the square corner to corner. Open it. Cut along the folds, not to the middle. Bend every other point to the middle.
  - Note: This is the usual paper pinwheel. Help with the cutting. The four bent points are the tilted blades.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the straw across the bottom of the upturned cup, so it sticks out on both sides. 2. Push the skewer through the straw. 3. Push the pinwheel's middle onto one end of the skewer and fix it with dough. 4. Tape the thread to the other end, with the clip tied on.  
  *Parent says:* Now the windmill. The skewer is the axle. It must spin freely inside the straw.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the straw on top of the upturned cup. Push the skewer through. Fix the pinwheel on one end. Tie the clip on the thread at the other end.
  - Note: Spin the skewer with your fingers. It should turn easily. If it rubs, make sure the straw is straight. Put the cup near the edge of a table. The pinwheel end of the skewer faces the fan, and the thread end sticks out past the table edge. Let the clip hang 30 cm below the axle.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the fan on a low setting. 2. Ask your child to guess the time to lift the clip at 50 cm and at 150 cm.  
  *Parent says:* Before we time it, guess. How many seconds near the fan? How many far away? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the time near the fan. Guess the time far away. Why do you think so?
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the windmill 50 cm from the fan, facing it. 2. Let the clip hang 30 cm down. 3. Start the clock when you let go of the pinwheel. 4. Stop when the clip reaches the axle. 5. Unwind and repeat 3 times.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? Let go of the pinwheel, and start the clock. Stop when the clip reaches the top.  
  *Child mode:* Put the windmill near the fan. Let go and start the clock. Stop when the clip reaches the top. Do it three times.
  - Note: Near a table fan on low, the clip often rises in 10 to 30 seconds. If the windmill does not turn at all, check that the blades all bend the same way and that the skewer turns freely.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Move the windmill to 150 cm from the fan and time 3 more tries in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now move it further away. Same fan, same clip. Time it again, three times.  
  *Child mode:* Move the windmill far from the fan. Time it again. Three times.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: what I think, a stronger wind does work faster. What I saw, near the fan the clip took about 15 seconds, far away about 45 seconds. Why, near the fan the air moves faster and pushes the blades harder.
  - Check: The clip rose much faster near the fan. Why? → **The wind is faster near the fan, and faster wind pushes the blades much harder**
### e3 · Soil or Water: which warms faster in the sun? (40 min)
- **question:** In the same sunshine, does soil or water get warmer in 30 minutes?
- **predict:** Guess first. After 30 minutes in the sun, will the soil or the water be warmer, or will they be the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two bowls that are the same size and kind. Fill both to the same depth, about 3 cm. Start them at the same temperature by leaving both in the shade for half an hour first. Put them side by side in the same sun, for the same time.
- **safety:** Do not look at the Sun. On a very hot day, the soil can get hot enough to sting your fingers, so touch it quickly and gently.
- **record:** Fill in the table with thermometer readings, or with 'cool', 'warm' or 'hot' if you test by touch. Circle which one warmed faster and which one cooled faster.
- **results table:** Bowl | At the start | After 15 minutes | After 30 minutes | 15 minutes after moving to the shade
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill one bowl 3 cm deep with soil and one 3 cm deep with water. 2. Leave both in the shade for half an hour. 3. Touch both, or measure both with the thermometer pushed 1 cm in.  
  *Parent says:* Here is our land and our sea. Feel them both. Are they the same now? Which do you think will get warmer in the sun, and why?  
  *Child mode:* This bowl is the land. This one is the sea. Feel both. Which will get warmer in the sun? Why?
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put both bowls side by side in the sun. 2. After 15 minutes, touch or measure both and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Fifteen minutes in the sun. Feel them both now. Which is warmer?  
  *Child mode:* Put both bowls in the sun. Wait 15 minutes. Feel them again. Which is warmer?
  - Note: Push the thermometer bulb 1 cm into the soil or water, and wait one minute before reading it. Shade the thermometer's top part with your hand, so the sun does not heat it directly.
  - Wait: Wait 15 minutes. Do something else meanwhile.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 30 minutes in the sun, measure again. Then move both bowls into the shade and measure again after 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Now the last reading in the sun. Then we'll put them in the shade, like night-time, and see which cools faster.  
  *Child mode:* Feel them after 30 minutes. Now move both to the shade. Wait 15 minutes. Which cooled faster?
  - Note: Usually the soil's surface gets several degrees warmer than the water, and in the shade the soil cools faster too. The water changes more slowly both ways. If the sun went behind a cloud, try again on a clear day.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning, and link it to the sea breeze.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you find? And on a real coast, which way would the breeze blow in the afternoon, and at night?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why. Which way would the afternoon breeze blow?
  - Check: The soil got warmer than the water in the sun. On a real coast in the afternoon, which way will the breeze blow? → **From the sea onto the land**

### a2 · Weigh the Air: the balloon balance (15 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up uses the pin. Keep balloon pieces away from small children, because they can choke on them.
- **record:** Draw the ruler before and after, and write which side went down.
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* Let's build a balance. Slide the thread until the ruler hangs flat. Then both sides weigh the same.  
  *Child mode:* Blow up two balloons. Tape one on each end of the ruler. Hang the ruler by a thread. Make it hang flat.
  - Note: Getting it level takes patience. Move the thread a few millimetres at a time. A little tape on the thread stops it slipping.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what will happen when one balloon loses its air.  
  *Parent says:* If I let the air out of one balloon, will that side go up, go down, or stay level? Why?  
  *Child mode:* One balloon will lose its air. Will that side go up, down, or stay? Why?
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Child mode:* Air has weight. So moving air can push things. Name one thing the wind pushes.

### a5 · Window Watch: what is the wind moving? (10 min)

   **Answer:** From many sailors' reports of the winds, and his own voyage. *Why:* Halley gathered reports from sailors who had crossed many oceans, and added what he had felt himself on a voyage to St Helena. Putting many people's observations together showed the pattern.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is wind?  
   Options: Air moving past something / A gas made by trees / Water vapour / The sound of air  
   **Answer:** Air moving past something. *Why:* Wind is air moving past something. Picking 'a gas made by trees' is an old idea. Trees are moved by the wind.
2. (mcq) Divya pumps up a flat football. Then she weighs it. What has changed?  
   Options: It is lighter / It is a little heavier / It weighs the same / It has no weight  
   **Answer:** It is a little heavier. *Why:* More air has been squeezed into the ball, and air has weight. So the ball is a little heavier. Picking 'lighter' comes from thinking air makes things float.
3. (mcq) When the fridge door opens, where does the cold air go?  
   Options: Up to the ceiling / It stays inside / Out at the bottom and down to the floor / Out at the top only  
   **Answer:** Out at the bottom and down to the floor. *Why:* Cold air is heavier for its size, so it pours out at the bottom and sinks to the floor. Picking 'up to the ceiling' mixes it up with warm air.
4. (mcq) Why does cool air slide under warm air?  
   Options: Cool air is faster / Cool air is denser, so it is heavier for its size / Warm air is sticky / Cool air is wetter  
   **Answer:** Cool air is denser, so it is heavier for its size. *Why:* Cool air is denser, so a litre of it is heavier, and it sinks under the lighter warm air. Picking 'cool air is faster' mixes things up: the particles in cool air move more slowly.
5. (mcq) Lakshmi lives in Puri, by the sea. Which way does the breeze usually blow in the afternoon?  
   Options: From the sea onto the land / From the land out to sea / Straight down / There is no breeze by the sea  
   **Answer:** From the sea onto the land. *Why:* In the afternoon the land is warmer than the sea, so a sea breeze flows in from the sea. Picking 'from the land' is the night-time land breeze.
6. (mcq) Why does the land breeze blow at night?  
   Options: The sea is asleep / The Moon pulls the air / The land cools faster than the sea, so the sea is warmer / Fishers need it  
   **Answer:** The land cools faster than the sea, so the sea is warmer. *Why:* At night the land cools down faster than the sea. Now the sea is warmer, its air rises, and the cooler land air flows out to sea.
7. (mcq) What holds a kite up in the sky?  
   Options: It is lighter than air / Moving air pushes on its tilted face, and the string holds it / The tail lifts it / It floats like a balloon  
   **Answer:** Moving air pushes on its tilted face, and the string holds it. *Why:* Moving air pushes on the kite's tilted face, and the string holds it against the wind, so the push holds it up. Picking 'it is lighter than air' is a common guess, but a kite falls when the wind stops.
8. (mcq) Kiran wants to fly his kite but there is no wind. What should he do?  
   Options: Run with the kite / Add a heavier tail / Wait for night / Put it in the fridge  
   **Answer:** Run with the kite. *Why:* Running pulls the kite through the still air, so air moves past it and holds it up.
9. (mcq) Why do all the blades of a windmill tilt the same way?  
   Options: To look pretty / To catch rain / So the wind pushes every blade round in the same direction / So the blades are lighter  
   **Answer:** So the wind pushes every blade round in the same direction. *Why:* Tilted blades turn the wind's push into a sideways push. When all tilt the same way, every blade is pushed round in the same direction, so the wheel turns.
10. (mcq) What does a wind turbine do?  
   Options: It makes wind / It uses the wind to turn a generator and make electricity / It stores rain / It measures the temperature  
   **Answer:** It uses the wind to turn a generator and make electricity. *Why:* The wind turns its blades, and the blades turn a generator, which makes electricity. Picking 'makes wind' mixes it up with a fan.
11. (mcq) Galileo thought the trade winds were air left behind by the spinning Earth. What did Halley show instead?  
   Options: There are no trade winds / The Moon makes the winds / The winds are blown by the sea / The Sun's heating drives the winds  
   **Answer:** The Sun's heating drives the winds. *Why:* Halley gathered sailors' reports into a map and argued that the Sun's heating drives the winds. Later, Hadley showed that the Earth's spin bends them.
12. (mcq) A kite gets stuck on an electric wire. What should you do?  
   Options: Pull the string hard / Climb up and get it / Leave it and tell a grown-up / Throw a stick at it  
   **Answer:** Leave it and tell a grown-up. *Why:* Never pull the string or climb to reach it. Electric wires can kill. Leave the kite and tell a grown-up.

## Applications
- Wind turbines make electricity, for example in the wind farms of Tamil Nadu and Gujarat.
- Sailboats and kattumarams travel by the wind.
- Builders place windows on opposite sides of a room so breezes cool it without a fan.
- Weather forecasters predict sea breezes, monsoons and cyclones by studying where the air is warm and cool.
- Farmers winnow grain in a breeze, so the light husk blows away and the heavy grain falls.
- Kite festivals, such as Uttarayan in Gujarat and Sankranti in Hyderabad.

## Open questions
- **How will the Indian monsoon change as the Earth gets warmer?** Computer models do not all agree. Some show more rain in total but more dry breaks and more very heavy days. Scientists are still studying it.
- **Why does the monsoon sometimes stop for weeks in the middle of the season?** These 'breaks' are linked to the movement of rain belts and to the oceans, but scientists cannot yet predict them well.
- **How do birds find thermals they cannot see?** Birds may feel the rising air on their wings, watch other birds, or notice warm ground. How much each one matters is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- A 1 litre bottle of air weighs about 1 gram. A big 20 litre water can full of air holds 20 litres. About how much does that air weigh? **About 20 grams.**. Each litre of air weighs about 1 gram. There are 20 litres. 20 groups of 1 gram is 20 grams. That is about as heavy as four 5-rupee coins. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Windmill That Lifts, the clip took 15 seconds to rise at 50 cm from the fan, and 45 seconds at 150 cm. How many times as long did it take far away? **3 times as long.**. 45 divided by 15 is 3. So the windmill took 3 times as long far from the fan. Check: 15 + 15 + 15 = 45. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In Soil or Water, both bowls started at 30 degrees. After 30 minutes in the sun, the soil was at 42 degrees and the water at 34 degrees. How many degrees did each one warm up? How many times as much did the soil warm? **The soil warmed 12 degrees and the water 4 degrees. The soil warmed 3 times as much.**. 42 minus 30 is 12. 34 minus 30 is 4. 12 divided by 4 is 3. So the soil warmed 3 times as much as the water. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
- Meera kept a Breeze Diary in Kochi for 5 days. The strip streamed away from the sea at 3 pm on 4 of the 5 days. On the fifth day it was cloudy and the strip hung still. If she keeps the diary for 10 sunny days, on about how many will she expect a sea breeze at 3 pm? Why? **On about 10 days, or nearly all of them.**. On every sunny day she recorded, the breeze came. The only day without it was cloudy. So on sunny days she can expect a sea breeze almost every afternoon. The pattern lets her predict. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L3`)
- A wind twice as fast pushes about 4 times as hard. A wind three times as fast pushes about 9 times as hard. Can you spot the pattern? How hard would a wind four times as fast push? **About 16 times as hard.**. 2 times 2 is 4. 3 times 3 is 9. So the pattern is the speed times itself. 4 times 4 is 16. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
