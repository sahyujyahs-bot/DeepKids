# Fair tests, step two: repeat, average and say only what you found
`sci.how-we-know.fair-tests.L4`

**Summary.** A paper spinner dropped from the same hand, five times, takes five slightly different times to land. Nothing was changed, and yet nothing repeated exactly. So when a longer wing seems to make the spinner fall slower, how can anyone tell the wing from the wobble? Your child names the three jobs in a test, tries more than two amounts, repeats each measurement, and works out an average and a range. They test paper spinners and paper bridges, learn what to do with an odd result, and write conclusions that say no more than the results show. They also meet Tu Youyou, who found a medicine by changing one hidden thing in how a plant was prepared.

**Intro.** *Drop a paper spinner and time how long it takes to land. Now drop it again in exactly the same way. The time is different. Do it a third time. Different again. You changed nothing, and the numbers still do not match.* So here is the puzzle. If the same spinner gives different times, how can you tell that a longer wing made it fall slower? Maybe the wing did it, or maybe it was just the wobble. In this unit you learn how scientists deal with the wobble. They name what they change, what they measure and what they keep the same. They repeat each measurement, and they look at the average and at how far the numbers spread. Then they write a conclusion that does not say more than the numbers do.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the three jobs in a test: the thing I change, the thing I measure, and the things I keep the same.
1. I can try more than two amounts of the thing I change, so I can see a pattern.
2. I can explain why measuring the same thing again gives slightly different numbers, and repeat a measurement several times.
3. I can work out an average and a range, and say what each one tells me.
4. I can spot an odd result, check it, and decide what to do with it without hiding it.
5. I can show results in a table, a bar chart or a line graph, and say which suits the question.
6. I can write a conclusion with a claim, evidence and reasoning that says no more than the results show.
7. I can tell a pattern from a cause, and say how hoping for a result can bend a test.

## Terms
- **Variable**: Anything in a test that can change, such as the length of a wing, the height of a drop or the time of day. *e.g.* In a spinner test, the wing length, the paper, the clip and the drop height are all variables.
- **The thing you change**: The one variable you choose to change on purpose, to see what it does. Scientists call it the independent variable. *e.g.* In the spinner test, the thing you change is the length of the wings.
- **The thing you measure**: The result you record, with a number and a unit. Scientists call it the dependent variable, because it depends on what you changed. *e.g.* In the spinner test, you measure the time in seconds that each spinner takes to fall.
- **The things you keep the same**: All the other variables, which you hold steady so they cannot change the result. Scientists call them control variables. *e.g.* Same paper, same clip, same drop height, same person dropping, and the fan switched off.
- **Measurement**: A number and a unit that tells how much of something there is. *e.g.* The spinner took 1.5 seconds to fall. The 1.5 is the number and seconds is the unit.
- **Trial**: One go at the test. *e.g.* Dropping a spinner once and timing it is one trial.
- **Repeat**: To do the test again in the same way, to see how much the results change. *e.g.* Drop each spinner five times, not once.
- **Average**: The typical size of a set of numbers. To find the average, add the numbers and divide by how many there are. Scientists also call it the mean. *e.g.* The times 14, 16, 15, 13 and 17 tenths of a second add up to 75. Divide by 5 to get an average of 15 tenths.
- **Range**: How far a set of numbers spreads, from the smallest to the biggest. To find it, take the smallest from the biggest. *e.g.* For the times 13 to 17 tenths, the range is 17 take away 13, which is 4 tenths.
- **Odd result**: A result that is far from all the others. Scientists also call it an outlier. *e.g.* Four drops took about 15 tenths and one took 28 tenths. The 28 is an odd result.
- **Reliable**: A test is reliable if you get about the same results each time you repeat it. *e.g.* If five drops of a spinner all take close to 15 tenths, the test is reliable.
- **Valid test**: A test is valid if it really measures what your question asks about, and not something else. *e.g.* Timing how long a spinner takes to fall measures the fall, but not how pretty it is, so it would not answer a question about beauty.
- **Conclusion**: What you decide from your results, written so that it says no more than the results show. *e.g.* In our drops, the spinner with the longer wings fell more slowly on average.
- **Claim, evidence, reasoning**: A claim is what you say is true. Evidence is the numbers or things you saw that support it. Reasoning is why the evidence supports the claim. *e.g.* Claim: longer wings fall slower. Evidence: average 21 tenths against 12. Reasoning: bigger wings catch more air.
- **Line graph**: A graph that joins points to show how a measurement changes as the thing you change gets bigger or smaller. *e.g.* A line graph of wing length and average fall time shows the time going up as the wings get longer.
- **Chance**: The part of any result that comes from luck and small changes you cannot control. *e.g.* A coin tossed ten times might give 4 heads one time and 7 heads another time, by chance.
- **Cause**: A cause is something that makes something else happen. A fair test can show a cause. A pattern alone cannot. *e.g.* Longer wings catch more air, which makes the spinner fall more slowly. This is a cause.
- **Bias**: A leaning that pushes your results one way, such as hoping a certain answer is true. *e.g.* A person who hopes the long wings are slowest may stop the stopwatch a little late on those drops.
- **Blind test**: A test in which the person measuring does not know which thing they are measuring, so hope cannot bend the result. *e.g.* A helper times the drops without being told which wing length each spinner has.
- **Air resistance**: The push of air against something moving through it. Bigger flat surfaces get more air resistance. *e.g.* A spinner with big wings feels more air resistance, so it falls more slowly.

## Explanations
### The three jobs in every test · *defined*
**Definition:** Every fair test has three jobs. There is one thing you change, one thing you measure, and a list of things you keep the same.

Say you want to know whether a longer wing makes a paper spinner fall more slowly. The thing you change is the wing length. The thing you measure is the time it takes to fall, in seconds. The things you keep the same are the paper, the clip, the height you drop from, the person who drops it and the stopwatch person. Scientists call these the independent variable, the dependent variable and the control variables. You do not need the long names, but you do need the three jobs. A test with two things changing is a test in which you cannot tell which one made the difference. A test with nothing measured gives no results. A test with a loose list of things to keep the same lets hidden differences slip in.
- e.g. Question: does more salt in water make a potato float? Change: the amount of salt. Measure: whether the potato floats. Same: the potato piece, the glass, the amount of water.
- e.g. Question: does a longer rubber band shoot a paper ball farther? Change: the length of the band. Measure: the distance. Same: the ball, the pull-back and the person.
- e.g. Question: does the amount of water change how many seeds sprout? Change: the amount of water. Measure: the number of seeds that sprout. Same: the kind of seed, the plate, the warmth.
### Try more than two amounts · *defined*
**Definition:** When you test an amount, such as a length or a temperature, try at least three settings. Three show you the shape of the pattern. Two cannot.

Suppose you test two wing lengths and the longer one falls slower. That is something. But you do not know whether the time rises steadily, or jumps and then stays flat. With three or four wing lengths you can see a shape. Does the time rise in even steps? Does it rise and then level off? Does it do something surprising in the middle? Three settings also give you a check on yourself. If the short and long wings differ but the middle one falls fastest of all, something odd is happening and you should look closer. Try to choose settings that are spread out evenly, such as 4, 6 and 8 centimetres.
- e.g. Test wing lengths of 4, 6 and 8 centimetres, not only 4 and 8.
- e.g. Test 0, 1 and 3 teaspoons of water on seeds, not only none and plenty.
- e.g. Test paper bridges with 0, 2 and 4 pleats to see how the strength grows.
### Why the same test gives different numbers · *defined*
**Definition:** Even a careful test gives slightly different numbers each time. The wobble comes from small changes you cannot control, and from small slips in measuring.

A spinner dropped five times will never fall in exactly the same way. A draught moves the air a little. The hand lets go a little differently. The spinner starts a bit tilted. And the person with the stopwatch presses the button a fraction early or late. Human reaction takes about a fifth to a quarter of a second. These small changes add up to a wobble in the numbers. The wobble is not a mistake. It is part of any real measurement. Because of it, one number from one try is never the whole story. We need several tries to see what is steady and what is wobble.
- e.g. Dropping the same spinner five times gives times such as 1.4, 1.6, 1.5, 1.3 and 1.7 seconds.
- e.g. Two people measure the same table and get 120 and 122 centimetres.
- e.g. A cricketer bowling the same ball ten times will not land it on exactly the same spot.
### Repeating, and the average · *defined*
**Definition:** Repeat each setting several times, add up the results and divide by the number of tries. This gives the average, which is steadier than any single try.

One try can be unusually high or unusually low. When you repeat the test, the highs and lows pull against each other. The average sits in the middle. To find it, add the numbers and divide by how many there are. If the spinner took 14, 16, 15, 13 and 17 tenths of a second, the sum is 75. Divide by 5. The average is 15 tenths, or 1.5 seconds. Averages are also useful for comparing: if one spinner averages 1.5 seconds and another averages 1.9 seconds, we can compare them more fairly than by looking at one drop each. But an average alone hides how much the numbers spread, so we also look at the range.
- e.g. Five drops: 14, 16, 15, 13, 17 tenths. Total 75, average 15.
- e.g. Three bridges held 8, 10 and 9 coins. Total 27, average 9.
- e.g. Rainfall in a week, measured daily, is averaged to show a typical day.
### The range: how far the numbers spread · *defined*
**Definition:** The range is the biggest number minus the smallest. It shows how much the numbers wobble.

Two sets of numbers can have the same average and be very different. One set could be 15, 15, 15, 15, 15. Another could be 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. Both average 15. The first is steady and the second is all over the place. The range shows the difference: 0 and 20. When we compare two spinners, the range tells us how much to trust the difference between their averages. If one averages 15 and another 19, and each has a range of 4, the difference of 4 is about the same as the wobble. We would want more tries. If their ranges are 1, the difference of 4 is clear.
- e.g. Times 14, 16, 15, 13, 17 tenths have a range of 4 tenths.
- e.g. Coins held: 8, 10, 9. The range is 2.
- e.g. Two bowlers with the same average speed can have very different ranges.
### The odd result: check, do not hide · *defined*
**Definition:** An odd result is far from all the others. Check how the test was done and repeat it. Do not rub it out, and do not use it without thinking.

In five drops of a spinner, four take about 15 tenths and one takes 28 tenths. What happened? Perhaps the spinner clipped the wall. Perhaps the timer stopped late. Perhaps a draught caught it. First, ask whether something went wrong that you can name. If so, write that down, and drop it again. If you cannot find a reason, keep the result, and do more tries. A result that appears again is real. A result that appears once may be a fluke. Never delete an odd result in secret. Many discoveries began as a strange number that someone kept and looked at again.
- e.g. Four drops take about 15 tenths and one takes 28. Check for a bump and drop again.
- e.g. One bridge holds 31 coins when the others hold about 11. Check if that bridge was folded differently.
- e.g. One of ten tomato plants grows twice as tall as the others. Look at the pot, the soil and the light before deciding.
### How many repeats are enough? · *debated*
Teachers often say three repeats, and five is better. But the right number depends on how big the wobble is compared with the difference you are looking for. If the wobble is small, three tries may do. If the wobble is large, you need many more. Nobody has a number that is right for every test. Scientists decide before they start, and they say how many they used.
- e.g. Three drops of a spinner are quick, but five show the wobble better.
- e.g. Weighing a coin ten times on a kitchen scale shows the scale's wobble clearly.
- e.g. Medicine trials use thousands of people, because the differences are small compared with how people vary.
- **Many school teachers:** Three repeats are a simple rule that children can manage, and it is enough to show whether a result is steady or not. Doing more may take too long.
- **Many statisticians:** No single number is right. How many repeats you need depends on how big the wobble is and how small a difference matters. A test with a large wobble needs many tries to be trusted.
### Hoping can bend a test · *defined*
**Definition:** When we hope for a particular result, small choices in measuring can lean towards it without us noticing. A blind test, in which the measurer does not know which thing is which, helps.

Suppose you hope the long-winged spinner is slowest. You may release it a little more gently, and you may stop the timer a little later. You are not cheating on purpose. This is how our minds work. To protect a test from this, scientists often use a blind test. A helper times the drops without knowing which wing length each spinner has. Or the spinners are labelled with secret letters. Another habit is to write down what you expect before the test, so that you can see afterwards whether your hopes and your result matched.
- e.g. A helper times the drops while the spinners are hidden in a bag and labelled with letters.
- e.g. Drug trials often keep doctors and patients from knowing who has the real medicine.
- e.g. A judge in a talent show who hears singers from behind a curtain cannot be pushed by how they look.
### Tables, bar charts and line graphs · *defined*
**Definition:** A table keeps every number in place. A bar chart compares separate things. A line graph shows how a result changes as an amount changes.

Start every test with a table, ready before you begin. Put the thing you change in the first column, and your trials and averages in the next columns. Use a bar chart when the things you compare are separate, such as four different bridge shapes. Use a line graph when the thing you change is an amount that can sit anywhere along a line, such as wing length or amount of water. A line graph shows the shape, such as a steady rise, a levelling off or a dip. Label both sides with what they show and the units, and give the chart a title. Plot averages, not single tries, and write the range next to each point if you can.
- e.g. A bar chart for four bridge shapes: flat, channel, pleated and tube.
- e.g. A line graph for wing lengths 4, 6 and 8 centimetres against average fall time.
- e.g. A table with a column for each trial and extra columns for the average and range.
### Writing a conclusion that says no more than you found · *defined*
**Definition:** A good conclusion has a claim, evidence and reasoning. It says what you found in your test, with its limits, and does not claim more.

A claim answers your question. Evidence gives the numbers that support it. Reasoning explains why the numbers support the claim. A good conclusion also has limits. It says what you tested and does not go beyond. If you tested three wing lengths, you can say what happened for those three. You cannot say what happens for a wing of 20 centimetres. If you measured on one day in one room, say that. Use careful words such as 'in our test', 'on average' and 'the evidence suggests'. If your results are not clear, say 'we cannot tell yet' and say what you would do next.
- e.g. Claim: longer wings fell more slowly. Evidence: the average time rose from 12 to 16 to 21 tenths. Reasoning: bigger wings catch more air. Limit: we tested only 4, 6 and 8 centimetres.
- e.g. Claim: in our two weeks, we could not tell whether tulsi changes the mosquito count. Evidence: a difference of about one a night, smaller than the swings.
- e.g. Claim: the pleated bridge held the most. Evidence: an average of 24 coins against 6 for the flat one.
### Pattern, chance and cause · *defined*
**Definition:** A difference between two sets of results may come from a real cause, from chance, or from a hidden difference in the test. Repeating and comparing the wobble helps tell which.

When two things come out differently, there are three possible reasons. First, the thing you changed really did it. Second, chance made a difference, as when a coin gives 7 heads one time and 4 the next. Third, something else was different that you did not notice. A fair test removes the third. Repeating and looking at the range deals with the second. If the difference between two averages is bigger than the wobble in each, chance is an unlikely reason. If the difference is smaller than the wobble, you cannot say. Finally, a pattern in a table does not by itself say that one thing makes the other. A fair test, or other careful evidence, is needed for that.
- e.g. Two identical plates of seeds gave 7 and 9 sprouted seeds. A difference of 2 can come from chance alone.
- e.g. A tulsi corner had about one mosquito fewer a night, but nights varied from 2 to 9. The difference is smaller than the wobble.
- e.g. Children with big shoes read better, but age lies behind both.
### Why a longer wing slows a spinner · *defined*
**Definition:** A spinner falls because gravity pulls it down. The air pushes back against its wings. Bigger wings meet more air, so the push is bigger and the spinner falls more slowly.

When the spinner falls, its wings spin like the blades of a helicopter. The spinning wings sweep through the air. The air pushes up against them, and this push is called air resistance. A bigger wing has more surface to push against, so the push is stronger. The spinner speeds up until the push of the air equals its weight. After that it falls at a steady speed. Bigger wings reach that balance at a slower speed. The paper and the clip weigh the same whatever the wing length, so only the wings differ. This is why seeds of maple trees, which have long wings, spin slowly to the ground and can travel a long way on the wind.
- e.g. A flat sheet of paper falls more slowly than the same sheet crumpled into a ball.
- e.g. A parachute is a very big wing for its weight, so it falls slowly.
- e.g. Maple seeds spin down on a single wing and drift far from the tree.
### Why a fold makes paper strong · *defined*
**Definition:** A flat strip of paper bends easily. A fold, a pleat or a tube puts the paper further from the middle line of the bend, and that makes it much stiffer.

Try bending a flat strip of paper across a gap. It sags at once. Fold two edges up to make a channel, and it holds far more. When a strip bends, the top side is squeezed and the bottom side is stretched. The paper in the middle does very little. A fold puts more paper at the top and the bottom, away from the middle, where it can do the most work. A tube or a pleated sheet does this very well. The same idea explains why steel girders are shaped like the letter I, and why bamboo is hollow. Engineers say that the shape matters as much as the material.
- e.g. A rolled newspaper can hold a heavy book, though a flat sheet cannot.
- e.g. Corrugated cardboard has pleats inside, which make it stiff for its weight.
- e.g. Bamboo and many plant stems are hollow tubes, which are stiff for their weight.

## Mostly agreed
- Repeating a measurement gives slightly different numbers each time, and this wobble is a normal part of measuring. *How we know:* Anyone who times the same spinner five times, or weighs the same coin ten times, sees the numbers differ a little.
- The average of several tries is steadier than any one try, and the range shows how far the numbers spread. *How we know:* Averages of repeated tries vary much less than single tries. This has been shown in countless measurements, from school timings to physics experiments.
- When you test an amount, trying at least three settings shows the shape of the pattern better than two. *How we know:* Two points can be joined by a straight line however the real result behaves. A third point can show a bend or a levelling off.
- An odd result should be checked and repeated, and recorded, and never quietly deleted. *How we know:* Scientific societies treat hiding results as a serious fault. Some important discoveries began as an odd result that someone kept.
- A conclusion should say no more than the results show. *How we know:* Scientists who study how well results can be repeated have found that results stated too strongly often do not hold up in later tests.
- A difference between two averages is only worth trusting if it is bigger than the wobble within each set of results. *How we know:* This is the basic idea behind the statistical tests that scientists use to tell a real difference from chance.

## How we found out
**Tu Youyou, a Chinese pharmacologist, and her research team, 1969 to 1971, and the Nobel Prize in 2015.** In 1969, Tu Youyou, a Chinese pharmacologist, joined a secret national project to find a medicine for malaria. Her team tested hundreds of plant extracts on infected mice. Sweet wormwood, an old Chinese herb, looked promising at first, but the results were uneven. People usually prepared herbal medicines by boiling or heating. Tu went back to an old handbook written by Ge Hong more than 1,600 years ago. It said to soak the herb in cold water and wring out the juice. She guessed that heat might be destroying the part that works. So she changed one thing, the heat of the extraction, and kept the plant the same. In 1971 a low-heat extract worked very well on infected animals. Tu and colleagues reportedly tried it on themselves first, to check it was safe. The medicine, artemisinin, now treats malaria around the world. Tu shared the Nobel Prize in 2015.  
*What people believed before:* Herbal medicines were usually prepared by heating or boiling, so heated extracts of sweet wormwood gave uneven results and some people thought the herb was not useful. Tu's team found that the heat was spoiling the active part, and a gentler extraction worked.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If I get the same answer once, I know it is right.** (Many children and adults.; *rejected*)  
  For: A clear result feels convincing, and some differences are so large that one try shows them.  
  Against: One try can be a fluke, or a slip of the stopwatch. Repeating shows whether the result stays the same. A result that comes only once cannot be trusted much.
- **Averages hide the truth, so scientists should only look at single results.** (Some people who distrust numbers.; *rejected*)  
  For: An average does hide how much the results spread.  
  Against: That is why scientists report the range as well. A set of numbers described by both the average and the range is more honest than one lucky try.
- **An odd result is a mistake, so delete it.** (Many students.; *rejected*)  
  For: Sometimes an odd result is a slip, such as a bump or a late stopwatch.  
  Against: Only checking can show whether it is a slip. If you cannot find a reason, it might be real. Deleting an odd result in secret hides what could be the most interesting part of the test.
- **Three repeats are always enough.** (A common school rule.; *debated*)  
  For: Three is quick, and it shows a rough spread.  
  Against: When the wobble is large compared with the difference you are testing, three repeats are not enough. Scientists decide on the number of repeats according to the size of the wobble.
- **If I keep everything the same except one thing, my test is perfect.** (Many people who learn about fair tests.; *debated*)  
  For: Keeping everything else the same is the heart of a fair test.  
  Against: No test keeps everything the same. Two paper strips are never exactly alike, and the air always moves a little. A good test keeps the important things the same, repeats the test, and says what was not controlled.
- **A good scientist has no hopes and no guesses.** (A popular idea about scientists.; *rejected*)  
  For: Hopes can bend the results, so it seems best to have none.  
  Against: Scientists do have hopes and guesses, and they cannot help it. What matters is how they protect the test from them, by writing predictions first, using blind tests and sharing their numbers for others to check.
- **Real science only counts when a number comes out.** (Some people who like measuring.; *rejected*)  
  For: Numbers make comparing easy and clear.  
  Against: Many careful tests produce words, drawings or counts, such as which seeds sprouted. What matters is that the observations are made carefully and recorded honestly.

## Roots
We have dropped spinners again and again, found averages and ranges, and learned to say no more than the numbers say. Now we ask what the wobble in every measurement tells us about the world and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Drop the same spinner from the same hand five times and you get five different times. Nothing was changed, and still nothing repeated exactly. A measurement is not one exact number. It is a small spread of numbers, and the honest answer is the whole spread.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a measured number is the true size of the thing, like a label stuck on it. But the number changes each time we measure, so what we really have is a spread.  
*Evidence:* In the Paper Spinner test, the same spinner gave different times on every drop. The coin tosses gave different counts of heads in every set of ten. Only the average and the range described what was steady and what wobbled.

- *who am i:* Count your breaths for one minute, three times, and you will get three different numbers. You are not one number. You are a spread, and so is everything about you.
- *what is life:* No two leaves, seeds or children are exactly alike. Living beings vary from one to the next, and that variety lets a kind of living being survive when the world changes.
- *what is this world:* Air moves, hands shake and tiny causes pile up. Exact sameness is rare in the world, so careful people measure many times.

Think of the five falls of the spinner. Each fall was the paper, the air in the room, your hand and your eye meeting for a moment. No two meetings were quite the same, because the room, the hand and the eye are always moving a little. One number from one meeting hides that. Many numbers show it. The world is not a row of identical copies. It is a crowd of things that are similar and never the same. The pattern we find is not one point. It is the shape of the crowd.

- **Udana 6.4 (Tittha Sutta):** A group of blind men each touched a different part of an elephant. Each described the elephant as something different, such as a pillar, a rope or a fan. Each was sure he knew the elephant, and they began to quarrel. (Each drop of the spinner is like one touch. It gives a true part of the picture, and no more. Many drops together give the shape of the whole.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.48:** The Gita teaches evenness of mind in success and in failure, and calls this evenness yoga. (A scientist who hopes for one answer can lean towards it. Staying even about whether the result is the hoped-for one helps the test stay honest.)
- **Analects of Confucius 2.17:** Confucius told his student that knowing means this: when you know something, to hold that you know it, and when you do not know something, to allow that you do not know it. (A conclusion that says only what the results show follows this teaching. 'We cannot tell yet' is also knowledge.)
- Science (settled): Every real measurement has some uncertainty. Scientists report a result together with its spread, and not as a single exact number.
- Science (debated): In quantum physics, some outcomes seem to be random in a deeper way than ordinary wobble, but what that means is still debated.
- Other view, Pierre-Simon Laplace, a French mathematician, in 1814: Laplace imagined a mind that knew every cause in the world at one moment. For such a mind, nothing would be uncertain, and the future would be as clear as the past. On this view the wobble is only our ignorance of small causes. It is not part of how the world is.
- Practice, Three counts of my breath (3 min): Sit still. Count your breaths for twenty seconds, breathing in and out normally. Write down the number. Rest for ten seconds. Count again. Rest. Count a third time. Compare the three numbers. They are probably not the same. Which one is your true number? Breathe in slowly three times.
- Ask: Which of your three breath counts is the real one?
- Ask: If every measurement of you wobbles, what is the 'real' you?
- Ask: Is the wobble in the world, or only in our tools?
- Ask: When did a single number from a single try fool you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Nothing was changed, and yet the same spinner took a different time on each drop. Where did the difference come from? (hint: Think about the air, your hand, the spinner's tilt and the person with the stopwatch.)
- *deep:* Is it possible to do exactly the same thing twice? (hint: Think about the position of every particle of air in the room. Could it be the same twice?)
- *deep:* If the wobble in the numbers is as big as the difference you are testing, what should you do? (hint: Think about more repeats, a bigger change, or a better way of measuring.)
- *big:* A medicine test can be repeated with thousands of people. Why is that sometimes worth the cost? (hint: Think about how different people are, and how small the effect of a medicine can be.)
- *big:* You hope your idea is right. How can you test it so that your hope does not bend the result? (hint: Think about writing your prediction first, a helper who does not know which is which, and sharing your numbers.)
- *big:* If a scientist deletes an odd result in secret, who is harmed? (hint: Think about the people who read the study, and the people who might have made a discovery from that number.)
- *deep:* Tu Youyou found that one hidden thing, how hot the plant was heated, decided whether the medicine worked. What hidden things might be changing your results? (hint: Think about the room, the person, the time of day and the paper.)
- *big:* When you cannot repeat something, such as an eclipse or a monsoon, how can you still be careful? (hint: Think about comparing many different events and recording exactly what was seen.)
- *deep:* Who decides what counts as a difference that matters? (hint: Think about a difference of half a second in a sprint and in a snail race.)

## Story: The Mosquitoes and the Tulsi
*A made-up story. The science in it is real: one count is one throw of the dice, and a difference needs to be bigger than the night-to-night wobble before we trust it. The flat, the counts and the tulsi test are imagined, and the story does not say whether tulsi keeps mosquitoes away.*

Aadhira lived in a flat in Kozhikode with her mother, her father and her Appooppan, her grandfather. Appooppan loved plants more than anything. On the balcony he kept a big pot of tulsi, with soft green leaves that smelled sharp and sweet.

"A tulsi near the window keeps the mosquitoes out," Appooppan always said. "In all my years, I have never been wrong about this."

Aadhira loved her grandfather, and she loved asking questions. One evening, while the mosquitoes whined around the lamp, she asked, "Appooppan, how do you know?"

He laughed. "Because I have always known."

"That is not the same," said Aadhira, and she went to find her notebook.

The balcony had two corners. Aadhira stuck a white card on the wall of each corner, at the height of her shoulder. The tulsi pot stood in the left corner. The right corner had nothing. "Tonight," she said, "I will count the mosquitoes that settle on each card in the ten minutes after the lights come on. I will keep everything else the same."

Appooppan crossed his arms. "You will see."

The first evening, Aadhira held the timer and counted. The tulsi corner had 2 mosquitoes. The empty corner had 9.

"See!" cried Appooppan, thumping the arm of his chair. "Two and nine! What did I tell you?"

Aadhira looked at the numbers. She felt a small pleasure too, because she liked a clear answer. But she also remembered something from school. She took a breath. "That is one evening, Appooppan. It is like one throw of the dice. Let us see what the next evening says."

On the second evening the tulsi corner had 7 and the empty corner had 5. On the third it was 6 and 8.

Appooppan stopped thumping his chair. Aadhira frowned at her notebook. "I changed nothing," she said, "and the numbers jump around. Two, then seven, then six."

"The mosquitoes are cheating," said Appooppan.

"No," said Aadhira. "They are just not the same every evening. Nothing is. So one evening cannot tell us the answer. We need many."

Then she noticed another problem. The right corner was next to the door, where the whole family walked in and out. "Perhaps the empty corner gets more mosquitoes because of the door, and not because of the tulsi. I changed two things at once. The pot, and the place."

Appooppan stared at her. He had never heard a nine-year-old talk like that. "So what do we do?"

"We swap," said Aadhira. "Every second evening, you carry the tulsi to the other corner. Then each corner has the tulsi half the time. Whatever the door does, it does to both."

"I carry the pot?" said Appooppan, but he was already getting up.

For fourteen evenings they counted, and Appooppan carried the pot. Some evenings the tulsi corner had more. Some evenings it had fewer. The counts ran from 2 up to 9, and no pattern was clear. By the end Appooppan was counting too, whispering the numbers.

On the fifteenth morning, Aadhira added up. She wrote the totals at the bottom of the page. "With the tulsi, 71 mosquitoes in fourteen evenings. Without the tulsi, 84. The difference is 13."

"Thirteen!" said Appooppan. "That is something."

"It is about one mosquito a night," said Aadhira. "But look at the numbers. One evening it was 2 and the next it was 9. The nightly wobble is much bigger than that one mosquito. So I cannot be sure that the tulsi did anything."

Appooppan said nothing for a while. Then he said, very slowly, "So you are telling me that I might be right, and that you cannot tell."

"Yes," said Aadhira. "That is what the numbers say. Not more."

She wrote the conclusion in her notebook. In our balcony, over fourteen evenings, the tulsi corner had slightly fewer mosquitoes on average, about one a night. But the numbers swung much more than that, so we cannot say the tulsi keeps them away. We tested one pot of one size. We did not test a bigger plant.

Appooppan read it twice. "Hm," he said. "A bigger plant. Perhaps I should buy a bigger plant."

"Perhaps," said Aadhira, and she turned to a fresh page. "Then we will count again."

That evening, Appooppan took the timer himself. Aadhira noticed that he wrote the numbers down in the notebook, very neatly, in his old-fashioned handwriting. Under the numbers he wrote one new line. Tonight, 4 and 6. Who knows?

**For the parent:** One result is one throw of the dice. The same test gives different numbers each time, so only many tries show whether a difference is real or just wobble.

- Ask: Why did Aadhira not agree with her grandfather after the first evening?
- Ask: What two things did Aadhira change at once at the start? How did she fix it?
- Ask: Why did Aadhira say the difference of about one mosquito a night was not enough to be sure?
- Ask: What could Aadhira and her grandfather do next to learn more?
- Ask: Why did Aadhira write 'we did not test a bigger plant' in her conclusion?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Paper Spinner: does a longer wing fall slower? (40 min)
- **question:** Does a paper spinner with longer wings fall more slowly? How much do the times wobble when you drop the same spinner again and again?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which spinner will fall slowest: short wings, middle wings or long wings? How much will the five times for the same spinner differ from each other? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** The thing we change is the length of the wings: 4, 6 and 8 centimetres. The thing we measure is the time to fall from the same height, in seconds. We keep the same: the paper, the size of the strip, the paper clip, the person who drops, the person who times, the height and the room with the fan switched off. We drop the spinners in turns, short, middle, long, short, middle, long, so tiredness and draughts affect all three. We repeat each spinner five times.
- **safety:** Use scissors with a grown-up near. Keep the fan off. Stand on the floor, not on a chair, to drop the spinners. Paper clips are small parts: keep them away from children under five.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Work out the average and range for each wing length. Draw a line graph of average time against wing length.
- **results table:** Wing length (cm) | Drop 1 (s) | Drop 2 (s) | Drop 3 (s) | Drop 4 (s) | Drop 5 (s) | Average (s) | Range (s)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the strips. Write three headings: Change, Measure, Keep the same. Ask your child to fill them in. Then ask for a guess and a reason. Also ask how much the five times for one spinner will differ.  
  *Parent says:* We will test spinners with different wing lengths. What do we change? What do we measure? What do we keep the same? Which spinner will fall slowest? How much will five times for the same spinner differ?  
  *Child mode:* Fill in: change, measure, keep the same. Which spinner will fall slowest? Why?
  - Ask: Which spinner will fall slowest, and why? (good: The one with the longest wings, because the air pushes them more.; The short wings, because they are lighter.; The middle, because it is just right.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write both guesses at the top. The second guess, about how much the times will differ, is new. Let your child say a number, such as a quarter of a second. Roles: with one child, the child times and writes while you drop. With 2 to 4 children, one times, one writes in the table and one watches the spinner land and calls 'bumped' if it hits something. The dropper and the timer stay the same for all 15 drops, so the others can swap jobs only between spinners. Two children can also make their own set of three spinners and run them as a second set of drops, which gives you ten drops for each wing length.
  - Check: In this test, which is the thing we change? → **The length of the wings**
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut three strips of paper 4 centimetres wide and 16 centimetres long. 2. On each strip, draw a line down the middle from one short end. Make the lines 4, 6 and 8 centimetres long. 3. Cut along the lines. 4. Fold the two flaps, which are the wings, in opposite directions. 5. Fold the bottom 1 centimetre of the stem up. 6. Clip a paper clip over the fold. 7. Write 4, 6 or 8 on each stem.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a line down the middle from the top, and cut it. That makes two wings. Fold one wing forward and one wing back. Fold the bottom up and clip it. Write the wing length on the stem.  
  *Child mode:* Cut three strips. Cut the wings 4, 6 and 8 centimetres long. Fold the wings. Clip the bottom.
  - Note: The wings are each 2 centimetres wide. The stem gets shorter as the wings get longer, but the strip and the clip weigh the same for all three. If a spinner flutters or tumbles instead of spinning, bend the wings a little more apart and try again.
  - Check: Why must the three strips and the three clips be the same? → **So only the wing length is different**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test each spinner once. Hold it at the sticky-note mark with the wings at the top and the clip at the bottom. Let go. Check that it spins and that all three spin. Mark the drop height with a sticky note on the wall.  
  *Parent says:* This is a practice drop. Let go, and watch the wings spin. Does each spinner spin as it falls? If one does not, we can bend its wings and try again.  
  *Child mode:* Practise one drop with each spinner. Do they all spin? Do not time yet.
  - Note: Mark the height on the wall with a sticky note at about the top of a grown-up's reach. The same grown-up drops every time, from the same mark, with the wings level and the arm stretched. Close the windows and switch off the fan. A spinner that will not spin is a real result. Write it down and try bending the wings a little.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The helper drops the spinners in turns: 4, 6, 8, 4, 6, 8, until each has been dropped five times. 2. The timer starts the stopwatch when the spinner leaves the hand and stops it when it touches the floor. 3. Write each time in the table to the nearest tenth of a second. 4. Do not rub out any time.  
  *Parent says:* Short, middle, long. Short, middle, long. Count 'ready, go' and start the timer when the spinner leaves the hand. Stop it when it lands. Write the time before the next drop.  
  *Child mode:* Drop in turns: 4, 6, 8. Time each fall. Write every time down. Do not rub out.
  - Ask: Why do we drop them in turns and not all five of one before the next? (good: So a draught or a tired arm affects all three spinners in the same way.; So no spinner gets an easier time.)
  - Note: Times from about 1 to 2.5 seconds are typical. If your spinners land in under a second, drop from a higher mark (a grown-up with an arm stretched up, standing on the floor), because a short fall makes the stopwatch error a big part of the time. The times for one spinner will differ by a few tenths of a second, which is the wobble. Reaction time at the stopwatch is about a fifth to a quarter of a second. Do not worry about this, and do not rub out a time that looks odd. Circle it instead. If a drop touches the wall or something else, write 'bumped' and drop again.
  - Wait: Take a short break after drop 8 if your arm or eyes get tired.

### e2 · The Paper Bridge: which fold holds the most coins? (37 min)
- **question:** A flat strip of paper sags at once. Which fold makes the strongest bridge: a channel with the edges folded up, a pleated fan, or a rolled tube? How many coins does each hold?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which shape will hold the most coins, and which the fewest? About how many coins will the flat strip hold? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** The thing we change is the shape of the paper: flat, channel, pleated or tube. The thing we measure is the number of coins held before the bridge sags to the table. We keep the same: the paper and the strip size, the gap between the books, the overlap on each stack, the cup that holds the coins, and the way we add the coins, gently and one at a time. We use a fresh strip for each try, because a bent strip is weaker. We test each shape three times, in turns.
- **safety:** Coins are small parts: keep them away from children under five, who might put them in their mouths. Do the test on a table, not on a shelf, so falling coins do not hit feet. Tape and scissors: a grown-up near.

  *Child mode:* Four bridge shapes. Which holds the most? Which the least? Why?
  - Ask: Which bridge do you think will hold the most, and why? (good: The tube, because it is round and thick.; The pleats, because folding makes it thicker.; The channel, because the edges are like walls.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write the guess and the reason at the top. If your child guesses a number for the flat strip, write it. Roles: with one child, you hold the strip steady and the child adds coins and counts. ### c1 · Slowest Fall: engineer the slowest spinner and prove it (40 min)

## Open questions
- **Is the wobble in a measurement only our ignorance of tiny details, or is some of it built into nature?** In everyday life, the wobble comes from small causes we cannot track. In quantum physics, some outcomes seem to be random in a deeper way. Physicists still argue about what that means.
- **How many repeats are enough, in a world where every test is different?** Statisticians have rules for many kinds of test. But no rule fits every case, and scientists still debate how strict they should be.
- **How many published results are wrong, because of chance, because of hopeful measuring, or because odd results were left out?** Scientists who study how well results can be repeated have found that some do not hold up. Nobody knows exactly how often, and the answer differs between fields.
- **Which other old remedies hold a real clue, and how can they be tested fairly?** Tu Youyou's story shows that an old text can hold a real clue, but most old remedies do not work. Sorting the good clues from the rest takes careful, fair tests, and much of that work is still to be done.
- **Can a test be fair if the thing you want to change cannot be changed, such as the weather or a person's age?** Scientists compare many cases or wait for nature to make the change. People still argue about how strong such evidence can be.
- **Why do people trust a single story more than a long run of numbers?** Psychologists have studied this for decades. Stories feel real, and numbers feel abstract, but nobody has a complete answer or a sure cure.

- Spinner A took an average of 14 tenths, with times from 12 to 16. Spinner B took an average of 19 tenths, with times from 17 to 21. Is the gap between the averages bigger than the spread within each set? **Yes. The gap is 5. The spread within each set is 4. So B is probably really slower.**. 19 take away 14 is 5. In A, 16 take away 12 is 4. In B, 21 take away 17 is 4. The gap of 5 is bigger than the spread of 4, which suggests the difference is more than wobble. A gap of 5 against a spread of 4 is only just bigger, so more drops would help. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
- Five bridge tries held 10, 12, 11, 11 and 31 coins. Find the average of all five. Then find the average without the 31. Which would you report, and what would you say? **All five: 15 coins. Without the 31: 11 coins. Report both, and say that the 31 was odd and needed checking.**. 10 plus 12 plus 11 plus 11 plus 31 is 75, and 75 divided by 5 is 15. Without the 31, the sum is 44, and 44 divided by 4 is 11. The 31 pulls the average up by 4. You should check it, repeat it, and say what you did. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- Over 14 evenings, a tulsi corner had 71 mosquitoes in total and the other corner had 84. About how many more per night was that? The nightly counts ran from 2 to 9, so the range was 7. Is the difference per night bigger or smaller than the range? **About 1 more per night. It is much smaller than the range of 7. So the difference is much smaller than the nightly wobble.**. 84 take away 71 is 13. 13 divided by 14 is a little less than 1. The nightly counts spread by 7, so a difference of about 1 is far smaller than the swings. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- Two plates of ten identical seeds with the same water gave 7 and 9 sprouted seeds. What share of each plate sprouted? How many percentage points apart are the two plates? **70 out of 100 and 90 out of 100. They are 20 percentage points apart, and they had identical treatment.**. 7 out of 10 is 70 out of 100, and 9 out of 10 is 90 out of 100. 90 take away 70 is 20. Two plates treated the same already differ by 20 percentage points, so a gap of 20 between two different treatments proves little. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- The averages for spinners with wings of 4, 6 and 8 centimetres were 12, 16 and 21 tenths of a second. Estimate the average for a 5 centimetre wing. Why is this a safer guess than one for a 20 centimetre wing? **About 14 tenths. It is between two tested points, but 20 centimetres is far outside anything tested.**. A 5 centimetre wing sits halfway between 4 and 6. Halfway between 12 and 16 is 14. For 20 centimetres, the pattern might bend or level off, and we have no evidence out there. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- A paper bridge with 0 pleats held 2 coins. With 2 pleats it held 10 coins. With 4 pleats it held 18 coins. If the pattern continued, how many would 6 pleats hold? Would you trust that guess? **26 coins. But I would not trust it without testing, because the pattern may not continue.**. The numbers go up by 8 each time we add 2 pleats. 18 plus 8 is 26. But a strip only has so much width, and more pleats may make the folds too narrow to help. Only a test can show. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A test has 3 settings with 5 drops each. Each drop and reset takes about 20 seconds. About how many minutes will all the drops take? **About 5 minutes.**. 3 times 5 is 15 drops. 15 times 20 seconds is 300 seconds. 300 seconds divided by 60 is 5 minutes. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In five sets of ten coin tosses, the heads were 4, 6, 7, 3 and 5. What is the average number of heads, and what is the range? **The average is 5 heads. The range is 4.**. 4 plus 6 plus 7 plus 3 plus 5 is 25. 25 divided by 5 is 5. The biggest is 7 and the smallest is 3, and 7 take away 3 is 4. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- Your stopwatch time could be wrong by up to 2 tenths of a second on each drop. Two spinners have times of 15 and 18 tenths. Could the real gap be zero? **Yes, it could. The gap of 3 could be reduced by up to 4 tenths, if one time was 2 too low and the other 2 too high.**. The first time could really be 17 and the second 16, which are close. The gap of 3 is smaller than the possible combined error of 4. Averaging several drops reduces the effect. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What are the three jobs in every test? → Change one thing, measure one result, and keep the other things the same.
- Why does the same spinner take a different time each drop? → Small changes in the hand, the air and the stopwatch make a wobble.
- How do you find an average? → Add the numbers and divide by how many there are.
- What is the range? → The biggest number minus the smallest.
- What should you do with an odd result? → Check it, repeat it and record it. Never hide it.
- Why try three amounts and not just two? → Three show the shape of the pattern.
- What is a blind test? → A test in which the person measuring does not know which thing is which.
- Do two things that go together always cause each other? → No. A third thing may cause both, or it may be chance.
- What is a conclusion that does not go too far? → One that says only what the results show, with its limits.
- Why does a fold make paper stronger? → It puts more paper far from the middle of the bend, where it does the most work.
