# Animal life cycles compared: how many young, how much care, how long a life
`sci.animals.animal-life-cycles.L4`

**Summary.** A house mouse lives about two years and an Asian elephant about sixty, yet most mammals get roughly the same number of heartbeats in a whole life. Your child lays out animal lives on a string, plays a dice game of many young against few, and sorts mammals, birds, insects and amphibians by how they begin, grow and have young of their own. They learn why some babies are born ready and some helpless, why many insects change their whole body shape, and how scientists work out the age of a shark or a clam. They also learn why a pattern like 'big animals live long' needs its exceptions checked.

**Intro.** *A grown mayfly may live for only a day. A giant tortoise can live for more than a hundred years. A hen lives for about eight. They all began as an egg. Why do lives that start the same end up so different in length?* Every animal goes round a life cycle, from egg or birth, to growing up, to having young of their own. In this unit you compare the cycles of mammals, birds, insects and amphibians. You lay out how long animals live on a piece of string, you play a dice game to test whether many young or few cared-for young leave more survivors, and you meet animals that live for a day and animals that live for centuries.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can compare how mammals, birds, insects and amphibians begin life, grow up and have young of their own.
1. I can explain why some young are born ready to move and feed, and others are born helpless, and what that means for the parents.
2. I can tell complete metamorphosis from incomplete metamorphosis, and give an animal for each.
3. I can read and compare lifespans, and say how true it is that bigger animals live longer, with exceptions.
4. I can explain the difference between many young with little care and few young with a lot of care, and test the idea with a model.
5. I can say why average and maximum lifespans differ, and why wild and captive animals may differ too.
6. I can use heartbeats to compare lives of animals of different sizes, and say what that pattern does and does not show.

## Terms
- **Lifespan**: How long an animal lives, from birth or hatching until death. *e.g.* A house mouse has a lifespan of about two years. An Asian elephant has one of about sixty years.
- **Gestation**: The time a mammal baby grows inside the mother's body before birth. *e.g.* An Asian elephant carries her calf for about 22 months. A mouse carries her pups for about three weeks.
- **Incubation**: Keeping eggs warm so the young inside can grow until they hatch. *e.g.* A hen sits on her eggs for about 21 days. A sea turtle leaves hers in warm sand for about two months.
- **Litter**: The group of young that a mammal mother gives birth to at the same time. *e.g.* A dog may have a litter of six puppies. A cow usually has one calf.
- **Clutch**: The group of eggs that a bird or reptile lays together in one nest. *e.g.* A sparrow lays a clutch of about four eggs. An olive ridley turtle lays about a hundred.
- **Parental care**: Everything parents do to keep their young alive, such as feeding, warming, guarding and teaching. *e.g.* A sparrow brings insects to her chicks many times a day. A frog leaves her eggs in the water and does nothing more.
- **Ready-born**: Young who can move about and find food soon after birth or hatching. Scientists call this precocial. *e.g.* A calf stands up within an hour. A newly hatched chick walks and pecks the same day.
- **Helpless-born**: Young who cannot see, move or feed themselves at first and depend on their parents. Scientists call this altricial. *e.g.* A newborn kitten is blind and cannot walk. A sparrow chick has no feathers and cannot open her eyes.
- **Mammal**: An animal with hair or fur whose mother feeds the babies milk made in her body. Humans are mammals. *e.g.* Cows, bats, dolphins, mice, elephants and people are all mammals.
- **Marsupial**: A mammal whose baby is born very tiny and finishes growing in the mother's pouch. *e.g.* A kangaroo joey is born after about a month, as small as a jelly bean, and climbs to the pouch.
- **Monotreme**: A mammal that lays eggs. There are only five kinds in the world, the platypus and four echidnas. *e.g.* A platypus lays soft eggs in a burrow and feeds the hatchlings milk.
- **Amphibian**: An animal with soft, wet skin that lays jelly-covered eggs and usually lives part of its life in water and part on land. *e.g.* Frogs, toads, newts and caecilians are amphibians.
- **Larva**: The young stage of an insect or amphibian that looks very different from the adult and has a different way of living. *e.g.* A caterpillar is the larva of a butterfly. A tadpole is the larva of a frog.
- **Nymph**: The young stage of an insect that grows up without a pupa and looks like a small adult without wings. *e.g.* A baby grasshopper and a young cockroach are nymphs. A dragonfly nymph lives in water.
- **Maturity**: The time when an animal is grown enough to have young of their own. *e.g.* A mouse is mature at about two months. An elephant takes more than ten years.
- **Heart rate**: How many times the heart beats in one minute. *e.g.* A resting person's heart beats about 70 times a minute. A mouse's beats about 600 times a minute.

## Explanations
### Four ways life begins · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals begin life in a few main ways. Amphibians lay soft jelly eggs in water. Insects lay eggs on leaves, soil, water or food. Birds lay hard-shelled eggs that parents keep warm. Most mammals grow their young inside the mother's body and feed them milk.

Each way is a different answer to the same problem: how to get a young animal safe, fed and warm until they can live on their own. A frog's jelly eggs need water, so a frog must find a pond. A bird's shell holds a store of food and water, so a bird's egg can be laid on dry land. A mammal keeps her young inside her own body, where it is warm and fed, and then feeds them milk. There are exceptions. A platypus is a mammal that lays eggs. A kangaroo is born tiny and finishes growing in a pouch. A few frogs skip the tadpole and hatch as tiny frogs. The rules are patterns in nature, and nature has exceptions.
- e.g. A frog lays hundreds of jelly eggs in a monsoon puddle.
- e.g. A crow lays 3 to 5 eggs in a nest and both parents feed the chicks.
- e.g. A cow carries her calf for about nine months and then feeds the calf milk.
- e.g. A housefly lays eggs on rotting food, where the maggots will have something to eat.
### Mammals: growing inside, then milk · *defined*
**Definition:** Most mammals carry their young inside the mother's womb. The time spent there is called gestation. After birth, the mother feeds the young on milk that her body makes.

Inside the womb, an organ called the placenta passes food and oxygen from the mother's blood to the baby, and carries waste away. This is why mammals can have few young and still keep them safe. Gestation is longer for bigger animals. A mouse carries her pups for about 20 days, a dog for about 63 days, a human for about nine months and an Asian elephant for about 22 months. After birth, milk is food and also protection, because it carries the mother's germ-fighting substances to the baby. Most mammal mothers also keep their young warm, guard them and teach them. Since 1972, the biologist Cynthia Moss and her team have followed the same elephant families in Amboseli, Kenya, and have recorded every calf born. Records like these are how scientists know how long elephants live. Some mammals, such as humans, elephants and chimpanzees, care for their young for many years.
- e.g. A mouse is pregnant for about three weeks and may have six pups.
- e.g. A human baby grows in the womb for about nine months.
- e.g. An Asian elephant calf is born after about 22 months and drinks milk for two years or more.
- e.g. A bat mother hangs upside down and her single pup clings to her for the first days.
### Birds: a shell, warmth and turns · *defined*
**Definition:** A bird lays hard-shelled eggs, and a parent keeps them warm by sitting on them. This is called incubation. When the chick is ready, she breaks the shell from inside with a small hard tip on her beak, called an egg tooth.

A bird's egg is a complete box of food, water and air, so the chick can grow outside the mother's body. One reason birds lay eggs may be weight, since carrying young inside would make flying harder. The eggs must stay at about 37 degrees Celsius, so a parent sits on them and turns them. In many birds, such as sparrows and pigeons, both parents take turns. In a hen's case, the hen sits on the eggs for 21 days. After hatching, birds go two ways. Chicks of hens, ducks and peafowl are ready-born and feed themselves within hours. Chicks of sparrows, crows, pigeons and parakeets are helpless-born and must be fed by parents for weeks.
- e.g. A hen's eggs hatch after 21 days, and the chicks follow her and peck at food at once.
- e.g. A pigeon's squabs are naked and blind, and are fed 'pigeon milk' by both parents.
- e.g. A house sparrow's eggs hatch after about two weeks, and both parents feed the chicks.
- e.g. A peahen sits on her eggs for about four weeks.
### Insects: two ways to grow up · *defined*
**Definition:** Insects grow up in two ways. In complete metamorphosis there are four stages: egg, larva, pupa and adult. In incomplete metamorphosis there are three: egg, nymph and adult. Metamorphosis means a change of body shape.

A butterfly, a housefly, a honeybee and a beetle go through complete metamorphosis. The larva (caterpillar, maggot, grub) eats and grows. Then it becomes a pupa, and inside the pupa its body is taken apart and built again into an adult. A grasshopper, a cockroach, a dragonfly and a mayfly go through incomplete metamorphosis. The nymph looks like a small adult and grows by shedding its skin, a few times, until it has wings. Why do some insects use a larva at all? One idea is that larva and adult can eat different foods in different places, so they do not compete. A caterpillar eats leaves, and a butterfly drinks nectar. Another is that each stage can specialise: the larva only grows, and the adult only finds a mate and lays eggs. Many adult mayflies do not eat at all.
- e.g. A lime butterfly: egg on a curry leaf, caterpillar, chrysalis, butterfly.
- e.g. A housefly: egg, maggot, pupa, fly. About ten days in warm weather.
- e.g. A grasshopper: egg, wingless nymph that sheds its skin several times, adult.
- e.g. A dragonfly: egg in water, nymph that hunts underwater for a year or more, adult.
- **One idea:** A larva and an adult eat different food, so the young do not take food from their own parents.
- **Another idea:** Each stage does one job well. The larva grows fast. The adult flies to find a mate and a new place to lay eggs.
### Amphibians: a life in two places · *defined*
**Definition:** Amphibians have thin, wet skin, and most lay jelly-covered eggs in water. The young are usually larvae that live in water and breathe with gills. The adults have lungs and live partly on land.

A frog's skin lets water and air pass through, so most amphibians must stay near water to stay moist. A frog's egg has no shell and would dry out on land. Most frogs lay hundreds or thousands of eggs and then leave. Most of those eggs and tadpoles are eaten. A few kinds break the pattern. Some bush frogs of the Western Ghats lay eggs on land and tiny frogs hatch with no tadpole stage. The Indian purple frog lives underground for most of the year and comes up for a few days in the monsoon to breed. Its tadpoles cling to rocks in fast streams with a sucker-like mouth. Caecilians are legless amphibians that look like worms, and some of them guard their eggs.
- e.g. A common frog lays hundreds of eggs in a monsoon puddle, and tadpoles hatch within days.
- e.g. A bush frog of the Western Ghats lays eggs on a leaf and tiny frogs hatch directly.
- e.g. The Indian purple frog (described by science in 2003 by S. D. Biju and Franky Bossuyt) spends most of the year underground.
- e.g. A newt lays eggs one by one on water plants.
### Born ready or born helpless · *defined*
**Definition:** Some young are ready-born and can move and feed soon after birth. Others are helpless-born and need their parents for food and warmth at first.

A calf, a hen's chick and a baby tortoise are ready-born. They were already well developed when they were born or hatched, and a ready-born egg usually has a big yolk. A kitten, a sparrow chick, a mouse pup and a human baby are helpless-born. They are born at an earlier stage and finish growing outside, with their parents' help. The two ways are different bets. A ready-born baby can run from a danger on day one, but the mother must make a bigger baby. A helpless-born baby is small and cheap to carry, but needs weeks of care. A human baby is born more helpless than the baby of any other ape, and the human brain finishes much of its growth after birth.
- e.g. A calf stands and walks within an hour of being born.
- e.g. A newly hatched sea turtle crawls to the sea on her own.
- e.g. A kitten's eyes open after about a week. A puppy's after about two weeks.
- e.g. A sparrow chick is naked and blind and needs about two weeks of feeding before it can leave the nest.
### Many young or few: two ways to bet · *debated*
A frog lays hundreds or thousands of eggs and gives no care. An elephant has one calf every few years and gives years of care. Both ways can keep a species going. The key number is how many young survive to have young of their own. In a population that is not growing, an average mother leaves about two grown-up offspring, whether she began with a thousand eggs or a single calf. Animals that lay many eggs lose most of them to hunger and predators. Animals that have few young protect each one. Biologists have argued for decades about how well this simple idea fits real animals.
- e.g. A frog lays hundreds of eggs and most are eaten.
- e.g. An ocean sunfish may lay hundreds of millions of eggs, and almost all of the young are eaten.
- e.g. An elephant has one calf about every four to five years.
- e.g. A sparrow raises two or three broods of about four chicks in a year.
- **The older idea (r and K):** Animals sit on a line. At one end are quick breeders with many young and little care. At the other end are slow breeders with few young and much care.
- **The newer idea (the fast-slow line):** Many traits move together. Animals with short lives mature early and have many young. Animals with long lives mature late and have few. But real animals are mixed, and the surroundings matter too.
### How long do animals live? · *defined*
**Definition:** Lifespan is how long an animal lives. Scientists give an average lifespan, which is the typical age at death, and a maximum lifespan, which is the oldest age ever recorded. A wild animal usually dies much younger than the maximum, from hunger, disease or predators.

Lifespans run from hours to centuries. A grown mayfly may live only about a day, but its nymph lived in water for a year or more. A honeybee worker lives about six weeks in summer, while the queen can live several years. A house mouse lives about two years, a dog about twelve and a person about 70 to 80. A giant tortoise can pass 100 years, and a tortoise named Jonathan on the island of St Helena is thought to have hatched around 1832. A bowhead whale has been found to live more than 200 years. A Greenland shark has been estimated to live at least 270 years. An ocean quahog, a clam, was found to be 507 years old. How do we know? Some animals keep records in their bodies. Shell rings, ear bones, the lens of the eye and growing layers are all counted. Treat any age claim with care, because without a record the age is only an estimate.
- e.g. Mayfly (adult): about one day.
- e.g. Honeybee worker: about six weeks in summer.
- e.g. Giant tortoise: more than 100 years.
- e.g. Greenland shark: an estimate of about 270 to 500 years.
### Why do lifespans differ so much? · *debated*
Across mammals, bigger animals tend to live longer. But there are many exceptions. Birds and bats live much longer than mammals of the same size. A naked mole-rat, about the size of a mouse, lives more than 30 years. Scientists have several ideas. Nobody has one answer that covers every animal.
- e.g. A mouse lives about two years. An elephant lives about sixty.
- e.g. A parakeet is small but can live 20 years or more.
- e.g. A naked mole-rat lives for more than 30 years, fifteen times longer than a mouse.
- e.g. Island opossums with no predators lived longer and aged slower than mainland opossums.
- **Body size and speed of living:** Small animals have fast hearts and fast bodies. Bigger animals have slower hearts. A slower pace may mean slower wear.
- **Danger from outside:** If most animals of a kind are eaten young, there is little reason for evolution to build a body that lasts long. Where danger is low, long life can evolve. In 1993, Steven Austad found that opossums on an island with no predators lived longer and aged more slowly than mainland opossums.
- **Repair:** Bodies that repair their cells and DNA well live longer. Whales and naked mole-rats seem to have special ways to keep their cells safe.
### The heartbeat pattern · *idea*
A mouse's heart beats about 600 times a minute and an elephant's about 30. In a lifetime, they add up to a surprisingly similar number. Multiply beats per minute by minutes in a year by years of life. A mouse gets about 630 million beats in two years. An elephant gets about 950 million in sixty years. A cat or a dog lands within a factor of two of a billion. This is a rough pattern across most mammals. It was described in the 1990s, for example by Howard Levine in 1997. It is not a law. Humans get about three times as many beats, and bats and many birds get far more. So a heart is not a counter that stops at a fixed number.
- e.g. A mouse: about 600 beats a minute for 2 years is about 630 million beats.
- e.g. A dog: about 100 beats a minute for 12 years is about 630 million beats.
- e.g. An elephant: about 30 beats a minute for 60 years is about 950 million beats.
- e.g. A human: about 70 beats a minute for 75 years is about 2.8 billion beats.
- **Some biologists:** Body size sets how fast the body's engine runs, and that also sets how fast the heart beats and how fast wear builds up. The billion is a result of that scaling.
- **Other biologists:** The match is partly a coincidence. Humans, bats and birds break it, and modern medicine has raised human life without changing the heart.
### Warm sand, cool sand: when temperature decides · *defined*
**Definition:** In some reptiles, the temperature of the nest decides whether the eggs become males or females. This is called temperature-dependent sex determination.

In sea turtles, warmer sand makes more females and cooler sand makes more males. In the olive ridley turtle, a nest at around 29 to 30 degrees Celsius gives a mix, warmer sand gives mostly females and cooler sand gives mostly males. A turtle nest is dug about 45 centimetres deep. About 100 eggs are laid, and the young hatch after roughly 45 to 60 days. Crocodiles also have temperature-dependent sex, and mugger crocodile mothers in India guard their nests. Because the sex depends on the sand, a hotter beach can produce mostly females. People who protect nests at beaches in Tamil Nadu and Odisha move eggs to shaded hatcheries and keep an eye on the sand temperature.
- e.g. Olive ridley turtles nest on the beaches of Odisha and Tamil Nadu from about November to March.
- e.g. Hatchlings come out at night and head for the brighter horizon, which is usually the sea.
- e.g. Street lights and torches can lead hatchlings the wrong way.
- e.g. Mugger crocodile mothers guard the nest and help the hatchlings reach the water.
### Can an animal avoid growing old? · *debated*
Most animals become more likely to die as they age. This is called senescence, which is the slow wearing down of the body. A few animals seem to age very little. Hydra, a tiny freshwater animal, was kept in a lab for years with no sign of a rising risk of death, but some scientists doubt this. Naked mole-rats do not seem to become more likely to die as they age, according to a 2018 study by Rochelle Buffenstein's team. Greenland sharks and tortoises live for centuries but still age, only slowly. Nobody yet knows whether any animal truly does not age.
- e.g. Hydra: a small freshwater animal that keeps making new cells.
- e.g. Naked mole-rat: a small underground mammal that lives more than 30 years.
- e.g. Giant tortoise: lives for more than a hundred years and slows down only late.
- e.g. Ocean quahog: a clam that can live for hundreds of years.
- **Some biologists:** A few animals do not age in a way we can measure. Studying them may help us learn how bodies stay young longer.
- **Other biologists:** Every animal ages, and the ones that seem not to are those we have not watched long enough. Slow ageing is not no ageing.

## Mostly agreed
- Every animal comes from a living parent, through an egg or a birth. *How we know:* No animal has ever been seen to form from mud or rotting meat. In 1668, Francesco Redi showed that maggots come from fly eggs by keeping flies off meat.
- Insects grow up in two ways: with a pupa stage, and without one. *How we know:* Scientists have watched the life cycles of thousands of kinds of insects, and every one fits one of the two patterns.
- Mammals, birds, amphibians and insects begin life in different ways, but all the ways need food, warmth or water and safety. *How we know:* The eggs and wombs of each group have been studied in detail.
- Lifespans vary enormously, from hours to centuries. *How we know:* Long records of zoo animals, shell rings, and the radiocarbon dating of eye lenses and shells give ages.
- Across many mammals, bigger animals tend to have slower hearts and longer lives. *How we know:* Studies of heart rate and lifespan in dozens of mammals show a clear pattern, with exceptions.

## How we found out
**Johannes Schmidt, a Danish biologist (1877 to 1933), 1904 to 1922.** For over two thousand years, nobody knew where eels came from. Eels live in rivers, but nobody ever saw a baby eel or an eel egg. About 350 BCE, the Greek thinker Aristotle taught that eels grew out of mud, or out of earthworms. In 1876, a young student named Sigmund Freud cut open about 400 eels looking for their reproductive organs, and found none. In 1904, a Danish scientist, Johannes Schmidt, found strange, flat, see-through baby fish at sea. Italian scientists had shown in 1896 that these were baby eels. Schmidt followed them, catching smaller and smaller ones, across the Atlantic ocean. By 1922, he had traced them to the Sargasso Sea, where adult eels are thought to swim from European rivers to lay eggs and die. The eel is not made from mud. It is a long-distance traveller with a life cycle that crosses an ocean. Even today, nobody has filmed wild European eels spawning.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that eels were born from mud or worms, because nobody ever found an eel egg or a baby eel. It was shown wrong when scientists found the tiny see-through young at sea and followed them back, size by size, to where they were born.

## Challenging perspectives
- **All mammals give birth to live young.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Almost all mammals do. Cows, dogs, bats, whales and people give birth.  
  Against: Platypuses and echidnas are mammals that lay eggs and feed the hatchlings on milk. Kangaroos give birth to tiny young that finish growing in a pouch.
- **Bigger animals always live longer.** (A common idea.; *debated*)  
  For: Across mammals, a mouse lives about two years, a dog about twelve and an elephant about sixty.  
  Against: Birds and bats live much longer than mammals of the same size. A naked mole-rat lives 30 years, and a giant tortoise outlives an elephant.
- **You can tell a tortoise's age by counting the rings on her shell.** (A popular belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Young tortoises add a ring with each growth spurt, much like a tree.  
  Against: Rings are added whenever food and rain change, not once a year, and they wear off as the shell ages. The rings of an adult tortoise cannot tell you her age.
- **A pupa is a sleeping stage in which the caterpillar rests.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: The pupa does not move or eat.  
  Against: Inside the pupa the body is being taken apart and rebuilt. Scientists find it is one of the busiest times of the insect's life.
- **Animals that lay thousands of eggs are poor parents.** (Some people.; *minority*)  
  For: Frogs and fish that lay thousands of eggs give them almost no care.  
  Against: It is a different bet, not a worse one. Some fish and frogs guard their eggs, and an elephant's care also fails at times. Both ways keep a species going.

## Roots
We have compared the lives of mammals, birds, insects and amphibians. Now we ask what the shapes of these lives tell us about time, and about the line of living beings that we belong to.

**Deep truth.** A mouse lives about two years and an elephant about sixty. Yet in a whole life, most mammals' hearts beat roughly a billion times. The mouse spends those beats fast, and the elephant spends them slowly. So a year is a long time for one animal and a short time for another.  
*Habit it breaks:* We measure every life with one clock, the calendar, and call a short life a small one. But each kind of animal runs at a different pace of heartbeats, breaths and growth. An animal's own pace may be a fairer ruler of her life than the calendar.  
*Evidence:* In the Lifespan Line, a mouse's heart rate of about 600 beats a minute for 2 years gives about 630 million beats. An elephant's rate of about 30 beats for 60 years gives about 950 million. The pattern is rough, because humans, bats and many birds get far more beats.

- *who am i:* You are an animal with your own pace. Your heart, your childhood and your lifespan are all set to the speed of a human life.
- *what is life:* Life is a chain of eggs and births that has never been broken. Every animal alive came from a living parent, who came from another, back billions of years.
- *what is this world:* Many clocks run at once. A mayfly lives for a day, a mouse for two years and a tortoise for two centuries, and each of them is fully alive in their own time.

Every life cycle is a circle that starts as a single cell and ends as the next cell. The egg of a frog, the egg of a hen and the cell inside a mother elephant are all alive before anyone hatches or is born. Nothing in the chain starts from nothing. The same living stuff goes round and round through many bodies, which are fast or slow, small or large. What looks like many separate lives of different lengths is one long line of living, passing through many bodies at many speeds.

- **Zhuangzi, chapter 1 (Free and Easy Wandering):** The Chinese teacher Zhuangzi wrote, as paraphrased here, that a morning mushroom knows nothing of the night, and a cicada knows nothing of spring and autumn. Far to the south, he said, there is a tree for which five hundred years is one spring. (A mayfly adult, a mouse and a tortoise each live inside a different length of time, as the mushroom and the tree do.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.27:** The Gita says that for one who is born, death is certain, and for one who dies, birth is certain. Birth and death follow each other round and round. (In a life cycle, every ending is also a beginning. A hen's egg is the end of one hen's work and the start of another.)
- Science (settled): All animals alive today share ancestors, and each one came from an unbroken line of living parents. No animal has ever been seen to start from non-living matter.
- Science (idea): Across many mammals, a whole life holds roughly a billion heartbeats. Humans, bats and many birds are far above it, so scientists still debate why.
- Other view, Many biologists: A heartbeat count is not a measure of how much a life is lived. The match between mammals is partly a coincidence of body size and speed. A long life and a short life cannot be compared by counting alone, and a day of a mayfly's life has no number that makes it bigger or smaller.
- Practice, Your own pace (3 min): Sit still. Put two fingers on your wrist or your neck. Count the beats you feel for 15 seconds. Now breathe out slowly, as slowly as a tortoise. Count again. Is it the same? Think about the little animals whose hearts beat ten times as fast as yours.
- Ask: Is a mayfly's one day a short life or a whole life?
- Ask: If a mouse and an elephant each get about a billion beats, who has the longer life?
- Ask: Where did the first egg in your family's line come from?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A mayfly lives a day as an adult and a tortoise lives a century. Is one of them living a bigger life than the other? (hint: Think about what a day is for a mayfly. What do they do in it? What makes a life long or short, the calendar or what happens in it?)
- *big:* Most animals that hatch never grow up. Is a life that ends early a failure? (hint: Think about the eggs that feed others, and who eats the tadpoles. Where does the energy of a tadpole go?)
- *deep:* Sparrows care for four chicks, a frog cares for none, an elephant for one. Which kind of parent is the best? (hint: Think about what each one has to spend, and what each one gets back. Is there a best, or do all fit their own world?)
- *deep:* If a mouse's heart and an elephant's heart beat about a billion times each in a lifetime, are their lives the same size? (hint: Is a life measured in years, in heartbeats, or in what is lived?)
- *big:* Sea turtles pick their young's sex with the warmth of the sand. What happens to a species if the world gets warmer? (hint: Think about what happens if almost all hatchlings are female. Who could help, and how?)
- *big:* Every animal alive today came from an unbroken line of parents that goes back billions of years. What does that make you? (hint: Count back: you, a parent, a grandparent. How many steps to the first egg?)

## Story: The Stick with the Notch
*The oil sardine is real. So are its spawning in the monsoon, its short life of a few years, its tens of thousands of eggs, and the scientists at India's marine fisheries institute in Kochi who measure landed fish. Chellanam is a real fishing village near Kochi. Jincy, Ammachi, Hema Madam, the stick and the notch are made up, and so are the details of the morning.*

The first heavy rain of June came to Chellanam in the night. By morning the whole beach smelled of the sea and of wet mud. Jincy ran down with her grandmother, Ammachi, before school. It was mathi season. Mathi are oil sardines, silver fish that swim in huge shoals off the coast of Kerala. For a few months every year, the boats come back so full that they sit low in the water.

Ammachi had sold fish on that beach for fifty years. She sat on an upturned crate behind her baskets, with her sari tucked up at her waist, and she called out to the buyers as the boats came in.

But the baskets looked wrong this year. Jincy picked up a mathi and held it against her palm. It did not even reach her wrist.

"When I was your age," said Ammachi, "a mathi was as long as my hand. These are children's fish."

Down the beach, a woman in a yellow raincoat knelt beside a wooden board with a ruler painted along its edge. This was Hema Madam, who came every morning from the fisheries institute in Kochi. She took a few fish from each basket as the baskets came off the boat, laid each one on the board, and wrote down its length. Jincy had been watching her for a week.

"May I help?" she asked.

Hema Madam gave her the notebook. Jincy wrote the numbers as Hema Madam called them out. Eleven. Twelve. Eleven. Ten. Twelve. Eleven. When the sun had climbed over the palms, there were a hundred numbers on the page, and Jincy saw something that Hema Madam had probably seen already.

"They are nearly all the same," she said. "Same small size. Where are the big ones?"

"Most of these hatched less than a year ago," said Hema Madam. "They are young. The big ones are fewer, because many have already been caught."

Jincy frowned. "But there are so many fish. Look at all the baskets. Does it matter if they are small? A fish is a fish."

Hema Madam sat back on her heels. "Let me ask you something. Where does a mathi come from?"

"From an egg."

"And where does the egg come from?"

Jincy thought of the chart she had drawn last term for her little brother. It was a circle with four pictures: an egg, a young animal, a grown animal, and then an egg again, with arrows going round. "From a grown mathi," she said. "A mother."

"How grown?"

"I do not know."

"Mathi lay their eggs in the monsoon, out in the sea," said Hema Madam. "A mathi is ready to lay when she is about a year old. And she does not live for long. Two or three years, most of them. So each mathi gets only two or three monsoons, or even only one, to lay her eggs. If she is caught before that, she never lays them."

Jincy looked down at the page full of small numbers. Every one of those fish was a young one that had not had her turn yet.

"But a mathi lays thousands and thousands of eggs," she said slowly. "I read it in a book. Tens of thousands. Surely that makes up for it."

"It does help," said Hema Madam. "But think what happens to those eggs. Other fish eat them. Birds and bigger fish eat the tiny young. The sea washes some of them away. Out of tens of thousands, only a handful grow up to be a mathi like these. The sea needs enough mothers, or even a huge number of eggs cannot keep the shoal going."

Jincy could see it now, the circle from her chart. A circle only turns if enough of the young live long enough to make eggs of their own. Cut it in the same place every year, and it stops.

She ran back to Ammachi's crate. The last boat had come in and the buyers were already shouting prices. "Ammachi," she said. "I need a stick."

Ammachi gave her a thin stick from the cooking fire. Jincy held it against Hema Madam's board and cut a notch at the length of a grown mathi, the length Ammachi called a hand. Then she cut a second notch beside it, to be sure.

"What is that for?" said Ammachi.

"Everything shorter than the notch goes in a different basket," said Jincy. "They have not laid eggs yet."

Ammachi laughed so loudly that two buyers turned round. "A child with a stick will tell the sea what to do!" But she took the stick. She laid it across the first of her baskets, and she looked at the fish for a long time. Then she picked out the very smallest ones, one by one, and put them in a basket by themselves.

Not everyone followed. Some boats sold everything, as they always had. Hema Madam said that one beach, with one stick, would not change the sea in a single season.

"Then how will we know if it worked?" asked Jincy.

"A mathi lives two or three years," said Hema Madam. "So we shall have to wait for two or three monsoons, and then measure again."

That evening, Jincy took the notebook home. On the last page, under the hundred numbers, she wrote the date, and then in big letters: "Measure again. June, two years from now."

**For the parent:** A life cycle only turns if enough of the young live long enough to lay eggs of their own. A fish caught before her first monsoon leaves no eggs, however many eggs a mother lays.

- Ask: Why did it matter to Jincy that the small fish had not laid eggs yet, even though a mathi lays tens of thousands?
- Ask: Why did Hema Madam say they must wait two or three monsoons to know whether the stick worked?
- Ask: If every mother lays tens of thousands of eggs, why does the sea still need enough grown mothers?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Survival Cup: many young, or a few cared-for young? (30 min)
- **question:** A frog family, a hen family and an elephant family start with different numbers of young and different amounts of care. After two dangers, which family has the most young left?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which family will have the most young left, and which will have the fewest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every young one faces two dangers, one as an egg or newborn and one while growing up. Each danger is one roll of the same die. What changes is the number of young at the start and the roll needed to live. Each family plays three rounds, because one round can be lucky or unlucky. This is a model with made-up numbers, so it shows an idea and does not measure real animals.
- **safety:** Keep small counters away from children under five, who might put them in their mouths. Dry chana and beans are also a choking risk for small brothers and sisters. If a child is allergic to chana or any bean, use buttons or paper squares.
- **record:** For each round, write how many young started, how many were left after danger 1 and how many were left after danger 2. Add up the survivors of three rounds for each family. Then draw one bar for each family.
- **results table:** Family | Round | Young at the start | Left after danger 1: egg or newborn | Left after danger 2: growing up
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read out the three families. Write down your child's guess and their reason before you start.  
  *Parent says:* A frog starts with 18 eggs. A hen starts with 5 eggs. An elephant has 3 calves, one after the other. Each young one faces two dangers. Which family will have the most young left at the end? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A frog has 18 eggs. A hen has 5. An elephant has 3 calves. Who will have the most young left? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that family will have the most left? (good: She starts with the most.; The hen keeps her chicks safe.; The elephant looks after her calves.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children pick the frog because of the big number, or the elephant because of the care. Both are good reasons to test.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 18 counters on the Frog plate, 5 on the Hen plate and 3 on the Elephant plate. Write the dice rules on paper. With 2 to 4 children, each child plays one family (the parent or the next child takes the third) and everyone else writes down the numbers. If you have only one die, pass it round.  
  *Parent says:* Every young one rolls the die once for each danger. A frog's young live on a 5 or a 6. A hen's young live on a 3, 4, 5 or 6. An elephant's young live on a 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6. The better the care, the more rolls that let a young one live.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the die once for every young one. A frog's egg lives on a 5 or 6. A hen's chick lives on 3 to 6. An elephant's calf lives on 2 to 6.
  - Note: The rules are made up to show an idea. A frog gives no care, so only 2 of the 6 numbers let a young one live. A hen guards her chicks, so 4 of 6 do. An elephant guards her calves even more, so 5 of 6 do.
  - Check: Why does an elephant's calf live on more numbers than a frog's egg? → **A mother who guards her young keeps more of them safe**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the frog family. Roll once for each egg for danger 1. Keep the ones that live. Roll again for danger 2. Write the numbers down. Play three rounds.  
  *Parent says:* Start with the frog. For danger 1, roll once for each egg. Move the ones that do not live to the Lost plate. Count what is left. Then roll again for danger 2. Write it down. Then put them back and play it two more times.  
  *Child mode:* Roll once for each frog egg. Keep eggs that live. Write the number left.
  - Ask: How many of the 18 eggs do you think will live through both dangers? (good: Not many.; About two or three.; Most will be lost.)
  - Note: A frog round usually leaves between 0 and 5 young. The average is about 2. It will vary a lot, and that is normal. Say 'that is how chance works', and do not call it a mistake.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the hen family the same way, with 5 chicks and the hen's rule. Play three rounds. Write the numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* Now the hen. She has 5 chicks, and she guards them. Roll once for each chick for danger 1, then again for danger 2. Write what is left. Play three rounds.  
  *Child mode:* Now the hen. Roll for each of the 5 chicks. A chick lives on 3 to 6.
  - Note: A hen round usually leaves between 1 and 4 chicks. The average is about 2.
  - Check: Why do we play three rounds for each family and not one? → **One round could be lucky or unlucky, so three give a fairer picture**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the elephant family. Roll once for each of the 3 calves for danger 1, then once for danger 2. Play three rounds. Write the numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* Last, the elephant. She has 3 calves, one after another. Roll for each calf for danger 1, then again for danger 2. Write what is left. Play three rounds.  
  *Child mode:* Now the elephant. Roll for each of the 3 calves. A calf lives on 2 to 6.
  - Note: An elephant round usually leaves between 1 and 3 calves. The average is about 2.
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the survivors for each family over three rounds. Draw a bar for each family. Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see in the numbers? Why do you think it happened? Was the family with the most young at the start the family with the most at the end?  
  *Child mode:* Add up each family's survivors. Draw three bars. What did you think? What did you see? Why?
  - Ask: The elephant started with 3 calves and the frog with 18. Why were their totals close? (good: Care helps each young one live.; The frog loses most of her eggs.; A few young with care can match many with none.)
  - Note: Over three rounds the expected totals are about 6 for the frog, 7 for the hen and 6 for the elephant, so they are close. Your numbers will wobble, and a gap of one or two is likely just luck. After your child has explained: the numbers were chosen to make a point. In nature, a mother frog and a mother elephant have found very different ways to leave about the same number of grown-up young. Real frogs lay far more eggs, and far fewer survive. If your child's results showed one family far ahead, repeat three more rounds and see if it lasts. With several children, add everyone's rounds together for a longer test. More rounds make the totals settle, so the three families come out closer.
  - Think: A frog lays thousands of eggs in real life, and a real elephant has one calf every few years. What might each mother have to spend to do it?

  - Think: A parakeet is much smaller than a dog but lives longer. Why might a small animal live a long time?

  *Parent does:* Count your pulse for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. Then show the heart rates: mouse 600, hen 300, cat 150, dog 100, human 70, elephant 30 beats a minute. Work out how many beats the mouse and the elephant get in a life.  
  *Parent says:* Count your pulse for 15 seconds, and multiply by 4. That is your beats a minute. A mouse's heart beats about 600 times a minute and an elephant's about 30. A year has about 525,000 minutes. Work out the beats in a year for each, then the beats in a whole life.

  - Ask: A mouse lives 2 years and an elephant 60. Which do you think gets more heartbeats in a life? (good: The elephant, because she lives so much longer.; The mouse, because her heart is fast.; They might be about the same.)

  - Check: A cat's heart beats 150 times a minute. She lives 15 years, with 525,000 minutes in a year. About how many beats is that? → **About 1.2 billion**

Birds: hard-shelled eggs warmed by parents, 1 to 12 eggs or more, care for weeks, no change of shape. Insects: eggs on food or leaves, from one to hundreds, usually no care (bees care for their young), a complete change with a pupa for butterflies and honeybees, and a gradual change with no pupa for cockroaches and dragonflies. Amphibians: jelly eggs in water, hundreds in most frogs, usually no care, tadpoles change a lot.

  *Child mode:* Which animals break their group's pattern? Find at least two.
  - Ask: Why do you think nature has exceptions? (good: Animals found different ways to survive.; Different places need different solutions.; Patterns are summaries, not laws.)
  - Note: Exceptions: the platypus is a mammal that lays eggs. The honeybee is an insect that feeds her young for weeks. The Indian purple frog spends nearly all the year underground. Some caecilians guard their eggs. Birds do not give birth, but some hen chicks are ready-born and some sparrow chicks are helpless. Say that a pattern in nature is something we notice, and exceptions are how we learn more.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a claim, evidence and reasoning about which group has the most care and which has the fewest young.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. Which group looks after its young the most? What in your table shows it? Why might it be that way?  
  *Child mode:* Which group cares most? What shows it? Why? Say it out loud.
  - Note: A strong answer says that mammals and birds usually care for their young a lot and have fewer, while amphibians and many insects have many young and little care. Ask for an exception from the table.
  - Think: Which group gives its young the least care, and how do their young survive?
### a3 · Rice-Grain Eggs: how big is a big number of eggs? (20 min)
- **safety:** Rice grains are small and can be put in the mouth by children under five. Keep them on the plate and pour the rice back into the tin afterwards.
- **record:** Your count for one teaspoon, your estimate for one katori, and a table showing how many katoris or sacks of rice each animal's eggs would fill.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a teaspoon of rice. Ask them to guess how many grains are in one level teaspoon. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Imagine each grain of rice is an egg. How many eggs do you think fit in one level teaspoon? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Each rice grain is an egg. How many in one teaspoon? Guess.
  - Note: A level teaspoon of ordinary rice holds roughly 150 to 300 grains, depending on the rice. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (7 min)**

  *Child mode:* Count the grains in one teaspoon. Make piles of ten.
  - Note: Most families find between 150 and 300. It depends on the rice. Write the number. It will be used in the next step. Counts will differ by a few grains. That is a result worth talking about: an estimate is a good guess, not an exact number.
  - Check: Why do we count in piles of ten and not one by one? → **It is faster and we make fewer mistakes**

  *Child mode:* How many teaspoons fill a katori? Multiply by your teaspoon count.

  - Note: With about 7,000 grains in a katori: a frog's 1,000 eggs is a bit under a katori. A rohu's 100,000 is about 14 katoris. A sunfish's 300 million is about 40,000 katoris, or roughly 6,000 kilograms of rice, as much as more than a hundred big sacks. These are estimates. Real egg numbers vary a lot with the size and age of the mother.

  - Ask: What do you think left this empty skin? (good: A nymph that climbed out and grew wings.; A cicada.; A grasshopper.)
  - Note: If nothing turns up, that is also a result. Skins are easiest to find in the monsoon and the weeks after, and much harder in winter.   *Child mode:* What does each find tell you? Which stage of the life cycle is it?
  - Note: A hard outer skin cannot stretch, so an insect must shed the old skin to grow. A mammal's skin stretches. A snake sheds her whole skin as she grows and from time to time.
  - Think: Why must a nymph or a snake shed her skin to grow, when a mammal does not?

### p1 · Banana Jar Nursery: watch a fly's whole life cycle in about twelve days (60 min, 12 days)
- **safety:** Fruit flies do not bite, but keep the jar away from food, wash your hands afterwards and do not eat anything from it. Let the flies go outside at the end. If grey or green mould grows, do not sniff it. An adult opens the jar, and a child with asthma or a mould allergy keeps away.
- **record:** A drawing for each day you looked, with a count of flies on day 2 and of the new flies, and the day you first saw maggots, pupae and new flies.
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up the jar with a slice of ripe banana. Leave it open on a kitchen shelf for a night so that fruit flies can find it. (10 min); day 2: Cover the jar with cloth once there are a few flies inside. Count and draw them. (10 min); day 3: Take the jar outside, in the shade, and take the cloth off so the grown flies fly away. Cover it again at once. The eggs stay on the banana. (10 min); day 5: Look for tiny white maggots on the banana. Draw them. (10 min); day 7: Look for brown capsules on the jar wall and the paper strip. These are pupae. Draw them. (10 min); day 12: Look each day from day 10. When new flies hatch, count them, then let them out outdoors and wash the jar. (10 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a slice of very ripe banana in a clean jar. Leave the jar open on a kitchen shelf for one night. Write a guess about what will happen.  
  *Parent says:* We will leave a slice of ripe banana in a jar and see what comes. Do you think anything will? What do you think will be in the jar in about twelve days? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put a banana slice in a jar. Leave it open one night. What will happen?
  - Note: If no flies come after two nights, leave it a night longer or put it near a window or on the balcony. In a very clean kitchen in winter it can take a few days. Do not use a cut fruit left near food. Keep the jar in a warm place out of direct sun, because above about 30 degrees the larvae die. With 2 to 4 children, take turns to look and draw each day. For a comparison, set a second jar in a cooler corner and see whether it is slower.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Check the jar. If a few small flies are inside, cover the jar with cloth and a rubber band. Count the flies and draw one. Tomorrow the grown flies will be let out, so the flies you count later are new.  
  *Parent says:* Look in the jar. Small flies have come, and now they are in it. Count them. Draw one. Where did they come from? How did they find the banana?  
  *Child mode:* Cover the jar with cloth. Count the flies. Draw one.
  - Ask: How did the flies find the banana? (good: They smelled it.; They flew in.; They came from outside.)
  - Note: The flies are fruit flies, the same kind that scientists such as Thomas Hunt Morgan studied in the 1900s. They find ripe fruit by smell. The females lay eggs on it. A female can lay up to about fifty to a hundred eggs in a day.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3, let the grown flies out. On day 5, find the tiny white maggots with a magnifying glass. Draw them. On day 7, find brown capsules.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely at the banana. Do you see tiny white worms? They are the larvae. On day 7, look for brown capsules. These are pupae. What is happening inside?  
  *Child mode:* Let the grown flies out on day 3. Look for white maggots on day 5. Look for brown capsules on day 7.
  - Ask: What is happening inside the brown capsule? (good: The maggot is changing into a fly.; The body is being rebuilt.; Something is growing wings.)
  - Note: To let the grown flies out on day 3, take the jar outside in the shade, take the cloth off, wait a few minutes, then cover it again. The eggs stay on the banana. Fruit flies take about 10 days from egg to adult at about 25 degrees: about a day as an egg, four days as a larva and four to five days as a pupa. The eggs here are laid on day 1 or 2, so expect the first new flies around day 11 to 13. In cooler weather, such as a north Indian winter, it can take three to four weeks, so keep waiting. If the banana is a wet mouldy mess, or nothing has appeared by day 20, start again in a warmer month. The pupa is not sleeping. The body is being rebuilt.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* From day 10, look each day. On the day new flies appear, count them. Compare with the first count. Let the flies go outside and wash the jar.  
  *Parent says:* Count the new flies. How many are there now? Is it more than the first count? Why? Now take the jar outside and let the flies go.  
  *Child mode:* Count the new flies. Is it more than before? Let them go outside.
  - Ask: Why are there more flies now than on day 2? (good: The first flies laid eggs.; The eggs grew into flies.; The young became adults.)
  - Note: Count by tapping the jar gently to settle them. Keep the answer. A single pair can start hundreds of flies in a month. This shows why animals that lay many eggs and give no care can still thrive. Release the flies outdoors.
  - Think: A fruit fly mother lays many eggs and gives no care. How is her way of having young different from a hen's?

### Animal life cycles compared (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which group of animals lays soft eggs covered in jelly, usually in water?  
   Options: Amphibians / Birds / Mammals  
   **Answer:** Amphibians. *Why:* Frogs, toads and newts lay jelly-covered eggs with no shell, so they need water or very damp places. Birds lay hard-shelled eggs, and most mammals do not lay eggs at all.
2. (truefalse) All mammals give birth to live young.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Platypuses and echidnas are mammals that lay eggs and feed the hatchlings on milk. Most mammals do give birth to live young, but this one has exceptions.
3. (sort) Sort each animal by how life begins for her young.  
   **Answer:** hen → Hard-shelled egg on land; crow → Hard-shelled egg on land; bullfrog → Jelly eggs in water; toad → Jelly eggs in water; cow → Grows inside the mother; human → Grows inside the mother. *Why:* Birds lay eggs with hard shells on land. Frogs and toads lay jelly eggs in water. Cows and humans are mammals, and the young grow inside the mother.
4. (fill) A young animal that can walk and feed soon after birth is called ____-born.  
   **Answer:** ['ready', 'precocial']. *Why:* Ready-born young, such as calves and hen's chicks, are well developed at birth. Helpless-born young, such as kittens and sparrow chicks, need their parents.
5. (match) Match each newborn to what she needs.  
   **Answer:** Calf → stands within an hour; Kitten → blind and needs her mother; Hen's chick → pecks at food on day one; Sparrow chick → needs feeding many times a day. *Why:* Calves and hen's chicks are ready-born. Kittens and sparrow chicks are helpless-born and depend on their parents for food and warmth.
6. (mcq) Which of these animals has a pupa stage in its life cycle?  
   Options: Housefly / Grasshopper / Dragonfly  
   **Answer:** Housefly. *Why:* A housefly goes from egg to maggot to pupa to adult. A grasshopper and a dragonfly grow up as nymphs, without a pupa.
7. (order) Put the stages of a butterfly's life cycle in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Egg on a leaf', 'Caterpillar eating', 'Pupa (chrysalis)', 'Adult butterfly']. *Why:* A butterfly goes through four stages. The caterpillar grows, the pupa is where the body is rebuilt, and the adult flies and lays eggs.
8. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Butterfly, housefly, honeybee, cockroach.  
   **Answer:** Cockroach, because it has no pupa. The other three do.. *Why:* A butterfly, a housefly and a honeybee go through complete metamorphosis with a pupa. A cockroach grows by shedding her skin as a nymph, with no pupa.
9. (mcq) Which of these animals has the longest lifespan?  
   Options: Giant tortoise / Hen / House mouse  
   **Answer:** Giant tortoise. *Why:* A giant tortoise can live more than 100 years. A hen lives about 8 years and a house mouse about 2.
10. (count) On the Lifespan Line, a dog's string is 12 centimetres and an elephant's string is 60 centimetres. How many times as long is the elephant's string?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* 60 divided by 12 is 5. The elephant's string is 5 times as long, because she lives about 5 times as many years.
11. (write) A friend says 'bigger animals always live longer'. Give one animal that shows this is not always true, and explain.  
   **Answer:** A parakeet is small but can live 20 years or more, longer than a dog. Or a giant tortoise outlives an elephant. A naked mole-rat is mouse-sized and lives over 30 years.. *Why:* A strong answer names an animal that breaks the pattern and compares the two animals. The pattern is true for many mammals, but not for all animals.
12. (mcq) A frog lays 1,000 eggs and gives no care. An elephant has one calf and cares for her for years. What is true about the two?  
   Options: Both can keep their kind going / Only the elephant's way works / Only the frog's way works  
   **Answer:** Both can keep their kind going. *Why:* Both ways work, because in a steady population an average mother leaves about two grown-up young. The frog loses most eggs, and the elephant protects her few.
13. (truefalse) A frog's tadpoles usually get a lot of care from their mother.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most frogs lay their eggs and leave. A few kinds, such as some caecilians and frogs that guard eggs, do give care, but most do not.
14. (draw) Draw a frog's life cycle as a circle with four pictures. Label each picture.  
   **Answer:** A circle with arrows: jelly eggs in water, a tadpole, a young frog with legs, an adult frog on land.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the four stages in order, with arrows going round to show the cycle starts again with the adult's eggs.
15. (perspective) Imran and Selvi are talking about frogs, which lay hundreds of eggs and then leave them.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side and uses the dice game. In the game, 18 frog eggs with no care left about as many survivors as 3 cared-for elephant calves, so both ways can work.. *Why:* Both views have a point. A frog gives no care, but her eggs are many. The Survival Cup showed that numbers and care can balance. Neither is better in itself, because each fits the animal's world.
16. (write) A mouse's heart beats about 600 times a minute and an elephant's about 30. Why do their lifetimes still hold a similar number of heartbeats?  
   **Answer:** The mouse's heart is about 20 times faster, but the elephant lives about 30 times as long. The two cancel out to a similar total, about half a billion to a billion beats.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a faster heart is paired with a shorter life, and a slower heart with a longer life, so the totals come out close. It also says that this is a rough pattern, not a law.

Both answers are acceptable if they give a reason.
16. (mcq) Why might a caterpillar and a butterfly eat different foods?  
   Options: So they do not compete for the same food / Because butterflies cannot chew / Because caterpillars do not like nectar  
   **Answer:** So they do not compete for the same food. *Why:* One idea is that a larva and an adult eat different foods in different places. That way the young do not compete with their parents. A caterpillar eats leaves to grow, and a butterfly drinks nectar.
### Animal life cycles compared (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) An ocean sunfish lays about 300 million eggs. If the sunfish population stays steady, about how many of an average mother's young grow up to have young themselves?  
   Options: About 2 / About 1,000 / About 300 million  
   **Answer:** About 2. *Why:* If the population is steady, each average mother is replaced by about two grown-up young over her life. All the rest are eaten or die. The huge number of eggs is how she gets those two.
2. (truefalse) Every mammal's heart beats exactly one billion times in a lifetime.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The pattern is rough. Most mammals are within about half and one and a half billion. Humans get about three times as many, and bats and many birds get more.
3. (write) Humans get about 2.8 billion heartbeats in a lifetime, three times more than a dog. Give one idea that could explain this, and say how you could test it.  
   **Answer:** One idea is that people have better food, clean water and medicine, which make our lives longer than a wild mammal's. To test it, compare how many heartbeats people had in places and times with less medicine.. *Why:* A strong answer gives an idea and a way to test it. Scientists really do not know the full reason, and they think it may be partly medicine and partly our biology.
4. (count) A horse's heart beats about 40 times a minute. Take a year as 525,000 minutes. If a horse lives 25 years, about how many million beats is that?  
   **Answer:** 525. *Why:* 40 times 525,000 is 21,000,000 beats in a year. Times 25 is 525,000,000, which is 525 million.
5. (mcq) In 1993, Steven Austad found that opossums on an island with no predators lived longer than mainland opossums. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Danger from outside can shape how long a kind of animal lives / Island food makes animals immortal / Opossums have special hearts  
   **Answer:** Danger from outside can shape how long a kind of animal lives. *Why:* Where animals are eaten young, evolution gives them little reason to build a body that lasts. Where danger is low, long life can evolve. This is one idea, supported by evidence in opossums.
6. (perspective) Two scientists are discussing Hydra, a small freshwater animal that seems not to age in the lab.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer asks how long the study ran, how many Hydra were watched, and whether they were tested in the wild. It says that more evidence is needed.. *Why:* Both views have a point. A claim that something never happens is hard to prove, because we can only watch for so long. It is a good example of an open question.
7. (sort) Sort each animal by whether the mother usually guards her young after they are born or hatched.  
   **Answer:** hen → Mother guards them; sparrow → Mother guards them; elephant → Mother guards them; mugger crocodile → Mother guards them; frog → Mother leaves them; sea turtle → Mother leaves them; ocean sunfish → Mother leaves them; housefly → Mother leaves them. *Why:* Birds and mammals usually guard their young, and mugger crocodile mothers guard their nests. Most frogs, sea turtles, fish and flies lay eggs and leave them.
8. (match) Match each insect stage to its job.  
   **Answer:** Larva → eats and grows; Pupa → the body is rebuilt inside; Nymph → sheds its skin to grow, without a pupa; Adult → finds a mate and lays eggs. *Why:* In complete metamorphosis, the larva grows, the pupa rebuilds, and the adult finds a mate. A nymph grows by moulting and looks like a small adult.
9. (order) Put these animals in order of how long the mother is pregnant or the egg waits, shortest first.  
   **Answer:** ["Hen's egg", 'Dog', 'Cow', 'Asian elephant']. *Why:* A hen's egg takes 21 days. A dog is pregnant for about 63 days, a cow for about 283 days and an elephant for about 650 days.
10. (fill) A baby grasshopper that looks like a tiny adult without wings is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['nymph']. *Why:* A nymph grows by shedding her skin. When she has shed it several times, she has wings and is an adult.
11. (write) Think of an island with no predators. Would you expect animals there to have many young or few? Give your reason.  
   **Answer:** I would expect fewer young and longer lives. With few dangers, each young one is likely to survive, so a mother does not need many. This is like what Steven Austad found in island opossums.. *Why:* A strong answer connects the number of young to how many survive, and to danger. It uses the many-or-few idea from the unit.
12. (draw) Draw a bar chart for four animals' lifespans: mouse 2 years, dog 12 years, elephant 60 years and human 75 years. Label the bars.  
   **Answer:** Four bars with heights in the ratio 2 to 12 to 60 to 75, labelled with the animals' names and years.. *Why:* A bar chart is for comparing. A strong chart has bars in the right proportion, a title, a label for each bar and a unit (years).
13. (mcq) Why can a helpless-born baby be 'cheaper' for her mother at the start?  
   Options: She is small and born early, so the mother does not need to carry or lay a big baby / She never needs any care / She grows up faster than a ready-born baby  
   **Answer:** She is small and born early, so the mother does not need to carry or lay a big baby. *Why:* A helpless-born baby is small at the start, so the mother spends less before birth. But it costs more later, in weeks or years of feeding and warmth.
14. (truefalse) A wild animal usually lives as long as the maximum lifespan for her kind.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The maximum is the oldest ever recorded, usually in a zoo or a safe place. Most wild animals die much younger from hunger, disease or predators.
15. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Giant tortoise, bowhead whale, Greenland shark, hen.  
   **Answer:** Hen, because she lives about 8 years. The other three live for a century or more.. *Why:* A giant tortoise, a bowhead whale and a Greenland shark can each live more than 100 years. A hen lives about 8.
16. (write) Why is a calendar year not the same size for a mouse and for an elephant? Use what you know about their hearts, growing up or having young.  
   **Answer:** A mouse's heart beats 20 times as fast, she grows up in two months and has young several times a year. An elephant takes more than ten years to grow up. So a year holds much more living for a mouse than for an elephant.. *Why:* A strong answer uses at least one fact, such as heartbeats, time to grow up or the number of litters, to show that animals live at different speeds.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these mammals lays eggs?  
   Options: Platypus / Cow / Bat  
   **Answer:** Platypus. *Why:* A platypus is a monotreme, a mammal that lays eggs and feeds the hatchlings on milk. Cows and bats give birth to live young.
2. (mcq) A newborn kitten is blind and cannot walk. What kind of newborn is she?  
   Options: Helpless-born / Ready-born / Egg-born  
   **Answer:** Helpless-born. *Why:* A kitten is helpless-born. She needs her mother for food and warmth. A calf is ready-born, and a hen's chick can feed herself on the first day.
3. (mcq) Which stage does a grasshopper NOT have in her life cycle?  
   Options: Pupa / Egg / Nymph  
   **Answer:** Pupa. *Why:* A grasshopper has incomplete metamorphosis, with egg, nymph and adult. She has no pupa. A butterfly, a fly and a bee do.
4. (mcq) What happens to a caterpillar's body inside the pupa?  
   Options: It is taken apart and rebuilt as a butterfly / It rests and does not change / It stops living for a while  
   **Answer:** It is taken apart and rebuilt as a butterfly. *Why:* A pupa looks still from outside, but inside the body is being rebuilt. It is one of the busiest times in the insect's life.
5. (mcq) On a line with one centimetre of string for every year, how long is the string for an elephant that lives 60 years?  
   Options: 60 centimetres / 6 centimetres / 600 centimetres  
   **Answer:** 60 centimetres. *Why:* One centimetre stands for one year, so 60 years is 60 centimetres.
6. (mcq) Which statement about lifespan is best supported by the evidence?  
   Options: Bigger mammals tend to live longer, but there are exceptions / Bigger animals always live longer / Size has nothing to do with lifespan  
   **Answer:** Bigger mammals tend to live longer, but there are exceptions. *Why:* Across many mammals, a mouse lives about 2 years, a dog 12 and an elephant 60. But parakeets, bats and tortoises are exceptions.
7. (mcq) The Survival Cup gave the frog family 18 eggs and the elephant family 3 calves. What did it show?  
   Options: Many young with no care and few young with care can leave similar numbers / A frog always has more young left / An elephant always has more young left  
   **Answer:** Many young with no care and few young with care can leave similar numbers. *Why:* In the model, 18 eggs facing a tough roll left about as many young as 3 calves with a better roll. Both ways can work.
8. (mcq) A pond has the same number of frogs every year. About how many of each mother's tadpoles grow up and have young?  
   Options: About 2 / About 100 / About 1,000  
   **Answer:** About 2. *Why:* If the population is steady, an average mother is replaced by about two grown-up young over her life. All the rest die, mostly as eggs and tadpoles.
9. (mcq) How do scientists estimate the age of a Greenland shark?  
   Options: By measuring radioactive carbon in the lens of her eye / By counting the rings on her fin / By weighing her  
   **Answer:** By measuring radioactive carbon in the lens of her eye. *Why:* The lens of the eye is made in the shark's first years and holds a record of the carbon in the sea at that time. The age is an estimate with a wide range.
10. (mcq) Can you tell an adult tortoise's age by counting the rings on her shell?  
   Options: No, rings are added when food and rain change, and wear off / Yes, one ring for each year / Yes, but only for male tortoises  
   **Answer:** No, rings are added when food and rain change, and wear off. *Why:* Shell rings are not a reliable clock for an adult. They grow with food and weather, and they wear away.
11. (mcq) A mouse lives about 2 years with a heart rate near 600, and an elephant about 60 years with a rate near 30. Which is closest to the truth?  
   Options: Their total heartbeats in a life are about the same, around half a billion to a billion / The elephant gets about 30 times as many / The mouse gets about 20 times as many  
   **Answer:** Their total heartbeats in a life are about the same, around half a billion to a billion. *Why:* The mouse's heart is about 20 times faster, and the elephant lives about 30 times as long. The two nearly cancel out.
12. (mcq) What does the heartbeat pattern NOT show?  
   Options: That every animal's heart stops after one billion beats / That a mouse's heart beats faster than an elephant's / That bigger mammals tend to live longer  
   **Answer:** That every animal's heart stops after one billion beats. *Why:* The pattern is rough. Humans get about three times as many beats, and bats and many birds get more. A heart is not a counter that stops at a fixed number.

## Open questions
- **Why do some small animals, such as bats and naked mole-rats, live so much longer than other animals of the same size?** Scientists have ideas about better repair of cells and fewer predators. Nobody has one answer that fits every animal.
- **Is there any animal that truly does not age?** Hydra and a few other animals show little sign of ageing in the lab. It is hard to prove that something never happens when we can only watch for a limited time.
- **Why do most mammals get about the same number of heartbeats in a life, but humans get three times as many?** Some scientists think body size sets the pace of life. Others think medicine and better living conditions have raised the human number. The whole story is not known.
- **Where and how do wild European eels spawn?** Scientists have followed the young back to the Sargasso Sea, but nobody has yet filmed wild eels laying eggs.
- **How will warmer beaches change the number of male and female sea turtles?** Warm sand makes more females. Studies elsewhere have found nests that are almost all female. Nobody knows how well turtles will adapt in the long run.

## Connected maths
- On the Lifespan Line the string pieces are 2, 8, 12, 15, 20, 60, 75, 150, 200 and 270 centimetres. How many metres of string is that in all? **8 metres and 12 centimetres.**. 2 plus 8 is 10, plus 12 is 22, plus 15 is 37, plus 20 is 57. 57 plus 60 is 117, plus 75 is 192, plus 150 is 342, plus 200 is 542, plus 270 is 812. 812 centimetres is 8 metres and 12 centimetres. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)
- A mouse's heart beats about 600 times a minute. A year has about 525,000 minutes. About how many beats is that in a year? And in 2 years? **About 315 million in a year, and about 630 million in 2 years.**. 600 times 525,000 is 315,000,000. Doubled for 2 years, it is 630,000,000. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A mouse lives 2 years and an elephant 60 years. A mouse's heart beats 600 times a minute and an elephant's 30. How many times longer does the elephant live? How many times slower is her heart? Which is bigger, and what does that mean for the total beats? **She lives 30 times as long, and her heart is 20 times as slow. The first is bigger, so she gets about 1.5 times as many beats.**. 60 divided by 2 is 30. 600 divided by 30 is 20. The elephant lives 30 times as long but her heart beats at one twentieth of the speed. 30 divided by 20 is 1.5, so her total is about one and a half times the mouse's. The totals are close but not equal. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A hen's egg waits 21 days to hatch. An Asian elephant waits about 650 days. About how many hens' waits fit into one elephant's wait? **About 31.**. 650 divided by 21 is about 31. An elephant waits about 31 times as long as a hen. (queues `math.division.L4`)
- In the Survival Cup, a frog's egg lives only if the die shows 5 or 6. What fraction of eggs live through one danger? What fraction live through both? If 18 eggs start, about how many are left after both dangers? **One third live through one danger, one ninth live through both, and about 2 of 18 eggs are left.**. 2 of the 6 numbers let an egg live, which is 2 out of 6, or 1 out of 3. For two dangers, 1 third of 1 third is 1 ninth. 18 divided by 9 is 2. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
- In the Survival Cup, a hen's chick lives on a 3, 4, 5 or 6, so 4 of 6 faces. Five chicks face two dangers. About how many chicks are expected to be left? Compare with the frog's 2 from 18 eggs. **About 2.2 chicks, a little more than the frog's 2.**. The chance for one danger is 4 out of 6, or 2 out of 3. For two dangers it is 2 thirds of 2 thirds, which is 4 ninths. 5 times 4 ninths is 20 ninths, about 2.2. That is a little more than 2. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- About 7,000 rice grains fill a katori. A rohu lays about 100,000 eggs. About how many katoris of rice would stand for her eggs? Round to the nearest whole katori. **About 14 katoris.**. 100,000 divided by 7,000 is about 14.3. Rounded to the nearest whole number, it is 14 katoris. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- People often say that only about 1 in 1,000 sea turtle hatchlings lives to be an adult. This is a rough estimate. A nest has about 100 eggs. If every egg hatched, how many nests would be needed to expect 5 adults? **About 50 nests.**. 5 adults need 5 times 1,000, which is 5,000 hatchlings. Each nest gives 100, so 5,000 divided by 100 is 50 nests. (queues `math.division.L4`)
