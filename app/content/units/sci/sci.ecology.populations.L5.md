# Carrying capacity: why no population grows for ever, and why hunters and hunted rise and fall
`sci.ecology.populations.L5`

**Summary.** One bacterium can split in two in about 20 minutes. If all her daughters kept splitting, they would soon outweigh the Earth. Yet that never happens. In this unit you will double rice grains square by square, grow a paper forest until it is full, and plot a real island's reindeer.

**Intro.** *Put one grain of rice on the table today. Tomorrow put two, the next day four, and double it every day. On which day would you need more rice than a whole one-kilogram packet holds? Guess the day, and say why you think so.* A one-kilogram packet holds roughly 50,000 grains. Most people guess a day far too late. In the first activity, Doubling Rice, you find the real day with real grains. Living beings multiply in the same doubling way. So here is the question for this unit. If every living being can double, why is the world not buried in bacteria, or rats, or deer? What stops them, and at what number?

## Terms
- **Population**: A population is all the members of one species who live in one place at one time. *e.g.* All the house crows of Chennai are one population. All the chital of Mudumalai are another.
- **Birth rate and death rate**: The birth rate is how many young are born in a population in one year. The death rate is how many members die in one year. *e.g.* If 20 kids are born in a goat herd in a year and 5 goats die, the herd grows by 15.
- **Immigration and emigration**: Immigration is members arriving from another place. Emigration is members leaving for another place. *e.g.* A young male tiger who walks from one forest to another emigrates from the first and immigrates into the second.
- **Exponential growth**: Exponential growth is growth by multiplying. The number is multiplied by the same amount in each equal step of time, for example doubled. *e.g.* 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 is exponential growth. Each number is double the one before.
- **Doubling time**: The doubling time is how long a population takes to become twice as big. *e.g.* Some bacteria in warm, rich food have a doubling time of about 20 minutes.
- **Limiting factor**: A limiting factor is anything a population needs that is in short supply, or anything that removes its members. It holds the number down. *e.g.* For mosquitoes in a dry month, the limiting factor is still water for their eggs.
- **Carrying capacity**: The carrying capacity of a place is the largest number of one species that the place can keep alive year after year. At that number the place is not damaged. *e.g.* A meadow that grows enough grass for 800 tahr through the dry months has a carrying capacity of about 800 tahr.
- **S-shaped curve**: An S-shaped curve is the graph of a population that grows slowly, then fast, then slowly again, and levels off at the carrying capacity. Scientists also call it logistic growth. *e.g.* The line graph from the Full Island Test is an S-shaped curve.
- **J-shaped curve**: A J-shaped curve is the graph of a population that keeps multiplying with nothing yet holding it back. It bends upward more and more steeply. *e.g.* Rabbits set free in Australia in the 1800s grew on a J-shaped curve for their first decades there.
- **Overshoot**: An overshoot happens when a population grows past the carrying capacity. The members then eat or use up more than the place can grow back. *e.g.* Reindeer on St Matthew Island overshot. They ate the slow-growing lichen faster than it could grow back.
- **Crash**: A crash is a fast fall in a population, when many members die in a short time. *e.g.* After the overshoot, the reindeer on St Matthew Island crashed from about 6,000 to 42.
- **Population density**: Population density is how many members live in a fixed area. It tells you how crowded they are. *e.g.* Ten tigers in 100 square kilometres is a high density for tigers.
- **Density-dependent factor**: A density-dependent factor is a limit that presses harder when a population is more crowded. *e.g.* A disease spreads faster in a crowded herd, because sick and healthy members meet more often.
- **Predator and prey**: A predator is an animal who hunts and eats other animals. The prey is the animal who is hunted. *e.g.* A rat snake is a predator of field rats. The rats are her prey.
- **Population cycle**: A population cycle is a rise and fall in number that repeats again and again, with about the same time between peaks. *e.g.* In northern Canada the number of snowshoe hares peaks about every ten years.
- **Time lag**: A time lag is a delay between a cause and its effect. *e.g.* Predators become many only after their prey has become many. The gap between the two peaks is the time lag.
- **Refuge**: A refuge is a place where prey are safe from their predators. *e.g.* Burrows, thorn bushes and thick grass are refuges for field rats.
- **Sample**: A sample is a small part of a group that you count or measure. From it you work out something about the whole group. *e.g.* Forty beans taken from a big bowl without looking are a sample of the beans in the bowl.
- **Mark and recapture**: Mark and recapture is a way to estimate a population. You mark some members, let them mix back in, and see what share of a later sample is marked. *e.g.* Tigers are told apart by their stripes in camera photos. The stripes are their marks.
- **Sustainable yield**: A sustainable yield is an amount you can take from a population every year without making the population smaller. *e.g.* If a pond's fish add 9 new fish each year, catching 9 each year is a sustainable yield.

## Explanations
### A population, and the four ways its number changes · *defined*
**Definition:** A population is all the members of one species who live in one place at one time. Its number changes in only four ways: births, deaths, members arriving and members leaving.

Scientists do not study 'tigers' in general. They study the tigers of one forest, because those tigers share the same food and meet each other. That group is a population. Births and arrivals make it bigger. Deaths and departures make it smaller. If more are added than removed, the population grows. If the two match, the number stays the same, even though the members keep changing. So every question about a population comes down to one thing. What is changing the births, the deaths, the arrivals or the departures?
- e.g. The house sparrows of one street in Madurai are a population. A sparrow who moves to the next street has emigrated.
- e.g. A pot of curd holds a population of bacteria. No bacteria arrive or leave, so only births and deaths change their number.
- e.g. India's tiger numbers are worked out one forest at a time, because each forest holds its own population.
### Doubling: why growth by multiplying is so fast · *defined*
**Definition:** Exponential growth is growth by multiplying. When each member leaves more than one young who lives, the population is multiplied by the same amount in each generation.

Growth by adding is steady: 2, 4, 6, 8. Growth by multiplying starts slowly and then races: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. Why does a living population multiply and not add? Because the young grow up and have young of their own. Every new member is also a new parent. A bacterium splits into two, and both daughters split again. In warm, rich food, the gut bacterium Escherichia coli can do this about every 20 minutes. Starting from one cell, that would be more than the weight of the Earth in about two days. This sum is true, and it has never happened. So something always stops the doubling. On a graph, growth by multiplying makes a J-shaped curve.
- e.g. In Doubling Rice, square 11 needed 1,024 grains and square 17 needed more than a one-kilogram packet.
- e.g. European rabbits were set free in Australia in the 1800s. Within some decades they had spread across most of the continent.
- e.g. Milk left out on a warm day turns sour within hours, because the bacteria in it double again and again.
### Limiting factors: what holds a number down · *defined*
**Definition:** A limiting factor is anything a population needs that is in short supply, or anything that removes its members.

Every living being needs energy, water, certain atoms such as nitrogen, and a place to live. When any one of these runs short, fewer young are born or more members die. The main limiting factors are food, water, space, light for plants, nesting places, predators, disease and the members' own waste. Why does only one factor matter at a time? Because growth stops at the first need that runs out, even if all the others are plentiful. A crop with plenty of water and sunlight still stays small if the soil has too little nitrogen. The German farm scientist Carl Sprengel wrote this in 1828, and the chemist Justus von Liebig made it famous in 1840. It is called the law of the minimum.
- e.g. Yeast in sugar water slows down as the sugar runs out and as the alcohol the yeast makes builds up.
- e.g. In a city, pigeons are limited mostly by food. Where people scatter grain every day, there are more pigeons.
- e.g. Under a thick banyan, almost no seedlings grow, because the limiting factor there is light.
### Carrying capacity and the S-shaped curve · *defined*
**Definition:** The carrying capacity of a place is the largest number of one species that the place can keep alive year after year. At that number the place is not damaged. A population that grows up to it and levels off makes an S-shaped curve.

In the Full Island Test, the forest began by nearly doubling. Then more and more seeds landed on squares that already had a tree, and those seeds died. At 36 trees every seed died, and the number stopped. Nothing told the trees to stop. Each tree did the same thing in every round. The number stopped because the island had 36 squares. That number, 36, was the island's carrying capacity. Scientists write it as the letter K. The Belgian mathematician Pierre-François Verhulst described this S-shaped growth in the 1830s and 1840s and named it logistic growth. In 1934 the Russian biologist Georgy Gause grew yeast and tiny pond animals in glass tubes. He counted them every day, and their numbers followed the S-shaped curve. Our test is simpler than a real forest. Real trees die, and real seeds can wait in the soil.
- e.g. Curd sets overnight and then stops getting much more sour. The bacteria have used much of the milk sugar, and their own acid slows them.
- e.g. The rhinos of Kaziranga were about a dozen in 1908. In 2022 the count was 2,613, and it rose by only 200 in the four years before.
- e.g. On the half island, the forest levelled off near 18 trees, because there were only 18 squares.
### Why growth is fastest in the middle · *defined*
**Definition:** A population adds the most new members when it is about half way to its carrying capacity.

Look at your Full Island table. In the first rounds almost every seed lived, but there were few trees to drop seeds. In the last rounds there were many trees, but almost every seed died. In the middle there were many parents and still many empty squares. That is when the most new trees were added in one round. Two things are multiplied together here: the number of parents, and the share of young who find room. The first goes up as the second goes down. Their product is largest in the middle. This matters to anyone who harvests a wild population. A fish population that is kept near half its carrying capacity adds the most new fish each year. This is a result of the simple model. Real fish populations are harder to measure, so real fishing rules need a safety margin.
- e.g. On the 36-square island, the biggest gain in one round came when there were about 18 trees.
- e.g. A full pond adds almost no new fish. A nearly empty pond also adds few. A half-full pond adds the most.
- e.g. A bamboo clump spreads fastest after it is well grown but before the clumps touch each other.
### Limits that press harder in a crowd · *defined*
**Definition:** A density-dependent factor is a limit that presses harder when a population is more crowded. A density-independent factor acts the same whether the members are few or many.

Why does a population settle near one number, and not drift anywhere? Because some limits grow stronger as the crowd grows. When members are crowded, each one gets less food. Disease passes more easily, because sick and healthy members meet more often. Predators gather where prey is easy to find. So in a crowd, births fall and deaths rise, and the number comes down. When members are few, each gets plenty, and the number goes up. This pushes the population back toward the carrying capacity from both sides. Other events do not care about crowding. A cyclone, a frost or a forest fire kills a share of the members whether they are few or many. Those events knock a population down, but they do not hold it at one number.
- e.g. In a crowded poultry shed, one sick hen can pass an illness to hundreds. In a small backyard flock the illness often stops.
- e.g. A cyclone on the Odisha coast fells coconut palms in thick groves and thin groves alike.
- e.g. In the Meadow Game, disease struck only herds of nine or more.
### Overshoot and crash · *debated*
**Definition:** An overshoot happens when a population grows past the carrying capacity. If the members damage what they live on, the number then falls very fast, which is called a crash.

In 1944, 29 reindeer were put on St Matthew Island, off Alaska. The island had thick lichen, which reindeer eat in winter, and no wolves. By 1957 there were about 1,350 reindeer, and in 1963 about 6,000. In the winter that followed, nearly all of them died. In 1966 the biologist David Klein counted 42 living reindeer among thousands of skeletons. Why did the number not level off gently, as in the Full Island Test? Because lichen grows back very slowly, over many years. The reindeer could keep multiplying on the stored lichen of past years after they had passed the number the island could feed. The limit arrived late, and all at once. Scientists agree on these counts. They do not fully agree on the cause of the deaths.
- e.g. Yeast in a closed bottle of sugar water multiply, then die off when the sugar is gone and the alcohol is strong.
- e.g. In Mizoram, rats multiply when the bamboo fruits, and die back when the fruit is finished.
- e.g. Chital brought to the Andaman Islands by the British have no large predators there. Studies find fewer young plants where the deer are many.
- **David Klein, who counted the reindeer in 1957, 1963 and 1966:** The herd had eaten the lichen down. The reindeer went into the hard winter of 1963 to 1964 thin and short of food. The overshoot and the weather together caused the crash.
- **Later researchers who studied the weather records of that winter:** The winter was one of the worst on record, with deep snow and strong winds. They argue that the weather was the main cause, and that many reindeer would have died even with more lichen.
### Carrying capacity is not one fixed number · *defined*
**Definition:** The carrying capacity of a place is not fixed. It changes with the seasons and with the weather from year to year. It also changes with what other living beings, including people, do to the place.

A hillside grows much more grass in the monsoon than in the dry months. So which season sets the carrying capacity? The hardest one. A herd must get through the hungriest weeks of the year, so the food of those weeks sets the number. A year of good rain raises the capacity, and a drought lowers it. Other species change it too. More deer on the same grass means less for each herd. People raise the capacity for some species and lower it for others. A rubbish heap raises it for street dogs and crows. A cut forest lowers it for hornbills. This is why scientists treat carrying capacity as a number that moves, and why real populations wobble around it.
- e.g. After a good monsoon in Rajasthan, there are more blackbuck fawns the next year.
- e.g. Open rubbish in a city raises the carrying capacity for rats, crows and street dogs.
- e.g. On the half island, the capacity fell from 36 to 18 when half the squares were taken away.
### Predator and prey: why the numbers rise and fall · *defined*
**Definition:** When a predator depends mostly on one kind of prey, the two numbers can rise and fall again and again. The predator's peak comes after the prey's peak.

In the Rats and Snakes Game, the rats multiplied first, because one snake caught few of them. With many rats, each snake caught enough to live and to raise young. So the snakes multiplied next. Many snakes then caught rats faster than the rats could breed, and the rats fell. With few rats, most snakes caught too little and died. With few snakes, the rats began to rise again. Why is the predator always late? Because predators can multiply only after there is plenty of prey, and raising young takes time. And they starve only after the prey has already fallen. This delay is called a time lag. The delay is what keeps the two numbers swinging. In 1925 and 1926 Alfred Lotka and Vito Volterra each wrote equations for this, and the equations make two waves, one behind the other.
- e.g. Fur traders' records from Canada show hare numbers peaking about every ten years, with lynx peaking a year or two later.
- e.g. In a paddy field, rat snakes and owls become more common a season after the rats do.
- e.g. Ladybird beetles arrive on a rose bush after the aphids have multiplied, and leave after the aphids are eaten.
### What really causes the waves? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists still test what drives the rise and fall of hares and other animals with cycles. It may be their predators, their food, or both together.

The simple story says the predator alone drives the waves. Real populations are less tidy. In the Yukon in Canada, Charles Krebs and his team fenced large areas of forest for eight years. In some they gave the hares extra food. In some they kept out lynx and coyotes. In some they did both. Extra food alone raised hare numbers, and so did fewer predators alone. Doing both raised them far more than the two effects added together. So the hares' own food and their predators act together. On Isle Royale, an island in a North American lake, scientists have counted wolves and moose every winter since 1958. The two numbers do swing, but not in neat waves. Hard winters, ticks and the wolves' small family tree have all changed the counts.
- e.g. On Isle Royale the wolves fell to two animals by 2016. Nineteen wolves were brought in during 2018 and 2019, and by the 2026 winter count there were 37.
- e.g. In India, the number of tigers a forest holds follows the number of deer, pigs and gaur there.
- e.g. Many populations do not cycle at all. Most birds and large mammals stay near one number.
- **The predator view:** Predators cause the hare cycle. Most hares that die in the falling years are killed by lynx, coyotes, owls and hawks.
- **The food view:** At the peak the hares eat the best twigs, and the plants that are left are poor food. Hungry hares breed less and are easier to catch.
- **Krebs and his team, 1995:** Neither alone explains it. Food and predators together had a much bigger effect than either one. The cycle comes from three levels acting on each other: plants, hares and predators.
### Why hunter and hunted do not wipe each other out · *defined*
**Definition:** Prey survive alongside their predators when some prey are always out of reach, in refuges or in places the predators have not found yet.

If a predator is very good at hunting, why does she not eat every last prey animal and then starve? In a small, simple space, that is exactly what happens. Georgy Gause put a hunting pond animal called Didinium in a tube with her prey, Paramecium. The hunters ate every one and then starved. When he gave the prey a place to hide, some prey lived on. When he added a few new animals of each kind every few days, the numbers rose and fell in waves. In 1958 Carl Huffaker tried with two kinds of mites on trays of oranges. In a simple tray, the hunting mites ate all the prey mites and died. When he made the tray large and patchy, with sticky barriers that slowed the hunters, the two rose and fell three times. So lasting waves need a world with hiding places and distance.
- e.g. Field rats have burrows. Deer have thick lantana and steep slopes. Small fish have weeds.
- e.g. In the Rats and Snakes Game, a new snake and new rats 'arrive from the next field' when one side dies out. Without that rule the game soon ends.
- e.g. A tank with one big fish and a few small ones needs plants, or the small fish are all eaten.
### Counting a population you cannot see all at once · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists estimate a population from samples. In mark and recapture, they mark some members, let them mix back in, and see what share of a later sample is marked.

Nobody can line up all the tigers of a forest and count them. In Count Without Counting, you marked 40 beans and mixed them back into the bowl. Then you took a new sample. If a quarter of that sample was marked, then your 40 marked beans were about a quarter of the bowl. So the bowl held about 160. Tigers carry their own marks, because no two tigers have the same stripes. In the 1990s the scientist K. Ullas Karanth set automatic cameras on forest paths at Nagarahole in Karnataka. He treated each photograph as a capture. India's national tiger estimate now uses the same idea. In 2022 it used cameras at more than 32,000 places and estimated about 3,682 tigers. An estimate is not an exact count. That is why the report also gives a range, from about 3,167 to 3,925.
- e.g. Fish in a tank are estimated by netting some, clipping a tiny mark on a fin, and netting again later.
- e.g. The Nilgiri tahr of Eravikulam are counted each April by teams who walk every block of the park on the same days.
- e.g. To estimate the ants of a nest, scientists mark some with a dot of paint and count the share of marked ants later.
### How many people can the Earth carry? · *debated*
**Definition:** Nobody knows one number for how many people the Earth can carry. The number depends on how people live.

People are animals, and the same four things change our number. There were about 1 billion people around 1804, and 8 billion in November 2022. The second billion took more than a hundred years to add. The eighth took about eleven. The growth is now slowing, mainly because families are having fewer children. In India a woman has about 2 children on average, according to the national health survey of 2019 to 2021. The United Nations expects the world's number to peak at about 10.3 billion in the 2080s. Does the Earth have a carrying capacity for people? In 1995 the scientist Joel Cohen collected the estimates that others had published. They ranged from under 1 billion to more than 1,000 billion. The estimates differ so much because the answer depends on how people choose to live.
- e.g. A person who eats mostly plants needs much less farmland than a person who eats meat every day.
- e.g. Fertiliser, canals and new seeds raised the amount of rice and wheat an acre can grow. That raised the Earth's capacity for people.
- e.g. A family uses the land and water of many far places, through the food, cloth and fuel they buy.
- **Thomas Malthus, 1798:** People multiply faster than food can be increased. So hunger and disease will always hold the number down.
- **Ester Boserup, 1965:** When people become crowded, they invent better ways to farm. More people can mean more food from the same land, so the limit moves.
- **Donella Meadows and her team, 1972:** On a finite planet, the use of soil, water and fuel cannot grow for ever. If it overshoots what the Earth renews, the fall can be sudden.
- **Joel Cohen, 1995:** There is no single number. It depends on what people eat, what they throw away, and how fairly they share. Those are choices, so science alone cannot answer the question.
### Under every limit: energy and atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** In the end, a population is limited by two things in the place where its members live. One is the flow of energy. The other is the supply of certain atoms.

Follow any limiting factor down and you reach energy or atoms. Food is energy caught from sunlight by plants, with atoms of carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus built in. A square metre of ground receives only so much sunlight in a year, and a plant catches only a small part of it. Each eater passes on only about a tenth of the energy to the next eater. So a place can hold many plants, fewer plant eaters and very few hunters. The Earth as a whole receives a fixed amount of sunlight. That sets a ceiling on almost all the life it can hold at one time. A little life near hot vents in the deep sea runs on chemical energy from rocks instead. No living being can multiply past the energy and atoms that reach them. This is why the doubling sum for bacteria can never come true.
- e.g. A tiger needs about 50 large prey animals a year, so a forest with few deer can hold very few tigers.
- e.g. Farm ponds turn green when fertiliser washes in, because nitrogen and phosphorus were the limit for the algae.
- e.g. The open ocean far from land holds little life, because the water there has very little iron and nitrogen.
### Bacteria sense how crowded they are · *defined*
**Definition:** Many bacteria release a chemical signal and sense how much of it is around them. This tells them how many of their own kind are near. It is called quorum sensing.

In 1970 Kenneth Nealson, Terry Platt and Woodland Hastings studied a sea bacterium who can make light. A few of these bacteria in a flask stayed dark. When they became crowded, they all began to glow together. Each bacterium gives off a little of a signal chemical. In a crowd the chemical builds up, and the bacteria sense it and change what they do. Bonnie Bassler and others later showed that very many kinds of bacteria do this. So bacteria take a kind of count of themselves and decide together. A population is not only pushed about by hunger and enemies. Its members also sense each other. Some scientists describe this as chemistry switching genes on and off, with no deciding in it.
- e.g. The glowing bacteria live inside a small squid, who uses their light to hide her shadow on moonlit nights.
- e.g. Some bacteria that cause illness wait until they are many before they attack, and they find out by quorum sensing.
- e.g. Bacteria on your teeth build a slimy coat together, and signals between them help them do it.

## Mostly agreed
- No population can grow by multiplying for ever. Sooner or later something its members need runs short. *How we know:* Every population ever followed, from yeast in a tube to reindeer on an island, has slowed, stopped or crashed. The doubling sum shows why it must.
- A population's number changes only through births, deaths, arrivals and departures. *How we know:* Animals have been counted over many years, such as the wolves and moose of Isle Royale since 1958. Every change in such counts can be explained by these four.
- In a simple, closed space, a growing population follows an S-shaped curve and levels off. *How we know:* Georgy Gause counted yeast and Paramecium in glass tubes every day in the early 1930s. Many laboratories have repeated this since.
- Crowding raises deaths or lowers births in most populations that have been studied closely. *How we know:* Field studies of birds, deer, fish and insects find fewer surviving young per parent in crowded years than in sparse years.
- The number of large predators in a place depends on the number of their prey. *How we know:* At 11 sites across India, the measured tiger density rose with the measured density of deer, pigs and wild cattle (Karanth and others, 2004).
- Predators and prey last together only where some prey are always out of reach. *How we know:* Gause's tubes in the 1930s and Huffaker's trays of oranges in 1958 both showed this. With no hiding places, the hunters ate all the prey and starved.

## How we found out
**Umberto D'Ancona and Vito Volterra in Italy, Alfred Lotka in the United States, Georgy Gause in Moscow, and Carl Huffaker in California., 1925 and 1926 (the equations), 1934 (Gause's book The Struggle for Existence) and 1958 (Huffaker's mites).** During the First World War, fishing in the Adriatic Sea almost stopped. Afterwards the Italian biologist Umberto D'Ancona studied the fish market records. He found that sharks and other hunting fish had become a larger share of the catch. He asked his father-in-law, the mathematician Vito Volterra, why. In 1926 Volterra wrote two equations, one for the hunted and one for the hunters. They gave two waves that rise and fall for ever. Alfred Lotka in America had written the same equations a little earlier. Many people then believed that any hunter and prey kept together would swing like this. In Moscow, Georgy Gause tested the idea in glass tubes with two tiny pond animals. There were no waves. The hunters ate all the prey and starved. Waves appeared only when he added a few new animals every few days. In 1958 Carl Huffaker got three waves from mites on oranges, but only on a large, patchy tray.  
*What people believed before:* That a hunter and the hunted, kept together, will always rise and fall in waves, as the equations say. Gause's tubes showed that in a small, simple space the hunters eat everything and both die out. Waves need hiding places, distance or new arrivals.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Nature is in balance, so the numbers of wild animals stay the same.** (A very old and common belief; *debated*)  
  For: Many populations of birds and large mammals do stay near one number for years, as the tahr of Eravikulam have.  
  Against: Hares, lemmings, locusts and rats rise and crash. Even steady populations wobble with the weather. A steady number is held there by births and deaths that keep changing, not by stillness.
- **Weather, not crowding, decides the numbers of most animals.** (The Australian ecologists Herbert Andrewartha and Charles Birch, 1954; *debated*)  
  For: They studied tiny insects called thrips on roses for years. Rain and heat explained most of the rises and falls. The insects were rarely crowded.  
  Against: Weather can knock a number down, but it cannot hold a number near one level. Most ecologists now think both act: weather shakes the number, and crowding pulls it back.
- **More people will always mean more hunger.** (Thomas Malthus, 1798; *debated*)  
  For: A population can multiply, while farmland can only be added to. Famines have happened where people outgrew their food.  
  Against: Since 1798 the number of people has grown about eight times, and food per person has grown too. Ester Boserup showed in 1965 that crowded farmers find ways to grow more. But soil, water and a steady climate are not without limit.
- **The best way to have fewer pests is to kill as many as you can.** (Common sense, and many pest sprayers; *rejected*)  
  For: Killing does lower the number for a while.  
  Against: If the food and breeding places stay, the survivors have plenty and multiply back to the same carrying capacity. Taking away the standing water lowers the capacity for mosquitoes, and that lasts.
- **A carrying capacity is a tidy idea on paper. Real places do not have one.** (Some field ecologists; *minority*)  
  For: Rain, fire and other species change the limit every year. In many places the population never reaches it, so it cannot be measured.  
  Against: Even a limit that moves is still a limit. The idea explains why a herd left alone does not fill the hillside. It works best as a range, not as one number.

## Roots
We have learned that every population stops growing at a limit set by the place its members live in. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Full Island Test, every tree dropped one seed in every round, from first to last. The forest still stopped at 36 trees, and at 18 on the half island. No tree chose that number, and no tree changed what they did. How many there are of any living being is not decided inside that being. It is set by what the place can give to all of them together.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'there are 3,682 tigers' as if the number belonged to tigers, the way stripes do. But the number belongs to the forests, the deer and the tigers together. Change the place and the number changes, with the same animals.  
*Evidence:* In the Full Island Test the rule for the trees never changed. Yet the forest levelled off at 36 on the full island and near 18 on the half island. In India, the number of tigers in a forest follows the number of their prey.

- *who am i:* You are one of about 8 billion people. That number, like the 36 trees, is held up by soil, rain, sunlight and other living beings.
- *what is life:* Every living being can multiply without end, and none ever does. Life is always pressing against what the place can give.
- *what is this world:* The world is not a box that living beings fill. The beings and the place set each other's numbers, as the rats and the snakes did.

One bacterium becomes two, two become four, and so the one becomes many. But the many never become endless. The sunlight, water and atoms of one place are shared out among all who live there. So each population's number is its share of one supply. When one number rises, others must fall. Counting any one species is really measuring a part of the whole place.

- **Thirukkural 479 (paraphrased):** “அளவறிந்து வாழாதான் வாழ்க்கை உளபோல / இல்லாகித் தோன்றாக் கெடும்.” The life of a person who lives without knowing their measure may look as if it is doing well. Then it is gone, and nothing is left to show for it. (Valluvar wrote this about a person's wealth. The reindeer of St Matthew Island lived it. The herd looked as if it was thriving until the year it vanished, because it had gone past the measure of the island.)
- **Tao Te Ching 77 (paraphrased):** “天之道，損有餘而補不足。” The way of nature is like the bending of a bow. The high end is brought down and the low end is raised. What has too much is reduced, and what has too little is added to. (This is how a crowded population and a sparse one behave. In a crowd, deaths rise and births fall. Where members are few, each has plenty and the number grows. Both are pulled toward the carrying capacity.)
- Science (settled): Populations in closed spaces level off at a carrying capacity, and crowding raises deaths or lowers births (Verhulst, 1838; Gause, 1934).
- Science (settled): Bacteria sense how crowded they are through chemical signals and change what they do together (Nealson, Platt and Hastings, 1970; Bassler and others).
- Science (debated): Whether the Earth has a carrying capacity for people, and what it is, depends on how people live (Cohen, 1995).
- Other view, Many biologists: A population's number is only a sum. Each animal eats, breeds and dies on their own account, and nothing is shared out or held together. 'The place sets the number' is a short way of describing millions of separate lives. Charvaka thinkers of ancient India would agree: only the separate things we can see are real, and the whole is just a word.
- Practice, The feeling of enough (3 min): Sit with a tumbler of water. Take one small sip and wait for one breath. Take another sip, and wait again. Keep going slowly. Notice the moment when your body says that is enough. You did not count the sips. Your body sensed its own limit. A population finds its limit in the same way, by sensing, not by counting.
- Ask: The forest stopped at 36 though no tree decided to stop. So who or what decided?
- Ask: Your family eats rice grown in a field far away. Is that field part of the place that carries you?
- Ask: If the number of tigers belongs to the forest and the deer too, what are we saving when we 'save the tiger'?
- Ask: People can choose how much each of us uses. Does that make our limit different from a deer's limit, or the same?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the Full Island Test the forest stopped at 36. Was the number 36 a fact about the trees, or about the island? (hint: What happened to the number when you halved the island but kept the same trees?)
- *deep:* A tigress has cubs and the deer are few. Is 'too many tigers' a fact about tigers, or about deer? (hint: Think of what you would have to count to know.)
- *deep:* The rats fed the snakes, and the snakes held back the rats. Which of the two was in charge? (hint: Look at your graph from the Rats and Snakes Game. Does one line ever move without the other?)
- *deep:* If a population has no number that it stays at, what do we mean when we say a species is 'doing well'? (hint: Think of the hares in Canada, whose number changes tenfold and back in ten years.)
- *big:* The Earth's limit for people depends on how people live. So who should decide how people live? (hint: A person who uses a lot leaves less room for others, including other species.)
- *big:* When people raise the carrying capacity for themselves, what happens to the capacity for everyone else? (hint: Think of a forest turned into a paddy field. Who gained room, and who lost it?)
- *big:* Bacteria sense how many of them there are. Is a population a crowd of separate beings, or one being with many bodies? (hint: Think of the bacteria who glow only together, and of a beehive or an ant nest.)

## Story: The Count on the Hill
*A made-up story set in a real place. Eravikulam National Park, near Munnar in Kerala, is real, and so are its Nilgiri tahr, who are wild mountain goats found only in the hills of South India. The park does close in February and March, when the kids are born, and the tahr are counted each April. The 2025 count was 841 tahr in the park, 827 the year before, and 894 in 2014, with more than a hundred kids born in a year. Ponni, her mother Selvi, her cousin Karthik and what they say and do are imagined.*

Ponni kept the numbers in the back of her maths notebook, where nobody would look.

Her amma, Selvi, was a watcher at Eravikulam. Every April the watchers and the forest officers walked the whole park for several days and counted the tahr. Every April, Amma came home with sore feet and a number, and Ponni wrote the number down.

This year Ponni was ten, and for the first time she had a second number. In March, while the park was closed to visitors, Amma had taken her up the Rajamala road to see the new kids. They were the colour of wet sand, and they jumped on rocks that Ponni would not have climbed for money. The officers said more than a hundred had been born on the hills that season.

So Ponni had done the sum herself. Last April, a little over eight hundred tahr. Add more than a hundred kids. This April there should be well over nine hundred.

Amma came home on the last evening and sat down on the step to pull off her boots.

'How many?' said Ponni.

'A little over eight hundred,' said Amma. 'Fourteen more than last year.'

Ponni looked at her notebook. 'That is wrong,' she said. 'You have missed a hundred.'

Amma laughed, but not unkindly. 'We walked every block. Two teams, every slope. We did not miss a hundred tahr, Ponni. They are not small.'

'Then where are the kids?'

Amma rubbed her foot and did not answer. That was her way. When Ponni asked a question that had a long answer, Amma waited to see how far Ponni would get on her own.

Her cousin Karthik was staying that week, down from Coimbatore. He was twelve and had opinions. 'Somebody is stealing them,' he said. 'Or the count is lazy. Eight hundred, every year? Nothing stays the same every year. In ten years there should be thousands. The whole of Munnar should be full of goats.'

'Tahr,' said Ponni.

'Goats,' said Karthik.

The next morning Amma had to check a path on the high slope, and she took them both. They climbed for two hours through grass that came up to Ponni's knees. It was green now. The first summer showers had come.

'In March this was the colour of a coir mat,' said Amma. 'In January it had frost on it.'

Near a stream they found a tahr lying under a rock ledge. Karthik thought she was asleep. She was very old, a female with a grey muzzle, and she had died some days before. Ponni did not want to look, and then she looked. Amma crouched and gently lifted the lip with a stick.

'See her teeth,' she said. They were worn almost flat. 'She could not grind dry grass any more. In the green months she managed. She did not get through the dry ones.'

Further up, on a path, there was a dropping full of hair, pale and stiff as bristles. 'Leopard,' said Amma. 'That is a kid's hair.'

Karthik had gone quiet. Ponni was counting something in her head, but it was not tahr.

At the top they sat on a rock and ate lemon rice from a steel box. Below them a herd of thirty or forty tahr grazed on a slope so steep it looked like a wall. The kids of this year stayed close to their mothers. There were not as many as Ponni remembered from March.

'Amma,' she said slowly. 'How much grass is there in January?'

'Ah,' said Amma, and put down her spoon.

'Because that is the real count,' said Ponni. 'Not the tahr. The grass. In the rains there is enough here for many more. But they have to get through January too. However many are born, the hill only feeds about eight hundred through the dry months. So the old ones die, and the weak kids, and the leopard takes some. And in April you count what the hill kept.'

'Then the forest department should bring fodder in January,' said Karthik. 'Lorries of it. Then you would have your thousands.'

Ponni thought about that. It sounded right, and it annoyed her that she could not at once say why it was not.

'And the year after?' she said at last. 'The thousands have kids. So you bring more lorries. And they eat this slope bare in between, because there are too many feet on it. And the first year the lorries do not come...' She did not finish.

Amma was looking at her with an expression Ponni had not seen before.

'There is one way to have more tahr,' Amma said. 'More hill. They once lived all along these mountains. The grasslands were planted over with tea and with wattle trees. Where a grassland is given back, the tahr can come back to it. That is the only kind of rise that lasts.'

They walked down in the afternoon. That night Ponni opened the back of her maths notebook. She crossed out 'TAHR' at the top of the page.

She wrote 'WHAT THE HILL CAN FEED'. Then she wrote this year's number under it, the same as before.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the story turns when Ponni stops asking where the kids went and asks how much grass there is in January. A population's number is set by what the place can give in its hardest season. That number is the carrying capacity. Births above it are matched by deaths. Feeding a herd past the capacity only postpones a bigger fall. More living room is what raises the number for good. Let your child find this from the questions, not from you.

- Ask: More than a hundred kids were born, yet the count hardly changed. What does that tell you about deaths on the hill that year?
- Ask: Why does January matter more than the rainy months for the number of tahr?
- Ask: What would happen in the years after, if lorries of fodder were brought every January?
- Ask: What is the one change that would let more tahr live in these hills for good, and why?
- Ask: Is there a place near you where the number of some animal seems to stay the same every year? What might be holding it there?

## Games
### g1 · The Meadow Game: how many chital should stay? (25 min)
- **rules:** Each player draws a meadow of 12 squares and puts 3 beans on it. Each bean is a chital. One bean sits on one square.; A turn is one year, and it has three parts. First, fawns are born: add 1 bean for every 2 chital on your meadow. With 3 chital you add 1. With 8 you add 4.; Second, you may send any number of chital away to start new herds. Move those beans into your 'new herds' circle. They are safe there and they score.; Third, roll the die and read the year card. Do what it says.; Last, count your herd. If there are more chital than the meadow can feed this year, the extra ones die. Two more die as well, because the grass was eaten down to the roots.; After ten years, add the beans in your circle to the beans on your meadow. The highest total wins. If you play alone, 25 or more is a strong score.
- **Printable board: A meadow for each player: 12 grass squares in 3 rows of 4, and a circle beside them marked 'new herds':** Meadow for player 1; Meadow for player 2; Meadow for player 3; Meadow for player 4
- **Printable score sheet: The year card: what each roll of the die means:** 1: Drought. This year the meadow feeds only 6.; 2: A leopard takes 1 chital.; 3: An ordinary year. Nothing happens.; 4: An ordinary year. Nothing happens.; 5: Good rain. Add 1 extra fawn.; 6: Disease. A herd of 9 or more loses half. A smaller herd loses none.; Too many for the grass? The extra chital die, and 2 more with them.
- **Printable score sheet: Score table for ten years:** Year; Herd at the start; Fawns born; Sent to new herds; Die roll; Herd at the end
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give each player a sheet of paper. 2. Draw 12 squares in 3 rows of 4, and a circle beside them. 3. Copy the year card where everyone can see it. 4. Put 3 beans on each meadow.  
  *Parent says:* Each bean is a chital on your meadow. The meadow grows enough grass for 12. Your job is to send out as many chital as you can in ten years.  
  *Child mode:* Draw 12 squares and a circle. Copy the year card. Put 3 beans on your meadow.
  - Note: Rajma, chana or small buttons all work as chital. Each player needs about 30.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play one year together as practice. 2. Add the fawns. 3. Ask each player how many chital they will send away, and why. 4. Roll the die and read the year card.  
  *Parent says:* Before you roll, decide how many to send away. A big herd has more fawns. But what could go wrong for a big herd? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Add 1 fawn for every 2 chital. Choose how many to send away. Say why. Then roll.
  - Ask: What could go wrong for a herd of 12? (good: A drought year feeds only 6, so 6 die and 2 more.; Disease takes half of a herd of 9 or more.; The fawns born next year will be too many for the grass.)
  - Note: Let each player choose freely. Some will keep every chital, and some will send them all away. Both choices teach something by year four.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play ten years. 2. Each year, write the numbers in the score table. 3. After year five, stop and ask who is ahead, and what size of herd they keep.  
  *Parent says:* We are half way. Look at the tables. Who has sent out the most? How big a herd do they keep on the meadow?  
  *Child mode:* Play ten years. Fill in your table each year. At year five, see who is ahead.
  - Note: Players who keep 6 to 8 chital usually do best. A herd of 12 is hit by drought, by disease and by its own fawns. A herd of 2 has almost no fawns.
  - Check: A herd of 12 has 6 fawns. The meadow feeds 12. What must the player do to lose none? → **Send away at least 6 chital**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add up each player's circle and meadow. 2. Compare the tables. 3. Ask what size of herd sent out the most chital. 4. Ask why a full meadow did not do best.  
  *Parent says:* Whose herd sent out the most? Was it the biggest herd, the smallest, or one in between? Why do you think that happened?  
  *Child mode:* Add up your beans. Which herd size sent out the most? Say why.
  - Ask: Why did a herd kept near the middle send out the most chital? (good: It had enough parents for many fawns.; It still had spare grass, so a drought or a few extra fawns did not kill any.; It was under 9, so disease did not halve it.)
  - Think: A fishing village wants the biggest catch every year, for ever. How full should they keep the sea?
### g2 · Which Curve? (15 min)
- **rules:** Draw the four curves on four pieces of paper, one on each. Lay them in a row on the table.; Shuffle the 16 story cards and put them in a pile, story side up.; One player reads the top card aloud. On the count of three, everyone puts a finger on the curve they think fits.; Turn the card over and read the answer. Each player who chose that curve wins a bean.; A player who can say what set the limit, or what caused the fall, wins a second bean.; When the pile is finished, the player with the most beans wins. If you play alone, 24 beans or more is a strong score.
- **Printable cards: 16 story cards to cut out (the answer is on the back):** One bacterium lands in a pot of warm milk. For the first hours she and her daughters double every half hour. (back: The J. Nothing is holding them back yet, so they multiply.); Yeast are put in a tube of sugar water. They multiply, then slow down, and the number stays level. (back: The S. The yeast grow up to what the tube can carry and level off.); Reindeer are put on an island with no predators. They become thousands, eat the lichen bare, and nearly all die in one winter. (back: The overshoot and crash. They went past the limit and the food was gone.); Hares in a northern forest become very many about every ten years, and very few in between. (back: The wave. The rise and fall repeats again and again.); Duckweed is put in a bucket. The small green plants spread until they cover the water, and then the cover stays the same. (back: The S. The surface of the water is the limit.); A few water hyacinth plants reach a lake. For the first months the patch doubles every few weeks. (back: The J. There is still plenty of open water, so nothing slows them.); Rats multiply when the bamboo fruits. When the fruit is finished they eat the rice crop, and then most of them starve. (back: The overshoot and crash. The extra food ended, and the rats were far too many.); Ladybirds and the aphids they eat on a rose hedge. Each becomes many and then few, summer after summer. (back: The wave. Predator and prey rise and fall one after the other.); Rhinos are protected in a park. They grow from a dozen to over two thousand in a century, and now the count rises only a little. (back: The S. Growth is slowing as the park fills.); Mosquitoes in the first weeks of the monsoon, when every tin and tyre holds water. (back: The J. There are far more breeding places than mosquitoes.); Algae in a pond after fertiliser washes in. The water turns thick green, the algae use up what they need, and they die and rot. (back: The overshoot and crash. The sudden food let them go far past the limit.); Lynx, the wild cats who hunt hares, in a northern forest over fifty years. (back: The wave. Their number follows the hares up and down, a little later.); Barnacles settle on a bare rock in the sea. They cover it until no space is left, and the number stays there. (back: The S. Space on the rock is the limit.); The number of people in the world from the year 1800 to the year 2000. (back: The J. The number grew faster and faster through those 200 years.); Locusts after good rain in the desert. They breed into huge swarms, eat everything green, and the swarms die away. (back: The overshoot and crash. They used up the food that the rain had grown.); Field rats and rat snakes in a paddy village, counted for twenty years. (back: The wave. More rats feed more snakes, and more snakes mean fewer rats.)
- **Printable board: 4 curve cards to draw: the J, the S, the overshoot and crash, and the wave:** The J: a line that bends upward more and more steeply; The S: a line that rises, then flattens under a limit; The overshoot and crash: a line that climbs past a limit, then falls almost to zero; The wave: a line that goes up and down again and again
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the four curves on four pieces of paper. 2. Lay them in a row. 3. Cut out the 16 story cards and shuffle them.  
  *Parent says:* Here are four shapes that a population's graph can take. Each card tells a true kind of story. We will match the story to its shape.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the four curves. Lay them in a row. Shuffle the story cards.
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the top card aloud. 2. Count to three. 3. Everyone points to a curve. 4. Turn the card and read the answer. 5. Ask what set the limit or caused the fall.  
  *Parent says:* Ask yourself two things. Did the number stop rising? And if it fell, did it rise again?  
  *Child mode:* Read the card. Did the number stop rising? Did it fall and rise again? Point to a curve.
  - Ask: How do you tell an S from an overshoot and crash? (good: In the S, the number levels off and stays.; In the overshoot, the number goes past the limit and then falls fast, because the food was used up.)
  - Note: The J cards are all populations that are still far below their limit. If your child asks 'but what happens next?', that is the right question. Every J becomes one of the other three in the end.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the beans. 2. Lay the four J cards together. 3. Ask what each of these populations will do later, and why.  
  *Parent says:* These four are all J curves. Can a J go on for ever? What do you think each one turns into later? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your beans. Find the four J cards. What will each one become later? Say why.
  - Check: A population is growing on a J-shaped curve. What must happen in the end? → **It slows, levels off or crashes**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Full Island Test: does a forest keep doubling for ever? (45 min)
- **question:** If every tree drops one seed in every round, does the forest keep doubling? And what happens to the forest if the island is half the size?
- **predict:** Guess first. You start with 2 trees and play 9 rounds. Will you end with about 18 trees, about 36, or more than 1,000? In which rounds will the forest add the most trees: the first rounds, the middle rounds or the last? Write both guesses and the reason for each.
- **fair test:** Use the same rule in every round: one roll of the dice for each tree that stood at the start of the round. In part 2, change only the size of the island. Start with 2 trees both times. Dice are chance, so no two games are the same. The shape of the graph is what you compare, not the exact numbers.
- **record:** Fill in the table for every round of both parts. Draw both forests on one line graph, in two colours. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
- **results table:** Round | Trees at the start | Seeds that found an empty square | Seeds that died | Trees at the end
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a square 18 centimetres wide on the paper. 2. Divide it into 6 columns and 6 rows, each 3 centimetres wide. 3. Number the columns 1 to 6 along the top and the rows 1 to 6 down the side. 4. Put a bean on any two squares. 5. Draw the results table.  
  *Parent says:* This grid is an island with room for 36 trees, one on each square. Two trees have arrived. Let's draw the table before we start.  
  *Child mode:* Draw 6 columns and 6 rows. Number them 1 to 6. Put a bean on any two squares. Draw the table.
  - Note: If you have one die, roll it twice: the first roll is the column and the second is the row. Decide which die is 'column' before you start, and keep to it.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the rule: each tree drops one seed in every round. 2. Ask for the first guess and its reason. 3. Ask for the second guess and its reason. 4. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Two trees, and each drops a seed every round, for 9 rounds. How many trees will there be at the end? In which rounds will the forest grow fastest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many trees after 9 rounds. Guess which rounds add the most. Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: It doubles each round, so it gets huge.; There are only 36 squares, so it cannot pass 36.; It grows fastest at the end, because there are the most trees then.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children say the forest will keep doubling, or that it will grow fastest at the end. The dice will answer.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the trees and write the number under 'Trees at the start'. 2. Roll the dice once for each of those trees. 3. If the square is empty, put a new bean there. 4. If the square has a bean, the seed dies: make a tally mark. 5. Play rounds 1 to 3 this way.  
  *Parent says:* Roll once for each tree that was here when the round began. A seed needs light. A seed that lands under a grown tree gets too little light and dies.  
  *Child mode:* Count the trees. Roll once for each one. Empty square: add a bean. Full square: the seed dies. Play 3 rounds.
  - Note: A seed that lands on a square filled earlier in the same round also dies. In these first rounds almost every seed lives, so the forest nearly doubles.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play rounds 4 to 9 in the same way. 2. One person rolls and calls the square. The other places the bean and keeps the tally. 3. Fill in the table after each round. 4. Stop early only if all 36 squares are full.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going to round 9. Watch the tally of seeds that died. What is happening to it, round by round?  
  *Child mode:* Play rounds 4 to 9. Fill in the table each round. Watch how many seeds die.
  - Note: These rounds have the most rolls, about 30 each near the end. Working as a pair keeps it quick. Late rounds add only one or two trees, and some add none. That slow end is the finding.
  - Check: The island has 30 trees. Why do most seeds die in this round? → **Most squares already have a tree**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On squared paper, mark rounds 0 to 9 along the bottom. 2. Mark trees from 0 to 40 up the side. 3. Plot the trees at the end of each round and join the points. 4. In the table, find the round that added the most trees.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's turn the table into a picture. Then find the round where the forest added the most trees. How many trees stood at the start of that round?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the graph. Rounds go along the bottom, trees up the side. Find the round that added the most.
  - Ask: What shape is the line? (good: It starts slowly, gets steep in the middle and goes flat at the top, like an S.)
  - Ask: When did the forest add the most trees? (good: In the middle rounds, when it had about 12 to 24 trees.; When about half the squares were full.)
  - Note: The biggest gain usually comes in round 4 or 5, when the island is about half full. Do not explain why yet. Ask your child what they notice about that round.
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the paper so that only rows 1 to 3 show. 2. Change the row rule: a roll of 1 or 2 means row 1, 3 or 4 means row 2, and 5 or 6 means row 3. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. 4. Start with 2 trees and play 8 rounds. 5. Plot this forest on the same graph in another colour.  
  *Parent says:* Half the island has gone under the sea. The trees are the same and the rule is the same. Where will this forest stop? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Fold the island in half. Guess where the forest will stop. Say why. Play 8 rounds. Plot it in a new colour.
  - Note: This part is quick, about 85 rolls in all. The forest levels off at or just under 18 trees.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the graph and the table side by side. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why it happened. 3. Your child writes the three sentences. 4. Only then share the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? And why did the forest stop where it did?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought. Say what you saw. Say why the forest stopped. Then write the three sentences.
  - Ask: Each tree did the same thing in every round. So why did the forest stop growing? (good: The island ran out of empty squares.; More and more seeds landed under trees and died.; The number of squares set the limit, not the trees.)
  - Ask: Why did the half island stop near 18? (good: It had only 18 squares.; The limit comes from the size of the place.)
  - Note: After your child has spoken: the forest stopped at the number of squares. Scientists call that number the carrying capacity of the island. The forest grew fastest in the middle because there were many parent trees and still many empty squares.
- **s8 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what one change would raise the limit of the paper island. 2. Ask what one change would lower it. 3. If there is time, test one idea for 5 rounds.  
  *Parent says:* You are in charge of the island now. What one change would let it hold more trees? What change would make it hold fewer? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Think of one change that raises the limit. Think of one that lowers it. Say why.
  - Think: Real trees die of age and storms. If two trees fell in every round, would the forest stay at 36?
### e2 · The Rats and Snakes Game: what happens to hunters and hunted over many rounds? (55 min)
- **question:** When snakes eat rats and both have young, what happens to the two numbers over 14 rounds?
- **predict:** Guess first. Draw what you think the rat line and the snake line will look like over 14 rounds. Will the two lines rise together, or one after the other? Which will reach its peak first? Write the reason for your guess.
- **fair test:** Keep the same cloth, folded to the same size. Stand at the same place for every toss, and toss each card once. Count carefully and use the same rules in every round. Tosses are chance, so every game is different. This is a model of a field. Real fields also have owls, weather and other food.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the cardboard if it is thick. Pick up every bean afterwards, so that small children and pets do not swallow them.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all 14 rounds. Draw one graph with two lines, rats in one colour and snakes in another. Mark each peak. Then write what you thought, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Round | Rats at the start | Snakes at the start | Rats eaten | Snakes who caught 3 or more
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut 16 squares of thin cardboard, each 10 centimetres wide. 2. Fold the cloth to about 40 by 30 centimetres and lay it flat on the floor. 3. Count out 80 beans into a bowl. 4. Draw the results table with 14 rows.  
  *Parent says:* The cloth is a paddy field. The beans are field rats and the cards are rat snakes. Let's get everything ready and draw the table.  
  *Child mode:* Cut 16 cardboard squares. Fold the cloth and lay it flat. Count 80 beans. Draw the table.
  - Note: Cereal boxes, sweet boxes and old notebook covers all work. The cloth stops the beans from rolling. On a bare floor they scatter too far.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the rules aloud. 2. Ask your child to draw the two lines they expect, and to say why. 3. Keep the drawing.  
  *Parent says:* A snake must catch 3 rats to live and have a young one. The rats that are left double. What will the two lines look like? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the rat line and the snake line you expect. Say why. Keep your drawing.
  - Ask: Which will peak first, and why? (good: The rats, because the snakes need many rats before they can multiply.; They rise together.; The snakes will eat all the rats.)
  - Note: Any drawing is fine. Many children draw two lines rising together, or the rats falling to zero.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Scatter 12 beans over the cloth from a hand's height. 2. Stand one big step away and toss 1 snake card flat onto the cloth. 3. Take away every bean under or touching the card, then take the card. 4. Three or more beans: the snake lives and has one young. Fewer: the snake dies. 5. Double the beans left on the cloth, up to 80. 6. Write the new numbers and play rounds 2 to 4.  
  *Parent says:* Every rat the card touches is eaten. A snake who eats 3 or more lives and has one young. A snake who eats fewer starves. The rats left over each have one young.  
  *Child mode:* Scatter the rats. Toss the snake card. Take away the rats under it. Three or more: the snake lives and has a young one. Double the rats left.
  - Note: If a card lands off the cloth, toss it again. If no snakes are left, add one, as if a snake came from the next field. If no rats are left, add 4. The field feeds 80 rats at most, so never put down more than 80.
- **s4 (22 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play rounds 5 to 14. 2. Scatter the rats afresh at the start of each round. 3. Toss every snake card once, and clear the eaten rats after each toss. 4. Never use more than 16 snakes. 5. Fill in the table each round.  
  *Parent says:* Now there are many rats. Watch what happens to the snakes over the next rounds. Then watch what happens to the rats.  
  *Child mode:* Play rounds 5 to 14. Scatter the rats each round. Toss every snake once. Fill in the table.
  - Note: In most games the rats fill the field by round 5 or 6. The snakes peak two or three rounds later, then the rats fall, and then the snakes fall. In some games the snakes stay few for a long time. Play on and see.
  - Check: The field has 14 snakes and only 30 rats. What will most likely happen to the snakes next? → **Many catch fewer than 3 and die**
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On squared paper, mark rounds 1 to 14 along the bottom. 2. Mark 0 to 80 up the side. 3. Plot the rats in one colour and join the points. 4. Plot the snakes in another colour. 5. Circle the highest point of each line.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw both lines on one graph. Circle the highest point of the rat line and the highest point of the snake line. Which came first?  
  *Child mode:* Draw both lines on one graph. Circle the top of each line. Which came first?
  - Ask: How many rounds after the rats' peak did the snakes peak? (good: About one to three rounds later.)
  - Note: The snake line is low, so it can look flat. If it does, draw the snakes again on their own graph with 0 to 16 up the side.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the guess drawing beside the real graph. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why. 3. Your child writes the three sentences. 4. Only then share the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? And why did the snakes' peak come after the rats' peak?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought. Say what you saw. Say why the snakes peaked later. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why did the snakes rise only after the rats? (good: A snake needs plenty of rats near her before she can catch 3.; Snakes multiply only when the rats are already many.)
  - Ask: Why did the rats fall? (good: There were so many snakes that they ate rats faster than the rats could double.)
  - Note: After your child has spoken: predators can multiply only after their prey is plentiful, and they starve only after the prey has fallen. That delay keeps both numbers swinging. One game shows about one and a half waves.
  - Think: What if the rats had a burrow where five of them were always safe? Would the waves be bigger or smaller?

### a2 · Count Without Counting: how many beans are in the bowl? (25 min)
- **record:** Write your first guess, the number marked in each of three samples, your estimate, and the real count. Write one sentence on how close the estimate came.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour 3 or 4 big handfuls of beans into the bowl. Do not count them. 2. Ask your child to guess the number by looking, and write it down. 3. Ask how they could find out without counting every bean.  
  *Parent says:* How many beans are in this bowl? Guess by looking. Now, how could we find out without counting them all? What is your idea?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many beans are in the bowl. Write it down. Think of a way to find out without counting all.
  - Note: Any idea is welcome: weighing a few, counting one cupful, measuring the depth. Each is a kind of sampling.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take out 40 beans. 2. Mark each one with a dot on both sides. 3. Let the ink dry for a minute. 4. Put them back and stir the bowl 20 times.  
  *Parent says:* We have caught 40 beans and marked them. Now they go back and mix with all the others. Stir well, so the marked ones are spread through the whole bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Take out 40 beans. Put a dot on both sides of each. Put them back. Stir 20 times.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. With eyes closed, scoop beans out with the cup until you have 40. 2. Count how many of the 40 are marked. 3. Put them all back, stir, and do this twice more. 4. Find the mean number of marked beans. 5. Divide 1,600 by that mean.  
  *Parent says:* If 8 of your 40 are marked, that is one fifth. So our 40 marked beans are about one fifth of the bowl. What does that make the whole bowl?  
  *Child mode:* Scoop 40 beans with eyes closed. Count the marked ones. Do it three times. Find the mean. Divide 1,600 by it.
  - Ask: Why do we divide 1,600 by the number marked? (good: The share of marked beans in the sample should match the share in the bowl.; 40 marked out of the whole bowl is the same share as the marked ones out of 40, and 40 × 40 = 1,600.)
  - Note: With about 200 beans, expect 6 to 10 marked in each sample. Three samples are used because one sample can be a fluke. If a sample has no marked beans at all, stir more and take it again.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now count every bean in the bowl, in heaps of ten. 2. Compare the real number with the estimate and with the first guess. 3. Ask why the estimate was not exact. 4. Ask how this could work for tigers.  
  *Parent says:* Now we count them all, to check. How close was the estimate? How close was your first guess? Why was the estimate not exactly right?  
  *Child mode:* Count all the beans in tens. Compare with your estimate and your guess. Say why they differ.
  - Note: An estimate within about a quarter of the real number is a good result. For tigers, the stripes are the marks. A camera photo is a capture, and a second photo of the same tiger is a recapture.
  - Check: You marked 40 beans. A new sample of 40 has 10 marked. About how many beans are in the bowl? → **160**
  - Think: What would go wrong with the estimate if the marked beans were stickier and clumped together?

- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what the forest would look like after one year with 20 tigers. 2. Lay out 10 beans as the prey. 3. Explain that tigers can take only about 1 bean in 10 each year. 4. Work out how many prey one tiger needs, and how many tigers 1,000 prey can feed.

  - Note: Forest A: 500 prey, so about 1 tiger. Forest B: 6,000 prey, so about 12 tigers. The real study found from 3 to 17 tigers in 100 square kilometres, and more tigers where there were more prey.
  - Check: A forest's deer are hunted until few are left. The tigers are not hunted. What happens to the tigers? → **Their number falls**

### p1 · Crowded Cups: does sowing more seeds give more methi? (60 min, 21 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up pokes the holes in the cups. Wash your hands after touching soil. Do not eat seedlings from a cup that has mould.
- **record:** Make a table with a row for each cup on days 7, 14 and 21: seeds sown, plants alive, height of the five tallest, leaf colour and plants fallen over. On day 21 add the harvest. Write what you thought, what you saw and why.

(10 min); day 14: Count and measure again. Look at leaf colour and at plants that have fallen over. (10 min); day 21: Count and measure for the last time.   *Child mode:* Sow 5, 25 and 100 seeds in three cups. Water them. Will 100 seeds give 20 times the methi? Say why.

  *Parent does:* 1. Count the plants standing in each cup. 2. Measure the five tallest in each cup, from the soil to the top. 3. Write the numbers in the table. 4. Ask what your child notices.  
  *Parent says:* It is day 7. How many seeds came up in each cup? Do the plants in the three cups look the same or different?  
  *Child mode:* Count the plants in each cup. Measure the five tallest. Write it in the table.
  - Note: In the first week the cups often look alike. A seedling's first food is stored inside the seed, so crowding hardly matters yet. In the crowded cup some seeds may sit on top of others and never root.
  - Wait: Leave the cups for 7 days, watering them the same.

  *Parent does:* 1. Count and measure again. 2. Look at the lowest leaves: are they green or yellow? 3. Count the plants that have fallen over. 4. Look for white fuzz, which is mould.  
  *Parent says:* It is day 14. The food stored in the seeds is used up. Now each plant must get light, water and soil food for herself. What has changed?  
  *Child mode:* Count and measure again. Check the leaf colour. Count the fallen plants. Look for white fuzz.
  - Note: Crowded seedlings are usually taller, thinner and paler, because they stretch for light and share the soil. Mould in the crowded cup is a result too. Disease spreads fastest where plants touch.
  - Wait: Leave the cups for 7 more days, watering them the same.
- **s4 (25 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count and measure for the last time. 2. Cut each cup's plants at the soil with scissors. 3. Lay the three harvests side by side, or weigh them. 4. Compare the harvest from 100 seeds with the harvest from 5. 5. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* The third cup had 20 times the seeds. Did it give 20 times the harvest? Tell me what you thought, what you saw, and why it came out that way.  
  *Child mode:* Cut the plants in each cup. Compare the three harvests. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Ask: Why did 100 seeds not give 20 times the methi? (good: All three cups had the same soil, water and light.; In the crowded cup each plant got a smaller share, so each stayed thin, and some died.; The cup set the limit, not the number of seeds.)
  - Note: After your child has spoken: the cup can only support so much plant, however many seeds start. This is carrying capacity in a living population. If all three cups still look healthy, the limit has not been reached yet. Keep them for another week.
  - Wait: Leave the cups for 7 more days, watering them the same.

## Challenges
### c1 · The Fishing Rule: the biggest catch that can go on for ever (30 min)
- **problem:** The pond is full, with 36 fish. Each year the fish breed, and then the village catches some. Find a rule for how many to catch each year. It must give the biggest total catch over 10 years, and the pond must be able to go on giving that catch afterwards.
- **your turn:** Now write your own fishing rule in one sentence. Run it for 10 years in a table. What was your total catch? How many fish are left? Could the village use your rule for 100 years?
- **constraints:** Use the growth table to find the new fish each year. Use the row nearest to your number of fish. If two rows are equally near, use the one with fewer fish.; The pond can never hold more than 36 fish.; At the end of year 10, the pond must hold at least as many fish as it did at the end of year 9.
- **general:** Catch a small number, such as 3, every year. The pond stays nearly full, so few new fish are added.; Catch whatever was added that year, and keep the pond where it is.; Catch a lot in the first years and less later.
- **creative:** First fish the pond down to 18, where the table adds the most. Then catch 9 every year, which is exactly what is added.; Keep the pond at 21, a little above the middle, and catch 9. A bad year then does less harm.; Close the pond for two years after any year in which the count falls below 12.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the growth table together: 6 fish add 5, 12 add 8, 18 add 9, 24 add 8, 30 add 5, and 36 add 0. 2. Ask where it comes from: the Full Island Test, where the forest grew fastest when half full. 3. Ask why a full pond adds no fish. 4. Draw a table with columns for fish at the start, new fish, catch, and fish at the end.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the table. A pond of 36 adds nothing, and a pond of 6 adds 5. Where does the pond add the most? Why would a full pond add none?  
  *Child mode:* Read the growth table. Find where the pond adds the most fish. Draw your ten-year table.
  - Ask: Why does a full pond add no new fish? (good: There is no food or room left for young fish, so they do not survive.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to choose a first rule and say why. 2. Run it for 10 years in the table. 3. Add up the total catch. 4. Check the last rule in the list of constraints.  
  *Parent says:* Choose your first rule. How many will you catch each year? Why that number? Let's run it for ten years and add up the catch.  
  *Child mode:* Choose a rule and say why. Run it for 10 years. Add up your catch.
  - Note: A common first rule is 'catch 10 every year'. It gives 100 fish in 10 years, which looks best. But the pond shrinks every year, from 36 to 13, so it fails the last constraint. No row of the table adds 10.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Try the three usual rules. 2. Try the creative rules. 3. Write each total catch in a list. 4. Find the rule with the biggest total that still passes the last constraint.  
  *Parent says:* Now try some other rules and compare the totals. Which rule gives the most fish and still leaves a pond that can go on?  
  *Child mode:* Try other rules. Write each total. Find the best rule that keeps the pond going.
  - Note: Fish the pond down to 18 in year one, then take 9 a year. That gives 18 + 9 × 9 = 99 fish, and the pond stays at 18 for ever. Catching 3 a year from a nearly full pond gives only 30.
  - Check: A pond holds 36 fish when full. At what number does it add the most new fish each year? → **About 18, when it is half full**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child writes their own rule in one sentence. 2. They run it for 10 years. 3. Ask what would happen in a year when the real pond added fewer fish than the table says.  
  *Parent says:* Write your own rule and test it. Then think about this. Real ponds have bad years. What would your rule do in a year when only 5 new fish came?  
  *Child mode:* Write your own rule. Test it for 10 years. What happens to it in a bad year?
  - Think: Real fishers cannot see how many fish are in the sea. How could they find out?
### c2 · Fewer Mosquitoes Without Spray: lower the limit (35 min)
- **problem:** Make a plan that lowers the number of mosquitoes at your home for a whole month. You may not use any spray, coil or poison. You can only change the place.
- **your turn:** Now write your own plan. List every place at home where water stands for more than a week. For each one, write what you will do and how often. After two weeks, check again. Which places did you miss?
- **constraints:** No spray, coil, oil or poison.; The plan must take no more than 10 minutes a week to keep up.; It must not harm other living beings, such as birds who drink from a bowl.
- **general:** Once a week, empty and scrub every container that holds still water: plant saucers, buckets, coolers and tins.; Keep water drums and overhead tanks tightly covered.; Throw away or turn over things that catch rain: coconut shells, old tyres, broken pots.
- **creative:** Change a bird bowl's water every three days. The birds still drink, but no mosquito finishes growing in it.; Keep a few guppies in a garden tank or a lotus pot. Guppies eat mosquito larvae, who are the young that live in water.; Make a street map of still water with your neighbours, and share the weekly check among the houses.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask what mosquitoes need in order to multiply. 2. Explain that the eggs are laid in still water, and that the young live in the water for about a week. 3. Ask which is easier to change: the mosquitoes, or the water.  
  *Parent says:* Killing mosquitoes lowers the number for a day or two. Then more grow up. What do they need that we could take away? Why would that last longer?  
  *Child mode:* Mosquito young grow in still water for about a week. What could we take away? Say why it would last.
  - Ask: Why does taking away still water last longer than killing mosquitoes? (good: It lowers the carrying capacity, so fewer can grow up at all.; If the water stays, the survivors lay eggs and the number comes back.)
- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Walk round the home with a torch. 2. Look at plant saucers, buckets, drums, coolers, the fridge tray, the roof and the area outside. 3. Mark each place with still water on a drawn plan. 4. Look in the water for tiny wrigglers.  
  *Parent says:* Let's hunt for still water. Check anything that could hold water for a week. If you see tiny wrigglers that jerk when the water is tapped, those are mosquito young.  
  *Child mode:* Walk round with a torch. Find every place with still water. Mark each one on your plan.
  - Note: A grown-up checks the roof, overhead tanks and anything outdoors. Common places people miss are the tray behind the fridge, air cooler tanks and the saucers under pots.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each marked place, choose an action: empty weekly, cover, turn over or throw away. 2. Write who will do it, and on which day. 3. Check the plan against the three constraints.  
  *Parent says:* For each place, what will we do, and how often? Remember, the young take about a week to grow. So how often must we empty the water?  
  *Child mode:* Write an action for each place. Write the day and who does it. Check the three rules.
  - Check: Mosquito young need about a week in still water. How often should a plant saucer be emptied? → **At least once a week**
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**

## Worksheets
### Populations and their limits (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a population?  
   Options: All living beings in one place / All members of one species in one place / All the animals of one country / One family of animals  
   **Answer:** All members of one species in one place. *Why:* A population is one species, in one place, at one time. Choosing 'all living beings in one place' describes a community, which holds many populations.
2. (fill) A population's number changes through births, deaths, members arriving and members ____.  
   **Answer:** ['leaving']. *Why:* These are the only four ways. Arriving is called immigration and leaving is called emigration.
3. (count) In a herd, 30 kids are born, 12 goats die, 2 arrive and 5 leave. By how many does it grow?  
   **Answer:** 15. *Why:* Add what comes in: 30 + 2 = 32. Add what goes out: 12 + 5 = 17. Then 32 − 17 = 15.
4. (mcq) Which list shows growth by doubling?  
   Options: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 / 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 / 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 / 1, 3, 5, 7, 9  
   **Answer:** 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. *Why:* In doubling, each number is 2 times the one before. The other lists add the same amount each time, which is much slower.
5. (truefalse) True or false: a population can keep doubling for ever if nobody disturbs it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Doubling soon needs more food and room than any place has. In Doubling Rice, square 17 already needed more than a whole packet.
6. (mcq) What is a limiting factor?  
   Options: The biggest animal in a place / A scarce need that holds a number down / The number of young in one year / A fence round a forest  
   **Answer:** A scarce need that holds a number down. *Why:* A limiting factor is whatever runs short first, such as food, water, room or nesting places. It can also be something that removes members, such as a predator.
7. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Carrying capacity → the largest number a place can keep alive year after year; Overshoot → growing past what the place can feed; Crash → a fast fall, when many die in a short time; Time lag → a delay between a cause and its effect; Refuge → a place where prey are safe. *Why:* These words are used all through the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
8. (write) In the Full Island Test, every tree dropped one seed each round. Why did the forest stop growing?  
   **Answer:** The island had only 36 squares. As it filled, more seeds landed on squares that already had a tree, and those seeds died. When every square was full, no seed could live.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the trees did not change. The room ran out. The number of squares set the limit.
9. (draw) Draw the graph of a population that grows slowly, then fast, then levels off. Mark the carrying capacity.  
   **Answer:** An S-shaped line that flattens at the top, with a dotted line across at the level where it flattens, labelled 'carrying capacity'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows all three parts: the slow start, the steep middle and the flat top. A line that goes straight up, or that keeps bending upward, has no limit in it.
10. (sort) Sort each story into the curve it makes.  
   **Answer:** yeast in a tube that multiply and then stay level → S-shaped curve; reindeer who eat an island bare and starve → Overshoot and crash; hares who become many about every ten years → Wave; duckweed that covers a bucket and stays → S-shaped curve; rats who multiply on bamboo fruit and then starve → Overshoot and crash; ladybirds and aphids on a hedge, summer after summer → Wave. *Why:* In an S, the number levels off and stays. In an overshoot, it passes the limit and falls fast. In a wave, the rise and fall repeat.
11. (mcq) Rats and rat snakes live in one field. The rats become many. What happens to the snakes next?  
   Options: They become many, a little later / They become fewer at once / Nothing changes / They leave the field  
   **Answer:** They become many, a little later. *Why:* With many rats, more snakes catch enough to live and raise young. That takes time, so the snakes rise after the rats. Choosing 'fewer' reads the link backward.
12. (order) Put the four steps of one predator and prey wave in order. Start with the prey rising.  
   **Answer:** ['Prey become many', 'Predators become many', 'Prey become few', 'Predators become few']. *Why:* More prey feed more predators. More predators eat the prey down. Fewer prey starve the predators. Then the prey can rise again.
13. (write) Why does the predators' peak come after the prey's peak, and not at the same time?  
   **Answer:** Predators can multiply only when prey is already plentiful, and raising young takes time. They start to starve only after the prey has already fallen.. *Why:* A strong answer names the delay and gives its cause. 'Because they eat them' is true but does not explain why the predators are late.
14. (mcq) You mark 40 beans in a bowl. A new sample of 40 has 8 marked. About how many beans are there?  
   Options: 48 / 200 / 320 / 1,600  
   **Answer:** 200. *Why:* 8 out of 40 is one fifth. So the 40 marked beans are about one fifth of the bowl, and 40 × 5 = 200.
15. (truefalse) True or false: the carrying capacity of a meadow is the same in a drought year and a wet year.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Less grass grows in a drought, so the meadow can feed fewer animals that year. Carrying capacity moves with the weather and the seasons.
### Curves, crowds and counting (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (count) One bacterium doubles every 20 minutes. How many are there after 2 hours?  
   **Answer:** 64. *Why:* Two hours is 6 lots of 20 minutes. Double 6 times: 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64.
2. (mcq) On a full island of 36 trees, how many new trees are added in a round?  
   Options: 36 / 18 / About 9 / None  
   **Answer:** None. *Why:* Every square has a tree, so every seed lands under a tree and dies. Choosing 36 forgets that seeds need an empty square.
3. (mcq) When did the paper forest add the most trees in one round?  
   Options: In the first round / When about half the squares were full / When nearly all were full / It added the same every round  
   **Answer:** When about half the squares were full. *Why:* In the middle there are many parent trees and still many empty squares. Early on there are few parents. Late on there is little room.
4. (fill) A limit that presses harder when a population is more crowded is called density-____.  
   **Answer:** ['dependent']. *Why:* Density means how crowded the members are. Disease and hunger are density-dependent, because they are worse in a crowd.
5. (sort) Sort each cause of death by whether crowding makes it worse.  
   **Answer:** a disease passed by touch → Worse in a crowd; a cyclone → The same, crowded or not; running short of grass → Worse in a crowd; a hard frost → The same, crowded or not; fights over nesting places → Worse in a crowd; a forest fire → The same, crowded or not. *Why:* Disease, hunger and fights grow worse as members crowd together. A storm, a frost or a fire kills a share of the members whether they are few or many.
6. (write) Reindeer on St Matthew Island passed the island's limit and then crashed. Why did they not level off gently?  
   **Answer:** They lived on lichen that had built up over many years and grows back very slowly. So the herd could keep growing after it passed what the island could renew. When the lichen was gone, the limit hit all at once.. *Why:* A strong answer says the limit arrived late, because the food was a store and not a yearly supply. Naming the hard winter as well is a fair point.
7. (count) Reindeer on an island fell from 6,000 to 42. How many died or were missing?  
   **Answer:** 5958. *Why:* 6,000 − 42 = 5,958. That is more than 99 in every 100.
8. (mcq) In a small glass tube with no hiding places, hunters and prey are kept together. What usually happens?  
   Options: They rise and fall for ever / The hunters eat all the prey, then starve / The prey eat the hunters / Both stay at one number  
   **Answer:** The hunters eat all the prey, then starve. *Why:* Georgy Gause found this in the 1930s. With nowhere to hide, every prey animal is caught. Lasting waves need refuges, distance or new arrivals.
9. (draw) Draw two wavy lines on one graph, for rats and for snakes. Show which peaks first.  
   **Answer:** Two wave-shaped lines. The rat line is taller. Each snake peak is a little to the right of a rat peak.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts every snake peak after a rat peak. Two lines that rise and fall exactly together leave out the time lag.
10. (mcq) Why are tigers told apart by their stripes in camera photographs?  
   Options: Stripes show how old a tiger is / No two tigers have the same stripes / Stripes show what a tiger eats / Only females have stripes  
   **Answer:** No two tigers have the same stripes. *Why:* Each tiger's stripe pattern is her own, like a fingerprint. So a photo counts as a capture of one known tiger, with no need to catch or mark her.
11. (write) An estimate from mark and recapture is not exact. Give one thing that could make it wrong.  
   **Answer:** The marked beans may not have mixed in well. The sample may be too small. Marked members may be easier or harder to catch than the others.. *Why:* Any one of these is a strong answer. The method works only if marked and unmarked members are equally likely to be in the second sample.
12. (count) A tiger needs about 500 prey animals in her forest. How many tigers can a forest of 3,000 prey feed?  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* 3,000 ÷ 500 = 6. The 500 comes from 50 prey eaten a year, when tigers can take about 1 in 10.
13. (odd) Three of these raise a pond's carrying capacity for fish. Which one lowers it?  
   Options: More water plants / A bigger pond / Sewage that uses up the oxygen / More insects falling in  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Fish need oxygen dissolved in the water. Rotting sewage uses it up, so fewer fish can live. The other three add food or room.
14. (perspective) Three people talk about the mosquitoes in their street.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says that killing lowers the number for a short time, and that the number returns if the breeding places stay. Clearing still water lowers the carrying capacity itself. It can also grant that spraying helps quickly in an outbreak.. *Why:* All three have a point. The lasting change comes from lowering what the place can carry.
15. (write) A village catches fish from a pond. Why is a half-full pond better for the village than a full one?  
   **Answer:** A full pond adds almost no new fish, because there is no food or room for young. A half-full pond has many parents and plenty of room, so it adds the most new fish each year to catch.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the number of fish from the number of new fish. The catch that can go on comes from the new fish.
### Why the numbers behave as they do (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Why do living populations grow by multiplying, and not by adding the same number each year?  
   **Answer:** Because the young grow up and have young too. Every new member is also a new parent, so the more there are, the more are added.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the number added depends on the number already there.
2. (count) Twenty-nine reindeer double every 2 years. How many are there after 10 years?  
   **Answer:** 928. *Why:* Ten years is 5 doublings: 58, 116, 232, 464, 928.
3. (mcq) A plant has plenty of water and light but stays small in poor soil. What does this show?  
   Options: Growth stops at the scarcest need / Plants do not need light / Water is bad for plants / Soil does not matter  
   **Answer:** Growth stops at the scarcest need. *Why:* This is the law of the minimum. The need in shortest supply sets the limit, however plentiful the others are.
4. (truefalse) True or false: at carrying capacity, no young are born.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Young are still born, but about as many members die as are born, so the number stays level. On the full island every tree still dropped a seed.
5. (draw) Draw a hill-shaped graph of 'new members added' against 'size of the population'. Mark where the hill is highest.  
   **Answer:** A curve that starts at zero, rises to a peak at about half the carrying capacity, and falls back to zero at the carrying capacity.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the peak in the middle and brings the curve down to zero at the limit. A line that keeps rising says a full place adds the most, which is the opposite of what the test showed.
6. (write) Scientists gave hares extra food in one area and kept predators out of another. Why did they also do both together?  
   **Answer:** To see whether food and predators act separately or together. Doing both raised hare numbers far more than the two effects added up, so the two limits work together.. *Why:* A strong answer says that testing one thing at a time cannot show how two causes combine.
7. (mcq) Mites on a tray of oranges rose and fell three times only when the tray was large and patchy. Why?  
   Options: Big trays are warmer / Some prey were always out of reach / The hunters ate oranges instead / Oranges make mites breed faster  
   **Answer:** Some prey were always out of reach. *Why:* In a patchy world, prey can settle on oranges the hunters have not found yet. So the prey are never all caught, and both kinds last.
8. (order) Put the events on St Matthew Island in order.  
   **Answer:** ['29 reindeer are put on the island', 'The herd grows to about 1,350', 'The herd reaches about 6,000 and the lichen is eaten down', 'Nearly all die in one hard winter', '42 reindeer are counted']. *Why:* The herd grew fast with no predators, passed what the island could renew, and then crashed.
9. (fill) Many bacteria sense how crowded they are by chemical signals. This is called ____ sensing.  
   **Answer:** ['quorum']. *Why:* A quorum is the number of members needed before a group acts. The bacteria act together only when enough of them are near.
10. (write) The world's population estimate for 2022 is given as 'about 8 billion', and India's tigers as 'about 3,682'. Why 'about'?  
   **Answer:** Both are estimates from samples and counts that cannot reach everyone. The tiger report gives a range from about 3,167 to 3,925 for this reason.. *Why:* A strong answer says that an estimate has a range, and that giving the range is more honest than giving one exact number.
11. (mcq) Published estimates of how many people the Earth can carry range from under 1 billion to over 1,000 billion. Why so wide?  
   Options: Nobody has tried to measure it / It depends on how people choose to live / The Earth changes size / Scientists cannot count people  
   **Answer:** It depends on how people choose to live. *Why:* Joel Cohen showed this in 1995. The number depends on what people eat, use and throw away, and how they share. Those are choices, not measurements.
12. (perspective) Three thinkers answer the question: must a growing number of people run out of food?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses evidence such as the S-shaped curve, the reindeer overshoot, or the way people have raised their own carrying capacity with new farming. It grants a fair point to another view.. *Why:* This is still argued about. Each view explains part of what has happened since 1798.
13. (write) Carrying capacity rose for people when forests became farms. What happened to the capacity for the forest's animals?  
   **Answer:** It fell. The same land, water and sunlight now feed crops for people, so less is left for deer, hornbills and tigers.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that one place has one supply. Raising one species' share lowers the share of others.
14. (odd) Three of these could make waves of predators and prey last. Which one would end them?  
   Options: Burrows for the prey / A large, patchy field / New animals arriving now and then / A small bare cage  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* In a small bare cage every prey animal is found and eaten, and then the hunters starve. The other three keep some prey out of reach or bring new ones in.
15. (mcq) A herd is fed by lorry every dry season and grows to three times the hill's own limit. What is the risk?  
   Options: The herd forgets how to graze / A crash if the lorries ever stop / The hill grows more grass / There is no risk  
   **Answer:** A crash if the lorries ever stop. *Why:* The herd is now above what the hill can carry by itself. It lasts only as long as the outside food comes. The many feet and mouths also wear the hill down.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is one population?  
   Options: All the birds in a park / All the house crows of one town / One crow and her nest / All the animals in India  
   **Answer:** All the house crows of one town. *Why:* A population is one species in one place. All the birds in a park are many species, so they are many populations.
2. (mcq) Births 40, deaths 25, arrivals 5, departures 20. What happens to the population?  
   Options: It grows by 20 / It shrinks by 20 / It stays the same / It doubles  
   **Answer:** It stays the same. *Why:* In: 40 + 5 = 45. Out: 25 + 20 = 45. The two match, so the number stays the same though the members change.
3. (mcq) A water plant doubles its patch every week. It covers half a pond today. When will it cover the whole pond?  
   Options: In one week / In two weeks / In a month / In as long as it took so far  
   **Answer:** In one week. *Why:* One more doubling turns a half into a whole. Choosing 'as long as it took so far' treats doubling like steady adding.
4. (mcq) Why can no population keep doubling for ever?  
   Options: Animals get tired of having young / Something its members need runs short / Doubling is against the law / Old members refuse to die  
   **Answer:** Something its members need runs short. *Why:* Each doubling needs twice the food and room. Any place has only so much, so a limiting factor always appears.
5. (mcq) A forest stops at 36 trees on an island of 36 squares. The island is halved. Where does it stop now?  
   Options: At 36 again / At about 18 / At about 72 / It never stops  
   **Answer:** At about 18. *Why:* The limit comes from the place. Half the squares means room for half the trees. Choosing 36 treats the limit as belonging to the trees.
6. (mcq) What is the carrying capacity of a place?  
   Options: The number born there in a year / The largest number it can keep alive for years / The heaviest animal it can hold / The number of species in it  
   **Answer:** The largest number it can keep alive for years. *Why:* It is the number the place can go on feeding and housing without being damaged. It can change with seasons and weather.
7. (mcq) A line graph rises slowly, then steeply, then goes flat. What does the flat part mean?  
   Options: Everyone has died / The population has reached its carrying capacity / Counting stopped / No young are born any more  
   **Answer:** The population has reached its carrying capacity. *Why:* On the flat part, births and deaths match, so the number stays level. Young are still being born.
8. (mcq) A graph climbs far above a limit line and then drops almost to zero. What is this called?  
   Options: An S-shaped curve / A wave / An overshoot and crash / Steady growth  
   **Answer:** An overshoot and crash. *Why:* The population went past what the place could renew, the food ran out, and most members died.
9. (mcq) In a field, the number of rats peaks in March. When would you expect the rat snakes to peak?  
   Options: In March too / A little later / A little earlier / Never  
   **Answer:** A little later. *Why:* Snakes multiply only after rats are plentiful, and raising young takes time. So their peak comes after the rats' peak.

   Options: They are kind to their prey / Some prey are always hidden or far away / Prey taste bad when few / Predators eat plants too  
   **Answer:** Some prey are always hidden or far away. *Why:* Refuges, distance and new arrivals keep some prey alive. In a small bare space with no hiding places, the prey are all eaten.

   Options: They ask villagers to guess / They count paw prints only / They photograph tigers and match their stripes / They count every deer  
   **Answer:** They photograph tigers and match their stripes. *Why:* Each tiger's stripes are her own. Photos from automatic cameras are treated as captures and recaptures, and the number is worked out from them.
12. (mcq) Which change would raise a forest's carrying capacity for tigers?  
   Options: More tigers brought in / More deer and other prey / Fewer trees / A fence round each tiger  
   **Answer:** More deer and other prey. *Why:* The number of tigers a forest can hold follows its prey. Bringing in more tigers without more prey only means some will starve or leave.

## Applications
- Tiger reserves in India are planned from the number of deer, pigs and gaur they hold. The prey sets how many tigers can live there.
- Fishing rules set how many fish may be caught, so that enough parents are left to add new fish each year.
- Health workers lower mosquito numbers by clearing still water, which lowers the carrying capacity for mosquitoes.
- Farmers leave space between plants, because crowded plants share the same light, water and soil and each grows less.
- Curd, idli batter and bread dough all depend on microbes who multiply and then slow down as their food runs low.
- Doctors and health planners use population graphs to work out how many schools, hospitals and vaccines a country will need.
- Wildlife managers count animals by samples, camera photos and mark and recapture, to know whether a population is rising or falling.
- City planners find that the number of street dogs, rats and crows follows the amount of open rubbish.

## Spotted around us
- Pigeons gathered where someone scatters grain each morning, at a temple tank in Chennai or a chowk in Jaipur. Food sets their number.
- A plant saucer or an old tyre with wriggling mosquito young in the water, in the first weeks of the monsoon.
- Water hyacinth covering a lake from bank to bank, as on parts of Vembanad in Kerala or Ulsoor in Bengaluru. The surface is full.
- Seedlings crowded thin and pale under a big neem or banyan, where almost none will grow up for want of light.
- A signboard at a tiger reserve such as Nagarahole, Bandipur or Kanha, giving the estimated number of tigers and of deer.
- Curd that set overnight and then stopped getting much more sour, because the bacteria had slowed down.
- Crows and street dogs gathered at an open rubbish heap. Where the rubbish is cleared, there are fewer.
- Fishing boats tied up at harbours on India's coasts during the yearly fishing ban in the monsoon, when many fish breed.

## Open questions
- **Why do snowshoe hares rise and fall across much of Canada at nearly the same time?** Predators and food explain one forest. Scientists still test what keeps forests thousands of kilometres apart in step. Weather and travelling predators are two ideas.
- **Is there a carrying capacity of the Earth for people, and can it be measured?** Published estimates range from under 1 billion to over 1,000 billion. The answer depends on how people choose to live, which science cannot decide.
- **Why do some populations cycle, while most stay near one number?** The equations allow both. For most wild species nobody has counted for long enough to tell which is happening, or why.
- **How many kinds of living beings sense their own numbers?** Quorum sensing is well shown in bacteria. Whether trees, fungi or herds do something like it is only beginning to be studied.
- **Did hunger or weather kill the reindeer of St Matthew Island?** The counts are agreed, but nobody was on the island that winter. Scientists who study the records still weigh the two causes differently.

- In Doubling Rice, square 8 has 128 grains. How many grains does square 12 have? **2,048 grains.**. Double four times, once for each square: 256, 512, 1,024, 2,048. So square 12 has 2,048 grains. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A paper forest had 2, 4, 7, 12, 19, 26, 31, 34, 35 and 36 trees at the end of rounds 0 to 9. In which round were the most trees added? **Rounds 4 and 5. Each added 7 trees.**. Find the gain in each round by subtracting: 2, 3, 5, 7, 7, 5, 3, 1, 1. The biggest gain is 7, from 12 to 19 and from 19 to 26. That is when the island was about half full. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
- You marked 40 beans. Three samples of 40 had 7, 9 and 8 marked beans. Estimate the number of beans in the bowl. **About 200 beans.**. The mean number marked is (7 + 9 + 8) ÷ 3 = 8. So 8 out of 40, or one fifth, of a sample is marked. The 40 marked beans are then about one fifth of the bowl. 40 × 5 = 200. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A tiger eats about 50 large prey animals a year, and tigers can take about 1 in 10 of the prey. A forest has 4,000 prey animals. How many tigers can it feed? **About 8 tigers.**. One tenth of 4,000 is 400 prey a year for tigers. Each tiger needs 50. 400 ÷ 50 = 8. (queues `math.division.L5`)
- Reindeer on St Matthew Island grew from 29 in 1944 to about 1,350 in 1957. About how many times did the herd double in those 13 years? **Between 5 and 6 times. So the herd doubled about every two and a half years.**. Keep doubling 29: 58, 116, 232, 464, 928, 1,856. After 5 doublings there are 928, and after 6 there are 1,856. The real count of 1,350 lies between them. 13 years ÷ 5.5 is about 2.4 years for each doubling. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A pond adds 9 new fish a year when it holds 18 fish, and 5 a year when it holds 30. A village wants to catch the same number every year for ever. Which pond size lets them catch more, and how many more in 10 years? **The pond kept at 18. It gives 40 more fish in 10 years.**. At 18 fish the village can take 9 a year, which is 90 in 10 years. At 30 fish they can take only 5 a year, which is 50. 90 − 50 = 40. (queues `math.multiplication.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What do we call all the members of one species living in one place? → A population.
- Name the four things that change a population's number. → Births, deaths, members arriving and members leaving.
- What comes next: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16? → 32. Each number is double the one before.
- What is the largest number a place can keep alive year after year called? → Its carrying capacity.
- What shape is the graph of a population that grows and then levels off? → An S-shaped curve.
- Which peaks first, the prey or the predator? → The prey. The predator's peak comes a little later.
- What does a tiger carry that lets scientists tell her from every other tiger? → Her stripes. No two tigers have the same pattern.
- What is the lasting way to have fewer mosquitoes: kill them, or remove still water? → Remove still water. That lowers the carrying capacity.
