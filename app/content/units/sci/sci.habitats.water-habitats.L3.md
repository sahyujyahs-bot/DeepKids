# Life in water: ponds, rivers and seas, and the air dissolved in them
`sci.habitats.water-habitats.L3`

**Summary.** A glass of cold water left in a warm room grows tiny bubbles on its sides. That air was dissolved in the water all along, where you could not see it. Ponds, rivers and seas are habitats, homes for living things, and this dissolved air is what their living things breathe. Fish take oxygen out of the water with their gills. A tadpole breathes with gills too, and grows into a frog who breathes with lungs and wet skin. Lotus plants have their roots in mud that has almost no air, and send air down to them through holes in their stems. Algae and water plants make food and oxygen in sunlight. Warm water holds less air than cold water, so on hot, still nights a pond can run short of oxygen. Sea water is salty, so most sea fish cannot live in a river, and most river fish cannot live in the sea.

**Intro.** *Fill a clear glass with cold water from the fridge. Leave it on the table for an hour. Then look closely at its sides. Tiny bubbles have appeared. Nobody blew into the water. So where did those bubbles come from?* Those bubbles are air. It was dissolved in the water, mixed in so finely that you could not see it. As the water warmed up, some of that air came out. Fish live on this hidden air. They cannot breathe the air above the water, and they do not breathe the water itself. They breathe the oxygen dissolved in the water. In this unit you test how much air water holds, and what changes it. Then you meet the living things of ponds, rivers and seas: fish, frogs, lotus and algae. Each of them has their own problem to solve to live in water.

## Terms
- **Habitat**: The place where a living thing finds everything they need: food, water, air, shelter and the right warmth. *e.g.* A temple tank is a habitat for fish, frogs, snails and water plants.
- **Aquatic**: Living in water. Aquatic animals and plants spend all or part of their lives in water. *e.g.* Fish, lotus and algae are aquatic. A crow is not.
- **Fresh water**: Water with very little salt in it. Ponds, lakes, most rivers and rain are fresh water. *e.g.* The Kaveri river and a village pond are fresh water.
- **Salt water**: Water with a lot of salt dissolved in it, like the sea. Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in every litre. *e.g.* The Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea are salt water.
- **Brackish water**: A mix of fresh and salt water. It is found where a river meets the sea. *e.g.* The backwaters of Kerala and Pulicat lake near Chennai are partly brackish.
- **Dissolve**: To mix into a liquid so completely that you cannot see it any more. *e.g.* Sugar dissolves in tea. Air dissolves in water.
- **Dissolved oxygen**: Oxygen gas mixed into water, too finely to see. Fish and other water animals breathe it. *e.g.* A cold, splashing river has plenty of dissolved oxygen.
- **Gills**: The breathing organs of a fish or a tadpole. They take oxygen out of the water and pass it into the blood. *e.g.* Lift a fish's gill cover at the fish shop, and you see red, feathery gills.
- **Algae**: Simple living things, usually green, that make their own food from sunlight, like plants. Many are too small to see one by one. *e.g.* The green slime on a pond, or the green inside an old flower vase, is algae.
- **Photosynthesis**: How plants and algae make food from water and carbon dioxide, using light. They give out oxygen as they do it. *e.g.* Water plants in a sunny pond give off tiny bubbles of oxygen.
- **Tadpole**: A young frog. Tadpoles live in water, have a tail, and breathe with gills. *e.g.* In the monsoon, puddles and ponds fill with black tadpoles.
- **Amphibian**: An animal that usually starts life in water and lives on land as an adult. Frogs and toads are amphibians. *e.g.* A frog hatches in a pond as a tadpole, then hops onto land.
- **Air canals**: Long empty tubes inside the stem and root of a water plant. They carry air down to the parts in the mud. *e.g.* Cut across a lotus stem, and you see a ring of holes. These are the plant's air canals.
- **Algal bloom**: When far too many algae grow in a pond or lake, often because sewage or fertiliser has run into it. The water turns thick and green. *e.g.* A lake near a city turns bright green after waste water flows into it.
- **Streamlined**: Shaped so that water or air flows smoothly around it. A streamlined body moves through water easily. *e.g.* Fish are pointed at the front and narrow at the tail, so they slip through water.

## Explanations
### Ponds, rivers and seas are habitats · *defined*
**Definition:** A water habitat is a pond, lake, river, stream or sea where living things find what they need. Some water habitats are still and some flow. Some are fresh and some are salty.

A pond or lake is still water. It warms up quickly in the sun, and plants can root in its muddy bottom. A river flows all the time. Its water is often cooler and full of air, but its living things must hold on or swim hard. The sea is salt water. It is huge and deep, and only its top part gets sunlight. Where a river meets the sea, the water is a mix of fresh and salty, called brackish water. Each of these habitats suits different living things.
- e.g. A temple tank or village pond: still, fresh water with lotus, frogs and fish.
- e.g. The Kaveri or the Godavari: flowing fresh water.
- e.g. The Bay of Bengal: salt water.
- e.g. The Kerala backwaters and Pichavaram mangroves near Chidambaram: brackish water.
### Water holds dissolved air · *defined*
**Definition:** Air dissolves in water. It mixes in so finely that you cannot see it. Cold water can hold more dissolved air than warm water.

When water touches air, some of the air slowly dissolves into it. This happens at the surface of every pond, river and sea. Splashing and wind mix in more. Cold water holds more dissolved air than warm water. Water from the fridge holds almost twice as much oxygen as water on a hot afternoon. When cold water warms up, it cannot hold all its air, so some comes out as bubbles. That is what you saw in the cold glass test. Boiling drives most of the air out, which is why boiled water made almost no bubbles.
- e.g. Tiny bubbles on the inside of a glass of cold water that has warmed up.
- e.g. Small bubbles on the bottom of a pan of water, long before it boils.
- e.g. A waterfall or a splashing stream mixes lots of air into the water.
### How a fish breathes · *defined*
**Definition:** Fish breathe the oxygen dissolved in water, using their gills. They take water in through their mouths and push it out over their gills.

Every animal needs oxygen to get energy from their food. A fish's problem is that water holds very little oxygen, about 30 times less than the same amount of air. So fish have to pass a lot of water over their gills. They gulp water in through the mouth, close the mouth, and push the water out under the gill covers. Each gill has hundreds of thin red folds, packed with tiny blood vessels. Oxygen passes from the water into the blood through these thin folds. The blood flows through the gills the opposite way to the water, which helps the fish take out most of the oxygen. Most fish cannot breathe in air, because out of water their gill folds stick together and stop working.
- e.g. Watch a fish in an aquarium: their mouth and gill covers open and close, again and again.
- e.g. Fresh fish at the fish shop have bright red gills.
- e.g. Some fish, like the murrel sold in many South Indian markets, can also gulp air at the surface.
### Why a pond can run short of oxygen · *defined*
**Definition:** A pond runs short of oxygen when its living things use oxygen faster than it comes in. This happens most on hot, still nights.

Oxygen gets into a pond in two ways. It dissolves in from the air at the surface, and algae and water plants make it in sunlight. Everything in the pond uses oxygen all the time: fish, snails, the plants and algae themselves, and the bacteria that rot dead leaves. At night, the algae stop making oxygen but keep using it. So the oxygen goes down all night and is lowest just before sunrise. In hot weather the water also holds less. That is why, on hot mornings, fish sometimes come up to gulp at the surface, where the water touching the air has the most oxygen.
- e.g. Fish gulping at the top of a pond at dawn in the hot months.
- e.g. Aquarium pumps bubble air into the water so the fish do not run short.
- e.g. Some city lakes have fountains and pumps that splash water to mix in air.
### Frogs: two lives, two ways to breathe · *defined*
**Definition:** A frog starts life in water as a tadpole who breathes with gills. They change into a frog who breathes with lungs and through their thin, wet skin.

A mother frog lays her eggs in water, in a clump of jelly. Out of each egg comes a tadpole. They have a tail for swimming and gills for breathing dissolved oxygen, like a fish. Over some weeks, their back legs grow, then their front legs. Their gills disappear and lungs grow, and their tail shrinks away. This big change of body is called metamorphosis. The adult frog breathes air with their lungs. They also breathe through their skin, but oxygen can only pass through skin that is wet. That is why frogs stay near water or in damp places, and come out after rain.
- e.g. After the first monsoon rains, frogs croak loudly in flooded fields and puddles.
- e.g. A tadpole in a puddle has a tail and no legs. A few weeks later they have four legs.
- e.g. The purple frog of the Western Ghats lives underground and comes up to breed in monsoon streams.
### The lotus: leaves in the air, roots in the mud · *defined*
**Definition:** A lotus grows from a thick stem buried in the mud at the bottom of a pond. Their leaves and flowers stand up above the water, and air canals carry air down to the buried parts.

A lotus has a problem. Their leaves need sunlight and air to make food. Their roots and thick underground stem sit in thick mud at the bottom of the pond, where there is almost no oxygen. Every living part of the plant needs oxygen. So the lotus has long air canals running through their leaf stalks and underground stem. Air goes in through the leaves and travels down these tubes to the parts in the mud. You can see the canals as a ring of holes when you cut across a lotus stem. The leaves also stand on long stalks, so they stay in the light when the water rises.
- e.g. Thamarai thandu, the lotus stem cooked in Tamil Nadu, and kamal kakdi in the north, show a ring of holes when sliced.
- e.g. Lotus leaves stand high above the water in a temple tank.
- e.g. Water lilies have floating leaves with their breathing pores on the top side, which faces the air.
### Algae: the tiny food makers of water · *defined*
**Definition:** Algae are simple living things that make their own food from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide, like plants do. They give out oxygen as they do it.

Some algae are long green threads, like the slimy paasi on a pond. Others are single cells, far too small to see alone. When millions of them grow together, the water turns green. Tiny animals eat the algae, small fish eat those animals, and bigger fish eat the small fish. So algae feed almost everything in the water. Algae in the sea also make a huge amount of oxygen, about half of all the oxygen made on Earth each year. But too much of a good thing is a problem. When sewage or fertiliser flows into a lake, algae grow far too fast. This is called an algal bloom. When the algae die, bacteria rot them and use up the dissolved oxygen, and fish can die.
- e.g. Green water in an old flower vase or a pot saucer left in the sun.
- e.g. Green slime on the steps of a temple tank.
- e.g. A lake turning bright green after waste water flows into it.
### Salt water and fresh water · *defined*
**Definition:** Sea water is salty, with about 35 grams of salt in every litre. River water, pond water and rain are fresh, with very little salt.

Water moves from the less salty side to the more salty side of a living skin. You saw this with the cucumber. In very salty water, water left the cucumber's cells, and the slice went floppy. A fish's body is less salty than the sea, and saltier than a river. So a sea fish keeps losing water to the sea. They drink sea water, and their gills push the extra salt out. A river fish has the opposite problem. Water keeps soaking into their body, so they make a lot of very watery urine. Each kind of fish is built for one kind of water. A few, like the hilsa, change as they travel. The hilsa lives in the sea and swims up rivers like the Godavari and the Hooghly to lay their eggs.
- e.g. Pulasa, the hilsa caught in the Godavari during the monsoon floods, is a famous dish in Andhra Pradesh.
- e.g. Sea fish in the market, like mackerel and sardine, come from the sea. Rohu and catla come from rivers and ponds.
- e.g. Mangrove trees at Pichavaram live in brackish water and push salt out of their leaves.
### Holding on in flowing water · *defined*
**Definition:** In a fast river or stream, living things must hold on, hide, or swim hard, or the water carries them away.

Flowing water pushes on everything in it. The faster it flows, the harder it pushes. A tall shape catches more of the push and topples. A low, flat shape lets the water slide over it. That is what you found in the flat or tall test. Many stream animals are flat and hug the rocks. Some small fish in fast streams of the Western Ghats have flat bodies and fins that work like suckers. Snails clamp down on stones. Fish that live in open river water are streamlined, pointed in front and narrow at the tail, so water flows smoothly around them. Fish in a river usually face upstream, so the water flows over their gills the right way.
- e.g. Snails stuck to the stones under a waterfall.
- e.g. Small flat fish clinging to rocks in fast hill streams of the Western Ghats.
- e.g. Fish in a stream holding still, all facing the same way, into the current.
### Do fish feel pain? · *debated*
Fish feel pain. Their bodies have nerve endings that sense harm, like ours do, and fish change what they do after they are hurt. Scientists still argue about what fish feel inside, because nobody can ask a fish. Here are the main views.
- e.g. Lynne Sneddon and her team, in Britain in 2003, found that trout with a sting in their lips rubbed their lips on the gravel and stopped eating for a while.
- e.g. Fish learn to avoid a part of a tank where something hurt them.
- **Lynne Sneddon, a British biologist, and many fish scientists:** Fish have nerves that sense harm, and they act as if they hurt: they stop eating, rub the hurt place and avoid what hurt them. So fish feel pain, and we should handle them kindly.
- **Brian Key, an Australian brain scientist, and some others:** Fish do not have the parts of the brain that people use to feel pain. So their reactions might happen without a feeling of pain.
- **Many scientists today:** A fish brain is built differently from ours, but a different brain can still feel. Every animal studied so far reacts to harm in their own way.

## Mostly agreed
- Water holds dissolved oxygen, and cold water holds more than warm water. *How we know:* Chemists have measured it at every temperature. You can see it at home: cold water grows bubbles as it warms.
- Fish breathe the oxygen dissolved in water, using their gills. They do not split water to get oxygen. *How we know:* In 1809, Alexander von Humboldt and Provençal measured the gases in water before and after fish lived in it. The dissolved oxygen went down and carbon dioxide went up.
- Tadpoles breathe with gills, and adult frogs breathe with lungs and through their wet skin. *How we know:* Biologists have watched tadpoles grow into frogs for hundreds of years, and studied their bodies at each stage.
- Algae and water plants make oxygen in sunlight. In the sea, algae and other tiny living things make about half of the oxygen made on Earth each year. *How we know:* Scientists measure oxygen in water by day and night, and satellites measure how much green algae is in the sea.
- Sewage and fertiliser in a lake can make algae grow far too fast, and this can use up the oxygen and kill fish. *How we know:* Scientists have measured this in lakes all over the world, including lakes in Indian cities.

## How we found out
**Alexander von Humboldt, a German explorer and scientist, and Provençal, a French chemist, with the older ideas of Anaxagoras, Diogenes of Apollonia and Aristotle, About 400 BCE (the Greek ideas) and 1809 (the measurements in Paris).** About 2,400 years ago in Greece, the thinkers Anaxagoras and Diogenes of Apollonia said that fish breathe air out of the water through their gills. Aristotle disagreed. He wrote that fish do not breathe at all, and that the water over their gills only cools them. Much later, after chemists found in the 1780s that water is made of hydrogen and oxygen, some people guessed that fish split the water itself to get its oxygen. In 1809, in Paris, the explorer Alexander von Humboldt and the chemist Provençal tested this. They kept fish in closed jars of water and measured the gases dissolved in the water before and after. The dissolved oxygen went down, and carbon dioxide went up, just as when we breathe air. So fish do breathe, and they breathe the oxygen dissolved in water, not the water. Anaxagoras had been close to right all along.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle thought fish do not breathe and that the water only cools their gills. Later, some people thought fish split water to get oxygen. Humboldt and Provençal measured the gases in the water and showed that fish take out the dissolved oxygen and give out carbon dioxide.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Fish breathe water.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Fish live in water and take water in through their mouths all the time.  
  Against: Fish breathe the oxygen dissolved in the water. Water with its air boiled out cannot keep a fish alive, even though it is still water.
- **Fish do not breathe at all. Water over the gills only cools them.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker, about 2,350 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: Fish never come up for air, and the water over their gills is cool.  
  Against: Measurements since 1809 show fish take oxygen out of the water and give out carbon dioxide, just as we do with air.
- **Green water is always dirty and bad.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Thick green lakes near cities often smell, and fish die in them.  
  Against: Some algae are a good sign: they feed the animals of a healthy pond and make oxygen. The trouble comes only when far too many grow, usually because of sewage or fertiliser.
- **A frog can live anywhere on land, as long as they can find insects.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Frogs hop around on land and catch insects there.  
  Against: A frog breathes partly through their skin, which must stay wet. In dry air they lose water and can die. Frogs also need water to lay their eggs.
- **Fish feel pain.** (Lynne Sneddon and many fish scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Fish have nerve endings that sense harm, and they change what they do after being hurt.  
  Against: Some scientists, like Brian Key, say fish lack the brain parts people use to feel pain.

## Roots
We have learned that water holds dissolved air, and how fish, frogs, lotus and algae live on it. Now we ask what life in water tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your cold glass of water grew bubbles as it warmed, though nothing was added. That air had been dissolved in the water all along, out of sight. Fish breathe that dissolved oxygen through their gills. Warm water holds less, so on a hot, still night a pond can run short of oxygen for its fish.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of water and air as two separate worlds: air is for breathing, and water is where you cannot breathe. But every pond, river and sea has air dissolved in it, and that is what its fish and tadpoles breathe.  
*Evidence:* Glass A, cold tap water, grew many bubbles as it warmed. Glass B, boiled water, grew almost none. Fish breathe that same dissolved oxygen, and measurements since 1809 show it falling as they breathe.

- *who am i:* You breathe air with lungs. A fish breathes the same oxygen, dissolved in water, with gills.
- *what is life:* A tadpole breathes with gills, then grows into a frog that breathes with lungs and skin. One life can change how they breathe.
- *what is this world:* Tiny algae floating in the sea make about half of the oxygen made on Earth each year.

A pond holds one store of dissolved air. Algae and water plants add oxygen to it in sunlight. Fish, tadpoles, snails and the plants themselves take from it all day and all night. So many different lives breathe from the same water, the way a whole family breathes the air of one room. When the store runs low, every one of them feels it.

## Story: The Pond That Gasped at Dawn
*The story is made up. The science in it is real: water holds dissolved oxygen, warm water holds less, algae make oxygen only in light, a pond with many algae has least oxygen just before sunrise, fish then gulp at the surface, and the murrel can breathe air.*

Every May, Kavya went to her grandparents' village near Thanjavur. Thatha's house stood next to the village pond, the kulam. It had stone steps on one side and lotus leaves on the other. In the evenings, frogs called from the edges, and fish made rings on the water.

Kavya was nine. She lived in a flat in Chennai, and she missed this pond all year.

This May was the hottest Thatha could remember. The pond was lower than Kavya had ever seen it. Six steps that were usually under water were now dry and cracked. The water had turned a deep green, and slimy green threads floated near the steps. Paati called it paasi.

On the first night, Kavya took a glass of cold water from the fridge to bed. She fell asleep before she drank it. In the morning the glass was warm, and its inside was covered in tiny silver bubbles. She tapped it. The bubbles shivered and stayed.

'Who put bubbles in my water?' she asked.

'Nobody,' said Thatha, holding the glass to the window. 'They were in there all along. Cold water holds air hidden inside it. When it warms up, it cannot hold as much, so the air comes out.'

Kavya looked at the glass for a long time. Air, hidden in water. She had never thought of that.

The next morning she woke very early, before the sun was up, and went to the steps with her cousin Arjun. The pond was grey and still. Then Kavya saw something strange. All over the water, small mouths were opening and closing at the surface. Hundreds of fish were up at the top, gulping. The water was covered in tiny rings.

'They're drinking,' said Arjun.

Kavya was not so sure. Fish lived in water. Why would they come to the top to drink it?

She ran to fetch Thatha. He came down the steps in his veshti, rubbing his eyes, and his face changed when he saw the pond.

'I have seen this before,' he said quietly. 'In a dry year when I was a boy. One morning, the fish were floating.'

'Are they going to die?' asked Arjun.

'Not if the rain comes soon,' said Thatha. But he did not sound sure.

At noon, Kavya went back. The sun was burning down. Not a single fish was at the surface. The pond looked calm and ordinary. She crouched by the green threads of paasi near the steps and looked closely. They were covered in tiny bubbles, just like her glass.

'Thatha, the paasi has bubbles too,' she called.

Thatha came and looked. 'Paasi is a plant, of a kind,' he said. 'They make their food from sunlight, like the leaves of the coconut tree. And when they do that, they give out a gas.'

'Oxygen,' said Kavya. She had learned that at school. Then she stopped. She thought of her glass. She thought of the fish at dawn, and the calm water at noon.

'Thatha,' she said slowly, 'do fish breathe the air that is hidden in the water?'

'They do,' said Thatha. 'They pass the water over their gills and take the oxygen out of it.'

Kavya's thoughts tumbled out. 'Then in the daytime the paasi makes lots of oxygen, so the fish are fine. But at night there is no sun. The paasi cannot make any. And everything in the pond keeps breathing all night, the fish and the snails and even the paasi. So by morning the oxygen is nearly used up.'

Thatha nodded slowly.

'And the water is so warm this year,' she went on. 'Warm water holds less air. My glass showed that. So the fish come up to the very top, where the water touches the air. That is where there is a little more oxygen.'

Arjun stared at her. 'So they weren't drinking. They were trying to breathe.'

Just then, a long, dark fish rose near the steps. They poked their snout right out of the water, took a gulp of air, and sank again. They did this even at noon.

'That one is a viral,' said Thatha. 'A murrel. They do not mind. They can breathe air, like us.'

For three more mornings, the fish gasped at dawn. Kavya and Arjun watched them every day. Thatha said he hoped for rain.

On the fourth evening, the sky over the fields turned dark purple. The wind came first, ruffling the whole pond into little waves. Then the rain came down hard and cold. It drummed on the lotus leaves and splashed across the water for an hour. The frogs went wild with joy, croaking until Kavya could not hear herself think.

Before sunrise the next morning, Kavya ran to the steps. The water was cooler, and the surface was calm. There were no gulping mouths, no rings. The fish were down below, where they belonged.

Kavya went back to the kitchen and filled a glass with cold water. She held it up to the grey morning light.

'There is a whole pond's worth of breath in here,' she said to Arjun. 'We just can't see it.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: fish breathe the oxygen dissolved in water. Oxygen dissolves in from the air at the surface, and algae and water plants make it in sunlight. Everything in the pond uses it all the time, so at night the oxygen falls, and it is lowest just before sunrise. Warm water holds less oxygen, so in a hot, low, green pond the dawn shortage can be serious. Fish then gulp at the surface, where the water touching the air holds the most oxygen. Cool rain and wind help, because cooler water holds more oxygen and the waves mix air in. The murrel can breathe air, so they cope.

- Ask: What were the tiny bubbles on the paasi at noon?
- Ask: How did the rain and the wind help the fish?
- Ask: Why did the murrel not mind the shortage of oxygen?

### g1 · Pond, River or Sea? (20 min)
- **rules:** Draw the three homes and the mixed middle on a big sheet.; Shuffle the cards and put them face down.; On your turn, take the top card. A living thing card: place it on its home and say one reason it lives there. A QUESTION card: answer it.; If you are right, keep the card. If not, put it at the bottom of the pile.; When the pile is gone, the player with the most cards wins.; Playing alone: play against the clock. Can you place all 24 cards in 10 minutes?
- **Printable board: Three homes to draw on a big sheet:** POND: still fresh water; RIVER: flowing fresh water; SEA: salt water; In the middle: MIXED, where a river meets the sea
- **Printable cards: 24 cards to cut out:** Lotus (Pond); Frog eggs in jelly (Pond); Green threads of paasi, or algae (Pond); Murrel, a fish that gulps air (Pond); Tadpole (Pond); Water lily (Pond); Snail clamped to a stone in a fast stream (River); Small flat fish holding on to rocks (River); Rohu, a big river fish (River); Ganga river dolphin (River); Hilsa swimming upstream to lay eggs (River, and they come from the Sea); Mackerel and sardines (Sea); Coral reef (Sea); Sea turtle (Sea); Tiny algae making half of Earth's new oxygen (Sea); Mangrove trees at Pichavaram (Mixed); Flamingos at Pulicat lake (Mixed); Irrawaddy dolphins in Chilika lake (Mixed); QUESTION: Why do fish gulp at a pond's surface at dawn? (Least oxygen at the end of the night); QUESTION: Does cold or warm water hold more air? (Cold); QUESTION: How does a tadpole breathe? (With gills); QUESTION: How does air reach a lotus's roots in the mud? (Through air canals in their stem); QUESTION: What did very salty water do to a cucumber slice? (Made it floppy, by pulling water out); QUESTION: Which shape stays put in a fast stream? (Low and flat)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the homes on a big sheet. 2. Shuffle the cards.  
  *Parent says:* Every living thing on these cards has a water home. Let's see if we know where.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the three homes. Shuffle the cards.
- **s2 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take turns. Place each living thing card on its home with a reason, or answer the question card.  
  *Parent says:* Where does it live? And why does it suit that home?  
  *Child mode:* Take a card. Where does it live? Say why.
  - Note: Ask for a reason each time. 'The flat fish lives in the river, because they hold on to rocks so the water doesn't carry them away.' The hilsa can go on the river or the sea: they live in the sea and swims up rivers to lay its eggs.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the cards and talk about the hardest one.  
  *Parent says:* Which card was hardest to place? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your cards. Which one was hardest?
  - Check: Which living thing lives in both the sea and rivers at different times of their life? → **The hilsa**
### g2 · Day and Night in the Pond: an oxygen token game (15 min)
- **rules:** Put 8 tokens in a bowl. This is the oxygen in the pond.; Each round, turn over a round card and do what it says.; Then every fish takes 1 token from the pond to breathe. The algae and the snails also use 1 token together.; If there are no tokens left for a fish, that fish swims up to the surface to gulp, and misses the next round.; Play 10 rounds. Count how many fish are still swimming.; Playing alone: you are 3 fish. Take 3 tokens each round, plus 1 for the algae and snails.
- **Printable tokens: 30 oxygen tokens: small paper circles marked O:** O
- **Printable cards: 10 round cards:** SUNNY DAY: algae add 6 tokens to the pond; SUNNY DAY: algae add 6 tokens; CLOUDY DAY: algae add 3 tokens; NIGHT: algae add 0 tokens; NIGHT: algae add 0 tokens; WIND: waves mix in 2 tokens; HOT AFTERNOON: warm water holds less, take 2 tokens out of the pond; RAIN: cool water and splashing, add 3 tokens; SEWAGE: extra rotting uses up 4 tokens; FOUNTAIN: a pump splashes in 3 tokens
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 8 tokens in the bowl. 2. Shuffle the round cards. 3. Sort the NIGHT cards to come one after the other, to show a long night.  
  *Parent says:* The bowl is the pond's oxygen. Each of you is a fish who must breathe one token every round.  
  *Child mode:* The bowl is the pond's oxygen. You are a fish. Take one token each round.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the rounds. After each card, say what it means in the real pond.  
  *Parent says:* Night! The algae can't make oxygen in the dark, but everyone still breathes. Watch the bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Turn a card. Do what it says. Then each fish takes a token.
  - Note: After NIGHT cards, the bowl runs low. That is the dawn shortage in the story. HOT AFTERNOON and SEWAGE make it worse. WIND, RAIN and FOUNTAIN help.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about when the fish were in most danger.  
  *Parent says:* When did the pond run out? What helped most?  
  *Child mode:* When did the tokens run out? What helped?
  - Check: In the game, when did the pond usually run short of oxygen? → **After nights in a row**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Cold Glass Test: is there air hidden in water? (40 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Is there air hidden in clear water? Does cold water hold more of it than warm water?
- **predict:** Guess first. We will warm three glasses of water: one fridge-cold, one fridge-cold that was boiled yesterday, and one from the tap. Which glass will grow the most bubbles on its sides? Which will grow the fewest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use three glasses of the same kind and size, washed the same way. Fill them to the same height. Stand them in the same warm water bath for the same time. Only the water inside changes. Look at all three at the same moments, at 5 minutes and at 15 minutes.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water and mixes the warm bath. The bath should feel warm, like a bath, and never hot. Wipe up spills so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the three glasses, with the bubbles you saw on each.
- **results table:** Glass | What water is in it | My guess: lots, some or almost none? | Bubbles after 5 minutes | Bubbles after 15 minutes
- **schedule:** day 1: A grown-up boils water for 5 minutes and pours it into a bottle filled right to the top. Cap it. Fill a second bottle with tap water. Put both bottles in the fridge overnight. (5 min); day 2: Fill glass A with cold tap water, glass B with cold boiled water and glass C with room-temperature tap water. Stand them in a warm bath. Watch for bubbles at 5 and 15 minutes. (35 min)
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Boil a pan of water for 5 minutes. 2. Let it cool for 10 minutes. 3. Pour it into a bottle, right to the top, and cap it tight. 4. Fill a second bottle with tap water. 5. Put both in the fridge overnight.  
  *Parent says:* Tonight we get the water ready. I am boiling some water. Tomorrow we find out what boiling did to it.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up boils some water. Then it goes in a full bottle. Both bottles go in the fridge till tomorrow.
  - Note: Fill the boiled-water bottle right to the top, so there is almost no air above the water. That keeps the air from dissolving back in as it cools. Label the bottles 'boiled' and 'tap'.
  - Wait: Leave both bottles in the fridge overnight.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label three glasses A, B and C. 2. Fill A from the cold tap-water bottle. 3. Fill B from the cold boiled-water bottle, pouring gently. 4. Fill C from the tap. 5. Fill all three to the same height, about 2 cm from the top.  
  *Parent says:* Here are three glasses of water. They all look the same. Look closely. Can you see any air in them?  
  *Child mode:* Fill the three glasses. A is cold tap water. B is cold boiled water. C is tap water. Look closely. Any bubbles yet?
  - Note: Pour B gently down the side of the glass, so you don't splash air into it. Room-temperature tap water from an overhead tank or a filter is fine for C. At this point there should be few or no bubbles in any glass.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess. Which glass will grow the most bubbles when it warms up, and which the fewest? Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Soon we will warm all three glasses. Which do you think will grow the most bubbles? Which the fewest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which glass will get the most bubbles? Which will get the fewest? Why?
  - Ask: Why did you choose that glass? (good: Because it is the coldest.; Because boiling took something out of it.; Because the tap water is already warm.)
  - Note: Don't explain anything yet. Any guess with a reason is good. Many children think the boiled water will bubble most, because boiling makes bubbles.
- **s4 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix one mug of hot water with two mugs of tap water in the basin. 2. Check it feels warm, not hot, on your wrist. 3. Stand the three glasses in it, side by side. 4. Start a timer.  
  *Parent says:* Now we warm all three glasses the same way, in the same bath. Let's set a timer for 5 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes a warm bath. Stand the three glasses in it. Start the timer.
  - Note: The bath should come about halfway up the glasses. If a plastic tumbler tips or floats, put a spoon in the bath beside it to steady it. While you wait, read a term card or talk about the fish in a pond.
- **s5 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At 5 minutes, shine the torch through the side of each glass. 2. Look at the bottom and the sides. 3. Write lots, some or almost none for each glass. 4. Look again at 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Shine the torch through each glass. Look at the sides and the bottom. Which one has the most bubbles?  
  *Child mode:* Shine the torch through each glass. Look for tiny bubbles. Which has the most?
  - Note: Glass A usually grows many tiny bubbles within 10 to 15 minutes. Glass C grows fewer. Glass B grows very few or none. If nothing happens by 15 minutes, add a little more hot water to the bath and wait 10 more minutes. Tiny bubbles are easier to see with a magnifying glass.
  - Check: Which glass grew the most bubbles? → **A: cold tap water**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tap the side of glass A gently and watch what the bubbles do.  
  *Parent says:* Tap glass A. What do the bubbles do? What do you think they are made of?  
  *Child mode:* Tap glass A gently. Do the bubbles float up? What are they made of?
  - Note: Some bubbles let go and rise to the top. They are air that was dissolved in the water. Nothing was added to the glass, so the air must have been in the water all along.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: clear water has air hidden in it, and cold water holds more. What I saw: glass A grew the most bubbles, glass C fewer, and the boiled water in B almost none. Why: as cold water warms, it cannot hold all its air, so some comes out. Boiling had already driven the air out of B. Then ask: a fish breathes this hidden air. Would a fish do better in a cool pond or a hot one?
  - Think: If warm water holds less air, what happens to fish in a pond on a very hot day?
### e2 · Salty Cucumber: why sea fish and river fish cannot swap homes (30 min)
- **question:** What does very salty water do to a living thing that is used to fresh water?
- **predict:** Guess first. We will put cucumber slices in plain water, in water as salty as the sea, and in very salty water. After 20 minutes, which slices will be stiff, and which will be floppy? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Cut all the slices from the same cucumber, all about half a centimetre thick. Use the same amount of water in each bowl. Leave them for the same time. Only the salt changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the cucumber. Don't eat the salty slices. They are far too salty.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw one slice from each bowl, held by its edge, showing how much it bent.
- **results table:** Bowl | Salt in one cup of water | My guess: stiff or floppy? | Bend test after 20 minutes | How it feels
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour one cup of water into each bowl. 2. Leave the first plain. 3. Stir 1 and a half teaspoons of salt into the second. This is about as salty as the sea. 4. Stir 2 tablespoons of salt into the third. 5. Label the bowls.  
  *Parent says:* These are three kinds of water. Plain, like a river. As salty as the sea. And far saltier than the sea.  
  *Child mode:* Make three bowls. Plain water. Sea water. Very salty water. Stir till the salt disappears.
  - Note: The very salty bowl may not dissolve all its salt. That's fine. The sea has about 35 grams of salt in each litre, which is about 1 and a half teaspoons in a cup.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut 6 slices from the middle of the cucumber, all about half a centimetre thick. 2. Hold one slice by its edge and see how it bends. 3. Put 2 slices in each bowl.  
  *Parent says:* Feel a fresh slice. Hold it by the edge. It hardly bends. Now let's put two in each bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Hold a fresh slice by its edge. Does it bend? Put two slices in each bowl.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what will happen in each bowl. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* In 20 minutes, which slices will be stiff and which floppy? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which slices will go floppy? Why?
  - Note: Don't explain yet. A common guess is that salt will make the slices harder, like salted pickles.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start a 20-minute timer. While you wait, read the card on salt water and fresh water, or play a game.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wait 20 minutes. Let's read about fish while the cucumber works.  
  *Child mode:* Wait 20 minutes. Read a card while you wait.
  - Wait: Wait 20 minutes.
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take one slice from each bowl. 2. Hold each by its edge and see how far it droops. 3. Squeeze each one gently. 4. Write what you find.  
  *Parent says:* Hold each slice by its edge. Which one droops most? Which still stands stiff?  
  *Child mode:* Hold each slice by its edge. Which droops? Which stays stiff?
  - Note: The very salty slices are usually floppy and bendy after 20 minutes. The plain-water slices stay stiff and crisp. The sea-strength slices may be a little bendier than the plain ones, or about the same. That is why we also used very salty water: it makes the change easy to see.
  - Check: The slice in very salty water went floppy. Why? → **Water moved out of the cucumber into the salty water**
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why. Then ask about fish.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you think, what you saw and why. Now imagine a river fish. Their body is much less salty than the sea. What would the sea do to it?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why. What would the sea do to a river fish?
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: very salty water pulls water out of living things. What I saw: the slice in very salty water went floppy, and the plain one stayed stiff. Why: water moved out of the cucumber into the saltier water. A river fish put in the sea would keep losing water like this. Sea fish drink sea water and push the salt out through their gills, so they can live there.
  - Think: Why can't we drink sea water when we are thirsty?
### e3 · Flat or Tall: which shape stays put in a fast stream? (25 min)
- **question:** In flowing water, which shape is hardest for the water to push away: tall, round or flat?
- **predict:** Guess first. We will make three shapes from the same amount of dough: a tall tower, a ball and a flat dome. Then we pour water at them. Which will the water push over or move first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same amount of dough for each shape. Put each shape in the same place on the plate. Pour the same mug of water from the same height, in the same way. Only the shape changes. Try each shape twice.
- **safety:** Do this in the bathroom, on the balcony or over the kitchen sink, and mop up spills so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw each shape, with an arrow showing which way the water pushed it.
- **results table:** Shape | My guess: moves first, second or last? | Try 1: did it move or fall? | Try 2: did it move or fall?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Divide the dough into three equal pieces. 2. Roll one into a tall tower, about as tall as your thumb. 3. Roll one into a ball. 4. Press one into a low flat dome, like an upside-down bowl.  
  *Parent says:* These three shapes are made of exactly the same amount of dough. Only their shape is different.  
  *Child mode:* Make three shapes from equal dough. A tall tower. A ball. A flat dome.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Prop one end of the plate on a book over the sink or bucket. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write the guesses.  
  *Parent says:* Which shape will the water push away first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which shape will the water push first? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that shape will move first? (good: Because it is tall, so the water hits more of it.; Because the ball can roll.; Because the flat one is stuck down.)
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press the tower gently onto the middle of the plate. 2. Pour the mug of water down the slope at it, from the top edge, steadily. 3. Watch what happens. 4. Do the same for the ball and the flat dome. 5. Repeat each one.  
  *Parent says:* Pour steadily from the top of the plate, the same way each time. Watch closely. Did it slide, fall over or stay?  
  *Child mode:* Pour water down the plate at each shape. Does it slide, fall or stay?
  - Note: Usually the tower topples or slides first, and the ball rolls away easily. The flat dome usually stays put, because the water slides over it and it has a wide base pressed to the plate. If nothing moves, tip the plate a little more or pour faster. If everything moves, pour more gently.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for claim, evidence and reasoning. Then talk about stream animals.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why? Now think of a snail or a small fish in a fast hill stream. Which shape helps them?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why. Which shape helps a stream snail?
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: low, flat shapes stay put best in flowing water. What I saw: the tower fell over, the ball rolled away, and the flat dome stayed. Why: water pushes harder on a tall shape, and slides over a flat one.
  - Check: Which shape stayed put best in the flowing water? → **The flat dome**

### a1 · Holes in a Lotus Stem: blow air through it (10 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the stem. Wash the stem before you put your mouth on it, and use a fresh piece for each person.
- **record:** Draw the slice and its holes. Count the holes.
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut a thin slice across the lotus stem. 2. Hold it up to the window. 3. Count the holes.  
  *Parent says:* This is a lotus stem. It grew in the mud at the bottom of a pond. Look through the slice. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the slice up to the light. Count the holes. What are they for?
  - Note: You should see a ring of large round holes, with smaller ones between. These are air canals. They run the whole length of the stem.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dip one end of the 6 cm piece into the glass of water. 2. Blow gently into the other end. 3. Watch the end under water.  
  *Parent says:* If these holes are tubes, air should go right through. Blow gently into the top. What comes out under the water?  
  *Child mode:* Put one end in the water. Blow gently into the other end. What comes out?
  - Note: Streams of bubbles should come out of the end under water. That shows the holes are open tubes that run along the stem. If no bubbles come, cut a shorter piece, about 3 cm, between two joints.
  - Check: Why does a lotus need air tubes in their stem? → **To carry air down to their parts in the mud, where there is almost no air**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the problem the lotus solves.  
  *Parent says:* The leaves are up in the air and light. The roots are deep in the airless mud. How does the lotus look after both?  
  *Child mode:* The leaves are up high. The roots are deep in mud. How does air reach the roots?
  - Note: If you can't find a lotus stem, look at a photo of one cut across, and blow through a drinking straw into water to see what a tube of air does.

### a3 · Where the River Meets the Sea: layers in a glass (15 min)
- **safety:** Use cold tea, not hot.
- **record:** Draw the glass with its layers and label them 'river water' and 'sea water'.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Half fill the glass with water. 2. Stir in 3 tablespoons of salt until most of it dissolves. 3. Let it settle for a minute.  
  *Parent says:* This salty water is our sea. Now we will pour a river onto it.  
  *Child mode:* Fill half a glass with water. Stir in lots of salt. This is the sea.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold a spoon upside down just above the salty water. 2. Pour the coloured water very slowly onto the back of the spoon. 3. Fill the glass.  
  *Parent says:* Pour very slowly, onto the back of the spoon. Watch where the coloured river water goes.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the coloured water slowly onto the spoon. Where does it go?
  - Note: The coloured fresh water should stay on top as a separate layer, because salty water is heavier than the same amount of fresh water. If it mixes, start again and pour more slowly.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about real places where this happens.  
  *Parent says:* In the Kerala backwaters and in Pulicat lake, sea water creeps in under the river water. Why do you think fish there must cope with both?  
  *Child mode:* Rivers meet the sea in some places. The water there is a mix. Who could live there?
  - Note: Water that is a mix of fresh and salt is called brackish. Mangrove trees, crabs and some fish live in it. In Kerala, the Thanneermukkom barrier was built across Vembanad lake to keep salt water out of the rice fields of Kuttanad.
  - Check: Why did the coloured fresh water stay on top? → **Salty water is heavier than the same amount of fresh water**

### p1 · Green Water Jar: grow a tiny pond (45 min, 21 days)
- **safety:** Wash your hands after touching the jar water. Never drink it. Cover the jars with a cloth tied with a rubber band, so mosquitoes cannot lay eggs in them.
- **record:** Draw both jars every week and write the colour of the water.
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up two jars of water with a pinch of pot soil. Put one in a sunny window and one in a dark cupboard. Cover both with cloth. (15 min); day 7: Look at both jars. Is either one turning green? Draw them. (10 min); day 14: Look again. Look closely at the sides with a magnifying glass. Draw them. (10 min); day 21: Compare the two jars and explain the difference. Then pour the water into a pot plant. (10 min)
- **s1 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill both jars with tap water that has stood open for a day. 2. Add a pinch of pot soil to each. 3. Cover each with a cloth. 4. Put one on a sunny windowsill and one in a dark cupboard.  
  *Parent says:* We are making two tiny ponds. One gets sunlight and one gets none. Let's see which one comes alive with green.  
  *Child mode:* Fill two jars. Add a pinch of soil. Cover them. One goes in the sun. One goes in the dark.
  - Note: Leaving tap water open for a day lets some of its chlorine escape. The soil or vase water brings in algae too small to see.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at both jars after one week. Draw them.  
  *Parent says:* Has either jar changed colour? Look at the side that faces the window.  
  *Child mode:* Look at both jars. Any green? Draw them.

## Applications
- Aquarium air pumps and filters bubble air into fish tanks.
- Fish farmers in Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal and Kerala run paddle wheels in their ponds at night to splash air in.
- Fountains and aerators in city lakes mix air into the water.
- Scientists measure dissolved oxygen to check whether a river or lake is healthy.
- Sewage treatment plants clean waste water before it reaches rivers, so it does not feed algal blooms.
- Barriers like the Thanneermukkom bund in Kerala keep salt water out of rice fields.
- Lotus stems, lotus seeds and water chestnuts are foods grown in ponds.
- Divers and submarines carry their own air, because people cannot take oxygen out of water.

## Open questions
- **What do fish feel?** Fish react to harm and learn to avoid it, but scientists still argue about what they feel inside, because nobody can ask a fish.
- **How many kinds of tiny algae live in the sea?** Scientists keep finding new kinds. Many are too small to tell apart without special tools, and most of the sea has never been sampled.
- **How will warmer seas and lakes change the life in them?** Warmer water holds less oxygen. Scientists are measuring how much the oxygen in the sea is falling, and what that will do, but nobody knows the full answer yet.
- **How did the first animals move from water onto land?** Fossils show fish with leg-like fins about 375 million years ago, but many steps of the story are still missing.
- **How many frog species live in India?** Scientists keep finding new frogs in the Western Ghats and the north-east, often a dozen or more in a single year.

- In the Cold Glass Test, Lakshmi counted the bubbles on the bottom of each glass after 15 minutes. Glass A had 48 bubbles. Glass C had 12 bubbles. How many times as many bubbles did glass A have? **4 times as many.**. Glass A had 48 bubbles and glass C had 12. 48 divided by 12 is 4. So glass A had 4 times as many. Check: 12 + 12 + 12 + 12 = 48. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Sea water has about 35 grams of salt in every litre. A bucket holds 10 litres of sea water. About how much salt is in it? **About 350 grams.**. Each litre has about 35 grams. 10 litres have 10 times as much. 35 times 10 is 350. So about 350 grams, which is about as heavy as a small packet of salt. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Day and Night token game, the pond starts with 8 oxygen tokens. Three fish and the algae and snails use 4 tokens every round. Two NIGHT cards come in a row, adding nothing. How many tokens are left after the two nights? **None. 8 - 4 - 4 = 0.**. Each round uses 3 tokens for the fish and 1 for the algae and snails, so 4. After the first night, 8 - 4 = 4 are left. After the second, 4 - 4 = 0. The fish must gulp at the surface. One more night would leave them short. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Kavya's water bath started at 40 degrees. After 15 minutes it was 34 degrees. By how many degrees did it cool? If it kept cooling at the same speed, what would it be after another 15 minutes? **It cooled by 6 degrees. After another 15 minutes it would be about 28 degrees.**. 40 - 34 = 6, so it cooled 6 degrees in 15 minutes. Another 15 minutes at the same speed would take off another 6: 34 - 6 = 28. Really, warm water cools more slowly as it gets near room temperature, so it might stay a little warmer. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
- A frog's tadpole stage lasts about 8 weeks. How many days is that? If a tadpole hatched on the 1st of July, about when would they become a frog? **56 days. About the 26th of August.**. A week has 7 days. 8 times 7 is 56. July has 31 days, so after 1st July, 30 days take us to the 31st of July. 56 - 30 = 26 more days takes us to about the 26th of August. Different frogs take different times, and warm water makes tadpoles grow faster. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What does a fish breathe? → The oxygen dissolved in water.
- Does cold or warm water hold more air? → Cold water.
- How does a tadpole breathe? → With gills.
- Name two ways an adult frog breathes. → With their lungs and through their wet skin.
- What are the holes in a lotus stem for? → Carrying air down to the parts in the mud.
- When do algae make oxygen? → In light.
- Name a water habitat that is salty. → The sea, such as the Bay of Bengal or the Arabian Sea.
- What is water called that is a mix of fresh and salty? → Brackish water.
