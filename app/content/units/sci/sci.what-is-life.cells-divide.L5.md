# Two ways to divide, cells with no job yet, and cells that die on time
`sci.what-is-life.cells-divide.L5`

**Summary.** Your fingers began as a flat paddle, so some of your cells had to die on time. How does a body make a hand? In this unit you will test which piece of a mint stem grows roots, and act out two ways cells divide with paper chromosomes.

**Intro.** *Cut a 6 centimetre piece from a pudina stem and stand it in water. In about a week it grows roots, and later it becomes a whole plant. The piece had no roots at all. So which cells made the new roots, and where in the stem were they waiting? Say what you think, and why.* You will test this yourself in the Pudina Pieces Test, with four kinds of pieces. Before that, here is a second puzzle. Every cell in your body came from one first cell, by copying. Copies are the same as each other. So why are a brother and a sister from the same two parents not copies of each other? Both puzzles are about how cells divide, and about what a cell is still able to become.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what mitosis does and what meiosis does, and how the cells they make are different.
1. By the end I can explain why egg and sperm cells carry half the chromosomes, and why two children of the same parents are not copies.
2. By the end I can say what a stem cell is, and how the stem cells of an early embryo differ from those in a grown body.
3. By the end I can explain what programmed cell death is, give three examples, and tell it apart from death by injury.
4. By the end I can run a fair test with cuttings, and say what it shows about where a plant keeps cells that can still divide.
5. By the end I can use doubling and halving to work out chromosome numbers and the number of possible egg cells.
6. By the end I can give two views on a question about cells that scientists have not settled.

## Terms
- **Chromosome**: A chromosome is one long thread of DNA, wound up tight. DNA is the long molecule that holds a cell's instructions. A human body cell has 46 chromosomes. *e.g.* A garden pea plant has 14 chromosomes in each body cell, and a rice plant has 24.
- **Matching pair**: Your 46 chromosomes are 23 matching pairs. In each pair, one chromosome came from your mother and one from your father. The two carry the same kinds of instructions, but often different versions. *e.g.* Both chromosomes of one pair may carry instructions for the ear lobe. One version may say 'attached' and the other 'free'.
- **Mitosis**: Mitosis is cell division that makes copies. The cell copies every chromosome and divides once. The two new cells each have the full set, the same as the first cell. *e.g.* When a scraped knee heals, skin cells at the edge of the scrape divide by mitosis.
- **Meiosis**: Meiosis is the special division that makes egg cells and sperm cells. The cell copies every chromosome once and then divides twice. The new cells have half the set, and no two are the same. *e.g.* In a hibiscus flower, meiosis happens inside the anthers, where pollen is made.
- **Sex cell**: A sex cell is a cell with half the chromosomes, made to join with another sex cell. Egg cells and sperm cells are sex cells. Scientists also call them gametes. *e.g.* A human egg cell has 23 chromosomes, and so has a human sperm cell.
- **Fertilisation**: Fertilisation is the joining of an egg cell and a sperm cell into one cell. That one cell has the full number of chromosomes again. *e.g.* 23 chromosomes from the egg and 23 from the sperm make the 46 of a fertilised human egg.
- **Crossing over**: Crossing over is a swap of matching pieces between the two chromosomes of a pair. It happens early in meiosis, and it mixes the mother's and father's versions on one chromosome. *e.g.* After crossing over, one chromosome can carry your grandmother's version at one end and your grandfather's at the other.
- **Specialised cell**: A specialised cell is a cell that has taken on one job, and has the shape and parts for that job. *e.g.* A red blood cell carries oxygen. A nerve cell carries signals. A root hair cell takes in water.
- **Stem cell**: A stem cell is a cell that has not taken a job yet. When she divides, she can make another stem cell and also a cell that goes on to specialise. *e.g.* Blood stem cells in your bone marrow make every kind of blood cell, every day of your life.
- **Embryonic stem cell**: An embryonic stem cell is a stem cell from an embryo a few days old. An embryo is a living being at the earliest stage of growing. These cells can become every kind of cell in the body. *e.g.* In a five-day-old embryo, a small group of inner cells will make the whole body.
- **Adult stem cell**: An adult stem cell is a stem cell in a body that is already formed, in a child or a grown-up. She can make only the few kinds of cell that her own part of the body needs. *e.g.* A skin stem cell makes new skin cells. She does not make blood or bone.
- **Bone marrow**: Bone marrow is the soft tissue inside large bones. Blood stem cells live there and make new blood cells. *e.g.* The marrow in your hip bones, ribs and backbone makes about two million red blood cells every second.
- **Node**: A node is the place on a plant stem where a leaf joins. A node holds a tiny bud, and cells that can start dividing again. *e.g.* On a pudina stem, the nodes are the small bumps where each pair of leaves is attached.
- **Programmed cell death**: Programmed cell death is the death of a cell by plan. The cell gets a signal and takes herself apart in a tidy way. The best-known kind in animals is called apoptosis (say: a-pop-TOE-sis). *e.g.* The cells between your fingers died this way before you were born, and that separated your fingers.
- **Necrosis**: Necrosis is the death of cells by injury, such as a burn, a crush or a loss of blood supply. The cells swell and burst, and the place becomes red and sore. *e.g.* The skin under a bad burn dies by necrosis. Nobody planned it, and the body has to clean it up.
- **Checkpoint**: A checkpoint is a pause in cell division. The cell checks that a step is finished before she goes on to the next step. *e.g.* A dividing cell waits until every chromosome is held by threads from both sides. Only then does she pull the copies apart.

## Explanations
### Two ways to divide: mitosis and meiosis · *defined*
**Definition:** Mitosis makes two cells that each have the full set of chromosomes, the same as the first cell. Meiosis makes cells with half the set, and each one is different.

Almost every division in your body is mitosis. The cell copies all 46 chromosomes. The copies line up one by one across the middle, and one copy of each goes to each side. Then the cell divides once. Both new cells have 46 chromosomes, with the same instructions. That is what a body needs for growing and mending: more cells of the same kind. Meiosis happens in only one place, the organs that make egg cells or sperm cells. It starts the same way, by copying every chromosome once. Then two things are different. First, the chromosomes do not line up one by one. Each chromosome finds its matching partner, and the pairs line up together. Second, the cell divides twice. The first division separates the partners. The second division separates the copies. The result is four cells with 23 chromosomes each. This is the simple picture. One more detail matters. In a testis, all four cells become sperm. In an ovary, the divisions are unequal, so there is one large egg cell and two or three tiny cells that break down.
- e.g. A fertilised egg divides by mitosis to make the trillions of cells of a body.
- e.g. A moong root gets longer because cells at its tip divide by mitosis.
- e.g. In a pumpkin flower, meiosis in the anthers leads to pollen, and meiosis in the ovules leads to egg cells.
- e.g. In the Two Dances activity, paper chromosomes show both divisions side by side.
### Why egg and sperm cells must have half · *defined*
**Definition:** An egg cell and a sperm cell join to start a new life. Each must bring half the chromosomes, so that the fertilised egg has the full number and not double.

Think what would happen without the halving. A human egg with 46 chromosomes would join a sperm with 46. The child would have 92 in every cell. The grandchild would have 184, and the number would double in every generation. Cells do not work with a doubled set like that, and a human embryo with 92 does not live. So the number must be halved before it is added together again. But the halving has to be exact. A sex cell does not get any 23 chromosomes. She gets one chromosome from each of the 23 matching pairs. That way no kind of instruction is missing and none is doubled. This is why the pairs line up as partners in meiosis. Lining up as partners is how the cell makes sure that one of each pair goes each way. Some plants do live well with doubled sets. Bread wheat has six sets of chromosomes. Their meiosis still halves the number exactly.
- e.g. A pea plant has 14 chromosomes. A pea egg cell has 7, and a sperm cell inside a pollen grain has 7. Together they make 14 again.
- e.g. In the Double Trouble activity, a katori of 14 seeds becomes 28, then 56, when nothing is halved.
- e.g. A rice plant has 24 chromosomes in body cells and 12 in sex cells.
### The shuffle: why no two egg cells are alike · *defined*
**Definition:** In meiosis, each matching pair of chromosomes is shared out by chance, and the partners first swap pieces. So every egg cell and every sperm cell carries a different mix.

Your mother has 23 pairs of chromosomes. In each pair, one came from her mother and one from her father. When she makes an egg cell, the egg gets one chromosome from each pair. Which one it gets is chance, and each pair is shared out separately. With one pair there are 2 possible eggs. With two pairs there are 4, and with three pairs there are 8. The number doubles with every pair. With 23 pairs it comes to 8,388,608 different eggs. A father can make as many different sperm. So the same two parents could have more than 70 trillion different children, before any swapping is counted. And there is swapping. Early in meiosis the two partners lie side by side and exchange matching pieces. This is called crossing over. After it, a single chromosome is part grandmother's and part grandfather's. In 1931 Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock proved, with maize plants, that the chromosomes really do exchange pieces. So a brother and a sister are not copies. Each grew from a different egg and a different sperm. Identical twins are the exception, because they grew from one fertilised egg that split in two.
- e.g. In the Shuffle Test, three coins stand for three pairs of chromosomes, and 24 tosses give up to 8 different egg cells.
- e.g. Two sisters share about half of their DNA on average, but never exactly the same half.
- e.g. You did not get exactly one quarter of your DNA from each grandparent. The shuffle gives a little more from some and a little less from others.
### Egg cells wait, and sperm cells are made every day · *defined*
**Definition:** A girl's egg cells begin meiosis before she is born, and then they pause for years. A boy's body starts meiosis when he is about 11 to 14, and after that makes sperm every day.

A baby girl is born with all her egg cells, about one to two million of them. Each has already started meiosis and stopped part of the way through. They wait like that through her whole childhood. When she is grown, usually one egg cell each month carries on. The egg finishes the last step of meiosis only if a sperm cell enters her. So the egg cell that became you began to form inside your mother before your mother was born. A man's body works differently. From the early teens, stem cells in the testes keep dividing. Their daughter cells go through meiosis, and it takes about ten weeks to make a sperm cell. Many millions are finished every day. Sperm cells also decide one more thing. In 1905 Nettie Stevens studied mealworm beetles and found two kinds of sperm, one with a large chromosome and one with a small one. Eggs fertilised by the small-chromosome kind became males. Humans are similar: an egg always carries an X chromosome, and a sperm carries an X or a Y.
- e.g. A woman of 35 releases an egg cell that has been paused in meiosis for more than 35 years.
- e.g. Of the million or more egg cells a girl is born with, only about 400 are ever released.
- e.g. Nettie Stevens saw the two kinds of sperm through her microscope at Bryn Mawr College in the United States.
### When the sharing goes wrong · *defined*
**Definition:** Sometimes two partner chromosomes fail to separate in meiosis. Then one sex cell gets an extra chromosome and another gets one too few.

A dividing cell is careful. She has a checkpoint: she waits until every chromosome is held by threads from both sides before she pulls. Even so, a pair sometimes goes to the same side together. If that sex cell is fertilised, every cell of the new embryo has 47 chromosomes, or 45. Most such embryos stop growing very early, often before the mother knows she is pregnant. With some chromosomes the embryo can live and grow. A person with three copies of chromosome 21 has Down syndrome. People with Down syndrome usually learn more slowly and often have heart or eye problems that doctors can treat. They go to school, work, and live full lives. In 1958, in Paris, Marthe Gautier grew cells from children with Down syndrome and was the first to see the extra chromosome. The paper about it put a male colleague's name first, and for fifty years he got most of the credit. Unequal sharing is more common in egg cells that have waited longer. One likely reason is that the links holding the paired chromosomes together weaken over the years of waiting.
- e.g. In the Blind Share challenge, you design a way to share pairs with no mistakes while blindfolded, and compare it with the cell's way.
- e.g. About 1 baby in 700 to 1,000 is born with Down syndrome.
- e.g. A cell with one unattached chromosome waits at the checkpoint, sometimes for hours.
### Stem cells: cells that have not taken a job yet · *defined*
**Definition:** A stem cell is a cell that can do two things when she divides: make another stem cell, and make a cell that goes on to specialise.

Most cells in your body are specialised. A red blood cell carries oxygen and will never be anything else. A stem cell is different, because she has not specialised. When she divides, one daughter can stay a stem cell, so the supply never runs out. The other daughter goes on to become a working cell. It is often said that stem cells can become any cell. That is true only for the earliest ones. A fertilised egg, and her first few daughter cells, can each make a whole body. A few days later the embryo is a hollow ball. A small group of inner cells will make every kind of cell in the body, more than 200 kinds. These are embryonic stem cells. Gail Martin gave them that name in 1981, when she grew them from mouse embryos. As the body forms, cells choose their paths, and the choices narrow. The stem cells left in a grown body are adult stem cells. Each kind makes only the cells of her own tissue. Blood stem cells make blood, and skin stem cells make skin.
- e.g. Blood stem cells were proved to exist in 1961 by James Till and Ernest McCulloch in Toronto, who counted the colonies of new blood cells in mice.
- e.g. Stem cells at the bottom of tiny pits in your gut wall remake the whole gut lining every few days.
- e.g. A plant keeps cells like these at every root tip, every shoot tip and every bud. Such a growing point is called a meristem.
### Stem cells at work in a grown body · *defined*
**Definition:** Adult stem cells replace the cells that a body uses up, such as blood cells, skin cells and gut lining cells. Doctors can move some of them from one person to another.

A red blood cell lasts about four months. Your body holds about 25 trillion of them, so about two million must be made every second. Blood stem cells in your bone marrow do this, without a break. Doctors use this in a bone marrow transplant. A person whose marrow is ill, for example with a blood cancer called leukaemia, is given blood stem cells from a healthy person. The new stem cells settle in the bones and make blood for the rest of that person's life. Hospitals in India, including Tata Memorial Hospital in Mumbai and Christian Medical College in Vellore, have done this since the 1980s. The clear front window of your eye, the cornea, has stem cells too. They sit in a ring at its edge. A splash of chuna (lime) in the eye can kill them, and then the cornea turns cloudy. Since 2001, doctors at L V Prasad Eye Institute in Hyderabad, led by Virender Sangwan with the scientist Geeta Vemuganti, have taken a tiny piece of that ring from the person's other eye and grown new surface from it. Many people have got their sight back this way. These are the stem cell treatments that are well proven: blood, skin for burns, and the surface of the eye. Many others are still being tested.
- e.g. After a blood donation, your bone marrow replaces the red cells in about four to eight weeks.
- e.g. Skin grown from a burn patient's own skin stem cells can cover large burns.
- e.g. A bone marrow donor's own marrow grows back within a few weeks.
### Can a specialised cell go back? · *defined*
**Definition:** Plant cells can often go back to dividing and build any part of the plant. Animal cells rarely do this by themselves, but scientists can make them go back.

In the Pudina Pieces Test, roots grow from a stem that had none. Some cells near the node were already part of a finished stem. When the stem is cut and stood in water, they start dividing again, and their daughters build a root tip. Plants can go much further. In 1958 Frederick Steward grew whole carrot plants from single cells of a carrot root, in a liquid that contained coconut water. In 1964 Sipra Guha and Satish Maheshwari at the University of Delhi grew small plants from the young pollen of datura flowers. Those plants had only half the usual chromosomes, because pollen is made by meiosis. Most animals, including humans, do less of this. A house gecko regrows a lost tail. A flatworm called a planarian can be cut into many pieces, and each piece regrows a whole worm, because stem cells are spread all through their body. A human regrows liver, skin and blood, but not an arm. For a long time people thought an animal cell that had specialised could never go back. In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka and Kazutoshi Takahashi in Kyoto switched on just four genes in mouse skin cells. The skin cells turned into cells very like embryonic stem cells. So the instructions for every job are still there in a specialised cell. They are closed, not lost.
- e.g. A potato piece with an 'eye' grows a whole plant, because the eye is a bud with dividing cells.
- e.g. If two thirds of a human liver is removed, the rest grows back to full size in a few weeks.
- e.g. A gecko's new tail has a rod of cartilage inside and no new bones, so it is a repair and not an exact copy.
### Programmed cell death: cells that die on time · *defined*
**Definition:** Programmed cell death is the planned death of a cell. The cell receives a signal, or senses damage inside herself, and takes herself apart neatly. In animals the main kind is called apoptosis.

A cell that dies by injury swells and bursts. Her contents spill out and irritate the cells around her, and the place becomes red and sore. That is necrosis. Apoptosis is very different. The cell shrinks. She cuts her own DNA into pieces and packs everything into small sealed parcels. Cleaner cells nearby swallow the parcels and reuse the material. Nothing spills, and nothing gets sore. Bodies use this in three ways. The first is shaping. Your hand began as a flat paddle. Finger bones formed inside it, and between about the sixth and eighth week the cells between them died, which freed the fingers. A tadpole's tail goes the same way when they become a frog. The second is choosing. A growing brain makes far more nerve cells than it keeps. In many parts, about half of them die, mostly those that did not link up well with others. Viktor Hamburger and Rita Levi-Montalcini first counted such deaths in chick embryos in 1949. The third is safety. A cell whose DNA is damaged beyond repair kills herself, and so does a cell taken over by a virus. This stops many cancers before they start. Plants use planned death too, with their own machinery. The cells that become a plant's water pipes build thick walls, then die and empty themselves, so that water can flow through.
- e.g. After a sunburn, skin peels because skin cells with badly damaged DNA have died by plan.
- e.g. A duck keeps webbed feet because a signal blocker in the duck embryo's foot stops the cells between the toes from dying.
- e.g. In the Dead on Purpose Hunt, you find hair, nails, wood, cotton and coir, all made of cells that finished their work by dying.
### Why would a cell die for others? · *debated*
**Definition:** In a body, a cell that dies by plan protects cells that carry the same instructions. Why single-celled beings also have programmed death is still argued about.

In a body, the answer is fairly clear. All the cells of a body come from one fertilised egg and carry the same instructions. Only the egg and sperm cells pass those instructions to the next generation. So when a damaged skin cell dies, the instructions she carried still go on, through the body she protected. But programmed death is also found in beings that are a single cell. That is harder to explain, and scientists argue about it. The death machinery is also oddly placed. In animal cells, one main death signal is a protein let out by the mitochondria, the parts that release energy from food. Xiaodong Wang's laboratory found this in 1996. Mitochondria were once free-living bacteria, as Lynn Margulis argued in 1967. Why the death switch sits with them is not known.
- e.g. A bacterium attacked by a virus sometimes destroys herself before the virus can multiply, which protects her sister cells nearby.
- e.g. Old yeast cells in a crowded colony can die in a tidy, programmed way.
- **Biologists who study bacteria and their viruses:** A single cell who dies when a virus infects her saves her neighbours. Those neighbours are her sisters, with the same instructions. So her death keeps her own instructions going.
- **Biologists who doubt this:** Much of what looks like planned death in single cells may be a breakdown, or a side effect of machinery that does other jobs. It may not have been shaped for the good of the neighbours.
- **A view from the whole:** A cell senses her own state and her neighbours, and decides. The colony or the body is the living being, and the cell acts as part of that being. Seen this way, the puzzle only comes from counting each cell as a separate life.

## Mostly agreed
- Body cells divide by mitosis, and both new cells get a full copy of every chromosome. *How we know:* Walther Flemming watched chromosomes being shared in dividing salamander cells in the 1870s and 1880s. Counts in thousands of kinds of cells since then agree.
- Egg and sperm cells have half the chromosomes of body cells, and fertilisation restores the full number. *How we know:* In 1883 Edouard van Beneden studied a roundworm with only 4 chromosomes. He counted 2 in the egg, 2 in the sperm and 4 in the fertilised egg.
- Paired chromosomes exchange pieces during meiosis. *How we know:* In 1931 Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock used a maize chromosome with a knob that could be seen in a microscope. When the inherited features crossed over, the knob crossed over too.
- One blood stem cell can make every kind of blood cell. *How we know:* In 1961 and 1963, James Till, Ernest McCulloch and Andrew Becker showed that each colony of mixed blood cells in a mouse's spleen grew from a single cell.
- Some cells die by plan, at fixed times and places, as a normal part of growing. *How we know:* John Sulston followed every cell division in a tiny roundworm. The same 131 cells died in every worm. Robert Horvitz then found genes that the cells need in order to die.
- A specialised animal cell still holds the instructions for other jobs. *How we know:* In 2006 Kazutoshi Takahashi and Shinya Yamanaka turned mouse skin cells into stem-like cells by switching on four genes. Laboratories around the world have repeated it.

## How we found out
**Carl Vogt. Viktor Hamburger and Rita Levi-Montalcini. John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie. John Sulston. Hilary Ellis and Robert Horvitz., 1842 (Vogt), 1949 (Hamburger and Levi-Montalcini), 1972 (the name apoptosis), 1977 to 1983 (Sulston's cell maps) and 1986 (the genes).** For a long time, scientists thought a cell died only when something harmed her. In 1842 Carl Vogt, working in Switzerland, watched toad tadpoles change and saw healthy cells disappear as new parts formed. Few people took notice. In 1949 Viktor Hamburger and Rita Levi-Montalcini counted nerve cells in chick embryos, and found that many died as a normal part of growing. In 1972, in Aberdeen, John Kerr, Andrew Wyllie and Alastair Currie described a tidy kind of cell death that looked the same in many tissues. They named it apoptosis, a Greek word for leaves falling from a tree. The proof that it is planned came from a roundworm one millimetre long. In Cambridge, John Sulston watched every cell of the worm divide, through a microscope, for years. The same 131 cells died in every worm, at the same moments. Then Hilary Ellis and Robert Horvitz found genes for dying. In worms with one of those genes broken, the 131 cells lived.  
*What people believed before:* That a cell dies only by accident, when something harms her. Sulston's worms showed that the same cells die at the same moments in every animal. Ellis and Horvitz showed that the cells need working genes in order to die.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A stem cell can become any kind of cell.** (A common way of speaking, and many advertisements; *rejected*)  
  For: It is true of the stem cells in an embryo a few days old. They make every kind of cell in the body.  
  Against: The stem cells in a child or a grown-up are adult stem cells. A blood stem cell makes blood cells and nothing else. Saying 'any cell' about them is wrong.
- **When cells die, something has gone wrong in the body.** (What most people assume; *rejected*)  
  For: Cells killed by a burn, a crush or a blocked blood vessel are a real loss, and too much cell death causes some illnesses.  
  Against: A healthy adult loses many billions of cells every day by plan. Fingers, a working brain and a safe immune system are all made with the help of cells dying on time. Too little cell death is one cause of cancer.
- **Stem cell injections can cure almost any illness.** (Some private clinics; *rejected*)  
  For: Bone marrow transplants, skin grafts for burns and stem cell repair of the eye's surface are real and well proven.  
  Against: For most other illnesses there is no good evidence yet. The Indian Council of Medical Research said in its 2017 guidelines that other stem cell treatments may be given only inside proper trials. Unproven injections have harmed people.
- **You get exactly one quarter of your DNA from each grandparent.** (A simple rule in many books; *rejected*)  
  For: You get exactly half from each parent, so a quarter from each grandparent is right on average.  
  Against: Each chromosome your mother gave you is a mix made by crossing over, and each pair was shared out by chance. So one grandmother may have given you 28 parts in 100 and one grandfather 22.
- **Meiosis and two parents are a waste. A living being would do better to copy herself.** (A real puzzle raised by the biologist John Maynard Smith in the 1970s; *debated*)  
  For: A female who copies herself passes on all her instructions, and every one of her young can have young. Some animals do this. Tiny water animals called bdelloid rotifers have been all female for millions of years.  
  Against: Almost all animals and plants still use meiosis and two parents. The leading idea is that the shuffle keeps a family ahead of fast-changing germs. A study of New Zealand snails found more two-parent breeding in lakes with more parasites.

## Roots
We have learned that cells divide in two ways, that some cells have no job yet, and that some cells die by plan. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In Paddle to Hand, the dough hand that held together was made by cutting dough away. Your real fingers were freed the same way, when the cells between them died on time. A frog's legs are finished with material from the dying tail. So in a living body, cells dying at the right time is part of growing, not a failure of it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of death as the opposite of life, something that only happens when life fails. Cells show that planned dying is one of the ways a body is built and kept safe. Too little of it gives webbed fingers and cancers.  
*Evidence:* In Paddle to Hand, fingers came from taking away. In the Dead on Purpose Hunt, hair, nails, wood and cotton were all cells that finished their work by dying. In a roundworm, the same 131 cells die in every worm, and those cells need working genes in order to die.

- *who am i:* The egg cell that became you began to form inside your mother before she was born. Your hand has fingers because some of your own cells died on time.
- *what is life:* Life carries on in two ways at once. Mitosis copies what works. Meiosis shuffles it into someone who has never lived before.
- *what is this world:* In the living world, ending is part of making. A tree's water pipes, a frog's legs and your fingers all needed some cells to finish.

One fertilised egg becomes trillions of cells by mitosis. The cells take hundreds of different jobs, and some die so that the others can form a hand or stay safe. Then meiosis makes single cells again, each a new mix, and one of them may begin another body. So a body is one cell that became many, and the many still act together as one.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 11 (paraphrased):** “三十輻共一轂，當其無，有車之用。 / 埏埴以為器，當其無，有器之用。” Clay is shaped into a pot, and the empty space inside is what makes the pot useful. Doors and windows are cut in a wall, and the openings are what make the room useful. (The gaps between your fingers are what make a hand useful. The emptiness inside a tree's dead pipe cells is what carries the water. In both, something was taken away by plan.)
- **Thirukkural 339 (paraphrased):** “உறங்குவது போலும் சாக்காடு உறங்கி / விழிப்பது போலும் பிறப்பு.” Thiruvalluvar says that death is like falling asleep, and birth is like waking from that sleep. (The verse puts dying and being born side by side, as two halves of one rhythm. In your body, millions of cells reach their end every second and millions are made. Both belong to being alive.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 2.27 (paraphrased):** “जातस्य हि ध्रुवो मृत्युर्ध्रुवं जन्म मृतस्य च। / तस्मादपरिहार्येऽर्थे न त्वं शोचितुमर्हसि।।” For one who is born, death is certain. For one who has died, birth is certain. (A gut lining cell is born, works for a few days and dies, and a new one takes her place. The gut carries on because both things keep happening.)
- Science (settled): Every cell weighs signals to live against signals to die before she acts. Biologists call this a cell fate decision, and they can measure the signals.
- Science (open): In animal cells, one main death signal is let out by the mitochondria, which were once free-living bacteria. Why the switch for death sits there is not known.
- Other view, Many cell biologists: Apoptosis is a chain of chemical reactions, in which certain proteins cut up other proteins. Words such as 'decides' or 'for the body' are only a way of speaking. Cells that carried this chain left more descendants, and that is the whole explanation.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your fingers exist because other cells of yours died. Were those cells part of you? (hint: They had your DNA and came from the same first cell. Think about what 'part of you' means.)
- *big:* The same two parents could have had 70 trillion different children. Why are you the one who is here? (hint: Think about chance. Is there an answer to 'why this one', or only to 'how'?)
- *deep:* A pudina cell that was part of a stem became a root. A skin cell stays skin. What does 'what I am' mean for a cell? (hint: Is a cell's job something she is, or something she is doing?)
- *deep:* A cell that senses damaged DNA takes herself apart. Is that a decision? (hint: She senses, compares and acts. Some scientists call it only chemistry. What would make something a decision?)
- *big:* The egg cell that became you formed inside your mother before she was born. When did you begin? (hint: Try several answers: at fertilisation, at that egg, at your grandmother, at the first cell on Earth.)
- *big:* If doctors could turn your skin cells into any cell, should they be allowed to make egg cells from them? (hint: It has been done in mice. Think about who could be helped, and what could go wrong.)
- *deep:* A planarian worm cut in two becomes two worms. Which one is the first worm? (hint: Both have the same DNA and both came from the same body. Does the question have an answer?)

## Story: Where the Tail Went
*A made-up story. Ammini, Unni and Saramma teacher are imagined. The science is real. A toad tadpole stops eating while the front legs come out, because the mouth and gut are being rebuilt. The cells of the tail die by plan, and cleaner cells carry the material back into the body, where it is used again. Carl Vogt first described planned cell death in 1842, in the tadpoles of a toad. A young toad with new lungs can drown in a steep-sided pot, so anyone who keeps tadpoles gives them a slope to climb out.*

The rain had come to Wayanad eleven days ago, and it had not stopped for more than an hour since. The ditch behind the pepper vines was full. In the ditch were toad tadpoles, hundreds of them, small and black, like peppercorns with tails.

Ammini was ten. She scooped eleven tadpoles into her grandmother's old bronze uruli, the cracked one that nobody cooked in any more. She set it on the back veranda with ditch water, a stone and a handful of water weed. Every evening she dropped in a boiled leaf of red cheera, and by morning the leaf was lace.

She kept a notebook. On the bottom of a white saucer she had drawn a ruler in ink, with a mark for every millimetre. Each morning she lifted the biggest tadpole into the saucer with a spoonful of water, and read the tail against the marks. The biggest tadpole was called Minnal, which means lightning, because of the way they shot across the uruli when a shadow fell.

For three weeks the tail column in the notebook hardly changed. Thirteen. Fourteen. Fourteen. The back legs came, thin as two threads. Then one morning a front leg pushed out through the skin, elbow first, like an arm through a sleeve. The other followed the next day.

After that the numbers went wrong.

Fourteen became eleven. Eleven became eight. And the cheera leaf lay on the water whole, with not one bite out of it.

"Minnal's tail is falling off," said her brother Unni, who was six. "Like the gecko's did."

Ammini thought so too, and it frightened her. Minnal was not eating. Minnal was getting shorter from the back. She had seen a rotten guava go like that, a little more gone each day.

She did everything she could think of. She changed the water morning and evening. She put in fresh cheera, then a crumb of cooked rice, then a mosquito she had caught herself. She filled the uruli to the brim, so that there would be plenty of clean water. Minnal ate nothing. Minnal sat on the stone and looked at her with eyes that seemed to stand higher on the head than before.

On the fourth morning the tail measured five.

Ammini fetched the torch and her mother's tea strainer. If the tail was falling off, the pieces had to be somewhere. She shone the torch along the bottom of the uruli. Then she poured the water, a cup at a time, through the strainer into a bucket.

She found weed. She found a snail shell, some grit, and the stalk of a cheera leaf. There was no tail, not one black crumb of it. And the water did not smell of anything rotten. It smelled of rain.

She sat down on the veranda step with the notebook on her knees. Unni sat beside her, because he could see that she was thinking and he wanted to be there when it finished.

She wrote down what she knew. The tail was shorter every day. Nothing had been eaten for four days. Nothing was lying on the bottom. Then she looked at Minnal for a long time, and wrote a fourth thing that she had not noticed until now. Minnal was not wasting. The back legs were thicker than last week. The front legs had fingers.

"Legs are made of something," she said slowly. "If Minnal has eaten nothing, what are the legs being made of?"

Unni shrugged.

She read down the tail column. Fourteen, eleven, eight, five. "It isn't falling off," she said. "It's going in."

"In where?"

"Into Minnal. The tail is what Minnal is eating."

She ran through the rain to the next house. Saramma teacher had taught biology at the high school in Kalpetta for thirty years, and now she grew orchids on old coconut husks. She listened to the whole of it without a word. Then she held out her hand for the notebook and read all four lines twice.

"You worked this out from the strainer and the legs?"

Ammini nodded.

"Then you have done what Carl Vogt did in 1842, and he had a microscope. You are right. And it is stranger than you think. Nobody bites the tail. The cells of the tail do it themselves. A signal comes through the blood. Each cell in the tail packs herself into small parcels. Cleaner cells swallow the parcels and carry the material back into the body. Part of each new leg was once tail."

"The cells die on purpose?"

"They die on time. Hold up your hand."

Ammini held it up.

"Spread your fingers. Before you were born, that hand was a little paddle with no gaps in it. The cells between your fingers did what Minnal's tail is doing now. That is why you can hold a pen."

Ammini looked at the gaps between her fingers. Through each gap she could see the rain falling on the pepper vines.

"Now," said Saramma teacher. "How deep is the water in your uruli?"

"To the top. I kept filling it. I wanted them to have plenty."

"A toad with new lungs and no tail does not want plenty of water. They want a way out of it."

Ammini ran home faster than she had come. She tipped out half the water. She found a broken roof tile behind the woodshed and leaned it from the bottom of the uruli to the rim, like a road. Unni held it steady while she packed the stone under it.

By evening Minnal sat on the tile, halfway up. The tail was a stub of two millimetres. Minnal's throat went in and out, in and out, breathing air.

Ammini opened the notebook. Under fourteen, eleven, eight, five and two, she wrote a nought. Then she crossed the nought out. Beside it she wrote: all of it, inside.

In the morning the tile was empty. Out by the pepper vines, something the size of her thumbnail hopped once, and was gone into the wet grass.

**For the parent:** For the parent: nothing fell off, and nothing was lost. A tadpole's tail is taken apart by the tail's own cells, which die by plan when a signal arrives in the blood, and the material is reused to finish the legs. Ammini's mistake was to treat a planned ending as an illness and try to stop it. Your child's own fingers were freed in the same way before birth.

- Ask: Ammini found no pieces of tail in the strainer. What did that tell her?
- Ask: Minnal ate nothing for four days, yet the legs grew thicker. Where did the material come from?
- Ask: How is a tail that is taken apart by plan different from a guava that rots?
- Ask: Which parts of your own body were shaped by cells dying on time?
- Ask: Why did Ammini lower the water and add a slope at the end?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Pudina Pieces Test: which piece of a stem can grow roots? (55 min, 9 days)
- **question:** Which part of a pudina stem can grow new roots: a piece with a node, a piece from between two nodes, or a leaf alone? And does a piece need its leaves to do it?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which of the four kinds of pieces will grow roots, and which will not? Say why you think so. Then guess where on a piece the roots will come out: from the cut end, or from somewhere else. Write your guesses down.
- **fair test:** Cut all the pieces from the same bunch on the same day. Give every kind the same water and the same bright window, out of hot sun. Change the water for all of them every two days. Use five pieces of each kind, because one piece alone can fail by bad luck.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the stems, or watches closely while the child cuts with small scissors.
- **record:** Fill in the table on days 3, 5, 7 and 9. Draw one piece of each kind on day 9, and mark where the roots are. Then write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Kind of piece | Day 3: how many have roots | Day 5 | Day 7 | Day 9 | Where the roots came out
- **schedule:** day 1: Cut five pieces of each of the four kinds. Stand them in water. Write down your guesses. (25 min); day 3: Change the water. Count the pieces with roots or white bumps. (5 min); day 5: Change the water. Count the pieces with roots. Note where the roots come out. (5 min); day 7: Change the water. Count again and measure the longest root. (5 min); day 9: Make the last count. Fill in the table and explain the result. (15 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at one pudina stem together. 2. Find the nodes, the small bumps where each pair of leaves joins. 3. Find the bare lengths of stem between the nodes.  
  *Parent says:* Run a finger down the stem. Where do the leaves join? Those places are called nodes. What is between them?  
  *Child mode:* Run a finger down the stem. Find where the leaves join. Those are nodes.
  - Note: Pudina leaves grow in pairs. The gaps between nodes are longest near the bottom of the stem. Choose firm green stems and leave any black or limp ones.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Describe the four kinds of pieces. 2. Ask which kinds will grow roots, and why. 3. Ask where on a piece the roots will come out. 4. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* Which of these four pieces will grow roots? Why do you think so? And where will the roots come out?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which pieces will grow roots. Say why. Guess where the roots will come out. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Any reason is fine at this stage.; The piece with leaves can make food, so it may grow best.; Roots may come from the cut end, because that is where the stem is open.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children expect roots from the cut end, and expect every piece of stem to root.
- **s3 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. From the top of a stem, cut piece A: about 6 centimetres, with two nodes. Pull the leaves off the lower node only. 2. Lower down, cut piece B: one node in the middle of about 3 centimetres of stem. Pull off its leaves. 3. Cut piece C from between two nodes, with no node on it. 4. Pick one large leaf with its stalk as piece D. 5. Do the same with four more stems.  
  *Parent says:* From every stem we cut the same four pieces. A has a node and leaves. B has a node and no leaves. C has no node. D is a leaf alone.  
  *Child mode:* Cut four pieces from each stem. A has a node and leaves. B has a bare node. C has no node. D is one leaf.
  - Note: Piece C will be short, perhaps 2 centimetres. That is fine. Look at both ends of C to be sure there is no node on it. Cut with a clean snip and do not crush the stem.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the five A pieces in a tumbler, with the lower node under 3 centimetres of water. 2. Lay the B, C and D pieces in three katoris, in water 1 centimetre deep. 3. Label each one. 4. Put them all at a bright window, out of hot sun.  
  *Parent says:* All four kinds get the same water and the same light. Why did we cut five of each, and not one?  
  *Child mode:* Stand the A pieces in the tumbler. Lay B, C and D in shallow water. Label them. Put them by a bright window.
  - Ask: Why five pieces of each kind? (good: One piece could fail by bad luck.; If four or five of a kind do the same thing, we can trust the result more.)
  - Wait: Leave them for 2 days, then change the water
- **s5 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On days 3, 5 and 7, pour off the old water and add fresh water. 2. Look at every piece, with a magnifying glass if you have one. 3. Count the pieces that have white bumps or roots. 4. Write down where on the piece the roots are. 5. On day 7, measure the longest root.  
  *Parent says:* Look for small white bumps first. They are roots just starting. On which kinds do you see them? Where on the piece are they?  
  *Child mode:* Change the water. Look for white bumps and roots. Count them. Where on the piece are they?
  - Note: The first white bumps often show by day 3 or 4, at the node. In cool weather everything takes a few days longer, so carry on to day 12. Throw out any piece that turns black and slimy, and write that down as a result.
  - Wait: Do this on days 3, 5 and 7
- **s6 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 9, count one last time and fill in the table. 2. Ask your child for the three sentences. 3. Ask which two kinds of pieces show whether leaves are needed. 4. Ask your child to draw where the root-making cells must be. 5. Only then read the explanation together.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why. Now compare B and D. One has a node and no leaf. The other has a leaf and no node.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed. Say what you saw. Say why. Compare B and D. Draw where the root-making cells are.
  - Ask: Piece B had no leaves and piece D was only a leaf. What do those two tell us? (good: B grew roots without leaves, so leaves are not what makes roots.; D had a leaf and no node and grew no roots, so the node is what matters.)
  - Ask: The roots came out at the node, not at the cut end. What does that tell us about the cells there? (good: Cells that can start dividing again are at the node.; The rest of the stem is finished and does not make new parts.)
  - Note: The usual result is that A and B grow roots at the node, and C and D do not. B is often slower than A, because leaves supply food. If a C piece does grow a root, look again for a node hidden at one end. Plant the rooted A pieces in a pot of soil, and they will grow into pudina plants.
  - Check: In the Pudina Pieces Test, a bare piece of stem with a node grew roots. A leaf alone did not. What does this show? → **Cells that can make new roots are at the node**
### e2 · The Shuffle Test: how many different egg cells can one mother make? (35 min)
- **question:** If a cell has three pairs of chromosomes, how many different egg cells can meiosis make? What happens to that number when there are four pairs?
- **predict:** Guess first. In 24 tosses of three coins, how many different results will you get? Will any result come up more than once? Say why you think so, and write your guesses down.
- **fair test:** Toss all the coins together each time, from the same tumbler, onto the same cloth. Write down every toss, even the ones that repeat. Do not skip a toss because it looks boring.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all 24 tosses. Then write the list of every possible result for three coins. Write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why.
- **results table:** Egg number | Coin 1 | Coin 2 | Coin 3 | Seen before? (yes or no)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the three coins in a row and call them pair 1, pair 2 and pair 3. 2. Explain that heads is M and tails is F. 3. Draw the table with 24 rows.  
  *Parent says:* A mother has two of each chromosome, one from her mother and one from her father. An egg gets only one of each pair. The coin decides which.  
  *Child mode:* Lay out three coins. Each coin is one pair. Heads is M. Tails is F. Draw a table with 24 rows.
  - Note: M stands for the chromosome this mother got from her own mother. F is the one she got from her father. Always read the coins in the same order: 1 rupee, 2 rupees, 5 rupees.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask how many different results 24 tosses will give, and why. 2. Ask whether any result will repeat, and why. 3. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* We will make 24 egg cells. How many different kinds will there be? Will any kind come up twice? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many different results you will get. Will any repeat? Say why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Any reason is fine at this stage.; There are only a few ways three coins can fall, so some must repeat.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Some children expect 24 different results, and some expect only 3 or 6.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shake the three coins in the tumbler and tip them onto the towel. 2. Write M or F for each coin, in order. 3. Write 'yes' if this result has come up before. 4. Do this 24 times.  
  *Parent says:* Each toss is one egg cell. Read the coins in order and I will write them down. Have we seen this one before?  
  *Child mode:* Shake the coins and tip them out. Write M or F for each coin. Have you seen this one before? Do it 24 times.
  - Note: Take turns to toss and to write. Most families see 7 or 8 different results in 24 tosses, and never more than 8.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count the different results. 2. Ask your child to list every possible result, in a careful order, to see if any is missing. 3. Start with one coin, then two coins, then three.  
  *Parent says:* How many different eggs did we get? Could there be one we never tossed? Let's list them all. Start with just one coin.  
  *Child mode:* Count the different results. Now list every possible one. Start with one coin. Then two. Then three.
  - Ask: One coin gives 2 results and two coins give 4. Why do three coins give 8? (good: Each of the 4 results can have M or F added for the third coin.; Every extra coin doubles the number.)
  - Note: A careful list is MMM, MMF, MFM, MFF, FMM, FMF, FFM, FFF. Let your child find the order. The idea to reach is that each extra pair doubles the number.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add the fourth coin. Ask how many results are possible now, and why. 2. Toss the four coins 10 times to see some of them. 3. Carry the doubling on in a table up to 10 pairs. 4. Read out the number for 23 pairs: 8,388,608. 5. Ask for the three sentences.  
  *Parent says:* Four pairs now. How many different eggs? A human has 23 pairs. Keep doubling and see how far you get.  
  *Child mode:* Add a fourth coin. How many results now? Keep doubling up to 10 pairs. Then hear the number for 23 pairs.
  - Ask: Two children of the same mother and father are never copies, unless they are identical twins. Why? (good: Each egg gets a different mix of the mother's chromosomes.; Each sperm gets a different mix too, and there are millions of possible mixes.)
  - Note: The doubling goes 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1,024 for ten pairs. Real meiosis mixes even more than the coins do, because paired chromosomes also swap pieces.
  - Check: In the Shuffle Test, each extra pair of chromosomes changed the number of possible egg cells. How? → **It doubled the number**

### c1 · Blind Share: share eight pairs with no mistakes, without looking (30 min)
- **problem:** Sixteen paper slips are numbered 1 to 8 twice. Blindfolded, share them between two bags so that each bag gets exactly one of every number. Your method must work three times in a row.
- **your turn:** Now design your own method. Test it three times and count the mistakes. Which idea does your method share with a dividing cell: holding pairs together, lining them up, pulling from both sides, or a checkpoint?

- **general:** Clip each pair together before the blindfold goes on. Then unclip one pair at a time and drop one slip in each bag.; Lay the pairs in a row down the middle of the table. Slide one slip of each pair to the left and one to the right.
- **creative:** Tie a thread to each slip of a pair. Two people pull from opposite sides of the table. A pair that is tied to only one side shows up at once, because it does not come apart.; Give the helper one job: to say 'go' only when every pair is held from both sides. ## Worksheets
### Mitosis, meiosis, stem cells and planned cell death (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which kind of division makes two cells with the same full set of chromosomes?  
   Options: Mitosis / Meiosis / Fertilisation / Crossing over  
   **Answer:** Mitosis. *Why:* Mitosis copies every chromosome and divides once, so both cells have the full set. Meiosis halves the set. Fertilisation joins two cells, and crossing over is a swap of pieces.
2. (mcq) A human body cell has 46 chromosomes. How many does a human egg cell have?  
   Options: 23 / 46 / 92 / 12  
   **Answer:** 23. *Why:* An egg cell has half the set, one chromosome from each of the 23 pairs. Choosing 46 forgets that the sperm cell brings 23 more.
3. (truefalse) True or false: meiosis happens in every part of the body.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Meiosis happens only in the organs that make egg cells or sperm cells. Everywhere else, cells divide by mitosis.
4. (fill) In meiosis the cell copies the chromosomes once and then divides ____ times.  
   **Answer:** ['two', '2', 'twice']. *Why:* One copying and two divisions give four cells with half the chromosomes each. One division would give two cells with the full set, which is mitosis.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Mitosis → division that makes two cells with the full set; Meiosis → division that makes sex cells with half the set; Stem cell → a cell that has not taken a job yet; Fertilisation → an egg cell and a sperm cell joining; Programmed cell death → a cell taking herself apart by plan. *Why:* These five words are used all through the unit. A strong answer could give an example of each.
6. (sort) Sort these cells by the kind of division that made them.  
   **Answer:** a new skin cell → Made by mitosis; a sperm cell → Made by meiosis; a cell at a root tip → Made by mitosis; an egg cell → Made by meiosis; a new liver cell → Made by mitosis; a young pollen grain → Made by meiosis. *Why:* Body cells for growing and mending come from mitosis. Sex cells, and the young pollen grains of a flower, come from meiosis.
7. (mcq) What can a stem cell do that a red blood cell cannot?  
   Options: Divide and make other kinds of cell / Carry oxygen / Travel in the blood / Use food for energy  
   **Answer:** Divide and make other kinds of cell. *Why:* A stem cell can divide, and her daughters can specialise. A red blood cell is fully specialised and never divides. Carrying oxygen is the red cell's own job.
8. (truefalse) True or false: a blood stem cell in a grown-up can make new nerve cells.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. An adult stem cell makes only the cells of her own tissue. A blood stem cell makes blood cells. Only the stem cells of a very young embryo can make every kind.
9. (mcq) How did your fingers become separate before you were born?  
   Options: The cells between them died by plan / The skin between them tore / Each finger was added one by one / The bones cut through the skin  
   **Answer:** The cells between them died by plan. *Why:* The hand begins as a paddle. The cells between the finger bones die by plan and are cleared away. Nothing tears, and nothing is hurt.
10. (fill) The death of cells by injury, such as a burn, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['necrosis']. *Why:* In necrosis the cells swell and burst, and the place becomes sore. Apoptosis is the planned, tidy kind of cell death.
11. (order) Put the steps of planned cell death in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The cell gets a signal, or senses damage inside herself', 'The cell shrinks and cuts up her own DNA', 'She packs herself into small sealed parcels', 'Cleaner cells swallow the parcels']. *Why:* The signal comes first, then the tidy taking apart, then the clearing up. Nothing spills at any step.
12. (write) In the Pudina Pieces Test, a bare node grew roots and a leaf alone did not. What does that show?  
   **Answer:** It shows that the cells that can make new roots are at the node. The bare node had no leaves and still made roots, so leaves are not what makes roots. The leaf had no node and made none.. *Why:* A strong answer uses both pieces: the node without a leaf, and the leaf without a node.
13. (mcq) Why does the Pudina Pieces Test use five pieces of each kind, and not one?  
   Options: One piece could fail by bad luck / Five pieces grow faster together / Pudina needs company / To use up the bunch  
   **Answer:** One piece could fail by bad luck. *Why:* A single piece can rot or dry out for reasons that have nothing to do with nodes. If four or five of a kind do the same thing, the result can be trusted.
14. (write) Name two things in your home made of cells that died by plan. Say what job the dead cells did.  
   **Answer:** For example: a wooden spoon and a cotton towel. Wood is made of a tree's dead, hollow water pipes and stiff fibres, which carried water and held the tree up. Hair, nails, coir, wool and feathers are also right.. *Why:* A strong answer names the living being the thing came from, and says what the dead cells did there.
15. (draw) Draw a cell with 4 chromosomes. Then draw the two cells she makes by mitosis.  
   **Answer:** One cell with 4 chromosomes, and two cells below it that each have the same 4 chromosomes.. *Why:* A strong drawing gives each new cell all 4 chromosomes. A drawing with 2 in each cell shows a mix-up with meiosis.
### Halving, shuffling and cells that can still divide (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why do matching chromosomes line up as partners in meiosis?  
   Options: So each new cell gets one of every pair / To make the cell bigger / So the DNA is copied again / To save energy  
   **Answer:** So each new cell gets one of every pair. *Why:* When partners line up together and then separate, each side gets one of every pair. Without the pairing, a cell could get two of one kind and none of another.
2. (count) A rice plant has 24 chromosomes in each body cell. How many are in a rice egg cell?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* A sex cell has half the set. 24 ÷ 2 = 12. A rice sperm cell brings 12 more, so the new seed has 24 again.
3. (count) A cell has 5 pairs of chromosomes. How many different sex cells can the shuffle make?  
   **Answer:** 32. *Why:* Each pair doubles the number. 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. Answering 10 comes from adding 2 for each pair, when each pair doubles it.
4. (fill) The swap of matching pieces between two partner chromosomes is called ____ ____.  
   **Answer:** ['crossing over']. *Why:* Crossing over happens early in meiosis. After it, one chromosome carries a mix of the two partners.
5. (truefalse) True or false: two sisters with the same parents always have exactly the same chromosomes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each sister grew from a different egg cell and a different sperm cell, and each of those had a different mix. Only identical twins start with the same set.
6. (odd) Three of these are made by mitosis. Which one is made by meiosis?  
   Options: A skin cell / A liver cell / A sperm cell / A root tip cell  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A sperm cell has half the chromosomes, so meiosis makes it. Skin, liver and root tip cells are body cells with the full set.
7. (sort) Sort each fact: is it about embryonic stem cells or adult stem cells?  
   **Answer:** can make every kind of body cell → Embryonic stem cell; lives in bone marrow → Adult stem cell; is in an embryo a few days old → Embryonic stem cell; makes only blood cells → Adult stem cell; remakes the gut lining every few days → Adult stem cell. *Why:* Embryonic stem cells are in an embryo a few days old and can make every kind of body cell. Adult stem cells stay in a formed body and make only the cells of their own tissue.
8. (write) A pea plant has 14 chromosomes. Explain what would happen over three generations with no meiosis.  
   **Answer:** Each parent would give all 14, so the child would have 28. The grandchild would have 56 and the next generation 112. The number would double every time. Meiosis halves it to 7 first, so 7 and 7 make 14 again.. *Why:* A strong answer shows the doubling with numbers, and says that halving before joining keeps the number steady.
9. (write) A duck has webbed feet and a hen does not. Explain the difference using planned cell death.  
   **Answer:** Both embryos start with paddle-shaped feet. In the hen, the cells between the toes die by plan, so the toes separate. In the duck, a signal blocks that death, so the cells stay and form the web.. *Why:* A strong answer says that both start the same, and that the difference is whether the cells between the toes die.
10. (label) Draw a pudina cutting standing in water. Label a node, a leaf, a new root and the water line.  
   **Answer:** ['Node: where the leaves join the stem', 'Leaf', 'New root: growing out at a node under the water', 'Water line']. *Why:* A strong drawing has the roots coming from a node, not from the cut end or from the bare stem between nodes.
11. (perspective) Three people talk about a cell that dies by plan.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses two examples, such as fingers, a tadpole's tail, sunburn or cancer. It chooses one view, and says what one of the others has right.. *Why:* The doctor is right about injury and illness. The biologist is right that bodies are built and kept safe by planned death. The third view asks a real question that science has not settled.
12. (order) Put the steps of meiosis in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The cell copies every chromosome', 'Partners line up side by side and swap pieces', 'The first division separates the partners', 'The second division separates the copies']. *Why:* Copying comes first. Then the partners pair up and cross over. The first division parts the partners, and the second parts the copies.
13. (mcq) Which of these is one cause of cancer?  
   Options: Damaged cells that fail to die / Too many red blood cells / Too much planned cell death / Cells that specialise  
   **Answer:** Damaged cells that fail to die. *Why:* A cell with damaged instructions normally takes herself apart. If she does not, and keeps dividing, a cancer can start. Too much planned death causes other problems, not cancer.
14. (mcq) Which stem cell treatment is well proven?  
   Options: A bone marrow transplant for blood cancer / An injection that cures any illness / Regrowing a lost arm / Making an old person young  
   **Answer:** A bone marrow transplant for blood cancer. *Why:* Bone marrow transplants have worked for decades. The other three are not possible today, and anyone who offers them is selling something unproven.
15. (mcq) A pudina piece cut from between two nodes grows one root. What should you check first?  
   Options: Whether a node is hidden at one end / Whether the water was too cold / Whether the piece was green enough / Nothing, because results never matter  
   **Answer:** Whether a node is hidden at one end. *Why:* An odd result deserves a second look before you change your idea. A node at the very end of the piece is the most likely cause.
### Mistakes, going back and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)

14. (mcq) A human has 23 pairs of chromosomes. About how many different egg cells can the shuffle make?  
   Options: 23 / 46 / About 8 million / About 100  
   **Answer:** About 8 million. *Why:* Each pair doubles the number, and doubling 23 times gives 8,388,608. Answering 46 comes from adding 2 for each pair, when each pair doubles it.
15. (mcq) Old yeast cells sometimes die in a tidy, programmed way. Why do they do it?  
   Options: Scientists disagree about why / It is proved to feed the colony / It never really happens / Yeast cells are not alive  
   **Answer:** Scientists disagree about why. *Why:* Some biologists say the death helps the yeast cell's sisters. Others say it may be a side effect of other machinery. The question is still open.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A scraped elbow heals. Which kind of division makes the new skin cells?  
   Options: Mitosis / Meiosis / Fertilisation / Crossing over  
   **Answer:** Mitosis. *Why:* Healing needs more skin cells of the same kind, with the full set of chromosomes. That is mitosis. Meiosis only makes sex cells.
2. (mcq) In the simple picture, how many cells does one cell make by meiosis?  
   Options: 2 / 4 / 8 / 46  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* Meiosis is one copying and two divisions, so one cell becomes four. Answering 2 is a mix-up with mitosis.
3. (mcq) A dog's body cells have 78 chromosomes. How many are in a dog's sperm cell?  
   Options: 39 / 78 / 156 / 23  
   **Answer:** 39. *Why:* A sex cell has half the set. 78 ÷ 2 = 39. The number 23 is right for humans only.
4. (mcq) Why are a brother and a sister from the same parents not copies of each other?  
   Options: Each grew from a different egg and sperm / They ate different food / Mitosis made many mistakes / One is older  
   **Answer:** Each grew from a different egg and sperm. *Why:* Meiosis gives every egg cell and every sperm cell a different mix of chromosomes. Food and age change a body, but they do not change which chromosomes it started with.
5. (mcq) Which cells can become every kind of cell in the body?  
   Options: Stem cells of an embryo a few days old / Blood stem cells / Skin stem cells / Red blood cells  
   **Answer:** Stem cells of an embryo a few days old. *Why:* Only the stem cells of a very young embryo can make every kind. Blood and skin stem cells make only their own tissue, and red blood cells do not divide at all.
6. (truefalse) True or false: your bone marrow makes new blood cells every second.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Blood stem cells in the marrow make about two million red blood cells each second, to replace those that wear out.
7. (mcq) Which of these is planned cell death?  
   Options: A tadpole's tail shrinking / Skin burnt by a hot pan / A crushed fingertip / A frozen toe  
   **Answer:** A tadpole's tail shrinking. *Why:* The tail cells die on a signal and are reused. Burning, crushing and freezing kill cells by injury, which is necrosis.
8. (mcq) How is planned cell death different from death by injury?  
   Options: The cell packs herself up and nothing spills / The cell swells and bursts / It only happens in plants / It always hurts  
   **Answer:** The cell packs herself up and nothing spills. *Why:* In planned death the cell shrinks into sealed parcels, and cleaner cells remove them. A burst cell that spills and causes soreness is the sign of injury.
9. (mcq) Roots grew at the node of a pudina piece and not between nodes. What is at the node?  
   Options: Cells that can start dividing again / More water / Only dead cells / The oldest cells  
   **Answer:** Cells that can start dividing again. *Why:* A node holds a bud and cells that can go back to dividing. The stem between the nodes is finished, and makes no new parts.
10. (mcq) Three pairs of chromosomes can be shuffled into 8 different sex cells. How many for four pairs?  
   Options: 10 / 12 / 16 / 24  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* Each extra pair doubles the number, and 8 × 2 = 16. Answering 10 comes from adding 2.
11. (mcq) Why do most animals have two parents, when copying looks simpler?  
   Options: There is a leading idea, still debated / It is fully proved / Nobody has studied it / Copying is impossible for animals  
   **Answer:** There is a leading idea, still debated. *Why:* The leading idea is that shuffled children are harder for germs to beat. But some animals copy themselves very well, so scientists still debate it.

## Applications
- Bone marrow transplants give a person with a blood cancer a new supply of blood stem cells from a healthy donor.
- Eye doctors repair a chemical burn of the cornea by growing new surface from stem cells taken at the edge of the person's other eye.
- Gardeners and farmers grow new plants from cuttings. Sugarcane, tapioca, money plant, rose and hibiscus are all planted as pieces of stem with a node.
- Tissue culture laboratories grow thousands of banana and orchid plants from small groups of cells, so that farmers get plants free of disease.
- Many cancer medicines work by pushing cancer cells to die by plan, as healthy damaged cells would have done.
- Plant breeders use the shuffle of meiosis. They cross two rice varieties and search among the mixed grandchildren for a plant with the best of both.
- Doctors can count the chromosomes of a baby before birth, to see whether the sharing in meiosis left one too many or one too few.

## Open questions
- **Why can a flatworm or a salamander regrow lost parts, when a human cannot regrow an arm?** Humans have many of the same genes, and small children can even regrow a fingertip. Nobody yet knows why the rest of this ability is switched off in us.
- **Can stem cells be used to repair a damaged heart, spinal cord or brain?** It works in some animal tests, and trials in people are going on. So far the results in people are small or mixed, and making the new cells join up correctly is hard.
- **Why did meiosis and two parents begin at all?** Copying passes on all of a mother's instructions and needs no partner. The idea that the shuffle keeps ahead of germs has some evidence, but it does not explain every case.
- **Why do single-celled beings such as yeast have a programmed way of dying?** It may protect their sister cells, or it may be a side effect of other machinery. Experiments have not yet settled it.
- **How does an egg cell stay healthy while paused in meiosis for forty years?** Scientists know that the links between the paired chromosomes weaken with time. They do not know how the cell protects everything else for so long.
- **Can working egg and sperm cells be made from skin cells?** In 2016 Katsuhiko Hayashi's team in Japan did it in mice, and healthy pups were born. Nobody has done it with human cells, and people disagree about whether anyone should.

## Connected maths
- In the Shuffle Test, 3 pairs of chromosomes gave 8 possible egg cells, and 4 pairs gave 16. How many are possible with 6 pairs? What is the rule? **64. Each extra pair doubles the number.**. 3 pairs give 8 and 4 pairs give 16. Double again for 5 pairs: 32. Double again for 6 pairs: 64. Every result so far can be followed by either chromosome of the new pair, so the number doubles each time. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A pea plant has 14 chromosomes. Suppose nothing were halved, so the number doubled in every generation. How many chromosomes would a plant have after 5 generations? **448.**. Double five times: 14, 28, 56, 112, 224, 448. That is 14 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2, or 14 × 32. No cell could manage a set that keeps doubling, which is why meiosis halves the number first. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- In a Pudina Pieces Test, 5 of 5 leafy node pieces and 4 of 5 bare node pieces grew roots. None of the 10 pieces with no node did. What percentage of node pieces rooted? One node piece failed. Does that change the conclusion? **90 percent of the node pieces rooted, and 0 percent of the others. The one failure does not change the conclusion.**. 9 of the 10 node pieces rooted. 9 ÷ 10 = 0.9, which is 90 percent. None of the 10 pieces with no node rooted. One piece can fail from rot or a bad cut. The gap between 90 percent and 0 percent is far too big to be luck. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Shuffle Test, three coins can fall in 8 ways. In 24 tosses, how many times would you expect each way? A family got MMM 6 times. Are their coins unfair? **About 3 times each. Six times is not enough to call the coins unfair.**. 24 ÷ 8 = 3, so each result is expected about 3 times. But chance is lumpy. In only 24 tosses, one result often comes up 5 or 6 times and another only once. To test the coins properly, the family would need hundreds of tosses. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- A cell has 2 pairs of chromosomes. List every different egg cell the shuffle can make. If a father can make as many kinds of sperm, how many different fertilised eggs are possible? **4 kinds of egg cell, and 16 different fertilised eggs.**. Call the pairs M or F. The egg cells are MM, MF, FM and FF, which is 4. Each of the 4 egg cells can join any of the 4 kinds of sperm. 4 × 4 = 16. For humans the same sum is about 8 million × 8 million, which is more than 70 trillion. (queues `math.logic.counting-ways.L5`)
- The longest root on a pudina piece was 4 millimetres on day 5, 12 millimetres on day 7 and 20 millimetres on day 9. How fast is it growing? How long will it be on day 11 if it carries on? **4 millimetres a day. About 28 millimetres on day 11.**. From day 5 to day 7 the root grew 8 millimetres, and from day 7 to day 9 another 8. That is 8 millimetres in 2 days, or 4 millimetres a day. Two more days add 8, so 20 + 8 = 28 millimetres. A real root may speed up or slow down, so this is a prediction to check. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
