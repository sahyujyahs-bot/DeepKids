# What is space? Near, far, and whether space goes on for ever
`sci.space-time.space.L2`

**Summary.** Walk away from a bottle, and it looks smaller, though the bottle stays the same. What changed is the space between you and the bottle. Space is the room around things and between them. The farther apart two things are, the more space is between them. Nobody knows yet whether space goes on for ever.

**Intro.** *Hold up your thumb at arm's length and close one eye. Can your small thumb hide a big building far outside your window?* A thumb is much smaller than a building. Yet if the building is far enough away, your thumb can hide it. In this unit you find out why. You hunt for spaces in your home and count steps to near and far things. You measure how big a bottle looks from far away. Then you make a tunnel of mirrors that seems to go on for ever, and ask whether space does.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that space is the room around things and between them, and find spaces in my home.
1. By the end I can use near, nearer, nearest, far, farther and farthest, and count steps to compare distances.
2. By the end I can show that the same thing looks smaller when it is farther away.
3. By the end I can say why the Sun, the Moon and a plane in the sky look small, though they are big.
4. By the end I can say that nobody knows yet whether space goes on for ever, and say why it is hard to find out.
5. By the end I can say that the space in my room is full of air, while outer space is almost empty.

## Terms
- **Space**: Space is the room around things and between them. Everything is somewhere in space. *e.g.* There is space between your bed and the wall. There is space between the Earth and the Moon.
- **Distance**: Distance is how much space there is between two things. We can measure it in steps, centimetres or kilometres. *e.g.* The distance from your bed to the door might be 5 steps.
- **Near and far**: Near means a small distance away. Far means a big distance away. *e.g.* Your pillow is near your head. The Moon is far from your bed.
- **Between**: Between means in the space that separates two things. *e.g.* In a row of chappals by the door, the middle chappal is between the other two.
- **Outer space**: Outer space is the space above the Earth's air, where the Moon, the Sun and the stars are. It is almost empty. *e.g.* Rockets from Sriharikota in Andhra Pradesh fly up through the air and into outer space.
- **Universe**: The universe is all of space and everything in it: every star, planet, rock, plant, animal and person. *e.g.* You, your home, the Earth, the Sun and all the stars are part of the universe.
- **Reflection**: A reflection is the picture you see in a mirror or in still water. It is made by light bouncing off the shiny surface. *e.g.* You see your face as a reflection in the bathroom mirror, or in a steel plate.
- **Estimate**: To estimate is to make a careful guess about a number, before you count or measure it. *e.g.* You estimate that the door is 6 steps away. Then you walk and count 5 steps.

## Explanations
### Space is what is between things · *defined*
**Definition:** Space is the room around things and between them. Everything is somewhere in space. Things can move because there is space to move into.

In the space hunt, you found space under your bed, between your fingers and inside an empty bottle. You can push two tumblers together, but there is still space all around them. Space is not only up in the sky. It is right here, between your nose and this page. Every thing takes up some space, and the space around it lets it move.
- e.g. The gap between your bed and the wall
- e.g. The room inside an empty tiffin box
- e.g. The space between the Earth and the Moon
### Near and far: distance · *defined*
**Definition:** Distance is how much space there is between two things. Near means a small distance. Far means a big distance.

In Step It Out, the bed was fewer steps away than the door, so the bed was nearer. We use steps for short distances at home. For longer ones, we use metres and kilometres. A kilometre is about 1,300 steps of a grown-up. The Moon is about 384,000 kilometres away. That is so far that walking there, day and night without stopping, would take about 9 years.
- e.g. 3 steps to the bed, and 7 steps to the door
- e.g. A short walk to the corner shop
- e.g. The very long way to the Moon
### Why far things look smaller · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing looks smaller when it is farther away, because it takes up less of what your eyes can see. From twice as far away, it looks about half as tall.

In the bottle strip test, the bottle never changed. But from 2 steps away it looked about half as tall as from 1 step. From 4 steps, it looked about a quarter as tall. That is why a big plane high in the sky looks as small as a bird. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon. It is also about 400 times farther away. So in the sky, the two look about the same size.
- e.g. A plane high up looking as small as a bird
- e.g. A tall gopuram that your thumb can hide from far away
- e.g. The Sun and the Moon looking the same size
### Is space empty? · *defined*
**Definition:** The space in your room is full of air, though you cannot see it. Outer space, between the stars, is almost empty.

The space between your bed and the wall looks empty. But it is full of air, made of tiny bits far too small to see. Wave your hand fast, and you feel them. High above the Earth, the air gets thinner and thinner. Out between the stars, a space the size of a sugar cube holds about one tiny bit of gas, or even less. Yet light from the Sun and stars crosses that space all the time.
- e.g. Air you feel when you wave your hand
- e.g. Thin air on a high mountain in Ladakh
- e.g. Almost empty space between the stars
### Does space go on for ever? · *debated*
Nobody knows. We can only see part of space. Light from very far away has not had time to reach us yet, so we cannot see what is beyond. In the mirror tunnel, you could not see the end of the row either. The copies got too small and dim. Not seeing an end does not tell us whether there is one. Scientists measure the shape of space to find clues, and they still disagree.
- e.g. The mirror tunnel, where the end was too small and dim to see
- e.g. Looking at the night sky and seeing only the nearest stars
- e.g. Asking 'and what is past that?' again and again
- **Many scientists today:** Space may go on for ever, with no end at all. Measurements of space so far fit this idea.
- **Other scientists:** Space may be very, very big but not endless. It could curve round on itself, like the skin of a ball, which has no edge but is not endless.
- **Archytas, a thinker in ancient Greece, about 2,400 years ago:** If space had an edge, you could stand there and push a stick out. Either the stick goes on, so there is more space, or something stops it, so there is something beyond. So space has no edge.

## Mostly agreed
- Everything is somewhere in space, and there is space between all things. *How we know:* In the space hunt, every thing you looked at had space around it, even two tumblers pushed together.
- The same thing looks smaller when it is farther away. *How we know:* In the bottle strip test, the bottle looked about half as tall from twice as far, and the bottle did not change.
- The Sun is much bigger than the Moon and much farther away. *How we know:* Astronomers have measured both. The Sun is about 400 times wider and about 400 times farther away. That is why they look the same size.
- Outer space is almost empty. *How we know:* Spacecraft have measured it. Between the stars, there is only about one tiny bit of gas in a space the size of a sugar cube.

## How we found out
**Henrietta Swan Leavitt, an American astronomer at Harvard College Observatory. Edwin Hubble, an American astronomer at Mount Wilson Observatory., Leavitt's rule in 1912. Hubble found the star in Andromeda in 1923 and announced his result in 1925..** A hundred years ago, many astronomers thought our Milky Way was the whole of space. The Milky Way is our home group of stars, called a galaxy. In the sky they could see faint, misty patches. Many thought these were small clouds inside the Milky Way. To find out, they needed to know how far away the patches were. Henrietta Swan Leavitt made that possible. She worked at Harvard College Observatory in the United States, studying photographs of stars, for very little pay. She studied stars that get brighter and dimmer, again and again, over days. In 1912 she showed that the slower such a star changes, the brighter it really is. If you know how bright a star really is, and see how bright it looks, you can work out how far away it is. In 1923, Edwin Hubble found one of these stars in the misty patch called Andromeda. Using Leavitt's rule, he showed that Andromeda is far outside the Milky Way. It is a whole galaxy of its own, and space is far bigger than people had thought.  
*What people believed before:* That the Milky Way was the whole of space, and the misty patches were small clouds inside it. Leavitt's way of measuring distance showed that Andromeda is a separate galaxy, very far away.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The sky is a roof over the Earth, with the stars stuck on it, all the same distance away.** (Many people long ago, in many countries, including the Greek thinker Aristotle.; *rejected*)  
  For: The stars all look equally far away. They all turn across the night sky together, as if stuck on one roof.  
  Against: In 1838, Friedrich Bessel measured how far away one star was. Since then, astronomers have measured many stars. They are at very different distances. Rockets fly up through the sky and find no roof.
- **Things far away really are smaller, and they grow as you walk nearer.** (Many young children think this at first.; *rejected*)  
  For: From far away, a bus or a person really does look small.  
  Against: In the bottle strip test, the bottle looked smaller from far away, but you could walk back and pick up the same bottle. Only the distance changed.
- **Space is a thing of its own, like a huge empty box. It would still be there even with nothing in it.** (Isaac Newton, an English scientist, about 340 years ago. Gottfried Leibniz, a German thinker, disagreed. He said space is only how things sit apart from each other.; *debated*)  
  For: Things move through space, and space seems to be there whether or not things fill it.  
  Against: We only ever measure space by the distances between things. With no things at all, nobody could tell if there was any space.

## Roots
We have hunted for spaces, counted steps, measured a bottle from near and far, and looked into a tunnel of mirrors. Now we ask what space tells us about the world, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Your bottle stayed the same size all through the test. Yet from twice as far away, it looked about half as tall. Only the space between you and the bottle had changed. So you cannot tell how big a thing is just by looking, unless you know how far away it is.  
*Habit it breaks:* We take how big a thing looks to be how big it is. The bottle test shows that the size we see depends on the space between us and the thing. That is how the Sun, about 400 times wider than the Moon, can look the same size.  
*Evidence:* In the bottle strip test, the 2-step strip was about half the 1-step strip, and the bottle never moved or changed.

- *who am i:* You take up a little space, and there is space all around you. Wherever you go, you are somewhere, near some things and far from others.
- *what is life:* Living beings need space: room for a seed's roots to spread, for a bird on a wire, for a child to run. A plant in a crowded pot grows poorly.
- *what is this world:* The world is things with space between them, from the gap under your bed to the huge distances between the stars. Nobody knows yet whether that space ever ends.

Space is what lets things be apart, so that there can be near and far. The bottle looked small only because of the space between you and it. Everything is conscious, the bottle and the stars too, each meeting the world in their own way. Many scientists disagree, and say only living beings with brains are aware.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 11:** Thirty spokes meet at the hub of a wheel, and the empty centre makes the wheel useful. A clay pot is useful because of the empty space inside. Doors and windows are useful because they are openings. (In the space hunt, the empty space inside the bottle and the tiffin box is what makes them useful. Space between things is not nothing. It is what lets us use and move things.)
- **Jain thought, the Tattvartha Sutra, chapter 5:** Jain thinkers taught that space, called akasha, gives room to everything. They said the part of space where things live has an edge, and that beyond it lies empty space that goes on without end. (Jain thinkers asked the same question as the mirror tunnel: does space go on for ever? Their answer was that the world has an edge, but space itself does not.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta:** The hymn asks how the world began, and ends by asking whether anyone really knows, perhaps not even the one who watches from the highest heaven. (Scientists today still cannot say whether space goes on for ever. Saying honestly 'nobody knows yet' is part of science.)
- Science (settled): From twice as far away, a thing looks about half as tall. This is why the Sun, about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, looks the same size.
- Science (settled): Outer space between the stars is almost empty, with about one tiny bit of gas in a space the size of a sugar cube.
- Science (open): Measurements of the shape of space fit space going on for ever, but they also fit space being very big and curving round on itself. Nobody knows which is true.
- Other view, Aristotle, a thinker in ancient Greece, about 2,350 years ago: Aristotle taught that there can be no truly empty space anywhere, and that the world is a ball with an edge, with nothing at all beyond it. Many people today also say that bottles, stars and space have no awareness at all.
- Practice, Feeling the space around you (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly. Now stretch your arms out wide, very slowly. Feel the space around your hands. Listen for one sound that is near, and one that is far away. Then open your eyes.
- Ask: The bottle looked small from far away. Is anything in the sky small, or does it only look small?
- Ask: If there were no space between things, could there be two things at all?
- Ask: Does it feel good, strange or both, that nobody knows whether space ends?

## Story: Nila and the Wall at the End of the Sky
*Nila, Adi and their Paati are made up. Paati means grandmother in Tamil. Archytas was a real thinker in ancient Greece.*

Nila was eight. She lived in a flat on the fourth floor in Madurai, with her Paati and her little brother Adi. Adi was five.

One evening the power went off. The fan stopped, and the flat went dark and warm. Paati lit a lamp, and they all went out to sit on the balcony.

From the balcony, they could see the tall gopuram of the Meenakshi temple, far across the roofs. Its top was lit up by the last of the sunset.

Adi held up his thumb and closed one eye. "Look, Akka," he said. "I can hide the whole gopuram with my thumb!"

Nila laughed. "Your thumb is not bigger than the gopuram," she said. "The gopuram is just very far away. Things look smaller when they are far."

Adi looked at his thumb, then at the gopuram. He moved his thumb away, and the gopuram came back. He put his thumb back, and it was gone again. He did this six times.

Then the stars began to come out, one by one. Adi lay back on the mat and looked up.

"Akka," he said, "where does the sky stop? Is there a wall at the end?"

Nila thought about it. "Let's play a game," she said. "I'll say a place, and you say what is past it."

"Past our balcony?" she asked. "The street," said Adi. "Past the street?" "The temple." "Past the temple?" Adi thought hard. "More roofs. Then the hills."

"Past the hills?" "The sea, I think." "Past the sea, and up, up into the sky?" "The Moon!" said Adi. "Past the Moon?" "The Sun. And then the stars."

"And past the very last star?" asked Nila.

Adi sat up. "A wall," he said. "A big wall at the end of the sky."

"All right," said Nila. "What is on the other side of the wall?"

Adi opened his mouth, then shut it. He frowned. "More space," he said slowly. "But then the wall is not the end."

Paati smiled in the lamplight. "A thinker in Greece asked that same question, about 2,400 years ago," she said. "His name was Archytas. He said: if I stood at the edge of space and pushed out a stick, where would the stick go? Either there is more space for it, or something is there to stop it. People are still thinking about his question."

"So does space go on for ever?" asked Adi.

"Nobody knows yet," said Paati. "Not me, and not the scientists either. That is one of the biggest questions there is."

Nila jumped up. "I can show you something that looks like for ever," she said. She went inside and came back with Paati's small hand mirror. She stuck a red bindi on the top edge of the mirror.

She took Adi to the big mirror on the cupboard door and held the hand mirror up in front of it, facing the big mirror. "Look over the top," she said.

Adi looked. He gasped. Inside the big mirror was the hand mirror. Inside that was another, and another, each one smaller, a long tunnel of mirrors going away. Each had a red bindi on top.

"It goes on for ever!" said Adi.

"Count them," said Nila.

Adi counted. "One, two, three" all the way to eleven. Then the bindis were too tiny and too dark to see.

"Is that the end?" he asked.

"I don't know," said Nila. "I can't see any more. But not seeing the end doesn't tell us if there is an end."

Just then the power came back. The fan began to turn, and the lights came on all over the street.

Adi went back to the balcony. He held up his thumb and hid the gopuram once more. Then he looked up at the stars. "They must be very, very far," he said, "to look so small."

**For the parent:** Far things look small because of the space between us and them, not because they are small. And when we ask what is past the farthest place, there always seems to be another question. Nobody knows yet whether space has an end.

- Ask: Adi hid the whole gopuram with his thumb. Was his thumb bigger than the gopuram? Why could it hide the gopuram?
- Ask: When Nila asked what was on the other side of the wall, why did Adi say the wall was not the end?
- Ask: In the mirror tunnel, Adi counted eleven bindis. Why could he not count any more?
- Ask: Nila said that not seeing the end doesn't tell us if there is an end. What did she mean?
- Ask: What do you think: does space go on for ever? What makes you think so?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bottle Strip Test: how big does a bottle look from far away? (25 min)
- **question:** When you stand twice as far from a bottle, how tall does the bottle look?
- **predict:** When you stand twice as far away, will the bottle look just as tall, a little shorter, or half as tall? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same bottle the whole time, and we never move it. The child holds the pencil the same way every time, with the arm straight and one eye closed, because a bent arm changes the size. Only the distance changes. We measure at each place twice, in case one try goes wrong.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting with scissors.
- **record:** Glue or lay the three strips side by side on a page, longest first. Write '1 step', '2 steps' and '4 steps' under them.
- **results table:** Where you stood | Strip from try 1 | Strip from try 2 | How many 4-step strips fit along it
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the bottle on a stool at one end of the longest room or hall. 2. From the bottle, take 1 big step and put a chappal down. 3. Take 1 more big step and put a second chappal. 4. Take 2 more big steps and put the third chappal.  
  *Parent says:* This chappal is 1 step from the bottle. This one is 2 steps. This one is 4 steps, twice as far as the second.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the chappals. One is 1 step away. One is 2 steps. One is 4 steps.
  - Note: Use your own big steps, and keep them all the same length. You need about 3 metres in a straight line. A hall, a corridor or the space from a bedroom door to the far wall works.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the questions below. 2. Write your child's guess and reason at the top of the paper. 3. Do not say who is right.  
  *Parent says:* From 2 steps, how tall will the bottle look? Now think of 4 steps, which is twice as far. Will it look just as tall, a little shorter, or half as tall? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* From twice as far, how tall will the bottle look? Why?
  - Ask: From twice as far away, how tall will the bottle look? Why? (good: The same, because it is the same bottle.; A bit smaller, because far things look small.; Half, because it is twice as far.)
  - Note: Most children guess 'the same' or 'a bit smaller'. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands with toes at the 1-step chappal. 2. Arm straight out, elbow locked, one eye closed. 3. Hold the pencil upright. Line up the tip with the top of the bottle. 4. Slide the thumbnail down to the bottom of the bottle. 5. Keep the thumb still, and lay the pencil on the paper strip. 6. You mark the paper at the tip and at the thumb.  
  *Parent says:* Hold your arm out straight, like a stick. Close one eye. Put the pencil tip at the top of the bottle. Now slide your thumb down to the bottom. Keep it still, and we'll mark it.  
  *Child mode:* Arm out straight. Close one eye. Tip at the top. Thumb at the bottom. Keep still.
  - Note: The arm must be straight every time, or the measure changes. If your child finds it hard, you hold your own arm out and measure, and your child checks. Do each place twice. The two marks should be close.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Move to the 2-step chappal and measure the same way. 2. Mark a new piece of the strip. 3. Then measure from the 4-step chappal. 4. Mark a third piece. 5. Write '1', '2' and '4' next to the marks.  
  *Parent says:* Now we're twice as far. Measure again, with your arm just as straight. Now the last chappal, twice as far again.  
  *Child mode:* Measure from 2 steps. Then measure from 4 steps.
  - Ask: As you stepped back, what happened to the gap between your pencil tip and your thumb? (good: It got smaller.; My thumb went up nearer the tip.)
  - Note: From 4 steps the mark may be only 2 or 3 centimetres long. That is fine. Small is the result.
- **s5 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the three marked pieces. 2. Lay them side by side, longest first. 3. Fold the 1-step strip in half and hold it next to the 2-step strip. 4. Count how many 4-step strips fit along the 1-step strip. 5. Fill in the results table. 6. Then read the cards on near and far.  
  *Parent says:* Let's fold the 1-step strip in half. Is half of it as long as the 2-step strip? How many little 4-step strips fit along the long one? Did the bottle change, or did something else change?  
  *Child mode:* Fold the long strip in half. Is it like the middle strip? Did the bottle change?
  - Ask: The bottle never moved. What changed? (good: How far away I stood.; The space between me and the bottle.; Only the distance.)
  - Note: Usually the 2-step strip is close to half the 1-step strip, and about 3 to 5 of the 4-step strips fit along the 1-step one. Steps that are not quite even, or an arm that bends, make it less exact. A strong answer uses the strips as evidence: same bottle, more space between, shorter strip.
  - Check: In the bottle strip test, you stood twice as far from the bottle. How tall did the bottle look? → **About half as tall**
### e2 · The Mirror Tunnel: does the row go on for ever? (15 min)
- **question:** In a tunnel of mirrors, how many copies can you count? Does the row go on for ever?
- **predict:** How many bindis will you be able to count: 2, 10, 100, or never-ending? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Two people count, each three times, holding the mirror in the same place. We count three times because it is easy to lose your place in a long row.
- **safety:** Glass mirrors break and cut. A grown-up carries the mirrors, and the child holds the hand mirror with both hands.
- **record:** Write each count in the results table. Draw the tunnel as you saw it, with the bindis getting smaller.
- **results table:** Who counted | Count 1 | Count 2 | Count 3 | What the last copies looked like
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stick a bindi on the top edge of the hand mirror, on the shiny side. 2. Stand your child about one step from the big mirror. 3. Ask the question below and write the guess.  
  *Parent says:* We're going to make a tunnel of mirrors. How many bindis do you think you'll see? 2, 10, 100, or more than anyone can count? Why?  
  *Child mode:* How many bindis will you see? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: How many bindis will you see? Why? (good: Lots, because mirrors copy each other.; Two, because there are two mirrors.; For ever, because it keeps going.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds the hand mirror with both hands, just under the eyes. 2. The shiny side faces the big mirror. 3. Your child looks over the top edge into the big mirror. 4. Tilt the hand mirror a tiny bit until the tunnel appears. 5. Your child counts the bindis, three times.  
  *Parent says:* Look over the top of the little mirror, into the big one. Tilt it slowly. Can you see the tunnel? Count the bindis, starting with the biggest.  
  *Child mode:* Look over the little mirror. Tilt it slowly. Count the bindis.
  - Note: If no tunnel shows, keep the hand mirror upright and turn it a little left or right. The tunnel often curves gently away. Most people count between 6 and 15 before the copies get too small and dark.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap, and you count three times too. 2. Write all the counts in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now it's my turn. Let's see if I count the same.  
  *Child mode:* Now your grown-up counts. Write the numbers down.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the questions below. 2. Then read the card on whether space goes on for ever.  
  *Parent says:* Could you count to the end? What did the last bindis look like? Was that really the end, or could you just not see more?  
  *Child mode:* Could you see the end? What did the last bindis look like?
  - Ask: Why could you not count any more bindis? (good: They got too small.; They got too dark.; They were too far down the tunnel.)
  - Note: Each copy looks smaller because the light from it has travelled farther, just like the bottle from far away. A strong answer says the row faded out, and that you could not tell whether it really ended.
  - Check: In the mirror tunnel, what happened to the bindis as the tunnel went away? → **They got smaller and dimmer**

### a1 · Space Hunt: find what is between things (10 min)

  - Note: Every answer is fine for now. Introduce the word: the room between things is called space.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child finds five spaces in the home. 2. Give hints if needed. Try between your fingers, under the bed and inside an empty bottle. 3. Also try between the fan blades, and between your child and the wall. 4. Write or draw each one.  
  *Parent says:* The room between things is called space. Let's hunt for five spaces. Look under, inside, around and between things.  
  *Child mode:* Find five spaces. Look under, inside, around and between.
  - Note: Keep fingers away from the fan blades. Look at the gaps only while the fan is switched off.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push the two tumblers together until they touch. 2. Ask the questions below. 3. Close the empty bottle with its cap and squeeze it.  
  *Parent says:* Now the tumblers touch. Is there still space? Where? This bottle looks empty. Squeeze it with the cap on. What is inside?  
  *Child mode:* The tumblers touch. Is there still space? Squeeze the shut bottle. What is inside?
  - Ask: Where is there still space? (good: All around the tumblers.; Inside them.; Above them, up to the ceiling.)
  - Ask: What is in the shut bottle? (good: Air. It pushes back when I squeeze.)
  - Note: The capped bottle pushes back because it is full of air. Space in a room is full of air, even where it looks empty.

  *Child mode:* Walk heel to toe. Count each step out loud.
  - Note: Heel to toe keeps every step the same size, so the counts are fair to compare.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to put the four places in order, from nearest to farthest. 2. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Which place is nearest? Which is farthest? Were your guesses too big, too small, or close?

## Applications
- Artists draw far things smaller and higher on the page, so a flat drawing looks deep. This is called perspective.
- Drivers and cyclists judge how much space there is before they turn, so they do not bump into things.
- Scientists at ISRO, including Ritu Karidhal and Nandini Harinath, worked on Mangalyaan, India's spacecraft that reached Mars in 2014. Getting there meant working out a path across a huge distance of space.
- Phone maps measure the distance between two places and tell us how long a journey will take.
- Astronomers use Henrietta Leavitt's rule to measure how far away other galaxies are.

## Spotted around us
- A tall temple gopuram in Madurai, Thanjavur or Srirangam looks small from far across the city, and huge when you stand at its gate.
- Railway tracks at a station in Kolkata or Hyderabad seem to come together far away, though they stay the same distance apart.
- Crows or pigeons on a wire in Guwahati or Pune sit with gaps between them, keeping some space for each bird.
- A plane in the sky over Bengaluru or Delhi looks as small as a bird, though it is very big.
- At night from a terrace in Kochi or Chennai, the Moon looks about as wide as a small pea held at arm's length, and the stars look like tiny dots.

## Open questions
- **Does space go on for ever, or does it have an end?** We can only see part of space, because light from farther away has not reached us yet. Measurements of the shape of space fit space going on for ever, but they also fit space being very big and curving round. Nobody knows yet.
- **Is space a thing of its own, or only the gaps between things?** Isaac Newton and Gottfried Leibniz argued about this over 300 years ago. Later, scientists found that space can bend and stretch, which you will learn about later. Thinkers still disagree about what space really is.
- **What is past the farthest galaxies we can see?** Telescopes keep finding galaxies farther and farther away. But light from beyond a certain distance has not had time to reach us, so we cannot see what is there.
