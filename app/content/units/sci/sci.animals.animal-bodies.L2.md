# How animals move, and how their mouths fit their food
`sci.animals.animal-bodies.L2`

**Summary.** A frog's back legs are longer than the rest of her body, but they stay folded up until she jumps. Animals move by pushing on something: legs push on the ground, wings push on air, and fins and webbed feet push on water. Snakes and earthworms have no legs, so they push with their whole body. Your child tests how far they can jump with straight legs, a small bend and a big bend in the knees. Then they find out how teeth and beaks fit the food each animal eats, from a cow's grinding teeth to a kingfisher's long, sharp beak.

**Intro.** *A frog sitting still looks as if she has short legs. Then she jumps, and her back legs open out longer than her whole body. Where were those long legs hiding, and why do they fold up like that?* Every animal that moves has to push on something. A crow's wings push on the air. A fish's tail pushes on the water. Your feet push on the floor. Animals also have mouths shaped for their food: sharp beaks, flat teeth, pointed teeth or no teeth at all. In this unit you test your own jump, crawl like a snake, flap like a bird, and grind a leaf the way a cow chews grass.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can name how different animals move: with legs, wings, fins, or their whole body.
1. By the end I can explain that legs push on the ground, and that bent knees give a longer push and a longer jump.
2. By the end I can say how wings push on air, and how fins and webbed feet push on water.
3. By the end I can say how a snake and an earthworm move without legs.
4. By the end I can name cutting, pointed and grinding teeth, and match teeth to the food an animal eats.
5. By the end I can match a bird's beak to the food that bird eats.

## Terms
- **Joint**: A joint is a place where two bones meet, so the body can bend there. *e.g.* Your knee, your elbow and your ankle are joints. A frog's long back legs have joints too.
- **Muscle**: A muscle is a part of the body that gets shorter to pull on a bone and move it. *e.g.* When you straighten your bent knee, the big muscle on the front of your thigh is pulling.
- **Jump**: To jump is to push hard on the ground with your legs, so that your whole body leaves the ground. *e.g.* A frog, a cat, a grasshopper and you all jump by bending the legs first, then straightening them fast.
- **Wing**: A wing is a body part that pushes on the air, so an animal can fly. *e.g.* A crow, a bat and a butterfly all have wings. A bat's wing is skin stretched over long finger bones.
- **Fin**: A fin is a flat body part that a fish uses to push on water, to steer and to keep steady. *e.g.* A rohu fish swings her tail fin from side to side to swim forward.
- **Webbed feet**: Webbed feet have thin skin stretched between the toes, like a paddle. *e.g.* Ducks and frogs have webbed feet, which push hard on water when they swim.
- **Crawl**: To crawl is to move along with the body low and close to the ground. *e.g.* An earthworm crawls. A baby crawls on hands and knees. A snake slides along on the belly.
- **Glide**: To glide is to move through the air without flapping, by spreading out flat. *e.g.* The flying lizard of the Western Ghats spreads flaps of skin and glides from one tree to the next.
- **Beak**: A beak is the hard mouth of a bird. Birds have beaks and no teeth. *e.g.* A sparrow's short, thick beak cracks seeds. A kingfisher's long, sharp beak grabs fish.
- **Cutting teeth (incisors)**: Incisors are the thin, flat teeth at the front of the mouth. They cut and bite off food. *e.g.* You use your incisors to bite a piece off a guava or a banana.
- **Pointed teeth (canines)**: Canines are the pointed teeth beside the front teeth. They grip and tear food. *e.g.* A tiger's long canines grip and tear meat. Your own canines are small and pointed.
- **Grinding teeth (molars)**: Molars are the wide, bumpy teeth at the back of the mouth. They grind food into small bits. *e.g.* A cow grinds grass with her big flat molars. You grind roti and rice with yours.
- **Herbivore, carnivore and omnivore**: A herbivore eats plants. A carnivore eats other animals. An omnivore eats both. *e.g.* A cow is a herbivore. A tiger is a carnivore. A crow, a bear and most humans are omnivores.

## Explanations
### How legs move a body · *defined*
**Definition:** Legs move a body by pushing back and down on the ground. The ground pushes back, and the body moves forward and up.

A leg is made of bones joined at joints, such as the knee. Muscles pull on the bones to bend and straighten the leg. When a leg straightens against the ground, the ground pushes the body the other way. A bent leg can push for longer than a straight one, and a longer push gives a faster start.
- e.g. When you walk, each foot pushes back on the floor, and you move forward.
- e.g. On a slippery floor your foot slides back, so the push is lost and you hardly move.
- e.g. A cat crouches low before jumping onto a wall, so her legs can push for longer.
### Animals built to jump · *defined*
**Definition:** Jumping animals have long back legs that fold up when resting and straighten very fast to push off.

A frog's back legs fold in three parts under her body, like a letter Z. When she jumps, they all straighten at once. Because the legs are long, they keep pushing on the ground for longer. A grasshopper's big back legs work like a stretched rubber band: they store up energy, then let it go all at once.
- e.g. A frog's back legs, opened out, are longer than her head and body together.
- e.g. A grasshopper's big back legs make the jump. The four smaller legs are for walking.
- e.g. A cat, a goat and you bend your knees before every jump.
### How wings fly · *defined*
**Definition:** A wing flies by pushing air down and back. The air pushes the wing up and forward, and that holds the animal up.

On the way down, a bird spreads the wing wide and flat, so the wing pushes a lot of air. On the way up, many birds bend the wing in a little, so it pushes less air the wrong way. A bird's wing is covered in feathers. A bat's wing is skin stretched over very long finger bones, and a butterfly has four thin wings.
- e.g. A pigeon claps her wings loudly as she takes off.
- e.g. A black kite circles high over the city, hardly flapping, on rising warm air.
- e.g. A fruit bat flies at dusk on wings made of skin.
### Gliding: flying without flapping · *defined*
**Definition:** A glider spreads out a wide, flat body or skin and falls slowly forward through the air, without flapping.

A flat, wide shape pushes on a lot of air as the animal falls, so the fall is slow and goes forward. In the forests of the Western Ghats, the flying lizard spreads flaps of skin held out on long ribs and glides between trees. The flying snake of India flattens her whole body and glides from a high branch. Flying fish glide above the sea on big, spread-out fins.
- e.g. A flying lizard can glide from one tree to another tree several metres away.
- e.g. A flying snake makes her body flat and wide before she leaps.
- e.g. A flying fish leaps out of the sea and glides on fins spread like wings.
### Swimming with fins and webbed feet · *defined*
**Definition:** Swimmers move by pushing water backwards with a tail, fins, flippers or webbed feet.

A fish bends her body from side to side, and her tail fin pushes water back. The other fins steer and keep her steady. A duck spreads her toes to push back, so the skin between them pushes a lot of water. Then she folds her toes to bring the foot forward. Frogs kick with long, webbed back legs.
- e.g. A rohu fish swings her tail from side to side.
- e.g. Ducks paddle in the backwaters of Kuttanad in Kerala.
- e.g. A frog in a pond kicks both back legs at once.
### Moving without legs · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals without legs move by pushing parts of their body against the ground and the things on it.

A snake bends her body into curves. Each curve pushes sideways against stones, sticks and bumps, and that pushes the snake forward. On very smooth glass she has little to push on, so she slides about and moves slowly. An earthworm makes her body long and thin, then short and fat, and tiny bristles grip the soil. A snail glides on a layer of slime.
- e.g. A rat snake moves fast through grass, pushing on the stems.
- e.g. An earthworm stretches forward, grips, and pulls the back part along.
- e.g. A snail leaves a shiny trail of slime on a wall after rain.
### Teeth fit the food · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals have teeth shaped for their food: incisors cut, canines grip and tear, and molars grind.

A cow eats grass. She has no top front teeth, only a hard pad of gum, and she tears grass off between that pad and her bottom front teeth. Her wide, flat molars grind the grass as her jaw moves from side to side. A tiger's long canines grip and tear meat, her back teeth cut like scissors, and her jaw moves mostly up and down. A squirrel's front teeth never stop growing, so gnawing keeps them short. Humans eat many kinds of food, so we have all three kinds of teeth.
- e.g. A child has 20 milk teeth. Most grown-ups have 32 teeth.
- e.g. An elephant's tusks are two very long front teeth.
- e.g. A snake swallows a frog whole, and her jaws stretch very wide.
### Beaks fit the food · *defined*
**Definition:** Birds have no teeth. Each bird's beak is shaped for the way that bird gets food.

A sparrow's beak is short and thick, strong enough to crack open seeds. A parrot's hooked beak cracks nuts and also helps the parrot climb. A kingfisher's beak is long, straight and sharp, for grabbing fish. A black kite's hooked beak tears meat. A duck's flat beak has tiny comb-like edges that let water out and keep food in. A crow's beak is a strong, middle-sized beak that suits almost any food, and crows eat almost anything.
- e.g. A white-throated kingfisher sits on a wire above a paddy field, watching for fish and frogs.
- e.g. A rose-ringed parrot holds a chilli in one foot and bites the chilli with her hooked beak.
- e.g. A house sparrow cracks rice grains with her short, thick beak.
### One bone plan in many limbs · *defined*
**Definition:** The arm of a human, the wing of a bat and a bird, the front leg of a frog and the flipper of a whale are built from the same set of bones.

Each one has one long upper bone, then two bones side by side, then a group of small wrist bones, then fingers or toes. In a bat the finger bones are very long, to hold the skin of the wing. In a whale they are wrapped inside a stiff flipper. This is because all these animals come from the same long-ago ancestors: fish with bony, fleshy fins.
- e.g. Feel your own arm: one bone above the elbow, two below it.
- e.g. A bat's wing is mostly hand.
- e.g. A frog's front leg has the same bones as your arm, shorter.
### How did flying begin? · *debated*
Birds come from small dinosaurs with feathers. But nobody saw the first flights, so scientists argue about how flying began.
- e.g. Some small feathered dinosaurs had feathers on all four limbs.
- e.g. Young chukar partridges flap their small wings to help them run up steep slopes.
- **The 'trees down' idea:** Small feathered animals climbed trees and glided down, like the flying lizard. Over a very long time, gliding turned into flapping.
- **The 'ground up' idea:** Small feathered animals ran fast on the ground and flapped to leap higher, to run up slopes or to catch insects. Over time, the flaps lifted them off the ground.
- **Many scientists today:** Maybe both happened, in different animals. The evidence is a few rare fossils, so the question is still open.

## Mostly agreed
- To move, an animal must push on something: the ground, the water or the air. *How we know:* On ice or a very slippery floor, feet slide back and a walker hardly moves. Slow-motion films show wings pushing air down and fish tails pushing water back.
- Teeth and beaks are shaped for the food an animal eats. *How we know:* Scientists can often tell what an animal ate from the shape of their teeth, even in fossils. Plant-eaters have wide grinding teeth. Meat-eaters have sharp tearing teeth.
- The limbs of birds, bats, frogs, whales and humans are built from the same set of bones. *How we know:* When scientists compare the skeletons, they find the same pattern of bones in each limb: one, then two, then small wrist bones, then fingers or toes.

## How we found out
**Charles Darwin and John Gould, then Rosemary Grant and Peter Grant, 1835 and 1837, then 1973 to 1978.** In 1835, Charles Darwin visited the Galápagos Islands in the Pacific Ocean. He collected many small brown birds. Their beaks were very different. Some were thick and strong, and some were thin and pointed. Darwin thought they were different kinds of birds, such as wrens, blackbirds and finches. Back in England in 1837, the bird expert John Gould looked at them closely. He found that they were all finches, close relatives of each other. Their beaks fitted different foods: big seeds, small seeds or insects. Darwin came to think that beaks change over many, many generations. He thought this was far too slow for anyone to watch. Then, in 1973, Rosemary Grant and Peter Grant began to measure every finch on Daphne Major, one tiny island. In 1977 almost no rain fell. The small, soft seeds ran out, and only big, hard seeds were left. Most of the finches died. Finches with deeper, stronger beaks could crack the big seeds, so more of them lived. Their chicks had bigger beaks too. The Grants had watched beaks change in just a few years.  
*What people believed before:* Darwin first thought the birds with different beaks were different kinds of birds, not all finches. He also thought beaks change far too slowly for anyone to see.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Snakes are slimy.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Snakes look shiny, and some animals without legs, like earthworms and snails, are slimy.  
  Against: A snake's skin is dry and covered in scales. Earthworms and snails are slimy, but snakes are not.
- **Animals with big, sharp teeth eat only meat.** (A common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Tigers and leopards have big canines and eat only meat.  
  Against: The sloth bear of India has big canines, but eats mostly termites, ants and fruit. Monkeys and fruit bats have pointed canines and eat lots of fruit.
- **Birds take off using only their wings.** (What many people think when they watch a bird fly; *rejected*)  
  For: Wings do the flying once a bird is in the air.  
  Against: Scientists filmed birds taking off and measured the push of their feet. Many birds get most of their starting speed from a jump with their legs, then the wings take over.
- **Bigger animals always jump farther.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: A big kangaroo or a horse can jump a long way.  
  Against: A tiny flea or grasshopper jumps many times the length of their own body. An elephant cannot jump at all. Size is only one part of the story.

## Roots
We have tested our own jumps, flapped like a bird, wiggled like a snake and ground leaves like a cow. Now we ask what the Jump Test tells us about moving, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** With straight legs you jumped only a short way, but with knees well bent you jumped much farther. Your feet push on the floor only while your legs straighten. A bigger bend gives a longer push, so you leave the floor faster. So a jump is made on the ground, before you ever leave it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a jump as the flying part, through the air. But once your feet leave the floor, nothing more can push you. The whole jump was made in the short push before, while you still touched the ground.  
*Evidence:* In the Jump Test, straight-legged jumps went only a little way, and jumps with a big knee bend went much farther. A frog's long back legs fold up in a Z, so her push on the ground lasts a long time.

- *who am i:* You are an animal who jumps the same way a frog does: bend first, then push.
- *what is life:* Every moving animal is always pushing on something: the ground, the water or the air.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of bodies shaped for different pushes: legs, wings, fins and webbed feet. And mouths shaped for different foods.

Your arm, a bat's wing, a frog's leg and a whale's flipper look very different. Inside, they are built from the same set of bones: one, then two, then small wrist bones, then fingers. All these animals came from the same long-ago fish with bony, fleshy fins. One plan of bones has grown into jumping legs, flapping wings, paddling flippers and your own hands. And each of them moves by pushing on the same ground, water and air that we all share.

- **Tirukkural 490, chapter 49, 'Knowing the fitting time':** Tiruvalluvar says: when the time is not right, wait still, like a heron. When the right moment comes, strike quickly, like a heron striking a fish. (A heron, a kingfisher and a frog all wait very still, ready, and then move in one fast strike or push. The waiting, with legs or neck bent, is part of the move.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 76:** Laozi says that living things are soft and bendable, and dead things are stiff and hard. What is stiff will break. What is soft and bendable will last. (In the Jump Test, stiff, straight legs pushed only a little. Bent legs, ready to straighten, gave the longest jumps. Being able to bend is what let your legs act.)
- Science (settled): A jump's length is set by how fast the body leaves the ground, and that depends on how hard and how long the legs push.
- Science (settled): The limbs of birds, bats, frogs, whales and humans are built from the same set of bones, because these animals share ancestors.
- Science (open): A frog chooses when to jump: she watches, waits and decides. How much a frog feels and knows as she does this is still an open question for science.
- Other view, Many biologists: A frog's fast escape jump is mostly a reflex: nerves in the body and brain set off the jump very quickly, without the frog stopping to think. In this view, choosing belongs to slower actions, like hunting.
- Practice, Crouch Like a Heron (3 min): Stand with your feet a little apart. Bend your knees, as if you are about to jump. Stay there, very still, like a heron by the water. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly, three times. Feel your legs ready to push. Now, very slowly, straighten your legs and stand tall. Did you feel the push in your legs, even slowly?
- Ask: When you were crouched and still, was the jump already there in your legs?
- Ask: Your arm and a bat's wing have the same bones. In what ways are you and a bat built alike?
- Ask: A frog waits, then jumps. Who decides the moment: the frog, her legs, or both together?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Before you jumped, your legs were bent and still. Was the jump already in your legs, waiting? (hint: Think about what your legs did in the small moment before your feet left the floor.)
- *deep:* Your arm, a bat's wing and a whale's flipper have the same bones. Are they three different things, or one thing in three shapes? (hint: Feel the bones of your arm. Then look at a picture of a bat's wing.)
- *deep:* A frog decides when to jump. What does a frog notice before she jumps? (hint: Watch how still a frog or a cat sits before a jump. What might they be watching for?)
- *big:* Every animal moves by pushing on the ground, the water or the air. Could anything move in a place with nothing to push on? (hint: Think about a person floating in space. What could they push on?)
- *big:* Humans can walk, run, jump, swim and climb, but cannot fly. Is it better to do many things a little, or one thing very well? (hint: Compare a crow, who eats almost anything, with a kingfisher, who is very good at catching fish.)

## Story: The Frog on the Bathroom Wall
*A made-up story. The common Indian tree frog really does come into bathrooms in Kerala in the monsoon, climbs tiles with sticky toe pads, and has long back legs that fold up when she sits.*

It rained all night in Kochi. In the morning, Anjali went into the bathroom to brush her teeth, and stopped.

A small brown frog was sitting on the white wall tiles, just above the water bucket. She had dark stripes on her back and big gold eyes.

"Ammamma!" Anjali called softly. "There is a frog on the wall!"

Anjali was seven. She lived on the first floor of a building near the backwaters, with Amma, her grandmother Ammamma, and her brother Joel, who was five. Ammamma had taught biology in a school for thirty years.

Joel came running first. "Catch her!" he shouted, and reached out his hand.

"Wait," said Ammamma, from the door. "Don't grab her. A frog's skin is thin and soft. She drinks water through her skin, and soap or dry hands can hurt her."

Joel pulled his hand back. They all looked at the frog. The frog looked at them, and did not move.

"How is she holding on to the wall?" asked Anjali. "The tiles are so smooth."

"Look at her toes," said Ammamma. "See the little round pads at the ends? They are sticky and soft. They press close to the tile, and wet tiles help them hold. This is a tree frog. Tree frogs climb trees, and walls too."

Anjali leaned closer. The frog's front legs were short and thin. Her back legs were folded up tight under her body, so she looked like a small brown ball.

"Her back legs are so short," said Anjali.

Ammamma smiled. "Are they? Keep watching."

Joel could not keep still. He took a step forward, and his slipper squeaked on the floor.

The frog jumped.

For one moment, Anjali saw her back legs open out. They opened like a folding ruler, in three parts. They were long, longer than the frog's whole head and body together. Then, plop, the frog was in the water bucket.

"Where did those long legs come from?" Anjali gasped.

"They were there all the time," said Ammamma. "Folded up, ready. A frog sits with her legs bent, so that she can push off fast."

In the bucket, the frog kicked both back legs at once. Her toes spread wide as she pushed back, and Anjali saw thin skin stretched between them, like a little fan. The frog shot across the water to the other side.

"Her feet are like a duck's!" said Joel.

"Yes," said Ammamma. "Webbed feet. The skin between the toes pushes a lot of water back, so she moves fast. When her legs come forward again, she pulls her toes together."

"Can we keep her?" asked Joel.

Ammamma shook her head. "She is a wild frog. She needs to eat insects and live outside, near the rain. We will take her to the plants by the gate."

Ammamma held a mug in the water, and the frog swam into the mug. Anjali put a piece of cardboard over the top, gently. They walked down the stairs together.

Near the gate, under the big banana plant, there was a puddle from the night's rain. Ammamma tipped the mug. The frog sat for a moment on the wet ground. Then she folded her legs, waited, and jumped into the leaves.

Back upstairs, Anjali could not stop thinking about the jump.

"Joel," she said, "jump like a frog."

Joel stood stiff and straight, and tried to jump without bending his knees. He hardly went anywhere. He wobbled and laughed.

"No," said Anjali. "Watch me." She crouched down low, with her knees bent, like the frog on the wall. She waited. Then she pushed hard with both legs and flew forward across the corridor.

"You went far!" said Joel. He crouched too, and this time he jumped much farther.

"So the jump starts before the jump," said Anjali slowly. "It starts when your legs are folded up."

Ammamma was listening from the kitchen. "The cat downstairs crouches before she jumps on the wall," she said. "The grasshopper on the money plant has huge back legs, folded up like the frog's. Even a pigeon bends her legs and jumps a little before she flies."

That night it rained again. From the plants by the gate came a loud tok, tok, tok. The frogs were calling to each other in the dark.

Anjali opened her notebook. She drew the frog twice: once folded up small on the wall, and once in the air with her long legs stretched out behind. Then, next to the frog, she drew herself, crouching at the start line, ready to jump.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Anjali sees that the frog's long back legs were there all the time, folded up. A jump begins with bent legs, and a longer push gives a longer jump. She tries it with her own body, and finds that she and the frog jump in the same way.

- Ask: Why did the frog look as if she had short back legs, when she was sitting on the wall?
- Ask: Why did Joel hardly move when he jumped with straight legs?
- Ask: How did the frog's webbed feet help her swim across the bucket?
- Ask: Why did Ammamma say they could not keep the frog?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Jump Test: do bent knees make you jump farther? (25 min)
- **question:** Does bending your knees before a jump make you jump farther? And does a bigger bend make a longer jump?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which jump will go farthest: straight legs, a small bend, or a big bend? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same child jumps every time, from the same start line, with both feet together and hands on hips. Hands on hips stop the arms from helping, so only the knee bend changes. We try each way three times, because one jump can be a fluke, and we keep the longest.
- **safety:** Jump barefoot on a dry floor, or on a dhurrie or mat that does not slide. Socks on tiles are slippery. Move chairs and tables out of the way, and land with soft, bent knees.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw a stick picture of yourself just before your longest jump.
- **results table:** How I bent my knees | Jump 1 | Jump 2 | Jump 3 | Longest jump
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear about 2 metres of floor. 2. Lay the dupatta straight across the floor as the start line. 3. Your child stands with toes just behind the line, feet together, hands on hips.  
  *Parent says:* This is our jumping track. Every jump starts with your toes behind the dupatta and your hands on your hips.  
  *Child mode:* Stand with your toes behind the line. Put your hands on your hips.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three ways: straight legs, a small bend, a big bend like sitting on a low stool. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Which jump do you think will go farthest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which jump will go farthest? Straight, small bend or big bend? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The big bend, because a frog crouches low.; Straight legs, because my legs are longer when they are straight.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Write it down exactly as your child says it. Do not explain anything yet. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child keeps the knees straight and stiff, and jumps forward using only ankles and toes. 2. Put a coin where the back of the nearest heel lands. 3. Measure from the line to the coin. 4. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Straight legs first. Keep your knees stiff, like sticks. Now jump as far as you can.  
  *Child mode:* Keep your knees stiff. Jump forward as far as you can.
  - Note: Measure from the start line to the back of the heel that landed nearest the line. With stiff knees most children only hop a short way.
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now your child bends the knees a little, then jumps. 2. Mark and measure each landing. 3. Do it three times and write each one down.  
  *Parent says:* Now bend your knees just a little bit, then jump. Hands stay on your hips.  
  *Child mode:* Bend your knees a little. Then jump!
- **s5 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now your child bends the knees a lot, as if sitting on a low stool, then jumps. 2. Mark and measure each landing. 3. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Now the big bend. Bend down low, as if you are sitting on a small stool. Then push off hard.  
  *Child mode:* Bend down low, like a frog. Then push hard!
  - Note: Do not let your child squat all the way down to the floor. A bend about as low as a small stool is enough. If they wobble, let them rest and try again.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Circle the longest jump for each way. Put the three longest jumps in order, from shortest to longest.  
  *Parent says:* Let's find your longest jump for each way. Which way won?  
  *Child mode:* Circle your longest jumps. Which way went farthest?
  - Check: Which jump usually goes farthest? → **A big bend, like sitting on a low stool**
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what they think now, what they saw, and why. Then tell them about the frog's folded legs.  
  *Parent says:* So, what did you find out? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? Why did bending help?
  - Ask: Why did the big bend make you jump farther? (good: My legs pushed on the floor for longer.; When my legs straightened, they pushed me hard.; Straight legs had nothing left to straighten.)
  - Note: A strong answer says what happened (claim), gives the numbers (evidence), and says why: the legs push only while they are straightening, so a bigger bend means a longer push. Then tell your child that a frog's long back legs fold up in a Z, so the frog gets a very long push.
### e2 · Press or Grind: how does a cow chew grass? (20 min)
- **question:** Which way breaks a leaf into small bits: pressing straight down, or rubbing round and round?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will pressing down hard or rubbing round and round break the leaf up more? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same kind of leaf, the same tumbler and the same plate. We move the tumbler 10 times each way, and press about as hard each time. Only the way of moving changes. We try each way with two leaves, because one leaf can be tougher than another.
- **record:** Stick or draw one pressed leaf and one rubbed leaf side by side, and write which way made smaller bits.
- **results table:** Way of crushing | What the leaf looked like after 10 moves | Any green juice on the plate?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put two leaves at one side of the plate and two at the other. 2. Show the two ways: press straight down and lift, or press and rub round and round.  
  *Parent says:* The tumbler is our tooth, and the plate is another tooth. We can crush the leaf two ways.  
  *Child mode:* The tumbler is a tooth. The plate is a tooth too.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Which way will break the leaf into smaller bits, pressing or rubbing? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Pressing or rubbing: which breaks the leaf more? Why?
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child presses the tumbler straight down on one leaf and lifts it, 10 times. 2. Do the same on the second leaf. 3. Look closely at both.  
  *Parent says:* Press straight down and lift. Count to ten. Press hard, like squeezing a lime.  
  *Child mode:* Press down and lift. Do it ten times. Look at the leaf.
  - Note: Pressed leaves usually go flat and get dark, bruised marks, and may leave a little juice, but they mostly stay in one piece. Leaves differ: curry leaves are tough and spinach is soft. If the result is not clear, count the bits.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now your child presses the tumbler on a new leaf and rubs it round and round and side to side, 10 times. 2. Do the same on the last leaf. 3. Compare all four.  
  *Parent says:* Now press and rub round and round. Count to ten. What happens to this leaf?  
  *Child mode:* Press and rub round and round, ten times. What happens?
  - Ask: What do the rubbed leaves look like? (good: They are torn into little bits.; There is green juice on the plate.; They are mushy.)
  - Note: Rubbed leaves tear into shreds and leave a green smear on the plate. Rubbing breaks the leaf because the two surfaces slide past each other, tearing it, instead of only squeezing it.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child puts two fingers on each cheek, just in front of the ears. 2. Then they chew a piece of roti slowly. 3. Ask how the jaw moves.  
  *Parent says:* Put your fingers here, in front of your ears. Now chew slowly. Does your jaw only go up and down?  
  *Child mode:* Fingers on your cheeks. Chew slowly. How does your jaw move?
  - Ask: How does your jaw move when you chew? (good: Up and down, and a little bit side to side.; It goes round a bit.)
  - Note: People's jaws move up and down and also a little from side to side, so our back teeth can grind. A cow's jaw swings far from side to side, and her wide molars rub the grass into a mush. A tiger's jaw moves mostly up and down, and her back teeth cut meat like scissors.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what your child thinks now, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which way broke the leaf more? So why does a cow move her jaw from side to side?  
  *Child mode:* Which way broke the leaf? Why does a cow chew side to side?
  - Check: A cow eats grass all day. Why does her jaw move from side to side? → **So her flat back teeth rub the grass into tiny bits**

## Worksheets
### Moving and Eating (core) (core, 11 questions)
1. (sort) Sort each animal by the main way they move.  
   **Answer:** a cat → Legs; a crow flying → Wings; a rohu fish → Fins; an earthworm → Whole body; a butterfly → Wings; a snake → Whole body. *Why:* A cat walks on four legs. A flying crow and a butterfly use wings. A fish swims with fins. An earthworm and a snake have no legs and move with the whole body.
2. (match) Draw a line from each beak to the food it is best for.  
   **Answer:** Short, thick beak → Seeds; Long, straight, sharp beak → Fish; Long, thin, curved beak → Nectar from flowers; Hooked, sharp beak → Meat. *Why:* Short, thick beaks crack seeds. Long, sharp beaks grab fish. Long, curved beaks reach nectar deep in flowers. Hooked beaks tear meat.
3. (count) Count the legs: one duck, one frog and one ant.  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* A duck has 2 legs, a frog has 4 and an ant has 6. 2 and 4 make 6, and 6 more makes 12.
4. (mcq) Which body part pushes on the water when a rohu fish swims forward?  
   Options: Her eyes / Her tail fin / Her scales  
   **Answer:** Her tail fin. *Why:* A fish bends her body from side to side, and the tail fin pushes water back. That pushes the fish forward. The other fins steer and keep her steady.
5. (truefalse) Birds have teeth inside their beaks.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Birds alive today have no teeth. Their beaks are shaped to crack, grab, tear, sip or strain their food.
6. (draw) Pick a food: seeds, fish or nectar. Draw the head of a bird with a beak that fits that food.  
   **Answer:** A bird head with a short, thick beak for seeds, a long, straight, sharp beak for fish, or a long, thin, curved beak for nectar.. *Why:* A strong drawing gives the beak a shape that suits the food it picked, because each beak is shaped for the way that bird gets food.
7. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: in the Jump Test, why did you hardly move with straight legs?  
   **Answer:** With stiff legs, my legs could not straighten any more, so they could not push on the floor for long.. *Why:* A strong answer says that legs push only while they straighten, and straight legs have no bend left to straighten.
8. (label) Label the teeth in this mouth: cutting teeth, pointed teeth, grinding teeth.  
   **Answer:** ['cutting teeth: the flat ones at the very front', 'pointed teeth: one on each side of the front teeth', 'grinding teeth: the wide ones at the back']. *Why:* Incisors at the front cut. Canines beside them grip and tear. Molars at the back grind food into small bits.
9. (odd) Three of these animals have wings. Which one does not?  
   Options: A bat / A housefly / A frog / A pigeon  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A frog has no wings. A bat has skin wings, a housefly has two thin wings, and a pigeon has feathered wings.
10. (fill) A place where two bones meet, so the body can bend, is called a ________.  
   **Answer:** joint. *Why:* Your knee, elbow and ankle are joints. A frog's long back legs bend at three joints, so they fold up small.
11. (order) Put the steps of a frog's jump in order.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3]. *Why:* A jump starts with bent legs. The push happens while the legs straighten, and only then does the frog leave the ground.
### Moving and Eating (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)

   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, then says what the other side gets right. For example: the tiger's teeth are best for meat, but they could not grind grass. The cow's teeth are best for grass, but they could not catch and tear meat.. *Why:* Each set of teeth is very good at one kind of food. Which is better depends on what the animal eats.    **Answer:** Rubbing round and round tore the leaf into bits and made green juice. Pressing only flattened it. A cow rubs her flat back teeth from side to side, so the grass is ground up small.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the result of the test (rubbing tore the leaf) and links it to the side-to-side chewing of a cow.
3. (mcq) Kavya, a girl in Class 2, sees two frogs of the same size. One has short back legs and one has long back legs. Which frog most likely jumps farther?

   **Answer:** ['toes: the three long parts pointing forward', 'web of skin: the thin skin between the toes', 'pushes water this way: backwards, behind the duck']. *Why:* The web of skin between the toes makes the foot a wide paddle. The duck pushes water backwards, and the water pushes the duck forward.
6. (truefalse) A snake's skin is slimy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A snake's skin is dry and covered in scales. Earthworms and snails are slimy. Snakes only look shiny.

   **Answer:** A pigeon flying → The air; A duck swimming → The water; A cat running → The ground; An earthworm → The soil. *Why:* Fliers push on air, swimmers push on water, and walkers, runners and crawlers push on the ground or the soil.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) How does an earthworm move without legs?  
   Options: By flapping / By stretching and squeezing the body, while tiny bristles grip the soil / By jumping  
   **Answer:** By stretching and squeezing the body, while tiny bristles grip the soil. *Why:* An earthworm makes her body long and thin, then short and fat, one part at a time. Tiny bristles grip the soil so she does not slide back. If you said jumping, think of the frog: jumping needs legs.
2. (mcq) What do a bird's wings push on to hold the bird up?  
   Options: The air / The ground / The bird's own body  
   **Answer:** The air. *Why:* Wings push air down, and the air pushes the wings up. You felt this when the flat notebook was hard to swing.
3. (mcq) Why do a frog's back legs fold up in a Z shape when she sits?  
   Options: To keep them warm / So they can straighten fast and give a long push when she jumps / Because they are broken  
   **Answer:** So they can straighten fast and give a long push when she jumps. *Why:* Folded legs are ready to straighten. Long, folded legs push on the ground for a long time, so the frog jumps far.
4. (mcq) Which teeth grind food into small bits?  
   Options: The flat front teeth (incisors) / The pointed teeth (canines) / The wide back teeth (molars)  
   **Answer:** The wide back teeth (molars). *Why:* Molars are wide and bumpy, and grind food. Incisors cut, and canines grip and tear. If you picked the front teeth, remember the cucumber bite: they made cuts, not mush.
5. (mcq) A tiger has very long pointed canines. What does a tiger eat?  
   Options: Grass / Meat / Nectar  
   **Answer:** Meat. *Why:* Long canines grip and tear meat. A tiger is a carnivore. Grass-eaters like cows have wide, flat grinding teeth instead.
6. (mcq) Which bird has a beak made for cracking seeds?  
   Options: A sparrow, with a short, thick beak / A kingfisher, with a long, sharp beak / A sunbird, with a long, curved beak  
   **Answer:** A sparrow, with a short, thick beak. *Why:* A short, thick beak is strong enough to crack seeds. A kingfisher's beak grabs fish, and a sunbird's beak reaches nectar.
7. (mcq) Why does a duck have skin between her toes?  
   Options: To push a lot of water back when she swims / To keep her feet clean / To help her fly  
   **Answer:** To push a lot of water back when she swims. *Why:* The web of skin makes the foot a wide paddle. The duck pushes water back, and the water pushes her forward.
8. (mcq) Which of these animals moves mostly with fins?  
   Options: A rohu fish / A crow / A gecko  
   **Answer:** A rohu fish. *Why:* A rohu fish swims with a tail fin and steers with side fins. A crow flies with wings, and a gecko runs and climbs on four legs.
9. (mcq) A duck's beak is wide and flat, with tiny comb-like edges. What are the edges for?  
   Options: To let water out and keep food in / To tear meat / To crack nuts  
   **Answer:** To let water out and keep food in. *Why:* A duck scoops up water with food in it. The comb-like edges let the water out and keep the small bits of food in.
10. (mcq) What does a snake push on to move forward?  
   Options: Stones, sticks and bumps on the ground / The air above her / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** Stones, sticks and bumps on the ground. *Why:* A snake pushes the curves of her body sideways against bumps on the ground. In the Snake Wiggle, the cushions gave you something to push on.
11. (mcq) A cow has no top front teeth. How does her jaw move when she chews grass?  
   Options: Only up and down / Far from side to side, so her molars grind / Her jaw does not move  
   **Answer:** Far from side to side, so her molars grind. *Why:* A cow's jaw swings from side to side, so her flat molars rub the grass into a mush. In the Press or Grind test, rubbing broke the leaf much more than pressing did.

## Open questions
- **Why did some fish first come out of the water and walk?** Long ago, some fish with bony, fleshy fins began to move on land. Scientists still argue about why: to find food, to escape hunters, or to move between drying pools.
- **Why do some birds hop and others walk?** Sparrows mostly hop, pigeons walk, and crows do both. Scientists have ideas, but there is no clear answer yet.
