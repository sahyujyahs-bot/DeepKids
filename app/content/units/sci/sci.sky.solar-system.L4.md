# The solar system: why a rocky planet and a gas giant are so different, and how we know
`sci.sky.solar-system.L4`

**Summary.** Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun, yet Venus is hotter. Jupiter is about 11 times as wide as the Earth, yet a lump of Jupiter weighs only about a quarter as much as a lump of Earth the same size, and Saturn is lighter than water. Four planets have solid ground and four have none, though all eight formed from one cloud around one Sun. Your child drops marbles into flour to see how craters give away the age of a world, sorts a jar into layers like a young planet, finds the rule that links a planet's distance to the length of its year, and walks a model where the Earth is a mustard seed. Along the way: why Mars lost its air, why a day on Venus is longer than its year, and why a message sent to a spacecraft at Mars is already many minutes old when it arrives.

**Intro.** *Four of the eight planets have solid ground. If you dropped a stone on Jupiter, it would never land, because there is no ground to land on. All eight planets formed from the same cloud of gas and dust, around the same young Sun. So why did they turn out as two quite different kinds of world?* In this unit you find out. You drop marbles into a tray of flour and learn how scientists read the age of a planet from its craters. You sort heavy things from light things in a jar, the way a young planet sorted itself. You work out how long sunlight takes to reach each planet, and why that makes driving a rover on Mars so slow. You also meet Inge Lehmann, a Danish scientist who found out what is inside the Earth without ever digging a hole.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain how rocky planets and giant planets differ in size, make-up, density and whether they have solid ground.
1. I can use density to say which planets are made of rock and metal and which of gas, and why Saturn would float in water.
2. I can explain how counting craters shows the age of a surface, and why the Moon and Mercury have many craters while the Earth has few.
3. I can explain how an atmosphere decides how hot a planet is, using Venus, the Earth, Mars and Mercury.
4. I can find, from data, the rule that links a planet's distance from the Sun to the length of its year.
5. I can work out how long light takes to travel across the solar system, and say why a message to Mars arrives late.
6. I can use a scale model to show that the solar system is mostly empty space, with tiny planets very far apart.

## Terms
- **Rocky planet**: A planet made mostly of rock and metal, with a solid surface you could stand on. It is small, dense and close to the Sun. *e.g.* Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars are the four rocky planets.
- **Gas giant**: A very large planet made mostly of the gases hydrogen and helium. It has no solid surface. Going deeper, the gas just gets thicker and hotter. *e.g.* Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants. They are made of the same gases as the Sun.
- **Ice giant**: A giant planet made of a lot of water, ammonia and methane, mixed with hydrogen and helium. Scientists call these 'ices' because they freeze solid in the cold far from the Sun. Inside the planet they are hot and fluid. *e.g.* Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. Their blue colour comes from methane in their air.
- **Density**: How heavy a thing is for its size. A dense thing packs a lot of material into a small space. *e.g.* A steel nut is much denser than a wooden block of the same size. The Earth is denser than Saturn.
- **Core**: The centre of a planet. The Earth's core is made mostly of iron and nickel, and it is hot and very heavy. *e.g.* The Earth's inner core is solid iron and nickel, about as hot as the surface of the Sun.
- **Crater**: A bowl-shaped hole made when a rock from space hits a planet or moon. Most craters are round, whichever way the rock came from. *e.g.* The Moon's face is covered with craters. Lonar Lake in Maharashtra fills a crater about 1.8 kilometres wide.
- **Atmosphere**: The layer of gas, or air, held around a planet by the planet's gravity. A thick atmosphere can trap heat. A thin one lets heat escape. *e.g.* Venus has a very thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide. Mercury has almost none.
- **Greenhouse effect**: The way some gases in the air let sunlight in but slow down the heat trying to leave, so the planet's surface becomes warmer. *e.g.* The greenhouse effect warms the Earth by about 33 degrees Celsius. On Venus, it makes the ground hotter than an oven.
- **Ellipse**: A stretched circle, shaped like an oval. Every planet moves round the Sun on a path that is an ellipse, though for most planets it is nearly a circle. *e.g.* You get an ellipse if you tilt a round plate and look at it from the side.
- **Astronomical unit**: The average distance from the Earth to the Sun, which is about 150 million kilometres. Astronomers use it as a measuring stick for the solar system. It is written AU. *e.g.* Jupiter is about 5.2 astronomical units from the Sun. That is 5.2 times as far as the Earth.
- **Light travel time**: The time light takes to travel from one place to another. Radio signals from spacecraft travel at the same speed as light. Light needs about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to come from the Sun to the Earth. *e.g.* Sunlight takes about 4 hours to reach Neptune. A message to Mars takes between 3 and 22 minutes, depending on where Mars is.
- **Frost line**: The distance from the young Sun beyond which water could freeze into ice. Closer in, it was too warm and only rock and metal could be solid. *e.g.* Scientists think the frost line was somewhere between 3 and 5 times as far from the Sun as the Earth. Jupiter formed beyond it.
- **Rotation**: Spinning round on its own axis, like a top. One full rotation is a day. A day is a different length on each planet. *e.g.* Jupiter rotates once in just under 10 hours. Venus takes 243 Earth days to rotate once.
- **Magnetic field**: The invisible region round a planet where its magnetism can be felt. The Earth's magnetic field is made by its moving liquid iron core. It helps to turn aside the fast particles blowing out from the Sun. *e.g.* A compass needle points north because of the Earth's magnetic field.
- **Scale model**: A smaller copy of something in which every size or distance is made smaller by the same amount. A good model for the solar system must shrink sizes and distances together. *e.g.* A map is a scale model. In a model where the Sun is 14 centimetres wide, the Earth is a 1 millimetre grain 15 metres away.
- **Orbiter and rover**: An orbiter is a spacecraft that goes round a planet or moon. A rover is a robot vehicle that drives on the surface of another world. *e.g.* India's Mangalyaan was an orbiter at Mars. Curiosity and Perseverance are rovers that drive on Mars.

## Explanations
### Rocky planets and giant planets differ in more than size · *defined*
**Definition:** The four inner planets are small, dense balls of rock and metal with solid ground. The four outer planets are giants with no ground, made mostly of gases and the 'ices' of water, ammonia and methane.

Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars are small, because only rock and metal could stay solid close to the young Sun. Jupiter is about 11 times as wide as the Earth. About 1,300 Earths would fit inside it. Jupiter and Saturn are made mostly of hydrogen and helium, the same gases as the Sun. Uranus and Neptune hold a lot of water, ammonia and methane, and they are called ice giants. A giant has no surface to land on. Going down, the gas gets thicker and hotter until it is more like a hot fluid. When astronomers need a 'surface' for Jupiter, they choose the level where the air pressure equals the pressure at sea level on the Earth.
- e.g. A spacecraft sent into Jupiter's clouds cannot land. It is crushed and heated long before it could reach anything solid.
- e.g. Mars has volcanoes and canyons because it has solid ground. Olympus Mons, a volcano on Mars, is about 22 kilometres high, and Mount Everest is less than 9.
- e.g. All four giants have rings, though only Saturn's are bright and wide enough to see with a small telescope.
### Density: how heavy a planet is for its size · *defined*
**Definition:** Density is the mass of a thing divided by the space it takes up. Water has a density of 1 gram in every cubic centimetre. A planet with a density below 1 is lighter than the same amount of water.

Astronomers weigh a planet by seeing how strongly its gravity pulls on a moon or a passing spacecraft. They measure its size with a telescope or a camera. Dividing one by the other gives the density. The four rocky planets are all dense. The Earth's density is about 5.5, Mercury's is about 5.4, Venus's about 5.2 and Mars's about 3.9. The giants are light for their size. Jupiter's is about 1.3, Uranus's about 1.3 and Neptune's about 1.6. Saturn's is only about 0.7. So Saturn is lighter than water. If you could find a bathtub big enough, Saturn would float. The Earth is the densest planet in the solar system.
- e.g. Rock on the Earth's surface has a density of about 3. The whole Earth is denser, at about 5.5, so the middle of the Earth must be much denser than rock. It is iron.
- e.g. Jupiter weighs about 318 times as much as the Earth but takes up about 1,300 times the space, so it is far less dense.
- e.g. Mars is the least dense of the rocky planets. It is small, so its own gravity squeezes its rock less.
### Why the planets are two kinds: the frost line and the growth of the giants · *debated*
The planets formed in a flat, spinning disc of gas and dust round the young Sun. Close to the Sun it was warm, and only rock and metal could be solid, so the inner planets stayed small. Beyond the frost line, which was probably about 3 to 5 times as far as the Earth, water could freeze too. There was much more solid stuff to gather, so planets there grew larger. Astronomers agree on this much. They do not agree on how the giants then grew. One idea says a heavy core formed first and then pulled in gas. Another says a clump of the disc collapsed all at once. Both ideas explain some of what we see, and neither explains everything.
- e.g. Jupiter and Saturn are made of hydrogen and helium, the gases that fill most of the disc. Rocky planets could not hold on to light gases.
- e.g. Planets are now found round other stars too. Some giant planets orbit very close to their stars, which no model of our own solar system expected.
- e.g. Comets and asteroids are leftovers that never grew into planets. Comets are mostly ice and dust, and asteroids are mostly rock and metal.
- **Many planet scientists, including James Pollack and his team in 1996:** Core accretion. A core of rock and ice grows first, to several times the Earth's weight. Then its gravity pulls in gas from the disc and the planet swells into a giant. This fits Jupiter, which seems to have a heavy centre, and it explains why Uranus and Neptune have more ices.
- **Alan Boss, an American astronomer, in 1997:** Disc instability. A thick part of the disc may collapse under its own gravity, all at once, into a giant ball of gas. It could be faster than building a core first, which matters because the gas in a disc disappears within a few million years.
### Inside a rocky planet: a core, a mantle and a crust · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth is made of layers. At the centre is a core of iron and nickel, with a solid inner part and a liquid outer part. Round it is a thick layer of hot rock called the mantle. On the outside is a thin, cool crust.

The Earth's radius is about 6,371 kilometres. The deepest hole people have drilled goes down only about 12 kilometres, so we have never seen the inside. We know it from earthquakes. When the ground shakes, waves travel through the Earth and are picked up by instruments on the other side. Waves bend, speed up, slow down and stop depending on what they pass through. The solid inner core is about 1,220 kilometres in radius. The liquid outer core reaches out to about 3,480 kilometres. The moving metal in the outer core makes the Earth's magnetic field. When the heavy iron sank to the middle long ago, the lighter rock floated above it. That is why the Earth's inside is layered.
- e.g. A boiled egg is a rough model: the yolk is the core, the white is the mantle and the shell is the crust. The Earth's crust is far thinner compared with its size.
- e.g. Mars and Mercury also seem to have iron cores. Mercury's core is very large in proportion to the planet.
- e.g. The Earth's inner core is about as hot as the surface of the Sun, but it stays solid because of the huge pressure squeezing it.
### Craters work as a clock for the age of a surface · *defined*
**Definition:** A surface that has many craters has been exposed to space rocks for a long time, so it is old. A surface with few craters is young, because something has covered or wiped away the older ones.

Space rocks have been hitting the planets for 4.5 billion years. A rock hitting a world digs a bowl-shaped crater, and it is round whichever way the rock came in, because the energy of the hit spreads out in all directions. Bigger and faster rocks dig bigger craters. A new crater that overlaps an old one cuts through it, so you can tell which came first. On the Moon, the bright highlands are covered with craters and are more than 4 billion years old. The dark plains, which are hardened lava, have far fewer craters and are about 3 to 3.5 billion years old. Counting craters per area is how scientists date places they have never touched. The Earth has fewer than 200 known craters, because water, wind, plants and moving crust wipe them away.
- e.g. Mercury and the Moon are covered with craters because they have almost no air and no rain to wear them down.
- e.g. Venus has only about 1,000 craters, spread evenly. This tells us its whole surface is young, probably only a few hundred million years old.
- e.g. Mars has an old, heavily cratered south and a younger, smoother north.
### How old is Lonar Lake, the crater in Maharashtra? · *debated*
Lonar Lake lies near the town of Lonar in Maharashtra. It fills a round crater about 1.8 kilometres across, dug into hard volcanic rock called basalt. Basalt is the same kind of rock that makes the surface of much of the Moon's dark plains, which is why scientists study Lonar. The crater is the work of a space rock that hit long ago. The lake's salty green water and the thrown-out rock round its rim are still there. But when did it happen? Earlier studies suggested about 50,000 years ago. A later study using the rock's radioactive argon suggested around half a million years. The question matters because it shows how hard it is to date an impact even when the crater can be walked around.
- e.g. The tests are done on tiny grains of rock that were melted by the impact. When melted rock cools, its natural radioactive clock starts again from zero.
- e.g. Lonar is one of very few craters in the world dug into basalt, which makes it a useful practice ground for studying the Moon and Mars.
- e.g. Temples built around the lake are many centuries old, so people have known the lake for a very long time.
- **Earlier studies, from the 1970s:** The crater formed about 50,000 years ago. This number is still repeated in many books.
- **Fred Jourdan and his team, in 2011:** The crater is much older, around 570,000 years. They reached this by dating melted rock from the impact with a method that uses the radioactive gas argon.
### Atmospheres: the blanket that sets a planet's heat · *defined*
**Definition:** An atmosphere is the gas held round a planet by its gravity. Its thickness and its gases decide how much heat the planet keeps. The greenhouse effect is the trapping of heat by gases such as carbon dioxide.

Sunlight warms a planet's ground. The ground then sends the heat out again. Some gases, such as carbon dioxide and water vapour, slow this heat down on its way out, a bit like a blanket. Mercury has almost no air, so its day side reaches about 430 degrees Celsius and its night side falls to about minus 180. The Earth has a medium air blanket that keeps the average temperature at about 15 degrees Celsius. Without it, the Earth would average about minus 18. Mars has a very thin blanket, less than 1 percent of the Earth's pressure, so it is cold. Venus has a huge blanket, mostly carbon dioxide, pressing on the ground about 90 times harder than the Earth's air. That is why Venus, at about 460 degrees Celsius, is hotter than Mercury.
- e.g. The air in a closed car in the sun gets hotter than the air outside, because the glass lets sunlight in but slows the heat leaving.
- e.g. Spacecraft that landed on Venus lasted no more than about two hours before the heat and pressure stopped them.
- e.g. The Earth's own greenhouse effect makes life possible. Extra carbon dioxide from burning coal and oil makes it stronger and warms the planet more.
### Why Mars lost its air and water · *idea*
Mars has dry river valleys, lake beds and rocks that formed in water. So billions of years ago it was warmer and wetter, with thicker air. Today its air is thin and cold, and its water is mostly ice. Most scientists think Mars lost much of its air to space. Mars is small, so its gravity is weak and holds gas less tightly. Mars also lost its strong magnetic field early. Without that shield, the solar wind, a stream of fast particles from the Sun, could blow the air away bit by bit. NASA's MAVEN spacecraft, which reached Mars in 2014, measured the air escaping to space. It is a good idea backed by measurements, but scientists are still working out how much of the loss came from this and how much from other causes.
- e.g. The Perseverance rover is exploring a dry river delta in Jezero crater, where a lake once lay.
- e.g. Mars today has a pale sky and sometimes huge dust storms that cover the whole planet.
- e.g. Mangalyaan, India's orbiter, carried instruments to study Mars's thin outer air and to look for the gas methane.
### Orbits are ellipses, and a planet's year follows a rule · *defined*
**Definition:** Every planet moves round the Sun on an ellipse. A planet moves faster when it is closer to the Sun. Square a planet's year and you get the same number as when you multiply its distance by itself three times, with the year in Earth years and the distance in astronomical units.

For about 2,000 years, people believed the planets must move in perfect circles. In 1609, Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer, showed that Mars moves on an oval instead. A few years later he found the rule linking distance and year. For the Earth, both numbers are 1, so the rule is easy to check. Mars is 1.52 AU from the Sun. Multiply 1.52 three times and you get about 3.5. Mars's year is 1.88 Earth years. Multiply 1.88 by itself and you also get about 3.5. The rule works for every planet, and for asteroids, comets and planets round other stars. Isaac Newton later showed that it follows from gravity getting weaker with distance. A planet's year is not the same as its day. A year is one trip round the Sun. A day is one spin on its own axis.
- e.g. Ceres, the largest object in the asteroid belt, is 2.77 AU from the Sun. Cube 2.77 and you get about 21. A year of about 4.6 Earth years squared is also about 21. That is Ceres's real year.
- e.g. A day on Venus lasts 243 Earth days, longer than its year of 225 Earth days. Venus also spins backwards compared with most planets.
- e.g. Mercury spins on its axis three times for every two trips round the Sun. So from sunrise to sunrise on Mercury takes 176 Earth days.
### How big the solar system is: light-minutes and empty space · *defined*
**Definition:** Light and radio signals travel at about 300,000 kilometres every second. Even at that speed, crossing the solar system takes minutes to hours. Almost all of the solar system is empty space.

The Earth is 1 astronomical unit from the Sun, and light needs 8 minutes and 20 seconds to cross it. So the Sun you see is the Sun of 8 minutes ago. Light takes about 43 minutes to reach Jupiter, and about 4 hours to reach Neptune. A signal to Mars takes between 3 and 22 minutes, depending on where the two planets are in their orbits. A radio message to the Voyager 1 spacecraft, the farthest thing people have sent into space, takes close to a day. So a rover on Mars cannot be driven with a joystick from the Earth. It is given a plan, and it uses its own cameras and computer to avoid danger. In a model with the Sun as a 14-centimetre ball, the Earth is a mustard seed 15 metres away, and Neptune is about 450 metres away. Between the planets there is almost nothing.
- e.g. When Mangalyaan fired its main engine to enter orbit round Mars in 2014, the signal confirming it took about 12 minutes to reach the Earth.
- e.g. If the Sun were switched off now, we would see it shining for 8 more minutes and 20 seconds.
- e.g. In a 1 to 10 billion model, the nearest star after the Sun would be about 4,000 kilometres away, further than the length of India.

## Mostly agreed
- The four inner planets are small and rocky, and the four outer planets are giants with no solid ground. *How we know:* Spacecraft have landed on Venus and Mars and orbited every planet. Weighing each planet by the pull on its moons or on passing spacecraft, and measuring its size, gives its density, which separates rock from gas.
- Saturn is less dense than water, and the Earth is the densest planet. *How we know:* Saturn's mass and size have been measured by the Cassini spacecraft, which orbited it for 13 years. Dividing the mass by the volume gives about 0.7 grams in every cubic centimetre, against 1 for water.
- The more craters a surface has, the older it is. *How we know:* Rocks that Apollo astronauts brought back from places with many and few craters were dated by their natural radioactivity. The crowded highlands were much older than the smooth plains.
- The Earth has a solid inner core inside a liquid outer core. *How we know:* Earthquake waves that pass through the middle of the Earth behave as they would if a solid ball sat inside a liquid shell. This was first suggested in 1936 and later checked with better instruments, with clear proof in 1971.
- Venus is hotter than Mercury because of its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere. *How we know:* Spacecraft that landed on Venus measured about 460 degrees Celsius and an air pressure about 90 times the Earth's. Mercury, closer to the Sun, has almost no air to trap heat.
- For every planet, the square of its year equals the cube of its distance from the Sun, in Earth years and astronomical units. *How we know:* Kepler found the rule in 1619 from measurements by Tycho Brahe. It has been checked on every planet, on asteroids and comets, and on planets round other stars.
- Light and radio signals take minutes to hours to cross the solar system. *How we know:* The speed of light has been measured in laboratories on the Earth. Controllers watch signals from spacecraft arrive at exactly the times that this speed predicts.

## How we found out
**Inge Lehmann, a Danish seismologist (1888 to 1993), building on the work of Richard Oldham, 1936.** Nobody has ever dug to the middle of the Earth, so for a long time people could only guess what was there. In 1906 an English geologist, Richard Oldham, noticed that waves from earthquakes were blocked or bent deep inside the Earth. That showed there was a core. Scientists soon decided the whole core was a single ball of liquid metal. Then Inge Lehmann, a Danish scientist who looked after earthquake records in Copenhagen, studied a large earthquake from New Zealand in 1929. Some faint waves turned up in a place where a completely liquid core should have left none. In 1936 she published a very short paper with a bold idea: inside the liquid core there is a solid ball, and it bounces the waves. At first not everyone agreed. Better instruments later showed she was right.  
*What people believed before:* That the Earth's whole core was one ball of liquid metal. Lehmann showed that faint waves in the 'shadow zone' could only come from a solid ball in the middle of the liquid.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The planets must travel in perfect circles.** (Astronomers from ancient Greece until about 1600, including Aristotle, Ptolemy and Copernicus, and Kepler at first; *rejected*)  
  For: A circle seemed the most perfect shape, and the heavens were thought to be perfect. A circle also matches what we see for most planets, which move on paths very close to circles.  
  Against: Tycho Brahe measured the position of Mars so carefully that it was off the circle by about a quarter of the width of the Moon. Kepler could not ignore that, and in 1609 he showed that Mars moves on an oval.
- **Craters on the Moon are old volcano craters.** (Many scientists in the 1800s, and some into the early 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: The Moon has volcanic plains, and some volcanoes on the Earth have round craters.  
  Against: In 1893 Grove Karl Gilbert, an American geologist, argued that Moon craters were made by impacts, and he tested it by shooting clay balls into soft clay. Real volcano craters are mostly at the tops of cones, while Moon craters are shallow bowls with raised rims. In 1960 Eugene Shoemaker found impact-shocked rock at Meteor Crater in Arizona.
- **Mars has canals built by clever Martians.** (Percival Lowell, an American astronomer, in the 1890s and early 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: Giovanni Schiaparelli, an Italian astronomer, drew thin lines on Mars in 1877 and called them 'canali', the Italian word for channels. Lowell saw them too and drew many more.  
  Against: Better telescopes did not show the lines. In 1965 the Mariner 4 spacecraft flew past Mars and photographed a dry, cratered world with no canals. The lines came from the eye joining up faint marks.
- **Venus, the Earth's neighbour of nearly the same size, must be a warm, wet world with jungles.** (Many writers and some scientists in the early 1900s; *rejected*)  
  For: Venus is nearly the Earth's size and is covered in thick white clouds, which could hide swamps.  
  Against: In 1962 the Mariner 2 spacecraft measured Venus's surface as hotter than an oven. In 1970 the Soviet Venera 7 landed there and sent signals for about 23 minutes from the hot ground.
- **A gas giant is a ball of gas all the way down, with no solid centre at all.** (Some planet scientists, especially since the Juno spacecraft's results in 2017; *debated*)  
  For: Jupiter has no surface, and Juno's gravity measurements suggest a centre that is not a small, sharp ball but a fuzzy, spread-out mix of heavy elements and hydrogen.  
  Against: Before Juno, many models gave Jupiter a clear, dense core of rock and ice. Some models still do, and the data can be read more than one way.

## Roots
We have looked at craters, layers, air, orbits and the long delays of light. Now we ask what these tell us about what a world is, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the crater tray, each drop left a dent, and newer dents cut into older ones. So the dents told you which hit came first. The Moon and Mercury keep theirs for billions of years, but Earth's rain, wind and moving ground erase most. So a planet's face is a diary, and a lively planet keeps rewriting it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a world as a finished object that simply is the way it looks now. But a surface is a record of what has happened to a world, and of how much that world can wipe out and begin again.  
*Evidence:* In the tray, a later crater cut through an earlier one, so the order could be read. When you sifted flour over the tray, the older craters were softened first. On the Moon, the crowded highlands are more than 4 billion years old, while the Earth has fewer than 200 known craters.

- *who am i:* Your body also keeps a record and renews itself. A scar stays, but most of your skin is replaced over weeks. You live on a planet that does the same with its surface.
- *what is life:* A world with moving ground, water and living things wipes out its old craters. A living thing also rebuilds itself all the time. Neither the Earth nor a living body stays the same for long.
- *what is this world:* Every planet began from one cloud, and each has had a different history. The Moon kept its history. The Earth turned its into soil, seas and living things.

A planet is not only a place. It is something that has happened, and is still happening. The Moon is like a diary left open on a shelf, with every old entry still there. The Earth is more like a diary that someone keeps rewriting. Its rain smooths out its craters, its plates carry old rock down and push new rock up, and its living things take old material and make it into new forms. What looks like permanence on the Moon is only the lack of anything that changes it. What looks like loss on the Earth is the same energy, put to use again. A surface tells you how much its world is still alive.

- **Tirukkural 336:** The greatness of this world is that someone who was here yesterday is gone today. (The Earth wipes out its craters as it wipes out everything, and keeps changing. Only a world that stays still can keep its past.)
- **Dhammapada 277:** All conditioned things are impermanent. When one sees this with wisdom, one turns away from suffering. (Even a planet is not permanent. The Moon's craters last a long time, but the Sun, the planets and the Moon itself had a beginning, and will have an end.)
- **Rig Veda 10.129.6:** Who really knows, and who can say here, where this creation came from? (Scientists have a good idea of how the planets formed, but they still argue about the details, such as how the giants grew. The hymn asks the question in the same spirit.)
- Science (settled): The more craters a surface has, the older it is. Counting craters is a main way to date planets and moons.
- Science (debated): Venus's surface is young, but whether it was renewed all at once or slowly is not agreed.
- Science (open): Whether life exists anywhere else in the solar system, such as under the surface of Mars, is not yet known.
- Other view, Some planetary geologists: A crater is only a hole made by the laws of physics. Saying that a world 'remembers' or 'forgets' puts a human idea on a lump of rock. The Moon has no history in any deeper sense than a pebble has. A diary needs a writer and a reader, and rock has neither.
- Practice, Leave a mark and watch it fade (3 min): Find a dusty shelf or a window ledge. Press one finger gently into the dust and make a clear dent. Look at it for a few breaths. Now think of the Moon, where a dent like this stays for billions of years. Then think of the Earth, where wind and rain would wipe it away. Breathe in slowly three times. Notice that you are standing on a world that keeps changing.
- Ask: When a world wipes out its craters, is it forgetting, or is it staying alive?
- Ask: If you could read the Earth's face like the Moon's, what would it tell you about the last billion years?
- Ask: Is a diary that cannot be rewritten more honest than one that can?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Venus is almost the same size as the Earth, and yet it is nothing like the Earth. What small differences can make two worlds so different? (hint: Think about the air. Venus has a very thick blanket of carbon dioxide and the Earth has a thin one. Where did each get its air?)
- *deep:* Saturn would float in water. Is heaviness something a thing has, or does it depend on how the thing is packed? (hint: Think of a steel nut and a steel ship. Both are made of steel. One floats and one sinks.)
- *big:* The Moon keeps every dent for billions of years, while the Earth wipes most of its dents away. Is a world that erases its past more alive than one that keeps it? (hint: Think about what wipes away the Earth's craters: water, wind, plants and moving ground. Are those things alive?)
- *deep:* The light of the Sun reaches us 8 minutes after it leaves. When you look at the Sun, is it 'now' there? (hint: A letter takes days to arrive and is already old when you read it. What is different about the light?)
- *big:* In a model with the Sun as a 14-centimetre ball, the nearest star is 4,000 kilometres away. What is all that space between the stars for? (hint: Is empty space really empty? What can pass through it, and what cannot?)
- *big:* We know of only one planet with life. Is the Earth lucky, or would many worlds like ours happen anyway? (hint: How would you find out with just one example? What would you want to count?)
- *deep:* Some scientists once said Jupiter's gravity protects the Earth from comets. Others say it also throws some towards us. How could you test either idea? (hint: You cannot run it again. What could you do instead, using computer models or craters?)

## Story: Twelve Minutes Late
*Kavya, her Thatha and her Amma are made up. The Mars Orbiter Mission, Mangalyaan, is real. It went into orbit round Mars on 24 September 2014, on India's first try. Its main engine burned for about 24 minutes. The signal confirming this took about 12 minutes to reach the Earth. Exact times in the story are rounded. Thatha's work as a telegraph operator is imagined.*

For a week, Kavya's mother had not slept at home. Amma was an engineer at the space centre in Bengaluru, and the whole team was waiting for one morning. Kavya was nine, and she had stuck a poster of Mars on the wall above her bed.

On that morning she sat in front of the television with her grandfather, Thatha, at six-thirty. Thatha had worked all his life as a telegraph operator, tapping out messages for the railways. He drank his tea slowly and said nothing, which was how he showed he was excited.

A man on the news was speaking. 'Within the hour, Mangalyaan will fire its big engine,' he said. 'It must slow the spacecraft down just enough for Mars to catch it. The engine has not been used for ten months. The burn will last twenty-four minutes.'

'Twenty-four minutes,' said Kavya. 'That is a long time to hold your breath. Thatha, why can't Amma's team watch and press stop, if something goes wrong?'

Thatha put down his glass. 'Ah. I know about that. In my day, a message from Madras to Delhi was not just sent. It was passed, station to station. By the time Delhi answered, the news was old.' He looked at her over his glasses. 'Space is a very long telegraph line, my dear. How long does a message take to reach Mars?'

Kavya did not know. But she had a pencil and the newspaper. Her teacher had told her that light travels three lakh kilometres every second. The man on the television had said Mars was about two hundred and twenty-five million kilometres away that day.

She wrote it as a long division and crossed out the zeros, the way she had been taught. Two hundred and twenty-five million divided by three lakh came to seven hundred and fifty. 'Seven hundred and fifty seconds,' she whispered. She counted on her fingers. 'That is twelve and a half minutes.'

Thatha raised his eyebrows. 'So a message takes twelve and a half minutes to get there. And the answer takes twelve and a half minutes to come back.'

Kavya sat up very straight. 'That is twenty-five minutes. The burn is only twenty-four. If something went wrong in the first minute, Amma would not even see it for twelve minutes, and her stop message would take twelve minutes more. The burn would be over before it arrived.'

'So?' said Thatha, smiling.

'So Mangalyaan has to do it all by itself,' said Kavya slowly. 'They have to tell it everything in advance. It has to decide for itself what to do. Nobody can drive it.' She looked at the television. The man had gone quiet, and a small clock was counting down.

Then the small clock on the screen reached zero. Nobody in the room moved. 'It has started,' whispered Kavya. Thatha shook his head gently. 'No, child. We are looking at old news. The burn began twelve minutes ago. In fact, it may be nearly over.'

Kavya stared at him. Somewhere out there, the engine had already fired. It had either worked or it had not. The answer already existed, travelling towards the Earth like a letter in the post. All the people at the space centre, her mother among them, could do nothing but wait for it to arrive.

They waited for twenty-four more minutes. Thatha's tea went cold. Then the people on the screen jumped to their feet and clapped. The signal had come. Mangalyaan was in orbit round Mars. Kavya screamed, and Thatha, who never raised his voice, laughed out loud.

Amma phoned at nine, hoarse and happy. 'We waited over half an hour for something that had already happened,' she said. 'Did you know the Earth and Mars have a delay?' 'Twelve and a half minutes, Amma,' said Kavya. 'I worked it out on the newspaper.' There was a pause. 'Did you?' said her mother.

Later, Kavya stood at the window. The Sun was high over the neem tree. She remembered that its light, too, had left it eight minutes ago. She waved at it anyway.

**For the parent:** For the parent: light and radio signals travel fast but not instantly. Across the solar system they take minutes to hours, so a message to Mars is old when it arrives, and a spacecraft far away must act on its own. Kavya works out the delay by dividing a distance by a speed. The idea in the story's turn is that the burn had already succeeded or failed before anyone on the Earth could know. What we see of a faraway place is always a record of the past.

- Ask: Why could Amma's team not simply press stop if the engine went wrong?
- Ask: How did Kavya work out that a message to Mars takes about twelve and a half minutes?
- Ask: When Thatha said 'We are looking at old news', what did he mean?
- Ask: Why is the Sun you see through the window always a little in the past?

### e1 · Crater Tray: what do dents in flour tell us about a world? (35 min)
- **question:** When a marble is dropped from higher up, how much wider is the crater it digs? And when one crater lands on another, how can you tell which came first?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. If you drop a marble from 8 times as high, will the crater be 8 times as wide, about twice as wide, or only a little wider? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same marble, the same tray, and flour of the same depth, smoothed flat before every drop. Drop the marble gently from a measured height, never throw it. Only the drop height changes, so any change in the crater comes from the height. Drop three times from each height, because one drop can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Flour dust can make you cough, so do not blow on the tray. Work away from a fan. Keep marbles away from children under five, who might swallow them. Wheat flour is a problem for anyone allergic to wheat or gluten: use rice flour or fine dry sand instead, and wash hands afterwards. If the flour is damp or lumpy, dry it in the sun and sift it first. Do not eat the flour or cocoa from the tray.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then draw a bar chart with one bar for each height, using the average width. Sketch the three overlapping craters and write which came first.
- **results table:** Drop height (cm) | Width of crater 1 (cm) | Width of crater 2 (cm) | Width of crater 3 (cm) | Average width (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the tray, the marble and the ruler. Ask for the guess and the reason. Write both down at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* The marble is a rock from space. The flour is the ground of a planet. If I drop the marble from 8 times as high, how much wider will the crater be? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The marble is a space rock. If you drop it from 8 times higher, how wide is the crater? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think the crater will be that much wider? (good: A faster rock hits harder, so it digs more.; Eight times as high should make a crater eight times as wide.; The crater will not change much, because the marble is the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say 8 times as wide. That is a natural idea, and the tray will answer it.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the tray on newspaper. Pour in flour to 3 centimetres deep. Shake the tray gently, and smooth the top with the edge of the ruler. Dust a thin layer of cocoa powder over it with the strainer.  
  *Parent says:* This is our planet's ground. We make it flat and smooth. The dark cocoa on top will show where the flour is thrown out.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the tray with flour. Make it flat. Sprinkle a little cocoa on top.
  - Note: If the flour feels damp, spread it on a plate in the sun for an hour first. Damp flour makes steep, sticky craters that fall in. The layer should look like fresh snow, with no marks on it.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand a metre scale beside the tray. Hold the marble at 10 centimetres above the flour and let go. Lay the ruler across the crater and read its width from rim to rim. Lift the marble out with a spoon. Smooth the flour, dust it again, and repeat. Do 3 drops at 10 centimetres, then 3 at 20, 3 at 40 and 3 at 80.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the marble high, then let it go. Do not push it. Read the width across the middle. Write the number down. Then smooth the flour again.  
  *Child mode:* Drop the marble from 10 centimetres. Measure the crater. Smooth the flour. Do it three times.
  - Note: Working alone with a grown-up: you drop and measure, they smooth the flour and write the numbers. With 2 to 4 children: one drops, one measures, one smooths and dusts, one writes, and swap after each height; each pair of children can run its own tray if you have two. Expect the craters to grow slowly. At 10 centimetres you may see about 3 centimetres wide, and at 80 centimetres about 4.5. Your numbers may differ by a centimetre or so. What matters is the pattern. Typical results only: your numbers may differ, and a damp tray or a different marble will change them. Measure from the top of the rim on one side to the top of the rim on the other.
  - Check: A marble dropped from 8 times as high made a crater only about one and a half times as wide. What does this tell you? → **Crater width grows with the speed of the hit, but much more slowly**
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Smooth and dust the flour again. Turn your back. Your grown-up drops the marble three times in three nearby spots from the same height, so each crater overlaps the one before. They keep the order secret. Now turn round and look at the craters. Decide which came first, second and third.  
  *Parent says:* I made three craters, one after another, and they overlap. Look at the edges. Where one crater cuts into another, which is newer? Which is the oldest?  
  *Child mode:* Your grown-up makes three overlapping craters in secret. Look at the edges. Which came first?
  - Note: Make the second crater overlap the edge of the first and the third overlap the edge of the second. Do not hide the marble in your fist. Tell your child the true order after they decide.
  - Check: Crater A has a complete round rim. Crater B's rim cuts into A, so a piece of A's rim is missing. Which crater is older? → **Crater A, because B cut into it**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take the sieve and shake a thin layer of fresh flour over half of the tray, to be 'weather'. Leave the other half alone. Compare the craters in the two halves.  
  *Parent says:* Half of this tray has had weather. Look at the craters in each half. Which craters are sharper? Which are softer? What could the Earth do that the Moon cannot?  
  *Child mode:* Sift some flour over half the tray. Look at both halves. Which craters are sharper?
  - Ask: On the Earth, what kinds of weather wipe out craters? (good: Rain washes the sides down.; Wind carries dust that fills them in.; Plants grow in them and rivers fill them up.)
  - Note: The craters under the flour layer will look softer and shallower, and the oldest craters will fade first. This is the same reason the Earth has so few craters and the Moon has so many.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see in the tray? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a faster rock digs a wider crater, but only a little wider, and the order of craters can be read. What I saw: from 10 centimetres the average was about 3 centimetres and from 80 centimetres about 4.5. A crater with a cut rim was older. Why: a hit throws flour out in all directions, and a later hit destroys whatever is in its way. The Moon has no weather, so it keeps every crater. Counting craters is how scientists date a surface.
### e2 · Two Tumblers in the Sun: does a lid make a hotter world? (35 min)
- **question:** Does ice melt faster in a tumbler with a clear lid than in an open tumbler, when both sit in the same sunshine?

- **safety:** A grown-up comes along if the test is on a balcony or terrace. Keep tumblers away from the edge. Do not touch a tumbler that has been in strong sun without checking that it is not hot. Plastic tumblers are safer than glass. Never look straight at the Sun.

  *Parent does:* Show your child the two tumblers and the film. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.

  *Child mode:* One tumbler is covered. One is open. Which ice melts first? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will melt first? (good: The lid keeps the heat in.; The open one has more air going past it.; They will melt at the same time, because the sun is the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the two tumblers on the black paper. Put three ice cubes in each. Stretch cling film over one tumbler. Hold it with a rubber band. Carry both to the sunny spot together.  
  *Parent says:* The black paper soaks up sunlight, so the tumblers get a good share of it. The film is our blanket of air. Carry them out together, side by side.  
  *Child mode:* Put ice in both tumblers. Cover one with film. Carry both into the sun.
  - Note: Take the ice straight from the freezer and set up quickly, so the ice has not already started melting. Place the tumblers an arm's length apart, so one does not shade the other. Working alone: you watch both tumblers and your grown-up keeps time. With 2 to 4 children: one child looks after each tumbler, one keeps the clock and one writes. Two tumblers are enough for any group.
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Start the timer. Every 5 minutes, look at both tumblers. Write down how much ice is left in each: all, half, little or none. Keep going until the ice in both has melted, or until 30 minutes have passed.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely at both tumblers. How much ice is left in each? Write it down. We check again in 5 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Every 5 minutes, look at both. How much ice is left? Write it down.
  - Note: Expect the ice in the covered tumbler to melt a little sooner, often by a few minutes. If both seem equal, repeat the test with 4 cubes each, or on a hotter, sunnier day. A cloudy or rainy day will not work, so choose a bright day around noon. Droplets inside the film are normal. If the covered ice melts later, say so honestly, and check that the film did not touch the ice or shade it. If there is no direct sun today, try another day. The result can be small, and a small difference is still a finding.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If you have a fever thermometer, wait until the ice has gone. Then push its tip into the air space of each tumbler. Read the number and write it down. Then talk about what you saw.  
  *Parent says:* Which melted first? How much sooner? Is that enough to be sure, or could it be a fluke? How could we find out?  
  *Child mode:* Which ice melted first? By how many minutes? Could we try it again?
  - Note:  A digital fever thermometer reads only above about 32 degrees Celsius. Stop if it shows HI.
  - Check: The ice in the covered tumbler melted sooner. What is the best reason? → **The film kept the warm air from escaping, so the air inside got hotter**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write what I think, what I saw, and why. Then tell your child how this is like Venus.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Venus is like the covered tumbler, with a thick blanket of air round it.  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a cover makes things warmer. What I saw: the covered tumbler's ice melted first, by a few minutes (your gap may be larger or smaller). Why: the film stopped warm air from leaving. Be honest that this is only a model. A lid stops warm air from rising away. On Venus, the gas carbon dioxide slows down heat rays on their way out. The two work differently in detail, but both act like a blanket.

## Worksheets
### Rock, Gas and Craters (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is true of all four rocky planets, and of none of the four giants?  
   Options: They have solid ground you could stand on / They have rings / They are made mostly of hydrogen and helium / They are all further from the Sun than Jupiter  
   **Answer:** They have solid ground you could stand on. *Why:* Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars have solid surfaces of rock and metal. The giants have no ground, because they are mostly gas and fluid. Picking 'rings' is a good thought, but all four giants have rings and no rocky planet does. That makes it a feature of giants, not of rocky planets.
2. (sort) Sort the eight planets into rocky planets, gas giants and ice giants.  
   **Answer:** Mercury → Rocky planet; Venus → Rocky planet; Earth → Rocky planet; Mars → Rocky planet; Jupiter → Gas giant; Saturn → Gas giant; Uranus → Ice giant; Neptune → Ice giant. *Why:* The four nearest the Sun are rocky. Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen and helium. Uranus and Neptune hold much more water, ammonia and methane, so they are called ice giants. A strong answer knows that the 'ice' in an ice giant is hot and fluid inside.
3. (truefalse) Saturn is so dense for its size that it would sink if you could drop it into a huge bath of water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Saturn's density is about 0.7, which is less than water's 1. It would float. A strong answer says Saturn is mostly light gas, so it is not heavy for its size.
4. (fill) Density tells you how heavy a thing is for its ____.  
   **Answer:** ['size']. *Why:* Density is the mass of a thing divided by the space it takes up. A steel nut is dense because it is heavy for its small size. A big balloon is not dense, because it is light for its big size.
5. (mcq) Planet X has a mass of 30 units and takes up 10 units of space. Planet Y has a mass of 6 units and takes up 10 units of space. Which is more likely to be a gas giant, and why?  
   Options: Planet Y, because it is lighter for its size / Planet X, because it is heavier / Planet X, because it has more mass / You cannot tell without knowing the colours  
   **Answer:** Planet Y, because it is lighter for its size. *Why:* Planet X has a density of 3 and Planet Y has a density of 0.6. A gas giant is light for its size, so it is Planet Y. Picking X because it is heavier compares mass only. A giant planet can have a huge mass and still be low in density.
6. (write) A round world has a density below 1, which is the density of water. Explain what this tells you about what it is made of.  
   **Answer:** Rock and metal are much denser than water, so a world lighter than water cannot be mostly rock. It must be made mostly of light gases such as hydrogen and helium, or of light ices.. *Why:* A strong answer links density to material. Rock is about 3 and iron about 8, both far above 1. Only gas or light ice can bring the average below 1.
7. (mcq) One region of the Moon has about 300 craters in a patch. A smooth plain next to it has about 30 craters in a patch of the same size. What does this suggest?  
   Options: The crowded region is older than the smooth plain / The smooth plain is older than the crowded region / Both regions are the same age / The crowded region has more volcanoes  
   **Answer:** The crowded region is older than the smooth plain. *Why:* Space rocks hit the Moon steadily over billions of years. A surface that has been exposed longer collects more craters. The smooth plain was covered by lava later, which wiped out its older craters. Picking 'smooth is older' mixes up smooth with ancient.
8. (order) Crater C cuts into the rim of crater B. Crater B cuts into the rim of crater A. Put the craters in order, oldest first.  
   **Answer:** ['Crater A', 'Crater B', 'Crater C']. *Why:* A crater that cuts into another one must have come later. So A is the oldest, B came next, and C is the newest. The order can be read from the shapes alone, without any dates.
9. (truefalse) Most craters are round, even when the space rock hits the ground at a slant.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A fast space rock puts so much energy into a small spot that it acts like an explosion, and the explosion spreads out in all directions. Only rocks that skim in at a very low angle dig an oval crater.
10. (mcq) Venus is further from the Sun than Mercury, yet Venus is the hotter planet. Which is the best reason?  
   Options: Venus has a very thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide that traps heat / Venus spins faster than Mercury / Venus is bigger than Mercury / Venus is nearer the Earth  
   **Answer:** Venus has a very thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide that traps heat. *Why:* Venus's thick atmosphere acts like a blanket and slows the heat escaping. Mercury has almost no atmosphere, so its heat escapes quickly. Picking 'bigger' or 'spins faster' does not explain heat. Picking 'nearer the Earth' is not a reason at all.
11. (match) Match each planet to its air.  
   **Answer:** Mercury → almost no air; Venus → very thick air, mostly carbon dioxide; Earth → medium air, mostly nitrogen and oxygen; Mars → very thin air, mostly carbon dioxide. *Why:* The thickness of the air sets how much heat a planet keeps. A strong answer says why Venus is hot, Mars is cold, and the Earth is mild.
12. (write) Mars has dry river valleys and lake beds. What does this tell you about Mars long ago, and what might have changed?  
   **Answer:** Liquid water once flowed on Mars, so it was warmer and had thicker air. Today the air is thin and cold. Most scientists think Mars lost much of its air to space, because it is small and lost its magnetic field.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the valleys as evidence. It also says that the loss of air is the best idea so far, not a settled fact.
13. (label) Label the layers of the Earth: the solid inner core, the liquid outer core, the mantle and the crust.  
   **Answer:** From the middle outwards: the solid inner core, the liquid outer core, the mantle, the crust.. *Why:* The core is iron and nickel. The mantle is hot rock and is the thickest layer. The crust is thin compared with the whole planet. A strong answer puts the layers in order from the middle.
14. (perspective) Two friends in Madurai, Hari and Sana, talk about the Moon's craters.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason from the craters. For Hari: the craters record the order of hits, as the overlapping craters in the tray did. For Sana: physics made the craters, and nobody wrote them down on purpose. Each friend has a true point.. *Why:* Both are using the word 'diary' in different ways. Hari means a record that can be read. Sana means something that is written by a writer. Both are correct about what they mean.
15. (draw) Draw a young, hot planet in two pictures. In the first, the iron and the rock are mixed. In the second, the heavy iron has sunk to the middle.  
   **Answer:** Picture one shows a ball with mixed dots of dark iron and light rock. Picture two shows a dark iron core at the middle with a layer of rock round it.. *Why:* A young rocky planet was partly melted, so the heavy iron could sink to the middle under gravity. A strong drawing shows the dense core and the lighter rock above it.
16. (mcq) If the Sun suddenly switched off right now, what would people on the Earth see for the next 8 minutes?  
   Options: The Sun would still shine, because its light is still arriving / The Sun would go dark at once / The Sun would get dimmer a bit at a time / The Sun would turn red  
   **Answer:** The Sun would still shine, because its light is still arriving. *Why:* Light from the Sun needs about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach the Earth. The light already on its way keeps arriving. Picking 'dark at once' forgets that light takes time to travel.
### Orbits, Light and Evidence (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A new planet is found 4 times as far from its Sun-like star as the Earth is from the Sun. Using the rule that the year squared equals the distance cubed, how long is its year?  
   Options: 8 Earth years / 4 Earth years / 16 Earth years / 2 Earth years  
   **Answer:** 8 Earth years. *Why:* The distance cubed is 4 times 4 times 4, which is 64. The year times itself must also be 64, and 8 times 8 is 64. So the year is 8 Earth years. Picking 4 assumes the year grows in step with the distance, which the planet data showed is not true.
2. (fill) A planet is 9 times as far from its star as the Earth is from the Sun. The distance cubed is 729. Its year, in Earth years, is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['27']. *Why:* The year times itself must also be 729. Trying numbers, 27 times 27 is 729. So its year is 27 Earth years. A strong answer shows the guess and check: 20 times 20 is 400, which is too small, and 30 times 30 is 900, which is too big.
3. (mcq) A rover on Mars sends a photo that takes 14 minutes to reach the Earth. Mission Control replies at once. How long after the photo was taken does the reply arrive at Mars?  
   Options: About 28 minutes / About 14 minutes / About 7 minutes / At once  
   **Answer:** About 28 minutes. *Why:* The photo takes 14 minutes to come to the Earth, and the reply takes 14 minutes to go back. 14 plus 14 is 28 minutes. Picking 14 forgets that the message has to travel both ways.
4. (write) Explain why the Sun you see in the sky is always a little in the past.  
   **Answer:** Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach the Earth. So the light you see left the Sun that long ago. You are seeing the Sun as it was 8 minutes ago.. *Why:* A strong answer connects speed, distance and time. The light is fast, but the Sun is very far away.
5. (truefalse) On Venus, a day (one full spin) lasts longer than a year (one trip round the Sun).  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Venus spins once in 243 Earth days and goes round the Sun in 225 Earth days. A strong answer says that a day and a year are different things, and one is not always longer than the other.
6. (order) Put these in order from the shortest year to the longest year: Saturn, Mercury, Mars, Earth, Jupiter.  
   **Answer:** ['Mercury', 'Earth', 'Mars', 'Jupiter', 'Saturn']. *Why:* The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer its year. So the order of years is the same as the order of distance: Mercury, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn.
7. (mcq) In a flour tray, a marble dropped from 4 times as high lands. What happens to the width of the crater?  
   Options: It is wider, but by much less than 4 times / It is exactly 4 times as wide / It is the same width / It is narrower  
   **Answer:** It is wider, but by much less than 4 times. *Why:* A faster hit digs a bigger crater, but crater width grows slowly. In the tray test, a drop from 8 times as high made a crater only about one and a half times as wide. Picking 'exactly 4 times' is the natural guess that the numbers did not support.
8. (perspective) Two scientists talk about how the giant planets grew.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks an idea and says what evidence would test it. For example, if Jupiter has a solid heavy core, that fits the first idea. It also names a strength of the other: the gas in a disc disappears within a few million years, so a faster way to build a giant is attractive.. *Why:* Both ideas explain part of what we see, and scientists have not settled it. It is good science to say what would count as evidence for each.
9. (match) Match each question about a planet to the way scientists find out.  
   **Answer:** What is the inside of the Earth like? → waves from earthquakes; How dense is a planet? → its pull on moons or spacecraft, and its size; How old is a surface? → counting its craters; How far away is a spacecraft? → how long its radio signal takes. *Why:* Each way uses something we can measure from far away. A strong answer could say why we can use waves for the Earth but not for Jupiter.
10. (mcq) In a scale model where 1 millimetre stands for 10,000 kilometres, the Earth is 15 metres from the Sun. Mars is about 1.5 times as far. About how far from the Sun is Mars in the model?  
   Options: About 23 metres / About 1.5 metres / About 150 metres / About 15 metres  
   **Answer:** About 23 metres. *Why:* 15 metres times 1.5 is 22.5 metres, which is about 23 metres. Picking 15 forgets to multiply. Picking 150 puts the decimal point in the wrong place.
11. (write) A spacecraft measures a new round world. How could two measurements, mass and size, tell you whether the world is rocky or a gas giant?  
   **Answer:** Divide the mass by the volume to find its density. If the density is high, like 4 or 5, the world is made of rock and metal. If it is low, like 1 or less, it is mostly gas or ice.. *Why:* A strong answer names density and links it to what the world is made of. Mass alone does not tell you, because gas giants have huge masses.
12. (truefalse) A spacecraft could land on the solid surface of Jupiter if it were strong enough.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Jupiter has no solid surface. Going down, the gas gets thicker, hotter and more squeezed, until it becomes a hot fluid. Astronomers choose a level where the pressure matches the Earth's sea level to call the 'surface', but this is a choice for measuring.
13. (label) Label this picture of the young solar system: the Sun, the frost line, the rocky planets and the giant planets.  
   **Answer:** The Sun at the middle. The rocky planets just inside the frost line. The frost line as a dotted circle further out. The giant planets outside the frost line.. *Why:* Inside the frost line it was too warm for ice, so only rock and metal gathered, and small planets formed. Outside, ice also gathered, so planets grew bigger. A strong answer puts the rocky planets inside and the giants outside.
14. (draw) Draw sunlight reaching Venus, and the heat from its ground trying to escape. Show what its thick air does to the heat.  
   **Answer:** Yellow arrows of sunlight come in through a thick layer of air. Orange arrows of heat go up from the ground, but many bounce back down from the air layer, so the ground gets hotter.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the air slowing the heat down on its way out, like a blanket. The sunlight gets in more easily than the heat gets out.
15. (sort) Sort these clues into the ones that point to an old surface and the ones that point to a young surface.  
   **Answer:** Crowded with overlapping craters → Old surface; Very few craters, spread evenly → Young surface; Smooth lava plain with few craters → Young surface; Highlands full of big and small craters → Old surface; Land that is often renewed by moving crust and weather → Young surface. *Why:* A surface collects craters as time goes by. A crowded surface has been exposed to space rocks for a long time. A surface with few craters has been renewed or covered. A strong answer says why renewal matters.
16. (mcq) Without any greenhouse effect at all, about what would the Earth's average temperature be?  
   Options: About minus 18 degrees Celsius / About 15 degrees Celsius / About 100 degrees Celsius / About 460 degrees Celsius  
   **Answer:** About minus 18 degrees Celsius. *Why:* With the natural greenhouse effect the Earth averages about 15 degrees Celsius. The effect adds about 33 degrees, so without it the average would be about minus 18. Picking 460 is Venus's temperature.
17. (fill) A planet circles a Sun-like star once every 8 Earth years. Its distance from the star, in astronomical units, is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['4']. *Why:* The year squared is 64. The distance cubed must be 64, and 4 times 4 times 4 is 64. So the planet is 4 astronomical units from its star, as far as four Earth-Sun distances.
### Evidence and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (perspective) Three people talk about Jupiter and the Earth.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one view and backs it with an idea about gravity: a big planet pulls comets strongly, so they can be redirected either way. It also gives another person's view its due. Shalini's point stands in every case, because models depend on starting numbers.. *Why:* All three have a point, and scientists do not agree. The question is which comets the planet changes and in which direction. This kind of disagreement is how science moves forward.
2. (mcq) Light takes about 8 minutes to cross 1 astronomical unit. A spacecraft is 30 astronomical units from the Earth, as far as Neptune is from the Sun. About how long does a radio message take to travel one way to it?  
   Options: About 4 hours / About 4 minutes / About 30 minutes / About 1 day  
   **Answer:** About 4 hours. *Why:* 30 times 8 minutes is 240 minutes, which is 4 hours. Picking 4 minutes forgets to multiply by 30. A reply would take another 4 hours, so a question and its answer take about 8 hours.
3. (write) Mercury is closer to the Sun than Venus is, yet Venus is the hotter planet. Use the idea of a blanket to explain why.  
   **Answer:** Venus has a very thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide that slows the heat leaving the ground, like a blanket. Mercury has almost no air, so its heat escapes quickly at night. A planet's temperature depends on how much heat it keeps as well as how much it gets.. *Why:* A strong answer says that temperature is the balance between heat coming in and heat getting out, and that air changes how fast heat gets out.
4. (mcq) Mars has many more craters than the Earth. What is the best reason?  
   Options: Mars has far less rain and no moving plates to wipe them out / Mars is closer to the asteroid belt, so only Mars was hit / The Earth was never hit by space rocks / Mars is made of a softer rock  
   **Answer:** Mars has far less rain and no moving plates to wipe them out. *Why:* The Earth was hit as much as Mars, but its water, wind, living things and moving crust wipe out craters. Mars has little of these. Picking 'the Earth was never hit' is wrong, because the Earth has about 190 known craters.
5. (truefalse) If the Earth had no water, no wind and no moving crust, it would be covered in craters like the Moon.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Earth has been hit by space rocks as often as the Moon has, or more, because it is bigger. The craters are missing only because the Earth keeps wiping them away. A strong answer names the things that wipe them.
6. (write) A book shows the Sun and eight planets in a neat row with equal gaps, all the same size on the page. Explain what is misleading about it.  
   **Answer:** The planets are much smaller than the Sun, and they are very far apart. The inner planets are close together and the outer planets are spread far apart. In a true scale model, the planets would be tiny specks metres apart.. *Why:* A strong answer names both the size and the distance. A book page cannot show both on one scale, so most books give up one.
7. (draw) Draw a scale model of the Sun and the Earth side by side, with the sizes and the distance between them.  
   **Answer:** A disc 14 centimetres wide for the Sun and a dot 1 millimetre wide for the Earth, 15 metres away. A break mark shows the long gap.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the Earth as a tiny speck and the gap as very long. If your drawing has the Earth close to the Sun, check the 15 metres.
8. (mcq) A spacecraft measures a round world with a density of 0.8. What is the best conclusion?  
   Options: It is made mostly of light gas or ice, not mostly of rock and metal / It is made of solid iron / It is certainly the Earth's twin / It has no mass  
   **Answer:** It is made mostly of light gas or ice, not mostly of rock and metal. *Why:* Rock and metal are far denser than water, which has a density of 1. A world with a density of 0.8 must be made of light stuff such as gas, with little rock. Picking iron would give a density near 8.
9. (sort) Sort these statements into things that were measured and things that are still an idea or an argument.  
   **Answer:** Saturn's density is about 0.7 → Measured; Venus's ground is about 460 degrees Celsius → Measured; Mars lost its air mainly to the solar wind → Still an idea or an argument; Jupiter has a fuzzy centre → Still an idea or an argument; Lonar crater is 50,000 years old → Still an idea or an argument. *Why:* Measurements can be repeated and checked. An idea is a best explanation that is still being tested. A strong answer says why the Lonar age is argued about, when two methods give different answers.
10. (mcq) A probe drops into Jupiter's clouds and sinks deeper. What happens to the pressure and the temperature around it?  
   Options: Both rise, until the gas becomes hot, thick fluid / Both fall, until it reaches a cold surface / The pressure rises and the temperature falls / Nothing changes, since there is no ground  
   **Answer:** Both rise, until the gas becomes hot, thick fluid. *Why:* Deeper in a giant planet there is more gas pressing down, so the pressure and the temperature rise. There is no solid ground to land on. Picking 'both fall' pictures a surface at the bottom, as on a rocky planet.
11. (write) Two methods date the Lonar crater and give 50,000 and 570,000 years. What should a careful scientist do before saying how old it is?  
   **Answer:** Test more samples, use more than one method, check where each method could go wrong, and report the range honestly until they agree.. *Why:* A strong answer does not just pick the newer number or the older one. It says that disagreement is information, and describes more tests.
12. (truefalse) Radio signals from a spacecraft travel faster than light, so there is no delay in talking to it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Radio waves are a kind of light and travel at the same speed, about 300,000 kilometres a second. Nothing is known to travel faster. So the delay is real, and for far spacecraft it can be hours.
13. (write) A friend says the planets are called wanderers because they move about with no pattern. Use what you know about orbits to show your friend that they follow a rule.  
   **Answer:** Every planet moves on an oval path round the Sun, faster when closer. The square of its year equals the cube of its distance, for every planet. So the planets wander across the stars, but by a rule that can be used to predict where they will be.. *Why:* A strong answer says why they look as if they wander: they move against the fixed stars. It then uses the year-and-distance rule as proof of a pattern.
14. (truefalse) The 'ices' inside Uranus and Neptune are frozen solid, like the ice in a freezer.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Scientists call water, ammonia and methane 'ices' because they freeze solid in the cold far from the Sun. Deep inside Uranus and Neptune they are squeezed and hot, so they are a thick, hot fluid. A strong answer says that the name tells what the material is, not how it feels.
15. (draw) Draw the young solar system with the frost line. Show where rocky planets formed, where giants formed, and explain why in one line.  
   **Answer:** A Sun at the middle of a flat disc. Small rocky planets inside a dotted frost line, because only rock and metal could be solid there. Large giants outside it, where ice could freeze as well, so there was more to gather.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the rocky planets close to the Sun and the giants far out, and gives temperature as the reason.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which pair correctly describes the two kinds of planet in the solar system?  
   Options: Four small rocky planets with ground, and four giants without ground / Four giants close to the Sun and four small ones far away / Four planets with rings and four without / Four planets with moons and four without  
   **Answer:** Four small rocky planets with ground, and four giants without ground. *Why:* The four inner planets are small and rocky, and the four outer ones are giants with no solid ground. Picking the second reverses the order. All the giants have rings, and almost every planet has or once had moons, so rings and moons do not split the planets into two groups.
2. (mcq) Why can a spacecraft not land on Jupiter?  
   Options: Jupiter is made of gas and fluid and has no solid surface / Jupiter is too small / Jupiter is too cold / Jupiter is moving too fast  
   **Answer:** Jupiter is made of gas and fluid and has no solid surface. *Why:* Going deeper into Jupiter, the gas simply gets thicker and hotter. There is nothing solid to land on. Picking 'too small' is wrong, because Jupiter is the biggest planet.
3. (mcq) A scientist finds a round world with a density of 5. Which is it more likely to be?  
   Options: A rocky planet with an iron core / A gas giant / A planet made mostly of ice / A star  
   **Answer:** A rocky planet with an iron core. *Why:* A density of 5 means the world is five times as heavy as the same volume of water. That is typical of rock and metal, like the Earth. Gas giants have densities of about 1 or less.
4. (mcq) The Earth's average density is about 5.5, but ordinary surface rock has a density of about 3. What does this tell you?  
   Options: The inside of the Earth must be denser than surface rock, such as heavy iron / The surface rock is lighter than it looks / The Earth is hollow / The surface rock is wrong  
   **Answer:** The inside of the Earth must be denser than surface rock, such as heavy iron. *Why:* The average of the whole Earth is higher than the rock on top, so something much denser must be inside. This is the iron core. Picking 'hollow' would make the Earth less dense, not more.
5. (mcq) Two plains on a moon look the same size. One has many craters and the other has very few. What is the best conclusion?  
   Options: The plain with many craters has been exposed for longer, so it is older / The plain with few craters is older / The plains are the same age / The plain with few craters was hit harder  
   **Answer:** The plain with many craters has been exposed for longer, so it is older. *Why:* Craters pile up with time, so more craters means a longer time. A plain with few craters was covered or renewed more recently. If you picked the second, remember that old does not mean smooth.
6. (mcq) The Earth and the Moon were hit by space rocks about equally often. Why does the Moon show far more craters?  
   Options: The Moon has no rain, wind, plants or moving crust to wipe them away / The Moon was hit more because it is smaller / The Earth's air stopped all the space rocks / The Earth is younger than the Moon  
   **Answer:** The Moon has no rain, wind, plants or moving crust to wipe them away. *Why:* The Earth rubs out its craters by weather, water, living things and moving crust. The Moon keeps all of them. The Earth's air burns up only small rocks, and does not stop the big ones that dig craters.
7. (mcq) A planet with no air sits close to its star. Another planet farther out is wrapped in thick carbon dioxide. Which could be hotter on its ground?  
   Options: The farther planet, because its thick air traps heat / The closer planet, because it gets more sunlight, whatever its air / They must be the same / Neither, since temperature does not depend on air  
   **Answer:** The farther planet, because its thick air traps heat. *Why:* A thick atmosphere slows down the heat leaving the ground, so a planet can become hotter than one that is closer but has no air. This is why Venus is hotter than Mercury.
8. (mcq) What does the greenhouse effect do to a planet?  
   Options: Gases in the air slow the heat escaping, so the surface is warmer / It makes the planet spin faster / It makes sunlight stronger / It makes the planet bigger  
   **Answer:** Gases in the air slow the heat escaping, so the surface is warmer. *Why:* Greenhouse gases let sunlight in but slow down the heat on its way out. The Earth is about 33 degrees Celsius warmer because of this. Picking 'stronger sunlight' mixes up what comes in with what stays.
9. (mcq) Which statement about the years of the planets is correct?  
   Options: Planets further from the Sun have longer years, and the year grows faster than the distance / All planets have a year of about the same length / A bigger planet always has a longer year / A planet further from the Sun has a shorter year  
   **Answer:** Planets further from the Sun have longer years, and the year grows faster than the distance. *Why:* A planet farther from the Sun has a longer path and also moves slower, so its year grows faster than its distance. Picking 'bigger planet' mixes up size and distance. Jupiter's year is long because of its distance, not its size.
10. (mcq) When you look at the Sun, what do you see?  
   Options: The Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago / The Sun exactly as it is now / The Sun as it will be in 8 minutes / The Sun as it was a day ago  
   **Answer:** The Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago. *Why:* Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to travel to the Earth. The light that enters your eye left the Sun then. Everything far away is seen as it was a little while ago.
11. (mcq) Why is a rover on Mars not driven live from the Earth like a remote-controlled toy?

   **Answer:** Each message takes minutes to arrive, so the rover would be driven far too slowly. *Why:* The delay in sending a command and getting the reply can be 6 to 44 minutes. So the rover is sent plans, and uses its own rules to avoid danger. Radio signals do reach Mars. They are just not instant.
12. (mcq) What must be true of a scale model of the solar system for it to be honest?

## Applications
- Choosing safe landing sites on the Moon and Mars, such as for India's Chandrayaan-3 lander in 2023, by studying the craters and boulders from orbit.
- Dating the surfaces of other worlds by counting craters, since no one can bring a rock from every place.
- Planning spacecraft journeys with Kepler's rule, so a probe arrives where a planet will be, not where it is now.
- Writing plans and safety rules for rovers on Mars, because messages take minutes to arrive.
- Designing parachutes, airbags and landing rockets for different thicknesses of air.
- Studying Venus's runaway greenhouse effect to understand how the Earth's own climate responds to extra carbon dioxide.
- Finding oil, gas and water underground by making small earthquakes and listening to the waves, the same idea as finding the Earth's core.
- Working out the distance of planets round other stars from the length of their years, using Kepler's rule.
- Testing how to nudge a dangerous asteroid, as the DART spacecraft did in 2022 when it hit a small asteroid and changed its orbit.
- Making maps, building plans and model aeroplanes, all of which shrink sizes and distances by the same scale.
- Teams of women scientists and engineers, reported among the leaders of the Mangalyaan mission, using these ideas to steer a spacecraft to Mars.

## Open questions
- **What is inside Jupiter?** The Juno spacecraft's gravity measurements suggest a fuzzy, spread-out centre, but the results can be read in more than one way.
- **Did Venus once have oceans, and if so, for how long?** Computer models say Venus could have had liquid water for a long time before it grew hot. No one has found proof yet.
- **Is there life on Mars, perhaps under its ground?** Mars once had rivers and lakes. Rovers are searching rocks for signs of ancient life, and none has been found so far.
- **Why does Venus spin backwards, and so slowly?** Ideas include a giant crash long ago and the pull of its thick air, but nobody can yet explain it.
- **How old is the crater at Lonar?** Different dating methods give ages of about 50,000 years and about 570,000 years, and scientists are still testing which is right.
- **How did the giant planets grow so fast, before the gas in the disc disappeared?** Gas discs last only a few million years, so a giant had to grow quickly. Core accretion may be too slow, and disc instability has problems of its own.

## Connected maths
- Mercury has a density of 5.4 and Saturn has a density of 0.7. About how many times as dense as Saturn is Mercury? **About 8 times.**. 5.4 divided by 0.7 is a little under 8, because 8 times 0.7 is 5.6. So Mercury is about 8 times as dense as Saturn. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In the walk model, the Earth is 15 metres from the Sun, and Jupiter is 5.2 times as far. How many metres from the Sun is Jupiter? Two of your steps make a metre. About how many steps is that? **78 metres, which is about 156 steps.**. 15 times 5 is 75, and 15 times 0.2 is 3, so 15 times 5.2 is 78 metres. Two steps for every metre makes 78 times 2, which is 156 steps. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L4`)
- Light travels about 300,000 kilometres every second. The Sun is about 150 million kilometres from the Earth. How many seconds does sunlight take to arrive? How many minutes and seconds is that? **500 seconds, which is 8 minutes and 20 seconds.**. 150,000,000 divided by 300,000 is 500. 500 divided by 60 is 8 with 20 left over. So it takes 8 minutes and 20 seconds. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- In the Crater Tray test, three drops from 40 centimetres made craters 4.0, 4.2 and 4.1 centimetres wide. What is the average width? Why is the average better than using one drop? **4.1 centimetres. One drop can be a fluke, and the average smooths out small mistakes.**. 4.0 plus 4.2 plus 4.1 is 12.3. 12.3 divided by 3 is 4.1. If one drop hit the flour at a slant, its crater would be odd, but the average is less affected. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- Region A of a moon has 120 craters in a patch. Region B has 30 craters in a patch of the same size. Space rocks hit at a steady rate. How many times as old is A as B? If B is 1 billion years old, how old is A? **4 times as old, so 4 billion years old.**. 120 divided by 30 is 4. At a steady rate, 4 times as many craters means 4 times as much time. 4 times 1 billion is 4 billion years. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A small world is 2 astronomical units from the Sun. Cube its distance. Then find a number that, multiplied by itself, gives the same answer. That is its year in Earth years. Give your answer to one decimal place. **About 2.8 Earth years.**. 2 times 2 times 2 is 8. 2.8 times 2.8 is 7.84, and 2.9 times 2.9 is 8.41. So 2.8 is closest to the number we want. The year is about 2.8 Earth years. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- At 10:00 a command is sent to a rover on Mars. The message takes 13 minutes to arrive. The rover sends a photo back at once, and it takes 13 minutes to return. At what time does Mission Control see the photo? If it waits for each photo before sending the next command, how many commands can it send in 2 hours? **10:26. Four commands in 2 hours.**. 13 plus 13 is 26 minutes, and 10:00 plus 26 minutes is 10:26. Two hours is 120 minutes. 120 divided by 26 is a little over 4, because 4 times 26 is 104 and 5 times 26 is 130. So only four commands fit. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- On Mercury, the time from one sunrise to the next is 176 Earth days. A Mercury year is 88 Earth days. How many Mercury years long is one Mercury day? **Exactly 2 Mercury years.**. 176 divided by 88 is 2. So between one sunrise and the next, Mercury goes round the Sun twice. A day on Mercury is two of its own years long. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What are the two kinds of planet in the solar system? → Rocky planets with solid ground, and giant planets with none.

- What does a lot of craters tell you about a surface? → That it is old.
- Why is Venus hotter than Mercury? → Its thick air traps heat like a blanket.
- How long does sunlight take to reach the Earth? → About 8 minutes and 20 seconds.
- What shape is a planet's path round the Sun? → An ellipse, which is a stretched circle.
