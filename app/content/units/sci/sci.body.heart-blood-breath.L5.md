# Lungs, gills, air tubes and hearts: how different animal bodies bring oxygen to their cells
`sci.body.heart-blood-breath.L5`

**Summary.** A litre of air holds about 26 times as much oxygen as a litre of pond water. So why does a fish die in air, with so much oxygen all round? And why can you never breathe out all the air in your lungs? In this unit you will watch a paintbrush to see what air does to a gill. You will run the Half-Breath Test with two tumblers to find how many breaths it takes to change the air in a lung. You will also compare the hearts of a fish, a frog and a human, and meet animals whose blood is not shut inside tubes.

**Intro.** *Air has far more oxygen in it than water has. A fish lifted out of a pond is surrounded by oxygen. So why does the fish suffocate?* Every cell of every animal needs oxygen, and every animal has to bring that oxygen in from outside. A fish takes oxygen from water with gills. You take it from air with lungs. A grasshopper takes air in through small holes along the sides of the body, and the blood plays almost no part. These are different answers to one problem. In this unit you will test a model gill and a model lung with your own hands. Then you will follow the oxygen into the blood and round the body, in your own body and in the bodies of other animals.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Respiratory system**: The organs that bring oxygen into a body from outside and send carbon dioxide out. In you, these are the nose, the windpipe, the lungs and the muscles that move air. *e.g.* A fish's respiratory system is the mouth, the gills and the gill covers.
- **Circulatory system**: The heart, the blood and the tubes that blood moves through. It carries oxygen, food, waste and warmth round the body. *e.g.* Your circulatory system carries oxygen from your lungs to your toes in under a minute.
- **Diffusion**: The spreading of particles from a place where there are many of them to a place where there are few. It happens by itself, because the particles never stop moving. *e.g.* Oxygen diffuses from the air in your lungs into your blood, because the blood arriving there holds less oxygen.
- **Trachea (windpipe)**: The tube that carries air from your throat down to your lungs. Rings of cartilage hold it open. *e.g.* You can feel the bumpy rings of your trachea at the front of your neck.
- **Bronchi**: The two tubes that the trachea splits into, one for each lung. Inside the lung they branch again and again into smaller tubes. *e.g.* The bronchi branch about 23 times before they reach the air sacs.
- **Alveolus**: One of the tiny air sacs at the ends of the smallest tubes in a lung. Oxygen passes through its thin wall into the blood. The plural is alveoli. *e.g.* An adult's two lungs hold about 480 million alveoli.
- **Tidal flow**: Flow that goes in and then out again by the same path, like the tide on a beach. Air in your lungs moves this way. *e.g.* Your breath is tidal. Water over a fish's gills is not. It goes in at the mouth and out at the gill covers.
- **Residual air**: The air that stays in your lungs even after you breathe out as hard as you can. Doctors call the amount the residual volume. *e.g.* An adult has about 1.2 litres of residual air.
- **Dead space**: The air in your nose, trachea and bronchi. It moves in and out with each breath, but it never reaches the alveoli, so it gives no oxygen to the blood. *e.g.* In an adult, about 150 millilitres of each breath only fills the dead space.
- **Gill**: An organ that takes oxygen out of water. It is made of very many thin plates full of blood, and water flows between the plates. *e.g.* The red, feathery parts under a fish's gill cover are the gills.
- **Gill filament**: One of the fine red threads of a gill. Each filament carries rows of even smaller plates, called lamellae, where the oxygen is taken up. *e.g.* The gills of one fish have hundreds of filaments, and each filament has many lamellae.
- **Operculum**: The hard flap that covers the gills of a bony fish. It opens and closes to help pump water over the gills. *e.g.* When a fish in a tank seems to be chewing, you are watching the operculum open and close.
- **Counter-flow exchange**: An arrangement in which two liquids flow past each other in opposite directions, with a thin wall between them. It lets one liquid pass on most of what it carries to the other. Scientists also call it countercurrent exchange. *e.g.* In a gill, blood flows forward while water flows backward, and the blood collects most of the oxygen.
- **Atrium**: A chamber of the heart that receives blood coming in. The plural is atria. *e.g.* Your heart has two atria, a right one and a left one.
- **Ventricle**: A chamber of the heart with a thick wall of muscle. It pumps blood out of the heart. *e.g.* Your left ventricle pumps blood to your whole body.
- **Single circulation**: A plan in which blood passes through the heart once on each trip round the body. The heart pumps it to the gills, and it goes straight on to the body. *e.g.* A fish has single circulation.
- **Double circulation**: A plan in which blood passes through the heart twice on each trip. One loop goes to the lungs and back. The other goes to the body and back. *e.g.* Humans, crows and crocodiles have double circulation.
- **Closed circulatory system**: A system in which the blood stays inside tubes all the way round. The blood never touches the cells directly. *e.g.* Humans, fish, earthworms and octopuses have closed circulatory systems.
- **Open circulatory system**: A system in which the heart pumps the blood out of short tubes into the spaces of the body. There the liquid washes round the organs before it drifts back to the heart. *e.g.* Grasshoppers, cockroaches, crabs and snails have open circulatory systems.
- **Haemolymph**: The liquid in an open circulatory system. It does the work of blood and of the liquid round the cells, because in these animals the two are one liquid. *e.g.* The pale liquid that comes out when a cockroach is crushed is haemolymph.
- **Tracheae (air tubes)**: The branching tubes that carry air through the body of an insect. The finest branches end right beside the cells. *e.g.* A grasshopper's tracheae carry air straight to the flight muscles.
- **Spiracle**: One of the small openings along the sides of an insect's body. Air goes in and out of the tracheae through the spiracles, and many insects can shut them. *e.g.* On a large caterpillar you can see the spiracles as a row of dots, one on each side of most segments.
- **Haemocyanin**: A protein that carries oxygen in the blood of octopuses, crabs, snails and many other animals. It holds copper where our haemoglobin holds iron, and it turns blue when it carries oxygen. *e.g.* An octopus has blue blood because of haemocyanin.
- **Lymph**: The watery liquid that leaks out of your capillaries and is collected by a second set of thin tubes. These tubes return it to the blood near the heart. *e.g.* About 3 litres of lymph go back into an adult's blood every day.

## Explanations
### Two systems for one job · *defined*
**Definition:** The respiratory system brings oxygen from outside to a thin, wet surface. The circulatory system carries that oxygen from the surface to the cells, and brings carbon dioxide back.

Your cells use oxygen to get energy out of food, and they make carbon dioxide as they do it. Most of your cells are deep inside you, far from the air. So two jobs have to be done. Air must be brought close to the blood, and blood must be brought close to every cell. The first job belongs to the respiratory system and the second to the circulatory system. They meet in the lungs, where air and blood are less than a thousandth of a millimetre apart. Not every animal splits the work this way. An insect's air tubes do both jobs, and a flatworm is so thin that the animal needs neither system.
- e.g. In you, air meets blood in about 480 million alveoli.
- e.g. In a fish, water meets blood on the plates of the gills.
- e.g. A flatworm is as thin as paper. Oxygen spreads in through the skin and reaches every cell without lungs, gills or blood.
### The path of air, from nose to alveoli · *defined*
**Definition:** Air goes in through the nose, down the trachea, into the two bronchi, through smaller and smaller branches, and ends in the alveoli.

The nose warms the air and makes it damp. Sticky mucus on the walls of the tubes traps dust. Tiny hairs called cilia sweep that mucus up towards the throat all day. The tubes branch about 23 times, which is why the inside of a lung looks like an upside-down tree. Each alveolus is wrapped in capillaries, the thinnest blood vessels. The wall between air and blood is far thinner than a hair. Oxygen crosses this wall by diffusion. It moves from the air, where there is more oxygen, into the blood, where there is less. Carbon dioxide crosses the other way for the same reason. No part of the lung pushes the oxygen across.
- e.g. Breathing through your nose on a cold morning in Ooty feels gentler than breathing through your mouth, because the nose warms the air first.
- e.g. When you cough up mucus, you are clearing dust that the cilia have swept up.
- e.g. Spread flat, the alveoli of an adult would cover most of a badminton court.
### Tidal flow: your lungs never empty · *defined*
**Definition:** Air moves in and out of your lungs by the same path. Each breath changes only part of the air in the lungs, and some air always stays behind.

Your lungs are bags with one opening. When you breathe out quietly, an adult's lungs still hold about 2 litres or more of air. The next breath brings in about half a litre. About 150 millilitres of that only fills the nose, trachea and bronchi, so about 350 millilitres of new air reaches the alveoli. There it mixes with the air that stayed behind. So one quiet breath changes roughly one seventh of the air deep in an adult's lungs. This is why the air in your alveoli has less oxygen than room air. Room air is about 21 parts oxygen in 100. The air you breathe out still has about 16 parts in 100. The mixing has a good side. The blood meets a steady mixture, so the oxygen in your blood hardly changes between one breath and the next.
- e.g. After you breathe out as hard as you can, about a litre of air is still in an adult's lungs. Nobody can push it out.
- e.g. Mouth-to-mouth rescue breathing works because breathed-out air still holds most of its oxygen.
- e.g. When you pant after a race, you take bigger and faster breaths, so more of the old air is changed each time.
### The path of blood: two loops at two pressures · *defined*
**Definition:** In double circulation, the right side of the heart pumps blood through the lungs at low pressure. The left side pumps the same blood through the rest of the body at high pressure.

Blood that has given up oxygen comes back from the body into the right atrium and then the right ventricle. The right ventricle pumps it to the lungs. It comes back, full of oxygen, to the left atrium and then the left ventricle. The left ventricle pumps it to the body. Doctors measure blood pressure in millimetres of mercury. In a resting adult, the left ventricle pushes with about 120 of these units at each beat. The right ventricle pushes with only about 25. The lungs need the gentle push, because their capillaries have very thin walls. The body needs the strong push, because the blood has to reach the brain above the heart and the toes far below.
- e.g. The wall of your left ventricle is much thicker than the wall of your right ventricle, because it pushes much harder.
- e.g. A giraffe's heart must push blood about 2 metres up to the brain, so a giraffe has very high blood pressure.
- e.g. The pulse at your wrist comes from the left ventricle. The beat of the right ventricle goes only to your lungs.
### Capillaries leak, and lymph brings the liquid back · *defined*
**Definition:** A closed circulatory system is not perfectly closed. Watery liquid leaks out of the capillaries all the time, and thin tubes called lymph vessels return it to the blood.

A capillary wall is one cell thick, with tiny gaps. The pressure of the blood pushes some of the watery part out through the gaps. This liquid bathes the cells. It is how food and oxygen cover the last short distance from the blood to a cell. Most of the liquid goes back into the capillaries further along. In an adult, about 3 litres a day does not. Lymph vessels collect it and pour it back into large veins near the heart. No heart pumps the lymph. It is squeezed along when the muscles round the vessels move, and valves stop it from flowing backwards.
- e.g. Feet and ankles can swell after a long train journey, because sitting still does not squeeze the lymph along.
- e.g. The clear liquid that oozes from a graze before it scabs is mostly this leaked liquid.
- e.g. The 'glands' in your neck that swell with a sore throat are lymph nodes, where the lymph is checked for microbes.
### Gills: breathing water · *defined*
**Definition:** A gill is a set of very thin plates full of blood. Water flows between the plates in one direction, and oxygen dissolved in the water diffuses into the blood.

Water is hard to breathe for three reasons. First, it holds little oxygen. A litre of fresh water holds about 8 cubic centimetres of oxygen, and a litre of air holds about 210. Second, water is about 800 times as dense as air, so moving it takes much more work. Third, oxygen spreads through water thousands of times more slowly than through air. Gills deal with all three. The water never turns round. It comes in at the mouth and leaves behind the operculum, so the water never has to be stopped and sent back. The plates are extremely thin and very many. And the blood in them flows against the water, which you will meet on the next card. Gills fail in air because nothing holds the plates apart. In water each plate floats free. In air the wet plates cling together, and most of their surface is lost.
- e.g. A fish in a tank opens the mouth and the gill covers in turn. That is a pump with two chambers, pushing water one way.
- e.g. Some sharks and tunas swim with the mouth open, and the swimming drives the water over the gills.
- e.g. A wet paintbrush out of water clumps into a point, the way gill filaments do.
### Counter-flow exchange: why direction matters · *defined*
**Definition:** In counter-flow exchange, blood and water flow in opposite directions on the two sides of a thin wall. All along the wall, the water has a little more oxygen than the blood next to it, so oxygen keeps crossing.

Oxygen diffuses only from where there is more to where there is less. Suppose blood and water flowed the same way. At the start the water would have much more oxygen, and oxygen would cross fast. Soon the two would hold equal amounts, and the crossing would stop. The blood could collect half the oxygen at most. Now turn the blood round. Blood that is nearly full meets the freshest water, which still has more oxygen than the blood has. Blood that is nearly empty meets water that is nearly used up, which still has a little more than the blood has. So oxygen crosses along the whole length. The gills of many fish take up about 80 parts in 100 of the oxygen in the water that passes over them.
- e.g. In the Bean Gill activity, the blood cup leaves with about 12 of 16 beans when it moves against the water, and never more than 8 when it moves with the water.
- e.g. The legs of a wading bird, such as a flamingo at Pulicat Lake, use the same plan for heat. Warm blood going down passes cold blood coming up and warms it.
- e.g. Engineers copy the plan in heat exchangers, which pass heat from one liquid to another in factories and power stations.
### Three heart plans: fish, frog and human · *defined*
**Definition:** A fish's heart has two chambers and pumps blood round one loop. A frog's heart has three chambers. A human heart has four chambers and pumps blood round two loops.

In a fish, one atrium receives the blood and one ventricle pumps it to the gills. From the gills it flows straight on to the body. The narrow gill capillaries take away much of the push, so the blood reaches the body at low pressure. That suits a fish, whose body is held up by the water. A frog has two atria and one ventricle. Blood from the lungs and skin and blood from the body partly mix in the ventricle. That suits a frog, who takes in much of their oxygen through the skin. In humans, the ventricle is divided in two. Blood from the lungs and blood from the body never mix, and each loop has a pump of its own. Birds and crocodiles also have four chambers. Birds and mammals came to this plan separately, from ancestors whose ventricle was not fully divided.
- e.g. Fish: 2 chambers, single circulation. Frog: 3 chambers. Crow, crocodile and human: 4 chambers, double circulation.
- e.g. Most lizards, snakes and turtles have three chambers, with a ventricle that is partly divided.
- e.g. In the Squirt Test, the water that has passed through cotton wool has lost its push, as blood has after the gills.
### A frog breathes in three ways · *defined*
**Definition:** A tadpole breathes with gills. An adult frog breathes with lungs and also through thin, wet skin and the lining of the mouth.

A frog has no diaphragm. To fill the lungs, a frog lowers the floor of the mouth to draw air in through the nostrils. Then the frog shuts the nostrils and raises the floor of the mouth, and this pushes the air down into the lungs. You can see the throat pumping. The skin does a large share of the work. It is thin, it is full of capillaries, and it is kept wet with mucus, because oxygen must dissolve in water before it can cross. Under water in a cold pond, a resting frog can get all the oxygen they need through the skin. This is also why a frog dries out and dies quickly in hot sun, and why chemicals in water harm frogs so easily.
- e.g. Watch the throat of an Indian bullfrog after the first monsoon rain. It moves up and down many times a minute.
- e.g. An earthworm has no lungs or gills and breathes only through damp skin.
- e.g. Tadpoles in a rain puddle have gills. As legs grow, lungs grow too, and the gills are lost.
### Bird lungs: air that flows one way · *defined*
**Definition:** A bird's lungs are stiff and do not swell and shrink. Thin bags called air sacs work as bellows and push air through the lungs in one direction.

Most birds have nine air sacs spread through the body. When a bird breathes in, fresh air goes mainly to the sacs at the back. When the bird breathes out, that fresh air is pushed forward through the lungs. So air moves through the lung tubes in the same direction during the breath in and the breath out. Fresh air does not mix with a large store of old air, as it does in our lungs. The blood flows across the path of the air, which also helps it collect oxygen. This is one reason birds can fly where the air is thin. Bar-headed geese fly over the Himalayas twice a year, between Central Asia and wetlands in India. Scientists who put trackers on them recorded one goose at 7,290 metres.
- e.g. A crow's tail bobs gently as the air sacs fill and empty.
- e.g. Bar-headed geese spend the winter at lakes and fields from Assam to Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. A bird has no diaphragm. Muscles of the ribs and belly move the breastbone to work the air sacs.
### Open and closed circulation · *defined*
**Definition:** In a closed circulatory system, blood stays inside vessels. In an open one, the heart pumps the liquid into the body spaces, where it washes over the organs directly.

A grasshopper's heart is a tube along the back. It pumps haemolymph forward and out into the space round the organs. The liquid drifts back and enters the heart again through small holes with valves. There are no capillaries. The pressure is low, and the liquid moves slowly. A closed system costs more to build and to run, because the heart must force blood through very narrow tubes. In return it can send blood fast, at high pressure, and it can send more to one organ and less to another. Insects, spiders, crabs, prawns and snails have open systems. Earthworms, octopuses, squids and all animals with backbones have closed ones. An earthworm has five pairs of muscular vessels that work as hearts.
- e.g. A cockroach can use the low pressure of an open system because their oxygen travels in air tubes, not in the haemolymph.
- e.g. A spider straightens some leg joints by raising the pressure of the haemolymph.
- e.g. An octopus, with a closed system and three hearts, can hunt fast. A garden snail, with an open system, lives slowly.
### Insects: air tubes, and blood that carries almost no oxygen · *defined*
**Definition:** An insect takes air in through spiracles and carries it through branching tracheae to every part of the body. The haemolymph of most insects plays almost no part in carrying oxygen.

The tracheae branch into finer and finer tubes. The tips are wet, and they end right beside the cells. Oxygen dissolves in that liquid and diffuses the last short distance into the cell. So in an insect, air is piped right to the cells. Oxygen spreads through air thousands of times faster than through water, which is why this works in a small body. Large or busy insects also pump. They squeeze the abdomen to push air along the big tubes, and they open some spiracles while closing others. The haemolymph carries food, waste, hormones and cells that fight microbes. This shows that carrying oxygen is one job blood can have, and not what makes a liquid blood.
- e.g. Watch a resting grasshopper or wasp. The abdomen pulses in and out as the insect pumps air.
- e.g. Many mosquito larvae hang head-down from the surface of still water and breathe air through a tube at the tail end.
- e.g. A few insects, such as the red larvae of some midges, do have haemoglobin. It helps them live in mud with very little oxygen.
### Blood of other colours, and blood with no colour · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals carry oxygen with different molecules. Haemoglobin, with iron, is red. Haemocyanin, with copper, is blue when it carries oxygen. A few animals carry oxygen with no such molecule.

In your blood, haemoglobin is packed inside red blood cells. In an earthworm, haemoglobin floats free in the liquid of the blood. In octopuses, crabs and snails, haemocyanin floats free, and the blood looks pale blue. An octopus has three hearts. Two push blood through the two gills, and the third pushes it round the body. The strangest blood belongs to the icefishes of the sea round Antarctica. As adults they have no haemoglobin, and their blood is clear. Oxygen is simply dissolved in the watery part. This carries less than a fifth as much oxygen as the blood of related fishes. Icefishes manage with very cold water, which holds more oxygen, and with a large heart that pumps a large amount of blood.
- e.g. Human: red blood, haemoglobin inside cells. Earthworm: red blood, haemoglobin loose in the liquid.
- e.g. Octopus, crab and snail: blue blood, with haemocyanin.
- e.g. Antarctic icefish: clear blood, with no haemoglobin at all.
### Is one way of breathing better than another? · *debated*
**Definition:** There is no agreed ranking of breathing organs or blood systems. Scientists compare them one job at a time.

Books often arrange animals in a line, from 'simple' hearts and gills to 'advanced' four-chambered hearts and lungs. Most biologists today do not accept that line. Here are the main views.
- e.g. A fish's gills take a larger share of the oxygen from water than your lungs take from air.
- e.g. A bird's lungs work at heights where a human climber needs bottled oxygen.
- e.g. There are more kinds of insect, with open blood and air tubes, than of all other animals together.
- **Many older textbooks:** Animals can be ranked. Two chambers are simple, three are in between, and four are the most advanced. On this view our own body is the finished design.
- **Most biologists today:** Each plan fits a way of life. A fish heart suits a body held up by water. Tidal lungs waste some of each breath but lose little water. No plan is the top of a ladder, and every animal alive today has ancestors as old as ours.
- **Engineers and some physiologists:** You can still compare how well each design does one job. For taking oxygen out of what flows past, bird lungs and fish gills beat ours. For keeping blood pressure high, a closed system beats an open one. 'Better' always needs the question 'better at what?'

## Mostly agreed
- Oxygen passes from the air in the lungs into the blood by diffusion alone. No cell pumps it across. *How we know:* In 1910 August and Marie Krogh measured the oxygen in lung air and in the blood leaving the lungs. The lung air always had the higher oxygen pressure. In 1915 Marie Krogh measured how fast a gas crosses a human lung, and diffusion was fast enough to explain all of it.
- The lungs never empty. A quiet breath changes only a small part of the air in them. *How we know:* Doctors measure the air left in the lungs by having a person breathe a known amount of a harmless gas, such as helium, and seeing how much it is thinned. Breathed-out air still holds about 16 parts of oxygen in 100, against 21 in room air.
- In fish gills, blood flows against the water, and the gills take up most of the oxygen in the water passing over them. *How we know:* Scientists have measured the oxygen in water just before and just after it crosses the gills. Many fish take up about 80 parts in 100. Under a microscope, the blood in the gill plates can be seen to run in the opposite direction to the water.
- In animals with backbones, blood stays inside vessels and goes round and round. Arteries and veins are joined by capillaries. *How we know:* William Harvey showed in 1628 that the blood must go round. In 1661 Marcello Malpighi looked at a frog's lung through a microscope and saw the capillaries that join the arteries to the veins.
- Air flows one way through a bird's lungs, both when the bird breathes in and when the bird breathes out. *How we know:* In 1971 scientists placed tiny flow sensors in the airways of ducks and recorded which way the air moved during each part of a breath.
- Insects carry oxygen as a gas in air tubes, and many of them pump air through the tubes. *How we know:* The air tubes can be seen under a microscope, ending beside the cells. In 2003 scientists used strong X-rays to film living beetles, ants and crickets, and saw the main tubes being squeezed and released in a rhythm.

## How we found out
**Marie Krogh and August Krogh, in Copenhagen. The idea they tested came from Christian Bohr and John Scott Haldane., 1910 for the seven papers by the Kroghs, and 1915 for Marie Krogh's paper on the human lung.** Around 1900, some of the best scientists thought that a lung works like a gland. Christian Bohr in Copenhagen and John Scott Haldane in Oxford had measured the oxygen in lung air and in the blood leaving the lungs. The blood seemed to have the higher oxygen pressure. Oxygen cannot diffuse from less to more. So, they said, the cells of the lung must push oxygen into the blood. August Krogh had been Bohr's student. Marie Krogh was a doctor. They were married, and they worked together. They built finer instruments and measured again, often on themselves. Every time, the lung air had the higher oxygen pressure. In 1910 they published seven papers. The last one said that oxygen crosses the lung by diffusion alone. In 1915 Marie Krogh went further. She had people breathe in a very small amount of the gas carbon monoxide, and measured how fast it left the air in their lungs. This showed how quickly a gas can cross a human lung. Diffusion was fast enough. Lung clinics still use a form of her test.  
*What people believed before:* That the cells of the lung push oxygen into the blood, as a gland pushes out a liquid. Careful measurements showed that the oxygen pressure is always higher in the lung air than in the blood, so oxygen can cross by diffusion with no pushing.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Each breath fills your lungs with fresh air and then empties them.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels that way. Air goes in, the chest rises, air goes out, the chest falls.  
  Against: About a litre of air cannot be breathed out at all, and more stays after a quiet breath. Each new breath is mixed into it. That is why breathed-out air still has about 16 parts of oxygen in 100.
- **A fish dies in air because air does not have the kind of oxygen a fish can use.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Fish live in water and take oxygen from water, so it seems that air must be useless to them.  
  Against: The oxygen in air and the oxygen dissolved in water are the same substance, and air holds far more of it. Out of water, the wet plates of the gills cling together and dry, so there is hardly any surface left for oxygen to cross. Fish such as the murrel and the climbing perch have an extra organ that stays open in air, and they can breathe air for hours.
- **A four-chambered heart is the most advanced heart, and the hearts of fish and frogs are primitive.** (Many older textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: Four chambers keep blood from the lungs and blood from the body fully apart, which allows a high blood pressure and a busy, warm body.  
  Against: A fish's heart suits a fish. A frog's mixed blood suits an animal who takes in oxygen through the skin and can stay under water for a long time without using the lungs. Fish and frogs alive today have as long a history behind them as we have.
- **The cells of the lung actively push oxygen into the blood, the way a gland pushes out sweat.** (Christian Bohr and John Scott Haldane, around 1900; *rejected*)  
  For: Their measurements seemed to show more oxygen pressure in the blood than in the lung air. Haldane also thought that secretion would explain how people get used to high mountains.  
  Against: August and Marie Krogh made more careful measurements from 1910 and found the oxygen pressure always higher in the lung air. Later work on mountains agreed with them. Diffusion alone is enough.
- **Blood is the liquid that carries oxygen, so every animal's blood must carry oxygen.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: In humans and all other animals with backbones, carrying oxygen is one of the main jobs of blood.  
  Against: In most insects the haemolymph carries almost no oxygen, because air tubes take it straight to the cells. The haemolymph still carries food, waste and hormones. So blood is better described as the liquid a body moves round to carry things.

## Roots
In this unit you found that one breath changes only part of the air in a lung. Here we ask what that tells us about breathing, and about counting things as separate.

**Deep truth.** In the Half-Breath Test, the tumbler was still brown after one pour, and after five. Each pour took out only part of the old water and stirred new water in. Your lungs have one opening too, and a quiet breath changes only about one seventh of their air. So the air deep in your lungs is always a mixture of many breaths.  
*Habit it breaks:* We count breaths as separate things, one after another, each going in and then coming out. We picture a lung as a bag that fills with new air and then empties. But a lung never empties, so every breath is stirred into the ones before it.  
*Evidence:* In the Half-Breath Test it took about seven pours to clear the tumbler when half the water was changed each time, and about twice as many when a quarter was changed. An adult's lungs still hold about 2 litres of air after a quiet breath out, and only about 350 millilitres of new air reaches the alveoli with the next breath. Breathed-out air still has about 16 parts of oxygen in 100.

- *who am i:* Some of the air in your lungs came in many breaths ago. Your lungs have not been empty since the first breath you took as a baby.
- *what is life:* Every animal has to bring the outside in. A fish lets water flow through, a goose pushes air through, a grasshopper pipes air to each cell, and you stir it in.
- *what is this world:* Oxygen moves by itself from where there is more to where there is less. Every lung, gill and air tube is a way of arranging that meeting.

One rule runs through this whole unit. Oxygen moves by itself from where there is more of it to where there is less. Round that one rule, living beings have built many bodies: gills with water flowing one way, lungs that mix, air tubes that reach each cell, and hearts with two, three and four chambers. The oxygen itself was made by plants, algae and microbes. So each of these bodies depends on other lives at every breath, and all of them depend on the same rule.

- **Bhagavad Gita 4.29:** The verse lists people who practise with the breath. Some, it says, offer the outgoing breath into the incoming breath, and others offer the incoming breath into the outgoing. (The verse is about a spiritual practice, not about lungs. But it speaks of the two breaths as poured into each other. The Half-Breath Test shows the same thing in the body. Each breath in is mixed into the air left by the breaths before it.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 12 (Diels-Kranz B12):** Heraclitus says that on those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow. (A river keeps its name and its banks while its water changes. Your lungs keep their shape while their air changes. In the lungs the change is never complete, because new air is always mixed with old.)
- **Anapanasati Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 118:** The Buddha teaches a person to breathe with full attention. Breathing in long, the person knows, 'I am breathing in long.' Breathing out short, the person knows, 'I am breathing out short.' (The teaching asks you to know each breath closely, without changing it. When you try, you can look for the exact moment where one breath ends and the next begins. The science of this unit says that inside the lungs there is no such sharp line.)
- Science (settled): Oxygen crosses from lung air into blood by diffusion alone. No cell pushes it. The body's work is to bring air and blood close together and to keep both moving.
- Science (settled): Almost all the oxygen in the air was made by living beings: by microbes called cyanobacteria, by algae and by plants.
- Science (open): Why mammals kept lungs that mix old air with new, while birds came to have lungs that air flows through, is not fully understood. Steady blood gases and saving water may both be part of the answer.
- Other view, Most physiologists and doctors: Breaths are separate events, and it is right to count them. Each breath is started by its own signal from the brain, has its own size, and can be measured. A nurse who counts 40 breaths a minute in a child with a fever has learned something true and useful. On this view, the mixing in the lungs is a detail of how each separate breath works, and it does not make the breaths any less separate.
- Practice, Where does a breath end? (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Breathe as you usually do, and do not change anything. Follow one breath in, all the way. Notice the moment when it turns round and starts to go out. Now follow it out. Is there a point where it is completely gone, or does the next breath begin first? Watch five breaths like this. Then open your eyes and tell me what you found.
- Ask: If the air in your lungs is a mixture of many breaths, how many breaths are in you right now?
- Ask: A gill needs water between its plates to work. Where does the gill end and the water begin?
- Ask: Oxygen crosses into your blood by itself. So who is doing the breathing: you, your muscles, or the oxygen?
- Ask: If you could breathe like a bird, with air flowing straight through, would anything about being you feel different?

## Story: The Well That Had to Breathe
*Malar, Pechi paatti, Murugan and this well are made up. These facts are real. Carbon dioxide and other gases can collect at the bottom of a well, and people die in wells every year because of them. Oxfam's guide to cleaning hand-dug wells tells workers to lower a lighted candle before anyone goes in, and not to enter if the flame goes out. The same guide says that lowering and raising a large bundle of twigs or branches can help to clear the air. A flame does not warn of every dangerous gas. No child should ever go into a well, a tank or a pit, and no adult should without training and a rope.*

The well behind Pechi paatti's house was forty feet deep, and in the month of Vaikasi it was nearly dry.

That was the time to clean it. Every summer someone went down with a basket and a short spade and sent up the mud and the rotted leaves, so that the spring at the bottom could run clear again. For forty summers that someone had been Paatti. This year her knees had said no. Malar's cousin Murugan, who was sixteen and could climb a coconut palm in the time it took to say so, was going instead.

'Not yet,' said Paatti.

She lit the small brass lamp, set it in a wire basket and let it down on a rope. Malar lay on her stomach at the edge and watched. The flame went down steady and yellow, past the first ring of stones, past the fig root that grew out of the wall, past the green line where the water had stood in December. Then, a little above the mud, it shrank to a blue bead and went out.

'Bad air,' said Paatti, and pulled the lamp up. 'Nobody goes down.'

'The rope shook it,' said Murugan.

'Then you hold the rope.'

He held it as still as a heron. The flame went out at the same place.

That had been Monday. Paatti took the palm-leaf lid off the well and said the bad air would go by itself, now that the well was open to the sky. On Tuesday the lamp went out a little above the mud. On Wednesday it went out a little above the mud.

'Three days with the lid off,' Murugan said. 'A room airs out in an hour.'

'A room has a door and a window,' said Malar. She had not known she was going to say it.

She was thinking about the afternoon wind. It had been blowing since two o'clock. It shook the neem tree and lifted the washing and rolled a dry leaf across the yard to the well. She watched the leaf tip over the edge. It did not blow about down there. It fell slowly and straight, turning, all the way to the mud, as if there were no wind anywhere in the world.

The wind crossed the mouth of the well and went on. It could not push new air in, because the air already inside had no other way out.

Then she thought of the kudam. Every morning she rinsed the brass water pot, the one with the narrow neck. If she only let a little clean water run in at the top, the cloudy water at the bottom stayed where it was. She had to pour some in, and swirl it, and pour it out, and do it again, and again. Each time the water came out only a little cleaner than before. Seven or eight times, on a bad day.

'Paatti,' she said, 'the well has only one mouth. We have to rinse it.'

Paatti looked at her for a while. Then she sent Murugan up the neem tree with the sickle.

They tied the cut branches into a bundle nearly as wide as the well, leaves and all, and hung it on the rope over the pulley. Malar and Murugan let it down to the bottom and hauled it up to the top, and let it down, and hauled it up. Going down, the bundle pushed the air below it up past its edges. Coming up, it drew air from the yard down behind it. Dust came out of the well as they worked, and the smell of wet stone.

After thirty pulls their arms shook. Paatti lowered the lamp. It burned a hand's width lower than before, and went out.

'It did nothing,' said Murugan.

'It did a hand's width,' said Malar. 'The first rinse never cleans the pot.'

Thirty more. The lamp went out just above the mud, but slowly this time, the flame leaning and thinning before it died. Thirty more, with Paatti counting aloud and the neighbour's daughter taking a turn on the rope. Malar's palms were red. A crow sat on the pulley post and watched the bundle go by, down and up, down and up.

At the fourth try the lamp reached the bottom and stood on the mud, small and yellow. Nobody spoke. Paatti counted to one hundred under her breath. The flame was still there.

'Now,' said Paatti. 'With the rope round your waist. The lamp stays down there beside you, and you look at it more than you look at the mud. If it goes blue, you come up, and you do not fill the basket first.' She turned to Malar. 'And the air does not stop moving.'

So Murugan went down, and the mud came up basket by basket, black and smelling of old rain. All that afternoon a smaller bundle of neem went on travelling down one side of the well and up again. Down. Up. Down. Up.

Malar pulled until the light turned orange. Somewhere after the two hundredth pull she noticed that she was breathing in as the bundle rose and out as it fell. She could not have said which of them had started copying the other.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the story never mentions lungs, but the well is built like one. It has a single opening, so wind across the top does not change the air at the bottom. Air has to be moved in and out through the same mouth, many times, and each time only part of the old air is changed. That is why the lamp burned only a hand's width lower after the first thirty pulls. Malar gets there by remembering how a narrow-necked pot is rinsed. Do not explain the link at once. Ask the questions below and let your child find it.

- Ask: Why did three days of wind not change the air at the bottom of the well?
- Ask: Why did the lamp burn only a little lower after the first thirty pulls?
- Ask: Which part of your body has one opening for air going in and coming out?
- Ask: How is rinsing a narrow-necked pot like the Half-Breath Test?
- Ask: What would be different if the well had a second opening near the bottom?

### e2 · The Squirt Test: what happens to the push after blood squeezes through capillaries? (25 min)

  - Note: Refill the bottle to the same level before each try. A dish-wash bottle usually squirts between 1 and 3 metres. Measure to the far end of the wet streak.
  - Check: In the Squirt Test, why do you squeeze three times for each set-up? → **One squeeze can be a fluke**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take off the cap. 2. Pack cotton wool firmly into the neck, about two finger-widths deep. 3. Put the cap on. 4. Squeeze from the same line, three times, and measure.  
  *Parent says:* Now the water must pass through thousands of tiny gaps before it gets out. Squeeze just as hard. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Pack cotton wool into the neck. Squeeze just as hard. Measure three times.
  - Note: The jet usually falls to less than half its distance, and often to a dribble. If it hardly changes, pack in more cotton wool, more tightly. If no water comes at all, loosen it a little.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Squeeze the plugged bottle into the tumbler until the tumbler is half full. 2. Take the plug out of the bottle. 3. Pour the caught water back in. 4. Squeeze from the line again and measure.  
  *Parent says:* This water has been through the plug and lost its push. How could we give it a strong push again? Try it.  
  *Child mode:* Catch the water that came through. Put it in the open bottle. Squeeze again and measure.
  - Note: The second squeeze sends the water as far as in the first test. This is the second pump. In your body, the left ventricle gives it.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why. 2. Ask which animal's blood is like step 3, and which is like step 4. 3. After they have answered, read the note below aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you guessed, what you saw, and why you think it happened. Which part was like a fish, and which was like you?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed. Say what you saw. Say why. Which step was like a fish?
  - Ask: Where did the push go? (good: It was used up forcing water through the tiny gaps.; Rubbing against the cotton slowed the water.)
  - Ask: Why does your heart have two ventricles? (good: One pushes blood through the lungs.; The other gives it a new push to reach the body.)
  - Note: The note to read: a fish's heart has one ventricle. It pushes blood through the capillaries of the gills, and the blood goes on to the body with much of its push used up. Your blood comes back to the heart after the lungs and gets a second, stronger push. The model leaves out a lot. Real capillaries are open tubes, not tangled fibres, and a fish's blood still flows steadily. A fish in water does not need high pressure.
  - Think: The right ventricle pushes gently and the left one pushes hard. What might happen to the thin lung capillaries if the right one pushed hard too?

## Worksheets
### Lungs, gills and hearts (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Where does oxygen pass from the air into your blood?  
   Options: In the alveoli / In the trachea / In the heart / In the nose  
   **Answer:** In the alveoli. *Why:* The alveoli are the tiny air sacs at the ends of the airways. Their walls are thin enough for oxygen to diffuse through. The trachea and nose only carry the air.
2. (mcq) What happens to the air in your lungs during one quiet breath?  
   Options: All of it is changed / Part of it is changed / None of it is changed / It turns into water  
   **Answer:** Part of it is changed. *Why:* A quiet breath brings in a small amount of new air, which mixes with the air that stayed in the lungs. If you chose 'all of it', remember that the lungs never empty.
3. (mcq) Why do gills stop working in air?  
   Options: Air has no oxygen / Air is too cold / The wet plates cling together / Air is too light to pump  
   **Answer:** The wet plates cling together. *Why:* In water each thin plate floats free. In air the wet plates stick to each other, and most of the surface is lost. Air has plenty of oxygen.
4. (mcq) How many chambers does a fish's heart have?  
   Options: One / Two / Three / Four  
   **Answer:** Two. *Why:* A fish's heart has one atrium and one ventricle. It pumps blood to the gills, and the blood goes straight on to the body.
5. (mcq) Which of these animals has an open circulatory system?  
   Options: Earthworm / Rohu fish / Crow / Grasshopper  
   **Answer:** Grasshopper. *Why:* A grasshopper's tube heart pumps haemolymph into the spaces of the body. The earthworm, the fish and the crow keep their blood inside vessels.
6. (truefalse) After you breathe out as hard as you can, your lungs are empty.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. About a litre of air stays in an adult's lungs even after the hardest breath out. It is called residual air.
7. (truefalse) Water flows over a fish's gills in one direction only.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Water comes in at the mouth and leaves behind the gill cover. It does not turn round, as the air in your lungs does.
8. (fill) The sheet of muscle under your lungs that pulls air in is the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['diaphragm']. *Why:* When the diaphragm tightens it moves down, the chest gets bigger, and air flows in.
9. (fill) The small openings along an insect's sides that let air in are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['spiracles']. *Why:* Air goes in through the spiracles and along the air tubes, the tracheae, to the cells.
10. (match) Match each animal to what they breathe with.  
   **Answer:** Human → Lungs; Rohu fish → Gills; Grasshopper → Air tubes; Earthworm → Damp skin. *Why:* Each of these is a thin, wet surface where oxygen can cross. They differ in where the surface is and how air or water is brought to it.
11. (order) Put the path of air into your lungs in order, starting from outside.  
   **Answer:** ['Nose', 'Trachea', 'Bronchi', 'Alveoli']. *Why:* Air enters at the nose, goes down the trachea, divides into the two bronchi and ends in the alveoli.
12. (sort) Sort each animal by kind of circulatory system.  
   **Answer:** crab → Open; cockroach → Open; snail → Open; human → Closed; octopus → Closed; earthworm → Closed. *Why:* In crabs, cockroaches and snails, the liquid leaves the vessels and washes round the organs. In humans, octopuses and earthworms, the blood stays inside vessels.
13. (write) Air holds more oxygen than water. Explain why a fish still suffocates in air.  
   **Answer:** A gill is made of very thin, wet plates. In water they float apart, and water touches every plate. In air they cling together and dry, so there is very little surface left for oxygen to cross.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the surface is lost. It does not say that air lacks oxygen or has the wrong kind.
14. (write) In the Half-Breath Test, why did two half-breaths not clear the tumbler?  
   **Answer:** The first breath left half of the dark water. The clean water mixed with it. The second pour took out half of the mixture, so a quarter of the dark water was still there.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the new water mixes with the old before the next pour, so each pour removes clean water as well as dark.
15. (draw) Draw the path of a fish's blood as one loop. Label the heart, the gills and the body.  
   **Answer:** One loop with arrows: heart, then gills, then body, then back to the heart. The blood is low in oxygen from the body to the gills, and rich in oxygen from the gills to the body.. *Why:* A strong drawing has a single loop, with the gills between the heart and the body. The blood passes through the heart once on each trip.
### Pressure, direction and leaks (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) About how much of the air deep in an adult's lungs does one quiet breath change?  
   Options: About one seventh / About one half / All of it / None of it  
   **Answer:** About one seventh. *Why:* About 350 millilitres of new air reaches the alveoli and mixes with roughly 2 litres already there. That is about one part in seven.
2. (mcq) In a gill, why does blood flow against the water and not with it?  
   Options: To cool the blood / To slow the water down / So oxygen keeps crossing all along / To stop the gill leaking  
   **Answer:** So oxygen keeps crossing all along. *Why:* Flowing against the water, the blood always meets water with a little more oxygen than the blood has. So oxygen crosses along the whole length.
3. (mcq) Which side of your heart pushes harder, and where does it send blood?  
   Options: The right, to the lungs / The left, to the body / Both push the same / The right, to the body  
   **Answer:** The left, to the body. *Why:* The left ventricle sends blood to the whole body at high pressure. The right ventricle sends blood only to the lungs, gently.
4. (mcq) What carries oxygen to the cells of a grasshopper?  
   Options: Red blood cells / Gills / The stomach / Air tubes  
   **Answer:** Air tubes. *Why:* Tracheae carry air from the spiracles right to the cells. The haemolymph of a grasshopper carries almost no oxygen.
5. (mcq) What happens to the watery liquid that leaks out of your capillaries?  
   Options: Lymph vessels return it to the blood / It is all lost as sweat / It collects in the feet for good / It turns into red blood cells  
   **Answer:** Lymph vessels return it to the blood. *Why:* Most of the liquid goes back into the capillaries. The rest, about 3 litres a day in an adult, is collected by lymph vessels and returned near the heart.
6. (truefalse) Air flows one way through a bird's lungs, on the breath in and on the breath out.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Air sacs work as bellows and push air through the stiff lungs in the same direction in both halves of the breath.
7. (truefalse) A frog's heart keeps blood from the lungs and blood from the body fully apart.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A frog has one ventricle, and the two kinds of blood partly mix in it. A human ventricle is divided in two.
8. (fill) The copper protein that makes an octopus's blood blue is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['haemocyanin', 'hemocyanin']. *Why:* Haemocyanin carries oxygen with copper. Our haemoglobin carries it with iron and is red.
9. (match) Match each animal to their heart.  
   **Answer:** Rohu fish → Two chambers; Frog → Three chambers; Crow → Four chambers; Octopus → Three separate hearts. *Why:* The fish has one loop and two chambers. The frog has two loops that meet in one ventricle. The crow has two separate loops. The octopus has one heart for each gill and one for the body.
10. (order) A drop of blood leaves your right ventricle. Put the next four places in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Lungs', 'Left atrium', 'Left ventricle', 'Body']. *Why:* The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs. It returns to the left atrium, passes into the left ventricle and is pumped to the body.
11. (count) In the Bean Gill, water starts with 16 beans. Moving the same way, how many can blood collect at most?  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* The two cups share until they are equal, at 8 each. After that nothing moves, because beans only go from the fuller cup to the emptier one.
12. (odd) Three of these animals keep their blood inside vessels. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Earthworm / Octopus / Snail / Rohu fish  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A snail has an open circulatory system. The earthworm, the octopus and the fish have closed ones.
13. (write) Explain why a second pump helps an animal with lungs. Use the word 'pressure'.  
   **Answer:** Blood loses pressure when it is pushed through the tiny capillaries of the lungs. The second pump, the left ventricle, gives it high pressure again so that it can reach the whole body. The lungs can then be kept at a gentle pressure.. *Why:* A strong answer says that pressure is lost in the lung capillaries and given back by the left ventricle. A very strong answer adds that thin lung capillaries need a gentle push.
14. (label) Label the four chambers of this human heart.  
   **Answer:** ['Right atrium: receives blood from the body', 'Right ventricle: pumps blood to the lungs', 'Left atrium: receives blood from the lungs', 'Left ventricle: pumps blood to the body, and has the thickest wall']. *Why:* A heart is drawn as if the person is facing you, so the person's right side is on your left. The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pushes hardest.
15. (perspective) Is a four-chambered heart better than a two-chambered heart?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. For example: blood loses its push in the gills, which matters little to a fish in water. But a bird could not fly over mountains with a single loop.. *Why:* Each view has something right. A strong answer names what the second pump gives and what it costs, and says for whom.
### Mixtures, models and other bodies (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Lungs that mix old air with new waste part of each breath. What do they gain?  
   Options: A steady mixture for the blood / More oxygen than a gill takes / Blue blood / A faster heartbeat  
   **Answer:** A steady mixture for the blood. *Why:* Because each breath changes only a little of the air, the oxygen and carbon dioxide next to the blood stay almost steady from breath to breath.
2. (mcq) What helps an icefish live with no haemoglobin?  
   Options: Icefish need no oxygen / Their gills work as lungs / Very cold water holds more oxygen / They breathe through ice  
   **Answer:** Very cold water holds more oxygen. *Why:* Cold water holds more dissolved oxygen, and a cold body uses less. An icefish also has a large heart that moves a great deal of blood.
3. (mcq) An animal has air tubes that reach every cell. What might the blood not need to carry?  
   Options: Food / Oxygen / Waste / Hormones  
   **Answer:** Oxygen. *Why:* If air is piped straight to the cells, the blood is not needed for oxygen. It still has to carry food, waste and hormones.
4. (mcq) In the Squirt Test, what stood for the capillaries of a gill?  
   Options: The squeeze bottle / The chalk line / The tumbler / The cotton-wool plug  
   **Answer:** The cotton-wool plug. *Why:* Water had to push through thousands of tiny gaps in the cotton wool, as blood pushes through tiny capillaries. The bottle stood for the ventricle.
5. (mcq) Oxygen pressure is higher in lung air than in the blood arriving. Which way does oxygen diffuse?  
   Options: From air to blood / From blood to air / Both ways equally / It does not move  
   **Answer:** From air to blood. *Why:* Diffusion goes from where there is more to where there is less. No pumping is needed. Carbon dioxide diffuses the other way for the same reason.
6. (truefalse) In an open circulatory system, the blood and the liquid round the cells are one liquid.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The liquid is called haemolymph. It leaves the vessels and washes over the organs directly.
7. (truefalse) The air you breathe out has no oxygen left in it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Breathed-out air still has about 16 parts of oxygen in 100. Room air has about 21. That is why rescue breathing can help a person.
8. (fill) Blood and water flowing in opposite directions across a thin wall is called ____ exchange.  
   **Answer:** ['counter-flow', 'countercurrent', 'counterflow', 'counter-current']. *Why:* Counter-flow, or countercurrent, exchange lets the blood collect most of the oxygen from the water.
9. (sort) Sort each breathing organ by how the air or water moves through it.  
   **Answer:** human lung → In and out the same way; dolphin lung → In and out the same way; frog lung → In and out the same way; fish gill → One way through; bird lung → One way through; shark gill → One way through. *Why:* The lungs of mammals and frogs are bags with one opening. Water passes through gills from mouth to gill opening, and air sacs push air through a bird's lungs in one direction.
10. (count) A tumbler is cleared by half-breaths. After 3 half-breaths, the old water left is one part in how many?  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Each half-breath leaves half of what was there. Half of a half is a quarter, and half of a quarter is an eighth. So one part in 8 is left.
11. (odd) Three of these animals carry oxygen in their blood. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Human / Octopus / Cockroach / Earthworm  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A cockroach's haemolymph carries almost no oxygen, because air tubes take oxygen to the cells. The other three carry oxygen with haemoglobin or haemocyanin.
12. (write) A model always leaves something out. Name one thing the Half-Breath Test leaves out about real lungs.  
   **Answer:** Any one of these. In real lungs the blood takes oxygen out and puts carbon dioxide in all the time. Real air is a gas and mixes without a spoon. Real breaths vary in size. Some of each breath only fills the airways.. *Why:* A strong answer names a real difference and says how it might change the result. A model is useful because it is simple, and honest only when we say what it leaves out.
13. (write) Explain why the blood in the Bean Gill collects more beans moving against the water.  
   **Answer:** Moving against the water, a blood cup meets fresher and fresher water as it fills. Just before it leaves, it meets a new water cup with 16 beans. So at every stop the water has more beans than the blood, and beans keep crossing.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the fullest blood meets the freshest water, so there is a difference at every stop. Moving the same way, the difference is gone after the first stop.
14. (draw) Draw an insect's air tubes from one spiracle to a muscle cell. Mark where the oxygen dissolves.  
   **Answer:** A hole in the body wall leads to a wide tube. The tube branches into finer and finer tubes. The finest tip touches the muscle cell and holds a little liquid. The oxygen dissolves in that liquid and diffuses into the cell.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the tubes getting finer, ending at the cell itself, with no blood vessel in between.
15. (perspective) Are your breaths separate things, one after another?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. For example: in the Half-Breath Test the water never came from one pour only, so the air inside is shared between breaths. But the movements of the chest can still be counted.. *Why:* Each view has something right. The movements of breathing are separate, and the air inside is mixed. A strong answer says which of the two it is talking about.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which two systems meet in the alveoli?  
   Options: Respiratory and circulatory / Digestive and nervous / Skeleton and muscles / Skin and kidneys  
   **Answer:** Respiratory and circulatory. *Why:* The respiratory system brings air to the alveoli. The circulatory system brings blood to the capillaries wrapped round them.
2. (mcq) Why can you never breathe out all your air?  
   Options: The nose is too narrow / Some air always stays in the lungs / The heart is in the way / Air is too heavy  
   **Answer:** Some air always stays in the lungs. *Why:* The chest cannot squeeze the lungs flat. About a litre stays in an adult's lungs after the hardest breath out.
3. (mcq) A tumbler loses half its dark water with each 'breath'. When is all the first water gone?  
   Options: After 1 breath / After 2 breaths / After 3 breaths / Never completely  
   **Answer:** Never completely. *Why:* Each breath leaves half of what was there: a half, a quarter, an eighth, and so on. The amount gets too small to see, but halving never reaches zero.
4. (mcq) What holds the thin plates of a gill apart?  
   Options: Small bones / The water between them / Air bubbles / A muscle in each plate  
   **Answer:** The water between them. *Why:* The plates float free in water. This is why they cling together when a fish is lifted into air.
5. (mcq) Blood and water flow the same way past each other. How much oxygen can the blood collect at most?  
   Options: About half / Almost all / None / About a tenth  
   **Answer:** About half. *Why:* Oxygen crosses until blood and water hold equal amounts, and then it stops. Flowing opposite ways, the blood can collect far more.
6. (mcq) In which animal does blood go from the breathing organ straight on to the body?  
   Options: A crow / A human / A fish / A crocodile  
   **Answer:** A fish. *Why:* A fish has single circulation. Blood leaves the gills and goes on to the body without returning to the heart first.
7. (mcq) Why does your right ventricle push more gently than your left?  
   Options: It is a tired muscle / Lungs need very little blood / It rests on every second beat / Lung capillaries have very thin walls  
   **Answer:** Lung capillaries have very thin walls. *Why:* The walls between air and blood in the lungs are extremely thin, so the blood there is kept at low pressure. All of the blood still passes through the lungs.
8. (mcq) Where does the haemolymph of a grasshopper flow?  
   Options: Inside capillaries / Through the body spaces, round the organs / Only in the wings / Inside the air tubes  
   **Answer:** Through the body spaces, round the organs. *Why:* A grasshopper has an open circulatory system. The tube heart pumps haemolymph out into the spaces of the body.
9. (mcq) What shares the work of breathing with a frog's lungs?  
   Options: Thin, wet skin / A diaphragm / Air sacs / Spiracles  
   **Answer:** Thin, wet skin. *Why:* A frog's skin is thin, wet and full of capillaries, and a large share of the oxygen crosses there. Frogs have no diaphragm.
10. (mcq) What do a bird's air sacs do?  
   Options: Store food for long flights / Take up most of the oxygen / Push air one way through the lungs / Pump blood to the wings  
   **Answer:** Push air one way through the lungs. *Why:* The air sacs are bellows. Oxygen is taken up in the stiff lungs, and the sacs keep air moving through them in one direction.
11. (mcq) In the Squirt Test, what gave the water its push back after the cotton wool?  
   Options: A thicker plug / A second squeeze / More water / A longer nozzle  
   **Answer:** A second squeeze. *Why:* The water lost its push in the plug. Only a second squeeze sent it far again. In your body, the left ventricle gives that second push.

## Applications
- A pulse oximeter is the small clip a nurse puts on a finger. It shines red light through the finger to measure how much of the haemoglobin is carrying oxygen.
- During some heart operations, a heart-lung machine takes over both systems. It adds oxygen to the blood and pumps it round the body while the heart is still.
- Babies born very early do not yet make enough surfactant, the soap-like liquid that stops the alveoli from sticking shut. Doctors now give it to them through a tube, and it has saved many lives.
- A dialysis machine cleans the blood of a person whose kidneys have failed. The blood and the cleaning liquid flow in opposite directions on two sides of a thin sheet, as blood and water do in a gill.
- Factories and power stations use counter-flow heat exchangers. A hot liquid and a cold liquid flow past each other in opposite directions, and nearly all the heat passes across.
- Fish farmers in Andhra Pradesh and West Bengal run paddle wheels in their ponds on hot nights. The wheels stir the water, so more of it meets the air and takes in oxygen.
- Health workers stop mosquitoes by covering water tanks and emptying old tyres and pots. A larva that has no still water surface to hang from has no way to reach air.

## Spotted around us
- At a fish stall, the seller lifts the gill cover to show bright red gills. The red is blood, very close to the surface of thousands of thin plates.
- Fish in a warm or crowded aquarium gather at the top and gulp. The water near the surface holds the most oxygen. A bubbler helps by stirring the water, so more of it meets the air.
- Mosquito larvae in a forgotten bucket hang from the surface, breathing air through a tube. A tight lid stops mosquitoes from laying eggs there at all.
- A crow resting on a wall. Look at the tail and the back, which bob gently as the air sacs fill and empty.
- A frog calling after the first monsoon rain. Watch the floor of the mouth pump up and down as the frog pushes air into the lungs.
- Live crabs and murrel at a market, kept in very little water. Crabs hold water round their gills inside the shell, and a murrel can gulp air.
- Mudskippers on the mud of a mangrove shore, as in the Sundarbans. They are fish who walk on land with their gill chambers full of water, and they also take in oxygen through wet skin.
- A clip on a finger at a clinic, with a red glow. It is a pulse oximeter. It shines light through the finger to measure how much of the haemoglobin is carrying oxygen.

## Open questions
- **Why do mammals have lungs that mix old air with new, when birds have lungs that air flows straight through?** Both kinds came from the simple lungs of early land animals. Steady blood gases, saving water and the way each group's ribs and muscles work have all been suggested. No single answer is agreed.
- **Why do many resting insects shut their spiracles for minutes and then open them in a burst?** Scientists have measured this pattern in many insects. Some think it saves water. Some think it helps in burrows with stale air. Others think it protects the cells from too much oxygen. The tests so far do not settle it.
- **How do bar-headed geese keep flapping in air that holds less than half the oxygen at sea level?** Trackers show that they follow valleys and climb in stages. Their haemoglobin holds oxygen more tightly, and their flight muscles have extra capillaries. How all of this adds up during a real climb is still being measured.
- **Will icefishes survive as the sea round Antarctica warms?** With no haemoglobin, an icefish depends on very cold water that holds a great deal of oxygen. Warmer water holds less, and a warmer fish needs more. Nobody yet knows how much warming icefishes can stand.
- **How did birds and mammals each come to have a ventricle divided in two?** The two groups reached four chambers separately. Scientists compare the growing hearts of lizards, turtles, chicks and mice to find what makes the dividing wall form. The full story is not yet worked out.

## Connected maths
- In the Half-Breath Test, each breath leaves half of the old water. What fraction of the first dark water is left after 3 breaths? After 4 breaths? **One eighth after 3 breaths, and one sixteenth after 4.**. After 1 breath, one half is left. After 2, half of a half, which is one quarter. After 3, half of a quarter, which is one eighth. After 4, half of an eighth, which is one sixteenth. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- Half-breaths cleared a tumbler in 7 breaths. Quarter-breaths cleared it in 15. Predict the number of breaths if each one changes an eighth. Say how you worked it out. **About 30 breaths.**. When the breath was made half as big, the count went from 7 to 15, which is about double. Halving the breath again should about double the count again: 15 × 2 = 30. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- After a quiet breath out, an adult's lungs hold 2,100 millilitres of air. The next breath brings 350 millilitres of new air to the alveoli. What fraction of the mixture is new air? **One seventh.**. The mixture is 2,100 + 350 = 2,450 millilitres. The new air is 350 of 2,450. Divide both by 350: 350 ÷ 350 = 1 and 2,450 ÷ 350 = 7. So one seventh is new. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the Bean Gill, blood moving with the water collected 8 of 16 beans. Moving against it, the blood collected 12 of 16. Write each as a percentage. Why can the first never pass 50%? **50% and 75%. Moving the same way, the two cups become equal, and equal means half each.**. 8 of 16 is one half, which is 50%. 12 of 16 is three quarters, which is 75%. Beans move only from the fuller cup to the emptier one. Travelling together, the cups stop sharing when they are equal. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In a Squirt Test, the open bottle squirted 210, 240 and 225 centimetres. Through cotton wool it squirted 45, 60 and 50 centimetres. Find the farthest of each. How many times as far did the open bottle squirt? **240 centimetres and 60 centimetres. The open bottle squirted 4 times as far.**. The farthest open squirt was 240 centimetres, and the farthest through the plug was 60 centimetres. 240 ÷ 60 = 4. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L5`)
- A litre of air holds about 210 cubic centimetres of oxygen. A litre of pond water holds about 8. About how many litres of water hold as much oxygen as 1 litre of air? **About 26 litres.**. Divide 210 by 8. 8 × 26 = 208, which is close to 210. So about 26 litres of water hold what 1 litre of air holds. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- A child's tumbler looked clear after only 3 half-breaths. Most families need 6 to 9. Give two things that could explain the low count. **The dark water may have been pale at the start. Or more than half was poured out each time.**. After 3 half-breaths, one eighth of the dark water should be left. Strong tea still shows at one eighth. So either the starting colour was weak, or each pour went below the half line. Poor light or no white paper could also hide a faint colour. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Where does oxygen cross from air into your blood? → In the alveoli, the tiny air sacs of the lungs.
- Can you breathe out all the air in your lungs? → No. Some air always stays behind.
- Which way does water flow over a fish's gills? → One way: in at the mouth, out behind the gill cover.
- How many chambers has a fish's heart? A frog's? Yours? → Two, three and four.
- What is an open circulatory system? → One where the blood leaves the vessels and washes round the organs.
- How does air reach the cells of an insect? → Through spiracles and air tubes called tracheae.
- Which side of your heart pushes harder? → The left side, which sends blood to the whole body.
- Why do gills fail in air? → The wet plates cling together, so the surface is lost.
