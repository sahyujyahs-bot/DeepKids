# How people learned about the sky, and made clocks and calendars from it
`sci.sky.history-of-astronomy.L3`

**Summary.** Long before clocks and phones, people in Babylon, Greece, India, China and the Maya lands watched the sky every night and wrote down what they saw. They found that the sky repeats: the Sun gives the day, the Moon gives the month, and the Sun's slow journey gives the year. Every calendar in the world is built from these three repeats. To count time at night, people made water clocks, such as the Indian ghati, a bowl with a hole that sinks in a set time. In this unit you build one, and find out how steady it really is. You also meet Parameshvara, a Kerala astronomer who showed that old tables of the sky had drifted, by watching eclipses for most of his life.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes. Say 'now' when you think one minute has passed. A grown-up checks with a clock. Were you early or late? Before clocks, how did people know the time at night?* Your own sense of time drifts, so people looked for something steadier. They found it in the sky. The Sun rises again every morning. The Moon grows full about every 29 and a half days. The Sun comes back to the same place among the seasons every 365 days. People counted these repeats and made calendars. They made water clocks to count the hours between sunset and sunrise. In this unit you make a sinking cup clock, test how steady it is, and meet the sky-watchers who taught the world to count time.

## Terms
- **Astronomy**: The study of the Sun, Moon, stars, planets and everything else in the sky. *e.g.* Watching the Moon each night and drawing its shape is a small piece of astronomy.
- **Astronomer**: A person who studies the sky. Long ago, astronomers watched with their eyes and wrote down what they saw. *e.g.* Aryabhata was an astronomer in India about 1,500 years ago.
- **Calendar**: A way of counting days, months and years, so people can plan ahead and agree on dates. *e.g.* The calendar on your kitchen wall shows Pournami, Amavasya and festival days.
- **Lunar month**: The time from one full Moon to the next, about 29 and a half days. 'Lunar' means to do with the Moon. *e.g.* From one Pournami to the next is one lunar month.
- **Solar year**: The time the Earth takes to go once round the Sun, about 365 and a quarter days. It brings the seasons back. 'Solar' means to do with the Sun. *e.g.* Pongal comes in the middle of January every year, because it follows the Sun.
- **Nakshatra**: One of 27 star groups along the Moon's path in Indian astronomy. The Moon moves into a new one about every day. *e.g.* Onam falls when the Moon is in the nakshatra Thiruvonam.
- **Nazhigai**: An old Indian unit of time, 24 minutes long. There are 60 nazhigai in one day and night. In Sanskrit it is called a ghatika or ghati. *e.g.* A Tamil panchangam still gives some times in nazhigai.
- **Water clock**: A clock that measures time by water flowing in or out of a bowl at a steady rate. *e.g.* The Indian ghati was a copper bowl with a tiny hole. Floating in water, it filled and sank in one nazhigai.
- **Sundial**: A clock that tells the time from the shadow of a stick or a wall in sunlight. *e.g.* The huge sundial at Jantar Mantar in Jaipur casts a shadow that moves across a curved scale.
- **Eclipse**: When one sky body blocks the light of another. In a lunar eclipse, the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. *e.g.* Old astronomers could say in advance on which night a lunar eclipse would happen.
- **Record**: A written note of what you saw and when, kept so you or others can look back at it. *e.g.* Babylonian sky-watchers wrote nightly records on clay tablets for hundreds of years.
- **Cycle**: Something that happens again and again in the same order, taking the same time. *e.g.* Full Moon, half Moon, no Moon, half Moon, full Moon again is a cycle.
- **Prediction**: A careful guess about what will happen, made from what you already know. *e.g.* Knowing the Moon's cycle, you can predict the date of the next Pournami.
- **Observatory**: A place built for watching the sky and measuring it. *e.g.* Jantar Mantar in Jaipur and Delhi are observatories built of stone, nearly 300 years ago.
- **Solstice**: The day when the Sun rises and sets furthest north or furthest south in the year. It seems to stop there and turn back. *e.g.* Around 21 June, the Sun sets at its furthest point north, so the day is longest in India.

## Explanations
### The sky repeats · *defined*
**Definition:** The sky has three big repeats. The Earth turns once a day. The Moon goes through its shapes in about 29 and a half days. The Earth goes round the Sun in about 365 and a quarter days.

Every morning the Sun rises in the east, crosses the sky and sets in the west. This happens because the Earth turns once each day. Every night the Moon looks a little different. After about 29 and a half days it comes back to the same shape. Over the year, the Sun rises a little further north or south each day, and the noon shadow grows longer and shorter. After about 365 days it starts again. People everywhere noticed these three repeats. Each one became a unit for counting time: the day, the month and the year.
- e.g. The word 'month' comes from the same old word as 'Moon'.
- e.g. In Tamil, 'thingal' can mean both the Moon and a month.
- e.g. The Sun takes about 365 days to come back to rising at the same point on the horizon.
### Calendars from the Sun and from the Moon · *defined*
**Definition:** A solar calendar follows the Sun, so it stays with the seasons. A lunar calendar follows the Moon's months. A calendar that uses both adds an extra month now and then to stay in step.

Twelve Moon months add up to about 354 days. That is about 11 days short of the Sun's year. So a calendar of only Moon months slides away from the seasons. The Islamic Hijri calendar is a Moon calendar, so Ramzan comes about 11 days earlier each year. Most Indian Hindu calendars use both. They count Moon months, but add an extra month, the adhika masa, about every two and a half to three years. Then the festivals stay in their seasons. The calendar on most Indian walls today, the Gregorian calendar, follows only the Sun. It adds a leap day on 29 February in most fourth years.
- e.g. Pongal follows the Sun, so it falls in the middle of January every year.
- e.g. Deepavali follows the Moon, so its date moves between October and November.
- e.g. Onam needs both: the Sun's month of Chingam and the Moon in the nakshatra Thiruvonam.
### Clocks before machines · *defined*
**Definition:** Before clocks with gears, people used shadows to tell the time by day, and water flowing through a small hole to measure time at night.

A stick in the sun casts a shadow that moves as the Earth turns. That is a sundial. But a sundial is no use at night or on a cloudy day. So people made water clocks. In Babylon, Egypt and China, water dripped out of one pot into another, and marks showed the time. In India, astronomers used the ghati, also called the ghatika. It was a copper bowl with a tiny hole in the bottom. It floated in a big pot of water, slowly filled, and sank. Then someone emptied it and floated it again. The old Indian astronomy book called the Surya Siddhanta describes such a bowl. The bowl was made so that it sank 60 times in one day and night, once every 24 minutes. The Tamil word for this time is nazhigai, and the Hindi word for a clock, ghadi, comes from ghati.
- e.g. The Tamil unit nazhigai is 24 minutes, one sixtieth of a day.
- e.g. An old water pot found at Karnak in Egypt, more than 3,000 years old, has marks inside for the hours.
- e.g. In China, an official named Su Song built a tall water-powered clock tower that also turned a model of the sky, around the year 1090.
### The Babylonians: writing the sky down · *defined*
**Definition:** The Babylonians lived in what is now Iraq. They wrote down what they saw in the sky night after night on clay tablets, and found patterns that let them predict eclipses.

The Babylonians wrote by pressing a reed into wet clay. For hundreds of years, their sky-watchers kept records of the Moon, the planets and the weather. Many of these tablets still survive. Because they kept records for so long, they found repeats too slow to notice in one lifetime. Eclipses of the Moon follow a pattern that repeats about every 18 years, and the Babylonians used it to say in advance when one might happen. They counted in sixties. That is why we still have 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour and 360 degrees in a circle.
- e.g. Babylonian records of the planet Venus rising and setting were written on clay more than 2,500 years ago.
- e.g. Thousands of Babylonian sky tablets are kept in the British Museum in London.
- e.g. Every time you read a clock, you count minutes the Babylonian way, in sixties.
### The Greeks: asking why, and measuring the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Greek thinkers tried to explain why the sky moves as it does, and used shadows and angles to measure the size of the Earth.

Around 240 BCE, Eratosthenes worked in the great library of Alexandria in Egypt. He learned that at noon on one day of the year, the Sun shone straight down a well in the town of Syene, further south. On the same day in Alexandria, a stick cast a short shadow. From the angle of that shadow and the distance between the towns, he worked out the size of the whole Earth, and got close to the real size. Hipparchus made a list of about 850 stars and found that the stars slowly shift over centuries. Most Greeks thought the Earth stood still in the middle of everything, with the Sun and planets on perfect circles. Hypatia, a woman astronomer and teacher in Alexandria, worked on the great astronomy book by Ptolemy about 1,600 years ago.
- e.g. Eratosthenes measured the Earth with a shadow, a distance and some careful thinking, without leaving Egypt.
- e.g. Aristarchus, a Greek, said the Earth goes round the Sun, but almost nobody agreed with him for nearly 1,800 years.
- e.g. Hypatia taught astronomy and mathematics in Alexandria until she was killed in the year 415.
### India: Jyotisha, nakshatras and the Kerala astronomers · *defined*
**Definition:** Jyotisha is the old Indian study of the sky and of time. Indian astronomers divided the Moon's path into 27 nakshatras, worked out calendars, and calculated eclipses.

The oldest Indian book about the sky is the Vedanga Jyotisha. It is more than 2,000 years old, though its exact age is argued about. It sets out a calendar for rituals, using the Sun, the Moon and the nakshatras. The Moon takes about 27 days to go round the sky past the same stars, so it spends about one day in each nakshatra. Many month names come from the nakshatra where the full Moon sits. Chaitra comes from Chitra, and Karthigai from Krittika. In 499, Aryabhata wrote that the Earth spins, and that eclipses are shadows. Much later, astronomers in Kerala, such as Madhava, Parameshvara and Nilakantha, did new mathematics and checked their numbers against the sky. Around 1730, Raja Jai Singh II built huge stone observatories, the Jantar Mantars, in Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi and Mathura.
- e.g. The Tamil month Karthigai is named after Krittika, the star group also called the Pleiades.
- e.g. Karthigai Deepam is lit on the full Moon when the Moon is in Krittika.
- e.g. The Jantar Mantar in Jaipur has a sundial as tall as a nine-storey building.
### China: the longest records of the sky · *defined*
**Definition:** Chinese astronomers kept careful written records of the sky for over 2,000 years, including comets, eclipses and new stars.

In China, astronomers worked for the emperor. Their job was to watch the sky every night and write down anything new. They noted comets, sunspots and eclipses. In the year 1054, they wrote about a 'guest star' that suddenly appeared, so bright it could be seen in the daytime for weeks. Today we know it was an exploding star. Its cloud of leftover gas, the Crab Nebula, is still there, and it is still spreading out. Chinese astronomers also divided the sky along the Moon's path into 28 parts, called lodges, a little like the Indian nakshatras.
- e.g. Chinese records of Halley's Comet go back to 240 BCE.
- e.g. Astronomers today still use the Chinese record of 1054 to work out how old the Crab Nebula is.
- e.g. Wang Zhenyi, a young woman in China in the 1700s, wrote books on astronomy and explained how eclipses happen.
### The Maya: counting Venus · *defined*
**Definition:** The Maya lived in what is now Mexico and Central America. They tracked the planet Venus and built calendars that fit several sky cycles together.

The Maya watched Venus very closely. Venus appears as a bright morning star, disappears, then appears as an evening star, and comes back to the morning about every 584 days. A Maya book called the Dresden Codex has a table of Venus's appearances, worked out far into the future. Five Venus cycles are 2,920 days, which is exactly eight years of 365 days. The Maya also used a 365-day year and a 260-day count. The two counts line up again every 52 years.
- e.g. 5 times 584 is 2,920, and 8 times 365 is also 2,920.
- e.g. The Dresden Codex is a folded Maya book about 800 years old, now kept in Dresden in Germany.
- e.g. At the pyramid of El Castillo in Mexico, around 21 March and 22 September, the late afternoon Sun casts a shadow on the staircase that looks like a snake.
### Why people checked their clocks and calendars against the sky · *defined*
**Definition:** A water clock or a calculated table drifts a little from the truth over time. The sky's repeats are much steadier, so people corrected their clocks and calendars by watching the sky.

A ghati bowl sinks a little faster or slower if the water is warmer, if the hole wears or if it is put down roughly. So astronomers checked it against the Sun and stars. Calculated tables of the sky drift too. Small errors in the numbers add up over hundreds of years. Parameshvara, in Kerala, saw that eclipses were not happening at the times the old tables said. He watched eclipses for decades and wrote a new, corrected system in 1431. Even the second was measured from the Earth's movement until 1967. Since then, it is measured with atomic clocks, which are steadier still.
- e.g. The sinking cup in this unit sinks in a slightly different time each round.
- e.g. Old tables made in 683 had drifted from the sky by the 1400s, and Parameshvara corrected them.
- e.g. Since 1972, a leap second has sometimes been added to clocks, because the Earth's spin is not perfectly steady.
### Why do we count minutes in sixties? · *idea*
We know that the Babylonians counted in sixties, and that we got 60 minutes and 360 degrees from their astronomy. Why they chose 60 is not known for sure. There are a few ideas.
- e.g. 60 can be split evenly into 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30 parts.
- e.g. You can count 12 finger joints on one hand with your thumb, and 5 fingers on the other hand make 5 lots of 12, which is 60.
- **Many historians of mathematics:** 60 splits evenly in many ways, so it is easy to share things into halves, thirds, quarters and fifths without fractions.
- **Some historians:** Two older counting systems, one in tens and one in sixes or twelves, may have been joined together.
- **Others:** People may have counted 12 finger joints on one hand with the thumb, and kept track of 5 lots of 12 on the other hand.
### Did sky-watchers learn from each other? · *debated*
Some ideas clearly travelled. Greek astronomy reached India about 1,800 years ago, and Indian books from that time use some Greek words. Later, Indian numbers and astronomy reached Baghdad, and from there Europe. For older times, historians argue.
- e.g. The Sanskrit word 'hora', for an hour, is thought to come from Greek.
- e.g. Indian books on astronomy were translated into Arabic in Baghdad in the 700s.
- **Some historians:** The Vedanga Jyotisha calendar has numbers similar to Babylonian ones, so they think Babylonian ideas reached India early.
- **Other historians:** The Vedanga Jyotisha grew out of Indian ritual needs, and anyone watching the same sky would find similar numbers. So the likeness does not prove copying.
- **Everyone agrees:** The Maya had no contact with Asia or Europe, and still found the same Moon month and year, because they watched the same sky.

## Mostly agreed
- The day comes from the Earth turning, the month from the Moon's cycle, and the year from the Earth going round the Sun. *How we know:* Anyone can watch the Sun rise each day, the Moon change over a month, and the noon shadow change through the year.
- Twelve Moon months are about 11 days shorter than a solar year. *How we know:* Twelve times 29 and a half is 354 days, and a solar year is about 365 days. Ramzan moves about 11 days earlier each year in the Gregorian calendar.
- Babylonian astronomers kept written records of the sky for centuries, and we still count 60 minutes in an hour and 360 degrees in a circle because of them. *How we know:* Thousands of their clay tablets survive and have been read by historians.
- Chinese astronomers recorded a new star in 1054, and it was an exploding star whose remains are the Crab Nebula. *How we know:* The Crab Nebula is spreading out. Working backwards from its speed gives a start date close to 1054.
- Indian astronomers used sinking bowl water clocks that measured one ghatika, 24 minutes. *How we know:* They are described in Indian astronomy books such as the Surya Siddhanta, and the unit survives in Tamil as nazhigai.

## How we found out
**Parameshvara of Vatasseri, a Kerala astronomer (about 1380 to 1460), Eclipse records from 1393. The Drigganita system was written in 1431..** In Kerala, astronomers worked out eclipses with a system of tables called Parahita. It was made by Haridatta in 683. For centuries, people trusted it to say when the Moon would be eclipsed. Parameshvara was born in about 1380 in Vatasseri, a village on the Nila river in Kerala. He did not only calculate. He went out and watched. He recorded eclipses from 1393 onwards, and he wrote that he had observed the sky for 55 years. He found that the eclipses came at different times from what the Parahita tables said. The old numbers had been very good once, but small errors had added up over 700 years. So in 1431 he wrote a new system with corrected numbers. He called it Drigganita, which means 'calculation that matches what is seen'. The wrong idea was that good tables could predict the sky for ever. The sky itself showed they had drifted. Astronomers in Kerala used his system for centuries.  
*What people believed before:* People thought the Parahita tables, made in 683, could predict eclipses correctly for ever. Parameshvara watched real eclipses for decades and showed that the tables had drifted from the sky, so he made corrected ones.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Ancient people did not know the Earth is round.** (A common idea today; *rejected*)  
  For: Many old pictures show a flat world, and people often say that sailors feared falling off the edge.  
  Against: Greek thinkers knew the Earth is round by about 350 BCE, from the curved shadow of the Earth on the Moon during eclipses. Eratosthenes measured its size. Aryabhata in India also wrote that the Earth is a sphere.
- **The Earth stands still in the middle, and the Sun, Moon and stars go round it.** (Most Greek astronomers, including Ptolemy, and most of the world for about 1,800 years; *rejected*)  
  For: The ground feels still, and the Sun really does seem to go round us every day.  
  Against: Aryabhata said the Earth spins. Copernicus and Galileo showed that the Earth goes round the Sun. Today spacecraft see it.
- **The heavens are perfect and never change.** (Many Greek thinkers, following Aristotle; *rejected*)  
  For: The stars keep the same patterns year after year, so the sky seems fixed for ever.  
  Against: Chinese astronomers recorded a 'guest star' in 1054 that appeared and faded. Comets come and go. The stars also shift slowly over centuries, as Hipparchus found.
- **The Maya calendar said the world would end in 2012.** (Many films, websites and newspapers around 2012; *rejected*)  
  For: One long Maya count of days reached a round number in December 2012.  
  Against: Maya experts say it was like a car's meter rolling over to a new round number. Maya writings mention dates long after 2012. Nothing happened.
- **The positions of the planets when you are born decide what kind of person you will be.** (Astrology, in many cultures. In India, the old Jyotisha books included both astronomy and astrology.; *rejected*)  
  For: Astrology is very old, many families use it, and people often feel their reading fits them.  
  Against: When scientists tested it, astrologers could not match birth charts to people's characters better than by chance. One such test was published in the journal Nature in 1985. Astronomy, the study of where things are and how they move, works very well.
- **Water clocks were too rough to be useful.** (A common idea today; *rejected*)  
  For: A bowl with a hole can be put down roughly, the hole can wear, and the water temperature changes.  
  Against: Astronomers checked them against the stars, and made them carefully. Water clocks were used for over 2,000 years in Egypt, Babylon, Greece, China, India and the Arab world. Your own cup clock may be steady to within a few seconds.

## Roots
We have built a water clock, tested how steady it is, and met the people who learned to count time from the sky. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your cup sank in nearly the same time each round, but never exactly the same. Clocks made of water, sand or springs drift a little like this. The Sun, Moon and stars repeat far more steadily. So for thousands of years, people set their clocks and calendars by the sky.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think time comes from the clock on the wall or the phone, and the sky just goes along with it. It is the other way round. The day, the month and the year were all counted from the sky, and the second itself was measured from the Earth's movement until 1967.  
*Evidence:* In the Sinking Cup Clock, three rounds with the same cup gave close but different times. Parameshvara found that even very good tables of the sky drifted over 700 years, and he corrected them by watching real eclipses.

- *who am i:* Your body keeps rhythms too: a heartbeat, a breath, and sleep each night. They drift, like your guess of a minute.
- *what is life:* Many plants measure how long the night is, and flower when the nights reach the right length. They keep a calendar of their own.
- *what is this world:* The world turns, the Moon circles and the seasons return. Every calendar on Earth is a way of counting those repeats.

Everything people count as time comes from a few movements in the sky. One turn of the Earth gives the day. The Moon going round gives the month. The Earth going round the Sun gives the year. From these few movements came many counts: nazhigai and hours, weeks and months, festivals, harvests and birthdays. Babylonians, Greeks, Indians, Chinese and Maya made very different calendars, and all of them count the same sky.

- **Rig Veda 1.164.48:** There is one wheel with twelve parts and three hubs. Three hundred and sixty spokes are fixed in it, and they cannot come loose. Who understands it? (The wheel is the year. Its twelve parts are like the months, and the 360 spokes are like the days. Old Indian sky-watchers saw the year as one turning wheel, the same repeat your Window Calendar shows.)
- **Ecclesiastes 3.1, in the Bible:** For everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven. (Farmers everywhere needed to know when to sow and when to harvest. Calendars from the sky told them, because the seasons come back with the Sun's year.)
- Science (settled): The day, the month and the year come from the Earth's spin, the Moon's orbit and the Earth's orbit round the Sun.
- Science (settled): The Earth's spin is very slowly getting slower, mostly because of the Moon's pull on the oceans, so days were shorter long ago.
- Science (open): What time itself is, and why it seems to flow only one way, is not known.
- Other view, Many physicists and clock makers today: The sky is no longer the best clock. Since 1967, the second is measured with atoms, which are far steadier than the Earth's spin. We now correct our clocks against atoms, and add leap seconds to keep them near the Earth.
- Practice, Feel your own clocks (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Put two fingers on the side of your neck. Can you feel your heartbeat? Count your heartbeats while I count 15 seconds. Now count your breaths for one minute. Open your eyes. Look out of the window at the sky. Your heart and breath are clocks inside you. The sky is the big clock outside.
- Ask: Your heartbeat, your cup clock and the turning Earth all repeat. Which one would you trust to count a whole year, and why?
- Ask: If people on Mars made a calendar, would it be the same as ours? What would be different?
- Ask: Is time something real in the world, or only our way of counting the sky's repeats?

## Story: Paati's Night Clock
*The story is made up. The facts in it are true: a nazhigai is 24 minutes, the old Indian ghati bowl sank once each nazhigai, and the Moon moves across the sky by about one fist held at arm's length every 40 minutes.*

It was a Sunday night in Madurai, and Sunday nights in Meenakshi's family had one rule. No phones, no TV, no screens of any kind. All the phones went into a steel box on top of the fridge at six o'clock.

Meenakshi was nine. Her brother Karthik was six. Amma had gone to Chennai for work, and her train would reach Madurai at nine o'clock. Appa was going to the station on his scooter to fetch her.

"How long till nine?" asked Karthik, for the fourth time.

Just before Appa went out to buy milk, he had said, "It is half past seven." Now he was gone. The wall clock in the hall had stopped, because its cell was flat. And the phones were in the box.

"We could open the box," said Karthik.

"Rule is rule," said Paati, their grandmother. She was sitting on the swing on the balcony, looking at the sky. "When I was small, in our village, there was no clock in our house at all. Do you know how my own paati knew the time?"

Karthik shook his head.

"By day, she looked at shadows. At night, she looked up." Paati pointed. The Moon was rising over the temple towers, big and nearly full. "The sky turns all night, slowly and steadily. Watch the Moon, and you can tell how much time has gone by."

"But how much is a lot of Moon?" asked Karthik.

Paati held her fist out at the end of her straight arm. "The Moon moves about this far, one fist, in a little less than an hour. About 40 minutes." She closed one eye. "Right now, the Moon is one fist above the top of the east tower."

"That is too slow to watch," said Karthik. "I want to count."

Meenakshi had an idea. She had read about it in her science book. "In the old days, astronomers had a clock made of water. A copper bowl with a tiny hole. It floats in a big pot, fills up slowly and sinks. Then you empty it and float it again."

"The ghati," said Paati, smiling. "In Tamil we called that time a nazhigai. Twenty-four minutes. Sixty of them in a day and a night."

Meenakshi ran to the kitchen. She found a small plastic cup, the kind left over from a birthday party. Paati heated a needle over the gas flame and made a small hole in the bottom. Meenakshi taped two one-rupee coins inside, so it would sink when full. Karthik dragged in the big steel vessel they used for soaking rice, and filled it with water.

Meenakshi set the cup gently on the water. It bobbed and floated. Then, very slowly, water started to come up through the hole, like a tiny spring.

They waited. The cup sat lower and lower. Then, with a soft gulp, it tipped and went under.

"One!" shouted Karthik.

Meenakshi fished the cup out, poured the water away and floated it again. Karthik made a mark on a paper for each sinking. Two. Three. Four. Five.

"Now we know how long one sinking is," said Karthik proudly.

"Do we?" asked Paati. "What does one sinking mean? Is it one minute? Ten minutes?"

Karthik frowned. The cup could count. But it could not tell him what it was counting.

Meenakshi looked up. She held out her fist at the end of her arm, the way Paati had. The Moon was now about one fist higher than before, two fists above the tower.

"The Moon has moved one fist," she said slowly. "That is about 40 minutes. And the cup sank five times. So one sinking is about 8 minutes."

Paati clapped her hands. "Now your clock has a meaning. The cup counts. The sky tells you what the count is worth."

"So how long till nine?" asked Karthik.

They worked it out together. Appa had said half past seven just when Paati first looked at the Moon. Forty minutes later made it about ten past eight. That left about 50 minutes till nine, which was about six more sinkings.

Karthik counted every one. The cup filled, tipped and sank. Meenakshi emptied it each time. On the sixth sinking, Karthik shouted, "Nine o'clock!"

Just then Appa came up the stairs with the milk. He had stopped to talk to a neighbour on the way. "What time is it?" both children shouted. Appa looked at his watch. "Six minutes past nine. Why?"

"We were only six minutes off!" said Meenakshi. "And we used a cup and the Moon." Appa laughed, picked up his keys and hurried off to the station.

"The cup was not quite the same each time," Meenakshi told Paati. "Once I put it down hard, and it sank faster."

"That is why the old astronomers checked their water clocks against the sky," said Paati. "The cup can be bumped. The Moon cannot."

When Amma came home, Karthik was already asleep on the swing, with five marks, and then six more, on his paper.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a water clock counts steadily, but on its own it does not say how long each count is. Meenakshi gave her cup a meaning by comparing it with the sky. The Moon moved one fist, about 40 minutes, while the cup sank five times, so each sinking was about 8 minutes. Old astronomers did the same with the ghati bowl. They made it sink in one nazhigai, and kept checking it against the Sun and stars, because a bowl can drift and the sky drifts far less.

- Ask: Why could the cup count, but not tell Karthik the time on its own?
- Ask: How did Meenakshi work out how long one sinking took?
- Ask: Why did the cup sink faster when Meenakshi put it down hard?
- Ask: Paati said the cup can be bumped but the Moon cannot. What did she mean?
- Ask: How could you use your fist and the Moon to tell how long a film lasts?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Sinking Cup Clock: does it sink in the same time every round? (40 min)
- **question:** Indian astronomers used a bowl with a hole that sank in a set time. If you float the same cup again and again, does it sink in exactly the same time each round?
- **predict:** Will the cup take exactly the same time each round, nearly the same, or very different times? Why do you think so? Then guess: if the hole is made a little bigger, will the cup sink faster or slower, and why?
- **fair test:** Use the same cup, the same hole, the same vessel and the same water for rounds 1, 2 and 3. Lower the cup gently, flat on the water, each time. Start the timer the moment the cup touches the water, and stop it the moment the rim goes under. Empty and shake the cup dry before each round. Only in round 4 do you change one thing, the size of the hole.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the hole, because pins and needles are sharp. Do it on a wooden board, pushing away from the hand. Wipe up spilt water at once so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then circle the shortest and the longest time from rounds 1 to 3, and write how many seconds apart they are.
- **results table:** Round | Hole | Time to sink (minutes and seconds) | Anything that went differently
- **s1 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the two coins flat in the centre of the cup's bottom, inside. 2. Push the pin through the middle of the bottom, between the coins if there is a gap, or next to them. 3. Twist the pen refill tip in the hole to widen it to about 2 millimetres, the width of the refill's tip.  
  *Parent says:* Long ago in India, astronomers used a copper bowl with a tiny hole, called a ghati. It floated, slowly filled, and sank. We are making our own. The coins make sure it sinks when it is full.  
  *Child mode:* We are making a water clock. It is a cup with a tiny hole. The coins help it sink.
  - Note: The hole size decides the time. A hole about 2 millimetres across in a 200 ml cup usually takes about 2 to 8 minutes. You will test this in the next step and adjust if needed.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the vessel with water at least 12 cm deep. 2. Lower the cup gently, flat on the water. 3. Start the stopwatch. 4. Watch until the rim goes under, and stop the stopwatch.  
  *Parent says:* Let's try it once, just to see. Watch the bottom of the cup. Can you see water coming in? Tell me when the rim goes under.  
  *Child mode:* Put the cup gently on the water. Start the timer. Watch the water come in. Stop the timer when it sinks.
  - Note: This is a practice round. If the cup takes more than 8 minutes, widen the hole a little and try again. If it sinks in under a minute, start again with a new cup and a smaller hole. Once it takes between 2 and 8 minutes, keep this cup. If the cup tips over early, move the coins to the very centre.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a prediction and a reason, and write both down before the timed rounds.  
  *Parent says:* Now we will float it three times in exactly the same way. Will it take exactly the same time each round? Nearly the same? Or very different? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Same time every round? Nearly the same? Very different? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the times will be the same, or different? (good: Same, because it is the same cup and the same hole.; A little different, because I might put it down differently.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Any reason your child gives is good. Write down the guess, so you can compare it with the results.
- **s4 (18 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty the cup, shake out the water and float it gently. 2. Time it until the rim goes under. 3. Write the time. 4. Do rounds 2 and 3 in exactly the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Let's do round 1. Put it down as gently as you can. Start the timer the moment it touches the water. Now we wait and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Round 1. Shake the cup dry. Float it gently. Start the timer. Write the time when it sinks. Do it three times.
  - Note: The times are usually close but not exactly the same, often a few seconds to about half a minute apart. A drop of water left in the cup, a tilt as it goes down, or a bump on the vessel can change the time. Write down anything that happened differently in the last column. If all three times match to the second, that is a real result too. Ask your child what would happen over 60 rounds.
  - Think: If each round can be a few seconds off, how far off could the cup be after 60 rounds, a whole day and night?
- **s5 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a guess: will a bigger hole make it sink faster or slower, and why? 2. Widen the hole by pushing the refill tip in a little further. 3. Time one more round.  
  *Parent says:* Now we change just one thing, the hole. If the hole is bigger, will the cup sink faster or slower? Why do you think so? Let's find out.  
  *Child mode:* Now the hole is bigger. Will it sink faster or slower? Why? Try it.
  - Note: A bigger hole lets water in faster, so the cup sinks sooner. Old clock makers had to get the hole exactly right, so that the bowl sank once every 24 minutes, one nazhigai.
  - Check: Round 4 had a bigger hole. What happened? → **The cup sank sooner, because water came in faster**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think about our cup clock now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think the times were not all exactly the same?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: what I think, the cup clock is nearly steady but not perfect. What I saw, rounds 1 to 3 took 3 minutes 10, 3 minutes 22 and 3 minutes 5 seconds, so 17 seconds between the shortest and longest. Why, small changes in how I put it down and leftover water changed the time a little. That is why astronomers checked their water clocks against the Sun and stars.
  - Check: Your cup sank in slightly different times each round. What did old astronomers do about this in their water clocks? → **They checked them against the Sun and stars, which repeat more steadily**
### e2 · The Fist Ruler: does a child's fist measure the same piece of sky as a grown-up's? (25 min)
- **question:** Sky-watchers measured how far apart things in the sky were by holding up a fist at arm's length. A child's fist is smaller than a grown-up's. Do they measure the same distance across the sky?
- **predict:** Stand in one spot and measure the gap between two things on a far wall, in fists. Who will count more fists, you or your grown-up? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both of you stand on the same spot, with one eye closed, and the arm fully straight. Measure the same gap three times each and take the middle answer.
- **safety:** Never measure near the Sun. Do this indoors or at night.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Write who counted more fists, and whose fist covers a bigger piece of the view.
- **results table:** Person | Fist width (cm) | Arm length, eye to fist (cm) | Fists across the gap: try 1, 2, 3
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand about 4 metres from a wall with a door or window. 2. Show how to hold a fist at the end of a straight arm, with one eye closed. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Sky-watchers used their fists as a ruler for the sky. Hold your fist out like this, with one eye closed. My fist is bigger than yours. Who do you think will count more fists across that door? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Hold your fist out on a straight arm. Close one eye. Who will count more fists across the door? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: You, because my fist is smaller.; The same, because your arm is longer too.)
  - Note: Let the reason come from your child. Many say the child will count more fists, because a child's fist is smaller. Don't correct them.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start with your fist on one edge of the door. 2. Stack fists one on top of the other, sideways, to the other edge. 3. Count. 4. Each of you does it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Put the side of your fist on the left edge of the door. Now put your other fist next to it, then move the first one along. Count until you reach the right edge.  
  *Child mode:* Fist on the edge. Next fist beside it. Keep going. Count the fists. Do it three times.
  - Note: Keep the arm straight and the head still. Counts usually come out close for the child and the grown-up, because a bigger hand comes with a longer arm. But they can differ by half a fist or more. Whatever you find is a real result.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure each fist across the knuckles, in cm. 2. Measure from the eye to the fist with the arm held out. 3. Write both numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure. How wide is your fist? How far is it from your eye? Now mine.  
  *Child mode:* Measure your fist. Measure your arm, eye to fist. Write both numbers.
  - Note: A grown-up's fist is often about 9 or 10 cm wide at about 60 cm from the eye. A child's might be 6 or 7 cm at about 45 cm. In both, the arm is about 6 to 7 times the fist's width, which is why the counts are often close. An adult's fist at arm's length covers about 10 degrees of sky.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think the counts came out the way they did?  
  *Child mode:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why?
  - Note: A good answer: I thought I would count more fists. We counted 5 and 4 and a half. My arm is shorter, so my small fist is closer and covers more of the view.
  - Check: A grown-up's fist is bigger than a child's. Why can they still measure nearly the same piece of sky? → **The grown-up holds the fist further away, on a longer arm**
### e3 · The Leaking Bottle Clock: does water run out at the same speed all the way? (25 min)
- **question:** Some old water clocks let water drip out of a pot, with marks inside for the hours. Does the water level drop by the same amount every minute?
- **predict:** Make a mark on the bottle every 30 seconds as it empties. Will the marks be the same distance apart, closer together at the bottom, or further apart? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use one bottle, one hole, and mark every 30 seconds exactly. Stand the bottle on the same flat place the whole time.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the hole with the drawing pin. Do this in the bathroom or over a bucket, and wipe up spills.
- **record:** Write the gaps between marks in the table. Draw the bottle with its marks.
- **results table:** Mark number | Time (seconds) | Gap from the last mark (cm)
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push the drawing pin through the side of the bottle about 3 cm from the bottom. 2. Take the pin out and push it through again to make the hole round. 3. Put a strip of tape from top to bottom on the bottle.  
  *Parent says:* In Egypt and Babylon, water clocks let water drip out of a pot. Marks inside showed how much time had passed. Let's make one from a bottle.  
  *Child mode:* We are making a dripping water clock. A grown-up makes a small hole near the bottom.
  - Note: Keep the bottle cap off, or water will not flow. A hole made with a drawing pin gives a thin jet that empties a litre bottle in a few minutes.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason before filling the bottle.  
  *Parent says:* We will mark the water level every 30 seconds. Will the marks be the same distance apart? Closer together near the bottom? Or further apart? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We mark the water every 30 seconds. Same gaps? Bigger gaps? Smaller gaps? Why?
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold a finger over the hole and fill the bottle to the top. 2. Stand it over the bucket. 3. Take your finger away and start the stopwatch. 4. Every 30 seconds, mark the water level on the tape.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? Finger off. Start the timer. Every time I say 'mark', draw a line where the water is.  
  *Child mode:* Take your finger off. Every 30 seconds, draw a line at the water.
  - Note: Watch the jet. At first it shoots far. As the water gets lower, it shoots less far, and the level drops more slowly. So the marks get closer together near the bottom. If the hole clogs, poke it again with the pin.
  - Think: Why does the jet shoot less far when the bottle is nearly empty?
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure the gaps between the marks with a ruler. Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Let's measure the gaps. What do you notice? Why do you think the water slowed down?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each gap. Do they get bigger or smaller? Why?
  - Note: A good answer: what I think, water runs out slower as the bottle empties. What I saw, the first gap was 3 cm and the last was 1 cm. Why, less water is pushing down on the hole.
  - Check: The marks got closer together near the bottom. Why? → **Less water above the hole pushes less hard, so it flows out more slowly**

### Sky-Watchers of the World (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Eratosthenes found that at noon, a stick in Alexandria cast a short shadow, while the Sun shone straight down a well in Syene. What did this tell him?  
   Options: The Earth's surface is curved / The Sun is very small / Alexandria had taller sticks  
   **Answer:** The Earth's surface is curved. *Why:* If the Earth were flat, the Sun would make the same shadow everywhere at once. The different shadows showed the ground is curved, and the size of the difference let him work out how big the Earth is.
2. (write) An eclipse pattern repeats about every 18 years. Why could the Babylonians find this pattern, when one sky-watcher on their own probably could not?  
   **Answer:** The Babylonians kept written records for hundreds of years. Later sky-watchers could read what earlier ones saw. One person sees only a few eclipses in a lifetime and might not remember them exactly. Long written records show patterns that are too slow for one person to notice.. *Why:* A strong answer links the long records to slow patterns.
3. (match) Match each sky-watcher to what they did.  
   **Answer:** Aryabhata → wrote that the Earth spins; Eratosthenes → measured the size of the Earth; Parameshvara → corrected old eclipse tables by watching; Jai Singh II → built the Jantar Mantar observatories; Hypatia → taught astronomy in Alexandria. *Why:* Each one added something different: an idea, a measurement, a correction, a building and teaching.
4. (perspective) People long ago argued about whether the Earth moves.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks Aryabhata, with evidence such as photos from space or Foucault's pendulum, and the boat example. 5. (truefalse) The Maya learned how to count Venus from the Babylonians.

   **Answer:** The planet Venus. *Why:* The Maya Dresden Codex has a table of Venus, which comes back every 584 days.
8. (mcq) What did Chinese astronomers record in 1054?  
   Options: A new 'guest star', which was an exploding star / The first telescope / The Earth going round the Sun  
   **Answer:** A new 'guest star', which was an exploding star. *Why:* They recorded a guest star bright enough to see by day. It was an exploding star, and its leftover cloud is the Crab Nebula.
9. (mcq) Parameshvara was an astronomer in Kerala about 600 years ago. How did he find that the old eclipse tables were wrong?  
   Options: He watched real eclipses for many years and compared them with the tables / He read it in a Greek book / He used a telescope  
   **Answer:** He watched real eclipses for many years and compared them with the tables. *Why:* He recorded eclipses for decades and saw they did not come when the old Parahita tables said. In 1431 he wrote a corrected system, Drigganita. Telescopes had not been invented yet.
10. (mcq) Why did astronomers check their water clocks against the Sun and stars?  
   Options: Water clocks drift a little, and the sky's repeats are much steadier / Water clocks only work in the daytime / The stars told them to  
   **Answer:** Water clocks drift a little, and the sky's repeats are much steadier. *Why:* A water clock can run fast or slow if it is bumped, if the water warms, or if the hole changes. The turning sky is much steadier, so it was used to set them right.
11. (mcq) How long is one nazhigai?  
   Options: 24 minutes / 60 minutes / 24 hours  
   **Answer:** 24 minutes. *Why:* There are 60 nazhigai in a day and night of 24 hours, so each one is 24 minutes.
12. (mcq) Karthigai Deepam is lit on a full Moon. Which star group is the Moon in that night?  
   Options: Krittika, the Pleiades / The Great Bear / Orion's belt  
   **Answer:** Krittika, the Pleiades. *Why:* The month Karthigai is named after Krittika. On Karthigai Deepam, the full Moon sits in Krittika.

## Applications
- Every calendar, from the wall calendar to the panchangam, counts the sky's repeats.
- Farmers use the seasons of the solar year to decide when to sow and harvest.
- Festival dates for Deepavali, Eid, Onam and Karthigai Deepam are worked out from the Moon and stars.
- The 60 minutes on every clock and the 360 degrees in a protractor come from Babylonian astronomy.
- Leap years keep the Gregorian calendar in step with the solar year.
- Eclipse predictions today use the same idea as the Babylonians: find the pattern, then predict.
- Old Chinese and Babylonian records help astronomers today date exploding stars and comets.
- Sailors once used the angle of the Pole Star and the Sun to find how far north they were.
- The panchangam in many Tamil and Kerala homes still gives times in nazhigai.

## Spotted around us
- The panchangam or calendar on your kitchen wall, with Pournami and Amavasya marked.
- The word 'ghadi' on a watch shop sign, which comes from the ghati water clock.
- The 60 little marks on a clock face, the Babylonian way of counting.
- Jantar Mantar in Delhi and Jaipur, giant stone instruments for measuring the sky.
- Karthigai Deepam lamps lit on the full Moon in Krittika, in homes and on the hill at Tiruvannamalai.
- The new crescent Moon that families watch for to begin and end Ramzan.
- Your balcony: the Sun setting in a slightly different place each week.

## Open questions
- **Why did the Babylonians count in sixties?** We know they did, but no writing survives that says why. There are several ideas, such as 60 splitting evenly into many parts.
- **Exactly how old is the Vedanga Jyotisha?** Its sky positions and its language point to different dates, and scholars argue over dates from more than 3,000 years ago to about 2,400 years ago.
- **Did Babylonian astronomy reach early India?** Some numbers are similar, but people watching the same sky would find similar numbers anyway. There is no clear record of the journey.
- **Were Maya and other ancient buildings lined up with the Sun on purpose?** Some line up with sunrise or sunset on special days. For many, it is hard to know if the builders meant it, or if it is chance.
- **How exactly were the old ghati bowls checked and made the right size?** The books describe the bowl, but few real old bowls survive, and the details of how makers tested them are not well known.
- **Why does the Earth's spin speed up and slow down by tiny amounts?** Tides, the Earth's core, ice and oceans all play a part. Scientists are still working out exactly how much each one does.

## Connected maths
- One nazhigai is 24 minutes. Meenakshi's family watched a film that lasted 5 nazhigai. How many minutes is that? How many hours? **120 minutes, which is 2 hours.**. 5 lots of 24 minutes. 24 plus 24 is 48. 48 plus 48 is 96. 96 plus 24 is 120. 120 minutes is 2 hours, because each hour is 60 minutes. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Kavya's sinking cup took 3 minutes 10 seconds, 3 minutes 25 seconds and 3 minutes 5 seconds. What is the gap between her fastest and slowest round? Which round was the odd one out? **20 seconds. Round 2 was the odd one out.**. The fastest was 3 minutes 5 seconds, and the slowest 3 minutes 25 seconds. 25 minus 5 is 20 seconds. Rounds 1 and 3 are only 5 seconds apart, but round 2 is 15 seconds slower than round 1. So round 2 was the odd one out. Maybe the cup was put down differently. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Venus comes back to the morning sky every 584 days. A year is 365 days. Is the Venus cycle less than one year, between one and two years, or more than two years? **Between one and two years.**. 584 is more than 365, so it is longer than one year. Two years are 365 plus 365, which is 730 days. 584 is less than 730. So it is between one and two years, about one year and seven months. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Ramzan began on about 1 March in 2025. A Moon calendar year is about 11 days shorter than our year. About when will Ramzan begin in 2026, and in 2027? **About 18 February 2026, and about 7 February 2027.**. 11 days before 1 March is 18 February, because February 2026 has 28 days: 1 March minus 1 day is 28 February, minus 10 more days is 18 February. 11 days before 18 February is 7 February. (queues `math.time.calendar.L3`)
- The marks on Rekha's bottle clock were 3.2 cm, 2.8 cm, 2.3 cm and 1.9 cm apart, one every 30 seconds. About how far apart do you think the next mark will be? Why? **About 1.4 or 1.5 cm.**. Each gap is about half a centimetre, or a little less, smaller than the one before: 3.2 to 2.8 is 0.4 less, 2.8 to 2.3 is 0.5 less, 2.3 to 1.9 is 0.4 less. So the next is about 1.9 minus 0.4 or 0.5, which is about 1.4 or 1.5 cm. The water slows down as the bottle empties. (queues `math.patterns.repeating-patterns.L2`)
- The sky turns about one fist, held at arm's length, every 40 minutes. Meenakshi saw the Moon one fist above a tower at 7:30. About what time will it be three fists above the tower? **About 8:50.**. From one fist to three fists is two fists more. Two lots of 40 minutes is 80 minutes, which is 1 hour 20 minutes. 7:30 plus 1 hour is 8:30, plus 20 minutes is 8:50. (queues `math.time.telling-time.L3`)
