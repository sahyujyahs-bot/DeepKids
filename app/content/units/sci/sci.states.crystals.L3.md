# Crystals: how salt and sugar grow their own flat faces
`sci.states.crystals.L3`

**Summary.** Leave salty water to dry, and the salt comes back as tiny cubes, though nobody cut them. A crystal is a solid whose particles join in the same pattern again and again, so it grows flat faces and sharp corners. Each substance has its own shape: salt makes cubes, phitkari makes two pyramids joined at the base, and sugar makes slanted blocks. In this unit your child dries the same salt water fast on a plate and slowly in a glass, and finds out what decides a crystal's size and what decides its shape. They also crack a crystal, grow their own kalkandu on a thread, and meet the giant crystals of a cave in Mexico.

**Intro.** *Look at a grain of kal uppu, the big crystal salt used in many South Indian kitchens. Its sides are flat and smooth, like a tiny window. Nobody in the salt pan cut or polished it. So where did those flat sides come from?* Salt from the sea is dried in the sun at salt pans, like the ones at Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu. The salt grains grow there by themselves. In this unit you grow salt crystals at home, fast on a plate and slowly in a glass. You look at them closely, crack one open, and compare salt with sugar and phitkari. By the end you will know what gives a crystal its flat faces, and what makes one crystal big and another tiny.

## Terms
- **Crystal**: A solid whose particles are joined in the same pattern, repeated again and again. Because of this pattern, a crystal has flat faces and sharp corners. *e.g.* A grain of salt, a grain of sugar, a lump of kalkandu and an ice cube are all crystals.
- **Face**: A flat side of a crystal. Light bounces off a face like a tiny mirror, so crystals sparkle. *e.g.* A salt cube has 6 faces, just like a dice.
- **Solution**: The clear liquid you get when a solid dissolves in a liquid. Salt water is a solution of salt in water. *e.g.* Salt water, sugar syrup and phitkari water are solutions.
- **Saturated solution**: A solution that holds as much of the solid as it can. If you add more, it just sits at the bottom. *e.g.* Stir salt into a cup of water, spoon after spoon. When salt stays at the bottom however much you stir, the water is saturated.
- **Evaporate**: When a liquid slowly turns into a gas and goes into the air, it evaporates. Water evaporates from wet clothes and puddles. *e.g.* When salty water evaporates, the water goes into the air and the salt stays behind.
- **Crystallise**: When particles come out of a liquid and join together to make a crystal, we say the substance crystallises. *e.g.* Salt crystallises when salty water dries. Honey sometimes crystallises in a cold month and turns grainy.
- **Phitkari (alum)**: A white crystal sold in Indian shops, called alum in English. It dissolves in hot water and grows clear crystals as the water cools. *e.g.* Barbers use a block of phitkari after a shave. In Tamil it is called padikaram.
- **Seed crystal**: A small crystal put into a solution on purpose, so that more particles join it and it grows bigger. *e.g.* To grow one big salt crystal, put one good small cube into fresh saturated salt water.

## Explanations
### What a crystal is · *defined*
**Definition:** A crystal is a solid whose particles are joined in one pattern, repeated again and again in every direction.

Particles are far too small to see. In a salt crystal, the particles sit in straight rows and columns, like the squares on graph paper, but in three directions. The pattern repeats millions of times across one grain. A crystal grows when more particles come out of the liquid and join the outside, each one fitting into the pattern. Because every new particle fits the same pattern, the outside stays flat.
- e.g. A grain of table salt is one small crystal, or a few stuck together.
- e.g. Kalkandu is one big sugar crystal, or a few joined together.
- e.g. Snowflakes are ice crystals. Their six arms come from the six-sided pattern that water particles make when they freeze.
### Why a crystal has flat faces · *defined*
**Definition:** A crystal has flat faces because new particles join it in rows that follow the same pattern. A row that is complete is flat, like a finished row of bricks in a wall.

Think of laying bricks. If every brick goes into the pattern, the wall has a flat side, even though nobody smoothed it. A crystal is the same, with particles instead of bricks. When you cracked a kal uppu crystal, the pieces also had flat sides and square corners. That is because the crystal breaks most easily between its rows of particles, so even the broken sides follow the pattern.
- e.g. Crack a lump of rock salt (sendha namak) and the pieces have flat sides that meet in square corners.
- e.g. A diamond cutter finds the directions in which a diamond splits most easily, and uses them.
### Each substance grows its own shape · *defined*
**Definition:** The particles of each substance join in their own pattern, so each substance grows its own crystal shape.

Salt particles join in a square pattern, so salt grows as cubes. Phitkari particles join in a different pattern, so phitkari crystals grow as two pyramids joined at their base, with triangle faces. Sugar particles join in yet another pattern, so sugar grains are slanted blocks with many faces. One crystal may grow lopsided, with one face bigger than another. But the angles between its faces stay the same for every crystal of that kind.
- e.g. Salt from the sea, salt from a mine and salt from your glass all make cubes.
- e.g. Kalkandu from a shop and sugar grains from the dabba have the same slanted faces.
- e.g. Phitkari grown in your glass has triangle faces, like the phitkari crystals in a museum.
### Slow growth makes bigger crystals · *defined*
**Definition:** When a solution dries or cools slowly, a few crystals start and keep growing, so they become big. When it dries or cools fast, very many crystals start at once, so each stays tiny.

On the plate, the water was thin and spread out, so it evaporated in a few hours. Salt particles were pushed out of the water all at once, and thousands of tiny crystals started everywhere. In the glass, the water evaporated slowly over days. Only a few crystals started, and new particles had time to join them. So the glass grew fewer, bigger crystals. Both were the same salt, and both made cubes.
- e.g. At the salt pans, slow drying grows bigger grains than fast drying.
- e.g. Kalkandu makers leave their syrup still for days, so the crystals grow big. Stirring and fast cooling give fine sugar instead.
- e.g. The biggest crystals ever found, in the Cave of the Crystals in Mexico, grew in warm water that changed very slowly for hundreds of thousands of years.
### Not every solid is a crystal · *defined*
**Definition:** In some solids, the particles are jumbled, with no repeating pattern. These solids are not crystals, and they have no flat faces of their own.

Glass is made from sand, and sand is mostly bits of quartz crystal. But glass is made by melting the sand and cooling it fast, so its particles freeze in a jumble. A boiled sweet is the same: it is sugar, cooled so fast from a hot syrup that the particles had no time to join in a pattern. So the same sugar can be a crystal, like kalkandu, or not a crystal, like a clear boiled sweet.
- e.g. A glass bangle is not a crystal. When it breaks, the broken edges are curved, not flat.
- e.g. A clear boiled sweet is sugar that is not a crystal. Kalkandu is sugar that is a crystal.
### How the very first tiny crystal starts · *idea*
Before a crystal can grow, a few particles must come together and stay together. Scientists call this start 'nucleation'. They know it happens more easily on a scratch, a speck of dust or a thread. That is why salt crystals often start at the edge of the water, and why kalkandu grows on a thread. But exactly how the first few particles find each other and stay together is still being studied. Some scientists think the particles first gather in a loose cloud, and only later line up into a pattern.
- e.g. A rough thread grows more crystals than a smooth glass wall.
- e.g. A seed crystal skips this step, because the pattern is already there for new particles to join.

## Mostly agreed
- A crystal is a solid whose particles are arranged in a pattern that repeats. *How we know:* Since 1912, scientists have shone X-rays through crystals. The X-rays bounce off the rows of particles and make neat patterns of spots, which show how the particles are arranged.
- Every crystal of one substance has the same angles between its faces. *How we know:* Nicolas Steno saw this in quartz in 1669. Scientists have measured it in thousands of kinds of crystals since then.
- Slow growth makes fewer, bigger crystals. Fast growth makes many small ones. *How we know:* Anyone can test it at home with salt water on a plate and in a glass. Factories that make sugar and salt control the speed to get the grain size they want.
- Most rocks, most metals and the enamel on your teeth are made of crystals. *How we know:* Under a microscope, rocks and metals show many small crystals packed together. X-rays show the crystal pattern in tooth enamel.

## How we found out
**Nicolas Steno, a Danish doctor and scientist who worked in Florence, Italy, 1669, with more measuring in France in the 1770s and 1780s.** Clear quartz, called rock crystal, has been collected for thousands of years. No two pieces look alike. Some are long and thin, some short and fat, some lopsided. So it seemed that crystals of one kind could grow in any shape, with no rule. In 1669, Nicolas Steno, a Danish doctor working in Florence, Italy, looked more carefully. He studied many quartz crystals and drew their outlines when cut across. The faces were of every size. But when he compared the angle between one face and the next, it was the same in every crystal. A lopsided crystal had the same angles as a neat one. Only the sizes of the faces had changed. Steno also wrote that a crystal grows by adding new layers to its outside, from the liquid around it. About a hundred years later, scientists in France measured many kinds of crystals with a new tool for measuring angles. Each kind had its own fixed angles. Today we know why. The particles in each kind of crystal always join in the same pattern.  
*What people believed before:* Crystals of one kind can grow in any shape, because no two look alike.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Clear rock crystal is ice that was frozen so hard it can never melt.** (The Roman writer Pliny the Elder, about 2,000 years ago. The word 'crystal' comes from the Greek word for ice.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rock crystal is clear and cold to touch, like ice, and it is often found high in snowy mountains.  
  Against: Rock crystal is quartz, which is made of silicon and oxygen, not water. It does not melt in a fire, and it sinks in water, while ice floats.
- **To grow the biggest crystals, make the salt water as salty as possible and dry it as fast as possible.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: More salt and faster drying do give more salt quickly.  
  Against: Fast drying starts thousands of crystals at once, so each one stays tiny. Big crystals need slow, still growth. Your plate and glass test shows it.
- **Crystals such as quartz can heal illnesses when you hold them or wear them.** (Many shops and some healers; *rejected*)  
  For: Many people say they feel calmer when they hold a crystal they like.  
  Against: In tests, people were given either real quartz or glass made to look like it, without being told which. Both groups said they felt the same things. So the feeling did not come from the crystal being real.
- **No two snowflakes are alike.** (A saying that is very common; *debated*)  
  For: A big snowflake grows its arms through changing air on its fall, so each one takes its own path. Two big, branched flakes that match exactly have never been found.  
  Against: In 1988, Nancy Knight, a scientist in the USA, found two tiny, simple snow crystals, shaped like short six-sided columns, that looked the same under her microscope.

## Roots
We have grown salt crystals fast and slowly, and seen that each substance grows its own shape. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your plate dried fast and grew many tiny salt grains. Your glass dried slowly and grew fewer, bigger ones. Under the lens, both had flat faces and square corners. The drying speed set their size, but the salt itself set their shape.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a neat shape must be made from outside, by a mould, a knife or a maker's hands. Salt makes its own cubes, because its particles always join in the same square pattern.  
*Evidence:* In the plate and glass test, the size changed but both made cubes. Cracked kal uppu broke into smaller blocks. Phitkari and sugar each grew their own different shape, in the same kind of glass.

- *who am i:* Your body grows crystals too. The enamel on your teeth is made of tiny crystals that your body built from the calcium in your food.
- *what is life:* Your body needs to know which way is down, even with your eyes shut. Inside each ear, tiny crystals rest on a soft jelly. When you tilt your head, they shift, and tiny hairs feel it.
- *what is this world:* Most rocks, most metals, sand, snow and the salt in the sea are made of crystals. Much of the solid world grew itself, particle by particle.

A salt crystal starts with a few particles that join and stay. Every particle that follows joins in the same way, millions of times over. Out of that one way of joining come the cubes on your plate, the heaps at Thoothukudi and the rock salt from old dried seas. One simple pattern shows up as countless crystals, big and small, in every place where salt water dries.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 64:** A tree as wide as your arms can reach grows from a tiny shoot. A tower nine storeys high starts from a heap of earth. A journey of a thousand miles starts beneath your feet. (Your biggest crystal grew the same way, one tiny particle at a time, over days. The slow glass shows that big things need many small steps, not a rush.)
- **Plato, Timaeus (about 360 BCE):** Plato, a Greek thinker, guessed that each of the elements is made of tiny shapes. He gave earth the cube, because it is the steadiest shape. (Plato was wrong about the elements. But he was right that tiny shapes, repeated, can build the things we see. Salt really is cube-shaped, because of the way its particles join.)
- Science (settled): The shape of a crystal comes from the way its particles are arranged. X-rays shone through crystals since 1912 show the arrangement.
- Science (open): Exactly how the first few particles come together to start a crystal is still being studied.
- Other view, Many chemists: Saying the salt 'sets its own shape' is just a way of describing the forces between its particles. The forces are what we measure. The cube follows from them, and nothing more is needed to explain it.
- Practice, Watch a crystal face (3 min): Hold one big salt crystal or a piece of kalkandu near the window. Turn it slowly until one face catches the light and shines. Keep it still there. Breathe in and out slowly five times. With each breath, picture one more row of particles joining that face. Now put it down. Your crystal grew without hurrying. Open your hands.
- Ask: Nobody shaped your salt cubes. Where do you think their shape came from?
- Ask: Is a crystal that grew for a week more special than one that grew in an hour? Why?
- Ask: Your body grows crystals in your teeth and ears. Did you make them, or did they make themselves?

## Story: Arjun's Sandy Kalkandu
*Nila, Arjun and their aunt Valli are made up. Kalkandu really is made by leaving strong sugar syrup still for many days, often with threads for the crystals to grow on. Stirring and fast cooling really do turn syrup into fine, sandy sugar.*

Every Pongal holiday, Nila and her cousin Arjun stayed with their aunt Valli in Madurai. Nila was ten and Arjun was eight. Valli Athai made kalkandu, the big clear sugar crystals that people buy for temples and for sweet pongal.

Her workshop was a cool back room with a stone floor. Along one wall stood rows of clay pots, each covered with a white cloth. Strings ran across the top of each pot, dipping down into the syrup inside.

"Nobody touches these pots," said Athai. "Not a tap, not a stir, not a peep under the cloth."

"How long do they stay?" asked Arjun.

"Many days," said Athai. "Kalkandu is in no hurry."

Arjun was in a hurry. He wanted to take kalkandu home to show his friends in Bengaluru, and the holiday was only one week long.

That afternoon, Athai made a fresh pan of syrup. She stirred sugar into hot water, more and more, until the water was thick and shining. She let it cool a little, and poured it into two small steel tumblers, one for each child. She gave each of them a short string tied to a stick.

"Hang your string in it," she said. "Then put your tumbler on the shelf in the back room, and leave it."

Nila hung her string, carried her tumbler to the shelf, and walked away.

Arjun hung his string too. Then he had an idea. "If it cools faster, it will grow faster," he whispered. He put his tumbler in a bowl of cold water from the pot and stirred the syrup round and round to help it along.

In a few minutes, the syrup went cloudy. Then it went white. Then it went thick, like wet sand. By evening, Arjun's tumbler held a hard white cake. His string was stuck in the middle of it.

"It's ruined," said Arjun. He scraped some out with a spoon. It was sweet, but it was just fine sugar, like the sugar in the dabba.

Nila looked at her own tumbler. Nothing had happened yet. The syrup was still clear.

For three days, Nila's syrup stayed clear, and Arjun said she was wasting her time. On the fourth day, Nila looked down into her tumbler, without touching it. Tiny clear points were sticking out of her string, catching the light.

By the seventh day, the string was thick with clear crystals, some as big as a fingernail. Athai lifted it out and hung it to dry. The crystals had flat, shining faces and sharp, slanted corners. They looked just like the kalkandu in the shop.

Arjun looked at Nila's string. Then he looked at his white cake. "Did you use better sugar?" he asked.

"Same pan," said Athai. "Same sugar. Same string."

Nila took a pinch of Arjun's sandy sugar and put it on a dark plate. She held her phone close and zoomed in. On the screen, each grain was a tiny clear block with flat faces and slanted ends.

"Look," she said. "Your grains have the same shape as my kalkandu. They are crystals too. They are just tiny."

"Then why is mine tiny and yours big?" asked Arjun.

Nila thought about it. "You cooled it fast and stirred it. So sugar came out everywhere at once, and millions of little crystals started. My syrup cooled slowly and stayed still. Only a few crystals started on the string, and all the sugar joined them, little by little."

Athai smiled. "That is why nobody touches my pots."

Arjun was quiet for a while. Then he asked, "Athai, can I have another tumbler?"

This time Arjun put his tumbler on the shelf and walked away. He did not touch it, not once, though he looked at it every morning. On the last day of the holiday it held three small clear crystals on its string. Arjun wrapped them in paper and took them home to Bengaluru.

On the bus, he unwrapped them and held one up to the window. The light came through its flat faces, and he thought about the millions of tiny grains in his first tumbler, each one the same shape as this.

**For the parent:** For the parent: both tumblers held the same sugar syrup. Arjun cooled his fast and stirred it, so huge numbers of tiny crystals started at once and the syrup set into fine grains. Nila's syrup cooled slowly and stayed still, so only a few crystals started on the string, and the dissolved sugar joined them over days. Under the phone, Arjun's grains had the same slanted faces as Nila's crystals. The speed decided the size. The sugar decided the shape. This is the same thing your child sees in the Fast Plate or Slow Glass experiment with salt.

- Ask: Arjun and Nila used the same syrup. Why did Arjun get sand and Nila get big crystals?
- Ask: What did Nila see when she zoomed in on Arjun's sandy sugar?
- Ask: Why do you think Valli Athai's rule was that nobody touches the pots?
- Ask: If you wanted kalkandu fast, is there any way to hurry it without making it sandy?
- Ask: Arjun thought better sugar made bigger crystals. How did Nila find out he was wrong?

## Experiments
### e1 · Fast Plate or Slow Glass: which grows bigger salt crystals? (45 min, 7 days)
- **question:** Does the speed of drying change the size of salt crystals? Does it change their shape?
- **predict:** Guess first. The plate will dry in a few hours. The glass will take about a week. Which will grow bigger crystals? Will the crystals be the same shape or different? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both use the same salt water, poured from the same tumbler, 2 big spoons each. Both sit side by side on the same shelf. The only difference is the dish: a wide plate spreads the water thin, so it dries fast, and a small glass keeps it deep, so it dries slowly.
- **safety:** Do not drink the very salty water. Keep the glass where nobody will knock it over.
- **record:** Fill in the table each time you look. Draw the biggest crystal from the plate and the biggest from the glass, side by side, as big as you can.
- **results table:** Day | Plate: what you see | Glass: what you see | Biggest crystal on the plate (mm) | Biggest crystal in the glass (mm) | Shape of the crystals
- **schedule:** day 1: Make the saturated salt water. Pour 2 big spoons on the plate and 2 in the glass. Put both on one shelf. Guess what will happen. (25 min); day 2: The plate should be dry. Look at its crystals with the phone camera. Measure the biggest. Look at the glass. (5 min); day 4: Look at the glass. Draw what you see. Measure the biggest crystal. (5 min); day 7: The glass should be dry or nearly dry. Measure its biggest crystal. Compare the two. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (10 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a pinch of salt on a dark plate. Look at it with the phone camera zoomed in.  
  *Parent says:* These are salt crystals. Look at their sides. Are they round, or flat? We are going to grow new ones from salt water.  
  *Child mode:* Look at salt with the phone zoomed in. Are the sides round or flat? Now we will grow new crystals.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour 1 cup of water into a steel tumbler. 2. Add 1 big spoon of salt and stir for one minute. 3. Keep adding salt, one spoon at a time, stirring each time. 4. Stop when a little salt stays at the bottom however much you stir.  
  *Parent says:* We keep adding salt until the water cannot hold any more. Then the water is saturated. Why do we want it so full of salt?  
  *Child mode:* Add salt, one spoon at a time. Stir each time. Stop when salt stays at the bottom.
  - Note: A cup of water holds about 4 or 5 big spoons of salt. Table salt can make the water a little cloudy, because of what is added to stop it caking. That is fine. Saturated water starts growing crystals as soon as a little water evaporates.

- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the plate 'fast' and the glass 'slow'. 2. Put both on the same shelf, side by side, away from the stove and out of reach.

### Flat Faces and Cubes (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is a crystal?  
   Options: A glass bangle / A grain of salt / Runny honey  
   **Answer:** A grain of salt. *Why:* A salt grain has its particles in a repeating pattern, so it has flat faces. A glass bangle has jumbled particles. Runny honey is a liquid.

   Options: Little balls / Little cubes / Little stars  
   **Answer:** Little cubes. *Why:* Salt particles join in a square pattern, so salt grows as cubes, like tiny dice.
3. (fill) Water that holds as much salt as it can, so that extra salt stays at the bottom, is called a ____ solution.  
   **Answer:** ['saturated']. *Why:* When salt stays at the bottom however much you stir, the water is saturated.
4. (truefalse) When salty water evaporates, the salt goes into the air with the water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Only the water goes into the air. The salt stays behind and crystallises.
5. (mcq) In the Fast Plate or Slow Glass test, both dishes got the same salt water and the same amount. Why?  
   Options: To use up all the salt water / So the only difference is how fast they dry / So they dry at the same time  
   **Answer:** So the only difference is how fast they dry. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. Here it was the speed of drying, set by the shape of the dish.
6. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Crystal → a solid with particles in a repeating pattern; Face → a flat side of a crystal; Evaporate → a liquid going into the air as a gas; Seed crystal → a small crystal that more particles join. *Why:* These four words come up all through the unit.
7. (sort) Sort these solids. Crystal, or not a crystal?  
   **Answer:** kalkandu → Crystal; a glass window → Not a crystal; phitkari → Crystal; a clear boiled sweet → Not a crystal; an ice cube → Crystal; a rubber band → Not a crystal. *Why:* Crystals have their particles in a repeating pattern. Glass, boiled sweets and rubber have their particles jumbled.
8. (write) Why does a salt crystal have flat sides, even though nobody cut it?  
   **Answer:** Because its particles join in the same pattern, row by row. A finished row is flat, like a row of bricks.. *Why:* A strong answer says the particles join in a repeating pattern, and that this pattern makes the sides flat.
9. (draw) Draw the biggest crystal you grew in the slow glass. Draw it as big as your hand. Label one face and one corner.  
   **Answer:** A cube or a square-sided block, with a face and a corner labelled.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows flat faces meeting at square corners.
10. (truefalse) A salt crystal from the sea and a salt crystal from your glass have the same shape.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both are salt, and salt particles always join in the same square pattern, so both grow cubes.

   **Answer:** ['pyramids']. *Why:* Phitkari particles join in their own pattern, which makes double pyramids with triangle faces.

   **Answer:** Zoom in close on one grain with a phone camera on a dark plate. *Why:* Sugar grains are small. Zooming in on one grain against a dark plate shows its flat faces. Dissolving it would break up the crystal.

5. (write) Arjun stirred his hot sugar syrup and cooled it in cold water. He got fine, sandy sugar instead of kalkandu. What should he do differently next time, and why?  
   **Answer:** Leave the syrup still and let it cool slowly, so only a few crystals start and the sugar has time to join them.. *Why:* A strong answer says both: no stirring, and slow cooling, and links it to fewer crystals starting.
6. (sort) Sort these. Will they give big crystals or small crystals?  
   **Answer:** salt water in a narrow glass on a quiet shelf → Bigger crystals; salt water spread on a plate under a fan → Smaller crystals; sugar syrup stirred while it cools → Smaller crystals; sugar syrup left still for a week → Bigger crystals; hot phitkari water cooled in the fridge → Smaller crystals; hot phitkari water cooled slowly in a box → Bigger crystals. *Why:* Slow and still gives bigger crystals. Fast, stirred or cold gives many small ones.
7. (draw) Draw what you think the particles look like inside a salt crystal, and inside a glass bangle.  
   **Answer:** Particles in neat rows and columns for salt. Particles in a jumble for glass.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a repeating pattern for the crystal and no pattern for the glass.
8. (mcq) Steno looked at many quartz crystals in 1669. Some were long, some short, some lopsided. What did he find was always the same?  
   Options: Their size / The angle between one face and the next / Their colour  
   **Answer:** The angle between one face and the next. *Why:* However lopsided, every quartz crystal had the same angles between matching faces. Only the sizes of the faces changed.
9. (write) Your seed crystal sat in fresh salt water and grew bigger. Why does new salt join the seed crystal instead of starting new crystals?  
   **Answer:** Because the seed already has the pattern. It is easier for particles to join a pattern that is there than to start a new one from nothing.. *Why:* A strong answer says the seed gives particles a ready pattern to join.
10. (fill) A crystal of one substance always has the same ____ between its faces, even if it grows lopsided.  
   **Answer:** ['angles']. *Why:* Steno found this in 1669. It is true for every kind of crystal.
11. (truefalse) A steel spoon is made of many tiny crystals packed together.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Metals are made of many small crystals, too small to see without a microscope.
12. (order) Put these in order, from the slowest drying to the fastest.  
   **Answer:** ['salt water in a covered narrow glass', 'salt water in an open narrow glass', 'salt water spread thin on a plate under a fan']. *Why:* A cover slows evaporation most. A narrow glass has a small surface. A thin layer under a fan dries fastest.
13. (mcq) Lakshmi's Paati cannot taste sugar. Lakshmi has two dabbas of white grains. Which test tells salt from sugar without tasting?  
   Options: Smell them / Zoom in on single grains: salt grains are cubes / Look at their colour  
   **Answer:** Zoom in on single grains: salt grains are cubes. *Why:* Salt grows cubes and sugar grows slanted blocks, so their shapes give them away. Both have almost no smell, and both are white.
14. (write) Snowflakes have six arms, and salt has four-sided faces. What does that tell you about water particles and salt particles?  
   **Answer:** They join in different patterns. Water freezes in a six-sided pattern, and salt joins in a square pattern.. *Why:* A strong answer says each substance's particles join in their own pattern, and the shape shows the pattern.
15. (mcq) Which home place is best for growing big salt crystals?  
   Options: On the window sill in strong wind / On a still shelf, in a narrow glass / Inside the fridge  
   **Answer:** On a still shelf, in a narrow glass. *Why:* A still place and a narrow glass give slow evaporation, so a few crystals grow big. Wind dries it fast. The fridge is damp and cold, so the water hardly evaporates.
### Crystals Big and Small (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (perspective) Three people talk about the giant crystals in the Cave of the Crystals in Mexico, some longer than a bus.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks Dr Rosa's slow, still growth, using the glass test. It says Meera is right that the water must hold enough of the mineral, and Kabir is right that the crystals needed room, but neither alone makes a few crystals grow huge.. *Why:* All three play a part. But a full solution that dries or cools fast makes many small crystals, as the plate showed. Slowness is the key.
2. (write) Sana says: 'My salt crystals are lopsided, so they are not real cubes.' Is she right? Explain using what Steno found.  
   **Answer:** She is partly right that they are not perfect cubes. But their faces still meet at square corners. Steno found that only the sizes of the faces change, not the angles.. *Why:* A strong answer separates face size, which can change, from the angles, which stay the same.
3. (write) Glass is made by melting sand, and sand is mostly broken quartz crystals. Why is glass not a crystal?  
   **Answer:** Because when the melted sand cools fast, its particles freeze in a jumble before they can join in a pattern.. *Why:* A strong answer says the pattern was lost when melted, and fast cooling stopped it forming again.
4. (mcq) Kavya puts two seed crystals in the same glass of salt water. One is a salt cube, one is a sugar grain. What will happen?  
   Options: Both grow salt / The salt cube grows, and the sugar grain dissolves / Both stay the same  
   **Answer:** The salt cube grows, and the sugar grain dissolves. *Why:* Salt water still has room for sugar, so the sugar grain dissolves. The salt particles join the salt cube, because it has their pattern.
5. (write) Your body needs to know which way is down, even with your eyes shut. Inside each ear, tiny crystals rest on a soft jelly. How might these crystals help?  
   **Answer:** When you tilt your head, the crystals are pulled down and shift on the jelly. Tiny hairs feel this, so you know which way is down.. *Why:* A strong answer links the crystals' weight to the pull of gravity, and says that the movement is felt.
6. (draw) Draw a model of a salt crystal growing. Show the particles, and one new particle joining. Then draw where you think it would not fit.  
   **Answer:** Particles in a square pattern, a new particle fitting into the next place in a row, and a crossed-out particle sitting crooked.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that a new particle joins in the pattern's next place.
7. (truefalse) Two crystals of the same substance always grow to the same size.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Size depends on how long and how slowly they grow. Shape and angles stay the same.
8. (write) Design a test to find out if putting a paper cover with holes over a glass of salt water makes bigger crystals. Say what you would keep the same.  
   **Answer:** Two glasses with the same salt water and amount, side by side. One covered, one open. Measure the biggest crystal in each after a week.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the cover, keeps everything else the same, and says what to measure.
9. (mcq) A sugar factory wants very fine sugar, like powder. What should it do?  
   Options: Cool the syrup slowly and keep it still / Cool it fast and stir it / Add salt  
   **Answer:** Cool it fast and stir it. *Why:* Fast cooling and stirring start very many tiny crystals. That is how Arjun's syrup turned sandy.
10. (fill) The first step of a crystal, when a few particles come together and stay together, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['nucleation']. *Why:* Scientists still study exactly how nucleation happens.
11. (write) Valli Athai says nobody may touch her kalkandu pots for many days. Why does shaking or stirring a pot spoil the kalkandu?  
   **Answer:** Shaking makes many new crystals start at once, so the sugar is shared among many small crystals, and none grows big.. *Why:* A strong answer links disturbing the syrup to many new crystals starting.

   **Answer:** Salt → cubes with square faces; Phitkari → double pyramids with triangle faces; Sugar → slanted blocks; Ice → six-sided plates and six-armed snowflakes. *Why:* Each substance's particles join in their own pattern, so each grows its own shape.
13. (mcq) Dorothy Hodgkin used X-rays and crystals to find the shape of penicillin. Why did she need a crystal?  
   Options: Crystals are pretty / In a crystal, the particles repeat in a pattern, so X-rays make clear spots / X-rays only work on salt  
   **Answer:** In a crystal, the particles repeat in a pattern, so X-rays make clear spots. *Why:* X-rays bounce off the rows of repeating particles and make a pattern of spots. From the spots, she worked out how the particles were arranged.
14. (write) Can you think of a reason why a big crystal might be useful to a scientist, even if small crystals of the same thing are easy to make?  
   **Answer:** A big crystal is easier to measure, cut and study, and X-rays give clearer spots from a bigger crystal.. *Why:* Any well-reasoned use is good: easier to see, cut, measure or study.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a crystal?  
   Options: Any solid that is clear / A solid whose particles are joined in a repeating pattern / A solid that sparkles  
   **Answer:** A solid whose particles are joined in a repeating pattern. *Why:* The repeating pattern of particles is what makes a crystal. Picking 'clear' misses that salt crystals can look white, and glass is clear but not a crystal. Picking 'sparkles' misses that glitter sparkles too.
2. (mcq) Why does a crystal have flat faces?  
   Options: Someone polished it / New particles join in the same pattern, row after row / It was pressed flat  
   **Answer:** New particles join in the same pattern, row after row. *Why:* Particles join in rows that follow the pattern, and a finished row is flat. Picking 'polished' is how cut gems are made, but salt and sugar grow flat faces by themselves.
3. (mcq) Nandini, a girl in Class 3, leaves a plate of salt water in the sun until it dries. Where did the white crust come from?  
   Options: The salt that was dissolved in the water / Dust from the air / The plate  
   **Answer:** The salt that was dissolved in the water. *Why:* The water evaporated into the air, and the dissolved salt stayed behind as crystals. Picking 'dust' is worth checking: a plate of plain water leaves almost nothing.
4. (mcq) You dry the same salt water on a wide plate and in a narrow glass. Which grows bigger crystals?  
   Options: The plate, because it dries first / The glass, because it dries slowly / Both the same  
   **Answer:** The glass, because it dries slowly. *Why:* Slow drying lets a few crystals keep growing. Picking 'the plate' is the common guess, because it finishes first, but it starts thousands of tiny crystals.
5. (mcq) In the plate and glass test, what was the same for both crystals?  
   Options: Their size / Their cube shape / How long they took  
   **Answer:** Their cube shape. *Why:* Both were salt, so both grew cubes. The speed of drying changed their size, not their shape.
6. (mcq) What shape do phitkari crystals grow in?  
   Options: Cubes, like salt / Two pyramids joined at their base / Round balls  
   **Answer:** Two pyramids joined at their base. *Why:* Phitkari particles join in their own pattern, which gives double pyramids with triangle faces. Picking 'cubes' is a natural guess after salt.
7. (mcq) Why do salt, sugar and phitkari each grow a different shape?  
   Options: They were grown in different glasses / The particles of each one join in their own pattern / They are different colours  
   **Answer:** The particles of each one join in their own pattern. *Why:* Each substance has its own pattern, and the shape shows the pattern. Picking 'different glasses' is worth testing: grow two in the same kind of glass, and they still differ.
8. (mcq) Which of these is not a crystal?  
   Options: Kalkandu / A glass bangle / Phitkari  
   **Answer:** A glass bangle. *Why:* Glass has its particles in a jumble, so it is not a crystal. Kalkandu and phitkari have their particles in a pattern.
9. (mcq) A clear boiled sweet is made of sugar. Why is it not a crystal?  
   Options: It cooled so fast that its sugar particles froze in a jumble / It has no sugar in it / It is too small  
   **Answer:** It cooled so fast that its sugar particles froze in a jumble. *Why:* Fast cooling gave the particles no time to line up. Kalkandu grows slowly, so its particles do. Picking 'no sugar' misses that it tastes sweet.
10. (mcq) What did Nicolas Steno find in 1669 by studying quartz?  
   Options: All quartz crystals are the same size / The angles between matching faces are the same in every quartz crystal / Quartz is frozen ice  
   **Answer:** The angles between matching faces are the same in every quartz crystal. *Why:* However lopsided, every quartz crystal had the same angles between its faces. Picking 'frozen ice' is the old idea from Pliny, which was wrong.
11. (mcq) Before Steno, what did many people think about crystals of one kind?  
   Options: That they could grow in any shape, since no two looked alike / That they were all perfect cubes / That they were alive  
   **Answer:** That they could grow in any shape, since no two looked alike. *Why:* No two quartz crystals look alike, so people thought there was no rule. Steno showed that the angles follow a rule, even when the face sizes don't.
12. (mcq) Shruti, a girl in Class 3, wants big kalkandu crystals on a thread. What should she do?  
   Options: Stir the syrup every day / Cool the syrup in cold water / Leave the syrup still for many days  
   **Answer:** Leave the syrup still for many days. *Why:* Slow, still growth gives big crystals. Stirring and fast cooling start too many small crystals, which is how Arjun's syrup turned sandy.

## Applications
- Salt pans along the coast of Tamil Nadu and Gujarat grow salt crystals by letting sea water evaporate in the sun.
- Sugar mills boil cane juice and control the cooling, so the sugar crystals come out the size they want.
- Kalkandu and thread mishri are grown on threads in still syrup for many days.
- Scientists grow crystals of the substances in medicines, then shine X-rays through them to see the shape of their particles. Dorothy Hodgkin found the shapes of penicillin and vitamin B12 this way.
- Quartz crystals keep time in most wrist watches and phones, because a small quartz crystal vibrates at a very steady speed.
- Computer chips are made from very pure silicon, grown as one huge crystal and then cut into thin slices.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how do the first few particles come together to start a crystal?** Scientists can watch crystals grow, but the very first moment is too small and fast to see well. Some think particles first gather in a loose cloud and only then line up. It is still being studied.
- **Why do two salt crystals in the same glass sometimes grow different-looking faces, such as hollow, stepped cubes?** Scientists know that edges can grow faster than the middle of a face when the water is very full of salt. Predicting the exact shape of any one crystal is still very hard.
- **How do living bodies build crystals exactly where they need them, such as in teeth and in the ear?** Biologists know that proteins guide where the crystals grow and what shape they take. Many of the details are still not known.
- **Are there crystal patterns that nobody has found yet?** New kinds of crystals are still being made in laboratories and found in rocks and meteorites. Nobody knows how many more there are.

## Connected maths
- Meenakshi's biggest plate crystal was 1 millimetre across. Her biggest glass crystal was 6 millimetres across. How many plate crystals, lined up side by side, would reach across the glass crystal? **6 plate crystals.**. 6 divided by 1 is 6. So 6 tiny plate crystals in a row are as wide as one glass crystal. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)
- A small salt cube is 2 tiny blocks long, 2 wide and 2 high. How many tiny blocks is it made of? If it grows to 3 blocks long, 3 wide and 3 high, how many blocks then? **8 blocks, then 27 blocks.**. One layer of 2 by 2 is 4 blocks. Two layers make 8. One layer of 3 by 3 is 9 blocks. Three layers make 27. So growing just one block wider on each side more than triples the number of blocks. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- How many faces, edges and corners does a salt cube have? Nila finds 4 salt cubes stuck together in a row. How many faces of the row can you see from outside? **A cube has 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 corners. A row of 4 has 18 faces showing.**. Each cube has 6 faces, so 4 cubes have 24. Where two cubes touch, 2 faces are hidden, one on each. A row of 4 has 3 joins, so 3 times 2 is 6 faces hidden. 24 take away 6 is 18. (queues `math.shapes-3d.faces-edges-vertices.L3`)
- Shruti measured her biggest sugar crystal. Day 3: 1 millimetre. Day 5: 3 millimetres. Day 7: 5 millimetres. If it keeps growing the same way, how big will it be on day 9? Why might it slow down later? **About 7 millimetres on day 9. It may slow down because the syrup has less sugar left to give.**. It grows 2 millimetres every 2 days: 1, 3, 5, so next is 7. But each crystal takes sugar out of the syrup. As the syrup holds less spare sugar, growth slows unless more water evaporates. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
