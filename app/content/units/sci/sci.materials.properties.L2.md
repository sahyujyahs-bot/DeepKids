# Properties of materials: which let light through, and which keep water out
`sci.materials.properties.L2`

**Summary.** You can see clearly through a clear polythene bag. So does water go through it too? Your child tests five materials from home. They look at a window through each one, and pour a spoon of water on each one. Light passes through the polythene, but the water stays on top. Your child also learns words for what materials are like: waterproof, stretchy, strong, transparent, translucent and opaque.

**Intro.** *Hold a clear polythene bag up to the window. You can see the trees and the sky through it. Now guess: if you pour water on it, will the water go through too?* Most children guess yes. If light can get through, water should get through too. You are going to test it. A property is what a material is like, such as waterproof or see-through. Every material has many properties. Some of them surprise us, so we test them.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can describe a material with the words waterproof, stretchy, strong, transparent, translucent and opaque.
1. By the end I can test which materials let light through and which let water through.
2. By the end I can explain why clear polythene lets light through but keeps water out, and why a tea strainer lets both through.
3. By the end I can say that one material has many properties, and that the job decides which ones matter.
4. By the end I can show that strong and stretchy are two different properties, and that a thread is strong for pulling but not for pushing.
5. By the end I can guess first, give a reason, and then say what my test showed.

## Terms
- **Property**: What a material is like. Hard, soft, waterproof and see-through are properties. *e.g.* Two properties of a steel plate: it is hard, and it keeps water out.
- **Waterproof**: Does not let water through. *e.g.* An umbrella, a polythene bag and a steel tumbler are waterproof.
- **Transparent**: Lets light through so well that you can see clearly through it. *e.g.* Window glass, spectacles and a clear polythene bag are transparent.
- **Translucent**: Lets some light through, but you cannot see a clear picture through it. *e.g.* A thin cotton hanky held up to a window glows, but you cannot see the trees.
- **Opaque**: Does not let light through. You cannot see through it at all. *e.g.* Kitchen foil, a steel plate and a wooden door are opaque.
- **Stretchy**: Gets longer when you pull it, without breaking. *e.g.* A rubber band and a hair band are stretchy. A cotton thread hardly stretches.
- **Strong**: Hard to break or tear. *e.g.* A cotton rope is strong. A paper napkin is not.
- **Fibre**: A very thin thread. Paper and cloth are made of fibres, with tiny gaps between them. *e.g.* Tear a newspaper slowly and look at the edge. The fuzz you see is fibres.

## Explanations
### One material has many properties · *defined*
**Definition:** A property is what a material is like, or what it does. Every material has many properties at the same time.

A steel tumbler is hard, shiny, waterproof and opaque. A polythene bag is soft, waterproof, a little stretchy and transparent. No two materials have exactly the same set of properties. When we choose a material for a job, we look for the properties that job needs.
- e.g. A raincoat needs to be waterproof and bend when you move.
- e.g. A window needs to be transparent and waterproof.
- e.g. A hair band needs to be stretchy and to spring back.
### Why some materials keep water out · *defined*
**Definition:** Water gets through a material only where there is a gap or a hole. A waterproof material has no gaps that water can pass through.

Cloth and paper are made of fibres, with tiny gaps between them. Water creeps into the gaps and comes out the other side. A tea strainer has holes you can see, so water runs straight through. Polythene, foil and steel have no gaps, so water stays on top. A banana leaf and a chembu leaf keep water out with a smooth, waxy skin.
- e.g. Water dripped through the cotton hanky in our test.
- e.g. Water sat on the polythene for a whole minute.
- e.g. Rasam stays on a banana leaf at a meal.
### Transparent, translucent and opaque · *defined*
**Definition:** Transparent materials let light through so you see a clear picture. Translucent materials let some light through, but no clear picture. Opaque materials let no light through.

Look through clear polythene and you see the window clearly. It is transparent. A cotton hanky glows, but the trees are gone. It is translucent. Kitchen foil looks dark. It is opaque. Even clear water is not perfectly transparent. In deep sea water, the light that comes back to your eye is blue. In 1921 the Indian scientist C. V. Raman sailed home from England and studied this blue. He showed that the water itself changes the light, and that the blue is not just the sky reflected.
- e.g. Transparent: a glass tumbler, spectacles, clear polythene.
- e.g. Translucent: a thin hanky, a paper kite held up to the sky, your closed eyelids.
- e.g. Opaque: foil, a steel plate, a wooden door.
### How light gets through solid plastic · *defined*
**Definition:** Light is not a material, and it does not need a hole. It can travel through some solid materials, such as glass, clear plastic and water.

Water is a material. It is made of tiny particles, and it can only pass where there is a gap. Light is different. It can pass straight through the particles of glass and clear plastic. A tea strainer lets light through in another way, through its holes. So a material can let light through and still keep water out.
- e.g. A glass window lets in sunlight but keeps out rain.
- e.g. A clear plastic bottle lets you see the water inside, and the water stays in.
- e.g. A tea strainer lets light and water through its holes.
### Strong and stretchy are different properties · *defined*
**Definition:** Stretchy means a material gets longer when pulled. Strong means it is hard to break. A material can be one, both or neither.

A rubber band is stretchy, and it snaps if you pull too hard. A cotton thread hardly stretches, yet it can pull a toy car. Spider silk is both strong and stretchy. Kevlar, a fibre made by the scientist Stephanie Kwolek, is very strong and hardly stretches. Strong also depends on the way you use a material. A thread is strong when you pull it, but it folds up when you push it.
- e.g. A rubber band holds a bundle of pencils tight because it is stretchy.
- e.g. A cotton thread pulls a toy car, but cannot push it.
- e.g. Kite string is strong but hardly stretches.

## Mostly agreed
- Every material has many properties at the same time. *How we know:* In our yes-or-no grid, the steel tumbler, polythene bag, rubber band and hanky each had a different set of answers.
- Water gets through a material only through gaps or holes. *How we know:* Under a magnifying glass, cloth and paper show tiny gaps between their fibres. Water goes through them, but not through polythene or foil, which have no gaps.
- Light can pass through some solid materials that have no holes, such as glass and clear plastic. *How we know:* A glass window lets in daylight but keeps out rain. In our test, water sat on the polythene, yet we could see the window through it.

## How we found out
**Stephanie Kwolek, an American chemist at DuPont, and Charles Smullen, who ran the spinning machine, 1965.** In the 1960s, Stephanie Kwolek was a chemist at DuPont, a company in the USA. Her team wanted a new fibre that was light and very strong, for car tyres. To make a fibre, chemists first make a liquid that is usually thick and clear, a bit like honey. A machine pushes the liquid through tiny holes, and it sets into threads. In 1965, Kwolek made a liquid that was thin and cloudy. Most chemists then thought a liquid like that was no good for making threads, and threw it away. Charles Smullen, who ran the spinning machine, did not want to try it. He worried it would block the tiny holes. Kwolek kept asking, and at last he agreed. The thread was amazingly strong and stiff. Weight for weight, the fibre that came from her work is about five times stronger than steel. It was named Kevlar. Today it is used in ropes, gloves and helmets that protect people. The cloudy liquid looked useless. Only a test showed how strong its threads could be.  
*What people believed before:* That a liquid for making fibres had to be thick and clear, so a thin, cloudy one was useless. Kwolek had her cloudy liquid spun, and it made one of the strongest fibres ever.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If you can see through something, water can get through it too.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: A tea strainer and a mosquito net are see-through, and water goes straight through them.  
  Against: A strainer and a net are see-through because of their holes. Glass and clear polythene have no holes. Light passes through them, but water stays out.
- **Stretchy things are always weak.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: A rubber band snaps if you pull it too far, and a balloon pops.  
  Against: Spider silk is very stretchy and also very strong for how thin it is. A stretchy material can still be hard to break.
- **Glass is a very slow liquid. Old windows are thicker at the bottom because the glass has flowed down.** (A story many people repeat; *rejected*)  
  For: Some very old windows really are thicker at the bottom.  
  Against: Old window glass was made by hand and came out uneven. Workers often put the thick edge at the bottom. Scientists have worked out that glass at room temperature would take far longer than the age of the Universe to flow that much.

## Roots
We have tested what gets through polythene, foil, cloth, newspaper and a tea strainer. Now we ask what that tells us about materials, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Water sat on the clear polythene and never dripped into the tumbler, so it has no holes. Yet you saw the window clearly through it. Light passed through the solid plastic, where water could not. What gets through a material depends on what is trying to get through.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of things as either open or closed, like a door. We expect anything that lets something through to have holes. The test showed one sheet of plastic open to light and closed to water.  
*Evidence:* In the light and water test, you saw the window clearly through the polythene. A teaspoon of water sat on it for a full minute, and the tumbler stayed dry. The tea strainer let both through, because it has holes.

- *who am i:* Your eyelids are translucent: shut them in a bright room and you see a glow. Your skin keeps most water out when you bathe.
- *what is life:* A chembu leaf grows a waxy skin that rain rolls off. Living beings grow the materials they need.
- *what is this world:* Sunlight passes through the air and a window to reach you, while the roof stops the rain. Each material lets some things in and keeps others out.

Polythene, foil, cotton and steel are all made of tiny particles holding on to each other. How the particles are packed decides what can pass. Water needs a gap, and polythene has none. Light needs no gap, and passes through the plastic itself. Everything is conscious, down to these tiny particles, and each material meets light and water in its own way.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** We cut doors and windows to make a room. It is the empty space in them that makes the room useful. (A tea strainer is useful because of its holes. A window is useful because it lets light in. The Tao Te Ching, from China, asks us to notice what the openings do.)
- **Isha Upanishad 15:** The face of truth is covered by a golden lid. Open it, so that I may see what is true. (A shiny golden lid is beautiful, but it is opaque, so it hides what is under it. To see, we need a material that lets light through, or we must lift the lid.)
- Science (settled): Glass and clear plastic have no holes that water can pass through. Light gets through because their particles let most light pass without soaking it up.
- Science (open): Light acts like a wave in some tests and like tiny packets of energy in others. What light really is underneath is still argued about.
- Other view, Many physicists: They say a sheet of plastic has no awareness at all. They explain everything it does with particles and forces, and they find no need for a mind to explain it.
- Practice, Light through your eyelids (3 min): Sit facing a bright window. Close your eyes gently. Is it fully dark? Notice the soft glow. That is light coming through your eyelids. Now cover your closed eyes with your palms. Notice how much darker it is. Take three slow breaths. Then take your hands away, and feel the glow come back.
- Ask: The polythene let light in but kept water out. Was it open or closed?
- Ask: When your eyes are shut and you see a glow, are you seeing?
- Ask: Is there anything that nothing at all can pass through?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Light went through the polythene, but water did not. What is different about light and water? (hint: Water needs a gap to squeeze through. Does light need a gap?)
- *deep:* Why does a tea strainer let tea through but keep the tea leaves back? (hint: Think about the size of the holes, and the size of the leaves.)
- *deep:* Is there anything that nothing at all can get through? (hint: Tiny particles called neutrinos come from the Sun. Almost all of them pass right through the whole Earth.)
- *big:* When your eyes are shut in a bright room, it is not fully dark. Are you still seeing something? (hint: Your eyelids are translucent. What gets through them?)
- *big:* A window keeps the rain out and lets the sky in. Is the window part of the inside of your home, or the outside? (hint: What does it let in, and what does it keep out?)

## Story: The Window That Let the Sky In
*Kochi gets heavy monsoon rain from about June to September. Thangam, Vinu, Amma, Ammamma and Reshma are made up.*

Thangam was seven. She lived with her mother, her grandmother and her little brother Vinu, on the fourth floor of a block of flats in Kochi, in Kerala.

In the monsoon, the rain in Kochi does not just fall. It pours for hours, and the wind blows it sideways.

One wet afternoon, Vinu was bouncing a ball in the kitchen. He was five. The ball flew up and hit the kitchen window. Crack! A long line ran across the glass.

Ammamma, their grandmother, came at once. "Stand back, both of you," she said. She put on thick gloves. Carefully, she lifted the cracked glass out of the frame and wrapped it in old newspaper. "Broken glass is sharp," she said. "Only a grown-up touches it."

Now there was a hole where the glass had been. Rain blew straight in, onto the shelf of steel tins.

"The glass shop is shut until tomorrow," said Amma, their mother. "We need something to cover the hole tonight."

Vinu ran to fetch a big piece of cardboard from an old box. Amma taped it over the hole. The rain stopped coming in. But the kitchen went dark.

"I can't see the rice in the pot," said Ammamma. "And my curry leaf plant by the window needs the daylight."

So they took the cardboard down. Thangam fetched a thin cotton towel, and Amma pinned it over the hole. Now the kitchen was bright again, with a soft white glow. But soon the towel was dark with water. Drops fell from the bottom of it onto the floor.

"The cardboard keeps the rain out, but it keeps the light out too," said Thangam. "The towel lets the light in, but it lets the rain in too."

"What about the tea strainer?" said Vinu. "You can see through it."

Everybody laughed, even Vinu. A tea strainer is full of tiny holes. The rain would pour through it, just like tea.

Thangam sat on the kitchen step and thought. "We need something we can see through, that has no holes at all."

Then she remembered. When Amma bought new pillows, they had come in a big, clear plastic bag. It was folded on top of the cupboard.

Thangam climbed on a stool and brought it down. She held it up to the hole. Through the plastic she could see the coconut trees, the grey sky and the rain. "Look!" she said. "It is like glass, but it is soft."

Amma taped the plastic over the hole and pulled it tight. They waited. Rain hit the plastic and ran down the outside in little streams. Not one drop came in. And the kitchen was full of grey daylight.

Vinu frowned. "But how does the light get in, if there is no hole?"

"Light doesn't need a hole," said Thangam. "It goes through the plastic itself. Rain is water, and water needs a gap to get through. This plastic has no gaps."

Vinu pressed his nose to the plastic. It was cold, and it smelled of new pillows. Outside, a crow sat on a wire, shaking the rain from their feathers. Vinu could see every drop.

Next morning, Reshma came from the glass shop with a new pane of glass. She fitted it into the frame and pressed soft putty along the edges.

"Glass does two jobs at once," Reshma told Vinu. "It lets the light in and keeps the rain out. It is also stiff and strong, so the wind cannot push it in."

Thangam folded the plastic bag and put it back on top of the cupboard.

"Keep it safe," said Ammamma. "One day we may need a window again."

**For the parent:** A window needs two properties at the same time: it must let light through and keep water out. Glass and clear plastic do both, because they have no holes, yet light passes through them.

- Ask: Why did the cardboard not work as a window?
- Ask: Why did everyone laugh when Vinu said 'tea strainer'?
- Ask: How did Thangam explain that light got in with no hole?

## Experiments
### e1 · Light and Water: what gets through? (25 min)
- **question:** Which materials let light through, and which let water through? If light gets through a material, does water always get through too?
- **predict:** Look at the five materials. For each one, guess: will light get through? Will water get through? Most of all, guess about the clear polythene. You can see through it. So will water go through it too? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every material gets the same two tests. For the light test, we hold each one close to one eye and look at the same window. For the water test, each one covers the same tumbler and gets 1 teaspoon of water. We wait 1 minute every time before we look.
- **safety:** Look at a window or a tube light, never straight at the Sun. Looking at the Sun can hurt your eyes, even through a material.
- **record:** Write your guesses at the top, with your reason for the polythene. Fill in the table. Draw the polythene and the tea strainer with water on them.
- **results table:** Material | Light: a clear picture, only a glow, or dark? | Water: stayed on top, or got through?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the five materials on the tray. 2. Let your child touch each one. 3. Ask the questions below. 4. Write their guesses and their reason for the polythene.  
  *Parent says:* We will do two tests on each one. Will light get through? Will water get through? Guess for each one. Now look at the polythene. You can see through it. Will water go through it too? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Touch each material. Guess: will light get through? Will water get through? Say why for the polythene.
  - Ask: Will water go through the polythene? Why? (good: Yes, because I can see through it, so it must be open.; No, because a polythene bag holds water.; Yes, because it is thin.)
  - Note: Don't say if a guess is right. Many children say yes, because they can see through it. Any guess with a reason is good.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand by the window together. 2. Your child holds each material about 2 cm from one eye. 3. They look at the window through it. 4. They say: a clear picture, only a glow, or dark. 5. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Hold it close to one eye and look at the window. Can you see the trees clearly? Is there only a glow? Or is it dark?  
  *Child mode:* Hold it near one eye. Look at the window. Clear picture, a glow, or dark?
  - Ask: Which ones gave a clear picture? (good: The polythene, because I could see the trees.; The tea strainer, through the little holes.)
  - Note: The polythene gives a clear picture. The strainer gives a picture through its holes, a little dim. The cloth glows. The newspaper glows faintly, with print in the way. The foil is dark. Remind your child never to look at the Sun.
  - Check: The foil let no light through. Which word is right for the foil? → **Opaque**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the glass tumbler on the tray. 2. Lay the polythene over the top. 3. Hold it on with the rubber band. 4. Press the middle gently so it dips a little, like a bowl.  
  *Parent says:* We put the polythene over the tumbler like a lid. If water gets through, we will see it at the bottom of the tumbler.  
  *Child mode:* Put the polythene on the tumbler. Hold it on with the rubber band. Make a little dip in the middle.
  - Note: The dip stops the water rolling off the edge. Keep the polythene fairly tight, so it does not touch the bottom.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour 1 teaspoon of water slowly into the middle. 2. Count slowly to 60. 3. Look at the tumbler from the side. 4. Lift the cover and feel the bottom of it. 5. Write the result. 6. Wipe the tumbler dry and do the next one. 7. Rest the strainer on top instead of using the band.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the water slowly into the middle. Now count to sixty with me. Look at the bottom of the tumbler. Is there any water? Feel underneath the cover. Is it wet or dry?  
  *Child mode:* Pour one spoon of water in the middle. Count to sixty. Look in the tumbler. Is there water? Feel under the cover.
  - Ask: Which ones kept the water out? (good: The polythene and the foil. The water sat on top.; The tumbler stayed dry under them.)
  - Note: Water sits on the polythene and the foil, and the tumbler stays dry. It drips through the cloth within a few seconds. The newspaper soaks it up and goes soggy. It is wet underneath, and a few drops may fall. Count that as 'got through'. Water runs straight through the strainer.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the polythene and the strainer side by side. 2. Ask your child to look closely at each, and hold each up to the window. 3. Ask the questions below. 4. Listen to their answer before you explain.  
  *Parent says:* You could see through the polythene and through the strainer. But water went through only one of them. Look closely. What does the strainer have that the polythene does not? Then how did the light get through the polythene?  
  *Child mode:* Look closely at the strainer and the polythene. What does the strainer have? How did light get through the polythene?
  - Ask: Why did water go through the strainer but not the polythene? (good: The strainer has holes. The polythene has none.; Water needs a gap. Light went through the plastic itself.)
  - Note: After your child answers, say it plainly. The strainer is see-through because of its holes, so water gets through. The polythene has no holes, so water stays on top. Light passes through the plastic itself.
  - Think: Can you think of something else that lets light in but keeps water out?

### What Gets Through? (core) (core, 11 questions)
1. (match) Draw a line from each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Waterproof → does not let water through; Transparent → you can see clearly through it; Opaque → lets no light through; Stretchy → gets longer when you pull it. *Why:* These are four of the property words in this unit.
2. (mcq) In the light and water test, what happened to the teaspoon of water on the clear polythene?  
   Options: It ran into the tumbler / It sat on top, and the tumbler stayed dry / It soaked into the polythene  
   **Answer:** It sat on top, and the tumbler stayed dry. *Why:* Polythene has no holes or gaps, so the water could not get through. Picking 'ran into the tumbler' may mean thinking see-through things let water through.
3. (sort) Sort these into 'lets light through' and 'blocks light'.  
   **Answer:** kitchen foil → Blocks light; clear polythene → Lets light through; a glass tumbler → Lets light through; a steel plate → Blocks light; a tea strainer → Lets light through; a wooden door → Blocks light. *Why:* Glass and polythene let light through the material itself. A tea strainer lets it through its holes. Foil, steel and wood are opaque.
4. (draw) Draw the tumbler with polythene on top, and the tumbler with the tea strainer on top, after you poured the water. Show where the water ended up.  
   **Answer:** Water sitting in a little pool on the polythene with a dry tumbler below. Water at the bottom of the tumbler under the strainer.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the water in two different places: on top of the polythene, and inside the tumbler under the strainer.
5. (truefalse) If you can see through something, water can always get through it too.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. You can see through clear polythene and glass, but water stays out. Light passes through the material itself, and water needs a gap.
6. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: why did the water go through the tea strainer?  
   **Answer:** Because the tea strainer has lots of small holes, and water goes through the holes.. *Why:* A strong answer names the holes. Water gets through a material only where there is a gap.
7. (odd) Three of these keep water out. Which one lets water through?  
   Options: A steel tumbler / A raincoat / A cotton towel / A banana leaf  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A cotton towel is made of fibres with gaps between them, so water soaks in and through. The other three are waterproof.
8. (label) Label each picture: transparent, translucent or opaque.  
   **Answer:** ['glass window: transparent', 'hanky: translucent', 'steel plate: opaque', 'foil: opaque']. *Why:* Through glass you see a clear picture. A hanky only glows. Steel and foil let no light through.
9. (count) Kamala, a girl in Class 2, tested 5 materials with water. 2 of them kept the water out. How many let the water through?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 5 take away 2 leaves 3.
10. (perspective) Selvi and her brother Ravi are choosing a cover for a small window in their flat.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one and gives a reason, such as 'the polythene kept water out in our test'. It also says what the other gets right, such as 'the net lets air in, and polythene does not'.. *Why:* Both are right about something. Polythene keeps rain out but blocks air. A net lets air in but also lets rain in. The best choice depends on the weather and the job.
11. (mcq) Anu wants to cover her school book in the rain, and still read its name on the front. Which cover is best?  
   Options: Newspaper / Clear polythene / Kitchen foil  
   **Answer:** Clear polythene. *Why:* Clear polythene is waterproof and transparent, so it does both jobs. Foil is waterproof but opaque. Newspaper lets water through.
### Strong, Stretchy, See-Through (stretch) (stretch, 11 questions)
1. (mcq) In the pull, not push activity, what happened when you tried to push the toy with the thread?  
   Options: The toy moved away fast / The thread bent and went floppy, and the toy stayed still / The thread broke  
   **Answer:** The thread bent and went floppy, and the toy stayed still. *Why:* A thread is strong when pulled, but it bends when pushed. So it cannot push a toy.
2. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: why does a rubber band hold a bundle of pencils tighter than a cotton thread?  
   **Answer:** The rubber band is stretched round the pencils, and it pulls back to get shorter. When pencils come out, it shrinks and stays tight. The thread cannot shrink, so it goes loose.. *Why:* A strong answer says the band is stretchy and springs back. The thread stays the same length.
3. (draw) Design a monsoon suit for a superhero. Label one part that is waterproof, one that is stretchy, one that is strong and one that is see-through.  
   **Answer:** For example, a waterproof polythene cape, stretchy rubber cuffs, a strong cotton belt and a see-through plastic face shield.. *Why:* A strong design gives each part a property that suits its job, and names a real material for it.
4. (perspective) Anjali and Meera are arguing about whether a rubber band is strong.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, and sees that both are partly right. A band is strong enough to hold pencils, but it snaps if stretched too far.. *Why:* Whether a material is 'strong' depends on the job. The same band is strong for one job and too weak for another.
5. (truefalse) Stretchy things are always weak.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Spider silk is very stretchy and also very strong for how thin it is.
6. (order) Put these in order, from the one that lets the most light through to the one that lets the least.  
   **Answer:** ['clear polythene', 'cotton hanky', 'newspaper', 'kitchen foil']. *Why:* Polythene gives a clear picture. A thin hanky glows brightly. Newspaper glows faintly. Foil lets no light through.
7. (fill) A material that lets some light through, but no clear picture, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['translucent']. *Why:* A thin hanky and your closed eyelids are translucent.
8. (mcq) Lakshmi wants a bathroom window that lets light in, but stops people outside from seeing in. Which material should she pick?  
   Options: Clear glass / Frosted glass, which is translucent / A steel sheet  
   **Answer:** Frosted glass, which is translucent. *Why:* Translucent frosted glass lets light in but shows no clear picture. Clear glass lets people see in. Steel blocks the light.
9. (match) Match each job to the property it needs most.  
   **Answer:** spectacles → transparent; a kite string → strong; a pyjama waistband → stretchy; an umbrella → waterproof. *Why:* Each job needs a different property. Spectacles must be see-through, a kite string must not break, a waistband must stretch, and an umbrella must keep rain off.
10. (label) Label the parts of an umbrella with the property each part needs.  
   **Answer:** ['cloth: waterproof', 'ribs: strong and stiff', 'handle: strong, and easy to hold']. *Why:* The cloth keeps rain off. The ribs hold the cloth out in the wind without bending. The handle must not break.
11. (count) In the Four Boxes game, Revathi put 4 cards in each of the 4 boxes. How many cards did she sort?  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* 4 boxes with 4 cards each: 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does opaque mean?  
   Options: You can see clearly through it / It lets no light through / It soaks up water  
   **Answer:** It lets no light through. *Why:* Opaque materials, like foil and wood, let no light through. Picking 'see clearly through it' mixes it up with transparent.
2. (mcq) Which of these is transparent?  
   Options: A glass tumbler / A steel tumbler / A wooden spoon  
   **Answer:** A glass tumbler. *Why:* You can see clearly through glass. Steel and wood are opaque.
3. (mcq) Nisha, a girl in Mysuru, pours a teaspoon of water on kitchen foil over a tumbler. What happens?  
   Options: The water sits on top / The water drips into the tumbler / The foil soaks it up  
   **Answer:** The water sits on top. *Why:* Foil has no holes, so it is waterproof. Picking 'soaks it up' mixes foil up with paper or cloth.
4. (mcq) You can see through clear polythene. Why does water still stay on top of it?  
   Options: The polythene has no holes for water, and light goes through the plastic itself / The water is too heavy / The polythene is wet already  
   **Answer:** The polythene has no holes for water, and light goes through the plastic itself. *Why:* Water needs a gap to get through. Light can pass through the plastic itself. Picking 'too heavy' may mean thinking heavy things cannot pass through holes.
5. (mcq) Why does a tea strainer let water through?  
   Options: It is made of steel / It is full of small holes / It is shiny  
   **Answer:** It is full of small holes. *Why:* Water goes through the holes. A steel plate has no holes, and keeps water out.
6. (mcq) Which two properties does a window need most?  
   Options: Stretchy and soft / Transparent and waterproof / Opaque and absorbent  
   **Answer:** Transparent and waterproof. *Why:* A window must let light in and keep rain out. Picking 'opaque' would make the room dark.
7. (mcq) Farhan, a boy in Kolkata, tries to push a toy car with a thread. What happens?  
   Options: The car moves fast / The thread bends, and the car stays still / The thread snaps  
   **Answer:** The thread bends, and the car stays still. *Why:* A thread is strong when pulled, but it bends when pushed. Picking 'snaps' may mean thinking a thread is weak. It is strong, but only for pulling.
8. (mcq) You take 3 pencils out of two bundles. Which bundle stays tight?  
   Options: The one held by a cotton thread / The one held by a rubber band / Both go loose  
   **Answer:** The one held by a rubber band. *Why:* The stretched rubber band shrinks back and keeps holding. The thread stays the same length and goes loose.
9. (mcq) Which material is best for a mosquito net?  
   Options: Clear polythene / A net with tiny holes / Kitchen foil  
   **Answer:** A net with tiny holes. *Why:* A net lets air through its holes but the holes are too small for mosquitoes. Polythene and foil would block the air.
10. (mcq) Before you test a material, what should you do first?  
   Options: Guess what will happen, and say why / Read the answer / Test it many times without thinking  
   **Answer:** Guess what will happen, and say why. *Why:* A guess with a reason helps you notice if the test surprises you.

## Applications
- Windows, spectacles and bottles use glass or clear plastic, because they are transparent and keep water in or out.
- Raincoats, umbrellas and school bag covers are made of waterproof materials.
- Tea strainers, sieves and mosquito nets have holes on purpose, to let some things through and keep others back.
- Hair bands, waistbands and the bands fruit sellers use are stretchy, so they hold things tight.
- Ropes, kite strings and fibres like Kevlar are strong when pulled, so they are used to pull, lift and hold.

## Open questions
- **Can people make a coating that keeps water off as well as a lotus leaf, and lasts for years?** Scientists have copied the tiny waxy bumps on lotus and chembu leaves. But the copies usually wear off when they are rubbed. Making one that lasts is still being worked on.
- **What is light, really?** In some tests light acts like a wave. In others it acts like tiny packets of energy. Scientists can work out exactly what light will do, but what it really is underneath is still argued about.
