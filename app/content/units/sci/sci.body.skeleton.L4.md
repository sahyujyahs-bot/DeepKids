# Skeletons and muscles: what bones do, and how muscles move them
`sci.body.skeleton.L4`

**Summary.** The muscle that bends your elbow is joined to your forearm only about 4 centimetres from the elbow. When a string is tied that close to a hinge, it has to be pulled very hard to hold up a weight far away. So when you lift a 1 kilogram bag of rice, that muscle pulls with about 8 kilograms of force. Your child tests this with a ruler, a rubber band and five coins. They also name the main bones and find out what bone is made of. Then they compare our inside skeleton with the outside skeleton of a crab or an ant.

**Intro.** *Bend your elbow and hold a 1 kilogram bag of rice in your hand. How hard is the muscle in your upper arm pulling: with 1 kilogram of force, with less, or with more?* Most people guess 1 kilogram. The real answer is about 8 kilograms. The reason is the place where the muscle is joined to the bone. In this unit you build a ruler arm and measure what changes when the muscle is tied in different places. You name twelve bones on a real body. You freeze one slab of plain ice and one slab of ice with torn paper inside, and drop them, to find out why a bone does not shatter like chalk. You also meet animals who wear their skeleton on the outside, and animals who have no hard skeleton at all.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can find and name twelve main bones on a body, from the skull to the shin bone.
1. I can explain the jobs of a skeleton: to hold the body up, to protect soft organs, to help it move, and to make blood.
2. I can explain that bone is alive, what it is made of, and how it grows and changes with use.
3. I can explain that a muscle moves a bone by contracting, and why muscles work in pairs.
4. I can test fairly how the place where a muscle is tied changes its force and its movement.
5. I can explain why muscles pull much harder than the weights we lift, using the idea of a lever.
6. I can compare a skeleton inside the body, a skeleton outside it and a body with none.
7. I can explain what the same arm bones in a human, a bat and a whale tell us.

## Terms
- **Skeleton**: The frame of hard parts that holds up an animal's body. It also protects the soft parts and gives the muscles something to pull on. *e.g.* Your skeleton is made of 206 bones. A crab's skeleton is the hard shell on the outside of her body.
- **Skull**: The bones of the head. They make a hard box around the brain and hold the eyes, nose and teeth. *e.g.* Knock gently on the top of your head. The hard thing you feel is your skull.
- **Backbone**: The chain of small bones down the middle of the back. Each small bone is called a vertebra. The backbone is also called the spine. *e.g.* Bend forward and run a finger down the middle of your back. The row of bumps is your backbone.
- **Rib cage**: The cage made by the ribs, the backbone and the breastbone. It protects the heart and the lungs. *e.g.* Press your fingers on your side and take a deep breath. You can feel your ribs move out.
- **Pelvis**: The ring of hip bones at the bottom of the backbone. It carries the weight of the upper body, and the thigh bones join it. *e.g.* Put your hands on your hips. The hard edges under your hands are the top of your pelvis.
- **Femur**: The thigh bone. It runs from the hip to the knee, and it is the longest bone in the body. *e.g.* A grown-up's femur is about a quarter of their height.
- **Bone marrow**: The soft, living material inside many bones. Red marrow makes new blood cells. *e.g.* The marrow inside your hip bones and backbone is making new blood cells right now.
- **Collagen**: A tough, bendy protein that makes long fibres. Bone is made of collagen fibres with hard mineral packed between them. *e.g.* Collagen stops a bone from snapping like a stick of chalk.
- **Muscle**: A body part that can get shorter and pull. Muscles joined to bones move the skeleton. *e.g.* The biceps is the muscle at the front of your upper arm. It bends your elbow.
- **Contract and relax**: A muscle contracts when it gets shorter and pulls. It relaxes when it stops pulling and can be stretched long again. *e.g.* When you bend your elbow, your biceps contracts and the muscle at the back of your arm relaxes.
- **Tendon**: A strong cord that joins a muscle to a bone. *e.g.* Bend your elbow and press into the bend. The tight cord you feel is the tendon of your biceps.
- **Joint**: A place where two bones meet. At most joints the bones can move. *e.g.* Your elbow is a joint between the upper arm bone and the two forearm bones.
- **Lever**: A stiff bar that turns about one fixed point. A push or pull at one place on the bar moves a load at another place. *e.g.* Your forearm is a lever. It turns at the elbow. The muscle pulls close to the elbow, and the load is in your hand.
- **Endoskeleton**: A skeleton inside the body, with the muscles and skin around it. *e.g.* Humans, crows, frogs, fish and lizards have an endoskeleton.
- **Exoskeleton**: A hard skeleton on the outside of the body, with the muscles inside it. *e.g.* A cockroach, a crab and a spider each have an exoskeleton.
- **Moult**: To shed an old exoskeleton and grow a bigger one. An animal with an exoskeleton must moult to grow. *e.g.* A young cockroach moults several times before she is fully grown.

## Explanations
### The main bones and their names · *defined*
**Definition:** A skeleton is the frame of hard parts that holds up a body. A grown-up human skeleton is usually counted as 206 bones.

Start at the top. The skull is a box of bones around the brain. The lower jaw is the only part of the skull that moves. The backbone is a chain of small bones called vertebrae. It runs from the skull to the hips. Twelve pairs of ribs curve round from the backbone, and most of them join the breastbone at the front. The collarbone and the shoulder blade hold each arm on. The upper arm has one bone, called the humerus. The forearm has two, called the radius and the ulna. The hip bones make a ring called the pelvis. The thigh bone is called the femur, and it is the longest bone you have. The kneecap sits in front of the knee. The lower leg has two bones. The thick one at the front is the shin bone, called the tibia. More than half of your 206 bones are in your hands and feet. A baby has more separate bones than a grown-up, because some bones join together as a child grows.
- e.g. Each hand has 27 bones, and each foot has 26.
- e.g. The smallest bone is deep inside your ear. It is smaller than a grain of rice.
- e.g. Sushruta, a surgeon of ancient India, counted 300 bones. His list included teeth and pieces of cartilage, which doctors today do not count as bones.
### What a skeleton is for · *defined*
**Definition:** A skeleton holds the body up, protects soft organs and gives the muscles something to pull on. In humans and other animals with bones, it also makes blood and stores minerals.

The first job is support. Your body is mostly soft and wet. Without a stiff frame, a body your size would sag on the ground. The backbone and the leg bones carry your weight. The second job is protection. The skull guards the brain. The rib cage guards the heart and the lungs. The backbone guards the spinal cord, the thick bundle of nerves that runs inside it. The pelvis guards the bladder and the lower gut. The third job is movement. Bones are stiff, so a muscle that pulls on a bone moves a whole arm or leg. The fourth job is hidden. The red marrow inside your bones makes new blood cells, about two million every second. The fifth job is storage. Almost all the calcium in your body is in your bones and teeth. When your blood needs calcium, bone gives some up. Your skeleton also grows with you, so it never has to be thrown away.
- e.g. Support: the femur and the tibia carry your whole weight when you stand on one leg.
- e.g. Protection: a cricket ball that hits a batter's chest meets the ribs first.
- e.g. Movement: you can throw a ball because stiff arm bones turn at the shoulder and elbow.
### Bone is alive, and it is made of two materials · *defined*
**Definition:** Bone is a living tissue. It is made of tough collagen fibres with hard mineral crystals packed between them.

A bone in a museum is dry and white, so people often think of bone as a kind of stone. A bone in a living body is pink, wet and full of cells. Blood vessels run through it, and nerves reach it, which is why a broken bone hurts. The hard part of bone is a mix of two materials. One is a mineral made of calcium and phosphate. The mineral is hard, but alone it would snap like chalk. The other is collagen, a protein that makes long, bendy fibres. Collagen alone would bend like rubber. Together they make a material that is both hard and tough. Most long bones are also hollow tubes with marrow inside. A tube is nearly as hard to bend as a solid rod, and it weighs much less. Bone cells never stop working. Some cells dissolve old bone, and others lay down new bone. In a grown-up, about one tenth of the skeleton is replaced each year.
- e.g. A chicken bone left in vinegar for some days loses its mineral and bends like rubber.
- e.g. A bone that has been burnt loses its collagen and crumbles when pressed.
- e.g. A broken arm in a plaster cast mends because bone cells build new bone across the break.
### How bones grow, and how they change with use · *defined*
**Definition:** A child's long bones grow at growth plates, which are bands of cartilage near each end. Bone also gets thicker where it is loaded often, and thinner where it is not.

Cartilage is a firm, bendy material, like the stiff part of your ear. Near each end of a child's long bone is a thin band of cartilage called a growth plate. The cartilage cells there keep dividing, and bone replaces them from behind. That makes the bone longer. By the late teens the growth plates turn into bone, and growing taller stops. Bone goes on changing all through life. Bone cells sense each load on the bone. Where a bone is bent and squeezed often, they build more bone. Where a bone is hardly used, they take bone away. The arm that a tennis player serves with has thicker bones than her other arm. Astronauts float in space and their legs carry no weight. They lose about one hundredth of the bone in their hips each month. So they exercise on machines for about two hours a day. Running, jumping and skipping in childhood build bone that lasts into old age.
- e.g. A doctor can tell a child's age roughly from an X-ray of the growth plates in the hand.
- e.g. An arm that spends six weeks in a plaster cast comes out thinner, and its bone is lighter.
- e.g. Mae Jemison, a doctor and astronaut, worked on a study of bone cells in space on her flight in 1992.
### Muscles pull, and they never push · *defined*
**Definition:** A muscle moves a bone by contracting, which means getting shorter and pulling. A muscle cannot push. So muscles work in pairs, with one to pull each way.

You have more than 600 muscles joined to your skeleton. Each one is joined to bones by tendons, which are strong cords. When a muscle contracts, it pulls its two ends towards each other, and the bone on one side moves. To move the bone back, a second muscle on the other side has to pull. The biceps at the front of your upper arm bends your elbow. The triceps at the back straightens it. While one contracts, the other relaxes and is stretched long. Nearly every joint has pairs like this. Muscles also work when nothing moves. When you stand still, muscles in your legs, back and neck pull gently all the time. They stop your skeleton folding at the joints. That pulling uses energy from your food and makes heat. This is one reason you feel warm after running.
- e.g. Bend your knee: the muscles at the back of your thigh contract. Straighten it: the muscles at the front contract.
- e.g. Rise on your toes: your calf muscle contracts and pulls your heel up.
- e.g. Shivering is many muscles contracting quickly, and the heat warms you.
### What happens inside a muscle when it contracts · *defined*
**Definition:** A muscle is made of long cells called muscle fibres. Inside each fibre are two kinds of very thin threads that slide past each other. When they slide together, the fibre gets shorter.

A muscle fibre is thinner than a hair and can be several centimetres long. Under a strong microscope, a fibre shows thousands of tiny stripes. In 1954 two pairs of scientists worked out what the stripes mean. Jean Hanson and Hugh Huxley worked in the United States. Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke worked in England. Both pairs found that a muscle has thick threads and thin threads lying side by side. When the muscle contracts, the threads do not shrink. Later work showed how they move. When a nerve sends a signal, tiny hooks on the thick threads catch the thin threads and pull them along. The threads slide over each other, like the fingers of your two hands sliding together. Each tiny pull uses energy that the cell gets from food. Millions of threads sliding a tiny distance add up to a muscle that shortens by a few centimetres.
- e.g. Lace the fingers of your two hands loosely and slide them together. Your hands get closer, but no finger gets shorter.
- e.g. A muscle that is used hard grows more threads inside each fibre, so the muscle gets thicker.
- e.g. The meat of a chicken leg pulls apart into strings. Each string is a bundle of muscle fibres.
### Bones are levers, and muscles pull close to the joint · *defined*
**Definition:** A lever is a stiff bar that turns about one fixed point. A forearm is a lever that turns at the elbow. The muscle pulls close to the elbow, and the load is far away in the hand.

A door is a lever that turns at its hinges. Push a door near the handle and it opens easily. Push it near the hinges and you must push much harder, though your hand moves only a little way. Your biceps pulls on your forearm in the same way as a push near the hinges of a door. Its tendon joins the forearm about 4 centimetres from the elbow in a grown-up. The middle of the hand is about 32 centimetres from the elbow. That is 8 times as far. So to hold a load in the hand, the muscle must pull about 8 times as hard as the load. This looks like a bad bargain, but the body gains two things. When the muscle shortens by 1 centimetre, the hand moves about 8 centimetres. The hand also moves 8 times as fast as the muscle shortens. Muscles are strong but can shorten only a little. The skeleton turns a short, strong pull into a long, quick movement. That is how you can throw a ball or swat a mosquito.
- e.g. In the Ruler Arm experiment, the rubber band tied nearest the pressed end stretches most. The loop it pulls on rises the least.
- e.g. Your calf muscle pulls on the heel bone, close to the ankle, and lifts your whole body onto your toes.
- e.g. A jaw closes hardest at the back teeth, which are nearest the jaw joint. That is why you crack a nut at the back of your mouth.
### Three kinds of skeleton: inside, outside and none that is hard · *defined*
**Definition:** An endoskeleton is a skeleton inside the body. An exoskeleton is a hard skeleton on the outside. Some animals have no hard skeleton, and their bodies are held firm by liquid under pressure.

Animals with a backbone are called vertebrates. Fish, frogs, lizards, birds and mammals, including us, are vertebrates. All have an endoskeleton, with the muscles wrapped round the bones. Animals without a backbone are called invertebrates, and they have many body plans. Insects, spiders, crabs, prawns and centipedes have an exoskeleton. It is a hard coat made mostly of a tough material called chitin. Their muscles are inside the coat and pull on it from within. The coat has thin, bendy places at the joints, so the legs can fold. A snail's shell is also a hard outside part, and the snail adds to the rim while growing. Earthworms, jellyfish and slugs have no hard skeleton. An earthworm's body is a row of rings filled with liquid, and their muscles squeeze against that liquid. The octopus has no hard skeleton either. Her only hard part is a beak.
- e.g. Endoskeleton: a crow, a rohu fish, a house gecko, an elephant, a human.
- e.g. Exoskeleton: an ant, a cockroach, a prawn, a scorpion, a crab.
- e.g. No hard skeleton: an earthworm, a jellyfish, a slug, an octopus.
### Each kind of skeleton has gains and costs · *defined*
**Definition:** No kind of skeleton is the best. Each one solves some problems and causes others.

An exoskeleton is armour and frame in one. It keeps water in, which helps a small animal on dry land. But a hard coat cannot stretch. To grow, the animal must moult. She splits the old coat, climbs out, and swells before the new coat hardens. For hours or days she is soft and easy to catch. A thick coat is also heavy for a large body, so animals with an exoskeleton are mostly small. The largest on land is the coconut crab, who lives on islands such as the Nicobars. She can weigh about 4 kilograms. An endoskeleton grows along with the body, so it is never shed. It can hold up a huge body, such as an elephant or a blue whale. But it leaves the soft outside of the body open to bites, cuts and drying. A body with no hard skeleton can squeeze through tiny gaps and needs no moult. But without stiff levers, moving on land is slow and takes a lot of muscle. Counted by the number of kinds, animals with an exoskeleton are far ahead. More than three out of every four known kinds of animal are insects, spiders, crabs and their relatives.
- e.g. A cockroach who has just moulted is white and soft for a few hours.
- e.g. A blue whale is about 30 metres long, and water helps to carry the weight of her bones.
- e.g. An octopus can pass through any hole that is bigger than her beak.
### Tricky cases: a tortoise, a shark and a starfish · *defined*
**Definition:** The place of the hard part, inside or outside, does not always tell you the kind of skeleton. You have to ask what the hard part is made from and whether it is under the skin.

A tortoise looks like an animal with an exoskeleton. But the shell is part of the endoskeleton. It is made from the ribs and the backbone, grown wide and joined together. A layer of horny plates covers it. A tortoise cannot leave the shell, just as you cannot leave your rib cage. A shark has an endoskeleton with no bone in it. The whole skeleton is cartilage, the same bendy material as your ear. Your own skeleton began as cartilage before you were born, and most of it turned to bone later. A starfish feels hard and looks armoured. But the hard parts are thousands of tiny plates that lie under the skin. So a starfish has an endoskeleton too, though starfish are invertebrates.
- e.g. A tortoise's shell has nerves and blood in it, and she can feel a touch on it.
- e.g. The tip of your nose and the rim of your ear are cartilage, with no bone inside.
- e.g. A dried starfish on a beach is stiff because of the small plates under the skin.
### The same bones in an arm, a wing and a flipper · *defined*
**Definition:** The front limbs of all four-limbed vertebrates are built from the same set of bones, in the same order. Scientists call parts like these homologous, which means they come from the same part in a shared ancestor.

Your arm has one bone above the elbow and two below it. Then come the small wrist bones and five sets of finger bones. A bat's wing has the same bones. Her finger bones are very long and thin, and skin is stretched between them. A whale's flipper has them too, short and flat, packed inside a paddle. So do a crow's wing, a frog's front leg and a cat's front leg. The jobs are very different: holding, flying, swimming, hopping and running. An engineer who started fresh would not build a wing and a flipper from the same parts. The English scientist Richard Owen described this pattern carefully in the 1840s. In 1859 Charles Darwin gave the reason that scientists accept today. All these animals come from the same ancestors, who had a limb with these bones. Over millions of years the same bones changed shape for different jobs.
- e.g. The long thin bones in a bat's wing are the bones of her fingers.
- e.g. A whale's flipper holds an upper arm bone, two forearm bones, wrist bones and finger bones.
- e.g. A giraffe and a mouse each have seven bones in the neck, as you do.
### Why did animals first make bone? · *debated*
**Definition:** The first animals with bone lived in the sea more than 450 million years ago. Their bone was not inside them. It was a coat of bony plates in the skin. Scientists do not agree on why bone first appeared.

Fossils show that bone began on the outside of early fish, as armour plates. No animal had a bony backbone until much later. Scientists have offered several reasons, and each fits some of the evidence.
- e.g. The oldest fossil fish with bone have a bony head shield and no bony backbone.
- e.g. The flat bones on top of your skull form in a sheet of soft tissue, with no cartilage stage. The first armour formed that way too.
- **The armour idea, put forward by the American fossil scientist Alfred Romer in 1933:** Large sea scorpions hunted in the same waters. Bony plates protected the early fish from them. So on this idea, bone began as a shield.
- **The mineral store idea:** Animals need calcium and phosphate every minute for their nerves and muscles. Plates of bone in the skin may have been a store of these minerals. The body could draw on the store when the water had too little. So on this idea, bone began as a place to keep minerals, a bit like a kitchen cupboard for food.
- **The sense organ idea:** The first bony plates lay around organs that sense tiny electric signals in the water. Hard mineral may have shielded those organs and helped them work. So on this idea, bone began as a part of an animal's sense of the world around her.

## Mostly agreed
- Bone is a living tissue that is taken apart and rebuilt all through life. *How we know:* Under a microscope, bone is full of living cells and blood vessels. Broken bones heal, and X-rays show bone getting thicker or thinner over months.
- Bone is made of collagen fibres and a hard mineral of calcium and phosphate. *How we know:* Acid dissolves the mineral and leaves a bendy bone made of collagen. Burning removes the collagen and leaves a brittle bone made of mineral.
- Muscles can only pull, and they shorten because thin threads inside them slide past each other. *How we know:* In 1954 Jean Hanson and Hugh Huxley studied muscle fibres under microscopes. So did Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke. Both pairs saw the bands in a fibre move closer while the threads stayed the same length.
- Muscles pull with forces much bigger than the loads we lift, because they are joined close to the joints. *How we know:* Giovanni Borelli worked this out with the mathematics of levers in a book printed in 1680. Modern doctors have put sensors inside artificial hip joints. During walking, the sensors measure forces of more than twice the body's weight.
- Bone gets stronger where it is loaded and weaker where it is not. *How we know:* The serving arm of tennis players has thicker bone than the other arm. Astronauts lose bone from their hips and legs during months in space.
- The limbs of all four-limbed vertebrates are built from the same bones. *How we know:* The bones can be matched one by one in skeletons of humans, bats, whales, birds and frogs. They can also be matched in fossils of their ancestors.

## How we found out
**Giovanni Alfonso Borelli, a teacher of mathematics in Italy, with Queen Christina of Sweden, who paid for his book, Borelli worked on the book for many years and died in 1679. It was printed in Rome in 1680 and 1681..** In the 1600s many scholars thought that the bones of the body worked like a crowbar. With a crowbar, a small push lifts a heavy stone. So they supposed that muscles lift heavy loads with small forces. Giovanni Alfonso Borelli, an Italian teacher of mathematics, doubted this. He looked at where muscles join the bones in humans and other animals. He found that almost every muscle joins its bone very close to a joint. That means the muscle works on the short side of the lever, the side close to the turning point, while the weight is on the long side. Then he did the sums that are used for levers. They showed that the muscles which bend the elbow must pull many times harder than the weight in the hand. He did the same sums for the legs and the back. In his old age Borelli was poor and taught mathematics in a school in Rome. Queen Christina of Sweden, who lived in Rome, agreed to pay for printing his book. He died on the last day of 1679. The book, On the Movement of Animals, came out in 1680 and 1681. Some of his numbers were too large, but later measurements showed that his main idea was right.  
*What people believed before:* That the body's levers help the muscles, so that a small muscle force lifts a big load. Borelli showed with the mathematics of levers that it is the other way round. Muscles join the bones close to the joints, so they must pull much harder than the load weighs.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Bones are dry and dead, like stones inside us.** (A common idea, because the bones we see in museums and on plates are dry or cooked.; *rejected*)  
  For: Bone is hard and contains mineral, and a skeleton lasts for hundreds of years after death.  
  Against: Living bone has cells, blood vessels and nerves. It grows, mends itself and changes with use. A stone does none of these.
- **Every person has exactly 206 bones.** (Most school books.; *rejected*)  
  For: 206 is the usual count for a grown-up, and it is a useful number to know.  
  Against: A newborn has many more separate pieces, which join up later. Some grown-ups have an extra rib or extra small bones in the hands and feet. The number depends on the person and on how you count.
- **Cracking your knuckles will give you arthritis, a disease of painful joints.** (Many parents and grandparents.; *rejected*)  
  For: The pop sounds as if something is being damaged.  
  Against: An American doctor, Donald Unger, cracked the knuckles of his left hand only, for more than fifty years. He found no arthritis in either hand, and larger studies agree. The pop comes from a gas bubble in the liquid of the joint. Scientists still argue about whether the sound is the bubble forming or collapsing.
- **Milk is the only way to get strong bones.** (Many advertisements.; *debated*)  
  For: Milk and curd are rich in calcium, and bone needs calcium.  
  Against: Ragi, sesame seeds, drumstick leaves and small fish eaten whole are also rich in calcium. Bone needs vitamin D from sunlight and the loading of running and jumping as well. Studies that follow large groups of adults do not find clearly fewer broken hips among those who drink more milk.
- **The skeleton also sends chemical messages to the rest of the body.** (Gerard Karsenty and his team in New York, from 2007.; *debated*)  
  For: In mice, bone cells release a substance called osteocalcin into the blood. Mice without it handle sugar badly and store more fat.  
  Against: Other teams bred mice without osteocalcin and did not see the same changes. How much this matters in humans is not yet known.
- **An inside skeleton is a better design than an outside one.** (A common idea among humans, who have one.; *rejected*)  
  For: An endoskeleton grows with the body and can carry very large animals.  
  Against: Animals with an exoskeleton have lived on Earth for more than 500 million years. More than three out of four known kinds of animal have one. Each plan fits some ways of living.

## Roots
In this unit you lifted a ruler arm with a rubber band. You also found your own tendon in the bend of your elbow. Here we ask what goes on inside you that you never feel, and where your bones come from.

**Deep truth.** In the Ruler Arm experiment, the band tied nearest the pressed end stretched most, though the coins never changed. Your biceps joins your forearm close to the elbow, like that band. So to lift a 1 kilogram bag of rice, the muscle pulls with about 8 kilograms of force. The forces inside your body are much bigger than the weights you lift.  
*Habit it breaks:* We feel a weight in the hand and suppose that our muscles pull just that hard. We judge how hard the body works by what we can see and feel from outside. The lever sums show that muscles, tendons and joints carry loads many times bigger than anything we hold.  
*Evidence:* In the Ruler Arm experiment the band was longest when tied 5 centimetres from the pressed end. That end also pushed up hard against the finger. Borelli worked out the same thing for real arms in 1680. Sensors inside artificial hip joints measure more than twice the body's weight at every step.

- *who am i:* You are stronger inside than you feel. Each time you lift a tumbler, your muscles, tendons and bones carry several times its weight. You notice none of it.
- *what is life:* Your bone cells sense each load and build bone where it is needed. A living body keeps rebuilding even its hardest parts to fit the way it lives.
- *what is this world:* The calcium in your bones came from your food, and before that from soil and rock. Much limestone rock was made from the shells and skeletons of sea animals. Hard parts of living beings become rock, and rock becomes hard parts again.

Your arm has the same bones as a bat's wing and a whale's flipper. All three came from one ancestor. The calcium in those bones came from rock, and all calcium was first made inside stars. Many sages taught that all the different beings in the world come from one source. Skeletons show two plain examples of this. The first is shared ancestors, because arms, wings and flippers are built from the same bones. The second is shared atoms, because the same kind of calcium atoms are found in all of them.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.5.3:** “तेजोऽशितं त्रेधा विधीयते / तस्य यः स्थविष्ठो धातुस्तदस्थि भवति / यो मध्यमः स मज्जा / योऽणिष्ठः सा वाक्” Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu that the body divides what we eat into three parts. He speaks of rich foods such as oil and ghee. Their coarsest part becomes bone, he says. The middle part becomes marrow, and the finest part becomes speech. (Uddalaka's list is not what science found. The mineral in bone comes from calcium in food, not from oil. But his main point is the same as this unit's: bone is not given once and for all. It is made from what we eat, day after day.)
- **Milindapanha (The Questions of King Milinda), Book 2, the chariot:** The monk Nagasena asks King Milinda whether the axle is the chariot, or the wheels, or the pole. The king says no to each. 'Chariot' is a name for all the parts working together. ('Arm' is a name of the same kind as 'chariot'. It is a name for several parts working together. No bone, muscle or tendon lifts a tumbler alone. In the Ruler Arm experiment the ruler, the band and the pressing finger each had a part. The lifting needed all three.)
- Science (settled): Every calcium atom in your bones was made inside a star long ago, before the Earth formed. The atoms reached you through rock, soil, plants and food.
- Science (open): Bone cells sense the tiny bending of a loaded bone and signal other cells to build or remove bone. Exactly how they sense it is still being worked out.
- Other view, Giovanni Borelli, René Descartes and many scientists since: They describe a body as a machine of levers, ropes and hinges. A pressure cooker's weight lifts when the steam gets strong enough, and nobody says the cooker decided to do it. In the same way, they say a bone cell that adds bone under load is only following chemical rules. On this view nothing more needs to be said about sensing or deciding.
- Practice, Feel the hidden pull (3 min): Sit and rest one forearm on your lap, palm up. Press two fingers of the other hand into the bend of that elbow. Now lift the forearm slowly, as if your hand held a full tumbler. Feel the cord under your fingers go tight. Hold it there for three slow breaths. Lower the arm and feel the cord go soft. That pull is there every time you lift anything. What is it like to notice it?
- Ask: In the Ruler Arm experiment the band pulled hardest where it moved least. Your muscles pull like that all day. Why do you think you never feel how hard they pull?
- Ask: Your bone cells add bone where you load it. So who shapes your skeleton: you, your cells, or the way you live?
- Ask: The calcium in your thigh bone was once in rock, and before that in a star. In what way is the bone yours?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your muscles pull much harder than the weights you lift, and you never feel it. What else does your body do without telling you? (hint: In the Ruler Arm experiment the band near the pressed end stretched most. Think about your heart, your gut and your bone cells.)
- *deep:* About one tenth of a grown-up's bone is replaced each year. After many years, is it still the same skeleton? (hint: The shape of the skeleton stays the same, but the material it is made of changes. Which of these two is the skeleton?)
- *deep:* A crab's hard coat protects her from outside. Your skeleton leaves your skin open to the world. Which of you feels more of what is around you, and why? (hint: Think about what your skin can feel, and about the hours when a crab is soft after a moult.)
- *big:* A bat's wing and your arm have the same bones. What does that say about how you and the bat are related? (hint: Imagine two families who each own an old cooking pot of the same make. They probably got it from the same great-grandmother. In the same way, the same bones in two animals suggest the same ancestor.)
- *big:* Astronauts lose bone when their bodies carry no weight. Does a body need the Earth's pull to stay whole? (hint: Bone cells build where bone is loaded. On Earth, your own weight loads your legs all day.)
- *big:* A skeleton can last thousands of years after an animal dies. What can bones tell us about a life, and what can they not tell? (hint: Bones can show height, age, old injuries and hard work. Think about what leaves no mark on bone.)

## Story: The Dancer Who Was Late for the Drum
*Thangam, Paati, Kamala Chithi and the puppet Mohini are made up. These facts are real: Tamil Nadu has an old string puppet theatre called bommalattam, and Kumbakonam is one of its homes. The muscle that bends the elbow is joined to the forearm close to the elbow. You can feel its tendon in the bend of your arm. In real bommalattam the arms are usually moved with rods. The single finger string here is a simpler puppet, used so that the idea is easy to see.*

The wooden dancer was called Mohini, and she was always late.

She hung by her strings from a beam in Paati's workroom in Kumbakonam, among kings, demons and a horse with one ear. Paati had carved her from a block of light wood forty years ago, and had repainted her eyes eleven times. Mohini could bow, turn and sway. What she could not do was throw up her right hand on the last beat of the drum.

That hand was Thangam's job. Thangam was nine, and this was the first festival at which Paati had trusted her with a string. It was only one string. It ran from Mohini's right wrist up to a loop on Thangam's finger. In two days the puppets would dance in the temple street, and Kamala Chithi would play the drum. At the end of the dance the drum went tha-dhi-gi-na-thom, and on thom the hand had to be in the air.

It never was. To lift the wooden hand as high as Mohini's head, Thangam had to raise her own hand just as far, the whole length of the puppet's arm. She pulled as fast as she could. The hand floated up after the beat, like someone lifting a pot of water.

"Late," said Chithi from behind the drum.

"I started early that time!"

"Then it was early and slow. A dancer's hand is not carried up. It is thrown."

Thangam looked at Paati, who was painting a demon's teeth and did not look up. "Mohini is not the first dancer with an arm," Paati said. "Go and see how the others are strung."

Thangam went along the beam. The king's string was tied at the wrist. The demon's string was tied at the wrist. Even the horse's front leg was tied just above the hoof. Every arm in the room was strung exactly like Mohini's. She sat down on the step, cross and hot.

Chithi was tightening the drum. Each time she struck it, her hand flew up from the drumskin faster than Thangam's eyes could follow. That hand was never late.

Thangam bent her own arm slowly and watched it. Nothing was tied to her wrist. So what lifted it? She pressed two fingers into the bend of her elbow and bent the arm again. Something rose under her fingertips. It was a cord, hard and tight, as thick as a pencil. It came down from the muscle of her upper arm and went into her forearm two finger-widths below the bend. It did not go to her wrist at all.

Paati had said that Mohini was not the first dancer with an arm. She had never said that the other dancers had to be puppets.

Thangam took Mohini down. She untied the string from the wooden wrist and knotted it round the forearm instead, two finger-widths below the elbow pin. She slipped the loop on her finger and pulled, only a little, the length of her thumb.

Mohini's hand flew up past her painted eyes.

"Again," said Chithi, and played. Tha-dhi-gi-na-thom. On thom the hand was there. Thangam laughed out loud. She did it again, and again. On the fourth pull the string snapped, and the hand dropped.

She looked at the broken end. Her finger hurt too. There was a red line where the loop had bitten into it. That same thread had lifted that same hand a hundred times from the wrist, and it had never broken.

Now Paati put down her brush. "A small pull for a long throw," she said. "Did you think it would cost nothing? Where you have tied it now, the string must pull many times harder than the hand weighs. Feel your own cord again. Is it a sewing thread?"

Thangam felt it. It was the hardest thing in her arm that was not bone.

Paati opened a tin and took out a length of thick waxed cord. She cut a small notch in the wooden forearm so that the cord would not slip. She wound a strip of soft cloth round Thangam's finger. "Your body paid that price long before any puppet maker did," she said. "It does not complain, so nobody notices."

On the festival evening the street smelled of jasmine and hot oil. The lamps were lit, and the kings and demons came down on their strings. Mohini bowed, turned and swayed. At the last line of the song the drum ran tha-dhi-gi-na, and Thangam's finger moved the length of her thumb.

Thom. Mohini's hand was in the air, exactly on the beat. A small boy in the front row threw up his own hand to match.

Afterwards Chithi gave Thangam a steel tumbler of buttermilk. As she lifted it to drink, she put two fingers of her other hand into the bend of her elbow. The cord rose and went tight. It had pulled like that every day of her life, and she had not once felt it.

**For the parent:** A string tied at the wrist must be pulled a long way, so the hand is slow. A string tied near the elbow needs only a short pull, so the hand is fast. But the string must pull much harder. Thangam finds that her own arm is strung near the elbow.

- Ask: Why does a hand move faster when the string is tied near the elbow, not at the wrist?
- Ask: Why does a string tied near the elbow have to be stronger?
- Ask: Press into the bend of your own elbow and bend your arm. How far from the bend is the cord you feel?

### e1 · The Ruler Arm: does it matter where the muscle is tied? (30 min)
- **question:** A ruler is a model forearm with coins at the hand end. A rubber band lifts it from 5, 15 or 25 centimetres from the elbow end. At which place does the band stretch most? At which place is the band lifted the shortest distance?

### e2 · The Paper Ice Test: why does a bone not shatter? (30 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Two slabs of ice are made the same size. One is plain ice. The other is ice with torn newspaper frozen inside it. Which slab breaks from a lower drop?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which slab will break first when you drop them: the plain ice or the paper ice? Or will they break at the same height? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two plates of the same size and fill both to the same depth, 1 centimetre. Freeze them side by side for the same time. Drop both slabs flat, from the same heights, onto the same floor. Only one thing changes: the paper in the ice. One try can be a fluke. If your freezer has room, make two slabs of each kind.
- **safety:** Do the drops on a bathroom or balcony floor, with a grown-up. Wet floors are slippery, so dry the floor at the end. Put the melted paper in the dustbin, not down the drain.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows, for drops from 10, 20, 40 and 80 centimetres. For each slab, write 'whole', 'cracked' or 'in pieces' after every drop.
- **results table:** Drop height | Plain ice: whole, cracked or in pieces? | Paper ice: whole, cracked or in pieces?
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess, make the plain slab and the paper slab, and put both in the freezer. (13 min); day 2: Take the slabs out, drop them from four heights, and say what the results show. (17 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. Show your child the plates and the newspaper. Explain the test. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will freeze two slabs of ice. One is plain. One has torn newspaper inside. Tomorrow we drop them. Which will break first, and why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* One slab is plain ice. One has paper inside. Which breaks first? Why?
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Some children expect the paper to make the ice weaker, because wet paper is weak.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tear the two pages into pieces smaller than a stamp. Soak them in the bowl for 10 minutes, then rub them into a mush. Pour water into the first plate, 1 centimetre deep. Spread the mush in the second plate and add water until it is just covered, 1 centimetre deep. Put both plates flat in the freezer.  
  *Parent says:* Tear the paper small and soak it. Now rub it between your fingers until it is a mush. The mush goes in one plate. Plain water goes in the other. Both must be the same depth.  
  *Child mode:* Tear the paper small. Soak it and rub it to mush. Fill both plates. Freeze them.
  - Note: Check the depth with the ruler. Press the mush down so that no dry paper sticks out. If the plates do not fit flat in the freezer, use two smaller steel lids.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2. Take both plates out. Dip the bottom of each plate in tap water for about 20 seconds. Slide the slabs out onto the towel. Let your child hold each slab in the towel and look at it against the light.  
  *Parent says:* Here are yesterday's slabs. Hold each one in the towel. Look through it at the window. What do you notice about each?  
  *Child mode:* Take the slabs out. Hold each in the towel. What do you notice?
  - Wait: Freeze the plates for at least 8 hours, until the next day
- **s4 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Go to a bathroom or balcony floor. Stand the ruler on the floor. Your child holds the plain slab flat at the 10 centimetre mark and lets go. Write whole, cracked or in pieces. Do the same with the paper slab. Repeat from 20 and 40 centimetres, and from 80 centimetres measured against a wall. Stop dropping a slab once it is in pieces.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the slab flat, level with the mark, and just let go. Do not throw it. What happened to the plain slab? Now the paper slab, from the same height.  
  *Child mode:* Drop each slab flat from each height. Write what happens each time.
  - Note: Work quickly, because ice gets weaker as it melts. The plain slab may still be whole after 80 centimetres. If so, hit each slab in the middle with the back of a heavy spoon. Count the hits until it breaks.
  - Check: In the Paper Ice Test, why must both slabs be the same thickness? → **So only the paper is different**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below. Then dry the floor.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Look at the broken edge of each slab. What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: What do you see at a crack in the paper ice? (good: Paper fibres going across the crack.; The two sides are still held together.; It looks hairy, not smooth.)
  - Ask: Which part of a bone is like the ice, and which is like the paper? (good: The hard mineral is like the ice.; The collagen fibres are like the paper.)
  - Note: In most homes the plain slab cracks or breaks from 20 to 40 centimetres. The paper slab usually stays in one piece from every height, though it may crack. Write down what you really found. The reason is that ice is hard, but once a crack starts it runs straight through. In the paper ice, thousands of fibres lie across the path of any crack and hold the two sides together. Bone is built in a similar way. The mineral in bone is hard and would crack like ice. The collagen fibres stop cracks from running through.
  - Think: Wet paper alone is floppy, and ice alone shatters. What does that tell you about a bone with too little mineral, or too little collagen?

## Worksheets
### Bones, muscles and skeletons (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which bones make a hard box round the brain?  
   Options: The skull / The ribs / The pelvis / The femur  
   **Answer:** The skull. *Why:* The skull is a box of bones round the brain. The ribs guard the heart and lungs, and the pelvis guards the bladder and lower gut.
2. (mcq) Which word means that a muscle gets shorter and pulls?  
   Options: Contracts / Relaxes / Moults / Stretches  
   **Answer:** Contracts. *Why:* A muscle contracts when it shortens and pulls on a bone. It relaxes when it stops pulling.
3. (fill) The longest bone in the body is the thigh bone, also called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['femur']. *Why:* The femur runs from the hip to the knee. In a grown-up it is about a quarter of the person's height.
4. (truefalse) A muscle can push a bone as well as pull it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A muscle works by getting shorter, so it can only pull. A second muscle on the opposite side pulls the bone back.
5. (match) Match each part of the skeleton to what it protects.  
   **Answer:** Skull → Brain; Rib cage → Heart and lungs; Backbone → Spinal cord; Pelvis → Bladder. *Why:* Each of these bones lies round something soft. Press gently on each one on your own body, and think about what is behind it.
6. (sort) Sort these animals. Is the skeleton inside the body or outside it?  
   **Answer:** a crow → Inside the body; a frog → Inside the body; a rohu fish → Inside the body; an ant → Outside the body; a crab → Outside the body; a spider → Outside the body. *Why:* Birds, frogs and fish have a backbone and bones inside. Ants, crabs and spiders have a hard coat outside, with the muscles within.
7. (mcq) A rubber band lifts a ruler arm that carries coins. Where must it be tied to stretch the most?  
   Options: Closest to the pressed end / At the middle / Closest to the coins / It stretches the same everywhere  
   **Answer:** Closest to the pressed end. *Why:* The ruler is a lever that turns at the pressed end. A pull close to that end must be much bigger to hold up the same coins. If you chose 'closest to the coins', that is where the pull is smallest.
8. (truefalse) Bone in a living body has blood vessels and living cells.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Living bone is pink and wet. Its cells build new bone and remove old bone all through life, and this is how a broken bone mends.
9. (order) Put these in order, to show how you bend your elbow.  
   **Answer:** ['A nerve signal reaches the biceps', 'The biceps contracts', 'The tendon pulls on the forearm bone', 'The forearm turns at the elbow', 'The hand rises']. *Why:* The muscle pulls only after a signal reaches it. The pull passes through the tendon to the bone, and the bone turns at the joint.
10. (fill) A strong cord that joins a muscle to a bone is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['tendon']. *Why:* Bend your arm and press into the bend of your elbow. The tight cord you feel is the tendon of your biceps.
11. (mcq) Why must a young cockroach moult?  
   Options: Her hard coat cannot stretch / Her coat gets dirty / She wants a new colour / Her legs fall off  
   **Answer:** Her hard coat cannot stretch. *Why:* An exoskeleton is hard and fixed in size. To grow, the cockroach splits the old coat and grows a bigger one.
12. (write) Explain in two sentences why your arm needs both a biceps and a triceps.  
   **Answer:** The biceps can only pull the forearm up, because a muscle cannot push. The triceps on the other side pulls the forearm back down to straighten the arm.. *Why:* A strong answer says that muscles only pull, and that the two muscles pull in opposite directions.
13. (write) Name two jobs of your skeleton and give one example of each.  
   **Answer:** Any two of these. Support: the leg bones and backbone hold me up. Protection: the skull guards the brain. Movement: muscles pull on the arm bones to throw a ball. Making blood: marrow in the bones makes blood cells.. *Why:* A strong answer names two different jobs and gives a real bone or body part for each.
14. (label) Label five bones on this skeleton: skull, ribs, backbone, pelvis and femur.  
   **Answer:** ['Skull: the bones of the head', 'Ribs: the curved bones round the chest', 'Backbone: the chain of small bones down the back', 'Pelvis: the ring of hip bones', 'Femur: the thigh bone, from hip to knee']. *Why:* A strong answer puts each label on the right bone. The femur is the single bone of the thigh, above the knee.
15. (mcq) Which of these foods is rich in calcium for building bone?  
   Options: Ragi / Sugar / Cooking oil / Salt  
   **Answer:** Ragi. *Why:* Ragi, milk, curd, sesame seeds and drumstick leaves are rich in calcium. Sugar, oil and salt have almost none.
### How skeletons work (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) The biceps joins the forearm close to the elbow. What does the body gain from this?  
   Options: The hand moves far and fast / The muscle pulls less hard / The arm weighs less / The elbow bends both ways  
   **Answer:** The hand moves far and fast. *Why:* A short pull close to the joint swings the hand a long way, quickly. The cost is that the muscle must pull much harder than the load weighs.
2. (mcq) Which part of bone stops a crack from running straight through it?  
   Options: The collagen fibres / The hard mineral / The marrow / The blood  
   **Answer:** The collagen fibres. *Why:* Collagen fibres lie across the path of a crack and hold the two sides together. The mineral makes bone hard, but mineral alone would snap like chalk.
3. (mcq) An astronaut floats in space for six months. What happens to her leg bones?  
   Options: They lose bone and get weaker / They grow longer / They turn into cartilage / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** They lose bone and get weaker. *Why:* Bone cells build bone where it is loaded. In space the legs carry no weight, so bone is removed faster than it is built.
4. (truefalse) A tortoise can climb out of her shell.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The shell is made from her ribs and backbone, grown wide and joined together. It is part of her skeleton.
5. (truefalse) A shark's skeleton is made of cartilage, with no bone.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Cartilage is the firm, bendy material in your ear. A shark's whole skeleton is made of it.
6. (sort) Sort these facts. Is each one about an exoskeleton or an endoskeleton?  
   **Answer:** It must be shed for the animal to grow → Exoskeleton; The muscles are inside it → Exoskeleton; It covers the outside of the body → Exoskeleton; It grows along with the body → Endoskeleton; The muscles are wrapped round it → Endoskeleton; It can hold up an elephant → Endoskeleton. *Why:* An exoskeleton is a hard coat outside the muscles, so it protects the surface but must be shed. An endoskeleton is inside the muscles and grows with the body.
7. (odd) Three of these animals have an exoskeleton. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: A scorpion / A prawn / An ant / A gecko  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A scorpion, a prawn and an ant each have a hard coat on the outside. A gecko has a backbone and bones inside.
8. (count) A load weighs 2 kilograms. The arm muscle pulls 8 times as hard. How many kilograms of force is that?  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* 8 times 2 is 16. The muscle pulls with about 16 kilograms of force, because it is joined so close to the elbow.
9. (order) Put these in order, to show how a child's long bone grows longer.  
   **Answer:** ['Cartilage cells in the growth plate divide', 'The growth plate gets thicker', 'Bone replaces the older cartilage', 'The bone is now longer', 'In the late teens the growth plate turns to bone']. *Why:* New cartilage is made first, and bone then replaces it from behind. When the whole plate has turned to bone, the bone can no longer grow in length.
10. (write) A stick of chalk snaps, and a rubber band bends. Explain why a bone does neither.  
   **Answer:** Bone is made of two materials together. The hard mineral stops it bending like rubber. The collagen fibres stop it snapping like chalk. So bone is both hard and tough.. *Why:* A strong answer names both materials and says what each one adds.
11. (write) An arm spends six weeks in a plaster cast. Explain why it comes out thinner.  
   **Answer:** The arm was not used, so its muscles were not worked and its bone was not loaded. Unused muscle gets thinner, and bone cells remove bone that is not loaded.. *Why:* A strong answer says that muscle and bone both change with use. Six weeks of rest is enough to see it.
12. (write) A bat's wing and a whale's flipper contain the same bones. What does this tell scientists?  
   **Answer:** It tells them that bats and whales come from the same ancestors, who had a limb with those bones. Over a very long time the same bones changed shape for flying and for swimming.. *Why:* A strong answer says the bones are shared because of shared ancestors, not because the jobs are alike.
13. (perspective) Engineers are designing a robot to explore a rocky planet. They disagree about its body.  
   **Answer:** Any choice with a reason from the unit. For example: a shell protects well, as a crab's does, but it limits size. A frame inside allows a large body, but the outside is unprotected. A soft body squeezes through gaps, but it is slow on land.. *Why:* Each plan has real gains and costs, as in living animals. A strong answer gives a gain, names a cost and uses an animal as evidence.
14. (draw) Draw an insect's leg and your own arm, both cut open. Show where the muscles are in each.  
   **Answer:** The insect's leg is a hard tube with the muscles inside it. The arm has the bones in the middle with the muscles wrapped round them and skin on the outside.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the hard part outside the muscles in the insect, and inside the muscles in the arm.
15. (fill) A hard skeleton on the outside of an animal's body is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['exoskeleton']. *Why:* 'Exo' means outside. A skeleton inside the body is called an endoskeleton.
### Testing ideas about skeletons (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A ruler arm's band is tied at 10 centimetres. The load is at 30 centimetres. How hard must the band pull?  
   Options: About 3 times the load / One third of the load / The same as the load / About 30 times the load  
   **Answer:** About 3 times the load. *Why:* The load is 3 times as far from the turning point as the band is. So the band must pull about 3 times as hard as the load pulls down.
2. (mcq) A mole digs hard. A horse runs fast. In which animal is the arm muscle joined further from the joint?  
   Options: The mole / The horse / Both the same / Neither has arm muscles  
   **Answer:** The mole. *Why:* A muscle joined further from the joint gives more force and less speed, which suits digging. A muscle joined close to the joint gives speed, which suits running.
3. (mcq) Which test best shows that mineral is what makes bone stiff?  
   Options: Soak one bone in vinegar, one in water / Weigh a bone on a kitchen scale / Paint a bone white / Measure how long a bone is  
   **Answer:** Soak one bone in vinegar, one in water. *Why:* Vinegar dissolves the mineral, and the bone goes bendy. The bone in water shows that soaking alone does not do this.
4. (mcq) The oldest fish fossils with bone have bony plates in the skin and no bony backbone. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Bone began on the outside of the body / Fish once had no skin / Backbones are older than skin / Bone was always inside the body  
   **Answer:** Bone began on the outside of the body. *Why:* The first bone was a coat of plates in the skin. Bone inside the body, such as a bony backbone, came later.
5. (truefalse) Every grown-up human has exactly the same number of bones.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. 206 is the usual count. Some people have an extra rib, or extra small bones in the hands and feet.
6. (truefalse) When you stand still, none of your muscles are working.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Muscles in your legs, back and neck pull gently all the time. Without them your skeleton would fold at the joints.
7. (match) Match each animal to the surprising fact about the skeleton.  
   **Answer:** A tortoise → The shell is made from ribs and backbone; A shark → The skeleton is all cartilage; A starfish → Tiny hard plates lie under the skin; An octopus → The only hard part is a beak. *Why:* Where the hard part lies does not always tell you the kind of skeleton. You have to ask what the hard part is made from and whether it is under the skin.
8. (count) About one tenth of a grown-up's bone is replaced each year. How many years to replace a whole skeleton's worth?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* Ten tenths make one whole. So in about 10 years, as much bone as a whole skeleton has been taken away and built again.
9. (fill) Body parts that come from the same part in a shared ancestor are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['homologous']. *Why:* Your arm, a bat's wing and a whale's flipper are homologous. They have the same bones because of a shared ancestor.
10. (write) A band lifting a ruler arm stretched most when tied near the pressed end. The load never changed. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The ruler is a lever that turns at the pressed end. A pull close to the turning point has little turning effect. So it must be much bigger to hold up the same load. A bigger pull stretches the band more.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the ruler turns about the pressed end. It also says that a pull close to that point has to be bigger.
11. (write) Ice with paper in it survives a drop that breaks plain ice. Explain what the paper fibres do.  
   **Answer:** When a crack starts in the ice, the fibres lie across it and hold the two sides together. The crack cannot run straight through, so the slab stays in one piece.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the fibres stop a crack from spreading. Collagen fibres do the same job in bone.
12. (write) No land animal with an exoskeleton is as big as an elephant. Give one reason why.  
   **Answer:** A hard coat thick enough to hold up a huge body would be very heavy. Also, the animal is soft after each moult. A huge soft body on land would sag under its own weight.. *Why:* A strong answer links size to the weight of the coat or to the danger of moulting. Either reason is accepted.
13. (write) Many people think bones are dead, like stones. What evidence would you show them?  
   **Answer:** A broken bone mends. Children's bones grow longer. A tennis player's serving arm has thicker bone, and astronauts lose bone in space. Stones do none of these things.. *Why:* A strong answer gives at least two things that living bone does and a stone does not.
14. (perspective) Three people talk about the place where the arm muscle joins the forearm.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that a band tied near the pressed end moved the load furthest for a short lift. Another is that a band tied further out needed less force.. *Why:* Each speaker is right about something. A strong answer uses both sides of the lever: force is gained only by giving up distance and speed.
15. (draw) Draw your forearm as a lever. Mark the elbow, the place where the muscle pulls, and the load.  
   **Answer:** A bar turning at one end, marked 'elbow'. An arrow pulling up very close to that end, marked 'muscle'. A weight at the far end, marked 'load in the hand'.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the muscle's pull between the elbow and the load, and much closer to the elbow.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) About how many bones does a grown-up human have?  
   Options: About 200 / About 20 / About 2,000 / About 60  
   **Answer:** About 200. *Why:* The usual count is 206. A baby has more separate pieces, and some join together during childhood.
2. (mcq) Where are more than half of your bones?  
   Options: In your hands and feet / In your skull / In your backbone / In your ribs  
   **Answer:** In your hands and feet. *Why:* Each hand has 27 bones and each foot has 26. That makes 106 of the 206.
3. (mcq) What does the red marrow inside your bones make?  
   Options: New blood cells / New teeth / Sweat / Hair  
   **Answer:** New blood cells. *Why:* Red marrow makes about two million new blood cells every second. Making blood is one of the hidden jobs of the skeleton.
4. (mcq) The backbone protects a thick bundle of nerves. What is the bundle called?  
   Options: The spinal cord / The tendon / The marrow / The growth plate  
   **Answer:** The spinal cord. *Why:* The spinal cord runs through holes in the vertebrae. It carries signals between the brain and the body.
5. (mcq) Which two materials together make bone both hard and tough?  
   Options: Mineral and collagen fibres / Fat and water / Chitin and wax / Cartilage and skin  
   **Answer:** Mineral and collagen fibres. *Why:* The mineral of calcium and phosphate makes bone hard. The collagen fibres make it tough, so that it does not snap.
6. (mcq) Where does a child's long bone grow longer?  
   Options: At growth plates near its ends / In the middle of the bone / Inside the marrow / At the skin  
   **Answer:** At growth plates near its ends. *Why:* Growth plates are bands of cartilage near each end of a long bone. New cartilage is made there, and bone replaces it.
7. (mcq) Your elbow is bent. Which muscle must contract to straighten it?  
   Options: The triceps, at the back of the arm / The biceps, at the front of the arm / The calf muscle / No muscle is needed  
   **Answer:** The triceps, at the back of the arm. *Why:* The triceps pulls on the back of the forearm and straightens the elbow. The biceps bends it, and a muscle cannot push.
8. (mcq) In a fair test of a ruler arm, what is the one thing you change?  
   Options: The place where the band is tied / The number of coins / The height the coins are lifted / The rubber band  
   **Answer:** The place where the band is tied. *Why:* The coins, the band and the lifting height stay the same. Then any change in the stretch of the band must come from the tying place.
9. (mcq) A muscle is joined to a bone very close to the joint. What does that mean for its pull?  
   Options: It must pull much harder than the load / It pulls less hard than the load / It pulls exactly as hard as the load / It does not need to pull  
   **Answer:** It must pull much harder than the load. *Why:* A pull close to the turning point has to be bigger to hold up a load far away. Your arm muscles pull about 8 times harder than the load in your hand.
10. (mcq) Which of these animals has an exoskeleton?  
   Options: A prawn / A frog / An earthworm / A shark  
   **Answer:** A prawn. *Why:* A prawn has a hard coat on the outside. A frog and a shark have a skeleton inside, and an earthworm has no hard skeleton.
11. (mcq) What is one cost of having an exoskeleton?  
   Options: The animal is soft after each moult / The animal has no muscles / The animal has no joints / The skeleton makes too much blood  
   **Answer:** The animal is soft after each moult. *Why:* A hard coat cannot stretch, so it must be shed. Until the new coat hardens, the animal is soft and easy to catch.
12. (mcq) A human arm and a bat's wing have the same bones. Why?  
   Options: Humans and bats share ancestors / Both are used for flying / Bats copied humans / It is only a chance likeness  
   **Answer:** Humans and bats share ancestors. *Why:* The ancestors of all four-limbed vertebrates had a limb with these bones. Over millions of years the same bones changed shape for different jobs.

## Applications
- Doctors use the way bone mends itself. A plaster cast or a metal plate only holds the broken ends still, and the bone cells do the joining.
- Surgeons replace worn hip and knee joints with metal and plastic ones. Sensors inside some of these joints showed how large the forces in the body are.
- A digger arm on a building site is a lever like your forearm. The piston that moves it pushes close to the joint, so a short push swings the bucket a long way.
- Bicycle frames, bamboo scaffolding and tent poles are hollow tubes, like long bones. A tube is nearly as stiff as a solid rod, but it is much lighter.
- Builders put steel rods in concrete and glass fibres in plastic. The fibres or rods stop cracks from running through, as collagen does in bone.
- Scientists who study old graves and crime scenes work out a person's height and age from the bones. Mildred Trotter's rules for height from the thigh bone are still used.
- Engineers build suits with a powered frame on the outside for people who cannot walk. They call such a suit an exoskeleton, after the outside skeleton of an insect.

## Open questions
- **Why did animals first make bone?** The oldest bone was armour in the skin of early fish. Scientists argue about whether it began as a shield, a store of minerals or part of a sense organ. Fossils alone may never settle it.
- **How does a bone cell sense that the bone is being loaded?** Bone cells sit in tiny spaces filled with liquid. When a bone bends a little, the liquid moves, and most scientists think the cells sense that movement. The steps from there to building new bone are still being worked out.
- **Does the human skeleton send chemical messages that change how the body uses sugar?** It seemed so in some mice. Other teams could not repeat the result, and studies in humans do not yet agree.
- **What makes the pop when a knuckle is cracked?** A gas bubble in the liquid of the joint is involved. A study with a scanner in 2015 said the pop comes as the bubble forms. A study with mathematics in 2018 said it comes as the bubble partly collapses.
- **How can people keep their bones on a journey to Mars?** The trip would take many months with no weight on the legs. Exercise machines slow the loss of bone but do not stop it fully. Nobody has yet tested a trip that long.

## Connected maths
- In the Ruler Arm experiment the coin end rose 12 centimetres every time. With the band tied at 5 centimetres, the thread loop rose only 2 centimetres. How many times as far as the loop did the coins move? **6 times as far.**. The coins moved 12 centimetres and the loop moved 2 centimetres. 12 divided by 2 is 6. So a short lift near the elbow end became a movement 6 times as long at the hand end. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In the Ruler Arm experiment the loop rose 2 centimetres when tied at 5 centimetres. It rose 6 at 15 centimetres and 10 at 25 centimetres. Find the pattern. How far would the loop rise if it were tied at 10 centimetres? **4 centimetres.**. Each time the tying place moves 10 centimetres along, the loop rises 4 centimetres more. That is 2 centimetres more for every 5 centimetres along. From 5 to 10 is one step of 5, so 2 plus 2 is 4. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A grown-up's biceps joins the forearm 4 centimetres from the elbow. A bag in the hand is 32 centimetres from the elbow. How many times as hard as the bag's weight must the muscle pull? What is the pull if the bag weighs 2 kilograms? **8 times as hard. For a 2 kilogram bag, about 16 kilograms of force.**. 32 divided by 4 is 8. The bag is 8 times as far from the elbow as the muscle is. So the muscle pulls 8 times as hard. 8 times 2 is 16. The real pull is a little more, because the forearm has weight too. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A grown-up has 206 bones. Each hand has 27 bones and each foot has 26. How many bones are in the two hands and two feet together? Is that more than half of all the bones? **106 bones. Yes, that is more than half.**. Two hands have 27 plus 27, which is 54. Two feet have 26 plus 26, which is 52. 54 plus 52 is 106. Half of 206 is 103, and 106 is more than 103. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- Here are four people's heights and thigh bones. For whom is the thigh bone exactly one quarter of the height? A thigh bone 44 centimetres long is found. About how tall was that person? **The child and the mother. The person with the 44 centimetre thigh bone was about 176 centimetres tall.**. A quarter of 132 is 33, and a quarter of 160 is 40. So the child and the mother fit exactly. A quarter of 148 is 37, and a quarter of 168 is 42. So the other two are 1 centimetre away. 4 times 44 is 176. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In a Paper Ice Test, three plain slabs first broke at drops of 20, 20 and 40 centimetres. Three paper slabs were all still in one piece after 80 centimetres. One plain slab lasted until 40 centimetres. Does that spoil the result? Why were three slabs of each kind better than one? **No, it does not spoil the result. Three slabs show how much the results vary.**. Even the best plain slab broke at 40 centimetres, which is half the height that every paper slab survived. So the two kinds do not overlap. With one slab of each kind, a single odd slab could have misled you. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
