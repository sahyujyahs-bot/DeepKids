# Seeds that wait: how a dry seed stays alive for years
`sci.seeds-and-life-cycles.seeds-and-germination.L5`

**Summary.** Dry moong seeds mostly sprout after a night in the freezer, but soaked seeds die. Why? In this lesson you will draw a chana waking up over four days, run the Freezer Test, and find out whether a seed that has begun to wake can be dried again.

**Intro.** *A scientist in the United States worked on a lotus seed from a dry lake bed in China. The seed was about 1,300 years old. She filed a small cut in the hard coat and put the seed in water. Soon the seed had a green shoot. How can a seed stay alive for 1,300 years?* The scientist was Jane Shen-Miller, and she reported the lotus seed in 1995. The seed had done almost nothing for all those years. They had not grown, and they had hardly used any food. Then water reached the young plant inside, and the seed germinated like a seed from last year. In this lesson you find out what keeps a dry seed alive. You put dry seeds and soaked seeds in the freezer. You dry seeds again after they have started to wake. Then you see how seed banks use the same science to keep seeds safe.

## Terms
- **Germination**: The start of growth in a seed. The seed takes in water and swells, the seed coat splits, and the first root comes out. *e.g.* A moong seed on a wet cloth germinates in about a day. You can see a small white root.
- **Embryo**: The young plant inside a seed. The embryo has a tiny root, a tiny shoot and one or two seed leaves. *e.g.* Open a soaked chana. The small pointed bump at one end is the embryo's root.
- **Seed coat**: The skin around a seed. The seed coat protects the embryo and controls how fast water gets in. *e.g.* The green skin of a moong seed and the red skin of a rajma are seed coats.
- **Food store (cotyledons)**: The food that the parent plant packed into the seed. In beans and dals, the food is in two thick seed leaves called cotyledons. *e.g.* The two halves of a chana are cotyledons. The embryo lives on this food until the first green leaves work.
- **Radicle and plumule**: The radicle is the embryo's first root. The plumule is the embryo's first shoot. The radicle always comes out first. *e.g.* The white tail on a moong sprout is the radicle. The plumule comes up a day or two later.
- **Viable**: Alive and able to germinate. A viable seed may be dry and still for years. *e.g.* Dry moong in a jar are viable. Boiled moong look the same, but they are no longer viable.
- **Dormant**: Alive, but not germinating. Some seeds stay dormant even when they have water, warmth and air. *e.g.* A lotus seed can lie dormant in wet mud for hundreds of years, because water cannot pass the hard coat.
- **Variable**: Anything in a test that can change. In a fair test you change one variable, measure one variable, and keep all the others the same. *e.g.* In the Freezer Test, one variable you change is whether the seeds go into the freezer.
- **Control group**: The group in a test that gets the ordinary treatment. You compare every other group with the control. *e.g.* In the Freezer Test, the dry seeds kept on the shelf are the control group.
- **Germination percentage**: How many seeds out of every 100 germinate in a test. *e.g.* If 18 of 20 seeds germinate, that is 90 out of 100, or 90 percent.
- **Sample**: A small number of seeds taken from a big batch and tested, to find out about the whole batch. *e.g.* A seed bank tests a sample of 50 or 100 seeds. The rest of the seeds stay in the freezer.
- **Moisture content**: How much of a seed's weight is water. *e.g.* A dry moong seed is about one tenth water. A moong sprout is more than three quarters water.
- **Ice crystal**: A small, sharp-edged piece of ice that forms when water freezes. *e.g.* When a soaked seed freezes, ice crystals form inside the cells and tear them.
- **Seed bank (gene bank)**: A place that keeps seeds of many kinds of plants alive for a long time. The seeds are dried, sealed and frozen. *e.g.* India's National Genebank in New Delhi keeps more than 4 lakh seed samples in cold rooms.
- **Soil seed bank**: All the living seeds lying in the soil of a place, waiting to germinate. *e.g.* Weeds that come up in a pot where you sowed nothing grew from the soil seed bank.
- **Regrow (regenerate)**: To sow some stored seeds, grow the plants, and collect fresh seeds to put back in the bank. *e.g.* When fewer than 85 in 100 stored seeds germinate, a seed bank regrows the sample.

## Explanations
### Inside a seed, and the steps of germination · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed has three parts: a seed coat, an embryo and a food store. Germination is the start of growth, and its steps always come in the same order.

The seed coat is the skin. The embryo is the young plant, with a first root called the radicle and a first shoot called the plumule. The food store feeds the embryo. In beans and dals the food is in two thick seed leaves called cotyledons. Germination has five steps. First, the seed takes in water and swells. Second, the seed coat splits. Third, the radicle grows out and turns downwards. Fourth, the plumule grows upwards. Fifth, the first green leaves open and start to make food. Until then the seedling lives only on the food store, so the cotyledons shrink as the seedling grows. This is why a seed can germinate in the dark.
- e.g. Soak a chana overnight and open the two halves. The tiny pointed embryo sits at one end.
- e.g. A moong seed on a wet cloth shows a split coat and a white radicle in about one day.
- e.g. The two thick halves of a moong sprout shrivel after a week, because the food has been used.
### What a seed needs to germinate, and how a fair test shows it · *defined*
**Definition:** Most seeds need three things to germinate: water, oxygen from the air, and enough warmth. Most seeds do not need light or soil.

Water softens the coat and starts the chemistry inside the embryo. Oxygen lets the embryo release energy from the food store. This is called respiration, and it is why seeds under deep water often fail. Warmth sets the speed. Moong germinate fastest at about 25 to 35 degrees Celsius, and hardly at all in a fridge. A fair test finds these needs one at a time. A variable is anything that can change in a test. You change one variable, such as water or no water. You measure one variable, such as how many seeds have a root. You keep every other variable the same: the kind of seed, the number, the cloth and the place. The group with the ordinary treatment is the control group. A few kinds of seed also need light. Lettuce seeds and many tiny weed seeds germinate only after light has reached them.
- e.g. Dry moong on a dry cloth: none germinate. The same moong on a wet cloth: nearly all germinate.
- e.g. Moong on a wet cloth in the fridge: very few germinate until the cup is moved to a shelf.
- e.g. Moong in a dark cupboard germinate as well as moong by a window.
### Why a test needs many seeds, many days and a percentage · *defined*
**Definition:** A germination test uses a sample of many seeds and runs for several days. The result is given as a percentage, which is the number that germinate out of every 100.

In any batch, a few seeds are dead or damaged before the test begins. With 2 seeds in a group, one dead seed changes the result from 100 percent to 50 percent. With 20 seeds, one dead seed changes the result by only 5 percent. So a bigger sample gives a steadier answer. Seeds also wake at different times, so a count on day 1 can miss seeds that wake on day 3. A test must run until no more seeds are waking. A percentage lets you compare groups of different sizes. For 18 of 20 seeds, each seed is worth 5 percent, so 18 seeds make 90 percent. A result that does not fit the pattern is called an odd result, or an anomalous result. Scientists write odd results down and repeat the test. They never rub them out.
- e.g. 18 of 20 seeds is 90 percent. 41 of 50 seeds is 82 percent.
- e.g. Seed labs test 100 seeds at a time, often four lots of 100.
- e.g. Seed packets in India print the germination percentage and the date of the test.
### A dry seed: alive, with almost no water · *defined*
**Definition:** A dry seed is alive, but nearly all activity inside has stopped. This is possible because the seed holds very little water.

While a seed ripens on the parent plant, the seed dries out on purpose. A stored moong seed is only about one tenth water. Your body is more than half water, and a moong sprout is more than three quarters water. Before drying, the embryo fills their cells with sugars and protective proteins. As the last water leaves, this mixture sets hard, a little like hard-boiled sugar candy. Scientists call this the glassy state. In a glass, molecules can hardly move, so the chemical reactions of life almost stop. The embryo uses almost no food and wears out very slowly. When water comes back, the glass softens within hours, and the reactions start again. Tardigrades, which are tiny water animals, and some mosses and ferns can dry out and wait in the same way.
- e.g. Dry moong, rajma and wheat in kitchen jars are alive and waiting.
- e.g. Sanjeevani, a small fern relative sold dry in Indian markets, turns green again in a bowl of water.
- e.g. A dried tardigrade can start moving again after years, once water returns.
### Why freezing kills a soaked seed and spares a dry seed · *defined*
**Definition:** Freezing harms living cells when the water inside them turns to ice. A dry seed has too little free water to make ice, so cold does not harm them.

Water grows bigger when it freezes, and it forms sharp crystals. A soaked seed is full of water. In a freezer, ice crystals form inside the cells. The crystals tear the thin skins, called membranes, around each cell and inside it. When the seed thaws, the cells leak and die. A dry seed holds only a little water, and that water is held tightly inside the glassy mixture. No ice crystals form, so nothing is torn. Cold then helps the seed, because cold slows the slow wearing out even more. So the result depends on two variables together. Cold alone does not kill the seed. Water alone does not kill the seed. Cold and water together do.
- e.g. Dry wheat seeds in a seed bank stay alive at minus 18 degrees Celsius for many decades.
- e.g. Frozen green peas from a shop do not grow, because they were wet when they were frozen.
- e.g. A wet leaf of palak left in the freezer turns limp and dark when it thaws.
### The point of no return: when a waking seed can no longer be dried · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed can be soaked and dried again safely for the first few hours. Once the radicle has come out, drying kills the seedling.

The embryo keeps their protection for the first hours of soaking. If the seed dries again in that time, the glassy state forms again and the seed waits. Farmers use this. Many soak seeds overnight, dry the surface and then sow, so the seeds come up faster. This is called seed priming. Once the radicle grows out, the embryo has changed. The new cells are large and full of water. The protective sugars and proteins have been used or taken apart. If these cells dry, they collapse and die. So germination has a point of no return, at about the time the root appears. A seed that wakes just before a long dry spell cannot go back to waiting. This is why the timing of waking matters so much to a plant.
- e.g. Moong soaked for 6 hours and dried for 2 days usually germinate well when wetted again.
- e.g. Moong with roots 1 centimetre long, dried for 2 days, usually do not grow again.
- e.g. Seedlings that come up after one light shower often die if no more rain follows.
### Dormancy: seeds that wait even when they have water · *defined*
**Definition:** A dormant seed is alive but does not germinate, even with water, warmth and air. Dormancy keeps a seed from waking at a bad time.

A dry seed in a jar waits because there is no water. Scientists call that resting. Dormancy is different, because a dormant seed waits even on wet soil. Some dormant seeds have a coat that water cannot pass, such as lotus and tamarind. Others hold chemical signals that stop the embryo until a sign arrives. The sign may be a cold season, light, or a heavy rain. Because germination cannot be undone, waiting for the right sign protects the seedling. Dormant seeds collect in the ground. All the living seeds in the soil of a place are called the soil seed bank. Some of them wait for one season, and some wait for many years.
- e.g. A lotus seed's coat is so hard that the seed can lie in wet mud for centuries.
- e.g. Apple seeds germinate only after some weeks of cold, which tells them winter has passed.
- e.g. Dig up a patch of bare soil, and weeds soon appear from seeds that were buried there.
### How long can a seed wait? · *idea*
Nobody knows the longest time a seed can stay alive. The answer is different for every kind of seed, and for every place the seed is kept. In ordinary kitchen storage, onion seeds last a year or two, and moong lasts several years. The oldest seeds known to have germinated were kept dry or sealed by nature. A date palm seed about 2,000 years old, from the desert fortress of Masada, germinated in 2005. A lotus seed about 1,300 years old, from a dry lake bed in China, germinated in the 1990s. Seeds die in the end because damage slowly builds up, even in the glassy state. The instructions in their DNA break in places, and the cell membranes become leaky. A waking seed repairs this damage in the first hours. When there is too much damage to repair, the seed can no longer germinate.
- e.g. Moth mullein seeds buried in a bottle in 1879 still germinated in 2021, after more than 140 years.
- e.g. Lotus seedlings grown from very old seeds sometimes had odd leaves, a sign of old damage.
- e.g. Mango and jackfruit seeds die quickly, because they cannot survive drying at all.
### How a seed bank keeps seeds alive · *defined*
**Definition:** A seed bank keeps seeds viable for a long time by keeping them very dry and very cold. Then it tests them and regrows them before they die.

A seed bank follows six steps. First, the seeds are collected, with a note of the place and the date. Second, they are cleaned and a sample is tested for germination. Third, they are dried slowly in a cool, dry room for some weeks. By the end they are only about one twentieth water. Fourth, they are sealed in packets that no air or damp can enter. Fifth, they are stored at about minus 18 degrees Celsius, which is the cold of a home freezer. Sixth, a sample is taken out every five or ten years and tested. Before a packet is opened, it is left closed until it reaches room temperature. Otherwise damp from the air would settle on the cold seeds. If fewer than about 85 in 100 germinate, the bank sows the seeds. The plants are grown, and the fresh seeds are stored. A seed bank also sends copies to other banks. The Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway opened in 2008. More than 13 lakh copies from banks around the world are kept there.
- e.g. India's National Genebank in New Delhi stores more than 4 lakh seed samples.
- e.g. The Millennium Seed Bank in England stores seeds of more than 40,000 kinds of wild plants.
- e.g. In 2015, scientists who had to leave Aleppo in Syria took back their copies from Svalbard and regrew them.
### Frozen banks or living fields: two ways to save seeds · *debated*
**Definition:** People agree that old crop varieties must be saved. They disagree about which matters more: frozen seed banks, or farmers who keep sowing the seeds.

A frozen seed is safe from floods, pests and war, but the seed does not change. The world outside keeps changing: new pests arrive, and the weather shifts. A crop that farmers sow every year keeps adapting. Each year, the plants that do well leave the most seed. But a field can be lost in one bad season. Some seeds cannot be frozen at all, because they die when they are dried. Mango, jackfruit and cocoa are like this, and they are kept as living trees.
- e.g. Rahibai Popere, a farmer in Maharashtra, has saved many old crop varieties by growing and sharing them.
- e.g. Women farmers of the Deccan Development Society in Telangana run village seed banks of millets. Seed is lent, and paid back after harvest.
- e.g. After the tsunami of 2004, farmers in Nagapattinam in Tamil Nadu went back to old paddy that can grow in salty soil.
- **Scientists who run frozen seed banks:** A freezer keeps thousands of varieties in one small room for a hundred years, at low cost. When a variety is lost from the fields, the bank can give it back. No village could keep growing so many varieties every year.
- **Farmers and scientists who keep seeds growing, such as the ecologist Debal Deb in Odisha:** A seed in a freezer stops adapting to new weather and new pests. A variety also needs farmers who know how to grow, cook and choose that crop. So the seeds should be sown every year, in many fields.
- **Many people who work with both:** The two ways cover each other's risks. Fields keep seeds adapting, and frozen banks are the back-up when a field is lost.
### Is a dry seed alive while nothing is happening inside? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that a dry seed can live again. They disagree about what to call the seed while almost nothing is happening inside.

In the driest, coldest seeds, scientists can find almost no activity at all. No growth, no feeding, and almost no respiration can be measured. Yet the seed germinates when water returns. The biologist David Keilin gave this state a name in 1959. He called it cryptobiosis, which means hidden life.
- e.g. A dry tardigrade and a dry seed both show almost no activity, and both can become active again.
- e.g. A boiled seed shows no activity either, but can never become active again.
- **Biologists who say life is something a body does:** Living means taking in food, respiring and repairing. A seed that does none of these is not living at that time. The seed keeps the ability to live, and that ability is what a boiled seed has lost.
- **Biologists who say life is something a body keeps:** The seed keeps all the parts and all the order needed for life, and guards them. Keeping that order through 1,000 years is itself what the living seed is doing. So the seed is alive all the time.
### Seeds beyond the Earth · *idea*
A dry seed can live through more than a freezer. From 2008 to 2009, scientists kept seeds of two small plants outside the International Space Station. The seeds stayed there for 18 months. The seeds had no air. They met strong ultraviolet light from the Sun, and they went from very hot to very cold on every orbit. Back on Earth, some of the seeds still germinated. The dark colours in the seed coat had shielded the embryo from the ultraviolet light. Some scientists ask whether dry seeds or spores could carry life from one planet to another. This is only an idea. Nobody has found any sign that it has happened.
- e.g. Tobacco and thale cress seeds germinated after 18 months outside the space station.
- e.g. Dry seeds have been flown to the Moon's orbit and grown afterwards on Earth.

## Mostly agreed
- A seed germinates in a fixed order: water goes in, the seed swells, the coat splits, the root comes out, and then the shoot. *How we know:* Anyone can watch this on a wet cloth or through the side of a glass jar. Time-lapse films of many kinds of seed show the same order.
- Most seeds need water, oxygen and warmth to germinate. Most seeds do not need light. *How we know:* Fair tests that change one variable at a time give the same result all over the world. Seeds in the dark germinate. Seeds without water, without air or in the cold do not.
- A dry seed lives through freezing, and a soaked seed does not. *How we know:* Seed banks freeze dry seeds at minus 18 degrees Celsius and germinate them decades later. The Freezer Test shows the same thing at home in five days.
- Drier and colder seeds live longer. *How we know:* Seed scientists have stored batches of seeds at many levels of dryness and cold, and tested samples for years. The drier, colder batches kept their germination longest.
- Some seeds can stay alive for more than a thousand years. *How we know:* A lotus seed and a date palm seed were dated by a test called carbon dating, which measures age. Both seeds were then germinated.
- Stored seeds slowly die, even when frozen, so seed banks must test them and regrow them. *How we know:* Seed banks that have tested the same samples for decades see the germination percentage fall. Some kinds fall within a few decades, and others take far longer.

## How we found out
**William James Beal, an American botanist, and the scientists at Michigan State University who have carried on his test, The bottles were buried in 1879. A bottle was opened in 2021, after more than 140 years. The last one is due in 2100..** In the 1800s, many people in Europe believed that wheat from ancient Egyptian tombs had been sown and had grown. The grain was said to be 3,000 years old, and sellers offered 'mummy wheat' for gardens. Botanists then tested grain that truly came from tombs. Not one grain germinated. The grain was brown and brittle, and the embryos were dead. So how long can a seed really live? In 1879, William James Beal began a test to find out. He was a botanist at a college in Michigan in the United States. He filled 20 glass bottles with slightly damp sand. Into each bottle he mixed 50 seeds from each of 21 kinds of plants, nearly all common weeds. He buried the bottles with their open mouths tilted down, so that rain could not fill them. Every few years a bottle was dug up and the seeds were sown. Beal knew the test would outlast him. In 2021, a team dug up a bottle after more than 140 years, and seeds of moth mullein still germinated. The last bottle is due to be opened in 2100.  
*What people believed before:* That wheat from Egyptian tombs, about 3,000 years old, could still grow. Tests on real tomb grain showed that it was dead. Beal's buried bottles then measured how long seeds really live, with seeds whose age was known.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Cold kills seeds.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Frost kills seedlings and young plants. Wet seeds left out on a freezing night do die.  
  Against: Cold kills only when there is water inside to turn into ice. Dry seeds live through a freezer, and seed banks depend on this.
- **Wheat found in the tombs of ancient Egypt can still grow.** (Many people in Europe in the 1800s, and some sellers of 'mummy wheat' since then.; *rejected*)  
  For: Travellers said they had sown grain from tombs and seen it grow. Seeds do live for a long time when they are dry.  
  Against: Botanists who tested grain that truly came from tombs never saw one grain germinate. The grain was brown and brittle. The wheat that grew had been mixed in later.
- **Once seeds are in a frozen vault, that crop is safe for ever.** (A common belief about seed banks.; *rejected*)  
  For: Dry, frozen seeds can live for a hundred years or more, and a vault is safe from floods and pests.  
  Against: Frozen seeds still age slowly, and some kinds die within decades. A bank must keep testing and regrowing its seeds. That needs fields, money and skilled people.
- **The best seed bank is a farmer's field, not a freezer.** (Many farmers' groups and some ecologists.; *debated*)  
  For: Seeds sown every year keep adapting to new weather and pests. The knowledge of how to grow them stays alive too.  
  Against: A flood, a drought or a war can wipe out a field in one season. Without a frozen copy, the variety is lost.
- **A fully dry seed is not alive. It is only able to become alive.** (Some biologists who study dried and frozen life.; *debated*)  
  For: In the driest seeds, no feeding, growth or respiration can be measured.  
  Against: The seed keeps and protects every part needed for life, and a dead seed does not. Many biologists call that being alive.

## Roots
In this lesson you froze dry seeds and soaked seeds, and you dried seeds that had started to wake. Here we ask what a waiting seed shows about life, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In the Freezer Test, dry moong lived through a night in the freezer, and soaked moong died. In No Going Back, moong who were dried after their roots came out died too. A dry seed can live through cold and drying that kill a seedling. So a plant is toughest as a dry seed, and once the root is out there is no way back.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a seed as the small, helpless start of a plant. We think of the grown plant as the strong one. Against cold and drought it is the other way round. The dry seed is the form in which a plant survives what no leaf or root can.  
*Evidence:* In the Freezer Test, dry moong from the freezer germinated about as well as dry moong from the shelf. Soaked moong from the freezer did not germinate. In No Going Back, seeds dried before the root appeared woke again. Seeds dried after the root appeared mostly did not. Seed banks freeze dry seeds for a hundred years. If your own results were different, write down what you saw.

- *who am i:* Your body cannot stop and wait. Your heart must beat every second, and you must drink every day. You began as one cell that started growing and never paused.
- *what is life:* Life does not have to keep moving to stay alive. A seed can become almost still for years, and then carry on from where they stopped.
- *what is this world:* The soil under a field, a park or a flower pot holds living seeds that nobody sowed. The ground is full of plants who are waiting.

A dry seed and a growing plant are one living being in two forms. One form is hard, dry and still, and can wait for a thousand years. The other form is soft, wet and growing, and cannot wait for a week without water. Every flowering plant passes from stillness to growth and back to stillness, as seed, plant and seed again. Many sages taught that stillness is the source that all moving things come from and return to.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** “किमत्र पश्यसीति । न किंचन भगव इति / तं होवाच यं वै सोम्यैतमणिमानं न निभालयस एतस्य वै सोम्यैषोऽणिम्न एवं महान्यग्रोधस्तिष्ठति” Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to split a banyan seed and say what is inside. The boy sees nothing at all. His father says that the great banyan tree stands up out of that fine essence which he cannot see. (A dry seed shows nothing when you open one: no movement, no growth. Yet the whole plant is there, held still. The Upanishad uses the seed to teach about the self, which science does not test.)
- **The Gospel of John 12:24:** Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, the grain stays alone. If the grain dies, a great harvest follows. (A seed that germinates stops being a seed for good, as No Going Back showed. A seed that never wakes stays one seed. The verse uses this to teach about giving oneself, which is a teaching and not a finding.)
- **Tao Te Ching 16:** “夫物芸芸，各復歸其根。 / 歸根曰靜，是謂復命。” All things rise and grow, and each one returns to its root. Returning to the root is called stillness. (A plant's year ends in seeds, which are the plant made still. From that stillness the next growth begins.)
- Science (settled): In a dry seed, the inside of each cell sets into a glassy solid in which molecules can hardly move. This is why the seed's chemistry almost stops.
- Science (settled): Tardigrades, some mosses and some ferns can also dry out, wait for years and become active again. This ability has arisen in many branches of life.
- Science (open): Whether a very dry, very cold seed has any activity at all, or none, is not known. Any activity is too small to measure so far.
- Other view, Some biologists who study dried and frozen living things: Life is a set of activities: feeding, respiring, repairing, growing. A fully dry seed does none of them, so at that time the seed is not living. The seed is a very well kept set of parts that can start living again. On this view, 'alive' and 'dead' are not enough, and a third word is needed for the dry seed.
- Practice, Still as a seed (3 min): Hold one dry seed in your closed hand. Sit as still as you can for one minute. Do not move a finger. Now notice what did not stop. Your breath went in and out. Your heart kept beating. The seed in your hand has been stiller than that for months. Open your hand and look at the seed for three slow breaths.
- Ask: A lotus seed did almost nothing for 1,300 years, and then grew. What was the seed during all those years?
- Ask: Once the root is out, a seed cannot go back. If you were a seed, what would you want to be sure of before you woke?
- Ask: You cannot become still and wait, as a seed can. What can a seed do that you cannot, and what can you do that a seed cannot?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A dry seed and a boiled seed both do nothing in a jar. One is alive and one is dead. What is the difference between them? (hint: Think about what each seed still has inside, and what happens to each when water arrives.)
- *deep:* A seedling cannot go back to being a seed. What other changes in living things cannot be undone? (hint: Think about an egg that has hatched, a flower that has opened, or a tooth that has fallen out.)
- *deep:* In the Freezer Test, neither cold alone nor water alone killed the seeds. Can a cause be two things together? (hint: Think about a wet floor and running. Which one makes you slip?)
- *big:* During a war in the 1940s, hungry scientists in Leningrad guarded sacks of seed and did not eat them. Why would seeds matter that much? (hint: The seeds were thousands of crop varieties collected by Nikolai Vavilov. Think about who would need them after the war.)
- *big:* A seed bank stops seeds from germinating for a hundred years. Is that done for the seeds, or for us? (hint: Think about what a seed would do without us. Then think about what the plants gain when a lost variety is sown again.)
- *big:* Farmers bred every crop over thousands of years, and nobody knows their names. Who should own the seeds in a seed bank? (hint: In the Svalbard vault, each box still belongs to the bank that sent it. Is that fair to the farmers?)

## Story: The Pot Under the Roof
*Thenmozhi, Paatti, Selvam Maama and their village are made up. These things are real. On 26 December 2004 a tsunami sent sea water over the fields of Nagapattinam district in Tamil Nadu. The salt stayed in the soil. Afterwards, farmers there went back to old paddy varieties that can grow in salty soil. Many families kept seed paddy in sealed mud pots with dried neem leaves. Counting 100 seeds onto a wet cloth is the test that farmers and seed labs really use. The numbers in Thenmozhi's count are made up.*

The sea came into Thenmozhi's village on a Sunday morning in December. By evening the water had gone back. The salt had not.

Nine months later, the family's paddy field still showed it. In the nursery bed, the seedlings from the shop seed had come up green. Then they had turned yellow from the tips, and then brown. Selvam Maama pulled up a seedling and held the little plant out. The roots were short and dark.

"Salt," he said. "The rain has not washed enough of it out. And I have no money for a second sack."

Paatti said nothing. She fetched the ladder, and she pointed at the roof.

Under the palm-leaf roof, tied to a rafter, hung a mud pot. The sea had filled the house to the height of Thenmozhi's chest that Sunday. The pot had hung above the water. The mouth was closed with a clay lid, and the lid was sealed with mud.

Maama brought the pot down. Paatti broke the seal with her thumbnail. Inside lay dry neem leaves, and under them paddy. The grains were darker than shop paddy, with long, stiff hairs at the tips.

"My mother grew this in the low field by the backwater," said Paatti. "The backwater is half sea. This paddy never minded."

"When did you fill this pot?" asked Maama.

"Two harvests ago."

Maama let the grains run through his fingers. "Twenty months, Amma. Through two monsoons, in this wet air. This is not seed any more. This is bird feed." He looked at the sky. "And if I sow the nursery from this pot and nothing comes up, the season is gone. There is no time to sow twice."

Thenmozhi picked up one grain and bit down. The grain was hard, and cracked between her teeth like a tiny stick.

"They are very dry," she said.

"Dry is not the same as alive," said Maama.

He was right about that, and Thenmozhi knew it. A dead grain and a living grain look the same. She turned the grain over. There was no way to see inside.

"Soak them," said Maama. "Soak the whole pot tonight. If they swell and sprout, we sow. If they rot, we know."

"And if they sprout, and then no rain comes for the field?" said Paatti. "A sprouted grain cannot go back into the pot, Selvam. Once the root is out, you sow that day or you lose them."

Maama had no answer. They stood around the pot. Thousands of grains lay in it, and nobody could ask them.

Thenmozhi looked at the grain in her hand. One grain could not speak for the pot. One grain might be the only dead one, or the only living one. But the pot was all the same harvest, dried on the same mat, sealed on the same day.

"We do not have to wake them all," she said slowly. "We can wake some. A hundred."

"And waste a hundred grains?"

"A hundred is half a handful, Maama. The pot holds thousands. The hundred will tell us about the rest."

Paatti was already tearing a square from an old gunny sack.

Thenmozhi wet the sack cloth and wrung it out. She stirred the pot with her arm, right to the bottom, and took grains from the top, the middle and the deep part. She counted them onto the cloth in rows of ten. Ten rows. She rolled the cloth up like a mat, tied it with a strip of banana fibre, and stood it in a tumbler in the corner where the cooking fire kept the wall warm.

On the second day she unrolled it. Nothing. The grains looked exactly as before, only darker with the wet.

"Bird feed," said Maama, and went out to the field.

On the third day, twelve grains had a white point at one end, no longer than an ant.

On the fifth day she counted fifty-eight.

On the seventh day she counted three times, moving each sprouted grain to one side of the cloth so that none was counted twice. Seventy-one grains had a root and a pale green spike. Twenty-nine lay as they had lain on the first day.

Maama squatted beside the cloth. "Twenty-nine dead," he said.

"Seventy-one alive," said Thenmozhi. "After twenty months. Out of every ten grains, seven will grow. So for every seven plants we want, we should sow ten grains."

Maama looked at the cloth for a long time. Then he laughed. "So now the pot has spoken," he said. "Through half a handful."

They sowed the nursery bed the next morning, thicker than Maama had ever sown it. The seedlings came up dark green. Thenmozhi went to look at the tips every day. They stayed green.

In January the paddy stood chest high in the field where the shop seedlings had died, and the long hairs on the grains shone in the sun. Paatti filled three pots from the first threshing, before a single grain was sold. One went back under the roof. Two went to the neighbours on either side.

Thenmozhi sealed the lid of their pot with mud. Then she took a piece of charcoal from the hearth. On the side of the pot she wrote the month and the year. Under that she wrote: 71 of 100.

"Next year," she told the pot, "I will ask you again."

**For the parent:** For the parent: nobody can tell a living seed from a dead one by looking. The only way to know is to wake the seed with water, and waking cannot be undone. So Thenmozhi wakes a sample of 100 grains and lets them speak for the whole pot. Her count, 71 of 100, also tells the family how thickly to sow. The paddy was alive after 20 months because the grains were kept dry in a sealed pot.

- Ask: Maama wanted to soak the whole pot to find out whether the paddy was alive. What was wrong with that plan?
- Ask: Why did Thenmozhi take grains from the top, the middle and the bottom of the pot?
- Ask: Would 10 grains have told the family as much as 100? Why or why not?
- Ask: The paddy in the pot stayed alive for 20 months in damp sea air. What kept the grains alive?
- Ask: Why did Paatti fill three pots before selling any grain, and give two away?

### g1 · Scoop and Guess: how many seeds must you test? (25 min)

After 3 rounds each, the player with more points wins.; Playing alone? Use the 80 percent mix. Take 5 seeds and write the percent alive. Put them back and shake. Do this 10 times. ### e1 · The Freezer Test: can a seed live through a night in the freezer? (50 min, 5 days)
- **question:** Does one night in a freezer kill a moong seed? Does the answer depend on whether the seed is dry or soaked?
- **predict:** Guess before you start. There are four groups of 20 seeds: dry on the shelf, dry in the freezer, soaked on the shelf and soaked in the freezer. How many of each 20 will germinate? Why do you think so? Write your four guesses and your reason.
- **fair test:** All 80 seeds come from one packet, and each group has 20. Every group germinates on the same kind of wet cloth, on the same shelf. Two variables are changed: dry or soaked, and shelf or freezer. The four groups test every mix of the two. Group A, dry on the shelf, is the control group. The groups get water at different times, so compare the final counts on day 5 and not the speed.
- **safety:** Do not eat any seeds from this test. The soaked seeds from the freezer go soft and may smell by day 5. Throw all the seeds away at the end and wash your hands.
- **record:** Fill in the table on days 3, 4 and 5. On day 5, turn each count into a percentage. Each seed out of 20 is worth 5 percent. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each group.
- **results table:** Group | My guess (out of 20) | With a root on day 3 | With a root on day 4 | With a root on day 5 | Percent on day 5
- **schedule:** day 1: In the morning, guess and soak 40 seeds. After 8 hours, put one dry group and one soaked group in the freezer. (15 min); day 2: Take the two groups out of the freezer. Put all four groups on wet cloths. (10 min); day 3: Count the seeds with a root in each group. Keep the cloths wet. (5 min); day 4: Count again. (5 min); day 5: Make the final count, work out the percentages and draw a bar chart. Say what you thought, what you saw and why. (15 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the plan: four groups of 20 seeds. Ask for a guess for each group, and the reason. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Some seeds will spend tonight in the freezer. Some will be dry and some will be soaked first. How many of each 20 will still germinate? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Four groups of seeds. Two go in the freezer. Guess how many of each 20 will grow. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think the freezer will or will not kill the seeds? (good: Cold kills plants, so the seeds will die.; Dry seeds are hard, so cold cannot hurt them.; Wet things turn to ice, and ice might break the seed.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A guess with a reason is what you need. Many children expect the freezer to kill both groups.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count out four groups of 20 seeds. Throw out any cracked seed. 2. Put groups C and D in a bowl with water 2 centimetres above them. 3. Keep groups A and B dry in two katoris. 4. Write down the time.  
  *Parent says:* We start in the morning. Groups C and D soak for 8 hours. Groups A and B stay dry.  
  *Child mode:* Count four groups of 20 seeds. Soak two groups in water. Keep two groups dry.
  - Ask: Why 20 seeds in each group, and not 2? (good: One seed might be dead already.; With 20 seeds, one dead seed changes the result only a little.)
  - Wait: Leave the seeds to soak for 8 hours
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 8 hours, drain the water. 2. Put soaked group D in one freezer bowl and dry group B in the other. Put both in the freezer. 3. Put soaked group C on a wet cloth on a plate and cover it. 4. Leave dry group A on the shelf.  
  *Parent says:* Look how much the soaked seeds have swollen. Now B and D go into the freezer for the night. A and C stay on the shelf.  
  *Child mode:* Drain the soaked seeds. Put B and D in the freezer. Keep A and C on the shelf.
  - Note: The soaked seeds should be swollen but should not yet show a root. In a very hot room, soak for 6 hours.
  - Check: In the Freezer Test, why do dry seeds go in the freezer too? → **To see what cold does to a dry seed**
  - Wait: Leave B and D in the freezer overnight, for about 12 hours
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take B and D out of the freezer. Leave them for 30 minutes. 2. Let your child look at D and C side by side, and touch them. 3. Label four plates A, B, C and D. 4. Put each group on a wet cloth on a plate. Group C is already on one. 5. Cover every plate and put all four on one shelf.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the two soaked groups. Is anything different about the group from the freezer? Now every group gets a wet cloth. A and B have had no water until now, so they will wake later than C and D. So we will compare the final count, not the speed.  
  *Child mode:* Take B and D out. Wait until they thaw. Look closely. Put every group on a wet cloth. Cover the plates.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the soaked seeds from the freezer? (good: They look darker.; They feel softer, or the skin is loose.; They look just the same as the others.)
  - Note: The cloth must be wet all through, with no water lying on the plate. The thawed soaked seeds may look darker and feel softer. Sometimes they look the same.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 3. Count the seeds with a white root in each plate. Write the four numbers. Sprinkle water if a cloth is drying.  
  *Parent says:* Count every seed that has a white root. Which groups are growing? Is that what you guessed?  
  *Child mode:* Count the seeds with a root on each plate. Write the numbers. Keep the cloths wet.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 4. Count again and write the numbers. Smell plate D from a distance and note any change.  
  *Parent says:* Count again. Have any more seeds woken? What is happening on plate D?  
  *Child mode:* Count again. Write the numbers. Look closely at plate D.
- **s7 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 5. 1. Make the final count. 2. Help your child turn each count into a percentage: count times 5. 3. Make a bar chart with four bars, drawn or built from lined-up seeds. 4. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 5. Then read the note below together.  
  *Parent says:* Each seed out of 20 is worth 5 percent. Work out the four percentages and make the bars. Now say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Count for the last time. Each seed is 5 percent. Make four bars, by drawing them or by lining up seeds. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: Which two groups show what cold does to a dry seed? (good: A and B. Both are dry, and only B was frozen.)
  - Ask: Which two groups show what cold does to a soaked seed? (good: C and D. Both were soaked, and only D was frozen.)
  - Ask: So what killed the seeds in group D: the cold, the water, or both together? (good: Both together. Cold alone did not kill B, and water alone did not kill C.)
  - Note: Usually groups A, B and C germinate almost fully, and group D hardly at all. Write down what you really found, even if it differs. The reason: a soaked seed is full of water. In the freezer the water turns to sharp ice crystals that tear the cells. A dry seed is only about one tenth water, and that water is held too tightly to form ice. So cold does not harm a dry seed. If some group D seeds grew, they may not have frozen right through.
  - Think: A seed bank keeps seeds in freezers. What must the bank do to every seed before freezing?
### e2 · No Going Back: can a waking seed be dried again? (44 min, 7 days)

  - Note: Usually groups A and B grow almost fully, and B is often a little faster. Group C mostly stays brown and still. Write down what you really found. Once the root grows, its new cells are large and full of water, and the protection has been taken apart. Those cells die when they dry. A few group C seeds may grow a new root from higher up.
  - Think: A seed cannot undo waking. How might that explain why many wild seeds wait for a heavy rain?

## Worksheets
### Seeds that wait (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which part of a seed is the young plant?  
   Options: The embryo / The seed coat / The food store / The soil around the seed  
   **Answer:** The embryo. *Why:* The embryo is the young plant, with a tiny root and a tiny shoot. The seed coat protects the embryo, and the food store feeds the embryo.
2. (order) Put the steps of germination in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The seed takes in water and swells', 'The seed coat splits', 'The radicle grows out and turns down', 'The plumule grows up', 'The first green leaves open']. *Why:* Water must go in first. Then the coat splits and the root comes out before the shoot. The leaves open last and start to make food.
3. (label) Label four parts on this picture of an opened bean seed: seed coat, cotyledon, radicle, plumule.  
   **Answer:** ['Seed coat: the thin skin around the outside', 'Cotyledon: one of the two thick halves, which hold the food', 'Radicle: the tiny pointed root at the edge', 'Plumule: the tiny folded shoot between the halves']. *Why:* The seed coat protects, the cotyledons feed, and the radicle and plumule are the two ends of the embryo.
4. (fill) The first root that comes out of a seed is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['radicle']. *Why:* The radicle is the embryo's root. It always comes out before the shoot, so the seedling can take in water.
5. (truefalse) A moong seed needs light to germinate.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Moong germinate in a dark cupboard, because the embryo lives on the food store. A few kinds of seed, such as lettuce, do need light.
6. (mcq) To test whether seeds need water, which two jars should you compare?  
   Options: Wet warm jar and dry warm jar / Wet warm jar and wet cold jar / Dry warm jar and wet cold jar / Two dry warm jars  
   **Answer:** Wet warm jar and dry warm jar. *Why:* Only the water should differ between the two jars. If warmth changed too, you could not tell which one caused the result.
7. (sort) Sort these. Do most seeds need each one to germinate?  
   **Answer:** water → Needed; oxygen from the air → Needed; warmth → Needed; light → Not needed; soil → Not needed. *Why:* Seeds germinate on a wet cloth in a dark cupboard, so they need neither soil nor light. Without water, air or warmth they do not germinate.
8. (count) 18 of 20 seeds germinate. Each seed is worth 5 percent. What is the germination percentage?  
   **Answer:** 90. *Why:* 18 times 5 is 90. So 90 out of every 100 seeds would germinate.
9. (write) A germination test uses 20 seeds in each group, not 1. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** In any batch a few seeds are dead already. With 1 seed, a dead one would give the wrong answer. With 20 seeds, one dead seed changes the result by only 5 percent.. *Why:* A strong answer says that single seeds can be dead or odd, and that many seeds make the result steadier.
10. (mcq) What happens inside a soaked seed in a freezer?  
   Options: Ice crystals tear the cells / The food store melts / The seed dries out safely / The embryo grows faster  
   **Answer:** Ice crystals tear the cells. *Why:* A soaked seed is full of water. The water freezes into sharp crystals that tear the cell membranes, and the cells die.
11. (truefalse) Dry moong seeds die after one night in a freezer.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A dry seed holds too little water to form ice, so the cold does no harm. Seed banks store dry seeds in freezers.
12. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Viable → Alive and able to germinate; Dormant → Alive, but not germinating; Sample → A few seeds tested from a big batch; Control group → The group with the ordinary treatment. *Why:* A viable seed can germinate. A dormant seed is viable but waiting. A sample stands for the whole batch, and the control is what the other groups are compared with.
13. (write) A seed with a root 1 centimetre long is dried for two days. What happens, and why?  
   **Answer:** The seedling dies. The new cells of the root are full of water and have lost the protection that dry seeds have. When they dry, they collapse.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the root marks a point of no return, and gives the reason: the growing cells can no longer survive drying.
14. (fill) A seed bank stores dry seeds at about minus ____ degrees Celsius.  
   **Answer:** ['18', 'eighteen']. *Why:* Minus 18 degrees Celsius is about the cold of a home freezer. Cold slows the ageing of a dry seed.
15. (perspective) Where are old crop seeds safest?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. For the freezer: dry, cold seeds age very slowly. For the field: frozen seeds do not adapt, and some seeds cannot be dried. For both: each covers the other's risk.. *Why:* Each view is partly right. A strong answer uses a fact about seeds, such as slow ageing in the cold. A strong answer also replies fairly to another view.
### Dry, cold and counted (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (write) In the Freezer Test, dry seeds germinated equally well from the freezer and from the shelf. What does this show?

   **Answer:** Damp from the room air settles as water on anything cold. If the packet were opened cold, the dry seeds would take in that water. Wetter seeds age faster.. *Why:* A strong answer links the cold surface to damp settling on the seeds, and damp to faster ageing.

   **Answer:** Fresh seeds replace the ageing ones. *Why:* Stored seeds slowly die, even when frozen. Sowing them while most are still alive gives fresh seeds of the same variety to store again.

   **Answer:** The soaked cell is full of water, with sharp ice crystals poking through the thin skin of the cell. The dry cell is small and solid, with no crystals and an unbroken skin.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows ice only where there is water, and shows the damage as torn cell skins.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Before the root comes out, the embryo still has the protection of a dry seed. Farmers use this to make seeds come up faster.
14. (match) Match each cause to its effect on a stored seed.  
   **Answer:** Kept very dry → Chemistry almost stops; Kept in damp air → The seed takes in water and ages fast; Kept cold and dry → Slow damage becomes even slower; Frozen while wet → Ice tears the cells. *Why:* Water speeds up the damage inside a seed, and cold slows the damage down. But cold with water inside the seed makes ice, which kills.
15. (perspective) Is a dry seed in a jar alive?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason: a dry seed shows almost no activity. Another: a dry seed germinates with water and a boiled seed never does, so something is kept in the dry seed.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement among scientists. A strong answer uses a fact, such as the difference between a dry seed and a boiled seed. A strong answer also replies fairly to another view.
### Think like a seed scientist (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Plan a fair test of warm and cool soaking water. Say what you change, measure and keep the same.  
   **Answer:** Change: the warmth of the soaking water. Measure: how many seeds have a root at each count. Keep the same: the kind and number of seeds, the soaking time, the cloth and the shelf.. *Why:* A strong plan changes only one variable and measures something that can be counted. A strong plan also uses many seeds and lists three things kept the same.
2. (write) A test finds that 2 of 2 seeds germinated: '100 percent'. Give two reasons not to trust this number.  
   **Answer:** Two seeds are too few: one dead seed would have made the result 50 percent. The two seeds may also not stand for the whole batch.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a small sample swings widely by chance. A sample must be big enough to stand for the batch.
3. (mcq) Two groups of 20 seeds score 95 percent and 90 percent. What is the fairest conclusion?  
   Options: No clear difference: it is one seed / The first group is clearly better / The second group is dying / The whole test failed  
   **Answer:** No clear difference: it is one seed. *Why:* With 20 seeds, 5 percent is a single seed. One seed more or less can happen by chance, so the two groups are about the same.
4. (write) Frozen shop peas do not grow. Dry peas from a jar do. Explain why, using water and ice.  
   **Answer:** The shop peas were full of water when they were frozen, so ice crystals tore their cells. The dry peas hold too little water to make ice, so their embryos are unharmed.. *Why:* A strong answer says that freezing harms only when there is water inside to form ice.
5. (truefalse) In the Freezer Test, cold alone killed the soaked seeds.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dry seeds in the same freezer lived, so cold alone does not kill. The soaked seeds died because cold and water were together.
6. (write) A light shower wakes half the seeds in a field. Then no rain comes. Predict what happens to each half.  
   **Answer:** The half that woke grow roots and then dry out and die, because a seedling cannot go back to waiting. The half that stayed dormant are still alive and can wake at the next rain.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the point of no return, and sees that the seeds that waited save the plant's chances.
7. (mcq) Why is waiting for a heavy rain safer for a desert seed than waking at the first drops?  
   Options: A woken seedling cannot go back to waiting / Heavy rain is warmer than light rain / Light rain washes the seeds away / Seeds wake only at night  
   **Answer:** A woken seedling cannot go back to waiting. *Why:* Germination cannot be undone. After a light shower the soil soon dries, and a seedling dies. A heavy rain wets the soil for long enough for roots to grow deep.
8. (fill) All the living seeds lying in the soil of a place are called the soil seed ____.  
   **Answer:** ['bank']. *Why:* The soil seed bank is why weeds appear in bare soil where nobody sowed anything. Some of its seeds wait for many years.
9. (sort) Sort these. Does each one make a stored seed live longer, or die sooner?  
   **Answer:** drier seeds → Lives longer; a colder store → Lives longer; a packet sealed against damp → Lives longer; warm, damp air → Dies sooner; a cold packet opened in damp air → Dies sooner. *Why:* Water and warmth speed up the slow damage inside a seed. Dry, cold and sealed storage slows it.
10. (count) A seed bank holds 600 seeds of one variety. Each test uses 50. How many tests can the bank do?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 600 divided by 50 is 12. So a bank cannot test too often, and must regrow the variety before the seeds run out.
11. (write) A seed vault is sealed and never opened or tested for 200 years. What might people find inside? Why?  
   **Answer:** Some kinds of seed may still be alive, and others will be dead. Even frozen seeds age slowly, and some kinds last only a few decades. With no tests, nobody regrew them in time.. *Why:* A strong answer says that freezing slows ageing but does not stop ageing. Testing and regrowing are part of keeping seeds.
12. (odd) Three of these seeds stay alive when they are dried. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Moong / Paddy / Wheat / Mango  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Moong, paddy and wheat dry out as they ripen and can be stored. A mango seed dies if dried, so the stone must be sown fresh.
13. (perspective) People once said that wheat from a 3,000-year-old tomb had grown. Is the claim true?  
   **Answer:** The botanist's demand is the strongest. Evidence that would settle it: the age of the grain measured by carbon dating, and a watched germination test on many grains. Real tomb grain has failed such tests.. *Why:* A strong answer separates what could be true from what has been shown, and names evidence that anyone could check.
14. (draw) Draw the line graph you expect: percent germinated over 4 weeks, in a dry jar and in a damp jar.  
   **Answer:** Two lines that start at the same high point. The dry jar line stays nearly flat. The damp jar line falls, slowly at first and then faster.. *Why:* A strong graph has weeks along the bottom and percent up the side. Both lines start together, and only the damp line falls.
15. (match) Match each test result to what it most likely means.  
   **Answer:** No seeds germinate, even in the control → The seeds were dead, or the test went wrong; Every group germinates equally → The changed variable does not matter here; One group is far below the control → The change harmed or stopped the seeds; A few seeds wake days after the rest → Seeds in a batch wake at different times. *Why:* The control group tells you whether the seeds and the test were sound. Only then can a difference between groups be trusted.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What comes out of a germinating seed first?  
   Options: The root / The shoot / A green leaf / A flower bud  
   **Answer:** The root. *Why:* The root, called the radicle, comes out first and turns down. The seedling needs water and a hold on the ground before anything else.
2. (mcq) What feeds a seedling before the first green leaves open?  
   Options: The food store in the seed / Sunlight / The soil / Rainwater  
   **Answer:** The food store in the seed. *Why:* The embryo lives on the food the parent plant packed into the seed. That is why a seed can germinate in the dark.
3. (mcq) Which three things do most seeds need to germinate?  
   Options: Water, oxygen and warmth / Water, light and soil / Soil, light and warmth / Light, oxygen and fertiliser  
   **Answer:** Water, oxygen and warmth. *Why:* Fair tests show that seeds without water, air or warmth do not germinate. Most germinate without light or soil.
4. (mcq) Seeds in a dark box germinate as well as seeds in the light. What does this show?  
   Options: These seeds do not need light / Seeds need darkness / The test was unfair / Light harms seeds  
   **Answer:** These seeds do not need light. *Why:* The two groups differ only in light, and the result is the same. So light does not matter for these seeds.
5. (mcq) 9 of 10 seeds germinate. What is the germination percentage?  
   Options: 90 percent / 9 percent / 19 percent / 45 percent  
   **Answer:** 90 percent. *Why:* Each seed out of 10 is worth 10 percent, and 9 times 10 is 90.
6. (truefalse) A test with 5 seeds gives a steadier answer than a test with 100 seeds.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. With 5 seeds, one dead seed changes the result by 20 percent. With 100 seeds, one dead seed changes the result by 1 percent.
7. (mcq) Why does a dry seed live through a night in a freezer?  
   Options: Too little water inside to form ice / The seed coat keeps the cold out / A dry seed is already dead / The seed grows a thicker coat  
   **Answer:** Too little water inside to form ice. *Why:* Freezing harms cells when the water in them turns to ice. A dry seed is only about one tenth water, held too tightly to freeze into crystals.
8. (mcq) Soaked seeds are frozen, thawed and put on a wet cloth. What is most likely?  
   Options: They do not germinate / They germinate faster / They germinate as usual / They become dry seeds again  
   **Answer:** They do not germinate. *Why:* Ice crystals formed inside the wet cells and tore them. The cells leak and die when they thaw.
9. (mcq) When can a soaked seed still be dried again safely?  
   Options: Before the root comes out / After the root comes out / After the leaves open / At any time  
   **Answer:** Before the root comes out. *Why:* For the first hours the embryo keeps the protection of a dry seed. Once the root grows, the new cells cannot survive drying.
10. (mcq) What is a dormant seed?  
   Options: A living seed that is not germinating / A dead seed / A seed with a root / A seed that was boiled  
   **Answer:** A living seed that is not germinating. *Why:* A dormant seed is alive and waiting. Some dormant seeds wait even when they have water, warmth and air.
11. (mcq) How does a seed bank keep seeds alive for decades?  
   Options: Dries, seals and freezes them / Soaks and freezes them / Keeps them warm and damp / Sows them all at once  
   **Answer:** Dries, seals and freezes them. *Why:* Dry seeds age slowly, and cold slows them further. Sealing keeps damp air out. Soaked seeds would be killed by ice.
12. (mcq) What can a farmer's field do for a crop variety that a freezer cannot?  
   Options: Let the crop keep adapting / Keep the seeds safe from floods / Stop the seeds from ageing / Hold thousands of varieties in one room  
   **Answer:** Let the crop keep adapting. *Why:* Seeds sown every year face each year's weather and pests, and the plants that do well leave the most seed. A frozen seed stays as the seed was.

## Applications
- Seed packets in India print the germination percentage and the date of the test. A farmer uses the number to decide how much seed to sow.
- Seed banks dry seeds and freeze them at about minus 18 degrees Celsius. India's National Genebank in New Delhi keeps more than 4 lakh samples this way.
- Many farmers soak seeds overnight and dry the surface before sowing. The seeds come up faster. This works because a seed can be dried again before the root appears.
- Families store grain and seed with dried neem leaves in sealed pots, tins and bins. Keeping the seeds dry matters most.
- When a flood, a drought or a war wipes out a crop variety, a seed bank can send seeds back. Scientists from Aleppo in Syria rebuilt their collection this way from 2015.
- Plant breeders search seed banks for old varieties that stand salt, heat or disease, and cross them with today's crops.
- Gardeners who turn over the soil soon see weeds, because digging brings buried seeds up to the light.

## Spotted around us
- Weeds coming up in a flower pot where you sowed nothing. They grew from the soil seed bank in the potting soil.
- The label on a seed packet at a nursery or seed shop. The label shows the germination percentage and the date of the test.
- Sprouts in a tiffin box. If dry moong from the shelf sprouts, the seeds were alive and resting all the time in the jar.
- A sealed tin, mud pot or bin of seed grain with dry neem leaves on top, in a store room.
- Lotus pods at a flower market or a temple tank. The dark seeds inside have one of the hardest coats of any seed.
- Paddy or wheat that sprouts on the standing plant when rain falls at harvest time. Once the grains have sprouted, they cannot be stored as seed.
- A bare field or roadside that turns green a few days after the first heavy rain of the monsoon.

## Open questions
- **What is the longest time a seed can stay alive?** The record so far is about 2,000 years, for a date palm seed. Nobody knows the limit, and tests like the buried bottles take centuries.
- **Is anything at all happening inside a very dry, frozen seed?** Any activity is too small to measure with today's tools. So nobody can say whether the seed's chemistry is very slow or fully stopped.
- **How does a waking seed repair damage that built up over many years?** Scientists see repair of DNA in the first hours after water enters. They do not yet know how much damage a seed can repair, or why repair finally fails.
- **Why do some seeds live for centuries and others for only a few years, even in the same freezer?** Oily seeds often age faster, but that does not explain all the differences. Seed banks are still collecting the data.
- **How can seeds that die when dried, such as mango and jackfruit, be stored for a long time?** Scientists can freeze the tiny embryos of a few kinds in liquid nitrogen. For most kinds, no method works well yet.
- **Will seeds frozen today still suit the weather and pests of a hundred years from now?** Nobody knows how fast the climate and pests will change. Nobody knows how well an old variety will cope when sown again.

## Connected maths
- In the Freezer Test, 19 of 20 dry seeds from the shelf germinated. From the freezer, 18 of 20 dry seeds germinated. What is each percentage? Is the difference big enough to matter? **95 percent and 90 percent. The difference is too small to matter.**. Each seed out of 20 is worth 5 percent. 19 times 5 is 95, and 18 times 5 is 90. The difference is one seed, and one seed can fail by chance. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- Look at the made-up Freezer Test chart. By how many percentage points did freezing lower the dry seeds? And the soaked seeds? What do the two answers tell you together? **Freezing lowered the dry seeds by 5 percentage points, and the soaked seeds by 100 percentage points. So the harm from cold depends on whether the seed is wet.**. Dry: 95 minus 90 is 5. Soaked: 100 minus 0 is 100. The same freezer did almost nothing to dry seeds and killed every soaked seed. So cold and water act together. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- A Rag Doll Test shows that a sack of paddy seed germinates at 80 percent. A farmer wants 2,000 seedlings. How many seeds should she sow? **2,500 seeds.**. 80 percent means 8 of every 10 seeds grow. 2,000 seedlings are 250 lots of 8. So she needs 250 lots of 10 seeds, which is 2,500. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In Scoop and Guess, 80 percent of the seeds in the dabba are rajma. In a scoop of 5, which counts of rajma give a guess within 10 of 80 percent? In a scoop of 25? **In a scoop of 5, only 4 rajma gives a guess within 10 of 80 percent. In a scoop of 25, any count from 18 to 22 rajma does.**. Out of 5, each seed is 20 percent, so 3, 4 and 5 rajma give 60, 80 and 100. Only 80 is within 10. Out of 25, each seed is 4 percent, so 18 to 22 rajma give 72 to 88. A bigger scoop has more ways to land close. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- William Beal buried bottles of seeds in 1879. One bottle was opened in 2021, and the last is due in 2100. How long was each wait? How old will a child born in 2016 be in 2100? **The first wait was about 140 years, and the last will be 221 years. A child born in 2016 will be 84 in 2100.**. 2021 minus 1879 is 142, which is about 140. 2100 minus 1879 is 221. 2100 minus 2016 is 84. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- In Three Jars, Three Weeks, the damp jar gave these made-up results. Between which two tests did germination fall most? A seed bank regrows seeds below 85 percent. By which test should these seeds have been regrown? **It fell most between week 1 and week 2. The seeds should have been regrown by week 1.**. The first fall is 95 to 80, which is 15 points. The second is 80 to 45, which is 35 points. The third is 45 to 15, which is 30 points. The line first goes below 85 at week 1, when it reads 80. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
