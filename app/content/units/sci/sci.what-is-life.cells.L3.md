# Cells: the tiny living parts every living thing is made of
`sci.what-is-life.cells.L3`

**Summary.** Every living thing is made of cells: you, a mango tree, an ant and a mushroom. A cell is the smallest part of a living thing that is alive on their own, and most cells are too small to see with your eyes alone. You will look for them in onion skin, using a phone camera with a drop of water on its lens, and test whether boiled curd can still set milk, or only living curd can. Some living things are just one cell, and that one cell senses, chooses and remembers. All life we know is made of cells, but nobody yet knows how the very first cell began.

**Intro.** *Look at the back of your hand. It looks like one smooth thing. In fact it is made of billions of tiny living parts, each one alive on their own. So why can't you see them?* About 360 years ago, an English scientist called Robert Hooke cut a thin slice of cork, the bark used for bottle stoppers. He looked at it through a microscope, a tool that makes tiny things look big. He saw rows of little empty boxes, like a honeycomb. He called them cells, from a Latin word for a small room. Today you will look for these little rooms in an onion from your kitchen. Then you will find out whether the cells in curd are alive.

## Terms
- **Cell**: The smallest part of a living thing that is alive on their own. Each cell is a tiny bag of living jelly, mostly water, with a thin skin all round. *e.g.* The thin skin inside an onion is one layer of cells, side by side like bricks in a wall.
- **Living thing**: Something that is alive. They take in food, grow, make more of their own kind and respond to the world. Every living thing is made of cells. *e.g.* A neem tree, a cat, a mushroom and you are all living things.
- **Microscope**: A tool with lenses that makes tiny things look much bigger, so we can see them. *e.g.* A doctor's lab uses a microscope to look at drops of blood.
- **Magnifier**: Any lens that makes things look bigger. A magnifying glass, a drop of water, or a phone camera zoomed in can all magnify. *e.g.* A drop of water on a clear plastic sheet makes the newspaper letters under it look bigger.
- **Lens**: A clear, curved piece of glass, plastic or water. It bends light so things look bigger or smaller. *e.g.* Spectacles have lenses. So does your eye.
- **Cell membrane**: The very thin skin around every cell. It holds the cell's watery inside in, and lets some things pass in and out. *e.g.* It works a little like the thin skin on a soap bubble, which holds the air inside.
- **Cell wall**: A stiff box that plant cells have outside their membrane. It gives them their straight edges and helps plants stand up. *e.g.* Onion cells look like bricks because of their walls. Your cheek cells have no walls, so they are soft and floppy.
- **Nucleus**: A small round part inside many cells. It holds the instructions, called DNA, that the cell uses to live, grow and split. *e.g.* When you colour onion skin with a drop of iodine antiseptic, a darker dot may show in each cell under a strong lens. That dot is the nucleus.
- **Single-celled living thing**: A living thing that is just one cell. That one cell does everything: they eat, move, sense and make more of their own kind. *e.g.* The bacteria that turn milk into curd are single-celled living things.
- **Many-celled living thing**: A living thing made of many cells that work together, each doing their own job. *e.g.* You, a banyan tree and an ant are many-celled living things.
- **Bacteria**: Tiny single-celled living things. They are found almost everywhere: in soil, water, curd and inside you. *e.g.* Bacteria in your gut help you break down food.
- **Yeast**: A tiny single-celled fungus. They eat sugar and give out a gas called carbon dioxide. The gas bubbles make bread dough rise. *e.g.* The bubbles in dosa and idli batter come from bacteria and yeast living in the batter.
- **Cell theory**: The big idea that all living things are made of cells, and that every new cell comes from a cell that was already there. *e.g.* A seed grows into a tree by their cells making more cells. No cell ever forms out of dust.
- **Stain**: A dye that colours parts of a cell, so they are easier to see. *e.g.* A drop of iodine antiseptic or blue fountain-pen ink on onion skin makes the cells' edges and the nucleus easier to see.
- **Sense**: To notice something about the world, like light, heat, a touch or a smell. *e.g.* A bacterium senses sugar in the water around them, much as you smell food cooking.
- **Neuron**: A nerve cell. Neurons are long, thin cells that pass signals to each other. Your brain has about 86 billion of them. *e.g.* When you touch something hot, neurons carry the message from your finger to your brain in a split second.
- **Millimetre**: A tiny length. Ten millimetres make one centimetre. On a ruler, it is the gap between the smallest lines. *e.g.* A small mustard seed, or rai, is about one millimetre across.

## Explanations
### What a cell is · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell is the smallest part of a living thing that is alive on their own. Each cell is a tiny bag of living jelly, mostly water, with a thin skin all round.

Think of a brick wall. The wall is big, but it is built from many small bricks. A living thing is built in the same way, from cells. But a cell is not a dead brick. Each cell is alive on their own. They take in food, use it, get rid of waste, and can make a new cell. The thin skin around every cell is called the membrane. It keeps the cell's inside in, and lets some things in and out. Most cells are so small that many could fit on the full stop at the end of this sentence.
- e.g. The thin skin inside an onion layer is one sheet of cells, like a wall of bricks.
- e.g. The inside of your cheek is lined with soft, flat cells that keep coming off and being replaced.
- e.g. A single grain of yeast powder holds many yeast cells, each one alive.
### All living things are made of cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living thing is made of one cell or many cells.

In 1838 a German plant scientist, Matthias Schleiden, said that every plant is made of cells. A year later his friend Theodor Schwann said the same about animals. Since then, scientists have looked at millions of living things. Every single one is made of cells: trees, worms, mushrooms, fish and people. Some are just one cell. Others, like you, are made of trillions. A grown-up's body has about 30 trillion cells. A trillion is a thousand times a thousand times a million. Things that were never alive, like stones, glass and plastic, have no cells at all.
- e.g. A mango leaf, a moth's wing and the skin of your finger are all made of cells.
- e.g. A stone or a steel spoon has no cells, because it was never alive.
- e.g. A wooden table is made of the walls of cells that were once alive in a tree.
### New cells come only from cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Every new cell is made when a cell that already exists splits into two.

Long ago, many people thought living things could appear out of mud, or that old meat could make maggots by itself. Careful tests showed this is not true. In 1852 a doctor called Robert Remak showed that new cells form only when a cell splits in two. Rudolf Virchow made the idea famous in 1855, with the Latin words 'omnis cellula e cellula'. It means every cell comes from a cell. So every cell in you came from a cell, which came from a cell, all the way back to the first cells on Earth. The same rule explains why we set curd with curd. A spoon of curd holds living bacteria, and in warm milk they split again and again. Remak was not given the credit he deserved for many years. Part of the reason was that he was Jewish, and universities in his country held Jewish scientists back.
- e.g. You began as one single cell. That cell split into two, then four, then many, and became you.
- e.g. A cut on your knee heals because the skin cells around it split and fill the gap.
- e.g. Curd sets because the bacteria from one spoon of curd keep splitting until the whole bowl is full of them.
### How small cells are · *defined*
**Definition:** Most cells are between a tenth of a millimetre and a thousandth of a millimetre across. That is too small to see with our eyes alone.

Your eyes can just about see a speck a tenth of a millimetre across, like a fine grain of dust. Many plant cells are about that size, so a strong magnifier can show them. Your cheek cells are smaller, about a twentieth of a millimetre. Bacteria are much smaller still. About a thousand of them could sit in a line across one millimetre. To see cells, we need a lens, which makes small things look bigger. A few cells are huge. A single cotton fibre, two or three centimetres long, is one cell. Some nerve cells in your body run all the way from the bottom of your back to your toes.
- e.g. A small mustard seed is about one millimetre across. About ten onion cells fit side by side across it.
- e.g. A cotton fibre from a cotton boll is one single long cell.
- e.g. The yolk of a new bird's egg starts as one single cell.
### How a magnifier shows us cells · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnifier is a curved, clear lens that bends light so a small thing looks bigger.

Light travels in straight lines through air. When it passes at a slant into water or glass, it bends. A lens is curved, so it bends the light from a tiny thing before it reaches your eye. The light then reaches your eye as if it came from a much bigger thing, so the tiny thing looks bigger. A round drop of water is a lens too. The smaller and rounder the drop, the more it bends light, and the more it magnifies. A microscope has two lenses, one after the other. The second makes the first one's picture bigger again. A school microscope can make things look 100 or even 400 times bigger.
- e.g. A drop of water on clear plastic makes newspaper letters under it look bigger.
- e.g. A magnifying glass makes the lines on your fingertip look like ploughed fields.
- e.g. A phone camera with a tiny water drop on its lens can show the faint outlines of onion cells.
### Onion cells: a wall of living bricks · *defined*
**Definition:** The thin skin inside an onion layer is one sheet of plant cells, each with a stiff wall around them.

When you peel the see-through skin from inside an onion layer, you are holding a sheet that is only one cell thick. That is why light passes through it, and why you can see the cells. Onion cells are long, narrow boxes, fitted together like bricks. Their straight edges come from the cell wall, a stiff box that plant cells have around their membrane. Walls help plants stand up without bones. If you add a drop of iodine antiseptic and look with a strong lens, a darker round dot often shows inside each cell. That is the nucleus, the part that holds the cell's instructions.
- e.g. Onion cells look like long bricks in rows.
- e.g. A stained onion cell often shows a dark dot, their nucleus.
- e.g. The thin purple skin peeled from under a Rhoeo leaf, a common pot plant, shows coloured cells in the same brick-like pattern.
### Cheek cells: the soft cells that line your mouth · *defined*
**Definition:** The inside of your cheek is lined with flat, soft animal cells with no walls.

If you gently scrape the inside of your cheek with a clean flat stick, you collect thousands of cells. Under a microscope they look like flat, rough-edged pancakes or fried eggs, often folded. They have no cell wall, so they are floppy and roundish, not boxy. Each one has a nucleus, a darker dot near the middle. A phone with a water drop is not strong enough to show their shape, because they are thin, clear and small. Your mouth sheds these cells all the time, and new ones grow from below to replace them. So you leave a few of your own cells on every cup you drink from.
- e.g. Cheek cells look like flat fried eggs, with a dark dot as the yolk.
- e.g. Animal cells, like cheek cells, have no wall. Plant cells, like onion cells, do.
- e.g. Your skin cells are flat like cheek cells. The outer ones are dead and keep flaking off.
### One cell can be a whole living thing · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living things are just one cell. That single cell eats, moves, senses and makes more of their own kind.

In 1674 a cloth-seller in Delft, a town in the Netherlands, looked at a drop of lake water through a tiny lens he had made himself. His name was Antonie van Leeuwenhoek. He saw little creatures swimming, turning and bumping into each other. He called them 'animalcules', little animals. Nobody had ever seen them before. Many were single cells. Later he even found bacteria in the white gunk scraped from his own teeth. Today we know that single-celled living things are the most common life on Earth. They live in soil, seas, hot springs, ice and inside us.
- e.g. The bacteria that set milk into curd are single cells.
- e.g. An amoeba in a pond is one cell that changes shape as they creep along.
- e.g. The green slime on an old water tank is made of millions of tiny algae, many of them single cells.
### Many cells working together · *defined*
**Definition:** In a many-celled living thing, cells stay together and share the work. Different cells do different jobs.

You began as one cell, about a tenth of a millimetre across, smaller than a full stop. That cell split again and again. As the cells multiplied, they became different kinds. Some became skin cells, some blood cells, some bone cells and some nerve cells. Your body has more than 200 kinds of cells, and they all came from that first one. A tree works the same way. Their root cells take in water. Their leaf cells make food from sunlight. Taken out of your body, most of your cells would soon die. Together, they make you.
- e.g. Red blood cells carry oxygen. Nerve cells carry messages.
- e.g. In a leaf, some cells make food from sunlight. Others form tiny openings, called stomata, that let air in and out.
- e.g. Cells in the root tip of a seedling keep splitting, so the root grows down.
### Brains are made of cells too · *defined*
**Definition:** A brain is a crowd of cells called neurons, which pass signals to each other.

When you think, remember or feel, cells in your brain are sending signals. A neuron is a long cell with branches. Tiny charged particles called ions flow in and out through their skin. This sends a signal along the cell, like a spark running down a fuse. At the end, the neuron releases chemicals that tell the next neurons what to do. Your brain has about 86 billion neurons. That number was counted carefully in 2009 by a team in the lab of a Brazilian scientist, Suzana Herculano-Houzel. So a thought is many cells sensing and signalling together.
- e.g. When you touch a hot tawa, nerve cells carry the message to your spine and you pull your hand back fast.
- e.g. Learning to ride a cycle makes some links between your neurons stronger.
- e.g. An ant's tiny brain has about a quarter of a million neurons.
### Can a single cell think? · *debated*
A single cell has no brain and no nerves. Yet scientists have watched single cells sense, choose and remember. A bacterium called E. coli swims towards sugar. They do this by remembering, for a few seconds, how much sugar they sensed before. If there is more now, they keep swimming. If there is less, they tumble and try a new direction. In an experiment, scientists squirted tiny specks at a trumpet-shaped single cell called Stentor, about one millimetre long. They tried one way to escape, then another, and then swam away. A yellow slime mould, which is one giant cell, found the shortest path through a maze. Scientists agree on what these cells do. They argue about whether to call it thinking.
- e.g. E. coli bacteria swim towards food by comparing now with a few seconds ago.
- e.g. Stentor tried bending away, then closing up, then swimming off when specks kept landing on them.
- e.g. A slime mould in Japan joined oat flakes in a pattern much like Tokyo's railway map.
- **What the cells show:** Every cell has their own kind of mind. Sensing, choosing and remembering are what minds do, and single cells do all three. Brains were built from cells that could already do this.
- **Some scientists who study slime moulds and bacteria:** Sensing, choosing and remembering are the basic parts of thinking. Single cells do all three. So thinking began long before brains, and brains built on what cells could already do.
- **Many brain scientists:** What a bacterium does is clever chemistry, not thinking. Real thinking needs many neurons linked in a brain. Calling it 'thinking' stretches the word too far.
- **Some philosophers:** The question depends on what we mean by 'thinking'. If it means 'using what you sense to choose well', cells think. If it means 'having an inner feeling of thinking', nobody knows if cells have that.
### Wood, cotton and cork: cells that were once alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Many things in our homes are made of cells that have died, leaving their walls behind.

When Robert Hooke looked at cork in 1665, the little rooms he saw were empty. The cork came from the bark of a cork oak tree, and its cells had died. Only their walls were left. Wood is the same. It is made of the strong walls of dead tree cells. Each cotton fibre is one long cell, a hair that grew on a cotton seed. Paper is made from crushed plant cell walls. So a wooden spoon is not alive, but it was made by living cells. A steel spoon was never made of cells at all.
- e.g. A cork bottle stopper is made of empty cell walls.
- e.g. A cotton kurta is woven from millions of single plant cells.
- e.g. A newspaper is made of the crushed walls of plant cells.
### Where the first cell came from · *idea*
All cells come from cells. So where did the very first cell come from? Nobody knows for sure. Fossils of tiny cells show that life began on Earth at least 3.5 billion years ago. One idea is that fatty, soap-like molecules in warm water formed tiny hollow balls, each with a thin skin like a cell membrane. Some of these balls may have trapped simple molecules that could copy themselves. Over a very long time, such balls might have become the first living cells. Scientists can make these hollow balls in labs and are testing the idea. Nobody has yet made a living cell from non-living matter.
- e.g. Soap-like fatty molecules in water can form tiny hollow balls with a thin skin, a little like a cell membrane.
- e.g. Some of the oldest signs of life are layered rocks called stromatolites, built up by mats of tiny cells.
- e.g. Every living thing today seems to come from one shared ancestor cell.

## Mostly agreed
- All living things are made of one or more cells. *How we know:* Scientists have looked at millions of living things with microscopes. Every plant, animal, fungus and bacterium is made of cells.
- Every new cell comes from a cell that was already there. *How we know:* Under the microscope, scientists can watch a cell split into two. Nobody has ever seen a cell form from non-living matter.
- A cell is the smallest thing that is alive on their own. *How we know:* A single cell can take in food, grow and split. The parts inside a cell cannot live on their own if the cell is broken open.
- Plant cells have a stiff wall. Animal cells do not. *How we know:* Onion cells look like boxes with straight edges. Cheek cells are soft and floppy. Stains show the plant walls clearly.
- Single cells can sense their world and change what they do. *How we know:* Bacteria swim towards food and away from harm. Scientists have tracked them under microscopes since the 1970s.

## How we found out
**Robert Remak, who showed it, and Rudolf Virchow, who made it famous, 1852 to 1855.** In 1838 and 1839, Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann said that plants and animals are made of cells. But where do new cells come from? Schwann thought they formed by themselves in a clear liquid inside the body, the way crystals form in salty water as it dries. Many scientists agreed with him. Robert Remak, a doctor in Berlin, was not sure. For years he watched cells in growing chick eggs and frog eggs under his microscope. Again and again, he saw a new cell appear only when a cell split in two. He never saw a cell form in the liquid. He published this in 1852. Three years later, Rudolf Virchow wrote the same idea as a famous Latin line, 'omnis cellula e cellula', which means every cell comes from a cell. Virchow became famous for it, and Remak was mostly forgotten. Part of the reason was that Remak was Jewish, and Prussian universities held Jewish scientists back. He was never made a full professor. Today historians give him the credit.  
*What people believed before:* New cells form by themselves in a clear liquid, like crystals in drying salt water. Remak watched growing eggs for years and saw new cells appear only when a cell split.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Living things can appear by themselves out of mud, meat or rotting food.** (Many thinkers for over 2,000 years, including Aristotle. This idea is called spontaneous generation.; *rejected*)  
  For: Maggots seem to appear on old meat. Tiny creatures seem to appear in water left standing.  
  Against: In 1668 Francesco Redi covered some jars of meat with cloth. No maggots appeared, because flies could not lay eggs. In the 1860s Louis Pasteur showed boiled broth stayed free of microbes if nothing could fall in. New life comes only from life.
- **Only brains can think. A single cell just follows simple chemistry.** (Many brain scientists today.; *debated*)  
  For: Brains do things no cell can, like talking and doing sums. A bacterium's choices can be explained by their chemicals.  
  Against: Our brain cells also work by chemistry and flowing charged particles. Single cells sense, choose and remember. Slime moulds solved mazes and learned to ignore harmless bitter tastes.
- **You have ten times more bacteria cells in you than your own cells.** (A rough guess made in the 1970s and repeated in many books.; *rejected*)  
  For: There really are huge numbers of bacteria in your gut.  
  Against: In 2016, Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs and Ron Milo counted more carefully. They found about as many bacteria cells as human cells, roughly one to one.
- **Leeuwenhoek was just a shopkeeper who got lucky.** (Some scientists in his time, who doubted a man with no university degree.; *rejected*)  
  For: He had no training in science, and he kept his lens-making method secret.  
  Against: He made hundreds of lenses, tested again and again, and wrote careful letters for 50 years. When the Royal Society in London doubted him, he sent signed letters from respected people in Delft who had seen the creatures too. Then the Society saw them for itself in 1677.
- **Plants can sense and respond, almost like animals, using electrical signals.** (Jagadish Chandra Bose, an Indian scientist, about 120 years ago, and some plant scientists today.; *debated*)  
  For: Bose measured electrical signals in plants that looked like signals in animal muscles. Today we know plant cells do send electrical and chemical signals.  
  Against: Plants have no nerves or brain. Many scientists say calling plants 'clever' or 'feeling' goes beyond the evidence.

## Roots
We have been learning that every living thing is made of cells, and that new cells come only from living cells. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Milk with living curd set into curd, but milk with boiled curd stayed runny. New curd bacteria came only from living ones, never from the milk itself. So every cell alive today, yours too, comes from an unbroken chain of living cells. That chain goes back to the first cells, over 3.5 billion years ago.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call a baby, a sprout or a fresh bowl of curd 'new life'. Their cells are new, but none of them started from nothing. Each one split off from a living cell, in a line that has never broken.  
*Evidence:* In the curd bowls, only milk given living curd bacteria set. Boiled curd, with its bacteria killed, set nothing. Remak saw new cells form only when a cell split, and Pasteur showed that boiled broth stays free of microbes if nothing living gets in.

- *who am i:* You began as one cell. All your trillions of cells came from that cell, by splitting again and again.
- *what is life:* Life is made of cells, and every cell comes from a living cell. Your cells are the newest links in a chain that has never broken since the first cells.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of tiny lives we cannot see, in every drop of tank water and every spoon of curd.

Every cell in you came from a cell, and that one from another, back to the first cells at least 3.5 billion years ago. The curd in your kitchen, the neem tree outside and you are all branches of that one line. This line carried more than chemicals. It carried the sensing and choosing that every cell does, and your own awareness grew out of it.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to break open a tiny banyan seed. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing at all.' 'From that subtle essence you cannot see, this whole great banyan tree grows.' (Shvetaketu saw nothing inside the banyan seed, yet the whole tree grows from that seed. Science finds that the tree is built of cells too small to see, each one made by another cell.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra of Umasvati, chapter 2:** Jain thought says every living being has a jiva, a living soul, even beings with only one sense, the sense of touch, like plants. (Jain teachers said that a being with only the sense of touch is still a living soul. Science finds that a single cell, with no eyes or ears, still senses the chemicals and touches around them.)
- **Aristotle, On the Soul (De Anima), Book II:** Aristotle taught that every living thing has a 'soul', meaning a power of life. Plants have the power to feed and grow. Animals also have the power to sense. (Aristotle gave the power to sense only to animals. Cell scientists now find that a single bacterium senses food and swims towards it.)
- Science (settled): All living things are made of cells, and all cells come from earlier cells, going back to a shared first ancestor billions of years ago.
- Science (debated): Single cells sense, choose and keep a short memory. Whether this is the beginning of thinking, or of awareness, is argued about.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India, and many scientists today: A chain of cells is a chain of chemistry. What is passed down is matter and chemical instructions, nothing more. A cell's sensing is chemistry, not awareness, and awareness ends when the body ends. Many careful people hold this view.
- Practice, The city of you (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. Put one hand on your chest and feel your heart beating. Each beat is billions of heart cells squeezing together. Now breathe in slowly. That breath brings oxygen to trillions of your cells. Breathe out. Now just notice: you are one, and you are also a whole city of tiny lives. Sit with that for three breaths.
- Ask: If every one of your cells is alive, which one is 'you'?
- Ask: Curd at home is set with older curd, which was set with older curd. How far back do you think that chain goes?
- Ask: You began as one cell. Was that cell already you?
- Ask: A single cell can sense and choose. Where do you think your own thoughts come from?

## Story: The Trumpet in the Tank Water
*The Foldscope paper microscope is real, and Stentor is a real single cell about a millimetre long. Scientists have watched Stentor bend away, reverse their hairs, shrink and swim off when specks land on them (Jennings 1902, Dexter and others 2019), though not every Stentor does these in the same order. Kavya, Rohan, the school and the temple tank are made up.*

The microscope came in a brown envelope, flat as a chapati. Kavya's teacher, Mrs Nair, had handed one to every child in Class 3. She said only this: 'Look at everything. Then look again.'

Kavya's cousin Rohan was staying with them for the summer holidays. He was twelve, and he knew things. He watched her press out the paper pieces along their dotted lines. 'A paper microscope,' he said. 'Next they'll give you a paper aeroplane and call it a rocket.'

Kavya did not answer. She folded the stiff blue card, tab into slot, the way the picture showed. She pushed the tiny glass bead of a lens into its hole. When she held it up to the window, it was hardly bigger than her palm.

That evening her mother was chopping onions for sambar. Kavya begged one thin, wet layer. From the inside of it she peeled a skin so fine she could see her fingers through it. She laid it on a glass slide, added a drop of water, and slid it in. Then she put her eye almost against the bead and turned towards the tube light.

At first, everything was a grey blur. She moved the slide a little. Then the blur snapped into shape, and she forgot to breathe.

The onion was a wall. Long, pale bricks lay fitted side by side, row after row, each one outlined in a thin, bright line. In some bricks there was a small dark pebble. She moved the slide and the wall went on and on, as if the onion had been built by tiny masons.

'Rohan, the onion is made of bricks!'

'Cells,' said Rohan, not looking up from his phone. 'Everybody knows that. Everything alive is made of cells. You are too.'

Kavya took a clean ice-cream stick and gently scraped the inside of her cheek. She smeared the clear wetness on a fresh slide and touched it with a drop of blue ink. Under the lens floated flat, blue, crumpled shapes, like fried eggs that someone had stepped on. Each had a dark yolk. They were hers. They had been inside her mouth a minute ago.

'These are me?'

'Some of you,' said Rohan. 'Don't get excited. Cells are just bricks. Bricks don't do anything.'

That night Kavya lay awake and looked at the back of her hand in the dark. She thought of the fried eggs, millions upon millions of them, all holding on to each other. Were they just bricks? They did not feel like bricks.

Early next morning, her mother walked with her to the temple tank. The steps were cool and green at the edges. Kavya dipped an empty jam jar and lifted out water, a few threads of green weed, and a little brown cloud of mud. At home she put one drop on a slide and looked.

The drop was a city. Clear slipper-shaped creatures zoomed past, spinning as they went. A grey blob crept along, pushing out a soft arm and then flowing into it. Tiny green beads rolled by. And on a thread of weed, holding on with a narrow foot, stood a pale blue-green trumpet.

Their wide mouth was fringed with hairs, beating so fast they blurred into a shimmer. Specks of mud swirled into the trumpet, like leaves into a whirlpool. Kavya looked at the sheet her teacher had given her. There they were, drawn in pencil. 'Stentor. One single cell. Almost big enough to see with your eyes.'

One cell. No brain, no eyes, no hands. Just one cell, standing on a weed and eating.

Rohan came to look, because Kavya would not stop squeaking. He watched for a whole minute. 'They're like a pump,' he said. 'They just suck things in. A machine.'

Kavya remembered her teacher's other words, written at the bottom of the sheet. 'If you want to know what something can do, ask it a question. Then wait.'

How do you ask a cell a question? Kavya thought about it. Then she took a single straw from the broom, dipped its tip in a pinch of powdered chalk, and held it over the slide. She tapped it once. Fine white specks drifted down into the drop, right over the trumpet.

The trumpet bent away. They leaned, slowly, to one side, like a person ducking from rain.

The specks kept falling. Kavya tapped again.

Now the shimmer of hairs stopped. Then it started again, but the other way round. Instead of pulling specks in, the trumpet was pushing them out, blowing them away from their mouth.

'Did you see that?' whispered Kavya.

Rohan had put down his phone.

She tapped a third time. The trumpet gave up blowing. All at once they squeezed themselves down into a tight little ball on the weed, the way Kavya curled up under her quilt when thunder came.

They waited. The ball slowly opened into a trumpet again. Kavya tapped one last time.

This time the trumpet did something new. They let go of the weed. Their narrow foot came free, and the whole cell turned and swam off, out of the chalky cloud and into clear water, until they were gone from the circle of light.

For a long moment neither of them said anything.

'They tried one thing,' said Kavya slowly. 'They bent. That didn't work, so they tried something else. They blew. Then they hid. Then they left.' She looked up. 'That's what I do when I can't open a jar. I try one way, then another way, then I call Amma.'

'But they haven't got a brain,' said Rohan. He did not say it like a know-it-all this time. He said it like a question.

Kavya thought of the pale bricks of the onion, and the blue fried eggs from her cheek. 'Your brain is made of cells, isn't it?' she said. 'Who taught them how to choose? Maybe they learned it from cells like this one. A long, long time ago.'

Rohan held out his hand for the paper microscope. Kavya gave it to him. He put his eye to the bead and stayed there a long time, looking for the trumpet in the clear water, as if they might come back to tell him more.

**For the parent:** A single cell, with no brain at all, sensed the specks, tried one thing, then another, and chose to leave. Brains are made of cells, so thinking may be older than brains.

- Ask: What did the onion skin look like to Kavya? What were the bricks really?
- Ask: Rohan said cells are just bricks that don't do anything. What did the trumpet cell do that changed his mind?
- Ask: How did Kavya 'ask the cell a question'? Can you think of another way to ask?
- Ask: Kavya said she tries one way, then another, when a jar won't open. When have you done that?
- Ask: Why do you think Rohan stopped sounding like a know-it-all at the end?

### g1 · Cells or No Cells? (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 27 cards. Shuffle them and put them face down.; On your turn, take the top card and read it out loud.; Put it on one mat: LIVING CELLS NOW, CELLS THAT WERE ONCE ALIVE, or NEVER MADE OF CELLS. Say why.; If the others agree, win one cell token. If someone disagrees, both of you give a reason, and the group decides.; If you draw a TEST IT card, everyone does the test. - **s3 (10 min)**

Accept 'living cells now' or 'once alive' if your child gives a reason.
  - Check: Curd from the kitchen. Which mat? → **Living cells now**

  *Child mode:* Look at your fingertip with the magnifier. Can you see cells?
  - Ask: Why can't we see cells on our fingertip with a magnifier? (good: They are too small.; The magnifier is not strong enough.; There are too many layers on top of each other.)
  - Check: A grain of salt under the magnifier looks like a little cube. Is the cube a cell? → **No, salt was never alive, so it has no cells**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the tokens. Then look at each mat together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the 'once alive' mat. Which card surprised you most?  
  *Child mode:* Count your tokens. Look at the 'once alive' mat. Which card surprised you?
  - Think: Is there any card you still are not sure about? How could you find out?
### g2 · Run and Tumble: be a bacterium (15 min)
- **rules:** One player is the bacterium. They close their eyes or wear a loose cloth over them.; The food maker hides a piece of food, like a cut lemon or a sweet, somewhere in the room.; The bacterium takes 3 steps forward in a straight line. This is a 'run'.; After each run, the food maker says 'stronger' if the bacterium is closer to the food than before, or 'weaker' if they are further away.; If it is stronger, the bacterium runs again the same way. If it is weaker, the bacterium tumbles: they spin slowly on the spot, stop, and set off facing a new way.; Count the runs it takes to touch the food. Then swap.
- **Printable cards: Smell cards: STRONGER and WEAKER:** STRONGER; WEAKER
- **Printable score sheet: Runs to reach the food:** Game 1 runs: ___; Game 2 runs: ___; Game 3 runs: ___
- **s1 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear a space with nothing to trip on. 2. Hide the food somewhere at floor or table height. 3. Gently cover the bacterium's eyes, or ask them to keep them shut.  
  *Parent says:* A bacterium is one tiny cell. They have no eyes and no brain. Yet they find food. Let's find out how, by being one.  
  *Child mode:* You are a bacterium. You are one cell. You have no eyes. Can you find the food?
  - Note: Move chairs and sharp corners away, and walk beside the child. A slow spin is enough for the tumble.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the game. After each 3-step run, say 'stronger' or 'weaker'. Count the runs.  
  *Parent says:* Run 3 steps. Stronger or weaker? Remember what it was a moment ago.  
  *Child mode:* Take 3 steps. Listen. Stronger? Keep going. Weaker? Spin and try a new way.
  - Ask: To know if the smell is stronger, what does the bacterium have to remember? (good: How strong it was before.; What it smelled a moment ago.)
  - Check: A bacterium runs, and the food smell gets weaker. What do they do? → **Tumbles and sets off in a new direction**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play twice more. Write the number of runs each time on the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* Did you get better? Why might the bacterium sometimes take a long time?  
  *Child mode:* Play again. Count your runs. Did you get faster?
  - Think: The bacterium only remembers a few seconds back. Would a longer memory help them?
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now about how a bacterium finds food? What happened in our game that shows it? Why does it work?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: 'I think a bacterium finds food by checking if the smell is getting stronger. I saw that I reached the lemon in 6 runs, even with my eyes shut. It works because each time it got weaker I turned, so I kept the good directions.'

## Experiments
### e1 · Onion Skin: is an onion made of tiny rooms? (30 min)
- **question:** Is an onion one smooth thing, or is it made of tiny pieces?
- **predict:** Before you look, draw what you think you will see when the onion skin is made 10 times bigger, and say why you think so. Then draw what you think you will see at 100 times bigger.
- **fair test:** Look at the same piece of onion skin with each tool, in the same light, so only the magnifier changes. Look for at least one minute with each, because eyes take time to settle.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the onion. Iodine stains skin and cloth and must not be swallowed, so a grown-up adds it. Keep water off the phone's charging point. Wash hands after.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the onion skin at the strongest magnification you have. Label one cell, one wall and, if you saw one, a nucleus.
- **results table:** Tool I used | How much bigger it makes things | What I saw (draw or write) | Could I see separate boxes? (yes or no)
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a whole onion. Cut it in half. Let them feel a layer.  
  *Parent says:* This onion looks like one smooth thing. Is it? Or is it made of tiny pieces we cannot see? What do you think?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the onion. Feel a layer. Is it one smooth thing, or made of tiny pieces? Draw your guess.
  - Ask: What do you think the onion is made of, if we zoom in? (good: Tiny pieces.; Lines or boxes.; I don't know, let's find out.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Snap one layer of onion backwards. 2. A thin, see-through skin hangs from the inside. Pull off a piece about the size of a stamp. 3. Lay it flat on the slide. 4. Add one drop of water. Smooth out any folds with a toothpick.  
  *Parent says:* This skin is so thin you can see your finger through it. It is only one cell thick. Let's lay it flat.  
  *Child mode:* Snap the onion layer. Pull off the thin see-through skin. Lay it flat on the slide. Add one drop of water.
  - Note: If the skin folds, float it in a little water and lift it out flat. Bubbles look like black-edged circles. They are not cells.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the skin with your eyes alone, then with a hand lens if you have one. 2. Put a tiny drop of water, the size of a mustard seed, on the phone's main camera lens. 3. Hold the onion skin against a bright window, or over a torch. 4. Bring the phone's lens very close, about 2 to 3 millimetres from the skin, without letting the drop touch it. Tap the screen to focus, and take a photo. 5. Zoom in on the photo.  
  *Parent says:* What changes as we use a stronger lens? When do you first see separate shapes?  
  *Child mode:* Look with just your eyes. Then use the phone with a water drop. Hold the skin up to the light. When do you first see shapes?
  - Note: What to expect. With eyes alone or a hand lens, you will see a clear sheet, perhaps with faint lines. With the phone and a water drop, you will usually see faint, blurry rows of long boxes. It can take several tries. If it stays blurry, move the phone a little closer or further away, use a smaller drop, put more light behind the skin, and hold the phone very still. Use the main camera at normal zoom, because some phones switch to another lens when you zoom in. A Foldscope or toy microscope shows the boxes clearly.
  - Check: Under a strong lens, the onion skin shows long boxes fitted side by side. What are they? → **Cells, the tiny living pieces the onion is made of**
- **s4 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up puts one drop of iodine antiseptic or blue ink on the skin. Wait one minute. Gently dab off the extra with tissue. Look again.  
  *Parent says:* The stain colours the cells. Look again. Do the boxes show up better now? Can you see a dot inside any of them?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up adds one drop of colour. Wait a minute. Look again. Can you see a dark dot inside a box?
  - Note: The stain makes the cells' edges darker. The dark dot, the nucleus, is often too small for a phone and water drop. That is normal. A Foldscope or microscope shows it.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child draws one patch of cells, as big as a page. Label a cell and a wall, and a nucleus if you saw one.  
  *Parent says:* Draw what you see, not what you think you should see. Make it big.  
  *Child mode:* Draw what you see. Make it big. Label one cell and one wall.
  - Think: Every onion in the market is built of these. How many cells do you think are in a whole onion?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think an onion is made of? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: 'I think an onion is made of tiny cells. With the phone and a water drop, I saw long boxes side by side. The onion is made of many small living parts, like a wall made of bricks.'
### e2 · Cheek Cells: are you made of tiny rooms too? (25 min)

- **s3 (8 min)**

  *Parent says:* Look for flat shapes like fried eggs. Can you find a dark yolk in one?

  - Note: Cheek cells are clear and thin, so they are hard to find. Use the stain and plenty of light. If you have no microscope, still make the smear. The faint blue haze on the slide is thousands of your cells. Then look at the third step picture to see their shape.
  - Check: How are your cheek cells different from onion cells? → **They are soft, flat and round-ish, with no stiff wall**

  *Parent says:* What is the same about your cells and the onion's? What is different?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your cell next to an onion cell. What is the same? What is different?
  - Ask: What is the same? (good: Both are cells.; Both have a dark dot.; Both are very tiny.)
  - Ask: What is different? (good: Onion cells are boxes, mine are floppy.; Onion cells have walls.; Mine are rounder.)
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think plant cells and your cells look different?  
  *Child mode:* What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: 'I think I am made of cells like the onion. I saw flat blue cells with dark dots. They were not boxes because my cells have no walls, but onion cells do, to help the plant stand up.'
### e3 · Curd Bowls: does milk need living cells to become curd? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Will boiled curd still turn warm milk into curd?
- **predict:** Which of the three bowls do you think will turn into curd by morning? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same milk, the same amount in each bowl, and the same warm place for the same time. Only change what you add. Use a clean spoon for each bowl, so no living curd gets into bowl C by mistake.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the milk and the curd. Hot milk burns. Bowl A is ordinary curd and can be eaten. Pour bowls B and C away.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all three bowls. Draw each bowl, tilted, to show if it is thick or runny.
- **results table:** Bowl | What I added | Next morning: thick or runny? | How it smells
- **schedule:** day 1: Boil one spoon of curd. Set up three bowls of lukewarm milk: A with living curd, B with nothing, C with the boiled curd. Boil the milk earlier, so it has time to cool. (18 min); day 2: Check the three bowls. Fill in the table. Say what you think, what you saw, and why. (7 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a bowl of curd and a cup of milk.  
  *Parent says:* To make curd, we stir a spoon of old curd into warm milk. Why do you think we need the old curd? What do you think is in it?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the curd and the milk. We add old curd to milk to make curd. Why? What is in it?
  - Ask: What do you think is in the old curd that turns milk into curd? (good: Something alive.; Tiny living things.; Bacteria.; I don't know, let's find out.)
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up puts 1 spoon of curd in a small steel vessel. 2. Heat it on a low flame, stirring, until it bubbles, and let it bubble for half a minute. 3. Let it cool for a few minutes. 4. Label three bowls A, B and C with tape.  
  *Parent says:* I am boiling this spoon of curd. Do you think boiled curd can still turn milk into curd? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up boils one spoon of curd. Then it cools. Can boiled curd still make curd? Why do you think so?
  - Note: Boiled curd splits into lumps and watery whey. That is fine. Let it cool before it goes into the milk. If you did the three bowls in the Habitats unit, tell your child this test is different: there, every bowl got living curd and only the place changed. Here, every bowl is in the same place and only the curd changes.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour the lukewarm milk into the three bowls, about half a cup each. 2. Bowl A: stir in 1 spoon of curd with a clean spoon. 3. Bowl B: add nothing. 4. Bowl C: stir in the boiled curd with another clean spoon. 5. Cover all three, and keep them together in a warm place overnight.  
  *Parent says:* Each bowl gets the same milk, in the same place, for the same time. Only one thing changes: what we add.  
  *Child mode:* Pour milk into the three bowls. Add curd to A. Add nothing to B. Add the boiled curd to C. Use a clean spoon each time. Cover them.
  - Note: The milk should feel warm but not hot on the inside of your wrist. Hot milk kills curd bacteria, and cold milk sets very slowly. In cold weather, wrap the bowls in a towel or keep them in a closed cupboard. They may need 10 hours or more.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Next morning, tilt each bowl gently. Write in the table whether it is thick or runny, and how it smells.  
  *Parent says:* Which bowls set? Is that what you guessed?  
  *Child mode:* Tilt each bowl slowly. Is it thick or runny? Smell it. Write it down.
  - Note: What to expect: bowl A is thick curd. Bowls B and C stay runny. In hot weather, B and C may turn a little sour or lumpy after a day, from a few bacteria that got in from the air. If you have a Foldscope or microscope, look at a tiny dab of bowl A in a drop of water. You may see tiny rods and dots: curd bacteria.
  - Check: Bowl A, with living curd, set into curd. Bowl C, with boiled curd, stayed runny. Why? → **Heat killed the bacteria in the curd, so there were no living cells to multiply**
  - Wait: Leave the bowls overnight, or for 6 to 10 hours.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say three things: what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think turns milk into curd? What did you see in our three bowls that shows it? Why did bowl C stay runny?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: 'I think living bacteria in curd turn milk into curd. Bowl A set, but bowl B and bowl C stayed runny. Boiling killed the bacteria in C, so there were no living cells to make more cells.'

### a3 · The Size Ladder: from you to a cell (20 min)

### Cell Spotter (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a cell?  
   Options: A tiny stone inside living things / The smallest living piece of a living thing / A room in a house / A kind of battery  
   **Answer:** The smallest living piece of a living thing. *Why:* A cell is the smallest piece of a living thing that is alive on their own. We also say 'cell' for a battery. It is the same word with a different meaning. 'A tiny stone' means thinking cells are dead building blocks, but each cell is alive.
2. (mcq) Which of these is made of cells?  
   Options: A steel spoon / A glass marble / A mango leaf / A plastic bucket  
   **Answer:** A mango leaf. *Why:* A mango leaf is part of a living tree, so it is made of cells. Steel, glass and plastic were never alive, so they have no cells. Plastic is made from oil, which came from ancient life, but plastic itself has no cells.
3. (truefalse) All living things are made of cells.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Every living thing ever studied, from bacteria to banyan trees, is made of one or more cells.
4. (truefalse) You can see most cells with just your eyes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most cells are too small to see without a lens. A few, like a cotton fibre or a tomato pulp cell, are big enough to almost see.
5. (mcq) The onion skin looked like long boxes side by side. Why do onion cells have straight edges?  
   Options: They are frozen / They have a stiff cell wall / They are made of glass / The stain makes them straight  
   **Answer:** They have a stiff cell wall. *Why:* Plant cells have a stiff wall outside their membrane. The walls give them straight, box-like edges. The stain does not change the shape. It only makes the walls easier to see.
6. (mcq) Your cheek cells looked like flat, floppy fried eggs. Why are they not boxes like onion cells?  
   Options: They were squashed by your teeth / They are not real cells / Animal cells have no stiff wall / They are dead stones  
   **Answer:** Animal cells have no stiff wall. *Why:* Animal cells, like yours, have a thin membrane but no stiff wall. So they are soft and roundish. Your teeth did not squash them: cheek cells are this shape even before they come off.
7. (match) Match each tool to something it can show.  
   **Answer:** Your eyes alone → a mustard seed; A hand lens or a phone with a water drop → the shiny cells in tomato pulp; A microscope or Foldscope → cheek cells; A very powerful microscope → the tiniest bacteria clearly. *Why:* Each stronger lens lets us see smaller things. Cheek cells need a microscope. Tomato cells are big enough for a hand lens.
8. (sort) Sort these. Made of living cells now, made of cells that were once alive, or never made of cells?  
   **Answer:** an ant → Living cells now; a wooden table → Cells once alive; a stone → Never cells; curd → Living cells now; a cotton shirt → Cells once alive; a cloud → Never cells. *Why:* Ants and the bacteria in curd are alive now. Wood and cotton are made of cells that died. Stones and clouds were never alive, so they never had cells.
9. (mcq) Where do new cells come from?  
   Options: From mud and dust / From the air / From cells splitting into two / From food turning into cells by itself  
   **Answer:** From cells splitting into two. *Why:* Every new cell comes from a cell splitting into two. 'Mud and dust' is the old idea that life can appear by itself, and careful experiments showed it is not true. Food gives cells what they need to split, but food alone never becomes a cell.
10. (fill) A living thing that is just one cell is called a ____-celled living thing.  
   **Answer:** ['single', 'one']. *Why:* Single-celled living things, like bacteria and yeast, do everything with just one cell.
11. (truefalse) A wooden spoon is alive.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A wooden spoon is made of the walls of tree cells that have died. It was made by living cells, but it is not alive now.
12. (order) Put these in order, from BIGGEST to SMALLEST: a bacterium, a big ant, an onion cell, a mustard seed.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* A big ant is about 1 centimetre long. A mustard seed is about 1 millimetre across. An onion cell is about a tenth of a millimetre. A bacterium is about a thousandth of a millimetre.
13. (draw) Draw a group of onion cells as you saw them. Label one cell, one wall and one nucleus.  
   **Answer:** Long boxes fitted side by side like bricks, each with a thin outline (the wall) and a dark dot (the nucleus).. *Why:* A labelled drawing shows that you looked closely and know the parts.
14. (write) Why did we need a magnifier to see onion cells? Use the word 'small' or 'tiny'.  
   **Answer:** Onion cells are too small for our eyes alone. Each is about a tenth of a millimetre wide. A lens bends light so they look bigger and we can see them.. *Why:* A strong answer says how small a cell is, and that a lens makes the cell look bigger by bending light.
15. (write) Name one way your cheek cells and onion cells are alike, and one way they are different.  
   **Answer:** Alike: both are cells and both have a nucleus, a dark dot. Different: onion cells are stiff boxes with walls, and cheek cells are soft and roundish with no wall.. *Why:* Comparing cells shows what all cells share, and how plant and animal cells differ.
16. (write) Meera, a girl in Class 3, says: 'An elephant is huge, so their cells must be huge too.' Is she right? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No. Most cells in an elephant are about the same size as the cells in an ant or in you. An elephant is bigger because they have far, far more cells, not bigger ones.. *Why:* This is a very common first guess. A strong answer says that big living things have more cells, not bigger cells.
### Cell Detective (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Asha set three bowls of warm milk overnight. Bowl A had a spoon of curd. Bowl B had nothing added. Bowl C had a spoon of curd that was boiled first. Only bowl A set. What is the best explanation?

   **Answer:** Living bacteria in the curd split again and again, and only bowl A had living bacteria. *Why:* Cells come only from living cells. The curd in bowl A held living bacteria, who multiplied in the warm milk. Boiling killed the bacteria in bowl C, and bowl B had none added. Bowl B also shows that milk does not turn into curd by itself.
2. (mcq) In Run and Tumble, a bacterium swims along and the smell of sugar gets weaker. What do they do next?  
   Options: Keep swimming the same way / Tumble and try a new direction / Grow eyes / Stop for ever  
   **Answer:** Tumble and try a new direction. *Why:* A bacterium compares what they smell now with a few seconds ago. If the smell is getting weaker, they tumble and try another way. 'Keep swimming' misses the clever part: they remember and compare.
3. (truefalse) A slime mould can be one giant cell, and a slime mould can find the shortest path through a maze.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 2000, Toshiyuki Nakagaki and his team put a slime mould in a maze with food at two ends. In the end the slime mould lay as one tube along the shortest path between the two foods.

   Options: A whole leaf / One cotton fibre / A cockroach / A strand of spider web  
   **Answer:** One cotton fibre. *Why:* Each cotton fibre grows from one cell on the cotton seed. A leaf and a cockroach are made of many cells. Spider silk is made by cells but is not a cell itself. Being thin and flat does not make something one cell.
5. (mcq) Why do cheek cells keep coming off inside your mouth, yet your cheek never wears through?

   **Answer:** Your mouth sheds old cells and makes new ones all the time. *Why:* The lining of your mouth is always shedding old cells, and cells below keep splitting to make new ones. That is why a gentle scrape does not hurt, and why the lining never runs out.
6. (truefalse) A cut on your knee heals because new skin is made out of nothing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The skin cells around the cut split again and again, and the new cells fill the gap. Every new cell comes from a cell.
7. (mcq) A tiny round water drop made the newspaper letters look bigger than a wide flat drop did. Why?  
   Options: It has more water / It is colder / It is more curved, so it bends light more / Small things always look bigger  
   **Answer:** It is more curved, so it bends light more. *Why:* The more curved a lens is, the more it bends light, and the bigger things look. Many people guess a bigger lens always magnifies more. Here the smaller, rounder drop won.

10. (mcq) Which is the fairest way to compare three magnifiers?

### Cell Thinker (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In the 1830s, Theodor Schwann thought new cells formed out of a clear liquid, the way crystals form in salty water. What evidence showed he was wrong?  
   Options: Cells are too small to see / Under the microscope, new cells were seen forming when one cell split into two / Crystals are alive / Salt water has cells in it  
   **Answer:** Under the microscope, new cells were seen forming when one cell split into two. *Why:* Robert Remak watched cells divide in growing chick eggs and showed that new cells come from old ones. Nobody has ever seen a cell form from liquid. A crystal grows by adding pieces on its outside. A cell grows from inside, and then splits.
2. (write) Why do you think cells are so small? Why isn't an elephant one giant cell?  
   **Answer:** Food and air get into a cell through their membrane, their thin skin. In a tiny cell, everything inside is close to the membrane, so food reaches it fast. In a giant cell, the middle would be too far away to get food in time. So big living things are made of many small cells.. *Why:* A strong answer links size to how far food and air must travel inside. A few giant cells, like slime moulds, find clever ways around this.
3. (mcq) A slime mould had to cross a bridge covered in a harmless bitter taste to reach food. At first they crossed slowly. After a few days, they crossed quickly. What does this show?  
   Options: They grew a brain / They got tired / They learned to ignore something harmless, a simple kind of memory / The taste disappeared by itself  
   **Answer:** They learned to ignore something harmless, a simple kind of memory. *Why:* In 2016, Romain Boisseau, David Vogel and Audrey Dussutour showed slime moulds get used to a harmless bitter taste. Getting used to something is called habituation. It is one of the simplest kinds of learning. They could not have grown a brain: they stayed one cell.
4. (truefalse) Many people think all bacteria are harmful. All bacteria make people ill.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most bacteria do us no harm, and many help. Curd bacteria turn milk into curd, and bacteria in your gut help you digest food. Only a few kinds cause illness.

   **Answer:** He wrote very careful descriptions and drawings, looked again many times, and sent signed letters from respected people who had seen the creatures too. Later the Royal Society saw them for themselves. I would show other people how to repeat my test, so they could see it with their own eyes.. *Why:* Science trusts things that other people can check for themselves. A strong answer says how others could repeat what he saw.
6. (mcq) A spoon of curd sets warm milk in about 8 hours in summer. In a cold winter kitchen it takes much longer. Why?  
   Options: Winter milk has no bacteria / Curd bacteria split more slowly when they are cold / Cold milk is too thick / Bacteria sleep only in winter  
   **Answer:** Curd bacteria split more slowly when they are cold. *Why:* Living cells do their work faster when they are warm, up to a point. In the cold, the curd bacteria split more slowly, so they take longer to become enough to set the milk. This is why many families keep the curd bowl in a warm corner in winter.
7. (write) You began as one single cell. Write two things that must have happened for that one cell to become you.  
   **Answer:** The cell split into two, then four, then many, again and again. The new cells became different kinds: skin, blood, bone and nerve cells.. *Why:* Growing means cells splitting, and cells becoming different kinds. A strong answer names both.
8. (mcq) Which of these is an idea that scientists are still testing, not a settled fact?  
   Options: All living things are made of cells / New cells come from cells / The first cells began as tiny fatty bubbles holding molecules that could copy themselves / Plant cells have walls  
   **Answer:** The first cells began as tiny fatty bubbles holding molecules that could copy themselves. *Why:* How the first cell began is still an open question. The fatty-bubble idea is one of the best guesses. The other three have been checked by millions of observations.
9. (mcq) In the story, Kavya dropped chalk specks onto a Stentor, a trumpet-shaped single cell with no brain. They bent away, then reversed their hairs, then shrank, then swam off. Which answer best describes this?  
   Options: Being pushed by the water / Doing exactly one thing for ever / Trying different responses, one after another / Sleeping  
   **Answer:** Trying different responses, one after another. *Why:* Stentor tried a series of different responses. In 2019, scientists repeated this experiment and found that most Stentors did try different things, though not always in the same order. Being pushed by the water would not explain why they changed what they did.
10. (perspective) Scientists watch a bacterium smell food, remember for a few seconds and turn towards the food. They disagree about what to call it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and uses evidence, for example: the bacterium in Run and Tumble had to remember the smell a moment ago and compare, which is more than a switch does. It then answers back fairly, for example from the brain scientists: the turn could come from a simple chemical switch inside the cell, with no choosing at all.. *Why:* This is a real, open argument. Everyone agrees on what the bacterium does. They disagree about the word 'thinking'. Listen for evidence, not for one right answer.
11. (write) A thermostat is the switch that turns off a water heater when the water is hot. It senses heat and acts. How is it different from a bacterium who senses food?  
   **Answer:** A thermostat was built by people, does not eat, grow or make more of itself, and always does the same one thing. A bacterium is alive, takes in food, compares now with a moment ago, and splits to make more bacteria.. *Why:* A strong answer compares carefully and names more than one difference. Whether either one 'thinks' depends on what we mean by thinking.
12. (order) Put these discoveries in time order, earliest first.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Hooke 1665, Leeuwenhoek 1674, Schwann 1839, Remak and Virchow in the 1850s. Each step was built on the ones before.
13. (truefalse) Your body has about as many bacteria cells as human cells.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A careful count in 2016 found roughly one bacterium for each human cell. The old saying, ten bacteria for each human cell, was wrong. Many books still repeat it.
14. (draw) Draw a bacterium finding food by run and tumble. Use straight arrows for the runs and little spirals for the tumbles.  
   **Answer:** Straight arrows that keep going when they head towards the food, and spirals followed by new arrows when they turn away, ending at the food.. *Why:* A model drawing shows the hidden steps: run, check the smell, then keep going or tumble.
15. (write) If every cell comes from a cell, where did the very first cell come from? Say what scientists know, and what they do not know yet.  
   **Answer:** Scientists know that every cell alive today came from an older cell, in a line going back about 3.5 to 4 billion years. They do not know yet how the very first cell began. One idea is that tiny fatty bubbles trapped molecules that could copy themselves. It is still being tested.. *Why:* A strong answer separates what is settled (cells from cells) from what is still open (the very first cell). It is honest about not knowing.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) In 1665, Robert Hooke saw tiny empty boxes in a thin slice of cork. Why were the boxes empty?  
   Options: The cork cells had died, and only their walls were left / All cells are empty inside / His lens was broken  
   **Answer:** The cork cells had died, and only their walls were left. *Why:* Cork is bark from a cork oak tree. The cork cells had died. Living cells are full of a busy, jelly-like inside with a nucleus. Hooke saw only the walls.
2. (mcq) A tiny bacterium and a huge banyan tree are both alive. What do they share?  
   Options: Both are made of cells: the bacterium of one, the tree of billions / Both have a brain / Both are made of wood  
   **Answer:** Both are made of cells: the bacterium of one, the tree of billions. *Why:* Every living thing is made of one or more cells. A bacterium is one cell. A banyan tree is made of billions of cells working together.
3. (mcq) Long ago, people saw maggots on old meat and thought the meat made them. In 1668, Francesco Redi covered some jars of meat with cloth and left others open. What did he find?  
   Options: Maggots grew only on the meat that flies could reach / Maggots grew on all the meat / The covered meat turned into flies  
   **Answer:** Maggots grew only on the meat that flies could reach. *Why:* Maggots hatch from eggs laid by flies. The cloth kept the flies off, so no maggots grew on that meat. Life comes only from life, just as cells come only from cells.
4. (mcq) The thin skin from inside an onion looked like long boxes under the magnifier. What were the boxes?  
   Options: Air bubbles / Onion cells / Scratches / Dust  
   **Answer:** Onion cells. *Why:* The boxes are cells, fitted together like bricks. Air bubbles are round, with thick dark edges.
5. (mcq) Why can we see cells in onion skin but not in a thick slice of onion?  
   Options: The skin is only one cell thick, so light passes through / The skin has bigger cells / The thick slice has no cells / Skin cells glow  
   **Answer:** The skin is only one cell thick, so light passes through. *Why:* Light must pass through the sample for us to see the cells clearly. A thick slice has many layers piled up, and they block the light. The whole onion is made of cells.
6. (mcq) How do you make a water-drop magnifier?  
   Options: Fill a bucket with water and look in / Put one small drop of water on clear plastic, and hold it just above what you want to see / Freeze a drop of water  
   **Answer:** Put one small drop of water on clear plastic, and hold it just above what you want to see. *Why:* A small round drop bends light like a lens, so things under it look bigger. The smaller and rounder the drop, the more it magnifies.
7. (mcq) When you stained them, both the onion cells and the cheek cells showed one dark dot. What is that dot?  
   Options: The nucleus, which both plant and animal cells have / A speck of dirt / An air bubble  
   **Answer:** The nucleus, which both plant and animal cells have. *Why:* Both plant cells and animal cells have a nucleus. The stain makes it darker than the rest of the cell. This is one way the two kinds are alike.
8. (mcq) Which of these is made of living cells right now?  
   Options: A sprouting moong seed / A paper notebook / A leather chappal / A steel tumbler  
   **Answer:** A sprouting moong seed. *Why:* A sprouting seed is alive and growing. Paper is made from the cells of trees that died, and leather from an animal's skin cells that died. Steel was never made of cells.
9. (mcq) A mustard seed is about 1 millimetre across. An onion cell is about a tenth of a millimetre. About how many onion cells fit across a mustard seed?  
   Options: 1 / 10 / 100 / 1,000  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* A tenth of a millimetre, ten times over, makes one millimetre. So about 10. About 1,000 bacteria would fit across the seed.
10. (mcq) What do scientists still argue about when it comes to bacteria?  
   Options: Whether bacteria are made of cells / Whether sensing, remembering and choosing, as bacteria do, should be called thinking / Whether bacteria can swim  
   **Answer:** Whether sensing, remembering and choosing, as bacteria do, should be called thinking. *Why:* Everyone agrees that a bacterium is one cell, and that many bacteria swim. They agree on what bacteria do. They argue about what to call it.
11. (mcq) A bacterium swims towards food. Which of these do all scientists agree that the bacterium does?  
   Options: They sense the smell, remember it for a few seconds and change direction / They see the food with an eye / They plan the whole route like a map  
   **Answer:** They sense the smell, remember it for a few seconds and change direction. *Why:* A bacterium has no eyes and no map. They compare the smell now with a few seconds ago, and keep going or tumble. That much is settled. Whether it counts as thinking is still argued.
12. (mcq) Who first saw living single cells, in a drop of lake water, in 1674?  
   Options: Isaac Newton / Robert Hooke / Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a cloth-seller in Delft / Charles Darwin  
   **Answer:** Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, a cloth-seller in Delft. *Why:* Leeuwenhoek made his own tiny lenses. Hooke named cells earlier, but he looked at dead cork.
13. (mcq) When you set curd, why must the milk cool to warm before you add the spoon of curd?  
   Options: Very hot milk would kill the curd bacteria, so none would be left to split / Hot milk is too thin to set / Curd bacteria only grow in fridge-cold milk  
   **Answer:** Very hot milk would kill the curd bacteria, so none would be left to split. *Why:* Curd bacteria are living cells. Very hot milk kills them, just like boiling the curd does. Warm milk lets them split fast. Fridge-cold milk slows them right down.

## Applications
- Doctors count blood cells under a microscope, in a blood test, to find out if someone is ill.
- Labs look at cells from a swab or a tiny piece of skin to find diseases like cancer early.
- Curd, idli, dosa, bread and dhokla are made with the help of living single cells: bacteria and yeast.
- Farmers and plant scientists look at plant cells to breed better crops, as E.K. Janaki Ammal did with sugarcane.
- Water testers look at drops of water under a microscope to check for harmful single cells.
- Scientists grow skin cells in labs to help people with bad burns heal.
- Engineers have used the rules a slime mould follows to help plan transport and computer networks.
- Paper microscopes like the Foldscope let schools and village health workers see cells cheaply.
- Police and forensic scientists find a person's cells, like cheek cells on a cup, to solve crimes.
- Cotton, jute, wood and paper are all made from plant cell walls.

## Spotted around us
- The thin skin inside an onion in your kitchen, a sheet one cell thick.
- Curd setting overnight, as billions of bacteria cells multiply.
- Idli and dosa batter rising, thanks to yeast and bacteria cells.
- Green slime on the walls of an old water tank or temple tank: millions of tiny algae, many of them single cells.
- A pathology lab in your town, with its microscopes for looking at blood cells.
- A cork in a bottle, the same kind of dead cells Robert Hooke saw in 1665.
- A cotton boll in a field, where each fluffy fibre is one long cell.
- Mushrooms sprouting after monsoon rain, made of many thread-like cells.

## Open questions
- **How did the very first cell begin?** Nobody was there, and no fossils of the first cells survive. Scientists test ideas in labs, like tiny fatty bubbles holding copying molecules, but no one has made a living cell from non-living matter.
- **Can a single cell feel anything?** We can see what a cell does, but not what, if anything, it is like to be that cell. Nobody knows how to test it.
- **Should we call what a single cell does 'thinking'?** Scientists agree cells sense, choose and remember a little. They disagree about where to draw the line for the word 'thinking'.
- **How does a slime mould, with no brain, decide which tubes to keep?** Scientists have good models: tubes that carry more flow grow thicker, and the rest fade. But the full story of how one cell coordinates all their tubes is still being studied.
- **Could there be life on other worlds that is not made of cells?** All life we know is made of cells. We have only ever studied life on Earth.
- **How many kinds of single-celled life are there on Earth?** Most have never been grown in a lab or named. Scientists find new ones in soil, seas and even clouds.
- **Exactly how many cells are in your body right now?** Careful estimates say about 30 trillion for a grown-up, but bodies differ and cells are always dying and being replaced.

## Connected maths
- In a phone photo of onion skin, Meera counted 10 cells side by side across 1 millimetre. How many cells would fit side by side across 1 centimetre? Remember, 1 centimetre is 10 millimetres. **100 cells.**. 10 cells fit across 1 millimetre. 1 centimetre is 10 millimetres. So it is 10 groups of 10 cells. 10 times 10 is 100. So about 100 onion cells fit across 1 centimetre. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In warm milk, a curd bacterium can split into two about once an hour. Start with 1 bacterium. How many will there be after 5 hours? **32 bacteria.**. Every hour, each bacterium splits in two, so the number doubles. After 1 hour there are 2. After 2 hours, 4. After 3 hours, 8. After 4 hours, 16. After 5 hours, 32. A spoon of curd already holds billions of bacteria, so by morning there are far more. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- On the size ladder, each step is 10 times smaller. You are about 1 metre. An onion cell is 4 steps down. How many times smaller than you is an onion cell? **10,000 times smaller.**. Each step is 10 times smaller. 4 steps is 10 times 10 times 10 times 10. 10 times 10 is 100. 100 times 10 is 1,000. 1,000 times 10 is 10,000. So an onion cell is about 10,000 times smaller than you. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- A grown-up's body has about 30 trillion cells. A year has about 30 million seconds. If you counted one cell every second, day and night, about how many years would it take? Hint: 30 trillion is a million times 30 million. **About a million years.**. Each year you could count about 30 million cells. 30 trillion is a million lots of 30 million. So it would take about a million years. (A year really has about 31.5 million seconds, so it is a little under a million years.) (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- In the Run and Tumble game, Farhan took 12 runs to find the lemon. Each run was 3 steps. How many steps did Farhan walk in all? **36 steps.**. Farhan took 12 runs of 3 steps each. 12 times 3 is 36. So Farhan walked 36 steps, even though the lemon was much closer in a straight line. That is how a bacterium moves: lots of small tries. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
